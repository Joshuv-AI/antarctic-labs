Shuffling the Stochastic Mirror Descent via Dual Lipschitz Continuity and Kernel Conditioning
Junwen Qiu, Leilei Mei, Junyu Zhang

Abstract
The global Lipschitz smoothness condition underlies most convergence and complexity analyses via two key consequences: the descent lemma and the gradient Lipschitz continuity. How to study the performance of optimization algorithms in the absence of Lipschitz smoothness remains an active area. The relative smoothness framework from Bauschke–Bolte–Teboulle (2017) and Lu–Freund–Nesterov (2018) provides an extended descent lemma, ensuring convergence of Bregman-based proximal gradient methods and their vanilla stochastic counterparts. However, many widely used techniques (e.g., momentum schemes, random reshuffling, and variance reduction) additionally require the Lipschitz-type bound for gradient deviations, leaving their analysis under relative smoothness an open area. To resolve this issue, we introduce the dual kernel conditioning (DKC) regularity condition to regulate the local relative curvature of the kernel functions. Combined with the relative smoothness, DKC provides a dual Lipschitz continuity for gradients: even though the gradient mapping is not Lipschitz in the primal space, it preserves Lipschitz continuity in the dual space induced by a mirror map. We verify that DKC is widely satisfied by popular kernels and is closed under affine composition and conic combination. With these novel tools, we establish the first complexity bounds as well as the iterate convergence of random reshuffling mirror descent for constrained nonconvex relative smooth problems.



Keywords:
Nonconvex optimization, non-Euclidean distance, random reshuffling, dual kernel conditioning, dual Lipschitz continuity, complexity, last-iterate convergence

Introduction

Consider a nonconvex problem
\min_{x\in\mathcal{Z}}f(x)
over a closed and convex set
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A standard assumption for both deterministic and stochastic methods is the
global Lipschitz smoothness
of
f
over
\mathcal{Z}
, which yields two key properties that facilitate the convergence analysis:

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\sf L}}{2}\,\|x-y\|^{2},\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{Z},

(1a)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq{\sf L}\|x-y\|,\!\qquad\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{Z}.

(1b)

However, the Lipschitz smoothness under
\ell_{2}
-distance may not properly capture the problem structure in many applications. For example, many reinforcement learning algorithms suffer much worse dimension-dependence if
\ell_{2}
-distance is used (instead of KL-divergence)
[
46
,
26
]
, and the D-optimal design problem
[
35
]
even fail to satisfy this condition. More examples include the quadratic inverse problem
[
5
]
, the Poisson inverse problem
[
19
]
, and log-portfolio optimization
[
17
]
, etc.

To overcome the lack of a descent lemma (
1a
), the concept of
relative smoothness
was proposed in
[
3
,
5
,
35
]
, from which the
extended descent lemma
follows:

\big|f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\big|\leq{\sf L}\mathcal{D}_{h}(x,y),

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}_{h}
is the Bregman divergence induced by some kernel
h
, providing a mirror descent update:

x^{k+1}=\argmin_{z}\,f(x^{k})+\langle v^{k},z-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\,\mathcal{D}_{h}(z,x^{k}),

(3)

with
v^{k}=\nabla f(x^{k})
and
0<\alpha_{k}\leq 1/{\sf L}
. As a majorization minimization scheme, a sufficient descent in terms of
\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k+1},x^{k})
is ensured by (
2
), and this
enabled a wealth of work on relative smooth optimization, as well as their vanilla stochastic counterparts, see
[
3
,
5
,
35
,
48
,
13
,
14
,
16
]
.

However, merely the extended descent lemma may not be sufficient to move beyond vanilla stochastic approximation schemes. For many widely adopted stochastic optimization techniques such as momentum
[
33
]
, random reshuffling
[
24
,
36
,
41
]
, and variance reduction
[
27
,
11
]
, a Lipschitz-type bound like (
1b
) is explicitly required for controlling stochastic error, which is not directly available under the relative smoothness framework. To bridge this gap, we demonstrate that if a function
f
satisfies the relative smoothness condition with some constant
{\sf L}
and kernel
h
, then a
dual Lipschitz continuity
holds for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h)
,

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq{\sf L}\,\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{X})}\cdot\rho_{h}(x,y),

(4)

where
\rho_{h}(x,y):=\|\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(y)\|
is a distance in the dual space induced by the mirror map
\nabla h
, and

\kappa(\mathcal{X}):=\;\max\,\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}h(x))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}h(y))}:x,y\in\mathcal{X}\right\}

is the condition number of
h
over
\mathcal{X}
. When
h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, one has
\kappa(\mathcal{X})\equiv 1
. But for non-quadratic kernels,
\kappa(\mathcal{X})
may vary as
\mathcal{X}
changes. To resolve the issue of a varying constant
\kappa(\mathcal{X})
, we note that Lipschitz bound is typically applied to consecutive iterates
x^{k}
and
x^{k+1}
whose dual space distance can be
uniformly bounded
by any predetermined constant
\delta>0
, under suitable step-size. Hence, we expect to uniformly control
\kappa(\mathcal{X})
for any
\mathcal{X}
with
\delta
-
bounded dual diameter
. In this paper, we formalize this intuition and introduce a dual kernel conditioning (DKC) regularity assumption. Combining DKC and (
4
), we can say the gradient
\nabla f
satisfies a
uniform local Lipschitz continuity
w.r.t. the non-Euclidean distance
\rho_{h}
, which is already sufficient for the purpose of bounding errors in many stochastic optimization methods.

In fact, the DKC assumption is very general and versatile. We show that this assumption holds for a wide class of kernels, including Shannon entropy, Burg’s entropy, Fermi-Dirac entropy, exponential kernel, and power kernels. We also prove that the kernel function class satisfying DKC is closed under nondegenerate affine compositions and compatible conic combinations.

Finally, to elaborate the power of the dual Lipschitz bound (
4
) and DKC regularity, we design and analyze a random reshuffling mirror descent (RRMD) method for solving the nonconvex relatively smooth empirical risk minimization problem:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{Z}}\quad f(x):=\frac{1}{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\;f_{i}(x).

(5)

We show that RRMD requires
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1.5})
to find some properly defined
\epsilon
-stationary point.
Using the dual metric
\rho_{h}
, if in addition the objective function
f
is definable, we are further able to prove the full-sequence convergence to a critical point of
f
without necessitating
\mathcal{Z}=\mathrm{dom}(h)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This is in significant contrast to existing results on relatively smooth definable problems that all require the full domain assumption
[
1
,
5
,
30
,
37
]
, which excludes many important kernels like Shannon entropy and Burg’s entropy.

Dual Lipschitz continuity and dual kernel conditioning

2.1
Relative smoothness

In this paper, we default the kernels to be associated with the feasible region
\mathcal{Z}
. Namely, we require the kernel
h\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{Z}))
to be strictly convex and is essentially smooth on its domain
\mathrm{dom}(h)
. By essential smoothness, the iterates (
3
) always remain in the interior
\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{Z})
, but are allowed to converge to a boundary point when necessary.

Definition 2.1

(Relative smoothness)
.

We say a function
f
is
{\sf L}
-smooth relative to the kernel function
h
for some constant
{\sf L}>0
if

{\sf L}h\pm f
are both convex on
\mathcal{Z}
.

If the function
f
is also twice continuously differentiable,
Definition

2.1
can also be restated as
-{\sf L}\nabla^{2}h(x)\preceq\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq{\sf L}\nabla^{2}h(x)
for
\forall x\in\mathcal{Z}
, see
[
35
]
.
Denote

\mathcal{D}_{h}(x,y):=h(x)-h(y)-\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle
the Bregman divergence induced by the kernel
h
, then the extended descent lemma (
2
) holds. In particular, by setting
h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, the relative smoothness reduces to the standard Lipschitz smoothness and the extended descent lemma (
2
) reduces to the standard descent lemma (
1a
).

2.2
Dual kernel conditioning and dual Lipschitz continuity

Denote
h^{*}
the convex conjugate of
h
, it is known that
(\nabla h)^{-1}=\nabla h^{*}
. Then the mirror descent step (
3
) allows an alternative form:
x^{k+1}=\nabla h^{*}\left(\nabla h(x^{k})-\alpha^{k}v^{k}\right)
with
v^{k}=\nabla f(x^{k})
. This corresponds to the original interpretation when mirror descent was first introduced
[
40
]
, where the descent step in fact takes place in the
dual space

\mathrm{Im}(\nabla h)
induced by the
mirror map

\nabla h
, hoping that the dual space can better adapt to the problem geometry under properly selected kernel
h
, see
Figure

1
.

Next, as motivated in the introduction, we establish a Lipschitz-type bound to fulfill the needs for more intricate theoretical tools in stochastic algorithms. First, adopting this dual space perspective of mirror descent, we define

\rho_{h}(x,y):=\|\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(y)\|,
which clearly induces a (non-Euclidean) distance because it is positive definite, symmetric, and satisfies triangle inequality.

Observe that many kernels like Shannon entropy possess a separable structure, i.e., the variable
x
allows a partition
x=(x_{[1]},x_{[2]},\ldots,x_{[m]})
into
m
blocks so that

h(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}(x_{[j]})
,
where
x_{[j]}
denotes the
j
-th block of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
h_{j}
functions are often identical but not necessarily so. Then we introduce the local kernel condition number.

The first illustrates the dual space interpretation of mirror descent. The second illustrates the construction of the integration path
z(t)
in
Lemmas

2.6
and
2.8
, by mapping the line segment between
\nabla h(x)
and
\nabla h(y)
back to the primal space. The third illustrates
\nabla h^{*}(\mathcal{B})
under quartic power kernel, where
\mathcal{B}
is a ball in the dual space. This shows that when
\nabla h(\mathcal{X})
is convex in dual space,
\mathcal{X}
may not necessarily be convex in primal space.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
h(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}(x_{[j]})
be a separable kernel associated with
\mathcal{Z}
, define

\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j}):={\min}_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(x)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j}):={\max}_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\lambda_{\max}\left(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(x)\right),

for any compact set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h)
. Then the local condition number of the kernel
h
over
\mathcal{X}
is defined as
\kappa(\mathcal{X}):=\max_{j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket}\big\{\kappa_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})\big\}
with
\kappa_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j}):={\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}/{\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{j}
denotes the projection of
\mathcal{X}
to the subspace of the
j
-th block
x_{[j]}
, for each
j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
.

By strict convexity and twice continuous differentiability of
h
, the quantity
\kappa(\mathcal{X})
is always well-defined on compact
\mathcal{X}
. Defining
\mathrm{diam}_{h}(\mathcal{Y}):=\sup\left\{\rho_{h}(x,y):x,y\in\mathcal{Y}\right\}
the diameter of a set
\mathcal{Y}
under the non-Euclidean distance
\rho_{h}(\cdot\,,\cdot)
, we introduce the dual kernel conditioning (DKC) regularity.

Definition 2.3

(DKC regularity)
.

We say an
m
-block kernel
h
satisfies the DKC regularity if for any given
\delta>0
, there exists a finite constant
\kappa_{\delta}>0
such that
\kappa(\mathcal{X})\leq\kappa_{\delta}
, for all
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\mathrm{diam}_{h_{j}}(\mathcal{X}_{j})\leq\delta
, for all
\forall j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
.

That is, for any fixed
\delta>0
, every component
h_{j}
is well-behaved with a
uniformly
bounded condition number
\kappa_{\delta}
, as long as it is restricted in a local region
\mathcal{X}_{j}
with
\delta
-bounded
\rho_{h_{j}}
diameter. This constant can often serve as a substitute for global Lipschitz constant in the analysis, enabling the analysis of random reshuffling mirror descent method (
Algorithm

1
). Moreover, the concept of DKC is a natural extension of the kernel conditioning regularity proposed in
[
47
]
that measures the size of local region
\mathcal{X}_{j}
by Euclidean distance and works exclusively for “unconstrained” kernels with
\mathrm{dom}(h)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, excluding various important entropy-based kernels. By leveraging the novel non-Euclidean distance
\rho_{h_{j}}(\cdot\,,\cdot)
, DKC is naturally suitable for characterizing the local behavior of kernels with finite singularity, as illustrated in the following proposition, with verification details presented in Appendix
B
.

Proposition 2.4
.

The following kernels all satisfy the DKC regularity condition:

•

Boltzmann-Shannon entropy
h(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\log(x_{i})
with
\mathrm{dom}(h)=\mathbb{R}_{++}^{d}
.

•

Regularized Burg’s entropy
h(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}-\log(x_{i})+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, with
\sigma>0
and
\mathrm{dom}(h)=\mathbb{R}_{++}^{d}
.

•

Fermi-Dirac entropy
h(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\log(x_{i})+(1-x_{i})\log(1-x_{i})
, with domain
\mathrm{dom}(h)=(0,1)^{d}
.

•

Power kernel:
h(x):=\frac{1}{r+2}\|x\|^{r+2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, with
r\geq 0
and domain
\mathrm{dom}(h)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

As can be observed from the analysis of
Proposition

2.4
, the key motivation of introducing the DKC-regularity for separable kernels in a block-wise sense lies in the potentially inconsistent scaling among different variables. Consider the bivariate Boltzmann-Shannon entropy

h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}h_{i}(x_{i})
with
h_{i}(x_{i}):=x_{i}\log x_{i}
, then
\nabla^{2}h(x)=\mathrm{diag}(x_{1}^{-1},x_{2}^{-1})
. Grouping
(x_{1},x_{2})
as one whole block yields

\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}h(x))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}h(x))}=\frac{\max\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}{\min\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}\to+\infty\quad\text{if}\quad\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\to+\infty.

That is, the kernel condition number can be huge even for singletons
\mathcal{X}=\{x\}
(hence
\mathrm{diam}_{h}(\mathcal{X})=0
), when
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
has largely different magnitudes. On the contrary, treating the kernel block-wise,
Proposition

2.4
demonstrates the well-behavedness of the kernel on each block. Nevertheless, if the blocks do not diverge excessively, the blocks can still be stacked together as a larger block without encountering pathological condition numbers, which will be useful in the analysis of
Lemma

2.7
.

Next, we introduce a desirable closedness property for the class of DKC-regular functions. For matrix
A
, denote
\sigma_{\min}(A)
and
\sigma_{\max}(A)
the minimum and maximum singular values, and denote
\kappa_{A}:=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}
the matrix condition number. The next proposition shows that the class of DKC-regular functions are well-posed and stay robust under various common operations.

Proposition 2.5

(Closedness of DKC regularity)
.

The DKC regularity is closed under scaling, compatible affine composition and conic combination in the following sense:

(a)
. Let
h
be a kernel with
m\geq 1
blocks,
A=\mathrm{Diag}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If each
A_{j}
is square and non-singular with size compatible to
x_{[j]}
, then the composition

\phi(x):=h(Ax+b)
satisfies DKC with

\kappa_{\phi,\delta}\leq\max_{j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket}\{\kappa_{A_{j}}^{2}\cdot\kappa_{h,{\delta}/{\sigma_{\min}(A_{j})}}\}
.

(b)
. Let
h
and
g
be two compatible kernels in the sense that
h
and
g
share the same block structure and
\langle\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(y),\nabla g(x)-\nabla g(y)\rangle\geq 0
. Then the conic combination
\phi(x):=\alpha h(x)+\beta g(y)
,
\alpha,\beta>0
satisfies DKC with

\kappa_{\phi,\delta}\leq\max\left\{\kappa_{h,{\delta}/{\alpha}},\kappa_{g,{\delta}/{\beta}}\right\}
.

It can be verified that all the kernels mentioned in
Proposition

2.4
satisfies the conditions of
Proposition

2.5
, and are hence closed under the corresponding operations.

Proof of (a).

As
h
can be separated into
m
blocks, it is then expressed as
h(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}(x_{[j]}).

Noting that
A=\mathrm{Diag}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})
with each
A_{j}
being square and non-singular with size compatible to
x_{[j]}
, we can further write the composition
\phi(x):=h(Ax+b)
as:

\phi(x)={\sum}_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}(A_{j}x_{[j]}+b_{[j]}).

We next focus on the
j
-th block. Define
\phi_{j}(z):=h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]})
, then

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=A_{j}^{\top}\nabla h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]}),\quad\nabla^{2}\phi_{j}(z)=A_{j}^{\top}\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]})A_{j}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\rho_{\phi_{j}}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\Big\|A_{j}^{\top}\big(\nabla h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]})-\nabla h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]})\big)\Big\|.

Let the set
\mathcal{X}_{j}
, which is compatible to
x_{[j]}
, satisfy
\mathrm{diam}_{h_{j}}(\mathcal{X}_{j})\leq\delta
under the metric
\rho_{\phi_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot)
. Thus, for all
y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta\geq\rho_{\phi_{j}}(y,z)\geq\sigma_{\min}(A_{j})\cdot\|\nabla h_{j}(A_{j}y+b_{[j]})-\nabla h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]})\|.

Since
h_{j}
fulfills DKC, based on the above relation, it holds that

\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}y+b_{[j]}))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]}))}\leq\kappa_{h,\delta}.

(6)

Consider the condition number of
\phi_{j}
on
\mathcal{X}_{j}
. It readily follows from (
6
) that

\displaystyle\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}\phi_{j}(y))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\phi_{j}(z))}

\displaystyle=\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{j}^{\top}\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}y+b_{[j]})A_{j})}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{j}^{\top}\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]})A_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A_{j})\sigma_{\max}(A_{j}^{\top})\cdot\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}y+b_{[j]}))}{\sigma_{\min}(A_{j})\sigma_{\min}(A_{j}^{\top})\cdot\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(A_{j}z+b_{[j]}))}\leq\kappa_{A_{j}}^{2}\cdot\kappa_{h,\delta}.\qquad\Box

Proof of (b).

Due to the shared block structure of
h
and
g
, we can decompose
h,g
and
\phi
as

h(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}(x_{[j]}),\quad g(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(x_{[j]})\quad\text{and}\quad\phi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{j}(x_{[j]})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(\alpha h_{j}(x_{[j]})+\beta g_{j}(x_{[j]})\big),

where the integer
m\geq 1
. We analyze the
j
-th block, whose result can extend to all blocks. Let the set
\mathcal{X}_{j}
satisfy
\mathrm{diam}_{h_{j}}(\mathcal{X}_{j})\leq\delta
under the metric
\rho_{\phi_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot)
. It then follows for all
y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
that

\displaystyle\delta^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{\phi_{j}}^{2}(y,z)=\|\nabla\phi_{j}(y)-\nabla\phi_{j}(z)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\alpha(\nabla h_{j}(y)-\nabla h_{j}(z))+\beta(\nabla g_{j}(y)-\nabla g_{j}(z))\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\|\nabla h_{j}(y)-\nabla h_{j}(z)\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|\nabla g_{j}(y)-\nabla g_{j}(z)\|^{2}+2\alpha\beta\langle\nabla h_{j}(y)-\nabla h_{j}(z),\nabla g_{j}(y)-\nabla g_{j}(z)\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\alpha^{2}\rho_{h_{j}}^{2}(y,z)+\beta^{2}\rho_{g_{j}}^{2}(y,z),

where the last line is due to
\langle\nabla h_{j}(y)-\nabla h_{j}(z),\nabla g_{j}(y)-\nabla g_{j}(z)\rangle\geq 0
. Hence, we have
\rho_{h_{j}}(y,z)\leq\delta/\alpha
and
\rho_{g_{j}}(y,z)\leq\delta/\beta
for any
y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
. Recall that both
h
and
g
satisfy DKC,

\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(y))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(z))}\leq\kappa_{h,\delta/\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}g_{j}(y))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}g_{j}(z))}\leq\kappa_{g,\delta/\beta}.

We now consider the condition number of
\phi_{j}
on
\mathcal{X}_{j}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}\phi_{j}(y))}{\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\phi_{j}(z))}=\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\frac{\alpha\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(y))+\beta\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}g_{j}(y))}{\alpha\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}h_{j}(z))+\beta\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}g_{j}(z))}\leq\max\{\kappa_{h,\delta/\alpha},\kappa_{g,\delta/\beta}\},

where we utilize
\frac{a+b}{c+d}\leq\max\{\frac{a}{c},\frac{b}{d}\}
to obtain the final inequality.
\Box

2.3
Dual Lipschitz continuity

Based on the previous preparation, we are able to provide the dual Lipschitz continuity bound under the
\rho_{h}
distance in the dual space of mirror descent.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
f
is
{\sf L}
-smooth relative to
h
with
m=1
block. Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h)
with image
\nabla h(\mathcal{X})
being convex in the dual space. Then

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq{\sf L}\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{X})}\cdot\rho_{h}(x,y)
, for
\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

By default, we consider the single block case
m=1
. According to
Definition

2.1
, we have

-{\sf L}I\preceq H_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\nabla^{2}f(x)\,H_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\preceq{\sf L}I\qquad\mbox{for}\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{Z},

where
H_{x}:=\nabla^{2}h(x)
. Then for any
x\in\mathcal{Z}
, we can observe that

\big\|\nabla^{2}f(x)H_{x}^{-1}\big\|\leq\big\|H_{x}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|\big\|H_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla^{2}f(x)H_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big\|\big\|H_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big\|\leq{\sf L}\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{X})}.

(7)

Consider the parameterized curve
z(t):=\nabla h^{*}(\nabla h(x)+t\cdot u)
,
t\in[0,1]
, with
u:=\nabla h(y)-\nabla h(x)
. Clearly,
z(0)=x
and
z(1)=y
. Due to the convexity of
\nabla h(\mathcal{X})
, the whole curve segment belong to
\mathcal{X}
, see
Figure

1
. Then using
\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(z(t))^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(z(t))\nabla^{2}h^{*}(\nabla h(x)+tu)u\mathrm{d}t\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\big\|\nabla^{2}f(z(t))\nabla^{2}h^{*}(\nabla h(z(t)))\big\|\mathrm{d}t\cdot\|u\|

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
\nabla h(x)+tu=\nabla h(z(t))
. Using the fact that
\nabla^{2}h^{*}(w)=[\nabla^{2}h(x)]^{-1}
with
x=\nabla h^{*}(w)
and (
7
), we further obtain that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big\|\nabla^{2}f(z(t))[\nabla^{2}h(z(t))]^{-1}\big\|\mathrm{d}t\cdot\|u\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\sf L}\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{X})}\cdot\|u\|.

Then substituting
\rho_{h}(x,y)=\|\nabla h(y)-\nabla h(x)\|
to the above inequality proves the lemma.
∎

As corollary of this lemma, if
f
is
{\sf L}
-smooth relative to a DKC-regular kernel
h
. Then fix any constant
\delta
such that the corresponding
\kappa_{\delta}
is mild, then for
\forall x,y\in\mathrm{int\ dom}(h)
, we have

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq{\sf L}\sqrt{\kappa_{\delta}}\cdot\rho_{h}(x,y)\quad\mbox{if}\quad\rho_{h}(x,y)\leq\delta.

(8)

That is, although
\nabla f(\cdot)
of a relatively smooth function may not be Lipschitz continuous under
\ell_{2}
-distance, it is locally Lipschitz continuous under a non-Euclidean
\rho_{h}
distance with a uniformly bounded local Lipschitz constant. This gives us a better intuition of why the mirror descent can better adapt to the local geometry of the relative smooth optimization problems, providing an appropriate substitute of (
1b
) in a more general non-Euclidean setting.
If
h
has
m\geq 2
blocks, the situation is slightly more complicated, and is characterized by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose
f
is
{\sf L}
-smooth relative to
h
with
m\geq 2
blocks. Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}_{m}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{j}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h_{j}),j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
. If both
\nabla h_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})
and
\mathcal{X}_{j}
are convex for all
j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
, then

\sum_{j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket}\frac{\|\nabla_{[j]}\;f(x)-\nabla_{[j]}f(y)\|^{2}}{\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}\leq{\sf L}^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\mathcal{X})\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{X})\sum_{j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket}\frac{\rho_{h_{j}}^{2}(x_{[j]},y_{[j]})}{\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})},\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},

where
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})=\min\Big\{8m,8e\Big[2+3\ln\Big(\frac{\max_{j}\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}{\min_{j}\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}\Big)\Big]\Big\}^{1/2}
is a finite constant.

The proof is moved to Appendix
C.1
for succinctness. This time, we assume both
\mathcal{X}_{j}
and
\nabla h_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})
to be convex. It can be verified that all the separable kernels in
Proposition

2.4
satisfy this assumption. Finally, we end this section with a technical lemma that is used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.8
.

For
\forall j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
, suppose
\mathcal{X}_{j}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h_{j})
and the image
\nabla h_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})
is convex, then

\frac{\rho_{h_{j}}(x_{[j]},y_{[j]})^{2}}{2\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}\leq\mathcal{D}_{h_{j}}(y_{[j]},x_{[j]})\leq\frac{\rho_{h_{j}}(x_{[j]},y_{[j]})^{2}}{2\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})}\quad\text{and}\quad\|x_{[j]}-y_{[j]}\|\leq\frac{\rho_{h_{j}}(x_{[j]},y_{[j]})}{\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})},\quad\forall x_{[j]},y_{[j]}\in\mathcal{X}_{j}.

Proof.

In the proof, let us assume
h
has
m=1
block without loss of generality. If
m\geq 2
, it suffices to repeat the same process for each
h_{j}
. Therefore, we will omit the subscript
[j]
or
j
. Now, define
g(t):=\mathcal{D}_{h}(z(t),x)
,
t\in[0,1]
, with
z(t)
being the same parameterized in the proof of
Lemma

2.6
. Clearly,
g(0)=\mathcal{D}_{h}(x,x)=0
and
g(1)=\mathcal{D}_{h}(y,x)
. Then direct computation gives

\mathcal{D}_{h}(y,x)=g(1)-g(0)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(t)\;\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}t\langle\nabla h(y)-\nabla h(x),\dot{z}(t)\rangle\;\mathrm{d}t.

(9)

Again, using
\nabla^{2}h^{*}(w)=[\nabla^{2}h(\nabla h^{*}(w))]^{-1}
we get

\dot{z}(t)=[\nabla^{2}h(z(t))]^{-1}(\nabla h(y)-\nabla h(x))
and hence

\frac{\rho_{h}(x,y)^{2}}{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\langle\nabla h(y)-\nabla h(x),\dot{z}(t)\rangle\leq\frac{\rho_{h}(x,y)^{2}}{\mu(\mathcal{X})}.

(10)

Combining (
9
) and (
10
) proves
\frac{\rho_{h}(x,y)^{2}}{2\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\mathcal{D}_{h}(y,x)\leq\frac{\rho_{h}(x,y)^{2}}{2\mu(\mathcal{X})}
. Next, by
y-x=\int_{0}^{1}\dot{z}(t)\mathrm{d}t
, we have

\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\|x-y\|\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{z}(t)\|\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\rho_{h}(x,y)\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\big\|[\nabla^{2}h(z(t))]^{-1}\big\|\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\rho_{h}(x,y)}{\mu(\mathcal{X})},

which completes the proof.
∎

3
Algorithm and preliminary properties

After introducing the tools of DKC-regularity and dual Lipschitz continuity, we illustrate how they can be used to analyze broader class of algorithms under relative smoothness by considering the
random reshuffling mirror descent (RRMD,
Algorithm

1
). In each epoch, RRMD generates a permutation
\pi^{k}
of
\llbracket n\rrbracket
and then performs mirror descent update (
11
) incrementally following the permuted data order. The permutation
\pi^{k}
introduces a (biased) without-replacement sampling scheme to RRMD, and is the key difference to SMD that adopts an unbiased with-replacement sampling scheme, see
[
39
,
20
]
.

In particular, when the permutations
\pi^{k}\equiv\pi^{1}
remains identical for all
k
, we call
Algorithm

1
the
incremental mirror descent
method (IMD). This also covers the case where the data points are shuffled only once at the beginning of the algorithm. We say that the permutation
\pi^{k}
is generated by
uniform shuffling
scheme if
\{\pi^{k}_{1},\ldots,\pi^{k}_{n}\}
are sampled
uniformly without replacement
from
\llbracket n\rrbracket
.

Algorithm 1

Random Reshuffling Mirror Descent (RRMD)

Input:
kernel function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, step-size
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}
, initial point
x^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

repeat

generate a permutation
\pi^{k}=\{\pi^{k}_{1},\ldots,\pi^{k}_{n}\}
of
\llbracket n\rrbracket:=\{1,\ldots,n\}

set
x^{k,1}=x^{k}

for

i=1

to

n

do

x^{k,i+1}\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\langle u,\nabla f_{\pi^{k}_{i}}(x^{k,i})\rangle+\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\mathcal{D}_{h}(u,x^{k,i})\right\}

(11)

end

for

set
x^{k+1}=x^{k,n+1}

until
termination condition meets

By the dual space interpretation of mirror descent and the essential smoothness of
h
on
\mathcal{Z}
, the iterations of
Algorithm

1
will also satisfy the following relationships:

\nabla h(y^{k,i+1})=\nabla h(y^{k,i})-\alpha_{k}\nabla f_{\pi_{i}^{k}}(y^{k,i})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla h(x^{k+1})=\nabla h(x^{k})-\alpha_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f_{\pi_{i}^{k}}(y^{k,i}).

(12)

3.1
Assumptions

To simplify the notation and presentation, from now on, we will default the discussion to the single-block case
m=1
, which allows us to drop all the subscripts in the block notations
x_{[j]},\mathcal{X}_{j},\mu_{j}(\cdot),\mathcal{L}_{j}(\cdot)
as well as the frequently appearing summation over
j\in\llbracket m\rrbracket.
This will greatly shorten expressions in the inequalities and will provide better readability and clarity, while all the proof can be parallelly extended to the general
m
-block kernel cases by directly adapting the proof to a block-by-block style.
Next, we formally introduce a few basic assumptions.

Assumption 3.1
.

There is a DKC-regular and
\mu
-strongly convex kernel
h
associated with the feasible region
\mathcal{Z}
and a constant
{\sf L}>0
so that each
f_{i}
is
{\sf L}
-smooth relative to
h
.

The relative smoothness condition (
3.1
) has become the standard framework for analyzing iterative methods that go beyond global Lipschitz continuity; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
3
,
5
,
30
,
35
]
. The validity of the DKC regularity has been extensively discussed.

Assumption 3.2
.

There is an
f^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f
is lower bounded by
f^{*}
on
\mathcal{Z}
. We also assume there exists a constant
{\sf G}>0
such that

\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|\leq{\sf G}
for all
x\in\mathcal{Z}
and
i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket
.

We impose the bounded gradient assumption to clearly demonstrate how dual Lipschitz continuity and DKC regularity can be leveraged to establish complexity results and last-iterate convergence.
3.2
helps isolate the key theoretical contributions from additional technical complications that arise when handling unbounded gradients. In
Section

6.1
, we also discuss how this assumption can be substantially relaxed. And the proof sketch is demonstrated therein.

3.2
Preliminary properties

Based on the preliminary discussions, we establish the elementary properties of RRMD.
First, we introduce the set
\mathcal{X}^{k}
and the upper bound
\bar{\alpha}
of the step-size:

\mathcal{X}^{k}:=\big\{u\in\mathrm{int\ dom}(h):\rho_{h}(u,x^{k})\leq\delta/2\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\alpha}:=\min\Big\{\frac{1}{2{\sf L}\kappa_{\delta}},\frac{\delta}{2{\sf G}}\Big\}.

(13)

Therefore, using the update formula (
12
) and
3.2
, it is clear that
0<\alpha_{k}\leq\bar{\alpha}/n
indicates

\|\nabla h(y^{k,i+1})-\nabla h(x^{k})\|\leq\alpha_{k}{\sum}_{j=1}^{i}\|\nabla f_{\pi^{k}_{j}}(y^{k,j})\|\leq\delta/(2n{\sf G})\cdot i{\sf G}\leq\delta/2,\qquad\forall i\leq n.

Then using
3.1
, we
obtain the following lemma that characterizes the update region of RRMD in each epoch and the condition number of the kernel
h
on this region.

Lemma 3.3
.

Given
3.1
and
3.2
, and let
\{x^{k}\},\{y^{k,i}\}
be generated by
Algorithm

1
with step-sizes
0<\alpha_{k}\leq\bar{\alpha}/n
. Then
y^{k,i}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
for all
i\in\llbracket n+1\rrbracket
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\kappa(\mathcal{X}^{k})\leq\kappa_{\delta}
.

That is, as long as
\alpha_{k}
is bounded above by
\bar{\alpha}
, the entire epoch remains within a reasonable region
\mathcal{X}^{k}
, on which the condition number of DKC-regular kernel
h
is uniformly controlled by
\kappa_{\delta}
. Next, we present the preliminary descent of the algorithm.

Lemma 3.4
.

Under the same setting of
Lemma

3.3
, the iterates generated by
Algorithm

1
satisfies

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})}{2n\alpha_{k}}\leq f(x^{k})+\underbracket{\frac{\kappa_{\delta}\cdot\alpha_{k}}{2\mu(\mathcal{X}^{k})}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla f_{\pi^{k}_{i}}(x^{k})-\nabla f_{\pi^{k}_{i}}(y^{k,i})\|^{2}}_{=:\mathcal{E}_{k}}.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Appendix
C.2
. It provides the preliminary descent-type property characterized by the Bregman difference
\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})
.

3.3
Stationarity measure

Different from the deterministic setting
[
3
,
5
]
,
\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})
here does not directly relate to the gradient. Therefore, we introduce the following stationarity measure tailored for stochastic mirror descent methods.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by
Algorithm

1
with step-size
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}
, we define

\mathcal{G}(x^{k}):=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},\hat{x}^{k})}{(n\alpha_{k})^{2}}\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{x}^{k}:=\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{Z}}\Big\{\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),u\rangle+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{h}(u,x^{k})}{n\alpha_{k}}\Big\}.

(14)

When
h(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, stationarity measure
\mathcal{G}(x^{k})
reduces to
\|\nabla f(x^{k})\|^{2}
. Then, we establish a crucial relationship between
\mathcal{G}(x^{k})
and
\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})
. The proof can be found in Appendix
C.3
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Under the setting of
Lemma

3.3
, we have
\hat{x}^{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
and

\frac{\|\nabla f(x^{k})\|^{2}}{\mu(\mathcal{X}^{k})}\leq 2\kappa_{\delta}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}(x^{k})\leq 2\kappa_{\delta}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})}{(n\alpha_{k})^{2}}+\frac{2\mathcal{E}_{k}}{n\alpha_{k}}.

3.4
Error estimates and approximate descent property

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathbb{P})
be the underlying probability space of the stochastic process
\{x^{k}\}
generated by
Algorithm

1
, where the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{k})
has a deterministic initial point
x^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. With
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{k}]
denoting the conditional expectation, and the fact that
\mu(\mathcal{X}^{k})\geq\mu
due to
3.1
, we bound the stochastic error
\mathcal{E}_{k}
, with derivation in Appendix
C.4
.

Proposition 3.7

(Stochastic error bound)
.

Consider the same setting of
Lemma

3.3
.

(a)

Then, for arbitrary permutation
\pi^{k}
, it holds deterministically that

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq\frac{(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{2\mu}\cdot n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

(b)

Moreover, if
\pi^{k}
is generated by uniform shuffling scheme, it holds in expectation that

\mathbb{E}_{k}[\mathcal{E}_{k}]\leq 4{\sf L}^{2}(\kappa_{\delta}n\alpha_{k})^{3}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})+\frac{4(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{\mu}\cdot n^{2}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

Combining the previous results, we obtain the approximate descent property for RRMD.

Proposition 3.8

(Approximate descent)
.

Consider the same setting of
Lemma

3.3
.

(a)

Then, for arbitrary permutation
\pi^{k}
, it holds deterministically that

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})}{2n\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})+\frac{(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{2\mu}\cdot n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3},

(15a)

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})+\frac{n\alpha_{k}}{4\kappa_{\delta}}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})+\frac{3(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{4\mu}\cdot n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

(15b)

(b)

Moreover, if
\pi^{k}
is generated by uniform shuffling scheme, and we require in addition that
\alpha_{k}\leq 1/(7\kappa_{\delta}^{2}n{\sf L})
, then it holds in expectation that

\mathbb{E}[f(x^{k+1})]+\frac{n\alpha_{k}}{8\kappa_{\delta}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(x^{k})]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(x^{k})]+\frac{6(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{\mu}\cdot n^{2}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

(16)

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.4
and
Proposition

3.7
(a), we have

f(x^{k+1})+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{h}(x^{k},x^{k+1})}{2n\alpha_{k}}\leq f(x^{k})+\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq f(x^{k})+\frac{(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{2\mu}\cdot n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3},

proving (

). Together with
Lemma

3.6
and
\kappa_{\delta}\geq 1
, the above inequality also implies

f(x^{k+1})+\frac{n\alpha_{k}}{4\kappa_{\delta}}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})\leq f(x^{k})+\frac{3}{2}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{k}.

Applying
Proposition

3.7
(a) to the above inequality, we establish (

). Next, taking conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]
on the above inequality, and using

3.6
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}[f(x^{k+1})]+\frac{n\alpha_{k}}{4\kappa_{\delta}}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})\leq f(x^{k})+6\kappa_{\delta}^{3}{\sf L}^{2}n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})+\frac{6(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{\mu}\cdot n^{2}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

As
\alpha_{k}\leq 1/(7n{\sf L}\kappa_{\delta}^{2})
, we know

\mathbb{E}_{k}[f(x^{k+1})]+\frac{n\alpha_{k}}{8\kappa_{\delta}}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})\leq f(x^{k})+\frac{6(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{\mu}\cdot n^{2}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

∎

4
Complexity bounds

In this section, we derive the sample complexity for RRMD method under various sampling schemes for generating the permutations
\{\pi^{k}\}
. First, we consider the general arbitrary permutation that includes the incremental mirror descent (IMD) method, and then we will show how uniform shuffling scheme can further improve the result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
3.1
and
3.2
hold and let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by
Algorithm

1
with
0\leq\alpha_{k}\leq\bar{\alpha}/n
. Let
\tilde{x}^{T}
be randomly sampled from
\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{T}\}
with
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{x}^{T}=x^{k})=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}}
, then

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\frac{4\kappa_{\delta}\cdot(f(x^{1})-f^{*})}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}+\frac{3\kappa_{\delta}^{3}{\sf L}^{2}{\sf G}^{2}}{\mu}\cdot\frac{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}.

(17)

In particular, if we choose a constant stepsize
\alpha_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{nT^{1/3}}
and require
T\geq\max\Big\{8{\sf L}^{3}\kappa_{\delta}^{3},\frac{8{\sf G}^{3}}{\delta^{3}}\Big\}
, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\mathcal{O}(T^{-2/3})
, corresponding to an
\mathcal{O}(n\epsilon^{-1.5})
sample complexity to ensure
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

Telescoping (
15b
) from
k=1,\ldots,T
yields

\frac{1}{4\kappa_{\delta}}{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})\leq(f(x^{1})-f(x^{T+1}))+\frac{3(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{4\mu}{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}.

Since
f(x)\geq f^{*}
for all
x\in\mathcal{Z}
, after multiplying
4\kappa_{\delta}/(\sum_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k})
on both sides, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]=\frac{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}\cdot\mathcal{G}(x^{k})}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}\leq\frac{4\kappa_{\delta}\cdot(f(x^{1})-f^{*})}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}+\frac{3\kappa_{\delta}^{3}{\sf L}^{2}{\sf G}^{2}}{\mu}\cdot\frac{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}},

which proves (
17
). Finally, setting
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{nT^{1/3}}
in the above inequality yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\Big[4(f(x^{1})-f^{*})+\frac{3(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{\mu}\Big]\cdot\frac{\kappa_{\delta}}{T^{2/3}}=\mathcal{O}(T^{-2/3}),

under the condition that
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{nT^{1/3}}\leq\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{n}
, or equivalently,
T\geq\max\Big\{8{\sf L}^{3}\kappa_{\delta}^{3},\frac{8{\sf G}^{3}}{\delta^{3}}\Big\}
. With
n
samples consumed in each epoch, the sample complexity for finding an
\epsilon
solution is
nT=\mathcal{O}(n\epsilon^{-1.5})
.
∎

It can be observed that when the permutations
\{\pi^{k}\}
are arbitrarily selected, including IMD, the algorithm can suffer an
\mathcal{O}(n)
linear dependence in the worst case. In the next theorem, we show how a uniform permutation can improve it to a much better
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
sublinear dependence.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
3.1
and
3.2
hold and let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by
Algorithm

1
with
0\leq\alpha_{k}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{7n{\sf L}\kappa_{\delta}^{2}},\frac{\delta}{2n{\sf G}}\right\}
. Let
\tilde{x}^{T}
be randomly selected from
\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{T}\}
with
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{x}^{T}=x^{k})=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}}
. If in addition, the permutations
\{\pi^{k}\}
are independently generated via uniform shuffling, then

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\frac{8\kappa_{\delta}\cdot(f(x^{1})-f^{*})}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}+\frac{48\kappa_{\delta}^{3}{\sf L}^{2}{\sf G}^{2}}{\mu}\cdot\frac{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n^{2}\alpha_{k}^{3}}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}.

(18)

If we choose constant step sizes
\alpha_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{n^{2/3}T^{1/3}}
and require
T\geq n\cdot\max\Big\{(7{\sf L}\kappa_{\delta}^{2})^{3},\frac{8{\sf G}^{3}}{\delta^{3}}\Big\}
, then we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{3}}T^{-\frac{2}{3}})
, corresponding to an
\mathcal{O}(n^{0.5}\epsilon^{-1.5})
sample complexity to ensure
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of
Eq.

18
, telescoping (
16
) and then dividing
{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}
on both sides of the inequality yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]=\frac{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(x^{k})]}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}\leq\frac{8\kappa_{\delta}\cdot(f(x^{1})-f^{*})}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}+\frac{48\kappa_{\delta}^{3}{\sf L}^{2}{\sf G}^{2}}{\mu}\cdot\frac{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n^{2}\alpha_{k}^{3}}{{\sum}_{k=1}^{T}n\alpha_{k}}.

Then, setting
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{n^{2/3}T^{1/3}}
in the above inequality gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\Big[8(f(x^{1})-f^{*})+\frac{48(\kappa_{\delta}{\sf L}{\sf G})^{2}}{\mu}\Big]\cdot\frac{\kappa_{\delta}}{n^{1/3}T^{2/3}}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{3}}T^{-\frac{2}{3}}),

as long as we require
\frac{1}{n^{2/3}T^{1/3}}\leq\min\big\{\frac{1}{7n{\sf L}\kappa_{\delta}^{2}},\frac{\delta}{2n{\sf G}}\big\}
, or equivalently,
T\geq n\cdot\max\Big\{(7{\sf L}\kappa_{\delta}^{2})^{3},\frac{8{\sf G}^{3}}{\delta^{3}}\Big\}
. The sample complexity for finding an
\epsilon
solution is
nT=\mathcal{O}(n^{0.5}\epsilon^{-1.5})
.
∎

Recall that SMD requires
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
samples to achieve
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\tilde{x}^{T})]\leq\varepsilon
, while RRMD has the bound
\mathcal{O}(n^{0.5}\varepsilon^{-1.5})
. As a remark, whenever the accuracy
\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
, RRMD can outperform SMD. We should also notice that the complexities in
Theorems

4.1
and
4.2
depend on the condition number
\kappa_{\delta}
of the kernel
h
, and implicitly depend on parameter dual space radius parameter
\delta
(which affects the range of step size
\alpha_{k}
). Our remark is that the condition number
\kappa_{\delta}
is usually moderate for constant level
\delta=\mathcal{O}(1)
. For instance, when the kernel

is the Boltzmann-Shannon or Fermi-Dirac entropy,
\kappa_{\delta}
is bounded above by
\exp(\delta)
, see Appendix

yields

6
Further discussions

In the previous analysis of RRMD, we adopt several simplification on problem setting in order to prevent key novelty, the newly proposed dual Lipschitz continuity and DKC regularity, to be buried under technicalities. In this section, we will illustrate how these requirements can be relaxed.

6.1
Relaxing the bounded gradients assumption

Throughout our analysis, we employ a bounded gradient assumption (see
3.2
). While this can be relaxed to a more general expected smoothness condition (
31
), adopting this generalization would significantly complicate the derivations and potentially obscure the central focus. Therefore, for clarity of exposition, we maintain the stronger bounded gradient assumption. In this subsection, we provide a proof sketch demonstrating how the general condition can be incorporated into the RRMD analysis.

The condition is formulated as follows: There exist constants
{\sf A},{\sf B},\tau\geq 0
such that

\frac{1}{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|^{2}\leq{\sf A}\left(f(x)-\underline{f}\right)^{\tau}+{\sf B}^{2}\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{Z}

(31)

where
\underline{f}
can be any lower bound of
f_{i}
over all
i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket
. This is a generalization of the standard
expected smoothness condition

[
22
,
23
,
44
]
, which corresponds to the special case
\tau=1
. By introducing a free parameter
\tau
, (
31
) captures wider behaviors, a few examples are discussed below.

Example 6.1
.

​​​​
If
3.2
is true, then condition (
31
) holds with
\tau=0
.

Example 6.2
.

​​​​
If each
f_{i}
is
{\sf L}
-smooth and lower bounded, then condition (
31
) holds with
\tau=1
.

Example 6.3
.

​​​​
For quadratic inverse problem where each
f_{i}(x):=\frac{1}{4}\big(x^{\top}M_{i}x-b_{i}\big)^{2}
, with
M_{i}\succeq 0
and
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
being known data points, then condition (
31
) holds with
\tau=3/2
.

Proof sketch under the relaxed assumption (
31
).
We begin with redefining the sets
\mathcal{X}^{k}
and the step-size upper bound
\bar{\alpha}_{k}
, previously defined in (
13
), as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{u\in\mathrm{int\ dom}(h):\rho_{h}(u,x^{k})\leq\frac{\delta}{2n\nu}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla f_{i}(x^{k})\|\bigg\}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\alpha}_{k}:=\min\bigg\{\frac{1}{2{\sf L}\kappa(\mathcal{X}^{k})},\frac{\delta}{2\nu}\bigg\}

where

for some
{\sf C}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Above,
\nu
plays the role of the gradients upper bound

. The main challenge is to derive a uniform bound on

for all

Step 1: Update region.
We prove by induction that when

,

Step 2: Diameter control.
The diameter of

Inductive step:
Assuming the above claim holds up to

. Then

•

•

From Step 2 and

Appendix B
Verification of DKC — Proof of

B.1
Boltzmann-Shannon entropy

Note that the Boltzmann-Shannon entropy
h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\log(x_{i})
is separable with each block being the
i
-th coordinate
x_{i}
. We only need to verify the kernel conditioning for each
h_{i}(z):=z\log(z)
. Note that for any points
z,z^{\prime}>0
, their distance in the dual space is given by

\rho_{h_{i}}(z,z^{\prime})=|\log(z/z^{\prime})|.

Since
h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{z}
, for any compact set
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h_{i})=(0,+\infty)
, the condition number over
\mathcal{X}_{i}
is

\kappa_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{i})=\sup_{z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\max\left\{\frac{z}{z^{\prime}},\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}\right\}=\sup_{z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\exp\big\{|\log(z/z^{\prime})|\big\}=\exp\left(\mathrm{diam}_{h_{i}}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\right).

Consequently, for any constant
\delta>0
, any compact set
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h_{i})
with
\mathrm{diam}_{h_{i}}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\leq\delta
satisfies the kernel conditioning regularity assumption with
\kappa_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\leq\exp\left(\delta\right)
.
\Box

B.2
Regularized Burg’s entropy

Similar to the verification of Boltzmann-Shannon entropy, we can express Burg’s entropy as

h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}-\log(x_{i})+\frac{\sigma}{2}x_{i}^{2}
. Now consider
h_{i}(z)=-\log(z)+\frac{\sigma}{2}z^{2}
. Direct computation gives

h_{i}^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{1}{z}+\sigma z\quad\text{and}\quad h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\sigma.

Given any
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{int\ dom}(h_{i})=(0,\infty)
with
\mathrm{diam}_{h_{i}}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\leq\delta
. For any points
y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{i}

\big[h_{i}^{\prime}(y)-h_{i}^{\prime}(z)\big](y-z)\leq\frac{\rho_{h_{i}}^{2}(y,z)}{\sigma}\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{\sigma},

which further implies

\Big(\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{y}\Big)(y-z)+\sigma(y-z)^{2}\leq\frac{\delta}{\sigma}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{y}{z}+\frac{z}{y}\leq 2+\frac{\delta}{\sigma}.

(32)

The condition number of
h_{i}

is computed by

\kappa_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{i})=\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\max\bigg\{\frac{z^{-2}+\sigma}{y^{-2}+\sigma},\frac{y^{-2}+\sigma}{z^{-2}+\sigma}\bigg\}.

For arbitrary

\displaystyle\frac{z^{-2}+\sigma}{y^{-2}+\sigma}=\frac{y^{2}/z^{2}+\sigma y^{2}}{1+\sigma y^{2}}=1+\frac{y^{2}/z^{2}-1}{1+\sigma y^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{y^{2}}{z^{2}}\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{4\delta}{\sigma}+5,

.
\Box

B.3

To show that kernel conditioning of the second kind is valid, it suffices to demonstrate that

Simple computation yields

\kappa_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{i})=\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\max\Big\{\frac{z(1-z)}{y(1-y)},\frac{y(1-y)}{z(1-z)}\Big\}=\sup_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\exp\{|\log(z(1-z))-\log(y(1-y))|\}.

Next, we show that

Case 1:

|z-\frac{1}{2}|\leq|y-\frac{1}{2}|

|\log(z(1-z))-\log(y(1-y))|=\log(z(1-z))-\log(y(1-y))=\log(\tfrac{z}{y})+\log(\tfrac{1-z}{1-y}).

(35)

We split this further into two sub-cases.

\log(\tfrac{y}{1-y})-\log(\tfrac{z}{1-z})\leq\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log(\tfrac{1-z}{1-y})+\log(\tfrac{y}{z})\leq\delta,

|\log(z(1-z))-\log(y(1-y))|\leq 2\log(\tfrac{z}{y})+\delta\leq\delta,

Sub-case 1-2:

B.4
Multi-block power kernel

The kernel
h(x)=\frac{1}{r+2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|x\|_{[j]}^{r+2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
,
r\geq 0
has domain
\mathcal{Z}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{d}
and can be written as

h(x)={\sum}_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}(x_{[j]})\quad\text{where}\quad\quad h_{j}(z):=\frac{1}{r+2}\|z\|^{r+2}+\frac{1}{2}\|z\|^{2},

we thus consider the condition number of each
h_{j}
over the set
\mathcal{X}_{j}
with bounded diameter in the sense that
\rho_{h_{j}}(y,z)=\|\nabla h_{j}(y)-\nabla h_{j}(z)\|\leq\delta
for all
y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
and for some
\delta>0
. Specifically, we want show that
\kappa_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})
is upper bounded by some
\delta
-dependent constant.
Clearly,
h_{j}
is
1
-strongly convex. Hence, for all
y,z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
satisfying
\mathrm{diam}_{h_{j}}(\mathcal{X}_{j})\leq\delta
, we have

\|y-z\|\leq\rho_{h_{j}}(y,z)\leq\delta.

The rest of proof is similar to
[
47
, Proposition 2.5]
.

Direct calculation shows
\nabla^{2}h_{j}(z)=(\|z\|^{r}+1)\cdot I+r\|z\|^{r-2}\cdot zz^{\top}
. For all
z\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
, it holds that
\lambda_{\min}(z)=\|z\|^{r}+1
and
\lambda_{\max}(z)=(r+1)\|z\|^{r}+1
. Let
z\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\|u\|
and
y\in\argmax_{u\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\|u\|
,

\kappa_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j})=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(y)}{\lambda_{\min}(z)}=\frac{(r+1)\|y\|^{r}+1}{\|z\|^{r}+1}\leq\frac{(r+1)(\|z\|+\delta)^{r}+1}{\|z\|^{r}+1}.

Hence, our goal boils down to finding the supremum of

\sup\bigg\{\frac{(r+1)(t+\delta)^{r}+1}{t^{r}+1}:t\geq 0\bigg\}.

If
t\leq 1
, we have
\frac{(r+1)(t+\delta)^{r}+1}{t^{r}+1}\leq(r+1)(1+\delta)^{r}+1
. On the other hand, when
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{(r+1)(t+\delta)^{r}+1}{t^{r}+1}\leq\frac{(r+1)(t+\delta)^{r}}{t^{r}}=(r+1)(1+\delta/t)^{r}\leq(r+1)(1+\delta)^{r}.

This completes the proof.
\Box

\displaystyle{\sum}_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\|\nabla_{[j]}\;f_{\pi^{k}_{i}}(x^{k})-\nabla_{[j]}f_{\pi^{k}_{i}}(y^{k,i})\|^{2}}{\mu_{j}(\mathcal{X}^{k}_{j})}
