Prognostic value of epicardial adipose tissue assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction after successful revascularization
Authors: Lai Wei, Chen-xu Zhao, Jian-xun Dong, Xun-han Qiu, Alex Pui Wai Lee, Heng Ge, Jun Pu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpicardial adipose tissue (EAT) around the myocardium may play a role in coronary artery disease, yet its prognostic value in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains uncertain.MethodsData from 540 STEMI patients enrolled in a registry study (NCT03768453) were analyzed. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) cine sequences were used to measure EAT volume. The primary endpoint was a composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). The Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the association between EAT volume and MACE. Correlation analyses were performed to explore relationships between EAT volume and myocardial injury markers. In addition, paired t-tests were applied to compare changes in EAT volume over the 6-month follow-up.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 4.5 years, MACE occurred in 65 patients (12.0%). Patients who experienced MACE had a significantly higher EAT volume (39.6 vs. 33.4 mL, P < 0.001) than those without MACE. EAT volume was an independent predictor of MACE after adjustment for clinical factors (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16, P = 0.037) and CMR parameters (HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07, P < 0.001). EAT volume was significantly correlated with myocardial injury markers, including infarct size (r = 0.15, P = 0.001) and microvascular obstruction (r = 0.15, P = 0.001). EAT volume remained stable at the 6-month follow-up (34.6 vs. 35.6 mL, P = 0.181).ConclusionsEAT measured non-invasively by CMR is an independent prognostic biomarker for adverse outcomes in STEMI patients. It may serve as a promising imaging marker for risk stratification in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02527-5.

Introduction

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) represents the most severe clinical manifestation of coronary heart disease (CHD) and remains a substantial global health burden. The widespread implementation of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), alongside advances in pharmacological therapies and medical devices, has substantially reduced in-hospital mortality among STEMI patients [1]. Nevertheless, long-term clinical outcomes in this population remain highly variable [2, 3], highlighting the need to identify additional prognostic factors after STEMI.

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is a specialized visceral fat depot located between the myocardium and the visceral pericardium. Given its close anatomical proximity and functional interplay with the heart, EAT has attracted growing research attention [4]. Under physiological conditions, EAT provides biochemical, mechanical and thermogenic support to the myocardium. In pathological states, however, EAT may exert deleterious effects via secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [5]. Previous studies have implicated EAT in the pathogenesis and progression of CHD [6]. For example, data from the Framingham cohort revealed significant associations between EAT volume, coronary artery calcification, and metabolic risk factors including hypertriglyceridemia and decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [7]. These findings suggest that EAT may induce local vascular toxicity by exacerbating perivascular inflammation and stimulating smooth muscle cell proliferation [8–10]. Nonetheless, controversies surrounding the “obesity paradox” complicate the interpretation of EAT’s role in cardiovascular health [11]. Notably, data from the National Lung Screening Trial indicated that reduced EAT volume was paradoxically associated with higher cardiovascular mortality in a community-based cohort [12].

Despite these findings, data on the prognostic value of EAT in patients with STEMI remain limited. Since inflammation plays a pivotal role in myocardial repair following infarction, EAT may markedly influence long-term clinical outcomes in this setting. Several small studies have identified EAT thickness in the superior interventricular groove as an independent predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) among STEMI patients [13]. However, larger, comprehensive studies quantifying total EAT burden and its impact on post-infarction outcomes are still needed.

Among current imaging modalities, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) offers high resolution for the non-invasive evaluation of EAT [14, 15]. In particular, steady-state free precession (SSFP) cine sequences allow highly accurate and reproducible quantification of EAT volume [16, 17]. Recent studies employing cine imaging have further validated the precision and reliability of this technique for EAT measurement [18–23]. Accordingly, the present study aimed to clarify the prognostic value of EAT in STEMI patients through comprehensive CMR-based EAT assessment, and to compare its predictive performance with other well-established CMR-derived indices.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This study analyzed data from the multi-center prospective EARLY-MYO-CMR registry (NCT03768453), which included all-comer STEMI patients who underwent CMR [24–26]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) first-diagnosed STEMI with typical ischemic syndromes and electrocardiogram evidence of ST-segment elevation (≥ 2 mm in ≥ 2 contiguous precordial leads or ≥ 1 mm in peripheral leads); (2) successful primary PCI within 12 h of symptom onset; and (3) CMR performed within 1 week of symptom onset. All patients received guideline-concordant standard treatment [27]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, with written informed consent from all participants. CMR imaging and analysis

CMR imaging was performed using a 3.0-Tesla scanner (Achieva TX; Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands). All sequences were acquired during breath-holding with a default field of view of 350 × 350mm2. A balanced, SSFP cine sequence was used to produce cine images (repetition time/echo time, 3.2/1.6 ms; 30 phases; flip angle, 45°; voxel size, 2.0 × 1.6 × 8.0 mm3). Late gadolinium enhancement images were acquired using an inversion recovery segmented SSFP sequence with an appropriate inversion time (repetition time/echo time, 3.5/1.7 ms; flip angle, 25°; temporal resolution, 190 ms; voxel size, 1.5 × 1.7 × 10.0 mm3 interpolated to 0.74 × 0.74 × 5.0 mm3) 10 min after intravenous administration of gadopentetate dimeglumine (0.1 mmol/kg; Magnevist, Bayer Healthcare Pharmaceuticals Inc., Wuppertal, Germany).

All CMR images were analyzed offline using a commercially available software package (CVI42; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Inc., Calgary, Canada). Volumetric indices, such as the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), were calculated using contiguous short-axis cine images covering the entire heart (slice thickness, 8 mm; gap, 0 mm). Infarction was defined as regions with signal intensity ≥ 5 standard deviations above that of the user-defined viable myocardium. Microvascular obstruction (MVO) was defined as persistent areas of hypoenhancement within the hyperenhanced infarcted territory. Both infarction size (IS) and MVO were quantified as percentages of LV myocardial mass (%LV). LV strains were measured and assessed according to previously established methods [3].

Quantification of EAT

Defined as the adipose tissue located between the myocardium and the visceral pericardium, i.e., the epicardium, EAT was quantified using short-axis SSFP cine CMR images as previously described [28]. First, the end-diastolic contours of both the myocardium and the epicardium were manually traced on contiguous short-axis images, extending from the mitral valve plane to the most apical plane containing visible cardiac adipose tissue. Next, regions of interest (ROIs) for EAT were delineated based on signal intensity at least 5 standard deviations above that of the user-defined viable myocardium, with manual adjustments performed as needed to optimize accuracy. Finally, the total EAT volume was calculated using the modified Simpson’s method, which integrates the ROI areas across all slices. The EAT index (EATi) was defined as the EAT volume normalized to body surface area. The measurement protocol for EAT is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Clinical endpoints and follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of MACE, defined as cardiovascular death, non-fatal recurrent myocardial infarction, hospitalization for heart failure, and stroke [29–31]. Clinical follow-up was conducted through telephone interviews or in-person clinical visits by two study nurses blinded to the study data. Follow-up was performed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge, and every 6 months thereafter. All reported MACE were confirmed via electronic medical records. Follow-up CMR imaging was conducted 6 months after discharge.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared with Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The intra- and inter-observer reliability of EAT measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with an ICC > 0.74 considered high reliability [32]. Associations between EAT and clinical outcomes were examined using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses, with EAT analyzed as a continuous variable. Two multivariate models were constructed to adjust for clinical characteristics (Model A) and CMR indices (Model B). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Variables with P ≤ 0.05 in univariate analysis, as well as other clinically relevant variables, were included in the multivariate models. Optimal cutoff values for EAT were identified by deriving risk scores from univariate Cox regression and determining cutoffs using survival analysis, with censoring appropriately handled. The robustness and clinical relevance of these cutoffs were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. Predictive performance was further evaluated using Harrell’s C-index and ROC-based metrics. Time-dependent ROC analyses were performed at prespecified time points to evaluate discrimination while accounting for censoring, and the corresponding AUCs and Youden indices were reported as supplementary measures. Additional analyses, including net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), assessed the incremental predictive value of EAT. Correlation analyses identified factors associated with EAT. Paired t-tests compared EAT volumes between acute and follow-up periods. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and outcomes

A total of 796 patients from the EARLY-MYO-CMR registry were initially enrolled. After selection, the final analysis included 540 STEMI patients who underwent successful PCI and CMR during hospitalization. The median time from symptom onset to CMR was 5 days (range, 2–7 days). Over a median follow-up period of 4.5 years, 65 patients (12.0%) experienced MACE (Fig. 1).

Baseline and CMR characteristics according to MACE

Baseline and CMR characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Patients who experienced MACE had significantly higher EAT volumes (39.6 vs. 33.4 mL, P < 0.001) and EATi (21.9 vs. 18.6 mL/m², P < 0.001) compared to those without MACE. In addition, patients with MACE were more likely to have hypertension (69.2% vs. 54.1%, P = 0.023) and exhibited higher levels of troponin I (45.7 vs. 33.8 ng/mL, P = 0.017). CMR parameters showed that patients with MACE had larger left ventricular end-systolic volumes (LVESV, 66.4 vs. 56.9 mL, P = 0.005), larger IS (33.7% vs. 27.2%, P = 0.021), and higher prevalence of MVO (69.2% vs. 50.7%, P = 0.008). Conversely, patients with MACE had lower LVEF (48.0% vs. 55.6%, P < 0.001) and reduced global strain indices, including global radial strain (GRS, 17.7% vs. 21.9%, P < 0.001), global circumferential strain (GCS, 12.5% vs. 15.0%, P < 0.001), and global longitudinal strain (GLS, 7.7% vs. 8.5%, P = 0.017). Notably, EAT measurements demonstrated excellent reproducibility. The intra-observer ICC was 0.991 (95% CI: 0.977–0.996), and the inter-observer ICC was 0.971 (95% CI: 0.927–0.988).

Prognostic impact of EAT on clinical outcomes

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that EAT and EATi were significant predictors of MACE. The values of area under curve for EAT and EATi were 0.685 (95% CI: 0.622–0.751) and 0.673 (95% CI: 0.619–0.747), respectively (Fig. 3). These were comparable to established CMR prognostic markers, including LVEF, IS, MVO, and strain indices (Supplementary Table S1). The optimal cutoff values for predicting MACE were 32.9 mL for EAT (sensitivity: 81.5%, specificity: 51.8%) and 17.9 mL/m² for EATi (sensitivity: 81.3%, specificity: 48.7%). Discrimination was first assessed using Harrell’s C-index derived from the Cox proportional hazards model. The model incorporating EAT yielded a C-index of 0.656 (SE 0.030). In addition, time-dependent ROC analyses (accounting for censoring) showed AUCs of 0.552, 0.460, and 0.432 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further confirmed the prognostic value of EAT and EATi. Patients with higher EAT volume or EATi (above the predefined cutoff values) had significantly lower MACE-free survival compared with those with lower values (log-rank P < 0.001 for all comparisons, Fig. 4).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified EAT as independent predictors of MACE (Tables 2 and 3). Two models were developed to adjust for clinical and CMR parameters, respectively. In the clinical model, EAT remained significant predictors after adjusting for variables such as age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, reperfusion time, troponin I, C-reactive protein and creatinine. The HR was 1.08 (95% CI: 1.02–1.16, P = 0.037) for EAT. In the CMR-adjusted model, EAT also remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, LVEDV, LVEF, IS, MVO, and global strain parameters. The HR was 1.04 (95% CI: 1.02–1.07, P < 0.001) for EAT. Exploratory Cox regression analyses for each individual MACE component (cardiovascular death, non-fatal recurrent myocardial infarction, hospitalization for heart failure, and stroke) were additionally performed and are summarized in Supplementary Table S4 to S11.

Reclassification analyses demonstrated that adding EAT to conventional prognostic models (including LVEF, IS, and MVO) significantly improved risk stratification for MACE, as evidenced by substantial gains in NRI and IDI (Supplementary Table S2).

Factors associated with EAT in STEMI patients

Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between EAT and key CMR parameters (Supplementary Table S3). EAT volume was negatively correlated with LVEF (r = -0.10, P = 0.022), GRS (r = -0.11, P = 0.023), and GCS (r = -0.14, P = 0.011) and positively correlated with IS (r = 0.15, P = 0.001) and MVO (r = 0.15, P = 0.001). Similar correlation patterns were observed for EATi, which showed negative associations with LV cardiac output (r = -0.09, P = 0.035), LVEF (r = -0.09, P = 0.042), and GCS (r = -0.10, P = 0.028) and positive associations with IS (r = 0.18, P < 0.001) and MVO (r = 0.15, P < 0.001).

Comparison of EAT during follow-up periods

Follow-up CMR data at 6 months were available for 89 patients. No significant changes in EAT volume were observed between the acute phase and the follow-up period (34.6 vs. 35.6 mL, P = 0.181, paired t-test).

Discussion

Our findings reveal that EAT volume and EATi quantified by cine CMR are independent prognostic factors for MACE in STEMI patients after adjustment for conventional clinical and CMR risk factors, and both parameters are significantly correlated with myocardial injury markers including IS and MVO during the acute phase of STEMI. Collectively, these results suggest a potential role of EAT in STEMI pathophysiology and confirm its value as a reliable imaging biomarker for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in clinical practice.

EAT, a specialized fat depot located between the myocardium and visceral pericardium, accounts for 20–50% of total cardiac mass and envelops approximately 80% of the myocardium [33]. Accumulating evidence indicates that EAT acts as a cardio-metabolic risk factor beyond conventional cardiovascular risk factors [34]. Prior studies have associated elevated EAT with adverse events, including impaired reperfusion after PCI, aggravated CHD, and higher risk of myocardial infarction [35–37]. Nevertheless, its prognostic role in STEMI patients remains unclear. Building on the findings of Toya et al., who demonstrated that CMR-measured EAT thickness in the interventricular groove predicts MACE among STEMI patients, our work further shows that EAT volume and EATi quantified by high-resolution cine CMR are independent predictors of MACE in a large STEMI cohort [13]. Relative to earlier studies, our investigation includes a larger sample, longer follow-up, and volumetric EAT measurement, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of total EAT burden. Overall, EAT volume and EATi deliver strong prognostic ability for STEMI, performing equally well as established cardiac risk markers.

Our study also identified significant correlations between EAT and core markers of myocardial injury, namely IS, MVO and troponin I. Under physiological conditions, EAT plays a cardioprotective role: it acts as an energy reserve that releases fatty acids to fuel myocardial metabolism [38], modulates coronary vascular tone through vasoactive substances including adiponectin and nitric oxide [39, 40], and offers mechanical support to the myocardium [41]. In the setting of myocardial infarction and other pathological states, however, EAT undergoes a phenotypic switch toward a pro-inflammatory profile. It secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), recruits macrophages [42, 43], induces lymphoid cluster formation, and promotes ventricular fibrosis [44]. Meanwhile, it exacerbates inflammation in adjacent coronary arteries, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis, plaque rupture and thrombosis [45, 46]. Our findings are consistent with these proposed mechanisms and indicate that elevated EAT correlates with myocardial injury markers in STEMI patients, predominantly through inflammatory cascades. Notably, this is a clinical observational study, and we have no direct molecular or histological data to confirm the above mechanisms. Further basic experimental research is therefore warranted.

We also examined short-term changes in EAT volume among STEMI survivors. CMR assessments performed at the 6-month follow-up did not demonstrate a significant change in EAT volume compared with acute-phase measurements. While this observation suggests a trend toward relative stability of EAT in the early post-infarction period, its value as a marker reflecting long-term metabolic status (in contrast to acutely fluctuating serum glucose and lipids) needs further validation [47, 48]. Given the limited number of patients who completed follow-up CMR, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Given the association between EAT and visceral fat, researchers have increasingly focused on relevant intervention strategies [49, 50]. Interventions targeting visceral fat reduction, including lifestyle modification and pharmacotherapies such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, have shown promising clinical benefits [51]. Notably, recent data indicate that tirzepatide, a novel GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist, can reduce paracardiac adipose tissue in patients with obesity-related heart failure [52]. Future work is warranted to explore whether therapies targeting EAT and its inflammatory phenotype could improve long-term outcomes in patients after STEMI.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, we cannot establish a causal relationship between EAT and adverse outcomes. The lack of PET imaging, histological analysis, circulating inflammatory markers and adipokine profiling prevents direct verification of the pro-inflammatory effects of EAT in the setting of STEMI. Second, potential selection bias exists. We excluded high-risk patients who were hemodynamically unstable, presented with severe renal dysfunction, or were unable to undergo CMR. Third, this registry did not record data on waist circumference, visceral obesity, lifestyle and socioeconomic factors. These unmeasured confounders may affect both EAT volume and patient prognosis. Fourth, follow-up CMR was performed in only 89 patients. This small sample size may result in limited statistical power to draw robust conclusions regarding the short-term stability of EAT volume. Finally, we only analyzed total EAT volume and did not perform regional quantification of EAT around the left and right ventricles.

Conclusion

CMR-measured EAT volume is an independent predictors of adverse outcomes among revascularized STEMI patients. Routine assessment of EAT during CMR has the potential to improve clinical risk stratification. Additional large-scale studies are required to confirm our findings.
