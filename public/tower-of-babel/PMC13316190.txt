Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is a rapidly escalating public health challenge worldwide [1]. Over the past two decades, global incidence has risen by 132% [1], with projections suggesting up to a 280% increase within a single generation (2004–2044) [2]. Notably, EC is one of the few cancers in which both incidence and mortality are increasing [3,4]. While many EC patients are cured with surgery, outcomes following relapse remain poor [5,6]. Rising mortality is increasingly driven by aggressive tumour subtypes [7], a growing proportion of which present at an early stage, challenging the long held assumption that early-stage EC is inherently low risk [3,7].

EC is a biologically heterogeneous disease, even within the same histological subtype. Advances in next-generation sequencing have transformed understanding of this diversity and defined molecular subgroups. The landmark classification by The Cancer Genome Atlas integrated proteomic, genomic, and transcriptomic data to define molecular subgroups that transcend standard histopathological classification [8]. This framework has since been translated into clinically applicable tools using immunohistochemistry and targeted sequencing [9,10], refining risk stratification.

These subgroups are characterised by distinct genomic landscapes with important prognostic implications. The copy-number high (p53-abnormal) subtype exhibits marked genomic instability, is enriched for high-grade tumours, and is associated with relatively low tumour mutational burden and poorer outcomes [11]. In contrast, mismatch repair–deficient (MMRd) tumours demonstrate high tumour mutational burden with recurrent mutational events and occur across both low- and high-grade disease. The POLE-ultramutated subgroup (POLEm), defined by pathogenic POLE mutations, is characterised by an exceptionally high mutational burden and represents a paradox, appearing high risk based on histopathological features such as grade, yet are associated with an excellent prognosis [8,11]. The NSMP (no specific molecular profile) group is a heterogeneous subgroup, frequently enriched for PTEN mutations and other aberrations in the PI3K pathway, with a subset of tumours associated with poorer outcomes [12]. In EC, tumour mutational burden is primarily determined by molecular subtype rather than histological grade or type, with both high- and low-grade tumours represented across subgroups with differing mutational profiles highlighting the importance of molecular testing in EC.

Advances in molecular profiling and the identification of actionable alterations, including ERBB2 (HER2) amplification and homologous recombination deficiency, have facilitated the development of targeted therapeutic approaches in advanced disease. In the early-stage setting, clinical trial platforms such as the RAINBO program [13] are evaluating molecularly stratified adjuvant treatment strategies, raising the prospect of more individualised care.

The current standard of care for EC is surgery, with adjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or both recommended for higher-risk disease. The PORTEC-3 study demonstrated a survival benefit in patients with EC that were given both chemotherapy and radiation following surgery (5 year overall survival (OS) 81% vs 76% p = 0.034) [14]. The benefit of the chemotherapy was seen predominantly in patients with stage III disease, however a non-statistically significant trend for benefit was seen in early-stage cases [14] and varied depending on the molecular subgroup [11]. Due to the limitations of the data, adjuvant treatment decisions remain challenging, particularly in early-stage and high–intermediate-risk disease, where clinicians must balance potential benefit against substantial toxicity, including persistent peripheral sensory neuropathy years after treatment [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]].

Personalising adjuvant treatment, particularly for early-stage patients at risk of relapse, represents a critical opportunity to improve outcomes. Conversely, avoiding overtreatment may improve quality of life and reduce pressure on overburdened healthcare systems. However, although molecular subclassification has improved risk stratification, it remains insufficient to guide truly individualised care [20,21], as relapse risk remains incompletely resolved within molecularly defined groups [11,22,23]. Consequently, current models remain imperfect at predicting recurrence, resulting in both overtreatment and undertreatment.

This gap highlights a critical unmet need: the detection of minimal residual disease (MRD). MRD refers to occult cancer cells that persist after surgery but remain undetectable on conventional imaging during the adjuvant decision window [24,25]. A reliable MRD biomarker would transform clinical decision making enabling precise risk stratification, identifying patients who require additional therapy, and sparing low-risk individuals from unnecessary treatment and toxicity.

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) enables minimally-invasive assessment of malignancy via a simple blood test [26]. Assessing ctDNA can both quantify the level of cancer burden and detect cancer driver mutations. In other cancer types such as colorectal and breast cancer [27,28], postoperative ctDNA positivity has been shown to indicate minimal residual disease (MRD) and have prognostic significance. Moreover, ctDNA has been successfully used to guide adjuvant therapy selection in colorectal cancer, reducing the number of patients’ requiring adjuvant therapy without compromising outcome [28]. Similarly in recent EC studies, ctDNA appears to have prognostic significance [29,30]. However, the optimal timing for perioperative ctDNA assessment remains unknown, particularly given that adjuvant treatment decisions in EC are often made within weeks of surgery. We therefore developed the CODEC study (Circulating tumOur Dna in Endometrial Cancer), a pilot study which aimed to evaluate the role of ctDNA as a prognostic biomarker in the perioperative setting of patients with EC undergoing surgery with curative intent and explore perioperative ctDNA dynamics. Here, we investigate the prognostic significance of ctDNA at four time points to examine the most suitable time for ctDNA assessment to take forward into further studies. As the relationship between ctDNA levels and tumour volume, as assessed by 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose PET/CT (FDG-PET) and MRI are unknown in EC, we also sought to determine whether ctDNA accurately reflects tumour burden and explore the clinical and tumour genomic features of cases with incongruent ctDNA results and relapse status.

Methods

Ethics

This research study (ACTRN12619001249145) was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) at Monash Health (HREC identification RES-19–0000107E) and The Royal Women’s Hospital (RWH) (HREC identification 17.20), Melbourne, Australia.

Participants, imaging and samples

Participants with a new diagnosis of Type 2 EC (Data Supplement) diagnosed on pipelle/curettage, planned for gynaecological surgery and consenting to the collection of biospecimens, including blood and tissue, were recruited from two sites in Melbourne, Australia (Epworth HeathCare (EH) and The Royal Women’s Hospital (RWH). Further inclusion criteria included preoperative radiological features such as myoinvasion for a proportion of participants (Data Supplement).

Following consent, participants underwent preoperative FDG-PET scan, pelvic MRI (Data Supplement) and a preoperative blood collection for ctDNA and germline DNA (Time Point 1) (Supp Table 1,2) and proceeded to surgery as per standard of care. During surgery, blood for ctDNA was collected (Time Point 2A) with a subsequent blood collection within 24 h of surgery (Time Point 2B) and 2–6 weeks postoperative (Time Point 3) (Supp Table 1,2). Additional blood collections occurred over 2 years of follow up (Time Points 4–16) (Supp Table 1,2). Blood samples were collected in 10 mLCell-Free DNA Blood Collection Tubes (Streck, USA) and underwent double centrifugation to separate the plasma, with 5 mL used for ctDNA extraction using the QIAamp Circulating DNA Extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany) (Data Supplement, Supp Fig. 1). FDG-PET was repeated at 12 months postoperatively and when feasible, at relapse (Supp Table 1,2).

The primary objective was to investigate the relationship between ctDNA positivity and relapse free survival (RFS) at the four perioperative time points (Time points 1–3). The secondary objectives were to examine the correlation between imaging and ctDNA defined tumour volume and explore perioperative ctDNA dynamics.

Sequencing of tumour samples

Tumour sequencing was performed in a centralised National Association of Testing Authorities (NATA) accredited laboratory at Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre using one of two hybrid capture based next generation sequencing technologies; the Comprehensive Cancer Panel (Agilent Design ID 3016,871) tumour-normal pipeline or TSO-50,0 [31] tumour only pipeline (Data Supplement). Molecular subclassification was performed using the Leiden/PORTEC classification based on immunohistochemistry and next generation sequencing [9,32] (Data Supplement, Supp Fig. 2). A pathogenic germline mutation (PM), or in the case of the tumour only pipeline, somatic mutations with potential ramifications for cancer risk in which germline origin could not be excluded, were fed back to the participant and a referral to a familial cancer clinic (FCC) offered (Data Supplement).

Testing for ctDNA

ctDNA was assessed using mutation-specific assays to maximise analytical sensitivity and specificity with droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) being the most well-established ctDNA testing modality at the time of the study [33,34]. Based on the tumour sequencing results, one mutation was chosen for each case prioritising reoccurring driver mutations. Using 5 microliters of input DNA, testing of ctDNA was done in duplicate at a minimum with a Bio-Rad PrimePCR assay or a custom design. Assay performance characteristics including limit of detection and specificity were established according to validated diagnostic frameworks [35]. Each ddPCR experiment included no template controls (NTCs), positive template controls (PTCs) and wild-type template controls (WTCs). ctDNA was considered detectable if two or more mutant droplets were detected [36]. For a time point to be deemed ctDNA-negative, a minimum of 3000 total DNA copies was required (Data Supplement). Data were processed and analysed retrospectively using QuantaSoft v1.7.4 (Bio-Rad), applying Poisson statistics to derive absolute concentrations (copies/µL). ctDNA was classified as positive or negative based on detection of mutant droplets above background thresholds. Variant allele fraction (VAF) was calculated as the proportion of mutant copies relative to total copies, and ctDNA levels were reported as mutant copies per mL of plasma, adjusted for input volume and dilution factors (Data Supplement). Treating clinicians and participants were not informed of ctDNA results.

Statistics

Univariable Cox proportional hazards models were performed for the primary endpoint (RFS) at the four defined perioperative time points to assess the impact of ctDNA positivity on survival outcomes. The ability of ctDNA to quantify tumour burden was assessed by comparing preoperative ctDNA levels (copies/mL) with FDG-PET defined metabolic volume (mL) and MRI-defined tumour volume (mm3). This comparison was conducted using graphical methods, descriptive statistics, and Spearmans rank correlation.

Participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Group differences for categorical variables were assessed using either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test depending on sample size. For continuous variables, group differences were examined using the Mann-Whitney U test. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Statistical analyses were prespecified and performed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.1; R Core Team 202,4 [37]) and “rms” R package (v6.8.1; Frank E Harrell Jr 2024) [38]. Two-sided p-values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The statistics are further discussed in the data supplement.

Results

CODEC cohort

Between June 2019 and March 2022, 50 participants were enrolled in the CODEC study (Fig. 1A). The median age of the participants was 67 years (range 41–87) and the median body mass index (BMI) was 31 (range 19–52) (Fig. 1B, Supp Table 3). The most common histological type was grade 3 endometroid adenocarcinoma (EAC) and fitting with standard clinical practice at the time of recruitment, the majority had lymph node assessment with sentinel nodes (Fig. 1B). FIGO 2009 staging was utilised. Most of the cohort (64%, 32/50) had early-stage disease, while 36% (18/50) presented with advanced-stage disease (Stage III/IV). Lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) was identified in 44% (22/50) of participants. Although all four molecular subgroups were represented, most tumours were either p53 mutant or classified as NSMP (Figs. 1B,2). The most common adjuvant treatment was combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy (Fig. 1B).

Outcomes

At the time of data cut off, 15/10/24, median follow up was 1271 days (1117 - 1645). Forty percent (20/50) of participants had relapsed and 28% (14/50) had died; thirteen from cancer related causes. Participants with advanced disease were more likely to relapse, however, relapse occurred in 24% (7/29) of those with stage 1 disease (Supp Table 4). The majority of relapses (55%, 11/20) were diagnosed based on symptoms or imaging performed for clinical purposes (including routine imaging for treatment decision making or assessment of symptoms). The remaining participants (9/20) had their relapse diagnosed incidentally on the 12-month CODEC specific FDG-PET scan (Supp Table 4). Treatment following relapse is discussed in the Data Supplement.

Tumour associated genomic features in the codec cohort

In line with literature, the most common genomic aberrations detected via sequencing of EC tissue in this cohort occurred in TP53, PIK3CA and PTEN (Fig. 2). A number of potentially actionable genomic changes were seen including in CCNE1, ERBB2, and KRAS (Fig. 2). While 12% (6/50) of the participants had at least one somatic mutation in BRCA1/2, most occurred at a low variant allele frequency (VAF, mean 16%, range 5–29) and almost exclusively in hypermutated cases with a high TMB (Fig. 2), indicating they were likely passenger mutations. Only one case with a BRCA1 somatic mutation (VAF 77%) occurred in a TMB low/ non-hypermutated case which was a p53 mutant carcinosarcoma. At the higher end of the published literature [39,40], 20% (10/50) of the CODEC cohort had a germline PM detected through the study (Fig. 2, Supp Table 5). The genomic findings are further discussed in the Data Supplement.

ctDNA collection and testing

At data cut off, all 50 participants had completed the serial blood collections, completing either the full two years of post-operative collections or all collections until relapse. Over six hundred blood samples were collected from the 50 participants in total, including from 16 participants from regional centres (Supp Table 6).

The tumour sequencing results informed ddPCR assay design. In order to limit the number of distinct ddPCR assays, priority was given to driver mutations which were observed in multiple tumours. Fourteen mutation specific assays in 5 genes were utilised for 30 cases (Fig. 3, Supp Table 7). ctDNA from the four perioperative time points (Time Points 1, 2A, 2b, 3) were tested, with additional time points assessed in four cases, with a total of 133 ddPCR assays performed (Data Supplement). The clinical features of the 30 cases that underwent ctDNA testing were consistent with the whole cohort with exception of age (Fig. 1B).

Preoperative detection of ctDNA is concordant with imaging defined tumour burden

Among 30 preoperative samples, ctDNA was evaluable (≥3000 copies analysed) in 26/30 cases, and detectable in 58% (15/26). ctDNA was identified across all disease stages (I–IV) and molecular subtypes (Fig. 3, Fig. 4A; Supplementary Tables 8–9).To assess concordance with tumour burden, preoperative ctDNA levels (copies/mL) were compared with imaging-derived tumour volume using FDG-PET (metabolic tumour volume) and pelvic MRI (anatomical tumour volume) (Supp Fig. 3). ctDNA levels demonstrated a significant positive correlation with both FDG-PET-defined tumour volume (Spearman’s ρ = 0.672, p = 0.0002) and MRI-defined tumour volume (ρ = 0.532, p = 0.007). FDG-PET and MRI tumour volumes were also correlated with each other (ρ = 0.586, p = 0.003). Median tumour volumes were also compared between ctDNA-positive and ctDNA-negative cases at Time Point 1 (Supp Table 10) where tumour volumes were significantly higher in ctDNA-positive cases for both FDG-PET and MRI.

Panel (C) demonstrates the time of collection (days) from surgery. Triangle represents time of death and circle represents time of disease relapse. ctDNA, circulating tumour DNA; NA, not applicable.

The association between stage and histoype was explored (Supp Tables 11 and 12). There was a positive association with stage and ctDNA values where advanced stage patients have a higher ctDNA content compared to early stage (Supp Tables 11). However, there was no association with stage and histotype (Supp Tables 12).

We then explored the prognostic significance of preoperative ctDNA detection (Supp Table 13). Recurrence occurred more frequently in patients with detectable ctDNA compared to those without (60% [9/15] vs 18% [2/11]; p = 0.03). However, the difference in recurrence-free survival did not reach statistical significance (HR 4.32, p = 0.062).

ctDNA positivity post EC surgery predicts relapse

As seen in other cancers [27,28,41], detection of ctDNA following surgery indicates MRD and therefore has strong biological rationale as a prognostic biomarker. In our cohort, a total of 27 samples were collected at this postoperative Time Point 3 (2–6) weeks following surgery and, of which 25 met the predefined criteria for evaluability and were included in the primary analysis. (Fig. 4B, Supp Table 8). ctDNA was detected at this timepoint in 32% of cases (8/25) (Fig. 4B, Supp Table 8,9). The recurrence rate was higher in the ctDNA positive group (75%, 6/8) than in those participants that were ctDNA negative (18%, 3/17) (p value =0.02). Furthermore, there was a statistically significant correlation between postoperative ctDNA positivity and relapse free survival (HR 6.53 p = 0.008 95% confidence interval 1.62–26.33) (Fig. 5 and Supp Table 13). In univariate analysis, ctDNA positivity at Time point 3 was the strongest prognostic factor after stage (Supp Table 14).

Kaplan Meier survival curve demonstrating the relapse free survival by days from the time of surgery according to ctDNA positivity postoperatively (Time Point 3). ctDNA, circulating tumour DNA; CI, confidence interval; HR, Hazard Ratio.

As postoperative ctDNA positivity appeared prognostic, we explored the cases where postoperative ctDNA detection and relapse status differed from the expected. Intriguingly, two of the participants with ctDNA detected postoperatively are yet to relapse (Fig. 4B, Supp Table 8,9) (Data supplement). One case, CODEC30, is a participant with Stage 1 MMRd Grade 3 EAC who is 36 months postoperative. Although ctDNA levels decreased following surgery, all perioperative time points for this participant were positive for ctDNA. However, the 12 month postoperative FDG-PET scan was negative. The other case (CODEC22) is 40 months postoperative, has multiple poor prognostic factors including a high risk histotype and molecular subgroup (p53 mutant carcinosarcoma) and was ctDNA positive at 3/4 perioperative time points (Supp Table 5,8,9). Interestingly, this participant received PORTEC-3 style adjuvant therapy with radiation and sensitising cisplatin followed by 4 cycles of platinum doublet chemotherapy and through this study was found to be homologous recombination DNA repair deficient (HRd) due to a somatic BRCA1 mutation, which is likely driving increased chemotherapy sensitivity [42,43]. Further longitudinal follow-up for relapse is required for both of these participants.

Of the nine cases that relapsed that had postoperative ctDNA testing, three were ctDNA negative at Time point 3. CODEC48 had an advanced stage p53 mutant carcinosarcoma, with metabolic volume on FDG-PET at diagnosis of 57 mL and an MRI defined tumour volume of 35.6 mm [3] (Supp Table 5). However, the case was a low shedder of ctDNA with a preoperative ctDNA level of only 1.36 copies/mL and the remaining perioperative ctDNA results being negative/undetected. The other two cases where ctDNA was not detectable perioperatively (0 mutant copies) were early-stage (uterine confined) EC cases. Interestingly, both were diagnosed incidentally with asymptomatic relapse on the 12-month FDG-PET as part of the study follow up with oligometastatic disease. Case CODEC37, a stage 1A p53 mutant EC had an isolated para-aortic lymph node at L3 without biopsy, and was salvaged with chemoradiation and remains in complete remission. While the FDG-PET may have been a false positive, testing of the ctDNA at the time of relapse detected 6 mutant copies/mL, supporting the radiological diagnosis (Supp Table 8). The other case, CODEC28, a stage 1A Grade 3 EAC, had oligometastatic recurrence in the vagina and pelvic side wall. This patient also received salvage chemoradiation and remains in remission. The ctDNA at relapse is untested. Notably this case was a mismatch repair deficient EC due to MLH1 hypermethylation (detected by clinical MLH1 promoter methylation testing) with a hypermutated phenotype. A KRAS c.38G>A mutation (tumour VAF 31%) was tracked in the ctDNA which may not have been a driver mutation.

Perioperative ctDNA dynamics and prognostic significance of ctDNA intraoperative and immediately postoperatively

As anticipated, when examining the cases with ctDNA results available at all four perioperative time points (19/30), we observed a trend towards a reduction in ctDNA levels over time that likely corresponded with the reduction in tumour volume occurring at surgery (Fig. 6, Supp Table 9). Moreover for the majority of cases, the ctDNA detectability across the four perioperative blood collections was consistent (12/19) (Supp Fig. 5, Supp Table 9). As expected, many cases with detectable ctDNA preoperatively had an increase in ctDNA level intraoperatively, likely due to the surgery releasing ctDNA into the blood stream [44] (Fig. 6). Importantly however, ctDNA positivity intraoperatively was not significantly associated with RFS (p value = 0.098) (Supp Fig. 6, Supp Table 13). We next compared the intraoperative and immediate postoperative ctDNA results (Time Points 2A and 2B). For the majority, the results were congruent (Supp Fig. 5). Incongruent detection was predominantly due to normalisation of ctDNA at the postoperative time point. Interestingly, in contrast to data in lung cancer [45], ctDNA collected within 24 h of surgery was shown to be prognostic (HR 5.595, p value = 0.0078) (Supp Table 13). For those that had both Time Points 2B and 3 collected, the results were congruent in the majority (Supp Fig. 5). Incongruent cases included two that became ctDNA negative by Time Point 3. Unsurprisingly there were a proportion of cases that had incongruent ctDNA results when comparing the preoperative Time point 1 ctDNA results to the Time point 3 (postoperative) ctDNA results (Supp Fig. 5). Interestingly a high number (46%) of cases that had ctDNA positivity before surgery became negative after surgery. Importantly no cases that were ctDNA negative prior to surgery become ctDNA positive after surgery (Supp Table 8).

Discussion

In this pilot study, we demonstrate the feasibility of perioperative ctDNA assessment in EC using a tumour-informed, single-mutation ddPCR approach. ctDNA was detectable across all stages and molecular subgroups, and preoperative ctDNA levels correlated with imaging-defined tumour volume and stage, supporting its role as a surrogate of tumour burden. Notably, despite the inherent limitations of a single-mutation assay (which may miss subclonal variants), ctDNA positivity remained prognostic, underscoring the robustness of the biological signal.

The optimal timing of ctDNA sampling for prognostication in solid tumours remains uncertain and understudied [46]. In this study, timing emerged as a key determinant of clinical utility. As anticipated, ctDNA levels increased intraoperatively in many patients, likely reflecting tumour manipulation; however, this was not associated with RFS, indicating limited prognostic relevance. Similarly, while preoperative ctDNA showed a trend toward increased relapse risk, consistent with prior EC studies [47], this did not reach statistical significance.

Our study demonstrated an association between early postoperative ctDNA positivity (2B) and RFS. However, early postoperative ctDNA results should be interpreted with caution, as they may be biologically confounded by perioperative DNA release and increased background cell-free DNA, potentially limiting sensitivity for MRD detection [48]. This issue was also highlighted in the DYNAMIC study, which found that ctDNA detection on postoperative day 1 was not prognostic, likely reflecting transient perioperative ctDNA release rather than persistent residual disease [45]. Although early postoperative testing may be susceptible to false-negative results at this time point, a positive ctDNA result at this timepoint is still likely to retain clinical significance [49]. However, clinical implementation of this timepoint would likely require repeat or serial sampling strategies to improve reliability and minimise false-negative findings.

In this study ctDNA positivity detected 2–6 weeks after surgery was strongly associated with inferior RFS. At this timepoint, ctDNA detection is less likely to reflect transient perioperative shedding and more likely to indicate persistent residual disease supporting this interval as the most informative window for MRD assessment.

Our findings both corroborate and extend prior evidence supporting postoperative ctDNA as a prognostic biomarker in EC. Recio et al. demonstrated the prognostic significance of postoperative ctDNA in early-stage disease [29], while a recent meta-analysis [50] showed that although both preoperative and postoperative ctDNA are associated with adverse outcomes, the prognostic effect is substantially stronger postoperatively. Together, these data support a model in which preoperative ctDNA primarily reflects tumour burden, whereas postoperative detection more accurately captures MRD. Recurrence occurred across almost all clinicopathological and molecular subgroups in our cohort with the exception of POLEm tumours, including a substantial proportion of patients with stage I disease and NSMP EC. These findings highlight the limitations of current clinicopathological risk stratification systems and underscore the need for biomarkers capable of identifying occult residual disease despite otherwise favourable disease features.

Compared with prior studies, the CODEC cohort demonstrated a higher ctDNA detection rates, both preoperatively (58%vs 22%) and postoperatively (32%vs 12%) [30]. These differences likely reflect the distinct technologies employed, with ddPCR offering greater analytical sensitivity than NGS-based approaches. However, while NGS may be less sensitive for low-level ctDNA detection, it provides broader genomic characterisation, including multi-mutation profiling and detection of emerging resistance alterations. A study with multiplex PCR approach however had fewer ctDNA-negative relapses [29]. As such, the optimal ctDNA platform will ultimately depend on the intended clinical application.

These findings have important translational implications. If ctDNA is to inform adjuvant treatment decisions, minimising false-negative MRD assessments will be essential. Achieving this will likely require optimisation of both assay sensitivity and postoperative sampling strategies, potentially including serial ctDNA monitoring.

Taken together, our data support postoperative ctDNA as a dynamic biomarker of residual disease with clear potential to refine recurrence risk assessment beyond existing clinicopathological models. The 2–6 week postoperative interval may represent the most informative window for integration into MRD-directed therapeutic strategies in EC.

This study has several limitations. The cohort size is modest, and not all ctDNA samples were assessable, limiting statistical power and precluding multivariable analysis. There was also heterogeneity in stage, histology and adjuvant treatment. While this could be seen as a limitation, we saw it as an advantage in being able to explore ctDNA across the spectrum of EC cases undergoing definitive EC surgery and was considered in the inclusion criteria. Also, while we appreciate that inclusion of Type 2 EC is now antiquated nomenclature, at the time of study design, this was still in use and used to select higher risk histologies. Importantly, correlation analyses between ctDNA levels and imaging-derived tumour volume were performed only in preoperative samples; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding this relationship at other perioperative timepoints. Additionally, the use of a single mutation ddPCR assay may have reduced sensitivity compared with multiplex or highly sensitive sequencing-based approaches.

In summary, this pilot study provides prospective insight into perioperative ctDNA dynamics in EC and highlights the importance of sampling timing. Preoperative ctDNA correlates with tumour burden, while postoperative ctDNA demonstrates a hypothesis-generating signal for prognostic value. Given the potential for biological confounding at early postoperative timepoints, the 2–6 week postoperative window appears to be the most clinically informative for detecting MRD. Further studies are required to optimise ctDNA assay methodology, determine whether ctDNA results can be generated within clinically relevant timelines for adjuvant treatment decision-making, and establish whether ctDNA-guided adjuvant therapy selection can improve clinical outcomes.
