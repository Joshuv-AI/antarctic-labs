Thymic evolution on serial CT in glucocorticoid-treated pediatric pneumonia: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Jing Tang, Dao Yuan, Yuan Fang, Donggang Pan, Shaoqing Gu, Chao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pediatric thymus is sensitive to infection-related stress and glucocorticoid exposure, yet data remain limited on short-term thymic changes on paired chest CT before and after glucocorticoid-containing treatment for childhood pneumonia. This study evaluated changes in thymic transverse and anteroposterior diameters and CT attenuation on paired chest CT scans.MethodsWe retrospectively included 90 children with pneumonia who received glucocorticoid-containing therapy and had paired chest CT scans. Two radiologists independently measured the thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation before and after treatment. We used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare pre- and post-treatment thymic parameters, and used age-stratified analysis, Spearman correlation, and multivariable linear regression to explore factors associated with thymic percentage changes.ResultsAfter treatment, thymic transverse diameter decreased from 2.80 cm to 2.40 cm, anteroposterior diameter decreased from 1.20 cm to 0.70 cm, and CT attenuation decreased from 50.00 HU to 37.50 HU; all three parameters were significantly lower after treatment (all P < 0.001). Age-stratified analysis showed that each age group experienced significant reductions in these parameters, with no significant between-group differences in percentage changes. Dose-related analysis showed that cumulative methylprednisolone-equivalent dose and daily methylprednisolone-equivalent dose were weakly negatively correlated with the percentage change in thymic transverse diameter. However, multivariable linear regression indicated that dose metrics were not significantly associated with percentage changes in thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, or CT attenuation.ConclusionIn children with pneumonia receiving glucocorticoid-containing treatment, short-term follow-up CT showed reductions in thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation. Recognizing these possible short-term thymic changes may help pediatricians and radiologists avoid overinterpreting follow-up CT findings as anterior mediastinal lesions.Given the lack of a non-glucocorticoid control and the potential for severe pneumonia to induce thymic involution, causality cannot be inferred. Thus, thymic CT changes are not validated biomarkers of glucocorticoid exposure or immune function.

Introduction

The thymus is a central immune organ responsible for T lymphocyte development and maturation. During childhood growth, the thymus undergoes phases of proliferation and involution that are integral to immune competence. The organ is also highly sensitive to pathogenic insults and pharmacologic agents. Stress, acute infection, glucocorticoid exposure, and cytoreductive therapies have been associated with thymic involution and altered thymic immune function [1–3]. In pediatric clinical practice, glucocorticoids are used as adjunctive therapy in selected children with pneumonia, particularly in severe or complicated cases. Theoretically, both the infection and the administration of exogenous hormones could jointly modulate thymic immune function, potentially resulting in thymic atrophy [4]. Despite this, the clinical implications have received limited attention, and systematic quantitative studies are lacking. Reports using imaging to document dynamic thymic changes after glucocorticoid-containing treatment for pneumonia are scarce [5–8].

The pediatric thymus is located in the anterior mediastinum. Because of its anterior mediastinal location and irregular three-dimensional morphology, assessment of the thymus on chest radiography or ultrasound can be limited by bone, intrathoracic air, and operator-dependent factors. Consequently, these modalities may be insufficient for detailed quantitative assessment of thymic morphology. Cross-sectional imaging, including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), provides more detailed assessment of thymic structure [9]. However, ionizing radiation from CT and the cost and availability of MRI limit their routine use for isolated thymic assessment in children. Chest CT is nevertheless commonly performed to assess and follow the course of pediatric pneumonia; paired CT scans obtained during treatment therefore offer an opportunity to observe short-term changes in thymic size and CT attenuation.

Previous work has documented age-related imaging changes in the normal pediatric thymus, stress-induced thymic involution, and rebound thymic hyperplasia following recovery or cessation of immunosuppressive therapy [10]. However, paired imaging studies characterizing short-term thymic CT changes in children with pneumonia receiving glucocorticoid-containing treatment remain limited. This study quantifies pre- and post-treatment changes in thymic transverse and anteroposterior diameters and CT attenuation, reports the magnitude of these changes across age groups, and investigates associations between clinical and treatment-related factors and thymic alterations.

Materials and methods

General data

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Jiangsu University (No. 2025KYW001—53). Because it was a retrospective analysis of routine clinical diagnosis and treatment data without additional interventions and all personally identifiable information was removed, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

We retrospectively collected data on hospitalized children diagnosed with pneumonia in the Department of Pediatrics at the People's Hospital Affiliated to Jiangsu University from February 2024 to February 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 28 days and ≤ 14 years; (2) diagnosis of pneumonia; (3) receipt of systemic glucocorticoid therapy for clinical indications such as severe pneumonia, pleural effusion, marked dyspnea or wheezing, or increased respiratory secretions; and (4) availability of paired chest CT examinations before and after glucocorticoid-containing treatment and complete clinical, laboratory, imaging, and glucocorticoid exposure data.

Exclusion criteria: (1) poor CT image quality or severe motion artifacts that prevented reliable thymic measurement, such as blurred thymic borders caused by respiratory or positional motion; (2) previous thoracic surgery, known thymic disease, or anterior mediastinal mass, including thymoma or thymic hyperplasia; (3) known congenital immunodeficiency, malignancy, autoimmune disease; or chronic systemic inflammatory disease; and (4) long-term use of glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressive agents before admission.

Glucocorticoid treatment and clinical data

All children received systemic glucocorticoid therapy in addition to antibiotic treatment. Glucocorticoids were administered intravenously or orally according to clinical need. Intravenous therapy predominantly used methylprednisolone, while a small number of patients received short courses of dexamethasone as clinically indicated. After intravenous treatment, some children were transitioned to oral prednisone with the dose tapered gradually to discontinuation.

All glucocorticoid doses were converted to methylprednisolone equivalents using standard anti-inflammatory equivalence ratios: 5 mg prednisone ≈ 4 mg methylprednisolone ≈ 0.75 mg dexamethasone. We recorded the cumulative total methylprednisolone-equivalent dose (mg), the cumulative equivalent dose per body weight (mg/kg), the daily equivalent dose (mg/kg/day), and the number of treatment days. CT examination method

All children underwent noncontrast chest CT. Imaging was performed on either a Siemens 64-slice CT or a Philips Brilliance 256-slice CT. Scans used a tube voltage of 100 kV, a tube current of 60 mAs, and a slice thickness of 0.625 mm or 0.5 mm. Cross-sectional images were reconstructed with soft-tissue (window width 350 HU, window level 40 HU) and lung (window width 1500 HU, window level − 600 HU) settings, and reconstructions were produced with a 5 mm slice thickness. Paired CT examinations were obtained before treatment and during short-term follow-up after glucocorticoid-containing treatment, and the interval between the two scans was recorded.

Image analysis

Transverse and anteroposterior diameters and CT attenuation of the thymus were measured on axial soft-tissue-window CT images at the aortic arch level before and after treatment. The aortic arch level was chosen because thymic morphology varies and this level frequently corresponds to the gland’s largest cross-section. Measurements were made independently by two radiologists with more than 10 and 20 years of experience, respectively. The maximum anteroposterior diameter was measured at the thickest point along the midline that separates the left and right lobes. The maximum transverse diameter was measured at the greatest lateral extent of the thymic lobes (Fig. 1). Two regions of interest (ROIs; area: 30 mm2) were placed in the left and right lobes on the largest section to measure CT attenuation, and the mean of the two ROIs was recorded. Simultaneously, pneumonia scores were assigned based on the cross—sectional CT images of the lung window before and after treatment, as follows: The assessors visually determined the extent of pneumonia involvement for scoring. The two lungs were divided into four lung regions: the right upper and middle lungs, the right lower lung, the left upper lung, and the left lower lung. The scores for each lung region (0—5 points) were calculated according to the percentage of the volume affected by inflammation: 0 points (no involvement), 1 point (1–5%), 2 points (6–25%), 3 points (26–50%), 4 points (51–75%), 5 points (> 75%). The sum of the scores of each lung region was the pneumonia score [11]. The thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation of the thymus before and after treatment were used to calculate the consistency between two observers using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (0—0.2, poor; 0.2—0.4, fair; 0.4—0.6, moderate; 0.6—0.8, good; 0.8—1.0, excellent). The percentage changes of the thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation of the thymus before and after treatment were calculated respectively to visually show the changes in the thymus. The percentage change = (value before treatment—value after treatment)/value before treatment × 100%. A positive value indicates a decrease after treatment, while a negative value indicates an increase after treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and MedCalc 23.3.4, and G*Power 3.1.9.7 was used to assess the sensitivity and power of relevant tests. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Variables with a normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation; nonnormal variables are reported as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as number and percentage. Because some paired differences and percentage changes were non-normally distributed, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare paired measurements of thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, CT attenuation value, and pneumonia score before and after treatment. Absolute change was defined as the pre-treatment value minus the post-treatment value; percentage change was defined as the absolute change divided by the pre-treatment value and multiplied by 100%.

In this study, guided by children's physiological characteristics and prior CT investigations of the pediatric thymus [10], participants were stratified into three age groups: 1–6 years, 7–10 years, and 11–14 years. Within each age group, pre- and post-treatment measurements were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The Kruskal–Wallis test assessed differences in percentage changes across the age groups. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations of age, BMI, pre-treatment WBC, pre-treatment CRP, and methylprednisolone-equivalent glucocorticoid exposure metrics with percentage changes in thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation. Equivalent methylprednisolone exposure was characterized by three metrics: cumulative equivalent total amount (mg), cumulative equivalent dose (mg/kg), and daily equivalent dose (mg/kg/day).

Multivariable linear regression was used to assess the adjusted associations between different equivalent exposure indicators of methylprednisolone and thymic percentage changes. Separate models used cumulative equivalent dose of methylprednisolone (mg/kg) and daily equivalent dose of methylprednisolone (mg/kg/day) as exposure variables. Each model adjusted for age, gender, BMI, the corresponding baseline thymic measurement (baseline transverse diameter for the transverse-diameter percentage-change model, baseline anteroposterior diameter for the anteroposterior-diameter change-rate model, and baseline CT attenuation value for the CT-value change-rate model), baseline pneumonia score, CT interval, and number of days of glucocorticoid treatment. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals and P values were estimated using HC3 robust standard errors. Measurement agreement between the two observers was assessed with the intra-class correlation coefficient.

This retrospective study did not include an a priori sample size calculation; instead, all eligible cases during the study period were analyzed. A G*Power sensitivity analysis indicated that, with N = 90, two-sided α = 0.05, and power = 80%, the smallest detectable correlation coefficient was approximately |r|= 0.29. Consequently, correlations with |r|< 0.29 may not be reliably detected. Age-stratified analyses, dose–response analyses, and multivariable regression were treated as exploratory. All hypothesis tests were two-sided. Unless otherwise noted, P < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant; P values from exploratory correlation analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Results

Characteristics of patients

During the study period, 1,586 children with pneumonia were admitted to our pediatric inpatient department, of whom 782 received glucocorticoid therapy. Applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria yielded 90 children for analysis; the screening flow is shown in Fig. 2. The cohort comprised 49 males and 41 females, aged 1 to 14 years, with a mean age of 8.21 ± 2.92 years. Etiologic testing was dominated by atypical pathogens: 85 cases (94.4%) were positive for Mycoplasma pneumoniae–related tests and 5 cases (5.6%) were positive for Chlamydia pneumoniae–related tests. Nucleic acid testing for respiratory viruses was positive in 5 children, and bacterial culture of sputum or respiratory specimens was positive in 6 children; some children had positive results on multiple etiological tests. The median duration of glucocorticoid therapy was 7.00 (5.00, 8.63) days, the median cumulative methylprednisolone-equivalent dose was 18.25 (12.47, 23.86) mg/kg, and the median interval between the two CT examinations was 10.00 (9.00, 11.00) days. After treatment, the pneumonia score declined from 4.00 (3.00, 5.00) before treatment to 2.00 (1.00, 2.00) (P < 0.001), indicating reduced pulmonary inflammatory involvement during follow-up. Discussion

During childhood, the thymus undergoes predictable physiological changes. Its weight and volume increase through the embryonic period and early childhood, peaking at puberty, then decline with age as thymic tissue is progressively replaced by fat, decreasing density and function [6, 7]. Computed tomography (CT) captures these changes in thymic volume and attenuation. Thymic CT attenuation (HU) is highest in infancy and falls as intrathymic fat increases with age. Transverse diameter and lobe thickness decline markedly with age, whereas anteroposterior diameter (AP diameter) shows no significant age correlation. Sex does not significantly influence thymic size or density [5, 12]. Higher body mass index (BMI) correlates with greater thymic fatty degeneration [13]. Thus, age and BMI are important contextual factors when interpreting CT features of the thymus in children with pneumonia who receive glucocorticoid-containing therapy.

Glucocorticoids are widely used to treat autoimmune and infectious diseases, but their use has been linked to accelerated thymic involution in patients [14]. Experimental studies indicate that glucocorticoids bind intracellular glucocorticoid receptors (GRs) and trigger the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway in double-positive (DP) thymocytes, disrupting thymocyte numbers and organization and causing apoptosis and thymic atrophy [15, 16]. Animal MRI studies have reported reductions in thymic volume after glucocorticoid exposure and have shown a strong correlation between MRI-measured thymic volume and the wet weight of the isolated thymus. Moreover, research has demonstrated a dose-dependent loss of thymic tissue with increasing hormone dose [17].

This study analyzed paired chest CT scans from 90 children with pneumonia before and after treatment. After glucocorticoid-containing treatment, the thymus exhibited significant short-term decreases in transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation, and the pre- and post-treatment differences were statistically significant. These findings align with prior reports of glucocorticoid-associated thymic involution, but they do not constitute definitive proof that exogenous glucocorticoids alone produced the observed changes. The reduced thymic diameters could signify transient involution or alterations in thymic tissue composition. Although diameters are two-dimensional measures and do not represent true three-dimensional thymic volume, they remain practical imaging metrics for describing short-term morphological changes on routine chest CT. A decrease in CT attenuation may reflect loss of soft-tissue components, a relative increase in fat components, or other local changes in tissue composition. This observation could relate to the capacity of glucocorticoids to induce thymocyte apoptosis and to promote fat deposition [18]. Nevertheless, a lower CT attenuation does not directly prove fat deposition nor confirm thymocyte apoptosis; it provides only an indirect indicator of altered thymic density on imaging. Prior reports linking respiratory symptoms to thymic enlargement have largely involved infants or young children, who may develop dyspnea or present with anterior mediastinal mass–like findings from thymic enlargement and sometimes receive glucocorticoid therapy [19]. Although our cohort comprised children treated with adjunctive anti-inflammatory therapy for pneumonia, the imaging data can still serve as a reference for evaluating thymic enlargement in children and may help avoid misclassifying transient thymic changes as anterior mediastinal masses or other space-occupying lesions.

Because thymic morphology and CT attenuation in children vary with age, we performed both age-stratified subgroup analyses and continuous age analyses. The subgroup analysis showed reductions in the thymus transverse and anteroposterior diameters and in CT attenuation after treatment across age groups, with no significant differences in the magnitude of change between groups. An exploratory Spearman correlation indicated a weak positive association between age and the relative change in CT attenuation. These findings imply that age may influence CT-value changes; however, the correlation was weak, subgroup sample sizes were limited, individual variability in CT changes was large, and the correlation P value was not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Therefore, this result should be considered exploratory and requires confirmation in further studies.

BMI and the rate of change in CT attenuation followed the same trend, indicating that individuals with higher BMI might experience a greater decline in thymic CT attenuation. The observed correlation, however, was weak and did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.064). Thus, the data are insufficient to establish BMI as a definitive determinant of thymic CT-value change. Given the physiological tendency for the thymus to undergo fat replacement with age and with changes in body habitus, children with higher BMI may have different baseline thymic tissue composition that influences CT-value dynamics. Because this study did not perform quantitative assessment of thymic fat, the impact of BMI on thymic CT-value change remains to be confirmed by further research.

This study found no significant correlation between pre-treatment WBC or CRP and the percentage changes of thymic transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, or CT attenuation. Prior work has shown that bacterial and viral infections can cause thymic atrophy [20]; proposed mechanisms include activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, increased endogenous glucocorticoids, and release of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, IFN-α, and TNF-α [21, 22]. Thus, infection-related stress and inflammation could plausibly contribute to short-term thymic changes. However, WBC and CRP only partially reflect peripheral inflammation and do not fully capture pneumonia severity, the magnitude of inflammatory stress, or the endogenous glucocorticoid response. Therefore, the lack of significant associations between WBC or CRP and thymic percentage changes should not be interpreted as evidence that infection-related stress or disease severity did not contribute to the observed thymic changes. A recent study by Collins et al. (2024) showed that, during systemic immune stress, glucocorticoids rather than pro-inflammatory cytokines primarily drive thymic atrophy [8]. This mechanism aligns with our imaging findings of reduced transverse and anteroposterior thymic diameters and lower CT attenuation following glucocorticoid-containing treatment. However, because this study lacked a pneumonia control group that did not receive glucocorticoids, and because glucocorticoid use depends on disease severity and clinical indications, we cannot attribute thymic changes solely to exogenous glucocorticoid exposure. Instead, infection-related stress, endogenous cortisol responses, and disease severity likely act together. Importantly, severe pneumonia itself represents a major systemic physiological stressor and may independently contribute to thymic involution. Pneumonia-related inflammation, nutritional burden, and activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis may increase endogenous glucocorticoid responses and affect thymic morphology even without exogenous glucocorticoid exposure [1, 20]. Therefore, the thymic changes observed in this cohort should be interpreted as potentially multifactorial, reflecting severe infection-related stress and glucocorticoid-containing treatment rather than the specific effect of exogenous glucocorticoids alone.

Dose-related and multivariable linear regression analyses did not reveal a stable linear relationship between glucocorticoid exposure dose and the rate of thymic change. Although this finding appears to contradict the observed association, it should not be taken as evidence of no relationship between glucocorticoid exposure and thymic alterations. All children in this study received systemic glucocorticoid treatment; thus, the dose analysis effectively compared how dose relates to the magnitude of thymic change within a population already treated with systemic hormones, rather than contrasting hormone-exposed and hormone-unexposed groups. It is plausible that the pediatric thymus is highly sensitive to glucocorticoids, such that once clinical treatment reaches a threshold dose, short-term imaging changes emerge and further dose increases do not produce proportional linear effects. Although we did not observe a clear linear dose–response relationship, this finding does not negate the imaging changes documented in this study: reductions in the thymus’ transverse and anteroposterior diameters and in CT attenuation after glucocorticoid-containing treatment. The relatively narrow glucocorticoid dose range in our cohort limited our ability to detect a dose-gradient effect. Cumulative dose also fails to capture important variables such as peak dose, timing of administration, treatment stage, interval since drug withdrawal, and endogenous glucocorticoid responses. Finally, short-term thymic changes may reflect concurrent influences including infection-related stress, disease severity, baseline thymic imaging features, nutritional status, and antibiotic therapy.

Confounding by indication is a major limitation of this study. In clinical practice, systemic glucocorticoids are preferentially given to children with more severe or complex pneumonia presentations, including severe pneumonia, pleural effusion, dyspnea/respiratory distress, increased respiratory secretions, or extensive imaging involvement. Those clinical features reflect greater inflammatory stress and disease severity, which can independently alter thymic morphology and CT attenuation. To reduce such confounding, we supplemented baseline data with severity- and indication-related measures and adjusted for baseline pneumonia score, CT interval, and days of glucocorticoid treatment in the multivariable linear regression model. Nevertheless, because glucocorticoid use was not randomized and no pneumonia control group without glucocorticoids was available, residual confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded.

This study also reveals substantial individual variability in short-term thymic changes. Although the overall trend was a reduction in transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and CT attenuation, some children showed stable or increased thymic diameter or CT attenuation after treatment. Prior work indicates that glucocorticoid effects on the thymus are heterogeneous: thymocytes at different maturation stages differ in sensitivity to glucocorticoid-induced apoptosis, and the intensity of glucocorticoid receptor signaling, thymocyte-subset composition, and the thymic epithelial microenvironment modulate the response [23–25]. The thymus also responds dynamically to changes in glucocorticoid exposure; following stress relief, dose reduction, or drug withdrawal, restorative enlargement or rebound hyperplasia can occur [6, 19, 26]. Therefore, in this study, the absence of thymic decrease or the observed increase in thymic parameters in some children after treatment may reflect baseline thymic composition, individual glucocorticoid sensitivity, disease recovery status, and thymic remodeling during convalescence. However, because of concerns about pediatric radiation exposure and medical costs, long-term imaging follow-up was not routinely performed. Consequently, we could not assess whether the thymus undergoes restorative enlargement or rebound hyperplasia after treatment cessation. When clinical follow-up is required for suspected thymic rebound enlargement or persistent anterior mediastinal fullness, prioritize radiation-free modalities: use ultrasound when the thymus is accessible, and MRI when detailed cross-sectional characterization is needed.

Considerations regarding radiation from diagnostic CT: The thymus is highly radiosensitive, and radiation can reduce thymic volume and thymocyte numbers [27]. However, pronounced thymic atrophy reported previously generally follows total-body radiotherapy or severe radiation accidents [28, 29]. In this study, children underwent diagnostic chest CT with radiation doses far below those scenarios, and such low exposures are theoretically insufficient to produce comparable morphological changes.

This study clarifies short-term changes in thymic diameter and CT attenuation during glucocorticoid treatment of pediatric pneumonia by using clinically paired chest CT scans. When children receiving glucocorticoids show a reduced transverse diameter, anteroposterior diameter, or CT attenuation of the thymus on short-term follow-up CT, clinicians should integrate recent infection status, disease severity, and glucocorticoid exposure history to avoid mistaking these transient changes for anterior mediastinal or other space-occupying lesions. Conversely, the findings do not support using thymic CT changes as imaging biomarkers of glucocorticoid efficacy, the degree of immunosuppression, or long-term immune function alterations.

Limitations and prospects

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the sample size is limited, and no pneumonia control group without glucocorticoid treatment was available. Second, the inclusion criteria required children to receive systemic glucocorticoids and to have paired CT examinations before and after treatment; therefore, the analyzed cohort likely represents a subgroup with more severe or complex pneumonia who required adjuvant anti-inflammatory therapy and repeated imaging, introducing selection bias. Third, glucocorticoids were administered according to clinical indications such as severe pneumonia, pleural effusion, dyspnea/wheezing, or increased respiratory secretions; these factors themselves reflect physiological stress and disease severity, so residual indication confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, although changes in thymic transverse and anteroposterior diameters can partly reflect thymic-volume change [12], these two-dimensional measures do not capture true three-dimensional thymic volume.

Fifth, long-term follow-up imaging was not routinely obtained, so thymic recovery or rebound hyperplasia could not be evaluated. Although 90 paired observations were sufficient to detect the pre- to post-treatment differences reported here, the sample size remained limited for assessing weak correlations, age-stratified effects, dose–response relationships, and multivariable regression. Thus, negative findings should not be taken as definitive evidence of absence, and these analyses should be considered exploratory. Sixth, etiological data were obtained from routine clinical tests and electronic medical records. Because some children tested positive on multiple etiological assays, we did not classify patients into viral, bacterial, or atypical pathogen pneumonia groups. Consequently, we could not assess the independent effects of specific pathogen types on short-term thymic imaging changes. Future prospective studies should include a pneumonia control group without glucocorticoid exposure, use standardized CT or MRI protocols and more precise thymic volume measurement methods, and integrate lymphocyte subset analysis, cytokine profiling, endogenous cortisol assessment, and long-term imaging follow-up to clarify the mechanisms, timing, and clinical significance of short-term thymic changes during pediatric pneumonia treatment.

Conclusion

This retrospective paired CT study demonstrates that short-term follow-up after glucocorticoid-containing therapy for childhood pneumonia is associated with reductions in the thymus’ transverse and anteroposterior diameters and in thymic CT attenuation, and that these changes appear across age groups. Recognizing these short-term thymic changes after treatment may help pediatricians and radiologists avoid misinterpreting them as anterior mediastinal lesions on follow-up CT. However, because no control group without glucocorticoid exposure was included, and disease severity, treatment indications, and systemic stress associated with severe pneumonia may confound the findings, this study cannot establish causality.Thymic CT changes should not be interpreted as validated biomarkers of glucocorticoid effect or immune function.
