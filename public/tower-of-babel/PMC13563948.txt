Procedural efficiency and safety of traction-assisted versus conventional endoscopic submucosal dissection for gastric tumors: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Marwan Desouky, Loubna Fadeli, Anas Al Mardini, Abdelwahap Elghezewi, Wesam Frandah, Ahmed Sherif, Muhammed Elhadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is an effective minimally invasive treatment for superficial gastric tumors. However, it is technically demanding and often associated with prolonged procedure time and a risk of adverse events. Traction-assisted ESD (TA-ESD) has been introduced to improve submucosal visualization and procedural efficiency; however, evidence regarding its benefit over conventional ESD (C-ESD) in gastric lesions remains inconsistent. This study aimed to compare procedural efficiency, resection quality, and safety between TA-ESD and C-ESD in patients with superficial gastric tumors.MethodsWe conducted a PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing TA-ESD with C-ESD for gastric tumors. Five electronic databases were searched through November 8, 2025. Only trials reporting intention-to-treat or modified intention-to-treat analyses were included. The primary outcome was procedure time. Secondary outcomes included en bloc resection, R0 resection, perforation, delayed bleeding, and traction-related damage to the specimen. Pooled estimates were calculated using the mean difference (MD) and risk ratio (RR). Subgroup analyses were performed based on endoscopist experience, tumor location, and tumor size.ResultsFive randomized controlled trials comprising 463 patients (228 TA-ESD and 235 C-ESD) met the inclusion criteria. TA-ESD was associated with a shorter procedure time than C-ESD (mean difference [MD] = -12.45 min, 95% CI: -20.67 to -4.23; P = 0.003); however, this finding was sensitive to leave-one-out analyses, with statistical significance lost after exclusion of either the Bi 2024 or Nagata 2021 trial. Procedure time was lower for both upper/middle-third gastric lesions (MD = -21.21 min, 95% CI: -30.26 to -12.17; P < 0.0001) and lower-third lesions (MD = -10.72 min, 95% CI: -17.79 to -3.64; P = 0.003). En bloc resection rates (risk ratio [RR] = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98–1.02; P = 0.98) and R0 resection rates (RR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.93–1.06; P = 0.83) were comparable between groups. There were no differences in perforation (RR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.07–3.97; P = 0.53) or delayed bleeding (RR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.29–2.09; P = 0.61). Traction-related specimen damage was rare, with a pooled incidence of 1.3%.ConclusionTA-ESD was associated with shorter procedure time than C-ESD, while maintaining comparable resection quality and safety. However, this association was sensitive to leave-one-out analyses and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, the overall certainty of evidence was limited, with three of five included trials judged to be at high risk of bias, and GRADE certainty for most secondary outcomes rated as low to very low. These limitations, together with the small number of available trials, underscore the need for further large, multicenter randomized studies using standardized traction techniques to better define the efficacy and safety of TA-ESD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05052-5.Keywords: Endoscopic submucosal dissection, Traction-assisted ESD, Gastric tumors, Procedure time, Resection outcomes, Randomized controlled trials, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is an advanced therapeutic technique enabled by major technological advancements, offering a minimally invasive approach with favorable outcomes for superficial gastric neoplasms [1–3]. Nevertheless, its widespread adoption in routine practice remains limited due to procedural complexity and prolonged operation times [4, 5]. Successful ESD requires substantial expertise, particularly for the precise identification and dissection of the submucosal layer [6–8]. Tumor location is a key determinant of procedural difficulty, with lesions in the upper and middle thirds of the stomach associated with significantly longer procedure times and higher technical risk [9, 10]. Traction-assisted ESD (TA-ESD) has been introduced to address these challenges by improving submucosal exposure and procedural efficiency [11–13].

Imaeda et al. [14] first reported traction-assisted gastric ESD using external grasping forceps, enabling safe and effective en bloc resection under direct visualization, with complete resections achieved without significant bleeding or perforation, while also simplifying and shortening the procedure. Subsequently, various TA-ESD techniques, both internal and external, have been developed to provide dynamic counter-traction, enhance submucosal visualization, and stabilize the lesion during dissection [15, 16].

The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines and technical reviews, along with recent narrative reviews, identify traction as a promising strategy to enhance the efficiency and safety of gastric ESD, while emphasizing that the supporting evidence is limited by small RCTs and heterogeneous study designs [15–18].

Several meta-analyses assessed the impact of TA-ESD across the gastrointestinal tract, with many reporting that TA-ESD reduced procedure time compared with conventional ESD (C-ESD), while preserving en bloc and R0 resection rates and decreasing the risk of perforation [19–24]. However, most of these analyses pooled esophageal, gastric, and colorectal lesions or combined randomized and non-randomized studies with heterogeneous traction techniques, limiting the applicability of their findings to gastric ESD, which has distinct anatomy and complication profiles [19–24]. Two recent device-specific meta-analyses [25, 26] included mixed study designs; one incorporated only a single gastric RCT [25], while the other included none [26]. The only gastric-focused meta-analyses to date also combined randomized and non-randomized studies, mixing intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) data, thereby increasing bias and undermining the validity of the pooled estimates [23, 27]. An updated gastric-specific meta-analysis including only RCTs reporting ITT or modified ITT (m-ITT) outcomes may provide a more methodologically robust estimate of the effect of TA-ESD compared with C-ESD by preserving randomization through ITT or m-ITT analyses.

We aimed to evaluate the impact of TA-ESD compared to C-ESD in patients with superficial gastric tumors, focusing on procedure time, en bloc and R0 resection rates, traction-related damage to the specimen, and key adverse events including perforation and delayed bleeding. We additionally explored whether effects differed by lesion location and endoscopist experience.

Materials and methods

This review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the ID CRD420261295697. This meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement guidelines throughout the conduct and reporting of the review [28]. The completed PRISMA checklist is detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Search strategy & selection criteria

A comprehensive search was conducted on November 8, 2025, across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating traction-assisted endoscopic submucosal dissection (TA-ESD) versus conventional ESD (C-ESD) for superficial gastric tumors. Full database-specific strategies are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Reference lists of included articles were manually searched for additional eligible trials.

We included RCTs evaluating any traction or countertraction technique/device versus no traction during gastric ESD, provided outcomes were reported using intention-to-treat (ITT) or modified ITT (mITT) analyses to appropriately account for crossover, thereby preserving randomization and minimizing selection bias. Trials reporting only per-protocol (PP) analyses were excluded to avoid potential overestimation of treatment effects resulting from the exclusion of non-adherent participants. We excluded non-randomized studies, trials not involving gastric ESD, and reports limited to PP analyses. We also excluded reviews, protocols, letters, conference abstracts, animal or ex vivo studies, duplicate or overlapping publications, non-English articles, and other ineligible formats.

Screening was conducted in Covidence (covidence.org, Melbourne, Australia) by two reviewers independently at the title/abstract stage and subsequently at full-text review. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by the senior author when necessary.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using standardized Excel forms. Collected variables included: (1) Trial characteristics: first author, publication year, country, study period, analysis set, traction technique, and operator experience; (2) Participants baseline and lesion characteristics: sample size, age, gender, tumor location, tumor diameter, and macroscopic type (3) Histopathological features of resected tumor including grading and depth; (4) Efficacy outcomes: procedure time, en bloc resection, R0 resection, traction-related damage to the specimen, dissection speed, horizontal margin involvement (HM1), specimen diameter and size; (5) Adverse events: perforation and delayed bleeding.

When continuous outcomes were reported as medians with ranges or interquartile ranges, corresponding means and standard deviations (SDs) were estimated using established methods [29, 30]. For “procedure time” stratified by tumor location, subgroup summary statistics were combined to align with our prespecified location categories using the StatsToDo “Combine Means and SDs” calculator [31, 32]. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or senior author adjudication.

Critical appraisal

Risk of bias for included RCTs was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) [33] across five domains (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting), with judgments of “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk” and an overall rating reached by consensus. The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach [34]. Outcomes reported in only two studies or as single-arm (without a comparator) were not evaluated with GRADE, as the limited evidence was insufficient to produce a reliable certainty rating.

Outcomes & adverse events

We set procedure time as the primary outcome, defined as the interval from the first submucosal injection to completion of submucosal dissection and lesion removal, including traction-device setup or attachment and end-of-procedure hemostasis when applicable. Secondary outcomes were (1) en bloc resection, defined as single-piece resection without snare use. (2) R0 resection, defined as en bloc resection with tumor-free horizontal and vertical margins. (3) Traction-related damage to the specimen, recorded as any tear, split, or device-related injury to the resected tissue during the procedure.

Adverse events included perforation, defined as endoscopic visualization of extra-luminal structures and/or radiologic evidence of free air or contrast leakage, and delayed bleeding, defined as post-ESD bleeding requiring endoscopic hemostasis and/or associated with clinical severity, such as Hb drop > 2 g/dL, transfusion, or surgery. Trial-specific definitions are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted in R (RStudio) using the meta package. Continuous outcomes were pooled as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the inverse-variance method, and dichotomous outcomes were pooled as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs using the Mantel–Haenszel method. For outcomes with zero events in one arm, we applied a continuity correction. Studies with zero events in both arms were not included in the RR pooling (assigned zero weight) because they do not contribute information for relative effect estimation.

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q-test and I² statistic, with I² interpreted as 0–25% (low), 26–50% (moderate), 51–75% (substantial), and 76–100% (considerable) heterogeneity. A random-effects model was used to incorporate between-study variability. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prespecified subgroup analyses explored potential clinical heterogeneity by endoscopist experience, tumor location, and tumor size. Tumor location and size subgrouping were conducted for procedure time. Endoscopist-experience subgrouping was applied to outcomes and adverse events, and subgroup differences were examined using formal tests for interaction where applicable.

Two trials categorized tumor size as ≤ 20 mm versus > 20 mm [35, 36], whereas one trial used < 20 mm versus ≥ 20 mm [37]. To standardize categories across studies while minimizing boundary-driven misclassification, tumor size was dichotomized as < 20 mm versus > 20 mm, enabling inclusion of all three trials; the impact of this harmonization decision was assessed in sensitivity analyses.

Robustness was further evaluated using leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. An additional sensitivity analysis of the primary outcome was conducted by restricting the meta-analysis to trials that used a consistent definition of procedure time. Studies that employed different definitions or did not report a definition were excluded. Funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were not performed because no outcome included at least 10 studies, making such assessments underpowered and potentially misleading.

Results

Study selection

The search yielded 690 records. After the removal of 282 duplicates, 408 unique citations were screened. After title and abstract review, 17 articles underwent full-text assessment; 5 studies met eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis [35–39]. Of the 12 studies excluded at the full-text stage, two otherwise eligible RCTs were excluded because they reported outcomes exclusively using per-protocol analyses and did not provide intention-to-treat (ITT) or modified intention-to-treat (m-ITT) data. The rationale for full-text exclusion is provided in Supplementary Table 4, and the selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. Reference-list manual screening did not identify any additional eligible studies.

Study characteristics and quality of included studies

The five included trials randomized 463 patients (228 TA-ESD; 235 C-ESD). Across studies, the mean age ranged from 63 to 75 years, and the population was predominantly male (59.4%). Tumors most commonly involved the lower third of the stomach in both arms (43.9% TA-ESD; 46% C-ESD). Operator expertise differed substantially: two studies were performed by a single expert [36, 37], one by trainees only [38], and two involved mixed expertise [35, 39]. The included RCTs employed different traction approaches, including the GTS partner system, the EndoTrac device, the spring-and-loop technique, the clip-flap method, and transnasal endoscope-assisted traction [35–39]. Outcomes

Procedure time

TA-ESD was associated with a shorter procedure time than C-ESD (mean difference (MD) = -12.45 min, 95% CI: -20.67 to -4.23; P = 0.003), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 28%, τ² = 20.21, P = 0.25; GRADE: moderate) (Fig. 3A).

The reduction was observed across tumor-location subgroups, for upper/middle-third lesions (MD = -21.21 min, 95% CI: -30.26 to -12.17; P < 0.0001) and for lower-third lesions (MD = -10.72 min, 95% CI: -17.79 to -3.64; P = 0.003), although evidence for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance (P for interaction = 0.073) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Across tumor-size strata, the direction of effect consistently favored TA-ESD. A reduction was noted for tumors < 20 mm (MD = -15.76 min, 95% CI: -24.47 to -7.04; P = 0.0004), whereas the effect for tumors > 20 mm was not statistically significant (MD = -18.25 min, 95% CI: -43.38 to 6.87; P = 0.155). No evidence of effect modification by tumor size was detected (P for interaction = 0.854) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Subgroup analyses by endoscopist experience were exploratory; while the reduction in procedure time was observed in expert-only studies (MD = -16.58 min, 95% CI: -23.68 to -9.48; P < 0.0001), estimates in trainee-only and mixed-operator strata were based on single trials and should be interpreted cautiously. No statistically significant between-subgroup differences were detected (P for interaction = 0.096) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

En bloc and R0 resection

En bloc resection rates were uniformly high and did not differ between TA-ESD and C-ESD (risk ratio (RR) = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98–1.02; P = 0.98), with low heterogeneity (I² = 0%, τ² = 0, P = 1.00; GRADE: moderate) (Fig. 3B). Similarly, R0 resection was comparable between groups (RR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.93–1.06; P = 0.83), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 29%, τ² < 0.001, P = 0.25; GRADE: low) (Fig. 3C).

Subgroup analyses by endoscopist experience did not suggest differential effects on en bloc resection (mixed-operators: RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98–1.02; P = 0.965 vs. experts-only: RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.04; P = 1.00 vs. trainees-only: RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.93–1.08; P for interaction = 0.999) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Similarly, for R0 resection, effects were consistent across experience levels: experts-only studies showed RR = 0.98 (95% CI: 0.88–1.10; P = 0.741), while the trainees-only study showed RR = 1.00 (95% CI: 0.90–1.12), with no evidence of subgroup interaction (P for interaction = 0.798) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

However, the trainees-only strata for both en bloc and R0 resection rates were based on single studies and should be interpreted cautiously.

Traction-related damage to the specimen

Specimen damage was rare, with a pooled event proportion of 1.3% (95% CI: 0.3%–5.1%) and low heterogeneity (I² = 0%, τ² = 0; P = 0.91) (Fig. 4A).

Subgroup analysis by operator experience showed consistently low specimen-damage rates, with experts-only pooled proportion of 1.3% (95% CI: 0.2%–8.3%) and a similar estimate in mixed-operator study (1.4%, 95% CI: 0.0%–7.4%), with no evidence of effect modification (P for interaction = 0.95) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Adverse events

Perforation was uncommon and did not differ between TA-ESD and C-ESD (RR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.07–3.97; P = 0.53), with low heterogeneity (I² = 0%, τ² = 0, P = 0.41; GRADE: very low) (Fig. 4B). Delayed bleeding likewise showed no statistically significant difference (RR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.29–2.09; P = 0.61), with low heterogeneity (I² = 0%, τ² = 0, P = 0.69; GRADE: very low) (Fig. 4C).

Endoscopist experience did not appear to modify the comparative effect on delayed bleeding (mixed-operators: RR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.16–2.87; P = 0.586; experts-only: RR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.12–2.60; P = 0.467; Trainees only: RR = 4.82, 95% CI: 0.24–95.58), and the interaction test was non-significant (P for interaction = 0.441) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Figs. 12–20) supported overall robustness of the findings, with no single study reversing the direction of effect for any outcome. For procedure time, however, statistical significance was sensitive to the omission of individual trials: excluding Bi 2024 [37] (MD = -9.13 min, 95% CI: -21.88 to 3.62; P = 0.161) or Nagata 2021 [36] (MD = -9.77 min, 95% CI: -20.87 to 1.33; P = 0.084) attenuated the pooled effect to non-significance, although the effect direction remained in favor of TA-ESD. A sensitivity analysis restricted to the two trials using a consistent definition of procedure time showed no significant difference between TA-ESD and C-ESD (MD = -8.03 min, 95% CI: -27.14 to 11.08; P = 0.41; I² = 71%) (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first RCT-based meta-analysis limited to studies employing ITT or m-ITT analyses in gastric ESD. In this meta-analysis of 5 RCTs involving 463 patients (228 TA-ESD and 235 C-ESD), TA-ESD was associated with shorter procedure time than C-ESD. This efficiency gain was achieved without evidence of differences in en bloc or R0 resection rates, or in the risks of perforation or delayed bleeding. The reduction in procedure time was generally observed across lesion location subgroups (U/M vs. L), and lesions < 20 mm in diameter appeared to have shorter procedure times. These findings suggest that TA-ESD may be a more time-efficient approach that preserves resection quality and procedural safety in gastric ESD.

The reduction in procedure time observed with TA-ESD in our analysis should be interpreted in the context of the existing meta-analytic literature summarized in Table 3. Unlike most prior syntheses, our study was restricted to RCTs conducted exclusively in the gastric setting and included ITT or m-ITT analyses across all eligible trials. In contrast, earlier RCT-based meta-analyses [19–21] largely pooled esophageal, gastric, and colorectal lesions and included only a small number of gastric RCTs reporting ITT outcomes, typically two trials at most, thereby limiting their ability to detect a gastric-specific time benefit. This methodological constraint likely explains why several pan-gastrointestinal (pan-GI) RCT meta-analyses reported no significant difference in gastric procedure time, despite consistently favorable overall estimates for traction [19–21]. Notably, the RCT-only network meta-analysis to date [22] ranked traction-assisted approaches among the fastest ESD strategies for gastric lesions and demonstrated shorter procedure time compared with C-ESD; however, this analysis did not isolate the direct pooled effect of TA-ESD versus C-ESD specifically in gastric tumors, precluding lesion-specific inference. Mixed-design meta-analyses [23–25, 27] that included observational data more consistently reported shorter procedure time with traction, including two gastric-only syntheses [23, 27]; however, the inclusion of non-randomized evidence limits causal inference. Against this background, our synthesis of five gastric RCTs suggests that traction may improve procedural efficiency in ESD. However, the small number of trials and sensitivity of leave-one-out analyses warrant cautious interpretation of the effect’s magnitude and certainty. Notably, the Kinoshita 2024 trial [35], the largest included study (n = 148), found no significant benefit of EndoTrac-assisted ESD. This may reflect differences in traction systems, operator experience, and a ceiling effect in high-volume centers. Overall, the reduction in procedure time appears modest and context-dependent.

In our gastric synthesis, TA-ESD did not result in higher en bloc or R0 resection rates compared with C-ESD. This neutral effect is consistent with the pattern observed across previous meta-analyses [20, 21, 23–25], the majority of which reported no significant differences for either endpoint in gastric ESD. Although the RCT-only network meta-analysis by Ramai et al. [22] ranked traction-assisted techniques favorably among multiple ESD strategies for en bloc resection rates, it did not isolate a direct gastric-specific comparison between TA-ESD and C-ESD, limiting inference for these outcomes. A recent meta-analysis by Zhang et al. [27] reported a benefit of TA-ESD for R0 resection; however, its inclusion of both observational studies and RCTs, along with a mix of per-protocol and intention-to-treat analyses, likely introduced bias and compromised the reliability of the pooled results. Within this context, our findings suggest that while traction may improve procedural efficiency, it does not materially alter resection completeness in gastric ESD, where en bloc and R0 rates are already high with conventional techniques.

In our synthesis, TA-ESD was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in perforation risk or delayed bleeding compared with C-ESD. This finding is concordant with most prior meta-analyses [20, 21, 25], particularly those restricted to RCTs, which consistently reported no significant differences for either safety endpoint in the gastric setting. Although three mixed-design meta-analyses [23, 24, 27], including two gastric-specific syntheses [23, 27], reported a lower perforation risk favoring traction, these analyses incorporated a limited number of randomized trials reporting ITT outcomes. They relied heavily on observational data, raising concerns regarding residual confounding related to case selection, operator experience, and traction deployment. Pan-GI RCT-based meta-analyses likewise did not demonstrate a clear gastric-specific perforation or bleeding benefit [20–22], likely reflecting both anatomical heterogeneity and insufficient power for uncommon adverse events when gastric lesions are analyzed as subgroups. Across all previous syntheses [20–25, 27], delayed bleeding rates in gastric ESD have remained comparable between traction-assisted and conventional techniques, a pattern reinforced by our findings and supporting the overall safety neutrality of traction with respect to post-procedural bleeding. Unlike our gastric-specific RCT synthesis, Awad et al. [26] pooled traction across mixed GI sites, including only one gastric cohort, which limits comparability with our results. Therefore, comparisons with pan-GI studies were restricted to analyses reporting explicit gastric data.

The 2023 ESGE technical review [18] recognizes TA-ESD as a promising adjunct in the gastric setting, particularly for lesions located in the greater curvature of the upper or middle stomach or when submucosal access is difficult. Our findings provide gastric-specific RCT evidence suggesting a potential procedural efficiency benefit of traction, without clear evidence of differences in resection quality or safety, thereby addressing an important evidence gap highlighted by current guidelines.

TA-ESD reduced procedure time by a mean of 12.45 min (15–20%) [35–39], potentially offering clinical and operational advantages such as reduced sedation exposure, procedural fatigue, and improved operating room turnover; however, these potential benefits require further validation. This efficiency likely stems from enhanced submucosal visualization and a stable dissection plane, which may facilitate more consistent resections in anatomically challenging locations. Importantly, this time benefit was observed without statistically significant differences in en bloc or R0 resection rates, or in the risk of perforation or delayed bleeding, suggesting that traction may improve efficiency without compromising key outcomes. However, given that the evidence is based on only five gastric-specific RCTs analyzed on an ITT or m-ITT basis, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and suggest a potential role in selected cases rather than routine adoption of traction.

These findings offer preliminary guidance for endoscopists evaluating TA-ESD. Given the limited evidence base, the observed efficiency and safety profile suggest that TA-ESD may assist less experienced operators by improving submucosal visualization. However, its potential to shorten the learning curve or standardize practice requires validation in larger, dedicated trials.

A key strength of this review is that it represents the first meta-analysis of RCTs focusing exclusively on gastric ESD, limited to studies reporting outcomes on an ITT or m-ITT basis. This approach preserves the integrity of randomization and may help minimize bias, unlike PP analyses, which may overestimate treatment effects by excluding participants who deviate from the assigned intervention. An additional strength is the novel assessment of traction-related specimen damage, which expands the evidence base beyond conventional efficacy and safety outcomes to include outcomes directly relevant to pathological evaluation. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, all included trials were conducted in East Asia, primarily in high-volume Japanese and Chinese centers with substantial ESD expertise, limiting generalizability to Western practice. In Western settings, lower procedure volumes, less standardized training, differing case mix, and variable access to traction devices may all influence outcomes. As a result, the observed benefits may not be fully reproducible outside experienced Asian centers. Second, variability in the definition of procedure time across included trials may introduce measurement heterogeneity. While two studies defined procedure time as the time from submucosal injection to completion of dissection or tumor removal [37, 39], only one trial [36] explicitly included traction device setup time, and one study did not provide a clear definition [35]. This inconsistency may have contributed to imprecision in the pooled estimate of procedural efficiency.

Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis restricted to trials with consistent procedure-time definitions did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between groups; however, this analysis was limited to two studies and therefore had limited precision. Third, the modest number of eligible trials and the relatively small total sample size (463 patients) limited statistical power, particularly for uncommon adverse events, leading to wide confidence intervals and greater uncertainty around pooled estimates. This limitation was further amplified by restricting inclusion to ITT or m-ITT analyses, which, although enhancing methodological rigor, resulted in the exclusion of two otherwise eligible RCTs and further reduced the available sample size. Accordingly, the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of the evidence as moderate for the primary outcome, while the secondary outcomes ranged from moderate to very low, reflecting these limitations. The statistical significance of the procedure-time reduction was sensitive to the omission of individual studies in leave-one-out analyses, reflecting the influence of a small number of trials on the pooled estimate. Rare complications and very late recurrences are more reliably assessed in large observational cohorts. Fourth, none of the included trials were judged to be at an overall low risk of bias. Blinding of endoscopists and outcome assessors was not feasible, introducing potential performance bias; however, because most efficacy and safety endpoints were objective, the risk of detection bias is likely limited. Fifth, clinical heterogeneity arises from the varied traction methods used across trials [35–39], which differ in direction, adjustability, setup complexity, and the degree of submucosal exposure, potentially affecting procedural efficiency, learning curve, and safety.

Despite these differences, pooling these approaches under a unified “traction-assisted” category is mechanistically justifiable, as all techniques aim to provide countertraction to enhance submucosal visualization and facilitate dissection. However, this strategy may obscure technique-specific advantages and represents an additional source of uncertainty not fully captured by statistical measures such as I². Therefore, the observed pooled effect cannot be attributed to any single traction method or device. Trials also varied considerably in lesion characteristics and operator experience [35–39]. Notably, the inclusion of the Ahn 2013 trial [38] warrants consideration, as it was conducted exclusively in a trainee setting and employed a transnasal endoscope traction technique that is not widely adopted in current practice. These factors may limit its contemporary relevance and may have influenced the pooled estimates, particularly for secondary outcomes such as en bloc resection, R0 resection, and delayed bleeding, where no statistically significant differences were observed. Less experienced operators may derive greater benefit from traction assistance, which could further contribute to clinical heterogeneity across studies. While we examined these differences qualitatively and, where feasible, through subgroup analyses, the limited sample size prevented direct comparisons of individual traction techniques. Although some evidence suggests that traction systems may differ in effectiveness and safety [25, 26], definitive conclusions will require large, head-to-head RCTs. Future studies should adopt standardized traction classifications and report key method characteristics to enable more precise comparisons. Finally, the limited number of eligible trials prevented a reliable assessment of small-study effects using funnel plots or regression-based methods.

Future research should determine which traction techniques maximize procedural efficiency and safety across lesion location, size, and operator experience. High-quality multicenter RCTs are needed that directly compare common traction methods, using standardized outcomes, considering operator expertise, cost-effectiveness, and patient-centered endpoints. Individual-participant data (IPD) meta-analyses combining RCTs and controlled cohorts could identify lesion subtypes that benefit most and guide the integration of traction techniques into structured operator training.

Conclusion

This gastric-specific, RCT-only, ITT-based meta-analysis suggests that TA-ESD may be associated with shorter procedure time than C-ESD. However, this finding was sensitive to leave-one-out analyses, as the statistical significance of the procedure-time outcome was lost upon excluding either Bi 2024 or Nagata 2021, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. No statistically significant differences were observed in en bloc resection, R0 resection, or adverse events. Given the limited number of included trials, the fact that three of five studies were at high risk of bias, and the low to very low certainty of evidence for most secondary outcomes, further high-quality multicenter RCTs and IPD meta-analyses are needed to clarify the true effect of TA-ESD and to identify patient and lesion subgroups most likely to benefit.
