A critical systematic review of the impact of sedentarism and physical inactivity on the cognitive performance of children
Authors: Nathália Petraconi, Jéssica Salles Henrique, Fabrízio dos Santos Cardoso, Flávia Maria Ribeiro Vital, Ricardo Mario Arida, Camila Bertini Martins, Sérgio Gomes da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSedentarism and physical inactivity are growing concerns for global health institutions, especially with the rising number of adolescents failing to meet daily physical activity guidelines. These inactive lifestyles can adversely affect cognitive functions during development. Thus, a systematic review of observational studies was conducted to evaluate the impact of sedentary behavior and physical inactivity on cognitive performance in children aged 6 to 12.MethodsEligible studies included English-language observational designs (such as cohort and case-control studies), enrolling neurotypical children classified as sedentary, insufficiently active, or physically inactive (experimental group), compared to active children (control group), according to ACSM recommendations. Studies were also required to report outcomes related to executive functions, academic performance metrics, memory, and attention. Systematic searches were conducted in MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus, from inception to February 2026. Risk of bias was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies—of Exposure (ROBINS-E) tool. Data were synthesized through a narrative method.ResultsOf the 5438 articles initially identified, two studies directly compared inactive and active groups with contrasting results. One showed negative associations between light, moderate physical activity, and total activity time with verbal and perceptual skills in the active group, alongside positive effects of vigorous PA and sedentary behavior on verbal skills. The other pointed positive dose-response effects of sustained moderate-to-vigorous PA trajectories on academic achievement (reading, mathematics, language). However, caution is advised when interpreting these results due to the high risk of bias in both studies, lack of control for confounding variables, and insufficient information on missing data and blinding procedures. While other studies show significant associations between physical activity and cognitive measures, it is crucial to question whether group compositions accurately reflect the cognitive effects of exercise. Given the limited number of articles meeting international physical activity organization criteria, caution is recommended when interpreting the cognitive effects of physical activity-related exposures in children.ConclusionsThis review introduces a novel data analysis approach that classified groups based on established physical activity recommendations, aiming to standardize group criteria. This method potentially sparks essential debates on group definitions and contributes to enhancing the methodological quality of future observational or experimental studies, enabling more robust and reliable data interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-026-03172-3.

Introduction

Physical inactivity has been a concern of health organizations. Besides being the fourth leading cause of the risk of death [52], physical inactivity has been seen as a prevalent behavior among adolescents aged between 11 and 17 years old [10]. Around 81% of adolescents do not engage in the 60 daily minutes of moderate or vigorous physical activity (PA) as strongly recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) for children and adolescents [10]. This recommendation aligns with the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines, which also advocate for at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) daily to benefit youth health [6].

Additionally to physical inactivity, sedentarism (defined as low energy expenditure behaviors—such as sitting or lying positions—during awake periods) is also associated with non-healthy behaviors [6, 10, 34]. Both conditions are related to the development of chronic non-communicable diseases, including heart illness, cancer, chronic respiratory disease, and diabetes [5, 89]. Moreover, studies have shown a negative association of sedentary behavior with cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and nutritional state in children [12, 38] and old adults [77].

Childhood is a critical period for brain structural and morphological changes [18, 27, 36]. Brain volume peaks in early infancy and then grows linearly until adolescence [37, 46], driven by neuronal myelination, pruning, and cell death as part of brain maturation [37, 59, 88].

Phylogenetically newer brain regions, such as those supporting higher-order cognitive functions, mature later than primitive areas like the sensorimotor cortex [18, 36, 46, 81]. The prefrontal cortex (PFC), linked to executive function responsible for regulating goal-directed behaviors [13], matures slowly from infancy through young adulthood [53]. Consequently, late-maturing areas like the PFC are more susceptible to environmental and behavioral influences.

Executive function mainly comprises three primary abilities: inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [4, 19]. As brain regions, these abilities have a distinct temporal course of development [16, 29]. Factorial analysis studies show variability in these factors during childhood. In preschoolers, one or two factors (mostly for working memory and inhibitory control constructs) predominate [61, 80, 87]. From age six, two and three factors’ models become evident, supporting the primary executive function abilities [80]. Thus, environmental stimuli during childhood critically influence executive function development [33, 58].

For example, impairment in executive function is associated with impulsivity [14], problems constructing oral and written sentences [41], and difficulty in adapting to new task rules [4, 19]. On the other hand, the good development of executive function may predict good performance in math and literacy [62] and favor the learning process in schools [74].

Physical inactivity and sedentarism may negatively impact cognitive development in children [78, 92], making it pertinent to study children aged 6 to 12. Factorial analysis supports that primary executive function is present around 6 years [80], while gray matter volume in the PFC peaks by age 12 [51]. Brain development slows after 13, likely due to neural pruning [79], and by 16, synaptic density approaches adults’ levels [47]. Thus, the 6–12 age range offers a window to study cognitive function with less influence from pruning and apoptosis tied to maturation.

Longitudinal designs provide a more accurate understanding of PA’s effects on cognitive function [54, 69] by better controlling confounding factors compared to other methodologies (e.g., cross-sectional studies). Variables such as sleep, socioeconomic status, parents’ educational level, puberty, and cognitively demanding sedentary tasks may influence cognitive outcomes like executive function and academic skills. For example, sleep impairments are negatively correlated with performance in inhibitory control and working memory tests in children [11]. Low-income adolescents perform worse in math and working memory tests compared to higher-income peers [32]. Parental education predicts children’s academic and professional success in adulthood [22, 63]. Some sedentary activities, such as educational activities (e.g., homework, reading, writing), are positively associated with cognitive flexibility [15]. Reading, compared to screen time, correlates with greater functional connectivity in language and executive function brain regions [45]. Accounting for these variables is essential, as they contribute to variability in cognitive development.

Previous systematic reviews have investigated the effects of PA [21, 31] and sedentarism [8, 56] on cognitive function and academic abilities, sometimes incorporating neuroimaging evidences such as brain connectivity or volume changes [82, 86]. Most reviews include diverse study designs, but often discuss them separately rather than integratively. A common limitation identified is the lack of consistent criteria defining different PA levels. Typically, inclusion criteria reference general PA or sedentary behavior terms without considering physical inactivity as a distinct condition. Notably, no review has applied the ACSM criteria to define the PA levels (active vs. inactive) and sedentary behavior when screening literature. Thus, a direct comparison between sedentary (energy expenditure < 1.5 METs for prolonged periods) and active (energy expenditure higher than 3.0 METs for at least 60 min daily) children, or inactive (not engaging in moderate or vigorous PA) and active children remains an underexplored area in systematic reviews.

Therefore, this review investigated how sedentary behavior and physical inactivity affect executive function and academic abilities in children aged 6 to 12 years by comparing children who are sedentary or inactive with those who are physically active (defined as engaging in at least 60 min of moderate or vigorous physical activity daily) based on observational studies.

Methods

The systematic review was elaborated according to the Handbook of Cochrane [44]. Essential information for the systematic review has been verified by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA; [68]). The protocol was based on the registration number (ID CRD42021274427) from the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The PRISMA checklists can be found in the Supplementary Material (Appendix B).

Eligibility criteria

Articles have been selected according to the following criteria.

Study type

Observational studies, such as cohort and case-control, were included.

Participants

Children aged 6 to 12, apparently healthy, without diagnosed diseases, deficiencies, or learning disabilities. Articles that partially included the specified age range were selected if it was possible to separate the results.

Exposition

The experimental group included individuals classified as sedentary, insufficiently active, or physically inactive following the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommendations [6]. Inactive individuals do not engage in moderate or vigorous physical activity, while insufficiently active individuals participate in moderate or vigorous activity but do not reach 60 min per day.

Physical activity intensity is defined absolutely by METs (a resting oxygen consumption rate metric, equivalent to 3.5 ml of oxygen per kilogram per minute), where light intensity corresponds to <3.0, moderate to 3.0–6.0, and vigorous to >6.0 METs per minute [6, 42, 50], and relatively by cardiorespiratory capacity percentages (moderate: 40–59%; vigorous: 60–84%) or perceived exertion scales (moderate: 5–6; vigorous: 7–8; e.g., modified Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion scale; [6]). Sedentary behavior involves activities with energy expenditure less than 1.5 METs (e.g., prolonged sitting, reclining, or lying down), measurable by self-report, time spent watching television or screen time (1–2 h; [3]), or devices such as accelerometers, where a cut-point of 100 counts·min−1 represents the time children spend seated [75].

The control group comprised individuals engaging in at least 60 min of daily MVPA. Accepted exposition measures included devices (e.g., accelerometers), questionnaires, and self-reports by parents or children to assess sedentary, inactive, or active behavior.

Comparator

For comparison between groups, articles were included if they had a group classified as active compared to another group considered sedentary or inactive as defined by ACSM.

Outcome measures

Articles should evaluate at least one variable related to cognitive performance, such as (1) executive function (scores or measures of computational tests), working memory (visuospatial or verbal), inhibitory control, or cognitive flexibility tests; (2) academic performance, reading, mathematics, spelling, and literacy scores; (3) memory; and (4) attention.

Articles were excluded from analysis if: (1) they verified the acute effect of physical activity; (2) they did not have a control group classified as sedentary or inactive; and (3) gray literature and non-observational studies.

Database and search strategy

The databases used were MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science, and Scopus. The literature search used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Emtrees. The descriptors were determined following the PECO strategy (participants, exposition, comparator, and outcome). The search was limited to articles published in English, with no temporal restrictions. Filters and descriptors for observational studies were applied to select the study type. A pilot search was conducted following these steps: first, the PECO elements in MeSH descriptors were identified; then, natural language words (NLWs) were looked up to enhance the search. All MeSH elements and NLWs were organized in a table according to their respective PECO strategy letter. Next, the keywords were combined using Boolean operators, parentheses, and truncation. The search strategy was then tested by reviewing the number of articles retrieved and examining the titles and abstracts to assess their relevance to the research question. Finally, filters related to study design were applied. The final search strategy is described in the Supplementary Material (Appendix A).

Data collection and analysis

Selection process

The search and selection of studies based on title and abstract using PECO descriptors, along with the exclusion of ineligible and duplicated studies, were independently performed by two authors (NP and JH). Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third author (FV) involved when necessary. Potential articles were saved in the clipboard section, downloaded in.ris format, and imported into Zotero software. In Zotero, duplicate articles were identified and removed, and the remaining articles were downloaded for full reading. At this stage, each author (NP and JH) independently screened the articles against the eligibility criteria. The authors also searched for potentially relevant articles in the reference lists of the previously selected studies. Following consensus between the authors, studies meeting the inclusion requirements were selected for full review.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted from eligible articles and recorded in an Excel sheet: (1) general information (main author’s name, publication year, country); (2) study details (study design, sample characteristics, data analysis); (3) intervention information (the instrument used to evaluate physical activity, sedentary behavior, and physical fitness); (4) cognition measures (the instrument used to measure cognitive function); and (5) outcome measures [quantitative measures of the variables of interest (cognition), such as mean, standard deviation, standard error, confidence interval, sample size, the proportion of categorical variables, and description of inferential statistics, including regression coefficients (linear and logistic), models, and covariates used]. The authors were contacted to request missing data. The risk of bias analysis images were generated using the Robvis tool, an online application developed by Cochrane [60].

Risk of bias analysis

Observational studies were analyzed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies—of Exposure (ROBINS-E) [43]. ROBINS-E consists of a planning step, a preliminary analysis, and the risk of bias assessment. The planning step evaluates the confusing variables that can impact the outcomes and should be considered in record analysis. Then, five sections of preliminary analysis are examined to verify the PECO’s attributes that guide the authors in continuing or not the bias assessment. After these sections, seven domains of risk of bias analysis are evaluated. These domains verified the exposition characteristics, participants’ recruitment, follow-up procedures, missing data, outcomes measurement, and results selection. The domains’ questions can be answered with “yes,” “probably yes,” “probably no,” “no,” or “without information.” Each domain can be classified as low risk, some concerns, high, or very high risk of bias. The same classification is used for the overall risk. A more detailed description of ROBINS-E procedures can be found in the Cochrane Handbook [43].

Two researchers (NP and JH) independently assessed the risk of bias for the included study using ROBINS-E, which provides a structured framework for evaluating potential biases in observational studies. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Data synthesis

Data were analyzed through narrative synthesis. Characteristics of the groups (active versus sedentary/inactive) were described, and exposure characteristics along with their effects were examined. Summary statistics were employed to identify and analyze reported data. In case of missing data, records would be excluded and the number of articles with missing information reported. Because there were insufficient eligible studies, a meta-analysis was not performed.

Results

Results of the search

From the last database search (February 2026), 5438 records were identified. After the title and abstract screening (n = 38) and duplicate removal (n = 20), 18 records were selected for eligibility criteria analysis. Only two studies were included [71, 84] and 16 records were excluded following these reasons: not having group analysis (n = 6, [1, 2, 39, 57, 70, 93]), participants’ age (n = 5, [7, 28, 67, 76, 91]), study design (n = 2, [17, 85]), the same level of physical activity between groups (n = 2, [40, 72]), and groups that did not achieve the ACSM’s recommendations [49]. Participants’ characteristics and instruments

A total of 147 Estonian children participated in the first selected study [71], having 51% of males. The assessments were made at two time points, one in kindergarten (mean age: 6.6) and the other 1 year later, in the first year of school (mean age: 7.6). The PA level was measured by accelerometers in both assessments (obtained measures: sedentary behavior, light, moderate, vigorous PA, MVPA, awake wearing time, and total PA). The analysis of the groups was based on the kindergarten evaluation (low versus upper quartiles of PA).

Body composition was measured using body mass index, nutritional state, and skin folds of triceps and subscapularis anatomical points. Physical fitness was evaluated by the PREFIT fitness test battery, and academic performance was assessed by the Modified Boehm Test of Basic Concepts—Third Edition, which consists of three main domains: conceptual, verbal, and perceptual abilities [71].

In the other longitudinal study, 202 Chinese primary school children participated [84], comprising 55% males with a mean baseline age of 9.4 years. Assessments were conducted at three annual time points (T0: 2018; T1: 2019; T2: 2020). PA levels were measured using ActiGraph GT3X accelerometers (≥10 h/day valid wear time, excluding ≥20-min zero strings during waking hours). Valid data required ≥1 weekend day and 2 weekdays. MVPA cutoff was ≥2296 CPM. Data were analyzed via compositional data analysis (CoDA). Groups were defined by 3-year MVPA trajectories identified through group-based trajectory models (low MVPA: n = 154; decline MVPA: n = 25; high MVPA: n = 23). Academic performance was evaluated through standardized school-administered tests in reading, mathematics, and language (0–100 scores).

Main findings

The acceptance of the first article on eligibility criteria was based on the moderate intensity of PA [71]. The upper quartile group (the actives) achieved 60 daily minutes of moderate PA, while the lower quartile group (the inactives) reached 33 and 41 daily minutes (kindergarten and school, respectively).

A positive association between sedentary behavior and verbal abilities was found in two proposed models (a—adjusted by maternal education; b—adjusted by sports club participation), in which an increased time in sedentary behaviors in kindergarten would get a higher score in verbal abilities in school first year [71].

An inverse effect was found in the upper quartile group for light, moderate, and total time of PA. The analysis showed that an increased time in these respective variables of PA would generate a lower score 1 year later for verbal abilities and similar for perceptual skills (same models as before). On the other hand, for vigorous PA, it was observed that a higher time spent in this intensity, a higher score in verbal ability would be obtained in school (same models) [71].

A significant association was found between vigorous intensity and perceptual ability in the lower quartile group. However, it is essential to note that there is no control for sedentary activities with great cognitive effort, such as homework and reading, that interfere with data interpretation.

The acceptance of the second article on eligibility criteria was based on its longitudinal analysis of MVPA trajectories over 3 years, during which MVPA levels clearly differed between groups, in which low MVPA maintained ~45 min/day, decline MVPA dropped from 75.7 to 40.5 min/day, and high MVPA sustained ~62 min/day over the 3 years [84].

At baseline, the high MVPA group achieved superior academic performance compared to the low MVPA group (reading 89.8 vs. 78.4; mathematics 94.5 vs. 80.8; language 94.7 vs. 83.1), and this difference was maintained throughout the 3 years observed [84].

After adjustment for confounders (age, sex, BMI, sleep duration, parental education, attitudes towards sports), the analysis showed a dose-response pattern where higher sustained MVPA was associated with progressively larger academic gains over the years. Compared to low MVPA, decline MVPA showed improved reading by 4.11 points and mathematics scores by 2.79 points. The high MVPA group demonstrated the largest gains across all domains, reading by 11.30 points, mathematics by 12.32 points, and language by 11.53 points [84].

However, it is essential to note that there is a substantial imbalance in the quantity of children across the low, decline, and high MVPA groups. The low MVPA group comprised 74.8% of participants (n = 154), while decline MVPA represented only 13.2% (n = 25) and high MVPA just 12.0% (n = 23). This uneven distribution may limit data interpretation regarding trajectory comparisons [84].

Risk of bias

The risk of bias analysis was made using ROBINS-E, which assesses the bias related to the exposition and outcomes (Fig. 2). For Reisberg et al. [71, 72], a higher risk of bias was classified in domain 1 (variant A based on baseline confounders) due to the lack of control of the listed confounders in this review. Variant B of domain 2 was classified as low risk of bias due to the small number of groups (only two), decreasing the chance of a classification error. Domains 3 and 4 had a low risk of bias, considering that exposition time was similar between groups, the selection process was not based on participant characteristics that could interfere with the outcome, and there were no co-interventions. Domain 5 had a high risk of bias due to the lack of information on missing data and descriptive measures of mothers’ educational levels. Some concern was the classification of domain 6 due to the absence of blinding information of participants and assessors. For Wang et al. [84], domain 1 (variant A) was classified as high risk due to lack of control of baseline confounders. Domain 2 (variant B) was low risk, given objective MVPA measurement via accelerometers at multiple timepoints. Domains 3 and 4 had low risk, as follow-up was comparable and no selection or co-intervention biases were identified. Domain 5 had high risk due to incomplete missing data reporting. Discussion

This systematic review sought to investigate the effects of sedentary, inactive, and active behaviors on cognitive abilities in children aged 6 to 12. The purpose was to examine whether an active lifestyle (engaging in 60 daily minutes of moderate or vigorous PA) could improve cognition (executive function and academic performance) when compared with non-active behaviors (sedentary or inactive) in observational studies. Two articles were selected concerning this review eligibility criteria [71, 84].

Reisberg et al. [71, 72] identified a negative association between light, moderate, and total PA and verbal and perceptual abilities in the active group. Additionally, an increase in sedentary or vigorous PA time within this group was positively associated with verbal abilities, contradicting the hypothesis of a positive association between PA and cognition. However, important confounders, such as cognitively demanding sedentary behavior [24], may have influenced these findings. Moreover, the absence of a descriptive distribution of maternal educational level, a covariate in the model, limits interpretation of its contribution to the observed negative effects.

In contrast, Wang et al. [84] found a positive dose-response relationship between sustained MVPA trajectories over 3 years and academic achievement (reading, mathematics, language) in Chinese primary school children (n = 202), with the high MVPA group (n = 23) showing significantly better scores than low (n = 154) or declining (n = 25) groups after confounder adjustment. This supports the hypothesis of cognitive benefits from active lifestyles meeting ACSM guidelines. However, the marked imbalance in group proportions limits statistical power and generalizability of these findings.

Given these complexities, exercise intensity emerges as a crucial variable to consider when investigating PA’s effects on cognition. Studies have shown that brain’s response can be modulated by exercise intensity, with light intensity possibly insufficient to stimulate molecular markers related to cognitive improvement compared to higher intensities [30, 73]. This is particularly relevant because only vigorous PA has demonstrated a positive association with academic abilities [71], while Wang et al. [84] found benefits specifically from sustained MVPA trajectories meeting ACSM guidelines. In addition, this review found that no studies have directly compared sedentary and active groups, highlighting a significant gap in the current literature. Understanding how varying PA intensities affect cognitive outcomes and the different cognitive impacts between sedentary and active groups is therefore an important direction for future research.

Unfortunately, a high prevalence of inactive adolescents (those who engage in some PA activity of moderate or vigorous intensity but do not achieve 60 daily minutes) is observed worldwide [10]. This pattern is also evident in primary school children, where Wang et al. [84] found that 74.8% (n = 154/202) followed low MVPA trajectories over 3 years, failing to meet ACSM recommendations. Considering that physical inactivity is associated with the development of chronic non-communicable diseases [55] and with an expenditure of approximately 53.8 billion dollars on the global health system in 2013 [20], this represents a major public health concern. Interestingly, these analyses did not consider the impact of physical inactivity on cognition.

Moreover, during pandemic-related restrictions, parents reported increased sedentary behavior time in children [23], and online surveys observed decreased physical activity metrics [9]. Longitudinal data indicate that sedentary behavior increased and MVPA decreased between 2019 and 2021 in children [35]. Additionally, studies showed that higher-income countries experienced a greater impact on physical activity levels and sedentary time compared to low-income countries [65]. Given prior concerns related to physical inactivity, the COVID-19 pandemic has likely exacerbated sedentary behavior and reduced physical activity among children. Therefore, targeted research and effective public policies are urgently needed to reverse these trends and promote healthier, more active populations.

In prior research involving the elderly population, the presence of indicators of physical inactivity has been linked to the onset of dementia [64, 66]. Molecular studies have also shown that low skeletal muscle activity is associated with disruptions in specific molecular systems that adversely affect learning and memory [48, 83]. Given that physical inactivity is the fourth leading cause of death, it is essential to comprehend its impact on brain function.

Studies with children investigating the effects of physical inactivity on cognitive function are limited despite evidence of its impact on the elderly. This may suggest that the concepts of PA levels do not fit the measures that define active and inactive groups. Notably, while Reisberg et al. [71, 72] found negative associations between PA and cognition, Wang et al. [84] identified distinct MVPA trajectories (low, declining, high) that clearly differentiated cognitive outcomes, showing that appropriate trajectory modeling can overcome these measurement challenges. The failure to determine groups (e.g., having groups with the same level of PA) interferes with interpreting results from the non-included studies that set out to examine the exposition of PA on cognitive measures.

No studies were found on sedentary behavior analysis. This is a contradictory result compared to prior studies investigating sedentarism’s effects on cognitive function. In preschoolers and adolescents, increased time spent in sedentary behaviors was associated2021b

respectively [8, 56]. In adults, sedentary behavior was related to cognitive decline [26] and an increase in the risk of dementia [90]. Although Wang et al. [84] indirectly support this by showing better academic outcomes in high MVPA trajectories (vs. low MVPA), the lack of direct sedentary behavior analysis in both included studies represents a critical gap.

However, these reviews do not consider a comparison with an active group, fail to consider the properties of physical inactivity in their analysis, and do not acknowledge the presence of physical activity [8, 26, 56, 90]. It is important to note that sedentarism is not the absence of PA but the prevalence of sedentary behaviors [34]. Another review investigates a similar question [21], but the authors do not propose a cutoff for physical activity levels as proposed by this review. Then, following methodological rigor to define groups (based on ACSM recommendations) could guide the studies to the real effect of sedentary behavior on cognitive function.

Some questions have been raised from this review analysis: (a) Does the time engaged in physical activity influence cognitive improvement (in different magnitudes: subtle–copious), which can be identified by statistical techniques? (b) Are the instruments used to evaluate academic performance appropriate to the children’s level of difficulty? (c) How much do the learning effects of cognitive tasks influence the outcomes? (d) Is the unique value of determining active and inactive levels (minimum of 60 daily minutes of moderate or vigorous intensity) an ideal method to identify the effects of physical activity and sedentary behavior? (e) Why does sedentary behavior not have a cutoff when classifying a subject as sedentary? (f) Why is the level of physical activity not classified based on the relation of active and not-active (sedentary) periods as correlated behaviors? (g) Would the effects of physical inactivity on children’s cognitive function be smaller, given that there are no indicators of inactivity for children under 11? The reasons for not having global data on physical inactivity for children aged 6–10 are also questionable.

Furthermore, caution is recommended when interpreting articles focused on this topic, including the risk of bias analysis, which can influence the outcomes of interest. Both selected articles presented a high risk of bias ([71, 72]: very high overall; [84]: high overall), which interferes with their analysis. The main points to pay attention to for bias analysis in observational studies are the control of confounding variables, information on how authors dealt with missing data, and details about the blinding process of participants and assessors.

The strength of this review lies in the use of specific concepts (sedentary behavior, physical inactivity, and physical activity) to determine the cutoff for the exposure of interest based on definitions established by international institutions on physical activity. Additionally, using comparators (active and inactive) to analyze the outcomes (cognitive functions or academic skills) enhances the review’s robustness. However, some limitations were observed: (1) the exclusion of articles not in English; (2) the need for searching in gray literature; (3) the exclusion of other potential study designs; and (4) the use of filters in selecting study types.

Therefore, based on the results of this systematic review, it is suggested for future studies: (a) consider confounding variables that may contaminate the results (e.g., pubertal status, sleep, parental educational level, and sedentary tasks with high cognitive demand); (b) improve the design of observational studies to improve the methodological quality and interpretation of results; (c) encourage analyses between groups to understand better the effects of sedentary behavior and physical activity on children; and (d) avoid generalizing findings from imbalanced groups. In this way, it will be possible to identify with greater precision the impact of sedentary behavior and physical inactivity compared to physical activity in developing individuals on cognitive functions.

Conclusion

Conclusions for future research

To better identify indicators of the effects of physical inactivity and sedentary behavior on cognitive functions, analyzing the parameters that characterize groups in children is recommended. It was observed that the observational articles published on this topic needed to establish clear criteria for inclusion and exclusion to differentiate groups based on the level of physical activity, which hinders reliable interpretations of the study’s objective. Therefore, it is suggested that future observational studies more precisely define inclusion criteria based on definitions of sedentarism, physical inactivity, and physical activity recognized by major expert societies and adopt stricter control of potential biases when designing the study. A better design for controlling confounding variables (such as sleep, socioeconomic status, parents’ educational level, puberty, and sedentary tasks with high cognitive demand) is recommended. It is also hoped that future observational studies will provide more transparent descriptions of the process of missing data and knowledge of exposure for researchers and participants. Finally, caution is needed to avoid generalizing findings from observational data with imbalanced groups.

Conclusions for practice

Given the inclusion of only two studies, the results found must be assessed with caution. No observational studies have directly investigated the differences between sedentary and active groups based on ACSM recommendations. This highlights a notable gap in the literature, which may be partially due to the focus on specific study designs applied during screening. Among studies comparing active and inactive groups, two were identified presenting contrasting results: (a) negative effects of light, moderate intensity, and total time of physical activity on verbal and perceptual skills in the active group; (b) a positive effect of sedentary behavior and vigorous physical activity on verbal skills in the active group; and (c) positive dose-response effects of sustained moderate-to-vigorous PA trajectories (high vs. low/declining groups) on academic achievement (reading, mathematics, language) in primary school children. Despite these results, caution is advised when interpreting the findings due to the high risk of bias of both studies. Finally, it is emphasized that this review presents a new approach to data analysis, which may stimulate essential debate on group criteria and enhance the methodological quality of future observational or experimental studies, allowing for more robust and reliable data interpretation.
