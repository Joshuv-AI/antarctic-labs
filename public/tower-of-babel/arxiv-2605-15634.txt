Sharp threshold for a one-dimensional thin film equation in the supercritical case
Shen Bian

Abstract
We study a one-dimensional thin film equation combining competitive effects of aggregation and repulsion, where repulsion is modeled by fourth-order diffusion and aggregation by backward second-order degenerate diffusion with exponent m > 0 m>0 . Under natural regularity constraints, we prove that for every m > 0 m>0 , there exists a unique (up to the mass-critical case m = 3 m=3 ) nonnegative, radially decreasing steady state U ∗ U_{*} which coincides with the extremal function of the sharp Sz.-Nagy inequality and is simultaneously the global minimizer of the free energy. Using this variational characterization in the supercritical regime 3 < m < ∞ 3<m<\infty , we show that finite-time blow-up occurs for all initial data whose initial free energy lies below the positive threshold F ⁡ ( U ∗ ) F(U_{*}) , provided the L m + 1 L^{m+1} -norm of the initial datum exceeds that of U ∗ U_{*} . Conversely, if the L m + 1 L^{m+1} -norm is below that of U ∗ U_{*} , the solution exists globally and its second moment diverges as t → ∞ t\to\infty . This sharp criterion significantly extends the previously known blow-up condition requiring negative free energy to a much wider class of initial data (see [ 2 ] ). Our results identify the steady state U ∗ U_{*} as the critical pivot linking variational structure to dynamical behavior, and provide a constructive method to determine blow-up versus global existence via an explicit L m + 1 L^{m+1} -norm comparison.

Introduction

The fourth-order thin film equation is a central model in lubrication theory, describing the evolution of a thin viscous film on a solid substrate. It takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(u^{n}u_{xxx}\right)_{x}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(u^{m}u_{x}\right)_{x},

(1)

where
u(x,t)
denotes the local film thickness. The exponent
n>0
is the mobility exponent, which encodes the boundary condition at the liquid-solid interface. Typical values are
n=3
for the no-slip condition and
n=1
for the perfect-slip (no-shear) condition characteristic of Hele-Shaw flows

[
1
,
11
,
12
,
17
]
. The exponent
m
arises from the disjoining pressure or a thickness-dependent body force. The case
0<m<3
corresponds to non-classical Van der Waals forces or power-law molecular interactions
[
33
,
34
,
36
]
. The value
m=3
recovers the classical Van der Waals force, which also appears together with
n=3
in the description of fluid droplets hanging from a ceiling
[
13
]
.
m>3
describes faster-decaying forces that localize the destabilizing effect to extremely thin films.

In this work, we focus on equation (
1
) with
n=1,m>0
, and consider nonnegative solutions of the following Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}=-(uu_{xxx})_{x}-(u(u^{m})_{x})_{x},&x\in{\mathbb{R}},~t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in{\mathbb{R}}.\end{array}\right.

Throughout this paper, initial data will be assumed to be

\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),\qquad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u_{0}(x)dx<\infty.

(4)

A fundamental property of the non-negative solutions to (
1
) is the formal conservation of the total mass:

\displaystyle M:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u_{0}(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(x,t)dx,\quad t\geq 0.

(5)

Equation (
1
) is governed by a fundamental competition between two nonlinear effects: the fourth-order term enforces stabilization and repulsion, whereas the backward second-order diffusion term drives destabilizing aggregation. This is well represented by the free energy

\displaystyle F(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(u_{x}\right)^{2}dx-\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx

(6)

satisfying the dissipation principle

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}F(u(\cdot,t))=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\left((u_{xx}+u^{m})_{x}\right)^{2}dx\leq 0,

(7)

which can be derived by multiplying (
1
) by
\mu=-u_{xx}-u^{m}
. The main property of this energy is that it consists of two terms: the gradient energy
\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(u_{x}\right)^{2}dx
and the potential energy
-\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx
.

Let us mention that (
1
) possesses a scaling invariance. If
u(x,t)
is a solution to (
1
), then the scaled function
u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{m-1}}u\left(\lambda x,\lambda^{\frac{4m-2}{m-1}}t\right)
is also a solution to (
1
). For the critical case
m=3
, the scaling reduces to the mass-invariant scaling
u_{\lambda}=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^{5}t)
, under which the second-order aggregative term
\lambda^{m+3}(u_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda}^{m})_{x})_{x}
and the fourth-order repulsive term
\lambda^{6}(u_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})_{xxx})_{x}
balance. For the subcritical case
0<m<3
, aggregation dominates repulsion for small
\lambda
, which prevents spreading, while for large
\lambda
, repulsion dominates aggregation, thus precluding blow-up. On the contrary, for the supercritical case
m>3
, aggregation dominates repulsion for large
\lambda
, and finite-time blow-up may occur. For small
\lambda
, repulsion dominates aggregation, allowing infinite-time spreading. This type of competition between aggregation and repulsion is a common feature in many nonlinear models, including Hele-Shaw flow, stellar collapse, chemotaxis models, and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (see
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
16
,
19
,
25
,
27
,
31
,
32
]
). In these models, the precise balance of the competing mechanisms critically governs the dynamics of solutions, ultimately determining the dichotomy between global existence and finite-time blow-up.

For the subcritical case
0<m<3
, this is the repulsion-dominated regime. Global existence of solutions to (
1
) for any initial data was proved in
[
3
]
. For the critical case
m=3
, this is the fair competition regime where repulsion and aggregation balance.
[
35
]
identified a critical mass
M_{c}
and studied infinite-time self-similar spreading below the critical mass and finite-time blow-up self-similar solutions above the critical mass. Further analysis of blowing-up symmetric self-similar solutions with zero contact angle has been done in
[
30
]
. Both expanding and blowing-up self-similar solutions were also explored in
[
14
,
15
,
29
]
. Global existence of non-negative weak solutions was proved in
[
24
]
with initial mass less than
M_{c}
.

For the supercritical case
m>3
, this is the aggregation-dominated regime. It is known from
[
2
]
that finite-time blow-up occurs for initial data with negative free energy. However, to the best of our knowledge, little is known about the global existence of solutions. Our main goal in this paper is to establish a sharp threshold that distinguishes global existence from finite-time blow-up for solutions to (
1
). To this end, we first present two theorems concerning stationary solutions, which are essential for the analysis. Based on these results, we will then show that finite-time blow-up actually occurs for all initial data whose initial free energy is below
F(U_{*})
with
F(U_{*})>0
. This significantly extends the previously known blow-up criterion originally requiring negative free energy to a much wider class of initial data (see Theorem
1.5
below). The main tool for the analysis of (
1
) is a special case of the Sz.-Nagy inequality
[
26
]
.

Lemma 1.1

(Sz.-Nagy inequality
[
26
]
)
.

Let the nonnegative function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and
f_{x}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then
f\in L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}})
for all
m>0
, and we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|f\|_{m+1}^{\frac{3(m+1)}{m}}\leq C_{*}\|f\|_{1}^{\frac{m+3}{m}}\|f_{x}\|_{2}^{2},

(8)

where the sharp constant is given by

\displaystyle C_{*}=\left(\frac{3}{4}Q\left(\frac{3}{2m},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)^{2},\quad Q(x,y)=\frac{(x+y)^{-(x+y)\Gamma(x+y)}}{x^{-x}y^{-y}\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}.

(9)

In particular, for
m=3
, the sharp constant
C_{*}=\frac{9}{4\pi^{2}}
.

Before presenting our main results, we first fix the notation to be used throughout the paper. For a given mass, we introduce the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{M}:=\{u\in L_{+}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}):\|u\|_{1}=M\}.

(10)

In the mass-critical case
m=3
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{M_{c}}:=\{u\in L_{+}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}):\|u\|_{1}=M_{c}\},

(11)

where

\displaystyle M_{c}:=\left(\frac{2}{C_{*}}\right)^{1/2},\quad C_{*}=\frac{9}{4\pi^{2}}.

(12)

For
m\neq 3
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}:=\{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}:\|u\|_{m+1}=P_{\ast}\},

(13)

where

\displaystyle P_{\ast}:=\left(\frac{3(m+1)}{2mC_{\ast}M^{\frac{m+3}{m}}}\right)^{\frac{m}{(m+1)(m-3)}}.

(14)

To investigate the dynamical behavior of solutions to (
1
), we require several properties of steady states. Steady states of the equation under consideration have already been studied in the literature. Positive periodic steady states in
C^{3}({\mathbb{R}})
and nonnegative steady states with connected support and either zero or nonzero contact angles were investigated in
[
21
,
22
]
. In the present work, the steady states relevant to our dynamical analysis are neither periodic nor of the equal-contact-angle type; instead, they belong to a different regularity class, namely
L^{1}\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. The critical dynamical threshold is closely related to the extremal functions of the Sz.-Nagy inequality.
Our first main result
establishes that these extremal functions are indeed steady states. Specifically, they are nonnegative, radially symmetric, and non-increasing. The precise statement is given below.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
0<m<\infty
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

For
m\neq 3
, the Sz.-Nagy inequality (
8
) admits a unique optimizer
U_{*}
in
\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
. This optimizer is the unique nonnegative, radially decreasing steady state of (
1
) with bounded support and zero contact angle.

(ii)

For
m=3
, the Sz.-Nagy inequality (
8
) admits infinitely many optimizers in
\mathcal{Y}_{M_{c}}
. These optimizers are nonnegative, radially decreasing steady states of (
1
) and form a one-parameter family under mass-invariant scaling.

We next turn to the global minimization of the free energy. Existing results in
[
20
]
show that positive periodic steady states act as energy-unstable saddle points, and that families of compactly supported steady states possess lower energy than their periodic counterparts. Building on this insight, the present work goes further by identifying the exact energy minimizers. In particular, we prove that the optimizers of the Sz.-Nagy inequality coincide with the global minimizers of the free energy.
Our second main result
characterizes the associated energy landscape as follows:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
0<m<\infty
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

For
3<m<\infty
,
U_{\ast}
is the unique global minimizer of
F(u)
over
\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}
, and is also the unique global minimizer of
F(u)
among all steady-state solutions to (
1
) with mass
M
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M,P_{\ast}}}F(u)=F(U_{\ast})=\frac{m-3}{3(m+1)}P_{\ast}^{m+1}>0.

(15)

(ii)

For
m=3
, any optimizer
u_{c}
of the Sz.-Nagy inequality (
8
) is a global minimizer of
F(u)
over

\mathcal{Y}_{M_{c}}
and satisfies
F(u_{c})=0
.

(iii)

For
0<m<3
,
U_{\ast}
is the unique global minimizer of
F(u)
over
\mathcal{Y}_{M}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}}F(u)=F(U_{\ast})=\frac{m-3}{3(m+1)}P_{\ast}^{m+1}<0.

(16)

Before proceeding, let us state the notion of weak solutions adopted throughout this paper.

Definition 1.4

(Weak solution)
.

Let
u_{0}
be initial data satisfying (
4
) and
T\in(0,\infty]
. A weak solution
u
to (
1
) is a nonnegative function satisfying

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}\bigl(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\bigr),

\displaystyle u\in L^{2}\bigl(0,T;H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\bigr),

\displaystyle u^{1/2}u_{xxx}\in L^{2}\bigl(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\bigr)

such that for every
0<t<T
and any
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\psi u(\cdot,t)\,dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\psi u_{0}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\psi_{x}uu_{xxx}\,dx\,ds-\frac{m}{m+1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\psi_{xx}\,u^{m+1}\,dx\,ds.

(17)

This weak solution is also a weak entropy solution satisfying

\displaystyle F(u(\cdot,t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\left|(u_{xx}+u^{m})_{x}\right|^{2}dxds\leq F(u_{0}),\quad 0<t<T.

(18)

We are now ready to present
our third main result
concerning the dynamical behavior of solutions in the supercritical regime.

Theorem 1.5
.

For
3<m<\infty
, assume
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})=\frac{m-3}{3(m+1)}P_{*}^{m+1}
.

(i)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, then the solution blows up in finite time.

(ii)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, then the solution exists globally in time, and its second moment satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u(\cdot,t)dx=+\infty.

Remark 1.6
.

1.

Our results provide a sharp dichotomy criterion for global existence versus finite-time blow-up of solutions in the supercritical regime
3<m<\infty
. The main difficulty we address arises from the fact that, unlike the critical case, mass conservation alone does not provide sufficient scaling control. Our approach overcomes this issue by fully exploiting the variational structure of steady-state solutions and its intrinsic connection to the free energy functional.

2.

We note that under
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
, the equality
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
cannot hold; see Remark
4.2
. For
F(u_{0})>F(U_{\ast})
, two scenarios remain possible: either the second moment of the solution diverges, or its
L^{m+1}
-norm remains unbounded. If both quantities are bounded, the solution may converge to
U_{\ast}
. We discuss these issues further in Section
4.4
.

Remark 1.7

(Physical interpretation: potential well and threshold dynamics)
.

The above result reveals sharp threshold behavior governed by the potential-well structure of gradient-flow dynamics:

1.

Initial energy below the steady-state energy

F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
implies the system has already “crossed the ridge”. Its evolution proceeds only
downhill (energy decreasing)
and cannot overcome the energy barrier.

2.

The initial
L^{m+1}
norm

\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}
determines
which downhill path
the system follows.

(a)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, the system enters a
gentle valley inside the potential well
, where
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}
and
\|u_{x}\|_{2}
remain uniformly bounded. Consequently, the free energy is bounded from below and decreases toward a limit
F_{\infty}<F(U_{\ast})
. The second moment is necessarily unbounded, implying mass escapes to spatial infinity and the solution decays locally on every compact set. Whether the solution profile, after suitable translation, converges to a steady state with energy lower than
F(U_{\ast})
remains an open question; it may instead converge to a singular measure at infinity.

(b)

If
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
, the system slides into a
steep ravine outside the potential well
, where attractive forces dominate, gradients grow rapidly, and finite-time blow-up (rupture) occurs.

The contributions of this work are threefold: to characterize the variational structure of the free energy, to identify ground-state solutions as extremals of the Sz.-Nagy inequality, and to establish a sharp threshold separating global existence from finite-time blow-up.

C
a generic positive constant that may vary from line to line, and write
C=C(\cdot,\cdots,\cdot)
to indicate a constant depending only on the parameters inside the parentheses.

Existence criterion

In this section, we establish the local existence and blow-up criteria for bounded weak solutions to the nonlinear equation (
1
). To this end, we first define the standard mollifier
J(x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
by

\displaystyle J(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Ce^{\frac{1}{|x|^{2}-1}},&|x|<1,\\
0,&|x|\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

where the constant C is normalized so that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}J(x)dx=1
.This mollifier will be used in the subsequent compactness analysis and limiting procedure. Inspired by
[
24
]
, we consider the regularized problem.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\left((u_{\varepsilon})_{xxx}+(u_{\varepsilon}^{m})_{x}\right)\right)=0,&x\in{\mathbb{R}},t>0,\\
u_{\varepsilon}(x,0)=u_{0\varepsilon}(x)\geq 0,&x\in{\mathbb{R}},\end{array}\right.

where
u_{0\varepsilon}(x)=J_{\varepsilon}*u_{0}(x)
with
J_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{0\varepsilon}\geq 0,\quad\|u_{0\varepsilon}\|_{1}=\|u_{0}\|_{1},\\
u_{0\varepsilon}(x)\to u_{0}(x)\text{ in }L^{q}({\mathbb{R}})\text{ for }1\leq q<\infty,\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0,\\
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u_{0\varepsilon}(x)dx\to\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u_{0}(x)dx,\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.\end{array}\right.

The results in
[
24
, Proposition 2]
and
[
24
, Theorem 3]
assert that for any

, if

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})}\leq C_{0},

(24)

where

is independent of
\varepsilon
, then there exists a subsequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
such that

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon_{n}}\rightharpoonup{*}u\text{ in }L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\right),

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon_{n}}\rightharpoonup{}u\text{ in }L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\right).

Furthermore,

is a non-negative weak entropy solution satisfying

\displaystyle F(u(\cdot,t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\left|u_{xxx}+(u^{m})_{x}\right|^{2}dxds\leq F(u_{0}),\quad 0<t<T.

(27)

According to the above analysis, a weak entropy solution to (

Proposition 2.1

(Local in time existence and blow-up criteria)
.

Under assumption (
4
) on the initial condition, there exist a maximal existence time
T_{w}\in(0,\infty]
and a free energy solution
u
to (
1
) on

If

\displaystyle\|u_{x}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to\infty\text{ as }t\to T_{w}.

Proof.

The main task in proving the local existence is to obtain the following
H^{1}
-estimates. By multiplying equation (
2
) by
-(u_{\varepsilon})_{xx}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{\varepsilon})_{x}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{\varepsilon})_{xxx}^{2}\sqrt{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{\varepsilon})_{xxx}(u_{\varepsilon}^{m})_{x}\sqrt{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{\varepsilon})_{xxx}^{2}\sqrt{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{\varepsilon}^{m})_{x}^{2}\sqrt{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{\varepsilon})_{x}^{2}dx+K_{1}(M)\right)^{\frac{2m+1}{2}},

where

in one space dimension, that is

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\leq\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2}^{2}+\|(u_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}.

3
Proofs of Theorems

and

This section constructs the variational framework needed for analyzing global existence and finite-time blow-up in the following section. We first study steady-state solutions of (
1
) and their relation to the free energy functional. Subsection

presents basic properties of steady states as necessary preliminaries.

Based on these preparations, we further analyze the Euler-Lagrange equation derived from the Sz.-Nagy inequality. We prove that the extremal functions of this inequality are steady-state solutions of (

3.1
Steady States

This subsection is devoted to a systematic investigation of steady-state solutions to equation (
1
). In
[
22
]
, Laugesen and Pugh considered positive steady states in
C^{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, as well as compactly supported touchdown steady states with regularity
C^{1}[c,d]\cap C^{3}(c,d)
. They proved that periodic positive steady states and touchdown steady states with equal contact angles satisfy

\displaystyle UU_{xxx}+U(U^{m})_{x}=0\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}.

(35)

In this work, we focus on solutions belonging to the function class
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. Such solutions are distinct from the periodic profiles and equal-contact-angle steady states investigated in previous literature. Instead, they correspond to critical points of the free energy under mass conservation, characterized by a constant chemical potential over their support and compact support consisting of finitely many intervals, with no additional constraints imposed on their contact angles.

Let us start by precisely defining steady states to (
1
).

Definition 3.1
.

Given
U_{s}\in L_{+}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, we call it a steady state of (

) if
U_{s}(U_{s}^{m})_{x}\in H^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}})
,
(U_{s})_{xxx}\in L_{loc}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, and it satisfies

\displaystyle U_{s}(U_{s})_{xxx}+U_{s}(U_{s}^{m})_{x}=0\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}

(36)

in the sense of distributions. Throughout this subsection, we assume the following:

(K1)

0<m<\infty
,
U_{s}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap C({\mathbb{R}})
with
U_{s}\geq 0
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}dx=M
.

(K2)

Let
\Omega:=\{x\mid U_{s}>0\}
and
U_{s}=0
in
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Omega
.

(K3)

If
\Omega
is unbounded, then
U_{s}
decays sufficiently fast at infinity.

(K4)

If

U_{s}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

We now present equivalent characterizations of steady states and show that the chemical potential is constant on each connected component of the support.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under assumptions
(K1)
–
(K4)
, let

\displaystyle\mu_{s}=-(U_{s})_{xx}-U_{s}^{m}.

(37)

Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

No dissipation:
\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}\left|(\mu_{s})_{x}\right|^{2}dx=0
.

(ii)

\Omega=\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}(a_{k},b_{k})
with
1\leq N<\infty
is a finite union of bounded intervals, and

\displaystyle\mu_{s}=C_{k}\quad\text{for }x\in(a_{k},b_{k}),

(38)

where
C_{k}
is a constant on each interval
(a_{k},b_{k})
.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
. Since
U_{s}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, the one-dimensional embedding implies
U_{s}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Since
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is dense in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\mu_{s}\left(U_{s}(\mu_{s})_{x}\right)_{x}dx=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}|(\mu_{s})_{x}|^{2}dx.

(39)

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
. Since
U_{s}\in C({\mathbb{R}})
, the set
\Omega
is open and consists of countably many disjoint open components, which may be unbounded. We write
\Omega=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}(a_{k},b_{k})
. Where
U_{s}>0
, equation (
36
) reduces to

\displaystyle(U_{s})_{xxx}+(U_{s}^{m})_{x}=0

(40)

in the sense of distributions on
\Omega
. It follows that

\displaystyle(U_{s})_{xx}+U_{s}^{m}=C_{k}\quad\text{on }(a_{k},b_{k}).

(41)

Different intervals may have different constants
C_{k}
.

We first claim that
\Omega
has no unbounded component. Suppose for contradiction that there exists an index
k_{0}
such that
(a_{k_{0}},b_{k_{0}})=(a,\infty)
. On this interval,

\displaystyle(U_{s})_{xx}+U_{s}^{m}=C_{k_{0}},\quad x\in(a,\infty).

Since
U_{s}\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, we have
U_{s}^{m}\to 0
and
(U_{s})_{xx}\to C_{k_{0}}
as
|x|\to\infty
. This contradicts
U_{s}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
unless
U_{s}\equiv 0
.

Next, we claim that
1\leq N<\infty
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
N=\infty
. Then all intervals are contained in a bounded set, so
L_{k}:=b_{k}-a_{k}\to 0
. Rescaling each interval to
(0,1)
via
x=a_{k}+L_{k}y
and setting
v_{k}(y)=U_{s}(a_{k}+L_{k}y)
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{k}^{\prime\prime}+L_{k}^{2}v_{k}^{m}=L_{k}^{2}\mu_{k},\quad v_{k}(0)=v_{k}(1)=0,\ v_{k}>0.

(42)

As
L_{k}\to 0
, the equation formally becomes
v_{k}^{\prime\prime}=0
, whose only solution with zero boundary conditions is
v_{k}\equiv 0
, contradicting
v_{k}>0
. Thus
N
must be finite, and
\Omega
is a finite union of bounded disjoint open intervals.
This completes the proof.
∎

A particularly important class is obtained by requiring that
U_{s}
be a critical point of the free energy
F(u)
with fixed mass
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\,dx=M
. As we show below, this additional condition forces the chemical potential
\mu_{s}
to be the same constant on all connected components.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume
(K1)
–
(K4)
. Let
U_{s}
be a critical point of
F(u)
subject to
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}\,dx=M
. Then

(i)

The following Pohozaev identity holds:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|(U_{s})_{x}\right|^{2}dx=\frac{2m}{3(m+1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx.

(43)

(ii)

The chemical potential satisfies

\displaystyle(U_{s})_{xx}+U_{s}^{m}=\overline{C}\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\Omega,

(44)

where
\overline{C}=\dfrac{1}{M}\dfrac{m+3}{3(m+1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx
.

(iii)

The following boundary condition holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(b_{k}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(b_{k})-a_{k}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(a_{k})\right)=0.

(45)

In particular, if
\Omega=[-L,L]
, then
U_{s}^{\prime}(L)=U_{s}^{\prime}(-L)=0
.

Proof.

By definition,
U_{s}
is a critical point of
F(u)
under the mass constraint
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}\,dx=M
. That is, for every
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfying
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\eta dx=0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}F(U_{s}+\varepsilon\eta)=0.

(46)

(i) Pohozaev identity.
Consider the scaling ansatz
U_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda U_{s}(\lambda x)
for
\lambda>0
. Since
U_{s}
is a critical point, the derivative of
F(U_{\lambda})
at
\lambda=1
vanishes; this can be verified by taking
\eta=U_{s}+x(U_{s})_{x}
, which has zero integral. A direct computation yields

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dF(U_{\lambda})}{d\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}=\frac{3}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|(U_{s})_{x}|^{2}dx-\frac{m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx=0.

(47)

This gives the Pohozaev identity

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|(U_{s})_{x}|^{2}dx=\frac{m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx.

(48)

(ii) Global constant chemical potential.
With the Pohozaev identity in hand, we further show that the chemical potential is globally constant. Choose two disjoint connected components
\Omega_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i})
and
\Omega_{j}=(a_{j},b_{j})
, and take nonnegative functions
\phi_{i}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{i})
,
\phi_{j}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})
such that
\int_{\Omega_{i}}\phi_{i}dx=\int_{\Omega_{j}}\phi_{j}dx
. Define
\eta=\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}
; clearly
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\eta\,dx=0
. Substituting
\eta
into (
46
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left((U_{s})_{x}\eta_{x}-U_{s}^{m}\eta\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left((U_{s})_{x}(\phi_{i})_{x}-U_{s}^{m}\phi_{i}\right)dx-\int_{\Omega_{j}}\left((U_{s})_{x}(\phi_{j})_{x}-U_{s}^{m}\phi_{j}\right)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{i}}\big((U_{s})_{xx}+U_{s}^{m}\big)\phi_{i}dx+\int_{\Omega_{j}}\big((U_{s})_{xx}+U_{s}^{m}\big)\phi_{j}dx

\displaystyle=-C_{i}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\phi_{i}dx+C_{j}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\phi_{j}dx.

Hence
C_{i}=C_{j}
. Denote this common constant by
\overline{C}
; then

\displaystyle(U_{s})_{xx}+U_{s}^{m}=\overline{C}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(49)

Multiplying (
49
) by
U_{s}
and integrating over
{\mathbb{R}}
gives

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|(U_{s})_{x}|^{2}dx=\overline{C}M.

(50)

Combining (
48
) and (
50
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\overline{C}=\frac{1}{M}\frac{m+3}{3(m+1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx.

(iii) Boundary conditions.
Recall that
\Omega=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}(a_{k},b_{k})
. Multiplying (
49
) by
x(U_{s})_{x}
and by
U_{s}
, respectively, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x(U_{s})_{x}^{2}\bigg|_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}dx-\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx=-\overline{C}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}U_{s}dx

and

\displaystyle\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}dx-\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx=-\overline{C}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}U_{s}dx.

Combining these yields

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}dx=\frac{m}{m+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}U_{s}^{m+1}dx+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(b_{k}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(b_{k})-a_{k}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(a_{k})\right).

Applying (
48
), we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(b_{k}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(b_{k})-a_{k}(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(a_{k})\right)=0.

In particular, when
N=1
with
\Omega=[-L,L]
, we have

\displaystyle L(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(-L)+L(U_{s})_{x}^{2}(L)=0,

which forces
U_{s}^{\prime}(-L)=U_{s}^{\prime}(L)=0
. The proof is complete.
∎

Using the Pohozaev identity, we now derive the free energy of steady states.

Corollary 3.4
.

The free energy of a steady state with mass
M
satisfies

\displaystyle F(U_{s})=\frac{m-3}{3(m+1)}\|U_{s}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0,&m>3,\\
=0,&m=3,\\
<0,&0<m<3.\end{array}\right.

This subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem

We first recall the Euler-Lagrange equation satisfied by extremals of the Sz.-Nagy inequality, which was established in

Lemma 3.5

(Extremals of the Sz.-Nagy inequality
[
26
]
)

Let

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof can be divided into two steps.

Step 1
(Proof of (i)). For

from (

\displaystyle P_{\ast}=\left(\frac{\alpha+2}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-\alpha-1}}=\left(\frac{3(m+1)}{2mC_{*}M^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{m}{(m-3)(m+1)}}.

(55)

For a given solution

to (

\displaystyle U_{\ast}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ast}}V_{0}\left(\frac{x}{\mu_{\ast}}\right),

Then

is also a solution to (

\displaystyle-(U_{\ast})_{xx}=U_{\ast}^{m}-\frac{1}{M}\frac{m+3}{3(m+1)}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\quad\text{a.e. in }\operatorname{supp}(U_{\ast})

(59)

Next, applying Theorem

(iii), we conclude that for

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=u^{m}-\frac{1}{M}\frac{m+3}{3(m+1)}P_{\ast}^{m+1},\quad 0<r<L,\\[5.69054pt]

Step 2
(Proof of (ii)). For

(Aggregation-dominated regime)

), we obtain

\displaystyle g\left(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}\right)=F(U_{\ast})\geq g\left(\|u\|_{m+1}\right)

(86)

for all
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}})
. Now, under the condition

\displaystyle F\left(u_{0}\right)<F\left(U_{\ast}\right),

(87)

there exists a constant
0<\delta<1
such that

\displaystyle F\left(u_{0}\right)<\delta F\left(U_{\ast}\right).

(88)

Since
F(u)
is non-increasing in time, it follows that

\displaystyle g\left(\|u\|_{m+1}\right)\leq F(u)\leq F(u_{0})<\delta F\left(U_{\ast}\right)=\delta g\left(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}\right).

(89)

On the other hand, since
\alpha+2<m+1
for
m>3
, a direct computation shows that the maximum of
g(x)
is attained at

\displaystyle x_{*}=\left(\frac{\alpha+2}{2C_{*}M^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-\alpha-1}}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

This implies that
g(x)
is strictly increasing for
x<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
. Therefore, if

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}<\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},

(90)

then (
89
) ensures that there exists
\mu_{1}<1
(depending on
\delta
) such that, for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{m+1}<\mu_{1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

(91)

Conversely,
g(x)
is strictly decreasing for
x>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
. Thus, if

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}>\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},

(92)

using (
89
) again, we conclude that there exists
\mu_{2}>1
(depending on
\delta
) such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{m+1}>\mu_{2}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

(93)

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We note that if
F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast})
, the case
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}
cannot occur. Indeed, it follows directly from Proposition
3.6
(i) that
F(u_{0})\geq F(U_{\ast})
whenever
\|u_{0}\|_{m+1}=\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}=P_{\ast}
.

4.2
Finite-time blow-up

This subsection is devoted to the finite-time blow-up part of Theorem
1.5
. We prove that solutions blow up in finite time when the initial
L^{m+1}
-norm exceeds the critical threshold. The analysis relies on the time evolution of the second moment, following the classical approach proposed in
[
18
]
. We define the second moment as

\displaystyle m_{2}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u(x,t),dx.

(94)

Theorem 4.3

(Finite-time blow-up)
.

Let
3<m<\infty
, and let
u(x,t)
denote the weak entropy solution to (
1
) obtained from Proposition
2.1
on
[0,T_{w})
. Under assumptions (
4
), (
78
), and (
81
), the solution to (
1
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{2}(t)=6F(u)-2\frac{m-3}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx,\quad 0<t<T_{w}.

(95)

Moreover,
T_{w}<\infty
, and
u(x,t)
blows up in finite time in the sense that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T_{w}}\|u_{x}(\cdot,t)\|_{2}=\infty.

Proof.

We present the formal computation; the passage to the limit from the regularized problem (
2
) can be justified as in the proof of
[
24
, Theorem 3]
without additional difficulty. The key ingredient is the time evolution of the second moment. Integrating by parts in (
1
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}xuu_{xxx}dx+\frac{2m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x(u^{m+1})_{x}dx

\displaystyle=-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}uu_{xx}dx-2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}xu_{x}u_{xx}dx-\frac{2m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|u_{x}|^{2}dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|u_{x}|^{2}dx-\frac{2m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=3\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|u_{x}|^{2}dx-\frac{2m}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=6F(u)-2\frac{m-3}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u^{m+1}dx.

(96)

Since
m>3
, we use (
96
) together with (
78
) and estimate (
82
) from Proposition
4.1
(with some
\mu_{2}>1
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{2}(t)

\displaystyle<6F(u_{0})-2\mu_{2}\frac{m-3}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{\ast}^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle<6F(U_{\ast})-2\mu_{2}\frac{m-3}{m+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{\ast}^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle=2(1-\mu_{2})\frac{m-3}{m+1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}<0.

(97)

Here we have used that
F(u)
is non-increasing in time and Corollary
3.4
. It follows from (
4.2
) that there exists
T>0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T}m_{2}(t)=0.

Next, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|x|\leq R}u(x)dx+\int_{|x|>R}u(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq CR^{\frac{m}{m+1}}\|u\|_{m+1}+\frac{1}{R^{2}}m_{2}(t).

(98)

Choosing
R=\left(\frac{Cm_{2}(t)}{\|u\|_{m+1}}\right)^{\frac{m+1}{3m+2}}
yields

\displaystyle M\leq C\|u\|_{m+1}^{\frac{2(m+1)}{3m+2}}m_{2}(t)^{\frac{m}{3m+2}},

(99)

which implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}\geq\lim_{t\to T}M^{\frac{3m+2}{2(m+1)}}m_{2}(t)^{-\frac{m}{2(m+1)}}=\infty.

Using the Sz.-Nagy inequality (
8
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T}\|u_{x}\|_{2}=\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Global existence

This subsection is dedicated to the global existence part of Theorem
1.5
. We prove that solutions exist globally in time when the initial
L^{m+1}
-norm lies below the critical threshold associated with
U_{\ast}
.

Theorem 4.4

(Global existence)
.

Let
3<m<\infty
. Assume that (
4
), (
78
), and (
79
) hold. Then the weak solution obtained in Proposition
2.1
exists globally on
[0,\infty)
and satisfies

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}+\|u_{x}(\cdot,t)\|_{2}\leq C\bigl(\|u_{0}\|_{1},\|(u_{0})_{x}\|_{2}\bigr),\quad 0<t<\infty

(100)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\left|u_{xxx}+(u^{m})_{x}\right|^{2}dxds\leq C\bigl(\|u_{0}\|_{1},\|(u_{0})_{x}\|_{2}\bigr),\quad 0<t<\infty.

(101)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}m_{2}(t)=\infty.

(102)

Proof.

The proof consists of two parts. Step 1 establishes the global existence of weak solutions. Step 2 proves that the second moment tends to infinity, thereby verifying (
102
).

Step 1
(Global existence). Under assumption (
79
), it follows from (
80
) in Proposition
4.1
that for any
t>0
, there exists
\mu_{1}<1
such that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}<\mu_{1}\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1}.

(103)

Furthermore, since the free energy
F(u)
is non-increasing in time, we have for any
t>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|u_{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m+1}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}^{m+1}+F(u_{0})\leq C\bigl(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},F(u_{0})\bigr),

(104)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\left|u_{xxx}+(u^{m})_{x}\right|^{2}dxds\leq F(u_{0})+\frac{1}{m+1}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\leq C\bigl(\|U_{\ast}\|_{m+1},F(u_{0})\bigr).

(105)

Combining the Sobolev embedding theorem with Hölder’s inequality for
1<r<\infty
, we infer from (
104
) that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{r}({\mathbb{R}})}\leq C,\quad\text{for any }1\leq r\leq\infty.

(106)

Therefore, for any
T>0
, using the arguments from Lemma 2 in
[
24
]
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, the following uniform regularity estimates hold:

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})),

(107)

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{r}({\mathbb{R}}))\quad\text{for any }1\leq r\leq\infty,

(108)

\displaystyle u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})),

(109)

\displaystyle u^{1/2}u_{xxx}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})),

\displaystyle u^{1/2}(u^{m})_{x}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})).

In addition, the second moment satisfies

\displaystyle m_{2}(t)\leq m_{2}(0)+6F(u_{0})t,\quad\text{for any }0<t<T.

(110)

Thus, uniform bounds for the terms in the free energy have been established. Following the global existence argument in
[
24
]
, we conclude for
3<m<\infty
that there exists a weak entropy solution to (
1
) on
[0,T]
for any
0<T<\infty
.

Step 2
(Proof of (
102
)). Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists an increasing sequence of times
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to\infty
such that

\displaystyle m_{2}(t_{k})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u(x,t_{k})dx

(111)

is bounded. Since
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, we claim that there exist a subsequence (still denoted
t_{k}
) and a function
u_{\infty}
such that as
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t_{k})\rightharpoonup u_{\infty}\text{ in }H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t_{k})\to u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),

(112)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t_{k})\to u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{m+1}({\mathbb{R}})

(113)

with
\|u_{\infty}\|_{1}=M
. Moreover, the second moment of the limit satisfies

\displaystyle 0<\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u_{\infty}dx\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx<\infty,

(114)

since concentration at a Dirac delta function at the origin is excluded by the uniform bound (
107
).

On the other hand, a direct consequence of (
103
) is that the free energy
F(u)
is bounded from below:

\displaystyle F(u_{0})-\liminf_{k\to\infty}F(u)(t_{k})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(x,s)\left|u_{xxx}+(u^{m})_{x}\right|^{2}dx\right)ds.

(115)

It follows that the Fisher information is integrable, and

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{t_{k}}^{\infty}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(x,s)\left|u_{xxx}+(u^{m})_{x}\right|^{2}dx\right)ds=0,

(116)

which implies that, up to a subsequence, the limit
u_{\infty}
as
k\to\infty
satisfies

\displaystyle\bigl((u_{\infty})_{xx}+u_{\infty}^{m}\bigr)_{x}=0\quad\text{a.e. in }\operatorname{supp}(u_{\infty}).

(117)

This follows by arguments similar to those in the existence proof in Section 2 of
[
6
]
. Therefore,
u_{\infty}
is a radially symmetric steady-state solution to (
1
) with mass
M
. By Proposition
3.6
(ii),

\displaystyle F(U_{\ast})\leq F(u_{\infty}).

(118)

However, (
113
) yields

\displaystyle F(u_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|(u_{\infty})_{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|u_{\infty}\|_{m+1}^{m+1}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\|u_{x}(\cdot,t_{k})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{m+1}\|u(\cdot,t_{k})\|_{m+1}^{m+1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{k\to\infty}F(u(\cdot,t_{k}))\leq F(u_{0})<F(U_{\ast}),

(119)

contradicting (
118
). Hence, (
102
) holds.

It remains to justify the convergences (
112
) and (
113
) used above. We first prove (
112
). Let
0\leq\chi_{R}\leq 1
be a function in
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
such that
\chi_{R}=1
on
B_{R}(0)
and
\chi_{R}=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus B_{2R}(0)
. We decompose

\displaystyle M=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{k})\chi_{R}dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{k})(1-\chi_{R})dx.

(120)

Then for sufficiently large
R
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{k})(1-\chi_{R})dx\leq\int_{|x|\geq R}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\int_{|x|\geq R}|x|^{2}u(\cdot,t_{k})dx<\varepsilon,

(121)

while

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{k})\chi_{R}dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u_{\infty}\chi_{R}dx.

Hence,

\displaystyle\Bigl|M-\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{k})\chi_{R}dx\Bigr|<\varepsilon\quad\text{as }R\to\infty,

(122)

which implies

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u_{\infty}dx=M>0.

(123)

This proves (
112
).

We next prove (
113
). Using the condition
1<m+1<\infty
and the compact Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(B_{R}(0))\hookrightarrow L^{q}(B_{R}(0))
for
1\leq q<\infty
, we obtain local strong convergence in
L^{m+1}(B_{R}(0))
for any fixed
R>0
. Moreover, it follows from the Sz.-Nagy inequality (
8
) that

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\geq R}u^{m+1}dx\leq C_{*}\left(\int_{|x|\geq R}udx\right)^{\frac{3+m}{3}}\|u_{x}\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{3}m}\leq C\bigl(M,C_{*},F(u_{0})\bigr)\left(\frac{m_{2}(t)}{R^{2}}\right)^{\frac{3+m}{3}}

(124)

upon using (
121
). Therefore, as
R\to\infty
and
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx=\int_{|x|<R}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx+\int_{|x|\geq R}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{|x|<R}\left|u(\cdot,t_{k})-u_{\infty}\right|^{m+1}dx+C\int_{|x|\geq R}\bigl(|u(\cdot,t_{k})|^{m+1}+|u_{\infty}|^{m+1}\bigr)dx\to 0.

This completes the proof of Theorem
4.4
.
∎

4.4
Possible dynamic behaviors for
F(u_{0})>F(U_{\ast})

For initial data satisfying
F(u_{0})>F(U_{\ast})
, energy dissipation no longer prevents solutions from approaching the steady state
U_{\ast}
. The long-time behavior of global solutions is conjectured to depend on the boundedness of two key quantities:

\displaystyle\textbf{(P1) }\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{m+1}<\infty,\quad\text{(concentration control)}

\displaystyle\textbf{(P2) }\sup_{t\geq 0}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}u(x,t)\,dx<\infty.\quad\text{(mass confinement)}

Based on the gradient-flow structure and analogy with related models, we expect the following scenarios:

1.

(P1) and (P2):
The solution is expected to converge (possibly up to translation) to
U_{\ast}
.

2.

(P1) and not (P2):
Mass is expected to escape to infinity while the profile remains bounded in
L^{m+1}
. After a suitable time-dependent translation that follows the escaping core, the profile may converge locally to a steady state.

3.

not (P1) and (P2):
Unbounded concentration growth within a confined region suggests the possibility of infinite-time singularity formation as a Dirac mass, with the free energy tending to
-\infty
.

4.

not (P1) and not (P2):
The solution may exhibit complex dynamics involving simultaneous concentration growth and mass dispersion, possibly leading to intricate asymptotic patterns.

Remark 4.5
.

With the exception of the first case, the precise asymptotic behavior in the other scenarios depends on further details of the model and the initial data. A rigorous analysis of these cases remains an open challenge.

4.5
Similarity solutions

We now use similarity solutions to describe the dominant dynamics of (
1
). Consider rescaling the length, height, and time scales of the solution according to

\displaystyle x=L\hat{x},\quad t=T\hat{t},\quad u(x,t)=H\hat{u}(\hat{x},\hat{t}).

(125)

Substituting this change of variables into (
1
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{H}{T}\frac{\partial\hat{u}}{\partial\hat{t}}=-\frac{H^{2}}{L^{4}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}\left(\hat{u}\frac{\partial^{3}\hat{u}}{\partial\hat{x}}\right)-m\frac{H^{m+1}}{L^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}\left(\hat{u}^{m}\frac{\partial\hat{u}}{\partial\hat{x}}\right).

(126)

Balancing the coefficients of the second- and fourth-order spatial terms shows that the film thickness scale is inversely proportional to the horizontal length scale:
H=L^{-\frac{2}{m-1}}
. Furthermore, balancing the time derivative term to obtain the distinguished limit gives the relation between the length scale and the time scale:

\displaystyle L=T^{\frac{m-1}{4m-2}},\quad H=T^{-\frac{1}{2m-1}}.

(127)

These scalings produce two classes of similarity solutions:

(i) Infinite-time spreading solutions:

\displaystyle H\to 0\text{ and }L\to\infty\text{ as }T\to\infty,

(ii) Finite-time blow-up solutions:

\displaystyle H\to\infty\text{ and }L\to 0\text{ as }T\to 0.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have investigated the dynamical behavior of a one-dimensional thin-film equation in the supercritical regime
3<m<\infty
, where the competition between fourth-order repulsive diffusion and aggregative nonlinearity leads to rich threshold phenomena. A key discovery of the present work is that the extremal function of the sharp Sz.-Nagy inequality, under appropriate mass and norm constraints, serves as the global minimizer of the free energy functional. This variational identity provides a natural and rigorous foundation for determining critical initial data.

By exploiting this variational characterization, we establish a sharp and optimal threshold criterion that classifies solutions according to their initial
L^{m+1}
-norm relative to the critical profile. The result reveals a complete dichotomy: initial data with sufficiently high concentration leads to finite-time blow-up, while data below the threshold yields global existence accompanied by infinite-time spatial spreading. Our threshold significantly extends classical results by allowing positive initial free energy, thereby covering a far broader class of initial configurations.

These findings highlight the crucial role of variational structures and sharp functional inequalities in understanding threshold dynamics for degenerate higher-order diffusion equations. Future directions include the rigorous analysis of long-time behaviors for solutions above the critical energy, the extension of the present variational framework to higher dimensions, and the investigation of threshold phenomena in related gradient-flow models with competing nonlinear effects.
