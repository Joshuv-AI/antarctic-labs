ABSTRACT
Elastic anisotropy in the subsurface can manifest from pre-existing structure or stress-induced
cracking – both phenomena are of interest at the Rock Valley site in Nevada. The azimuthal
dependence of Rayleigh wave ellipticity, also known as H/V, can reveal elastic anisotropy, but the
effect of isotropic variability in H/V must first be accounted for. To that end, we simulate synthetic
seismograms from a ring of sources in a fully 3-D isotropic velocity model that captures the geology
at Rock Valley. We measure H/V from these waveforms and compare them with H/V predictions
obtained using 1D models extracted at specific locations from the 3D model. We find that an
average of the azimuthal H/V measurements from the 3-D simulations and 1-D predictions agree,
but that the azimuthal variability of H/V is a large fraction of the mean value. These results estimate
the noise character in azimuthal H/V so it can be factored out of future anisotropic simulations.

1. INTRODUCTION

Underground explosions produce changes in the subsurface stress field and subsurface fracture (e.g.
Trivino & Mohanty, 2015; Wang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2017). Even when seismograms don’t
record the waveforms associated with these events, a comparative measurement of the stress or
fracture field pre- and post-shot may be sufficient to reveal information about the character and size
of the explosion. However, remotely measuring the stress field or fracture state, and particularly
subtle changes in these, is a challenging task. Seismologists approach this task using various tools to
probe the stress and fracture state of the crust. The most robust seismic measurements of stress
involve inverting the focal mechanisms of a set of earthquakes for the principal stress directions and
shape ratio in a volume sampled by those earthquakes (Gephart & Forsyth, 1984; Michael, 1984).
Another technique is shear-wave splitting that measures the azimuthal anisotropy in the crust by
comparing the arrival times of orthogonally-polarized upward-propagating S-waves – this anisotropy
is often attributed to principal stresses preferentially closing cracks (Crampin & Chastin, 2003). Both
methods rely on a sufficient distribution of earthquakes in time and space. For greater temporal and
spatial flexibility, ambient noise interferometry allows us to design experiments with arbitrary station
placement and virtual source locations. The anisotropy of ambient noise-derived surface wave phase
velocity has been used with success in some scenarios (Lin & Ritzwoller, 2011b), but requires a good
isotropic model of phase velocity and an accounting for non-physical isotropic bias (Lin &
Ritzwoller, 2011a). In contrast, measurements of Rayleigh wave ellipticity, the ratio of the amplitude
of the radial motion to the vertical motion, here referred to as the Horizontal to Vertical ratio (H/V)
to conform with similar near-surface techniques, do not rely on a wave propagation model.
Additionally, H/V is sensitive to shallower portions of the crust than phase velocity at the same
periods, allowing application of higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) measurements, common for
longer periods, from waveforms to investigate shallow structure. The azimuths of minimum H/V in
an azimuthally anisotropic crust are associated with either a maximum horizontal compressive stress
direction or a pre-existing structural fabric, similar to shear-wave splitting (Lin & Schmandt, 2014).
Whether this method is sensitive enough to measure the kind of local-scale stress rotation or
fracture creation we might observe from an explosion in the presence of existing isotropic
heterogeneity is what we seek to understand.
However, to understand the scale at which we can measure stress-induced anisotropy, anisotropic
structure, or stress rotation, we first need to understand the inherent isotropic variability in H/V
associated with a heterogeneous velocity model embedded with surface source locations. To make
this characterization, we employ 3-D seismic simulations through a realistic velocity model tailored
to Rock Valley and compare the resulting observations of H/V with predictions from robust 1-D
solutions made from 1-D velocity profiles extracted from the same velocity model. We simulate 10
surface force sources collocated with station locations that are approximately equally spaced in
azimuth from the network center. The collocation of sources and stations is intended to reflect the
virtual source arrangement of ambient noise interferometry. We process synthetic 3-component
seismograms recorded on 370 stations placed in the model that reflect the real Rock Valley seismic
network. In this work we present the methods we used to simulate our data and process our H/V
measurements, assess our H/V results, and characterize the azimuthal variability of H/V as a
function of station location.

2. METHODOLOGY

The goal of this research is to characterize isotropic measurements of Rayleigh wave H/V in a
realistic velocity model tailored to the Rock Valley site. To accomplish this, we first simulate
benchmark 1-D H/V solutions for Rayleigh waves in a layered velocity model. To simulate 3-D
seismograms, we used an isotropic velocity model based on a geological framework model (GFM)
built for the region (Prothro, 2020). Three-dimensional simulations were computed with Parelasti
(Preston, 2019), a finite difference elastic wave solver, and the resulting seismograms were processed
in the same manner as observational Rayleigh wave data.

2.1.       1-D H/V

We simulated Rayleigh wave H/V directly in one dimension using the surface wave modal
summation package of Computer Programs in Seismology (CPS; Hermann, 2013). Using the GFM,
we extracted the 1-D velocity and density structure in the z dimension at each of the 370 stations
placed in the model and truncated the model to begin at the local surface elevation. This truncated,
layered model was used as the input for CPS, and H/V values were computed at 50 logarithmically
spaced frequencies between 0.1 and 10 Hz. Additionally, we computed the depth-dependent velocity
sensitivity kernels of H/V at relevant frequencies (0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 Hz) for analysis.

2.2. 3-D Waveform Simulations

We simulated solutions to the isotropic, elastic wave equation in a 3-D cartesian model using
Parelasti (Preston, 2019). Parelasti employs staggered-grid finite difference solutions to the velocity-
stress equations (Preston, 2018). We based our model on the GFM of Prothro (2020), with velocities
assigned to each of 4 layers according to laboratory-determined values for comparable samples
(Table 1). The model has a domain of 36 km by 24 km by 14 km in the x, y, and z directions,
respectively, and cell dimensions of 20 m by 20 m by 20 m. The x and y directions are parallel to the
local Northing and Easting coordinates for Universal Transverse Mercator zone 11N, with the x-y
origin (0,0) at the southwest corner of the model. The z coordinate is oriented positive downwards
(into the earth), with 0 elevation at mean sea level. In addition to 12 km below sea level, the model
extends 2 km above sea level. Of the 2 km above sea level, the average surface elevation is about 1
km – the remaining model thickness is atmosphere. We ran simulations to 30 seconds, with 1
millisecond time steps, and applied a mutiaxial perfectly matched layer absorbing boundary
condition with 0.01 cross factor to the model edges. For each source location, we used a vertical
force source at the surface. We convolved the Green’s function at each receiver with a Gaussian
source time function (STF) in post processing. This Gaussian STF was constructed to have a 2 Hz
corner frequency, and then further filtered with a 0.1-2 Hz bandpass, 4-pole zero-phase Butterworth
filter (Figure 1). The simulations were run for 10 surface source locations collocated with 10 stations
forming an outer ring around the 370-station network (Figure 2). By using a vertical force source, we
simulated the Green’s functions for vertical-vertical and vertical-radial cross-correlations in ambient
noise seismology. Due to the distribution of coherent noise sources, the presence of incoherent

noise, and other factors present in real data, the ambient noise cross-correlation is an approximation
of the exact surface force Green’s functions computed here (Nakata et al., 2019).

2.3. Determining H/V from 3-D Simulations

From each synthetic source, simulating a virtual source in ambient noise interferometry, we
recovered synthetic 3-component (vertical, east, north) seismograms at every receiver station, which
represents virtual receivers in ambient noise cross-correlation. The raw waveforms at each receiver
were tapered with a 10% Hanning window, and the horizontal components were rotated in two
different manners to compare effects of wave propagation through the model. First, into the
geographic backazimuth of the source station and, second, the dominant polarization direction. The
polarization backazimuth was determined from an eigenvalue decomposition of the 3-component
waveforms following Jurkevics (1988) and Vidale (1986). Due to the variability of the polarization
backazimuth at high frequency, we chose to use the polarization backazimuth obtained from
waveforms lowpass filtered with a 0.3 Hz corner frequency. While the rotation direction was
determined from lowpass-filtered waveforms, the rotation was applied to the unfiltered waveforms
from which the H/V measurements were made. To measure the H/V, we employed a spectral
approach, similar to Nakamura (1989)’s HVSR technique, but applied only to the known radial
component, rather than a summation of the horizontal components. After rotating the horizontal
waveforms into the radial and transverse directions, we applied a Fourier transform to the radial and
vertical seismograms and divided their amplitude spectra. This quotient is the spectral amplitude of
H/V. Two sets of H/V data were computed according to rotation to retrieve the radial component’s
estimate, one for the polarization rotation and another for the geographic rotation.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Seismogram Record Sections (3-D Simulations)
For each source in the 3-D model, we simulated in 3-D one 30 second, 3-component output
seismogram for each of the 370 receiver stations, including the collocated source station. These
seismograms contain both body wave and surface wave arrivals, with the largest amplitudes
produced by the surface waves. A record section of the vertical component arrivals for source 1 is
depicted in Figure 3, with receivers organized by distance from the source. We note that the P-wave
is observable as the first low amplitude arrival, and that the high amplitude surface wave follows
with increasing separation as the receiver distance increases. We observe that the coda of the
waveforms, the trail of seismic phases after the surface wave arrival, becomes longer with increasing
offset, indicating the effect of seismic scattering in the simulation from 3-D velocity heterogeneity.
We also observe that the high amplitude moveout is linear, as expected for a cylindrically
propagating wave. The moveout velocity of this phase is 2.6 km/s, which is about 0.9 VS of the
Paleozoic layer. Along with the dispersive behavior of the wave, discussed below, this confirms the
phase is a surface wave, moving at an average group velocity that is primarily sensitive to the lowest
model layer. The grouping of overlapping traces in the middle of the distance axis corresponds to
the large array of stations in the middle of the model that all have similar offset and a similar Green’s
function.

H/V values measured from 3-D – simulated waveforms, and the local 1-D model velocity. We

layers, such as for stations resting directly on the Pz layer, compared to those resting on high
velocity contrast layers, such as for stations overlying the basin in the model center. To illustrate the

3.6. Azimuthal Variability of H/V

 23

In addition to the average H/V observed from our 3-D simulations, we also measure the standard
deviation of H/V as a function of source azimuth for each receiver station. This azimuthal
variability can be observed in Figure 6 and 7, panel D. While the station-specific means of log H/V
in aggregate follow a normal distribution (Figure 11, panel A), the standard deviation of station-
specific log H/V is itself log-normally distributed, a nested log-normal structure (Figure 11, panel B).
The distribution of azimuthal average log H/V is approximately zero-mean for frequencies below
0.5 Hz, indicating that H/V is equally distributed above and below a ratio of 1 among all stations.
Frequencies between 0.5 Hz and 1 Hz have a mean azimuthal average of log H/V slightly less than
0, indicating H/V ratios less than 1. The standard deviation of log H/V is smaller for frequencies
less than 0.5 Hz, with a mean value of 0.47, compared to frequencies between 0.5 and 1 Hz that
have a mean of 0.62. These log values correspond to a ratio range of 0.62-1.60 and 0.54-1.86 times
the mean H/V value. The mean over 50 logarithmically-distributed frequencies between 0.1 and 1
Hz of the standard deviation of log H/V is plotted station-by station in Figure 12. We observe that
the mean of the standard deviation is greatest in the center of the network, corresponding to stations
with the largest azimuthal range of source backazimuths. The stations that are collocated with
sources have the highest mean standard deviation of H/V. This effect is absent when the collocated
source is removed, suggesting that this is a simulation anomaly for near-source seismograms.

4. DISCUSSION

The 3-D waveforms produced by Parelasti (Figure 3) have many of the characteristics that we might
expect from seismograms recorded in the real earth, most notably the transfer of energy from first
arriving surface waves into the coda of the seismogram. This simulation is entirely elastic and
deterministic, so this “scattering” behavior is the result of numerous reflections, refractions, and
diffractions in and around the heterogeneous 3-D model, without the addition of stochastic velocity
variations. Additionally, we observe that the polarization direction of the incoming seismic energy,
particularly at larger offsets from the source (> 10 km) is chaotic and apparently uncorrelated with
the source direction. This behavior validates the GFM-based velocity model as a useful tool for
characterizing seismogram behavior in the real earth, where incoherent phases are an important
fraction of recorded seismic energy, and the structural imprint on seismograms increases with higher
frequencies (e.g. Sato et al., 2012).
Traditionally, horizontal seismograms are rotated into radial and transverse directions to a surface
wave source, with Love waves appearing only on the transverse component while Rayleigh waves
appear on the radial and vertical components (Stein & Wysession, 2009). Technically, the direction
of the Rayleigh wave arrival, which we are specifically interested in, may be in an offset orientation,
reflective of velocity inhomogeneity between the source and receiver. Rotation in this true
orientation would minimize the leakage of energy from Love waves into the radial component, and
Rayleigh waves into the transverse component. We hope to measure this true orientation by finding
the direction of largest polarization of the 3-component seismograms and call the measured value
the polarization backazimuth. However, we find that even at low frequencies (< 0.3 Hz), the
polarization direction at some distance ceases to correlate with the source direction and may be
more closely related to 3-D velocity structure or phase complexity. Thus, we hypothesize that for
stations where the true orientation might be rotated furthest from the geographic backazimuth, such
as for longer-offset stations, the polarization direction may not be an accurate reflection of the
Rayleigh wave arrival direction, and a polarization rotation may be no better than a random offset
from the geographic backazimuth. For stations which are closer to the source, (e.g. 313 and 152 for
source 1, Figure 6 and 7), the geographic and polarization average H/V agree within their standard
deviations, and a rotation may not necessarily be justified at the expense of treating near and far
stations differently (polarization vs geographic rotations). Based on these results, the potential
increase in accuracy from using a polarization rotation at our frequencies of interest may be offset by
the uncertainty inherent in the measured polarization direction. Therefore, we suggest that a
geographic rotation is sufficient to analyze H/V.
Importantly, we find that 1-D predictions of H/V and 3-D azimuthal average observations of H/V
agree closely for most stations (Figure 6, 7 and 10), showing that an appropriate sampling of source
paths averages out the 3-D complexity present in the model. The normalized difference of H/V is
zero mean and less than 1 for all frequencies, indicating that the average difference of H/V between
3-D and 1-D predictions is smaller than the standard deviation of 3-D H/V (Figure 11, panel C).
For the stations that overlie one layer (e.g. 313), the H/V ratio agrees with the analytic value for a
homogeneous half space Rayleigh wave, ~0.66, or -0.4 in log H/V (Stein & Wysession, 2009).
However, there are a few aspects that are not well captured or described by the 1-D model. One of
these aspects is the finite frequency sensitivity of the propagating waves to lateral heterogeneities,
observed in Figure 8, but not in Figure 9. In the central part of the array, the 3-D mean values for
H/V vary smoothly from station to station, even over abrupt transitions in surface or subsurface
velocity layers. In the context of inverting or fitting real, 3-D data, this result emphasizes the lateral

 27

averaging effect of finite-frequency H/V for wavelengths larger than anomalies or layer transitions
of interest, as was observed in Miller et al. (2025) for HVSR data at a nearby array.
Another aspect of distinction for our 3-D observations is the significant azimuthal standard
deviation of source-dependent H/V. This standard deviation is observed to be at a maximum when
the source and receiver are collocated – we ignore these stations for analysis because they are likely
influenced by the very-near-source behavior of Parelasti (Figure 12). For all other stations, standard
deviation increases toward the center of the seismic network. We hypothesize that this is a result of
greater azimuthal source coverage. For stations nearer to the network edge, azimuthal coverage is
compressed, and the number of unique source paths being average is decreased, leading to lesser
variability, but likely higher bias in H/V.
The standard deviation in H/V is a significant fraction of mean H/V (Figure 11, panels A and B),
between 0.5 and 1.8, for one standard deviation. These azimuth-dependent variations appear
randomly distributed in aggregate, suggesting they contribute only to noise, but the angle over which
they are correlated can be further explored. This will be a key question to answer for future work
measuring the azimuthal variability of H/V as an indicator of synthetic elastic anisotropy in a similar
GFM-based model. The current best practice for measuring azimuthal anisotropy involves averaging
measurements over finite azimuth bins (~20 degrees) to increase signal-to-noise ratios for coherent
H/V changes (Lin & Schmandt, 2014). Our results indicate that for 180° variations in isotropic H/V
(strike only), we do not see coherence over bins of that size, but a further densification of source
locations will more definitively constrain the coherence or incoherence of isotropic variability over
small backazimuth ranges. With an estimate of isotropic H/V variability, we can distinguish these
effects from those related to anisotropic H/V variability in a velocity model that includes anisotropy.
Effectively, we will be able to characterize our ability to recover anisotropy from realistic synthetic
data for the given region.

 28

5. CONCLUSION

To measure the theoretical isotropic Rayleigh wave H/V at Rock Valley, we compare predictions of
1-D and 3-D H/V produced from a 3-D velocity model based on a region-specific GFM. We
generate 1-D H/V data directly from 1-D profiles extracted from the 3-D model using benchmark
solutions from Computer Programs in Seismology. We process synthetic waveforms produced by a
finite difference solver of the elastic wave equation, Parelasti, and generate estimated H/V from the
simulated waveforms. We generate these waveforms for 10 sources collocated with stations at the
edge of the Rock Valley network, producing an azimuthal spread of source-dependent
measurements of H/V. We find that the 1-D and mean 3-D values for H/V agree within the
standard deviation of 3-D H/V, in aggregate, suggesting that path complexity is being sufficiently
averaged out with a set of azimuthally distinct sources. However, we find that the isotropic
azimuthal variability is a significant fraction of the mean 3-D H/V, implying that it may be a
significant source of noise that could inhibit accurate measurements of anisotropic azimuthal
variability of H/V. Knowing the isotropic variability, as characterized here, may be sufficient to
remove its effects and make an accurate anisotropic measurement with real and simulated data.
