Beyond exon 19 and 21: positioning lazertinib and the next generation of strategies for uncommon EGFR mutations
Authors: Takayuki Takahama
Published: 2026

The landscape of targeted therapy for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has evolved dramatically, yet the management of uncommon EGFR mutations (accounting for approximately 10–20% of EGFR-mutated cases) remains a persistent challenge compared to the classical exon 19 deletions and exon 21 L858R substitutions. While the efficacy of third-generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) is well-established in common mutations, their role in the heterogeneous landscape of uncommon mutations—comprising G719X, L861Q, S768I, and others—has largely relied on retrospective data or small phase II cohorts. In this context, the study by Park and colleagues published in the Journal of Thoracic Oncology provides crucial prospective evidence for lazertinib, a highly selective third-generation EGFR TKI, positioning it as a potent contender in this niche but clinically significant arena (1).

The current standard and the challenge of “uncommon” heterogeneity

To appreciate the significance of Park et al.’s findings, one must first recognize the established benchmarks. For years, afatinib (a second-generation TKI) has been the preferred first-line agent, supported by pooled analyses of the LUX-Lung trials which demonstrated a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 10.7 months (2). More recently, this position was solidified by the phase III ACHILLES (TORG1834) study, which confirmed the superiority of afatinib over standard platinum-doublet chemotherapy (mPFS 10.6 vs. 5.7 months) in treatment-naïve patients (3).

However, the clinical application of afatinib is often hampered by dose-limiting toxicities, such as severe diarrhea and rash, which can compromise quality of life. Furthermore, uncommon mutations are not a monolith; they exhibit varying sensitivities to TKI classes, and the risk of central nervous system (CNS) metastasis remains a concern where second-generation TKIs have historically shown limited penetration compared to third-generation agents.

Lazertinib: validating the third-generation approach

The phase II multicenter trial by Park et al. enrolled 36 patients with uncommon EGFR mutations (excluding exon 20 insertions) in a TKI-naïve setting. The primary endpoint of the study was the objective response rate (ORR). The cohort included patients with solitary major uncommon mutations as well as compound uncommon mutations (comprising 11.1% of patients). The trial successfully met its primary endpoint, reporting an impressive ORR of 50.0% and a promising mPFS of 10.8 months (1). Furthermore, reflecting the heterogeneous nature of uncommon EGFR mutations, efficacy varied significantly depending on the specific mutation subtypes. Patients with solitary G719X mutations exhibited an ORR of 57.1% and a notably prolonged mPFS of 20.3 months. Meaningful responses were also observed in S768I and L861Q subtypes (ORRs of 60% and 58%, respectively), while patients with compound uncommon mutations achieved a high ORR of 75.0% (1). These figures are strikingly consistent with the established benchmarks for afatinib (mPFS 10.7 months) and data regarding osimertinib from the KCSG-LU15-09 (mPFS 8.2 months) (4) and UNICORN (Japan) (mPFS 9.5 months) (5) studies (see Table 1).

Crucially, the study by Park et al. highlights the favorable toxicity profile of lazertinib. Unlike the high rates of grade 3 adverse events often seen with afatinib, lazertinib demonstrated a manageable safety profile consistent with other third-generation TKIs. This suggests that lazertinib could offer a “best of both worlds” scenario: efficacy comparable to the established standard (afatinib) but with the superior tolerability and CNS penetrance characteristic of third-generation agents.

Navigating the evolving landscape: monotherapy vs. combination

While the confirmation of lazertinib’s activity is a significant step forward, the therapeutic ceiling for monotherapy in uncommon mutations appears to hover around a PFS of 10–11 months. The field is now pivoting from asking “which TKI works?” to “how can we improve upon TKI monotherapy?”.

Recent data from the CHRYSALIS-2 study (Cohort C) has demonstrated that the combination of amivantamab and lazertinib achieves an ORR of 52% in atypical mutations (6), suggesting that dual targeting may deepen responses. This hypothesis is currently being tested in the WJOG17323L (AGEHA) study (jRCTs031250560) (7). This Japanese randomized phase II trial is directly comparing the combination of amivantamab and lazertinib against afatinib monotherapy. The design of AGEHA is particularly rigorous as it utilizes the verified standard of care (afatinib) as the control arm. If the AGEHA study demonstrates superiority, it could fundamentally shift the paradigm from TKI monotherapy to dual-targeted bispecific antibody combinations as the new first-line standard. Concurrently, the NEJ067 (OPAL2) study is investigating the combination of osimertinib with chemotherapy, offering another potential avenue to overcome primary resistance (8).

In addition, several novel EGFR inhibitors are currently under clinical investigation, including zipalertinib and sunvozertinib, which may further expand treatment options for patients with uncommon EGFR mutations (9,10).

Limitations and future directions

Despite the promising data from Park et al., important limitations persist in this field. First, the term “uncommon mutations” masks significant heterogeneity. In Park’s study, as in others, response rates varied numerically between subtypes (e.g., G719X vs. L861Q). The sample size of individual phase II trials is invariably too small to draw statistically robust conclusions for specific subtypes.

Second, the biological mechanisms of resistance in uncommon mutations treated with lazertinib remain poorly understood. While Park et al. reported re-emergence of L861Q and acquired TP53/RET/APC mutations in post-progression liquid biopsies, a deeper investigation into acquired resistance is needed to inform sequential therapy. Recent preclinical data further emphasize the heterogeneous nature of these mutations and the necessity of precision strategies. An in vitro study demonstrated that while third-generation TKIs exhibit varying sensitivities—being active against L861Q but generally inactive against S768I—lazertinib notably possesses the ability to overcome secondary resistance mutations, such as T790M and a novel T725M, which emerge after afatinib or osimertinib therapy (11). This suggests that lazertinib might not only serve as a promising front-line option but also as a tailored sequential therapy in specific acquired resistance pathways.

Conclusions

The study by Park et al. is a welcome addition to the literature, validating lazertinib as an effective and well-tolerated option for uncommon EGFR mutations. It reinforces the shift towards third-generation TKIs as a preferred backbone due to their therapeutic index. However, with the efficacy of monotherapy plateauing around 1 year, the field eagerly awaits the results of next-generation combination strategies like the AGEHA and NEJ067 trials. Until then, lazertinib represents a compelling therapeutic option, offering patients comparable efficacy to afatinib with a potentially superior quality of life.
