Compiling Deterministic Structure into SLM Harnesses
Zan-Kai Chong School of Science and Technology Kwansei Gakuin University Japan zankai@ieee.org Hiroyuki Ohsaki School of Science and Technology Kwansei Gakuin University Japan ohsaki@kwansei.ac.jp Bryan Ng School of Engineering & Computer Science Victoria University of Wellington New Zealand ckbryan@hotmail.com

Abstract
Enterprise deployment of small language models (SLMs) is constrained by epistemic asymmetry, i.e., small models lack the capacity to self-correct reasoning errors, while frontier LLMs face prohibitive costs and data sovereignty restrictions for high-volume processing. We propose Semantic Gradient Descent (SGDe), a teacher-student optimisation framework that compiles agentic workflows into discrete execution plans comprising directed acyclic graph topologies, system prompts, and deterministic executable code. The trailing e in SGDe distinguishes our discrete, compilation-based framework from stochastic gradient descent. Unlike continuous weight optimisation, SGDe operates in a discrete semantic space where a frontier teacher agent generates natural language critiques that function as directional gradients to iteratively refine the SLM’s workflow artefacts. We formalise SGDe within a Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning framework, establishing theoretical bounds on sample complexity that enable convergence with as few as three training examples on targeted synthetic tasks by leveraging the teacher as a statistical prior. Empirical evaluation on a challenging GSM-Hard-derived test set constructed via adversarial synthesis demonstrates that compiled workflows achieve 91.3% accuracy at a training sample size of m = 5 m=5 , and 99.3% at m = 3 m=3 within the small- m m regime motivated by Corollary 1, corresponding to a +26.3% to +34.3% absolute improvement over state-of-the-art prompt optimisers. In the terminology of the emerging paradigm of harness engineering, SGDe departs from prior workflow optimisation frameworks by treating the placement of deterministic executable code, i.e., which subtasks to delegate to a Python runtime and which to retain as LLM calls, as a trace-driven, per-node optimisation target. This generalises the static whole-problem offloading of program-aided approaches such as PAL and PoT. The teacher compiles two complementary deterministic structures into the harness: capability offloading, which delegates subtasks to deterministic Python when the SLM cannot execute them reliably, and structural consensus, which wraps variance-sensitive reasoning steps in fan-out/fan-in subgraphs aggregated by deterministic voting.



I
Introduction

Modern frontier large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-5, Claude,
and Gemini exhibit strong generalisation. They can interpret arbitrary
documents, reason across domains, and execute complex multi-step tasks
with minimal task-specific training. This has fuelled expectations
that enterprise artificial intelligence (AI) will likewise act as
a general-purpose reasoning engine, handling diverse real-world inputs
with consistent competence.

Yet, in practice not all production AI systems are deployed in such
use cases. Some enterprise systems are engineered for narrow, well-defined
use cases in predictable domains, not for open-ended, uncontrolled
scenarios. Organisations therefore use specialised models for specific
document types, such as ID cards, bank statements, or insurance claims,
rather than generic systems that can reliably interpret arbitrary
user uploads
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
.
This specialisation reflects an economic constraint and is reminiscent
of the advances in expert systems in the 1970s
[
4
]
.
Although frontier models are broadly capable, their deployment in
enterprise environments is often impractical due to prohibitive inference
costs, stringent regulatory compliance requirements, and data sovereignty
restrictions surrounding sensitive enterprise data.

On the other hand, small language models (SLMs) are typically characterised
by their heavily reduced parameter counts and optimised for task-specific
deployment. They are often preferable to costly advanced LLMs in specialised
domains, as SLMs can achieve comparable performance at a fraction
of the computational cost. Realising these gains, however, requires
addressing epistemic asymmetry, i.e., when a student agent must both
perform a task and judge its own reasoning, it struggles to reliably
detect hallucinations and other errors in its own outputs
[
29
,
8
]
.
Additionally, current distillation techniques, which typically train
a smaller student agent to replicate the static outputs or probability
distributions of a larger teacher model, generally only focus on transferring
knowledge through output mimicking or fine-tuning. These approaches
pay little attention to the structural compilation of workflows (i.e.,
how the orchestration of prompts, tool chains, and control-flow topology
shapes execution), which could yield additional performance gains.

Recent industry discourse has coalesced around the concept of harness
engineering, the discipline of designing the complete non-model infrastructure
(the “harness”) surrounding an LLM or SLM to make agentic behaviour
reliable, observable, and production-ready
[
13
,
20
]
.
In this framing, an agent is simply a combination of model and harness.
The present work contributes a formal, PAC-bounded approach to harness
engineering: Semantic Gradient Descent (SGDe) automatically compiles
and iteratively refines a fully executable harness
\theta=\left\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right\}

offline, eliminating epistemic asymmetry while preserving the low
inference cost and data-sovereignty advantages of SLMs. This harness-level
formulation generalises program-aided reasoning, which fixes the offload/retain
decision as a global prompting convention
[
7
,
6
]
,
by lifting that decision to a per-node, trace-driven optimisation
target. Here
\mathcal{G}
denotes the directed acyclic graph (DAG)
topology,
\mathcal{P}
the set of specialised system prompts, and

\mathcal{C}
the set of deterministic executable code.

Nevertheless, this pipeline lacks a numerical loss function with which
to optimise this structural orchestration. Both prompts and the workflow
topology are semantic and therefore non-differentiable. To address
these limitations, SGDe translates the continuous parameter updates
of standard machine learning into a discrete, text-and-code-based
architecture for compiled agentic workflows. Our approach is grounded
in a strict teacher-student paradigm that consists of a lightweight
SLM functioning as a compiled execution engine operating within rigidly
defined workflow boundaries, together with a frontier LLM teacher
that analyses execution traces, attributes errors, and synthesises
optimised workflow artefacts. This decoupling reduces inference cost
and eliminates epistemic asymmetry by ensuring that evaluation and
optimisation occur independently of runtime inference. Consequently,
the student maximises performance and minimises cost while the teacher
ensures correctness and efficiency.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we introduce
substrate compilation as a new unit of optimisation for agentic workflows.
The teacher selects on a per-node basis among three co-equal compilation
actions, i.e., prompt refinement, capability offloading (rewriting
a node as deterministic code), and structural consensus (wrapping
a node in a fan-out/fan-in ensemble with deterministic aggregation)
— adaptively in response to per-node failure attribution, directly
addressing the capability and reliability gaps that motivate SLM deployment.
Second, we derive a resource-bounded PAC analysis in which the effective
hypothesis space
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
is recast as the set of feasible
per-subtask compilation choices (code, single LLM call, or fan-out/fan-in
consensus), yielding the bound
\left|\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}\right|=\text{O}\left(3^{k}\right)

in the number of distinct subtask types
k
. This bound is mechanistic
rather than merely asymptotic: it explains both why SGDe converges
with as few as three training examples on structurally homogeneous
tasks and why it degrades predictably when the target domain spans
multiple divergent task families. Third, we empirically validate SGDe
on a challenging GSM-Hard-derived test set constructed via adversarial
synthesis from a single seed question, achieving 91.3% accuracy at
a training sample size of
m=5
and 99.3% at
m=3
, corresponding
to a +26.3% to +34.3% absolute improvement over the strongest intra-substrate
baseline (DSPy), while requiring only commercially viable training
sets.

II
Related Work

This section reviews the theoretical foundations of our framework
across knowledge distillation, agentic fine-tuning, discrete prompt
optimisation, and workflow topologies. We highlight a critical, unresolved
gap in the literature: while existing methods effectively optimise
continuous model weights or isolated text strings, they lack mechanisms
to dynamically optimise the discrete, structural orchestration of
compiled agentic workflows.

II-A

Teacher-Student Distillation for Small Language Models (SLMs)

Recent advances have produced a clear bifurcation between massive
frontier models and efficient SLMs (sub-billion to ~10
billion parameters) engineered to address prohibitive inference costs,
latency, and data governance constraints
[
11
,
21
,
14
,
10
]
.
To bridge the performance gap, the literature relies heavily on the
teacher-student paradigm for knowledge distillation
[
1
]
.
A highly parameterised teacher generates soft targets or reasoning
trajectories; a smaller student is trained to minimise statistical
divergence via differentiable losses such as Kullback-Leibler divergence.

While effective for transferring static, domain-specific knowledge,
this parameter-centric architecture exhibits critical limitations
when applied to agentic workflows: it updates continuous weights via
numerical loss functions but lacks the mechanisms to optimise the
discrete, semantic structures of prompt orchestration or control-flow
topologies. It also exacerbates epistemic asymmetry, as SLMs with
reduced parameter capacities frequently lack the internal capability
to judge their own reasoning trajectories or reliably detect hallucinations

[
29
,
8
]
.
Recent extensions to trajectory-based agentic distillation
[
27
,
5
]

fine-tune the student on the teacher’s action sequences, but remain
dependent on continuous parameter updates and consequently mask rather
than resolve the asymmetry
[
12
]
.

II-B

Discrete Prompt Optimisation

As an alternative to weight updates, recent frameworks treat large
language models as discrete optimisers. Approaches such as Optimisation
by PROmpting (OPRO) and DSPy formalise natural-language instructions
as tunable hyperparameters
[
25
,
9
]
.
A powerful LLM evaluates task outputs, generates prompt variations,
and iteratively selects those that maximise a predefined metric.

These methods successfully translate gradient-based search into discrete
semantic space. However, they are fundamentally limited to optimising
text alone, assuming the underlying execution architecture (tool chains
and operation sequence) is already optimal. In complex enterprise
settings, the bottleneck is frequently not the prompt but the structural
orchestration of the workflow itself. Current prompt optimisers cannot
alter programmatic control flow or adjust structural granularity,
leaving architectural failures unresolved.

II-C

Program-Aided Reasoning and the Runtime Boundary

A complementary line of work predates the workflow-optimisation literature
and targets the same capability gap that motivates capability offloading,
i.e., the observation that LLMs reliably decompose numerical problems
but unreliably execute the arithmetic. PAL
[
7
]
and
Program-of-Thoughts (PoT)
[
6
]
both exploit this observation
by prompting the LLM to emit a Python program whose execution, rather
than the LLM’s own token generation, produces the final answer, establishing
program-aided reasoning as a static, whole-problem prompting pattern
applied uniformly at inference time. SGDe lifts this offload decision
from a fixed template to a per-node, trace-driven optimisation target;
Section
III-D
presents the detailed
comparison.

II-D

Agentic Workflow Topologies and Their Semantic Optimisation Frameworks

As LLM applications have evolved from linear prompts to multi-step
pipelines, graph-based topologies such as AutoGen and Graph of Thoughts
have become prevalent
[
2
,
24
]
.
These enable sophisticated decomposition but remain static: the DAG
is manually engineered prior to deployment and cannot adapt to the
cognitive or context-window limits of the underlying SLM.

Recent work has begun to treat these topologies as optimisable objects.
We organise existing approaches along a single axis that we argue
is decisive for SLM deployment: the unit of optimisation, i.e., what
the framework is permitted to change about the workflow. Prior frameworks
fall into two categories, both of which optimise within a fixed model-call
substrate; we term these intra-substrate optimisers.

The first category optimises textual or prompt-level variables within
a fixed computational graph. TextGrad
[
26
]

introduced textual gradients that back-propagate through such a graph,
while Wang et al.
[
23
]

formalised semantic backpropagation by propagating sibling-node context
to generate more accurate semantic gradients. APO/ProTeGi
[
16
]

and DSPy
[
9
]
similarly
refine prompts and few-shot demonstrations against a fixed pipeline
structure. In all of these, every node remains an LLM call before
and after optimisation; only the instructions injected at those nodes
change.

The second category treats the workflow topology itself as mutable.
AFlow
[
28
]
is the most prominent example, employing
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) over code-represented workflows to
discover effective DAG structures. Although AFlow represents workflows
as code, the nodes it composes remain model calls: the optimiser rewires
how LLM invocations are connected, but does not reassign work away
from the LLM. The substrate, the set of computations the LLM is responsible
for, is held fixed.

SGDe expands the unit of optimisation to the substrate itself. We
term this a substrate compiler: at every node
v\in V
, the teacher
may retain
v
as an LLM call, delegate it to deterministic Python
code
c\in\mathcal{C}
(capability offloading, Section
III-D
),
or wrap it in a fan-out/fan-in consensus subgraph (structural consensus,
Section
III-F2
). This distinction is load-bearing
for SLM deployment specifically: for frontier LLMs, the gap between
a prompt and a Python function is narrow and intra-substrate optimisation
captures most available gains, but for SLMs the same gap is precisely
where deployment fails. Substrate compilation is composable with intra-substrate
methods, i.e., AFlow-style search could decide node connectivity while
SGDe decides node type; we therefore evaluate SGDe against the strongest
intra-substrate baseline (DSPy) to isolate its contribution.

III
Semantic Gradient Descent (SGDe) Architecture

To enable practical on-premise deployment of agentic workflows, we
propose the semantic gradient descent (SGDe) framework, a teacher-student
learning approach structured analogously to a standard machine learning
paradigm. While traditional supervised training relies on differentiable
loss functions to update continuous numerical weights, SGDe mirrors
this training cycle in a discrete semantic space. In each iteration,
an advanced teacher agent acts as an optimiser, systematically refining
the discrete workflow artefacts (system prompts, DAG topologies, and
executable code) to guide a lightweight student agent toward optimal
task execution.

III-A

Running Example

To anchor the framework in a concrete task and reduce the cognitive
load of introducing the architectural components in parallel, we use
a single GSM-Hard task as a running example throughout this section,
and revisit it in Section
V-C
and Appendix

:

For his birthday, Jamal received a cash gift sufficient to buy
one 55-dollar video game and one 25-dollar controller with 10 dollars
left over. How many dollars did Jamal receive?
(gold answer: 90).

Solving this requires parsing the item prices and the leftover amount,
then applying the rule “amount received = total cost + leftover” (
90=55+25+10
).
The narrative is mildly inverted relative to the more common “change
received” framing: Jamal
receives
the gift rather than paying,
a property we exploit in Section
V-C

to differentiate the
m=3
and
m=5
compiled harnesses.

At
t=0
, the workflow is a single monolithic chain-of-thought node
that reads the question, reasons through cost and leftover, and emits
the final number. The 1.5B-parameter student frequently parses the
operands correctly but fails at arithmetic, prompting the
t=1
update,
namely the capability-offloading refinement of Section
III-D

(Figure

), which decomposes the monolithic node
into an LLM extraction node followed by a deterministic Python evaluation
node. Subsequent iterations may further wrap the extraction step in
a structural-consensus subgraph (Section
III-F2
,
Figure

) when the teacher detects high
variance. The fully compiled harness produced after termination is
the object evaluated in Section
V-C
.

III-B

Architecture Components

The core principle of the proposed framework is the strict separation
of execution and optimisation. We implement this through a teacher-student
machine learning architecture consisting of the following components:

•

Tasks: The tasks are assumed to be from the same domain. Under the
typical machine learning paradigm, the tasks will be divided into
training tasks and the test tasks.

•

The Student Agent (Trained Model), denoted as
\mathcal{S}
: A lightweight
SLM that serves strictly as a compiled execution engine for the compiled
workflow. It operates within the rigid boundaries of the provided
instructions and remains conceptually blind to the overarching optimisation
process.

•

The Teacher Agent (Optimiser), denoted as
\mathcal{T}
: A frontier,
high-capacity LLM tasked exclusively with offline workflow optimisation.
The teacher does not execute the daily high-volume tasks. Instead,
it observes the student’s execution logs, identifies structural or
logical failures, and rewrites the underlying workflow artefacts to
permanently resolve errors.

III-C

Two Levels of Optimisation: Granularity and Compilation

SGDe’s optimiser operates at two distinct levels of abstraction. At
the granularity level, the teacher reshapes the task partition via
node decomposition and node fusion (Section
III-F1
).
These operations do not alter any node’s substrate; they determine
the subtask boundaries on which compilation subsequently acts. At
the compilation level, once the partition is settled, the teacher
selects at each node
v\in V
among three substrate choices: (i)
prompt refinement, retaining
v
as an LLM call under a revised prompt

p\in\mathcal{P}
; (ii) capability offloading (Section
III-D
),
re-typing
v
as a deterministic code node
c\in\mathcal{C}
; and
(iii) structural consensus (Section
III-F2
),
replicating
v
with perturbed prompts and aggregating via a deterministic
voting node. The three compilation actions are mutually exclusive
at a given node and collectively exhaustive; they address orthogonal
failure modes, namely instruction quality, capability gap, and output
variance, respectively. Granularity operations are orthogonal to this
ternary and may be applied in combination with any compilation action,
with the per-node choice driven by the teacher’s semantic gradient

g_{\text{sem}}
rather than fixed in advance. This two-level, adaptive,
per-node unification distinguishes SGDe from prior work in which each
action is applied in isolation or globally.

III-D

The Capability Offloading Principle

The observation that arithmetic and other deterministic sub-computations
are better delegated to a Python interpreter than to an LLM’s token
generator is not itself new: PAL
[
7
]
and PoT
[
6
]

established this principle as a static prompting pattern, applied
uniformly to every instance at inference time. What distinguishes
SGDe is that the offload decision is lifted from a fixed template
to a per-node, trace-driven optimisation target. At every node
v\in V
,
the teacher evaluates, on the basis of empirical failure attribution
over a training set, whether the computation assigned to
v
is reliably
executable by deterministic code, and if so, rewrites
v
from an
LLM call into a code node
c\in\mathcal{C}
. We refer to this trace-driven,
per-node selection rule as the capability offloading principle to
distinguish it from the static, whole-problem offloading pattern of
PAL and PoT. Under this view, the execution plan
\theta=\left\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right\}

is not merely a workflow but a partition of the task between the probabilistic
student and the deterministic runtime, and it is this partition, rather
than the prompts or the topology in isolation that SGDe optimises.

capability offloading and structural consensus that jointly produce

(Section

are present in the winning harnesses.

III-E

The Discrete Parameter Space

Instead of continuous numerical weights, the student’s operational
logic is defined by a discrete execution plan (denoted by
\theta
).
Orchestrated via graph-based workflow engines, this plan is fully
interpretable and comprises three distinct artefacts:

•

Workflow Topology (
\mathcal{G}
): A DAG that dictates routing logic,
conditional branching, and the sequence of operations.

•

System Prompts (
\mathcal{P}
): A set of natural language instructions
injected at specific nodes within the DAG to guide the student’s generation,
extraction, or reasoning capabilities.

•

Executable Code (
\mathcal{C}
): A set of deterministic scripts and
tool configurations utilised for rigid data manipulation, external
API integration, or programmatic formatting (e.g., regular expression-based
extraction).

III-F

Dynamic Granularity and Structural Refinement

The semantic gradient
g_{\text{sem}}
directs the teacher agent
to perform discrete structural refinement on the execution plan
\theta_{t}

(where
t
denotes the discrete time epoch where the refinement occurs).
Unlike continuous weight updates, this refinement operates via the
following complementary mechanisms: granularity adjustment and structural
consensus.

III-F
1
Granularity Adjustment via Cognitive Load Balancing

Whereas capability offloading (Section
III-D
)
rewrites a node across the substrate boundary by replacing an LLM
call with deterministic code, granularity adjustment operates within
the LLM substrate: it resizes the scope of nodes that remain LLM calls,
without changing their type. The two mechanisms are complementary
and may be applied in combination at the same node.

At time epoch
t
, the teacher analyses execution traces denoted
by
\text{Tr}_{t}
to identify cognitive bottlenecks, i.e., specific
nodes
v
where the student agent exhibits reasoning failures attributable
to context bloat or lost-in-the-middle attention degradation.

•

Node Decomposition: If the semantic gradient fails due to excessive
reasoning complexity within a node
v\in V_{t+1}
, the teacher constructs
an expanded topology
\mathcal{G}_{t+1}=\left(V_{t+1},E_{t+1}\right)

by decomposing
v
into a chain of sub-nodes
\left\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{k}\right\}\in V_{t+1}
.
Each sub-node
v_{i}
assumes a partition of the original scope such
that

\bigcup^{k}_{i=1}\text{scope}\left(v_{i}\right)=\text{scope}\left(v\right),

(1)

where
\text{scope}\left(\cdot\right)
denotes semantic domain (sub-task
responsibility and input-output coverage). The edge set is updated
to
E_{t+1}
by redirecting all incoming edges of
v
to
v_{1}
,
all outgoing edges of
v
from
v_{k}
, and enforcing sequential
dependencies
v_{1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow v_{k}
. Each sub-node
receives a specialised prompt
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+1}
targeting
a narrower sub-task.

•

Node Fusion: Conversely, if consecutive nodes
v_{i}
,
v_{i+1}

exhibit redundant inference without accuracy gains, the nodes are
merged to minimise latency. This is governed by the heuristic: apply
the merge operation

\text{fuse}(v_{i},v_{i+1}),

(2)

which collapses the two nodes into one, updates the edge set
E_{t}
,
and synthesises a single specialised prompt, if

\hat{R}\left(\theta_{\text{merge}}\right)-\hat{R}(\theta_{t})<\epsilon_{\text{cost}},

(3)

and the node-level accuracies on the execution trace are statistically
indistinguishable, i.e.,

\text{acc}\left(\text{Tr}_{t},v_{i}\right)\approx\text{acc}\left(\text{Tr}_{t},v_{i+1}\right).

(4)

Granularity operations sit at a different level of abstraction from
the three compilation actions of Section
III-C

decomposition and fusion determine the subtask partition, whereas
the compilation actions determine how each subtask in that partition

in Figure

v_{\text{parse}}
and an evaluation
sub-node

III-F
3
Heuristic Update Synthesis

The complete update rule combines these elements. The teacher agent

\mathcal{T}_{\text{optimiser}}
generates a candidate plan
\theta_{t+1}

by conditioning on the master prompt
\Phi
, the semantic gradient

g_{\text{sem}}
, and the current plan
\theta_{t}
, and emitting
a structurally valid successor, i.e.,
\theta_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}_{\text{optimiser}}\left(\Phi,g_{\text{sem}},\theta_{t}\right)

subject to
\text{valid}\left(\theta_{t+1}\right)
, where

enforces syntactic correctness (acyclicity of
\mathcal{G}
, type
consistency in
\mathcal{C}
). This discrete, teacher-induced transition
replaces the continuous parameter update
\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla\hat{R}

found in stochastic gradient descent (SGD), from which SGDe is deliberately
distinguished: rather than moving along a gradient direction in a
metric space, the optimiser samples a single successor from the teacher’s
induced distribution over valid plans and accepts it by greedy hill-climbing
(Section
IV-C3
).

III-G

Iterative Optimisation Lifecycle

The framework operates in a continuous lifecycle of execution, evaluation,
and semantic updating to minimise the empirical risk (
\hat{R}
)
over a training set of tasks, formalised in Algorithm
1
.

III-G
1
Phase 1: Constrained Execution (The Forward Pass)

The lifecycle begins by deploying the student agent to process the
training set. As the student traverses the DAG, the orchestrator generates
a comprehensive execution trace logging all inputs, intermediate reasoning
steps, tool outputs, and the final response. The system then calculates
an empirical risk score, which functions as the semantic loss function
of the architecture, evaluating both task accuracy and inference efficiency.

III-G
2
Phase 2: Semantic Error Attribution (The Backward Pass)

If the empirical risk exceeds an acceptable threshold, the teacher
agent initiates the optimisation phase. Because the workflow artefacts
are discrete, standard mathematical gradient calculation is undefined.
Instead, the teacher analyses the execution trace to perform semantic
error attribution, generating a natural language critique (
g_{\text{sem}}
)
that isolates the exact locus of failure.

III-G
3
Phase 3: Dynamic Granularity Adjustment

A key feature of the teacher’s heuristic update is adjusting the structural
granularity of the workflow topology (
\mathcal{G}
). If the teacher’s
critique identifies that the student SLM failed due to context bloat
or lost-in-the-middle attention degradation, the teacher dynamically
increases the granularity. It decomposes a single, complex reasoning
node into multiple, smaller sequential nodes (see Section
III-F1
).
Conversely, if redundant nodes inflate inference costs without improving
accuracy, the teacher merges them. This dynamic granularity ensures
the workflow is calibrated to the specific cognitive limits of the
chosen student SLM.

Note that the teacher generates a candidate plan
\tilde{\theta}
.
If
\hat{R}\left(\tilde{\theta}\right)<\hat{R}_{\text{current}}
,
the algorithm accepts the update, otherwise it retains the existing
best candidate plan,
\theta_{\text{best}}
.

IV
Mathematical Model

In conventional supervised learning the loss is a number and the gradient
is a vector pointing toward lower loss. In SGDe the gradient is not
a vector at all. The
semantic loss
still reduces to a scalar
(an aggregate of task accuracy and workflow cost), but only as the
trigger that decides whether to update. The corrective signal itself
is the
semantic gradient
: the teacher’s natural-language verdict
on what went wrong with the student’s execution trace, repurposed
as a directional signal that tells the optimiser which workflow artefact
to rewrite. The forward pass therefore augments “compute a number”
with “collect an execution trace and a teacher verdict”, and the backward
pass replaces “multiply gradients” with “rewrite a discrete artefact
in response to the critique”.

The artefacts being rewritten are the discrete components of a compiled
agentic workflow: the DAG topology, the system prompts injected at
each node, and the callable tool functions. The remainder of this
section formalises this parameterisation and the optimisation procedure
that operates on it.

IV-A

Model Parameterisation

Our SGDe framework mirrors the standard machine learning paradigm,
with a discrete parameter space distributed between the executable
artefacts deployed by the student agent (acting as the trained model)
and the contextual directives utilised by the teacher agent (acting
as the optimiser). The execution plan at discrete training iteration

t
, denoted
\theta_{t}
, is the tuple

\theta_{t}=\left\{\mathcal{G}_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{C}_{t}\right\},

(6)

where
\mathcal{G}_{t}=\left(V,E\right)
is the directed acyclic
graph (DAG) defining the topology,
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is the set of
system prompts injected at specific nodes
v\in V
, and
\mathcal{C}_{t}

represents the set of callable tool functions, implemented as deterministic
Python scripts or API endpoints. The teacher agent
\mathcal{T}

is additionally guided by a master prompt
\Phi
that establishes
the immutable optimisation directives and structural constraints governing
its behaviour.

IV-B

Forward Pass and Empirical Risk

To satisfy PAC learning requirements, optimisation is not performed
on a single task instance, but rather over a batch of tasks. Let
\mathcal{D}

be the real-world joint distribution of tasks and their ground-truth
answers within the target domain. We draw a finite training set of

m
paired samples,
\mathcal{Z}=\left\{\left(\tau_{1},y^{*}_{1}\right),\cdots,\left(\tau_{m},y^{*}_{m}\right)\right\}\sim\mathcal{D}
.

During the forward pass, the student agent executes the training set

\mathcal{Z}
, completely constrained by the execution plan
\theta_{t}
.
For each task
\tau_{i}
, the student at epoch
t
emits a prediction

\hat{y}_{t,i}=\mathcal{S}\left(\tau_{i};\theta_{t}\right)
along
with an execution trace
\text{Tr}_{t,i}
that logs all intermediate
reasoning steps, tool outputs, and final responses. We define a composite
semantic loss function
\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{y},y^{*},\text{Tr}\right)

that evaluates the execution based on both task accuracy (by comparing

\hat{y}
against
y^{*}
) and workflow inference cost (derived
from
Tr
).

In standard machine learning, the ultimate goal is to minimise the
true generalisation error
R\left(\theta_{t}\right)
, which is the
expected loss over the entire task distribution. However, because
the true distribution is unknown, we calculate the empirical risk

\hat{R}\left(\theta_{t}\right)
, representing the average loss over
our finite training set. This formulation is defined as:

R\left(\theta_{t}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(\tau,y^{*}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\tau;\theta_{t}\right),y^{*},\text{Tr}\left(\tau;\theta_{t}\right)\right)\right],

(7)

and

\hat{R}\left(\theta_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{i=1}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{S}\left(\tau_{i};\theta_{t}\right),y^{*}_{i},\text{Tr}_{t,i}\right).

(8)

This empirical risk score
\hat{R}\left(\theta_{t}\right)
serves
as the objective metric. If it exceeds an acceptable error threshold,
it triggers the backward pass, prompting the teacher agent to evaluate
the execution traces and formulate a semantic update.

IV-C

Semantic Error Attribution (The Backward Pass)

Because the parameter space of the execution plan
\theta_{t}
is
entirely discrete, calculating a standard numerical gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\hat{R}

is mathematically undefined. To overcome this, our framework replaces
the continuous backpropagation with an LLM-driven heuristic update
aimed at minimising the empirical risk
\hat{R}\left(\theta_{t}\right)
.

We therefore introduce a two-role teacher: the critic
\mathcal{T}_{\text{critic}}

produces the semantic gradient
g_{\text{sem}}
(a natural-language
critique that functions as a discrete directional signal), while the
separate optimiser
\mathcal{T}_{\text{optimiser}}
consumes this
signal and emits a candidate successor plan via
\theta_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}_{\text{optimiser}}(\Phi,g_{\text{sem}},\theta_{t})
.
This decomposition mirrors the classical two-stage structure of gradient-based
optimisation, namely (i) compute a directional signal, followed by
(ii) use it to propose a feasible update; the analogy is structural
rather than metric: no norm on execution plans is defined, and the
optimiser’s output is a discrete candidate rather than the metric
projection of a continuous step. Convergence relies on greedy acceptance
against
\hat{R}
(Algorithm
1
),
not on any contraction property of a projection operator.

IV-C
1
Fault Localisation (Semantic Gradient Formulation)

When the empirical risk exceeds the acceptable threshold, the teacher
agent initiates the backward pass by evaluating the execution traces
(
\text{Tr}_{t}
) of the training set. Acting as a diagnostic critic,
it performs semantic error attribution to generate a natural language
critique
g_{\text{sem}}
. This critique, which is the semantic gradient,
functions as a discrete directional gradient, isolating the exact
locus of failure, such as identifying a specific node
v\in V
within
the workflow topology
\mathcal{G}_{t}
that is causing systemic
data extraction errors. The semantic gradient is formulated as:

g_{\text{sem}}=\mathcal{T}_{\text{critic}}\left(\Phi,\text{Tr}_{t},\hat{R}\left(\theta_{t}\right)\right).

(9)

IV-C
2
Candidate Generation (The Optimiser Step)

Once the semantic gradient is formulated, the teacher transitions
into its optimiser role and synthesises a candidate plan for the next
iteration. We deliberately describe this as candidate generation rather
than projection: the optimiser does not minimise a distance to
g_{\text{sem}}

over
\Theta_{L}
under any defined metric; it samples a single plausible
successor conditioned on the critique. Acceptance is handled separately
by the hill-climbing rule of Section
IV-C3
.
The candidate plan is given by

\theta_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}_{\text{optimiser}}\left(\Phi,g_{\text{sem}},\theta_{t}\right).

(10)

IV-C
3
Greedy Hill-Climbing and Extension to Tabu Constraints

A critical vulnerability in LLM-driven autonomous optimisation is
the propensity for infinite loops, wherein the agent continuously
cycles through previously evaluated failure states. The optimisation
procedure employs greedy hill-climbing: the system maintains only
the best-performing execution plan
\theta_{\text{best}}
and accepts
a candidate
\tilde{\theta}
only if
\hat{R}(\tilde{\theta})<\hat{R}(\theta_{\text{best}})
.

Empirically, the teacher’s strong prior
P(\theta)
ensures rapid
convergence without revisiting failed configurations. Formally, however,
greedy selection lacks guarantees against cyclic oscillation. The
framework readily extends to Tabu-constrained search by maintaining
a history buffer
\mathcal{H}_{t}
of previously evaluated plans.
By injecting the master prompt
\Phi
with explicit instructions
to never generate an execution plan identical to any configuration
stored in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, we enforce the probabilistic constraint

\Pr\left(\theta_{t+1}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)\approx 0
. This would
provide bounds against cycling when optimising with weaker teacher
models or noisier semantic gradients.

We note that the absence of a projection operator in the formal sense
is not a gap in the analysis. The convergence argument of the following
subsection rests on three properties: finiteness of the bounded hypothesis
space
\Theta_{L}
, monotone greedy acceptance of candidate plans,
and the teacher’s prior concentration on the realisable subspace
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
.
None of these requires a metric on execution plans, so no projection
operator need be defined for the bound to hold.

IV-D

Theoretical Guarantees: Resource-Bounded PAC Learning

Because the space of all possible Python scripts and prompts is infinite,
we establish theoretical convergence guarantees by modelling the teacher’s
optimisation loop within a bounded PAC learning framework.

IV-D
1
Assumption 1 - Bounded Turing Computability and Realisability

We assume the target objective is Turing-computable and can be expressed
by an optimal execution plan
\theta^{*}
within a maximum description
length
L_{\text{max}}
. This length is measured in context tokens
and maximum DAG depth, which effectively restricts our search space
to a finite hypothesis class
\Theta_{L}
. Furthermore, we assume
realisability, i.e., there exists at least one
\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{L}

such that the empirical risk
\hat{R}
over the training set evaluates
to zero, denoted as
\hat{R}\left(\theta^{*}\right)=0
.

Concretely, we take
\Theta_{L}
to be the set of valid execution
plans
\theta=\left\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right\}

of bounded description length, where each node
v\in V
is labelled
either as an LLM call (governed by some prompt
p\in\mathcal{P}
)
or as a deterministic code call (governed by some script
c\in\mathcal{C}
).
Each
\theta\in\Theta_{L}
therefore induces a partition of the task
into sub-computations assigned to the probabilistic student or to
the deterministic runtime, and
\Theta_{L}
is the space of all such
bounded partitions together with their connecting topology.

The topology
\mathcal{G}
may additionally contain fan-out/fan-in
consensus subgraphs (Section
III-F2
), in which
multiple LLM nodes redundantly execute the same sub-computation before
a deterministic voting node aggregates their outputs. Such subgraphs
are expressed through
\mathcal{G}
together with the two node labels
above and do not introduce additional node types, so
\Theta_{L}

remains the same space of bounded execution plans.

IV-D
2
Lemma 1 - Occam’s Razor Bound for Finite Hypothesis Classes

We restate the classical realisable-case Occam bound
[
3
,
17
]

for reference, as it underpins the sample-complexity analysis that
follows. Under Assumption
IV-D1
, the probability
of the teacher finding a plan that perfectly fits the training set
but fails in production (
R\left(\theta\right)>\epsilon
) is strictly
bounded. To ensure with confidence
1-\delta
that the optimised
plan has a true generalisation error
R\left(\theta\right)\leq\epsilon
,
the required sample size
m
satisfies

m\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\ln|\Theta_{L}|+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

(11)

This bound is standard; its utility for our framework depends entirely
on obtaining a tight characterisation of the effective hypothesis
space
|\Theta_{L}|
. Corollary
IV-D3
establishes
such a characterisation under the capability offloading principle.

IV-D
3
Corollary 1 - The LLM Prior as an Offloading Feasibility Map

The contribution of this subsection is a mechanistic characterisation
of the effective hypothesis space
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
searched
by the teacher under the capability offloading principle, which when
substituted into Lemma
IV-D2
yields the sample-complexity
bound observed empirically in Section
V-C
.

Although
\left|\Theta_{L}\right|
is astronomically large due to
the combinatorial space of executable code and natural-language prompts,
the frontier teacher
\mathcal{T}
does not search
\Theta_{L}

uniformly. Under the capability offloading principle (Section
III-D
),
the teacher’s pre-training knowledge functions specifically as a learned
map from subtask descriptions to offloading feasibility: for each
candidate node
v
, the teacher estimates whether
v
is reliably
executable by deterministic code and selects the partition accordingly.

This reframes the effective hypothesis space
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}

not as an arbitrary subset of
\Theta_{L}
, but as the set of feasible
partitions of the task graph, i.e., those in which every node assigned
to the deterministic runtime is in fact code-executable, and every
node assigned to the student is within its capability envelope. The
cardinality of
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
is therefore not bounded by
the size of the prompt or code space, but by the number of distinct
subtask types present in the target task family. Formally, if a task
domain admits
k
distinct subtask types (e.g., for GSM-Hard-style
problems: operand extraction, arithmetic evaluation, unit conversion,
final formatting), then
\left|\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}\right|=\text{O}\left(3^{k}\right)

rather than
\text{O}(|\text{prompt space}|\times|\text{code space}|\times|\text{topology space}|)
,
since the teacher’s compilation decision at each subtask type is essentially
ternary: delegate to the deterministic runtime, retain as a single
LLM call, or wrap in a fan-out/fan-in consensus subgraph. Here
k

denotes the number of distinct subtask types in the partition induced
by the granularity operations of Section
III-F1
;
granularity operations reshape this partition but do not enlarge the
per-subtask choice set, so the bound
\left|\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}\right|=\text{O}\left(3^{k}\right)

is unchanged by their inclusion.

Substituting into Lemma 1, the sample complexity becomes

m\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(k\cdot\ln 3+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right),

(12)

which is linear in the number of subtask types and independent of
the raw size of the prompt or code spaces. For a structurally homogeneous
task family such as the GSM-Hard-derived test set used in Section

V
,
k
is small (on the order of three
to five). The substantive content of the bound is therefore that sample
complexity is linear in
k
rather than logarithmic in the raw prompt
or code spaces; the bound is a sufficient condition, not a tight prediction.
At standard tolerances (
\epsilon=0.1
,
\delta=0.1
) it evaluates
to
m\gtrsim 11k+23
, so the empirical convergence observed at
m=3

is faster than the worst-case guarantee. We attribute this gap to
the teacher’s prior concentration on
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
; the
observed
m=3
behaviour is consistent with this prior rather than
formally predicted by the bound.

V-D

Computational Cost

The teacher is invoked offline only. Training is one-time per compiled
harness and bounded by the user’s chosen accuracy threshold; the saved
harness reported in Appendix
A
required
a single successful teacher call before terminating at full batch
accuracy on the seed problem, with a configured upper bound of nine.
At inference, the harness in our experiments executes three student
calls per query (three solver nodes feeding a deterministic majority-vote
node) and zero frontier-API calls. The three solvers run as one parallel
DAG layer, so the consensus fan-out incurs minimal additional wall-clock
latency. The student calls are served by the on-premise GPU, whose
cost is amortised over query volume; the structurally important property
is that the deployed harness is frontier-free at runtime, regardless
of query volume.

VI
Discussion and Future Work

VI-A

Boundary Conditions: Task Homogeneity and Topological Overfitting

While SGDe demonstrates strong sample efficiency for narrowly scoped
tasks, we now empirically validate the boundary conditions of Assumption

IV-D1
by testing the realisability assumption
under structural heterogeneity. Recall that our PAC bounds assume
the existence of an optimal execution plan
\theta^{*}
within a
coherent hypothesis space
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
on which the teacher’s
prior concentrates. When the training batch mixes questions synthesised
from two distinct seed questions representing highly divergent structural
problem spaces (denoted as
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
), this assumption
is violated: no single DAG topology can simultaneously realise minimum
empirical risk for both domains.

As illustrated in Figure
5
, evaluating on this
mixed-task test set reveals topological overfitting as a boundary
condition rather than a performance failure. The zero-shot baseline
student agent achieves a mean accuracy of
\mu=45.3\%
. In this setting,
DSPy attains
\mu=47.3\%
(Range: 33.3% to 70.0%), whilst the SGDe-optimised
workflow reaches
\mu=48.7\%
(Range: 40.0% to 56.7%). Although
SGDe remains marginally superior to both the unoptimised baseline
and the DSPy prompt optimiser, the modest overall gains and the variance
observed confirm our theoretical prediction: when forced to reconcile
conflicting semantic critiques within a single DAG, the teacher agent
produces structural improvements tailored to
Q_{1}
that subtly
degrade performance on
Q_{2}
.

This result empirically validates the design guideline proposed in
Section
III
: SGDe-optimised workflows
must be aggressively compartmentalised into narrow, domain-specific
subgraphs rather than attempting to create a single generalist DAG
that spans widely divergent task structures. The interference observed
in Figure
5
is not a practical limitation of
the method, but rather the expected cost of violating the narrow-domain
assumption stated in Section
I
. Consequently,
the convergence with as few as three examples demonstrated in Section

V-C
applies strictly to independent,
specialised harnesses optimised for structurally homogeneous task
distributions. The PAC guarantees of Corollary 1 hold specifically
when the teacher’s prior concentrates on a coherent subspace
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Mixing domains will fragment this subspace and prevent efficient compilation.

VI-B

Scope and Limitations

Our empirical evaluation is restricted to GSM-Hard-style structured-reasoning
tasks. We chose this benchmark as a controlled proxy that isolates
the failure modes the framework targets, namely parsing, arithmetic,
and format-extraction coupling, without confounding factors from OCR,
document layout, or domain-specific schema. The enterprise scenarios
cited in Section
I
(financial-statement extraction,
insurance-claim processing, compliance verification) are treated as
motivation for the framework’s design rather than as validated use
cases; empirical validation on those tasks is outside the scope of
this paper.

VI-C

Future Work: Agentic Routing and Topological Ensembles

To resolve the mild multi-task interference observed in Figure
5
,
we suggest exploring the integration of a deterministic routing layer
at the apex of the execution architecture. Rather than forcing the
teacher agent to compile a single, generalist DAG that suffers from
topological overfitting, we propose extending the framework to support
a mixture-of-topologies.

In practical deployments utilising graph-based orchestrators, this
approach manifests as an initial intent-classification node. This
lightweight classifier evaluates the semantic structure of the incoming
task and conditionally routes the execution trace to a specialised,
pre-compiled SGDe subgraph (e.g., routing compound-interest queries
exclusively to subgraph A, and payment-reconciliation tasks to subgraph
B). By compartmentalising the hypothesis space, the teacher agent
can independently optimise each subgraph without triggering conflicting
semantic critiques (
g_{\text{sem}}
). We hypothesise that this topological-ensemble
architecture will allow the system to handle a diverse array of generalised
enterprise requests while preserving the high accuracy, minimised
inference costs, and tight PAC learning bounds inherent to specialised
SGDe compilation.

for related work on routing and mixture-of-experts architectures.

VII
Conclusion

We introduced Semantic Gradient Descent (SGDe), a teacher-student
framework that compiles the agent harness
\theta=\left\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right\}

offline by translating the continuous optimisation loop of machine
learning into discrete refinement of workflow artefacts. Its principal
contribution is to make the boundary between the model and the runtime
itself optimisable: at each node, the teacher selects adaptively among
prompt refinement, capability offloading, and structural consensus
(Section
III-C
), so the execution plan becomes
a partition of the task between the probabilistic student and the
deterministic runtime.

This reframing gives the PAC analysis mechanistic teeth. Corollary
1 bounds the effective hypothesis space
\left|\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}\right|

by
\text{O}\left(3^{k}\right)
, explaining both convergence from
as few as three training examples on structurally homogeneous tasks
and the predictable degradation observed on the mixed-task boundary
condition of Section
VI-A
. Empirically,
SGDe reaches 91.3% mean accuracy on the GSM-Hard-derived test set
at
m=5
and 99.3% at
m=3
, within the small-
m
regime motivated
by Corollary 1, a +26.3% to +34.3% absolute improvement over the
strongest intra-substrate baseline (DSPy), with every winning harness
combining offloaded code nodes and consensus subgraphs.

By making the SLM/runtime boundary a first-class optimisation target
under PAC guarantees, SGDe offers a practical pathway toward certifiable,
on-premise agentic systems. Extension to heterogeneous enterprise
workflows via the mixture-of-topologies architecture outlined in Section

VI-C
remains future work.

Walk-through and Mechanism

Both saved DAGs were evaluated on the running-example task (Section

III-A
; gold answer: 90). The mild inversion
of the narrative (Jamal receives rather than pays, and “left over”
replaces “change received”) exposes the divergence.

Two pieces of system mechanics matter for what follows. First, the
harness applies a deterministic answer-extractor: it scans each solver’s
output for “####” markdown markers and returns the first number
following the last such marker, or the final number in the output
if none are present. Second, the vote node returns the majority of
the parsed values, falling back to a tie-breaker that sorts the values
ascending and selects the smallest when no majority exists. In the
table that follows, “Derived” denotes the number reasoned in the body
of a solver’s output and “Parsed” denotes the number the extractor
returns from it; the two diverge precisely when a solver’s formatting
choices steer the extractor away from the body answer.

(formula solver). The prompt articulates

Format-extractor coupling
(verify solver). The solver derives

rather than the derived total. Parsed: 10.

The simple solver, which emits a plain English derivation “Money given

The three parsed values [100, 10, 90] disagree, the tie-breaker
selects the smallest, and the harness returns 10. The
m=3
DAG avoids
both pathologies because all three solvers share one prompt: identical
markdown structure (no extraction-path divergence), identical rule
(no over-application asymmetry), and only temperature noise to break
ties.

The general pattern is that specialisation interacts poorly with a
deterministic vote-and-extract aggregation path. Diverse prompts produce
diverse output formats, and diverse formats expose the extractor to
different last-marker selections; diverse rules expose the student
to different inverted-narrative failure surfaces. Larger
m
widens
the teacher’s attribution signal, and the teacher uses that wider
signal to specialise the program in precisely the direction the deterministic
aggregation path punishes. Smaller-batch teachers cannot observe enough
variance to motivate specialisation, and so converge on a homogeneous
DAG that the same path rewards.
