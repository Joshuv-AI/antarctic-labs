Furthest neighbor.

Nearest neighbor and furthest neighbor searches are basic and well-studied proximity problems in computational geometry. In the furthest neighbor problem, the goal is to preprocess a given set
P
of
points in a metric space
(V,\mathsf{dist})
to quickly answer

In the approximate version, an accuracy parameter

\mathsf{dist}(v,p)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\max_{p^{\prime}\in P}\mathsf{dist}(v,p^{\prime})
. The furthest neighbor problem is closely related to computing the diameter of point sets. In Euclidean spaces, computing approximate furthest neighbors can be efficiently reduced to computing approximate nearest neighbors
[
36
]
. In the graph setting, the distance to the furthest neighbor of a vertex is called the
eccentricity
of the vertex. Computing the diameter and eccentricities is a fundamental graph problem that has been studied for decades.

A natural approach to the furthest neighbor problem is to identify,
in the preprocessing phase, a small set

that correctly represents

for any

Such a set

Definition 1.1
(Furthest Neighbor

In general, a

Coreset has been a very well-studied topic since it offers a simple approach for solving problems in massive datasets: simply construct a coreset of the (big) input and solve the problems on the coreset. 1.1
Our main results

Our first result is a formal connection between the
\varepsilon
-comatching index and a coreset for furthest neighbor, overcoming obstacle (i). Unfortunately, the above minimality argument stops working if we consider weights. Instead, we devise a new reduction based on linear programming. Specifically, we round a set-cover-like linear program for metrics where the ball set system has a bounded VC-dimension. The discussed results so far uncover a surprising separation between

A remark on extending to bounded genus graphs.

For Theorems
1.6
and
1.18
,
we use a standard tool of cutting open a graph of Euler genus

into a planar graphs
by cutting
\mathcal{O}(g)
shortest paths and single edges.
Observe that the argumentation
used in the proof of the separation lemma is also useful in this setting:
if we partition the pairs of vertices of a comatching (or triples of vertices of a double ladder) into buckets according
to the approximate distance profile
to the cutset, then within a single bucket, all shortest paths
\llbracket p_{i}q_{j}\rrbracket
cannot
touch the cutset (or we get a similar contradiction as in the separation lemma), and thus essentially induce a comatching (or a double ladder) in a planar graph.

by
\mathcal{A}

if for every

.
One of the basic properties of set systems of bounded VC dimension is the following.

Lemma 3.1
(Sauer-Shelah lemma)

The number of sets in
a set system
\mathcal{A}
of VC-dimension at most
d
over a universe
U

is at most

\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{|U|}{i}=\mathcal{O}(|U|^{d}).

Let
(V,\mathsf{dist})
be a metric space.
For

and

B(v,r)=\{u\in V~|~\mathsf{dist}_{G}(v,u)\leq r\}
.
The set system
\{B(v,r)~|~v\in V(G),r>0\}
of subsets of

is called
the ball set system of
(V,\mathsf{dist})
.

Graphs with positive edge-weights naturally induce a metric space on their set of vertices
via shortest path distances.
In such metric spaces, if the underlying graph excludes

Theorem 3.2
(
[
16
,
10
]
)

Let

as a minor.

at most
h-1
.

For a metric space
(V,\mathsf{dist})
and

, for every

we define

the distance profile of

to

as a function

\mathsf{prof}_{Z}[v](z)=\mathsf{dist}(v,z)

With a distance profile

An important property of the set system

(Theorem 3

)

Let

as a minor and let

.

3.2
Tree decompositions of planar graphs

The following theorem encapsulates the main construction leading to the
celebrated Lipton-Tarjan separator theorem for planar graphs.

Theorem 3.5
(see
[
40
]
)

Let
G
be a plane triangulation
(i.e., a simple connected graph embedded in the plane where every face is a triangle)
and let
H
be a spanning tree of
G
with a designated root
r
.
Let
T
be a subgraph of the dual of
G
with
V(T)
being the set of all faces of
G

and
E(T)
being the duals of all edges of
E(G)\setminus E(H)
.
Furthermore, for every
t\in V(T)
, let
\beta(t)\subseteq V(G)
consist
of the vertex sets of the three upward paths in
H
from the vertices of the face
t
to
the root
r
.

Then,
T
is a tree and
(T,\beta)
is a tree decomposition of
G
.
In particular, for every edge
t_{1}t_{2}\in E(T)
, the adhesion
\beta(t_{1})\cap\beta(t_{2})

is the vertex set of the two upward paths in
H
from the endpoints of the common edge
of the faces
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
to the root
r
.

We will need also the following standard argument.

Lemma 3.6

Let
k,n>0
. Let
G
be a planar graph on
n
vertices and let
(T,\beta)
be a tree decomposition of
G
such that the degree of every vertex in
T
is at most
3
. Then, for any weight function
\chi:V(G)\to\{0,1\}
, one of the following holds:

•

there is a bag
b\in V(T)
such that
\chi(\beta(b))\geq\frac{1}{4k}\chi(V(G))
, or

•

there exists a subset
F\subseteq E(T)
of
k
edges such that the union of bags of each connected component of
T-F
has weight at least
\frac{1}{4k}\chi(V(G))
.

Let
W=\chi(V(G))
. Now, by induction, we assume that there is no bag
b\in V(T)
such that
\chi(\beta(b))\geq W/4k
and we have picked
i-1
edges such that each connected component of
T-F
has weight at least
W/4k
. We can always find a connected component
C
of weight at least
W/i\geq W/k
.

We now pick the
i^{th}
edge in
C
as follows: start with an arbitrary edge
(u,v)
and check if it separates
C
into two components of size at least
W/4k
each. If not, let
H
be the component with higher weight attached to
v
(w.l.o.g.). The vertex
v
cannot have degree
1
, since then
H
is a single vertex with weight at least
3W/4k
. If
v
has degree
2
, then take the potential cut edge to be
(v,w)
(considering
w
is the other neighbor of
v
). If
\chi(V(C-H)\cup\{\beta(v)\})\geq W/4k
, we are done. Otherwise, continue with the next edge towards
w
. If
v
has degree
3
, let
v_{1},v_{2}
be the other neighbors of
v
, and let
T_{1},T_{2}
resp. be the subtrees corresponding to them. Since
\chi(\beta(v))<W/4k
, we have
\chi(V(T_{1})\cup V(T_{2}))\geq W/2k
. This means one of
T_{1}
or
T_{2}
has weight at least
W/4k
. Assume
\chi(V(T_{1}))\geq W/4k
. If
\chi(C-T_{1})\geq W/4k
, we cut the
(v,v_{1})
edge. If not, we continue towards
v_{1}
and its other neighbors. Note that in this whole procedure, the weight of the lighter component is strictly increasing. Therefore, there must exist a cut edge with separates
C
into components having weight at least
W/4k
each. We perform this operation
k
times to obtain a set of
k
edges as desired.

3.3
The routing lemma

We will also need the following routing lemma.

Lemma 3.7

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and let
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
be a family of trees that are subgraphs of
G
such that:

•

V(T_{1})\cap V(T_{i})\neq\emptyset
for every
i\in[k]
,

•

\bigcup_{i\in[k]}V(T_{i})=V(G)
.

Then, there exists a spanning tree
H
of
G
rooted at some vertex
r
, such that for every
v\in V(G)
, the path from
v
to
r
can be partitioned into
\mathcal{O}(\log k\cdot\log|V(G)|)
parts, each being a subpath of one of the trees
T_{1},T_{2},...,T_{k}
.

By our assumptions,
\cup_{i}T_{i}
spans
V(G)
, and each
T_{i}
has a non-empty intersection with
T_{1}
. We start by showing a randomized procedure that generates a spanning tree
H
which achieves the
\mathcal{O}(\log k)
bound in expectation.

We choose
r
as an arbitrary vertex of
V(T_{1})
and root each tree
T_{i}
in an arbitrary vertex of
V(T_{1})\cap V(T_{i})
for every
i\in\{2,\dots,k\}
.
Let
\pi
be a randomly and uniformly chosen permutation
\pi=(\pi_{2},\pi_{3},...,\pi_{k})
of the set
\{2,\dots,k\}
. For the sake of simplicity, we put
\pi_{1}=1
. Let
<_{\pi}
be the linear ordering on
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
induced by
\pi
, i.e., we have
T_{i}<_{\pi}T_{j}
iff
\pi^{-1}(i)<\pi^{-1}(j)
.

For each
v\in V(G)
, we put
T_{v}
to be the
<_{\pi}
-smallest tree
T_{i}
which contains
v
. For
v\neq r
, we put
e_{v}
to be the edge connecting
v
to its parent in
T_{v}
. Note that such edge always exists as the roots of all
T_{i}
belong to
V(T_{1})
and
T_{1}
is
<_{\pi}
-smallest. We define
H
as the union of all edges
e_{v}
for all
v\in V(G)-\{r\}
. It’s easy to see that
H
is indeed a spanning tree of
G
.

Finally, for the proof of the desired property. Pick any
v\in V(G)-\{r\}
and let
P_{v}
be the path from
v
to
r
in
H
.
(We think of
P_{v}
as oriented from
v
to
r
.)
Let
\mathcal{T}_{v}
be the set of trees
T_{i}
that intersect
P_{v}

Order
\mathcal{T}_{v}
as
T_{i_{1}},\ldots,T_{i_{\ell}}
according to the
earliest vertex of the intersection with
P_{v}
, breaking ties arbitrarily.
We say that an index
a
is
important
if

T_{i_{a}}
is
<_{\pi}
-minimum among
\{T_{i_{1}},\ldots,T_{i_{a}}\}

and
used
if
T_{i_{a}}=T_{u}
for some
u\in V(P_{v})
.
Observe that a used index is necessarily important.

Consider the statement
A_{a,v}
:
the index
a
is important for the vertex
v
.
The crucial observation is that

\Pr(A_{a,v})\leq\frac{1}{a}.

Indeed, the path
P_{v}
up to the intersection with
T_{i_{a}}

does not depend on the position of the tree
T_{i_{a}}

in the order
\pi
, so
all
a
positions of
T_{i_{a}}
with respect
to
\{T_{i_{1}},\ldots,T_{i_{a-1}}\}
in the order
<_{\pi}

are equally probable, and only one makes
a
important for
v
.

Now, let
X_{v}
be the random variable for the number of trees
T_{i}
needed to cover
P_{v}
.
Observe that
X_{v}
is bounded by the number of used indices for
v
,
as for every
u\in V(P_{v})\setminus\{r\}
we can cover the edge
going upwards from
u
with
T_{u}
.
This quantity is, in turn, bounded by the number of important indices for
v
.
Hence, in expectation
E_{\pi}[X_{v}]\leq\Sigma_{a=1}^{\ell}\Pr(A_{a,v})\leq\Sigma_{a=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{a}
, which is the harmonic series; the finite harmonic series up to
L
is known to be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\log L)
.

Now, we proceed with the deterministic construction. For a set

\emptyset\neq W\subseteq V(G)
, let
X_{W}
be equal to
X_{v}
assuming we sample
v
uniformly randomly from
W
.
Again,
E[X_{W}]=\mathcal{O}(\log k)
.
By the Markov inequality,
P(X_{W}\geq 2E[X_{W}])\leq\frac{1}{2}
, hence for at least half of all pairs
(\pi,v)
, the value of
X_{v}
assuming
\pi
is at most
2E[X_{W}]
. In particular, there exists a permutation
\pi_{0}
s.t.
\Pr(X_{W}\leq 2E[X_{W}]\mid\pi=\pi_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
Thus, fixing
\pi=\pi_{0}
, the
v
-to-
r
paths for half
of the vertices
v\in W
will be covered by fewer than
2E[X_{W}]=\mathcal{O}(\log k)
trees.

We perform the above procedure iteratively. We first put
W=V(G)
, find
\pi_{0,V(G)}
and corresponding
H
(according to our randomized procedure), and fix the parent vertex according to
H
for every
v
for which we can cover the
v
-to-
r
path in
H
using at most
2E[X_{W}]
trees. Afterwards, we restrict
W
to vertices with parent not yet fixed and repeat this step.

At every step, the vertices with parent already fixed form a connected subtree containing
r
. Every step fixes at least half of the vertices not yet fixed, hence the total number of steps will be at most
\mathcal{O}(\log|V(G)|)
. Any
v
-to-
r
path in the final tree obtained this way can be decomposed into
\mathcal{O}(\log|V(G)|)
subpaths, one for each step, and each of those is coverable with
\mathcal{O}(\log k)
trees, which gives us the desired
\mathcal{O}(\log k\cdot\log|V(G)|)
bound.

From Comatching to Coreset: Proof of
Theorem

1.4

This section is devoted to the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.
We will need a classic tool: an LP rounding approximation algorithm for Hitting Set
in set systems of bounded VC dimension.

4.1
Hitting Set LP

For a family
\mathcal{A}
of subsets of a universe
U
, a set
X\subseteq U

is called a
hitting set
of
\mathcal{A}
if
X\cap A\neq\emptyset
for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
The Hitting Set problem, given
U
and
\mathcal{A}
, asks for a hitting set
of minimum possible size.

Below is the LP relaxation for the natural formulation of the Hitting Set problem
and its dual (which is a set packing problem).

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{min}&\sum_{u\in U}x_{u}&\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\sum_{u\in A}x_{u}\geq 1&\forall_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\\
&x_{u}\geq 0&\forall_{u\in U}\\
\end{aligned}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\begin{aligned} \mathrm{max}&\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}y_{A}&\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}~|~u\in A}y_{A}\leq 1&\forall_{u\in U}\\
&y_{A}\geq 0&\forall_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\\
\end{aligned}

We need the following classic result about rounding the Hitting Set LP
in set systems of bouned VC-dimension.

Theorem 4.1
(
[
45
,
41
]
)

There exists a universal constant
c
such that every Hitting Set
instance
(U,\mathcal{A})
admits a solution of size at most
c\cdot d\cdot\tau^{\ast}\cdot\log(\tau^{\ast})
,
where
d
is the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{A}
and
\tau^{\ast}
is the optimum value
of the linear programming relaxation. Furthermore, such a solution can be found in polynomial time.

4.2
The proof

We are now ready to prove

1.4
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
As for
\varepsilon\geq 1
the task of finding an
\varepsilon
-coreset for furthest neighbor

\varepsilon<1
.
Let
(V,\mathsf{dist})
be a metric space, let
P\subseteq V(G)

be the VC-dimension of the ball set system of

We assume

Let

More formally, for each
i\in[L]
, we define a tree
T_{i}
as the union of paths
\cup_{j:j\neq i}\llbracket p_{i}q_{j}\rrbracket
. Such union is indeed a tree, given our guarantee that any two shortest paths remain disjoint after branching out for the first time.
Before we show the construction, we will need the following claim.

Claim 5.10

The number of vertices of
G
is of
\mathcal{O}(L^{4})
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be
G
before it was triangulated. We clearly have that
|V(G)|=|V(G^{\prime})|
. As the instance corresponding to
G^{\prime}
is clean, by the definition of it being clean, we have that
G^{\prime}
is exactly the union of all trees
T_{i}
. There are
2L
vertices of degree
1
(terminals) and the remaining vertices are of degree at least
3
(again, because our instance is clean). Every vertex of degree at least
3
either has degree at least
3
in one of trees
T_{i}
, or lies at an intersection of two different shortest paths between terminals, with no common terminals.

In the first case, it is well known that in any tree, the number of vertices of degree at least
3
is at most the number of its leaves. Since we have
L
trees and each of those has at most
L
leaves, this gives us
L^{2}
such vertices total.

In the second case, we have at most
L^{2}
different paths that can intersect. By our unique path assumption, every intersection of any two paths is a single subpath, hence such pair can only contribute to at most
2
vertices of degree
3
in
G^{\prime}
, i.e., both endpoints of said common subpath. This gives us at most
2L^{4}
such vertices total.

Assuming
L\geq 3
, the trees
T_{i}
satisfy the conditions of
Lemma

3.7
: let
H
be the spanning tree resulting from the lemma.
By
5.10
,
\log|V(G)|=\mathcal{O}(\log L)
,
so in fact any path in
H
can be decomposed into
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}L)
paths,
each contained in a single tree
T_{i}
.

We will argue that we can easily bound the number of bad pairs w.r.t. any separator induced by our tree decomposition. Note, that any separator
S
induced by an adhesion in
H
is now coverable by
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}L)
shortest paths in
G
, each of them being a subpath of some
T_{i}
. This follows from the fact that all edges of such separator always form two vertex-to-root paths in
H
.

Claim 5.11

There are at most
\mathcal{O}(\log^{8}L\cdot\varepsilon^{-8})
pairs in
\mathcal{M}
that are
separated
by
S
.

Every subpath of any
T_{i}
is of length at most
2R
. Let
\alpha
denote the smallest possible size of a collection of subpaths of
T_{i}
’s covering whole
S
(
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}L)
by our previous observation). The claim now follows by applying
5.8
with said
\alpha
and
\beta=2
.

Claim 5.12

There are at most
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}L)
pairs in
\mathcal{M}
that are
contaminating

S
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the smallest subset of
(T_{i})_{i=1}^{L}
covering whole
S
.
Each path in a tree
T_{i}
consists of at most two subpaths of terminal to terminal paths (like
\llbracket p_{i}q_{j}\rrbracket
)
for some
j\neq i
; hence there is a set
\mathcal{P}

of
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}L)
terminal to terminal paths that
cover the whole
S
.

For any contaminating pair
\sigma
, there is an edge
e_{\sigma}\in E(S)
such that
e_{\sigma}\not\in G^{\neg\sigma}
.
The edge
e_{\sigma}
needs to be covered by
\mathcal{P}
by a path
with endpoint in
\sigma
.
Since there are at most
2|\mathcal{P}|=\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}L)

pairs
\sigma
with endpoint in
\mathcal{P}
, the claim follows.

The remaining pairs are
good
with respect to
S
, that is, they are neither separated by
S
nor contaminate
S
.

5.3.3
Good pairs with noncrossing links

We are now ready to prove the main lemma of the subsection.
Here is the main idea behind the proof: because the number of bad pairs is small w.r.t. any separator, if there are sufficiently many pairs in
\mathcal{M}
, then we can choose a balanced separator
S
of
T
such that there exists a pair which is good and lies inside
\xi_{S}
. Furthermore, by our definitions, the link of such pair must be contained entirely within
\xi_{S}
.

In the following proof, we want to find sufficiently many good pairs whose links are disjoint. And, as a direct consequence of the above observation, if we can find good pairs lying on different faces of some separators, then the desired condition will be satisfied.

Consider a spanning tree
H
produced by
Lemma

3.7
and consider a tree decomposition
(T,\beta)
given by
Theorem

3.5
for
H
.

Define a weight function
\chi:V(G)\to\{0,1\}
which assigns
1
to terminals of all pairs in
\mathcal{M}
and assigns
0
to all other vertices. The total weight over all vertices is then
2L
. Since we assign a positive weight only to leaf vertices, the weight of every bag is at most
3
. We will fix a parameter
\alpha
, roughly of order
L^{1/2}
, the value of which will be derived later.

We use
Lemma

3.6
to split
T
in a balanced way w.r.t.
\chi
. That is, we find a set
F\subseteq E(T)
of
\alpha
edges such that the union of bags of each connected component of
T-F
has weight at least
\frac{\chi(V(G))}{4\alpha}=\frac{L}{2\alpha}
. The other case, where one bag is of weight at least
\frac{1}{4\alpha}\chi(V(G))
, cannot hold if
L
is large enough.

For
f\in F
, put
S_{f}
as the separator induced by the adhesion of
f
, as defined at the beginning of the subsection. Put
X=\bigcup_{f\in F}V(S_{f})
. Fix any connected component
C
of
T-F
. Look at the set
V_{C}
of vertices defined as the union of bags of
C
minus the vertices of
X
. We argue that
V_{C}
contains both terminals of at least one good pair.

By
5.11
and
5.12
, there are at most
\alpha\cdot\mathcal{O}(\log^{8}L\cdot\varepsilon^{-8})
pairs which are not good w.r.t. to any
S_{f}
, hence twice as much terminal vertices belonging to any such pair. Moreover, every set
V(S_{f})
is a union of two paths on non-virtual edges, hence contains at most
2
vertices of originally degree
1
, hence at most
2
terminal vertices. Therefore, the total number of terminal vertices in
V_{C}
which are either bad or in
X
is at most
\alpha\cdot c\cdot\log^{8}L\cdot\varepsilon^{-8}
, where
c
is some universal constant. The number of all terminal vertices in
V_{C}
is
\chi(V_{C})\geq\frac{L}{2\alpha}
, hence if we set
\alpha
such that

4\cdot\alpha^{2}\cdot c\leq L\log^{-8}L\cdot\varepsilon^{8},

then every
V_{C}-X
will contain at least one terminal of a pair, say
\sigma_{C}
, which will be good w.r.t. all
S_{f}
.

Now, consider any two components
C_{1},C_{2}
and their corresponding pairs
\sigma_{C_{1}},\sigma_{C_{2}}
. There is some edge
f^{\prime}\in F
separating them in
T
, hence they lie on different sides of the loop
\xi_{f^{\prime}}
. In particular, the links
\sigma_{C_{1}},\sigma_{C_{2}}
are separated by
\xi_{f^{\prime}}
, and hence are nonintersecting.

This way, we have picked
\alpha=\Theta(L^{1/2}\log^{-4}L\cdot\varepsilon^{4})
pairs with pairwise nonintersecting links, one per each component of
T-F
. Restricting our instance to these pairs gives us a noncrossing instance, hence
\alpha\leq L_{\asymp}(\varepsilon)
, and therefore
L=\mathcal{O}(L_{\asymp}(\varepsilon)^{2}\log^{8}L_{\asymp}(\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon^{-8})
, which finishes the proof.

5.4
Bounding linked noncrossing instances

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,\mathcal{M})
be a linked non-crossing instance.
We will frequently use the following uncrossing argument.

Claim 5.13

Let
\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b},\sigma_{c},\sigma_{d}\in\mathcal{M}
be
not necessarily distinct.
If
\llbracket p_{a}q_{b}\rrbracket\cap\llbracket p_{c}q_{d}\rrbracket\neq\emptyset
,
then

\mathsf{dist}(p_{a},q_{b})+\mathsf{dist}(p_{c},q_{d})\geq\mathsf{dist}(p_{a},q_{d})+\mathsf{dist}(p_{c},q_{b}).

Let
u
be a vertex in the intersection of
\llbracket p_{a}q_{b}\rrbracket

and
\llbracket p_{c}q_{d}\rrbracket
.
Split both
\llbracket p_{a}q_{b}\rrbracket
and
\llbracket p_{c}q_{d}\rrbracket
at
u

and observe that the subpath of
\llbracket p_{a}q_{b}\rrbracket
from
p_{a}
to
u

and the subpath of
\llbracket p_{c}q_{d}\rrbracket
from
u
to
q_{d}
form a walk
from
p_{a}
to
q_{d}
while the remaining parts,
i.e., the subpath of
\llbracket p_{c}q_{d}\rrbracket
from
p_{c}
to
u

and the subpath of
\llbracket p_{a}q_{b}\rrbracket
from
u
to
q_{b}
form a
walk from
p_{c}
to
q_{b}
. The claim follows.

The first immediate corollary is the following:

Claim 5.14

\llbracket p_{i}q_{j}\rrbracket\cap\llbracket p_{j}q_{i}\rrbracket=\emptyset,\forall\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{M}.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\llbracket p_{i}q_{j}\rrbracket
intersects
\llbracket p_{j}q_{i}\rrbracket
. Then,
by
5.13
,

2(1-\varepsilon)R\geq\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},q_{j})+\mathsf{dist}(p_{j},q_{i})\geq\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},q_{i})+\mathsf{dist}(p_{j},q_{j})\geq 2R.

This is a contradiction.

5.4.1
Equidistant structure

We now filter
\mathcal{M}
to get the following substructure.

Definition 5.15

An
equidistant structure
in
\mathcal{I}

is a triple
(\sigma^{\prime}=(p^{\prime},q^{\prime}),\sigma^{\prime\prime}=(p^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime\prime}),\mathcal{N})

where
\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
,
\sigma^{\prime}\neq\sigma^{\prime\prime}
,

\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\}

and the following holds:

1.

For every
\sigma=(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}
,

\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(p,q^{\prime})=\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(p^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime})\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q,p^{\prime})=\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q^{\prime\prime},p^{\prime}).

2.

For every
\sigma_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1}),\sigma_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathcal{N}
,

\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(p_{1},q^{\prime\prime})=\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(p_{2},q^{\prime\prime})\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q_{1},p^{\prime\prime})=\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q_{2},p^{\prime\prime}).

3.

For every distinct
\sigma_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1}),\sigma_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathcal{N}
,
the path
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
does not intersect

\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
nor
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket
.

The
size
of the equidistant structure
(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{N})
is
|\mathcal{N}|
.

Lemma 5.16

Assume
\mathcal{I}
does not contain an equidistant structure
of size larger than
L
. Then,
|\mathcal{M}|=\mathcal{O}(L\varepsilon^{-12}).

Pick arbitrary
\sigma^{\prime}=(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}
.
Tag each
\sigma=(p,q)\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\sigma^{\prime}\}

with the following tuple:
(\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(p,q^{\prime}),\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q,p^{\prime}))
.
There are
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
possible tags.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\sigma^{\prime}\}

be a subset of pairs with the same tag.

Pick arbitrary
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=(p^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}

and re-tag each pair
\sigma=(p,q)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\setminus\{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\}

with the new tuple
(\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(p,q^{\prime\prime}),\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q,p^{\prime\prime}))
.
Again, there are
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
possible tags
and
let
\mathcal{M}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}\setminus\{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\}

be a subset of pairs with the same tag.

Let
Z
be a set of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
vertices such that
every vertex of
\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket\cup\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket
is within distance

\delta
from a vertex of
Z
.
By
Lemma

5.5
, there are
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-8})
possible
rounded distance profiles to
Z
.
By
Lemma

5.6
, if
\sigma_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1}),\sigma_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
are such that the rounded distance profiles
of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
to
Z
are equal, then
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
does not
intersect
\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
nor
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
Consequently, if
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
is a set
of pairs
(p,q)
with the same rounded distance profile of
p
to
Z
,
then
(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{N})
is an equidistant structure.
The lemma follows.

Fix an equidistant structure
(\sigma^{\prime}=(p^{\prime},q^{\prime}),\sigma^{\prime\prime}=(p^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime\prime}),\mathcal{N})
in
\mathcal{I}
. Our goal is to bound
L=|\mathcal{N}|
by
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
.

5.4.2
Forbidden intersections

We now make a number of observations about disjointness of some paths in
the equidistant structure
(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{N})
. Note that
\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket\cap\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\emptyset
based on
5.14
.

Claim 5.17

For every
\sigma=(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}
,
we have
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p\rrbracket\cap\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\emptyset

and
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q\rrbracket\cap\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\emptyset
.

By contradiction, assume that
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p\rrbracket
and
\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
intersect.
By
5.13
,

(1-\varepsilon)R+\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})\geq\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p)+\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})\geq\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p^{\prime\prime})+\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime},p)\geq R+\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime},p).

However, from the definition of an equidistant structure,

\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})=\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q^{\prime},p)
, which implies

|\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})-\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime},p)|<\delta
, a contradiction.

The proof of
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q\rrbracket\cap\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\emptyset

is symmetrical.

5.17
motivates the following definition.
Let
\hat{\gamma}^{\prime}
be a minimal part of the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
that connects

\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
with
\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket

and let
′
be a curve from
q^{\prime\prime}
to
p^{\prime\prime}
consisting
of the subpath of
\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
from
q^{\prime\prime}
to the endpoint of
\hat{\gamma}^{\prime}
,
the curve
\hat{\gamma}^{\prime}
, and the subpath of
\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
from the endpoint
of
\hat{\gamma}^{\prime}
to
p^{\prime\prime}
. As for
(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}
we have that
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p\rrbracket
intersects with
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime}\rrbracket
only on its prefix, does not intersect with
\hat{\gamma}^{\prime}
(as the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
does not intersect
G^{\neg\sigma^{\prime}}
, which contains
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p\rrbracket
) and does not intersect
\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
(per
5.17
), we conclude that the only intersection of
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p\rrbracket
with
′
is the common prefix of
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p\rrbracket
and
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime}\rrbracket
. Similarly, the only intersection of
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q\rrbracket
with
′
is the common prefix of
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q\rrbracket
and
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
Furthermore, the definition of an equidistant structure ensures that
for every distinct
\sigma_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1}),\sigma_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathcal{N}
,
the path
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
is disjoint with
′
.

Intuitively, in what follows, one should think of
′
as a “rupture”
in the plane that connects
q^{\prime\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}
. We note that we defined
′
this way, instead of possibly more natural connection consisting of
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime}\rrbracket
, the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
, because the latter one might have self-intersections, while
′
cannot.
We will argue about relative positions of different
objects with respect to
′
.

Before we continue, we make one more observation.

Claim 5.18

For every distinct
\sigma_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1}),\sigma_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathcal{N}
,
the path
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
does not intersect

\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{2}\rrbracket
nor
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q_{1}\rrbracket
.

By contradiction, assume that
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
and
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{2}\rrbracket
intersect. By
5.13
,

(1-\varepsilon)R+\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{2})\geq\mathsf{dist}(p_{1},q_{2})+\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{2})\geq\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{1})+\mathsf{dist}(p_{2},q_{2})\geq\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{1})+R.

However, from the definition of an equidistant structure,

\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{1})=\widehat{\mathsf{dist}}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{2})
, which implies

|\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{1})-\mathsf{dist}(q^{\prime\prime},p_{2})|<\delta
, a contradiction.

The proof for
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q_{1}\rrbracket
is symmetrical.

5.4.3
Order around
p^{\prime\prime}
and
q^{\prime\prime}

At this stage, we now have a sufficient toolbox to reason about the order on the pairs of
\mathcal{N}
.
Consider the shortest path tree with the root
q^{\prime\prime}
and leaves

\{p~|~(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}\}\cup\{p^{\prime}\}
. Since every terminal
has only one incident edge of finite weight (i.e., not a virtual one),
there is a well-defined clockwise cyclic order of the leaves

\{p~|~(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}\}\cup\{p^{\prime}\}
. Let us transform this cyclic order into a linear order by declaring
p^{\prime}
as its first element, and then delete
p^{\prime}

from this order, obtaining as a result a linear clockwise ordering of

\{p~|~(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}\}
; we denote the induced
ordering of
\mathcal{N}
as
\pi_{q^{\prime\prime}}
.
We define the order
\pi_{q^{\prime\prime}}
analogously.
The following claim unifies the two orders:

Claim 5.19

\pi_{p^{\prime\prime}}=\pi_{q^{\prime\prime}}
.

Assume, contrary to our claim that
\pi_{p^{\prime\prime}}\neq\pi_{q^{\prime\prime}}
.
Let
\sigma_{1}=(p_{1},q_{1}),\sigma_{2}=(p_{2},q_{2})\in\mathcal{N}

be such that
\sigma_{1}
is before
\sigma_{2}
in
\pi_{p^{\prime\prime}}

but
\sigma_{1}
is after
\sigma_{2}
in
\pi_{q^{\prime\prime}}
.

We consider a closed curve consisting of paths:

\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{1}\rrbracket,\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket,\llbracket q_{2}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket,

and the curve
′
.
Since
\mathcal{I}
is a linked noncrossing instance,
the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
does not intersect
the paths
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{1}\rrbracket,\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket,\llbracket q_{2}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
.
The properties of the equidistant structure ensure that
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket

does not intersect
\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket\cup\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
We infer that
the paths
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{1}\rrbracket,\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket,\llbracket q_{2}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
,
and the curve
′

form a closed curve without self-intersections,
which we call
\gamma
.

Observe that the path
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{2}\rrbracket
does not intersect

\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
(
5.18
),
nor
\llbracket q_{2}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
(
5.14
),
nor
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket
(
5.17
),
nor the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
. Furthermore,
it may share a prefix starting at
q^{\prime\prime}
with
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime}\rrbracket

and
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{1}\rrbracket
, but then departs so that the clockwise
order in the shortest path tree rooted at

is
p_{2}
,
p_{1}
,
p^{\prime}
.

Symmetrically, the path
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q_{1}\rrbracket
does not intersect

\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
(
5.18
),
nor
\llbracket p_{1}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
(
5.14
),
nor
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime}\rrbracket
(
5.17
),
nor the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
. Furthermore,
it may share a prefix starting at
p^{\prime\prime}
with
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q^{\prime}\rrbracket

and
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q_{2}\rrbracket
, but then departs so that the clockwise

is
q^{\prime}
,
q_{1}
,

.

This implies that
q_{1}
and
p_{2}
lie on different sides
of the closed curve without self-intersections
\gamma
.

Consider now the path
\llbracket q_{1}p_{2}\rrbracket
.
It does not intersect
\llbracket p_{1}q_{2}\rrbracket
(
5.14
),
nor
\llbracket p^{\prime\prime}q_{2}\rrbracket
nor
\llbracket q^{\prime\prime}p_{1}\rrbracket
(

),
nor
\llbracket p^{\prime}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
nor
\llbracket q^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
(equidistant structure)
nor the link of
\sigma^{\prime}
(linked noncrossing instance
\mathcal{I}
).
Hence, it cannot intersect

.
This is a contradiction.

5.19
allows us to enumerate

\mathcal{N}
as
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{L}
according
to
\pi_{p^{\prime\prime}}=\pi_{q^{\prime\prime}}
. For brevity, let
\sigma_{i}=(p_{i},q_{i})
for

.

5.4.4
A box, Type A and Type B pairs

Let
1\leq i\leq L
. Let
\hat{\gamma}_{i}
be a minimal part
of the link of
\sigma_{i}
that connects
\llbracket p_{i}q^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
with
\llbracket q_{i}p^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
.
Let

See

First, we recall the definition of a double ladder.

See
1.15

To prove

1.16

44
]

A

of the set system of open neighborhoods, that is,

Theorem 6.6
(Theorem 1.2 of
[

]
)

For every

, there exists

such that every

Fix positive integers

.
Let

be a metric space whose ball set system has

and let
\mathcal{M}
be a
(k,\varepsilon)
-comatching
in
(V,\mathsf{dist})
of size

.
Enumerate
\mathcal{M}
as

\mathcal{M}=\{(p_{1},X_{1}),(p_{2},X_{2}),\ldots,(p_{L},X_{L})\}.

Furthermore, for each

, enumerate

(q_{j}^{1},q_{j}^{2},\ldots,q_{j}^{k})
.
Let

be the distance from the definition of the
(k,\varepsilon)
-comatching

\mathcal{M}
.

For every

, we define four graphs

H_{i}^{\rightarrow}
,
H_{i}^{\leftarrow}
,

\bar{H}_{i}^{\rightarrow}
,
\bar{H}_{i}^{\leftarrow}

as follows.
The vertex set of all four graphs is

.
For

, we have

if
and only if
\mathsf{dist}(p_{a},q_{b}^{i})\leq(1-\varepsilon)R

and

\mathsf{dist}(p_{a},q_{b}^{i})\leq(1-\varepsilon/2)R
.
Similarly,
for

if and only if
\mathsf{dist}(p_{b},q_{a}^{i})\leq(1-\varepsilon)R

\mathsf{dist}(p_{b},q_{a}^{i})\leq(1-\varepsilon/2)R

(Note the difference of
\varepsilon
vs
\varepsilon/2

Claim 6.7

For every

,

,

\bar{H}_{i}^{\leftarrow}
,
\bar{H}_{i}^{\rightarrow}

Observe that every neighborhood in any of the four aforementioned graphs

[
6
]
.

Let
\delta=\delta(d)
be the exponent of
Theorem

6.6

for the VC-dimension bound of

Repeated application of

6.6
to every

,

\bar{H}_{i}^{\leftarrow}
,

each of the graphs

,

,

(\varepsilon/2)
-comatching or an

-double ladder

7
The Size of Double Ladders

This section is devoted to the proof of
Theorem

1.17
, which establishes a bound on the size of
\varepsilon
-double ladder. The structure of this section closely parallels that of
Section

5
, with most arguments applying on the set of triplets
\mathcal{D=}\{(p_{i},\mathfrak{b}_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{L}
in place of the pairs
\mathcal{M}=\{(p_{i},q_{i})\}_{i=1}^{L}
. The analysis diverges slightly during the final contradiction involving some topological case analysis, but the overall strategy remains the same. Consequently, the resulting bound differs from the
\varepsilon
-comatching bound by a
\operatorname{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1})
factor.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. We will use a notation and definitions analogous to that introduced for
\varepsilon
-comatchings in
Section

5
. A pair
(G,\mathcal{D})
is an
instance
if
G
is a plane edge-weighted graph and
\mathcal{D}
is an
\varepsilon
-double ladder. Again, we assume that
G
comes with a fixed embedding in a plane. The vertices
p,\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{t}
for any triple
(p,\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{t})\in\mathcal{D}
are called
terminals
.

Similarly to the proof for comatchings, we assume that the lengths of all paths are pairwise different, and that the shortest paths between any pairs of terminals
(p_{1},\mathfrak{t}_{2})
and
(p_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{2})
are unique.
Again, we assume that our instance is
clean
, that is, that all the terminals are of degree
1
, that
G
contains no vertices of degree
2
, and that every edge lies on some shortest path between either
(p_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{j})
or
(p_{j},\mathfrak{b}_{i})
for some triples
(p_{i},\mathfrak{b}_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{i}),(p_{j},\mathfrak{b}_{j},\mathfrak{t}_{j})\in\mathcal{D}
with
i<j
.

We use the same notation:
\llbracket p_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{2}\rrbracket,\llbracket p_{1}\mathfrak{b}_{2}\rrbracket
to denote the shortest paths between terminals;
G^{\mathcal{D}_{0}}
for
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
to denote the subgraph of
G
consisting only on shortest paths between terminals of
\mathcal{D}_{0}
; and
G^{\neg\sigma}
as a shorthand for
G^{\mathcal{D}-\{\sigma\}}
for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}
.

We say that an instance
(G,\mathcal{D})
is
local
if for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}
, the terminals of
\sigma
lie in the same face of
G^{\neg\sigma}
. For a triple
\sigma=(p,\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{t})\in\mathcal{D}
, a
link
is a curve in good position w.r.t. G, connecting
p
to either
\mathfrak{b}
or
\mathfrak{t}
, which does not intersect any edge of
G^{\neg\sigma}
. A
linked instance
is an instance
(G,\mathcal{D})
with two links between resp.
p
and
\mathfrak{t}
, and
p
and
\mathfrak{b}
, fixed for every
(p,\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{t})\in\mathcal{D}
. A linked instance is
noncrossing
if all links are pairwise disjoint.

The proof of
Theorem

1.17
will, similarly to comathing case, consist of the three following steps.

Lemma 7.1

For a real
\varepsilon>0
, let
L^{\text{\#}}_{\mathrm{local}}(\varepsilon)
be the maximum size
of a
\varepsilon
-double ladder in a local instance.
Then, the maximum size of a
\varepsilon
-double ladder in any instance is of

\mathcal{O}(L^{\text{\#}}_{\mathrm{local}}(\varepsilon)^{9}\varepsilon^{-8})
.

Lemma 7.2

For a real
\varepsilon>0
, let
L^{\text{\#}}_{\asymp}(\varepsilon)
be the maximum size
of a
\varepsilon
-double ladder in a linked noncrossing instance.
Then, the maximum size of a
\varepsilon
-double ladder in a local instance is of

\mathcal{O}(L^{\text{\#}}_{\asymp}(\varepsilon)^{2}\log^{8}L^{\text{\#}}_{\asymp}(\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon^{-8})
.

Lemma 7.3

For a real
\varepsilon>0
, the maximum size of a
\varepsilon
-double ladder
in a linked noncrossing instance is at most
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
.

Putting these three lemmas together immediately gives the proof of
Theorem

1.17
.

7.1
Distance profiles and separation toolbox

We start by introducing the notation for distance-based arguments, analogous to the one used in the case of comatching. We additionally restate and prove some separation lemmas adapted to our current setting.
For the remainder of the proof,
(G,\mathcal{D})
is a clean
\varepsilon
-double ladder instance with at least three terminal triplets. We put

, enumerate

Claim 7.4

If

from

, as

\llbracket p_{i_{1}}\mathfrak{t}_{i_{2}}\rrbracket

\llbracket p_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{i_{2}}\rrbracket

(1-\varepsilon)R

Similarly,

\llbracket\mathfrak{b}_{i_{1}}p_{i_{2}}\rrbracket

(1-\varepsilon)R

Analogously to the comatching case, for a set

Lemma 7.5

For

In particular, by the Sauer-Shelah Lemma, its size is bounded by

The following observations are restated, but require some different arguments to prove in our new setting.

Lemma 7.6

Let

and

Here we show a polynomial coreset bound for furthest neighbors of points in a polygon with holes. This follows from

Lemma 8.1

Let
f(\varepsilon)
be the maximum size of
\varepsilon
-coresets for furthest neighbors in planar metrics for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then we can construct an
\varepsilon
-coreset of size
f(\varepsilon/8)
for furthest neighbors of any point set in a polygon with holes.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a polygon with holes on the plane, and
P
be a set of points in
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}
be the geodesic distance function between points in
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\Delta
be the diameter of
P
. Let
P^{+}
be the set of points that are within distance
\Delta/\varepsilon
from
P
. Observe that for any point
q\in V\setminus P^{+}
, any point
p\in P
is an
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate furthest neighbor of
q
. Thus, as long as the coreset is non-empty, points in
V\setminus P^{+}
are taken care of.

Herein, we focus on points in
P^{+}
. Let
Q
be an
(\varepsilon\Delta/8)
-net of
P^{+}
. That is, every point
x\in P^{+}
has a point
y\in Q
such that
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\Delta/8
and every two points
x,y\in Q
has
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)>\varepsilon\Delta/8
. We say that a subset
C\subseteq P
is a furthest
(\varepsilon/8)
-coreset for
P

restricted to
Q
if for every
q\in Q
, there exists a point
p\in C
such that:

\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(p,q)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(p^{*},q)

where
p^{*}
is the furthest neighbor of
q
in
p
. That is,
C
only preserves the furthest neighbors of points in
Q
. The following claim shows that it suffices to restrict to
Q
; the proof will be delayed until later.

Claim 8.2

Let
C
be a furthest neighbor
(\varepsilon/8)
-coreset of
P
restricted to
Q
. Then
C
is a furthest neighbor
\varepsilon
-coreset for
P
restricted to
P^{+}
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be an edge-weighted planar graph such that (i) the vertex set satisfies
Q\cup P\subseteq V\subseteq\mathcal{P}
, (ii)
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(x,y)=\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in P\cup Q
, and (iii) for every edge
E=(u,v)
,
w(u,v)=\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(u,v)
. Such a graph
G
exists
[
5
]
. (The basic idea to construct
G
is to take the geodesics connecting all pairs of points in
Q
, and place a vertex at the intersection of any two geodesics; the graph is planar by the planarity of the polygon.)
Next we construct a furthest neighbor
(\varepsilon/8)
-coreset
C\subseteq P
of size
f(\varepsilon/8)
for the shortest path metrics of
G
. Since
G
preserves pairwise distances between points in
P\cup Q
,
C
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset for
P
restricted to
Q
. Thus, by
8.2
,
C
is a furthest neighbor
\varepsilon
-coreset for
P
restricted to
P^{+}
and hence the whole domain
\mathcal{P}
.

Now we show
8.2
, which is simply an application of the triangle inequality.

Let
x
be a point in
P^{+}
and
y
be a point in
Q
such that
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\Delta/8
;
y
exists by the definition of
Q
. Let
x^{*}
and
y^{*}
be the furthest neighbors of
x
and
y
in
P
. Since the diameter of
P
is at most
\Delta
, by triangle inequality:

\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*}),\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(y,y^{*})\geq\Delta/2

Since
C
is an
(\varepsilon/8)
-coreset restricted to
Q
, there exists a point
\hat{y}\in C
such that:

\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\hat{y})\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(y,y^{*})

Our goal is to show that
\hat{y}
is a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate furthest neighbor of
x
, as follows:

\begin{split}\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,\hat{y})&\geq\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(y,\hat{y})-\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\qquad\text{(triangle inequality)}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(y,y^{*})-\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\qquad\text{(by definition of $\hat{y}$)}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(y,x^{*})-\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\qquad\text{(since $y^{*}$ is furthest neighbor of $y$)}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)(\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})-\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y))-\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\qquad\text{(triangle inequality)}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})-2\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})-\varepsilon\Delta/4\qquad\text{(since $\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\Delta/8$)}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon/8)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})-(\varepsilon/2)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})\qquad\text{(since $\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})\geq\Delta/2$)}\\
&\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathsf{dist}_{\mathcal{P}}(x,x^{*})~,\end{split}

(3)

as desired.

9
Bounded genus graphs

The upper bounds for metrics induced by graphs embeddable in surfaces of bounded Euler genus will follow from a reduction to planar metrics via the use of
cut-graphs
. We will reuse below some of the formalism defined for a similar argument in
[
38
]
.

For a surface
\Sigma
, a graph
H
embedded in
\Sigma
is its
simple cut-graph
if it has a single face homeomorphic to an open disc. In particular, the surface
\Sigma\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
obtained from
\Sigma
by cutting along the embedding of the edges of
H
is a disk.

Let
G
be a graph embedded in
\Sigma
and let
H
be its subgraph that is a simple cut-graph of
\Sigma
.
We define
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
as the graph embedded in
\Sigma\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
obtained from
G
as follows.
First, let
\sigma
be the (unique) facial walk of
H
. Each edge
e
of
H
is contained exactly twice in
\sigma
and each vertex
v
of
H
is contained in
\sigma
as many times as the degree of
v
in
H
.
To obtain
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
, we replace
H
with a simple cycle
C_{\sigma}
whose vertex set is the set of copies of vertices of
H
and whose edge set is the set of copies of edges of
H
defined the obvious way. Notice that
\sigma
also prescribes for every edge
uv
of
G
between a vertex
u\in V(G)\setminus V(H)
and a vertex
v\in V(H)
, to which copy of
v
in
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
the vertex

should be adjacent to in
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
.

The key component of our reduction is the following well-known result; see, e.g.
[
8
,
13
,
30
]
.

Lemma 9.1

For every integer
k\geq 1
and for every edge-weighted connected graph
G
embedded on a surface
\Sigma
of Euler genus at most
g
and every vertex
u\in V(G)
, there is a subgraph

of
G
with the following properties:

•

H
is a simple cut-graph of
\Sigma
, and

•

V(H)
is the union of the vertex sets of
\mathcal{O}(g)
shortest paths in
G
that have

as a common endpoint.

In particular, if
G
has diameter
\Delta
, then the set of copies of the vertices of
H
in
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
can be covered with
\mathcal{O}(g)
paths of length at most
\Delta
in
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
.

The upper bound proofs for
\varepsilon
-comatchings and
\varepsilon
-double ladders follow a very similar structure; hence, we will define the following notion of an
\varepsilon
-double semi-ladder to capture both structures.

Definition 9.2
(
\varepsilon
-double semi-ladder)

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(p_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{1},\mathfrak{t}_{1}),(p_{2},\mathfrak{b}_{2},\mathfrak{t}_{2}),\ldots,(p_{\ell},\mathfrak{b}_{\ell},\mathfrak{t}_{\ell})\}\subseteq V^{3}
be a sequence of
\ell

ordered triplets
of points in a metric
(V,\mathsf{dist})
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a parameter. We say that
\mathcal{D}
is an
\varepsilon
-double semi-ladder
if there exists
R>0
such that:

1.

for every
1\leq i<j\leq\ell
,

\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{j})\leq(1-\varepsilon)R\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathsf{dist}(p_{j},\mathfrak{b}_{i})\leq(1-\varepsilon)R.

2.

for every
1\leq i\leq\ell
,

\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{i})>R\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},\mathfrak{b}_{i})>R;

An
\varepsilon
-double semi-ladder instance is a pair
(G,\mathcal{D})
where
G
is a graph and
\mathcal{D}
is an
\varepsilon
-double semi-ladder in the graph metric induced by
G
.

Note that this notion generalizes both
\varepsilon
-comatchings and
\varepsilon
-double ladders: the former can be obtained by putting
\mathfrak{b}_{i}=\mathfrak{t}_{i}=q_{i}
and the latter is a special case where we also require the distances
\mathsf{dist}(p_{j},\mathfrak{t}_{i}),\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},\mathfrak{b}_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq\ell
to be large.
The reduction itself is realized by the following lemma.

Lemma 9.3

Fix any integer
g\geq 0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
(G,\mathcal{D}=\{(p_{i},\mathfrak{b}_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{i})\}_{i\in[L]})
be an
\varepsilon
-double semi-ladder instance such that
G
is embeddable in a surface of Euler genus at most
g
. Then, there exists a set of indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{L^{\prime}}\in[L]
, a planar graph
G^{\prime}
and a map
\eta:V(G)\to V(G^{\prime})
such that:

•

\mathsf{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(\eta(p_{j_{x}}),\eta(\mathfrak{t}_{j_{y}}))\geq\mathsf{dist}_{G}(p_{j_{x}},\mathfrak{t}_{j_{y}})
and
\mathsf{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(\eta(p_{j_{x}}),\eta(\mathfrak{b}_{j_{y}}))\geq\mathsf{dist}_{G}(p_{j_{x}},\mathfrak{b}_{j_{y}})
for
x,y\in[L^{\prime}]
,

•

\mathsf{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(\eta(p_{j_{x}}),\eta(\mathfrak{t}_{j_{y}}))=\mathsf{dist}_{G}(p_{j_{x}},\mathfrak{t}_{j_{y}})
for
1\leq x<y\leq L^{\prime}
,

•

\mathsf{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(\eta(p_{j_{y}}),\eta(\mathfrak{b}_{j_{x}}))=\mathsf{dist}_{G}(p_{j_{y}},\mathfrak{b}_{j_{x}})
for
1\leq x<y\leq L^{\prime}
,

•

L=\mathcal{O}(g^{4}\varepsilon^{-8})\cdot L^{\prime}
.

Let

R:=\min_{i\in[L]}\min(\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},\mathfrak{b}_{i}),\mathsf{dist}(p_{i},\mathfrak{t}_{i}))

and set
\delta:=\varepsilon R/100
. W.l.o.g. we can assume that every edge of
G
lies on either a path
\llbracket p_{i}\mathfrak{t}_{j}\rrbracket
or
\llbracket p_{j},\mathfrak{b}_{i}\rrbracket
for some
1\leq i<j\leq L
, and so by the same argument as in
7.4
, that the diameter of
G
is at most
8R
.

Let
u\in V(G)
be an arbitrary vertex of
V(G)
. Let
H
be a subgraph of
G
obtained by applying
Lemma

9.1
to
G
with an endpoint
u
. For
v\in V(G)
, let
\mu(v)
denote all vertices of
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
into which
v
is split after performing the cut. In particular,
\mu(v)=\{v\}
if
v\not\in V(H)
. For any
u,v\in V(G)
and
u^{\prime}\in\mu(u),v^{\prime}\in\mu(v)
, we have
\mathsf{dist}_{G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\geq\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)
, that is, the cutting procedure can only increase the distances between the vertices. Moreover, if
\mathsf{dist}_{G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)
, then the shortest path from
u
to
v
in
G
must cross
V(H)
. From each
\mu(v)
we select one vertex arbitrarily and call it
\eta(v)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be a projection of
\mathcal{D}
onto
G\textrm{\lx@mvs@CutLeft\lx@text@tweaked{xoffset,yoffset}{\CuttingLine}}H
, that is,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{(\eta(p_{i}),\eta(\mathfrak{b}_{i}),\eta(\mathfrak{t}_{i})):i\in[L]\}
. Note that all pairs
(\eta(p_{i}),\eta(\mathfrak{b}_{i}))
and
(\eta(p_{i}),\eta(\mathfrak{t}_{i}))
are still at distance at least
R
, however, some pairs
(\eta(p_{i}),\eta(\mathfrak{t}_{j}))
or
(\eta(p_{j}),\eta(\mathfrak{b}_{i}))
close in
\mathcal{D}
possibly also ended up far apart after cutting.

Let
X=\bigcup_{v\in V(H)}\mu(v)
. Since

\displaystyle\forall{u,v\in V(C^{i}),u\neq v}.

(4)

The local gadget.

Let
i\in[h]
. A
local gadget

H_{i}
consists of:

•

a cycle
C^{i}
;

•

a root vertex
r
connected to every vertex of the cycle
C^{i}
with a path of length
d_{i}
;

•

for every
v\in C^{i}
, a pendant vertex
\hat{v}
, connected to
v
with a
path of length
\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{j}
(note that for
i=1
we have just
\hat{v}=v
).

Note that the connections to the root
r
do not distort the distances between vertices

v\in V(C^{i})
in cycle gadgets and sets
X_{u}
, so (
4
) still holds
in a local gadget.

Arranging in a tree-like fashion.

We now arrange local gadgets in a tree-like fashion.
A subtree of level
h
is just a local gadget
H_{h}
and the root of the subtree is the root of
H_{h}
.
For
1\leq i<h
, a subtree of level
i
consists of a local gadget
H_{i}

plus, for every
v
on the cycle
C^{i}
in
H_{i}
, a copy of the subtree of level
i+1

with its root identified with
v
. The root of the subtree of level
i
is the root
of the local gadget
H_{i}
used in its construction.

A subtree of level
1
is the final graph
G
.
Note that
G
is planar, as the blocks of
G
are local gadgets
H_{i}

that are trivially planar.

Comatching definition.

Let
L
be the union of all vertices on cycles
C^{h}
in all level-
h
local gadgets
in the construction. Note that

|L|=\prod_{i=1}^{h}\left(1+k_{i}(2d_{i}+1)\right).

(5)

For a vertex
v\in L
, we denote
v^{h}=v
and for
1\leq i<h

by
v^{i}
we denote the root of the local gadget
H_{i+1}
on the path upwards from

v
in the construction.
As the shortest path from
v
to
v^{i}
consists of the paths from

v^{j}
to
v^{j-1}
(which is of length
d_{j}
) for
j=h,h-1,\ldots,i+1
,
we have

\mathsf{dist}(v,v^{i})=\sum_{j=i+1}^{h}d_{j}.

(6)

For every
v\in L
, construct a set
X_{v}
as follows.
For every
i\in[h]
, look at the local gadget
H_{i}
on the path upwards from
v
in the construction. Let
C^{i}
be the cycle in
H_{i}
. For every
u\in X^{i}_{v^{i}}
, insert
\hat{u}
into
X_{v}
.

Observe that

|X_{v}|=\sum_{i=1}^{h}k_{i}=k.

(7)

We claim that
(v,X_{v})_{v\in L}
is a
(k,d)
-comatching. We verify this in the next two lemmata.

Lemma 10.3

For every
v\in L
,
\mathsf{dist}(v,X_{v})\geq d+1
.

Let
\hat{u}\in X_{v}
.
Assume that
\hat{u}
is the pendant vertex attached to a vertex
u

of the cycle
C^{i}
in a local gadget
H_{i}
on the upward path from
v
in the construction.
Note that both
v^{i}
and
u
separate
v
from
\hat{u}
in
G
, thus

\mathsf{dist}(v,\hat{u})=\mathsf{dist}(v,v^{i})+\mathsf{dist}(v^{i},u)+\mathsf{dist}(u,\hat{u}).

The first term is computed in (
6
).
For the second term, by (
4
), as
u\in X^{i}_{v^{i}}
,

\mathsf{dist}(v^{i},u)\geq d_{i}+1.

Finally, the path from
u
to
\hat{u}
is of length

\mathsf{dist}(u,\hat{u})=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{j}.

The lemma follows as
d=\sum_{j=1}^{h}d_{j}
.

Lemma 10.4

For every two distinct
v,w\in L
, we have
\mathsf{dist}(v,X_{w})\leq d
.

Let
H_{i}
be the local gadget that is the lowest common ancestor of
v
and
w

in the construction.
As
v\neq w
, we have
v^{i}\neq w^{i}
.
By (
4
), there exists
u\in X^{i}_{w^{i}}

with
\mathsf{dist}(v^{i},u)\leq d_{i}
. We claim that
\mathsf{dist}(v,\hat{u})\leq d
;
this proves the lemma as
\hat{u}\in X_{w}
.
Clearly,

\mathsf{dist}(v,\hat{u})\leq\mathsf{dist}(v,v^{i})+\mathsf{dist}(v^{i},u)+\mathsf{dist}(u,\hat{u}).

The first term is computed in (
6
).
The second term is at most
d_{i}
by the choice of
u
.
Finally, the path from
u
to
\hat{u}
is of length

\mathsf{dist}(u,\hat{u})=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{j}.

The lemma follows as
d=\sum_{j=1}^{h}d_{j}
.

Choosing
t
,
k_{i}
and
d_{i}
.

For the final bound of Theorem
1.13
,
we choose
h=\min(k,d)
and split
k
into
k_{i}
s and
d
into
d_{i}
s as evenly as possible.
Then, if
k\leq d
, we have
h=k
,
k_{i}=1
for each
i\in[h]
, and

1+k_{i}(2d_{i}+1)=2d_{i}+2\geq 2\left\lfloor\frac{d}{k}\right\rfloor+2.

Otherwise, if
d\leq k
, we have
h=d
,
d_{i}=1
for each
i\in[h]
, and

1+k_{i}(2d_{i}+1)=1+3k_{i}\geq 3\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d}\right\rfloor+1.

The size of the constructed comatching follows from (
5
).
