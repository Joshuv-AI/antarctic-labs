Executive Summary
The rapid growth of renewable generation is creating challenges for the California grid in the
form of the “duck curve,” with increasingly steep generation ramping requirements and growing
curtailment of renewable resources. In response, the state is considering using dynamic
electricity tariffs that vary with grid conditions in near-real time to incentivize customers to shift
consumption and help flatten the duck curve. Dynamic tariffs raise concerns about the potential
for adverse financial impacts on utility customers, both in the magnitude and variability of their
utility bills, as well as equity issues arising from potential cost shifting among customer groups.
Further, the potential benefits of these tariffs, namely desirable changes to customer demand
that could result in cost savings on the grid, have not been well quantified. Prior research by this
study’s authors explored how dynamic tariffs impact customer bills if customers do not change
their demand in response to such tariffs (i.e., exhibit inelastic demand) (Gerke et al. 2022a, also
referred to as “the inelastic study”). The present study extends that work by modeling what
happens if customers respond elastically to changes in their electricity price relative to today’s
prevailing time-of-use (TOU) rates (i.e., exhibit elastic demand). Both studies build on a rich
dataset of 411,000 customer meter profiles for 2018-2019, and a resulting model of total
demand across all investor-owned utilities (IOUs) that was developed for Phase 4 of the CPUC
Demand Response Potential Study (Gerke et al. forthcoming).

The dynamic tariff studied here, presented in detail in Gerke et al. (2022a), is a multi-component
tariff that recovers all utility costs via a purely volumetric, hourly-varying electricity rate. In this
study, we use the “class-neutral” rates that are presented in the addendum to that report; these
rates are designed such that a customer with the class average load shape would have the
same bill under their business-as-usual (BAU) tariff as the new dynamic tariff. In response to
these time-varying prices, we estimated changes in hourly electricity consumption using the
economic concept of customer own-price elasticity of electricity demand. Multiple scenarios for
customer elasticity were studied, with the elasticity estimates originating from studies of real-
time pricing programs piloted or deployed across the U.S. (EPRI 2021a). Figure ES-1 shows the
elasticities assumed in this report; the Time Variant elasticity scenario is represented by the
bars and is taken directly from the literature. Residential elasticity varies from 0.04 to 0.18
across the day, while commercial and industrial (C&I) elasticity ranges from 0.05 to 0.26.1,2 Two
additional scenarios, Low and High elasticity, model the impacts if elasticity were constant
across the day at the lowest or highest values for each group of customers. Finally, to estimate
the possible impacts of widespread deployment of load flexibility technologies, an Automation
scenario was analyzed, in which the Time Variant elasticities are multiplied by a factor of 2.5
based on the findings of a meta-analysis of customer own-price elasticity of electricity demand
(Neenan and Eom 2008).

These elasticities were used to calculate hypothetical customer response to the dynamic tariff
for all residential, commercial, and industrial customers in California. To do so, customer BAU
time-of-use tariffs were converted into approximate hourly values, with fixed costs and demand
charges distributed across all hours. Then, the price difference between each customer’s BAU
tariff and the dynamic tariff in each hour could be calculated and used with the hourly elasticity
estimate to determine the change in customer demand in each hour. These impacts are
aggregated to the system level and used to assess impacts to the grid via the net load, daily
ramping requirements, effective demand response (DR) resources, and metrics that describe
the system need for DR. This analysis assumes all customers enroll in, and respond according
to their elasticity to, the dynamic tariff, though the results section will discuss how bulk power
system impacts may scale with participation level.

Impact of dynamic prices on customer bills
Customer bills were calculated for current (business-as-usual, BAU) rates, and under the
dynamic tariff with varying levels of elastic load response, both with and without a subscription

option5. Results for these bills are summarized via two metrics: effective average rate and bill
variability. Effective average rate (in units of cents per kWh) is used to assess the overall cost of
electricity to each customer. This metric is calculated as the total annual bill divided by total
annual consumption and is used in this study to account for changes in the total bill due to both
changes in the cost of electricity and the quantity of electricity consumed. Because this study
considers an elastic response to dynamic prices, customers may have a net annual increase or
decrease in consumption depending on their baseline tariff relative to hourly prices, and
therefore presenting the effective average rate controls for this change. Bill variability, which
was introduced in the inelastic study, captures the month-to-month variation in customers’ bills
relative to their average bill.

Figure ES-4 shows the median value of effective average rate across residential customers with
and without behind-the-meter photovoltaic (PV) systems, while Figure ES-5 shows the same for
C&I customers without PV in small, medium, and large size categories. It should be noted that
for PV customers, we divide their total bills by their total electricity consumption, including self-
generation, and therefore their baseline rates are dramatically lower than non-PV customers. In
general, PV customers are structural non-benefiters on the dynamic rates designed for this
study. This is because we assume a symmetric price (where customer electricity exports are
compensated at the same hourly value as imports would be charged), and dynamic prices are
typically lowest during the daytime, when PV customers are most likely to export power. This
reduces the compensation PV-exporting customers receive. Because PV and non-PV
customers coexist within each customer class, the lower compensation for PV customers
translates to lower costs for non-PV customers, and thus the majority of non-PV customers save
money on a dynamic rate, relative to BAU tariffs, even if they do not respond to the rate.
Whereas the lower bound of the PV customer bill distribution (i.e., the PV customers with the
lowest net annual bills) is higher on a dynamic rate, relative to BAU tariffs. It should be noted

In the residential sector, non-PV customers save 0.8 ¢/kWh on average by responding to the

Impact of dynamic pricing on emissions
As customers change their load in response to dynamic prices, the mix of generation resources
and the associated emissions also change. The change in emissions resulting from small
 viii

changes in demand in a given hour – a metric known as the marginal emission factor – can be
used to quantify the total emissions impacts of the changes in system load studied here. We
modeled marginal emissions factors for every hour in our 2019 baseline load model, using the
avoided cost calculator framework6 to mimic the expected relationship between marginal
emissions and wholesale prices. We then multiply the marginal emissions rates by the change
in hourly system-wide demand throughout the year, and aggregate to calculate the total change
in emissions. Figure ES-8 shows the resulting emissions impacts of dynamic pricing in the short
term, under Time Variant elasticity, throughout the year.

When responding elastically to dynamic prices, total electricity demand increased (2%)7, while
total annual emissions increased only very slightly (by 114,000 metric tons, or 0.3% of CAISO’s
reported 2019 emissions). This means that new load is added to the grid at a marginal
emissions rate of 0.033 kg/kWh, substantially lower than the baseline average of 0.238 kg/kWh.
When all electricity demand in the state is considered, this also means that response to dynamic
prices causes a 1.7% reduction in average per-kWh emissions. As shown in Figure ES-8,
dynamic prices effectively reduce emissions in the evenings, though these reductions are offset
by the increase in consumption in other hours. We find that prices are not always well aligned
with marginal emissions rates; this primarily occurs in the winter when there are times that
wholesale prices are higher (representing operation of fossil fuel plants that correlate to higher
emissions), despite system net load being relatively low (which leads to low dynamic prices
being sent to customers). Therefore, customers are at times incentivized by these prices to
increase load even if emissions are higher than average. If loads were to be given a price signal
more closely aligned with emissions (or given an emissions signal directly), we would expect a
larger decrease in reductions from load response.

Conclusions
Several key takeaways emerge from this analysis. In the short term, dynamic pricing can
provide sizable system load impacts across the year. Assuming that most customers are
enrolled, load impacts in peak net load hours are on par with current demand response
programs, even if customers exhibit low elasticity. With widespread adoption of dynamic rates,
and moderate to high elasticity, the system could be meaningfully reshaped to significantly
reduce peak net loads and ramping requirements, even in the face of increases in total annual
demand. Further, we see a subtle reduction in emissions in parallel with these lower costs,
representing an additional societal benefit.

However, our results show that the financial incentive to respond to the dynamic rate is quite
small. Assuming customers have a choice, it is important that the expected benefits of selecting
the dynamic tariff over other alternatives outweigh the costs. These costs may include hard
costs such as technology that enables the customer to actually respond to the tariff, perceived
or actual risk of bill increases, or transaction costs from understanding and selecting these more
complex tariffs. It is possible that a few percentage points of bill savings will not be enough to
encourage customer selection. Accordingly, as California rolls out dynamic tariffs it will be
important to develop and make available technologies that offer co-benefits (such as
convenience or greater control) to customers, while also offering incentive programs for
adoption of technologies that can more easily respond to prices.

It is important to remember the context and limitations of this study. First, the modeling and
results presented here represent 2019 customer loads being served by the 2019 electricity grid,
and both customer loads and the energy technologies that serve them are evolving rapidly. The
Phase 4 DR Potential Study, which projected demand and VRE generation to 2050, showed a
 x

drastic rise in newly electrified loads – particularly for electric vehicle (EV) charging, but also for
water heating, space heating, and appliances (Gerke et al. 2022b). These new loads
significantly alter hourly demand: peak net load and gross load each rise by almost 50% by
2050, and peak net load moves from August to November. Net load ramps steepen, with the
daily net load curve beginning to dip below zero by 2025 and doing so consistently by 2040.
These changes will have profound impacts on system prices, as well as both the need and the
ability of load to respond to these prices, likely increasing the benefits of price response as
loads become more flexible and daily price differentials become more pronounced. While it is
challenging to model future dynamic prices (mainly due to a lack of wholesale price forecasting
models), it will be important to consider the future grid along with the responsiveness of new
flexible loads in future research.

Additionally, the limitations of the elastic response model employed in this study should be
noted. The elasticity values employed herein are based on limited data, which is somewhat
dated, and applied very coarsely to all customers and end-use loads. Further, while elasticity is
a good high-level representation of customers’ response to varying price signals, the actual
mechanisms of load response, whether via behavioral modifications or load flexibility
technologies, may vary substantially from the general theory of price-elasticity response.
Flexible loads will likely employ “smart” controls, optimization algorithms, or other frameworks
when responding to dynamic prices. Modeling these load responses more accurately, at the
end-use level, will be especially important in light of the load changes expected in the future
(e.g. from increased EV adoption and demand). As new technologies enable customer
responsiveness to dynamic prices, quantifying the behavior and magnitude of load response will
significantly benefit future research on dynamic rates.

1 Introduction

1.1 The need for flexible loads
The rapid growth of variable renewable energy (VRE) generation poses challenges for the
California Independent System Operator (CAISO) and California utilities in managing the
California power grid. A significant and growing penetration of solar photovoltaic (PV)
generation, in particular, means that the system net load (i.e., gross demand less VRE
generation), which must be met by dispatchable generation resources, takes on a profile with
sharp morning and evening peaks and a midday trough, with steep ramps at sunrise and
sunset. This profile, often referred to as the “duck curve” (CAISO 2016), means that periods of
low demand, such as the winter and spring seasons, see frequent curtailment of renewable
resources to avoid oversupply (CAISO 2019a). This curtailment represents a significant
opportunity cost in the form of increased generation costs and foregone emissions reductions.
In addition, the steep ramps mean that meeting morning and evening peak loads require either
a substantial quantity of flexible generation resources that can rapidly increase and decrease
generation or less flexible resources to run as base load throughout the day, further increasing
curtailment of VRE. This places additional constraints on the available generation portfolio to
meet peak loads, further driving up costs. During high-demand periods, the inherent
unpredictability of VRE generation adds additional challenges, as during the August 2020
California heat event, in which a sudden reduction in wind contributed to a grid emergency and
subsequent rolling blackouts (CAISO, CPUC, and CEC 2021). In addition to these challenges,
meeting California’s decarbonization goals will require widespread electrification of major end
uses such as space heating, water heating, and transportation, which may create new
difficulties for grid management.

Given this set of challenges, there has been increasing recognition of the value in demand-side
flexibility, both to reduce loads during extreme peak periods, as well as to shift the timing of
loads to reduce curtailment and flatten daily ramps. The second phase of CPUC’s California
Demand Response (DR) Potential Study (Alstone et al. 2017) identified four different types of
DR that can provide different services to the grid, including traditional shed DR for dispatchable
peak reduction, shift DR for dispatchable load-shifting, shimmy DR for ancillary services, and
shape DR, which provides both shed and shift value through behavioral reshaping of customer
demand on an ongoing basis via time-varying electricity rates. Considerable attention was paid
in the ensuing period to the potential of shift DR and pathways to enabling it in California (Gallo
et al. 2018; Gridworks 2019; Gerke et al. 2020). More recently, summer peak load management
challenges and resulting grid emergencies (CAISO, CPUC, and CEC 2021; CAISO 2022) have
motivated a renewed focus on new pathways to enhance California’s shed DR resources.
Against this backdrop, a variety of research efforts has sought to assess and develop
technological pathways for enabling energy efficiency and demand flexibility as grid resources,
both in California (Piette et al. 2022) and nationally (Satchwell et al. 2021; Langevin et al. 2021).

 1

The need for load flexibility to manage both extreme peaks and routine ramps suggests that
demand-side load reshaping could be particularly effective.

It has long been argued (Borenstein, Jaske, and Rosenfeld 2002) that dynamic retail electricity
rates reflecting the time-dependent cost of generating and delivering electricity may be an
effective way to capture beneficial load flexibility. The most familiar version of this is so-called
real-time pricing (RTP), in which the time-varying wholesale price of electricity is included as the
energy component of a retail tariff. Utilities have substantial experience with such tariffs, though
uptake has historically been low (Barbose, Goldman, and Neenan 2004), and numerous studies
have shown that customers respond elastically to such tariffs by reducing load during high-
priced periods and increasing load in response to low prices (Herriges et al. 1993; Patrick and
Wolak 2001; Schwarz et al. 2002; Taylor, Schwarz, and Cochell 2005; Boisvert et al. 2007;
Faruqui and Sergici 2010; Neenan and Eom 2008; Allcott 2011; EPRI 2021a). Economic
modeling studies have shown that such responses can have significant grid benefits in the form
of reduced system costs (Borenstein 2006; Holland and Mansur 2006; Imelda, Fripp, and
Roberts 2022).

In June 2022, staff at the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) issued a white paper
describing a dynamic pricing framework called the California Flexible Unified Signal for Energy
(CalFUSE). Following its release, the CPUC opened a rulemaking proceeding (CPUC 2022) to
consider modifying its electricity ratemaking principles and establish guiding principles for
developing dynamic tariffs to enable load flexibility. In a related action, the California Energy
Commission (CEC) adopted new load management standards requiring California utilities to
offer hourly-varying rates to customers (CEC 2022); CalFUSE represents one possible
implementation of such rates. The CalFUSE concept is based on a pilot study (Cazalet,
Kohanim, and Hasidim 2020), performed for the (CEC in Southern California Edison (SCE)
territory, called the Retail Automated Transactive Energy System (RATES). The RATES tariff
goes beyond traditional RTP by incorporating multiple dynamic components, including
wholesale prices as well as the costs of marginal generation and delivery system capacity, with
the capacity cost component increasing rapidly as loads approach system capacity limits.8 Two
follow-on pilots using RATES-like tariffs are now underway in the service territories of SCE and
Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E).

quantify their potential impacts on system load shapes, grid costs, and customer bills. A recent
study by the authors of the present report (Gerke et al. 2022a) developed a hypothetical multi-
component dynamic tariff, conceptually similar to the RATES tariff, and considered the impacts

Understanding the impacts of a dynamic tariff on system load shape and system costs,
however, requires considering how customers might respond elastically to a dynamic rate in
order to reduce their electricity bills. Phase 4 of the California DR Potential Study (Gerke et al.

fraction of the technical potential may be available at relatively low cost. This study utilized a
nominal dynamic tariff and a single value of elasticity (for high and low elasticity scenarios)
informed by prior studies (Neenan and Eom 2008). However, it considered only the load
modifications that might occur during periods when supply-side shed or shift DR were likely to
be dispatched, rather than computing the full system benefits of load reshaping that might occur
throughout the year.

1.3 Overview of this study
In this report, we model the hourly effects of elastic customer response to the hypothetical multi-
component dynamic tariff that we considered in the inelastic study; more specifically, we use the
class-neutral rates presented in the inelastic study addendum. We analyze the impacts on
system load shapes, customer bills, and system costs across all hours of the year. To do this,
we estimate the customer price elasticity of electricity demand and apply that to a set of
representative customer load shapes derived from hourly meter data obtained for the Phase 4
DR Potential Study. We consider the impacts of customer response in the short term, where
dynamic rates are set based on past baseline demand, though in reality the changes in load
response would eventually have an impact on price formation. To model how the market might
adjust to changing customer demand, we develop a detailed regression model to distinguish
how wholesale prices respond to changes in system load among a wide array of other important
variables.

As a first attempt at estimating the hourly impacts of a multi-component dynamic tariff, this study
is subject to several important boundaries and limitations. We consider one specific dynamic
tariff structure in this study and note that other structures are possible within the broader
CalFUSE framework. Specifically, as in the inelastic study, we consider a dynamic tariff
structure that recovers all costs (including non-marginal costs) via a purely volumetric, time-
varying rate, with no fixed charges or demand charges. The tariff is assumed to be based on the

load forecasts and wholesale prices from the CAISO day-ahead market. We do not consider
elements with a higher time resolution, such as spot market prices or transactive elements on

penetration of VRE, changes in market structure and electricity prices, etc.). Differently
structured dynamic tariffs would have different amplitudes of hourly variation and therefore yield

subscription-based tariff, based on the customer’s actual load shape from a different year. As
we found in the inelastic study, different subscription options can yield significantly different
impacts on customer bills.

Additionally, when estimating customer load response to the dynamic tariff, we use a simple
framework based on an hourly “own-price” elasticity, in which customers respond simply by
increasing or decreasing their consumption in response to the price within each hour, without
considering more complex substitution or arbitrage strategies that consider differences in price
from hour to hour throughout the day. We also apply the same elasticity to the full load of all
customers in a given sector without considering the possibility that different end-uses or
subsectors might have different elasticities. Further, because we were not able to identify data
on the elasticity of agricultural customers, we did not model the response of such customers in
this study; including these customers in future studies may contribute significant additional
flexibility. Finally, in this report, we consider the impacts that might occur from universal
adoption of such tariffs by all customers and note how impacts will scale with lower enrollment
and participation.

In the next section, we describe the methodology used in this study, including the underlying
customer load data; our assumptions about customer elasticity; the hypothetical multi-
component dynamic tariff; the approaches to estimating customer load response, and the
resulting impacts on customer bills and system loads in the short term; our wholesale price
model and method for estimating price impacts; and our metrics for quantifying the system
impacts. Section 3 then describes the impacts on customer bills and system loads that could be
expected in the short term from our hypothetical dynamic tariff, including the effective shed and
shift DR resources that the load impact represents. Finally, we discuss the implications of these
findings and the ways in which this study can inform the development of future real-world
dynamic electricity tariffs.

2 Methodology
Figure 1 presents a schematic representation of the modeling steps. To estimate the impacts of
customer response to dynamic pricing, we leverage a set of representative hourly load shapes

Potential Study (Gerke et al. forthcoming), as well as individual customer load shape data that
was used in the development of those clusters. To model impacts on the system load shape, we

non-residential customers. These constitute the set of inputs for the customer-level analysis

individual cluster load shapes and calculate the new bills that the customers would face under
the modified load shape.

Summing the resulting cluster load shapes yields the modified system load shape, which can be
summarized through analysis of peak load, ramping requirements, and “effective DR”. Using the
system load shape, we also calculate various metrics that assess the system need for further
DR, which indicates the benefits of the customer response. Another key system impact is how
wholesale prices will change when serving the new, modified load as opposed to the baseline
load. To estimate this, we develop a regression model that predicts how the wholesale
electricity price will change in response to changes in load, based on actual market data from
2018-2019. The remainder of this section provides details on each step of the modeling
process.

2.1 Customer load data
Customer load data was collected as part of the Phase 4 of California DR potential study. The
data request and collection took place in two stages. The first stage provided demographic
information of all active accounts in the three service territories, from which 3% of accounts
were sampled. In the second stage, LBNL received 2018 and 2019 time series data for the
sampled accounts. This data was further subjected to several steps of preprocessing to account
for rooftop PV generation and estimate consumption during events of extreme heat when power
is shut off. After preprocessing, LBNL developed a multi-step clustering approach to cluster
customers based on similarities in demographic and geographic characteristics and load shape
patterns. A brief summary of each step is provided below.

2.1.1 Customer meter data
The first stage of the data request involved receiving descriptive information on all customer
accounts that were active as of 2019 across the three California IOU service territories, a total of
13.6 million accounts. Data included customer location, tariff code, NAICS10 code, CARE11 rate
subsidy status, annual energy consumption, peak demand, and CEC Title 24 climate zone.
From this data, 3% of customer accounts were sampled and their AMI data for 201812 and 2019
was requested. The stratified sampling approach ensured that the sampled accounts
adequately represented diversity across all the customer groups in California. Then, two weights
were computed for each sampled customer – a customer weight and an energy use weight –
intended to represent the total number of customers, as well as the total energy use each
sampled customer represented in the sample.

The second stage of the data request involved receiving AMI data for over 400,000 customers
across all sectors. Significant preprocessing of this data was required before it could be used as
inputs to load modeling. This involved correcting for PV generation for customers with rooftop
solar, as well as estimating the hourly consumption of affected customers during Public Safety
Power Shutoff (PSPS) events (which are generally called to mitigate wildfire risks due to
extreme heat). Details about these steps are presented in Gerke et al. (forthcoming). This step
is followed by clustering customers, which is summarized in the following subsection. Finally,
CAISO system load was modeled based on customers’ AMI data for 2019.

2.2 Customer elasticity assumptions and scenarios
Own-price elasticity (referred to simply as “elasticity” throughout this report) is an economic
concept in which each time period (i.e., each hour of the day) is considered independently of the
other hours. A customer is assigned an own-price elasticity value in each hour and reacts to
price changes in that hour according to this value without considering prices in other hours.
Own-price elasticity represents the percentage change in demand for a 1% change in price,
where the demand changes in the inverse direction of the price. For example, if an own price
elasticity is 0.10, and the dynamic hourly price is double the BAU tariff (i.e., 100% price
increase), people will respond by decreasing consumption by 10% within that hour.

The degree to which a customer reacts to a tariff with day-ahead dynamic prices (dynamic tariff)
can be modeled in several ways. For this study, we choose elasticity for two reasons. First, we
aim for an approach that is easy to explain and verify. Second, when comparing different
approaches, we found elasticity to have the best empirical data in the literature on which to base
our assumptions. For similar reasons, several past studies that have modeled the impacts of
dynamic pricing on system loads have also used an approach based on elasticity (Borenstein
2006; Imelda, Fripp, and Roberts 2022). By using a method that can trace its inputs and
assumptions back to empirical observations, the intent is to ground our estimates in reality as
much as possible and avoid speculative hypotheses about how customers will adapt their
electricity consumption to changes in prices. Other candidate modeling approaches, such as
substitution elasticity and linear optimization, require additional investigation to find reliable input
data and assumptions, and thus will only be considered in future research.

The 2021 Benchmarking Study of U.S. Regulated Utility Real Time Pricing Programs,
Architecture and Design (EPRI 2021a) by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) lists
RTP own-price elasticity estimates from other studies in the literature (Table 5-2 and Figure 5-1
of the benchmarking study). In the cited studies, hourly elasticity values are given both for
residential and commercial and industrial (C&I) customers. The elasticity values for residential
customers are derived from a load impact study for the Power Smart Pricing RTP program
offered by Ameren Illinois (Navigant 2011). As shown in Figure 2, they follow a smooth diurnal
pattern with the highest values from 4-6pm and the lowest values from 4-6am. For the C&I
customers, the elasticity values are derived from a study of multiple different RTP programs in
the C&I sectors (Taylor, Schwarz, and Cochell 2005). Here, the hourly pattern is much more
irregular: there is a high variability in the hour-to-hour elasticity (see Figure 2). The C&I elasticity
values before noon are generally lower than for residential customers, followed by a spike at
1pm, a sharp drop at 4pm, and a rebound at 5pm that persists throughout the evening. It is
difficult to explain this variation, and it is hard to know how broadly applicable these values are.

2.3 Tariff assumptions and scenarios
In this study, we calculate customer response to a dynamic tariff based on the difference
between the hourly dynamic electricity rate and the effective rate that customers pay under
prevailing present-day tariffs, which we refer to throughout as “business-as-usual” (BAU) tariffs.
This section details the approach to computing hourly dynamic rates and comparable hourly
BAU rates against which to compute the changes in price experienced by customers under
dynamic pricing.

2.3.1 Dynamic tariff framework and assumptions
We model customer response to a hypothetical multi-component dynamic tariff with an optional
subscription component, using a tariff structure that is identical to the one used in the inelastic
study addendum. In this section, we briefly summarize the different cost components used in
the tariff, how they vary with grid conditions, and how they are parameterized and calibrated.
We also describe the subscription-based tariff and the approach to determining a subscription
load shape for each customer. The tariff represents an example implementation of concepts in
the CPUC CalFUSE white paper (Madduri et al. 2022) and is based specifically on the tariff
used in the CEC RATES pilot (Cazalet, Kohanim, and Hasidim 2020). However, the tariff differs
from these papers in that it is class-neutral as opposed to universal. We direct readers to the
inelastic study (Gerke et al. 2022a) for full details of the tariff framework.

Dynamic tariff structure and cost components
The tariff structure we consider is designed to recover all required utility revenues via a single
tariff that applies to each customer class in a given IOU, where customer classes are defined
based on the otherwise applicable tariff for each customer, shown in Table 1. The tariff is purely
volumetric, with a $/kWh rate for electricity consumption that varies hourly and no other charges
such as demand charges or fixed charges. This structure is intended to represent a maximalist
 10

approach to a tariff that provides a time-varying price signal that reflects real-time grid
conditions. In a real-world application, some of the costs that are recovered through the
volumetric rate might instead be recovered through fixed or demand charges, which would mute
the hourly price signal experienced by customers. For the purposes of calculating customer
response and market dynamics, we also assume that the tariff has day-ahead notification to
customers, with the hourly rates for each day computed and presented to customers in the
evening on the previous day, based on day-ahead load forecasts from CAISO and the IOUs and
on wholesale prices from the CAISO day-ahead market (DAM).

Our hypothetical multi-component dynamic tariff recovers revenue for several different utility
cost components through an hourly-varying rate as follows:
 ● Wholesale prices for energy delivered to customers are passed through directly based
 on final hourly prices from the CAISO day-ahead market, aggregated to the default load
 aggregation point (DLAP) associated with each IOU’s service territory.
 ● System losses associated with delivering energy to customers are assumed to scale
 quadratically with the gross load in each IOU’s service territory, with a coefficient chosen
 so that average assumed losses match the average losses reported by each IOU.
 ● Marginal generation capacity costs are recovered according to a piecewise quadratic
 function of the hourly net load (gross load minus VRE generation) on the CAISO system,
 with the generation capacity cost being positive above, and negative below, a given
 threshold value of the net load. The coefficient of this function is chosen to recover peak
 generation capacity costs16 approved in each IOU’s most recent general rate case
 (GRC) proceeding during the peak net load hour. The threshold value is set at the
 highest load at which negative wholesale prices occur, which we take as a proxy for the
 point at which it is desirable to incentivize increased utilization of existing VRE capacity.
 ● Marginal flexible generation capacity costs are recovered according to a quadratic
 function of the three-hour ramp leading up to each hour, reflecting the amount of fast-
 ramping generation required to meet load in that hour. The coefficient of this function is
 chosen to recover each IOU’s marginal cost for flexible generation capacity, as
 calculated from its most recent GRC filings, during the maximum ramp.
 ● Marginal distribution-system capacity costs are recovered according to a piecewise
 quadratic function of the hourly gross load on each IOU’s grid, with the cost being
 positive above, and negative below, a threshold set at 15% of the maximum system
 capacity, which is estimated as 115% of peak load. The coefficient is chosen to recover
 the marginal distribution capacity cost from each IOU’s most recent GRC proceeding,
 during the peak gross load hour.

Subscription-based tariff structure
When computing customer bills under the dynamic tariff, we follow the approach of the inelastic
study by computing bills for scenarios with and without a subscription component. The
subscription component allows customers to pre-purchase electricity at their present-day (BAU)
tariff based on a pre-determined load shape and quantity that may be specific to a customer
class or an individual customer. Deviations from the subscribed load are then billed or credited
at the hourly dynamic rate. In this way, any marginal change in the customer’s energy
consumption is always exposed to the dynamic rate, but the bulk of the customer’s consumption
will typically be billed at the BAU tariff. This structure allows customers to experience stable and
predictable bills, while still being incentivized to shift marginal loads away from high-cost
periods.

In the inelastic study, we considered several different options for subscription load shapes, and
we found that customer bill impacts were minimized across all customer classes by a “self”
subscription, in which the customer subscribes to their own load from a previous year. In this
study, we consider only this self-subscription option when computing subscription-based bills;
and since we are analyzing customer loads from 2019, each customer is assumed to subscribe
to a load defined by their actual 2018 hourly consumption. Customers used slightly less
electricity in 2019 relative to 2018: for residential customers, the median ratio for 2019 to 2018
demand was 0.992, while for C&I customers the median ratio was 0.984. Half of residential
customers consumed within +/- 13% of their 2018 demand in 2019, while for C&I customers the
variation was even smaller, at -10% (25th percentile) to +7% (75th percentile).

2.3.2 Business-as-usual effective hourly rates
To be able to compare present-day BAU rates with the dynamic rate, our approach is to convert
the BAU rates into an effective purely volumetric rate. The challenge is that BAU rates have
fixed costs and demand charges that are not directly related to the customer’s consumption in
any given hour. In contrast, our dynamic tariff structure is purely volumetric; its only component
is an energy charge. To compare a BAU tariff with the dynamic tariff, we therefore translate all
non-volumetric components into an assumed volumetric component, “fixed/demand adder.” The
sum of the fixed/demand adder and the tariff’s energy charge results in an “effective” $/kWh
hourly BAU rate that is more directly comparable to the fully volumetric dynamic tariff. We refer
to this as the “effective baseline rate.” The effective baseline rate then represents the rate that
customers are used to, and any deviation of the dynamic rate from the effective baseline rate
results in a response in consumption behavior based on the model’s elasticity assumptions.

To calculate the portion of marginal cost stemming from non-volumetric costs in any given
month, we can divide them by the customer’s overall monthly consumption to yield an average
 13

per-kWh cost. This results in a fixed/demand adder, which is added to the customer’s volumetric
energy rate in each hour. This is shown on a per-sector basis in Figure 4. Note that customers
are grouped by their BAU rate to compute their monthly average fixed/demand adder. While it is
generally possible to compute the fixed/demand adder per customer, doing so introduces
significant noise in our results because customers with very small consumption (e.g., 100 kWh
per month) and fixed bill components of $25/month for their meter would be assigned a
fixed/demand adder of 25 ¢/kWh, doubling their BAU rate. Further, some customers have
negative monthly consumption due to behind-the-meter PV generation, which would result in
negative fixed/demand adders. Therefore, to reduce noise in the baseline rates, we decided to
average the consumption and monthly bills across all customers of a BAU rate, getting more
reasonable fixed/demand adders.

As shown in the leftmost subplot of Figure 4, residential customers have a relatively low fixed
cost, with the non-volumetric portion of their BAU rate between 1.8 ¢/kWh and 4.2 ¢/kWh each
month of 2019. The other three subplots show that many non-residential customers have much
higher, and much more variable, fixed/demand adders. This is because many BAU rates for
non-residential customers include demand charges, which are based on peak load and
therefore are generally much higher in summer than the rest of the year. Customers with low
peak demand are on different BAU tariffs (TOU-GS-1, B1, and TOU-A) than those with high
peaks, and therefore those customers have lower fixed/demand adders. However, because of
their low consumption, the marginal cost per kWh can still be higher than for high consumption
customers, as the fixed and demand charges are spread across a smaller amount of
consumption (e.g., B-10 vs. B-20 in the winter months).

2.4 Modeling elastic customer response
Load impacts are modeled in three steps, described in more detail in the following subsections.
First, elastic load impacts are modeled for clusters of customers (described in Section 2.1.2)
using the elasticity assumptions for each given scenario. Clusters give us complete coverage of
all modeled load in California, and modeling load impacts on clusters is important in enabling
calculation of total system impacts. However, clusters are not useful for calculating customer bill
impacts, as there is likely significant load variation within each cluster that meaningfully impacts
customer bills. Further, calculating bills on the cluster level would “smooth out” impacts from
customers that behave quite differently within the same cluster. Instead, cluster load shape
impacts are translated to impacts on individual customers’ load shapes. The second step is to
model resulting bill impacts for individual customers’ time series data, based on the load
impacts calculated on the cluster (and then customer) level. Two metrics are used to describe
the impact on bills, effective average rate and bill variability; these metrics are described in
 15

detail below. Finally, cluster-level load impacts are aggregated to calculate system-level
impacts, which are used to calculate the grid-level metrics described in Section 2.6.

2.4.1 Load shape impacts
Cluster-level impacts

about the elasticity of the customer’s load in each hour. Since we are working from a baseline
load shape with an associated BAU tariff, the difference between the new dynamic price in each

the dynamic price is higher than BAU, customers will reduce their consumption in that hour, and
if the dynamic price is lower than BAU, customers will take advantage of low prices and
increase consumption.

The dynamic and self-subscription bills can then be compared to BAU bills, as well as the

describing the impacts of dynamic rates, and of various levels of load response, on customer
bills: effective average rate and bill variability. These metrics are described in more detail below.

Effective Average Rate
The inelastic study reported results for the change in absolute dollar values of customer bills.
But when customers respond elastically to dynamic prices, the absolute value of the bill is not a
useful result, as customers are getting a different service level (via electricity consumed) before
and after the dynamic tariff is applied. Customers reduce consumption when prices are high,
lowering their bills, but these lower bills are not necessarily a positive due to the lower level of
service. Conversely, customers increase consumption when prices are lower than the BAU rate;
this may result in bills going up, even though prices were low. Instead of focusing on absolute
dollar values, or fractional changes in absolute amounts, a metric is developed to capture
whether the amount customers typically pay for a given service level is changing.

This metric is the “effective average rate.” It represents the average price paid for each unit of
electricity consumed by the customer, calculated as their total bill divided by their total
consumption in that year. For NEM customers, estimated PV self-consumption is included as
part of their total consumption.

Bill variability
In addition to concerns about changes to average energy bills, customers transitioning to a
dynamic tariff may reasonably be concerned about changes in the month-to-month variability of
their bill, since unexpectedly high bills in some months can have a negative impact on
household or business cash flow, even if the average monthly bill impact is small. Thus, we

2.5 Wholesale price calculation
Dynamic price response can effectively mitigate the upward pressure on wholesale electricity
prices during periods of high demand and low supply. For assessing the short-term effects of
dynamic tariffs, our model calculates dynamic tariff values and predicts elastic demand
responses at both the customer and system levels, assuming initial market conditions remain
constant. However, for a more realistic representation, we account for the market's ability to
adapt to the reshaped demand. These effects can be refined through iterative processes, where
electricity prices and impacts on demand are recalibrated at both the system and customer
levels. As a preview, we present wholesale prices that have been recalculated based on
updated system-level net loads, reflecting customer responses to the dynamic rates in real-time.
While not detailed here, the model can iterate until a new equilibrium is achieved.

regression model to describe the price-dependent day-ahead supply curve. In this equation,

the year and the net load requested at hour t) and maximum net load (Cappers 2005; Oh 2003).

limited, the ratio decreases and prices rise. The model has the advantage of capturing sharp
increases near the maximum net demand while avoiding issues of applying the inverse of very
low to negative wholesale prices.23

2.6 Quantifying system-level impacts
Dynamic tariffs present an opportunity to reduce load during the highest-price hours and shift it
into lower-price hours. These load impacts can be quantified through the lens of traditional
demand response resources in order to present the impact of dynamic tariffs in units that may
be more familiar to relevant audiences. We calculate the effective shed and shift DR resources
(see Section 1.1 for DR terminology and background) that are realized through the dynamic rate
load impacts modeled in this study, as well as how these DR resources impact system load
shape and the need for further procurement of DR or other flexible resources.

2.6.1 Effective shed and shift
In order to quantify the system-level load impacts of responding to a dynamic tariff, we calculate
the effective shed and shift DR resources, which represent the additional demand response-
type resource that is made available by implementing a dynamic tariff. We use inputs from the
DR-Path model developed in Phase 4 of the CPUC DR Potential Study (Gerke et al.

 24

forthcoming) to calculate these values. Specifically, we use the dispatch probability of shed and
shift DR in each hour of the year, based on actual net load values in 2019. In short, the
probability of needing shed DR is highest during peak load hours, while the probability of
dispatching shift DR is based on the steepness of the 3-hour net load.

Effective shed, then, is the average load reduction in each hour, weighted by the shed dispatch
probability. Effective shift is the average amount of energy shifted across 3-hour windows,
weighted by the probability of shift in those hours. To calculate the effective shed and shift DR
values, we first calculate system-level load impacts as described in Section 2.4.3. We then
multiply the hourly net load from each scenario with the dispatch probability of shed or shift DR.
The sum of these values over a period of 8760 hours gives the total DR resource available in
each scenario. The difference between this value in a given scenario and the inelastic scenario
yields the effective DR value available via a change in the load shape.

2.6.2 System-level need for DR
Further, we quantify how the system need for DR changes in response to dynamic rates. Since
a change in price elicits a customer response to increase or decrease load in a given hour, we
can expect significant changes in hourly net load values. These changes in net load will in turn
affect the need for DR at the system level. To summarize the impact of changes to the net load
shape, we computed system metrics recently described in the literature (Murthy, Satchwell, and
Gerke 2022) that can capture how the need for shed and shift DR changes for different net
loads. We can summarize the most important changes in the system’s load shape using two
sets of metrics, as defined in Table 3. (Murthy et al. describe a third set of metrics related to DR
program design, but those are less relevant to this study and hence not evaluated.)

Table 3. Definition of metrics for describing the system-level need for DR.

 Category Metric name Metric Definition

 Shed DR Peak load Maximum value of hourly net load in a year

 Peakiness Height of the peak net load above the 100th highest net load

 Shift DR Routine ramping Maximum daily absolute 3-hour net load ramp on the day

As shown in Table 3, two metrics quantify how a system’s need for shed DR changes. The
value of shed DR comes from its ability to reduce the need for expensive peaker plants that
exclusively operate to serve load during peak hours. Therefore, these metrics are based on the
system’s top 100 net load values. In the first metric, we evaluate changes to peak net load
values. An increase in the peak net load indicates an increased need for DR, in the short term,
with a fixed generation stack. In the second metric, we compute system “peakiness” as the
quantity of load that needs to be served only in the top 100 hours. In the long term, as

 25

generation stacks evolve and system economics change, this metric becomes important
because an increase in system peakiness would mean an increased need for DR.

Shift DR brings value to a system by reducing the need for flexible or ramping generators –
plants that are utilized to manage upward and downward ramps in a system – which have high
production costs and emissions. Shift DR metrics are based on the absolute daily maximum 3-
hour net load ramps in a system; we consider the top 25 ramping days. The metric discussed
here measures the change in routine ramping in the system, as the value of the 25th highest
ramp. This represents moderately high ramps that occur frequently in a system. An increase in
this metric indicates an increased need for shift DR.

2.7 Impacts on utility revenue and estimated system costs
The dynamic tariff framework used in this study is calibrated to recover exactly each IOU’s
actual 2019 revenue, given actual 2019 loads. Put differently, the dynamic tariff recovers the
required utility revenue so long as customers do not change their consumption patterns in
response to it. In the inelastic study, that assumption allowed us to assess changes in bill
impacts across customer classes under the assumption of fixed total revenue. In this study, by
contrast, we are explicitly modeling how customers might change their load shape to reduce
their electricity bills, and this will necessarily have an impact on utility revenue.

As elaborated in Section 2.3.1, the hourly dynamic rate is determined based on the hourly
system-level gross and net loads at each iteration. Our multi-component dynamic tariff
conceptually represents the total cost of energy delivery for each hour. Assuming it accurately
reflects actual hourly system costs, multiplying the tariff by the load should yield the actual total
system costs. The discrepancy arising from billing according to the tariff from the previous
iteration represents an over- or under-collection of revenue.

However, as discussed in the inelastic study, the tariff development algorithms are illustrative
rather than rigorous. Consequently, the changes in system costs computed here should also be
interpreted as illustrative rather than predictive.

kWh of their annual consumption. The top boxplot, with a median value of 32.6 ¢/kWh, indicates
how much non-PV customers pay according to their BAU rates. Under the dynamic rate,
customers who do not change their load (No Response or inelastic scenario) pay 29.5 ¢/kWh on
average, which according to our model incentivizes them to increase demand. The stronger the
response (i.e., Low, Time Variant, or High Elasticity), the lower the effective average rate, with a
minimum rate of 28.4 ¢/kWh for customers responding with High elasticity. With the subscription

but again, it decreases if customers respond to the dynamic rate. PV customers, on the other
hand, have a very low BAU rate of 4 ¢/kWh, because much of their demand is met by solar self-
consumption. By assigning a relatively low dynamic rate to their grid exports, the effective
average rate in the No Response case is 8.4 ¢/kWh. When responding, residential PV
customers experience a fractional decrease in self-consumption, which slightly increases their
effective average rates.

The concept of bill variability was introduced in Section 2.4.2. Figure 12 shows the median
variability of all residential customers’ bills. The bill variability of different elasticities is compared
to the BAU bill variability, which is shown as a dashed line. On present-day electricity rates,
non-PV residential customers have a median variability of 118%, meaning that a customer with
an average bill of $100/month may experience monthly bills ranging from $40-160. In the
dynamic-only non-PV case, the median variability nearly doubles to 217%, as customers are
exposed to widely varying prices throughout the year in addition to their naturally varying
consumption. On the dynamic-only rate, residential non-PV customers may have lower overall
average bills (e.g., $80/month), but with a wider range of monthly bills (e.g., $20-$200). By
responding, the variability can be slightly reduced; for non-PV customers with a self-subscription
rate, their bill variability is nearly identical to the BAU case.

3.3 System-level impacts
Dynamic tariffs have been identified as beneficial at the system level for shaving peak load and
shifting load from peak to off-peak hours, thereby reducing steep ramps. In this section, we
discuss how dynamic tariffs can affect the system in the short term under different elasticity
scenarios. We first examine how the system load shape changes across different elasticity
scenarios on specific days (e.g., peak net load day, highest ramping day), and as described in
Section 2.6, we compute effective DR resources and analyze how the system need for DR

changes using metrics from prior studies. Again, note that all the impacts discussed in these
short-term analysis sections assume universal enrollment and response to dynamic tariffs.

3.3.1 System load shape impacts
System-wide load shape impacts of the dynamic rate are highlighted by looking at the peak net
load day and the day with the steepest net load ramp. Figure 16 shows system-level load shape
impacts for a top peak event (dashed vertical line), which occurred in August 2019. The top
panel shows the dynamic rate as a bold line, reaching $1.00/kWh at the time of the peak net
load, as well as the effective baseline rates for various customer groups. The lower two panels
show the system-level response for net load and gross load in GW, with a bold black line
indicating the No Response (inelastic) scenario. The green line represents the Low Elasticity
scenario, which has a load reduction of about 1 GW at the peak hour, while yellow and red
represent the Time Variant and the High Elasticity scenarios. Finally, a dashed line represents
the Automation scenario, which assumes greater responsiveness than has historically been
seen. In the High Elasticity scenario, the net load is nearly flattened out, decreasing the peak
from 40 GW to below 30 GW, highlighting the potentially significant impacts of dynamic pricing if
full participation and high elasticity are achieved.

3.3.2 Effective shed and shift resources
Figure 18 shows the effective shed (top) and shift (bottom) DR resources that we compute from

values represent the amount of load reduction and shifted energy, respectively, that customer
response to dynamic pricing yields during times when DR would otherwise likely be dispatched.
It is evident that the dynamic tariff is an effective way to reduce load during peak hours. As
shown in the figure, even a Low Elasticity scenario provides a significant amount of shed DR,

DR resource that could be enabled by installing the best currently available technology at every

3.3.5 Emissions impacts of dynamic pricing
As customers change their load in response to dynamic prices, the mix of generation resources
and the associated emissions also change. The change in emissions resulting from small
changes in demand in a given hour – a metric known as the marginal emission factor – can be
used to quantify the total emissions impacts of the changes in system load studied here.
Therefore, we modeled marginal emissions factors for every hour in our 2019 baseline model,
using the avoided cost calculator framework24 to understand the expected relationship between
marginal emissions and system conditions. The ACC revealed a consistent relationship
between marginal emissions and wholesale energy prices, shown in Figure 25. As shown, when
the energy price is at or below zero, it is assumed that renewable, zero-emissions energy is
being dispatched on the margins, while at the highest prices natural gas plants are on the
margin with an emissions rate of approximately 0.67 kg/kWh. February 2019 saw a spike in
natural gas prices, so the emissions curve is shifted to the right to account for the same types of
generation being dispatched at higher costs.

Figure 25. Marginal emissions in relation to energy prices, as shown in the 2021 version of the Avoided
Cost Calculator. Values for 2019 are shown.

wholesale price of course depends on the loads the system is facing, there are other

study, described in Section 2.5, describes several of these indicators. Because we use
wholesale prices as an indicator of what units are being dispatched, and therefore what the

 4 Discussion
 This section examines our results from previous sections to identify potential system-level
 impacts from dynamic pricing. Several high-level findings emerge from the analysis: 1) system-
 level peaks and ramps can be significantly reduced by customer response; 2) customer bill
 savings from load response are possible, if modest; and 3) system costs and emissions can
 also be reduced, although much of these savings are unknown. This section also considers the
 implications for dynamic tariff development and policy, and specifically how customers could be

 46

incentivized to enroll. Finally, the section discusses limitations of the analysis and outlines
potential directions for future research.

4.1 System-level load impacts
The potential short-term impacts of a dynamic tariff on system-level load are considerable,
especially during hours when there is a high need for flexibility. If all customers in California
were to participate and respond to the dynamic tariff, even the lowest-elasticity scenario could
yield 2 GW of effective shed DR resource (Figure 18), which corresponds to a 5% reduction in
the peak load. The Time Variant scenario reduced peak load by 14.4%, and with higher
elasticity values, peak load is reduced by over 26% (Automation Scenario), with an effective
Shed DR resource of over 15 GW. Further, the peakiness of the system is also reduced
significantly; the Low Elasticity scenario reduces peakiness by 12.6%, and the Automation
scenario by 63%. This represents a reduction in the need for rarely used peaking generation
resources such as peaker plants and supply-side DR and should result in lower capacity costs
on the grid in the long run.

Further, the effective shift DR resource, which represents the average amount of energy shifted
across steep ramping periods, is about 7.55 GWh in the Time Variant Elasticity scenario (Figure
18). This corresponds to a reduction of about 25% in routine ramping needs; the High Elasticity
and Automation scenarios reduce these needs by about 40%. Finally, we note that agricultural
customers were not included in this study but may also yield a significant additional resource.
Phase 4 of the California DR potential study found that agricultural pumping has a technical
shed potential of 1 GW and shift potential of 1.8 GWh in 2025. It seems plausible that a
substantial fraction of that could be captured via dynamic rates.

Finally, limited customer enrollment or response to dynamic pricing can significantly limit the
achieved benefits of a dynamic tariff. All results discussed above assume universal enrollment
without any customers opting out. It therefore represents an upper bound of potential response
under the assumptions used. Most simply, if enrollment was, say, 15% across the board, we
would expect to realize 15% of the described impacts. For example, the Time Variant scenario
would reduce peak load by just over 2%, instead of 14.4%. Another consideration is how
enrollment and participation may vary by customer sector. In the peak net load hour, residential
customers are responsible for 51% of the load reduction, while commercial and industrial
customers contribute 34% and 15%, respectively. So, if half of C&I customers participated in
dynamic price response, but only 10% of residential customers, you would still obtain about
30% of the modeled peak reduction (4.32% reduction in peak net load).

4.2 Customer bill impacts
With no load response, each customer class, on average, does not see a significant bill impact
due to the design of the class-neutral tariff. However, are structural differences between PV and
non-PV customers, such that customers without PV save under the dynamic tariff, on average,
with individual bill impacts ranging from -30% to +10% for residential customers, and -60% to
 47

+40% for C&I customers. Conversely, customers with PV, on average, see bill increases,
because their export compensation decreases and prices are generally higher at times they are
importing from the grid. Bill variability increases for all customers without PV, while PV
customers see a reduction in variability in part because their lowest bills, with large amounts of
solar export, are higher than in the BAU case, both lowering the range of bills and increasing the
average bill. In all cases, a subscription based on the individual customer’s prior-year load
shape significantly reduces bill impacts and limits variability.

With or without a subscription, dynamic pricing offers customers an opportunity to reduce their
bills by shifting demand to times of lower prices. Customers without PV are able to reduce their
average effective rate ($/kWh) by a few percentage points across all sectors we modeled, with
larger reductions occurring with greater elasticities. For residential customers, the most
significant bill savings observed was around 5% of the annual value under High elasticity; at the
Low elasticity level no customer saved more than 1.5%. For C&I customers, bill savings above
10% were observed in some extreme cases, but again, unless customers are highly elastic their
bill savings will likely be limited to a few percent. Customers with PV can also save 5-10% of
their average effective rate, depending on their level of elasticity and their baseline consumption
patterns. However, some may see an increase in the average rate they pay for grid-supplied
electricity, since price response will encourage them to self-consume more of their generation,
leaving their grid purchases concentrated in higher-price hours. This is an artifact of the way our
effective average rate was calculated (normalizing over all grid-purchased electricity, not over all
electricity consumed) and does not represent an increase in costs to the customer; their overall
costs will still decrease as they respond to prices.

It is uncertain if customers will be willing to select a dynamic tariff in order to capture the
estimated magnitude of bill savings. Dynamic tariffs are inherently uncertain, could be highly
variable, and may seem overly complicated to consumers such that the potential to save a few
percent on their bill may not seem worth it. As prices and the shape of the system net load
evolves in the future, we may see more “steep” price profiles that enable larger bill savings to
occur. However, if the price design is more conservative than the price profile used in this study
(namely, if only marginal electricity costs are included), price profiles will be flatter and potential
bill savings will be lower which may not produce a strong enough economic incentive to warrant
selection of the tariff.

Additionally, customers will potentially need to invest in automation technology to effectively
manage their load in response to prices, and the bill savings observed in this study may not be
worth the up-front cost. However, certain customers may see much more significant benefits
from automation, particularly as customers continue to electrify significant end use loads into the
future; a trend that is not captured in this analysis of 2019 demand. Further, these automation
technologies will enable loads to respond in a much more tailored, and possibly more
aggressive, way than the elastic response framework used here represents. For example, a
smart EV charger could easily move 100% of a customer's charging demand to the lowest-
priced time of day (or week), potentially resulting in more substantial bill savings for the
customer than if they were on a standard tariff.
 48

4.3 Impacts on utility costs and revenue
The model results show that customer response to dynamic-only tariffs would flatten the system
net load, and significantly reduce peak net loads on the grid. This would be expected to reduce
the total costs of operating the grid, though this study does not explicitly model the reduction in
grid capacity investments or other operational costs that would be required to realize the cost
savings. One way of estimating the potential cost savings from reduced capacity requirements
is to observe the marginal capacity costs reported in utility rate case filings. Based on data
collected for the inelastic bill impacts study (Gerke et al. 2022a), utilities reported marginal
capacity costs of $70-80/kW for firm generation, with additional marginal costs for flexible
generation, distribution, and transmission. As a first-order estimate, this would imply that the
15% peak net load reduction (observed in the Time Variant scenario) would save $520M of
marginal firm generation capacity costs, after accounting for additional savings from a 15%
planning reserve margin. While these cost savings are significant, they only represent 1.2% of
the total revenue estimated to be collected from BAU tariffs across the customer base. But as
noted, other cost savings would be expected from other types of marginal capacity costs, and in
the longer-term, savings from non-marginal costs incurred due to capital investments.

Wholesale costs are another source of cost savings, which we did model in this study. While
significant reductions in wholesale price were possible in peak hours (20 to 60%, depending on
the elasticity scenario), across the entirety of the year the average wholesale energy cost only
decreased about 1%. If electricity demand remained constant, and if all demand was exposed to
these prices, this would result in $57.7M in wholesale market cost savings. However, this model
was based on a historic market dispatch, and therefore does not capture cost savings that
would incur from changes in the overall requirements of the generation fleet. This historical
model also considers external factors that impacted the 2019 market, such as natural gas prices
and weather patterns, that may impact results. Additionally, the nature of a regression model is
such that it may not capture the nuance of discontinuous dispatch patterns, particularly in low-
price hours when demand is slightly increased due to the elastic response. These hours, shown
above the red lines in Figure 24, offset some of the wholesale cost savings from hours when
load is reduced, though in reality these small increases in consumption in low-net load hours
may not directly lead to increases in wholesale prices.

As discussed in Section 3.4, total utility revenue collection decreases $300M when customers
respond elastically to the dynamic tariffs; this is analogous to aggregate customer bill savings.
This only represents a 0.7% reduction in revenue, though the overall increase in demand is
such that customer average rates ($/kWh) decline 2.7%. Further research is needed to better
understand the cost savings the utility would incur under such load response, so that the price
design can be evaluated to assure cost savings are being adequately passed onto the
customers. Based on our rough estimates of some cost savings, it is possible that the utility and
customer bill savings are aligned, or that the chosen price design would result in a revenue
surplus (where the utility saves more than the customers). That surplus would then be
accounted for and passed back to customers in future filings, resulting in additional bill savings.
However, these long-term bill savings would likely be spread across all customers, not just

 49

those who respond, and may generally limit the incentive for customers to enroll in and respond
to the dynamic prices. It is important that customers can observe and realize the full benefits of
their load behavior in the form of near-term bill savings.

4.4 Implications for dynamic tariff development and policy
In this study, we quantified the potential impacts of dynamic tariffs on system load shapes, grid
costs, and customer bills. This analysis was based on net system loads and wholesale prices in
2019, and the hourly price elasticities we used in the analysis were determined based on
previous study results, and generally reflect modest response to prices. These results show
impacts from dynamic pricing that are generally lower than we originally thought, and even
somewhat less than the impact of present-day demand response programs.

As our study results are calculated based on historical elasticity levels; increases in customers’
ability to respond to dynamic prices can lead to larger benefits. This can be achieved either by
enrolling more customers on dynamic tariffs or by boosting the elasticity of key end uses.
Regarding enrollment, there are several strategies the CPUC and IOUs could implement to
drive improvements in acceptance rates, including advertising the potential benefits of dynamic
pricing in lowering average bills (for residential and small C&I customers) and in having more bill
control while maintaining consistent energy services (by changing the schedules of non-critical
demands without foregoing services). Considering that studies of customer attitudes have
shown relatively high interest in hourly-varying prices and their benefits (EPRI 2021a, 2021b,
2021c), providing more information through a variety of channels would be helpful. To increase
the elasticity of demand, customers will need to adopt recent improvements in automation
technologies, and utility programs should focus on customers with naturally flexible loads.

4.5 Limitations and future research directions
This study represents an initial effort to model customer response to multi-component dynamic
tariffs in California. Thus, the study has some shortcomings and potential opportunities for future
improvements. In this section, we briefly discuss several such areas.

Limited and outdated data on customer elasticity: A key input parameter to estimate the
customer load response to dynamic tariffs is customer elasticity. In the absence of recent
empirical data on customer elasticity in California, we conducted a literature review and
proposed a simple hourly own-price elasticity framework. The selected hourly price elasticities
for residential and C&I customers are obtained from literature, yet the literature we relied on is
fairly limited and outdated. This is especially true because new, connected technologies for
flexibility could significantly change customer elasticity, along with various energy storage
technologies such as batteries and thermal storage. Additional research on recent customer
responsiveness to dynamic tariffs, especially in California, could improve the study results.

Single-customer elasticity estimates for the entire customer base within sectors: The hourly
price elasticity values we proposed were applied equally to all different end uses and customer
 50

types within the residential and C&I sectors. In reality, different end uses are likely to have
different elasticity profiles depending on their operation schedules and structural limitations to
flexibility. For example, an educational institution and a dining establishment have significantly
different needs in the evening hours and would likely have different elasticities. Considering that
real customer responsiveness to dynamic tariffs is more diverse than what was assumed in the
study, future studies with elasticities varying by end-uses or site utilization are warranted.

Use of baseline load to set prices: Our analysis considers a year-long price profile that is
calculated based on the assumed baseline load. Therefore, we only calculate the initial load
response that would occur in the short-term. In the longer term, the response to dynamic pricing
would reshape the system load, and eventually that response would likely be factored into
consideration of energy procurement and market transactions, changing the price that
customers see. As prices evolve in response to the new system load shape, load response
would also evolve, until an equilibrium is reached.

Definition of bill variability: In this report, we describe how customers could enroll in a two-part
tariff, where the first part of the tariff is paying the BAU rate for a previously subscribed load
shape and the second part pays for any deviation from the subscription using the dynamic-only
rate. Bill variability is defined as the difference between maximum and minimum bills, divided by
the annual average of monthly bills. This definition implies that bill variability would be greatly
reduced if customers stuck to their general load shape, and that any response to the dynamic
rate would increase variability. In practice, there is more of an asymmetric assessment of bill
impacts - a decrease of the customer’s bill in any month is unlikely to be considered a negative,
whereas customers typically worry about bill increases instead. Therefore, in future research, a
refined definition of bill variability should be developed.

Simplified approach to automation: To account for the effect of technology in automating
customer response to dynamic rates, we applied a rough multiplier to the full load of all
customers and end uses in each sector for the Automation scenario. This is simplistic and may
not realistically capture the varied effects of automation. In practice, different automation
technologies will have different impacts on flexibility and will provide flexibility for different
durations (e.g., EV charging is likely much more flexible than cooking or lighting). New studies,
such as the pilot programs currently underway in SCE and PG&E, in addition to the EPIC
CalFlexHub program, could further illuminate customer response patterns and facilitate a better
understanding of flexibility potential by customer type, end use, and technology25.

Future studies that develop a more granular and detailed approach to modeling customer
response – ranging from different degrees of flexibility for different end uses, different flexibility
response curves, and strategies for each customer type and end use – could address the
shortcomings listed above. These future studies would also identify the most important
customers and end uses to target for dynamic rates. Additionally, more research is needed to
understand the impacts that price response would have on system costs in terms of wholesale

market prices, generation, transmission, and distribution capacity costs, and other operational
costs. Finally, studies should consider the future potential of dynamic tariffs, especially given the
increasing electrification of buildings, with some loads (such as EVs and water heaters)
providing high flexibility due to having naturally built-in storage.

5 Conclusions
This study presented the potential demand and bill impacts of an hourly dynamic tariff that was
revenue neutral on a class-average basis, if customers respond elastically to changes in their
electricity price relative to today’s prevailing time-of-use rates. Multiple scenarios for customer
elasticity were studied, with the elasticity estimates originating from studies of real-time pricing
programs piloted or deployed across the U.S. The elasticity estimates were used to calculate
hypothetical load response and bills from dynamic tariffs in the short term, where the dynamic
price is based on the baseline system load. Further, we assess the impacts of customer load
response to dynamic prices on wholesale energy market prices and grid emissions.

At the system level, we found that in the short term, dynamic rates can reduce peak net load
and provide an effective Shed DR resource in line with today’s DR programs under the lowest
level of elasticity assumed, with significantly larger impacts at higher elasticity levels. These
load impacts result in reductions in wholesale market prices and emissions (per unit of electricity
consumed), though these reductions are modest (around 1%). We further find that responding
elastically to the dynamic price produces modest incremental bill savings for each customer
(generally less than 3%), with aggregate bill savings of $300M occurring in the moderate
response scenario, despite a 2% increase in consumption.

It is important to remember the context and limitations of this study. First, the modeling and
results presented here represent 2019 customer loads being served by the 2019 electricity grid,
and both customer loads and the energy technologies that serve them are evolving rapidly. New
end-use loads will significantly alter hourly demand in the future, while increasing generation
from variable renewable electricity sources will cause the duck curve to worsen. These changes
will have profound impacts on system prices, as well as both the need and the ability of load to
respond to these prices. We would expect the benefits of dynamic tariffs to grow as loads
become more flexible and daily price differentials become more pronounced. It will be important
to consider the future grid along with the responsiveness of new flexible loads in future
research. Additionally, the limitations of the elastic response model employed in this study
should be noted. The elasticity values employed herein are based on limited data, which is
somewhat dated, and applied coarsely to all end-use loads. While elasticity may be a good
high-level representation of customers’ response to varying price signals, the actual
mechanisms of load response may vary substantially from the general theory of price-elasticity
response. Modeling these load responses more accurately, at the end-use level, will be
especially important considering the load changes expected in the future (e.g., from increased
EV adoption and demand). As new technologies enable customer responsiveness to dynamic
prices, quantifying the behavior and magnitude of load response will significantly benefit future
research on dynamic rates.
