Surgical management of a giant left atrial appendage aneurysm and chronic atrial fibrillation
Authors: Ahmet Zengin, Cemalettin Yılmaz, Taylan Adademir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeft atrial appendage aneurysm (LAAA) is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly that is associated with potentially life-threatening complications, including thromboembolism and arrhythmias. We report a 36-year-old male patient with giant LAAA (66 × 46 mm) and chronic atrial fibrillation (CAF) presenting with palpitations. Surgical resection of LAAA and Cox-Maze IV procedure were performed under cardiopulmonary bypass. The patient was discharged in sinus rhythm, remained asymptomatic, and had normal echocardiographic findings at follow-up.Keywords: Left atrial appendage aneurysm, Cox-Maze IV, Cardiac surgery, Atrial fibrillation surgery

Introduction

Left atrial appendage aneurysm (LAAA) is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly, with approximately 216 cases reported in the literature since 1962 [1, 2]. LAAA is clinically significant because it can cause life-threatening complications, including systemic thromboembolism, atrial tachyarrhythmias, and cardiac dysfunction [3]. Although LAAA can be fatal, it is often diagnosed in middle age following presentation with seemingly benign symptoms such as palpitations. The coexistence of LAAA with chronic atrial fibrillation (CAF) presents unique therapeutic challenges requiring both anatomical correction and rhythm management. In this report, we present the surgical treatment of a 36-year-old male patient with giant LAAA and CAF who presented with palpitations.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old male patient was admitted to our clinic with palpitations. He had no relevant past medical history. Physical examination was unremarkable, and laboratory findings were within normal limits. Electrocardiography demonstrated atrial fibrillation (Fig. 1). Transesophageal echocardiography (Toshiba Medical System) revealed a LAAA measuring 60 × 40 mm (Fig. 2) with a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65% and no other structural cardiac pathology. Computed tomography angiography was performed to exclude additional vascular abnormalities and confirmed a giant LAAA measuring 66 × 46 mm (Fig. 3), with no other anomalies detected. The slight discrepancy in measurements reflects differences in imaging modalities and cardiac cycle phases. The patient was hospitalized and scheduled for cardiac surgery consisting of LAAA resection and Cox-Maze IV procedure due to the large aneurysm size, presence of atrial fibrillation, and potential thromboembolic risk.

After median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass was established via aortic arterial cannulation and bicaval venous cannulation. On the beating heart, right atriotomy was performed, and right atrial Cox-Maze IV lesion sets were applied using an argon cryoprobe (Cardioblate CryoFlex 10-S Surgical Ablation Probe, Medtronic, MN, USA). Subsequently, aortic cross-clamping was applied, and cardiac arrest was achieved with Del Nido cardioplegia. The LAAA was completely resected, and the left atrium was closed with primary suturing (Fig. 4). Pathological examination of the resected tissue revealed a thin, dilated atrial wall without thrombus or other cardiac anomalies. Following left atriotomy, left atrial lesion sets were completed using argon cryoprobe (Cardioblate CryoFlex 10-S Surgical Ablation Probe, Medtronic, MN, USA) and radiofrequency ablation (Atricure, USA). Cardiopulmonary bypass time was 147 min, and aortic cross-clamping time was 105 min. Following weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, the patient maintained sinus rhythm. Postoperatively, intravenous amiodarone (total 1200 mg) was administered to the patient to maintain sinus rhythm, followed by oral amiodarone 200 mg daily for three months. Anticoagulation with warfarin was continued for one month and discontinued after confirmation of sustained sinus rhythm on Holter monitoring and absence of thrombus on echocardiography. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 7 in sinus rhythm (Fig. 5). At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with normal echocardiographic findings.

Discussion

Left atrial appendage aneurysm (LAAA) is a rare congenital anomaly associated with potentially life-threatening complications, including systemic thromboembolism, atrial tachyarrhythmias, and cardiac dysfunction. Despite these risks, patients may present with nonspecific symptoms such as palpitations, as observed in our case.

The management of LAAA remains controversial due to its rarity. While conservative management may be considered in asymptomatic patients with small aneurysms, surgical intervention is generally recommended in symptomatic patients, those with large aneurysms, or in the presence of arrhythmias or thromboembolic risk [4]. In our patient, several factors supported surgical intervention: the large size of the aneurysm, the presence of chronic atrial fibrillation, and the potential risk of thromboembolic complications. In addition to aneurysm resection, a Cox-Maze IV procedure was performed to address the underlying arrhythmia. This combined approach was preferred because atrial fibrillation in such patients may originate from structural and electrical remodeling associated with the aneurysm, and resection alone may not restore sinus rhythm.

Imaging modalities are essential for the diagnosis and evaluation of LAAA and inform subsequent treatment. Transthoracic echocardiography is the primary diagnostic method, but it has low diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity. Transesophageal echocardiography provides clear visualization of the structure and composition of the LAAA and its connection to the left atrium, and should be strongly considered if the diagnosis is suspected after transthoracic echocardiography [5]. Cardiac CT and MRI can clearly visualize LAAA relationships and the attendant surrounding structural and vascular anomalies in detail. In our case, LAAA was diagnosed by transesophageal echocardiography and subsequently confirmed by cardiac computed tomography.

LAAA resection with the maze procedure is a standard curative treatment because the arrhythmia originates in the degenerative left appendage. The Cox-Maze IV procedure demonstrates high success rates in maintaining sinus rhythm [6]. Our case demonstrates successful surgical management of a giant LAAA with concomitant CAF, performing resection and Cox-Maze IV ablation. Combined surgical and electrophysiological management may provide improved rhythm outcomes compared to aneurysm resection alone.

In conclusion, surgical resection of giant LAAA combined with Cox-Maze IV ablation is an effective treatment strategy for patients with LAAA and chronic atrial fibrillation. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention can prevent potentially fatal complications. Despite the rarity of this condition, clinicians should maintain awareness that serious cardiac anomalies may present with simple symptoms in middle-aged patients.
