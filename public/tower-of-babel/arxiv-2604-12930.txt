Building and maintaining a System of Intracellular Compartments
Amit Kumar 1,2, Madan Rao 2

Abstract
Organelle patterning and its heritability remain central mysteries in cell biology, highlighting the fundamental tension between genetic inheritance and self-assembly. Here, we explore the nonequilibrium assembly and size control of the Golgi complex and endosomes, amid a continuous flux of membrane traffic, within a stochastic framework of mechanochemical fusion-fission cycles that violate detailed balance. Using a dynamical systems approach, we identify distinct, robust regimes, ranging from fixed points to limit cycles with definite phase relations. We identify these dynamical regimes with diverse phenotypes, from stable cisternae to periodic, cell-cycle-dependent dissolution/reassembly to cisternal progression. We analyse its dynamic response to systematic perturbations or driving protocols and make definite predictions that may be tested experimentally. Our analysis reveals that the two competing models of Golgi organization—vesicular transport and cisternal progression—are, in fact, two phases of the same underlying nonequilibrium process. Finally, our framework offers a strategy for controlling cisternal chemical identity and number and by modulating the interplay between glycosylation enzymes and membrane fission-fusion dynamics.

In this paper, we study the stochastic time evolution of the sizes of the Golgi cisternae subject to nonequilibrium fusion and fission modelled as
discrete Markov cycles.
By integrating over the cycle time of the “fast” fusion-fission processes, we derive dynamical mean field equations for the cisternal sizes. The active mechanochemistry of the fusion-fission cycles naturally

We identify such nonequilibrium steady states with distinct phenotypic outcomes such as vesicle transport, cisternal progression, or the periodic dissolution and reformation of cisternae across the cell cycle.
We analyse its dynamic response to systematic perturbations or driving protocols and make definite predictions that may be tested experimentally. Our work applies to cisternal dynamics and their organisation in a variety of cellular contexts, both in health and in disease.
Finally, we study the robustness of this system of cisternae

Taken as a whole, our study argues that the Vesicular Transport and Cisternal Progression models and their variants are different phenotypic outcomes of the same underlying physical description that

A

can be represented by

\displaystyle\frac{d\bf{X}}{dt}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\lambda},t)

(5)

where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector whose time (

along with

The

comprises all possible states

. Within this space, we identify

—such as

(FP) given by
\mathbf{f(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\lambda},t)}=0

(limit cycles, LC) — whose stability is defined by their

(multiple coexisting attractors), and a

Bifurcations
describe qualitative changes in a system’s behaviour, e.g., number or stability of equilibria/periodic orbits, as a parameter varies. Bifurcations can be local, driven by stability changes at a specific fixed point (detectable via linearization), or global, which reshape larger trajectories in phase space and cannot be detected by local analysis. We will be concerned with the following bifurcations in this work,

1.

Saddle-Node
(SN)
bifurcation
is a local bifurcation, that happens when two fixed points, that differ in stability along at least one direction, coalesce and vanish (or equivalently are created) as one varies the bifurcation parameter. It is accompanied by critical slowing down of system trajectory near the vanished fixed points, known as

strogatz

2.

(b))
strogatz

3.

In extending our analysis to multiple cisternae (Fig.

Proposition 1

Consider the general flux system,

\displaystyle\dot{M_{1}}

\displaystyle=

(i)

Corollary 1

It follows that the necessary condition for the above flux system to have complex eigenvalues, and hence limit cycles, is that either or both of the intercisternal flux kernels

IV.3
Robustness of the solution space

So far, our analysis has been mean field, where we have ignored the effect of noise, an inevitable presence in cellular systems. Cellular noise can arise from parametric noise, extrinsic noise (say, influx rate) and intrinsic noise (say, low copy number or chemical). An important requirement of organelle biogenesis in a living cell should be robustness under noise.
In Sect.
III
and
S4.1
, we have discussed the
structural stability
of the single cisterna dynamical system Eq. (
6
) under parametric noise,
i.e. states in the vicinity of a viable solution also converge to the same solution class or phenotype. The structural stability of the
2
-cisternae dynamical system Eqs.(
12
), (
13
), although not as straightforward as the single cisterna, can be established numerically (see Fig.
S14
and
S6
for details) by checking that the number of distinct roots that the dynamical system admits matches its algebraic capacity (the generic number of roots that the system algebraically supports)
using the Gröbner basis
Cox2015
;
golubitzky
.

It has been suggested that the structural stability of a dynamical system ensures that the root structure of the system is robust to low amplitude extrinsic noise
cobb
.
Changes in the root structure of the
1
-cisterna and
2
-cisternae systems in the presence of extrinsic multiplicative noise can be studied by extending the deterministic system to a Langevin system
kampen
, for instance, by adding a multiplicative noise with noise strength
\sqrt{2\xi_{i}(M_{i})}
, which is a smooth positive function solely dependent on the size of the cisterna
i
.
For the 1-cisterna case, taking the noise strength
\xi(M)=\epsilon M
, which would be the case if the stochastic addition and removal of vesicles are proportional to the cisternal size,
we see that the nucleation rate is modified
v\,a_{0}\rightarrow(v\,a_{0}-\epsilon)

provides robustness against noise with strength

S7

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have presented a general nonequilibrium framework to study the establishment, control, robustness and dynamical response of a system of intracellular membrane bound organelles, such as the Golgi complex, which are subject to a nonequilibrium flux of material processes through synthesis, transport, fusion and fission. To do this, we have had to derive macroscopic fusion and fission flux kernels from microscopic considerations, that form an
embedded feedback control system
that ensure the assembly of multiple Golgi cisternae. This assembly, depending on the functional form of the flux kernels, admit different solution classes, which we identify with distinct phenotypic outcomes such as vesicular transport, cisternal progression, or the periodic dissolution into tiny vesicles. We have studied how these outcomes may be entrained with the cell cycle, giving rise to cell state dependent cisternal organisation.

We have determined how the different phases of cisternal assembly dynamically respond to systematic perturbations, both external and internal. In future we propose to study how specific dynamical responses to experimentally realisable protocols, may enable the quantitative reconstruction of the fusion and fission kernels and its embedded feedback structure, providing insights into the physical
principles underlying cisternal assembly.
We showed that the deduced effective fusion and fission kernels give rise to stable unfoldings that maintain the solution structure under perturbations with finite strength. We have further elaborated on this issue by subjecting the dynamical system model to intrinsic and extrinsic noise and derive bounds on the noise strengths for which system remains in the same solution class, hence a robust solution structure.

There remain many open issues which we hope will be taken up in the future –

1.

While the cellular dynamics discussed here, provides a framework to study its biogenesis and the control and maintenance of cisternal size, it says says nothing about what controls cisternal number
Alkesh
. This could likely arise from additional cisternal attributes not included in our study of cisternal dynamics. Indeed cisternae are identified not only by their position relative to the ER and PM, but also by their lumenal or membranal chemical content, such as cisternal specific enzymes. We therefore proposed to extend the nonequilibrium framework developed here to include the dynamics of cisternal enzymes and their retention
Alkesh
. This may be done by coupling the mechanochemical enzymes involved in the fission-fusion cycles to the
glycosylation enzymes, a preliminary attempt using a spatial model was made in
Sachdeva2016
.

2.

An important question in cell biology is how organelle size scales with cell size
chan
;
marshallS
. In the context of our Golgi assembly model, this translates to asking which parameters of the model depend on cell size and how. The rate of influx from the ER and the fission and fusion parameters, such as levels of fisogens and fusogens, that characterise the nonequilibrium cycles clearly depend on ploidy and general metabolic activity and must therefore scale with cell size
chan
.

3.

Since finite size cellular structures are a consequence of the nonequilibrium dynamics and feedback control, we may ask for the thermodynamic cost
in creating and maintaining stable cisternae
vaikuntanathan
.

Finally, we believe our model for the biogenesis, maintenance and control of the Golgi cisternae can be extended to other intracellular organelles
marshallS
:
open, nonconserved systems
like the Golgi, such as the Endosomal and Lysosomal systems, and
closed, conserved systems
such as the Mitochondrial system.

\displaystyle+

S1.2
Physico-chemical basis for nonequilibrium flux kernels

In our analysis we consider a finite cisternal pool of fusogens and fisogens, set by the expression levels of the cell. This constraint can be imposed in different ways, e.g.,

(i)

Constant rate ensemble
– In this ensemble, fusogens/fisogens are assumed to be available at a constant rate. Specifically, the rate of fusogen/fisogen arrival per unit time is a strict constant
r_{0}
, described by
P(r)=\delta(r-r_{0})
. Because the subunits arrive independently, the probability of observing
n
arrivals within a given time window follows a Poisson distribution
[
88
]
.

(ii)

Finite pool ensemble
– In this ensemble, we assume that at any given time, the cell contains a finite total number of fusogens/fisogens,
N_{total}
. Consequently, each time an enzyme engages in a fusion, fission or transport event, one fewer fusion/fission enzyme remains available at the cisterna. If the size of the Golgi is
M
, the number of available free enzymes is dictated by a function
\mathcal{P}_{s}(M)
, which represents the number of independent active patches at the cisternal membrane. The number of free enzymes available is therefore
m=N_{total}-\mathcal{P}_{s}(M)
. The probability of observing exactly
m
free fusogens/fisogens (which governs the fluctuating arrival rate) follows a binomial distribution, given by

\displaystyle P(m)=\binom{N_{total}}{m}\,p^{m}\,(1-p)^{N_{total}-m}

(S13)

where
p
is the probability that a given enzyme is currently free in the pool rather than bound to the Golgi, determined by the specific binding and unbinding rates of the system
[
16
,
17
]
.

Here, we choose to work with the constant rate ensemble. However, the methods used in this work (for instance, steps for reduction to a lower dimensional system, see
S2
) can be used for other ensembles as well.

),(
S11

S12

Appendix S2
Reduction to a low dimensional dynamical system

Given that the master equation in the above section consists of highly non-linear terms, it becomes analytically intractable

]

We will address this issue through a culmination of three methods: (i) a graphical method where we look at the fixed points of the mean field equations (ii) a phase diagram based method where we analyse the solution classes of the master equation in the space of its parameters using simulations (Gillespie algorithm) and map it to the space of macroscopic parameter, of a plausible macroscopic model. The resulting comparison confirms or invalidates the assumed macroscopic model. (iii) an algebraic method where we construct an effective function (of cisternal size as well as the fusogens/fisogens) from different parts of the sum in Eqs. (

),(
S28

S2.3
An algebraic criterion for dimensional reduction

Here, we present an algebraic method to arrive at the macroscopic rates from the microscopic model. We derive the functional form that the steady state microscopic current takes for various functional forms of microscopic transition rates in the Markov cycles and the probability distributions for enzyme/site availabilities. To this end, we derive the functional form for the steady state current in a Markov cycle.

Steady state current in a Markov cycle:

We will compute steady-state current in a general Markov cycle with
3
and
4

3-nodes:

Consider a Markov system with three nodes (Fig.

(a)), with rates given by
(a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime},c,c^{\prime})
, where unprimed indices correspond to forward transitions and primed indices correspond to reverse transitions. With the transition rate matrix
[

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\begin{bmatrix}-a-c^{\prime}&a^{\prime}&c\\
a&-b-a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime}&b&-c-b^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}

(S34)

One can show that current in the cycle is given by the ratio of two factors, the numerator
\mathcal{A}
is given by the Kolmogorov current condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=abc-a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}

whereas denominator

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
a&-b-a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime}&b&-c-b^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}

(S35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\underbrace{ab+bc+ca}_{\textrm{forward sum}}+\underbrace{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}+b^{\prime}c^{\prime}+c^{\prime}a^{\prime}}_{\textrm{backward sum}}+\underbrace{ab^{\prime}+bc^{\prime}+ca^{\prime}}_{\textrm{mixed sum}}

Here, we have broken the above sum in three parts. The second factor can be obtained from the first by priming two indices and the third factor can be obtained from the forward sum priming one of the indices, given that the multiplying factors do not correspond to outfluxes at the same node.
Hence, sums like
(a^{\prime}b+b^{\prime}c+c^{\prime}a)

(ii)

Let the intercisternal fluxes are dependent on the size of the donor cisterna alone, i.e.,

Proof.

The proof to (i) is straightforward as it leads to two independent one dimensional system, that can only have real eigenvalues.

Proof of (ii) is as follows: in the reduced form (ii), above equation modifies to,

\displaystyle\dot{M_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{in}(M_{1})-\mathcal{J}^{fis}_{12}(M_{1})+\,\mathcal{J}^{fus}_{21}(M_{2})

(S76)

\displaystyle\dot{M_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{J}^{fus}_{12}(M_{1})-\mathcal{J}^{fis}_{21}(M_{2})-J_{ex}(M_{2})

The Jacobian matrix around any fixed point of the above system will have the form

J=\begin{pmatrix}a-k&d\\
k_{1}&-c\end{pmatrix}

As mentioned above, in this work we are concerned with local bifurcations (saddle-node, Hopf) as well as global bifurcations (SNIC, SNLC). In this section, we present the method to predict local as well as global bifurcations based on eigenvalue analysis at the fixed points of a dynamical system. Although the eigenvalue analysis cannot completely predict a global bifurcation, we will see that if we study the solution structure of the system over the parameter space of the system, the change in the stability of the fixed points in the system, as we vary the parameters, may hint towards the kind of global bifurcations that might have caused the change in the solution class of the system.

Computing the phase difference:

Since the solutions of the above dynamical system have nonlinear waveform, and frequency of the system is amplitude dependent, usual methods such as Fourier or Hilbert transform might give inaccurate results (Bedrosian theorem might not hold
[
116
]
) and computing the phase difference between the two time series is not straightforward. If the system has well-defined peaks, we can use peak to peak analysis to compute the phase difference using the following procedure – For each signal, detect consecutive peaks and assign phase linearly between them
(0,2\pi)
. The phase difference between two signals/time-series is simply: phase difference,
\Delta\phi(t)=2\pi
(time between peaks/time period).
We use a variant of this method – we compute the time period
\tau
from the numerical solution and fit a sinusoidal function
A_{1}+A_{2}\,sin(2\pi t/\tau+c)
to the solutions obtained for
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
from the differential equations Eqs.(
12
),(
13
). The phase difference can then be derived from the correlation of the fitted functions. Note that the sinusoidal fitting extracts phase from the entire waveform’s distribution and is a better representative of the true temporal relationship between broad or asymmetric signals than peak to peak’s single-point estimate. It essentially captures the “mean behaviour” or the macroscopic phase shift of the oscillators.

Appendix S7
Robustness of phases to extrinsic and intrinsic noise

S7.1
Robustness to extrinsic noise

As suggested in the above section and
III

6
) corresponds to a family of functions with the root structure, robust under small changes in parameters. The structural stability of the system further ensures that the root structure of the dynamical system is robust to intrinsic and extrinsic noise.
In the following calculations, we subject the dynamical system corresponding to single and multiple cisternae to extrinsic and intrinsic noise and look for bounds on the noise strength for the solutions to remain in the same solution class. To quantify the robustness to extrinsic noise, we assume that each cisterna
i

M_{i}

\xi_{i}(M_{i})

[

\displaystyle={\cal K}^{fus}(M_{i})-{\cal K}^{fis}(M_{i})-{\cal K}^{tr}_{a}(M_{i})+{\cal K}^{tr}_{r}(M_{i})+\sqrt{\xi_{i}(M_{i})}\eta_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}(\vec{M})+\sqrt{\xi_{i}(M_{i})}\eta_{i}(t)

where

Appendix S8
De novo cisternal assembly time

S8.1
De novo assembly of single cisterna

In the main text, we have used the functional form of the de novo formation time for single cisterna (
III
). In this section, we sketch a non-perturbative method for estimating this, which involves computation of time taken by a general nonlinear system to escape a saddle-node ghost and reach the asymptotic solution. Let us consider Eq.(
S54
) (Eq.(
6
) in the main text) again,

\frac{dM}{dt}=v\,\left(a_{0}+\frac{M^{2}}{C_{1}+M^{2}}\right)-\frac{d\,M}{C_{2}+M}

(S88)

By integrating the above equation, we can compute the time taken by the above system to reach a desired steady-state cisterna size
M^{*}
,

\int_{0}^{\tau_{denovo}}dt=\int_{0}^{M^{*}}dM\,\left[v\,\left(a_{0}+\frac{M^{2}}{C_{1}+M^{2}}\right)-\frac{d\,M}{C_{2}+M}\right]^{-1}

(S89)

which in principle should give the functional dependence of cisterna de novo formation time,
\tau_{denovo}
on system parameters. However, above integral diverges logarithmically at the roots of Eq.(
S88
). One way around this is to work in a regime where system can have at most one fixed point
M^{*}
(i.e., cubic discriminant
\Delta<0
), and then analyse
\tau_{denovo}
as we take different limits, such as
\Delta\rightarrow 0
. For the above integral Eq.(
S89
), this involves tedious expressions containing formula for cubic roots. To tackle this, we proceed with computation of cisterna formation time in the following sub-models:

Saturated fission kernel:
We take a piecewise continuous approximation for fusion kernel and a linear approximation for fission kernel, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\cal K}^{fus}=v(a_{0}+\Theta(M-C_{a}))\hskip 14.22636pt{\cal K}^{fis}=M/C_{b}

(S90)

\Theta
being the Heaviside function. It has two stable steady states given by
M^{*}=\{a_{0}\,C_{b}\,v,(1+a_{0})\,C_{b}\,v
}. We compute the integration time up to the larger steady state. Integration with the above kernels give,

\displaystyle{\tau_{denovo}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{C_{a}}dM\,\left[v(a_{0}+\Theta(M-C_{a})-M/C_{b}\right]^{-1}+\int_{C_{a}}^{qM^{*}}dM\,\left[v(a_{0}+\Theta(M-C_{a})-M/C_{b}\right]^{-1}

(S91)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\,C_{b}\log(a_0 C_b v-C_a)\,+\,C_{b}\log(a_0 C_b v)}_{\textrm{Saddle-node }1}\,\underbrace{+\,C_{b}\log\left(C_{b}v(1+a_{0})-C_{a}\right)}_{\textrm{Saddle-node }2}\,-\,C_{b}\log\left(C_{b}v(1+a_{0})(1-q)\right)

In the above expression, the argument of of the two clubbed logarithmic functions give the condition for saddle-node bifurcation, i.e,
C_{a}/(a_{0}C_{b}v)=1
and
C_{a}/((a_{0}+1)C_{b}v)=1
. From Eq.(
S91
), expression for saddle-node
1
(Fig.
