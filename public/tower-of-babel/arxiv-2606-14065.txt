Probing the Broken Spatial Symmetry of a Stratified Medium with Structured Light
Arani Maiti, Sauvik Roy, Nirmalya Ghosh, Ayan Banerjee, Subhasish Dutta Gupta

Abstract
We study near-symmetric resonant stratified media to show how a tiny broken spatial symmetry can effectively be probed by structured light with or without orbital angular momentum. This is achieved by examining both the in-plane and out of plane Goos-Hänchen and Imbert Fedorov shifts, respectively, in the reflected light, magnified by resonant enhancement and weak value amplification. We show that non-reciprocity in reflection for illumination from opposite ends can result in different shifts, even to the extent of shifts with opposite signs for tiny imbalance resulting from the broken symmetry. We believe that our results can lead to new type of extra-sensitive sensors for any agent (eg. refractive index, displacement, etc.) that can break the symmetry.

Consider the folded Kretschmann-like configuration supporting coupled guided modes (see Fig.

\lambda

. In the following Sections, the GH and IF shifts are analyzed by computing the centroid displacement of the reflected intensity distribution through spatial averaging for both the models of the broken symmetry.

Symmetry breaking with translation

Now, we draw attention to the asymmetric structure shown in Fig.
1
(b), where the spatial symmetry is broken by a small translation
\delta_{d}=0.0005~\mu m
of the coupled guides keeping the other parameters fixed. Again, for reference, the results for the plane wave reflectivity and phase are shown in Figs

In what follows we present the results for reflected beam shapes using the angular spectrum decomposition.

3.3
Nonreciprocity in IF and vortex-induced shifts

We now focus on the influence of symmetry breaking on the IF shift under different polarization measurement schemes. For conventional IF shift measurements using incident left- or right-circularly polarized Gaussian beams (results not shown), the difference between the forward and backward propagating beams remains relatively small and is often difficult to resolve experimentally. This motivates the exploration of alternative polarization-selective strategies capable of amplifying the underlying nonreciprocal transverse displacement.

Projective Measurements

Recall that finite-width beam consists of a distribution of wave vectors surrounding the central wave vector. Upon reflection, each component experiences a different polarization transformation, resulting in a reflected beam that is no longer uniformly polarized. This spatially varying polarization distribution gives rise to markedly different centroid shifts when different polarization components of the reflected field are selectively analyzed
[
20
,
44
]
. We first consider a projective measurement scheme in which an incident
s
-polarized Gaussian beam is projected onto the
+45^{\circ}
polarization state after reflection. The strong resonant suppression of the reflection coefficient
r
gives rise to a small transverse IF shift. The corresponding reflected beam profiles for forward and backward illumination are displayed in Figs.
5
(a) and
5
(b), respectively.
Note that one has a more intense reflected beam for the forward propagation as compared to that for backward direction. In order to amplify the nonreciprocal response, we employ a weak-value amplification
[
45
,
19
,
20
,
44
]
protocol. The incident beam is prepared in the
s
-polarized state
(0,1)^{T}
and the reflected beam is post-selected onto a nearly orthogonal state
(1,\epsilon)^{T}
. In this regime, the weak spin orbit interaction between the polarization and spatial degrees of freedom is dramatically amplified through near-orthogonal post selection
[
21

, resulting in a giant transverse displacement depicted in Figs.

(c) and
5
(d). To quantify the sensing performance, we calculate the differential transverse shift

\Delta_{y}=y^{F}_{IF}-y^{B}_{IF}
. Similar to the methodology adopted in Sec. 3.2, the sensitivity is extracted from the slope of the differential shift as a function of refractive-index variation. The resulting differential shifts for the
+45^{\circ}

projection scheme and the weak-value amplification protocol are shown in Figs.
5
(e) and
5

1.1304*10^{-8}
RIU/nm). These results demonstrate that even a relatively simple symmetry-broken resonant structure can exhibit exceptionally high refractive-index sensitivity when combined with polarization-selective amplification techniques. Remarkably, the achieved sensitivity compares favorably with, and in some cases exceeds, values reported in several recent IF-shift-based sensing platforms
[
29
,
30

. For brevity, we have restricted the present discussion to RI asymmetry. However, an analogous nonreciprocal IF response can also be obtained through translation-induced symmetry breaking, exhibiting qualitatively similar behavior and sensing characteristics.

Vortex Induced Shifts

We now turn our attention to optical beams carrying OAM and investigate whether Laguerre Gaussian (LG) beams can provide a viable platform for RI sensing in symmetry-broken resonant structures. To this end, a small RI mismatch is introduced into the system, following the same procedure described in Sec.
3.2
. The IF shifts are then calculated for forward and backward propagating LG beams with topological charges
\ell=1
(see Fig.
6
(a)) and
\ell=2
, and the corresponding results for the shifts as functions of
\theta
are presented in Fig.
6
(b). Unlike the Gaussian beam case, where opposite sign IF shifts can be observed for forward and backward propagation at a given angle of incidence, the LG beam exhibits qualitatively different behavior. As evident from Fig.
6
(b), no such positive–negative shift pair exists at a common angle. The dispersion-like features originate from the intrinsic vortex structure of the LG beam with increasing effect for larger
\ell
. The spatial (angular) GH shift becomes coupled to the angular (spatial) IF shift, with the vortex charge
\ell
governing the strength of this coupling
[
46
,
47
]
. Due to the absence of opposite-sign shifts at a single angle of incidence, LG beams are not ideally suited for sensing applications based on the symmetry-breaking mechanism discussed in this work. Nevertheless, a sensing protocol can still be implemented through a double-measurement scheme, wherein the beam shift is evaluated at two distinct angular positions. Specifically, the angle of incidence for forward propagation is chosen at the dip, while for backward propagation it is selected at the corresponding peak. The resulting differential response between these two measurements can then serve as the sensing signal. To gain further insight into the beam dynamics, we tune the angle of incidence to the dip and peak positions identified in Fig.
6
(b) and calculate the corresponding reflected beam profiles for an LG beam with topological charge
\ell=1
. The results are shown in Figs.
6
(c)–
6
(f). In contrast to the incident doughnut-shaped intensity distribution shown in Fig.
6
(a), the reflected beam profiles exhibit substantial distortion in the vicinity of resonance. The sharp resonant response dramatically modifies the angular spectrum of the beam
[
21
]
, leading to a severe deformation of the vortex structure and a partial/total loss of its characteristic ring-shaped profile. Such distortions are a direct consequence of the strong spectral filtering imposed by the resonant multilayer structure, where different spatial harmonics experience significantly different reflection amplitudes and phases. These observations further highlight the complex interplay between resonance, OAM, and symmetry breaking in determining the beam shift characteristics of structured light.

Conclusions

In conclusion we have studied the nonreciprocal effects in both GH and IF shifts when the spatial and temporal symmetry of a coupled waveguide structure is slightly disturbed. We have shown how a tiny displacement can be sensed by the giant differential GH shift for forward and backward propagation. We also demonstrated how a tiny refractive index mismatch can be accurately determined leading to theoretical sensitivities of the order of
10^{-9}
RIU/nm. The giant differential GH shift resulting from the direction dependent alternating signs of the individual shifts can be traced to contrasting direction dependent phases with sharp jumps due to high-
Q
coupled guided modes. In contrast to the existing literature our results are based on angular spectrum method yielding also the reflected beam shapes. We have shown clearly how focusing can distort the beams to the extent of generating a weak side lobe, which can affect the estimation of the beam centroid. We have reported an interesting counterintuitive direction dependent enhancement of the reflected beam energy due to losses, as compared to the lossless structure. Finally we presented projective measurement results for the IF shifts for lineaerly polarized and OAM carrying beams, demonstrating clearly the scope of weak value amplification for enhancing the shift for near-orthogonal projection. Our studies reveal the advantages of GH-shift based sensors over those based on the IF shifts with or without OAM.
