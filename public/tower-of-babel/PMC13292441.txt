Background

The Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) was launched in Türkiye in 1991 as part of national efforts to promote, protect, and support breastfeeding [1, 2]. While BFHI plays a critical role in the initiation of breastfeeding and prevention of prelacteal feeding [3–5], previous studies indicate that cultural and family characteristics, occupational conditions, exposure to disasters, and migration create distinct and persistent challenges that influence breastfeeding practices beyond the early postnatal period [6–11]. These findings highlight the need for context-specific and equitable support programs that systematically identify barriers and provide targeted breastfeeding support for different populations [11–22]. Over time, the scope of baby-friendly practices has expanded beyond hospitals to encompass the broader health system and community settings [13–18, 23, 24]. In Türkiye with this multi-level framework, the Baby-Friendly Family Medicine Initiative was introduced in 2002, followed by the Baby-Friendly Neonatal Intensive Care Unit Program in 2012 [1, 25]. To ensure that breastfeeding promotion becomes a sustained societal norm rather than a facility-based intervention, the Baby-Friendly Province Program was initiated in 2002, followed by the Golden Baby-Friendly Province Program in 2008 (Table 1).

Currently, all 81 provinces in Türkiye are designated as Baby-Friendly, and 73 provinces have achieved Golden Baby-Friendly Province status [1, 2]. According to the Baby-Friendly Province criteria, a minimum of three community-based social activities promoting breastfeeding awareness must be organized annually, and following designation, at least one social activity per year must be conducted and formally reported to the Ministry of Health (Table 1). In line with the Gold Baby-Friendly Province criteria, at least ten social activities must be conducted annually; after designation, at least three social activities per year are required, along with submission of an annual report.

In addition, Baby-Friendly Supporter Organizations are expected to conduct at least one social activity per year (Table 2). In Türkiye, Baby-Friendly Supporter Organizations encompass a wide range of institutions across different sectors, including public institutions (e.g., municipalities, governorates, and public directorates), healthcare-related settings (e.g., pharmacies and health centers), private sector workplaces and industry (e.g., factories and companies), commercial and public spaces (e.g., shopping malls, cafés, and restaurants), transportation hubs (e.g., airports and bus terminals), as well as educational and social facilities (e.g., kindergartens, family life centers, and public education centers). This broad institutional diversity reflects a multisectoral and community-integrated approach, demonstrating that breastfeeding support in Türkiye is embedded not only within the health system but also across workplaces, public institutions, and community life.

Breastfeeding support in Türkiye is delivered as a continuous, system-integrated public health service across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care. This continuity is maintained through routine follow-up within family medicine units—from pregnancy through the postpartum and infancy periods—as well as through specialized services in hospitals and neonatal intensive care units. In addition, all facilities are periodically evaluated according to national Baby-Friendly criteria to ensure institutional sustainability. Within this framework, World Breastfeeding Week (1–7 August) and National Breastfeeding Week (1–7 October)—the latter symbolically referencing the 40th week of pregnancy in Turkey—serve as intensified, multisectoral campaign periods that enhance public visibility, community engagement, and awareness of these ongoing services. These campaigns do not constitute the primary mode of service delivery, nor are they intended to extend week-based activities across the entire year; rather, they function to amplify and make visible the continuous, system-based breastfeeding support already embedded within the health system and community structures.

From an international perspective, the Baby-Friendly Community Initiative (BFCI) was first launched in the United Kingdom in 1998 and has since been implemented in a variety of settings, including low- and middle-income countries such as India, Kenya, Cambodia, and Gambia, as well as high-income countries including Australia, Norway, Canada, New Zealand, and Italy [14–19, 26, 27]. Since its formal conceptualization and expansion by UNICEF and the WHO in the late 1990s and early 2000s, the BFCI has emphasized the importance of creating a welcoming and supportive environment for breastfeeding families. As outlined in its 7-point plan, BFCI implementation operates across multiple levels [18, 27–29]. At the individual level, it prioritizes workforce development through staff training in breastfeeding support. At the community level, it incorporates integrated support mechanisms such as home-based counseling, mother support groups, and workplace support [18, 27]. At the structural level, it promotes the establishment of physical resources such as breastfeeding corners and community-based support centers [18, 19]. Together, these components reflect a shift from facility-based care toward sustained, community-embedded support systems that combine professional services with peer and social support [30]. Compared with the BFHI, BFCI remains more challenging to standardize and evaluate due to its engagement with broader social and community-level determinants. However, previous studies suggest that BFCI is associated with improvements in exclusive breastfeeding at six months and may support longer breastfeeding duration, complementary feeding practices and improved infant nutrition outcomes [14, 18, 31].

Community-based breastfeeding promotion

Baby-Friendly Provinces in Türkiye have increasingly adopted innovative, community-based, and multisectoral approaches to breastfeeding promotion. These initiatives extend beyond clinical settings, aiming to reach diverse population groups, address social inequities, and foster community ownership of breastfeeding as a shared public health responsibility. The implemented activities were conceptually grouped into six thematic areas.

Trust-building and individualized support approaches

These activities emphasize interpersonal contact, continuity of care, cultural sensitivity, and equity-focused outreach, particularly for vulnerable and hard-to-reach populations.

Home visits to build trust and encourage breastfeeding

Home visits are conducted by healthcare professionals (family physicians and/or midwives/nurses) for the follow-up of pregnant and breastfeeding women [32], while social care workers are involved in cases where social risk factors are identified. As an institutionalized component of the health system, they are structurally sustained; however, the intensity of visits may vary depending on regional resources and population needs. A risk-based prioritization approach is therefore applied to ensure that disadvantaged and hard-to-reach populations receive targeted and equitable support. During these visits, trained healthcare professionals establish direct, one-to-one contact with families to assess breastfeeding practices, provide individualized counseling, and offer hands-on practical support (Fig. 1). This approach enables context-specific, on-site guidance, facilitates early identification of breastfeeding challenges, and supports timely problem management.

Home visits from Trabzon and Bayburt provinces

Tent visits for seasonal agricultural workers, addressing breastfeeding challenges under temporary and insecure living conditions

As part of on-site healthcare services delivered to seasonal agricultural workers and their families, tent visits are conducted to reduce health inequalities and improve access to care (Fig. 2). These visits are implemented throughout the year in regions where seasonal migration occurs, as an extension of primary health care services [33]. The visits are carried out by physicians and/or midwives/nurses and target vulnerable populations living under temporary and insecure conditions, who often have limited access to routine healthcare services.

Breastfeeding support through tent visits for seasonal agricultural workers in Eskişehir

During these visits, comprehensive information on breastfeeding, feeding practices and infant care is provided to pregnant mothers and mothers. Educational materials and informational brochures are distributed, participants’ questions are addressed, and individualized counseling is offered. Breastfeeding practices are also assessed, and practical guidance is provided to address context-specific challenges.

This outreach approach aims to improve equitable access to breastfeeding support, facilitate early identification of breastfeeding difficulties, and extend baby-friendly practices to underserved and hard-to-reach populations.

Breastfeeding counseling and support in disaster and emergency settings

The major earthquake that struck Türkiye on 6 February 2023 affected the provinces of Adıyaman, Kilis, Diyarbakır, Gaziantep, Osmaniye, Şanlıurfa, Adana, Malatya, and Hatay. Following the disaster, healthcare professionals conducted outreach visits to AFAD emergency tents [34]. During these visits, breastfeeding counseling was offered to pregnant women and mothers, alongside information on the importance of breast milk and optimal infant feeding practices (Fig. 3). Individual questions were addressed, and practical guidance was provided to support breastfeeding under challenging conditions.

Breastfeeding support through tent-based outreach in disaster and emergency contexts in Osmaniye

These activities aimed to ensure the continuity of baby-friendly practices in emergency settings, support maternal and infant health, and provide targeted assistance to vulnerable populations during the recovery process.

Outreach services for migrant mothers and pregnant women, tailored to linguistic, cultural, and social needs

At the Migrant Health Center, educational sessions and informational booths are organized for migrant pregnant women and mothers (Fig. 4). These services are delivered by Syrian healthcare professionals with consideration of linguistic and cultural differences, aiming to ensure accessible and culturally appropriate care [35]. During these events, participants receive information on the importance of breastfeeding, the benefits of breast milk, and essential infant care practices. Individual questions are addressed, and counseling is provided to support informed decision-making. This approach aims to improve access to health services for migrant populations, enhance breastfeeding awareness, and provide equitable, culturally sensitive support within diverse community settings.

Breastfeeding outreach services for migrant mothers and pregnant women in Gaziantep province

Targeted breastfeeding education for hearing-impaired mothers, delivered through sign-language–supported educational videos

In Bursa province, a sign language–supported educational video was developed by healthcare professionals to improve access to breastfeeding information for mothers with hearing impairments. The video explains the importance of breast milk, appropriate breastfeeding techniques, and basic infant care practices using sign language, supported by visual content (Fig. 5). This initiative aims to provide accessible and inclusive health education, enhance breastfeeding awareness, and address communication barriers that may limit access to routine counseling services. It also contributes to increasing social awareness of inclusive maternal and child health practices. Following central-level evaluation, this material has been identified as a good practice example and is planned to be disseminated to other provinces through digital platforms and integration into training modules.

Targeted breastfeeding education for hearing-impaired mothers in Bursa province; The visuals convey key breastfeeding messages, including: (a) “Dear mothers and expectant mothers,” (b) “Breast milk is the most natural and freshest food,” (c) “Your baby needs nothing else—not even water,” and (d) “Skin-to-skin and close bonding with your baby”

Mothers and families in the early parenting period: community engagement and social inclusion

These activities aim to strengthen family support systems and normalize breastfeeding within the household and the immediate social environment. This initiative has been clarified as a community-based awareness campaign coordinated by Provincial Health Directorates under the Ministry of Health, in collaboration with local governments, educational institutions, and community partners (including youth groups and civil society stakeholders).

“Meeting with Mothers” events accompanied by live music

As part of the “Hand in Hand with Mothers for Future Generations” initiative and related “Meeting with Mothers and Music Recital” events organized in different provinces, mothers and pregnant women were brought together in supportive community settings, particularly during Breastfeeding Weeks (Fig. 6).

“Hand in Hand with Mothers for Future Generations” and “Meeting With Mothers and Music Recital” Events from Çanakkale and Bayburt provinces

These activities were designed to emphasize that breastfeeding is not solely a practice confined to healthcare settings, but a public health priority that benefits from community-wide engagement. To raising awareness about breastfeeding, the events provided opportunities for social interaction, emotional support, information sharing, and engagement with healthcare professionals. The inclusion of classical music recitals aimed to create a calm and supportive environment, facilitating participation and interaction. Within this context, interactive discussions, educational sessions, and counseling activities were conducted to provide information on breastfeeding and infant care. Overall, these initiatives represent community-based approaches intended to support maternal well-being, enhance awareness, and promote breastfeeding-friendly environments.

Provision of traditional postpartum refreshments (e.g., lohusa sherbet) during community gatherings

In Türkiye, lohusa sherbet is a traditional beverage offered to mothers after childbirth as a symbol of good health and recovery. It is traditionally believed that, due to its sweet and fluid nature, lohusa sherbet may support lactation and increase breast milk production [36]. Within the scope of Breastfeeding Week, this cultural practice was incorporated into both healthcare-based and community-based settings Lohusa sherbet was offered to mothers in hospital postpartum units following delivery, as well as during community events organized by Provincial Health Directorates in collaboration with local municipalities, such as public stands set up in parks and community spaces, (Fig. 7). This approach aimed to acknowledge cultural traditions while creating a supportive and familiar environment for mothers. It also provided an opportunities to integrate breastfeeding-related messages, education, and counseling within culturally meaningful and socially engaging contexts.

Serving of traditional postpartum Sherbet (Lohusa sherbet) from Siirt province

The distribution of traditional ‘Lohusa Sherbet’ is presented not only as a cultural custom but as a strategic tool for postnatal social support and maternal hydration, bridging cultural heritage with modern breastfeeding advocacy [36].

Yoga sessions designed for mothers and infants to support maternal well-being

Yoga activities organized for mothers and their infants included practices aimed at supporting both physical and psychological well-being (Fig. 8). The sessions incorporated movements and breathing techniques designed to promote relaxation and strengthen mother–infant interaction. Brief educational messages on breastfeeding and infant care were also integrated into these sessions in İzmir.

Yoga activity for mother–infant Dyads in İzmir and laughter yoga activity for pregnant women in Kırklareli

In addition, with the collaboration of Kırklareli University and the Provincial Directorate of Health, laughter yoga was incorporated into the training curriculum of the Ayçiçeği Antenatal School, operating within a Healthy Life Center as part of the Normal Birth Action Plan (Fig. 8). This activity was introduced as a complementary approach to support the well-being of pregnant women attending antenatal education programs. Previous studies suggest that laughter yoga may contribute to pregnant women’ stress management, emotional relaxation [37–39], maternal attachement, and breastfeeding self-efficacy [40–42]. The activities conducted in Türkiye, such as yoga for nursing mothers and music-centered meetings, share significant thematic similarities with community-based initiatives in other regions, such as ‘Breastfeeding benches in parks‘ [43–45]. These global parallels underscore a universal trend in public health: transitioning from clinical consultations to social environments that normalize breastfeeding as a lifestyle [46].

Crawling races and family-oriented interactive activities promoting positive early-life experiences

Within the framework of the National Breastfeeding Week (1–7 October 2025), a crawling race event was organized by the Antalya Provincial Directorate of Health (Fig. 9). Small gifts were distributed to all participating infants, while those placing first, second, and third were awarded symbolic prizes. This event was designed as a family-oriented activity to encourage community participation and create a positive and engaging environment for families with young children. It also provided an opportunity to share information on breastfeeding and early child development within an informal and accessible setting.

Family-oriented crawling event during national breastfeeding week in Antalya provinces

Participatory events like crawling races are utilized to visually symbolize the role of breast milk in achieving infant developmental milestones, effectively communicating the long-term health benefits of breastfeeding to a wider audience.

As part of the intensified awareness efforts during Breastfeeding Week activities, various theatre-based activities were organized to engage the public and healthcare professionals on breastfeeding-related topics.

At Ödemiş State Hospital, İzmir, healthcare professionals voluntarily prepared and performed a theatrical play entitled “Champion Breast Milk,” designed to convey key messages on the importance of breast milk for infant health and development (Fig. 11). Through scenario-based storytelling, the performance aimed to present breastfeeding-related clinical information in a more accessible and culturally resonant format for the community. Similarly, within the framework of National Breastfeeding Week (1–7 October 2025), a theatre performance was organized at Antalya Training and Research Hospital specifically for hospital staff to reinforce professional awareness and knowledge regarding breastfeeding support. These theatre-based activities represent an interactive health communication strategy used to disseminate breastfeeding-related messages at both community and institutional levels, providing a creative alternative to traditional educational methods.

In addition, Health Vocational High School students organized awareness walks carrying banners with messages such as “Breast Milk Is the First and Most Natural Food” and “Milk = Bonding, Breastfeeding = Life-Giving Milk”, thereby engaging with the wider community. Similar banner-led marches were organized in several provinces under different slogans including “Hand in Hand for Mothers and Babies”, “Walk for Healthy Future Generations”, “Let us protect this treasure together”, “Breast milk is a miracle”, and “The miracle lies in your milk”, to communicate breastfeeding-related messages in public settings.

Within the framework of Breastfeeding Week 2025, and in alignment with World Walking Day, a coordinated march was conducted by the Adana Provincial Directorate of Health between 1 and 7 October (Fig. 12). These activities were designed to engage community members and disseminate breastfeeding-related messages in accessible and participatory ways.

To extend breastfeeding promotion beyond healthcare settings, activities were also conducted in public spaces with high community traffic. In this context, outreach meetings were organized with mothers and pregnant women in marketplaces and field settings. At information booths established by healthcare professionals, participants received guidance on breastfeeding and infant care, their questions were addressed, and individualized counseling was provided (Fig. 13).

Activities organized through public education centers to reach broader segments of society

These centers provide lifelong learning opportunities and vocational training across diverse fields, including healthcare, handicrafts, and family literacy, to a broad demographic of adult learners. During targeted periods such as Breastfeeding Week, healthcare professionals conduct specialized training sessions and outreach visits for attendees. For instance, hands-on demonstrations on breastfeeding techniques and infant care are provided to participants in various vocational and personal development courses. In addition, educational sessions were provided to women attending Qur’an courses, focusing on the importance of breast milk and ways to support breastfeeding within the community (Fig. 14). By leveraging the extensive network of Public Education Centers, the Baby-Friendly Province initiative effectively transitions breastfeeding promotion from clinical settings to everyday community learning environments.

Environmental sustainability and breastfeeding

Linking breastfeeding promotion with planetary health, climate awareness, and environmental responsibility:

Tree-planting activities highlighting breastfeeding as an environmentally sustainable infant-feeding practice

Breastfeeding promotion activities in Türkiye have been integrated with environmental sustainability initiatives, aligning with global public health perspectives that recognize breast milk as a sustainable and eco-friendly resource. In this context, tree-planting activities were organized in several provinces. A prominent example is the “Let Us Grow Two Saplings Together” project in Bayburt (Fig. 19), where 1,098 saplings—representing the total number of infants born in the province throughout the year—were planted in the Arslan Mountain Provincial Forest. During these events, provincial health authorities provided targeted counseling to mothers. The high-level participation of local and institutional authorities reflects a multisectoral commitment to both maternal-child health and environmental preservation.

Tree-planting activities highlighting breastfeeding from provinces

Conceptually, these initiatives highlight the ecological dimensions of breastfeeding, which is a ‘zero-waste’ form of nutrition requiring no packaging, industrial processing, or transportation [47]. By linking the growth of a sapling to the healthy development of an infant, the program fosters a shared societal responsibility for nurturing both the next generation and the environment. This approach illustrates how breastfeeding can be framed within broader themes of ‘One Health,’ planetary health, and long-term community well-being, moving beyond clinical boundaries into the realm of environmental ethics.

Visual communication and creative public messaging

Enhancing the visibility of breastfeeding through innovative, symbolic, and high-impact communication strategies:

“Turquoise light” illumination events symbolizing hope for a healthy and sustainable future through breastfeeding

To increase the visibility of breastfeeding promotion, the color ‘turquoise’ has been used as part of the institutional identity of the Baby-Friendly Program in Türkiye (Fig. 20). The choice of turquoise—symbolizing nature, vitality, and well-being—provides a culturally resonant and aesthetically cohesive element for public communication [48].

“Turquoise light” illumination as a symbol of breastfeeding awareness in Denizli and Rize provinces

During World (1–7 August) and National (1–7 October) Breastfeeding Weeks, this visual identity was amplified through the illumination of landmark buildings, public squares, and digital infrastructure in shopping centers across various provinces. These activities extend breastfeeding-related messaging beyond clinical settings into everyday urban environments, creating a symbolic and recognizable visual presence in public spaces. The consistent use of this color across different settings may contribute to its recognition in association with breastfeeding promotion and supports the coherence of national public health communication efforts [49]. Framing breastfeeding as an environmentally sustainable practice, these initiatives highlight its minimal ecological footprint, while symbolic actions such as turquoise lighting demonstrate the visible support of local governments.

Large-scale banners and billboards displayed in public spaces, and wrapped vehicles

In city centers and high-traffic areas, billboards featured posters and messages encouraging breastfeeding, while institutional vehicles and hospital entrance gates were covered with visual materials highlighting the “Importance of Breastfeeding” and the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding of the Baby-Friendly Initiative (Fig. 21). These visual and environmental arrangements were intended to increase the visibility of breastfeeding-related messages and to create supportive environments for mothers within both public and healthcare settings.

Conclusions

This viewpoint presents that Baby-Friendly Province initiatives in Türkiye as a comprehensive, multi-level approach that extends breastfeeding promotion beyond health facility–based interventions. The framework is characterized by its vertical and horizontal integration across primary, secondary, and tertiary healthcare levels, together with multisectoral community engagement. Large-scale activities (e.g., stadium banners, cycling events, and aerial displays) are not implemented by a single institution, but are carried out through multisectoral collaboration. These initiatives are coordinated by Provincial Health Directorates under the Ministry of Health, in partnership with local governments, sports clubs, event organizers, and relevant public and private stakeholders, following the necessary administrative and permission processes. By positioning breastfeeding support as a coordinated public health priority rather than an isolated clinical task, the program provides a structured yet adaptable platform for locally tailored activities. The requirement for regular community-based activities and annual reporting to the Ministry of Health supports the continuity of the Baby-Friendly designation as an ongoing dynamic process rather than a one-time achievement. While Breastfeeding Weeks serve as intensified periods for public visibility, the continuity of the program is fundamentally sustained through routine prenatal and postpartum care within family medicine units and the rigorous annual monitoring required to maintain the ‘Baby-Friendly’ designation. The diversity of implemented activities—including outreach to vulnerable populations (including seasonal agricultural workers and migrant communities through dedicated Migrant Health Centers), disaster-responsive interventions, youth engagement, and environmental sustainability initiatives—illustrates how national policies can be translated into context-specific community practices. However, an important limitation is the absence of systematic evaluation of these activities in relation to breastfeeding outcomes. While this viewpoint provides a descriptive overview of implementation practices, it does not include data on breastfeeding prevalence or duration. The development of standardized indicators and longitudinal monitoring systems will be essential to assess the effectiveness of these approaches. Strengthening these systems through the systematic classification and coding of community-based activities across provinces may enable longitudinal assessment of their contribution to breastfeeding outcomes, thereby supporting a more robust evaluation of effectiveness.

By addressing social norms, environmental sustainability, inclusiveness, and disaster preparedness, these approaches provide a framework for positioning breastfeeding as a shared societal responsibility and a public health priority across the life course. Overall, the Turkish Baby-Friendly Province experience offers insights for countries seeking to strengthen Baby-Friendly Community Initiative (BFCI) implementation through community-oriented and system-integrated approaches.
