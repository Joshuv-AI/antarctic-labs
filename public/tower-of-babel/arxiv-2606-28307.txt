Second-Order KKT Guarantees for Bregman ADMM in Nonconvex and Non-Lipschitz Optimization
Shuang Li, Zhihui Zhu, Qiuwei Li

Abstract
We analyze Bregman ADMM for nonconvex linearly constrained problems under two-sided relative smoothness, a condition that replaces the standard Lipschitz gradient assumption with a Hessian comparison relative to a Bregman kernel. This setting covers polynomial objectives arising in matrix and tensor models for which a global Lipschitz-gradient constant need not exist. We show that on an invariant open state-space domain, one iteration of Bregman ADMM defines a smooth primal–dual fixed-point map whose strict-saddle KKT points are unstable fixed points; consequently, from random initialization the iterates converge to a strict saddle with probability zero. Combined with existing first-order convergence results, this yields almost-sure second-order stationarity of limiting KKT points. We extend the analysis to a multi-block star consensus formulation for distributed optimization. The technical novelty lies in a determinant reduction with a Bregman-specific symmetrization and scaling step in the two block spectral argument, together with a null space cancellation exploiting the star graph structure in the consensus case. Numerical experiments on distributed matrix factorization illustrate the theory, and a symmetric tensor factorization example demonstrates the broader Bregman proximal splitting idea beyond the separable consensus setting.

First-order convergence and complexity for nonconvex ADMM.

For nonconvex linearly constrained problems and related consensus formulations, convergence of ADMM to first order stationary points has been studied under various regularity conditions.
[
17
]
establishes convergence for certain nonconvex consensus and sharing formulations under regularity assumptions on the objective and subproblems and sufficiently large augmented Lagrangian penalty parameters, while
[
37
]
analyzes a Bregman modification of ADMM for nonconvex composite problems and proves convergence to stationary points of the associated augmented Lagrangian under suitable assumptions.
[
39
]
further proves global convergence of ADMM for nonconvex nonsmooth problems by combining conditions that ensure lower boundedness, sufficient descent and a subgradient bound, together with the Kurdyka Łojasiewicz (KŁ) inequality. More recently,
[
10

develops finite iteration convergence guarantees under restricted strong convexity without requiring smoothness or differentiability, and
[
29

proposes communication efficient decentralized primal dual methods for smooth nonconvex consensus optimization.

Second-order stationarity and strict-saddle avoidance.

Moving beyond first-order stationarity is useful because many structured nonconvex problems have benign landscapes in which all second-order stationary points are globally optimal; see, e.g., low-rank matrix recovery
[
14
]
and tensor decomposition
[
13
]
. For ADMM-type methods,
[
18
]
uses a dynamical-systems viewpoint and the stable manifold theorem
[
34
, Theorem III.7]
to show that linearized ADMM avoids strict saddles almost surely under a Lipschitz-gradient assumption.
Beyond ADMM, strict-saddle avoidance has been extended to broader classes of first-order and proximal-type algorithms. In particular,
[
31
]
shows that a stable-manifold argument continues to apply under vanishing stepsizes (covering, among others, mirror-descent-type updates), and
[
9
]
establishes that proximal methods avoid (active) strict saddles for weakly convex nonsmooth objectives.
We note that
[
9
]
works in the nonsmooth weakly convex regime and introduces the notion of
active strict saddle

Bregman geometry and relative smoothness.

A key limitation of Lipschitz-gradient analyses is that
\|\nabla^{2}f\|
may be unbounded in many applications of interest (e.g., polynomial objectives arising in matrix/tensor models), so the standard smoothness constant does not exist globally. Relative smoothness and Bregman first-order methods
[
2
,
3
]
provide a principled alternative by replacing Euclidean geometry with a problem-adapted distance-generating function
h
(see also the Bregman divergence
[
6
,
5
]
). Bregman-divergence-based ADMM methods have been studied for first-order convergence, e.g.,
[
37
, Theorem III.8]
and
[
36
, Theorem 3.9]
; we discuss specific Bregman ADMM variants next.

Bregman ADMM variants.

Early Bregman variants of ADMM were proposed to better match the problem geometry and to simplify subproblems by replacing Euclidean proximal terms with Bregman divergences; see, e.g., the Bregman ADMM of
[
38
]
. More recent work has developed first-order convergence theory in nonconvex and multi-block settings. For instance,
[
27
]
studies an improved Bregman ADMM with convergence-rate guarantees,
[
32
]
considers a Bregman proximal-linearized scheme for separable sums coupled by a difference of functions term, and
[
8
]
analyzes a distributed multi-block variant for nonconvex and nonsmooth sharing problems; see also
[
36
, Theorem 3.9]
for a representative first-order result. What is missing from this line of work is a mechanism excluding convergence to strict saddles. Our contribution fills this gap: under relative (bi-)smoothness, strict saddles are unstable fixed points of the induced smooth map, and random initialization therefore rules out convergence to them almost surely. Combined with standard first-order convergence assumptions, this yields second-order stationarity of limiting KKT points.

At the same time, the extension from the Euclidean and Lipschitz setting of
[
18
]
to Bregman ADMM is not a trivial substitution of proximal terms. The Euclidean analysis in
[
18
]
also reduces its instability proof, after a suitable transformation, to a real symmetric spectral argument. In the Bregman setting, however, replacing the Euclidean proximal term by a Bregman divergence introduces metric blocks from the kernel Hessians, so the determinant reduction and sign change argument must be rederived in the Bregman geometry. In the two block case, this requires a diagonal rescaling that symmetrizes the reduced spectral problem and a correspondingly rescaled strict saddle direction. In the consensus case, the proof is again not a direct lift of the Euclidean argument: the star consensus structure creates a hub and peripheral coupling pattern, and the key sign change relies on a null space cancellation of the consensus penalty, since the equal
\mathbf{x}
test direction lies in the kernel of the star graph Laplacian. These are the main new technical ingredients behind the strict saddle result in the Bregman setting.

4
Extension to Multi-block Case

The two-block analysis already contains the essential mechanism. We now show how the same approach extends to a global consensus model, which covers the distributed settings most relevant for our applications.

4.1
Global consensus problem

Consider the following unconstrained centralized problem

\minimize_{\mathbf{x},\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}f_{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}),

(10)

where

One can distribute this problem across a network of

1

[
18

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j})\;\;\mbox{s.t.}\;\;\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{x}_{1},\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall j\geq 2.

(11)

Problem (

\mathbf{x}_{j}
are constrained to agree, while the local variables
\mathbf{y}_{j}

The constraints

encode a star-topology (global consensus) model in which agent

More general consensus topologies (e.g., edge-wise constraints

over a connected graph) can be written as linear consensus constraints and reformulated into a global consensus form by introducing an auxiliary global variable, but extending the proof to a specific reformulation requires checking the induced fixed-point-map structure in addition to strong convexity of the resulting Bregman-proximal subproblems. For clarity, we focus on the star/global consensus case.

Remark 5.1

(Intuition for the test direction)
.

The strict-saddle condition (

At

First, we show that for
\mu\to 0^{+}

5.2
Proof sketch for the two-block case (
Theorem

3.1
)

The full proof is in
Appendix

A
; here we outline the key steps and highlight where the argument differs from the single-block case above.

Step 1: Fixed-point map and Jacobian.

One iteration of
Algorithm

2
is decomposed as
g=g_{3}\circ g_{2}\circ g_{1}
(primal
\mathbf{x}
-update, primal
\mathbf{y}
-update, dual ascent). By the implicit function theorem applied to the two primal optimality conditions,
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
\Omega
; their Jacobians are nonsingular because the corresponding subproblem Hessians with respect to the updated primal variables are positive definite. The dual update
g_{3}
has a nonsingular block triangular Jacobian, and hence
Dg=Dg_{3}\,Dg_{2}\,Dg_{1}
is well defined and nonsingular on
\Omega
.

Step 2: Jacobian at a fixed point.

At a strict-saddle fixed point
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\star})
, the Jacobian evaluates to
Dg=\mathbf{I}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
with

{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{N},

where
\mathbf{M}
collects the Bregman-regularized subproblem Hessian blocks, while
\mathbf{N}
collects the remaining curvature and primal dual coupling blocks arising from the augmented Lagrangian and the Gauss Seidel ordering (the explicit expression is given in (
48
) of
Appendix

A
).
It suffices to find
\mu>0
with
\det({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}+\mu\mathbf{I})=0
.

Step 3: Schur reduction and symmetrization.

After Schur-complementing the dual block (as in the single-block case), the determinant condition reduces to the singularity of a
2\times 2
block matrix
\mathbf{C}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}
. Unlike the single-block case,
\mathbf{C}(\mu)
is
not
symmetric: its
(1,2)
and
(2,1)
blocks carry different scalar coefficients depending on
\mu
. The remedy is a diagonal similarity
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{I}_{n},\alpha(\mu)\mathbf{I}_{m})
with
\alpha(\mu)=\sqrt{(2+\mu+1/\mu)/(2+1/\mu)}
, which symmetrizes
\mathbf{C}(\mu)
to a real-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{J}(\mu)=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{C}(\mu)\mathbf{D}
without changing its determinant.

Step 4: Sign change via a
\mu
-dependent test direction.

The symmetrization changes the natural test direction
(d_{x},d_{y})
to
(d_{x},s(\mu)d_{y})
with
s(\mu)=\alpha(\mu)\to 1
as
\mu\to 0^{+}
. Using the KKT constraint
\mathbf{A}d_{x}+\mathbf{B}d_{y}=\mathbf{0}
and the strict-saddle negativity
d_{x}^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{1}d_{x}+d_{y}^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{2}d_{y}<0
, one shows
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{J}(\mu))<0
for small
\mu>0
, while
\mathbf{J}(\mu)/\mu\to\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{F}_{1}+\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{H}_{1}+\rho\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A},\,\mathbf{F}_{2}+\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{H}_{2}+\rho\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B})\succ 0
gives
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{J}(\mu))>0
for large
\mu
. The intermediate value theorem then yields the desired zero crossing. Equivalently,
\det({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}+\mu\mathbf{I})=0
for some
\mu>0
, so
Dg=\mathbf{I}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
has an eigenvalue with magnitude greater than one.

5.3
Proof sketch for the consensus case (
Theorem

4.2
)

The full proof is in
Appendix

B
. Here we indicate how the single block mechanism and the two block spectral argument extend to the star consensus formulation of
Section

4
.

Step 1: Fixed point map and nonsingularity.

One iteration of
Algorithm

3
defines a smooth fixed point map on the state
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{J},\mathbf{y}_{J},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{J})
. The update decomposes into
2J+1
elementary maps, one
\mathbf{x}_{j}
update and one
\mathbf{y}_{j}
update for each agent, followed by the dual update. Relative bi-smoothness, strong bi-convexity of the kernels, and the stepsize condition make each local Bregman proximal Hessian positive definite, so the Jacobian of each
\mathbf{x}_{j}
and
\mathbf{y}_{j}
update is nonsingular by the implicit function theorem. The dual update has a nonsingular block triangular Jacobian. Hence every elementary Jacobian is nonsingular, and the chain rule gives
\det(Dg)\neq 0
on
\Omega
.

Step 2: Jacobian at a strict saddle fixed point.

At a strict saddle KKT point of the consensus problem, the Jacobian again has the form
Dg=\mathbf{I}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
. As in the two block case, it is enough to find
\mu>0
such that
\det({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}+\mu\mathbf{I})=0
. After a positive row column scaling that symmetrizes the determinant condition and after eliminating the dual blocks by Schur complementation, the condition reduces to the singularity of a real symmetric matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu)
built from the local Hessian blocks and the star consensus coupling.

Step 3: Star consensus cancellation.

The key new feature is the star graph structure. A feasible strict saddle direction has equal
\mathbf{x}
components across all agents, say
(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{y}}^{J})
, because the constraints impose
\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
. This equal
\mathbf{x}
direction lies in the kernel of the star graph Laplacian. Consequently, the consensus penalty and the Schur complement correction vanish on the test direction, leaving only the block Hessian contribution. As
\mu\to 0^{+}
, the corresponding quadratic form has a well-defined limit, and this limit is exactly the strict saddle negative curvature.

Step 4: Large
\mu
positivity and instability.

For large
\mu
, the scaled matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu)/\mu
converges to a block diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are the positive definite Bregman proximal Hessian blocks. Thus
\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu))>0
for large
\mu
, while the previous step gives
\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu))<0
for small positive
\mu
. Continuity of the eigenvalues gives a zero crossing, hence an eigenvalue of
Dg
with magnitude greater than one. Applying
Theorem

5.1
then excludes convergence to strict saddle KKT points almost surely.

6
Numerical Experiments

We consider two polynomial models: distributed matrix factorization and symmetric tensor factorization. Both fall outside the globally Lipschitz-smooth regime. The former fits the separable consensus framework of
Theorem

4.2
and admits an explicit relative-bi-smoothness certificate; the latter is included because the same splitting idea leads to explicit Bregman-proximal updates even though the resulting objective is not separable across agents. For distributed matrix factorization, the corresponding centralized problem has a benign strict saddle landscape under standard assumptions, so second order stationary points are globally optimal; the tensor example is used as an algorithmic illustration of the same Bregman proximal splitting idea beyond the separable setting.

6.1
Distributed matrix factorization

Given a data matrix
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with column partitioning

\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Z}_{1}&\mathbf{Z}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{Z}_{J}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbf{Z}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m_{j}}
,
suppose
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Z})=r
and let
\mathbf{X}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
span the column space of
\mathbf{Z}
. The centralized and distributed matrix factorization problems are

\displaystyle\underline{\operatorname{Centralized}}:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\mathbf{X},\{\mathbf{Y}_{j}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{Z}_{j}\|_{F}^{2};

(28)

\displaystyle\underline{\operatorname{Distributed}}:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\mathbf{X}_{j}\},\{\mathbf{Y}_{j}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|\mathbf{X}_{j}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{Z}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}\penalty\ \;\;\mbox{s.t.}\;\;\penalty\ \mathbf{X}_{j}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\penalty\ \forall j\geq 2.

(29)

The distributed formulation enforces exact consensus on the shared factor while allowing local right factors, a standard model in distributed low-rank matrix recovery
[
43
]
.

Applicability of
Theorem

4.2
.

Each local loss
f_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\mathbf{Y}_{j})\doteq\|\mathbf{X}_{j}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{Z}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}
is a polynomial of bidegree
(2,2)
in the entries of
(\mathbf{X}_{j},\mathbf{Y}_{j})
, hence relatively bi-smooth under a suitable kernel by
Proposition

2.2
. In contrast, many standard consensus algorithms (e.g., linearized ADMM
[
18
]
, decentralized gradient descent
[
42
]
, and EXTRA
[
33
]
) require global Lipschitz gradient assumptions, which do not hold for such polynomial objectives. The centralized formulation (
28
) has a strict-saddle landscape with no spurious local minima under the rank condition above and standard identifiability conditions
[
43
,
30
]
, so second-order stationary points are global minimizers.

Kernel choice and block subproblems.

With
\mathbf{X}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{j}\times r}
, we use the product-type bi-kernel

h_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\mathbf{Y}_{j})\doteq\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}+1\Big)\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}+1\Big),

(30)

which is strongly bi-convex. Writing
h_{j}=h_{x,j}\cdot h_{y,j}
with
h_{x,j}(\mathbf{X}_{j})\doteq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}+1
and
h_{y,j}(\mathbf{Y}_{j})\doteq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}+1
, the product-kernel identity in
Section

2
gives

\displaystyle D^{x}_{h_{j}}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime};\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=h_{y,j}(\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\prime})\,D_{h_{x,j}}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}+1\Big)\|\mathbf{X}_{j}-\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle D^{y}_{h_{j}}(\mathbf{Y}_{j},\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\prime};\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=h_{x,j}(\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime})\,D_{h_{y,j}}(\mathbf{Y}_{j},\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}+1\Big)\|\mathbf{Y}_{j}-\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}.

(31)

In
Algorithm

3
, the Bregman term
\frac{1}{\eta}D^{x}_{h_{j}}(\mathbf{X}_{j},\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k-1};\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1})
contributes a quadratic proximal regularizer
\frac{\alpha_{j}^{k}}{2}\|\mathbf{X}_{j}-\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k-1}\|_{F}^{2}
with iteration-dependent coefficient

\alpha_{j}^{k}\doteq\frac{1}{\eta}(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1}\|_{F}^{2}+1)
;
the subsequent
\mathbf{Y}_{j}
-update uses
\frac{\beta_{j}^{k}}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{j}-\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1}\|_{F}^{2}
with
\beta_{j}^{k}\doteq\frac{1}{\eta}(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k}\|_{F}^{2}+1)
.
For a non-hub agent (

), setting the gradient of the
\mathbf{X}_{j}
-subproblem to zero yields the
r\times r
normal equation

\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k}\big(2\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1\top}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1}+\gamma_{j}^{k}\mathbf{I}_{r}\big)=2\mathbf{Z}_{j}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}^{k-1}+\rho\mathbf{X}_{1}^{k}+\alpha_{j}^{k}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k-1},

where
\gamma_{j}^{k}\doteq\rho+\alpha_{j}^{k}
(the hub-node update has the same structure with
(J{-}1)\rho

). Since
\gamma_{j}^{k}>0
, the coefficient matrix
2\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1\top}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1}+\gamma_{j}^{k}\mathbf{I}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is positive definite, so each block update reduces to an
r\times r
linear solve. The
\mathbf{Y}_{j}
-update carries no consensus constraint; its normal equation is
\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k}(2\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k\top}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k}+\beta_{j}^{k}\mathbf{I}_{r})=2\mathbf{Z}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{k}+\beta_{j}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{j}^{k-1}
.

Results.

We generate a noiseless rank-

Symmetric tensor factorization

As a second application, we consider the symmetric tensor factorization problem

\displaystyle\minimize_{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}}\|\mathcal{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{u}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{F}^{2},

7
Conclusion

The main point of the paper is that the strict-saddle-avoidance mechanism familiar from smooth first-order methods survives in the Bregman ADMM setting, even when global Lipschitz smoothness is unavailable. What is nontrivial is not the dynamical-systems principle itself, but the verification that it continues to apply after replacing Euclidean proximal terms by Bregman regularization. In the two block case this requires a determinant reduction and symmetrization and scaling step specific to the Bregman setting in the spectral argument, while in the consensus case it relies on the special hub and peripheral structure of the star graph and the associated null space cancellation of the consensus penalty. Once these ingredients are in place, the iteration can again be viewed as a smooth primal–dual map on an invariant open domain, so strict saddle KKT points correspond to unstable fixed points and random initialization rules out convergence to them almost surely. Combined with standard first-order convergence results, this gives second-order stationarity of limiting KKT points.

Several natural questions remain. In the two-block setting, our analysis assumes a separable objective
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=f_{1}(\mathbf{x})+f_{2}(\mathbf{y})
, allowing nonzero cross-Hessian terms
\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}f
would widen the scope of the theory. In the consensus setting, the current result still relies on sum-separability across agents, whereas the symmetric tensor splitting in
Remark

6.1
suggests that interesting non-separable consensus formulations should also be accessible. The key technical barrier is that non-separability introduces off-diagonal Jacobian blocks between agents, breaking the star-shaped block sparsity used in
Appendix

B
. A promising approach is to treat these inter-agent couplings as perturbations of the separable Jacobian and show that the negative-curvature direction still dominates for small enough coupling, we leave a rigorous treatment to future work. It would also be worthwhile to understand whether the same dynamical picture persists for stochastic or mini-batch variants of Bregman ADMM.

Remark A.1

.

In the single-block ALM (

5.2
) one can take a fixed

\rho\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
vanishes on

independently of

With two blocks the cross-coupling

Before symmetrization, the reduced matrix

This scaled direction lets the KKT constraint

and the quadratic form tends to

\displaystyle\phi(\mu)

(from the definition of

), and in the last line we rewrote

We first use

to show that

for sufficiently small

for sufficiently large

gives the desired zero crossing.

•

First, since

by KKT condition (

Second, we show that

for some sufficiently large

we have

(from relative smoothness, strong convexity of

, and

) and

for some sufficiently large

If

, and

Lemma B.2

(Extension to general
J
)
.

Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

4.2
, for any
J\geq 2
:

1.

\det(Dg(\mathbf{z}))\neq 0
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\Omega
;

2.

every strict-saddle KKT point of (
11
) in
\Omega
is an unstable fixed point of
g
.

Proof.

The proof uses exactly the same two ingredients as the
J=3
case.

Nonsingularity.

One full iteration decomposes into
2J+1
elementary maps: one
\mathbf{x}_{j}
-update and one
\mathbf{y}_{j}
-update for each agent, followed by the dual update. Each Jacobian is block triangular with diagonal blocks given by the same implicit-function-theorem matrices as in the displayed
J=3
computation, namely
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{j}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{j}
, and identity blocks for untouched variables and dual updates. Since every
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
block is positive definite by relative bi-smoothness and the stepsize condition, every elementary Jacobian is nonsingular; hence
\det(Dg)\neq 0
for arbitrary
J
by the chain rule.

Instability.

The coupling matrix
\mathbf{T}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2Jn\times(J-1)n}
has column
j-1
(
j=2,\ldots,J
) with
-s_{1}\mathbf{I}
in the
\mathbf{x}_{1}
-block and
+s_{1}\mathbf{I}
in the
\mathbf{x}_{j}
-block (all other blocks zero). With the test direction
\mathbf{d}=(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{y}}^{J},\mathbf{0},\ldots,\mathbf{0})
(all
\mathbf{x}
-components equal to
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}}
), each column of
\mathbf{T}(\mu)
contributes
(-s_{1}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}}+(s_{1}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}
, hence
\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{p}}=\mathbf{0}
.

The general-
J
structure of the Schur-reduced matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu)=\mathbf{S}(\mu)+\tfrac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}(\mu)\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2Jn\times 2Jn}
is as follows. Since
\mathbf{T}(\mu)
has nonzero entries only in the
\mathbf{x}
-rows, the additive correction
\tfrac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}(\mu)\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}
affects only
\mathbf{x}
–
\mathbf{x}
blocks, with all
\mathbf{y}
-rows and
\mathbf{y}
-columns equal to zero. Restricting to the
\mathbf{x}
-coordinates
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{J})
, we obtain

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}(\mu)\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}\end{bmatrix}_{\mathbf{x}\text{-blocks},\mathbf{x}\text{-blocks}}=\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{\mu}\begin{bmatrix}(J{-}1)\mathbf{I}&-\mathbf{I}&-\mathbf{I}&\cdots&-\mathbf{I}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{\mu}\,\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{star}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n},

where
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{star}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times J}
is the graph Laplacian of the star with hub node
1
. The constant vector
(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}})
lies in
\ker(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{star}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n})
, so
\tfrac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}(\mu)\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}
contributes nothing to
\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu)\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{p}}
when all
\mathbf{x}
-components of
\mathbf{d}
are equal. Moreover, since
s_{1}^{2}=(1+\mu)\rho
, the entries of
\mathbf{T}(\mu)
are
O(\sqrt{\mu})
, so
\mathbf{T}(\mu)\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}=O(\mu)
and
\tfrac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}(\mu)\mathbf{T}(\mu)^{\top}=O(1)
. The cross-agent entries of
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu)
are therefore at most
O(\sqrt{\mu})
(from
\mathbf{S}(\mu)
) plus
O(1)
(from
\tfrac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{\top}
), both of which are
o(\mu)
. Therefore, dividing by
\mu
and sending
\mu\to\infty
yields the block-diagonal limit

\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\mu)}{\mu}\;\xrightarrow{\mu\to\infty}\;\mathrm{diag}\bigl({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{J},{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{J}\bigr)\succ 0,

where each
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
block is positive definite by relative bi-smoothness and the stepsize condition, exactly as in the
J=3
case. For the
\rho
-cancellation at
\mu=0
, write
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x},\mathrm{stack}}=(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}})
. The consensus penalty contribution is
\rho\,\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x},\mathrm{stack}}^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{star}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n})\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x},\mathrm{stack}}=\rho\sum_{j=2}^{J}\|\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}=0
, because
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{x},\mathrm{stack}}
is in the kernel of the star-graph Laplacian. Combined with the strict-saddle negativity of the corresponding quadratic form at
\mu=0
, this gives the same sign change and hence the same intermediate-value argument for arbitrary
J\geq 2
.
∎

This completes the proof that strict-saddle KKT points in
\Omega
are unstable fixed points of the fixed-point map of
Algorithm

3
.
By
Theorem

5.1
, the set of initial points in
\Omega
from which the iteration converges to such a strict-saddle KKT point has Lebesgue measure zero; since random initialization is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, the probability that the iterates converge to such a strict-saddle KKT point is zero.
∎
