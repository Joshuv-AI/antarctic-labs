Dynamical low-rank methods for the Wigner equation I: separable difference potential Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Sihong Shao, Yuehan Shao

Abstract
Recent advances in dynamical low-rank approximation (DLRA) have demonstrated its effectiveness in high-dimensional simulations. However, existing DLRA algorithms still face significant challenges when handling systems that involve complex collision terms, including the pseudo-differential operator ( Ψ ​ DO {\rm\Psi}\textup{DO} ) in the Wigner equation, a representative operator characterized by nonlocality. It is deserving to carry out a series of works to develop the DLRA algorithms for solving the Wigner equation. As the first step in this series of works, we propose an efficient DLRA algorithm for the Wigner equation, using a separable decomposition of the difference potential. We combine this separable assumption with two often-used truncations of Ψ ​ DO {\rm\Psi}\textup{DO} , namely 𝒦 \mathcal{K} -truncation and 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} -truncation, to obtain a kind of separated representation of Ψ ​ DO {\rm\Psi}\textup{DO} . Complexity analysis and several challenging experiments, including harmonic oscillators, Gaussian barrier scattering, electron-electron scattering, and a Helium-like system, all of which satisfy the separable assumption, confirm that the proposed DLRA algorithm has significant advantages, achieving a reduction in computational effort by one to two orders of magnitude in both runtime and memory requirements compared to the full-grid approach. It is worth noting that, even in the absence of a predetermined low-rank structure for the solution, DLRA can still serve as a numerical scheme that balances efficiency and accuracy.

dynamical low-rank approximation, the Wigner equation, the pseudo-differential operator, Fourier method

Although the Wigner function may exhibit negative values, this quasi-distribution function remains an important way to understand and forecast quantum phenomena in many fields, such as nanoelectronics

1.1
The Wigner equation

The Wigner equation with a time-independent potential considered in this paper is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)=-\frac{\hbar\bm{k}}{m}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)+\Theta_{V}[f](\bm{x},\bm{k},t):=\mathcal{L}f,

(1)

where
\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\bm{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the spatial variable,
\bm{k}\in\Omega_{\bm{k}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the Fourier conjugated wave vector,
d
is the dimension,
m
is the mass,
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant and the nonlocal pseudodifferential operator (
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
) is defined as

\displaystyle\Theta_{V}[f](\bm{x},\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{k}^{\prime},t\right)V_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\textup{d}\bm{k}^{\prime},

(2)

\displaystyle V_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\hbar(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{y}}D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\textup{d}\bm{y},

(3)

\displaystyle D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=V\left(\bm{x}+\frac{\bm{y}}{2}\right)-V\left(\bm{x}-\frac{\bm{y}}{2}\right).

(4)

Here
D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is central difference of potential function
V(\bm{x})
, the Wigner kernel
V_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{k})
is Fourier transformation of
D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
\Theta_{V}[f](\bm{x},\bm{k},t)
is the convolution of
f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)
and
V_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{k})
.
We can obtain another form of
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
by changing the order of integration

\displaystyle\Theta_{V}[f](\bm{x},\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\hbar}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{y}}D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\rho(\bm{x},\bm{y},t)\textup{d}\bm{y},

(5)

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x},\bm{y},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\left(\bm{x},\bm{k}^{\prime},t\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{y}}\textup{d}\bm{k}^{\prime}.

(6)

The
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
is the most complex and important component in the Wigner equation, representing the fundamental distinction between the classical Vlasov equation and the Wigner equation.
This equation evolves in a
2d
-dimensional phase space, thus confronting the severe challenge of the curse of dimensionality (CoD) along with the nonlocality.

Despite the long history and broad application of the Wigner equation, its numerical solution continues to present significant challenges nowadays.
Over the past three decades, significant progress has been made in developing Wigner equation solvers, which can be roughly classified into particle-based stochastic and grid-based deterministic approaches.
The particle-based stochastic approaches such as Monte Carlo method
[
32
,
39
]
and stochastic particle method
[
42
]
typically achieve only
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
convergence due to their inherent statistical nature, where
N
is the number of samples.
In contrast, the grid-based deterministic approaches exhibit superior convergence rates because of their concise mathematical principle, including the finite difference method
[
19
]
, operator splitting method
[
37
,
2
]
, spectral element method (SEM)
[
40
,
45
,
7
,
46
]
,
moment method
[
34
]
, weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO) method
[
13
]
, etc.
While CoD confined deterministic methods to low-dimensional problems, rapid advances in high-performance computing, alongside techniques such as tensor approximations that mitigate CoD, have enabled deterministic methods to tackle 5D and 6D problems.
The dynamical low-rank approximation (DLRA) method we consider next serves as one such approach for mitigating CoD.

1.2
DLRA: the projector-splitting integrator

Emerging from the Dirac-Frenkel time-dependent variational principle
[
12
]
, the DLRA method
[
30
]
has evolved into a powerful class of numerical integrators.
This method has been widely used in kinetic theory, including the Vlasov equation
[
18
,
17
]
, Boltzmann equation
[
16
,
24
]
and radiation transfer equation
[
36
,
15
]
.
The main idea is to constrain the unknown function
f
to a low-rank manifold, then deriving evolution equations of basis functions through tangent space projection.
This approach reduces the original
2d
-dimensional problem to several
d
-dimensional subproblems, effectively alleviating CoD in traditional grid-based methods.

The DLRA method begins by assuming that the Wigner function
f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)
admits a separable low-rank representation of the following form

f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)\approx\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}X_{i}(\bm{x},t)K_{j}(\bm{k},t)S_{ij}(t),

(7)

where
r
is the rank, the coefficient matrix
S=(S_{ij})
is invertible, the basis function
X_{i}(\bm{x},t)\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\bm{x}})
and
K_{j}(\bm{k},t)\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\bm{k}})
are orthonormal

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{i},X_{j}\right\rangle_{\bm{x}}=\delta_{ij},\quad\left\langle K_{i},K_{j}\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}=\delta_{ij},

(8)

with
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\bm{x}}
and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}
being the standard
L^{2}
inner products on
L^{2}(\Omega_{\bm{x}})
and
L^{2}(\Omega_{\bm{k}})
, respectively, and
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta.
Therefore, for fixed time
t
, the Wigner function
f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)
lies in the fixed rank-
r
manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r}=\biggl\{g\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mid g(\bm{x},\bm{k})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}X_{i}(\bm{x})S_{ij}K_{j}(\bm{k})\text{ with invertible }S=\left(S_{ij}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle X_{i}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega_{\bm{x}}\right),K_{j}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega_{\bm{k}}\right)\text{ with }\left\langle X_{i},X_{j}\right\rangle_{\bm{x}}=\delta_{ij},\left\langle K_{i},K_{j}\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}=\delta_{ij}\biggr\}.

Then project
\mathcal{L}f
in
eq.

1
onto the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}_{r}
, yielding the reduced system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)=\mathcal{P}_{f}(\mathcal{L}f),

(10)

where the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{f}
acting on a generic function
g
can be written as
[
30
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{f}g=\mathcal{P}_{\bar{K}}g-\mathcal{P}_{\bar{K}}\mathcal{P}_{\bar{X}}g+\mathcal{P}_{\bar{X}}g.

(11)

Here,
\bar{K}
and
\bar{X}
denote the vector spaces spanned by the basis functions
K_{j}
and
X_{i}
,

\displaystyle\bar{K}=\operatorname{span}\{K_{j}\mid j=1,2,\cdots,r\},\qquad\bar{X}=\operatorname{span}\{X_{i}\mid i=1,2,\cdots,r\},

(12)

and
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{K}},\mathcal{P}_{\bar{X}}
are the orthogonal projectors onto
\bar{X}
and
\bar{K}
, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\bar{K}}g=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\langle K_{j},g\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}K_{j},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{\bar{X}}g=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}\left\langle X_{i},g\right\rangle_{\bm{x}}.

(13)

To avoid the possible ill-conditioning of the coefficient matrix
S

[
30
,
29
]
, the projector-splitting integrator
[
35
]
substitutes (
11
) into the reduced Wigner system
eq.

10
and decomposes
eq.

10
into three subproblems:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\bar{K}}\left(\mathcal{L}f\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{P}_{\bar{K}}\mathcal{P}_{\bar{X}}\left(\mathcal{L}f\right),

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\bar{X}}\left(\mathcal{L}f\right).

(16)

The decomposition of the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{f}
forms the basis of projector-splitting integrator.
Exploiting orthogonality conditions, we can further simplify each subproblems and proceed in the following three substeps, respectively:

Z
-step

We can represent the low-rank assumption for
f
in
eq.

7
as
f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}Z_{j}(\bm{x},t)K_{j}(\bm{k},t)
with
Z_{j}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}(\bm{x},t)S_{ij}(t),
then rewrite Eq.
eq.

14
as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z_{j}K_{j}+Z_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}K_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\langle K_{j},\mathcal{L}f\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}K_{j}.

Making an important observation that
\{K_{j}(\bm{k},t)\}_{1\leq j\leq r}
are independent of time
t
(see
[
35
, Lemma 3.1]
) in this substep, and using orthogonality conditions
\left\langle K_{i},K_{j}\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}=\delta_{ij}
, we derive the following differential equation for
Z_{j}(\bm{x},t)
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z_{j}(\bm{x},t)=\left\langle K_{j},\mathcal{L}f\right\rangle_{\bm{k}},\qquad j=1,...,r.

(17)

S
-step

We can argue similarly to obtain that Eq.
eq.

15
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}S_{ij}(t)=\left\langle X_{i}K_{j},\mathcal{L}f\right\rangle_{\bm{x},\bm{k}},\qquad i,j=1,...,r.

(18)

During this substep, both
\{K_{j}(\bm{k},t)\}_{1\leq j\leq r}
and
\{X_{i}(\bm{x},t)\}_{1\leq i\leq r}
remain unchanged.

L
-step

Similarly, we can represent
eq.

7
as
f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}(\bm{x},t)L_{i}(\bm{k},t)
with
L_{i}(\bm{k},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}S_{ij}(t)K_{j}(\bm{k},t).
By similar arguments, Eq.
eq.

16
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}L_{i}(\bm{k},t)=\left\langle X_{i},\mathcal{L}f\right\rangle_{\bm{x}},\qquad i=1,...,r.

(19)

During this substep,
\{X_{i}(\bm{x},t)\}_{1\leq i\leq r}
remains unchanged.

This projector-splitting integrator has been shown to be robust to small singular values of
S
and own first order rate
[
29
]
.
For more details, we refer the reader to
[
18
]
which provides similar approach for the Vlasov-Poisson equation.

1.3
The main challenge

While
eq.

14
-
eq.

16
appear to offer a framework for applying DLRA, the efficiency relies on the separability of the right-hand side
\mathcal{L}f
.
The vast majority of problems solved using DLRA possess separability in their right-hand side terms, such as the Vlasov equation
[
18
,
17
]
and the radiative transfer equation
[
36
,
15
]
.
Some studies utilize the steady-state properties to simplify the problem, for instance, the Maxwellian distribution is employed to reduce complexity when solving the Boltzmann-BGK equation
[
16
]
.
However, this approach is not well suited for solving non-steady-state problems.

The so-called separability here refers to the fact that, under the low-rank assumption
eq.

7
satisfied by
f
,
\mathcal{L}f
can also be naturally expressed in a form that separates the variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{k}
.
Separability allows the projection to be computed directly via the low-rank components
X_{i},S_{ij},K_{j}
, without the full tensor
f
.
For example, the advection term
\frac{\hbar\bm{k}}{m}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f
possesses separability, since

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\bm{k}}{m}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}=1}^{r}\nabla_{\bm{x}}X_{i^{\prime}}(\bm{x},t)\cdot(\bm{k}K_{j^{\prime}}(\bm{k},t))S_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(t),

and then the projection onto the
\{K_{j}(\bm{k},t)\}_{1\leq j\leq r}
becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle K_{j},\frac{\hbar\bm{k}}{m}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x},\bm{k},t)\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}=1}^{r}\nabla_{\bm{x}}X_{i^{\prime}}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\left\langle K_{j},\bm{k}K_{j^{\prime}}(\bm{k},t)\right\rangle_{\bm{k}}S_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(t),

where
\bm{x}
and
\bm{k}
dependent terms can be computed separately.
In contrast, the intrinsic nonlocality of
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
introduces strong coupling between the variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{k}
, preventing it from possessing the separability.
Consequently, evaluating its projection would typically necessitate reconstructing the full tensor, which undermines the efficiency aims of the low‑rank approach.
This strong coupling within
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
represents the main difficulty to applying DLRA to the Wigner equation.

2
Separable assumption

The intrinsic nonlocality of
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
in (
2
) places the first obstacle for any numerical solver and some kinds of truncation have been introduced in the literature.
If the potential
V(\bm{x})
admits a Taylor expansion in
\bm{x}
-space, then the difference potential
D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
can be written as

\displaystyle D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\frac{2}{(2l+1)!}\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{(2l+1)}V\left(\bm{x}\right)\left(\frac{\bm{y}}{2}\right)^{2l+1}.

(20)

Substituting
eq.

20
into
eq.

3
and using the basic properties of Fourier transform yields direatly the so-called Moyal expansion of
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}

\displaystyle\Theta_{V}^{M}[f](\bm{x},\bm{k},t)=\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{(2l+1)!}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^{2l}\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2l+1}V\cdot\nabla_{\bm{p}}^{2l+1}f,

(21)

where
\bm{p}=\hbar\bm{k}
is the momentum.
In particular, the first term in the summation on the right-hand side is
\nabla_{\bm{x}}V\cdot\nabla_{\bm{p}}f
, indicating that the Wigner equation reduces to the classical Vlasov equation when
\hbar
tends to zero. In the early stage, one has to truncate the infinite series summation
eq.

21
to a finite number of terms
[
25
,
26
]
. Obviously, the resulting scheme possesses certain separability and the primary reason is that in Eq.
eq.

20
for the difference potential
D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, the variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are separated.
Based on this observation, we introduce the separable assumption for the difference potential
D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle D_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\approx\sum_{s=1}^{R}D_{X,s}(\bm{x})D_{Y,s}(\bm{y}),

(22)

where
R
denotes the separation rank, and
D_{X,s},D_{Y,s}
are functions that depend only on
\bm{x},\bm{y}
, respectively.

Nowadays, the following two kinds of truncation, named after the truncation of the outermost infinite integral in
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
, are often used
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
45

:

•

\mathcal{Y}
-truncation: starting from
eq.

5
and

6

•

\mathcal{K}
-truncation: starting from

After applying the separable difference potential assumption

\displaystyle\Theta_{V}[f](\bm{x},\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{R}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\overline{X}_{i,s}(\bm{x},t)S_{ij}(t)\overline{K}_{j,s}(\bm{k},t)\right],

(23)

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{i,s}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=D_{X,s}(\bm{x})X_{i}(\bm{x},t),

(24)

and the resulting

.

Dynamical low-rank approximation for the Wigner equation

In this section, we substitute

23
into three substep equations

-

19
to derive more precise forms of each part, and design their numerical scheme.

Z
-step

Plugging
eq.

23
into
eq.

17
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z_{j}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{s=1}^{R}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{r}D_{X,s}(\bm{x})c_{j,j^{\prime},s}Z_{j^{\prime}}(\bm{x},t)-\frac{\hbar}{m}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{r}\bm{a}_{j,j^{\prime}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}Z_{j^{\prime}}(\bm{x},t).

\displaystyle B_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\quad\quad(B_{u})_{i,j}=(\bm{b}_{i,j})_{u}=\left\langle X_{i},\nabla_{x_{u}}X_{j}\right\rangle_{\bm{x}},\quad(u=1,\dots,d),

(29)

\displaystyle D_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\quad\quad(D_{s})_{i,j}=d_{i,j,s}=\left\langle X_{i},\overline{X}_{j,s}\right\rangle_{\bm{x}},\quad(s=1,\dots,R).

In the

Although both first-order Lie splitting and second-order Strang splitting have been observed to achieve their respective convergence orders in practice, existing theoretical analyses can only guarantee first-order accuracy

3.1
Discretization

For the purpose of numerical solution, we first truncate the computational domain to a bounded rectangular region
\Omega=\Omega_{\bm{x}}\times\Omega_{\bm{k}},
then we consider the uniform grid mesh in
\bm{x}
-space and
\bm{k}
-space,
\bm{x}
-space using
N_{x}
grid points in every coordinate direction with the spacing
\Delta x_{u}
and
\bm{k}
-space using
N_{k}
grid points in every coordinate direction with the spacing
\Delta k_{u}.

For the dual
\bm{y}
-space, we choose
N_{y}
grid points in every coordinate direction with the spacing
\Delta y_{u}
.

Once the grid discretization is determined, for a fixed time
t
, we can obtain
X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}^{d}\times r}
to be the matrix composed of row vectors collected from
X_{i}
on the chosen spatial grid.
It’s obvious that the resulting matrix entries depend on both the discretization performed and the ordering of the grid points.
Similarly,
K(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{k}^{d}\times r}
can be defined in the same way.
Then, the low-rank approximation of the Wigner function can be reconstructed at any time from the low-rank factors via the computation

\displaystyle F(t)=X(t)S(t)K^{T}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}^{d}\times N_{k}^{d}}.

(32)

However, due to its prohibitive cost, this reconstruction is unnecessary for our algorithm.
We only employ it in combination with Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
[
14
]
to obtain the initial values of the low-rank factors and to reconstruct the solution at selected critical time points.
Similarly, the separable assumption
eq.

22
for the difference potential
D_{V}
can be computed via SVD, and this computation is required only once during the initialization stage.
In each time step, since a uniform grid is employed,
\overline{K}_{j,s}
can be efficiently computed via the standard Fast Fourier transform (FFT).
It is also worth noting that the Poisson summation formula is often used in conjunction with truncation
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
45
]
, however, it is not required in the implementation of our DLRA algorithm.

3.2
Numerical scheme

In this subsection, we provide a detailed description of the procedure for solving
eq.

25
,
eq.

28
, and
eq.

31
.

We first consider the
S
-step
eq.

28
. It is an ordinary differential equation and there is no source of stiffness in it.
Hence, we perform its time integration by classical explicit fourth order Runge-Kutta scheme RK4.

Next, let us consider the
Z
-step
eq.

25
and use the vector notation
Z=(Z_{1},...,Z_{r})
, we can rewrite the evolution equation for the
Z
-step
eq.

25
in matrix formulation as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{R}D_{X,s}(\bm{x})Z(\bm{x},t)C_{s}^{T}-\frac{\hbar}{m}\sum_{u=1}^{d}\nabla_{x_{u}}Z(\bm{x},t)A_{u}^{T}.

(33)

For simplicity, we only consider the two-dimensional case here.
By further splitting the
Z
-step, we obtain the following two subflows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar}{m}\nabla_{x_{1}}Z(\bm{x},t)A_{1}^{T},

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar}{m}\nabla_{x_{2}}Z(\bm{x},t)A_{2}^{T}+\sum_{s=1}^{R}D_{X,s}(\bm{x})Z(\bm{x},t)C_{s}^{T}.

(35)

As we mentioned before, the coefficients matrix
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are symmetric, thus equations
eq.

34
and
eq.

35
are hyperbolic.
It is evident that
eq.

34
can be solved in time by the method of characteristics, while
eq.

35
can be efficiently solved via an exponential integrator (see
[
23
]
for more details).

Finally, we consider the
L
-step
eq.

31
.
Extending the vector notation similar to
Z
to
X,K,Y,L,S,
and
\overline{K}_{s}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}L(\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{R}\overline{K}_{s}S^{T}D_{s}^{T}-\frac{\hbar}{m}\sum_{u=1}^{d}k_{u}L(\bm{k},t)B_{u}^{T}.

(36)

Unlike the
Z
-step, the hyperbolicity is maintained by the coefficient matrix
D_{s}
, hence it is necessary to examine the relationship between
L
and
\overline{K}_{s}S^{T}
and to exploit this connection in solving the
L
-step equation.

For example, for
\mathcal{Y}
-truncation, using the relationship provided in
table

1
, apply the Fourier transform in
\bm{k}
-direction to
eq.

36
and define

\displaystyle L(\bm{k},t)=K(\bm{k},t)S^{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{y}}U(\bm{y},t)\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

(37)

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{d}k_{s}L(\bm{k},t)B_{s}^{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{y}}V(\bm{y},t)\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

(38)

then the
L
-step
eq.

36
turns to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}U(\bm{y},t)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\hbar}\sum_{s=1}^{R}D_{Y,s}(\bm{y})U(\bm{y},t)D_{s}^{T}-V(\bm{y},t).

(39)

Therefore, we proceed to solve equation
eq.

39
involving the variable
U(\bm{y},t)
, where
V(\bm{y},t)
can be regarded as a function of
U(\bm{y},t)
.
When
R
is approximately the same size as the dimension
d
, similar to the situation in the
Z
-step, we can likewise exploit the symmetry of the coefficient matrix
D_{s}
and combine a splitting method with an exponential integrator to solve the corresponding equations.
Conversely, when
R
is relatively large, the Runge-Kutta method is more suitable for
eq.

39
. Similarly, for
\mathcal{K}
-truncation, utilizing the convolution property of Fourier transform and the notation in
eq.

37
and
eq.

38
, we can obtain the evolution equation for
U(\bm{y},t)
as same as
eq.

39
.

5
Conclusion and discussion

This work presents the first application of DLRA to the Wigner equation.
Our key innovation lies in introducing separable assumption
eq.

22
and combine it with two established approaches for
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
to transform
{\rm\Psi}\textup{DO}
into a separated form
eq.

23
.
Comprehensive numerical tests on challenging non-stationary systems demonstrate both the accuracy and efficiency of our approach.
Even in the absence of a predetermined low-rank structure for the solution, DLRA can still serve as a numerical scheme that balances efficiency and accuracy.
These findings open new possibilities for simulating high-dimensional quantum systems.

While the current framework demonstrates promising results, several advanced strategies could further enhance the algorithm’s performance.
First, the second-order robust BUG integrator
[
5
]
provides stable second‑order accuracy and enables feasible adaptive rank control.
Second, changing the way of low-rank such as dynamical tensor approximation
[
31
]
and hierarchical low-rank approximation
[
1
]
may help reduce the required rank.
Third, emerging approximate interpolation techniques
[
6
,
10
,
11
]
could be adopted to accelerate the computation.
Finally, beyond the extension in computational efficiency, we will consider more complex potentials in subsequent work, including singular potentials, discontinuous potentials, and even pure Coulomb potentials.
These directions will be explored in our future research.
