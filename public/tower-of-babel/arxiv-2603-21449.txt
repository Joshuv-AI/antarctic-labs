Rationality and computability of the covering radius for sofic shifts
Tom Meyerovitch, Aidan Young

Abstract
. The covering radius of a shift space is a quantity of interest for information-theoretic applications of data transmission over noisy channels. We prove that the covering radius of a primitive sofic shift is a rational number, and describe an algorithm to compute the covering radius from a labeled graph presentation.



1.
Introduction

The covering radius of a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
is the smallest number
R>0
such that for any
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, there exists
v\in\mathcal{C}
that differs from
w
in at most
R
coordinates.
We refer the reader to the book
[
1
]
for an extensive account of covering radius of codes and a wide range of applications related to this notion.

The covering radius of a shift space
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the natural analog for infinite sequences. We recall the formal definition in
Section

2.4
. Covering radii of shift spaces have been studied in
[
3
]
as a key parameter framework for implementing error-correction in constrained systems.

In
[
3
]
, the covering radius has been explicitly computed for some fundamental examples of sofic shifts, including run length-limited shifts with certain parameters. Sofic shifts, and run length-limited shifts in particular, occur in the context of constrained coding. Sofic shifts (see
Section

2.3
for a definition) constitute an important family that come up in real-word data transmission and storage applications. The computations of the covering radius in
[
3
]
were preformed on a somewhat ad-hoc basis. In all the examples, the covering radius turned out to be a rational number. The following questions naturally occurred: Is the covering radius of a sofic shift always a rational number? Is there a general algorithm to compute the covering radius of a sofic shift?

Here we answer these questions in the fundamental case that the underlying sofic shift is primitive.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(G,L)
be a primitive labeled graph.
Then the covering radius
R(X_{\mathcal{G}})
is a rational number.

Theorem B
.

There exists an algorithm that receives as input a primitive labeled graph
\mathcal{G}
and computes the covering radius
R(X_{\mathcal{G}})
in finite time.

In the above,
X_{\mathcal{G}}
is the edge shift corresponding to the labeled graph
\mathcal{G}
. We will recall the definition of an edge shift in the following section, specifically in (
2
).

We obtain these results as part of a more general framework that can be expressed as a certain two-player game, as in
[
6
]
.
The proof methods and results presented in this paper are rather elementary and combinatorial in nature.

The paper is organized as follows: In
Section

2
, we quickly recall basic concepts and terminology and notation about words, labeled graphs and shift spaces. We also recall formally the definition of a shift space. In
Section

3
, we present a certain binary operation on integer-valued bi-variate functions which we call “tropical convolution” that will be used in the proof of our main results.
In
Section

4
, we present a variation on the mean payoff games introduced by A. Ehrenfeucht and J. Mycielski in
[
2
]
, and deduce
A
and
B
from
C
, a result about these modified mean payoff games.

In
Section

5
, we consider shift spaces
of “optimal strategies” for the mean payoff games introduced in
Section

4
. We prove that these shift spaces are sofic shifts.
Finally, in
Section

6
, we conclude with further remarks and open problems.

2.1.
Words and codes

Let
\Sigma

\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.
We refer to
\Sigma
as the “alphabet”, and refer to
n
-tuples in
\Sigma^{n}
as “words” of length
n
over the alphabet
\Sigma
.

A

is a set of words
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
,
and the

2.2.
Labeled graphs

In this paper by a
graph
, we always mean a
finite, directed
graph, namely a pair
G=(\mathcal{V}_{G},\mathcal{E}_{G})
, where
\mathcal{V}_{G}
is the finite set of vertices and
\mathcal{E}_{G}
is the finite set of (directed) edges. Every edge
e\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
has a
source

s(e)\in\mathcal{V}_{G}
and a
target

t(e)\in\mathcal{V}_{G}
.
We allow parallel edges, i.e. it is possible that two vertices have more than one edge with a common source and common target. We also allow self-loops, i.e. edges such that
s(e)=t(e)
.
A
walk
of length
n
in the graph
G
is a tuple
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{G}^{n}
such that
t(e_{k})=s(e_{k+1})
for every
1\leq k<n
. Given
v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{G}
we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v\to w)
the the set of length
n
walks in
G
that start at the vertex
v
and terminate at the vertex
w
:

(1)

\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v\to w):=\left\{(e_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{E}_{G}^{n}\penalty\ :\penalty\ s(e_{1})=v,t(e_{n})=w,\penalty\ t(e_{i-1})=s(e_{i})\penalty\ \forall 1<i\leq n\right\}.

We also denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v)

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{G}}\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v\to w),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}(G)

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{G}}\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v),

and

\mathcal{W}_{\infty}(G):=\left\{(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{E}_{G}\penalty\ :\penalty\ t(e_{n})=s(e_{n+1})\penalty\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Given
q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G)
and
1\leq i\leq n
we denote by
q_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
the
i
th edge in
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
.

Given
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
;
u,v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{G}
;
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,u\to v)
; and
q\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(G,v\to w)
, the
concatenation
which we denote by
p*q\in\mathcal{W}_{n+m}(G,u\to w)
. is given by

(p*q)_{i}=\begin{cases}p_{i}&\textrm{if $1\leq i\leq n$,}\\
q_{i-n}&\textrm{if $n+1\leq i\leq n+m$.}\end{cases}

A directed graph
G=(\mathcal{V}_{G},\mathcal{E}_{G})
is called
irreducible
if any two vertices are connected by a walk.

A
cycle

in
G
is a walk
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
such that
s(e_{1})=t(e_{n})
. Note that we do
not
assume that a cycle satisfies any injectivity condition. An irreducible
graph is called
primitive
if the greatest common divisor of all cycle lengths is 1.

A labeled graph is a pair
\mathcal{G}=(G,L)
, where
G
is a graph and
L:\mathcal{E}_{G}\to\Sigma
is a
labeling function
on the edges of
G
, where
\Sigma
is a finite set.
Given
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G)
we denote

L(p):=(L(p_{1}),\ldots,L(p_{n}))\in\Sigma^{n}.

Given a labeled graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,L)
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathcal{G})\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
the words of length
n
that occur as a the labels of some walk of length
n
on the underlying graph
G
:

\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathcal{G}):=\left\{(L(p)\in\Sigma^{n}\penalty\ :\penalty\ p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G)\right\}.

We say that
\mathcal{G}=(G,L)
is irreducible (primitive) when the underlying graph
G
is irreducible (primitive).

2.3.
Symbolic dynamics: Shift spaces and sofic shifts

Let
\Sigma
be a finite set of “symbols”, for instance
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.
We refer to
\Sigma
as the “alphabet”, and refer to tuples in
\Sigma^{n}
as
words of length
n
over the alphabet
\Sigma
. We denote the set of words over
\Sigma
by
\Sigma^{*}:=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\Sigma^{n}
.

Considering
\Sigma
as a finite discrete topological space, the space
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
of
\Sigma
-valued functions on
\mathbb{Z}
can be equipped with the product topology, which makes it a compact metrizable topological space.
It is convenient to think of the elements of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
as bi-infinite sequences with values in
\Sigma
.
Given
x\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
m\leq n
we denote

x_{[m,n]}:=(x_{m},\ldots,x_{n})\in\Sigma^{m-n+1}.

The
shift map
is the function
\sigma:\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

\sigma((x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}})=(x_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Then
\sigma:\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a homeomorphism.
A
shift space
or
subshift
is a closed,
\sigma
-invariant subset
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Given a shift space
S
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote
by
\mathcal{C}_{n}(S)
the set of admissible words of length
n
for
S
. That is:

\mathcal{C}_{n}(S):=\left\{x_{[1,n]}\penalty\ :\penalty\ x\in S\right\}\subseteq\Sigma^{n}.

Also denote

\mathcal{C}(S):=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{C}_{n}(S).

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a shift space
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
. We say that a set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}

generates

S
if

S=\left\{x\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ x_{[m,n]}\not\in\mathcal{F}\penalty\ \forall m\leq n\right\}.

It is well-known (c.f.
[
5
, Theorem 6.1.21]
) that a set
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a shift space if and only if there exist
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
that generates
S
, although
\mathcal{F}
is not in general unique. A shift space
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
that can be generated by a
finite
set
\mathcal{F}\subset\Sigma^{*}

is called a
shift of finite type (SFT)
.

A shift space
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is called
sofic
if there exists a labeled graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,L)
such that
S
is set bi-infinite sequences that are obtained by reading the
L
-labels of a bi-infinite walk in
G
. We call such a labeled graph
\mathcal{G}
a
labeled graph presentation
of
S
. Equivalently, a sofic shift is a shift space of the form
X_{\mathcal{G}}
where

(2)

X_{\mathcal{G}}=\left\{x\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ x_{[m,n]}\in\mathcal{C}_{n-m+1}(\mathcal{G})\penalty\ \forall m\leq n\right\}.

It is well known that any shift of finite type is a sofic shift. For details and discussion of sofic shifts and their importance, see
[
5
, Chapter 3]
.
In particular, sofic shifts are a natural model for information storage and transmission.

A sofic shift
X
is irreducible (primitive) if there exists an irreducible (primitive) labeled graph
\mathcal{G}
such that
X=X_{\mathcal{G}}
. We note that there are equivalent “intrinsic” definitions, not involving a representation by a labeled graph. See for instance
[
5
, Chapter 6]
.

2.4.
The covering radius of shift space

The
hamming distance
between two words
u,v\in\Sigma^{n}
is the number of coordinates in which
u
differs from
v
:

d_{H}(u,v):=|\{1\leq i\leq n\penalty\ :\penalty\ u_{i}\neq v_{i}\}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(u_{i},v_{i}),

where

\delta(a,b)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if }a=b\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The
covering radius
of a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
is given by

R(\mathcal{C}):=\max_{v\in\Sigma^{n}}\min_{w\in\mathcal{C}}d_{H}(v,w).

Let
X\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a shift space. Following
[
3
]
, the
(lower/upper) covering radius
of
X
are defined respectively by

\underline{R}(X)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}R(\mathcal{C}_{n}(X))\mbox{ and }\overline{R}(X)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}R(\mathcal{C}_{n}(X)).

Whenever
\overline{R}(X)=\underline{R}(X)
, their common value is called the
covering radius
of
X
. When the limit exists we will denote the covering radius by

R(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}R(\mathcal{C}_{n}(X)).

We define the
hamming pseudo-metric
on
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
by

\overline{d}_{H}(x,y)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}d_{H}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})),\penalty\ x,y\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}.

Given
y\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
X\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}

define

\overline{d}_{H}(y,X):=\inf_{x\in X}\overline{d}_{H}(y,x).

Similarly, define

\underline{d}_{H}(x,y):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}d_{H}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})),\penalty\ x,y\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}

and

\underline{d}_{H}(y,X)=\inf_{x\in X}\underline{d}_{H}(y,x).

It is easy to see from the definition of the (upper) covering radius
R(X)
that for any subshift
X\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
and any
y\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}

\overline{d}_{H}(y,X)\leq\overline{R}(X).

It was shown in
[
3
, Proposition 7]
that the limit
R(\mathcal{G}):=R(X_{\mathcal{G}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}R(\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathcal{G}))
exists for any labeled graph
\mathcal{G}
. In fact, the statement of
[
3
, Proposition 7]
is slightly more general.

Tropical convolution and maximal functions

In this section, we present and discuss a binary operation called tropical convolution on the space
\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{E\times E}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}:=\mathbb{Z}\cup\{+\infty\}
-valued functions on a product space
E\times E
, where
E
is an arbitrary
finite
set. This operation will be important for proving
C
, which in turn will help us prove
B
and
A
.

Given two functions
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{E\times E}
define the
tropical convolution

f*g
by

(f*g)(u,w)=\min_{v\in E}\left(f(u,v)+g(v,w)\right),\penalty\ u,w\in E.

A routine verification shows that the tropical convolution is an associative operation in the sense that
(f*g)*h=f*(g*h)
, so we will freely use expressions such as
f*g*h
. Given subsets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{E\times E}
, we use the notation

A*B:=\{f*g\penalty\ :\penalty\ f\in A,\penalty\ g\in B\}.

Tropical convolution can be seen, as the name would suggest, as a tropical analog of the more classical notion of convolution, where we replace the classical ring
(\mathbb{Z},+,\cdot)
with the tropical minplus semiring
(\mathbb{Z}_{\infty},\min(\cdot,\cdot),+)
. This idea of tropical convolution has been used previously in the construction of neural networks, as in
[
4
]
.

We define a partial order on the set
\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{E\times E}
by

f<g\penalty\ \Longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \forall v,w\in E\,\left(f(v,w)<g(v,w)\right).

Given
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{E\times E}
, we say that
f\in A
is
<
-maximal
in
A
if there does not exists
g\in A
such that
f<g
.
We denote the set of
<
-maximal elements in
A
by
A^{\dagger}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{E\times E}
be finite sets. If
f\in A
,
g\in B
are such that
f*g\in(A*B)^{\dagger}
and
f*g
is
\mathbb{Z}
-valued (i.e. does not attain the value
+\infty
), then
f\in A^{\dagger}
and
g\in B^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

We prove that
f
is
<
-maximal in
A
. If
f
takes the value
+\infty
, then
f
is trivially
<
-maximal, so consider the case where
f
is
\mathbb{Z}
-valued. If
f
is
\mathbb{Z}
-valued and not
<
-maximal in
A
, then there exists
f^{\prime}\in A
with
f^{\prime}>f
. Since
f*g
is assumed to be
\mathbb{Z}
-valued, we can write

\displaystyle(f*g)(u,w)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in E,\\
g(v,w)\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\left[f(u,v)+g(v,w)\right]

\displaystyle<\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in E,\\
g(v,w)\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\left[f^{\prime}(u,v)+g(v,w)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\min_{v\in E}\left[f^{\prime}(u,v)+g(v,w)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(f^{\prime}*g\right)(u,w)

for all
u,w\in E
.

Thus
f*g<f^{\prime}*g
, contradicting the
<
-maximality of
f*g
in
A*B
.

An analogous argument proves that
g
must be
<
-maximal in
B
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For any finite sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
we have

(A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=(A*B)^{\dagger}.

Proof.

From
Lemma

3.1
, it follows that

(A*B)^{\dagger}
consists exactly of the elements of
A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger}
that are
<
-maximal in
A*B
.
In particular, any element of
(A*B)^{\dagger}
is a
<
-maximal element of
A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger}
, so

(A*B)^{\dagger}\subseteq(A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger})^{\dagger}.

To complete the proof, we need to check that any element
f*g\in A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger}
that is
<
-maximal in
A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger}
is also
<
-maximal in
A*B
; we prove the contrapositive, that if
f*g
is if not maximal in
A*B
, then
f*g
is not maximal in
A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger}
.
By the assumption that
A
and
B
are finite, if
f*g\in A*B
is not
<
-maximal in
A*B
, then there exists
f^{\prime}*g^{\prime}\in A*B
which is
<
-maximal in
A*B
and
f*g<f^{\prime}*g^{\prime}
(otherwise we get an infinite
<
-chain in
A*B
, contradicting finiteness).
By the earlier argument, if
f^{\prime}*g^{\prime}\in A*B
is
<
-maximal in
A*B
, then
f^{\prime}\in A^{\dagger}
and
g^{\prime}\in B^{\dagger}
, meaning
f*g
is not maximal in
A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger}
. By contraposition, it follows that any element of
(A^{\dagger}*B^{\dagger})^{\dagger}
is also in
(A*B)^{\dagger}
.
∎

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f\in\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
, let
f+n\in\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
be the function given by pointwise incrementing

by
n
.
The direct unraveling of the definitions show that for any
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
we have:

(3)

(f+n)*g=f*(g+n)=(f*g)+n.

Given
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, set

A+n:=\{f+n\penalty\ :\penalty\ f\in A\}.

Then (
3
) translates to the fact that for any
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
the following holds:

(4)

(A+n)*B=A*(B+n)=(A*B)+n.

For
f\in\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
, we denote

\displaystyle\|f\|

\displaystyle:=\max_{u,v\in E}|f(u,v)|,

\displaystyle\max f

\displaystyle:=\max_{u,v\in E}f(u,v),

\displaystyle\min f

\displaystyle:=\min_{u,v\in E}f(u,v),

\displaystyle\Delta f

\displaystyle:=\max f-\min f.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}^{E\times E}
and
f:=f_{1}*f_{2}*f_{3}
.
Then

(5)

\Delta f\leq 2\|f_{1}\|+2\|f_{3}\|.

Proof.

For any finite set

\phi,\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\min_{x\in X}\phi(x)-\min_{x\in X}\psi(x)=\min_{x\in X}\max_{y\in Y}(\phi(x)-\psi(y))\leq\min_{x\in X}(\phi(x)-\psi(x)).

For any fixed

\phi(u,u^{\prime}):=f_{1}(v,u)+f_{2}(u,u^{\prime})+f_{3}(u^{\prime},w)

Thus,

Taking maximum over

and minimum over

we get (

∎

Given a finite set

, set

\Delta A:=\max_{f\in A}\max f-\min_{g\in A}\min g.

4.
A zero sum two player mean payoff game

In this section we derive the main results, namely
B
and
A
as a special case of a slightly more general result. Underlying this more general result are certain types of zero-sum two-player game. We introduced and studied these games in
[
6
]
and called them “non-alternating mean-payoff games.” As mentioned in
[
6
]
, these games can be considered as a variant or analog of the much more classical “alternating” mean-payoff games introduced in
[
2
]
. We will quickly recall the rules of these games.

Let
G=(\mathcal{V}_{G},\mathcal{E}_{G})
and
H=(\mathcal{V}_{H},\mathcal{E}_{H})
be two graphs, and let
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a function which we will refer to as “the payoff function.” For the rest of this section we will assume that
G
and
H
are primitive. The game takes place between two players whom we name Alice and Bob. The
n
-turn game goes as follows: First, Alice will choose a walk
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)
of length
n
, after which Bob will choose (with full knowledge of Alice’s play) a walk
q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)
of length
n
; the game will then conclude, and Bob will pay Alice
\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(q_{i},p_{i})
pesos.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

V_{n}(G,H,P):=\max_{p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)}\min_{q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(q_{i},p_{i})

and

(6)

V(G,H,P):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}V_{n}(G,H,P).

It is known that the limit in (
6
) exists, see for instance

[
7
, Proposition 2.8]
or
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
. The fact that the limit exists will will also follow from
Lemma

4.8
later in this article. We can think of this as the value of a kind of infinite version of the game.

Our main result in this section is the following:

Theorem C
.

Let
G
and
H
be primitive graphs, and let
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a payoff function. Then
V(G,H,P)
is a rational number.
Furthermore, there exists an algorithm that computes
V(G,H,P)
from
G,H,P
in finite time.

From
C
we can deduce
A
and
B
directly as follows:

Proof of
A
and
B
, assuming
C
.

Let

We now introduce some more notation in preparation for the proof of

and the payoff function

The following lemma explains the relevance of the tropical convolution operation to concatenation of walks:

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
;
u,v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{H}
;
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H,u\to v)
; and
q\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(H,v\to w)
we have

M(p*q)=M(p)*M(q).

Proof.

For every
u,w\in\mathcal{V}_{H}
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(8)

\mathcal{W}_{n+m}(H,u\to w)=\biguplus_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{H}}\left\{q*q^{\prime}\penalty\ :\penalty\ q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H,u\to v),\penalty\ q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(H,v\to w)\right\}.

Hence if
u,w\in\mathcal{V}_{G}
,
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)
and
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(H)
with
p
terminating at the initial vertex of
p^{\prime}
, then

M(p*p^{\prime},u\to w)=\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{G}}\inf_{q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,u\to v)}\inf_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(G,v\to w)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(q_{i},p_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}P(q^{\prime}_{i},p^{\prime}_{i})\right).

It follows that

M(p*p^{\prime},u\to w)=\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{G}}\left[M(p,u\to v)+M(p^{\prime},v\to w)\right].

We thus have

M(p*p^{\prime})=M(p)*M(p^{\prime}).

∎

Given

, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}(v\to w)

\displaystyle:=\{M(p)\penalty\ :\penalty\ p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H,v\to w)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,v\to w)

\displaystyle:=\left\{p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H,v\to w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ M(p)\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(H,v\to w)^{\dagger}\right\}.

For notational convenience, for

and

\displaystyle:=\left(\mathcal{M}_{n}(v\to w)\right)^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{\dagger}(v\to w)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\dagger}(v\to w)-V_{n}(G,H,P).

Also, given

\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,v):=\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{H}}\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,v\to w)

We refer to the elements of

The relevance of

to the computation of

is expressed by the following simple lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

V_{n}(G,H,P)=\max_{u,v\in\mathcal{V}_{H}}\max_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\dagger}(u\to v)}\min f.

Proof.

From (
7

V_{n}(G,H,P)=\max_{u,v\in\mathcal{V}_{H}}\max_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(u\to v)}\min f.

Let

Because

is a finite set, for every

there exists
f^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\dagger}(u\to v)
such that

, and in particular

. It follows that
\max_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{-}(u\to v)}\min f<\max_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\dagger}(u\to v)}\min f

From this, the result follows directly.
∎

Lemma 4.3

5.
The space of non-improvable paths

As in
[
6
]
, the value of
V(G,H,P)
can be regarded as the Nash equilibrium payoff in the following
two-player game:

•

Alice chooses a bi-infinite walk

in the graph
H

•

Bob observes the walk
p
chosen by Alice and chooses a bi-infinite walk

in

.

•

Bob pays Alice
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{j},p_{j})
.

In this section we will investigate the space of “optimal strategies” for Alice and Bob in a certain sense.

Given a graph
G
, we define

X_{G}:=\{x\in\mathcal{E}_{H}^{\mathbb{Z}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z}\;\left(t(x_{n})=s(x_{n+1})\right)\}.

to be the
edge shift
corresponding to
G
(c.f.
[
5

). The shift space
X_{G}
is a shift of finite type, and in particular sofic.

The quantity
V(G,H,P)
defined by (

The following result,
which is a particular case of

, shows how to express the value

using shift-invariant probability measures on

and

The following result follows directly from

:

Theorem D
.

Let
G,H
be primitive graphs and
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Z}
. Then

(18)

V(G,H,P)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathrm{Prob}_{\sigma}(X_{H})}\inf_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Prob}_{\sigma}(X_{H}\times X_{G})\cap(\pi_{H})_{*}^{-1}(\{\mu\})}\int P(x_{0},y_{0})d\lambda(x,y),

where
\operatorname{Prob}_{\sigma}(X_{H})
(resp.
\operatorname{Prob}_{\sigma}(X_{H}\times X_{G})
) denotes the family of shift-invariant Borel probability measures on
X_{H}
(resp.
X_{H}\times X_{G}
).

We now describe certain shifts that in a certain well-defined sense capture all the “optimal strategies” of Alice and Bob.
Consider primitive graphs
G,H

and
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Z}
.
For
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)
,
let

\mathcal{R}(p):=\bigcup_{v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{G}}\left\{q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v\to w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ M(p,v\to w)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{j},p_{j})\right\}.

Define

X_{H}^{\dagger}:=\left\{x\in X_{H}\penalty\ :\penalty\ x_{[m,n]}\in\mathcal{W}_{n-m+1}^{\dagger}(H)\penalty\ \forall m<n\right\}.

and

(19)

Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}:=\left\{(x,y)\in X^{\dagger}_{H}\times X_{G}\penalty\ :\penalty\ y_{[m,n]}\in\mathcal{R}(x_{[m,n]})\penalty\ \forall m<n\right\},

where

denotes the restriction of

to the set

.

It is easy to verify that both
X_{H}^{\dagger}
and

are closed and shift-invariant.
The following lemma shows that they are non-empty.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
X_{G}
and
X_{H}
are non-empty, then
both
X_{H}^{\dagger}
and
Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
are non-empty. Moreover, for every
x\in X_{H}^{\dagger}
there exists
y\in X_{G}
such that
(x,y)\in Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

We first show that
X_{H}^{\dagger}
is non-empty. Because
X_{H}
is non-empty for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)
is a non-empty finite set. So for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist non-improvable walks, meaning that
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}_{n}(H)\neq\emptyset
. We can then apply a compactness argument to find a point
x\in X_{H}^{\dagger}
.

Now, using essentially the same arguments, we will show that for every
x\in X_{H}^{\dagger}
, there exists
y\in X_{G}
such that
(x,y)\in Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H)
there exists

v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{G}

such that
\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,v\to w)\neq\emptyset
.
By definition of
M(p,v\to w)
as a minimizer, there exists
q\in\mathcal{W}(G,v\to w)
such
M(p,v\to w)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{j},p_{j})
, that is
q\in\mathcal{R}(p)
. Also, if
p^{\prime}
is the sub-walk of
p
obtained by removing the first and last edges, and
q^{\prime}
is the sub-walk of
q\in\mathcal{R}(p)
obtained by removing the first and last edges, then
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(p^{\prime})
. Indeed, otherwise, if
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{n-2}(G,v^{\prime}\to w^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{R}(p^{\prime})
, then
there exists
\tilde{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{n-2}(G,v^{\prime}\to w^{\prime})
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}P(\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{j},p^{\prime}_{j})=M(p,v\to w)<\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}P(q^{\prime}_{j},p^{\prime}_{j}),

So by replacing the sub-walk
q^{\prime}
by
\tilde{q}^{\prime}
in
q
, we would obtain
\tilde{q}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G)
having the same initial and terminal vertices as
q
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(\tilde{q}_{j},p_{j})<\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{j},p_{j}),

contradicting the assumption that
q\in\mathcal{R}(p)
.

Fix
x\in X_{H}^{\dagger}
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, choose a walk
\left(q^{(k)}\right)_{i=-k}^{k}\in\mathcal{R}\left(x_{[-k,k]}\right)
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the point
y^{(k)}\in\left(\mathcal{E}_{G}\cup\{\theta\}\right)^{\mathbb{Z}}
given by

\displaystyle y_{i}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}q_{i}^{(k)}&\textrm{if $-k\leq i\leq k$,}\\
\theta&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then by the compactness of
(\mathcal{E}_{G}\cup\{\theta\})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the sequence
\left(y^{(k)}\right)_{k=1}^{\infty}
will have a limit point
y
, and we can verify directly that the limit point
y
will satisfy
y|_{[m,n]}\in\mathcal{R}\left(x_{[m,n]}\right)
for all
m\leq n
. Thus
(x,y)\in Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
.
∎

The following proposition relates

with the shift-invariant probability measures on

and

Theorem E
.

Let

be primitive graphs and

. Then

(1)

Let

be a shift-invariant probability measure on

\mu\left(\left\{x\in X_{H}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\min M(x_{[1,n]})=V(G,H,P)\right\}\right)=1,

\mu\left(X^{\dagger}_{H}\right)=1

(2)

\lambda\left(\left\{(x,y)\in X_{H}^{\dagger}\times X_{G}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(y_{i},x_{i})=V(G,H,P)\right\}\right)=1,

(2c)

\lambda\left(Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}\right)=1

Proof.

Proof of claim (1)
:

(1c)
\Rightarrow
(1a)
:
By

4.7
, there exists
C\in\mathbb{N}
such that

and therefore for any

Let

Then by the above discussion, we have

(1a)
\Rightarrow

Since

We have thus proved (1a)

(1b)

Theorem F
.

Both
X^{\dagger}_{H}
and
Y^{\dagger}_{(H,G)}
are sofic shifts.

For the proof of
F
, we will use a sufficient condition for soficity.
We first recall a well-known characterization of sofic shifts. Given a shift space
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, and an admissible word
w\in\mathcal{C}(S)
, the
follower set
of
w
in
S
, which we denote by
F(S,w)
, is defined to be the set of all words
v\in\mathcal{C}(S)
that can admissibly follows
w
. That is:

F(S,w):=\left\{v\in\mathcal{C}(S)\penalty\ :\penalty\ wv\in\mathcal{C}(S)\right\}.

A shift space
S
is sofic if and only if it has a finite number of follower sets
[
5
, Theorem 3.2.10]
, that is

|\{F(S,w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ w\in\mathcal{C}(S)\}|<+\infty.

Given a set of words
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma^{n}

and
n\in\mathbb{N}
denote by

\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
the set of words
in which no word from
\mathcal{F}
occurs as a subword, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{n}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:=\left\{w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\Sigma^{n}\penalty\ :\penalty\ (w_{i},\ldots,w_{j})\not\in\mathcal{F}\penalty\ \forall 1\leq i\leq j\leq n\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{n}(\mathcal{F}).

If
\mathcal{F}
is a set of forbidden words that generates a shift
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
(c.f.

Definition

2.1
),
then we have
\mathcal{C}_{n}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
; however, strict inclusion is possible.
In the literature,
\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})
is sometimes called “the set of locally admissible words for
S
” (suppressing the dependence on the set of forbidden words
\mathcal{F}
).
A simple compactness argument
shows that if

\mathcal{F}
generates
S

and
w\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})
, then
v\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(S)
if and only for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
u,w\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{k}(\mathcal{F})
such that
uvw\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})
.
For
w\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})
, we set

\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},w):=\left\{v\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})\penalty\ :\penalty\ wv\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})\right\}.

We refer to the set
\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},w)
as the
local follower set of
w
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a shift space generated by a set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma^{k}
of forbidden words, where
\Sigma
is a finite alphabet.
If the set of local follower sets
\{\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ w\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})\}
is finite, then
S
is sofic.

Proof.

For a shift space
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
and configuration
x\in S
, define the
future set
of
x
by

F_{\infty}(S,x)=\left\{(y_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\penalty\ :(\ldots,x_{-3},x_{-2},x_{-1},y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)\in S\right\}.

It is well known that a shift space
S
is sofic if and only if there are finitely many future sets (see
[
5
, exercise 3.2.8]
for an explanation of how these future sets can be used to construct a labeled graph presentation of
S
), in the sense that the following holds:

|\{F_{\infty}(S,x)\penalty\ :\penalty\ x\in S\}|<+\infty.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a generator for
S
and assume that the set of
\{\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ w\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})\}
is a finite set.
Since
\mathcal{F}

genereates
S
,
it follows that for any
x\in S
we have

F_{\infty}(S,x)=\left\{y_{[0,+\infty)}\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ y_{[0,n]}\in\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},x_{[-k,-1]})\penalty\ \forall n,k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Since
\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},x_{[-(k+1),-1]})\subseteq\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},x_{[-k,-1]})
for every
x\in S
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and

\left|\left\{\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ w\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F})\right\}\right|<+\infty
, it follows that for any
x\in S
there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

F_{\infty}(S,x)=\left\{y_{[0,+\infty)}\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ y|_{[0,n]}\in\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},x|_{[-k,-1]})\penalty\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=\hat{F}(\mathcal{F},x|_{[-k,-1]}).

In particular, in this case the set of future sets of
S
is a subset of the set of local follower sets of
\mathcal{F}
, and in particular it is finite, hence
S
is sofic.
∎

Proof of

.

Observe that the following set of forbidden words generates

By

n\geq N
.
It follows that for any
n\geq N
we have that
\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger})=\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H)
.
It’s trivial that there are only finitely many local follower sets of the form
\hat{F}\left(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p\right)
for
p\in\bigcup_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{C}_{n}(S)
, so it remains to be shown that there are only finitely many values that
\hat{F}\left(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p\right)
can take for
p\in\hat{C}_{n}\left(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger}\right),n\geq N
.

For
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H,u\to v)
, set

\overline{M}(p):=M(p)-V_{n}(G,H,P).

Then
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)
if and only if
\overline{M}(p)\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}_{n}(u\to v)
.
Hence, for any
n\geq N
, we have that
p\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger})
if and only if there exist
u,v\in\mathcal{V}_{H}
such that
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)
.
For
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)
, we have

\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p)=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{H}}\left\{q\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(H,v\to w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ \overline{M}(p*q)\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n+m}^{\dagger}(H)\right\}.

For
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{V}_{H}\times\mathcal{V}_{H}}
denote

\left(f*g\right)_{n,m}:=f*g-V_{n+m}(G,H,P)+V_{n}(G,H,P)+V_{m}(G,H,P).

It follows from
Lemma

4.1
that
for any
n,m\in\mathbb{N}

\overline{M}(p*q)=(\overline{M}(p)*\overline{M}(q))_{n,m}.

We conclude that for
n\geq N
and
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)

\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p)=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{H}}\left\{q\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(H,v\to w)\penalty\ :\penalty\ (\overline{M}(p)*\overline{M}(q))_{n,m}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n+m}^{\dagger}(H)\right\}.

Also, since there exists a constant
V^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
V_{n+k}(G,H,P)=V_{n}(G,H,P)+V^{\dagger}
and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}(H,u\to v)=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n+k}(H,u\to v)
for any
n\geq N
(c.f.
Lemma

4.8
), we see that

(f*g)_{n+k,m}=(f*g)_{n,m}\penalty\ \forall n\geq N.

From that it follows that for any
n\geq N;u,v\in\mathcal{V}_{H};
and
p\in\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}_{n+k}(H,u\to v)
, there exists
\tilde{p}\in\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}_{n}(H,u\to v)
such that
\overline{M}(p)=\overline{M}(\tilde{p})
, and in that case we have that

\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p)=\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},\tilde{p})
.
We thus conclude that any local follower set of
X_{H}^{\dagger}
is of the form

\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p)
for some
n\leq N+k,p\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(X_{H}^{\dagger})
.
This shows that the set
\{\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger},p)\penalty\ :\penalty\ p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(H),n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of local follower sets is finite, hence
X_{H}^{\dagger}
is sofic.

We will now prove that
Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
is also sofic. The proof strategy is similar to the previous part.
Observe that the following is a generator for
Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{H}^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathcal{E}_{G}^{\mathbb{Z}}
:

\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{E}_{H}^{n}\times\mathcal{E}_{G}^{n}\penalty\ :\penalty\ p\not\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F}_{H}^{\dagger})\mbox{ or }q\not\in\mathcal{R}(p)\right\}.

We will prove the soficity of
Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
by applying
Lemma

5.2
and showing that

\left|\{\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger},(p,q))\penalty\ :\penalty\ (p,q)\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger})\}\right|<+\infty.

Hence for
n\geq N,m\in\mathbb{N},(p,q)\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger})
, and

(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger})

such that

p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)
and
q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,u^{\prime}\to v^{\prime})
,
we have that
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger},(p,q))
if and only if there exists
w\in\mathcal{V}_{H}
and
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}
such that
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}^{\dagger}(H,v\to w)
and

M(p*p^{\prime},u^{\prime}\to w^{\prime})=M(p,u^{\prime}\to v^{\prime})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}P(q^{\prime}_{j},p^{\prime}_{j}).

By unraveling the definitions, applying
Lemma

4.1
and moving terms around, the last equation can be rewritten as follows:

(\overline{M}(p)*\overline{M}(p^{\prime}))_{n,m}(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})-\overline{M}(p,u^{\prime}\to v^{\prime})+V_{m}(G,H,P)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P(q^{\prime}_{j},p^{\prime}_{j}).

In particular, for every
n\geq N+k
,
p\in\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)
and
q\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(G,u^{\prime}\to v^{\prime})
there exist
\tilde{p}\in\mathcal{W}_{n-k}^{\dagger}(H,u\to v)
,
\tilde{q}\in\mathcal{W}_{n-k}(G,u^{\prime}\to v^{\prime})
such that
\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger},(p,q))=\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger},(\tilde{p},\tilde{q}))
.
Just like in the first part of the proof, this shows that the set of local follower sets
\{\hat{F}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger},(p,q))\penalty\ :\penalty\ (p,q)\in\mathcal{\hat{C}}(\mathcal{F}_{(H,G)}^{\dagger})\}
is finite, hence
Y_{(H,G)}^{\dagger}
is sofic.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

It is known that
X_{H}^{\dagger}
can fail to be of finite type, as shown in
[
7
, Section 3.1]
.

As a corollary, we see that for any primitive graphs and payoff function
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Z}
the Nash equilibrium of corresponding non-alternating mean payoff game is obtained by periodic strategies in the following sense:

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
G
and
H
be primitive graphs and
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Z}
. Then there exist
infinite walks
p\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(H)\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{H}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
q\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{G}^{\mathbb{N}}
that are both periodic in the sense that there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{n+k}=p_{n}
and
q_{n+k}=q_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and so that

(1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{i},p_{i})=V(G,H,P)
;

(2)

for any
\tilde{q}\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(G)
we have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{i},\tilde{p}_{i})\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{i},p_{i});

and

(3)

for any
\tilde{p}\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(H)
there exists
\tilde{q}\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(G)
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(\tilde{q}_{i},\tilde{p}_{i})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(q_{i},p_{i}).

Proof.

By
F
,
Y^{\dagger}_{(H,G)}
is a sofic shift.
It is well-known that any sofic shift admits a periodic point, essentially because any infinite walk in a finite directed graph must contain a cycle. If
(x,y)\in Y^{\dagger}_{(H,G)}
is a periodic point, then it follows directly from the definition of
Y^{\dagger}_{(H,G)}
that
p=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(H),q=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},\ldots)\in\mathcal{W}_{+\infty}(G)
satisfy the assertions in the statement.
∎

6.
Concluding remarks and open questions

In this article, we dealt only with primitive graphs. Some specific statements are known to fail for general (reducible) graphs: For instance the statement of
D
can fail if we do not assume that
G
and
H
are irreducible.
See
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
for examples of various pathologies exhibited in this context by non-primitive graphs.
However, it is likely that the primitivity assumption can be removed in the statement of
A
and
B
through clever use of the periodic decomposition of irreducible graphs in order to break the problem into several primitive “pieces,” although this is beyond the scope of this article.

Another point worth addressing is the assumption that the payoff function
P
takes rational values. There is no further difficulty generalizing to rational-valued payoff functions
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Q}
, just by multiplying by a common denominator. However, if we consider payoff functions that take real values, the same questions remain unsolved.

Question 6.1
.

Does there exist an algorithm that takes as input two primitive graphs
G,H
, and a payoff function
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
, and outputs an “exact value” for
V(G,H,P)
in finite time? Is
V(G,H,P)\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
?

Question 6.2
.

Let
G,H
be primitive graphs, and
P:\mathcal{E}_{G}\times\mathcal{E}_{H}\to\mathbb{R}
an arbitrary payoff function. Is the shift space
Y^{\dagger}_{(H,G)}
defined by (
19
) a sofic shift? Does it always contain a periodic point?
