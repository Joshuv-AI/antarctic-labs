 ABSTRACT
Whole-slide images (WSIs) are central to digital pathology but present major computational challenges
because of their gigapixel scale and multiresolution pyramid structure. This report presents the RADIANCE
AIM-3 minimum viable product (MVP), an end-to-end pipeline for transforming WSIs into structured,
searchable vector representations for downstream retrieval and analytics. The pipeline processes multiresolution
WSIs into patch-level representations, preserves spatial correspondence across resolution levels, and
stores the resulting embeddings and metadata in a searchable vector framework. Designed as a scalable,
reproducible, and model-agnostic system, the MVP supports foundation-model-based feature extraction
using HIPT, H-optimus-0, and Virchow2 and is implemented for HPC-oriented execution and shard-based
storage. By maintaining semantic, spatial, and hierarchical relationships across WSI levels, the system
establishes a foundation for large-scale computational pathology workflows and enables embedding
repositories that support future retrieval, visualization, and integrative analytics applications.

 1

 1. INTRODUCTION
Digital pathology has enabled large-scale histopathologic analysis through whole-slide images (WSIs),
which are gigapixel-resolution scans stored in multi-resolution pyramid formats. These pyramids allow
efficient viewing across magnifications (e.g., 20×, 10×, 5×), but their size and hierarchical organization
introduce significant computational challenges. The information from each resolution provides low-resolution
thumbnail image of slide to microscopic features in full resolution image. For efficient large scale analysis
and traversal of pyramid levels, the images are converted into patches. This makes it more suitable for
machine learning and downstream analytics.
Recent advances in self-supervised and transformer-based models have demonstrated that patch-level
embeddings can encode meaningful histo-morphologic features. When indexed in vector databases, these
embeddings enable quicker implementation of downstream tasks. Yet, most existing approaches focus
on modeling innovations rather than building reproducible, model agnostic, scalable infrastructure that
bridges WSI images and searchable embedding repositories.
To address this gap, the RADIANCE AIM-3’s Minimum Viable Product (MVP) establishes an end-to-end
pipeline that transforms WSIs into structured vector representations through multi-resolution traversal,
distributed patch extraction, embedding generation, and spatially aware indexing.
This MVP is developed and evaluated using the Childhood Cancer Data Initiative (CCDI) Molecular
Characterization Initiative (MCI) dataset, a large, nationally coordinated resource of pediatric cancer
pathology data. Leveraging CCDI MCI provides a realistic, heterogeneous, and clinically relevant dataset
to validate scalability, reproducibility, and interoperability.
The RADIANCE AIM-3’s MVP provides a scalable, end-to-end solution for transforming WSIs into
searchable vector representations. Key contributions include:
 • End-to-End WSI-to-Embedding Pipeline: A unified workflow connecting pyramid traversal, patch
 extraction, embedding generation, and vector database indexing.
 • MPI-Based Distributed Patch Extraction: HPC-oriented parallel extraction that improves
 throughput and supports large-cohort processing.
 • Multi-Resolution Awareness: Explicit use of WSI pyramid levels to support magnification-specific
 analysis and hierarchical representations.
 • Spatially Indexed, Traceable Embeddings: Structured embedding storage with WSI identifiers,
 pyramid levels, patch coordinates, and metadata mappings to preserve provenance.
 • Model-Agnostic Embedding: A modular embedding stage designed to incorporate state-of-the-art
 models (e.g., transformer-based encoders) without redesigning the pipeline.
 • Validation on CCDI MCI Dataset: Demonstration of the workflow on the NCI CCDI MCI WSI
 collection, highlighting applicability to real-world datasets.

2.1 DATA SOURCE AND PYRAMID REPRESENTATION
2.1.1 Input Data
Data from the Childhood Cancer Data Initiative (CCDI) Molecular Characterization Initiative (MCI) was
used for our MVP demonstration. A total of 4,187 participant images were downloaded from the National
Cancer Institute (NCI) Imaging Data Commons (IDC) (Clunie et al. 2025; Fedorov et al. 2023). The WSI
of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained tissue were in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine
(DICOM) format. Demographic, phenotype, and molecular characterization data were also downloaded
and will be linked to the embedding in the vector database.
2.1.2 Pyramid Geometry
Let:
 • L0 denote the highest resolution level
 • Lk denote a downsampled level
 • dk denote the downsampling factor for level Lk
Spatial mapping between levels is defined as:

                                          (xL0 , yL0 ) = dk · (xLk , yLk )
This deterministic relationship enables cross-level spatial mapping.

 3

2.2 PATCH EXTRACTION PIPELINE
2.2.1 Patch Generation Strategy
In this workflow, whole-slide images (WSIs) stored as DICOM patches are processed using a level-wise
patch extraction strategy optimized for large-scale, distributed computation. For each slide, OpenSlide
is used to access the multi-resolution pyramid (Goode et al. 2013), and patches are extracted at selected
resolutions (e.g., level 0 and level 1). Tiling is performed in the coordinate system of the target level, while
patch origins are correctly mapped back to level-0 space—which is required by OpenSlide’s read_region
interface—to ensure complete and spatially consistent coverage of the entire WSI. Patch extraction
parameters such as patch size (256×256 px) and stride are applied uniformly across levels to generate
comparable grids of patches. MPI parallelization distributes patch indices across workers to accelerate
extraction on HPC systems like Frontier. During saving, patches are converted to RGB and metadata is
stripped to avoid PNG text-chunk limitations, ensuring clean and efficient storage. This method yields
complete, level-specific patch sets that preserve geometric alignment across pyramid levels.

2.2.2 White Patch Identification
The number of level 0 patches is in scale of 80-100k patches per WSI, it becomes essential to incorporate
methods to retain only informative patches. One of basic methods of informativeness here is indicated
by the presence of non-white pixel data. The WSI image contains a lot of free space beyond the tissue
image, that free space is typically imaged as white pixels. The white patch flag indicating if the patch
contains predominating (determined by user-defined thershold) white pixels. The white patch flag is stored
in metadata to allow downstream filtering without modifying raw extraction outputs.

2.2.3 MPI-Based Parallelization
Patch extraction is parallelized using MPI to enable distributed execution on HPC systems. The spatial
grid of patch indices is partitioned across MPI ranks such that each rank processes a disjoint subset of
patches. This ensures complete coverage of the image without overlap or duplication.
Partitioning is deterministic and based solely on patch indices, enabling reproducibility across repeated
runs. Outputs are written independently by each rank and later aggregated at the WSI level. This strategy
provides near-linear scalability while preserving spatial integrity of the extracted patch sets.
The limitation is currently MPI parallel is done across CPUs, since OpenSlide library is CPU bound.

2.3 MULTI-LEVEL SPATIAL MAPPING
2.3.1 Centroid Mapping:
Centroid-based mapping provides a simple and robust way to relate patches extracted at different
OpenSlide pyramid levels. Each patch has a centroid defined in its own level’s coordinate system, and
because every OpenSlide level is generated from level-0 by a known scale factor, we can map any centroid
across levels using the stored downsample values. For a patch at source level L, its centroid (cxL , cyL ) is
first converted to level-0 coordinates via

 (cx0 , cy0 ) = (cxL ∗ downsample[L], cyL ∗ downsample[L]) (1)

To locate the corresponding position at a target level K, that same point is scaled back down:

 (cxK , cyK ) = (cx0 /downsample[K], cy0 /downsample[K]) (2)

 4

By dividing (cxK , cyK ) by the patch size at level K, we obtain the target patch index (iK , jK ). This approach
avoids geometric assumptions and works across all levels because the downsample factor precisely
captures how each level relates to the base resolution. As long as the centroid of each patch and the
level-specific downsample are recorded during extraction, any two levels can be reliably mapped without
reopening the slide or accessing pixel data. Centroid mapping ensures 1:1 mapping; every patch of source
level will have patch in target level.

2.4 EMBEDDING AND ATTENTION GENERATION
Embeddings are fixed-length vector representations of image patches that summarize visual and morphologic
content in a form suitable for quantitative analysis. Attention maps represent token-wise weighting
patterns within transformer models and indicate which patch regions receive higher model focus.
Prior work commonly uses embeddings for tasks such as clustering, retrieval, classification, and slide-level
aggregation, while attention maps are used for interpretability, quality control, and region-of-interest
inspection. Following these established uses, we generate both outputs at patch level for each model and
WSI pyramid level.
At a high level, each input patch is transformed as

 Patch → Foundation Model → (Embedding, Attention Map). (3)

For every WSI level, outputs are serialized into paired embedding and attention files using the same
coordinate ordering (sorted by (x, y)), ensuring one-to-one alignment between representation tensors and
patch metadata.

2.4.1 Model 1: HIPT (Hierarchical Image Pyramid Transformer)
What it is. HIPT is a pathology-oriented hierarchical vision transformer framework introduced by
Mahmood Lab (Chen et al. 2022). The original HIPT workflow is hierarchical (256 → 4K → WSI):
ViT-256 first encodes 256 × 256 patches, then ViT-4K aggregates those patch embeddings within each
4096 × 4096 region.
Source code and model location.
 • Official GitHub repository: https://github.com/mahmoodlab/HIPT
 • ViT-256 checkpoint (model weights): vit256_small_dino.pth
 • ViT-4K checkpoint (model weights): vit4k_xsmall_dino.pth
Model properties used in our pipeline.
 • Input patch size: 256 × 256
 • Transformer depth: 12 blocks (ViT-Small/16 backbone)

 5

 • Number of attention heads: 6
 • Embedding size: 384-dim (CLS token)
 • Token structure: 1 CLS token + 256 patch tokens (no register tokens)
How outputs are extracted. HIPT natively exposes last-layer self-attention. We compute embeddings
via the standard forward pass and extract attention via the built-in self-attention accessor, then keep
CLS-to-patch attention and reshape to a 16 × 16 map per head.
Why we use only ViT-256 in this report. Although HIPT includes a hierarchical 256 → 4K → WSI
design, we use only the ViT-256 component because this stage requires patch-level embeddings directly
aligned with native WSI pyramid levels for comparison across multiple foundation models. This is
technically valid because ViT-256 is a standalone pretrained encoder (checkpointed independently and
then reused as a frozen feature extractor), while ViT-4K is an optional aggregation stage built on top of
ViT-256 embeddings rather than a prerequisite for obtaining correct patch-level representations.

2.4.2 Model 2: H-optimus-0 (Bioptimus)
What it is. H-optimus-0 is a 1.1B-parameter pathology foundation ViT released by Bioptimus (Saillard
et al. 2024). It is used here as a high-capacity encoder for robust patch-level feature extraction.
Source code and model location.
 • Hugging Face model card and weights: https://huggingface.co/bioptimus/H-optimus-0
 • Loaded through timm hub integration using hf-hub:bioptimus/H-optimus-0
Model properties used in our pipeline.
 • Backbone family: ViT-g/14
 • Transformer depth: 40 blocks
 • Input patch size: 224 × 224
 • Patch size: 14 (thus 16 × 16 patch-token grid)
 • Number of attention heads: 24

attention weights directly, we manually reconstruct final-layer Q, K, and V computations, apply scaled
dot-product and softmax, remove register-token indices, and retain CLS-to-patch attention as 16×16 spatial
maps.

2.4.4 Cellular level Embeddings
We evaluate the state-of-the-art in terms of cell level embedding generation from whole slide imaging.
Three methods currently define the frontier for cell-level embedding generation from WSIs. CCRL
(Nakhli et al. 2023) uses SimCLR/MoCo contrastive SSL on cropped single-cell images with a ResNet
encoder (11.7M parameters), producing 512-d embeddings validated on only 2 tissue types. LEMON
(Chadoutaud et al. 2026) advances this with a ViT-S/8 backbone (22M parameters) trained via MoCov3
on millions of 40×40 px nucleus crops, yielding compact 384-d morphological embeddings across 5 public
benchmarks. CellViT++ (Hörst et al. 2026) operates at a different scale entirely — a plug-in foundation
encoder exceeding 300M parameters processing 256×256 px patches — producing 384–1280-d per-cell
embeddings jointly with instance segmentation masks and nuclear type classification across 6 datasets
spanning both breast and colorectal tissue.

2.4.5 Execution Strategy on HPC
Distributed extraction is executed on OLCF Frontier, where each node provides 8 GPUs. The same
extraction script (extract_embeddings_attention.py) is used across levels, while SLURM launch
parameters are adapted to level-specific workload size. This section describes the production configuration
used in this report.
Orchestrator style (Levels 0–2). We use run_orchestrator.sh as a queue-driven orchestrator. It
discovers eligible WSIs for a target model/level, skips completed outputs, and submits one distributed
srun task per WSI. Each WSI task reserves M nodes (10 for Level 0, 5 for Level 1, 1 for Level 2) and
launches M × 8 ranks. Inside extract_embeddings_attention.py, the process group is initialized
when world size > 1; patches are partitioned across ranks; each rank computes embeddings/attention for its
shard; and rank 0 gathers and writes final artifacts.
Job-stealing / work-conserving behavior. The orchestrator queue plus SLURM scheduling provides
dynamic load balancing. WSIs have variable patch counts, so some finish earlier than others. As resources
are freed by completed WSI tasks, queued WSIs are launched on those resources. For example, in Level
2 with run_orchestrator.sh (1 node per WSI), when one WSI finishes and its node becomes free, the
next queued WSI immediately takes that node. This behaves as job-stealing and reduces tail idle time.
Level 3 optimization. For Level 3, the orchestrator enables per-GPU distribution by launching single-GPU,
single-process tasks (one WSI per GPU) instead of multi-rank distributed tasks.
Using run_orchestrator_level3.sh, each node can run up to 8 concurrent WSI tasks, and completed
GPU slots are immediately reused by queued WSIs.
Why GPU count is chosen by level (overhead vs workload). Using more GPUs is not always faster. As
GPU count increases, communication, synchronization, process-group initialization, and result-gather
overhead also increase. For high-patch workloads (e.g., Level 0), this overhead is amortized and larger
GPU allocations improve throughput; for lower-patch workloads (Levels 2–3), overhead can dominate and
reduce efficiency. For this reason, we choose distributed GPU/node allocation based on patch-count regime
at each level: more GPUs for high-patch levels and fewer GPUs for low-patch levels.
Resilience and restart behavior. The orchestrator records failures, skips completed outputs, and supports
safe resubmission. Re-running the same configuration processes only missing or failed WSIs, enabling
robust recovery for multi-thousand-WSI campaigns.

2.5 VECTOR DATABASE DESIGN

 Figure 2. Vector Database Pipeline

The final stage of the pipeline aggregates all extracted representations into a scalable vector database
designed for efficient large-scale retrieval. Each entry in the database corresponds to a single image patch
and stores several components: (1) the high-dimensional embedding vector generated by the feature
extraction model, (2) patch-level spatial metadata describing the coordinates of the patch within the whole
slide image (WSI), (3) identifiers linking the patch to its originating WSI, and (4) patient-level metadata
enabling cohort-level analysis. This structure preserves both semantic similarity information and spatial
context, allowing retrieved embeddings to be mapped directly back to their precise location in the source
slide.
Due to the scale of the dataset, which contains approximately 166 million patch embeddings, we
implement the vector store using Milvus Lite, a lightweight vector database built on top of SQLite storage
(Wang et al. 2021). To enable efficient parallel ingestion on high-performance computing infrastructure,
the database is sharded across multiple files, with each compute rank writing to an independent shard.
In our deployment, 16 parallel ingestion processes each produced a Milvus shard database, containing a
subset of the total embeddings. Each shard maintains the same schema and collection structure, allowing
them to be queried independently or combined during retrieval.

 10

This sharded design significantly accelerates large-scale ingestion while avoiding write contention in a
single database file. During retrieval, queries can be executed across shards in parallel and the results
merged to produce the final nearest-neighbor set. The resulting vector database therefore supports efficient
similarity search across hundreds of millions of pathology patches while preserving the spatial and
hierarchical relationships necessary for slide-level and patient-level analysis.

3.4 ATTENTION MAPS
All attention visualizations in this subsection are generated from the same level-2 patch of the same
sample slide (0a0cf8a9-fa95-4e8c-bbc0-5ac3b713d705) to enable direct model-to-model comparison
under identical tissue context. For each model, we report per-head maps and a fused map. The fused view
combines complementary summaries of head behavior: a mean aggregation (stable consensus attention
across heads) and a max aggregation (salient regions emphasized by any head). Using both provides a
quick balance between robustness (mean) and sensitivity to specialized heads (max), which is useful for
rapid qualitative inspection.
To further interpret these attention outputs, we apply threshold analyses at both the pixel level and the
patch level.
Pixel-level thresholding: For each fusion method (mean, max, and max-minus-mean across attention
heads), the fused attention maps are placed into a spatial grid matching the WSI layout, then rank-normalized
to percentiles [0, 100] using scipy.stats.rankdata (following HIPT’s approach). The top-K%
threshold retains only pixels with percentile rank ≥ (100 − K), and all others are zeroed out. For example,
Pixel Top 10% keeps only pixels at or above the 90th percentile of attention values across the entire spatial
grid.
Patch-level thresholding: Each patch receives a saliency score equal to the mean of its fused attention
map. Patches are ranked by this score, and only the top-K% patches are retained; all attention values in
non-selected patches are set to zero. The retained patches’ attention maps are then placed into the spatial
grid and percentile-normalized. For example, Patch Top 10% (16/160) means the 16 highest-scoring
patches out of 160 are shown with their full spatial attention detail, while the remaining 144 patches are
masked out.
Both approaches use the coolwarm colormap (blue = low, red = high), with background or missing patches
filled as zero, consistent with HIPT’s visualization conventions.

 4. CONCLUSION
The RADIANCE AIM-3 MVP demonstrates a scalable and reproducible pipeline for transforming
DICOM whole-slide images into structured, searchable vector representations while preserving spatial
and hierarchical relationships across resolution levels. By integrating multiresolution patch extraction,
cross-level spatial mapping, foundation-model-based embedding generation, and vector database ingestion,
the system establishes a technical foundation for large-scale computational pathology retrieval and
analytics workflows.
At the current stage, the MVP remains a systems prototype and does not yet include several capabilities
needed for a more complete operational platform. In particular, the present implementation does not
incorporate tissue segmentation or stain normalization, has not yet undergone clinical validation of
the generated embeddings, and currently relies on a non-distributed vector indexing approach without
a user-facing interface layer. These limitations define the most immediate directions for continued
development. Future work will focus on extending the framework through multi-scale embedding fusion,
adaptive patch sampling, distributed vector database deployment, clinical validation studies, and interactive
visualization tools. Together, these additions will strengthen the robustness, scalability, and practical utility
of the platform for downstream pathology research and translational applications.
