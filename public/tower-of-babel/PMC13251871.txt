Scalable hierarchical textile fibers toward personalized wearable atmospheric water harvesting
Authors: Chuxin Lei, Yongzheng Zhang, Lu He, Yuyang Wang, Juan Wu, Weixin Guan, Yaxuan Zhao, Qiang Fu, Keith P. Johnston, Kai Wu, Guihua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sorption-based atmospheric water harvesting (AWH) offers a decentralized, sustainable solution to global freshwater scarcity, enabling clean water in diverse environments. However, translating ideal sorption properties of small-scale materials into practical, large-scale systems faces critical kinetic challenges. Here, we conceptualize a hierarchical textile fiber for wearable AWH, addressing the scaling limitations of traditional sorbents. These fibers feature an open-pore surface topology and internal hierarchical pore structures, which accelerate surface vapor liquefaction and subsequent water transport, demonstrating exceptional water uptake and rapid sorption kinetics across varying relative humidity (RH). When woven into textiles, the fibers maintain efficient vapor diffusion through their macroporous, breathable architecture, achieving a 3- to 10-fold improvement over traditional sorbents at scale. We engineered a wearable prototype combining the AWH textile with a portable collector, achieving 3.76 to 7.45 literswater per kilogramsorbent per day and collecting 410 to 894 milliliters across 20 to 80% RH. By overcoming kinetic limitations, our study advances AWH toward scalability and wearability with implications for global water sustainability.

INTRODUCTION

Water scarcity threatens two-thirds of the global population, underscoring the urgent need for sustainable and accessible clean water solutions (1–3). The atmosphere, with an estimated 12,900 trillion liters of water, represents an immense but underused reservoir capable of addressing this crisis (4–6). Sorption-based atmospheric water harvesting (AWH) offers a promising approach, using sorbent materials that directly capture and release water vapor from the atmosphere through temperature differentials (7, 8). Its broad adaptability and climatic applicability make it a viable solution for water collection across diverse regions. Recent advances have yielded high-performance sorbent materials, including metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) (9, 10), salt-based composites (11, 12), and hydrogels (13–15), which offer high water uptake (16), fast sorption/desorption kinetics (17, 18), and stable cycling. A critical factor in determining water production is water diffusivity through the sorbent, set by pore size, tortuosity, and diffusion distance (7, 19). While kinetics issues can be optimized at the microscale, such as tuning water evolution in MOFs (10) or reducing the dimensions of hydrogel and salt-based composites (17, 20, 21), real-world AWH systems require large quantities of sorbents, ranging from hundreds of grams to kilograms. This scaling introduces substantial mass transfer resistance and longer diffusion pathways, markedly lowering sorption efficiency (Fig. 1A). Bridging this gap between ideal laboratory-scale performance and large-scale practicality remains a major obstacle to implementing AWH technology to daily water needs.

Fibers, an ancient material essential to human life, have evolved in tandem with the advancement of civilization (22–24). Their one-dimensional configuration allows structural, mechanical, and functional properties engineered at the microscale to be extended continuously over macroscopic lengths (25–28). Moreover, woven assemblies yield wearable, breathable textiles with interconnected air channels that promote vapor transport and moisture exchange (29–32), offering a promising route to translate laboratory sorbents into practical, large-area AWH devices. At the single-fiber level, vapor first liquefies at the surface before diffusing radially inward. Conventional porous fibers typically exhibit skin-core structures with limitedly controlled surface topologies and internal pores, markedly increasing mass transfer resistance and slowing sorption kinetics (Fig. 1A). This intrinsic limitation at the fiber level propagates through the textile, reducing effectiveness and constraining use at scale. Overcoming this challenge requires fine regulation of both surface and internal porosity within individual fibers and the design of hierarchical textile architectures capable of sustaining efficient moisture capture during motion, key prerequisites for practical, wearable AWH.

Here, we adopted the molecular conformation transition of amphiphilic hydroxypropyl cellulose (HPC) to develop hierarchical open porous fibers (HOP-Fibers) with tailored surface topology and internal pore structures. By modulating HPC’s molecular interactions shifting from intermolecular hydrogen bonding with water to intramolecular hydrophobic associations, we enable in situ formation of an open-pore surface and a tunable hierarchy of internal pores during a continuous wet-spinning process (Fig. 1A). Guided by Gibbs free energy changes during sorption, this architecture enhances surface liquefaction and accelerates radial water transport, yielding fast sorption kinetics and high uptake across a broad relative humidity (RH) range. The HOP-Fibers can be continuously spun and woven into large-area textiles (Fig. 1B). In contrast to massively assembled sorbents that suffer from compromised sorption kinetics at scale, textiles woven from our HOP-Fibers maintain impressive sorption capabilities, achieving a 3- to 10-fold improvement over traditional sorbents (Fig. 1C). To demonstrate practicality, we integrate the textile with a portable collection module in a wearable prototype. Across 20 to 80% RH, it delivers a specific water productivity of 3.76 to 7.45 literswater kgsorbent−1 day−1 and an actual collected water of 410 to 894 ml, outperforming values reported for most state-of-the-art AWH systems (Fig. 1D). This hierarchical fiber textile strategy clarifies structure-function design principles for AWH sorbents and advances the technology toward scalable, wearable use in resource-limited and mobile settings, benefiting travelers, field workers, and military personnel.

RESULTS

Design of single fibers and their AWH properties

Conventional wet-spun fibers typically exhibit a skin-core structure with uniform pores (U-Fiber), characterized by a dense, nonporous outer surface and an internal nanoporous network (fig. S1). This structure is thermodynamically unfavorable for efficient moisture harvesting due to its limited surface liquefaction and restricted inward water transport. To overcome these limitations, we harnessed the transition in molecular conformation of HPC during continuous wet spinning (fig. S2), followed by salt loading and freeze drying. In aqueous environments, HPC chains adopt a hydrophilic, collapsed state due to hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups and water molecules (33). However, when incorporated into a pre–cross-linked cellulose spinning solution containing LiOH and urea, hydroxide ions substantially weaken these hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic forces drive the formation of intramolecular interactions with HPC itself (Fig. 2A and fig. S3) (34). This will induce a critical coil-to-globule transition of HPC within the spinning solution. Rheological analysis and experimental observations confirmed that these HPC globules can remain stable in solution, ensuring good spinnability without precipitation (figs. S4 and S5). By exploiting this critical coil-to-globule transition, we can enable the in situ formation of phase-separated HPC domains during wet spinning, and as the solvent is removed, abundant large residual pores will form on both the fiber surface and interior.

Dynamic light scattering analysis reveals that the phase transition behavior of HPC and the resulting pore structure are strongly dependent on the LiOH concentrations in the spinning solution (Fig. 2B). At low LiOH concentrations (<1 wt %), the hydrodynamic radius of HPC remains largely unchanged, leading to a uniform nanoscale pore structure within the U-Fiber. In contrast, higher LiOH concentrations greatly intensify phase separation, promoting the formation of larger HPC globules with substantial water content (20, 35, 36). This results in a tunable proportion of larger surface and internal pores in the fiber after the water was removed (Figs. 1A and 2B). Two-dimensional Raman mapping further confirms that the walls of these larger pores are composed of HPC macromolecules, validating their formation mechanism through phase separation (fig. S6).

To further refine the hierarchical pore structure, we adjusted the HPC-to-cellulose ratio. Unlike pure cellulose fibers, which exhibit a uniform nanoscale pore structure, fibers with higher HPC content develop more pronounced hierarchical porosity and an open-pore surface. Samples with increasing HPC ratios, designated as HOP-Fiber-1, HOP-Fiber-2, HOP-Fiber-3, and HOP-Fiber-4 (Materials and Methods), demonstrate a progressive enhancement in pore hierarchy. Among them, the prominent hierarchical porosity of HOP-Fiber-4 (fig. S7), with an HPC/cellulose weight ratio of 3:5, forms the efficient sorbent network that largely improves water transport compared to fibers with uniform porosity. This effect is validated by water contact angle tests (figs. S8 and S9) and dynamic observations (movies S1 and S2).

Incorporating LiCl, a highly hygroscopic salt, further enhances the moisture absorption capacity of the fiber-based sorbents. The overall sorption process occurs in three stages: surface vapor liquefaction, radial water intake through large pores, and subsequent water transport from larger to smaller pores (Fig. 2C and fig. S10). To elucidate the thermodynamic driving forces at each stage, we performed a Gibbs free energy analysis across different pore structures and configurations. We model surface liquefaction at the pore morphology (fig. S11), quantifying how fiber surface topography modulates the Gibbs free energy change and thus the capability for liquefaction (text S1). The results reveal that open pores lower the postabsorption free energy state, promoting surface vapor liquefaction (fig. S12). Moreover, large pore size further reduces the Gibbs free energy change for vapor liquefaction, facilitating a faster onset of droplet formation (fig. S13). These theoretical insights align with experimental findings, where HOP-Fiber exhibits rapid surface liquefaction at 30% RH, generating numerous microdroplets within minutes (Fig. 2D and movie S3), underscoring its rapid liquefaction kinetics.

Beyond surface liquefaction, efficient liquid water transport within the fiber is also essential for optimizing overall sorption performance. Instead of remaining unstable and being lost through drainage or evaporation, the liquefied water should be rapidly transported from the fiber surface into the internal structure, thereby minimizing mass transfer resistance and accelerating both vapor and liquid uptake (18). Thermodynamically, unlike the uniform and slow water transmission in U-Fiber, liquid water preferentially enters large pores due to their lower flow resistance (fig. S14) before migrating to smaller pores for saturation, driven by Young-Laplace pressure differences (37). We model water transport across a range of pore sizes, quantifying how size governs the Gibbs free energy change and, in turn, transport capability (texts S2 and S3). A higher proportion of large pores substantially accelerates the moisture absorption rate (fig. S15). The hierarchical pore structure of our fiber optimizes this process, facilitating rapid water transport and improving sorption kinetics. In situ observations validate this mechanism, as increased transparency in the marked rectangular regions indicates efficient inward water movement into internal pores for storage (Fig. 2D). In addition, multiphase fluid dynamics simulations illustrate the sequential flow of liquid through the hierarchical pore network, where water initially enters larger pores before progressively filling smaller ones (Fig. 2E and figs. S16 and S17). These simulations closely align with mathematical models, highlighting the critical role of pore architecture in enhancing liquid transport and sorption efficiency.

A dynamic vapor sorption (DVS) system was used to directly evaluate the AWH performance of the fiber sorbents. Among all tested samples, HOP-Fiber-4, which exhibited the highest hierarchical porosity and demonstrated superior surface liquefaction and liquid water transport, resulted in the best overall AWH performance compared to the U-Fiber and other HOP-Fiber variants (Fig. 2, F to G, and fig. S18, A and B). The three-staged vapor sorption isotherm of HOP-Fiber-4 at 25°C shows efficient water uptake, particularly under low-humidity conditions (fig. S18C). Specifically, it achieved water uptakes of 1.16 g g−1 at 15% RH, 1.50 g g−1 at 30% RH, and 2.68 g g−1 at 60% RH (Fig. 2G). Beyond its high uptake capacity, HOP-Fiber-4 benefited from the combined effects of rapid surface liquefaction and efficient liquid transport, leading to accelerated sorption kinetics. The time required to reach 80% of its saturated water uptake at 15, 30, and 60% RH was 64, 19, and 20 min, respectively, outperforming most state-of-the-art sorbents (fig. S19 and table S3). In addition, the HOP-Fiber-4 enabled efficient water release with mild heating at 60°C (fig. S20). The fiber also demonstrated excellent stability, maintaining robust cyclic performance. Operating at 30% RH, it sustained 36 complete sorption-desorption cycles per day, with each cycle requiring just 20 min for water capture and release (fig. S21). These findings highlight the critical role of pore structure in achieving high-performance, stable AWH operation.

Scalable fiber textiles and their AWH properties

Traditional powder-based and bulk sorbents often suffer from reduced sorption kinetics due to substantially increased mass transfer resistance and longer transport distances when scaled up, such as in larger areas or thicker assemblies. This issue poses a major hurdle for scalable AWH applications. To overcome this, we leveraged the HOP-Fiber-4 to fabricate woven textiles, enabling large-scale water harvesting (Fig. 3A). The hierarchical porosity of individual fibers, combined with the interconnected channels within the woven structure, ensures efficient water diffusion throughout the textile. To evaluate the AWH properties of these textiles, we conducted sorption tests on samples with varying sizes and thicknesses. A woven HOP-Fiber-4 textile with an area of 400 cm2 demonstrated an impressive capability of 1.04 g g−1 at 30% RH within 1 hour, ~75% of the performance observed in a 1-cm2 sample (Fig. 3B). Even with increased diffusion distance in thicker textiles, a 24-layer HOP-Fiber-4 textile maintained efficient sorption kinetics, reaching 0.96 g g−1 at 30% RH within 1 hour (Fig. 3C). These findings underscore the potential of our woven textile approach for scalable, high-performance AWH, offering obvious advantages over conventional powder, film, or bulk sorbents (fig. S22).

To explore the scalability of the woven textile strategy, we used industry-standard equipment and protocols for mass production of HOP-Fibers (Fig. 3D, fig. S23, and movie S4). Using a 20-nozzle spinneret, liters of spinning solution were extruded into a coagulation bath, continuously producing HOP-Fiber-4 at a high rate of 509 m hour−1. After salt loading and freeze drying, the scale-up HOP-Fiber-4 maintained AWH properties comparable to its laboratory-scale counterpart (Fig. 3E). We then used a braiding machine to weave the HOP-Fiber into square meter–scale textiles (Fig. 1C). The resulting woven textile demonstrated reliable mechanical durability, withstanding various deformations such as rolling, folding, and twisting, enhancing its practicality for real-world water harvesting applications (Fig. 3F). Compared to traditional sorbents, our fiber-based textile sorbents offer superior water uptake, fast sorption kinetics, low material costs, scalable and eco-friendly synthesis, and a distinct wearable design (Fig. 3G, tables S4 and S5, and text S4), demonstrating strong potential for practical AWH implementations.

Practical outdoor testing of AWH clothing

Building on the flexibility and durability of the woven textiles, we integrated them into a wearable AWH system in the form of a jacket. The jacket includes four moisture-capture units: two measuring 0.04 m2 and two measuring 0.01 m2, each covered with a protective layer (fig. S24). These units are strategically assembled on the front and back to maximize moisture-capture efficiency. Designed for active use, the AWH clothes allow the wearer to engage in various activities such as running and climbing while continuously capturing moisture, offering convenience for travelers and soldiers in arid environments (Fig. 4A and movie S5). To enhance practicality, we developed a foldable, lightweight, and portable water collection unit that works in conjunction with the wearable moisture-harvesting system (Fig. 4B and movie S6). After moisture is captured, the textile units are positioned on a flexible heater. The foldable collection system, featuring an inner condensation surface treated to be hydrophobic (fig. S25), is then inflated. The moisture-capture units are heated using a dc power supply within the sealed collection system, causing the captured moisture to evaporate and condense as droplets that flow into a collection ditch (Fig. 4C, fig. S26, and movies S7 and S8). All components of the moisture-harvesting and water collection system can be compactly stored in a lightweight, portable bag, making it highly convenient for water harvesting during outdoor expeditions and travel (fig. S27).

We conducted outdoor experiments in the arid environment of Xichang, China, to evaluate the water production capacity of our AWH fiber clothing using continuous cycling (1.5 hours for sorption and 1 hour for desorption). In this arid region, the AWH clothing demonstrated a notable water production capacity of 3.76 literswater kgsorbent−1 day−1 (4.10 literswater msorbent −2 day−1), accumulating a total of 410 ml of water daily (Fig. 4D and fig. S28). Additional tests were also performed in other environments, including a semiarid region in Austin, USA (figs. S29 and S30), and a humid region in Chengdu, China (figs. S31 and S32). The collected water exhibited minimal Li+ residues, meeting World Health Organization standards of drinking water (fig. S33). Across a range of RH levels from 20 to 80%, the AWH clothing achieved water production of 3.76 to 7.45 literswater kgsorbent−1 day−1 (4.10 to 8.94 literswater msorbent −2 day−1) and an actual collected volume of 410 to 894 ml (Fig. 4E), outperforming most state-of-the-art sorbent–based systems (fig. S34 and table S2). On the basis of AWH performance data from field tests and global average annual RH values (38), the water production potential of our fiber textiles can be estimated for different locations (Fig. 4F). Compared with previously reported portable AWH concepts, our AWH clothing system introduces an approach that combines high collected water volume, competitive productivity, low complexity in the wearable configuration, and genuine portability in a textile-based format (table S6). This combination of high efficiency, adaptability, and scalability positions our system to drive the next generation of practical, wearable AWH.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we report a sustainable, cost-effective, and scalable hierarchical fiber textile for wearable AWH. Guided by thermodynamic principles and enabled by molecular conformational engineering, the fibers have open-pore surfaces and hierarchical internal porosity that promote surface liquefaction and accelerate radial water transport, yielding fast sorption kinetics and high uptake across varying RHs. Unlike conventional sorbents whose kinetics deteriorate at scale, these advantages are preserved when the fibers are woven into breathable, moisture-capturing textiles. The textiles integrate readily into wearable systems capable of producing potable water during daily, in-motion use. Looking ahead, coupling these textiles with low-grade or solar-thermal regeneration, on-body energy harvesting, and embedded sensing could enable continuous, on-the-move potable-water production. By shifting AWH from bulk modules to clothing and shelters, this approach points to distributed, affordable water security for off-grid communities, disaster response, and arid-region livelihoods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

The raw cellulose (cotton pulp) with 95% α-cellulose content was provided by Hubei Chemical Fiber Co. Ltd. (Xiangyang, China). LiOH, urea, Na2SO4, KOH, and epichlorohydrin (ECH) were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Co. HPC (average Mw: ~100,000) and LiCl were bought from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were purchased and used without further purification.

Fabrication of U-Fiber

A 100-g aqueous solution of LiOH/urea (8 wt % LiOH and 15 wt % urea) was precooled to −12°C. Approximately 4 g of raw cellulose was dissolved in the solution through intense mechanical stirring, resulting in a transparent cellulose solution. To remove bubbles, the solution was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. Next, 1 g of ECH was added to the cellulose solution for pre–cross-linking, stirred at low speed for 2 hours. After pre–cross-linking, the solution was centrifuged again to remove bubbles, yielding the spinning dope. The viscous spinning dope was then extruded using a laboratory-scale extruder with a speed of 800 μl/min and solidified in a coagulation bath (1 liter of 5 wt % H2SO4 and 5 wt % Na2SO4 in water). The resultant hydrogel fiber was collected using a Nelson-type roller at a speed of 10 rpm. Subsequently, the hydrogel fiber was washed in 2 liters of deionized water for 24 hours to remove any residues. The hydrogel fiber was then soaked in 500 ml of 2 M LiCl solution for 24 hours to load the hygroscopic salt into the fiber materials. Last, the fiber was freeze dried for 24 hours, rendering it ready for use.

Fabrication of HOP-Fiber

A 100-g aqueous solution of LiOH/urea (8 wt % LiOH and 15 wt % urea) was precooled to −12°C. Varying amounts of cellulose (3.5, 3, 2.75, and 2.5 g) were then dissolved in the solution through intense mechanical stirring. Following this, specific amounts of HPC (0.5, 1, 1.25, and 1.5 g) were added to each respective solution and homogenized until clear. Each solution contained 4 wt % total solutes (cellulose and HPC). The solutions were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to remove bubbles. Approximately 1 g of ECH was added to each cellulose solution for pre–cross-linking, followed by low-speed stirring for 2 hours. After pre–cross-linking, the solutions were centrifuged again to eliminate any remaining bubbles, yielding the spinning dope. The viscous spinning dope was extruded using a laboratory-scale extruder at a speed of 800 μl/min and solidified in a coagulation bath (1 liter of 5 wt % H2SO4 and 5 wt % Na2SO4 in water). The resulting hydrogel fibers, named HOP-Fiber-1, HOP-Fiber-2, HOP-Fiber-3, and HOP-Fiber-4 according to their respective cellulose and HPC ratios, were collected using a Nelson-type roller operating at 10 rpm. The HOP-Fibers were then washed in 2 liters of deionized water for 24 hours to remove any residues, followed by soaking in 500 ml of 2 M LiCl solution for 24 hours to load the hygroscopic salt into the fiber materials. Last, the fibers were freeze dried for 24 hours, rendering them ready for use. In the experiments, to balance AWH properties and mechanical strength for subsequent weaving, our HOP-Fiber-4 was designed with the highest ratio of HPC.

Large-scale production of HOP-Fiber-4

The composition of the spinning solution for HOP-Fiber-4 remains consistent with the aforementioned recipe. To reduce fabrication costs, NaOH was used as a substitute for LiOH in large-scale production, as it effectively dissolves cellulose in the NaOH/urea solution system (39). Initially, 5 liters of spinning dope was slowly added into the feed barrel and extruded using a pilot-scale extruding machine at a pressure of 0.2 MPa. The spinning solution was solidified in the first coagulation bath (4 liters of 5 wt % H2SO4 and 5 wt % Na2SO4 in water) and subsequently transported to the second and third coagulation baths (4 liters of deionized water each) using a drive roll at a speed of 20 rpm. The washed hydrogel fiber was then collected by a Nelson-type roller operating at 30 rpm. The hydrogel fibers were washed in deionized water for 24 hours to remove any residues, followed by soaking in a 2 M LiCl solution for 24 hours to load hygroscopic salt into the fiber materials. Last, the fibers were freeze dried for 24 hours, rendering them ready.

Fabrication of assembled multilayer textiles and textiles with different areas

For the assembled multilayer textiles, AWH fibers were woven onto a substrate in an alternating warp and weft pattern at 1-cm intervals. Each layer consisted of five fibers, each measuring 5 cm in length, and the total area of all textiles was maintained at 25 cm2. To evaluate the moisture capture capacity, we tested assembled textiles with varying numbers of layers: 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 layers. For the assembled textiles of varying areas, AWH fibers were woven onto a substrate in an alternating warp and weft pattern, with all textiles maintaining a consistent thickness of four layers. We tested the moisture capture capacity of the assembled textiles with varying areas: 1, 6.25, 25, 56.25, 100, 225, and 400 cm2.

Weaving of scalable AWH textiles and fabrication of AWH clothes

The fiber was first used as the warp and wound frame on a homemade weaving machine. Next, the fiber was taken as a single weft thread and woven through the warp threads in an alternating pattern. This process was repeated continuously until the entire textile was completed. For practical applications, the weaved textiles (two pieces of 0.01 m2 and two pieces of 0.04 m2) were encapsulated in highly porous polytetrafluoroethylene to form moisture-harvesting units. These units were designed to be easily attached and detached from the clothing. The wearable AWH clothes effectively harvest moisture in different working environments, such as during running, climbing, and hiking.

Fabrication of water release and collection system

Our portable water collection system includes AWH clothes with four moisture-harvesting units, a foldable condenser, a heater, a dc power supply, a cycle cooling system, and an inflator. The inner surface of the polyvinyl chloride foldable condenser was evenly covered by a hydrophobic coating (Piqnano, X5 hydrophobic coating). To set up the water release and collection system, the inflator was used to fully inflate the condenser. The heater was connected to the dc supply, and the harvesting units were placed on the heater for water release, with the desorption temperature set to 60°C. During desorption, the released water condensed on the inner surface of the condenser, and the droplets flowed down to the collectors. All collected water was stored in the water collection bag.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were captured using an FEI Quanta 650 ESEM to observe the surface and cross-sectional morphology of the fibers and the microstructure of the textiles. All fiber samples were freeze dried 24 hours before SEM imaging. Dynamic light scattering was used to determine the hydrodynamic radius of HPC particles in different solvent environments. Raman spectra and mapping were obtained using a Raman spectrometer (HORIBA, HR Evolution; 532 nm). The evaporation behavior of the fibers was measured with a differential scanning calorimeter (TA Instruments, DSC 250), with a scan rate of 5°C min−1 for all samples. The moisture sorption process of the fibers was recorded using an optical microscope (BM2100POL). Water contact angles of the fiber samples were characterized using a KRÜSS DSA25 instrument (KRUSS Scientific, Germany). Sorption and desorption performance were tested using a DVS system (Surface Measurement Systems Ltd., DVS Adventure). Before testing, fiber samples were preheated at 90°C and 0% RH for 60 min in the testing chamber, followed by stabilization at 25°C and 0% RH for 30 min. The airflow rate for DVS testing was set at 200 ml min−1 for all standard tests. Ion concentrations were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (Agilent 7500ce). Mechanical properties of the fiber samples were tested by an LLY-06B electronic monofiber strength tester (Laizhou Electron Instrument Co. Ltd., China) at 30% RH with a stretching speed of 10 mm min−1.

Water vapor sorption for the assembled multilayer textiles and the assembled textiles with different areas

Before vapor sorption tests, the assembled multilayer textiles were dried in a vacuum oven at 90°C for 6 hours. The completely dried textiles were then placed in a homemade RH-controlled system. The initial weight of the assembled textiles, m1, was recorded before testing. The RH of the testing system was stabilized using an oversaturated CH3COOK solution, with an airflow rate of 400 ml min−1. A hygrometer was used to record RH and temperature, while a microbalance measured weight changes because of sorption. After 1 hour, the weight of the assembled multilayer textiles, m2, was recorded. The sorption performance was calculated as (m2 – m1)/m1. The same testing method was applied to assess the absorption performance of textiles in different areas.

Simulation method of water transport within the HOP-Fiber

The model uses the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) with the pseudopotential model proposed by Qian and Chen (40). This model effectively captures the core physics of multiphase fluid dynamics by describing microscopic interaction forces. The pseudopotential’s ability to automatically track phase interfaces makes it particularly advantageous for simulating complex multiphase flow systems. In LBM for single-component two-phase flow, the fluid is characterized and described through the mesoscale particle distribution function. The fluid particle distribution function evolves and undergoes collisions on a lattice with a defined structure. The simulation parameters are defined as follows: We initially defined a computational domain of 150 × 150 × 150 lattice units, assuming that the upper part of the fiber framework is connected with liquid water. The model adopts periodic boundary conditions on all sides, a nonequilibrium extrapolation pressure outlet boundary at the lower boundary, and a nonequilibrium extrapolation fixed density (pressure boundary) at the upper boundary. The interface between the fiber structure and the fluid uses a bounce-back boundary. All simulations are performed using lattice units. The temperature in the equation of state is set to 0.7 Tc, with the density of the liquid phase is set to ρl = 8.08 and the density of the gas phase is set to ρg = 0.0556. The contact angle of the fibers is 60°. Taking the properties of water at 20°C as a reference, the unit conversion is based on dimensionless unit transformation:

1) We determined the length conversion ratio φl and assumed that one lattice unit represents 0.1 μm and then φl = 1 × 10−7 m.

2) We determined the time conversion factor φt. Given the physical properties of water at 20°C, the kinematic viscosity v = 1 × 10−6 m2 s−1, the relaxation time of 1, and a lattice viscosity of 0.166667, we have: 0.166667*φl2/φt = 1 × 10−6 m2 s−1. Solve this, we get φt = 1.667 × 10−9 s, meaning one unit time step represents 1.667 × 10−9 s.
