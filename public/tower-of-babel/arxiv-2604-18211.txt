Weak solutions and weak-strong uniqueness for
a Cahn-Hilliard type model with chemotaxis

Abstract
We prove existence of weak solutions and weak-strong uniqueness for a mathematical model which couples the evolution of a phase-parameter φ \varphi satisfying a Cahn-Hilliard type relation with the one of an additional variable σ \sigma influencing the phase separation process. The main application of the model refers to cancer growth processes, where σ \sigma may represent the concentration of a chemical substance affecting the evolution of the tumor, and is governed by a nonlinear parabolic equation characterized by a cross-diffusion term alike that occurring in the Keller-Segel model for chemotaxis. This term is also responsible for the most relevant difficulties in the mathematical analysis of the system. Complementing previous results on the model, we prove here global in time existence for a very weak notion of solution to which a suitable energy imbalance and a logarithmic inequality for the nutrient are added. Noting that the system also admits local in time “strong” solutions, we can also exhibit a weak-strong uniqueness result whose proof exploits in an essential way the entropy-type inequality satisfied by weak solutions. Key words: Cahn-Hilliard equation, chemotaxis, Keller-Segel model, weak solutions, weak-strong uniqueness, tumor growth models. AMS (MOS) Subject Classification: 35K61, 35D30, 35Q92, 92C50.

Introduction

In this article, we consider the PDE system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\mu=0,\qquad\mu=-\Delta\varphi+F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\chi\sigma,

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\,\Omega\times(0,T),

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\dive(\sigma\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)))=\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma,

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1b)

equipped with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\nabla\mu=0={\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\nabla\varphi,\qquad{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot(\sigma\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)))=0,\quad\text{on }\,\partial\Omega\times(0,T)

(1c)

and the initial conditions

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\qquad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0},\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(1d)

Here
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
(we could
actually consider any dimension
d\geq 2
, however we prefer to state the results
in the physically meaningful cases
d=2,3
for simplicity),
{\boldsymbol{n}}
stands for the outer
normal unit vector to
\partial\Omega
, and
T>0
is an arbitrarily large, but otherwise fixed,
final time. In Problem (
1
),
\varphi
represents the order parameter
of a phase separation process and is subject to the Cahn-Hilliard dynamics prescribed by
system (
1a
), where
\mu
acts as an auxiliary variable representing the chemical
potential. The additional variable
\sigma
denotes the concentration of another
chemical substance affecting the phase separation process.
The evolution of
\sigma
is governed by the nonlinear
parabolic equation (
1b
), which is characterized by a cross-diffusion term reminiscent
of that occurring in the Keller-Segel model for chemotaxis
[
20
]
.

The function
F:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty]
appearing in (
1a
)
represents a non-smooth configuration potential, assumed to be
\lambda
-convex (i.e., convex
up to a quadratic perturbation). Even though more general
choices could be considered, for simplicity we shall directly assume
F
to be given by a variant of
the Flory-Huggins configuration potential for phase separation, namely

F(r)=(1+r)\ln(1+r)+(1-r)\ln(1-r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}r^{2},

(2)

where
\lambda\geq 0
. In particular, the above choice enforces the variable
\varphi

to take values in the interval
[-1,1]
during the evolution, with
\varphi=\pm 1
corresponding
to pure configurations or phases.

The PDE system (
1
) can be derived from
the following free energy functional:

\displaystyle{\cal E}(\varphi,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\big(\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+\chi\sigma{(1-\varphi)}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3)

In particular, equation (
1a
) may be obtained as a balance
law by setting

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}+\dive{\bf J}_{\varphi}=0,

where, as is typical for the Cahn–Hilliard equation, the flux
{\bf J}_{\varphi}

is prescribed as
{\bf J}_{\varphi}=-\nabla\mu
and the chemical potential
\mu
is
defined as the variational derivative of the free energy with respect to the order parameter,
namely
\mu:=\delta{\cal E}/\delta\varphi
.
Note that also equation (
1b
) can be obtained as a
balance law for the nutrient flux
{\bf J}_{\sigma}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}+\dive{\bf J}_{\sigma}=\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma,\quad\text{with }\,{\bf J}_{\sigma}:=-\sigma\nabla\mu_{\sigma},\quad\mu_{\sigma}:=\frac{\delta{\cal E}}{\delta\sigma}=\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi),

where the mobility function is proportional to
\sigma
, hence,
in particular, it degenerates (in fact linearly) as
\sigma\searrow 0
.
It is worth observing that this condition also guarantees the minimum principle for

\sigma
, namely
\sigma
keeps being nonnegative if that occurs
at the initial time.

Here,
\chi
is the chemotactic coefficient, which will be
assumed constant, and strictly positive in view of physical considerations
(cf. (
6
) below), even though, mathematically, our
results would extend to the case
\chi<0
as well.
Moreover,
\alpha
is a sufficiently regular
function of the two variables
\varphi
and
\sigma
.
A typical example for
\alpha
is

\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)=h(\varphi)(1-\ell\sigma^{p}),\quad p\in(0,1],\penalty\ \penalty\ \ell>0,

(4)

where
h
is a nonnegative and non-decreasing
interpolation function such that
h(-1)=0
and
h(1)=1
.
In particular, the above choice, in the case
p=1
, corresponds
to a logistic behavior of the right-hand side of (
1b
)
with respect to
\sigma
, as was assumed, for example, in
[
28
]
.
Regarding the potential (
2
), we point out that, even if this choice is generally
considered the physically most realistic one in the framework of Cahn-Hilliard type models,
it is also the source of a number of mathematical difficulties (cf., e.g.,
[
6
,
21
,
26
]
)
in view of its “singular” character. As first noted in
[
28
]
, however, as one considers
the coupling with the Keller-Segel-like relation (
1b
), the choice of a singular potential
is almost compulsory, as it automatically implies the boundedness of the phase parameter in between

-1
and
1
. In turn, such a property is crucial in order to guarantee the coercivity
of the functional (
3
), and in particular to control the coupling term in it.

Let us note that the system (
1
) can be seen as a particular example in a wide class of
tumor growth models accounting for chemotaxis effects (cf.
[
18
,
28
,
30
]

for further extensions to models also including the velocity field). In this setting,
the phase parameter
\varphi
represents the difference between the tumor cells and healthy
cells volume fractions, and is normalized in such a way that the level sets
\{\varphi=1\}

and
\{\varphi=-1\}
describe the regions occupied by the pure (“tumor” and “healthy”) phases,
respectively, whereas the variable
\sigma
denotes the concentration of the nutrient (glucose
or oxygen, for instance) responsible for tumor growth.

To our knowledge, the coupling between the Cahn-Hilliard system and the Keller-Segel-like
relation (
1b
) was first suggested in
[
1
]

(as one specific possibility among a wide class of models
introduced there), and then proposed again, with a more specific
motivation, in
[
28
]
. In the latter paper,
the mass balance equation
\eqref{eq:1}_{1}
also included a non-zero right-hand
side
S(\varphi,\sigma)
representing the mass source and describing how the nutrient supply affects
the evolution of the tumor. In
[
28
]
,
existence of weak solutions was proved under suitable assumptions on
S
, but uniqueness
was left as an open problem, at least for general
\alpha

and in the class of weak solutions (while it was proven to hold for smoother
solutions). This is why in the present paper we are willing to prove
weak-strong uniqueness of solutions, meaning that, if a weak and a strong solution of the
system stem from the same initial data, then they must coincide at least on the time interval where
the local strong solution exists. Our results are proved only for zero right-hand side
in
\eqref{eq:1}_{1}
(meaning that the tumor mass remains constant in time during
the evolution of the system); however, they may extend to the case of
an “affine linear” source of the form

S(\varphi,\sigma)=-m\varphi+\overline{h},

(5)

where
\overline{h}
and
m
are constants with
m>0
and
|{\overline{h}}|<m
.
On the other hand, as first observed in
[
17
]
, for
singular potentials the case of
nonlinear
mass source terms
is much more delicate to deal with from the point of view of uniqueness
(and of weak-strong uniqueness as well), because of the lack of a contractive
estimate for the spatial mean of
\varphi
(which, instead, it is easy
to prove in the case of (
5
)).

From the physical point of view, the main justification for considering
our model can be observed by integrating the nutrient equation (
1b
)
over an arbitrary (smooth) subdomain
V\subset\Omega
. Then,
using the boundary conditions, one obtains

\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\int_{V}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial V}\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}S+\int_{V}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x-\chi\int_{\partial V}\sigma\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}S;

(6)

namely, as physically expected, the flux of nutrients across
\partial V
,
driven by consumption by tumor cells, is proportional to the actual value of

\sigma
: the more nutrient is present, the more it may flow away.

Actually, it was this simple observation to lead the
authors of
[
28
]
to propose the Keller-Segel-like relation (
1b
)
in place of the more standard expression

\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta(\sigma-\chi\varphi)=\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma,

(7)

which was considered in most of the previous papers dealing with
diffuse-interface models for tumor growth (cf., e.g.,
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
and
references therein). We may notice that, if we integrate (
7
)
over the reference domain
V
, the factor
\sigma
in the last integrand in (
6
) disappears,
meaning that the flux of nutrients across
\partial V
caused by consumption
by tumor cells is independent of the actual value of
\sigma
. This behavior is
rather questionable from the physical viewpoint and can also result in the loss
of the minimum principle; namely,
\sigma
satisfying (
7
)
may well become negative at some point, which is something unexpected
as
\sigma
represents a concentration. As already observed, such
a behavior cannot occur in the case of (
1b
).

Let us comment a bit more on recent results in the literature related to system
(
1
). As noted above, the idea of considering chemotaxis/transport effects
in equation (
1b
) comes from the recent paper
[
28
]
, where various
properties like existence, uniqueness and regularity of solutions,
also depending on the space dimension
d=2,3
, were proved in the case
of a logistic source of the form (
4
).
Regarding more specifically the case of dimension
d=2
, still in the case of a
logistic source in (
1b
) and also considering a mass source in
\eqref{eq:1}_{1}
,
more recently some additional results concerning regularity and uniqueness
have been proved in
[
18
]
, where transport effects driven
by a macroscopic velocity field
{\boldsymbol{u}}
assumed to satisfy a Brinkman-type law
were added to the model. Velocity effects were also considered in the paper
[
30
]
,
dealing with the case where (
1b
) accounts for a nonlinear (i.e., power-like)

chemotactic sensitivity

\gamma
; namely, one has

\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\chi\dive{(\gamma(\sigma)\nabla\varphi)}=\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma,

(8)

where
\gamma
is assumed to behave like
\sigma
for
\sigma\sim 0
and
like
\sigma^{2-p}
,
p\in(12/11,2]
, for large
\sigma
. It is worth observing
that the resulting “subcritical” character of the cross-diffusion term
in
[
30
]
allows for the choice of a bounded
\alpha
, i.e. to
get rid of the stabilizing effect coming from a source of logistic type.
The model considered in
[
30
]
in the specific case
p=2
,
and the existence result proved there, will also serve as an approximation for our
system (
1
). We also quote the recent paper
[
32
]
, where the long-time behavior
of the system considered in
[
28
]
(including in particular the logistic source
in (
1b
), but neglecting the mass source in (
1a
)
1
) was analyzed
from the point of view of infinite-dimensional dynamical systems proving the existence
of the global attractor for the associated evolutionary process.
Finally, we would like to mention the recent
contribution
[
2
]
, where the authors introduce a different approach to
multiphase tumor growth models including chemotaxis in the first Cahn-Hilliard
equation
\eqref{eq:1}_{1}
, so that it is the tumor mass that is “transported” by the nutrient in their setting.

Comparing our specific results with the mathematical literature in the field,
we point out
that, while weak-strong uniqueness has been extensively investigated in the context of
phase-field models (cf., e.g.
[
24
]
), also in connection to liquid crystal flows
(cf., e.g.,
[
12
]
) or damage processes
(cf., e.g.,
[
23
]
), to our knowledge the present paper is one of the first
works where a phase field model for tumor growth including a “singular” potential
is addressed under this perspective.

In order to briefly introduce our mathematical approach, we point out once
more that the quadratic cross diffusion term occurring in (
1b
)
and “inherited” from the Keller–Segel (KS) model
[
20
]

represents the main source of analytical complications. In particular, at least in
three space dimensions, global existence for system (
1
)

without the logistic degradation term in (
1b
)
, to our knowledge
was still an open problem. Actually, the results proved in
[
28
]
strongly
rely on the presence of the logistic source, whereas the “nonlinear
sensitivity” considered in
[
30
]
prescribes the cross-diffusion
term to have a “subcritical” character. In the present contribution, we
can give a positive answer to the problem of existence of
solutions for (
1
) (i.e., considering “linear” sensitivity
and omitting logistic sources); our notion of solution, however, will
be weaker compared to that considered, say, in
[
28
,
30
]
.
The key point in our argument consists in considering a sort
of renormalized version of (
1b
), which, roughly speaking,
is obtained by dividing (
1b
) by
\sigma
and rearranging the
diffusion terms by means of suitable chain-rule formulas; see, for instance, (
74
) below.
This procedure, in turn, is admissible thanks to a reinforced version of the
minimum principle for
\sigma
; namely, we can show that
\sigma
is

strictly
positive almost everywhere and that
\ln\sigma\in L^{1}(\Omega)

at the time
t>0
, provided that the same holds at the initial time.
Working on the renormalized version
of (
1b
) and using a suitable uniform integrability argument,
we can actually prove strong
L^{1}
- (hence pointwise) convergence of
\ln\sigma
,
which is a key step in order to identify the cross-diffusion term in
the limit. It is worth noting that, due to the supercritical behavior
of that term, even for very smooth initial data, we
do not expect that additional regularity (or parabolic smoothing) properties
for
\sigma
could be proved. We also remark that the notion of weak solution considered here
is complemented by an “integrated” entropy inequality
(cf. (
15e
) below),
which is crucial for the sake of proving our weak-strong uniqueness result.

Plan of the paper.
In Section
2
, we introduce some notation and
state our three main results: existence of (global in time) weak solutions,
existence of (local in time) strong solutions and weak-strong uniqueness.
Then, in Section
3
we prove some preliminary lemmas which are needed in
order to clarify the concept of weak solution, especially for what concerns regularity
properties and validity of the energy and entropy inequalities.
Then, existence of weak solutions is proved in Section
4
by
using the system with subcritical sensitivity as an approximation of our model, deriving some
a-priori estimates uniform with respect to the regularization parameter,
and then passing to the limit by means of compactness arguments. Next, in
Section
5
we prove existence of local-in-time strong
solutions by obtaining a further set of a-priori regularity estimates and
using a local version of Grönwall’s lemma. Finally, in the last Section
6

the weak-strong uniqueness result is proved by deriving a suitable relative energy inequality,
entailing that a weak and a strong solution originating from the same initial datum must
coincide on the largest time interval in which they simultaneously exist.

Assumptions and main results

2.1
Notation

We start introducing a set of notation which will be useful in
order to rigorously formulate our mathematical results.
Letting
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
,
we set
H:=L^{2}(\Omega)
and
V:=H^{1}(\Omega)
.
We will generally write
H
in place of
H^{d}
(with similar notation for other spaces),
whenever vector-valued functions are considered. We denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)
the
standard scalar product of
H
and by
\|\cdot\|
the associated Hilbert norm.
Moreover, we equip
V
with the usual norm
\|\cdot\|_{V}^{2}=\|\cdot\|^{2}+\|\nabla\cdot\|^{2}
.
Identifying
H
with its dual space
H^{\prime}
by means of the scalar
product introduced above, we obtain the chain of continuous
and dense embeddings
V\subset H\subset V^{\prime}
.
We indicate by
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle
the duality pairing between
V^{\prime}
and
V
,
or, more generally, between
X^{\prime}
and
X
, where
X
is some Banach space continuously
and densely embedded into
H
. Next, for a given Banach space
X
, the space

\C_{w}([0,T];X)
consists of the functions on
[0,T]
taking values in
X

that are continuous with respect to the weak topology of
X
.

The total variation of a function
E:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\R
is given by

\TV(E,[0,T]):=\sup_{0=t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}=T}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lvert E(t_{k-1})-E(t_{k})\rvert,

where the supremum is taken over all finite partitions of the interval
[0,T]
.
We denote the space of all bounded functions of bounded total variations on
[0,T]

by
\Bv([0,T])
. We recall that the left- and right-hand limit of a function
E\in\Bv([0,T])

are well defined in any point in time
t\in[0,T]
and will be denoted by
E(t-)
and
E(t+)
, respectively.
Similarly, we will denote by
\Bv([0,T];X)
the Banach space of the functions that have
bounded total variation with respect to the norm of the generic Banach space
X
.
By
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
, we denote the space of nonnegative
Borel measures on
\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]
. Finally,
for a real number
a\in\R
,
a_{+}
and
a_{-}
will denote its non-negative
and non-positive parts, respectively.

2.2
Weak solutions

Our main assumptions on the coefficients of the problem can be summarized as follows:

Hypothesis 2.1
.

We assume
\Omega
to be a regular bounded domain in
\R^{d}
, for
d\in\{2,3\}
;
moreover, we assume the chemotactic response coefficient
\chi
to be strictly
positive and the function
\alpha:\R^{2}\to\R
to be Lipschitz continuous
and to satisfy the uniform boundedness condition

\underline{\alpha}\leq\alpha(r,s)\leq\overline{\alpha}
for every
(r,s)\in\R^{2}

and for some constants (not necessarily positive)
\underline{\alpha}\leq\overline{\alpha}
.
Furthermore, we assume the potential
F
to be given by (
2
) with

\lambda\geq 0
. For later convenience, we denote as
\beta

the “monotone part” of
F^{\prime}
(or, more precisely, of the

subdifferential

\partial F
). Namely, we set

\beta(r)=\ln(1+r)-\ln(1-r),\quad r\in(-1,1),

(9)

in such a way that
F^{\prime}(r)=\beta(r)-\lambda r
.

It is also worth detailing here our assumptions on the initial data:

Hypothesis 2.2
.

We assume the following conditions:

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}\in V,\qquad F(\varphi_{0})\in L^{1}(\Omega),\qquad\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\in(-1,1),

(10)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega),\quad\sigma_{0}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{a.e.\ in }\,\Omega,\quad\sigma_{0}\ln(1+\sigma_{0})\in L^{1}(\Omega),

(11)

\displaystyle\ln\sigma_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega),\quad(1+(\ln\sigma_{0})_{-})\ln(1+(\ln\sigma_{0})_{-})\in L^{1}(\Omega).

(12)

Remark 2.1
.

As the hypotheses on
\sigma_{0}
are a bit technical, some words of explanation
are in order. Basically, (
11
) specifies the “behavior at infinity”
of
\sigma_{0}
, which has to be “slightly better than
L^{1}
”. We will actually
prove that this uniform integrability property is maintained in time and
is very useful to get strong
L^{1}
-convergence. Relation (
12
)
has a similar role and in a sense specifies the “behavior at
0
” of
\sigma_{0}
.
Actually, as our entropy formulation is written in terms of the auxiliary variable

\ln\sigma
, condition (
12
), which is also maintained in
time, ensures some uniform integrability of
\ln\sigma
, which plays a role
when we take the limit in the entropy relation.

Remark 2.2
.

Considering the behavior of
\alpha
for large
\sigma
, it may be worth observing that,
in our results, we only need that the
positive
part of
\alpha
stays bounded; indeed,
a fast growth at infinity of the negative part of
\alpha
(e.g. as in the logistic
case (
4
)) would have a stabilizing effect and guarantee additional
a-priori summability. As that case has already been considered in detail
in
[
18
,
28
]
, we preferred to assume directly
\alpha
bounded,
which reduces a bit the technical complications in the proofs.

Recalling (cf. (
3
)) the energy functional associated
to system (
1
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\varphi,\sigma):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+F(\varphi)+\chi(1-\varphi)\sigma+\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(13)

we can now detail our notion of weak solution to system (
1
):

Definition 2.3
.

A triple
(\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
defined over
\Omega\times(0,T)

is called a weak solution to
the system (
1
), if it satisfies the regularity properties

\displaystyle\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;V),

(14a)

\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot,\cdot)\in(-1,1)\text{\ \ and }\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)>0\text{\ \ a.e.\penalty\ in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(14b)

\displaystyle\varphi\in\C_{w}([0,T];V)\cap H^{1}(0,T;V^{\prime}),

(14c)

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)),

(14d)

\displaystyle\ln\sigma\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)\cap\mathrm{BV}(0,T;(W^{1,p}(\Omega))^{*})\ \text{ where }p>d,

(14e)

\displaystyle\sigma\in\C_{w}{([0,T];L^{1}(\Omega))}\cap H^{1}(0,T;(W^{2,p}(\Omega))^{*})\ \text{ where }\,p>d,

(14f)

\displaystyle\sigma(\ln\sigma+1)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)),

(14g)

\displaystyle\beta(\varphi)\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi)|)\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)),\qquad\beta(\varphi)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)),

(14h)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sigma}\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))\in L^{2}(0,T;H),

(14i)

and the weak formulations

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,\psi\rangle\,+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\penalty\ in }(0,T),

(15a)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\sigma,\vartheta\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))\cdot\nabla\vartheta\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma\vartheta\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\penalty\ in }(0,T)

(15b)

are fulfilled for any
\psi\in V
and

\vartheta\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
,
p>d
,
together with the pointwise relation

\displaystyle\mu=-\Delta\varphi+F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\chi\sigma\quad\text{a.e.\penalty\ in }\,\Omega\times(0,T)

(15c)

and the additional boundary condition (in the sense of traces on
\partial\Omega
)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\varphi=0\quad\text{a.e.\penalty\ in }\,(0,T).

(15d)

Moreover, there exists a computable
number
\zeta\in[0,\infty)
, depending only on the assigned
data of the problem (and in particular on the initial data), such that

-\langle\ln\sigma,\theta\rangle\big|_{0}^{T}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla\ln\sigma|^{2}-\nabla\ln\sigma\cdot\nabla\theta-\chi\theta\nabla\ln\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi+\chi\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
+\|\theta_{-}\|_{\C(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ln\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\zeta\right]+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\theta+\ln\sigma\partial_{t}\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 0,

(15e)

for every
\theta\in\C^{1}([0,T];W^{1,p}(\Omega))
, where

Lemma 2.7

(Square-root Lipschitz continuity)
.

Let
\alpha\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
with the derivative
\alpha^{\prime}
being bounded
and such that

for all
r\geq 0

Then, there exists a constant

such that, for all

|\alpha(\theta)-\alpha(\eta)|\leq C|\sqrt{\theta}-\sqrt{\eta}|.

Moreover, there holds
that

for all
\theta\geq 0

Proof.

Assuming, with no loss of generality,
\eta<\theta
, applying
Taylor’s formula to the function
r\mapsto\kappa(r):=\alpha(r^{2})
,
we observe that there exists a point

\xi\in(\sqrt{\eta},\sqrt{\theta})
such that

\alpha(\theta)-\alpha(\eta)=\kappa(\sqrt{\theta})-\kappa(\sqrt{\eta})=2\xi\alpha^{\prime}(\xi^{2})(\sqrt{\theta}-\sqrt{\eta}).

Then, taking the absolute values and inserting the bound on the derivative of
\alpha

we find that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\sup_{\xi\geq 0}|2\xi\alpha^{\prime}(\xi^{2})|\leq C
,
which provides the first assertion.
Then, the second one follows simply by choosing
\eta=0

\varepsilon\searrow 0
so to get in the limit, up to taking a suitable subsequence,
a solution in the sense of Def.
2.3
.
In order for this argument to work, we need however to
reinforce a bit our assumptions on the regularized initial data
and to specify how they behave with respect to the regularization
parameter
\varepsilon
. Concerning
\varphi_{0}^{\varepsilon}
, as
the same condition (
10
) is required both in the statement
of Theorem
2.4
and in that of Theorem
4.1
,
there is nothing to modify and we can simply assume
\varphi_{0}^{\varepsilon}\equiv\varphi_{0}
,
independently of
\varepsilon
. Conversely,
in order to construct
\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon}
,
we preliminarly notice that, comparing (
35
) with
Hypothesis
2.2
, two differences
arise: the additional
summability required in (
35
) and the lack of the second
of (
12
) in the statement of Theorem
4.1
.
As already observed, the latter condition is required to prove the additional summability of
\sigma
close to
0
, a property
which was not observed in
[
30
]
, while the first of (
35
)
is necessary to obtain the parabolic
regularity (
39
) at the regularized level. Then,
starting from
\sigma_{0}
as in Hypothesis
2.2

and taking
\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\min(\sigma_{0},\varepsilon^{-1/2})
, it is readily seen
that the resulting family
\{\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\}
complies with (
35
).
More precisely, by the dominated convergence theorem, one has

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\to\sigma_{0}\quad\text{strongly in }\,L^{1}(\Omega),

(44)

\displaystyle(\ln\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{-}=(\ln\sigma_{0})_{-}\quad\text{for all }\,\varepsilon\in(0,1),

(45)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|\leq c,

(46)

with
c
independent of
\varepsilon
.
It is also worth observing that, due to the choice of the singular potential (
2
),
(
37
) implies in particular

|{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}(x,t)|<1\quad\text{for a.e.\penalty\ }\,(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)

(47)

and every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

Remark 4.1
.

The regularity proved in the above theorem appears to be optimal for what regards
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
;
actually, for the Cahn-Hilliard equation with logarithmic potential (
2
),
in three space dimensions, even for
C^{\infty}
initial data, it is not known whether
(
36
), (
37
) could be improved, at least as far as
global
in time
estimates are looked for. On the other hand, as observed in
[
30
, Rem. 2.6]
,
assuming better conditions (compared to (
35
)) on the initial datum,
it is rather simple to improve the regularity of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, provided that
the regularity of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
(on which depends the cross-diffusion term)
allows for that. Indeed, in the case of bounded sensitivity,
(
34b
) basically behaves as a standard parabolic equation
of quasi-linear type.
We also remark that the regularity properties
(
36
)-(
40
) refer to the three-dimensional case; for
d=2
,
a slightly improved statement holds; indeed, as already noted, the
“Sobolev” exponents
6
in (
36
),
(
37
) in 2D may be replaced by
any exponent
P\in[1,\infty)
.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
2.4
: a priori estimates

In this part we consider an
\varepsilon
-dependent family
(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})

of weak solutions to system (
34
), as provided by Theorem
4.1
,
and derive multiple
a priori
estimates, independent of
\varepsilon
,
in order to subsequently let
\varepsilon\searrow 0
and attain a solution to the
original system in the sense of Definition
2.3
.
We report the procedure in some detail since the argument provides
a good insight about the basic variational and structure properties of the system.

First of all, integrating (
34b
) in space and time, using
Hypothesis
2.1
and the comparison principle for ODE’s,
we immediately observe an estimate of the mass of
{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}
,
i.e.,

e^{\underline{\alpha}t}\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq e^{\overline{\alpha}t}\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }\,t\in[0,T].

Analogously, integration of the first (
34a
) provides the mass
conservation property

\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }\,t\in[0,T].

(48)

Additionally, we may observe that condition (
11
) and
a simple argument based on Stampacchia’s truncations method ensure that

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\geq 0\quad\text{for a.e.\penalty\ }\,(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

(49)

Next, in order to derive the energy-dissipation law, we multiply (
34b
) by

\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})
, add (
34a
)
1

multiplied by
{\mu_{\varepsilon}}
as well as (
34a
)
2
multiplied by
\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}
,
and integrate over
\Omega
. Then, we note that (
34b
) can be
equivalently rewritten as

\partial_{t}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-\di\Big[\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\big(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})\big)\Big]=\alpha({\varphi_{\varepsilon}},{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}){\sigma_{\varepsilon}},

(50)

from where, by simple calculations, we arrive at the estimate

\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\int_{\Omega}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}(\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}+F({\varphi_{\varepsilon}})+\chi{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
+\int_{\Omega}\frac{{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}}{1+\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}}\big|\nabla(\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}))\big|^{2}+|\nabla{\mu_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\leq\int_{\Omega}\alpha({\varphi_{\varepsilon}},{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}){\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\big(\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})\big)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(51)

Accordingly with the above relation, we introduce the approximate energy as

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon}):=\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+F(\varphi_{\varepsilon})+\chi\sigma_{\varepsilon}(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(52)

Then, it is worth observing that there exist constants
\kappa>0
and
C_{0}>0
, independent
of
\varepsilon
, such that the
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}
enjoys the following uniform
coercivity property:

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\geq\kappa\Big(\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}\ln(1+\sigma_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|_{V}^{2}+\|F(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1\|^{2}\Big)-C_{0}.

(53)

Note in particular that
C_{0}
depends on the assigned parameters
\lambda
and
\chi
.
With similar arguments one can also deduce the boundedness condition

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\leq c\Big(\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}\ln(1+\sigma_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|_{V}^{2}+\|F(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1\|^{2}+1\Big).

(54)

Here and below,
c>0
denotes a generic positive constant depending only on the
assigned problem data and independent of the approximation parameter
\varepsilon
.
Note that (
53
) and (
54
) are based on Hypothesis
2.1

and on relation (
47
), which, in turn, depends in an essential way
on the fact that the logarithmic potential (
2
) is
not
regularized.

Next, it is not difficult to prove the following control
of the right-hand side of (
51
):

\int_{\Omega}\alpha({\varphi_{\varepsilon}},{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}){\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\big(\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq c\big(1+\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\big).

(55)

Hence, thanks to Grönwall’s lemma,
(
51
) entails the following a priori bounds:

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;V)}+\|F(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(56)

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}\ln(1+\sigma_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(57)

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}^{1/2}}{(1+\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}})^{1/2}}\nabla\big(\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})\big)\Big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}\leq c,

(58)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}\leq c,

(59)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H)}\leq c.

(60)

Note in particular that, to control the energy at
t=0

uniformly in
\varepsilon
, we have used that the approximating initial
data are designed so to satisfy (
44
)-(
46
).

To proceed, we test the second (
34a
) by
\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0}
,
where we have set
m_{0}:=|\Omega|^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x=|\Omega|^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x
,
where the second inequality follows from the mass conservation (
48
) and the
fact that
\varphi_{0}
is taken independent of
\varepsilon
(namely, we did not need
to regularize it). Then, we obtain

\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}+\chi\sigma_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x.

(61)

Next, we observe that the following relation:

\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\kappa\|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-c,

(62)

holds for suitable constants
\kappa>0
and
c\geq 0
independent of
\varepsilon

(but depending on the datum
m_{0}
),
where we recall that
\beta
denotes the “monotone part” of
F^{\prime}
.
Actually, to to prove the above, one can proceed as in
[
27
]

to which we refer the reader for details. Concerning the last integral on the
right-hand side of (
61
), we first observe that, by the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality,
there follows

\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mu_{\varepsilon}-|\Omega|^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq c\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|\leq c\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|,

(63)

the last inequality following from (
56
). Next, by (
47
)
and (
57
),

\int_{\Omega}\chi\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq c\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq c.

(64)

Replacing (
62
)-(
64
) into (
61
)
and squaring the resulting relation,
it is then not difficult to deduce the additional bounds
(see also
[
30
, Sec. 3]
for further details)

\displaystyle\|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}\leq c,

(65)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V^{\prime})}\leq c.

(66)

The bounds derived so far depend directly on the fundamental physical balance
laws and basically coincide with those proved in
[
30
]
. The subsequent
part of the proof instead is totally new as we now look for estimates
in a weaker regularity setting, which corresponds, roughly speaking, to the
Orlicz space usually denoted as
L\log L
(see, e.g.,
[
10
]
).

To this aim, we may test the second of (
34a
) by

\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon}):=\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)\sign(\varphi_{\varepsilon})
,
which is an admissible test function because,
thanks to the second of (
37
),
\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\in L^{P}(\Omega\times(0,T))
for
every
P\in[1,\infty)
. Note also that
the second of (
34a
) holds (at least)
as a relation in
L^{2}(0,T;H)
and that, thanks to the fact
\beta(0)=0
,

\rho
is a monotone function. Now, a well-known integration by parts formula
for (possibly singular) monotone operators gives

-\int_{\Omega}\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.

(67)

The above can be proved for instance replacing
\rho
with its Yosida
approximation (see,
[
3
,
5
]
), proving the formula at the regularized
level, and then taking the limit (see also
[
31
, Lemma 2.4]
for a similar
argument).
Hence, based on the above considerations, the procedure implies

\int_{\Omega}\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\varphi_{\varepsilon})\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x+\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{\varepsilon}\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x.

(68)

Then, in order to control the right-hand side, we first notice that

\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\varphi_{\varepsilon})\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\|\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|^{2}.

(69)

Moreover, it is clear that

\|\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\ln^{2}(1+|\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\,\mathrm{d}x+c,

(70)

with
c
independent of
\varepsilon
. The control of the last term in (
68
)
is a bit more delicate. To get it, we need to observe that
\psi(r)=e^{r}
,
r\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\psi^{*}(s)=s(\ln s-1)
,
s\geq 0
(in fact
\psi^{*}
may be intended to be
identically
+\infty
for
s<0
) are convex conjugate functions. Hence, we may apply the
Fenchel-Young inequality, which gives (recall that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
a.e.)

\displaystyle\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{\varepsilon}\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\chi\int_{\Omega}\psi^{*}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x+\chi\int_{\Omega}\psi(|\rho(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)\,\mathrm{d}x+\chi\int_{\Omega}(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{\varepsilon}\ln(1+\sigma_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\,\mathrm{d}x+c,

(71)

where the last inequality follows from elementary considerations,
similarly with (
70
).
Replacing (
69
)-(
71
) into (
68
), we then obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{\varepsilon}\ln(1+\sigma_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}x+c,

(72)

whence, integrating in time and recalling (
56
)-(
57
) and (
65
),
we deduce the additional bound

\|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\ln(1+|\beta(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|)\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(73)

Next, in order to derive an entropy estimate (which, in turn, helps us to write
a weak formulation of (
34b
)
suitable for taking the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0
),
we multiply (
34b
) by
\frac{1}{{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\delta}
for
\delta>0
.
Actually, as
\delta>0
is fixed, the function
(\cdot+\delta)^{-1}
is bounded and
globally Lipschitz over
[0,+\infty)
; hence, recalling that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\geq 0

almost everywhere, this procedure is admissible. It is then easy to infer

\displaystyle\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)-\Delta\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)-|\nabla\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)|^{2}-\chi\dive\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)(1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon})}\nabla(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\bigg)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -\chi\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)^{2}(1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon})}\nabla\sigma_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})=\frac{\alpha(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta}.

(74)

Note that the chain rule formulas
used to derive the above relation are apparently
formal, but could easily be made rigorous. Actually, as observed in Remark
4.1
,
the regularity (
39
) is not optimal, and, possibly smoothing out the
initial datum, better conditions could be proved at the
level
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For instance, the chain rule formula used to deduce

(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)^{-1}\partial_{t}\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{t}\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)

is rigorous as far as
\partial_{t}\sigma_{\varepsilon}
lies, say, in
L^{2}
. This is not part of
(
39
), but, as said, it can be shown to hold if
\sigma_{0}^{\varepsilon}
is
smoother (we omit details for brevity).

To derive an additional estimate, we multiply (
74
) by
-1

and integrate over
\Omega
. Using the no-flux boundary conditions
(
34c
) and noticing that

\chi\bigg|\int_{\Omega}\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}\nabla\sigma_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})}{(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)^{2}(1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon})}\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\|^{2}+c\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|^{2},

(75)

with
c
being independent both of
\varepsilon
and of
\delta
, noting also that
the control of the right-hand side of (
74
) is straightforward
thanks to the uniform boundedness of
\alpha
, we
readily obtain the bounds

\|\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}\leq c.

(76)

Next, we consider a test function
\theta\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, where
p>d
so that
we can take advantage of the continuous embedding

W^{1,p}(\Omega)\subset C^{0}({\overline{\Omega}})
,
and multiply (
74
) by
\theta
. Then, simple considerations
permit us to deduce that

\|\partial_{t}\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\|_{L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega)^{\prime})}\leq c,

(77)

where we point out once more that the constants
c
occurring, in particular, in
(
76
)-(
77
) are independent of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
.
Finally, in order to derive the last
a priori
estimate,
we may multiply (
34b
) by
\varepsilon^{2}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}
and
integrate over
\Omega
in order to observe

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\|{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\|\nabla{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\chi\frac{\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}}{1+\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}}\nabla{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\varepsilon\nabla{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\alpha({\varphi_{\varepsilon}},{\sigma_{\varepsilon}})\varepsilon^{2}|{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\chi^{2}}{2}\|{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\|\nabla{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+\overline{\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}\|{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}.

(78)

Hence, using also (
46
),
Grönwall’s lemma allows us to deduce the bound

\varepsilon\|{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}\leq c\,.

(79)

Now, we aim to show that we can let
\delta\searrow 0
in (
74
).
In particular, we claim that

\ln(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\to\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{strongly in }\,L^{2}(0,T;V).

(80)

To prove this fact, we notice that, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, a.e. on the set
\Omega_{n}=\Omega_{n}(t):=\{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)>1/n\}
, one has

|\nabla\ln({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\delta)|^{2}=\frac{|\nabla{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}}{({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\delta)^{2}}\leq\frac{|\nabla{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}}{{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}^{2}}=|\nabla\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}.

(81)

Indeed, as far as its argument stays larger than
n^{-1}
, the function

\ln
is Lipschitz; hence the above follows from
standard chain rule formulas in Sobolev spaces.
On the other hand, as
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)>0
almost everywhere in
\Omega

(and for all
t\in[0,T]
with a possible exception of a set of zero
measure), (
81
) holds on the union, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of the sets
\Omega_{n}
,
which coincides with
\Omega
up to a set of zero measure. Hence,
integrating (
81
) in space and time, one readily deduces

\displaystyle\|\nabla\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\delta\searrow 0}\|\nabla\ln({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\delta)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}^{2}

(82)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\delta\searrow 0}\|\nabla\ln({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\delta)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}^{2}\leq\|\nabla\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}^{2},

where the first inequality follows from the point-wise a.e. convergence and Fatou’s lemma. This
gives the desired (
80
).
As a consequence, it is easy to realize that
one can take the limit
\delta\searrow 0
in (
74
) so to
deduce

\displaystyle\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\Delta\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-|\nabla\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-\chi\dive\Big(\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -\chi\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})=\alpha(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon}).

(83)

Note that, as a byproduct of the argument, we have improved the information
(
49
) on the sign properties of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, which turns out to be

strictly
positive almost everywhere.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
2.4
: passage to the limit

Our next task consists in taking the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, and, to
this purpose, we start considering relation (
34b
). First of all, we observe
that from (
56
), (
65
)-(
66
), and the Aubin–Lions lemma, there follows that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}\to\varphi\quad\text{weakly star in }\,H^{1}(0,T;V^{\prime})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and strongly in }\,L^{\infty}(0,T;H),

(84)

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}\to\mu\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;V).

(85)

Moreover, noting that the analogue of (
76
)-(
77
) holds
for
\delta=0
thanks to semicontinuity of norms with respect to weak or
weak star convergence, one also has

\displaystyle\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\ell\quad\text{weakly star in }\,BV(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega)^{\prime})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V),\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{strongly in }\,L^{2}(0,T;H),

(86)

for a suitable limit function
\ell
. Here and below, it is intended that all
convergence relations hold for a suitable (nonrelabelled) subsequence
of
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Note also that we applied above a generalized version of the
Aubin–Lions lemma (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Corollary 7.9]
or
[
33
]
).

As in particular a.e. convergence holds
in
Q
, the equintegrability condition (
57
) readily implies

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}=e^{\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\to e^{\ell}=:\sigma\quad\text{strongly in }\,L^{P}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)).

(87)

Here and below,
P
will be a generic exponent such that
P\in[1,\infty)
.
In the sequel, we will mostly privilege the notation
\sigma
and
write
\ln\sigma
in place of
\ell
. From (
86
)
we obtain in particular that
\sigma>0
a.e. in
Q
.
Now, (
87
) also implies that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{1/2}\to\sigma^{1/2}\quad\text{weakly star in }\,L^{\infty}(0,T;H)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and strongly in }\,L^{P}(0,T;H).

(88)

Similarly, we claim that

\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{1/2}}{(1+\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}})^{1/2}}\to\sigma^{1/2}\quad\text{weakly star in }\,L^{\infty}(0,T;H)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and strongly in }\,L^{P}(0,T;H).

(89)

To prove the above, one may notice that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{1/2}}{(1+\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}})^{1/2}}-\sigma^{1/2}\right|

4.4
Proof of Theorem

To conclude the proof of Theorem

To this aim, we first observe that, from (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\nabla\ln\sigma\quad\text{weakly in }\,L^{2}(0,T;H).

(114)

Hence, recalling (

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\to\nabla\ln\sigma\cdot\nabla(1-\varphi)\quad\text{weakly in }\,L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)).

Let us now go back to the regularized version (

) of the entropy inequality.
Then, in order to convert it into an integral relation, we multiply it by (minus) a test
function
\theta\in C^{1}([0,T];W^{1,p}(\Omega))
,

.
Integrating by parts some terms and performing standard manipulations, setting also

\ell_{\varepsilon}=\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}
for simplicity, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\ell_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\Omega}\ell_{\varepsilon}(T)\theta(T)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\theta_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ =-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\Omega}\ell_{\varepsilon}(0)\theta(0)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\theta_{-}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

In principle, we would like to take the
\limsup
, as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
,
in the above relation. However, in order to manage the last term, we first
need to add and simultaneously subtract from the right-hand side the quantity

\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

The presence of such a correction term is what actually permits us to take the

. In particular, we may observe that, thanks to (

),
(

), (

(and not just

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell|^{2}\theta_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\theta_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\theta_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

(118)

by weakly lower semicontinuity. Some more words need to be spent on
the relation

\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}-\int_{\Omega}\ell_{\varepsilon}(T)\theta(T)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\langle\ell(T),\theta(T)\rangle.

To obtain it, we first observe that, as

for

(see, e.g.,

we may complement (

\ell_{\varepsilon}(t)\to\ell(t)\quad\text{weakly star in }\,W^{1,p}(\Omega)^{\prime}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for {\bf every} }\,t\in[0,T],

(120)

which in particular entails (
119
).

Moving to the terms on the right-hand side of (

),
it is apparent that the first two integrals converge to the expected limits
(in particular, for the integral depending on the initial datum, the dominated convergence
theorem is used). Hence, we only need to take care of the contributions quadratically

\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}
. Namely, recalling that we added and subtracted the

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\big(\theta_{-}-\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\big(\theta_{-}-\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ =-\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\big(\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}-\theta_{-}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell|^{2}\big(\theta_{-}-\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Concerning the latter quantity in (

\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ell_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\zeta_{\varepsilon},

with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\ell\partial_{t}\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle\ell(T),\theta(T)\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla\ell|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\ell\cdot\nabla\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

Next, we move to the energy inequality, which, rewritten in the integral form,

expressed in the statement of Theorem

. To obtain it,

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}+K

is chosen such that

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
(note also that

\varepsilon

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}}{1+\varepsilon{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}}\big|\nabla(\ln{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}-1)+\chi(1-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}))\big|^{2}+|\nabla{\mu_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq M_{0}(T)\quad\text{for every }\,t\in[0,T],

As before, we will consider the

and will get a a correction term in the limit. This is due to the fact that, on the one hand, for the

has no sign properties; hence, the corresponding

To detail our argument, we first refine the control of
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}

the integrand in
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}r_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon,1}

\displaystyle:=\alpha(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\big(\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}+1\big),

(128)

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon,2}

\displaystyle:=\alpha(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})\big(\sigma_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\chi\sigma_{\varepsilon}(1-\varphi_{\varepsilon})-1\big).

, as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, of the above relation.
Then, setting

\mathcal{D}:=\int_{\Omega}\sigma\big|\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))\big|^{2}+|\nabla\mu|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t):=\mathcal{E}(t)+K

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(t),

(132)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\Big(-\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)

\displaystyle=-\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\ \leq-\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{D}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

Indeed, using (

100
) and (

), (

It remains to manage the last two integral terms in (

)-(

for every

From the above, we readily obtain

is non-increasing. More precisely,
noting that, for any finite partition

[0,T]
, there holds

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})-\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(t_{k-1})|\leq|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(T)-\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(0)|+2{\tilde{c}}T,

(135)

with
{\tilde{c}}
as in (
134
), recalling also that
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is
bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T)
independently of
\varepsilon
(cf. (
127
)),
we immediately deduce that the total variation of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded
with respect to
\varepsilon
.

As a consequence, applying once more Helly’s selection principle, we may assume that,
for
every

s\in[0,T]
, there exists the
\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(s)
.
Next, using (
96
) together with (
87
) and (
100
), we
readily obtain

r_{\varepsilon,2}\to\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\big(\sigma+\chi\sigma(1-\varphi)-1\big)=:r_{2}\quad\text{say, weakly in }\,L^{1}(Q).

(136)

In order to deal with the term
r_{\varepsilon,1}
, we first observe that, if

{\overline{\alpha}}\leq 0
(and consequently
\alpha
is nonpositive), then
we may directly conclude that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}r_{\varepsilon,1}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\big(\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(137)

by means of Fatou’s lemma. Hence, in this case, we obtain the
energy inequality with no need for correction terms.

Conversely, let us
assume that
{\overline{\alpha}}>0
. In this case, we may decompose

r_{\varepsilon,1}
as follows:

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon,1}

\displaystyle=(\alpha(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})-{\overline{\alpha}})\big(\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}+1\big)+{\overline{\alpha}}\big(\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}+1\big)

\displaystyle=:r_{\varepsilon,11}+r_{\varepsilon,12}.

(138)

Noting that
r_{\varepsilon,11}\leq 0
almost everywhere and applying Fatou’s lemma, we then deduce

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}r_{\varepsilon,11}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=-\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(-r_{\varepsilon,11})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)-{\overline{\alpha}})\big(\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(139)

Now, defining
Z_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}r_{\varepsilon,12}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t
,
we deduce that
\{Z_{\varepsilon}\}
is a bounded sequence of real-valued numbers such that
Z_{\varepsilon}\to Z\geq 0
at least for a subsequence.
From the a-priori estimates (
57
) and (
60
) it can be observed
that these numbers are bounded by the assigned problem parameters and initial values. We then conclude that

\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}r_{\varepsilon,12}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau={\overline{\alpha}}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\big(\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\ln\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}+1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq{\overline{\alpha}}Z_{\varepsilon},

(140)

for every
0\leq s<t\leq T
. Of course, by Fatou’s lemma, the above also implies

{\overline{\alpha}}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\big(\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq{\overline{\alpha}}Z.

(141)

Hence, collecting the above considerations and taking the
\liminf
of (
130
),
we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(s)-\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{D}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\big(\sigma\ln\sigma+\chi\sigma(1-\varphi)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +{\overline{\alpha}}\Big(Z-\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\big(\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Big),

(142)

where we point out that the last integral is nonnegative in view of (
141
).
In general, we are not able to prove that, for every (or even for a.e.)
s\in(0,T)
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(s)=\mathcal{F}(s)
. However, on account of
(
44
)-(
46
), using also the dominated convergence theorem,
one can easily prove that this holds at least for
s=0
.
Hence, replacing
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathcal{E}
and rearranging terms, it is immediate
to get (
15f
), which concludes the proof of the theorem.

Remark 4.2
.

We point out that, if we denote as
E(t)
the limit as
\varepsilon\searrow 0

of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(t)
, which exists for every

thanks to Helly’s theorem,
then the “corrected” energy functional
E(t)
satisfies (by semicontinuity)

\mathcal{F}(t)\leq E(t)
for every
t\in[0,T]
; moreover,
E
satisfies the following
alternative version of the energy inequality (
142
):

\displaystyle E(t)+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{D}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq E(s)+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\big(\sigma\ln\sigma+\chi\sigma(1-\varphi)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +{\overline{\alpha}}\Big(\int_{s}^{t}E(\tau)-\mathcal{F}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Big),\quad\text{for {\bf every} }\,0\leq s<t\leq T.

(143)

4.5
Weak sequential stability of the solution set

As observed in Remark
3.1
, we designed the solution concept
(including, in particular, the entropy (
15e
) and energy (
15f
)
inequalities in the formulation), so to guarantee the weak sequential stability of
the solution set. This is an important property,
for instance for selection principles or further coupling of the model and, roughly speaking,
corresponds to the fact that any cluster point of any “bounded”
family of weak solutions (in the sense of Def.
2.3
,
hence including the entropy and energy relations) is a weak solution again.
This fact is made precise by the following result:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\{(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n})\}
be a sequence of solutions
in the sense of Definition
2.3
, all
emanating from the same initial datum
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0})
under
Hypothesis
2.2
. Let
\{(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n})\}
satisfy
the assumptions of Theorem
2.4
, including
the entropy and energy inequalities (
15e
) and (
15f
),
with the (bounded) functions
\{\zeta_{n}\}
and
\{Z_{n}\}
therein being bounded by the
assigned problem parameters, hence independently of
n
. Moreover,
let us assume that, for some
c>0
independent of
n
, there holds
the additional estimate

\big\||\ln\sigma_{n}|\ln(1+|\ln\sigma_{n}|)\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(144)

Then, every cluster point of
\{(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n})\}

is still a weak solution in the sense of Definition
2.3

fulfilling in particular the regularity conditions
(
14a
)-(
14i
).

Remark 4.3
.

It is worth clarifying a bit the meaning of the statement, which is somehow
delicate as uniqueness is lacking and many weak solutions in the regularity
class specified by Def.
2.3
may emanate from a single initial
datum. What we can show is that, if a family of such solutions also satisfies the entropy and energy
relations uniformly with respect to
n
and with the functions
\zeta_{n}
and
Z_{n}

being bounded by the assigned system parameters,
then such a family is weakly sequentially
stable, and in particular it also fulfills, uniformly in
n
, all the a-priori
estimates corresponding to the regularity properties (
14a
)-(
14i
).
This does not exclude that from the same initial datum there may also emanate
other “bad” or “unphysical” weak solutions that do not satisfy the
entropy and energy inequalities in the quantitative way prescribed
by the statement.
Moreover, the additional assumed estimate (
144
) is not required for
inferring the formulations of Definition
2.3
but merely the
first regularity statement of (
14e
),
i.e.,

\ln\sigma\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Let us assume that a triple
(\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
arises as a cluster point, i.e.,
is a limit, in a suitable sense, of a (nonrelabelled) subsequence of

\{(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n})\}
. We shall then prove that this “cluster”
triplet still verifies all the conditions in Def.
2.3
.
To this aim, we first observe that the mass balance properties at the
level
n
can be deduced by choosing
\psi=1
in (
15a
)
and, respectively,
\vartheta\equiv 1
in (
15b
); it is
also apparent that these conditions are maintained as we let
(the subsequence of)
n
go to
\infty
.

Next, as the entropy and energy inequalities (
15e
) and (
15f
)
hold uniformly with respect to
n
, we can recover basically
all the a priori estimates corresponding to the regularity properties
(
14a
)-(
14i
), with the notable exception
of the last of (
14e
), which will be discussed in a while.

Actually, one first uses the energy estimate (
15f
), which, adapting
some regularity arguments from the previous section, provides most of the
information. Then, choosing
\theta=1
in (
15e
) one deduces
a priori estimates corresponding to the first two controls in (
14e
).
To be precise, in order to get that

\|\ln\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(145)

the entropy inequality should be stated on a generic subinterval
(0,t)

(which is possible, as is observed in Remark
2.3
).
Note, however, that (
145
) alone does not exclude that
the limit
\ln\sigma
could exhibit concentration phenomena
(i.e., be a non-absolutely continuous measure) with respect to space
variables.

As mentioned, obtaining the last estimate in (
14e
) is more
delicate as the BV-regularity of
\ln\sigma_{n}
does not allow, at
least directly, to integrate by parts back the corresponding terms in
(
15e
) so to get an estimate of the time derivative.
To overcome this issue, we take advantage of the results proven in
Section
3
. In particular, using Lemma
3.3
, we infer
a measure-valued formulation which is equivalent to (
15e
). Indeed,
from the lemma, we deduce the existence of a family of
measures
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\N}\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])

such that

-\int_{\Omega}\ln\sigma_{n}\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\Big|_{0}^{T}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}|^{2}-\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}\cdot\nabla\theta-\chi\theta\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}+\chi\nabla\varphi_{n}\cdot\nabla\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\xi_{n}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi_{n},\sigma_{n})\theta-\ln\sigma_{n}\partial_{t}\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

(146)

for all
\theta\in\C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T];\R)
with

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\xi_{n}(t,x)\leq\|\theta_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\left[\zeta-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right],

where
\zeta
is independent of
n
as the initial data
are fixed.
Now, a comparison in (
146
) allows us to find an estimate for the
time derivatives of
\{\ln\sigma_{n}\}
via

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}\ln\sigma_{n},\theta\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t:=-\langle\ln\sigma_{n},\theta\rangle\Big|_{0}^{T}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\ln\sigma_{n}\partial_{t}\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}|^{2}-\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}\cdot\nabla\theta-\chi\theta\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}+\chi\nabla\varphi_{n}\cdot\nabla\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\xi_{n}(t,x)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi_{n},\sigma_{n})\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(1+\chi)|\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}|^{2}+\chi|\nabla\varphi_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\|\xi_{n}\|_{\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])}+\bar{\alpha}T|\Omega|\right]\|\theta\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\left[\|\nabla\ln\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}+\chi T\|\nabla\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H)}\right]\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}.

The above relation implies that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\ln\sigma_{n}\|_{\mathcal{M}([0,T];(W^{1,p}(\Omega))^{*})}\leq C\quad\text{for }\,p>d.

with
c>0
independent of
n
.

Having now all the a-priori estimates corresponding to (
14a
)-(
14i
)
at our disposal, we now see that it is possible to pass to the limit
as (a subsequence of)
n
goes to
\infty
and obtain
that the “limit” triplet
(\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
is still a weak solution
in the sense of Def.
2.3
.
Actually, most of the procedure closely follow the arguments
performed in the previous section for taking the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0

in the approximation scheme, and some parts of the argument
are, in fact, even simpler as here we do not need to take care of
the technical complications involving the nonlinear sensitivity.

For this reason, we will just focus on the parts which involve some novelty.
First of all, we observe that the uniform integrability of (the negative
part of)
\ln\sigma_{n}
, i.e., condition (
144
),
is
not
a consequence of the energy and entropy relations.
Moreover, it cannot be reproduced (at least in a rigorous way) without referring
to a suitable regularization. Actually, the test function

\gamma(\ln\sigma_{n})
used before (where

\gamma
is defined in (
103
)), cannot be inserted in
relation (
15e
) due to its poor regularity properties.
This is why (
144
) is taken as an additional assumption
in the statement of Prop.
4.2
. Using (
144
),
we can actually deduce the analogue of (
113
),
which improves (
145
) and excludes that the limit of

\ln\sigma_{n}
could exhibit concentration phenomena.

With this information at disposal it is apparent that we can take
the limit in equations (
15a
)-(
15c
) and in the
initial and boundary conditions (
15d
) as well. Hence,
to conclude the proof,
it is sufficient to show that also the entropy and energy inequalities
(
15e
) and (
15f
) are stable with respect to
n\nearrow\infty
,
or, in other words, that they are still satisfied in the limit by the “cluster”
solution
(\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
.

Starting with the energy inequality (
15f
) (where we recall that the
real-valued numbers
Z_{n}
are bounded independent of
n
as the initial data are fixed),
it is easy to see that the estimates obtained before and the very same semicontinuity
argument performed in Subsec.
4.4
imply that it is possible to take

n\nearrow\infty
therein and obtain an analogous inequality in the limit.
Finally, we need to show that the entropy inequality is also stable with
respect to
n
. Therefore, we again observe that the sequence
\{\zeta_{n}\}

is a sequence of bounded numbers such that
\zeta_{n}\to\zeta
for a
suitable subsequence. The limit passage in inequality (
15e
)
follows now in the same way as in Subsection
4.3
.
∎

5
Existence of local-in-time strong solutions

In this part, we prove Theorem
2.5
. Before that, however, we prefer to give a number of
remarks aimed at clarifying the “spirit” of the local existence result.

Remark 5.1
.

In the statement we implicitly assumed
d=3
,
and, for simplicity, also the proof will be carried out in
the three-dimensional setting. As already noticed, the
only notable difference in the case
d=2
is represented
by the (better) Sobolev exponents.

Remark 5.2
.

It is worth observing that the regularity properties
(
19
)-(
21
),
as well as the underlying assumption (
17
), correspond,
in the Cahn-Hilliard terminology, to the outcome of the so-called “second energy estimate”,
a procedure which is fully compatible with the singular potential (
2
) and which,
as first observed in
[
27
]
, provides the maximal regularity available
so far for this type of system. Indeed, the main obstacle preventing the validity
of a
global
regularity estimate comes from the supercritical
character of the quadratic cross diffusion term in
(
1b
) and not from the structure of system (
1a
).

Remark 5.3
.

As far as one looks for a
local in time
result, even in presence of
a singular potential of the form (
2
), it is still possible to
further improve the regularity of
\varphi
, provided that the initial
datum satisfies the following “separation property”:

-1+\delta\leq\varphi_{0}(x)\leq 1-\delta\quad\text{for every }\,x\in\Omega\ \ \text{and some }\,\delta\in(0,1/2).

(147)

Actually, from the second of (
19
), one may deduce

\|\varphi(t)-\varphi_{0}\|_{V}\leq t^{1/2}\|\varphi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};V)}\leq C_{0}t^{1/2},

(148)

for every
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, where the constant
C_{0}
only depends on the

L^{2}(0,T_{0};V)
-norm of
\varphi_{t}
. Combining the above with
the third of (
19
) and using interpolation it
is not difficult to infer

\|\varphi(t)-\varphi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}t^{\eta},

(149)

where
\eta\in(0,1/2)
is a computable exponent and
C_{1}

only depends on known quantities. Recalling
(
147
), we then readily deduce

-1+\delta/2\leq\varphi(x,t)\leq 1-\delta/2\quad\text{for every }\,(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T_{1}],

(150)

where
T_{1}\in(0,T_{0}]
is a computable time. Once the above
“local separation property” is achieved,
F^{\prime}
basically
loses its singular character and can be treated as a “smooth”
function, which essentially implies that, over
(0,T_{1})
, the regularity
of the local solution might be improved at will, provided of course
that the initial data are taken smooth enough.

Proof.

In the following part, we sketch the proof of Theorem
2.5
. For the
sake of brevity, we shall derive a number of additional a-priori estimates
by working directly on the equations of system (
1
)
without referring to any specific regularization or approximation scheme. We actually
believe that such a procedure, though formal, is more suitable for emphasizing
the real difficulties of the procedure without insisting on technicalities.
We also implicitly assume that the a-priori estimates already proved for
weak solutions keep holding in the current setting, and we will use them
whenever needed.

That said, we start with testing (
1b
) by
\sigma
so to obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\|\sigma\|^{2}+\|\nabla\sigma\|^{2}=\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(151)

In order to control the first term on the right-hand side, we
may observe that

\displaystyle\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq c\|\sigma\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\sigma\|\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c\|\sigma\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{3/4}\|\sigma\|^{1/4}\|\nabla\sigma\|\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\|\varphi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq c\|\sigma\|_{V}^{7/4}\|\sigma\|^{1/4}\|\varphi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/2},

(152)

where we have used elementary interpolation of
L^{p}
-spaces and a well-known
Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality. In order to control the last term, the simplest
way consists in testing the second of (
1a
) by
-\Delta\varphi
so to
obtain

\|\Delta\varphi\|^{2}\leq\lambda\|\nabla\varphi\|^{2}-\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x,

(153)

where we have integrated by parts the semilinear term and used the
\lambda
-monotonicity
of
F^{\prime}
(we have already observed that this argument works in spite of the “nonsmoothness”
of
F^{\prime}
). Hence, using (
14c
) and well-known elliptic regularity results,
it is not difficult to deduce

\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c\big(1+\|\Delta\varphi\|^{2}\big)\leq c\big(1+\|\sigma\|^{2}\big)+c\|\nabla\mu\|.

(154)

Replacing the above into (
152
), we obtain

\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\leq c\|\sigma\|_{V}^{7/4}\|\sigma\|^{1/4}\big(1+\|\sigma\|^{1/2}+\|\nabla\mu\|^{1/4}\big).

(155)

Plugging this relation into (
151
), employing the boundedness of
\alpha

(cf. Hypothesis
2.1
), and adding
\|\sigma\|^{2}
to both-hand sides
so to recover the full
V
-norm on the left-hand side, we deduce

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\|\sigma\|^{2}+\|\sigma\|^{2}_{V}\leq c\|\sigma\|_{V}^{7/4}\|\sigma\|^{1/4}\big(1+\|\sigma\|^{1/2}+\|\nabla\mu\|^{1/4}\big)+c\|\sigma\|^{2}.

(156)

Let us now set
2Y(t):=\|\sigma(t)\|^{2}
,

m(t):=\|\nabla\mu(t)\|^{2}
and notice that, rearranging
terms, (
156
) implies the following differential inequality:

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}(t)+\|\sigma(t)\|^{2}_{V}

\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \leq c\big(\|\sigma(t)\|_{V}^{7/4}Y(t)^{1/8}+\|\sigma(t)\|_{V}^{7/4}Y(t)^{3/8}+\|\sigma(t)\|_{V}^{7/4}Y(t)^{1/8}m(t)^{1/8}+Y(t)\big).

(157)

Then, applying Young’s inequality, we infer

Y^{\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma(t)\|^{2}_{V}\leq c\big(1+Y^{3}(t)\big)+\tilde{c}m(t)Y(t),

(158)

where
\tilde{c}>0
behaves like the generic
c
. Let us now set

M(t):=\tilde{c}\int_{0}^{t}m(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

(159)

and observe that, by (
14a
),

\kappa\leq e^{-M(t)}\leq 1\quad\text{for all }\,t\in[0,T]

(160)

and for some
\kappa\in(0,1]
depending only on the problem data.
Then, setting
Z(t):=e^{-M(t)}Y(t)
, it is not difficult to deduce
from (
158
) that

Z^{\prime}(t)\leq c(1+Z^{3}(t)).

(161)

Applying the comparison principle for ODE’s, we then deduce
that for a suitable, and computable,
T_{0}\in(0,T]
depending on the problem data,
there holds

\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};H)}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};V)}\leq c,

(162)

where the second estimate also considers the dissipative contribution
on the left-hand side of (
158
). The above information
also has some effect on the regularity of
\varphi
. Indeed, going
back to the second (
1a
), we may interpret it as a time-dependent
family of elliptic problems; namely, we have

-\Delta\varphi(t)+\beta(\varphi(t))=h(t):=\lambda\varphi(t)+\mu(t)+\chi\sigma(t),

(163)

where, by Sobolev’s embeddings, it is a standard matter
to verify that
h\in L^{2}(0,T_{0};L^{6}(\Omega))
(with the exponent

6
replaced by any
P\in[1,\infty)
in the two-dimensional case).
Then, a well-known elliptic regularity argument implies

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};W^{2,6}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(164)

To be more precise, the above estimate is obtained by (formally)
testing the second (
1a
) by
\beta(\varphi)^{5}
,
which is a monotone function of its argument as a direct check shows,
so that we can use in particular the analogue of (
67
).
Then, the procedure implies
that

\|\beta(\varphi)\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};L^{6}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(165)

Then, with the above information at hand, regularity results of Agmon-Douglis-Nirenberg
type readily entail (
164
). This relation, in turn, can help improve the
regularity of
\sigma
. Indeed, we may rewrite (
1b
) in the form

\sigma_{t}-\Delta\sigma=\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma-\chi\sigma\Delta\varphi-\chi\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi,

(166)

and we may apply suitable regularity results to the above equation.
In particular, we perform an additional “parabolic” estimate
by testing (
166
) by
\sigma_{t}
. Simple calculations then yield

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\|\nabla\sigma\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}x-\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{t}\sigma\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x-\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{t}\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

(167)

Thanks to Sobolev’s embeddings, the
terms on the right-hand side can be controlled as follows:

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq c\|\sigma\|\|\sigma_{t}\|\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\sigma_{t}\|^{2}+c\|\sigma\|^{2},

(168)

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq c\|\sigma\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\sigma_{t}\|\|\Delta\varphi\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\sigma_{t}\|^{2}+c\|\sigma\|_{V}^{2}\|\Delta\varphi\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2},

(169)

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq c\|\sigma_{t}\|\|\nabla\sigma\|\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\sigma_{t}\|^{2}+c\|\nabla\sigma\|^{2}\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,6}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(170)

Collecting the above computations, (
167
) gives

\frac{1}{4}\|\sigma_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\|\nabla\sigma\|^{2}\leq c\|\sigma\|^{2}+c\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,6}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\sigma\|_{V}^{2}.

(171)

Then, adding to both sides of the above relation the
inequality

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\|\sigma\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{8}\|\sigma_{t}\|^{2}+c\|\sigma\|^{2},

(172)

so to recover the full
V
-norm on the left-hand side (under the time
derivative), and applying
Grönwall’s lemma to the resulting relation, it is a standard matter to
deduce the additional bounds

\|\sigma\|_{H^{1}(0,T_{0};H)}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};V)}\leq c,

(173)

where we have used (
164
) and the fact

\sigma_{0}\in V
, which is a consequence of (
18
).

Thanks to the above estimates, going back to (
166
),
we may notice that the last two terms on the right-hand side
can now be controlled as follows:

\displaystyle\|\sigma\Delta\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};H)}\leq c\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega))}\|\Delta\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};L^{6}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(174)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};H)}\leq c\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};H)}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(175)

where we have also used the continuous embedding

W^{2,6}(\Omega)\subset W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.
Taking (
174
)-(
175
) into account, comparing
terms and applying elliptic regularity results in (
166
),
we deduce

\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{0};H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(176)

To proceed, we need to observe that properties (
173
)-(
176
)
seem not yet sufficient to perform the weak-strong uniqueness argument.
Indeed, the above conditions, at least for
d=3
, do not imply the uniform boundedness
of
\sigma
, which is used in an essential way in the proof.

In order to further improve the smoothness of solutions, we then need to go back to
the Cahn-Hilliard system and perform the so-called
second energy estimate
.
Namely, we test the first (
1a
) by
\mu_{t}
and sum the result
to the time derivative of the second (
1a
) tested by
\varphi_{t}
.
We omit giving the details since the argument is rather standard
and one can refer to several papers (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Sec. 3]
)
where it is carried out extensively. We just point out that,
in the current regularity setting, the argument may have a formal character,
but, on the other hand, it could be easily justified by approximation.
In particular, the procedure is fully compatible with the occurrence of the singular
potential as, in fact, it only relies on the
\lambda
-convexity of
F
.
Moreover, we observe that the first of (
173
) is sufficient
to manage the coupling term depending on
\sigma
(indeed, for that aim
what is needed is the
L^{2}
-regularity of
\sigma_{t}
which is part
of (
173
)). That said, using also the condition (
17
)
on the initial data, we deduce

\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(0,T_{0};V^{\prime})}+\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,T_{0};V)}+\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};V)}\leq c.

(177)

Next, again owing to Sobolev’s embeddings, applying elliptic regularity results
to the second (
1a
), and using the second of (
173
),
we infer

\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};W^{2,6}(\Omega))}+\|\beta(\varphi)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{6}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(178)

where, as before, the exponents
6
may be replaced by any
P\in[1,\infty)

in the two-dimensional case. Next, as a consequence of
(
177
)-(
178
), we also have

\displaystyle\|\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega))}\leq c\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega))}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq c,

(179)

\displaystyle\|\sigma\Delta\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega))}\leq c\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{6}(\Omega))}\|\Delta\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{6}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(180)

In particular, it may be worth remarking that, when deducing relation (
179
),
we have applied to
\nabla\sigma
the continuous embedding

L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};H)\cap L^{2}(0,T_{0};V)\subset L^{4}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega)).

(181)

Then, by Sobolev’s embeddings,
(
18
) implies in particular

\sigma_{0}\in W^{3/2,3}(\Omega)\subset B^{3/2}_{3,3}(\Omega)\subset B^{3/2}_{3,4}(\Omega)

(182)

(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 6.2.4 and Thm. 6.4.4]
).
The above condition allows us to apply to equation (
166
)
inhomogeneous (i.e., of the so-called
L^{p}
-
L^{q}
type,
here with
p=4
,
q=3
) parabolic regularity results of
Agmon-Douglis-Nirenberg type (see,

e.g.,

[
9
, Thm. 2.1]
or
[
8
]
),
implying

\|\sigma_{t}\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega))}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{0};W^{2,3}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(183)

Hence, noting the interpolation property (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Par. 6.4]
,
for the properties of the real interpolation operator

(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta,q}
in the framework of Sobolev and Besov
spaces)

\big(W^{1,4}(0,T_{0};L^{3}(\Omega)),L^{4}(0,T_{0};W^{2,3}(\Omega))\big)_{1/3,q}\subset L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{\infty}(\Omega)),

(184)

which holds for every
q\in(1,\infty)
thanks to embedding properties of Sobolev
and Besov spaces, from (
183
)

we eventually deduce

\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{0};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq c.

(185)

This concludes the proof of the theorem as the regularity properties
(
19
)-(
23
) are direct consequences of
(
177
), (
178
), (
183
) and (
185
).
In particular, it is immediate to check that the summability of the second
space derivatives both of
\varphi
and of
\sigma
, thanks to suitable
trace theorems, allows us to interpret both the system equations
and the boundary conditions in the pointwise sense.

∎

6
Relative energy inequality and weak-strong uniqueness

In the following argument, using a notation which is rather customary in weak-strong
uniqueness proofs, we shall rename as
(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\sigma})
the local strong
solution provided by Theorem
2.5
. Moreover,
we will note as
V_{0}
and
V_{0}^{\prime}
the closed subspaces of
V
and,
respectively,
V^{\prime}
, consisting of the function(al)s having zero spatial mean.
Then,
-\Delta
(i.e., minus the Neumann Laplacian)
turns out to be invertible as an operator from
V_{0}
to
V_{0}^{\prime}

and we will denote as
(-\Delta)^{-1}:V_{0}^{\prime}\to V_{0}
its inverse mapping.
We also point out that, respectively on
V_{0}
and
V_{0}^{\prime}
, the norms

\|v\|_{V_{0}}^{2}:=\|\nabla v\|^{2},\qquad\|\xi\|_{V_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}:=\langle\xi,(-\Delta)^{-1}\xi\rangle

(186)

are equivalent to the standard norms. We will use the above norms whenever
necessary.

That said, the
relative energy

\mathcal{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\varphi}):=\int_{\Omega}\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\left(\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{M}{2}\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|_{V^{\prime}_{0}}^{2},

and the
relative dissipation
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\varphi})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}|\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma}|^{2}-\chi\tilde{\sigma}(\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma})\cdot(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+M\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}+(F^{\prime}(\varphi)-F^{\prime}(\tilde{\varphi}))(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})+\lambda|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
M>0
will be chosen later on.
Then, recalling that
\tilde{\mu}
is
given by
\tilde{\mu}:=-\Delta\tilde{\varphi}+F^{\prime}(\tilde{\varphi})-\chi\tilde{\sigma}
,
we (formally) introduce the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}-\Delta\tilde{\mu}\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{\sigma}-\Delta\tilde{\sigma}-\chi\dive(\tilde{\sigma}\nabla(1-\tilde{\varphi}))-\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\tilde{\sigma}\end{pmatrix},

so that the system can be abstractly reformulated as
\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})=0
.

With these definitions at hand, we are able to state the relative
energy inequality.
Actually, as
\varphi(0)=\tilde{\varphi}(0)
, the mass conservation
property gives

\int_{\Omega}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\varphi}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all }\,t>0;

(187)

namely,
\varphi(t)-\tilde{\varphi}(t)\in V_{0}
for every
t\in[0,T]
.
Then, using Ehrling’s lemma, we also observe, for later convenience, that

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|^{2}\leq\eta\|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}\|^{2}+C_{\eta}\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|_{V^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}

(188)

for a possibly small constant
\eta>0
and a correspondingly large constant
C_{\eta}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
(\varphi,\sigma)
be a weak solution in the sense of
Definition
2.3
and
(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})
a pair of functions
satisfying, over the interval
(0,T)
, regularity
conditions analogous to (
14
), (
19
),
and (
22
)-(
23
).
Then, the
relative energy inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})+\mathcal{W}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\leq\int_{\Omega}\left[\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)-\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\right](\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})+\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\left[\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\Big)\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\\
+\int_{\Omega}M\chi(\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})+\lambda M|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

(189)

is fulfilled for a.e.
0\leq t\leq T
.

Proof.

First of all, we observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\left(\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma,1\rangle-\langle\partial_{t}\ln\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}\rangle-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\tilde{\sigma}(\ln\sigma-\ln\tilde{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x.

(190)

Next, integrating equation (
15b
) over
\Omega

and choosing
\vartheta\equiv 1
, we infer

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x.

(191)

Now, let us insert
\theta=\tilde{\sigma}\phi
in (
15e
)
with
\phi\in\C^{1}([0,T))
and
\phi\geq 0
. To be precise,
it may be worth noting that, under the conditions on
\tilde{\sigma}

(
22
)-(
22
)
assumed here, such a function
\theta
does not properly belong
to
C^{1}([0,T];W^{1,p}(\Omega))
,
p>d
, as would required in
Def.
2.3
; on the other hand, one can easily
prove that the validity of relation (
15e
) can be
extended to test functions
\theta
with the (weaker)
regularity corresponding to (
22
)-(
23
).
In addition to that, as noted in Remark
5.3
, the
regularity of
\tilde{\sigma}
(hence that of
\theta=\tilde{\sigma}\phi
) could be
further improved at the price of technical complications.
That said, we infer via Lemma
3.2
that

\displaystyle-\langle\partial_{t}\ln\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}|\nabla\ln\sigma|^{2}-\nabla\ln\sigma\cdot\nabla\tilde{\sigma}-\chi\tilde{\sigma}\nabla\ln\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi+\chi\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\tilde{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)\tilde{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\leq 0.

(192)

Next, we test (
1b
) (written for
(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\varphi})
) by

\ln\sigma-\ln\tilde{\sigma}
. We note that this test is indeed justified due to
the additional regularity assumed for
\tilde{\sigma}
. Straightforward computations lead to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\tilde{\sigma}(\ln\sigma-\ln\tilde{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\tilde{\sigma}-\chi\tilde{\sigma}\nabla\tilde{\varphi})\cdot(\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\tilde{\sigma}(\ln\sigma-\ln\tilde{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x.

(193)

Summing (
191
) with (
192
),
subtracting (
193
), and using (
190
),
we then deduce

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}|\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma}|^{2}-\chi\tilde{\sigma}(\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma})\cdot(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\leq\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)(\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})-\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\tilde{\sigma}\ln\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
=\int_{\Omega}\left[\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)-\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\right](\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})+\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\left[\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(194)

Above, we also used the fact that
\partial_{t}\tilde{\sigma}\ln\tilde{\sigma}=\partial_{t}(\tilde{\sigma}(\ln\tilde{\sigma}-1))
.
Next, we write (
15a
) for
\varphi
and for
\tilde{\varphi}
, take the difference, and choose

\psi=(-\Delta)^{-1}(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})
therein, which is allowed thanks to (
187
).
Then, some manipulations lead to

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\deriv\!{}}{\deriv\!t}\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|_{V_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}(\mu-\tilde{\mu})(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(195)

Correspondingly, we compute the difference of (
15c
) for the weak and the strong
solution, test it by
\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}
, and integrate over
\Omega
so to obtain

\int_{\Omega}(\mu-\tilde{\mu})(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x=\|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\big((F^{\prime}(\varphi)-F^{\prime}(\tilde{\varphi})-\chi(\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})\big)(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x.

(196)

Then, replacing (
196
) into (
195
) we are led to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|_{V_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}+(F^{\prime}(\varphi)-F^{\prime}(\tilde{\varphi}))(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x\,

\displaystyle\quad=\chi\int_{\Omega}(\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(197)

Adding (
197
) multiplied by
M
to (
194
)
and adding
M\lambda\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|^{2}

to both sides, we infer the asserted inequality (
189
).
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
(\varphi,\sigma)
be a weak solution in the sense of Definition
2.3

and
(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})
be a strong solution in the sense of Theorem
2.5
,
such that
(\varphi(s),\sigma(s))=(\tilde{\varphi}(s),\tilde{\sigma}(s))
for some

s\in[0,T_{0})
. Then, it holds that

(\varphi(t),\sigma(t))=(\tilde{\varphi}(t),\tilde{\sigma}(t))
for all
t\in[s,T_{0})
.

Proof.

First of all, let us choose

M:=\max\{1,\chi^{2}\|{\tilde{\sigma}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\}
.
Then, it is a standard matter to check that

\mathcal{W}
is nonnegative. More precisely, by Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}|\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma}|^{2}+\chi\tilde{\sigma}(\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma})\cdot(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi})+M|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}|\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma}|^{2}+M|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(198)

Then, applying Lemma
3.1
, we get that

(\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})\leq 4\left(\tilde{\sigma}|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}+\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\left(\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\right)\right),

(199)

as well as

\displaystyle\left[\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)-\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\right](\sigma-\tilde{\sigma})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\max\left\{1,|\overline{\alpha}|,|\underline{\alpha}|\right\}\left(\tilde{\sigma}\left[\alpha(\varphi,\sigma)-\alpha(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\right]^{2}+\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leq C_{\alpha}\left(|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}+|\sqrt{\sigma}-\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}}|^{2}+\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\right),

(200)

where (
24
) has been used, and the constant(s)
C_{\alpha}

(whose value is allow to vary on occurrence) may also depend on
the
L^{\infty}
-norm of
\tilde{\sigma}
.

Now, noting that the estimate of the right-hand side of (
200
)
is straighforward, we may insert (
199
) and (
200
)
into (
189
). Hence, in view of estimate (
188
),
it only remains to estimate the difference of the square-roots
of
\sigma
and
\tilde{\sigma}
. To this end, we first observe that the term

\Lambda(u|\tilde{u}):=e^{\frac{1}{2}u}-e^{\frac{1}{2}\tilde{u}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\tilde{u}}(u-\tilde{u})

is nonegative for all
u,\tilde{u}\in\R
since
x\to e^{\frac{1}{2}x}
is a convex
function and secondly that

0\leq\Lambda(\ln\sigma|\ln\tilde{\sigma})=\sqrt{\sigma}-\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}}(\ln\sigma-\ln\tilde{\sigma}).

By elementary considerations, we then obtain

\displaystyle(\sqrt{\sigma}-\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}})^{2}\leq\sigma-2\sqrt{\sigma}\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}}+\tilde{\sigma}+2\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}}\Lambda(\ln\sigma|\ln\tilde{\sigma})=\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}(\ln\sigma-\ln\tilde{\sigma}),

(201)

which can now be used to estimate the difference of
the square roots of
\sigma
and
\tilde{\sigma}
appropriately.

Indeed, under the assumptions of this corollary,
we find by integrating (
189
) over
(s,t)

and using (
201
) that

\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{W}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
\leq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}C_{\alpha,M,1}\Big(\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}-\tilde{\sigma}\ln\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}\Big)\Big)+C_{\alpha,M,2}|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(202)

for suitable constants
C_{\alpha,M,1},C_{\alpha,M,2}>0
.
Then, applying once more Ehrling’s lemma, we deduce

C_{\alpha,M,2}\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|^{2}\leq\frac{M}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C_{\alpha,M,3}\|\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}\|_{V^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}.

Inserting this and (
198
) into (
202
) implies

\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\Big|_{s}^{t}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}|\nabla\ln\sigma-\nabla\ln\tilde{\sigma}|^{2}+M|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\tilde{\varphi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\sigma|\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

so that the assert follows directly from Grönwall’s lemma.
∎
