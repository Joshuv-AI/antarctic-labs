Managing Emotion in Clinical Supervision Through Oller‐Vallejo's Model of Ego States
Authors: Paul Linsley, John Hurley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis paper examines the value of Oller‐Vallejo's (2001) ego state model as a framework for understanding emotional dynamics in mental health nursing clinical supervision, and its integration with Proctor's (1986) model of supervision.BackgroundMental health nursing is characterised by high emotional demand, relational complexity and exposure to psychological distress. Clinical supervision is increasingly recognised as essential for supporting emotional well‐being, professional development and safe practice. However, there remains a need for more psychologically informed frameworks to better understand the emotional processes occurring within supervisory relationships.MethodThis paper adopts a conceptual, theory‐informed approach, drawing on published literature relating to ego state theory and clinical supervision models. A narrative synthesis is used to explore how these frameworks can be integrated to enhance understanding of emotional processes in supervision.DiscussionThe ego state concepts of caregiving, caregetting and individuating are used to explore emotional roles and relational dynamics within supervision. These are considered alongside Proctor's (1986) restorative, formative and normative functions of supervision. The integration of these models provides a structured way to understand emotional containment, dependency, autonomy and professional development within supervisory relationships in mental health nursing.ConclusionThe paper proposes that combining ego state theory with established supervision models offers a psychologically informed framework for enhancing clinical supervision. This has implications for supporting emotional resilience, improving reflective practice and strengthening workforce well‐being and sustainability in mental health nursing.

Introduction

Mental health nursing is characterised by sustained relational engagement, high emotional intensity, and frequent exposure to psychological distress, trauma, and risk. Mental health nurses are required to provide safe, evidence‐based, and compassionate care while simultaneously regulating their own emotional responses within increasingly pressurised healthcare systems. Against this backdrop, clinical supervision and reflective practice are no longer optional professional supports but essential mechanisms for safeguarding practitioner wellbeing, ethical practice, and patient safety (Edgar et al. 2023). Increasingly, supervision is understood not merely as a professional requirement, but as a vital relational space that enables emotional processing and ethical sense‐making in complex clinical environments.

Furthermore, this paper argues that effective clinical supervision constitutes a critical organisational and professional responsibility within contemporary mental health services. In doing so, it responds to growing concerns about workforce sustainability, moral distress, and the emotional toll of mental health work. Drawing on Oller‐Vallejo's (2001) ego state model, the paper proposes a psychologically informed framework for understanding the emotional dynamics that shape supervisory relationships. The model's three ego states, caregiving, caregetting, and individuating, offer a nuanced lens through which to examine how emotional needs, power, dependency, and professional autonomy are negotiated within supervision.

These ego states are explored in relation to Proctor's (1986) tripartite model of supervision, possibly the most used and most known of the supervision models, which conceptualises supervision as encompassing restorative (emotional support), formative (learning and development), and normative (accountability and governance) functions. By integrating these frameworks, the paper advances an emotionally attuned model of reflective supervision that foregrounds relational awareness and emotional containment. This integrated approach aligns with contemporary priorities in mental health nursing, including trauma‐informed care, emotional intelligence, staff retention, and workforce sustainability, and sets the foundation for the discussion that follows.

Emotional Labour, Moral Distress, and the Contemporary Mental Health Nursing Context

Mental health nursing involves significant emotional labour, defined here as the active management of personal emotions in order to meet professional expectations and respond therapeutically to others. Nurses are required to remain empathic, calm, and emotionally available in the face of distress, aggression, suicidality, and profound human suffering. This emotional work is central to therapeutic engagement and recovery‐oriented practice; however, it also exposes nurses to cumulative emotional strain. In acute and crisis settings, emotional labour is further intensified by risk‐management responsibilities, rapid decision‐making, and exposure to unpredictable clinical situations.

Contemporary evidence indicates that mental health nurses experience disproportionately high levels of burnout, compassion fatigue, moral distress, and psychological distress compared with other nursing specialities (Foster et al. 2024). Moral distress, arising when nurses are constrained from acting in accordance with their professional values due to organisational or systemic pressures, has become a significant concern within under‐resourced services. When such distress remains unacknowledged or unsupported, it can undermine professional identity and contribute to disengagement, workforce attrition, sickness absence, and diminished quality of care.

At the same time, mental health nurses consistently report strong professional commitment and derive meaning from therapeutic relationships, recovery‐oriented practice, and witnessing patient change over time (Alonazi et al. 2023). These sources of meaning are often closely tied to nurses' emotional investment in their work. However, sustained exposure to emotionally demanding practice without adequate reflective containment can erode confidence, heighten anxiety, and contribute to defensive or risk‐averse practice. Contemporary literature increasingly recognises that resilience should not be conceptualised as individual emotional toughness, but rather as a relational and organisationally supported capacity to remain emotionally engaged without becoming overwhelmed (Bui et al. 2023). This reframing underscores the need for structured, supportive processes, such as clinical supervision, to contain emotional labour and sustain reflective practice.

Clinical Supervision and Reflective Practice: Evidence, Ethics, and Workforce Sustainability

Clinical supervision is widely conceptualised as a formal, collaborative process that supports reflection, learning, and professional accountability. Within mental health nursing, supervision provides a structured and psychologically safer space in which practitioners can explore the emotional, ethical, and relational dimensions of their work. Importantly, this space enables emotional experiences to be articulated and examined rather than silenced or individualised. Reflective practice, embedded within supervision, allows nurses to examine not only clinical decisions and actions but also emotional responses, unconscious processes, and relational patterns that influence practice.

Research demonstrates that high‐quality clinical supervision is associated with improved practitioner wellbeing, increased emotional intelligence, enhanced clinical confidence, and safer patient outcomes (Snowdon et al. 2021). Emotional intelligence, encompassing the ability to recognise, interpret, and regulate emotions in oneself and others, is widely regarded as a core competency in mental health care, where therapeutic relationships are central to effective intervention (Goleman 1997). Supervision therefore functions not only as a learning mechanism, but as a developmental process that strengthens relational and emotional capabilities essential to safe practice.

Supervision also plays an important ethical and supportive role. By fostering reflective capacity, supervision enables nurses to recognise moral tensions, manage countertransference, and engage in thoughtful decision‐making in high‐risk or emotionally charged situations. This reflective containment is particularly important within organisational cultures that may implicitly discourage emotional expression, vulnerability, or uncertainty. Models such as Hawkins and Shohet's (2012) concept of “finding rules” highlight how supervision can counteract cultures of emotional suppression and promote reflective curiosity, ethical awareness, and professional integrity. “Finding rules” refers to the process by which supervisors and supervisees collaboratively identify both explicit and implicit expectations, norms, and boundaries within the supervisory and organisational context. By surfacing these often unspoken rules, supervision creates a space where nurses can explore tensions between professional values and organisational demands, reflect on their own responses, and negotiate ethical practice with greater clarity and confidence. This process not only supports ethical decision‐making but also reinforces psychological safety, enabling nurses to openly discuss mistakes, uncertainties, and emotional challenges without fear of judgement or reprisal.

From a workforce perspective, supervision is increasingly linked to staff retention, psychological safety, and service sustainability. National and international policy documents emphasise the importance of regular, high‐quality supervision as a core component of clinical governance and safe staffing strategies. However, despite this recognition, access to consistent and skilled supervision remains uneven across mental health services, reinforcing the need for models that clearly articulate supervision's emotional, relational, and organisational value.

Contemporary Supervision Models and Ongoing Challenges

Clinical supervision in mental health nursing is informed by a range of contemporary models, commonly categorised as developmental, orientation‐specific, integrative, or evidence‐based. Developmental models attend to the evolving needs of practitioners across career stages; orientation‐specific models align supervision with therapeutic frameworks such as psychodynamic or cognitive–behavioural approaches; integrative models flexibly combine multiple perspectives; and evidence‐based models emphasise links between supervision and measurable clinical outcomes.

While these models differ in emphasis, contemporary supervision literature increasingly converges on the importance of emotional processing, relational awareness, and reflective depth. Nevertheless, significant barriers continue to limit the effectiveness of supervision in practice, including time pressures, workload demands, inconsistent supervisor training, role ambiguity, power imbalances, and a lack of organisational prioritisation (Snowdon et al. 2021).

These challenges highlight the limitations of supervision approaches that focus primarily on task performance, compliance, or managerial oversight. Without explicit attention to emotional dynamics and relational processes, supervision risks becoming perfunctory, avoidant of affect, or experienced as surveillance rather than support.

Integrating Ego State Theory Into Reflective Supervision Practice

Oller‐Vallejo's (2001) ego state model offers a contemporary and psychologically grounded framework for addressing the emotional and relational complexity of supervision. Integrating principles from attachment theory and transactional analysis, the model conceptualises interactions as shifting between caregiving, caregetting, and individuating ego states. These states reflect different relational needs, levels of dependency, and degrees of professional autonomy, providing a structured lens for understanding the dynamics that emerge within supervisory relationships.

Applied to clinical supervision, ego state theory provides supervisors with a conceptual language for recognising the diverse emotional and relational positions that supervisees may occupy during reflective encounters. This framework emphasises that supervision is not simply a procedural or managerial activity, but a relational process in which emotional responses, intersubjective dynamics, and professional development are deeply intertwined. The model highlights how supervisory interactions can simultaneously address emotional containment, professional learning, and the fostering of autonomy, illustrating the multilayered nature of reflective practice.

When aligned with Proctor's (1986) restorative, formative, and normative functions, ego state theory enables a balanced approach that integrates emotional support, skill development, and accountability. This alignment reinforces the argument that supervision is fundamentally relational, requiring emotional attunement, reflexivity, and ethical sensitivity. By attending to the interplay of ego states, supervisors can cultivate environments in which supervisees develop self‐awareness, reflective capacity, and professional confidence while maintaining emotional resilience.

By foregrounding emotional awareness and relational dynamics, this integrated model addresses contemporary challenges in mental health nursing. It positions clinical supervision and reflective practice as essential infrastructures for sustaining compassionate, ethical care, supporting emotional intelligence, and maintaining a resilient and professionally grounded workforce. Furthermore, it encourages organisations to recognise that the quality of supervision is inseparable from workforce wellbeing, ethical practice, and patient outcomes, highlighting supervision as a core component of professional and organisational sustainability.

Drawing on two foundational psychological theories, transactional analysis and attachment theory, Oller‐Vallejo's (2001) ego state model situates care dynamics within a broader emotional and relational context. Transactional analysis is a theory of personality and communication that explains how individuals interact through distinct ego states (Parent, Adult, and Child), while attachment theory focuses on how early emotional bonds influence relationships and emotional regulation throughout life. Building upon these concepts, the model delineates three interrelated ego states—Care Getting, Caregiving, and Individuating—which broadly correspond to the classical Child, Parent, and Adult ego states. Each state represents a specific emotional orientation and behavioural stance, shaping how individuals relate to others, process their experiences, and navigate the complexities of care work. Oller‐Vallejo (2001) developed the model through clinical observation of carers and patients, aiming to better understand the emotional and relational dynamics underlying help‐seeking and caregiving behaviours. The model offers a relational framework for identifying patterns of dependence, caregiving, and individuation (the process by which an individual develops a distinct, autonomous sense of self) within care interactions. It is argued here that the model's concepts offer potential relevance for clinical supervision, particularly within trauma‐informed and emotionally focused approaches. Its emphasis on emotional positioning and relational roles aligns with the reflective, supportive, and developmental tasks central to effective supervision. By applying this framework, supervisors may better facilitate emotional awareness, relational attunement, and resilience among practitioners working in emotionally demanding environments.

Care Getting (Child Ego State)

The care getting function reflects an individual's inherent need for nurturance, reassurance, safety, and validation. Within the healthcare context, this ego state is often seen in patients who are in distress, seeking containment and clarity from clinicians. However, practitioners themselves are not immune to occupying this state, particularly in moments of uncertainty, emotional depletion, or professional self‐doubt. When clinicians find themselves overwhelmed by clinical demands, interpersonal tensions, or the cumulative weight of vicarious trauma, they may unconsciously revert to care getting behaviours.

Emotional demands and upset can manifest as increased dependence on colleagues or supervisors, difficulty tolerating ambiguity, emotional reactivity, or a heightened sensitivity to feedback. If unacknowledged or unsupported, prolonged activation of this ego state can lead to professional withdrawal, impaired decision‐making, and reduced emotional availability for patients. Within supervision, recognising when a practitioner is operating from the care getting state allows supervisors to respond with compassion rather than judgement, acknowledging the underlying need while gently guiding the supervisee toward reflection, regulation, and reintegration of their professional agency. Importantly, validating the care getting state does not imply regression or weakness; rather, it humanises the clinician's emotional landscape and opens the door to authentic connection, vulnerability, and growth.

New graduates, in particular, may be more prone to occupying the care getting state as they transition into professional roles. The steep learning curve, emotional intensity of clinical work, and evolving sense of identity can heighten their vulnerability to emotional overload and self‐doubt. Without appropriate support, this group may become especially susceptible to feelings of disconnection or inadequacy, factors strongly linked to early attrition in the health and social care workforce. In this way, supervision that is attuned to the care getting dynamic not only nurtures individual development but also becomes a critical strategy for workforce retention. Recognising and responding to this state compassionately can reduce burnout risk, reinforce professional identity, and create a culture of safety and belonging for emerging practitioners.

Caregiving (Parent Ego State)

The caregiving ego state reflects the clinician's capacity and motivation to provide structure, guidance, containment, and emotional support to others. It draws from both the nurturing and controlling aspects of the Parent ego state in transactional analysis and is often the most socially reinforced stance in healthcare cultures. In many ways, caregiving is at the heart of the clinician's role; it involves compassion, advocacy, responsibility, and ethical action.

However, when the caregiving ego state becomes overactivated or unbalanced, it can obscure the mental health nurse's capacity for critical reflection and emotional self‐care. Clinicians may become overidentified with the rescuer role, leading to overfunctioning, enmeshment, or difficulty maintaining boundaries. Emotional overinvestment in clients may result in emotional contagion or an unconscious need to derive self‐worth from being indispensable. This can compromise clinical objectivity, undermine team dynamics, and contribute to compassion fatigue or burnout.

Within supervision, exploring the caregiving ego state is an important element. Supervisors can help practitioners identify the motivations and emotional drivers behind their caregiving stance, challenging perfectionistic tendencies or rescuing behaviours when necessary. Supervision becomes a space not only to reinforce healthy caregiving but also to examine when it arises from unresolved personal narratives or unmet emotional needs. Through reflection, practitioners can learn to provide care that is both empathic and sustainable, grounded in professionalism rather than overextension. For example, a nurse who consistently works through breaks or takes on additional shifts to avoid letting colleagues or patients down may be overextending themselves in a way that risks emotional exhaustion or burnout.

Individuating (Adult Ego State)

The individuating ego state is the cornerstone of professional maturity and emotional integration. It is characterised by emotional regulation, self‐awareness, reflective thinking, and intentional action. Unlike the reactive tendencies of the care getting or caregiving states, individuating allows practitioners to respond rather than react, to hold complexity, navigate moral ambiguity, and remain grounded amid emotional turbulence.

This ego state enables clinicians to assess situations objectively, make ethically sound decisions, and remain attuned to both their own emotional responses and those of others without becoming overwhelmed. In the context of trauma‐informed supervision, individuating is especially important. It provides the internal scaffolding for resilience, boundary‐setting, and empathic detachment, skills that are indispensable in trauma work where emotional intensity is high and professional demands are relentless.

Importantly, individuating is not a static trait but a dynamic capacity that can be cultivated and strengthened over time. High‐quality supervision plays a pivotal role in this development. Through guided reflection, honest dialogue, and relational containment, supervisors support supervisees in accessing and expanding their individuating capacities. Over time, this helps clinicians integrate their emotional experiences with professional competencies, leading to more adaptive coping, clearer clinical reasoning, and sustained wellbeing.

To further explore the practical application of Oller‐Vallejo's ego state model, this paper now examines Proctor (1986), a widely used framework in nursing and allied health. By aligning the ego states—caregetting, caregiving, and individuating—with Proctor's three supervisory functions (formative, restorative, and normative), we aim to deepen the understanding of how supervision can simultaneously support learning, emotional wellbeing, and professional accountability. This integration offers a more holistic approach to supervision that responds to the emotional complexities of mental health nursing.

Proctor's Model of Clinical Supervision

Among the most widely applied frameworks in nursing and allied health is Proctor's Model of Clinical Supervision (1986), which outlines three interconnected functions: formative, restorative, and normative. The formative function centres on education, skill development, and reflective learning. The restorative function addresses the emotional wellbeing of practitioners, offering support and opportunities to manage work‐related stress. The normative function is concerned with professional accountability, regulatory standards, and ethical practice.

This tripartite model has been found to promote resilience, professional competence, and safe clinical practice across diverse healthcare settings. More recently, literature has expanded on Proctor's original formulation by highlighting the role of reflection as a central element across all three supervisory domains (Simon Fagernäs et al. 2023). Reflection is now recognised as central not only for learning and skills development, but also for managing emotional demands and navigating ethical complexities within practice.

Using such a model of clinical supervision creates a structured environment where clinicians can explore their experiences, receive feedback, and engage in personal and professional growth. Viewing Proctor's model through the lens of ego state theory provides a more nuanced and psychologically informed understanding of the supervision process. Through the process of reflective supervision, supervisees are encouraged to engage critically with their experiences, considering not only the immediate events but also alternative interpretations, potential outcomes, and their own emotional responses. This deepened reflection supports the development of emotional literacy and clinical insight, helping practitioners to identify how they might approach similar situations differently in the future. Importantly, this process allows them to explore the personal and often subconscious impact of their work, enabling them to examine how chronic emotional strain might be subtly influencing their behaviours, thought processes, interpersonal dynamics, and overall wellbeing.

Normative Supervision and the Caregiving State

The normative function of supervision is fundamentally concerned with accountability, ethical standards, and professional responsibility. It ensures that clinicians operate within the boundaries of safe, competent, and ethical practice. This function closely interacts with the caregiving ego state, which embodies the clinician's motivation to protect, nurture, and guide others. While caregiving is central to the helping professions, challenges arise when this state becomes unbalanced or rigid.

Practitioners operating predominantly from an unregulated caregiving ego state may present with overinvolvement, idealism, or perfectionistic tendencies. These patterns, while often rooted in good intentions, can lead to blurred boundaries, emotional overextension, or even moral distress, particularly when clinicians feel they are not doing “enough” or are powerless to alleviate suffering. Over time, this can compromise clinical objectivity, provoke ethical conflicts, or contribute to burnout.

Through the normative lens, supervisors can help supervisees reflect on these relational dynamics and internal expectations. By explicitly exploring how the caregiving ego state informs behaviour, supervisors can challenge unrealistic standards, reframe guilt or frustration, and reinforce the value of boundary‐setting as an ethical act, not a withdrawal of care. Supervision becomes a space to recalibrate caregiving, balancing empathy with clinical detachment, compassion with responsibility. This fosters a more sustainable, ethical practice that protects both patients and practitioners.

Formative Supervision and the Individuating State

The formative function of supervision is aimed at professional development, learning, and skill acquisition. It is inherently future‐focused, supporting supervisees in building self‐awareness, reflective capacity, and clinical competence. This function resonates most strongly with the individuating ego state, which emphasises autonomy, emotional regulation, and integrated decision‐making.

Supervisors operating in the formative mode encourage supervisees to examine their experiences critically, inviting them to explore how their thoughts, feelings, and behaviours interact with their clinical judgement. By fostering reflective dialogue, supervisors help practitioners begin to distinguish emotional reactivity from thoughtful response and to develop a metacognitive awareness of their internal processes.

This growth supports the development of a flexible, durable professional identity. As practitioners strengthen their individuating capacities, they become more adept at moving fluidly between the care getting and caregiving states, responding to patient needs without becoming enmeshed and seeking support without losing professional agency. Formative supervision thus becomes a vital site for cultivating emotional intelligence, adaptive functioning, and clinical maturity.

Supervisors can also model individuating by sharing their own reflective process transparently, inviting supervisees into a collaborative learning space where growth is normalised, and professional identity is shaped over time. Moreover, supervisors must model the individuating state themselves, offering a stable, attuned presence that holds space for emotional exploration while reinforcing professional boundaries and growth. This modelling serves as both containment and invitation, encouraging supervisees to internalise a reflective, balanced approach to their own emotional and clinical practice.

Restorative Supervision and the Care Getting State

The restorative function of supervision centres on emotional support, renewal, and psychological containment. It acknowledges that clinicians are exposed to distressing material and emotionally taxing relationships, and that this work inevitably leaves an imprint. This function aligns directly with the care getting ego state, which surfaces when practitioners feel overwhelmed, vulnerable, or depleted.

Supervisees in this state may express helplessness, dependency, fatigue, or emotional confusion. They may seek reassurance or support in a way that echoes the childlike need for safety and attunement. Without a trauma‐informed approach, these expressions may be misread as unprofessional or lacking resilience; however, restorative supervision reframes them as natural and valid responses to difficult work.

In this space, supervisors play a containing and empathic role, offering validation, normalising emotional responses, and holding the supervisee's distress with sensitivity. Attunement to the care getting state allows supervisors to intervene gently, acknowledging emotional needs without pathologising them.

Furthermore, critically naming the care getting ego state in supervision can reduce shame and enhance self‐understanding. When supervisees recognise that they are seeking care, they can engage more reflectively with their experience, gradually shifting toward the individuating ego state. This transition is facilitated through compassionate dialogue, co‐regulation, and the supervisor's commitment to emotional safety.

Restorative supervision, of the type described here, supports practitioners in reintegrating their sense of competence and confidence, enabling them to return to their work grounded and replenished rather than drained or demoralised. Through reflective supervision, supervisees are encouraged to engage critically with their experiences, considering not only immediate events but also alternative interpretations, potential outcomes, and their own emotional responses. This deeper level of reflection fosters the development of emotional literacy and clinical insight, helping practitioners to identify how they might approach similar situations differently in the future.

From the supervisor's perspective, integrating Oller‐Vallejo's ego states with Proctor's supervisory functions allows for a more emotionally dynamic, relationally responsive approach to support. Supervisors are not simply facilitating a process; they are co‐creating a reflective space where emotional states, of both supervisee and supervisor, are named, explored, and held.

In this model, the supervisor is not a neutral observer but an active participant whose own ego state shifts (e.g., between caregiving, individuating, or even care getting under stress) can influence the supervision dynamic. Recognising these internal shifts enables supervisors to model emotional regulation and reflexivity, reinforcing formative learning while maintaining emotional safety.

The relational dynamic between supervisor and supervisee is therefore shaped not only by professional roles but also by emotional positioning. For example, a supervisee in a care getting state may unconsciously evoke a caregiving stance in the supervisor, potentially reinforcing dependency if not held within clear boundaries. Conversely, a highly individuated supervisee may challenge or resist guidance, requiring the supervisor to draw more firmly on the normative function to ensure ethical and clinical standards are upheld.

These interactions are further complicated by each participant's developmental stage. Supervisees early in their careers may operate more frequently from care getting or unbalanced caregiving states, requiring a supervisory approach that is both restorative and gently formative. Supervisors must assess not only the content of clinical challenges but also the supervisee's capacity for emotional processing and self‐reflection.

Similarly, the developmental maturity of the supervisor influences the quality of supervision. Novice supervisors may be more reactive, potentially conflating their own emotional responses with those of the supervisee, particularly if they share similar ego state vulnerabilities. More experienced supervisors are better able to maintain a ‘supervisory meta‐position’, one that allows for simultaneous attunement to the emotional field, containment of distress, and facilitation of growth.

Importantly, the supervision process also creates space for exploring the personal and often subconscious impact of clinical work, allowing practitioners to examine how chronic emotional strain may be subtly influencing their behaviours, thought patterns, interpersonal dynamics, and overall wellbeing. Supervisors who are emotionally attuned and developmentally aware can support supervisees in separating personal triggers from professional responses, an essential skill when ego states are activated by transference, trauma echoes, or value‐based conflicts.

However, this is not a straightforward process. For many nurses, distinguishing between similar or overlapping emotional states, such as discomfort, irritation, frustration, or anger, can be particularly challenging, especially in fast‐paced or emotionally charged environments. In situations involving aggression, moral distress, or high‐risk clinical decisions, emotions can be intense, conflicting, and difficult to articulate. This complexity is compounded by the fact that emotions are inherently subjective and socially influenced. For example, terms like “angry,” “infuriated,” or “enraged” may refer to a shared emotional experience but differ significantly in intensity and cultural connotation.

Moreover, emotions rarely occur in isolation. In most clinical situations, professionals experience a nuanced, often shifting blend of emotional states. For instance, a nurse may feel both sadness and anger in response to a distressing patient encounter: “I was upset because of what happened, but as I got more upset, I also felt angry, until I couldn't hold it back and started crying.”

Emotional experiences are also fluid and cumulative, with their expression influenced by factors such as age, gender, culture, and social norms. Concepts such as emodiversity—the ability to experience a broad and varied range of emotions—and meta‐emotions—how individuals feel about their own emotional responses—further complicate this landscape. In addition, individual histories and lived experiences inevitably shape how emotions are felt, expressed, and understood. Supervisees may find their emotional responses deeply entangled with personal memories or relational dynamics, especially when these resonate with a patient's experiences or behaviours.

In this context, supervision becomes a space not only for processing clinical practice but also for cultivating emotional agility. Supervisors can support this development by naming patterns, validating emotional nuance, and encouraging supervisees to explore meaning without judgement. Integrating trauma‐informed, culturally sensitive, and developmentally tailored strategies allows the supervision space to become both a holding environment and a transformative one.

The Integration of the Framework as Part of Clinical Practice

In clinical practice, particularly in healthcare and mental health settings, professionals must blend technical expertise with emotional intelligence to provide holistic care. The therapeutic relationship is deeply influenced by not only clinical decisions but also the emotional dynamics between caregivers and care receivers. Emerging research highlights that emotions play a central role in clinical judgement, shaping decision‐making and the quality of patient care.

The goal of integrating Oller‐Vallejo's ego state model in clinical supervision is not merely to recognise and label emotional states, but to master the model in a way that clinicians can utilise it seamlessly in real‐time clinical practice. Once practitioners have a thorough understanding of the model, it becomes a practical, ongoing tool for emotional self‐assessment. By incorporating ego state awareness into their daily routine, mental health nurses can enhance their ability to make balanced decisions, engage in compassionate caregiving, and maintain emotional resilience. In active clinical environments, Oller‐Vallejo's ego state model serves as a practical tool for emotional awareness, communication, and ethical decision‐making. Summary

Integrating Oller‐Vallejo's ego state model into clinical supervision offers a relational framework for enhancing emotional awareness, resilience, and ethical decision‐making in healthcare. It supports real‐time emotional self‐assessment, reduces compassion fatigue, improves team communication, and strengthens clinical reasoning.

The model also deepens patient engagement and relational effectiveness, particularly in emotionally intense settings. By recognising the clinician's emotional subjectivity as integral to care, it promotes a more humanised, emotionally intelligent approach that values vulnerability and attuned responsiveness.

Through conscious engagement with caregiving, caregetting, and individuating states, practitioners can integrate emotional and cognitive processing to support their growth. When embedded in supervision and daily practice, the model fosters a reflective culture, improves team dynamics, and enhances workforce retention, especially for early‐career professionals at risk of emotional overload.
