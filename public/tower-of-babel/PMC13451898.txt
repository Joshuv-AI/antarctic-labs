One Health: An epistemological genealogy
Authors: Joana Afonso, Júlio Belo Fernandes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concept of One Health is commonly framed as an integrative model linking human, animal, and environmental health. While this articulation has been essential for policy development and institutional alignment, it remains descriptive mainly and can obscure the paradigm's epistemological foundations. This article argues that One Health should be understood not merely as a coordination framework, but as the contemporary consolidation of a long-standing interdisciplinary mode of knowing health. Using a genealogical approach, we trace convergent lines of thought across philosophy, ecology, medicine and veterinary science, public health, nursing, and social sciences. Across these traditions, recurring commitments emerge: health as relational and situated; causality as multi-level, non-linear, and distributed across human–animal–environment interactions; and ethical responsibility toward the ecological conditions that sustain life. Recognising this lineage reframes One Health as a way of knowing: one that requires concepts, methods, and responsibilities commensurate with entangled human–animal–environment realities.Keywords: One Health, Epistemology, Interdisciplinarity, Genealogy, Health paradigms, Care

Introduction

The increasing prominence of One Health in global health discourse reflects a growing recognition that complex health challenges, such as zoonotic pandemics, antimicrobial resistance, climate change, and biodiversity loss, cannot be adequately addressed within disciplinary or sectoral silos [1], [2]. In its most common formulation, One Health is an integrated approach that brings together human, animal, and environmental health [3]. This description has been instrumental in fostering intersectoral collaboration and legitimising new forms of governance and research funding.

However, this operational framing also carries a significant conceptual limitation. By presenting One Health primarily as a pragmatic response to contemporary problems, it implicitly suggests that interdisciplinarity is a recent achievement, driven by external pressures rather than embedded in the intellectual history of health sciences. Such a view risks reducing One Health to a managerial or technocratic device, an arrangement of sectors, rather than recognising it as the expression of a deeper epistemological orientation.

This article contends that One Health is best understood as the culmination of a long genealogy of thought in which health has been conceptualised as relational, contextual, and inseparable from broader ecological and social systems. Long before the contemporary label gained currency, building on Schwabe's earlier One Medicine [4] formulation and later popularised through early-2000s initiatives such as the 2004 “One World, One Health” Manhattan Principles convened in New York [5], diverse professions and disciplines grappled with the same fundamental question: how can health be understood when humans are recognised as biologically, environmentally, and socially entangled with other forms of life?

Several authors have reconstructed the emergence of One Health through largely chronological accounts that trace its origins, key milestones, and lineage [6], [7], [8]. While these historical narratives have been invaluable in documenting the evolution of the concept, they primarily describe its historical development rather than examining the epistemological continuities that transcend specific historical moments.

The aim of this paper is therefore not to recount a linear history of One Health, nor to identify its ‘founders’, but to conduct an epistemological genealogy. By tracing convergent lines of thought across multiple domains, the paper demonstrates that One Health is interdisciplinary not merely because it formally links human, animal, and environmental health, but because it emerges from a shared intellectual struggle to move beyond reductionist, anthropocentric, and sector-bound models of health.

This paper adopts an epistemological genealogical approach to examine One Health as a mode of knowing rather than a recent coordination framework. The genealogy is used here in a critical, non-linear sense: instead of reconstructing a complete historical timeline or attributing origins to single “founders”, it traces convergent problematisations of health across traditions that have repeatedly challenged reductionist, anthropocentric, and sector-bound explanations. The selection of sources was guided by their explicit engagement with relational conceptions of health, multi-level causality, and ethical responsibility toward ecological conditions of life, across philosophy, ecology, medicine and veterinary science, public health, social sciences, and nursing. The account is therefore interpretive and selective by design.

Its aim is conceptual clarification, showing how dispersed intellectual commitments became mutually intelligible in contemporary One Health, rather than providing an exhaustive intellectual history.

While necessarily partial, this genealogical reconstruction foregrounds the epistemic commitments that persist across domains and continue to shape what One Health can legitimately claim as knowledge and responsibility.

Philosophical foundations of One Health

The philosophical roots of One Health can be traced to early forms of holism in natural philosophy and medicine. In the Hippocratic corpus, notably in Airs, Waters, and Places, health is understood as the outcome of dynamic interactions between bodies, environments, climates, and modes of living. Disease emerges not as an internal malfunction detached from context, but as a disturbance in a broader relational field [9], [10].

This early medical philosophy already articulated several epistemological commitments that resonate with One Health. First, it rejected universal explanations of disease in favour of contextual reasoning. Second, it assumed continuity between human bodies and their environments rather than a strict separation between organisms and their surroundings. Third, it implied that effective intervention required both environmental and individual action.

Crucially, this was not merely a descriptive stance but an ontological one: humans were conceived as embedded within nature, not standing outside it. This relational ontology contrasts sharply with later Cartesian dualisms that would come to dominate biomedical thinking, separating mind from body and humans from nature [11]. The re-emergence of relational thinking in One Health can thus be read as a partial epistemic correction to centuries of reductionist abstraction.

Ecology

Alexander von Humboldt's integrative vision of nature represents a foundational moment in this epistemic shift. Humboldt rejected the fragmentation of natural phenomena into independent variables, insisting instead on the mutual interdependence of climatic, geographical, biological, and human factors [12], [13]. His work did not merely describe natural complexity; it proposed a new way of knowing, in which understanding arises from tracing relationships rather than isolating components. This relational epistemology resonates strongly with contemporary One Health approaches, which seek to understand health outcomes through the interactions among environmental change, animal populations, and human activity.

Crucially, Humboldt's influence extended beyond natural history into the broader scientific imagination. By demonstrating that environmental conditions shape biological patterns across scales, he helped establish the intellectual foundations for later ecological and epidemiological thinking [14], [15]. From a One Health perspective, this legacy is evident in the recognition that health risks such as zoonotic spillover, vector-borne disease, and antimicrobial resistance are inseparable from land use, climate variability, and ecosystem disruption.

Ecology further advanced this epistemological transformation by systematically challenging linear causality. Concepts such as feedback loops, threshold effects, non-linearity, and emergent properties destabilised the assumption that complex phenomena could be adequately explained by summing individual components [16]. Health, within this framework, cannot be reduced to the condition of isolated organisms, but emerges from the dynamic functioning of ecological systems over time.

This shift has direct implications for One Health. Contemporary challenges addressed under the One Health banner cannot be meaningfully understood without adopting ecological models that account for system-level dynamics [17]. Ecology thus provided not only conceptual tools but an epistemic rationale for moving beyond individualistic and species-specific explanations of health.

Aldo Leopold further articulated the ethical dimension of this ecological epistemology. His formulation of a land ethic extended systems thinking into the moral domain, reframing humans as members of ecological communities rather than as external managers or masters of nature [18], [19], [20]. This ethical ontology directly contests anthropocentric assumptions that have historically shaped both biomedical and public health interventions [20].

Leopold's contribution is particularly significant for One Health because it foregrounds responsibility toward system integrity, rather than focusing solely on human outcomes. From this perspective, environmental degradation is not merely a background risk factor for human disease, but a moral and epistemic failure to recognise the conditions that sustain health across species. This reasoning aligns closely with contemporary planetary health and One Health discourses, which increasingly emphasise sustainability, intergenerational responsibility, and ecological resilience [21], [22].

Epistemologically, integrating ecological systems thinking into health represents a decisive shift from control-oriented to relationship-oriented knowledge [23], [24]. It demands attentiveness to scale, time, and unintended consequences, features that challenge conventional interventionist models. One Health inherits this challenge: to act within complex systems without reducing them to technical problems, and to balance human health objectives with the integrity of the ecological systems upon which all health ultimately depends.

Medicine and veterinary science

The nineteenth century marked a pivotal moment in the epistemology of health, as advances in pathology, microbiology, and comparative medicine brought the biological continuity between species into sharper focus. Rudolf Virchow stands out as a central figure in this transformation. His insistence that there should be no dividing line between human and veterinary medicine was not a rhetorical flourish, but a direct challenge to anthropocentric and profession-bound epistemologies that had long structured medical knowledge.

By introducing the concept of zoonosis, Virchow drew attention to the fact that some diseases do not neatly fit within professional jurisdictions. By highlighting how specific disease processes cross species and are shaped by environmental and social conditions, his work implicitly questioned the adequacy of strictly discipline-based boundaries for understanding and responding to such phenomena [25]. This insight carried profound implications: if causality operates across biological and ecological continuities, then knowledge organised around isolated professional domains is structurally incapable of producing adequate explanations.

Virchow's work in social medicine further reinforced this epistemological stance. He conceptualised disease as simultaneously biological, environmental, and social, and argued that medical knowledge detached from living conditions, political arrangements, and economic structures was inherently incomplete [26]. In this sense, Virchow articulated an early critique of mono-level explanations that remains central to contemporary integrative health paradigms. Virchow's contribution, therefore, lay not simply in recognising links between human and animal disease, but in exposing the epistemological inadequacy of maintaining separate explanatory frameworks for phenomena that transcended species boundaries.

At the same time, veterinary medicine was undergoing its own scientific transformation. Figures such as John McFadyean helped establish veterinary pathology and bacteriology as rigorous scientific disciplines, demonstrating that the study of animal diseases generated knowledge with implications extending beyond veterinary practice itself [27]. His work reinforced comparative approaches to disease and contributed to the recognition that biological processes could not be adequately understood within single-species frameworks. Rather than following human medicine, veterinary science evolved as a parallel intellectual tradition, generating concepts and evidence that would increasingly inform comparative understandings of disease.

Comparative medicine emerged as the intellectual space in which these parallel traditions became increasingly integrated. Rather than treating veterinary knowledge as subsidiary to human medicine, comparative approaches viewed biological continuity across species as a source of scientific explanation. This shift encouraged reciprocal learning between medical and veterinary sciences and challenged the assumption that knowledge should remain organised around professional boundaries.

Within this emerging comparative tradition, William Osler extends and reframes this epistemic shift in a manner particularly relevant to contemporary One Health discourse. Although primarily remembered as a founding figure of modern clinical medicine, Osler's intellectual formation was deeply influenced by veterinary science and comparative pathology [28], [29]. His early exposure to animal disease and anatomical variation fostered an understanding of health grounded in biological continuity rather than human exceptionalism.

Osler's clinical philosophy resisted strict reductionism at a time when laboratory-based biomedicine was gaining dominance. He consistently emphasised the importance of understanding disease within the broader context of the patient's environment, habits, and living conditions, warning against the dangers of abstracting pathological processes from the realities in which they unfold [30]. This stance positioned clinical judgment as a form of integrative reasoning, rather than a mechanical application of isolated biological findings.

Epistemologically, Osler contributed to a conception of medicine that requires the continuous integration of biological knowledge, environmental awareness, and experiential understanding [31]. His insistence on bedside medicine, observation across species, and sensitivity to variation anticipated key principles now central to One Health. Importantly, Osler's influence extended beyond theory. Through his role in medical education and professional culture, he helped institutionalise an ethos of integrative thinking that shaped generations of physicians [31], [32]. This legacy resonates with contemporary One Health efforts to cultivate professionals capable of moving across disciplinary boundaries and engaging with health as a systemic and relational phenomenon.

Taken together, the co-evolution of medicine and veterinary science established an epistemological tradition grounded in biological continuity, comparative reasoning, and cross-species explanations of disease. Rather than representing independent trajectories, both disciplines evolved through mutual influence, progressively challenging anthropocentric and profession-bound understandings of health. These converging traditions would later provide much of the intellectual foundation upon which Schwabe articulated One Medicine and, subsequently, the broader epistemological framework of contemporary One Health.

Public health and social medicine

Public health has long functioned as a crucible for integrative ways of thinking about health, not merely by expanding the scope of intervention, but by fundamentally reconfiguring how causality, responsibility, and scale are understood [33]. Unlike clinical medicine, which traditionally centres on individual bodies, public health emerged from the recognition that disease patterns are shaped by shared environments, collective practices, and social organisation [34], [35].

John Snow's work during the nineteenth-century cholera epidemics is often credited with opening new ways of understanding disease patterns in relation to environment and community structures. Historical interpretations suggest that Snow's investigations went beyond the narrow identification of a single contaminated water source, encouraging later generations to consider how disease dynamics might be associated with patterns of water use, urban infrastructure, and collective exposures [36], [37]. While Snow's individual contributions have been variously interpreted and mythologized [38], his efforts nonetheless highlighted the potential value of examining disease at the population and shared-condition levels rather than focusing solely on individual susceptibility and isolated cases. This more contextual perspective on causation laid conceptual groundwork for subsequent developments in population health thinking.

Taken together, this line of inquiry foregrounded an epistemological intuition that remains relevant to One Health: causality in health is often distributed across infrastructures, social practices, and ecological conditions. From this perspective, disease is not merely something that happens to individuals, but something that emerges from the organisation of life in common.

Social medicine further deepened this epistemology by making explicit the political dimensions of health knowledge. Rudolf Virchow's assertion that medicine is a social science, and that politics is medicine on a large scale, represented a direct challenge to the notion of value-neutral or purely technical health expertise [39]. Health, in this tradition, is inseparable from power relations, economic structures, and governance arrangements.

Epistemologically, social medicine destabilised the boundary between scientific explanation and social critique. It asserted that understanding disease requires analysing how social inequalities, labour conditions, housing, and environmental degradation shape vulnerability and exposure [40]. This insight aligns closely with One Health's contemporary concern for structural drivers of risk, including deforestation, industrial agriculture, urbanisation, and climate-related displacement.

Crucially, public health and social medicine also transformed assumptions about where intervention should occur. By demonstrating that effective responses to disease often lie outside the clinical encounter, in sanitation systems, housing policy, environmental regulation, and social protection, these traditions expanded the epistemic and moral boundaries of health action [35], [41]. This expansion prefigures One Health's insistence that health interventions must engage sectors such as agriculture, urban planning, and environmental management.

From an epistemological standpoint, public health established that causal explanations in health are inherently multi-level, spanning biological, environmental, social, and political domains. This recognition remains central to One Health, particularly in addressing globalised health threats that cannot be contained within national borders or professional jurisdictions. In this sense, One Health inherits from public health not only an integrative ambition but a critical understanding of health as a collective, contextual, and politically mediated phenomenon.

Social sciences

Anthropology and related social sciences introduced a critical epistemological dimension to the genealogy of One Health by demonstrating that relationships between humans, animals, and environments are not merely biological or ecological, but profoundly mediated by culture, meaning, and social organisation. This body of work challenged the assumption that health-related behaviours can be fully explained by biological risk factors or environmental exposures alone.

Early anthropological studies of subsistence, domestication, and human–animal relations revealed that practices central to health, such as food production, animal husbandry, and interaction with natural environments, are embedded in symbolic systems and moral economies [42], [43]. Claude Lévi-Strauss' structural anthropology, for instance, showed how cultural classifications of animals and nature shape everyday practices, including those relevant to health, hygiene, and disease avoidance [44]. From this perspective, the boundary between human and non-human life is not a given, but a culturally constructed distinction with practical consequences.

The contribution of social sciences becomes particularly salient in the analysis of risk and contamination. Mary Douglas' work on purity, danger, and risk showed that perceptions of contamination are structured by moral and social order rather than solely by biomedical probability [45], [46]. This insight is directly relevant to One Health, especially in the context of zoonotic disease control, biosecurity measures, and surveillance systems. Policies grounded exclusively in technical risk assessment often fail because they neglect how communities interpret danger, responsibility, and acceptable forms of intervention [47], [48].

Epistemologically, the social sciences affirm that valid knowledge about health is not limited to quantitative indicators, surveillance data, or predictive models. Interpretive, qualitative, and experiential forms of knowledge are essential for understanding how health is lived, negotiated, and sustained in everyday contexts [49], [50], [51]. Without this dimension, One Health risks reproducing a technocratic form of reductionism, replacing biological isolation with systemic abstraction while remaining disconnected from lived realities.

Nursing contribution

Florence Nightingale's environmental theory articulated a conception of health grounded in the continuous and dynamic interaction between individuals and their surroundings. Nightingale advanced an understanding of care as the deliberate shaping of environmental conditions—air, water, light, cleanliness, noise, and nutrition—to support the body's inherent life processes [52]. Health, in this view, is not produced through direct technical intervention alone, but emerges from the quality of the environments in which life unfolds.

Epistemologically, Nightingale's work represents a significant departure from both mechanistic biomedical models and abstract systems thinking. Her theory does not conceptualise environment as an external variable acting upon a passive body, but as a relational field continuously negotiated through care practices [52], [53].

A further convergence lies in Nightingale's insistence on systematic observation and statistical evidence as drivers of reform [54], [55]. Her use of data to identify avoidable harm and to redesign environments prefigures contemporary One Health emphases on surveillance and data-informed decision making, but with a distinctive nursing inflection: indicators are meaningful insofar as they translate into concrete environmental adjustments in the places where people live and receive care [56].

Nightingale's practice was also inherently transdisciplinary. Although modern team structures did not exist in her time, her reforms mobilised engineers, architects, and administrators to create sanitary infrastructure that enabled health [57]. This anticipates One Health's insistence that complex problems demand coordinated, cross-sectoral action—and it highlights nursing's capacity to connect “systems” to implementation at the local level, through the design of therapeutic and healthy environments.

Finally, Nightingale's legacy aligns with One Health through an orientation to continuous system improvement: health practice as iterative learning, feedback, and redesign [57]. In One Health terms, nursing contributes a mode of knowing that is proximal and responsibility-bearing, insisting that environmental determinants must be understood as lived, situated, and actionable, and that environmental indicators can be integrated into care, governance, and policy without becoming a merely technocratic exercise [58].

Beyond its historical contributions, nursing offers a distinctive epistemological position within One Health through its sustained engagement with health as lived, situated, and responsibility-bearing practice. Nursing knowledge is generated not only through abstract models or population-level indicators, but through continuous interaction with people, families, and environments in concrete contexts of care [59].

This proximity requires nurses to integrate biological processes, environmental conditions, social relations, and ethical judgment in real time, often under conditions of uncertainty and constraint [60]. In this sense, nursing does not merely operationalize integrative frameworks developed elsewhere; it produces a form of situated knowledge that is intrinsically relational and attentive to the consequences of action.

This positioning gives nursing a critical role in translating One Health principles across scales. While One Health is frequently articulated at the level of policy, governance, and systems design, nursing practice operates at the interface where these abstractions encounter everyday life [58]. Decisions about ventilation, hygiene, nutrition, mobility, medication management, or environmental adaptation are not neutral technical acts, but interventions that enact assumptions about health, risk, and responsibility. Nursing thus functions as a connective epistemic practice, linking system-level reasoning with the moral and practical realities of care, and ensuring that integrative health approaches remain grounded in lived experience rather than collapsing into technocratic abstraction [58].

One Health as epistemological convergence

The formal articulation of One Health, particularly through Calvin Schwabe's formulation of One Medicine and its subsequent institutional uptake, is best understood as a meaningful moment of epistemological convergence. Schwabe did not create these sensibilities from scratch; instead, he gave them a recognisable name and a more organised framing, building on epistemological commitments that had already emerged across several traditions [8], [61]. By offering a precise conceptual vocabulary and a persuasive professional rationale, he helped make previously dispersed ideas more mutually intelligible and, importantly, easier to carry into education, research priorities, and institutional discourse [62]. His contribution can therefore be read as an enabling synthesis, one that supported the circulation of older insights across disciplinary and professional settings.

Viewed in this light, the emergence of One Health reflects a process of conceptual consolidation grounded in substantive intellectual labour. Ideas that had circulated in parallel across philosophy, medicine, veterinary science, ecology, public health, the social sciences, and nursing were not simply “already there” in a usable form; they required a figure capable of connecting, translating, and legitimising them in ways that could reorganise professional boundaries and modes of collaboration. The power of One Health, therefore, lies not only in its deep historical roots but also in the successful articulation of those roots as a shared epistemic programme.

Understanding One Health as epistemological convergence shifts attention away from its formal structure toward its underlying logic. One Health is not defined by the number of sectors it links or the institutional arrangements it enables, but by the types of questions it makes possible and the assumptions it actively resists. It challenges explanatory models that isolate health phenomena within single species, disciplines, or levels of analysis, and rejects the notion that technical control can substitute for contextual and relational understanding.

This epistemological reading also clarifies a recurrent tension within contemporary institutional deployments of One Health. When integration is operationalised primarily through coordination mechanisms, governance arrangements, or technical toolkits, interdisciplinarity risks becoming procedural rather than substantive. In such configurations, complex human–animal–environment relations are often translated into indicators, surveillance categories, and risk management protocols that prioritise measurability and control over contextual understanding. While these instruments may enhance short-term responsiveness, they can inadvertently reproduce the very reductionism that One Health initially sought to overcome, substituting biological isolation with system-level abstraction [63]. From a genealogical perspective, this represents not a failure of implementation, but a narrowing of the epistemic commitments that give One Health its critical and transformative force.

At its core, One Health privileges relational explanations over isolated causal accounts, recognising that health and disease emerge from entangled biological, environmental, and social processes. It favours systems thinking over component-based reasoning, acknowledging that interventions in one domain inevitably reverberate across others often in unpredictable ways. Importantly, it also foregrounds ethical responsibility over purely technical optimisation, insisting that health action remain accountable to the conditions of life it seeks to protect.

This epistemological framing also clarifies the risks inherent in contemporary institutional appropriations of One Health. When reduced to a coordination mechanism among sectors or a toolkit for managing risk, One Health risks losing the very epistemic depth that justified its emergence. In such cases, interdisciplinarity becomes procedural rather than substantive, and integration is measured by administrative alignment rather than conceptual coherence.

By contrast, recognising One Health as a convergence of epistemologies preserves its critical and transformative potential. It reasserts One Health as a way of knowing that demands reflexivity, attentiveness to context, and openness to multiple forms of knowledge. Only under these conditions can One Health function not merely as a framework for action, but as a sustained challenge to reductionist and anthropocentric models of health.

Conclusion

By tracing convergent lines of thought across multiple domains, this analysis has shown that One Health is interdisciplinary not simply because it formally links human, animal, and environmental health, but because it crystallises a long-standing intellectual struggle to move beyond reductionist, anthropocentric, and sector-bound ways of knowing health.

Across philosophy, medicine, veterinary science, ecology, public health, the social sciences, and nursing, and necessarily other traditions beyond the scope of this account, recurring epistemic commitments become visible: health as relational rather than isolable, causality as multi-level rather than linear, and humans as embedded within more-than-human worlds rather than standing outside them. Read genealogically, One Health appears less as a recent policy innovation than as the consolidation of an epistemic orientation that demands integration across explanation, method, and responsibility.

The implications are consequential. If One Health is treated primarily as a coordination device, interdisciplinarity risks becoming procedural, and integration risks being measured by administrative alignment rather than conceptual coherence. Conversely, if its genealogical depth is taken seriously, One Health can sustain methodological pluralism, attend to lived and situated realities, and remain ethically accountable to the ecological and social conditions through which health is produced.

Preserving this epistemological orientation is the condition for One Health to remain transformative rather than harden into a technocratic framework.
