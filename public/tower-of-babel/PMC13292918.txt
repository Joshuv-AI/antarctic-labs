Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a common and potentially fatal vascular disease, with its rupture (rAAA) representing a vascular emergency with a high mortality rate despite recent advances in surgical and endovascular management [1, 2]. Although hypertension is a key risk factor for aneurysm formation and rupture, secondary causes are rarely considered in acute settings.

Pheochromocytoma is a rare catecholamine‐secreting tumor of the adrenal medulla, with an annual incidence of approximately 0.5–0.8 per 100 000 individuals [3]. Excess catecholamine secretion may lead to serious cardiovascular complications, such as myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, and cerebrovascular events [4]. Aortic catastrophes associated with pheochromocytoma, including aortic dissection and aneurysm rupture, have been reported but remain exceedingly rare [5, 6, 7].

Several case reports have suggested a possible association between pheochromocytoma and AAA development or rupture, potentially mediated by prolonged catecholamine‐induced hypertension and direct vascular wall injury [5, 8]. However, rAAA as the initial clinical manifestation leading to the diagnosis of an otherwise unrecognized pheochromocytoma has been reported sporadically. Herein, we describe a rare case of rAAA leading to the diagnosis of clinically unsuspected pheochromocytoma.

Case Presentation

A 57‐year‐old man with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with sudden‐onset severe left lower abdominal pain. He had not undergone regular medical checkups and had no known history of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or endocrine disorders. On arrival, he was in hemorrhagic shock, with blood pressure of 69/43 mmHg and a heart rate of approximately 120 beats per minute. Emergency contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a ruptured infrarenal AAA measuring 76 × 66 mm, accompanied by a large retroperitoneal hematoma consistent with a Fitzgerald type IV rupture (Figure 1a). Additionally, a well‐defined, hypervascular left adrenal mass approximately 20 mm in diameter was incidentally detected, showing strong contrast enhancement (Figure 1b). Given the life‐threatening nature of rAAA, the patient underwent emergent endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) on the day of admission. The procedure was completed successfully without intraoperative complications, and postoperative hemodynamic stability was achieved. Blood pressure was maintained within a range of 100–130/50–80 mmHg. Postoperative evaluation focused on the incidental adrenal lesion. Detailed history‐taking revealed longstanding symptoms suggestive of excess catecholamine, including episodic headaches, excessive sweating, persistent tachycardia since early adulthood, orthostatic hypotension, unexplained weight loss, polyuria, polydipsia, and chronic constipation. Despite these symptoms, the patient had never been evaluated for endocrine disease. Biochemical testing showed normal plasma catecholamine levels, while 24‐h urinary fractionated metanephrines demonstrated a mild elevation of normetanephrine (0.42 mg/day; reference range, 0.1–0.28 mg/day). Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a left adrenal nodule that was hypointense on both T1‐ and T2‐weighted images, with strong peripheral enhancement on dynamic contrast‐enhanced imaging (Figure 2a–c). 123I‐metaiodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy demonstrated intense tracer uptake corresponding to the adrenal lesion, strongly suggesting pheochromocytoma (Figure 2d–f). α‐adrenergic blockade with doxazosin was initiated and gradually titrated. After sufficient preoperative preparation and resolution of the retroperitoneal hematoma, elective laparoscopic left adrenalectomy was performed 6 months after EVAR (Figure 3a). Intraoperative systolic blood pressure remained below 120 mmHg without marked fluctuations. Histopathological examination confirmed pheochromocytoma (Figure 3b,c). Postoperatively, blood pressure normalized without antihypertensive medication, and the patient remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

This case involved a rare but clinically important presentation of rAAA as the initial manifestation of undiagnosed pheochromocytoma. Although hypertension is a major risk factor for AAA, secondary hypertension due to endocrine disorders is not commonly considered in emergency vascular settings [1, 2]. The present case highlights the need for heightened awareness of pheochromocytoma as a potential underlying cause of catastrophic vascular events. Routine endocrine screening for all patients with AAA is not warranted; however, targeted evaluation should be considered when suggestive clinical or imaging findings are present. Excessive catecholamine secretion in pheochromocytoma leads to persistent or episodic hypertension and severe vasoconstriction, which may contribute to vascular wall degeneration and increased susceptibility to aneurysm formation or rupture [4, 5]. Several reports have described the coexistence of pheochromocytoma with AAA or aortic dissection, supporting a possible causal relationship [6, 7, 8]. However, most reported cases involved known adrenal tumors or non‐emergent aortic disease, whereas rAAA as the first clinical manifestation remains extremely rare. Diagnostic challenges were evident in the case reported here. Biochemical findings were borderline and did not meet classical diagnostic thresholds for pheochromocytoma. Previous studies have shown that small or intermittently secreting tumors may exhibit normal or mildly elevated catecholamine levels, potentially leading to underdiagnosis if biochemical criteria alone are used [9]. In such cases, functional imaging and careful clinical assessment play a crucial role. Despite inconclusive biochemical findings, 123I‐MIBG scintigraphy was performed because of a hypervascular adrenal lesion on CT and longstanding catecholamine‐related symptoms. Functional imaging may be considered when clinical suspicion remains high despite equivocal findings. Therapeutic decision‐making in patients with pheochromocytoma complicated by rAAA is complex. Standard management of pheochromocytoma requires adequate preoperative α‐adrenergic blockade to prevent hypertensive crisis, but this is not feasible in emergency situations [3, 10]. In the present case, emergent EVAR allowed rapid hemorrhage control with minimal physiological stress, enabling safe subsequent endocrine evaluation and definitive tumor resection. Similar staged approaches have been reported in patients with pheochromocytoma complicated by aortic dissection or aneurysm [7, 11]. When pheochromocytoma and AAA coexist, treatment priority should be individualized according to aortic risk and hemodynamic status [7, 8]. In life‐threatening aortic events, EVAR may serve as a bridge to delayed adrenalectomy by providing rapid aortic control with less physiologic stress and without direct tumor manipulation [7, 10].

Conclusion

Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm may represent a rare but life‐threatening initial manifestation of undiagnosed pheochromocytoma. In patients with rAAA accompanied by an incidental adrenal mass or symptoms suggestive of catecholamine excess, pheochromocytoma should be considered. Careful evaluation of incidental adrenal lesions and a staged treatment strategy consisting of emergency EVAR followed by elective adrenalectomy after adequate α‐adrenergic blockade can provide a safe and effective approach in this challenging clinical setting.
