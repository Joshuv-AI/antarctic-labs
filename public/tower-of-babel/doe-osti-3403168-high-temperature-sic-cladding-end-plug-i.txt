 SUMMARY
This report documents the design of a High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR)
irradiation experiment intended to evaluate irradiation effects on the hermeticity
of silicon carbide (SiC) end plug specimens under a radial fast neutron flux
gradient at representative light-water reactor (LWR) temperatures of
approximately 300 °C. The overarching goal of this work is to statistically evaluate
SiC end plug hermeticity and mechanical properties following irradiation using the
high-throughput irradiation capability discussed here. Each specimen consists of a
short section of SiC fiber–reinforced SiC (SiC/SiC) tube with a single monolithic
SiC end plug joined to one end. The experiment allows up to 66 specimens to be
irradiated in six different stacks within a dry, sealed irradiation capsule derived
from the previously developed high-temperature SiC/SiC cladding bowing
experiment. The neutronics basis, thermal analysis, and HFIR readiness of the
experiment are discussed in this report for two possible design cases. The first
design case is based on existing approval documentation and components that are
on hand and approved for use, so the experiment insertion would require only
specimen receipt, specimen pre-irradiation characterization, experiment assembly,
and final fabrication package approval. The second design case provides improved
thermal robustness and the preferred end plug geometry but requires fabrication of
a modified holder and revisions to the HFIR approval documentation, in addition
to the other activities required for the first design case, before insertion.

 High-Temperature SiC Cladding End Plug
 Irradiation Design and HFIR Readiness
 1. INTRODUCTION
Silicon carbide fiber–reinforced silicon carbide (SiC/SiC) ceramic matrix composites are a promising
accident-tolerant fuel cladding concept for light-water reactors (LWRs) because of their high-temperature
stability, oxidation resistance, low neutron absorption, and favorable behavior under severe accident
conditions. Despite these advantages, several performance issues must be resolved before SiC/SiC cladding
can be broadly deployed; these issues include hydrothermal corrosion, dimensional stability during
irradiation, joint and end plug performance, and hermeticity retention [1-5].
Hermeticity is a central requirement for fuel cladding concepts to prevent unacceptable release of fission
products and maintain a robust barrier between the fuel and coolant during normal operation and accident
scenarios. For SiC/SiC cladding, hermeticity is complicated by the need to join composite tube bodies to
end plugs on each end of the tube. These joints often include monolithic SiC end plugs, composite tube
material, joining layers that may contain various interphases, and local geometric transitions [6, 7]. Even if
a joint is hermetic before irradiation, its long-term performance can be affected by irradiation-induced
differential swelling, material property mismatches, and residual fabrication stresses, which may cause local
cracking, debonding, or microstructural degradation of the joint region [8].
Loss of hermeticity due to irradiation-induced swelling in SiC is one failure mode of particular interest.
Because swelling in SiC depends strongly on irradiation temperature and dose, differential swelling can
develop across the end plug, joint, and tube components [9-11]. More specifically, under radial neutron flux
(or dose) gradients, swelling will increase at a faster rate in regions of high flux until the swelling
approaches saturation, at which point the swelling in the lower-flux regions will eventually equal the
swelling in the higher-flux regions. Similarly, the strong temperature dependence of SiC swelling results in
greater swelling in the low-temperature regions than in the high-temperature regions. If the temperature
variations continue indefinitely—as in a radially varying temperature state caused by the internal heat
generation of the end plug—then the differential swelling will remain even after swelling saturation. The
differing compositions of the tube, end plug, and joint materials can further amplify the differential swelling
near the joint. This differential swelling can develop local stresses at the plug-to-tube interface, and these
stresses are dependent on the joint design and the dose-dependent magnitude of the differential swelling.
These stresses can produce local cracking or otherwise degrade the joined interface to change the
hermeticity of the specimen [8].
These uncertainties, coupled with limited existing irradiation data on hermeticity retention under LWR-
relevant conditions, create a need for a dedicated irradiation experiment to assess SiC end plug joint
performance under representative LWR conditions, identify potential failure initiation sites and
mechanisms, and compare the ability of different joining approaches to retain hermeticity. To address this
need, the experiment described in this report is designed to irradiate up to 66 SiC specimens, each consisting
of a short 45 mm SiC/SiC tube section joined to a monolithic end plug, in the vertical experiment facilities
(VXFs) of the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) to evaluate joint hermeticity under relevant neutron
irradiation at LWR temperatures of approximately 300 °C. The experiment is intended to quantify the
effects of irradiation on joint hermeticity, characterize any resulting degradation, and compare candidate
end plug designs and joining methods to support the continued development and qualification of SiC/SiC
cladding for LWR applications. Because of the large number of specimens available for the vehicle, the
expected outcomes of the testing include a statistical evaluation of the radiation response of the SiC end
plug materials. The data will be critical for assessing the failure probability of the SiC joint section and for
providing feedback on the joint processing.

 1

The irradiation vehicle is based on the previous dry high-temperature SiC/SiC cladding bowing experiment
platform. This decision was made to reduce the design, analysis, and documentation effort required for
HFIR insertion and to preserve the significant fast neutron flux gradients characteristic of the reflector
positions of HFIR that would drive differential swelling [12, 13]. Six long SiC/SiC cladding tubes were
irradiated in that experiment, whereas the present experiment retains the same general platform but replaces
the long cladding tubes with short end plug specimens arranged in six axial stacks. This report first describes
the irradiation vehicle and the two design cases being evaluated: (i) a 9 mm end plug / 13 mm holder hole
configuration that uses approved documentation and on hand hardware from the previous bowing
experiment and (ii) a 15 mm end plug / 10 mm holder hole configuration that requires revised
documentation and a new holder but provides improved thermal performance. The report then summarizes
the neutronics basis and presents thermal analyses for the end plug specimen stacks, including the effects
of specimen eccentricity and end plug thickness. Finally, the readiness implications of the two design cases
are compared, including the tradeoffs among insertion time, swelling saturation, and thermal performance.

 2. EXPERIMENT DESIGN

 2.1 Irradiation Vehicle
The cladding end plug irradiation vehicle, shown in Figure 1, is derived from the previously developed dry
high-temperature SiC/SiC cladding bowing experiment to reduce the required design, analysis, and
documentation effort [12, 13]. The experiment is compatible with irradiation in HFIR’s large VXFs, which
are highlighted in blue in Figure 2. The large VXFs are compatible with experiment housings with outer
diameters (ODs) of approximately 69 mm (2.75 in.) and lengths of approximately 609.6 mm (24 in.) and
provide an average thermal flux of 4.3 × 1014 n/(cm2⋅s) and an average fast flux of 1.2 × 1013 n/(cm2⋅s) [14].
The platform consists of a sealed aluminum capsule containing an aluminum holder with titanium caps and
tie rod, passive SiC thermometry, and up to 66 end plug specimens. A wave spring is located at the top of
the holder to permit thermal expansion of the holder without allowing the holder to freely slide in the
housing. A series of indexing features and engraving marks controls the angular location of the specimens
relative to the core in order to permit accurate dose tracking of each specimen. The components of the
cladding end plug experiment, except the specimens themselves, are identical to those of the previous
bowing experiment.
Each specimen, illustrated in Figure 1c, consists of a short SiC/SiC tube section with a monolithic end plug
joined to one end and has an overall length of approximately 45 mm. The tubes have nominal dimensions
representative of SiC-based fuel cladding, with an OD of 9.5 mm and an inner diameter of 7.5 mm. The
actual end plug geometry and joint configuration can vary, so a simple representative geometry of a
cylindrical plug located inside the tube is used here for thermal analysis. The specimens will be loaded in
six axial stacks within the internal holder, with up to 11 specimens per holder hole, in the same locations
occupied by the long cladding tubes in the bowing experiment. This stacked configuration allows for many
specimens in a single capsule and exposes the specimens to axial- and radial-dependent dose rates to allow
a range of final accumulated doses in the specimens. Similarly, the significant fast neutron flux gradients
characteristic of the reflector positions of HFIR will drive differential swelling within each specimen.

Temperature control is provided primarily by the gas gap between the internal holder and the capsule
housing and the selected fill gas composition, which together tune the gas gap conductance, holder
temperature, and specimen temperatures. This sealed configuration allows specimen temperatures to be
raised above the HFIR coolant temperature and controlled near representative LWR temperatures of
approximately 300 °C. To maximize the number of specimens possible to package in one capsule and to
ensure ease of disassembly, the specimens are not centered in the holder holes with centering thimbles;
thus, each specimen can be centered, eccentric, or in contact with the holder wall. This loose-stack condition
is important for the thermal analysis because specimen-to-holder contact changes the local heat transfer
path and can alter the predicted temperature state of the specimen.

Following irradiation, the experiment assembly will be transported to and disassembled at an Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) hot cell facility. The outer housing of the experiment will be carefully cut
open at the upper plenum, just above the centering cap and tie rod. After extracting the inner assembly
from the outer housing and removing the locknut and tie rod, the centering caps can be detached and the
end plug specimens can be removed from the holder. The holder holes are sized to accommodate the
swelling of the end plug specimens with sufficient clearance so that the specimens can slide freely out of
the holder in the hot cells. The primary post-irradiation metric will be plug-to-tube hermeticity; other
supporting measurements may include dimensional inspection, visual examination, x-ray computed
tomography, and selected destructive examinations of the joint region such as end plug push-out testing.
The passive SiC thermometry included in the experiment will be used to assess the irradiation
temperatures achieved during the experiment.

 2.2 Design Cases
HFIR is currently scheduled for two long outages. The first is scheduled for July 2028 through August
2029, and a second similar outage is scheduled after only four cycles in late 2029. As a result, the near-term
readiness of the end plug experiment is of greater importance than usual so that the swelling of the
specimens has adequate irradiation time to approach saturation. Two design cases are compared in this
report to evaluate the tradeoff between near-term readiness and preferred experiment thermal performance.
For both cases, a 45 mm overall specimen length is used with 11 specimens per stack, for a total of 66. The
holder OD or fill gas composition was adjusted iteratively for each case so that the average temperature of
the end plugs is predicted to be approximately 300 °C. All other experiment components are identical
among cases, unless noted otherwise below.

 4

The first case, shown in Figure 3a, uses 9 mm thick end plugs and 13 mm diameter holder holes. This
configuration is permitted under the HFIR documentation previously approved for the bowing experiment
and uses existing hardware from that experiment that is already approved for HFIR use, aside from the new
specimens. As a result, it represents the most immediately executable design. However, the large holder
hole allows greater specimen eccentricity within each stack, which increases the possible variation in
temperature gradients depending on whether an individual specimen is centered or touching the holder.
The second case, shown in Figure 3b, uses 15 mm thick end plugs and a modified holder with 10 mm
diameter specimen holes. This is the preferred end plug thickness and holder geometry. The thicker plug
represents a more desirable end plug geometry, and the smaller holder hole improves thermal performance
by limiting the range of possible specimen eccentricity within the stack while still allowing adequate
clearance to accommodate the swelling of the specimen. The main disadvantage of this case is the time
required to receive HFIR approval for revisions to the existing documentation and to fabricate a new holder
before experiment assembly can begin.

At the time of this writing, the desired end plug specimen geometry and test matrix have not been finalized.
Thus, Case 1 and Case 2 are intended to provide bounding cases to permit trade-off comparison between
the designs to guide this finalization, with particular consideration paid to the upcoming long outages of
HFIR and the thermal performance of the experiment.

 3. DESIGN ANALYSES
The present end plug experiment is a derivative of the previously developed dry SiC/SiC cladding bowing
irradiation platform. The neutronics basis is taken from the previous bowing analyses, whereas the thermal
analysis is updated to include the end plug specimen stacks. The following sections summarize the
neutronics basis and describe the thermal analysis results for the two end plug design cases.

 3.1 Neutronic Analysis
The neutronics basis for this experiment is taken from the previous SiC/SiC cladding bowing analysis that
was performed using Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) models of the experiment in the VXF-19 position of
HFIR, as detailed in previous reports [12, 13]. Although the previous neutronics analysis was conducted
with long cladding tubes rather than discrete tube sections with end plugs, the differences in specimen
geometry are expected to have little effect on the average peak heat generation rates (HGRs), Gaussian fits,
or displacements per atom (dpa) accumulation used in subsequent analyses. Accordingly, the previous
neutronics analysis continues to provide the design basis for expected heat generation and dose
accumulation in the end plug experiment.
In the previous analysis, the peak HGRs were found to occur at end of cycle (EOC), when HFIR’s control
elements are fully withdrawn. The average peak HGRs at EOC for the front specimen holder positions,
center specimen holder positions, back specimen holder positions, aluminum components, and titanium
components are 1.89, 1.70, 1.56, 1.93, and 2.67 W/g, respectively. These values were used in the design
basis thermal models with Gaussian fits of the HGRs with respect to axial position in HFIR.
The previous analysis also indicated that the maximum specimen dose rate is 1.22 × 10−3 dpa/day for the
specimens in the two front holder positions, whereas the specimens in the middle and back holder positions
achieve maximum dose rates of 1.04 × 10−3 dpa/day and 0.92 × 10−3 dpa/day, respectively. Here, dpa is
used for convenient correlation with existing SiC swelling models, and 1 dpa is approximately equal to
1 × 1021 nfast/cm2. The three-pair arrangement of holder positions provides a range of irradiation conditions
in a single experiment and is intended to support comparison of end plug performance as a function of total
dose. The spatially dependent accumulated doses in the cladding tubes after four HFIR cycles are shown in
Figure 4 to illustrate the variation in dose expected.

 3.2 Thermal Analysis
The 3D CAD model for the dry SiC end plug experiment, shown in Figure 1, was imported into ANSYS
for thermal analysis. The HGRs reported in Section 3.1 were applied to the experiment components, and a
convection boundary condition was applied to the outer wall of the capsule with a bulk coolant temperature
of 50 °C and a convection heat transfer coefficient of 12,400 W/m2⋅°C [16]. The top and bottom of the
experiment are assumed to be adiabatic. The ORNL Irradiation Engineering Group manages a database of
design and analysis calculations (DACs) that include temperature- and dose-dependent thermophysical
material properties, which were used in this analysis. In addition to the DACs, the SiC/SiC composite tubes
were modified with a density of 2.7 g/cc and a thermal conductivity of 3 W/m⋅K, which is typical of
composite SiC/SiC materials after achieving swelling saturation [17]. These experiments are unlikely to
reach full saturation, so the thermal conductivity will likely be higher. As a result, the temperature variations
in the specimens will likely be lower than those reported here.
The heat generated internally within each component must be conducted through the various components
and gas gaps to ultimately be rejected to the HFIR coolant. As a result, the gas gap size and the fill gas
composition are often tuned to control the specimen temperature. In this experiment, the holder-to-housing
gas gap is the most influential gas gap for the specimen temperature. Case 1 is intended to use an existing
holder that is approved for HFIR insertion, so the diameter of the holder is fixed; therefore, the fill gas
composition must be adjusted to achieve the desired specimen temperatures. Case 2 requires a new holder
to be fabricated, so the holder diameter may be adjusted to achieve the desired specimen temperatures with
a fill gas of 90% He, balance Ar to maintain flexibility to account for manufacturing tolerances in the holder
diameter.
The thermal analysis is intended to evaluate both the average temperature of the specimens and the
temperature gradients through the end plug, joint, and tube because these gradients will affect the swelling
strain gradients along with the dose rate gradients. While the dose rate effects are expected to have a larger
effect on the swelling gradients and joint hermeticity, it is nonetheless important to characterize the
expected temperature gradients in the end plug specimens.

3.2.1 Thermal Results
The initial thermal models were intended to establish baselines for each case that optimized the average
specimen temperatures to approximately 300 °C. The results are summarized in Table 1, and temperature
contour plots are shown in Figure 5. Rather than taking the average temperature of the entire specimen, the
average temperature was calculated based on the volume of the end plug geometry itself since the
temperature of the lower portion of the tube is not critical to the experiment. Furthermore, the temperature
of the lower portion of the tubes is generally lower than the end plug region, so including the whole tube
would skew the results lower than what is representative of the end plug region. It is important to note that
the averages are volume-weighted rather than nodal-weighted, so they are not dependent on local mesh
densities. Additionally, 97.5% maximum and minimum temperatures represent the bounds of the
temperature region that includes 97.5% of the volume of the end plugs.
The temperature gradients are defined as the difference in temperature between the centroid of the end plug
and the outside diameter of the tube at the same axial position as the end plug centroid. These values were
calculated with a command script in ANSYS Mechanical for four different radial directions for each
specimen, each 90 degrees apart. These gradient values are intended to capture the magnitude of the
temperature gradients that may affect the differential swelling of the end plugs.

approximately 70 °C and 50 °C for Case 1 and Case 2, respectively. This range allows end plug performance
to be evaluated over several irradiation temperatures within a single experiment. At the same time, near-
identical temperatures can be achieved among multiple specimens in particular axial and holder position
combinations, particularly for specimens in the same axial position within one of the three holder position
pairs. This allows the experiment to produce meaningful statistical results at several different irradiation
temperatures. It is worth noting that variations in the irradiation temperature will still lead to the same
radiation damage mechanism, namely point-defect swelling in SiC [18]. A temperature range of
270–330 °C alone would cause a difference in linear swelling of approximately 0.05% [9]. Therefore, a
temperature profile that differs slightly from the typical LWR coolant temperature will not be critical for
assessing end plug performance.

3.2.2 Effects of Specimen Eccentricity
The initial baseline models were analyzed with all specimens centered in the holder; however, the
experiment does not include centering thimbles. Centering thimbles were omitted to maximize the number
of specimens that can be packaged in one capsule and to ensure that the specimens can be easily removed
in the hot cells after irradiation. As a result, many of the specimens will most likely be touching the holder
at any given time due to the handling of the experiment and vibrations of the coolant flow during irradiation.
The next round of modeling investigated the effects of specimen eccentricities on the specimen
temperatures and temperature gradients. To do so, one specimen near the center of each stack was shifted
radially to just touch the holder, while all other specimens remained centered. These six specimens were
shifted in different directions to represent the random nature of the specimen positions. Specimens near the
center of the stack were chosen since they would produce the most significant changes in temperature
distributions due to the higher HGRs near the midplane of HFIR. The thermal analysis cases were then
reanalyzed with only the six specimen positions changing to directly evaluate the effects of the
eccentricities. The results from this analysis are summarized in Table 2. Here, the average temperature
refers to the average temperature of the end plugs of the six offset specimens only. Note that the average
temperatures here are above the target 300 °C temperature because the specimens selected are concentrated
near the midplane of HFIR.

3.2.3 Effects of End Plug Thickness
To evaluate the effects of the end plug thickness alone, the centered Case 1 model was rerun with end plug
thicknesses of 4.5 mm and 15 mm, while all other parameters remained the same. Whereas the 9 mm end
plug thickness represents the limit of the currently approved HFIR documentation, the 15 mm thickness
represents a more preferred end plug thickness that has been used in more recent testing. The 4.5 mm
thickness represents the thickness used in previous end plug irradiations in the flux trap of HFIR [6].
The analysis results revealed a slight increase in average end plug temperature with increased thickness, as
expected due to the increased internal heat generation associated with greater thickness. The 4.5 mm and
15 mm end plugs resulted in average temperatures that were approximately 10 °C lower and 11 °C higher
than the 9 mm end plug results, respectively. The temperature gradient varied only slightly between cases,
with the 4.5 mm, 9 mm, and 15 mm cases resulting in 8.5 °C, 8.1 °C, and 8.7 °C gradients, respectively. As
a result, this analysis shows that the end plug thickness is not a primary driving parameter for the
experiment’s thermal performance if the holder OD and fill gas composition can accommodate the small
changes in average specimen temperatures.

 3.3 Swelling Saturation Analysis
The irradiation-induced swelling of SiC is strongly dependent on both dose and temperature, which vary
depending on specimen location within the experiment. Therefore, the predicted specimen temperatures
and dose distributions were used to estimate the volumetric swelling and swelling saturation for each end
plug specimen position. The average accumulated dose for each end plug was calculated from curve fits of
the previous neutronics analysis results [13]. The average end plug temperature for each end plug was
determined from the thermal analysis performed here, as shown in Figure 6. These values were then used
with the dose- and temperature-dependent SiC swelling correlation from Katoh et al. to calculate the
volumetric swelling and swelling saturation for each specimen position as a function of HFIR irradiation
cycles [9]. These calculations are intended to provide average values for each specimen rather than to
quantify the swelling gradients within each specimen, which would be driven by local temperature and dose
gradients in the specimens that are not accounted for here.
The calculated results for Case 1 are shown as a function of HFIR irradiation cycles in Figure 8 for each
holder–position pair and each axial specimen position. As expected, the volumetric swelling and swelling
saturation increase rapidly over the first several HFIR cycles and then increase more gradually with
subsequent cycles. The highest swelling saturation occurs near the axial midplane of the experiment, where
the greatest accumulated doses and temperatures occur. This result reveals that the combined effects of the
higher dose rates and reduced saturation swelling—caused by the higher relative temperatures—at the
midplane outweigh the reduced swelling rate also caused by the higher temperatures. The volumetric
swelling itself reveals a similar trend for the first several HFIR cycles, before the maximum swelling begins
to shift lower in the stack due to the lower temperatures there. The front holder positions, 2 and 3, reach the
highest saturation because they are closest to the core and receive the highest dose rate, followed by holder
positions 1 and 4 and then holder positions 5 and 6. This, combined with the axial saturation distribution,
provides a range of swelling saturation levels within a single experiment, which will support comparison
of end plug hermeticity over a range of accumulated irradiation conditions. For example, after five cycles,
the swelling saturation for Case 1 is expected to range from approximately 43% to 78% with an average of
approximately 66%.

The calculated swelling and saturation values for Case 2 are within 0.06% and 1%, respectively, of those
reported for Case 1. This is because the specimen positions and dose rates are nearly identical between the
cases, and the small differences in average end plug temperature have only a minor effect on the swelling
and saturation. The effect of specimen eccentricity on swelling saturation was also evaluated for the six
offset specimens included in the eccentric thermal analysis. For this comparison, the dose for each selected
specimen was held constant and the swelling and saturation calculations were repeated using the average
end plug temperature of the offset specimens. The resulting change in calculated swelling saturation was
small, with maximum differences of less than 0.4% for Case 1 and less than 0.1% for Case 2 over the first
10 HFIR cycles. Similarly, the change in the volumetric swelling was less than 0.08% for Case 1 and 0.02%
for Case 2. These results indicate that neither the differences in average end plug temperature between the
two design cases nor those caused by specimen eccentricity are large enough to materially affect the average
swelling or saturation of the specimens. However, the changes in temperature gradients in the eccentric
specimens would affect the local swelling gradients in different regions of the specimen, which may affect
the end plug joint performance.

 4. HFIR READINESS
The two design cases have different readiness implications. Case 1, with 9 mm end plugs and 13 mm holder
holes, is already fully approved for insertion into HFIR, and all required parts are on hand except for the
specimens. This means that the remaining readiness activities are primarily specimen receipt, pre-
irradiation characterization, and the experiment assembly and documentation. This case therefore provides
the shortest path to insertion and the lowest risk of schedule delay. At the time of this writing, it is feasible
to insert this experiment option as early as cycle 520 in early 2027 with prompt specimen delivery and pre-
irradiation characterization. This would allow the specimens near the midplane of HFIR to reach swelling
saturations as high as ~90% by the end of cycle 529, as shown in Figure 9, before the planned long outages.
Case 2, with 15 mm end plugs and 10 mm holder holes, provides moderately improved thermal performance
but requires a new holder to be manufactured and minor revisions to several previously approved
documents, including drawings, safety calculations, and experiment authorization documentation.
Depending on the specimen delivery timeline, this would likely delay insertion relative to Case 1. At the
time of this writing, it is feasible to insert this experiment option as early as cycle 524 in late 2027 with
prompt specimen delivery and pre-irradiation characterization. This would allow the specimens near the
midplane of HFIR to reach ~80% swelling saturation by the end of cycle 529, as shown in Figure 9, before
the planned long outages.
As a result, the primary tradeoff between design cases is between the thermal performance of the experiment
and the percentage of swelling saturation achieved by the specimens. Case 1 is suitable if insertion timing
is the dominant constraint to achieve the highest swelling saturation possible. Case 2 is preferred if the
thermal performance of the experiment is valued more highly than the ~10% loss in swelling saturation
required to modify documentation and manufacture a new holder.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
This report documents the design basis and HFIR readiness status for a high-temperature SiC cladding end
plug irradiation experiment intended to evaluate the effects of irradiation-induced dimensional change and
differential swelling on plug-to-tube joint hermeticity. The experiment is derived from the previously
developed dry SiC/SiC cladding bowing irradiation platform and uses the same general experiment design.
In the present design, the six long cladding tube specimens used in the bowing experiment are replaced by
six axial stacks of short end plug specimens, allowing up to 66 total specimens to be irradiated in a single
capsule at representative LWR temperatures of approximately 300 °C.
The neutronics basis for the experiment is taken from the previous bowing experiment analysis. Because
the end plug specimens occupy the same general holder positions as the long cladding tubes, the previous
HGR and dose calculations remain appropriate for the present design basis. The previous analysis predicts
maximum dose rates of approximately 1.22 × 10−3, 1.04 × 10−3, and 0.92 × 10−3 dpa/day at the midplane of
HFIR for the front, center, and back holder positions, respectively. This radial variation and the axial
variation of the HFIR flux profile provide a range of irradiation conditions in a single capsule. Furthermore,
the large number of specimens can generate meaningful statistical results from a single experiment.
The primary new analysis for this report is the thermal evaluation of two design cases. Case 1 uses 9 mm
thick end plugs in the existing 13 mm diameter holder holes. This case is already approved for HFIR
insertion and uses existing parts, but it is less thermally robust because the larger holder holes allow greater
specimen eccentricity within the loose stacks, which leads to decreased specimen temperatures and
increased temperature gradients in the specimens. In the centered configuration, Case 1 produces an average
end plug temperature of 310.3 °C, which is above the 300 °C target but represents the lowest achievable
temperature with the existing holder and 100% He fill gas. When selected midplane specimens are offset
to touch the holder, the average temperature of those specimens decreases by approximately 18 °C and the
maximum end plug temperature gradient increases from 8.1 to 38.3 °C.
Case 2 uses the preferred 15 mm thick end plugs in a modified holder with 10 mm diameter specimen holes
and a slightly larger OD. This case requires fabrication of a new holder and minor revisions to previously
approved drawings, safety calculations, and experiment authorization documentation, but it provides
improved thermal control due to the reduced range of the possible specimen eccentricity and the ability to
tune the gas gaps to achieve the target average temperature. In the centered configuration, Case 2 produces
an average end plug temperature of 301.8 °C using a 51.9 mm holder OD and 90% He, balance Ar fill gas.
When selected midplane specimens are offset to touch the holder, the average temperature decreases by
only approximately 3 °C, and the maximum temperature gradient increases from 10.4 °C to 18.5 °C.
Therefore, the main thermal advantage of Case 2 is the substantially reduced sensitivity of the specimen
temperature distribution to specimen eccentricity.
The comparison between design cases is summarized in Table 3. Case 1 provides the lowest-risk path to
near-term insertion because the design work and HFIR approval documentation are complete, and all
required hardware is available except for the specimens. With prompt specimen receipt, Case 1 could
support insertion as early as HFIR cycle 520, following completion of specimen pre-irradiation
characterization, experiment assembly, and final fabrication package approval. This would allow the
highest-dose specimens to reach approximately 90% swelling saturation before the planned long HFIR
outages. Case 2 provides the more thermally robust design and uses the preferred end plug geometry.
However, although its design basis is established, it would require fabrication and approval of a modified
holder and revisions to HFIR approval documentation, in addition to the activities required for Case 1,
which would likely delay insertion until at least cycle 524 and reduce the projected swelling saturation to
approximately 80%. Therefore, the design selection depends on whether the program prioritizes near-term
readiness and irradiation time (swelling saturation) or improved thermal performance and preferred
specimen geometry.
