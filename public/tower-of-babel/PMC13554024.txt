Retrospective single-center cytohistologic correlation study of fine-needle aspiration cytology for excised superficial enlarged lymph node lesions in different disease entities
Authors: Xiang Shi, Yingyuan Wangsun, Yan Lei, Sanyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymph nodes are essential components of the human immune system, and lymph node lesions present considerable challenges for pathological diagnosis due to their complexity and heterogeneity. Conventional diagnosis mainly relies on histopathological examination of surgical specimens, whereas fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) of superficial enlarged lymph nodes is a rapid, minimally invasive, and clinically valuable tool for preliminary etiological identification and targeted treatment planning. This study aimed to summarize the cytomorphological features of FNAC in four major categories of superficial enlarged lymph node lesions (lymph node reactive hyperplasia (LNRH), non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), and lymph node metastatic carcinoma (LNMC), evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of FNAC using histopathological results as the gold standard, and provide evidence-based references for clinical preliminary diagnosis and treatment decision-making. We retrospectively analyzed 133 consecutive cases of lymph node FNAC followed by surgical resection and histopathological confirmation at a single center from January 2023 to December 2024. FNAC smears were stained with Liu’s solution, histopathological sections with hematoxylin and eosin, and immunohistochemistry was applied for disease-specific marker detection. The overall diagnostic accuracy of FNAC was 89.47%. The sensitivity was highest for LNMC (95.52%, 95% CI: 87.23–98.97%), and the specificity was 100.00% for HL (95% CI: 97.19–100.00%). NHL showed a sensitivity of 80.95% and a positive predictive value of 84.00%, while LNRH had a negative predictive value of 90.91%. Chi-square test revealed significant intergroup differences in cellular atypia, cell distribution patterns, and characteristic cells among the four lesion types (P < 0.001). In conclusion, FNAC is a moderately reliable minimally invasive screening tool for superficial enlarged lymph node lesions. Clarifying its diagnostic efficacy and distinct cytomorphological features provides valuable morphological and quantitative evidence for clinicians to conduct preliminary classification and formulate appropriate treatment strategies.Keywords: Fine-needle aspiration cytology, Lymph node reactive hyperplasia, Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, Lymph node metastatic carcinoma

Introduction

As a critical component of the human immune system, lymph nodes are widely distributed and function in lymph fluid filtration, pathogen clearance, and immune response initiation [1]. In pathological states including infection, inflammation, and neoplasia, lymph nodes frequently become enlarged and serve as primary diagnostic targets [2]. Such lesions reflect local disorders and may indicate systemic diseases, making them essential for pathological evaluation [3]. Although multiple diagnostic modalities exist, accurate diagnosis of lymph node lesions remains clinically challenging. Histopathological examination represents the gold standard for classifying lymph node lesions [4], whereas FNAC is a convenient, minimally invasive tool for rapid preliminary assessment of superficial lymphadenopathy [5].

Enlarged lymph nodes are generally categorized into benign reactive hyperplasia, malignant lymphoma, and metastatic carcinoma, with multiple subtypes in each group [6, 7]. FNAC is the first-line minimally invasive method, aiming primarily to distinguish benign from malignant lesions and guide further management rather than define specific entities [8]. Pathologists therefore focus on these key differential diagnoses during FNAC evaluation.

This study aimed to: (1) summarize the cytomorphological features of FNAC in four major superficial lymph node lesions; (2) assess the diagnostic performance of FNAC using histopathology and immunohistochemistry as references; and (3) provide clinical guidance for the preliminary classification of lymph node lesions by FNAC.

Materials and methods

Patient demographics and inclusion/exclusion criteria

A total of 133 consecutive patients who underwent lymph node FNAC at the Department of Pathology, Qianjiang Central Hospital, from January 2023 to December 2024 were enrolled in this study. Consecutive enrollment was defined as the inclusion of all patients meeting the study’s inclusion criteria and no exclusion criteria during the study period, with no selective sampling based on lesion type, patient age, or clinical symptoms. All patients presented with superficial enlarged lymph nodes and underwent subsequent surgical resection for histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation. Inclusion criteria:

(1) Patients with clinically detected superficial enlarged lymph nodes who underwent FNAC; (2) Cases with complete histopathological and immunohistochemical results after surgical resection for diagnostic verification; (3) Patients who provided written informed consent for study participation and publication of relevant data.

Exclusion criteria:

(1) Cases with inadequate FNAC smears (insufficient cellularity, severe artifacts, or poor staining quality) that precluded reliable cytological evaluation; (2) Patients who received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy prior to FNAC; (3) Cases lacking complete clinical data or follow-up histopathological confirmation; (4) Patients who declined to provide written informed consent.

FNAC procedure and case review

FNAC procedures were performed by experienced pathologists and clinicians at the study center using a 22-gauge disposable sterile aspiration needle attached to a 10 mL disposable syringe, with no local anesthesia administered for superficial lymph node puncture. For each patient, the target lymph node was stabilized by manual compression of the puncture site; the needle was inserted into the center of the enlarged lymph node, and negative pressure was applied by pulling the syringe plunger. The needle was moved back and forth 5–10 times within the lymph node parenchyma to ensure adequate cellular sampling, after which negative pressure was released prior to needle withdrawal. After puncture, the aspirated cellular material was immediately extruded onto clean glass slides and spread into a thin, uniform smear using a second glass slide; 2–3 smears were prepared for each lesion to ensure sufficient sampling. Smears were fixed in absolute ethanol for 30 s and then stained. All FNAC smears were independently reviewed by two senior pathologists; a consensus diagnosis was reached for all cases, and discordant diagnoses were resolved via re-evaluation by a third senior pathologist and group discussion. Only cases with both FNAC and subsequent tissue biopsy results were included in statistical analysis; FNAC cases without tissue biopsy confirmation were excluded.

Cytological and histopathological staining

Liu’s ready-to-use stain for FNAC smears

FNAC smears were stained using Liu’s stain method, which consists of two solutions: Solution A (cytoplasmic red staining) and Solution B (nuclear purple staining). Briefly, cell smears were fixed with absolute ethanol for 30 s, incubated with Solution A for 15 s, followed by Solution B for an additional 15 s. After thorough rinsing with distilled water, smears were air-dried and observed under a light microscope.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histopathological sections

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue Sect. (4 μm thick) were incubated with hematoxylin reagent at 37 °C for 5 min, then transferred to a hematoxylin staining bath for an additional 5 min. Sections were differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid-ethanol solution for 1 s, immediately followed by eosin staining at 37 °C for 3 min. After gradient dehydration with ethanol and clearing with xylene, sections were mounted with neutral balsam, and images were captured using a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Standardized IHC staining protocol

Fresh tissue specimens were fixed in 4% neutral buffered formaldehyde for 24 hours, then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. FFPE Sect. (4 µm thick) were mounted on adhesion slides and baked at 60 °C for 4 hours. Sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer under high pressure for 3 minutes. Slides were then incubated with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 30 minutes to block endogenous peroxidase activity, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% Tween-20 (PBS-T), and blocked with 10% goat serum at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the following rabbit polyclonal primary antibodies (incubated at 4 °C overnight): anti-CD20 (cat. no. 60271-1-Ig, 1:200; ProteinTech Group, Wuhan, China), anti-CD3 (cat. no. 17617-1-AP, 1:200; ProteinTech Group), anti-CD21 (cat. no. 24374-1-AP, 1:200; ProteinTech Group), anti-CD30 (cat. no. 17852-1-AP, 1:200; ProteinTech Group), anti-pan-cytokeratin (PCK, EPR28285-45, 1:100; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-leukocyte common antigen (LCA, cat. no. 20103-1-AP, 1:100; ProteinTech Group), and anti-BCL2 (cat. no. 12789-1-AP, 1:100; ProteinTech Group). After overnight primary antibody incubation, slides were incubated with a secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP multimer, cat. no. PV-0023, 1:100; Bioss, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 20 minutes. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Finally, slides were dehydrated, cleared, mounted with neutral balsam, and imaged using a light microscope.

Rationale for antibody marker selection

IHC staining was performed to verify the clonality of lymphoid cells, identify tumor cell lineages, and differentiate the four major lymph node lesion types. Marker selection was based on clinical diagnostic needs and international pathological guidelines:

Lymphoid lineage identification:

CD20 (B-cell specific marker) and CD3 (T-cell specific marker) were used to distinguish the B/T-cell origin of lymphoma and confirm the mixed T/B-cell population in LNRH; CD21 (follicular dendritic cell marker) was used to evaluate the integrity of the lymph node follicular structure, a key feature distinguishing LNRH from lymphoma.

HL-specific diagnosis:

CD30 was selected as the core marker for identifying Reed-Sternberg (RS) cells, the pathological hallmark of HL.

LNMC identification:

PCK (pan-cytokeratin) was used to detect the epithelial origin of tumor cells, and LCA (leukocyte common antigen) was used as a negative marker for carcinoma to distinguish metastatic tumor cells from endogenous lymphoid cells.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Histopathological results were used as the gold standard to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of FNAC for lymph node lesions. The overall diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of FNAC for the four lesion types (LNRH, NHL, HL, LNMC) were calculated. 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were used to reflect the sampling variability of each diagnostic index. A 4 × 4 confusion matrix was constructed to quantify diagnostic concordance and error distribution between FNAC and the gold standard for all lesion types.

For intergroup comparison of cytological features, three key categorical indicators were selected: cellular atypia (none/mild/moderate/severe), cell distribution pattern (scattered/diffuse/clustered), and presence of characteristic cells (present/absent). The chi-square (χ²) test was applied to compare the distribution of these features among the four lesion types, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and demographic characteristics

The neck was the most common site for superficial enlarged lymph nodes across all lesion types, including lymphoma and LNMC. Axillary lymph nodes were also frequently involved. Malignant lymphadenopathy (lymphoma and LNMC) was more prevalent in patients aged over 40 years. Cervical lymph nodes represented the predominant metastatic site for papillary thyroid carcinoma, whereas axillary lymph nodes were the major metastatic site for breast cancer. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics, including age distribution, sex, and lesion location, are summarized (Table 1). Specific information regarding histopathological subtypes, lesion location, and clinical features for NHL, HL, and LNMC is also provided (Table 1). Lymph node reactive hyperplasia (LNRH)

Histopathologically, LNRH was characterized by an intact lymph node architecture with regular distribution of lymphoid follicles, interfollicular areas, and medullary sinuses. IHC staining revealed the typical scattered distribution of the T-lymphocyte marker CD3 and follicular distribution of the B-lymphocyte marker CD20. FNAC smears showed a hemorrhagic background (attributable to puncture-induced bleeding) with an abundant mixed population of T and B lymphocytes of varying sizes and occasional phagocytes, with no cellular atypia. Since the T-cell-rich areas of the lymph node are larger and more easily sampled by puncture, small lymphocytes were more numerous than large lymphocytes in cytological smears (Fig. 2).

A FNAC smear (Liu’s stain): Scattered mixed T/B lymphocytes of variable sizes, hemorrhagic background, no cellular atypia. B H&E staining: Intact lymph node architecture with regular distribution of lymphoid follicles, interfollicular areas, and medullary sinuses. C CD20 IHC: Follicular distribution of B lymphocytes (germinal centers). D CD3 IHC: Scattered distribution of T lymphocytes in interfollicular areas. E CD21 IHC: Intact follicular dendritic cell network in germinal centers (key feature of LNRH)

Non-hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL)

NHL is a clonal lymphoproliferative disorder, histopathologically characterized by the uniform proliferation of neoplastic lymphocytes, typically in a diffuse pattern. IHC staining confirmed disrupted lymph node architecture and clonal proliferation of neoplastic T or B lymphocytes. FNAC smears revealed a large number of neoplastic lymphocytes with consistent morphology, slightly increased cell size, and distinct atypical features (Fig. 3). The detailed clinical and pathological information of 21 NHL cases diagnosed by FNAC is shown in Table 1, including major subtypes such as follicular lymphoma (FL), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma (MALT), Burkitt lymphoma, and small lymphocytic lymphoma/chronic lymphocytic leukemia (SLL/CLL).

Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL)

HL is pathologically distinguished by the presence of characteristic Reed-Sternberg (RS) cells. Histopathologically, scattered binucleated or mononucleated RS cells (typically large with prominent nucleoli) were observed, with consistent CD30 positivity on IHC staining. FNAC smears often showed a small number of large-nucleated or binucleated cells against a background of numerous mixed small lymphocytes. A diagnosis of HL should be suspected when these cells are significantly larger than the surrounding lymphocytes (Fig. 4). Table 1 presents the relevant clinical and pathological information of 5 HL cases, including 4 cases of nodular sclerosing Hodgkin lymphoma (NSHL) and 1 case of nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (NLPHL), all located in the neck.

Lymph node metastatic carcinoma (LNMC)

Lymphatic metastasis is a major route of cancer dissemination, making LNMC a relatively common finding in clinical practice. FNAC smears showed metastatic cancer cells that were frequently distributed in clusters, with some forming organoid structures and exhibiting significant cellular atypia. In contrast, endogenous lymphocytes were relatively scattered and lacked nest-like clustering (Fig. 5). Diagnostic efficacy of FNAC

Using histopathological results as the gold standard, the overall diagnostic concordance rate of FNAC for lymph node lesions was 89.47% (119/133), with diagnostic efficacy varying across different lesion types (Table 2). LNMC exhibited the highest diagnostic sensitivity (95.52%, 95% CI: 87.23–98.97%), and sensitivity decreased to 90.70% (95% CI: 77.95–97.41%) when papillary thyroid carcinoma was excluded from the analysis. HL had a diagnostic specificity of 100.00% (95% CI: 97.19–100.00%) with a sensitivity of 80.00% (95% CI: 28.36–99.49%). For NHL, the sensitivity was 80.95% (95% CI: 56.34–94.43%) and the PPV was 84.00% (95% CI: 60.40–96.68%). LNRH showed a sensitivity of 85.00% (95% CI: 70.17–94.36%) and an NPV of 90.91% (95% CI: 83.09–95.80%). The specificity of FNAC for all four lesion types was ≥ 94.00% (Table 2).

Cellular atypia:

LNRH was characterized by absent or mild atypia in the vast majority of cases (90.00%, 36/40), with only 10.00% (4/40) showing moderate atypia and no cases of severe atypia. In sharp contrast, severe atypia was the predominant finding in NHL (76.19%, 16/21), HL (80.00%, 4/5), and LNMC (89.55%, 60/67), with only a small subset of cases presenting moderate atypia and no absent or mild atypia observed in these three lesion types.

Cell distribution pattern:

Scattered distribution was the typical morphological feature of LNRH (85.00%, 34/40), while diffuse distribution was the main pattern for NHL (90.48%, 19/21) and HL (80.00%, 4/5). Clustered distribution was the hallmark of LNMC, accounting for 92.54% (62/67) of such cases, and this distribution pattern was rarely observed in the other three lesion types.

Presence of characteristic cells:

LNRH had the lowest positive rate (10.00%, 4/40). In comparison, NHL, HL, and LNMC all exhibited a 100% positive rate for characteristic cells, with each lesion type displaying its distinct characteristic cellular morphology.

Discussion

Lymph nodes are a core component of the human immune system, and their lesions include benign reactive hyperplasia, malignant lymphoma, and metastatic carcinoma. FNAC is capable of identifying the characteristic cytological features of each lesion type [6, 9–11]. LNRH is characterized by a mixed population of medium and small lymphocytes, abundant phagocytes, and the absence of cellular atypia [12–14]. Lymphoma presents with morphologically uniform clonal atypical lymphocytes, and HL can be distinguished by pathognomonic RS cells [15–20]. LNMC is defined by clustered highly atypical tumor cells, with nodal metastasis patterns correlating to the primary tumor site [21]. These distinct cytomorphological features support FNAC as a minimally invasive first-line screening tool for enlarged lymph nodes in clinical practice.

This study confirms the moderate clinical value of FNAC in the diagnosis of lymph node lesions, with an overall diagnostic accuracy of 89.47%. The high sensitivity of FNAC for LNMC (95.52%) and perfect specificity for HL (100.00%) both stem from their easily identifiable cytological characteristics [22, 23]. Quantitative analysis of key cytological features further supports the diagnostic value of FNAC: cellular atypia aids in benign-malignant differentiation, cellular distribution patterns distinguish lymphoma from LNMC, and characteristic cells enable the identification of specific lesion subtypes [6]. Notably, false-negative results of FNAC are predominantly concentrated in NHL, HL, and LNMC, and such errors are attributable to focal lesions or suboptimal sampling rather than misinterpretation of typical cytological features. This indicates that diagnostic errors are mainly caused by technical sampling factors, rather than inherent limitations of cytological assessment itself.

Despite its clinical utility, FNAC has notable inherent limitations that restrict its application and preclude it from replacing surgical biopsy in most cases. First, FNAC only provides preliminary, non-specific results, with its core value limited to the initial benign-malignant stratification of lymph node lesions rather than definitive subtype diagnosis. Second, improper smear preparation is prone to inducing cytological artifacts (Fig. 6); small or focal lesions such as in situ follicular lymphoma carry a high risk of missed diagnosis (Fig. 7); and benign dendritic cells may be misidentified as RS cells or metastatic tumor cells due to their large cell size (Fig. 8). Third, FNAC lacks the structural context provided by histopathological examination, making subclassification of LNMC nearly impossible except for papillary thyroid carcinoma (which has distinct cytological features). Most critically, FNAC exhibits relatively low sensitivity (80.95%) and PPV (84.00%) for NHL, with significant morphological overlap between early-stage NHL and LNRH, rendering reliable diagnosis or exclusion of NHL unattainable by FNAC alone. Cytological artifacts, rare/focal lesions, and morphological overlap across different lesion types further exacerbate the diagnostic challenges of FNAC, potentially leading to misdiagnosis of benign lesions as malignant or missed diagnosis of small malignant lesions.

This study has several major limitations:

First, it was a single-center study conducted at a tertiary hospital, with only one cytological staining method and a standardized IHC panel employed, which limits the generalizability of the results to different clinical practice settings. Second, the small sample size of HL cases (n = 5) is closely associated with the inherently low clinical incidence of HL; the rigorous inclusion criterion of enrolling only cases confirmed by surgical histopathology further reduced the available HL cases, resulting in insufficient statistical power for this subgroup and a lack of robust statistical generalizability for relevant diagnostic efficacy indicators. Therefore, large-scale multicenter studies are urgently needed to extend the study period, accumulate more HL cases, and adopt diverse staining protocols and clinical practice models.

With a clear understanding of FNAC’s limitations, the technique can be selectively used for the preliminary clinical triage of enlarged lymph nodes, but definitive diagnosis and treatment decision-making must be combined with confirmatory tests. For cases presenting with severe cellular atypia and clustered distribution (suggestive of LNMC) or detectable RS cells (pathognomonic for HL) on FNAC, targeted further examinations (e.g., IHC staining) can be performed based on the high specificity of FNAC to avoid unnecessary diagnostic delays. However, FNAC cannot replace histopathological examination, and surgical biopsy remains essential for definitive diagnosis, subtype classification, and treatment guidance. For cases with mild to moderate atypia and diffuse/scattered distribution, FNAC has limited discriminatory ability due to morphological overlap between early-stage NHL and LNRH, necessitating priority surgical biopsy combined with IHC testing. Only when FNAC demonstrates classic features of LNRH (no cellular atypia, scattered mixed lymphocytes, and identifiable phagocytes) can clinical follow-up or etiological treatment be considered, and close monitoring of nodal morphological changes is still required for such cases.

As a minimally invasive examination, FNAC can reduce unnecessary surgical resections by reliably identifying benign LNRH, thereby lowering the risk of surgical complications such as scarring, nerve injury, and lymphedema. For patients with a confirmed primary malignant tumor, FNAC can rapidly determine lymph node metastasis status, provide evidence for the formulation of initial treatment plans, reduce the need for separate diagnostic resections, and optimize the clinical diagnosis and treatment process. The key to maximizing the diagnostic efficacy of FNAC lies in its application as part of a multimodal diagnostic system in combination with other techniques: core-needle biopsy (CNB) compensates for the lack of architectural tissue information in FNAC, and the two methods exhibit comparable sensitivity and overall diagnostic accuracy in detecting lymph node metastasis of solid tumors such as breast cancer and melanoma [22, 23]; flow cytometry enables objective assessment of lymphocyte clonality, serving as a core tool for distinguishing NHL from LNRH and effectively making up for the inherent deficiency of FNAC in this differential diagnosis. The combined application of these techniques with FNAC can significantly improve the diagnostic accuracy of lymph node lesions and address the core limitations of FNAC in the evaluation of NHL and other challenging lesions.

Cytological testing is a core tool for the diagnosis and triage of malignant tumors, and its clinical value can be further enhanced by combining it with disease-specific adjunct assays and standardized diagnostic systems [24–28]. For lymph node lesions, the Sydney classification system addresses the lack of standardized reporting terminology for FNAC and enables risk stratification of lesions. A stepwise diagnostic approach integrating clinical context, cytological morphological clues, and adjunct assays can effectively reduce the risk of misdiagnosis [27, 28]. Immunocytochemical and molecular detection techniques have improved the diagnostic triage efficacy for thyroid and bladder malignancies [24–26], and such techniques are also expected to complement FNAC in the evaluation of lymph node lesions by identifying objective markers for clonality and malignant features.In summary, FNAC is an effective first-line screening tool for enlarged lymph nodes, but its clinical utility relies on a full recognition of its inherent limitations-particularly the diagnostic challenges in distinguishing NHL and the statistical limitations arising from the small HL sample size in this study. FNAC should be integrated into a multimodal diagnostic system to fully leverage the complementary advantages of CNB, flow cytometry, IHC staining, and histopathological examination. Definitive diagnosis and treatment planning for lymph node lesions must be based on a comprehensive analysis of FNAC findings and confirmatory test results, with surgical biopsy remaining the gold standard for the diagnosis of most malignant or indeterminate lymph node lesions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, FNAC is a clinically useful, minimally invasive and rapid first-line screening tool for superficial enlarged lymph nodes, with an overall diagnostic accuracy of 89.47% in this study. Its characteristic cytomorphological findings for different lymph node lesions (LNRH, NHL, HL, LNMC) enable tentative preliminary benign-malignant triage, helping clinicians optimize clinical diagnosis and treatment workflows by avoiding unnecessary surgical biopsies for benign cases and prioritizing further evaluation for suspected malignancies.

FNAC has inherent limitations, including relatively low diagnostic performance for non-Hodgkin lymphoma due to morphological overlap with reactive hyperplasia, and statistical constraints from the small Hodgkin lymphoma sample size in this single-center study. Its clinical utility can be improved when combined with a multimodal diagnostic approach integrating IHC, CNB and flow cytometry. Future large-scale multicenter studies with diverse detection protocols and study populations are warranted to validate and refine FNAC’s diagnostic performance for lymph node lesions, further supporting its rational application in clinical practice.
