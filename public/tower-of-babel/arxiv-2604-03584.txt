Abstract
. When inclusions in a composite are separated by a very small gap, high contrast between the inclusion and matrix properties can induce strong amplification of the underlying field inside the narrow region. Quantifying this field concentration phenomenon is important both for the theory of composite materials and for practical applications. This survey reviews substantial progress over the past three decades. In particular, we survey a set of elliptic equations and systems for which optimal estimates or sharp asymptotic characterizations have been obtained, and we highlight several interesting open questions.

1.
Introduction

Composites are prevalent both in nature and in engineered materials. Strengthening fibers are embedded within a matrix that protects them and transfers loads between fibers, resulting in composites with properties superior to those of the individual components. Many models for composites can be reduced to partial differential equations with coefficients that jump across material interfaces. A common and practically important scenario is when two inclusions come very close to one another. Even if the boundary data are smooth, the narrow gap between inclusions can force the solution to change rapidly across a short distance, which may create very large gradient fields. Understanding the field concentration phenomenon quantitatively is important because it can trigger material failure (see, for example,
[
4
,
35
]
). In some other cases, inclusions are deliberately designed to create the field concentration to achieve desired enhancement of the field (see, for example,
[
45
,
31
]
).

Theoretical analysis of field concentration phenomenon traces back to effective medium theory. In the pioneering work
[
34
]
, the electrical or thermal resistance of the narrow gaps between the inclusions was analyzed in order to estimate the effective conductivity of a medium dense arrays of nearly touching perfectly conducting or insulating cylinders (2D) or perfectly conducting spheres (3D). See also
[
24
]
for related analysis in elasticity. Over the past three decades, substantial progress has been made in quantitatively characterizing field concentration. In this article, we survey some of the important recent progress.

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}\Big(a(x)\nabla{u}(x)\Big)=0&\mbox{in}~\Omega,\\

This survey focuses on several representative high-contrast models for which substantial progress has recently been made. Our goal is not only to collect sharp estimates, but also to highlight several qualitative mechanisms that distinguish the different models. In the linear perfect conductivity problem, the optimal blow-up rate is by now well understood and, remarkably, is largely independent of the local geometry of the inclusions. By contrast, for the insulated problem in dimensions

, the optimal exponent depends on the local geometry through an eigenvalue problem on the sphere. In nonlinear insulated problems involving the
p
-Laplacian, threshold phenomena appear and the behavior changes depending on the relation between
p
and the dimension. For imperfect bonding interfaces, the interfacial effect may introduce an additional lower-order term, leading to a new dichotomy between boundedness and blow-up.

Perfect conductivity problem, elasticity with hard inclusions

2.1.
Linear perfect conductivity problem

We begin with the classical high-contrast limit in which both inclusions are
perfect conductors, that is,

k_{1}=k_{2}=\infty.

In this regime, the potential is constant on each inclusion, and the conductivity

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\mbox{in}~\widetilde{\Omega},\\

Among the models surveyed in this paper, the linear perfect conductivity problem
is the most thoroughly understood. In particular, the optimal blow-up rate of

2.2.
Lamé system with hard inclusions

The scalar perfect conductivity problem has a natural elasticity analogue. Instead of letting the scalar conductivity inside the inclusions tend to
+\infty
, one sends the Lamé parameters
(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})
in the inclusions to
+\infty
. In this limit, the inclusions become rigid: the strain tensor
e(u)
vanishes in the inclusions, while the displacement remains continuous across the interfaces. In this case, the Lamé system (
1.4
) with (
1.5
) becomes

\begin{cases}\operatorname{div}\big(\mathbb{C}^{0}e(u)\big)=0&\mbox{in}~\widetilde{\Omega},\\
u|_{+}=u|_{-}&\mbox{on}~\partial D_{j},~j=1,2,\\
e(u)=0&\mbox{in}~D_{1}\cup D_{2},\\
\int_{\partial D_{j}}\partial_{\nu}u|_{+}\cdot\psi^{\alpha}\,d\sigma=0&j=1,2,~\alpha=1,2,\ldots,n(n+1)/2,\\
u=\varphi&\mbox{on}~\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Here
\{\psi^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{n(n+1)/2}
is the basis of the linear space of rigid displacement in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is

\{e_{i},x_{j}e_{k}-x_{k}e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j<k\leq n\},

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This problem admits a natural variational characterization. The displacement
u

is the minimizer of the elastic energy among all admissible deformations that are
rigid inside the inclusions:

I[u]=\min_{v\in\mathcal{A}}I[v],

where

I[u]:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\Big(\mathbb{C}^{0}e(v),e(v)\Big)\,dx,

and

\mathcal{A}=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):e(u)=0~\mbox{in}~D_{1}\cup D_{2},u=\varphi~\mbox{on}~\partial\Omega\}.

Compared with the scalar case, the elasticity problem is more delicate because of
its vector-valued structure. Nevertheless, the sharp concentration rates turn out to be the same as in the perfect conductivity problem. Bao, Li, and Li
[
8
,
9
]
proved the same upper bounds as in (
2.3
), and Li
[
40
]
later established the optimality of these rates. In two dimensions, Kang and Yu
[
33
]
obtained a precise asymptotic
expansion for
\nabla u
by constructing singular functions from those arising in
the scalar perfect conductivity problem. We refer the reader to Section 3 of
[
32
]
for a more detailed description of their construction.

Thus, at the level of blow-up rates, the hard-inclusion Lamé system closely
parallels the scalar perfect conductivity problem. At the same time, the vectorial
structure introduces additional obstruction, and the available sharp asymptotic results are correspondingly more involved.

2.3.
Perfect conductivity problem with
p
-Laplacian

We next turn to a nonlinear analogue of the perfect conductivity problem. Here the
inclusions are still assumed to be perfect conductors, but the background matrix
obeys a power-law current-electric field relation

J=\sigma|E|^{p-2}E,\quad p>1,

(2.4)

where
J
,
E
, and
\sigma
represent current, electric field, and conductivity, respectively. This power law is used to model a range of nonlinear media, including certain dielectrics, polymer processing, plasticity, viscous flow models in glaciology, and electro- and thermo-rheological fluids. See
[
12
,
27
,
36
,
46
]
and the references therein.

The perfect conductivity problem incorporating the power law (
2.4
) can be modeled by the following
p
-Laplace equation with
p>1
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)&=0&&\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega},\\
u&=U_{j}&&\mbox{on}~\overline{D_{j}},~j=1,2,\\
\int_{\partial D_{j}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nu&=0,&&j=1,2,\\
u&=\varphi&&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

where
U_{j}
are constants determined by the third line of (
2.5
).

Compared to the linear case
p=2
, the nonlinear perfect conductivity problem (
2.5
) was much less understood. For
n\geq 2
, Gorb and Novikov
[
26
]
and Ciraolo and Sciammetta
[
15
]
proved the following upper bounds:

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &C\varepsilon^{-\frac{n-1}{2(p-1)}}&&\mbox{when}~p>\frac{n+1}{2},\\
&C\varepsilon^{-1}|\ln\varepsilon|^{\frac{1}{1-p}}&&\mbox{when}~p=\frac{n+1}{2},\\
&C\varepsilon^{-1}&&\mbox{when}~1<p<\frac{n+1}{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

These bounds were shown to be optimal in their respective papers. Their
arguments rely strongly on the explicit fundamental solution of the
p
-Laplace equation together with the maximum principle.

A more refined question is whether one can go beyond blow-up rates and identify
the leading singular behavior of the solution, in analogy with the linear theory.
This was addressed in a joint work with Zhu
[
21
]
, where the first asymptotic expansion for the
nonlinear perfect conductivity problem was obtained by developing a Schauder
estimate in the narrow region. A key point in that analysis is the structure of the
touching limit
\varepsilon=0
, which reflects the threshold

p=(n+1)/2

Let
D^{0}_{1}
and

Let

When

takes the same constant

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}(|\nabla u_{0}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{0})&=0&&\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega}^{0},\\

\varepsilon\in(0,1)

x\in\Omega_{1/4}^{\varepsilon}
, we have

\nabla u(x)=\Big(0^{\prime},\frac{U_{1}-U_{2}}{\varepsilon+f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})}\Big)+h.o.t.

The significance of Theorem
2.3
is that it identifies the leading
singular term in the nonlinear perfect conductivity problem without relying on the
explicit fundamental solution of the
p
-Laplace equation. Instead, the argument
is based on a Schauder theory adapted to the narrow-gap geometry. This provides
a different route from the earlier blow-up estimates in
[
26
,
15
]
, although the proof still uses the maximum principle at one stage. It would be very interesting to develop an alternative approach that avoids the maximum principle and, more importantly, could be extended to nonlinear systems.

3.
Insulated conductivity problem

3.1.
Linear insulated conductivity problem

We next consider the opposite extreme regime

k_{1}=k_{2}=0,

for which the inclusions are perfect insulators. In this case, the conductivity
problem (
1.1
) reduces to

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=0&&\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}&=0&&\mbox{on }\partial(D_{1}\cup D_{2}),\\
u&=\varphi&&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

which is known as the insulated conductivity problem. This reduction is explained, for instance, in the appendix of
[
7
]
.

Compared with the linear perfect conductivity problem, progress for the insulated problem has been slower and less comprehensive. Using a harmonic conjugate argument, Ammari et al.
[
3
,
2
]
showed that in two dimensions the insulated problem has the same blow-up rate,
\varepsilon^{-1/2}
, as the perfect conductivity problem. In all dimensions
n\geq 2
, Bao, Li, and Yin
[
7
]
obtained the upper bound

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/2}.

In three dimensions, Yun
[
51
]
proved a sharper blow-up rate restricted on the shortest line segment connecting two inclusions: if
D_{1},D_{2}
are unit balls, then the blow-up rate on this segment is of order

\varepsilon^{-1+\sqrt{2}/2}.

Progress in dimensions
n\geq 3
then stalled for several years, until
[
43
]
, where Li and the second-named author used Harnack’s inequality and the maximum principle to improve the general upper bound to
\varepsilon^{-1/2+\beta}
for some
\beta>0
. Shortly thereafter, Weinkove
[
48
]
used a Bernstein-type argument to produce a more explicit constant
\beta(n)
for
n\geq 4
when the inclusions are balls. Meanwhile, identifying the optimal blow-up rate in dimensions
n\geq 3
was highlighted by Kang in his ICM 2022 lecture
[
32
]
as one of the central open questions in the area.

The optimal blow-up rate was finally identified in our joint work with Li
[
19
,
18
]
. A striking feature of this result is that, unlike the perfect conductivity problem, the optimal exponent for the insulated problem in dimensions
n\geq 3
depends on the local geometry of the inclusions. More precisely, it is determined by an eigenvalue problem on
\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
involving the principal curvatures of the two boundaries at the closest points.

To explain the result, we focus on the local problem near the origin,

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{1},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}&=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{+}\cup\Gamma_{-}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.2)

In
[
19
]
, we considered the case when
f,g\in C^{2,\gamma}
and

f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})=a|x^{\prime}|^{2}+O(|x^{\prime}|^{2+\gamma})\quad\mbox{for}~~0<|x^{\prime}|<1,

with
a>0
,
0<\gamma<1
. This includes the case when two inclusions are balls. The following optimal estimate was proved in
[
19
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
n\geq 3
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
, let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
be a solution of (
3.2
). Then

|\nabla u(x)|\lesssim_{n,\gamma,a}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}(\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\quad\forall x\in\Omega_{1/2},

(3.3)

where

\alpha:=\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}+4(n-2)}}{2}\in(0,1)

(3.4)

is the optimal exponent.

The optimality of (
3.3
) was also established.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
n\geq 3
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
, let
\Omega=B_{5}
, and
D_{1},D_{2}
be the unit balls centered at
(0^{\prime},1+\varepsilon/2)
and
(0^{\prime},-1-\varepsilon/2)
, respectively. Let
\varphi=x_{1}
and
u\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
be the solution of (
3.1
). Then

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega}\cap B_{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}})}\gtrsim_{n}\varepsilon^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}},

where
\alpha
is given by (
3.4
).

The result above already shows that, even for balls, the insulated problem behaves
very differently from the perfect conductivity problem for dimensions
n\geq 3
.
In
[
18
]
, this analysis was extended to general strictly convex inclusions. More precisely, assume that

f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+O(|x^{\prime}|^{2+\gamma})\quad\mbox{for}~~0<|x^{\prime}|<1,

with
a_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,n-1
,
0<\gamma<1
. Then the optimal
exponent is determined by the eigenvalue problem

-\operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\Big(a(\xi)\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}u(\xi)\Big)=\lambda a(\xi)u(\xi),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-2},

(3.5)

where
a(\xi)=\xi^{t}D^{2}(f-g)(0^{\prime})\xi
. Let
\lambda_{1}
be the first nonzero eigenvalue of (
3.5
) and

\alpha(\lambda_{1}):=\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}+4\lambda_{1}}}{2}\in(0,1).

We established gradient estimates similar to (
3.3
) for any
\alpha<\alpha(\lambda_{1})
. We also showed that the exponent
\alpha(\lambda_{1})
is optimal, in the sense that estimate (
3.3
) does not hold for
\alpha>\alpha(\lambda_{1})
. Finally, the endpoint estimate
\alpha=\alpha(\lambda_{1})
was recently obtained by Li and Zhao
[
39
]
.

The main idea in our work can be summarized as follows. Because the Neumann boundary data is zero on the boundaries of both inclusions, the leading order contribution of
\nabla u
in the narrow gap is expected to be tangential. This motivates us to study the vertical average of the solution
\bar{u}
in the narrow gap region, i.e.,

\bar{u}(x^{\prime}):=\fint_{-\varepsilon/2+g(x^{\prime})<x_{n}<\varepsilon/2+f(x^{\prime})}u(x^{\prime},x_{n})\,dx_{n},\quad|x^{\prime}|<1.

For the case when inclusions are unit balls, it turns out that
\bar{u}
satisfies a degenerate equation

\operatorname{div}((\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})\nabla\bar{u})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial_{i}(\overline{a^{in}\partial_{n}u})-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial_{i}(e^{i}\partial_{i}\bar{u})\quad\text{in}\,\,B_{1}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

where

|a^{ni}(x)|=|a^{in}(x)|\leq C\varepsilon|x^{\prime}|\quad\mbox{and}\quad|e^{i}(x^{\prime})|\leq C|x^{\prime}|^{2+\gamma}.

In order to obtain the optimal estimate of
\nabla\bar{u}
, we studied this degenerate equation and established the following regularity result.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
n\geq 3
,
\sigma>1
,
\sigma-1\neq\alpha
, let
\bar{u}\in H^{1}(B_{1}^{\prime})
be a solution of

\operatorname{div}\Big[(\varepsilon+r^{2})\nabla\bar{u}\Big]=\operatorname{div}F+G\quad\text{for}\,\,(r:=|x^{\prime}|,\xi)\in(0,1)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-2},

where
F
and
G
satisfy

|F(x^{\prime})|\lesssim r^{\sigma-2}(\varepsilon+r^{2}),\quad|G(x^{\prime})|\lesssim r^{\sigma-3}(\varepsilon+r^{2})\quad\mbox{for}~~x^{\prime}\in B_{1}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Then

\displaystyle\Big(\fint_{\partial B_{R}^{\prime}}|\bar{u}-\bar{u}(0)|^{2}\,d\sigma\Big)^{1/2}\lesssim R^{\tilde{\alpha}},

where
\tilde{\alpha}:=\min\{\alpha,\sigma-1\}
, and
\alpha
is given by (
3.4
).

The optimal exponent
\alpha
comes from the homogeneous part of the degenerate equation. By taking a spherical harmonic decomposition, the first mode
U_{1}
of the homogeneous solution satisfies the ODE

U_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{n-2}{r}+\frac{2r}{\varepsilon+r^{2}}\right)U_{1}^{\prime}(r)-\frac{n-2}{r^{2}}U_{1}(r)=0,\quad r\in(0,1).

When
\varepsilon=0
, the function
r^{\alpha}
is an exact solution, and for
\varepsilon>0
, the
maximum principle yields the decay estimate

|U(r)|\leq r^{\alpha}|U(1)|.

This is the origin of the optimal exponent in (
3.3
).

At this point, the linear insulated problem is fairly well understood at the level of sharp blow-up rates. What remains missing is a precise asymptotic expansion for

\nabla u
, analogous to what is known for the perfect conductivity problem.
Another major challenge is to extend the theory to systems such as the Lamé
system: the existing arguments rely strongly on the scalar structure of the equation and on the maximum principle, and these features do not readily persist for the vector-valued system.

3.2.
Insulated conductivity problem with
p
-Laplacian

We now consider the nonlinear analogue of the insulated conductivity problem. As in Section
2.3
, we assume that the background matrix obeys the power-law current-electric field relation (
2.4
). In that setting, the insulated conductivity problem takes the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)&=0\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}&=0\quad\mbox{on}~\partial{D}_{i},~i=1,2,\\
u&=\varphi\quad\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.6)

Although the nonlinear perfect conductivity problem had already been studied in
[
26

15

20

p=3

\varepsilon^{-1/2}

Our starting point is a universal pointwise estimate, valid for all

Thus, exactly as in the linear insulated problem, the natural scale in the narrow

(\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

4.
Conductors with different signs

Sections
2
and
3
focus on high-contrast regimes where the gradient field typically concentrates as
\varepsilon=\operatorname{dist}(D_{1},D_{2})\to 0
. However, there are important mixed configurations in which this
concentration mechanism breaks down. A particularly striking example occurs when
one inclusion approaches the insulating limit while the other approaches the perfect conducting limit. In that case, the gradient may remain bounded even though both phases are individually extreme. This phenomenon was first discovered by Ji and Kang
[
28
]
.

In two dimensions, Ji and Kang studied the conductivity problem (
1.1
) for
two circular inclusions with

0<k_{1}<1,\quad k_{2}>1,

using spectral properties of the Neumann-Poincaré operator. Among other results,
they proved that

|\nabla^{m}u|\leq C\left(-\frac{(k_{1}+1)(k_{2}+1)}{(k_{1}-1)(k_{2}-1)}-1+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)^{-m+1},\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular, in the limiting regime
k_{1}\to 0
and
k_{2}\to\infty
, this implies

|\nabla^{m}u|\leq C\varepsilon^{-(m-1)/2},\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

The estimate shows that the gradient is bounded independent of
\varepsilon
, although higher-order derivatives may still blow up.

In
[
23
]
, a sharper picture was obtained. We show that, under suitable regularity assumptions on the domain and data, all derivatives
\nabla^{m}u
are in fact uniformly bounded for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We briefly describe two cases.

We first consider the planar case
n=2
, where
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are disks of
radii
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively. We study the inhomogeneous transmission problem

\operatorname{div}(a(x)\nabla u)=\operatorname{div}f\quad\mbox{in}~\Omega,

(4.1)

where
a(x)
is the piecewise-constant conductivity in (
1.2
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
,
\mu\in(0,1)
, and
1/2<r_{1},r_{2}<10
. Assume
0<k_{1}<1/2
,
k_{2}>2
, and let
u
be a weak solution of (
4.1
). Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
f
is piecewise
C^{2m-1,\mu}
, and for some constant
C_{m}>0
,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{m},~~~\|f\|_{C^{2m-1,\mu}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq C_{m},~~~\|f\|_{C^{2m-1,\mu}(D_{j})}\leq C_{m}\min\{1,k_{j}\},j=1,2,

then

|\nabla^{m}u(x)|\lesssim_{m,\mu,r_{1},r_{2}}\left\{\begin{aligned} &C_{m}&&\mbox{in}~~\Omega_{1/2},\\
&\frac{C_{m}}{k_{1}+1}&&\mbox{in}~~D_{1},\\
&\frac{C_{m}}{k_{2}+1}&&\mbox{in}~~D_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we use the Green function method developed in
[
17
]
. In contrast to
[
28
]
, our argument estimates the
m
-th order derivatives via the
m
-th order finite differences. This allows us to exploit the intrinsic cancellations in the alternating series and, in particular, to avoid an artificial loss in
\varepsilon
. An interesting question is whether one can extend the result in Theorem
4.1
to higher dimensions
n\geq 3
.

We next turn to general dimensions
n\geq 2
and to strictly convex inclusions of
general shape. Here we focus on the extreme mixed-contrast configuration

k_{1}=0,\qquad k_{2}=\infty.

In this limit, the conductivity problem (
1.1
) reduces to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta{u}=&0&&\mbox{in}~\widetilde{\Omega},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=&0&&\mbox{on}~\partial{D}_{1},\\
u=&C&&\mbox{on}~\partial{D}_{2},\\
\int_{\partial{\mathcal{B}}_{2}}\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial\nu}=&0,\\
u=&\varphi&&\mbox{on}~\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2)

where
C
is some constant determined by the fourth line of the equation.

In this mixed regime, the behavior is significantly different from both the perfect conductivity and insulated problems discussed earlier. Instead of blow-up in the narrow gap, one obtains uniform control of derivatives of every order.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
u\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
be a weak solution of (
4.2
). For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, some constants
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
C_{m,\alpha}>0
, if

\|f\|_{C^{m,\alpha}(\{|x^{\prime}|<1\})}+\|g\|_{C^{m,\alpha}(\{|x^{\prime}|<1\})}\leq C_{m,\alpha},

then there exist constants
\mu\in(0,1)
and
C
, depending only on
n
,
m
,
\alpha
, and
C_{m,\alpha}
such that

|\nabla^{m}u(x)|\leq C\mu^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}+|x^{\prime}|}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\quad\mbox{for}~~x\in\Omega_{1/2}.

The proof essentially follows an energy argument in
[
5
]
, combined with modifications adapted to the mixed boundary conditions. The same conclusion also remains valid when the Laplacian is replaced by the
p
-Laplacian.

This section illustrates that high contrast alone does not always lead to field concentration. The specific interface configuration matters in an essential way. In the perfect conductivity and insulated problems, the narrow gap amplifies the field, while in the mixed extreme-contrast setting considered here, the interaction between the Dirichlet and Neumann conditions suppresses that mechanism. This provides an important counterpoint to the blow-up phenomena surveyed in the previous sections.

5.
Conductivity problem with imperfect bonding interfaces

The preceding sections concern perfectly bonded interfaces, for which the classical transmission conditions (
1.3
) hold. However, in many applications, the interfacial effects cannot be neglected. For example, contact resistance created by surface roughness may substantially reduce the effective conductivity of a composite medium. As emphasized in
[
47

A convenient way to model imperfect bonding is through a thin coating around each

where
\nu
is the inward normal vector on
\partial D_{j}
,
U_{j}
is some constant determined by the third line of (
5.1
). The solution
u\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
to equation (
5.1
) is indeed the minimizer of a functional in an appropriate function space:
I[u]=\min_{v\in\mathscr{A}}I[v]
, where

\displaystyle I[v]

\displaystyle:=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}|\nabla v|^{2}+\gamma^{-1}\int_{\partial D_{1}}|v-(v)_{\partial D_{1}}|^{2}+\gamma^{-1}\int_{\partial D_{2}}|v-(v)_{\partial D_{2}}|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega}):\,v=\varphi~~\mbox{on}~~\partial\Omega\},\quad(v)_{\partial D_{j}}:=\fint_{\partial D_{j}}v\,d\sigma,\ j=1,2.

This model was studied in two dimensions by Fukushima et al.
[
25
]
, who considered equal disks in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with far field asymptotic
\varphi=x_{2}
. They proved that the gradient remains bounded independently of
\varepsilon
, in sharp contrast with the perfect conductivity problem without the membrane, where blow-up occurs as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Motivated by biological membrane models, they conjectured that such boundedness should persist for all
\gamma>0
and all boundary data.

In
[
22
]
, a recent joint work with Zhu, we showed that the conjecture holds only when the bonding parameter
\gamma
is sufficiently small, and there is an unexpected dichotomy of field concentration phenomenon depending on the size of
\gamma
. In summary,
[
22
]
establishes three main results:

1.

Upper bound for the gradient
: We establish a key upper bound of order
\varepsilon^{-1/2}
for the gradient of the solution to (
5.1
). A direct consequence of this result is that if
\widetilde{\Omega}
is symmetric with respect to
x_{n}
, and the boundary value
\varphi
is odd in
x_{n}
, then the gradient is bounded independent of
\varepsilon
. This extends the boundedness result in
[
25
]
to all dimensions
n\geq 2
, to more general inclusions
D_{1},D_{2}
, and to more general boundary data
\varphi
.

2.

Absence of field concentration for small
\gamma
: We show that when the bonding parameter
\gamma
is sufficiently small, the gradient of the solution is bounded independent of
\varepsilon
. This confirms the conjecture raised in
[
25
]
under the additional assumption that
\gamma
is sufficiently small.

3.

Dichotomy for field concentration
: We show that when
\gamma
is large, the conjecture fails, and the first boundedness result is highly unstable. More precisely, if the boundary data is perturbed slightly away from being odd in
x_{n}
(for example,
\varphi=\delta x_{1}+x_{n}
for some small
\delta
), then the gradient of the solution may blow up. In particular, when
D_{1},D_{2}
are unit balls,
\Omega=B_{5}
, and
\varphi=x_{1}
, we prove a dichotomy for the field concentration phenomenon: the gradient of the solution to (
5.1
) is bounded when
0<\gamma\leq 1
, but blows up when
\gamma>1
. Moreover, we establish the optimal gradient estimate when
\gamma>1
. Our analysis shows that in this regime the solution behaves much like the insulated conductivity problem. In fact, as
\gamma\to\infty
, our estimates recover the corresponding results for the perfectly bonded insulated conductivity problem.

The first main result is an anisotropic pointwise gradient estimate in the narrow neck region. As in Theorem
3.4
, we show that the relevant scale is
(\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, rather than the neck height
\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2}
. This local estimate is fundamental to the analysis throughout the paper
[
22
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
\gamma>0
,
\varepsilon<1/4
, and
u\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
be the solution of (
5.1
). Then, for any
x\in\Omega_{1/2}
and
r=\frac{1}{4}(\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we have

|\nabla u(x)|\lesssim_{n,\gamma}r^{-1}\|u-(U_{1}+U_{2})/2\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(\Omega_{x,r})}+|U_{1}-U_{2}|.

(5.2)

Here

\|f\|_{L^{2}_{avg}(\Omega)}:=\Big(\fint_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

A quick consequence is that the gradient remains bounded under the odd symmetry assumption mentioned above. In this case, the symmetry ensures that the constants on the two inclusions cancel, so the singular term in the estimate above vanishes.

The next theorem shows that such boundedness does not require symmetry when the bonding parameter is sufficiently small.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any
\beta\geq 0
, if
0<\gamma\ll 1
, then

|\nabla u(x)|\lesssim_{n,\gamma,\beta}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+|x^{\prime}|)^{\beta}\|u-(U_{1}+U_{2})/2\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}+|U_{1}-U_{2}|\quad\mbox{in}~\Omega_{1/2}.

(5.3)

The most interesting phenomenon occurs in the large-
\gamma
regime. Assume now that
D_{1},D_{2}
are
C^{2,\sigma}
, and that the gap has the expansion:

f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})=\mu\,|x^{\prime}|^{2}+O(|x^{\prime}|^{2+\sigma})\quad\mbox{for}~~0<|x^{\prime}|<1,~\mu>0.

Suppose also that, for some horizontal direction
x_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq n-1
, the domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
is symmetric with respect to
x_{j}
and boundary data is odd in
x_{j}
. Then the solution is odd in
x_{j}
, which implies that
U_{1}=U_{2}=0
.
Define

\alpha=\alpha(n,\gamma,\mu):=\frac{-(n-1)+\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}+4(n-2+2/(\mu\gamma))}}{2}.

(5.4)

Then
\alpha<1
if and only if
\gamma>1/\mu
. This is exactly the threshold for gradient blow-up.

Theorem 5.3
.

Under the assumption above, we have

•

when
0<\gamma\leq 1/\mu
,

|\nabla u(x)|\lesssim_{n,\gamma,\mu}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}\quad\mbox{in}~\Omega_{1/2},

(5.5)

•

when
\gamma>1/\mu
,

|\nabla u(x)|\lesssim_{n,\mu}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}(\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\quad\mbox{in}~\Omega_{1/2},

(5.6)

where
\alpha
is given in (
5.4
).

It is further shown that the upper bound in the blow-up regime is sharp.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\Omega=B_{5/\mu}
,
D_{1},D_{2}
be balls of radius
1/\mu
centered at
(0^{\prime},1/\mu+\varepsilon/2)
and
(0^{\prime},-1/\mu-\varepsilon/2)
, respectively,
\varphi(x)=x_{1}
, and let
u\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
be the solution of (
5.1
). Then there exist positive constants
c
and
C
, depending only on
n
,
\gamma
, and
\mu
, such that

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{c\sqrt{\varepsilon}})}\geq\frac{1}{C}\varepsilon^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}.

The threshold also admits a natural interpretation. When
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are balls of radius
R
, we have
\mu=1/R
, so the critical value becomes
\gamma=R
. This is precisely the value for which the inclusions are neutral to uniform fields: when
\gamma=R
, the linear functions
x_{j}
automatically satisfy both the Robin interface condition and the zero-flux constraint.

The work in
[
22
]
establishes the first sharp blow-up theory for conductivity problems with imperfect bonding interfaces and reveals a new phenomenon beyond the perfectly bonded models discussed in Sections
2
-
4
: the occurrence of a threshold in the bonding parameter. For small
\gamma
, the membrane suppresses field concentration; for large
\gamma
, the solution behaves more like that of the insulated problem and blow-up may occur.

The proof relies on three main ingredients. The first is the anisotropic gradient estimate (
5.2
). To establish it, we construct a flattening map that preserves the Robin boundary conditions on both boundaries, together with a carefully chosen auxiliary function to handle the inhomogeneous boundary condition. This approach is quite robust and applies to a broad class of equations and boundary conditions. Estimate (
5.3
) is then obtained by an iteration argument, in which the smallness of
\gamma
yields a suitable decay of
u
near the origin. Finally, the mechanism behind Theorems
5.3
and
5.4
is closely related to that of the insulated problem. After a dimension reduction argument, one reduces the problem to a degenerate elliptic equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. The crucial new feature is that the Robin condition contributes an additional zeroth-order term in the reduced equation. It is precisely this term that produces the dichotomy at
\gamma=1/\mu
. In particular, as
\gamma\to\infty
, estimate (
5.6
) recovers the corresponding estimate for the perfectly bonded insulated problem in Section
3.1
, as the constant does not depend on
\gamma
.

On the other hand, as
\gamma\to 0
, the problem (
5.1
) converges to the perfect conductivity problem (
2.1
). However, estimate (
5.5
) does not carry over as it depends on
\gamma
. The first-named author, together with Li and Zhao
[
16
]
, studied the regime
\gamma\ll 1
and derived a new estimate that bridges problems (
5.1
) and (
2.1
).

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
0<\gamma\ll 1
,
\varepsilon<1/4
, for
x\in\Omega_{1/2}
, we have

|\nabla u(x)|\lesssim_{n}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma+\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2}}}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&\mbox{for}~n=2,\\
&\frac{1}{{(\gamma+\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2})}|\ln(\varepsilon+\gamma)|}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&\mbox{for}~n=3,\\
&\frac{1}{{\gamma+\varepsilon+|x^{\prime}|^{2}}}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&\mbox{for}~n\geq 4.\end{aligned}\right.

The proof is based on new gradient estimates for elliptic equations, in both
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the reduced dimension space
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, as
\gamma\to 0
, the maximum principle, and an energy iteration argument in the spirit of
[
5
]
. These upper bounds were shown to be optimal in
[
16
]
.

A natural next step is to study inclusions with finite conductivities
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
with an LC-type imperfect bonding interface. It is well known that, without an LC-type interface, the gradient remains uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
. See
[
42
,
41
]
. It would be interesting to determine whether the addition of an imperfect bonding interface can cause blow-up and, if so, what the optimal blow-up rate is.

By contrast, little is known about HC-type interfaces. In two dimensions,
[
25
]
shows that insulators with an HC-type interface and perfect conductors with an LC-type interface are dual to each other. It would be interesting to study HC-type interfaces in dimensions
n\geq 3
, although this appears to be challenging due to the complexity of the transmission conditions.

6.
Concluding remarks and open problems

The results surveyed in this article illustrate that field concentration in composite media is governed by a subtle interaction among contrast, geometry, nonlinearity, and interface effects. In the linear perfect conductivity problem, the sharp blow-up rate is by now well understood and is largely independent of the detailed local geometry of the inclusions. By contrast, for the insulated problem in dimensions
n\geq 3
, the optimal exponent depends on the local quadratic geometry of the boundaries through an eigenvalue problem on
\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
. In the nonlinear insulated problem, threshold phenomena appear, and in the imperfect bonding problem, the Robin interface produces a new dichotomy in the bonding parameter. These features show that, even within the narrow-gap setting, different high-contrast regimes can exhibit significantly different concentration mechanisms.

Although substantial progress has been made, several important questions remain
open.

First, for the linear insulated conductivity problem, the optimal blow-up rate is now known, but a precise asymptotic expansion for the gradient is still missing.

Second, for the insulated problem with
p
-Laplacian, the picture is relatively clear only in two dimensions. In particular, the optimal blow-up exponent is still unknown for
n\geq 3
. It would be especially interesting to determine whether
p=n+1
is the correct threshold in higher dimensions.

Third, most of the sharp results discussed here concern scalar equations. Extending the insulated theory to systems, especially to the Lamé system of elasticity, remains a major challenge. The existing arguments for the scalar insulated problem rely heavily on the maximum principle and on a reduction to a scalar degenerate elliptic equation, and these tools do not readily extend to vector-valued systems.

Fourth, Section
4
shows that in certain mixed extreme-contrast configurations the gradient may remain uniformly bounded, in sharp contrast with the blow-up phenomena in the perfect conductivity and insulated problems. While Theorem
4.1
establishes this behavior in two dimensions, an interesting open problem is whether the same result can be extended to higher dimensions
n\geq 3
.

Fifth, for imperfect bonding interfaces, the LC-type problem for perfect
conductors is now fairly well understood, but very little is known when the inclusions have finite conductivities
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. Since the corresponding
perfectly bonded bounded-contrast problem does not exhibit blow-up, it would be
very interesting to understand whether the addition of an imperfect interface can
create field concentration and, if so, to determine the sharp rate.

Finally, HC-type imperfect interfaces remain largely unexplored, especially in
dimensions
n\geq 3
. Even the correct qualitative picture is not yet clear in this setting, partly because the effective transmission condition is substantially more complicated than that in the LC-type case.
