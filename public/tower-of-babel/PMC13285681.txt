IL-8 blockade enhances anti-PD-1 therapeutic activity in renal cell carcinoma in a next-generation patient-derived xenograft model
Authors: Ryuta Sobu, Yuka Kobayashi, Soki Kashima, Takamitsu Inoue, Takayuki Sumiyoshi, Hiroshi Hamana, Masashi Takeda, Yuko Hiroshima, Shinsuke Seki, Nobuhiro Fujiyama, Kyoko Masuda, Yudai Funakoshi, Shuhei Okada, Keisuke Okubo, Shuhei Takahashi, Yuya Sekine, Hiromi Sato, Mizuki Kobayashi, Ryohei Yamamoto, Taketoshi Nara, Mitsuru Saito, Shintaro Narita, Hiroshi Nanjo, Hiroyuki Kishi, Takeshi Watanabe, Hiroshi Kawamoto, Shusuke Akamatsu, Takashi Kobayashi, Tomonori Habuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are widely used for advanced renal cell carcinoma (aRCC); however, predictive biomarkers of response remain insufficiently defined, and treatment resistance is common. We comprehensively analyzed clinical samples from 51 patients with aRCC treated with anti-PD-1 therapy to identify resistance factors. Immunohistochemical analysis of tissue microarrays showed no significant correlation between baseline tumor microenvironment features and therapeutic response. In contrast, peripheral blood flow cytometry demonstrated persistently elevated monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) in non-responders. A next-generation patient-derived xenograft (NG-PDX) model established by co-engrafting tumor tissue and peripheral immune cells from the same patient recapitulated patient-specific tumor-immune interactions, using MHC class I/II-knockout immunodeficient mice. In this model, combined blockade of PD-1 and IL-8, a key chemokine recruiting MDSCs, tended to suppress tumor growth. These findings identify immune features associated with PD-1 resistance in aRCC and support NG-PDX models as a platform for evaluating rational immunotherapeutic combinations.Subject areas: Therapy, Immune response, Cancer

Introduction

The incidence of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is increasing worldwide, with more than 400,000 new cases reported annually.1 For localized RCC, standard treatments—including partial and radical nephrectomy, as well as other modalities such as radiofrequency ablation, cryoablation, and stereotactic radiotherapy—have improved the 5-year cancer-specific survival rate to over 90%.2,3 In contrast, despite significant advancements in the systemic treatment landscape for advanced RCC (aRCC), including the establishment of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as a key treatment option in frontline, subsequent-line, and adjuvant settings over the past few decades, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate remains low, at approximately 24%–40% in aRCC.4,5,6 Additionally, it remains unclear which patients will benefit from ICI therapy prior to treatment initiation; therefore, the identification of predictive biomarkers is urgently needed. In other solid tumors, several biomarkers of ICI efficacy, including tumor mutation burden,7,8 DNA mismatch repair mechanism-associated microsatellite instability,9 CD8+ T cell infiltration,10 and PD-L1 expression,11 have been reported. However, these markers have failed to adequately predict treatment response to ICIs in aRCC.12,13,14 This highlights that the mechanisms underlying the efficacy of ICI therapy in RCC remain poorly understood.

Prior clinical studies have reported that elevated circulating or tumor-associated interleukin-8 (IL-8) levels are associated with poor outcomes in patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and that IL-8 promotes the recruitment and accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).15,16,17,18 However, the mechanisms through which IL-8 modulates ICI efficacy in a human tumor-immune context remain poorly defined.

Even when potential mechanisms of resistance to ICIs are identified, examining human cancer-immune responses in vivo has been challenging due to the lack of humanized ICI-therapeutic animal models. Specifically, there are no physiological models that incorporate both patient-derived tumors and autologous immune cells without xenoreactivity. The syngeneic model is valuable for replicating the TME within the mouse immune system; however, substantial differences between human and mouse immune systems limit its ability to accurately recapitulate human cancer-immune interactions.19 Therefore, the identification of reliable biomarkers to predict the effectiveness of ICIs, as well as the development of novel patient-derived in vivo models for functional validation, remain critical and urgent challenges in cancer immunology.

To address these knowledge gaps, we undertook an integrated analysis combining clinical specimens and experimental modeling. First, we analyzed tumor tissues and paired peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected before and during ICI therapy from patients with aRCC to identify immune features associated with therapeutic response and resistance. Based on these clinical observations, we next established a next-generation patient-derived xenograft (NG-PDX) model, in which both tumor tissue and PBMCs were derived from the same patient, to functionally evaluate the effects of combining inhibition of factors identified through clinical specimen analyses with PD-1 inhibition within a human TME.

Finally, to validate the mechanistic insights from these findings in independent external data, we performed analyses focusing on a transcriptional signature in a randomized phase 3 clinical trial dataset. Through these complementary approaches, we sought to assess whether MDSC inhibition could be associated with an improvement in ICI efficacy in aRCC, thereby providing mechanistic insight.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics and clinical features of the study cohort

Fifty-one patients with RCC were enrolled in this study; responders exhibited complete or partial responses (R; n = 11), while non-responders exhibited disease progression (NR; n = 11) according to the best response based on irRECIST20 (Figures 1A and 1B). The median age of the patients was 68 years in both the R and NR groups (ranges: 49–85 and 42–88 years, respectively). According to the International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) risk classification, nine and two patients in the R group were categorized as intermediate and poor risk, respectively. In the NR group, one, eight, and two patients were categorized as favorable, intermediate, and poor risk, respectively. Baseline characteristics were largely similar between the two groups; however, as shown in Table S1, 7 of 22 patients had received metastasis-directed radiation at baseline, distributed 1/11 among R versus 6/11 among NR (p = 0.032) (Figure 1C; Table S1). This likely reflects clinical practice whereby patients with symptomatic or high-burden metastases are more likely to receive radiation.

Baseline TME features in non-responders

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) were constructed from 15 surgical tissue samples, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to evaluate tumor lesions prior to nivolumab administration (Figures 1D and S1A). Quantitative analysis by single-cell image cytometry showed a trend toward increased infiltration of Foxp3+ cells in the NR group; however, no significant differences were observed between the R and NR groups in the mean percentages of tumor-infiltrating CD4+, CD8+, Foxp3+, PD-L1+, or PD-L2+ cells (p = 0.613, 0.694, 0.094, 1.000, and 0.867, respectively) (Figures 1E and S1B). The composition of specimens included in the TMA differed between groups: NR samples (n = 8) consisted of two biopsies, one partial nephrectomy specimen, and five radical nephrectomy specimens, whereas all R samples (n = 7) were derived from radical nephrectomy specimens. To account for potential confounding by specimen type, we performed a subset analysis restricted to radical nephrectomy specimens (R: n = 7; NR: n = 5). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in CD4, CD8, Foxp3, PD-L1, or PD-L2 expression within this subset (p = 0.432, 0.755, 0.343, 0.432, and 0.432, respectively) (Figure S1C). Although this analysis is limited by the small sample size and potential cohort heterogeneity, these findings do not support an association between the examined immune cell populations and clinical response in this cohort, consistent with previous reports showing no correlation between T cell infiltration or PD-L1 status and therapeutic efficacy.10,11,21

Longitudinal dynamics of peripheral CD8+ T cells during ICI treatment

Flow cytometry was used to evaluate the dynamics of PBMCs (Figure S2). At 2 weeks, NR exhibited significantly higher frequencies of TIM-3+CD8+ and CD39+CD8+ T cells (p = 0.026 and 0.016, respectively), while R showed a significantly lower presence of TIGIT+CD8+ cells compared to baseline (p = 0.037); no significant differences were detected at week 4 relative to baseline across any of these subsets (Figures 2A and S3). Interestingly, the proportion of peripheral CD25+CD4+ cells was significantly higher in R at 2 weeks compared to baseline (p = 0.005).

Higher frequencies of M-MDSCs at all time points in non-responders

In aRCC, it has been reported that elevated expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-8, CXCL5, and MIP-1α within tumor tissues promotes the accumulation of MDSCs, which exhibit immunosuppressive effects.22 Therefore, we investigated the frequency of MDSCs at multiple time points to evaluate their potential impact on ICI efficacy. Notably, at all assessed time points—baseline, week 2, and week 4—the frequency of CD14+CD11b+ cells, representative of monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs), was significantly higher in the NR compared to R group (Figure 2B). The frequency of CD15+CD11b+ cells, representing polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs), showed no significant differences between the groups (data not shown).

A heatmap illustrating the frequency of CD14+CD11b+ cells in individual patients revealed consistently higher frequencies at baseline in the NR group, with many showing no decrease during treatment. In contrast, the R group exhibited significantly lower baseline frequencies of CD14+CD11b+ cells. In particular, among the few responders with relatively high frequencies of those cells at baseline, a progressive decrease was observed following ICI initiation (Figure 2C). Accordingly, M-MDSC levels are associated with non-response to PD-1 blockade in our cohort. These findings suggest that M-MDSCs show a more robust and consistent association with resistance to PD-1 blockade than T cell exhaustion markers, and we reasoned that targeting M-MDSCs could be a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of ICIs.

Development of the next-generation PDX

We aimed to establish an NG-PDX model that replicates the patient TME in vivo by utilizing RCC tumor samples and PBMCs from the same patients with RCC. To minimize xenoreactive effects caused by human immune-cell transplantation, we employed MHC class I/II-deficient immunodeficient mice (NOG-ΔMHC).23,24 First, we compared xenogeneic graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)-related effects between conventional NOD/Shi-scid, IL-2RγKO Jic (NOG), and NOG-ΔMHC mice following transplantation of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) (Figure 3A). Body weight declined significantly in NOG mice starting around day 28 after hPBMCs transplantation, whereas NOG-ΔMHC mice maintained stable body weight (Figure 3B). GVHD scores25,26 increased in NOG mice starting at day 28 and were significantly higher than those observed in NOG-ΔMHC mice (Figure 3C). After a month, peripheral blood of NOG-ΔMHC mice exhibited stable engraftment with a balanced ratio of human CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 3E). Immunofluorescence of spleens in NOG-ΔMHC mice revealed the presence of not only CD4+ and CD8+ T cells but also the engraftment of CD20+ and CD11b+ cells (Figure 3D). PDX tumors established from RCC patients at our institution consistently showed stable growth and preserved histological features with the primary tumors (Figures 3F and S4A–S4C; Tables S2A–S2C). Tumor specimens were obtained from 23 patients with RCC (12 radical, 10 partial nephrectomies, and 1 lymph node biopsy). Conventional PDXs were successfully established in 3 cases (13%), originating from one partial nephrectomy (Akita University renal cancer [AURC]-5), one radical nephrectomy (AURC-16), and one lymph node biopsy (AURC-17). In the NG-PDX model, flow cytometry analysis was performed on primary PBMCs from healthy donors, peripheral blood from NG-PDX mice, and tumor-infiltrating T cells (TILs). CD8+ T cells from primary PBMCs did not express CD69 or PD-1, whereas CD8+ T cells from NG-PDX peripheral blood expressed PD-1, and TILs expressed both PD-1 and CD69 (Figure S4C). These results indicated that the transferred human immune cells were activated upon recognizing tumor antigens in vivo. To evaluate the functional immune response, we administered anti-human PD-1 antibodies to NG-PDX mice (Figure 3G). Treated mice exhibited a significantly higher proportion of Ki67+CD8+ tumor-infiltrating cells compared to controls (Figures 3H and 3J). Furthermore, we isolated TILs from the excised tumors and performed single-cell TCRβ CDR3 sequencing on sorted CD8+PD-1+ T cells. In the control group, the most expanded TCRβ clones differed across all mice, whereas mice treated with anti-human PD-1 antibody showed clonal expansion of the same TCRβ CDR3 sequence across all individuals (Figure 3I). T cell receptor (TCR) clonotype enrichment was evaluated using a depth-invariant analytical approach. After equal-depth subsampling across samples, all anti-PD-1-treated mice independently exhibited the same Top-1 clonotype, whereas this clonotype was absent in control mice (permutation p = 0.028), indicating a reproducible, treatment-associated clonal expansion rather than an artifact of sampling depth. Hence, these results suggest that CD8+ TILs with antitumor potential may not dominate at baseline, but tumor-specific exhausted T cells can be reactivated and clonally expanded following PD-1 blockade in the TME.

Combination therapy with IL-8 and PD-1 blockade showed potential antitumor activity in the NG-PDX model

We next investigated whether combination therapy targeting PD-1 and IL-8 would enhance antitumor activity in the NG-PDX model. Anti-IL-8 and/or anti-hPD-1 antibodies were administered intraperitoneally to NG-PDX mice (Figure 4A), and tumor growth was monitored longitudinally (Table S3). Although tumors in the combination therapy group tended to show reduced growth compared with those in the other groups (Figure 4B), no statistically significant differences in tumor volume were observed among the treatment groups at the final time point (day 20) (Figure S5B). Notably, because PDX models often exhibit substantial inter-animal variability in baseline tumor burden and tumor growth kinetics,27,28,29 we also evaluated tumor growth based on tumor volume change rates. In this analysis, relative tumor growth was significantly suppressed in the combination therapy group compared with the control, anti-PD-1 monotherapy, and anti-IL-8 monotherapy groups (p = 0.029, p = 0.029, and p = 0.016, respectively) (Figure S5C). These findings suggest that dual blockade of IL-8 and PD-1 has the potential to suppress relative tumor growth in anti-PD-1-resistant tumors in the NG-PDX model.

Histological analysis revealed that the number of infiltrating CD11b+ cells was relatively reduced in tumors from mice treated with the combination therapy compared to the control or either monotherapy group (Figure 4C). Consistent with these findings, quantitative image analysis showed that anti-PD-1 monotherapy significantly reduced the proportion of intratumoral CD11b+ cells compared with control (p = 0.003), while anti-IL-8 monotherapy produced a greater reduction (p < 0.001). Although combination therapy also reduced the percentage of CD11b+ cells relative to control (p = 0.002), no additional decrease was observed compared with anti-IL-8 monotherapy alone (p = 0.655), and the difference between anti-PD-1 monotherapy and the combination was not significant (p = 0.180). Notably, anti-IL-8 monotherapy resulted in a significantly lower proportion of CD11b+ cells than anti-PD-1 alone (p = 0.029), suggesting that IL-8 blockade is the primary driver of reduced myeloid infiltration in this model (Figures 4C and 4D). In addition, to assess immune cell-independent direct effects of anti-IL-8 antibody, anti-IL-8 monotherapy was evaluated in a PBMC-free NG-PDX model (Figures S6A and S6B; Table S4). No significant differences in tumor volume change rate were observed between the anti-IL-8-treated and control groups at any measurement time point (p = 0.69). In vitro experiments further demonstrated that anti-IL-8 antibody alone did not exert cytotoxic effects on RCC cell lines (Figures S7A–S7C). These results support the conclusion that the therapeutic effects observed in this study are mediated through immunomodulation of the TME rather than direct antitumor activity of anti-IL-8 antibody alone. To assess whether IL-8 blockade directly affects immune cell viability, we treated hPBMCs with anti-IL-8 antibody and observed no cytotoxic effects (Figure S8). IL-8 is known to recruit both PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs15; thus, IL-8 blockade likely disrupted this recruitment and alleviated the immunosuppressive TME, resulting in a synergistic effect with PD-1 inhibition.

High M-MDSC gene signature is associated with poor OS in the CheckMate 025 trial

In our institutional cohort, we performed Kaplan-Meier survival analyses stratified by high versus low peripheral M-MDSC levels; however, due to the limited sample size (n = 22), these analyses did not reach statistical significance (Figure S9). Therefore, to further evaluate the clinical relevance of M-MDSCs in an adequately powered setting, we investigated the association between an M-MDSC gene signature and clinical outcomes following anti-PD-1 therapy using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from an independent cohort of the CheckMate 025 trial, which demonstrated the clinical efficacy of the anti-PD-1 antibody nivolumab in patients with aRCC. Importantly, patients with a high M-MDSC gene signature exhibited significantly worse OS in the nivolumab treatment group (hazard ratio [HR], 1.49; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–2.15; p = 0.032) (Figure 4E). In contrast, no significant association was observed between the M-MDSC signature and OS in patients treated with the mTOR inhibitor everolimus (HR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.65–1.49; p = 0.930). These findings show that high M-MDSC enrichment is associated with poor OS specifically in the nivolumab arm of CheckMate 025, indicating its role as a negative predictive biomarker for PD-1 blockade in aRCC. They also provide external evidence that aligns with and supports the mechanistic results from our NG-PDX model and the exploratory findings from our limited institutional cohort.

Discussion

We explored predictive biomarkers of ICI efficacy through the analysis of clinically accessible samples, including pre-treatment tumor tissues and PBMCs collected before and during anti-PD-1 therapy.

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between R and NR in the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD4+, CD8+, Foxp3+, PD-L1+, PD-L2+ cells, or other immune markers in pre-treatment tumor tissues. In contrast, longitudinal analyses of peripheral immune biomarkers in our cohort (e.g., TIM-3+ and CD39+ CD8+ T cells and M-MDSCs) revealed trends associated with reduced responsiveness to PD-1 blockade. Specifically, NR showed increased frequencies of TIM-3+CD8+ and CD39+CD8+ T cells, whereas R demonstrated a decrease in TIGIT+CD8+ T cells. TIGIT is an early expressed exhaustion marker, whereas TIM-3 is considered a marker of late-stage T cell exhaustion, and TIM-3 has been reported to be highly expressed on CD39+ T cells.30,31 In light of these findings, the immune phenotypes observed in our study suggest that, despite PD-1 blockade, CD8+ T cell exhaustion may progress toward a terminal state in NR, whereas CD8+ T cells in R may retain relatively greater functional potential. These peripheral features support the notion that systemic immune dysregulation, rather than local features of the primary tumor alone, contributes to resistance in NR. Nevertheless, the absolute magnitude of changes in these T cell subsets was small, and their distributions overlapped considerably between groups. Accordingly, these T-cell-related findings should be interpreted as exploratory and correlative rather than as definitive determinants of treatment response.

In comparison to these T-cell-related findings with modest effect sizes, the frequency of M-MDSCs in peripheral blood cells remained consistently higher in NR at all time points. This trend was observed uniformly across individual cases. Both MDSC subpopulations, PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs, have been reported as poor prognostic factors in patients treated with ICI therapy,32,33,34,35 and M-MDSCs have demonstrated stronger T cell suppressive capacity compared to PMN-MDSCs.15 Moreover, M-MDSCs can be induced when human monocytes are cultured with the supernatant derived from 786-O RCC cells,36 suggesting that soluble tumor-derived factors from RCC may drive M-MDSC induction, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive TME. M-MDSCs are known to suppress the proliferation of autologous T cells isolated from patients with cancer, and tumor-infiltrating M-MDSCs contribute to tumor immune evasion through multiple mechanisms.15,37

MDSCs (PMN- and M-MDSCs) are recruited by IL-8, which is also implicated in angiogenesis, tumor growth and invasion, and neutrophil recruitment during cancer progression.15 According to previous reports, IL-8 is released from tumor tissues, and its serum concentrations correlate with tumor burden.38 Elevated plasma IL-8 levels have been reported as predictive markers of resistance to ICI therapy.15,16,17 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that IL-8 inhibition could overcome resistance to ICI therapy by limiting MDSC recruitment and/or suppressive function. To evaluate the antitumor effects of IL-8 inhibition, we conducted a functional in vivo analysis of the antitumor effects of IL-8 blockade in combination with PD-1 inhibition.

For the in vivo model, we established an NG-PDX model using patient-derived tumors, matched PBMCs, and NOG-ΔMHC mice lacking murine MHC class I and II. A defining feature of this platform is its autologous design, in which both the engrafted tumor tissue and immune cells originate from the same patient, thereby minimizing alloreactive immune responses that are inherent to humanized mouse models employing unrelated donor PBMCs. This approach reduces the confounding influence of alloimmunity on tumor control and enables evaluation of immune responses that are driven predominantly by patient-specific tumor antigens. The NG-PDX model also permits functional in vivo assessment of immune checkpoint blockade in a patient-derived human tumor-immune microenvironment. This enables direct interrogation of treatment-induced immune dynamics, including changes in immune cell activation, proliferation, and intratumoral composition, under physiologically relevant conditions.

In therapeutic experiments using this model, combined PD-1 and IL-8 blockade showed a trend toward reduced tumor growth. Interestingly, tumors treated with anti-IL-8 antibody exhibited decreased intratumoral CD11b+ myeloid cell infiltration, suggesting that IL-8 blockade may modulate the recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, including MDSCs, into the TME. Such modulation of the myeloid compartment may help limit the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and thereby enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Consistent with our preclinical findings, early-phase clinical studies have begun to explore IL-8 blockade as a therapeutic strategy in cancer. A first-in-human phase 1 trial of the anti-IL-8 monoclonal antibody demonstrated manageable toxicity and acceptable safety in patients with metastatic or unresectable solid tumors.39,40 These clinical observations support the feasibility of targeting the IL-8-myeloid axis in combination with PD-1 blockade and align with the immunomodulatory effects observed in our preclinical NG-PDX model. In addition, our data suggest that reduced intratumoral infiltration of CD11b+ cells may represent one of the components underlying the observed therapeutic effect. These observations collectively suggest that the myeloid/IL-8 axis, particularly M-MDSCs, may represent a promising therapeutic target to overcome resistance to ICI therapy.

Notably, the NG-PDX model we developed introduces several innovations relative to existing in vivo platforms.19 When human PBMCs were inoculated into conventional immune-deficient mice, such as NOG or NSG, the mice developed GVHD due to xenoreactivity, leading to mortality. Although MISTRG mice are another immunodeficient model in which human cytokine genes have been knocked in, enabling the reconstitution of human immunity upon transplantation of human hematopoietic stem cells,41,42 in this model, T cells derived from human hematopoietic stem cells undergo thymic selection in the murine thymus, resulting in a TCR repertoire that differs from that of human T cells. Prior studies using NOG-ΔMHC mice have reported models involving intravenous co-injection of human PBMCs and human tumor cell lines.23,43,44,45,46 However, tumor shrinkage in these models may result from alloimmune responses between the human T cells and tumor cell lines, and therapies such as anti-PD-1 antibodies have further augmented this alloreactivity.

Our NG-PDX model enables long-term in vivo evaluation of human immune responses while suppressing xenogeneic GVHD through the use of autologous paired tumors and PBMCs in NOG-ΔMHC mice.24,45,46,47

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to establish a human ICI therapeutic model in vivo using NOG-ΔMHC mice with paired patient-derived tumor tissue and immune cells. This model enables functional evaluation of patient-specific anti-tumor immune response in a physiologically relevant context. Indeed, in contrast to prior IL-8 studies that have primarily reported correlative relationships with clinical outcomes, our work includes functional evaluation using an autologous human tumor and immune model. Collectively, these data extend existing correlative observations and provide a framework for mechanistic studies of patient-specific immune resistance and therapeutic response in RCC.

In summary, we identified immune-related features associated with response and resistance to ICI therapy in aRCC, including T cell exhaustion markers and M-MDSCs. Using an autologous NG-PDX model incorporating patient-matched tumors and PBMCs, we further observed that IL-8 blockade alone was insufficient to control tumor growth, whereas combined IL-8 and PD-1 inhibition tended to modulate the immunosuppressive TME and showed potential antitumor activity. Importantly, this study highlights the autologous NG-PDX platform as a unique system for functional interrogation of immune resistance mechanisms. While our findings support further investigation of IL-8-targeted combination strategies for overcoming resistance to PD-1 blockade in aRCC, additional work is needed to fully establish the underlying causal mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

First, immune features identified in surgically resected primary tumors were correlated with clinical responses assessed in metastatic lesions; therefore, tissue-based findings are interpreted as correlative. Second, because Foxp3 staining was not performed in peripheral blood analyses, increases in CD4+CD25+ cells cannot be definitively attributed to regulatory T cells. Third, while our study focused on the modulation of the myeloid compartment, broad evaluation of immune cell states within the TME could provide additional mechanistic insight into the observed therapeutic trends and the potential synergistic effects of this combination. Fourth, technical constraints related to limited clinical material and cell viability precluded direct in vitro functional assays testing whether IL-8 blockade alters the suppressive activity of M-MDSCs. Fifth, autologous NG-PDX models require ongoing access to viable PBMCs from living donors. Finally, patient-level analyses in our institutional cohort were based on a relatively small sample size and were primarily correlative in nature. To address this limitation, we complemented these analyses with signature-based evaluation in an external phase III bulk RNA-seq dataset from CheckMate 025, which provided independent support for the observed associations. However, these findings should not be directly extrapolated to other ICI-based combination regimens without further validation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study approval and animal ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Graduate School of Medicine, Akita University (approval numbers: 790, 924, 1479, 1669, and 2053), and was performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All specimens were collected from healthy individuals and patients after obtaining written informed consent.

All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee at Akita University (approval number: a-1-0506) and were performed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). Predefined humane endpoints were established according to the approved experimental protocol and included body weight loss of more than 20% within 2–3 days or more than 25% within 7 days, or signs of severe distress such as persistent recumbency or crouching. These criteria were uniformly applied across all in vivo experiments. For tumor-bearing model experiments in Figures 3, 4, S2, S4, S5, and S6, the criteria included tumor burden exceeding 10% of body weight, tumor diameter exceeding 20 mm, tumor volume exceeding 4000 mm3 (maximum permitted tumor volume), or the presence of tumor ulceration. Animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation or cervical dislocation at the scheduled time points or when these humane endpoints were reached, or when signs of distress or compromised health status were observed during monitoring.

Clinical data annotation and collection of specimens

We analyzed patients with aRCC who received the anti-PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab (3 mg/kg) every two weeks or a combination of nivolumab (3 mg/kg) and the anti-CTLA-4 antibody ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) every three weeks at the Department of Urology, Akita University Hospital between 2015 and 2020. For Figures 1 and 2, we retrospectively reviewed computed tomography (CT) images of tumors and evaluated changes in tumor lesions from baseline. Based on the irRECIST criteria, a reduction of 30% or more was defined as a responder, and an increase of 20% or more as a non-responder. Clinical data were compared between the two groups (Figure 1C). Blood samples were collected at predefined time points: at baseline (prior to drug administration on day 1 of treatment), and at week 2 and week 4 for nivolumab, or at week 3 and week 6 for ipilimumab plus nivolumab, following the initiation of ICI therapy. IHC was performed on TMAs constructed from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) surgical or biopsy specimens collected for RCC diagnosis. PBMCs were sequentially isolated from patient blood samples, cryopreserved, and analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 1A). Radiotherapy history was reviewed for all patients. All radiotherapy events were directed toward metastatic lesions (including central nervous system irradiation in some cases), and their timing relative to ICI initiation varied among patients. In accordance with irRECIST, previously irradiated lesions were excluded from target lesion assessment, and objective response was evaluated solely on the basis of non-irradiated measurable disease. Baseline peripheral blood biomarkers were collected prior to ICI initiation to capture the systemic immune status.

Mouse models

Six-week-old female NOG mice were purchased from In-Vivo Science Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), and experiments were initiated when the mice reached nine weeks of age. These mice were used for xenogenic GVHD and conventional PDX models.

Five- to seven-weeks-old female NOG-ΔMHC23,24 were purchased from In-Vivo Science Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). These mice were used to establish NG-PDX models.

Cell lines

Human renal cancer cell lines ACHN, 786-O, and Caki-1 (ATCC; STR-authenticated; mycoplasma-negative) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Method details

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemical staining

For Figures 1D and 1E, TMAs were constructed at the Pathology Institute (Toyama, Japan) using paraffin blocks of primary tissues obtained from surgical or biopsy specimens of patients who underwent procedures at our institution prior to ICI administration. Briefly, a urologist (R.S.) examined the hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections of each paraffin block to identify areas containing residual RCC. Triplicate cores, each measuring 2.0 mm in diameter, were extracted from the cancerous regions and embedded into the TMAs. Tissues with insufficient residual tumor were excluded. A total of 15 patients were included in the TMA analysis, consisting of 7 responders and 8 non-responders. Not all patients from the clinical cohort could be incorporated into the TMA because tumor blocks were unavailable for five cases (e.g., specimens obtained at outside institutions), and two additional cases contained insufficient residual tumors to allow core extraction. IHC was performed on the TMA using the following primary antibodies: CD4 (1:100, Leica, NCL-L-CD4-368), CD8 (1:100, DAKO, #M7103), Foxp3 (1:50, BioLegend, #320102), CD11b (1:1000, GeneTex, GTX113089), CD20 (1:300, eBioscience, 14-0202-82), HLA-DR/DP/DQ (1:750, Abcam, ab7856), MPO (1:1000, Abcam, #ab208670), CD163 (1:2000, Proteintech, 16646-1-AP), and PD-L2 (1:200, Abcam, ab244332). Additionally, IHC for CD56 was performed using the Ventana Discovery XT automated staining system (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, AZ, USA) with the primary antibody (1:50, Novocastra, #NCL-CD56-1B6). Ready-to-use antibodies were applied according to the manufacturers’ protocols: CD10 (ROCHE, #760-2705), CD31 (ROCHE, #760-4378), and PD-L1 (ROCHE, #790-4860).

All IHC procedures were performed at the Department of Clinical Pathology or the Division of Molecular Medicine, Bioscience Educational and Research Support Center, Akita University Graduate School of Medicine. For Figure 3H, double immunostaining for Ki67+CD8+ cells was conducted at the Center for Anatomical, Pathological, and Forensic Medical Research, Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine. The primary antibodies used were CD8 (1:100, Dako, M7103) and Ki67 (1:100, Leica, NCL-L-Ki67-MM1). Staining was performed using the avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (ABC) method (ABC Elite, Vector Laboratories)48 and Mouse to Mouse HRP (ScyTek Lab) methods. Images were obtained using a NanoZoomer slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). Following digital slide acquisition, immune cell densities were quantified using HistoQuest software (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria), as described previously.49,50 For each TMA core, nuclei identified by hematoxylin counterstaining were counted and used as the total cell number, while cells positive for DAB staining were classified as immunopositive cells. The proportion of immunopositive cells was calculated as the ratio of immunopositive cells to total cell count. To account for intratumoral heterogeneity and ensure statistical independence, three independent tumor cores were analyzed for each patient. The mean value of these three cores was calculated and used as the representative value for each patient in subsequent statistical comparisons. Differences between responders and non-responders were assessed based on these patient-level data.

For Figure 3D, the spleens of mice were excised and embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound (SAKURA FINETEK), and frozen at −80 °C. Xenogeneic GVHD models

Six-week-old female NOG mice were purchased from In-Vivo Science Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), and experiments were initiated when the mice reached nine weeks of age. For Figure 3A, PBMCs were isolated from the peripheral blood of a healthy donor, and 6.0 × 106 cells were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected into each mouse on days 1 and 2. Immune cells administered i.p. rapidly enter the circulation, and this route provides greater procedural stability and reproducibility compared with intravenous injection.51,52,53,54 Body weight and GVHD scores25 were monitored twice weekly for all mice. On day 31, the blood and spleen were collected from the mice. Blood samples were analyzed by flow cytometry, and spleens were embedded in either paraffin blocks or OCT compounds for further analysis.

Establishment and maintenance of conventional PDX model

RCC tumor tissue samples were obtained from patients who underwent radical or partial nephrectomy for RCC at the Department of Urology, Akita University Hospital. The inclusion criterion was a tumor size of at least 2 cm, as determined by preoperative CT. PDX models were established based on previously reported methods.55,56 Briefly, fresh tumor tissue was dissected from the resected specimens and subcutaneously implanted into the backs of NOG mice. The tumor diameters were measured weekly using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: tumor volume (V) = (length × width2)/2.23 For passaging or cryopreservation, tumors were excised under isoflurane anesthesia when they reached approximately 15 mm in diameter to preserve tissue quality and viability for serial transplantation and storage. Tumor fragments were either inoculated into other NOG mice for further passaging or placed into cryotubes filled with STEM-CELLBANKER (#CB047, ZENOAQ, Fukushima, Japan) and stored at -80°C. The establishment of PDX was defined as consistent tumor growth after three passages.

Next-generation PDX model

Five to seven weeks old NOG-ΔMHC23,24 were purchased from In-Vivo Science Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The established xenograft tumor was excised subcutaneously from conventional PDX mice under isoflurane anesthesia, cut into 5 mm fragments, and implanted subcutaneously into the backs of NOG-ΔMHC mice under isoflurane anesthesia. Cryopreserved tumors were thawed in a 37 °C water bath, thoroughly washed in 4°C PBS, then cut and implanted in the same manner. PBMCs with a total of 1.0 × 106 cells or more were injected i.p. into each NOG-ΔMHC mouse. The PBMCs were collected from the same patient who provided the tumor sample used for the PDX and were isolated from peripheral blood. Importantly, PBMCs were autologous to each corresponding tumor, being collected from the same patient who provided the tumor specimen used for PDX establishment, thereby eliminating the possibility of HLA alloreactivity. Flow cytometry was performed on the peripheral blood from each mouse, and mice that did not show engraftment of human CD45 (hCD45), hCD3, hCD4, or hCD8 were excluded from the analysis. The tumor diameters were measured once or twice weekly using digital calipers.

TCR repertoire analysis

For Figure 3I, we performed single-cell sorting of intratumoral CD3+CD8+PD-1+ cells (90 cells sorted per group), followed by sequencing of the TCRβ CDR3 region to analyze the TCR repertoire. The procedure was conducted according to a previously established method57 and is briefly summarized as follows: primers for RT-PCR were designed based on the leader and constant region sequences of human TCR genes annotated in the IMGT database (http://www.imgt.org). An adaptor sequence was added to the 5′ end of each leader primer. For human TCRα, 41 leader primers (AL primers) and 3 constant primers (Cα primers) were used; for TCRβ, 39 leader primers (BL primers) and 3 constant primers (Cβ primers) were used. Each sorted single T cell was placed into an individual well, and 5 μL of RT-PCR mix was added per well. One-step RT-PCR was performed under the following conditions: 45 °C for 40 min for RT reaction, 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 52 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. The PCR products, 10-fold diluted with nuclease-free water, were used for a second PCR round. TCRα and TCRβ cDNA were amplified separately. Specifically, 2 μL of the diluted first-round product was added to 18 μL of either the second PCR α or β mix in a 96-well PCR plate. Second-round PCR was performed under the following conditions: 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 52 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The amplified TCRβ products were then sequenced using the Cβ_RV3 primer. TCR repertoire was analyzed with the IMGT/V-Quest tool (http://www.imgt.org/).57 T-cell clones expressing identical TCRβ CDR3 sequences and consisting of more than two cells were defined as clonally expanded populations.58 To evaluate treatment-associated convergence of dominant TCR clonotypes, we analyzed the distribution of the most frequent TCRβ CDR3 sequences across mice. Clonotype frequencies were calculated per mouse based on identical TCRβ CDR3 amino acid sequences, and repertoires were downsampled to an equal depth corresponding to the minimum number of TCR reads across mice to account for sequencing depth differences. The most frequent clonotype for each mouse was defined as the TCRβ CDR3 sequence(s) with the highest frequency, including ties. Treatment-specific sharing was defined as the presence of at least one such clonotype shared among all PD-1–treated mice and absent from control mice. Statistical significance was assessed using a mouse-level permutation test with treatment labels randomly reassigned while preserving group sizes.

Sample preparation of blood and tissue from PDX models

Mouse PBMCs were isolated by red blood cell lysis using ACK buffer, followed by centrifugation at 1400 rpm for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the purified mouse PBMCs were promptly analyzed. Tumor tissues excised from PDX models were minced with surgical scissors and dissociated using a gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). The cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1400 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. After removal of the supernatant, the cells were subjected to density gradient centrifugation using 40% Percoll (GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) for further enrichment.59

ICI therapeutic models using NG-PDX

For Figure 3G, an anti-PD-1 antibody therapeutic experiment was conducted using NG-PDX mice, which were divided into anti-PD-1 antibody-treated and control groups, with 3 mice assigned to each group. Two weeks after subcutaneous inoculation of PDX tumors, 3.0 × 106 PBMCs were administered i.p. The anti-human PD-1 antibody (BioXcell, clone J116, #BE0188) was administered i.p. at a dose of 250 μg once per week. The control group received 250 μg of isotype IgG1 (BioXcell, #BE0083) under the same schedule. Body weight and tumor size were monitored twice weekly using digital calipers. The mice were euthanized at week 5, and peripheral blood, spleen, and tumor tissues were collected. Peripheral blood was analyzed by flow cytometry. Spleens were embedded in FFPE blocks. Tumor samples were partially embedded in FFPE, and the remaining tumor tissue was processed using the Percoll method to isolate TILs, followed by flow cytometry and single-cell sorting of CD3+CD8+PD-1+ cells.

For Figures 4A–4D, a combination therapy experiment using anti-hPD-1 and anti-IL-8 antibodies was conducted in NG-PDX mice. Mice were randomly assigned to control (Isotype IgG + Isotype IgG), anti-hPD-1 antibody monotherapy (anti-hPD-1 antibody + Isotype IgG), anti-IL-8 antibody monotherapy (Isotype IgG + anti-IL-8 antibody), and combination therapy (anti-hPD-1 antibody + anti-IL-8 antibody) groups, with 3-5 mice per group depending on the availability of engrafted tumors and donor PBMCs. On day 0, PDX tumors were subcutaneously inoculated. PBMCs (autologous to the corresponding tumor donor) were injected i.p. on day 5 (6.0 × 106 cells), and day 8 (2.3 × 106 cells). The anti-hPD-1 antibody was administered at 250 μg twice weekly. The anti-IL-8 antibody (R&D Anti-Human CXCL8/IL-8 Mab, #MAB-208-100) was administered at 0.5 μg on days 4, 6, and 9. The dose of the anti–IL-8 antibody was set at 0.025 mg/kg (0.5 μg per 20-g female mouse) to effectively neutralize human IL-8 in the NG-PDX model, based on the estimation that the resulting plasma concentration in mice would exceed the in vitro ND50. Body weight and tumor size were measured twice weekly. For tumor growth analyses, statistical comparisons were performed using tumor volume at the final time point (day 20), which served as the prespecified primary endpoint. On day 20, the mice were euthanized, and the peripheral blood, spleens, and tumors were harvested. Peripheral blood was analyzed by flow cytometry, and spleens and tumors were embedded in FFPE for subsequent analysis.

Quantitative analysis of intratumoral myeloid cells was performed in FFPE-embedded NG-PDX tumors. Tumor sections immunostained for human CD11b were analyzed using HistoQuest software. For each mouse, viable tumor regions were manually selected to exclude necrotic areas. CD11b+ cells and total nuclei were quantified based on DAB and hematoxylin signals, respectively, and the proportion of intratumoral myeloid cells was calculated as the percentage of CD11b+ cells relative to the total number of nuclei within the defined regions.

Signature score analysis

For Figure 4E, a set of gene signatures was defined by considering 12 genes related to M-MDSCs, including S100A9, S100A8, ARG1, ARG2, NOS2, IL10, VEGFA, CD14, TGFB1, TNF, STAT3, and IL6.18 The OS data for the CheckMate 025 cohort (NCT01668784) were analyzed using a previously published TPM matrix.12 High- and low-signature groups were defined using median splitting. Signature scoring and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted separately for patients treated with the anti-PD-1 antibody nivolumab and those treated with the mTOR inhibitor everolimus.

Cytotoxicity and viability assay in renal cancer cell lines

For viability assays, ACHN, 786-O, and Caki-1 cells were seeded into 24-well flat-bottom plates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells per well in 400 μL and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then treated for 48 h with anti-IL-8 monoclonal antibody (R&D Anti-Human CXCL8/IL-8 Mab, Cat. #MAB208) at concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, 1, or 2 μg/mL. An isotype-matched control antibody (Syd Lab In Vivo Grade Recombinant Mouse IgG1 Isotype Control Antibody, #PA007126) was used at the same concentrations as a negative control. In addition, doxorubicin (Selleck Doxorubicin, #S1208) was administered at 0.01, 0.1, 1, or 5 μg/mL for 48 h as a positive control to confirm assay sensitivity.

Cell viability was assessed by dead-cell staining and cell counting. Following treatment, cells were detached with trypsin–EDTA, resuspended in complete medium, and mixed 1:1 with 0.4% trypan blue solution. Live and non-viable (trypan blue–positive) cells were counted using a manual hemocytometer or an automated cell counter. Percent viability was calculated as live cells/(live + dead cells) × 100.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) or R v4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software, version 11 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical comparisons of clinical data were conducted using either the Mann-Whitney U test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the variable type. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for flow cytometry and in vivo paired data analysis, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for image cytometry and unpaired in vivo data analysis. An alpha level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. For gene signature analysis, differences in survival were evaluated using a two-sided log-rank test with a significance threshold of 0.05. Where asterisks are shown in the figures, they indicate statistical significance (p < 0.05). The specific statistical test used for each comparison is described in the corresponding figure legend.
