taVNS alleviates depressive-like and metabolic dysfunction in T2DD mice with modulation of hypothalamic 5-HT signaling
Authors: Yuzhengheng Zhang, Qing Zhou, Ningyi Zou, Chen Xin, Peijing Rong, Shaoyuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes mellitus and depression are mutually reinforcing, yet combined pharmacotherapy is limited by side effects and poor tolerability. Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a non-invasive neuromodulatory approach with emerging metabolic and antidepressant potential, although its central mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we investigated whether hypothalamic serotonergic signaling is involved in the dual effects of taVNS in a mouse model of type 2 diabetes with depression (T2DD). T2DD was induced in db/db mice by chronic unpredictable mild stress, followed by taVNS intervention (2/15 Hz, 1 mA, 30 min/day for 3 weeks). Behavioral performance, body weight, and fasting blood glucose were assessed, while brain-wide neuronal activity was mapped using fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST). Hypothalamic 5-HT release was monitored by fiber photometry, and 5-HT content and 5-HT₁A receptor expression were quantified by ELISA and Western blotting. TaVNS significantly reduced body weight and fasting blood glucose, alleviated depression-like behaviors, and improved locomotor activity. Brain-wide mapping revealed state-dependent modulation of neuronal activity, characterized by widespread activation under physiological conditions and suppression of stress-induced hyperactivation in T2DD. Notably, taVNS induced robust hypothalamic 5-HT release, increased long-term 5-HT levels, and upregulated 5-HT₁A receptor expression. Correlation analyses further linked enhanced hypothalamic serotonergic signaling to improved glycemic control and behavioral outcomes. These findings suggest that hypothalamic 5-HT signaling may contribute to the dual metabolic and antidepressant effects of taVNS and highlight its potential as an integrative neuromodulatory intervention for metabolic–psychiatric comorbidity.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Diseases

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus with comorbid depression (T2DD) is highly prevalent and imposes a substantial global burden. Approximately 10–30% of patients with T2DM develop depression [1, 2], which further impairs glycemic control, forming a vicious cycle [3]. Current combined pharmacotherapies face limitations including side effects, drug interactions, and poor adherence. The traditional separation of metabolic and psychiatric care is inadequate for this intertwined condition. Although growing evidence supports integrated interventions targeting both physical and mental health [4], effective dual-action therapies remain limited.

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS), a non-invasive neuromodulation technique, has attracted increasing attention. The vagus nerve is a key communication pathway between the central nervous system and peripheral organs, regulating emotion, stress responses, and metabolic homeostasis [5]. While invasive vagus nerve stimulation is approved for treatment-resistant depression, it requires surgical implantation with associated risks and costs. In contrast, taVNS delivers low-intensity electrical stimulation to the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, enabling safe, non-surgical modulation [6]. Clinical studies have reported improvements in depressive symptoms such as mood, anxiety, and sleep disturbances [7, 8], while preclinical evidence indicates that taVNS attenuates weight gain and improves glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in type 2 diabetes models [9, 10]. However, most previous studies have examined these metabolic and antidepressant effects in isolation, and whether taVNS can exert coordinated therapeutic actions through shared central neural mechanisms in metabolic–affective comorbidity remains largely unclear. These findings nonetheless suggest that taVNS may represent a promising integrative intervention for T2DD.

The serotonin (5-HT) neurotransmitter system has emerged as a potential mechanistic link in the pathogenesis of T2DD. Widely distributed in the central nervous system, 5-HT plays a dual role in emotional and metabolic regulation [11]. Within the hypothalamus, 5-HT suppresses appetite, promotes satiety, and modulates insulin secretion and glucose homeostasis [12, 13]. Pharmacological enhancement of central 5-HT signaling, such as with fenfluramine or 5-HT2C receptor agonists, reduces food intake and improves glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity [14], whereas disruption of 5-HT synthesis or receptor function induces hyperphagia, weight gain, and insulin resistance [13].

In parallel, impaired 5-HT signaling is closely associated with depression. According to the monoamine hypothesis, reduced brain 5-HT contributes to depressive symptoms, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors exert antidepressant effects by enhancing serotonergic transmission [15]. Diabetic animal models exhibit reduced central 5-HT levels and signaling activity, which may underlie comorbid depression-like behaviors [16]. Together, these findings suggest that 5-HT dysregulation represents a shared central mechanism linking metabolic and affective dysfunction in T2DD.

Although taVNS has demonstrated glucose-lowering and antidepressant effects in separate experimental or clinical contexts, little is known about how vagal neuromodulation reorganizes large-scale brain activity under conditions of metabolic–emotional comorbidity. In particular, the potential central substrates that may simultaneously link to metabolic and affective improvements remain poorly defined. The hypothalamus is widely recognized as a critical integrative center linking peripheral metabolic signals with neuroendocrine and emotional responses. It receives visceral afferent information indirectly from brainstem vagal relay nuclei and interacts with monoaminergic systems involved in mood regulation. Therefore, the present study employed fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST) to systematically map taVNS-induced changes in neuronal activation across the whole brain in a mouse model of type 2 diabetes with comorbid depression. Given the integrative role of the hypothalamus in coordinating energy balance, glucose homeostasis, and stress-related emotional responses, we further focused on hypothalamic serotonergic signaling as a candidate mechanism linking these dual therapeutic outcomes. By combining brain-wide activity mapping with real-time monitoring of hypothalamic 5-HT release and molecular analyses, we aimed to investigate the potential involvement of hypothalamic serotonergic activity in the metabolic and antidepressant effects of taVNS.

Methods

Animals

SPF-grade male db/db mice (n = 10) and db/m mice (n = 10), aged 7 weeks, were obtained from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK [Su] 2020-0009). Animals were housed at the Institute of Acupuncture and Moxibustion, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, under controlled conditions (temperature 20–25 °C, humidity 30–50%) with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and adequate ventilation.

All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Acupuncture and Moxibustion, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No. Y2024-03-11-03) and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines on the Humane Treatment of Laboratory Animals and the Guidelines for Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892-2018). Animals were anesthetized, kept warm, and physiologically monitored throughout the experiments to ensure adequate anesthesia depth. In addition, animals were observed daily for general health status, and predefined humane endpoints included severe body weight loss ( > 20%), persistent hypoactivity, impaired grooming, or signs of marked distress. Supportive care was provided when necessary to minimize suffering. At the end of the study, animals were euthanized by anesthetic overdose. No animals or samples were excluded from the analysis. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined prior to the start of the experiments, and no deviations occurred during the study. Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence.

Modeling

Experiment 1: db/db mice, carrying a leptin gene mutation, were used as a spontaneous model of type 2 diabetes mellitus, while db/m mice served as non-diabetic controls. To better mimic the pathophysiological features of T2DD, the db/db mice in the model and model+taVNS groups were subjected to a 3-week chronic unpredictable mild stress paradigm combined with social isolation. Stressors included: cold water swim at 4 °C for 4 min, warm water exposure at 40 °C for 4 min, 24 h food deprivation, 24 h water deprivation, cage tilting at 45° for 24 h, reversed light/dark cycle for 24 h, foot shock (0.2 mA), and tail pinch for 1 min. Cage tilting, reversed light/dark cycle, foot shock, and tail pinch were administered twice weekly, while the remaining stressors were applied once weekly. Mice exhibiting depressive-like behaviors, as verified by the tail suspension test and open field test, were considered to have successfully developed the T2DD phenotype.

Following one week of acclimatization, db/m mice were randomly assigned to the control group (n = 3) or control+taVNS group (n = 3), while db/db mice were allocated to the model group (n = 3) or model+taVNS group (n = 3). The inclusion of the control+taVNS group was intended to characterize baseline neuromodulatory responses to taVNS under physiological conditions, thereby facilitating evaluation of potential state-dependent neural modulation. All mice received a tail vein injection of rAAV-PHP.eB-cFos-EYFP to label neuronal activation patterns for subsequent whole-brain analysis.

Experiment 2: After acclimatization, db/m mice were assigned to the control+taVNS group (n = 5), and db/db mice to the model+taVNS group (n = 5), for real-time monitoring of 5-HT release using a 5-HT fluorescent probe combined with fiber photometry. In parallel, another cohort of mice was established: db/m mice as the control group (n = 5), and db/db mice randomly divided into the model group (n = 5) and model+taVNS group (n = 5). Hypothalamic 5-HT content and 5-HT₁A receptor protein expression levels were assessed using ELISA and Western blot analyses. Sample size was determined with reference to previous studies employing similar experimental paradigms and expected effect sizes rather than formal a priori power calculations [10, 17, 18].

Body weight and FBG measurements

After one week of acclimatization, body weight was measured weekly at a fixed time using an electronic balance. FBG levels were assessed once per week at the same time point using tail-tip blood sampling and a Bayer Contour glucometer (Shanghai JianZhen Medical Technology Co., Ltd.).

Tail vein injection of AAV in mice

Mice were secured using a Li-type restrainer. After fixation, the tail was disinfected with 75% ethanol to dilate the veins. The AAV vector rAAV-PHP.eB-cFos-EYFP (purchased from Brain Case Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was prepared at a titer of 3.89 × 10¹² vg/mL. Each mouse received 1 × 10¹¹ vg of the virus via tail vein injection, using a 1 mL syringe. The viral solution was diluted in 100 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline.

Behavioral tests

Investigators performing behavioral tests and molecular analyses were blinded to group allocation. Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using coded group identifiers to maintain blinding throughout the study.

Tail suspension test

Each mouse was suspended by the tail (1 cm from the tip) using adhesive tape attached to a box 60 cm above the floor and facing a video camera. Following a 2-min habituation period, immobility time during the subsequent 4 min was recorded using a video tracking system. Immobility was defined as the absence of active struggling, with the mouse remaining motionless.

Open field test

The open field test was used to assess locomotor and exploratory behaviors in mice. The open field consisted of a black opaque arena (100 × 100 × 40 cm) with the floor divided into 25 equal squares. Mice were placed in the center and allowed to explore freely for 5 min, with the first 2 min serving as habituation. During the final 3 min, horizontal locomotor activity (squares crossed) and vertical exploratory activity (rearing counts) were recorded using a video-based system. Tests were conducted in a quiet, dimly lit room, and the arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. Horizontal activity was used as an indicator of general locomotor activity, whereas vertical activity reflected exploratory behavior and motivational state. These parameters are widely used behavioral indices in open field paradigms for assessing locomotor and exploratory activity in rodents [19, 20].

Intervention

taVNS intervention began in week 3. Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (4% for induction, 2% for maintenance, both mixed with 99.5% O₂; Hebei Jiusheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.). Under anesthesia, self-adhesive conductive magnets (positive and negative poles) were non-invasively affixed to the bilateral cavum conchae. Slight fluttering of the auricle indicated effective stimulation. Stimulation was delivered daily at midday (zeitgeber time ZT 5–8), 30 min per session, for three consecutive weeks. Parameters were: sparse-dense wave pattern, 2/15 Hz alternating frequency, 1 mA intensity, using a HANS-100A electro-acupuncture stimulator (Nanjing HANS Corp.). These settings and electrode locations have been validated in previous studies [17].

Group assignments and interventions were as follows:

Control group: ad libitum food and water, no stimulation.

Control + taVNS group: identical anesthesia and taVNS protocol as above for 3 weeks.

Model group: T2DD modeling, ad libitum food and water, no stimulation.

Model + taVNS group: T2DD modeling followed by the same taVNS protocol as the Control + taVNS group for 3 weeks.

Fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography imaging and data processing

Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane in oxygen (1 L min⁻¹), perfused, and brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. Fixed brains were embedded in agarose, oriented with the longitudinal axis parallel to the sample holder, and mounted on a three-axis translation stage immersed in 0.01 M PBS. Whole-brain fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST) was performed using a BioMapping 9000 system (Oebio Biologics, China) with an optical section thickness of 40 µm. Each imaging cycle consisted of vibratome sectioning (77 Hz, X-axis speed 0.2 mm s⁻¹), stage translation, light-sheet scanning, and repositioning for subsequent sectioning.

Raw image stripes from a single detection channel were pre-processed by lateral illumination correction using polynomial fitting, followed by stripe stitching within each optical plane. Images were saved as 16-bit TIFF files with LZW compression. Pre-processing was implemented in MATLAB and C + +. Data were down-sampled and registered to the Allen Mouse Common Coordinate Framework v3 (CCFv3) using the Brain Registration, Identification and Encoding Framework (BRIEF), and deformation parameters were applied for co-localised visualization.

Three-dimensional visualization and analysis were performed using Amira v2020.1 (FEI, France) and Imaris v9.7.2 (Bitplane, Switzerland), including region-of-interest extraction, reslicing, maximum-intensity projection, and volume or surface rendering. Stress-related hyperactivation was operationally defined as a relative increase in regional c-Fos–positive neuronal density in T2DD mice compared with non-stressed control mice, quantified using atlas-registered whole-brain datasets and region-of-interest–based analyses.

5-HT fluorescent probe injection and fiber-optic implantation

Mice were anesthetized with 0.3% pentobarbital sodium and placed in a stereotaxic frame. After scalp shaving and disinfection with 75% ethanol, a midline incision was made to expose the skull. The bregma was identified using the Franklin and Paxinos mouse brain atlas, and stereotaxic coordinates for the target region were determined. A small craniotomy was drilled, and the dura was opened with a 30-gauge needle.

A glass micropipette connected to a micro-injector was filled with rAAV-hSyn-5-HT3.0 (0.3 µL) and lowered into the target nucleus [21]. Virus was infused at 0.01 µL min⁻¹, and the pipette was left in place for 10 min to allow diffusion and prevent backflow. An optical fiber with a ceramic ferrule was subsequently implanted 100 µm above the injection site and secured in position.

Fiber photometry recording

After a 3-week viral expression period, fiber photometry was performed to monitor hypothalamic 5-HT activity in vivo. Mice were habituated to patch cords for 1 h daily over 3 days, then anesthetized with 0.3% pentobarbital sodium and connected to an optical fiber. Following baseline recording, taVNS stimulation was applied for 30 min, and fluorescence signals were continuously acquired throughout the recording period.

Excitation was provided by a 480 nm LED, and emission signals were filtered, amplified, and sampled at 100 Hz. Data were analyzed using MATLAB (R2022b) and normalized as ΔF/F = (F − F₀)/F₀, where F₀ represents the baseline fluorescence.

Elisa

After the intervention, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 0.3% pentobarbital sodium (50 mL/kg). The hypothalamus was rapidly dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and homogenized in ice-cold buffer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis.

Hypothalamic 5-HT levels were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and 5-HT concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve.

Western blot

Western blotting was performed to assess hypothalamic 5-HT1A receptor expression. Tissues were homogenized and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes.

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against 5-HT1A receptor (rabbit polyclonal, Abcam, ab85615, 1:1000) and β-tubulin (mouse monoclonal, Abcam, ab6046, 1:2000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Abcam, ab205718, 1:5000) and goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Abcam, ab205719, 1:5000).

Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified by densitometric analysis. Relative 5-HT1A expression was normalized to β-tubulin.

Data statistics

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0, and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 8.4.0. Sample size was determined based on previous studies using similar experimental paradigms and effect sizes reported in the literature. Group sizes were selected to ensure adequate detection of biologically meaningful differences while minimizing animal use. No formal a priori power calculation was performed. Data were first assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. Data were first assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met. When assumptions of normality were not satisfied, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. All statistical tests were two-sided. Correlations were assessed using linear regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Changes in body weight, fasting blood glucose, and behavioral phenotype at different time points

No significant differences in body weight were observed among groups before T2DD modeling (0 w; P > 0.05). After modeling (3 w), body weight in both the model and model + taVNS groups was significantly higher than that in the control group (P < 0.001), with no difference between the control and control + taVNS groups. After three weeks of taVNS intervention (6 w), body weight in the model + taVNS group was significantly lower than that in the model group, indicating that taVNS significantly attenuated body weight gain in T2DD mice (P < 0.01; Fig. 1A).

Statistical significance: ***P < 0.001 vs. Control group; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 vs. Model group.

Fasting blood glucose levels did not differ among groups at baseline (0 w; P > 0.05). At 3 w, fasting glucose was significantly elevated in the model and model + taVNS groups compared with controls (P < 0.001), whereas taVNS had no effect in control mice. After taVNS treatment (6 w), fasting glucose was markedly reduced in the model + taVNS group compared with the model group (P < 0.001; Fig. 1B).

Horizontal and vertical locomotor activities showed no baseline differences among groups (0 w). Both measures were significantly reduced in the model and model + taVNS groups after modeling (3 w; P < 0.001), with no change in control + taVNS mice. After three weeks of taVNS (6 w), horizontal (P < 0.001) and vertical (P < 0.05) locomotor activities were significantly increased in the model + taVNS group compared with the model group (Fig. 1C, D).

Similarly, no group differences in tail suspension immobility time were observed at baseline (0 w). After modeling (3 w), immobility time was significantly increased in the model and model + taVNS groups (P < 0.001), whereas taVNS had no effect in control mice. Following taVNS intervention (6 w), immobility time was significantly reduced in the model + taVNS group relative to the model group (P < 0.001; Fig. 1E).

Effects of taVNS on c-Fos protein expression in primary, secondary, and tertiary brain regions under physiological conditions

Whole-brain coronal, sagittal, and horizontal reconstructions showed that taVNS markedly increased c-Fos expression across multiple brain regions under physiological conditions (Fig. 2A). Quantitative analyses confirmed a robust elevation of c-Fos protein density in the control + taVNS group compared with controls.

At the primary level, c-Fos density was significantly increased in the cerebrum, brainstem, and cerebellum (P < 0.001 for all; Fig. 2B). Consistently, secondary brain regions—including the cerebral cortex, cerebral nuclei, diencephalon, midbrain, hindbrain, cerebellar cortex, and cerebellar nuclei—showed pronounced c-Fos upregulation following taVNS (P < 0.001 for all; Fig. 2C). At the tertiary level, widespread increases in c-Fos expression were observed in cortical and subcortical structures, including the striatum, globus pallidus, thalamus, hypothalamus, brainstem nuclei, and cerebellar regions (P < 0.001), with additional elevations in midbrain sensory-related regions and deep cerebellar nuclei (P < 0.01; Fig. 2D).

Effects of taVNS on c-Fos protein expression in primary, secondary, and tertiary brain regions in T2DD mice

Whole-brain coronal, sagittal, and horizontal reconstructions revealed that taVNS generally attenuated c-Fos expression across multiple brain regions in T2DD mice compared with untreated T2DD controls (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analyses confirmed a broad normalization of pathological neuronal activation following taVNS intervention. Consistent with our operational definition, stress-related hyperactivation refers to the relative elevation of regional c-Fos–positive neuronal density in T2DD mice compared with non-stressed controls.

At the primary level, c-Fos protein density in the cerebrum and brainstem was significantly reduced in the T2DD + taVNS group (P < 0.001), while no change was observed in the cerebellum (Fig. 3B). At the secondary level, taVNS markedly decreased c-Fos expression in the cerebral cortex, cerebral nuclei, diencephalon, midbrain, and hindbrain (P < 0.001), whereas c-Fos density was increased in the cerebellar nuclei (P < 0.001) with no change in the cerebellar cortex (Fig. 3C).

At the tertiary level, taVNS significantly reduced c-Fos expression in widespread cortical and subcortical regions, including the striatum, globus pallidus, hypothalamus, brainstem nuclei, and midbrain sensory- and motor-related areas (P < 0.01). In contrast, increased c-Fos density was observed in the thalamus and multiple cerebellar regions, including the hemispheric region and deep cerebellar nuclei (P < 0.001), while the interposed nucleus showed a moderate increase (P < 0.01). No significant change was detected in the cerebellar temporal area (Fig. 3D).

Effects of taVNS on c-Fos protein expression in the hypothalamus of mice

Under physiological conditions, taVNS markedly increased c-Fos expression across multiple hypothalamic subregions compared with controls (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analyses revealed significant increases in c-Fos–positive cell density in nuclei involved in emotional and visceral regulation, including the dorsomedial nucleus, arcuate nucleus, preoptic areas, mammillary nuclei, ventrolateral preoptic nucleus, and paraventricular nucleus of the stria terminalis (P < 0.05–0.001; Fig. 4B, C).

In contrast, in the T2DD model, taVNS significantly reduced c-Fos–positive cell density in key hypothalamic subregions exhibiting pathological hyperactivation, including the dorsomedial nucleus, ventrolateral preoptic nucleus, arcuate nucleus, and posterior lateral hypothalamic area (P < 0.05–0.001; Fig. 4D). These findings indicate that taVNS differentially modulates hypothalamic activity under physiological and pathological conditions, promoting activation in the healthy state while normalizing excessive neuronal activity in T2DD.

Immediate effect of taVNS on hypothalamic 5-HT release in T2DD model mice

Real-time fiber photometry revealed dynamic changes in hypothalamic 5-HT activity following taVNS stimulation. Prior to stimulation, both groups exhibited low baseline fluorescence signals, with ΔF/F values remaining near baseline and minimal activity observed in heatmaps.

Following taVNS, the Control + taVNS group showed a modest increase in ΔF/F signals (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the Model + taVNS group exhibited a more pronounced and sustained elevation in ΔF/F, accompanied by markedly enhanced heatmap signals (Fig. 5B). Compared with controls, T2DD mice displayed a greater and longer-lasting increase in hypothalamic 5-HT activity following taVNS.

Long-term effects of taVNS on hypothalamic 5-HT content and receptor expression in T2DD model mice

At the end of the experiment, hypothalamic 5-HT levels were quantified by ELISA. Compared with the Control group, the Model group exhibited significantly reduced hypothalamic 5-HT content (P < 0.01), whereas taVNS intervention significantly increased 5-HT levels in the Model + taVNS group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5C).

Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed a marked decrease in hypothalamic 5-HT₁A receptor expression in the Model group compared with controls (P < 0.001). This reduction was significantly reversed by taVNS, with elevated 5-HT₁A expression observed in the Model + taVNS group (P < 0.001; Fig. 5D).

Correlation analysis between fasting blood glucose, behavior, and hypothalamic 5-HT levels and receptor expression after taVNS

Behavioral analyses further revealed that horizontal locomotor activity in the open field test was positively correlated with both hypothalamic 5-HT levels (R² = 0.89, P = 0.0154) and the 5-HT/β-tubulin ratio (R² = 0.89, P = 0.0153; Fig. 6C, D). Vertical locomotor activity showed similar positive correlations with hypothalamic 5-HT content (R² = 0.78, P = 0.0471) and the 5-HT/β-tubulin ratio (R² = 0.83, P = 0.0309; Fig. 6E, F).

In contrast, immobility time in the tail suspension test showed a negative but non-significant correlation with hypothalamic 5-HT content (R² = 0.85, P = 0.264) and with the 5-HT/β-tubulin ratio (R² = 0.90, P = 0.142; Fig. 6G, H). Together, these analyses indicate that taVNS-associated improvements in glycemic control and locomotor behavior are closely linked to enhanced hypothalamic serotonergic signaling.

Discussion

fMOST enables high-resolution, subcellular-level imaging across the entire mouse brain, with voxel sizes reaching the submicron scale [22]. By embedding and hardening brain tissue followed by automated ultra-thin sectioning, fMOST integrates optical microscopy to visualize the distribution of individual neurons and their axonal projections throughout the brain. Compared to high-field MRI, which struggles to achieve sub-100 µm resolution and cannot resolve single neurons or specific circuits, fMOST offers superior spatial resolution while maintaining whole-brain coverage. It bridges macro- and micro-level analysis by providing comprehensive brain-wide imaging alongside detailed anatomical and molecular marker information. In this study, fMOST was applied in combination with rAAV-PHP.eB-cFos-EYFP viral labeling to reconstruct the 3D distribution of the neuronal activity marker c-Fos across the whole brain in both physiological and T2DD conditions before and after taVNS intervention. Results showed that under physiological conditions, taVNS broadly activated multiple brain regions, including the cortex, thalamus, hypothalamus, and cerebellum. In contrast, under T2DD conditions, activation was markedly reduced in several brain areas such as the hypothalamus, striatum, and midbrain sensory-related regions, while activation in thalamic and cerebellar nuclei was enhanced. Interestingly, taVNS produced opposite patterns of c-Fos modulation under physiological and pathological conditions. In healthy animals, taVNS induced widespread increases in c-Fos expression, indicating activation of vagal afferent pathways and downstream neural circuits. In contrast, under the T2DD condition, taVNS reduced c-Fos expression in several brain regions that exhibited stress- or disease-related hyperactivity. This bidirectional effect may reflect state-dependent neuromodulation, whereby neuromodulatory interventions enhance neural activity under physiological conditions but normalize aberrant hyperactivation under pathological states. Importantly, such context-dependent modulation suggests that taVNS may facilitate adaptive neural responsiveness in intact networks while promoting functional normalization in circuits exhibiting stress-related dysregulation, rather than exerting uniformly excitatory or inhibitory effects. Previous studies of vagus nerve stimulation have reported similar normalization effects on dysregulated brain circuits involved in stress, inflammation, and metabolic control [23–25]. Therefore, the reduction of c-Fos expression observed in the T2DD model may represent a restoration of abnormal neural activity toward a more physiological state rather than simple suppression of neuronal activity. Such state-dependent regulation may contribute to the therapeutic effects of taVNS in disorders involving both metabolic and emotional dysregulation. Notably, taVNS recruited partially distinct hypothalamic nuclei under physiological and T2DD conditions. This difference likely reflects state-dependent neural modulation, whereby neuromodulatory interventions engage different neural circuits depending on the baseline functional state of the brain. Similar state-dependent effects of vagus nerve stimulation on brain activity have been reported previously [26].

Although the combination of a genetic diabetes model with chronic stress does not fully replicate the complexity of clinical comorbidity between type 2 diabetes and depression, this strategy has been widely used to investigate metabolic–affective interactions. The db/db mouse is a well-established model of type 2 diabetes and has been reported to exhibit neurobehavioral deficits and depression-like phenotypes [27]. Moreover, chronic stress paradigms such as CUMS reliably induce depression-like behaviors and can exacerbate metabolic dysfunction in diabetic models [28]. Therefore, combining db/db mice with CUMS provides a practical approach for modeling key metabolic and behavioral features relevant to T2DD.

Based on this disease model, this study represents a significant extension of the existing literature on taVNS. Previous research on taVNS has primarily focused on models of depression or stress, attributing its antidepressant effects mainly to the suppression of neuroinflammation and regulation of the stress axis. For example, taVNS has been shown to activate the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway to reduce hypothalamic neuroinflammation, thereby exerting antidepressant effects [18]. Other studies have demonstrated that taVNS at specific frequencies can attenuate hyperactivity of the HPA axis in chronically stressed rats, improving depressive-like behaviors [29]. These studies emphasize immune-inflammatory and endocrine stress pathways and have contributed to understanding the antidepressant mechanisms of taVNS. However, most are limited to models of emotional disorders and lack a systematic evaluation of brain functional alterations under comorbid metabolic-emotional conditions. In particular, the whole-brain activation pattern of taVNS remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, the present study applied taVNS to a T2DD model and, for the first time, used high-resolution whole-brain fMOST imaging to generate a three-dimensional atlas of taVNS-induced neural activation under both physiological and pathological conditions. This approach allowed us to systematically characterize the central targeting profile of taVNS at the whole-brain level. Moreover, we highlighted the potential involvement of the 5-HT neurotransmitter system in the effects of taVNS, thereby expanding the mechanistic perspective beyond inflammation and stress modulation. This broader perspective extends the current understanding of taVNS. While previous studies have confirmed its antidepressant properties, we further revealed its potential in improving metabolic parameters, suggesting that taVNS efficacy is not limited to emotional regulation but also involves metabolic control. Such dual effects were not observed in prior studies that focused solely on depressive models. Existing literature has mainly reported the effects of taVNS on depressive symptoms [6] and functional brain connectivity [30], whereas our study demonstrates that taVNS exerts both antidepressant and metabolic effects under pathological conditions of metabolic disturbance. Furthermore, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis proposed by Martin et al [31], which suggests that dysregulation of the central insulin–5-HT axis may contribute to the pathophysiology of depression. Our results provide direct experimental support for this theory. Overall, this study enriches the mechanistic understanding of taVNS, extending its potential application from depression alone to the broader context of metabolic-affective comorbidities. In sum, our findings support and expand current knowledge on taVNS and offer new insights into its complex central mechanisms of action.

This study demonstrates that taVNS can effectively ameliorate both metabolic dysregulation and depressive-like behaviors in a T2DD mouse model. Whereas previous treatments for diabetes or depression have typically focused on each condition in isolation, our findings provide compelling evidence for a dual-action strategy, suggesting that taVNS may serve as a dual-action neuromodulatory strategy. Specifically, taVNS improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in T2DD mice, while also significantly alleviating behavioral phenotypes associated with despair and anhedonia. These results indicate that vagus nerve–targeted neuromodulation may correct underlying physiological disturbances in complex comorbid states like T2DD, thereby simultaneously enhancing metabolic control and emotional well-being. This integrative effect offers promising insight for the treatment of psychosomatic comorbidities and underscores the potential of central neuromodulation to harmonize metabolic and affective regulation. Our study highlights the potential involvement of the 5-HT neurotransmitter system in the effects of taVNS. Previous research has demonstrated that vagus nerve stimulation can upregulate the activity of central monoaminergic neurons; notably, sustained VNS has been shown to enhance the firing rate of serotonergic neurons in the dorsal and median raphe nuclei, thereby increasing 5-HT release. In the present study, we observed significant changes in 5-HT levels and receptor expression in brain regions such as the hypothalamus following taVNS intervention, which coincided with improvements in depressive-like behaviors. One plausible mechanism is that taVNS stimulates the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, activating afferent signals that project to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla, and subsequently modulate key nuclei in the ascending neurocircuitry involved in emotion and metabolism. For example, vagal afferents may influence the locus coeruleus and raphe nuclei—both monoaminergic centers—through NTS projections, thus increasing norepinephrine and 5-HT levels in forebrain regions. This hypothesis aligns with our observed enhancement of central 5-HT signaling and is consistent with prior findings showing that taVNS activates brain regions similar to those engaged by conventional VNS [32]. Importantly, the serotonergic system not only regulates mood but also plays a role in hypothalamic integration of metabolic signals. Therefore, modulation of 5-HT pathways by taVNS may yield dual benefits—antidepressant effects and improved metabolic regulation. Our findings support this notion: by enhancing central 5-HT neuronal activity, taVNS may help restore 5-HT dysfunction associated with insulin resistance in the T2DD model, which may contribute to improvements in emotional and metabolic disturbances [33]. The hypothalamus serves as a central regulator of energy metabolism, orchestrating physiological processes such as appetite, body weight, and glucose homeostasis. It also plays a critical role in stress responses and emotional regulation, primarily through the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [34]. In our study, T2DD model animals exhibited neurobiological abnormalities in specific hypothalamic subregions, including imbalances in neural circuits related to stress and metabolism. Notably, these abnormalities were partially restored following taVNS intervention, suggesting that taVNS may exert therapeutic effects by reestablishing homeostatic balance within the hypothalamus. Particularly, we observed altered neuronal activity in the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH)—a nucleus closely associated with feeding and stress regulation—after taVNS stimulation, indicating that such regions may serve as critical nodes integrating metabolic signals and emotional states. Previous literature supports the role of the hypothalamus in depression, where depressive symptoms are often accompanied by hypothalamic inflammation and HPA axis hyperactivity; suppression of hypothalamic inflammation has been shown to alleviate depressive-like behaviors [35]. Similarly, under metabolic dysregulation, altered hypothalamic sensitivity to peripheral hormones such as insulin and leptin may impair both metabolic control and emotional responses [36]. Collectively, our findings align with the current understanding that the hypothalamus acts as a key integrative hub for metabolic and emotional information and plays a central role in the pathogenesis of T2DD. The regulatory effect of taVNS on hypothalamic function further supports the notion that targeting this hub may simultaneously alleviate metabolic and emotional dysfunctions, offering novel insights into the neural circuit mechanisms underlying mind–body comorbidity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Neuronal activation was inferred from c-Fos expression, which provides indirect evidence of activity and represents an endpoint marker that does not resolve cell-type specificity, causal mechanisms, or sustained functional dynamics. Although taVNS was associated with altered hypothalamic serotonergic activity, causal circuit-level evidence is lacking, as no loss-of-function or receptor blockade approaches were performed. The precise source of hypothalamic serotonin (e.g., raphe projections vs. local mechanisms) remains unclear. In addition, the absence of a dedicated sham condition limits conclusions about neural specificity, and potential somatosensory, motor, or metabolic confounds cannot be fully excluded. The relatively short intervention period also precludes assessment of long-term stability and compensatory effects. Finally, contributions from non-serotonergic pathways (e.g., neuroendocrine, inflammatory, or other monoaminergic systems) may also be involved. Further studies with refined controls and circuit-level approaches are needed. As a non-invasive neuromodulation technique, taVNS demonstrates distinct advantages in addressing comorbidities such as T2DD. First, it shows potential to simultaneously improve both metabolic and emotional disturbances, addressing the current lack of integrated clinical strategies [36, 37]. Conventionally, diabetes and depression are managed separately by different disciplines, leading to fragmented care. taVNS offers a unified intervention that targets both conditions, improving therapeutic efficiency. Second, it is safe, well-tolerated, and free from drug-drug interactions, making it suitable for long-term chronic disease management [38]. Its portability and ease of use also suggest future applicability in home-based care, potentially reducing medical costs. Moreover, with adjustable stimulation parameters, taVNS can be personalized according to dominant symptom profiles [39]. Taken together, taVNS presents strong prospects for T2DD intervention. With future refinement of indications and standardization of protocols, it may become a vital component of comprehensive treatment for this complex condition.
