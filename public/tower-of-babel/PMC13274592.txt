Noninvasive whole-brain imaging of glymphatic dynamics

ABSTRACT
Cerebrospinal fluid circulation through the glymphatic system plays a crucial role in removing metabolic waste from the central nervous system. However, the mechanism underlying the brain-wide glymphatic dynamics is not yet fully understood, in part due to the lack of glymphatic imaging technologies on deep brains. Here, we report a hybrid imaging technology that integrates three-dimensional photoacoustic tomography and ultrasound localization microscopy (3D-PAULM), enhanced by a photoacoustic dye with strong optical absorption in the second near-infrared window (NIR-II). 3D-PAULM allows for continuous, noninvasive, whole-brain imaging in mice through intact skull, providing superresolution mapping of the brain vasculature and highly sensitive tracing of the NIR-II dye in the glymphatic system. Using 3D-PAULM, we investigated the glymphatic function impaired by ischemic stroke, aging, and anesthesia. Our results provide insights into glymphatic transport under various physiological as well as pathological conditions and establish 3D-PAULM as a valuable tool for preclinical glymphatic research.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation is essential for brain homeostasis, facilitating nutrient distribution and the removal of metabolic waste (1–5). The glymphatic system, a specialized perivascular pathway mediated by astrocytic vascular endfeet, transports CSF and clears brain waste solutes like amyloid-β, α-synuclein, and tau (3, 6–9). Understanding the glymphatic system has substantially advanced our knowledge linking sleep, neurodegenerative disorders, and waste clearance (10–13). Given its importance in neurological health, it is critical to further study the glymphatic system for developing targeted therapies for brain disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease (10), traumatic brain injury (14), stroke (15), and Parkinson’s disease (12).

Various imaging technologies have been used to investigate the glymphatic system in both preclinical and clinical studies, including two-photon microscopy (3, 16, 17), transcranial fluorescence imaging (18, 19), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (15, 20–23), positron emission tomography (PET) (24), single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) (25), and histological analysis (3). However, current imaging techniques have respective limitations. Two-photon microscopy offers a high spatial resolution but requires craniotomy or skull thinning, limiting its field of view (FOV) and penetration depth as well as disrupting brain hemostasis. Transcranial fluorescence imaging provides a wide FOV but still suffers from limited penetration. MRI enables whole-brain imaging on both animal models and humans, yet its spatial and temporal resolutions are still insufficient for capturing glymphatic dynamics. While PET and SPECT facilitate dynamic brain imaging, they have limited spatial resolution and short half-life of radioactive tracers. Histological techniques are useful for ex vivo analysis but cannot capture real-time glymphatic dynamics. Overall, pure optical imaging methods are constrained by penetration depth on small animals for whole-brain CSF flow visualization, whereas nonoptical techniques lack the necessary spatiotemporal resolution. Those limitations underscore the need for noninvasive, high-resolution, deep-penetration imaging technologies for glymphatic research.

We present an imaging approach that integrates three-dimensional photoacoustic tomography and ultrasound localization microscopy (3D-PAULM), enhanced by a second near-infrared window (NIR-II) tracer, benzo indole butyl diphenylaminocyclopentene heptamethine (BIBDAH). Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) traces the BIBDAH flow in the glymphatic system (26). PAT can also provide noninvasive cortical vascular imaging using hemoglobin as the endogenous contrast; however, it lacks the spatial resolution for whole-brain microvascular mapping. In contrast, ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) complements PAT’s limitation by providing whole-brain superresolution vascular imaging (27). 3D-PAULM is applied to monitor glymphatic dynamics, revealing impaired glymphatic function under different pathological and physiological conditions. These findings collectively demonstrate the potential of 3D-PAULM in broad glymphatic research.

RESULTS

3D-PAULM

The perivascular space (PVS), surrounding cerebral vessels (CVs), plays a pivotal role in transporting macromolecules from CSF (Fig. 1A). We now understand that fluid exchange between CSF and the PVS is governed by two primary transport mechanisms: advection and diffusion (28). Advection, driven by arterial pulsation and systemic pressure gradients, facilitates rapid and directional CSF transport along the PVS. In contrast, diffusion dominates the later stages, allowing passive solute redistribution to support gradual clearance from deeper brain regions. In this study, we present a continuous, noninvasive, in vivo transcranial brain imaging technology, 3D-PAULM, for visualizing the PVS and CVs at the same time using a single integrated system. To image the CVs, exogenous gas-filled microbubbles (MBs) are administered intravascularly to serve as acoustic scatterers. The nonlinear oscillations of MBs under ultrasound (US) excitation enable superresolution vascular imaging beyond the diffraction limit by localizing individual MBs flowing in the blood vessels. To image the PVS, the NIR-II tracer BIBDAH is slowly infused into the cisterna magna (CM) to trace the glymphatic flow. The tracer is imaged by PAT, which detects acoustic signals generated through optical absorption. The estimated photoacoustic (PA) intensities from BIBDAH in the PVS are around 100-fold stronger than the PA intensities from the blood vessels (see Materials and Methods). Meanwhile, our previous results have demonstrated the high biocompatibility and safety of BIBDAH (29). In addition to PAT-derived CSF tracer dynamics, ULM provides independent quantitative measures of vascularity, tortuosity, and flow speed across experimental conditions, thereby establishing its distinct functional contribution. Together, the integrated ULM and PAT provide complementary hemodynamic and molecular contrast, enabling comprehensive evaluation of glymphatic function in the context of whole-brain vessels.

The integrated 3D-PAULM imaging system is shown in Fig. 1B. A 2D semispherical transducer array operating at 4 MHz is used for transcranial PAT and ULM, providing balanced penetration depth through the intact skull and spatial resolutions within the FOV, when compared to conventional high-frequency matrix array–based systems (30, 31). 3D-PAULM achieves a near-central, best-case diffraction-limited spatial resolution of 260 μm laterally and 390 μm axially for PAT, while ULM further improves the resolutions of vasculature to ~50 μm both axially and laterally (fig. S1). To expand the FOV for whole-brain imaging, a 2D motorized translation stage facilitates raster-scanning of the animal, covering an extended FOV of 14 mm by 14 mm by 10 mm. The final whole-brain imaging takes 12 s for PAT and 2 min for ULM (see Materials and Methods). All animal experiments were conducted with mice in the supine position to enhance CSF circulation (Fig. 1C) (32). MBs were administered via retro-orbital injection, and the NIR-II tracer was injected into the CM. Representative images of CVs and PVS are shown in Fig. 1 (D and E).

Imaging the whole-brain CVs and PVS in healthy young mice

To study the functional characterization of CVs and PVS at the whole-brain level, we first imaged CVs using ULM and then acquired time-lapse images of PVS over 60 min using PAT. A representative volumetric rendering of in vivo 3D-PAULM images obtained from a healthy young mouse is shown in Fig. 2A. The CSF tracer signal was segmented into distinct regions of interest (ROIs) on the basis of adjacent vasculature (Fig. 2B), e.g., the transverse sinus (TS), superior sagittal sinus (SSS), olfactory sinus (OS), and circle of Willis (CoW), which includes posterior cerebral artery (PCA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), and anterior cerebral artery (ACA). By delineating these vascular regions, we aim to analyze local glymphatic dynamics and their interactions with major draining pathways, providing insights into regional variations in glymphatic activity. In general, the PVS around CoW serves as a key marker for assessing CSF entry into the glymphatic system, while its clearance may follow the venous network, primarily along the TS, SSS, OS, and other internal cerebral veins, via the lymphatic drainage system (e.g., meningeal lymphatic). By quantifying CSF influx and efflux through these pathways, we can evaluate the changes in the glymphatic system under different physiological and pathological conditions.

To further investigate the glymphatic transport dynamics, we collected the time-lapse PA images of the NIR-II tracer distribution (Fig. 2, C to K, and movie S1) and integrated tracer dynamics with the vascular map generated by ULM. Our results show that immediately following the intracisternal injection, the NIR-II tracer rapidly transported along major arteries and their arteriolar branches, particularly at the base of the brain, consistent with the presence of perivascular sheaths surrounding cerebral surface arteries and arterioles (33). This fast, directional transport along arteries corresponds to advection-driven CSF flow, where bulk motion facilitates rapid tracer movement within the glymphatic network. In the early phase (<10 min), the tracer initially accumulated around the CoW region and progressively entered the PVS. During the mid-phase (10 to 20 min), substantial tracer accumulation was observed in the rhinal fissure and olfactory bulb (OB) regions, primarily originating from the olfactory artery (OA). In contrast, within the late phase (>20 min), tracer accumulation was slowly concentrated near the TS but had not yet progressed to the regions near other cerebral veins, e.g., the SSS. After that, macroscopic intra-axial CSF transport directed the tracer along the SSS. Note that in the later phase, the tracer transport process slowed down and gradually transited to a diffusion-dominant phase, as the tracer flew along perivenous pathways toward the dorsal regions of the brain. The combined effects of advection and diffusion then drove the tracer from the SSS to other cerebral vein regions (34), further intensifying the PA signals in the OB region at the study end point. These observations are consistent with previous ex vivo optical imaging results (3). For clarity and comparability, the same definitions of the early, mid-, and late phases were used in all subsequent analyses.

Glymphatic flow in the ventral area has been inaccessible by optical imaging. Furthermore, the skull has dense structures and extensive subarachnoid cisterns, making glymphatic imaging in this region particularly challenging (35). Using 3D-PAULM, we examined glymphatic transport dynamics by analyzing the time-lapse PA signal intensities in three major ROIs: CoW, TS, and OB (Fig. 2L). Depth-dependent optical attenuation in PAT prevents comparing the absolute signal intensity across brain regions at different depths. Therefore, we quantified the relative signal changes within each ROI. The PA signal dynamics clearly identified the distinct glymphatic transport patterns in CoW as the primary influx region and OB as the outflux site. Following the NIR-II tracer injection, the PA signal intensities increased in all regions, reflecting CSF flow predominantly driven by the arterial pulsations (16). The differences in signal rising times across ROIs suggest the temporal pattern of glymphatic transport. During the early phase, advection facilitated rapid CSF tracer exchange between the CM and the PVS. In the mid-phase, the transport dynamics might transition from convention-driven to diffusion-mediated. In the late phase, diffusion became the dominating transport mechanism, as indicated by a gradual signal decrease in CoW and a progressive increase in OB. The NIR-II tracer level in the TS region remained relatively stable over time, suggesting its potential “transient reservoir” role in the glymphatic clearance. This equilibrium may reflect a dynamic balance between the CSF influx via arterial pathways and efflux via lymphatic drainage. Comparison between the advection-dominating phase (20 min) and the diffusion-dominating phase (60 min) revealed such regional differences (Fig. 2M). After the 3D-PAULM imaging session, the brains were extracted, and ex vivo fluorescent images were acquired to verify the tracer distribution (fig. S2). Collectively, these imaging findings in healthy young animals might indicate directional CSF transport toward the lymphatic system and thus support the previous understanding that glymphatic clearance primarily follows perivenous pathways rather than returning to the subarachnoid space (28). In the following sections, we have applied the same workflow to assess glymphatic functions under different physiological and pathological conditions.

Local glymphatic impairment due to ischemic stroke injury

The strong relation between arterial pulsation and glymphatic function has led to growing interest in how ischemic stroke affects waste clearance in the central nervous system (15, 19). To address this question, we used 3D-PAULM to image the mice 48 hours after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) surgery on the right hemisphere (Fig. 3A). A representative ex vivo fluorescent image of a brain section with label vessels and the 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC)–stained tissue sections confirm the infarcted region (Fig. 3B). ULM images also revealed reduced vascular density and flow speed in the affected hemisphere (Fig. 3, C to E). This study used tMCAO with partial reperfusion. Thus, the observed changes in vascular density and flow velocity were expected to be moderate. Quantitative analysis confirmed perfusion reduction in the stroke-affected right hemisphere compared to the contralateral left hemisphere (Fig. 3F), reflected by the total vascular volume on the stroke side, which was lower compared to the control side. Combined ULM and PAT images further demonstrated PVS dynamics between healthy and ischemia-reperfusion–injured brains (Fig. 3, G and H). Healthy mice showed dense, well-connected microvascular networks, with symmetric NIR-II tracer distribution between the two hemispheres. In contrast, stroke caused asymmetric NIR-II tracer distribution with suppressed tracer influx, also verified by ex vivo fluorescent images of the PVS (fig. S3).

Time-lapse monitoring illustrated NIR-II tracer dynamics in the stroke mice (Fig. 4, A to I, and movie S2). During the early-to-mid-phases, the tracer distribution already showed differences between the two hemispheres, suggesting that early-phase CSF influx into the PVS was impaired by tMCAO. In the late phase, tracer accumulation became higher in the venous structure of the intact hemisphere, whereas the stroke-affected hemisphere exhibited reduced tracer accumulation, indicating impaired arterial inflow and pulsation. Although tMCAO had a secondary impact on the downstream venous flow, the tracer signals near the SSS and TS were preserved. These findings might suggest that tMCAO primarily disrupts advection-driven CSF flow along the arterial inflow pathways without a clear impact on lymphatic outflow. This observation is consistent with prior MRI-based studies, which show that the glymphatic function depends heavily on arterial integrity and is vulnerable to ischemia-induced vascular disturbances such as subarachnoid hemorrhage (15, 36).

Quantification of ULM-derived vascular metrics was performed as detailed in Materials and Methods. As shown in Fig. 4J, the stroke hemisphere exhibited reduced vascularity compared to the control side. In contrast, vascular tortuosity was not different between the two hemispheres. Notably, the mean flow speed was higher in the stroke hemisphere, likely resultant from the detection of large, fast-flow vessels, while slow-flow microvessels became less visible after stroke. The statistical results confirmed that ischemia injury reduced the NIR-II tracer accumulation compared to the control group (Fig. 4K). To quantify the impact of ischemic stroke on the glymphatic system, we compared the PA signal intensity change over time and the ratios between two hemispheres in both stroke and control groups (N = 3). The control group underwent the same procedures including MB and NIR-II tracer administration as well as the full imaging session but without tMCAO. The corresponding time-lapse 3D-PAULM images of additional mice are shown in figs. S4 and S5. Glymphatic dysfunction due to ischemia injury was assessed by calculating the ratio of PA signal intensities between the stroke hemisphere (right) and the intact hemisphere (left). Only the diffusion dominant phase was analyzed, which was relatively stable. The stroke hemisphere exhibited a ratio of ~0.8, while the control group maintained a stable ratio of ~1.0 throughout the entire observation period (Fig. 4L). Moreover, the relatively low CSF flow rate in both hemispheres observed in the stroke group suggests that the ischemia injury also had a global impact on the glymphatic function over the whole brain. It is likely because tMCAO can elevate the intracranial pressure and disrupt PVS flow, all of which can lead to global glymphatic transport deficits.

Global glymphatic impairment due to anesthesia and aging

To demonstrate 3D-PAULM for assessing CSF dynamics under different physiological conditions, we investigated the global effect of aging and anesthetic agents on the glymphatic influx and efflux. As shown in Fig. 5A, isoflurane on young mice resulted in much reduced glymphatic flow when compared with young mice under ketamine/xylazine (K/X). Isoflurane induced pronounced vasodilation and CSF stagnation, producing the low overall NIR-II tracer intensity (Fig. 5B) (37). Aged mice (>18 months) under K/X also showed reduced glymphatic flow when compared with young mice. Previous studies have shown that aging is closely associated with declined CSF-interstitial exchange and a major risk factor for AQP4 (aquaporin 4) depolarization, vessel stiffening, reduced pulsation, and mild plasma leakage (11, 38–40), all of which have been linked to glymphatic dysfunction. In the ULM vasculature density and PA intensity maps (Fig. 5C), young K/X mice displayed completed CSF pathways, while in aged mice under K/X and young mice under isoflurane, the stagnation of CSF flow was observed, accompanied by a decrease in overall signal intensity.

Young K/X mice exhibit a closed CSF flow loop with balanced NIR-II tracer distribution, while aged K/X and young isoflurane groups show disrupted CSF flow, indicating impaired glymphatic transport.

We further studied glymphatic transport in aged mice using 3D-PAULM. Representative time-lapse imaging revealed suppressed CSF circulation in aged mice (Fig. 6, A to I, and movie S3), affecting both the advection and diffusion phases. Slower NIR-II tracer accumulation in the CoW region reflected that the pulsation in the deep arteries was reduced in aged mice compared to that in young mice. In addition, the meningeal lymphatic vessels, particularly in the dorsal skull, were less engaged in the NIR-II tracer circulation in aged mice, indicating compromised CSF drainage (35). The PA signal intensity in the OB region was also reduced, suggesting age-related atrophy of the nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus (41). Ex vivo fluorescent images comparing young and aged mice further confirmed the age-related glymphatic dysfunction (figs. S6 and S7). Quantification of ULM-derived vascular metrics revealed aged-dependent differences in Fig. 6J. Aged mice showed reduced vascularity and lower mean flow speed compared to young mice, consistent with reduced vascular perfusion (42). In contrast, tortuosity was increased in aged animals, indicating aged-related vascular remodeling.

We define T50 as the time point when the PA signal reaches 50% of its maximum value, which can be used to evaluate the glymphatic advection influx speed. The increased T50 value in the aged mice is likely due to the reduced pulsation of the arteries (Fig. 6K), a key factor for age-related impairment of glymphatic transport (38). We further analyzed the TS and CoW regions on young and aged mice (Fig. 6, L and M; additional mice in figs. S8 and S9), which were closely related to the glymphatic influx and efflux. We observed that the young mice had a rapid and steady signal increase, indicating efficient glymphatic influx, but aged mice exhibited a slower response. In the CoW region, the PA signal declined faster in aged mice (Fig. 6L), which may reflect a reduction in local flow resistance caused by vessel enlargement and hyperpermeability (43), allowing the NIR-II tracer to be shunted via other routes such as the spinal cord, subarachnoid space, or rhinal fissure. Moreover, diffusion-driven CSF efflux was impaired in the aged mice, reflected by the prolonged tracer signals on the dura (Fig. 6I).

We also investigated the impact of isoflurane anesthesia on the glymphatic system by additional experiments using 5% isoflurane for anesthesia induction and 2% isoflurane for maintenance. Positive pressure ventilation was consistently applied across all conditions to mitigate respiratory variation (44, 45). Under high-concentration isoflurane anesthesia, the time-lapse 3D-PAULM images showed initial tracer entry into the PVS around the CoW region (Fig. 7, A to I, and movie S4), consistent with the early CSF pathways. In the early phase, the tracer had flowed along the PVS near the PCA and TS. However, unlike K/X anesthesia, the tracer failed to approach and showed no clear accumulation in the OB region. Instead, the tracer accumulated in the TS region (37), forming a different distribution pattern that persisted throughout the entire imaging session, consistent with the ex vivo fluorescent images (fig. S10). The same glymphatic dynamics were consistently observed in other mice (figs. S11 and S12). ULM quantification showed no substantial differences in vascularity or tortuosity between K/X and isoflurane anesthesia (Fig. 7J). However, the mean flow speed was higher under isoflurane compared to K/X. This suggests that while vascular structure and density remain comparable between anesthetic regimens, isoflurane induces elevated flow dynamics, consistent with its known vasodilatory effects (46, 47).

Mechanistically, the dampened arterial pulsation under isoflurane likely reduced the perivascular driving force critical for the CSF exchange (Fig. 5A), although the apparent inflow speed (T50) remained similar to that under K/X (Fig. 7K), which mainly reflects bulk advection of the tracer from the CM to basal cisterns. In this scenario, the tracer front reaches the CoW with comparable timing in both groups, but the weaker pulsatile mixing and impaired vascular-perivascular coupling may lead to a lower absolute PA signal (Fig. 6B). We compared the dynamic NIR-II tracer distribution in the CoW and TS regions of the mice under isoflurane and K/X anesthesia (Fig. 7, L and M). Under K/X anesthesia, both regions exhibited a rapid rise of the tracer signals, indicating normal CSF advection and stable glymphatic transport. However, the CoW region in the isoflurane group showed a faster decline of the tracer signals, likely due to insufficient inflow and accelerated washout through extracranial pathways such as the subarachnoid and spinal routes (19, 48). The stagnation of tracer signals in the TS region demonstrated the isoflurane’s inhibitory effects on both influx and efflux glymphatic pathways. These observations are consistent with the ex vivo fluorescence imaging using Evans Blue, which also showed tracer stagnation under isoflurane (37). Isoflurane-induced cerebral vasodilation may also increase intracranial pressure and induce venous congestion (49), further restricting perivascular flow. Last, while isoflurane anesthesia suppresses pulsatile CSF propulsion and thus reduces the glymphatic flow, K/X anesthesia introduces a sleeping-like state characterized by synchronized low-frequency vasomotion (enhancing the δ wave) and an enlarged interstitial space (1, 8), promoting the glymphatic flow (fig. S13).

DISCUSSION

In this work, we have applied 3D-PAULM to simultaneously image the PVS and CV enhanced by NIR-II tracers and MBs, respectively. 3D-PAULM offers a unique imaging platform for glymphatic research, achieving a submillimeter spatial resolution with second-level temporal resolution, addressing the limitations of conventional imaging techniques for noninvasive in vivo glymphatic mapping. Unlike pure optical imaging methods, which are constrained by limited penetration depth, 3D-PAULM enables deep-brain imaging in small animal models, allowing for comprehensive assessment of whole-brain glymphatic transport dynamics. While MRI and PET allow for deep-brain imaging, their low spatiotemporal resolutions hinder continuous tracking of glymphatic flow patterns. By contrast, 3D-PAULM offers balanced imaging depth, spatiotemporal resolution, and molecular sensitivity to precisely capture glymphatic transport dynamics.

Selecting an optimal PA tracer for monitoring glymphatic transport requires balancing the molecular size, optical absorption, biostability, and compatibility with the CSF environment. Conventional small molecules such as methylene blue (319 Da) (50) and indocyanine green (775 Da) (51) have excellent hydrophilicity and rapid diffusion and thus are suitable for fluorescence assays of tracking CSF-interstitial exchange. However, their PA signals are not stable because of photobleaching and tissue clearance. By contrast, BIBDAH, a NIR-II heptamethine cyanine dye (0.8 to 1.0 kDa), has strong optical absorption at 1064 nm, yielding robust PA contrast in deep tissues, together with superior photostability and prolonged tissue retention. Moreover, BIBDAH’s small molecular weight allows for its movement through both perivascular pathways without aggregation.

While 3D-PAULM can provide high-resolution, deep-brain imaging of the glymphatic dynamics, several technical challenges still remain. One limitation is the quantification of the tracer concentration, which is complicated by depth-dependent signal attenuation. Future work could involve depth-dependent signal calibration to improve measurement accuracy (52). Another consideration is the biostability of the tracer for longitudinal studies. Although our experiments over 60 min showed only slight reduction in the PA signal intensity, longer studies may be hindered by the tracer degradation. Future studies may screen metabolic-resistant dyes and/or apply continuous tracer administration to maintain signal consistency. In addition, PA imaging often suffers from the limited-view detection and skull-induced aberration, which may hinder the visualization of some PVS structures ascending or descending within the brain (53–56). Although the semispherical geometry provides a receiving angular coverage of 85.8°, partially mitigating the limited-view problem, it does not fully address this issue (fig. S14). Future advancements in large aperture transducers, multiangle signal detection, and model-based image reconstruction may help improve the visualization of all PVS structures, facilitating more complete assessment of the glymphatic transport (57). In the future work, this multimodal framework may be extended to investigate downstream lymphatic pathways (58).

Our study provides several insights into the glymphatic function under different physiological and pathological conditions. In healthy young mice, the NIR-II tracer followed the perivascular pathway initially accumulated at the CoW region and then progressively transported to the TS, SSS, and OB regions. This dynamic process was consistent with previous findings on advection- and diffusion-driven glymphatic transport in normal conditions (59). However, in the stroke model with tMCAO, the local glymphatic transport was impaired, particularly in the stroke hemisphere (15). Tracer influx into the CoW region was delayed, and the outflow was reduced, indicating disrupted advection-driven glymphatic transport. This impairment was likely resultant from an ischemia-induced decrease in arterial pulsation. In addition, a stroke-induced increase in the intracranial pressure and venous congestion may further restrict the CSF flow, leading to tracer stagnation in the PVS. These findings support the hypothesis that stroke not only affects local blood circulation but also impairs the global glymphatic clearance, potentially contributing to long-lasting poststroke neurological deficits.

We expanded our investigation by studying global factors such as aging and anesthesia, which are known to broadly influence glymphatic transport. Our results reveal that aging exerts a profound impact on glymphatic function by impairing both the advective and diffusive processes. The decline in glymphatic transport in aged mice is multifactorial and related to loss of perivascular AQP4 polarization and neuroinflammation (38). In addition, we observed functional impairments in key glymphatic pathways. The meningeal lymphatic vessels in the dorsal skull of the aged mice showed reduced involvement in CSF clearance, implying compromised lymphatic drainage. Furthermore, diffusion-driven transport from the PVS was suppressed in the aged mice. The prolonged tracer accumulation in the TS region suggests blockage in the efflux pathways, indicating that both the influx and clearance of the glymphatic system are disrupted during aging. Similarly, for high-concentration isoflurane anesthesia, the glymphatic transport was relatively slow (37). Together with the fact that the initial influx into the CoW was lower, the tracer was concentrated in the TS and cerebellar vein regions without reaching the SSS and OS. This tracer stagnation suggests that isoflurane impairs the diffusion phase of the glymphatic flow. Unlike stroke, aging and anesthesia lead to global suppression on the glymphatic dynamics, a combined effect of vasodilation, reduced pulsation, and compromised venous drainage (19, 38, 40, 48, 49).

Overall, our study reported the impact of various pathological and physiological conditions on glymphatic transport, demonstrating the need for precise imaging techniques such as 3D-PAULM. As the sample size was limited, this work was principally intended as a technical demonstration of the 3D-PAULM platform, highlighting its ability to track the whole-brain CSF. Larger cohort studies are required to further strengthen the biological findings. By providing high-resolution, deep-brain imaging, 3D-PAULM closes the critical gap in existing imaging technologies, offering a powerful tool for studying CSF dynamics and translational potential for noninvasive assessment of the glymphatic function (60). These findings by 3D-PAULM can pave the way for better understanding and treating neurological conditions associated with impaired glymphatic functions, ultimately contributing to improved brain health and longevity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

PAULM system setup

A customized semispherical US array with 256 individual elements (Imasonics, France) was used to simultaneously acquire PA and US signals. The semispherical array has a 40-mm radius, an 85.8° angular coverage, and a central opening (11-mm diameter) for inserting an optical fiber bundle for PA excitation. Each element had a circular-trapezoidal shape with average lateral and elevational dimensions of ~3.37 and 2.87 mm (fig. S15), respectively, and corresponding pitches of 3.70 and 3.07 mm (~85% aperture fill factor) at the array surface. However, different from the conventional linear or planar matrix arrays, the focused element distribution of the semispherical array can suppress the grating-lobe formation in the central focal zone (~8 mm in diameter) by satisfying the spatial Nyquist sampling condition. In the focal zone near the center of a semispherical array, the spatial sampling density is high and nearly isotropic because detectors surround the target from many angles, whereas planar arrays provide sparse and anisotropic sampling that degrades rapidly with depth. In other words, the semispherical array is effectively a dense array in the focal zone despite the fact that its element pitch size is much larger than the acoustic wavelength. The central frequency is 4 MHz, operating in transmit/receive mode with a −6-dB bandwidth of 45%. The system provided a spatial resolution of ~260 μm (lateral)/~390 μm (axial) and an FOV of ~10 mm by 10 mm by 10 mm. Resolution degradation could be observed off-center by point spread function mapping across the imaging FOV (fig. S16). The received signals were digitized in real time by a programmable US scanner (Vantage 256, Verasonics, Kirkland, WA) at 20.83 megasamples/s and transferred to a PC via PCI-Ex4 connection for data storage and processing. Acquisition routines were executed in MATLAB (R2019b, MathWorks Inc., US) using customized scripts, enabling a real-time imaging preview at 30 fps.

Before PA imaging, synthetic aperture US imaging with multiple element transmission was performed for ULM, where signals from each transmission event were received using the full array aperture. The transmitted diverging waveforms had a central frequency of 4 MHz, one complete burst cycle, and a 20-V peak-to-peak driving voltage, resulting in a volumetric frame rate of 403 Hz. For PA imaging, a 1064-nm Q-switched Nd:YAG (yttrium-aluminum-garnet) laser (DRL 100, Quantel Laser, France) delivered <10-ns pulses at a 30-Hz repetition rate, with a 30-mJ pulse energy transmitted via an optical fiber bundle (Dolan Jenner, ~50% coupling efficiency). The optical fluence at the animal’s skin surface remained within ANSI Z136.1 safety limits. Because of the relatively limited FOV, raster scanning using a 2D motorized translation stage was performed across the mouse brain, covering an FOV of 14 mm by 14 mm by 10 mm via nine scanning positions with a 3-mm step size. The overlapping regions between adjacent scanning positions were used to register and stitch the images for volumetric reconstruction. For each scanning position, 4800 volumetric US frames were acquired for ULM, while PA data were averaged over 30 laser pulses to improve the signal-to-noise ratio and reduce motion artifacts. The final C-scan imaging times were 12 s for PA and 2 min for ULM, which included data acquisition and scanning.

Postmortem fluorescence imaging and TTC staining

After the imaging session by 3D-PAULM, for fluorescence labeling, mice were anesthetized (1.5%), and a catheter was inserted into the external jugular vein. A total of 80 μl of diluted lectin solution (Lectin-AF649, Thermo Fisher Scientific, L32472, or Lectin-AF488, Vector Laboratories, DL-1174; diluted 1:1 with saline) was slowly injected. After allowing circulation for 30 min, the incision was sutured, and the animals were perfused transcardially with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were then harvested for wide-FOV fluorescence imaging first. The brains were imaged using fluorescence stereomicroscopes (Leica M205 FCA) controlled via customized software to confirm successful CM injection. Then, the brains were fixed in 4% PFA for 2 days, dehydrated in 30% sucrose for another 2 days, and cryosectioned. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Axio Imager upright microscope.

For TTC staining, after extracting the brain, we placed it in a rodent brain matrix. The brain was laid flat, and then the blades were used to slice the brain into 1-mm sections. The sections were placed in a 2% TTC solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) at room temperature for 15 min. After incubation, the TTC solution was removed, and the brain slices were either photographed/scanned or fixed in 10% PFA for subsequent use.

EEG recording

For electroencephalogram (EEG) recording, four screws with wire lead electrodes (Pinnacle model no. 8403, Lawrence, KS) were implanted into the skull. Two screws were inserted about 2 mm anterior to the bregma and two about 2 mm posterior to the bregma on each side to the midline. A head-mount device (Pinnacle model no. 8201, Lawrence, KS) was then soldered to the wire leads of the screws, each one representing EEG channels 1 and 2, EEG common, and ground. Animals recovered for 2 days following the procedure. For EEG recordings, awake animals were placed in a behavioral/recording chamber, where their activity was observed, while bilateral EEG signals were recorded (Pinnacle system) for 15 min. The mice were then anesthetized with either K/X or isoflurane (1.5%), and an EEG was recorded for 40 min under anesthesia. EEG signals from each channel were sampled at 2 kHz and analyzed using Welch’s method for spectral density estimation in the δ (0.5 to 4 Hz), θ (4 to 8 Hz), α (8 to 13 Hz), and β (13 to 30 Hz) frequency bands (expressed as the integrated power in μV2 for each band). The relative power percentage of each band was then computed.

NIR-II tracer fabrication

BIBDAH was obtained from Spectrum Info Ltd. (catalog no. S01451), and Kolliphor HS15 (no. 42966) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. To prepare the micellar emulsion, 25% (w/v) HS15 was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline by sonication for 120 min at 60°C, forming a slightly viscous emulsion. BIBDAH powder was then directly dissolved into 20 ml of the HS15 emulsion and further sonicated for 60 min at 60°C to ensure homogeneous dispersion. The solution was subsequently centrifuged at 1000 RCF (relative centrifugal force) for 3 min, with no visible precipitation observed. The final BIBDAH concentration was 2 mg/ml, and the solution was filtered through a 0.22-μm polyethersulfone filter. The resulting BIBDAH micelles had an average diameter of 12 nm with a polydispersity index of <0.1, as determined by dynamic light scattering using a Nanobrook 90 Plus PALS instrument in dilute phosphate-buffered saline.

BIBDAH characterization

The NIR-II tracer BIBDAH was used by PAT for imaging the PVS. BIBDAH has a peak absorption of around 1064 nm (fig. S17) (29, 61), which is the fundamental wavelength of the commonly used Nd:YAG lasers. We first tested the biostability of BIBDAH, following intravascular administration in mice. The BIBDAH signals in the blood remained stable for at least 1 hour (fig. S18), demonstrating its long circulation time. To ensure biocompatibility and minimize intracranial pressure changes, we administered a small dose of BIBDAH solution into the CM (10 μl, 2.0 mg/ml), resulting in an in situ concentration of ~0.4 mg/ml. We compared the PA intensities from a BIBDAH solution (0.4 mg/ml) at 1064 nm with those from whole blood (fig. S19). BIBDAH exhibited stronger PA intensities than the whole blood by >20 dB (100-fold). Given that hemoglobin is the most dominating endogenous absorber at 1064 nm in the brain in vivo, these results demonstrate that BIBDAH is well suited as a whole-brain CSF tracer. To further reduce signal contributions from hemoglobin for in vivo experiments, we subtracted the baseline PA images obtained before the BIBDAH injection, which enhanced the imaging contrast of BIBDAH. Following the PA imaging, we took the fluorescence images of the extracted mouse brains, providing validation of the PA results.

Animal preparation

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Duke University and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations (Protocol Registry Number: 60681). Adult male CD-1 mice (2 to 3 months old for the regular group and >18 months old for the aged group) were used for all experiments and were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Sample sizes of at least three for each group were chosen to ensure sufficient statistical power. No animals were excluded, unless technical failure occurred (e.g., unsuccessful injection). Each experiment was performed on independent animals, which were considered biological replicates. Multiple imaging frames and repeated acquisitions within the same animal were treated as technical replicates. Experiments were repeated across animals to ensure reproducibility.

Mice were initially anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in 100% oxygen within an induction chamber until the loss of the righting reflex was observed. The mouse was positioned on a stereotaxic frame, with the head secured using ear bars to induce flexion. After disinfecting the surgical area, a midline skin incision was made, and the suboccipital muscles were carefully retracted bilaterally to expose the dura mater overlying the CM. A 30G needle was then precisely inserted through the dura mater into the CM and secured with glue. The prepared NIR-II tracer solution was loaded into a microinjection pump and then was slowly injected into the CM before the imaging session (10 μl in total, 2 μl/min), which was connected to the catheter for controlled infusion. During surgery, anesthesia was maintained using 1.5% isoflurane via inhalation. Following surgery, mice were transferred to the 3D-PAULM imaging system. A bolus injection of 100 μl of MBs (1.00 ± 0.20 × 108 bubbles/ml, Revvity, US) was administered via the retro-orbital sinus for cerebral vascular imaging. The contrast agents are lipid-shelled, gas-core MBs with a median diameter of ~0.83 μm (manufacturer specification). The stock solution was diluted 10 times before injection to ensure sufficient sparsity for reliable localization. After that, we started the injection of NIR-II tracer and continuous PA imaging. Unless otherwise specified, anesthesia was maintained throughout the experiment using K/X (mixture: 10:1 ratio; ketamine, 100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), with ambient air supplied via a nose cone. To investigate the effects of different anesthetic methods, a separate group of mice was anesthetized with isoflurane (2%, inhalational) for comparison.

Data processing

A sparse matrix–based 3D delay-and-sum method was used for PA image reconstruction (62), with a voxel size of 60 μm in all dimensions. The pulse energy was sampled using a power meter to record pulse-to-pulse energy variations, which were used to calibrate the optical fluence for accurate intensity quantification. A bandpass filter (0.5 to 9 MHz) was used to reduce noise (63). ULM integrates high-frame-rate US acquisition, adaptive signal processing, and MB tracking to reconstruct high-resolution vascular images and blood flow maps (movie S5). To balance the image quality and frame rate, in-phase/quadrature US data were acquired using a synthetic aperture method, achieving a frame rate of up to 403 Hz (fig. S20). For US imaging, in-phase and quadrature-phase volumes were reconstructed using the same 3D delay-and-sum algorithm and voxel size as PA imaging. To mitigate motion artifacts caused by breathing, a cross-correlation coefficient analysis was applied, rejecting frames with a correlation below 0.99. To suppress stationary tissue signals from the skull, scalp, and other nondynamic structures, spatial-temporal singular vector decomposition was performed in batches of 240 B-mode volumes. To extract MB signals for ULM, an adaptive singular vector decomposition method was used. This method estimated mean Doppler frequencies and adaptively removed the largest singular values, enabling the generation of Power Doppler volumetric images (64). This process yielded high-contrast Power Doppler images, effectively capturing MB fluctuations in the CVs. For superresolution ULM, a normalized cross-correlation–based localization method was used to achieve a subdiffraction resolution (fig. S21) (35). The filtered MB images were interpolated to achieve a resolution of 12-μm voxel size (one-fifth of the original voxel size). Each interpolated frame underwent 3D normalized cross-correlation with the empirically determined point spread function of the imaging system and localized the center positions of MBs. Using the uTrack algorithm for MB tracking, the linear assignment algorithm was implemented to link localized MB positions across frames (65). A maximum pairing/linking search radius of 5 pixels was assigned, and a linear motion Kalman filter model was applied to restrict the maximum linking angle to 45°, promoting robust trajectory estimation. On the basis of the reconstructed MB trajectories, flow direction and velocity maps were computed, providing functional imaging information on blood flow dynamics.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using MATLAB (R2022a, MathWorks, MA), and all statistical graphs were generated within this environment. Student’s t tests were used to assess the statistical significance of differences. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Visualization of combined ULM and PAT

To ensure clear visualization between modalities in combined ULM and PAT rendering, each modality was encoded using distinct information (66). ULM intensity maps were displayed using a customized depth-encoded jet color map, in which color represents axial position, thereby preserving 3D structural information. In contrast, PAT signals were displayed using a hot color map, where color represents the PA intensity of the CSF tracer.

To minimize color overlapping, the ULM depth color map was modified by removing the orange to dark red tones to avoid interference with the PAT hot scale. Dark blue tones were also excluded to prevent overlapping with the background. Furthermore, the depth-encoding direction was defined such that warmer colors in the ULM map correspond to superficial cortical regions, where PAT signals are typically the strongest. Cooler colors represent deeper structures, which facilitate visual distinction of lower-intensity deep-brain PA signals while maintaining spatial depth information.

Quantification of ULM metrics

ROIs were manually delineated, with 3D boundaries reconstructed using a shape-based interpolation framework that operates on binary contours via distance-transform interpolation. Boundary interpolation was performed slice-wise using distance-map zero-crossing reconstruction with chamfer-based distance transforms to approximate the Euclidean distance. The reconstructed vascular volumes were subsequently skeletonized using topology-preserving centerline extraction to generate connected vascular graphs for structural analysis (46). Quantitative ULM metrics, including vascularity, tortuosity (defined as the path length normalized by the Euclidean distance, generally referred to as the distance metric), and mean flow velocity, were computed following established ULM quantification methodologies (67), enabling comprehensive characterization of vascular morphology and hemodynamics.
