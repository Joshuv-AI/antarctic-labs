Optimal Rates for Differentially Private Hypothesis Testing with E-values
Ben Jacobsen, Tomas Gonzalez, Gavin Brown, Kassem Fawaz, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
E-values have attracted considerable interest in recent years as flexible tools for enabling anytime-valid and adaptive data analysis. Hypothesis testing is at the core of many of these applications, which can often involve private or sensitive data. In this work, we answer a simple but important question: given two distributions ℙ \mathbb{P} and ℚ \mathbb{Q} , what is the maximum achievable e-power when testing X ∼ ℙ n X\sim\mathbb{P}^{n} against X ∼ ℚ n X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{n} with e-values that satisfy ε \varepsilon -differential privacy? We characterize the optimal rate for this problem and provide an algorithm which matches it exactly. In the sequential setting, when observations arrive one-by-one and the analyst chooses when to halt, we give matching upper and lower bounds on the stopping times of any private e-process. Numerical experiments confirm the practicality of our algorithms, which require less data than the recently proposed DP-SPRT across a range of sequential testing problems and privacy levels.



1
Introduction

Hypothesis testing is ubiquitous in modern scientific practice and also one of the most fundamental problems in statistical inference.
We consider simple hypothesis testing: an algorithm receives a dataset
X\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and must evaluate whether it was sampled from a distribution
\mathbb{P}
(the null hypothesis) or
\mathbb{Q}
(the alternative).
In the classical, decision-theoretic setting, we wish to output a single decision
\phi(X)\in\{0,1\}
, where the output
\phi=1
is interpreted as rejecting the null hypothesis in favor of the alternate.
To prevent false discoveries, we ask that
\mathbb{P}[\phi(X)=1]\leq\alpha
for some fixed significance level
\alpha
.
The performance of the test is then measured by its true-positive rate or
power
,
\mathbb{Q}[\phi(X)=1]
.

Recently, a complementary paradigm has emerged based on the concept of
e-values

[
33
]
.
Instead of binary decisions, algorithms for hypothesis testing in this setting output a real-valued, non-negative value
E
which
quantifies the degree to which
X
provides evidence for the alternate hypothesis over the null.
We call
E
an
e-variable
and refer to realizations of
E
as
e-values
, but gloss over this distinction when speaking informally.
Larger e-values present more evidence for the alternative, because we expect the e-value to be small under the null, as per its definition.

Definition 1.1
.

An
e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
is a non-negative random variable
E
satisfying
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[E]\leq 1
.

One can directly convert
E
into a hypothesis test, rejecting the null if
E
exceeds
1/\alpha
; this is a level-
\alpha
test due to Markov’s inequality.
We measure the e-value’s performance by its (e-)power.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
E
be an e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
. Its
e-power against
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]
. We say that E is
log-optimal
if
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log(E^{\prime}/E)]\leq 0
for any other e-variable
E^{\prime}
for
\mathbb{P}
.

A major advantage of this approach is that
statistical inferences drawn from (products of) e-values often remain valid even when decisions about data collection and experimental design are made adaptively, whereas those same conditions typically invalidate type-I error control in classical methods
[
33
,
17
]
.
There is thus good reason to hope that more widespread use of e-values and their sequential analog, e-processes, could mitigate issues related to the misuse of p-values which have contributed to the ongoing scientific reproducibility crisis
[
40
,
2
,
36
,
17
,
31
,
8
,
18
]
. Methods based on e-values are also broadly useful for modern statistical workflows in which the number and timing of analyses are rarely fixed in advance such as adaptive clinical trials
[
38
]
, online active experimentation
[
47
,
25
]
, online multiple testing
[
49
]
, sequential change detection
[
37
]
and auditing
[
46
,
7
,
16
]
.

Many statistical applications are driven by sensitive individual-level data, however. This motivates the need for methods which additionally satisfy
differential privacy
(DP), a rigorous and widely used definition of data privacy which guarantees that the result of a statistical analysis cannot depend too much on any one person’s data
[
14
,
15
,
1
]
.

Definition 1.3
.

For
\varepsilon\geq 0
, an algorithm
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{O}
is
\varepsilon
-differentially private
if for all datasets
X
and
X^{\prime}
differing in one entry and all
S\subseteq\mathcal{O}
we have

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(X)\in S]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})\in S]
.

There is a large body of literature investigating differentially private hypothesis testing in the classical decision-theoretic setting
[
6
,
42
,
24
,
34
,
45
,
39
,
9
]
, including several works that study sequential testing problems
[
11
,
50
,
27
,
29
,
4
]
.
We are aware of only three papers on differential privacy and e-values.

48
and
35
consider
local
DP, wherein privacy protections are applied to individual data points before aggregation
[
23
]
.
But while the privacy guarantees provided by local DP are very strong, they can also come at the cost of substantial utility degradation and are not appropriate for all statistical applications.

Our work therefore focuses on the more commonly used
central
model of DP, where the privacy protections apply to the output of a trusted aggregator.
To our knowledge, the only work that considers e-values in this setting is that of
10
.
The algorithms described in that work only apply to testing distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
with bounded likelihood ratios, however, which fails to hold even in the simple case of testing Gaussians.

We aim to provide a more complete understanding of DP e-values for hypothesis testing.
Within this context, our major contributions are:

•

An instance-specific hardness result describing the maximum possible e-power achievable by any
\varepsilon
-DP e-value when testing
\mathbb{P}
against
\mathbb{Q}
(
Theorem

2.2
).

•

The construction of an
\varepsilon
-DP e-value which achieves optimal e-power for any arbitrary pair of distributions, up to lower order terms (
Theorem

2.4
).

•

An instance-specific lower-bound on the expected stopping time of any sequential test based on thresholding an
\varepsilon
-DP e-process, i.e., the amount of data which must be collected on average to achieve a given level of statistical power (
Theorem

3.1
).

•

An efficient algorithm for computing
\varepsilon
-DP e-processes which simultaneously matches this bound, up to a constant multiplicative factor, for
all
stopping times larger than some tunable minimal threshold (
Algorithm

1
,
Theorem

3.2
). In
Section

4
, we compare
Algorithm

1
against the recently proposed DP-SPRT of
27
and find that our algorithm achieves consistently earlier stopping times across a range of settings.

Given the central importance of simple hypothesis tests, our results also provide a foundation for investigating many other interesting problems involving robust, adaptive, and privacy-preserving statistical methods based on e-values. For instance, a natural next step would be to investigate
\varepsilon
-DP e-values for composite hypothesis testing; in that setting, it is often possible to directly apply hardness results for simple hypothesis testing, such as our
Theorem

2.2
, via so-called least-favorable pairs
[

]
. A similar idea arises in statistical estimation, where the difficulty of estimating a parameter
\theta
to error
\pm\alpha
is strongly connected to worst-case difficulty of testing a distribution
D_{\theta}
against
D_{\theta^{\prime}}
for
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\leq\alpha

[
12
]
. It is therefore likely that the ideas we present could be extended to better understand the complexity of e.g. anytime-valid confidence sequences
[
32
,
31
]
under central DP, much as the foundational work of
6
on private simple hypothesis testing in the decision-theoretic setting helped contribute to breakthroughs in instance-optimal DP estimation algorithms
[
26
,
3
]
.

Preliminaries and Notation

The formal definition of differentially private e-variables requires some care: DP is a property of a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}
while the e-variable property is about the output
E=\mathcal{M}(X)
, a random variable.
We draw a clear distinction between these two objects.

Definition 1.4

(Differentially private e-variable and e-power)
.

We say that a non-negative random variable
E=\mathcal{M}(X)
is an
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
whenever
\mathcal{M}
is
\varepsilon
-DP and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}[\mathcal{M}(X)]\leq 1
, where the expectation is taken with respect to both the input data
X\sim\mathbb{P}^{n}
and the internal randomness of
\mathcal{M}
. The e-power of
E
against an alternative
\mathbb{Q}
is
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{n}}[\log\mathcal{M}(X)]
.

We use capital letters like
P
and
Q
for distributions and corresponding lowercase letters like
p
and
q
for their densities.
\mathcal{M}(X)
is a random variable representing the output of
\mathcal{M}
on input
X
, while
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
represents the output distribution of
\mathcal{M}
when
X\sim\mathbb{P}^{n}
. We use the notation
\mathbb{E}^{R^{n}}[f]
to represent the expectation of
f
when the underlying dataset
X
is an i.i.d. sample of
n
points from
R
. When
n
is clear from context, we will sometimes abbreviate this to
\mathbb{E}^{R}[f]
. If
f
is randomized, then the expectation is taken with respect to both the input data and the internal randomness of
f
unless otherwise stated.

We mainly state our results in terms of continuous distributions and ignore measure-theoretic details in the main body of the paper. We collect a number of standard definitions and lemmas related to differential privacy, information theory, and e-values in
Appendix

A
. Formal proofs of most of our results are deferred to
Appendix

D
.

2
Batch Setting

2.1
Characterization of the Optimal E-power under Pure DP

In this section, we present our results for the batch setting, i.e., when the analyst receives all the data at once.
Without privacy, the maximum achievable e-power is straightforward: if
E
is an e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
, then
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{n}}[\log E]\leq\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}
and this bound is achieved exactly by the likelihood ratio e-variable
R=\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{n}}{d\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x)

[
33
]
. We are interested in understanding the corresponding optimal rate under central DP constraints:

\mathfrak{R}_{n,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{Q}~\|~\mathbb{P})\coloneq\sup_{\varepsilon\text{-DP e-variables }E\text{ for }\mathbb{P}}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{n}}[\log E]}{n},\qquad\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)\coloneq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{R}_{n,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{Q}~\|~\mathbb{P}).

(1)

In our search for optimal private e-values, we find it useful to decouple the privacy properties from the e-variable properties.
More formally, consider fixing an arbitrary
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.
How should we turn its output into an e-variable?
We observe that we now have a different testing problem: distinguishing null
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
from alternate
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})
.
Crucially, by the post-processing property of differential privacy, we can consider how to construct an e-variable from the output of
\mathcal{M}
without considering privacy explicitly.

Absent privacy constraints, the optimal e-value is simply the likelihood ratio.
Its e-power is simply the Kullback–Leibler divergence
\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}
.
Thus, for any fixed DP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
, we can post-process its output into an e-variable
E_{\mathcal{M}}
with e-power

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{n}}[\log E_{\mathcal{M}}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{n}\\
Y\sim\mathcal{M}(X)\end{subarray}}\left[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})}(Y)\right]=\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\right)}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))},

and this is the best way to process
\mathcal{M}
’s output.
Immediately, we have the following characterization of the rate.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any null
\mathbb{P}
, alternate
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, the following equality holds:

\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\mathcal{M}\hskip 2.84544pt\varepsilon\text{-DP }}\frac{\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\right)}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}}{n}.

A maximization problem similar to the one defined by
Proposition

2.1
has previously been studied in the context of local DP
[
13
]
, where it is known that the optimum is achieved by a specific family of two-value “staircase” distributions
[
22
]
.
The problem is much less well understood under central DP, however, and so it is not immediately evident how to use this result to argue optimality.

Our core results for the batch setting attack this problem from two directions: an upper bound in
Section

2.3
and lower bound in
Section

2.4
.
The attacks are coordinated: both depend on the analysis of a particular intermediate distribution
\widetilde{Q}
that serves as a bridge between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. We present the construction of
\widetilde{Q}
in
Section

2.2
.

Our bounds match exactly, up to lower order terms, meaning that the private e-values we construct are asymptotically log-optimal for all pairs of distributions. As a consequence, we derive the following strong duality result characterizing the maximum KL divergence between central DP mechanisms, which may be of independent interest:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{X}
. For any
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{D}
and
\varepsilon>0
, the following equality holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\mathcal{M}\hskip 2.84544pt\varepsilon\text{-DP }}\frac{\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\right)}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}}{n}=\inf_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}.

(2)

We remark that right-hand side of (
2
) can be interpreted as a relaxation of the problem of finding the worst corrupted alternate in robust statistics
[
19
]
, where it is known that the optimal test statistic is obtained by clipping the likelihood ratio
d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}
to some interval
[c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}]

[
21
]
. Our problem differs in that we allow
\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}
to vary with
\mathbb{P}
,
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\varepsilon
instead of fixing a particular level of contamination in advance. Nonetheless, we show that a (different) clipped version of the likelihood ratio is similarly optimal for testing under central DP.

2.2
Constructing the Intermediate Distribution

In this section we present our construction of
\widetilde{Q}
and some associated quantities upon which our later results rely.
We present the construction tersely, mentioning only that
\widetilde{Q}
is the distribution where the infimum on the right-hand side of (
2
) is realized.
We give further explanations about
\widetilde{Q}
alongside the relevant arguments and visualize the construction of
\widetilde{Q}
for a specific pair of distributions in
Fig.

1
.

Fix null
\mathbb{P}
, alternate
\mathbb{Q}
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary. We introduce the quantities
c_{1}(\lambda)=e^{-\varepsilon/2+\lambda-1},c_{2}(\lambda)=e^{\varepsilon/2+\lambda-1}
, and define the following subsets of
\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}=\{x:q(x)<c_{1}(\lambda)p(x)\},

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}=\{x:q(x)>c_{2}(\lambda)p(x)\},\qquad

\displaystyle M_{\lambda}=\mathcal{X}\setminus(A_{\lambda}\cup B_{\lambda}).

We then define the key quantity
\lambda^{*}
as the solution to the following implicit equation

f(\lambda)\coloneq c_{1}(\lambda)\mathbb{P}(A_{\lambda})+\mathbb{Q}(M_{\lambda})+c_{2}(\lambda)\mathbb{P}(B_{\lambda})=1.

(3)

We establish the existence of a solution in
Section

D.1
, and will therefore
proceed to use
A
,
B
, and
M
as shorthand for
A_{\lambda^{*}}
,
B_{\lambda^{*}}
, and
M_{\lambda^{*}}
respectively. Similarly, we will use
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
as shorthand for
c_{1}(\lambda^{*})
and
c_{2}(\lambda^{*})
.

We are now ready to present our new distribution
\widetilde{Q}
, whose density is defined as follows:

\widetilde{q}(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1}\cdot p(x)&x\in A,\\
q(x)&x\in M,\\
c_{2}\cdot p(x)&x\in B.\\
\end{cases}

(4)

This integrates to 1 by the definition of
\lambda^{*}
, ensuring that
\widetilde{Q}
is in fact a probability distribution.

2.3
Bounding the Optimal Rate

Our proof technique for deriving an upper bound on e-power is based on techniques of
5
, which were subsequently extended by
6
to characterize optimal private algorithms for symmetric hypothesis tests.
A key component of our approach is the following coupling and group-privacy lemma, which applies to general intermediate distributions.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q},Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, and let
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{O}
be an arbitrary
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism. Then:

\frac{1}{n}\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\right)}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}\leq\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}.

Proof.

Our proof is based on the construction of couplings between several key random variables: the original dataset
X
, the output of our algorithm
\mathcal{M}(X)
, and a ‘shadow dataset’
\tilde{X}
which we will introduce shortly. We will define one coupling of these random variables under the alternate (i.e. when
X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{n}
) and a different one under the null (i.e. when
X\sim\mathbb{P}^{n})
.

Under the alternate, we use the total-variation coupling
\gamma
between
\mathbb{Q}
and
Q^{\prime}
(
Lemma

A.0.5
). That is, for
(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\sim\gamma
, we have
\gamma(X_{i}\neq\tilde{X}_{i})=w\coloneq\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})}
. We sample the true dataset
X
and the shadow dataset
\tilde{X}
jointly from
\gamma^{n}
, so that
X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{n}
and
\tilde{X}\sim Q^{\prime n}
. We additionally define a sequence of indicator random variables
B=B_{1:n}
, where
B_{i}\coloneq\mathbb{I}[X_{i}\neq\tilde{X}_{i}]\sim\text{Bern}(w)
.

Under the null, we instead sample
X\sim\mathbb{P}^{n}
and set
\tilde{X}=X
. For technical reasons that will become clear shortly, we will not set the distribution of
B_{i}
under the null to be an indicator variable. Instead, we require that
\mathbb{P}(B_{i}=1\mid\tilde{X}=\tilde{x})=\mathbb{Q}(B_{i}=1\mid\tilde{X}=\tilde{x})
, i.e., the conditional distributions match.

For a random variable
W
, we use
\mathbb{Q}_{W}
to denote the distribution of
W
under the alternate, and likewise for
\mathbb{P}_{W}
under the null. The conditional distribution of
W
given a realized value
z
of a random variable
Z
is written as
\mathbb{Q}_{W\mid z}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{W\mid z}
).

With this setup done, our goal is to bound
\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}
. We begin by applying the data processing inequality:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}

\displaystyle\leq\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X),\tilde{X},B})}.

By the chain rule for KL divergence (
Lemma

A.0.2
), it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(X)})}

\displaystyle\leq\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X},B}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X},B}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X},B}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X},B})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X},B}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X},B})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X},B}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X},B})}

(5)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x},b\sim\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X},B}}\left[\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)\mid\tilde{x},b}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(\tilde{x})\mid\tilde{x},b}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)\mid\tilde{x},b}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(\tilde{x})\mid\tilde{x},b})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)\mid\tilde{x},b}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(\tilde{x})\mid\tilde{x},b})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\mathcal{M}(X)\mid\tilde{x},b}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}(\tilde{x})\mid\tilde{x},b})}\right],

(6)

where we used the fact that for
X\sim\mathbb{P}_{X\mid\tilde{x}}
,
X=\tilde{x}
by construction and thus
\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{M}(\tilde{x})
. Next, we will bound each of these terms separately. To bound
Eq.

3
Sequential Setting

In sequential hypothesis testing, new data is collected gradually over time until some condition is met and the experiment is concluded. The number of data points used to make any final decision is therefore a random variable,
N
, called a
stopping time
. It is typical for
\mathbb{E}[N]
to be much smaller than the minimum
n
required in the batch setting
[
44
]
; in fact, the Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)
[
43
]
was first developed during World War II in response to the US Navy’s need for efficient alternatives to the likelihood ratio test. Much like the likelihood ratio test, however, the SPRT is decision-theoretic and requires the analyst to commit to a stopping condition in advance.

The analogue in our setting is the
e-process
, which is a sequence of e-variables
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots
with the additional property that
E_{N}
is itself an e-variable for any stopping time
N
. This leads to some very useful properties, such as optional stopping (we may stop the experiment at any time and draw valid conclusions from our final output) as well as optional continuation (if our results are inconclusive, a second scientist can run a follow-up experiment and merge the resulting e-values by multiplication).

The theory of e-processes is intimately connected with that of (non-negative) martingales, which have an interesting game-theoretic interpretation as the accumulated wealth of a gambler betting against the null
[
36
]
. We collect several standard definitions and results about martingales in
Appendix

A
.
28
provide an accessible introduction to the tools we require.

Definition 3.1
.

A process

, and (c)

Any test supermartingale is an e-process, but the converse is not true in general. By Ville’s inequality, any e-process can be used to construct a level-

As in the batch setting, the precise definition of a private e-process requires some care. Essentially, privacy must be defined with respect to the sequence of data points that our algorithm receives as input, whereas e-process properties must be established with respect to the private output sequence.

3.1
Defining the Right Metric

The standard metric for quantifying the power of an e-process
M
is the
asymptotic growth rate of
M
under
\mathbb{Q}
, which is defined by the lower limit
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\log(M_{t})/t
(if it is a constant). In particular, for any pair of distributions
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
, the non-private likelihood ratio process
M_{t}=\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{t}}{d\mathbb{P}^{t}}(x_{1:t})
achieves the optimal asymptotic growth rate of
\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}

. A weaker metric, usually applied in the context of composite alternates, is to consider the asymptotic e-power
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{t}}[\log E_{t}]/t
.

The fact that both metrics are stated in terms of limits is justified in the non-private setting, where the asymptotic behavior of likelihood ratio processes and their extensions is closely related to their local behavior for finite samples and increments. But in our case, the addition of privacy constraints introduces a tradeoff: by batching many points together before producing a new output, we can minimize the total error from noise at the cost of additional latency. Metrics defined in terms of limits essentially disregard one half of this tradeoff and therefore incentivize clearly impractical algorithm designs, e.g., involving outrageously large batch sizes.

For this reason, we argue that DP e-processes should be evaluated in terms of their expected value at finite stopping times.
Moreover, because one of the main advantages of sequential hypothesis testing with e-processes is that we can choose whether to gather more data adaptively without hurting the statistical validity of our conclusions, it would be ideal to have a single private algorithm which is able to perform well at all stopping times simultaneously. In the following section, we prove
Theorem

3.1
, which characterizes the best guarantee of this form that we could hope for.

3.2

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

We remark that results with a similar form to

The picture is much more complicated in the central DP setting, and the main technical novelty in the proof of

Batch to E-process Conversion

As in the batch setting, we begin by considering the problem of directly privatizing an existing e-process through additive noise:

Lemma 3.1.1

Let

5
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated simple hypothesis testing with
\varepsilon
-DP e-values.
In the batch setting, we characterized
\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
, the optimal instance-specific e-power achievable by any e-value under
\varepsilon
-DP, and constructed a concrete
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm which matches that rate exactly.
In the sequential setting,
we proved a universal lower bound on the expected stopping time of any
\varepsilon
-DP e-process and showed that our
Algorithm

1
matches that lower bound up to an arbitrarily small constant factor.
Moreover, we found empirically that our algorithm is able to consistently outperform an existing state-of-the-art algorithm for DP sequential testing.
In future work, we hope to extend our results to other natural settings such as composite hypothesis testing with DP e-values, as well as relaxed notions of privacy such as approximate DP where the optimal achievable e-power remains unknown.

Appendix B
Improving our private e-variable for finite sample sizes

The private e-variable introduced in
Eq.

8
is asymptotically log-optimal. This does not imply that
Eq.

8
cannot be improved for specific input distributions, however. In particular, because we directly add Laplacian noise, our private e-variable is always unbounded, even when the non-private likelihood ratios themselves are not. As we show below, this implies that the construction is suboptimal in a fairly strong sense.

Proposition B.1

(Suboptimality of unbounded private e-variables)
.

If
E
is an unbounded
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable, then there exists a function
f
such that
f(E)
is an
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log(\frac{E}{f(E)})]<0
.

Proof.

Since
E
is an
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable, we can write it as
\mathcal{M}(X)
for some
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
. Then, by
Lemma

D.0.1
, the unique log-optimal e-variable for testing
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})
against
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
is the likelihood ratio
E_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
, and by group privacy,
E_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
is bounded in
[e^{-n\varepsilon},e^{n\varepsilon}]
. Taking contrapositive, we conclude that no unbounded
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable can be log-optimal, even when compared against mechanisms that operate solely by post-processing its outputs.
∎

This observation naturally suggests the following modification of our original construction, which is log-optimal for its own output distribution by design:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log(1-\lambda+\lambda E^{*}(x_{t}))+Z_{b},\qquad E=\frac{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})}{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})}(\mathcal{M}(X)).

(9)

When can we actually compute this value? Under the hypothesis that the
x_{i}
are drawn i.i.d. from some distribution
D\in\{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\}
,
\mathcal{M}(x_{1:n})
is a sum of independent random variables. So, a straightforward sufficient condition is the ability to compute the CDF of the untruncated likelihood ratio under
D
, which is in turn sufficient to compute the characteristic function of
E^{*}
,
\varphi_{D}(t)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim D}[\exp(it\cdot\mathbb{E}^{*}(x))]
. In this case, we can recover the likelihood under
D
as

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\varphi_{D}(t)^{n}\cdot\frac{1}{1+b^{2}t^{2}}\right)(\mathcal{M}(X)),

(10)

where
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denotes the inverse Fourier transform. We remark that, although the exact e-power of this e-variable typically does not have a closed form, log-optimality allows us to lower-bound it by the e-power of any other e-variable derived from the same private output, including the one described in
Theorem

2.4
.

Appendix C
A nearly-optimal distribution-independent bounded e-variable

In this appendix, we present the truncated, scaled likelihood ratio (
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
) statistic, a simple bounded e-variable which does not require any form of distribution-dependent tuning, and show that it achieves e-power of at least
\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
. This makes the
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
statistic a viable alternative to
E^{*}
in settings where computing the distribution-dependent tuning parameter
\lambda^{*}
is computationally intractable, provided that
\varepsilon
is at least somewhat larger than 1. For constant
\varepsilon\leq 1
, we can still match the optimal rate up to constant factors by computing
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
at a larger, fixed value of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and then taking fractional powers, which we show yields a bounded e-variable with e-power of at least
\frac{\varepsilon}{5}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
. In either case, we can then privatize our bounded e-variable, which we show can reduce its e-power by at most a factor of 2.

C.1

\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)

.

\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}

\tau=D_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max\{q(x)-e^{\varepsilon}p(x),0\}~dx.

C.2
Constructing a distribution-independent test statistic

We begin by designing a bounded test statistic that achieves
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]=\Theta(\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right))
as long as
\varepsilon
is somewhat larger than 1. Then, we will extend to
\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
by transforming
E
in a way which reduces its log-sensitivity while preserving its e-power up to a constant factor.

Lemma C.1.1
.

Define the truncation operator
f_{c}(x)=\min(c,x)
. Fix
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
and let
R=\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(x)
. Then
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)]\geq\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
.

Proof.

As in the proof of the upper-bound, define the sub-distribution
\bar{q}(x)=\min(q(x),e^{\varepsilon}p(x))
and let
q^{\prime}(x)=\bar{q}(x)/(1-\tau)
be the normalized density function. This allows us to write
Q=(1-\tau)Q^{\prime}+\tau Q^{\prime\prime}
. Let
B=\{x:\log(q(x)/p(x))>\varepsilon\}
. Now, have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)\log(f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R))~dx

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{B}q(x)~dx+\int_{\mathcal{X}\setminus B}q(x)\log\left(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)~dx

\displaystyle=\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(B)+\varepsilon\tau+\int_{\mathcal{X}\setminus B}q(x)\log\left(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)~dx.

Next, we compute
\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,P^{\prime}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}
:

\displaystyle(1-\tau)\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,P^{\prime}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{q}(x)\log\left(\frac{q^{\prime}(x)}{(1-\tau)p(x)}\right)~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B}e^{\varepsilon}p(x)\log\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}p(x)}{p(x)}\right)~dx+\int_{\mathcal{X}\setminus B}q(x)\log\left(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)~dx-(1-\tau)\log(1-\tau)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(B)+\int_{\mathcal{X}\setminus B}q(x)\log\left(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)~dx-(1-\tau)\log(1-\tau).

It follows that:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\tau)\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,P^{\prime}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})})+\tau\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\tau)\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,P^{\prime}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})})+\tau\varepsilon}{(1-\tau)\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q^{\prime}\,\middle\|\,P^{\prime}\right)}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})}{\KLop(Q^{\prime}\,\|\,P^{\prime})})+\tau\varepsilon+(1-\tau)\log(1-\tau)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau\varepsilon}{\tau\varepsilon+(1-\tau)\log(1-\tau)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau\varepsilon}{\tau\varepsilon-\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}

and thus that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)]\geq\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
.
∎

For
\varepsilon>0
, we define the
truncated scaled likelihood ratio statistic
:

\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)=e^{-\varepsilon}+(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\left(f_{1+e^{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(x)\right)\right).

Theorem C.2
.

The
tsLR
statistic satisfies the following properties:

•

\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
is an e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
.

•

For all
x
,
\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
.

•

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)]\geq\frac{(\varepsilon-1)(1-e^{-\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
.

Proof.

First, we have
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)]=e^{-\varepsilon}+(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[f_{1+e^{\varepsilon}}(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(x))]\leq 1
because
f_{1+e^{\varepsilon}}(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(x))
is an e-variable.

Next, we show that
\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
has bounded sensitivity. Clearly,
\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq-\varepsilon
. Simultaneously, we have
\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq\log(e^{-\varepsilon}+(1-e^{-\varepsilon})(1+e^{\varepsilon}))=\log(e^{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon
. So, our statistic has bounded log-sensitivity of
2\varepsilon
.

Finally, we show that the
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
has nearly-optimal e-power for sufficiently large
\varepsilon
. We will bound the e-power of our statistic against
f_{\varepsilon}
on the three regions
A=\{x:p(x)>q(x)\}
,
B=\{x:q(x)>(1+e^{\varepsilon})p(x)\}
, and
M=\mathcal{X}\setminus(A\cup B)
.

On
A
, we have
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)=e^{-\varepsilon}+(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\geq\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}
because
\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\leq 1
, and so
\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)
. On
B
, we have
\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon=\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}(R)}
. Finally, on
M
, we have
\log(\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x))\geq(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\geq(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)
. We conclude that
\log(\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x))\geq(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\log f_{e^{\varepsilon}}(R)
pointwise, and therefore that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}(x)]\geq\frac{(\varepsilon-1)(1-e^{-\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right).

∎

The
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
statistic can be used directly for sufficiently large
\varepsilon
. For example, if
\varepsilon>2.3
, then one can calculate that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
. For
\varepsilon\leq 1
, however, it has no provable guarantees in general.

C.3
Extending the distribution-independent test statistic

We overcome this challenge in two steps. First, we observe that if
E^{\prime}
is an e-variable such that
\log E^{\prime}\in[-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}]
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E^{\prime}]=\mu
, then
E=\exp(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\log E^{\prime})
is an e-variable (by concavity) which satisfies
\log E\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]=\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mu
. Second, we observe that the optimal rate
\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
is clearly a non-decreasing function of
\varepsilon
, i.e.
\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Q}~\|~\mathbb{P})\geq\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)
. This allows us to construct the following continuous family of candidate bounded-sensitivity e-variables:

\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\coloneq(\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{\prime}}\in[e^{-\varepsilon},e^{\varepsilon}],

and we can choose
\varepsilon^{*}\geq\varepsilon
in order to optimize our lower bound on their e-power:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{*}}]\geq\sup_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq\min(1,\varepsilon)}\left(\frac{(\varepsilon^{\prime}-1)(1-e^{-{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right).

The right-hand side is a linear function of
\varepsilon
, and therefore the value of the unconstrained optimum is independent of
\varepsilon
. We numerically calculate that the maximum is obtained at
\varepsilon^{*}\approx 2.334
where the value of the right-hand side is approximately
0.221\cdot\varepsilon
. For
\varepsilon>2.334
, the optimum is attained at the extreme point
\varepsilon^{*}=\varepsilon
, i.e. the unadjusted
\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}
statistic. For
\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
, we simplify these constants slightly to conclude that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\text{tsLR}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{*}}]\asymp\min\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{5},1\right)\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)=\Theta(\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right))

Finally, we prove a slightly modified version of
Theorem

2.4
for e-variables with log-range exactly
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, which allows us to derive a distribution-independent
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable satisfying

\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]}{n}\asymp\min\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{10},\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)=\Theta(\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)),

as desired.

The formal statement of the modified theorem follows:

Theorem C.3
.

Let
E\in[e^{-\varepsilon},e^{\varepsilon}]
be an e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]=\mu
. Then, for every
\varepsilon=O(1)
, there exist computable values

and

such that
\exp(\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(E;\lambda))
is an
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
and:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(E;\lambda)]\geq\frac{n\mu}{2}-\log(n\mu)-O(1)

We provide the (rather technical) proof below. It is conceptually similar to the proof of
Theorem

2.4
, except that the particularly symmetric range of
[e^{-\varepsilon},e^{\varepsilon}]
allows us to derive a more precise closed-form expression for the optimal choice of
\lambda
, which in turn gives a tighter characterization of the final error.

Proof of
Theorem

C.3
.

Let
E\in[e^{-\varepsilon},e^{\varepsilon}]
be an e-variable for
\mathbb{P}
. Then
the non-private test statistic from
Eq.

8
instantiated with
E
has log-sensitivity:

R_{\lambda}=\log\left(\frac{1-\lambda+\lambda e^{\varepsilon}}{1-\lambda+\lambda e^{-\varepsilon}}\right).

In order to ensure that we end up with a valid e-variable, we need
\mathbb{E}[e^{Z_{b}}]=\frac{1}{1-b^{2}}<\infty
, which implies that
b<1
. Meanwhile, to satisfy
\varepsilon
-DP, we require that
b\geq\frac{R_{\lambda}}{\varepsilon}
. Writing
\mu=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]
and using the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
, we have that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{\Lambda}_{S}(\lambda)]=n\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log(1-\lambda+\lambda E)]+\mathbb{E}[Z_{b}]-\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(Z_{b})]\geq\lambda n\mu+\log(1-b^{2}).

We will choose
b=\frac{R_{\lambda}}{\varepsilon}
exactly and reparameterize with
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}-s
. Then:

\displaystyle R_{s}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1+s-\frac{1}{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-s)e^{\varepsilon}}{1+s-\frac{1}{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-s)e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1+2s+(1-2s)e^{\varepsilon}}{1+2s+(1-2s)e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\log\left(\frac{1+2s+(1-2s)e^{\varepsilon}}{(1+2s)e^{\varepsilon}+1-2s}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\log\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}+1+(2-2e^{\varepsilon})s}{(2e^{\varepsilon}-2)s+e^{\varepsilon}+1}\right).

Taking derivatives, we get:

\displaystyle R^{\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{2-2e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}+1+(2-2e^{\varepsilon})s}-\frac{2e^{\varepsilon}-2}{(2e^{\varepsilon}-2)s+e^{\varepsilon}+1}

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(2-2e^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(e^{\varepsilon}+1+(2-2e^{\varepsilon})s)^{2}}+\frac{(2e^{\varepsilon}-2)^{2}}{((2e^{\varepsilon}-2)s+e^{\varepsilon}+1)^{2}}.

At
s=0
, we get
R^{\prime}_{s}=-4\tanh(\varepsilon/2)
and
R^{\prime\prime}_{s}=0
. Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(2-2e^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(e^{\varepsilon}+1+(2-2e^{\varepsilon})s)^{2}}

\displaystyle>\frac{(2e^{\varepsilon}-2)^{2}}{((2e^{\varepsilon}-2)s+e^{\varepsilon}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle((2e^{\varepsilon}-2)s+e^{\varepsilon}+1)^{2}

\displaystyle>(e^{\varepsilon}+1+(2-2e^{\varepsilon})s)^{2}

\displaystyle(e^{2\varepsilon}-1)s

\displaystyle>(1-e^{2\varepsilon})s

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle>0.

So, we have that
R_{s}
is concave on the interval
[0,1/2]

R_{0}+R^{\prime}_{0}s=\varepsilon-4\tanh(\varepsilon/2)s
. Rewriting our objective function with the same change of variables, we get:

\left(\frac{1}{2}-s\right)n\mu+\log\left(1-\left(1-\frac{4\tanh(\varepsilon/2)s}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\right).

Let
C=\frac{4\tanh(\varepsilon/2)}{\varepsilon}
. Then the expression simplifies to:

\left(\frac{1}{2}-s\right)n\mu+\log\left(1-\left(1-Cs\right)^{2}\right).

Applying the first-order optimality condition with respect to
s
, we get:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-n\mu+\frac{2C(1-Cs)}{1-(1-Cs)^{2}}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(1-Cs)^{2}n\mu+2C(1-Cs)-n\mu

\displaystyle(1-Cs)

\displaystyle=\frac{-C\pm\sqrt{C^{2}+n^{2}\mu^{2}}}{n\mu}

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{C-\sqrt{C^{2}+n^{2}\mu^{2}}+n\mu}{Cn\mu}

At this point, we introduce the change of variables
n\mu=B

C=B\sinh(u)
. Then:

Cs=\frac{B\sinh(u)-B\cosh(u)+B}{B}=\sinh(u)-\cosh(u)+1=1-e^{-u},

and so

\log(1-(1-Cs)^{2})=\log(1-e^{-2u}).

Simultaneously, we have that:

Bs=\frac{B\sinh(u)-B\cosh(u)+B}{B\sinh(u)}=1+\frac{1-\cosh(u)}{\sinh(u)}=1-\tanh(u/2).

So, all together, the value of our objective function becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{B}{2}-\left(1-\frac{e^{u}-1}{e^{u}+1}\right)+\log(1-e^{-2u})

\displaystyle=\frac{B}{2}-\frac{2}{e^{u}+1}+\log(1-e^{-2u})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{B}{2}-1+\log(1-e^{-2u}).

Using the logarithmic representation of
u=\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{C}{B}\right)
, this simplifies to:

\frac{B}{2}-1+\ln\left(\frac{2Ce^{-u}}{B}\right)=\frac{B}{2}-\ln(B)-1+\ln\left(2C\right)-u,

and expanding the definition of
B
then yields the theorem statement.
∎

\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)\leq\sup_{\mathcal{M}~\varepsilon\text{-DP}}\frac{\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\middle\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\right)}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}{\KLop(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{n})\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))}}{n}.

But, for any arbitrary
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism
\mathcal{M}
,
Lemma

D.0.1
tells us that the right-hand side is attained exactly by the
\varepsilon
-DP e-variable
E_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
, and we conclude that the inequality is in fact an equality.
∎

In
Theorem

2.3
, we show that the e-power of the non-private e-variable
E^{*}
exactly matches the upper bound given by
Theorem

2.2
under the minor technical condition that the set
\mathcal{X}
is a Polish space, which encompasses essentially all interesting cases.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Polish space, and let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{X}
endowed with the weak topology. Define:

f(Q)\coloneq\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(Q\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(Q,\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(Q,\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q,\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(Q,\mathbb{Q})},\qquad\widetilde{Q}\coloneq\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}f(Q).

We will begin by showing that
\widetilde{Q}
exists and is almost-surely unique. To this end, we observe that
f(\mathbb{P})=\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}
is feasible. So, it suffices to optimize over the set
S\coloneq\{Q\mid\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(Q\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(Q\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(Q\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}\leq\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\}
, which is weakly compact because
\mathcal{X}
is a Polish space. Then, because
f
is weakly lower semi-continuous, it follows from the extreme value theorem that a minimizer of
f
exists in
S
. Finally, because KL divergence is strictly convex over
S
while TV distance is convex,
f
is strictly convex and we conclude that the minimizer is unique.

Having established existence and uniqueness, we now turn to analyzing the specific form of the optimum. For a fixed distribution
R
with density
r
, we define
g(x)=q(x)/p(x)
and
s(x)=r(x)/p(x)
. Then we can expand the objective function as:

\displaystyle f(R)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}r(x)\log\left(\frac{r(x)}{p(x)}\right)~dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|r(x)-q(x)|~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\frac{r(x)}{p(x)}\log\left(\frac{r(x)}{p(x)}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\frac{r(x)}{p(x)}-\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right|\right]p(x)~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[s(x)\log\left(s(x)\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|s(x)-g(x)\right|\right]p(x)~dx.

We will minimize this quantity with respect to
s(x)
, subject to the constraint that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}s(x)p(x)~dx=1
. We define the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(R,\lambda)=f(R)+\lambda(1-\int_{\mathcal{X}}s(x)p(x)~dx)
and compute:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\log s(x)+1-\lambda+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sign[s(x)-g(x)]\right]p(x)~dx,

which yields the first-order optimality condition:

\log s(x)+1-\lambda+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sign[s(x)-g(x)]=0

for all
x
. Rearranging, we conclude that:

s(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1}\coloneq e^{-\varepsilon/2+\lambda^{*}-1}&g(x)<c_{1},\\
c_{2}\coloneq e^{\varepsilon/2+\lambda^{*}-1}&g(x)>c_{2},\\
g(x)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\lambda^{*}
is the unique value of the Lagrange multiplier such that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}s(x)p(x)~dx=1
. From here, we define the sets:

A=\{x\mid q(x)/p(x)<c_{1}\},\quad B=\{x\mid q(x)/p(x)>c_{2}\},\quad M=\mathcal{X}\setminus(A\cup B),

which yields the following expression for the density of the
\widetilde{Q}
:

\widetilde{q}(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1}\cdot p(x)&x\in A,\\
q(x)&x\in M,\\
c_{2}\cdot p(x)&x\in B.\end{cases}

We are now ready to define our e-variable,
E^{*}(x)\coloneq\frac{\widetilde{q}(x)}{p(x)}
. Immediately, we observe that
c_{2}/c_{1}=e^{\varepsilon}
and therefore that
E^{*}
has bounded log-sensitivity of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, we can compute that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E^{*}]-\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}(q(x)-\widetilde{q}(x))\log(c_{1})~dx+\int_{B}(q(x)-\widetilde{q}(x))\log(c_{2})~dx

\displaystyle=\left(\widetilde{Q}(A)-\mathbb{Q}(A)\right)[\varepsilon/2-\lambda^{*}+1]+(\mathbb{Q}(B)-\widetilde{Q}(B))[\varepsilon/2+\lambda^{*}-1]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})},

where we used the fact that
\widetilde{Q}(A)-\mathbb{Q}(A)=\mathbb{Q}(B)-\widetilde{Q}(B)=\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}
. It follows that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E^{*}]=\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+\varepsilon\mathchoice{\TVop\!\left(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q}\right)}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})}{\TVop(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})},

as desired.
∎

We present the proof of
Theorem

2.4
, which establishes that the private test statistic from
Eq.

8
matches the optimal rate up to lower-order terms.

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
.

The non-private test statistic from
Eq.

8
has log-sensitivity:

R_{\lambda}=\log\left(\frac{1-\lambda+\lambda c_{2}}{1-\lambda+\lambda c_{1}}\right).

In order to ensure that we end up with a valid e-variable, we need
\mathbb{E}[e^{Z_{b}}]=\frac{1}{1-b^{2}}<\infty
, which implies that
b<1
. Meanwhile, to satisfy
\varepsilon
-DP, we require that
b\geq\frac{R_{\lambda}}{\varepsilon}
. Writing
\mu=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log E]
and using the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
, we have that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{\Lambda}_{S}(\lambda)]=n\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log(1-\lambda+\lambda E)]+\mathbb{E}[Z_{b}]-\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(Z_{b})]\geq\lambda n\mu+\log(1-b^{2}).

We will choose
b=\frac{R_{\lambda}}{\varepsilon}
exactly and reparameterize with
\lambda=1-s
. Taking the Taylor expansion of
R_{s}
around
s=0
, we can approximate:

\displaystyle R_{s}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{(1-s)c_{2}+s}{(1-s)c_{1}+s}\right)=\log((1-s)c_{2}+s)-\log((1-s)c_{1}+s)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{s}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-c_{2}}{(1-s)c_{2}+s}-\frac{1-c_{1}}{(1-s)c_{1}+s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-c_{2})((1-s)c_{1}+s)-(1-c_{1})((1-s)c_{2}+s)}{((1-s)c_{2}+s)((1-s)c_{1}+s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{s(1-c_{2}-1+c_{1})+(1-s)(c_{1}-c_{1}c_{2}-c_{2}+c_{1}c_{2})}{((1-s)c_{2}+s)((1-s)c_{1}+s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1}-c_{2}}{((1-s)c_{2}+s)((1-s)c_{1}+s)}

\displaystyle R_{s}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon-\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{1}c_{2}}s+O(s^{2}).

Let
\beta=\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{1}c_{2}}>0
. Then we have that
b=1-s\beta/\varepsilon+O(s^{2}/\varepsilon)
and thus that
\log(1-b^{2})\approx\log(2s\beta/\varepsilon)=\log(s)+O(1)
for sufficiently small
s
. Plugging this approximation into our objective function, we get:

(1-s)n\mu+\log s+O(1),

and applying the first order condition with respect to
s
, we arrive at an asymptotically optimal value of
s=\frac{1}{n\mu}
, which does indeed converge to 0. With this choice, we obtain that our objective value satisfies

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{\Lambda}_{S}(\lambda)]\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n\mu}\right)n\mu+\log(1/(n\mu))-O(1)=n\mu-\log(n\mu)-O(1),

as desired.
∎

Remark D.1
.

In practice, the optimal value of
\lambda
(and
s
) should be computed directly through bisection. The particular value
s=\frac{1}{n\mu}
is useful as a proof technique to capture the asymptotic behavior of the estimator, but is typically suboptimal for finite sample sizes.

Lemma D.0.2

Theorem

3.1
provides an upper bound on the expected value of a
\varepsilon
-DP e-process
(E_{t})
at any random stopping time
N<N_{max}
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

We begin by establishing some notation. By
Lemma

D.0.2
, we can assume without loss of generality that
E_{t}
is a likelihood ratio process generated by the outputs of our DP mechanism. We will denote the corresponding log-likelihood ratio process by
L_{t}=\log\frac{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{t})}{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{t})}(O_{1:t})
, which is adapted to the natural output filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(O_{1:t})
.

From here, we recall the total-variation coupling
\gamma
between
\mathbb{Q}
and
\widetilde{Q}
presented in
Section

2.1
: for
X,\tilde{X}\sim\gamma
we have
X\sim\mathbb{Q}
,
\tilde{X}\sim\widetilde{Q}
, and
w\coloneq\Pr[X_{t}\neq\tilde{X}_{t}]=TV(\widetilde{Q},\mathbb{Q})
. Let
X_{1:n},\tilde{X}_{1:n}\sim\gamma^{n}
under the alternate, and let
X_{i}=\tilde{X}_{i}\sim\mathbb{P}
under the null. In addition, we define a sequence of binary random variables
B_{1:n}
such that
B_{t}=\mathbb{I}[X_{i}\neq\tilde{X}_{i}]
. Under the null, we define
B_{t}
so that
\mathbb{P}(B_{t}=1\mid\tilde{X}_{t})=\mathbb{Q}(B_{t}=1\mid\tilde{X}_{t})
.

Finally, we introduce the shorthand
D(Y)=\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{Y}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{Y}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{Y}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{Y})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{Y}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{Y})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{Y}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{Y})}
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{Y}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
) represents the distribution of
Y
under
\mathbb{Q}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}
). This notation will prevent the following calculations from becoming unwieldy.

With these preliminaries concluded, our basic proof strategy can be seen as an extension
Lemma

2.1.1
: we will use the data-processing inequality to upper-bound
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L_{N}]=D(O_{1:N})
by the divergence
D(O_{1:N},\tilde{x}_{1:N},b_{1:N})
, which will turn out to be easier to analyze. Intuitively, the latter quantity represents the achievable e-power of a hypothetical mechanism which is given additional side information about the input, and this will yield a valid upper-bound on the achievable e-power of real-world mechanisms that lack that information. The first step in this process is to define an appropriate potential function
\Phi^{\prime}
which will allow us to largely ignore the internal workings of our particular mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.

Constructing the potential function.

Formally, we define the augmented log-likelihood ratio process
L^{\prime}_{t}=\log\frac{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{t})}{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{t})}(O_{1:t},B_{1:t})
, which is adapted to the finer filtration
\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma(O_{1:t},B_{1:t})
. The conditional expected increments of
L^{\prime}_{t}
satisfy:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L^{\prime}_{t}-L^{\prime}_{t-1}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1}]=D(O_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1}).

We will also define the potential function
\Phi^{\prime}_{t}=D(\tilde{X}_{1:t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t})
.

Next, we apply the chain rule for KL divergence (
Lemma

A.0.2
) to the joint distribution of
O_{t}\otimes B_{t},\tilde{X}_{1:t}
conditioned on
\mathcal{G}_{t-1}
to get:

\displaystyle D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{1:t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=D(O_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(\tilde{X}_{1:t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t})]

\displaystyle=D(O_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Phi^{\prime}_{t}].

Separately, we apply the chain rule to the joint distribution of
O_{t}\otimes\tilde{X}_{t}\otimes B_{t},\tilde{X}_{1:t-1}
to compute:

\displaystyle D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{1:t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=D(\tilde{X}_{1:t-1}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})]

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}_{t-1}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})].

Because the left-hand sides of the two equations above are equal, we can equate the right-hand sides:

D(O_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Phi^{\prime}_{t}]=\Phi^{\prime}_{t-1}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})],

and by our formula for the expected conditional increments of
L^{\prime}_{t}
, it follows that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L^{\prime}_{t}+\Phi^{\prime}_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1}]=L^{\prime}_{t-1}+\Phi^{\prime}_{t-1}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})].

Because KL divergence is non-negative, we conclude that the process
\tilde{L}^{\prime}_{t}=L^{\prime}_{t}+\Phi^{\prime}_{t}
is a submartingale under the alternate for the finer filtration
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. Also by non-negativity, we have
\tilde{L}^{\prime}_{t}\geq L^{\prime}_{t}
.

A fixed-time upper bound.

We now turn our attention to the increments. Once again applying the chain rule for KL divergence, we have:

D(O_{t},\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})=D(\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},b_{t},\tilde{x}_{1:t})].

We will bound each term separately. Since the
\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}
pairs are i.i.d. and
B_{t}
has the same conditional distribution given
\tilde{x}_{t}
under the null and the alternate, we have that
D(\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{1:t-1})=D(\tilde{X}_{t},B_{t})=\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}
.
Simultaneously, we have that:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i-1},b_{i},\tilde{x}_{1:i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[D(O_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},b_{1:t},\tilde{x}_{1:t})]

\displaystyle=D(O_{1:t}\mid b_{1:t},\tilde{x}_{1:t}),

where the first equality follows because
O_{i}
is independent of
b_{i+1:t},\tilde{x}_{i+1:t}
and the second equality follows inductively from the chain rule for KL divergence (
Lemma

A.0.2
). Next, we apply joint convexity along with group privacy (see
Eq.

7
) to conclude that:

\displaystyle D(O_{1:t}\mid b_{1:t},\tilde{x}_{1:t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}\varepsilon.

Importantly, this is an almost sure upper bound on the sum of the expectations, and not merely a bound on the expectation of the sum. It follows that under
\mathcal{G}
, we can decompose
\tilde{L}^{\prime}_{t}=W_{t}+A_{t}
for some martingale
W_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}w_{i}
and some process
A_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}
satisfying
A_{t}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}[\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+b_{i}\varepsilon)]
almost surely.

Extension to bounded stopping times.

Now, let
N
be any
\mathcal{F}
stopping time which is upper-bounded by
N_{max}<\infty
. Then it is also a stopping time for the finer filtration
\mathcal{G}
. We can therefore write:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{L}^{\prime}_{N}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N_{max}}(w_{t}+a_{t})\{N\geq t\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N_{max}}(w_{t}+[\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+b_{t}\varepsilon)])\{N\geq t\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N_{max}}[\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+b_{t}\varepsilon)]\{N\geq t\}\right],

where the last line follows from linearity of expectation along with the optional stopping theorem (
Lemma

A.0.6
) applied to the martingale
W_{t}
, which is valid because
N\leq N_{max}
almost surely. Now, we can take expectation over
b
. Because
\{N\geq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\perp b_{t}
, Wald’s equation (
Lemma

A.0.7
) gives us that:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N_{max}}\left(\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+b_{t}\varepsilon\right)\{N\geq t\}\right]=\mathbb{E}[N]\left(\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+w\varepsilon\right),

and by rearranging, this gives us that:

\mathbb{E}[N]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{L}^{\prime}_{N}]}{\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+w\varepsilon}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L^{\prime}_{N}]}{\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+w\varepsilon}.

This is nearly what we want, except that it’s stated in terms of the sequence
L^{\prime}_{t}
. To conclude, we observe that by the data processing inequality, we have that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L_{N}]=D(O_{1:N})\leq D(\tilde{x}_{1:N},b_{1:N},O_{1:N})=E^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{L}^{\prime}_{N}]
, giving us finally that:

\mathbb{E}[N]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L_{N}]}{\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+w\varepsilon}.

Using the fact (proved in
Section

2
) that
\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)=\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\widetilde{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}{\KLop(\widetilde{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})}+w\varepsilon
then yields the theorem statement. This concludes the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.
∎

Proposition

Proof of

Let
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}
be any
\varepsilon
-DP sequential test with power
1-\beta
and level
\alpha<1-\beta
. We will write the output process as
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots
, interpreting
\phi_{t}=1
to mean that
\mathcal{M}
rejected the null hypothesis at or before time
t
. Let
N
be the stopping time of
\mathcal{M}
, and assume that it is bounded above by an arbitrary constant
N_{max}<\infty
(this is satisfied by all real-world tests). For
n>N

\mathcal{M}

\mathbb{Q}(\phi_{N}=1)=1-\beta
and

Now, define the likelihood ratio process
M_{t}=\frac{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Q}^{t})}{d\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{t})}(\phi_{1:t})
. In the proof of
Lemma

D.0.2
, we show that
M_{t}
is an
\varepsilon
-DP e-process. So, by
Theorem

3.1
along with the definition of KL divergence, we have the following upper bound:

\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}})}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log M_{N}]\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[N]\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right).

On the other hand, by the data-processing inequality along with our assumption that
\mathcal{M}
has power
1-\beta
and level
\alpha
, we have the lower bound:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{1:N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{1:N}})}

\displaystyle\geq\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{N}}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{N}}\right)}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{N}})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{N}})}{\KLop(\mathbb{Q}_{\phi_{N}}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\phi_{N}})}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\KLop\!\left(\text{Bern}(1-\beta)\,\middle\|\,\text{Bern}(\alpha)\right)}{\KLop(\text{Bern}(1-\beta)\,\|\,\text{Bern}(\alpha))}{\KLop(\text{Bern}(1-\beta)\,\|\,\text{Bern}(\alpha))}{\KLop(\text{Bern}(1-\beta)\,\|\,\text{Bern}(\alpha))}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)\log((1-\beta)/\alpha)+\beta\log(\beta/(1-\alpha)).

Rearranging, we conclude that:

\mathbb{E}[N]\geq\frac{(1-\beta)\log((1-\beta)/\alpha)+\beta\log(\beta/(1-\alpha))}{\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbb{Q}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}\right)},

completing the proof.
∎

We remark that a ‘converse’ to the construction in the preceding proof holds as well: given any
\varepsilon
-DP e-process
E
, we can post-process it into a
\varepsilon
-DP level-
\alpha
sequential test by defining
\phi_{t}=\mathbb{I}[\exists i\leq t\,.\,E_{i}\geq 1/\alpha]
. In other words, any hardness result for
\varepsilon
-DP sequential tests can be immediately turned into a hardness result for
\varepsilon
-DP e-processes, and visa versa.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1.1
.

We can decompose the ratio
E_{t}/E_{t-1}
into:

\exp(\lambda(\tilde{\Lambda}_{t}-\tilde{\Lambda}_{t-1})-K_{t}(\lambda))=\exp(\lambda(\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t-1}))\exp(\lambda(\xi_{t}-\xi_{t-1})-K_{t}(\lambda)).

Because
M_{t}
is a test (super)martingale, we know that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\exp(\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t-1})\mid\Lambda_{1:t-1}]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[M_{t}/M_{t-1}\mid M_{1:t-1}]\leq 1
. Then, for
\lambda\in[0,1]
, the map
x\mapsto x^{\lambda}
is concave, and so Jensen’s inequality gives us that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\exp(\lambda(\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t-1}))\mid\Lambda_{1:t-1}]\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\exp(\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t-1})\mid\Lambda_{1:t-1}]^{\lambda}\leq 1
. Applying the definition of
K_{t}(\lambda)
, it follows that
\exp(\lambda(\xi_{t}-\xi_{t-1})-K_{t}(\lambda))\leq 1
as well. Finally, we use independence to conclude that
E_{t}
is a test (super)martingale for
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{N}})
and thus an
\varepsilon
-DP e-process.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

The fact that
Algorithm

1
satisfies
\varepsilon
-DP follows from standard analysis of the Laplace mechanism. To see that it is an e-process, let
\mathcal{G}_{j}=\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}}
denote the ‘coarsened’ filtration seen by the batched process, so that
\Lambda_{j}-\Lambda_{j-1}=\sum_{i=t_{j-1}+1}^{t_{j}}\log E(x_{i})
. By
Lemma

3.1.1
,
(\widetilde{E}_{t_{j}})
is an
\varepsilon
-DP test (super)martingale for
\mathcal{G}_{j}
. Expanding back out to
\mathcal{F}
clearly preserves the martingale property since
\widetilde{E}_{i}=\widetilde{E}_{i-1}
exactly for any
i\neq t_{j}
, and so we conclude that
(\widetilde{E}_{i})
is a test (super)martingale for the original output filtration
\mathcal{F}
as well.

To analyze its expected stopping times, define
J(t)=\max\{j:t_{j}\leq t\}
to be the number of batch updates up to time
t
. Let
C_{\lambda}=-\log(1-c^{2}\lambda^{2})
. Our goal is to minimize the following competitive ratio between the original non-private process and our output process:

\sup_{N\geq t_{1}}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda_{N}]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\widetilde{E}_{N}]}=\sup_{N\geq t_{1}}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda_{N}]}{\lambda\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda_{t_{J(N)}}]-J(N)C_{\lambda}}.

We remark that the minimum time
t_{1}
is necessary because the competitive ratio would otherwise be
\infty
at early time steps when we don’t have enough data to release anything. With this constraint, the supremum will always occur when
N=t_{J(n)+1}-1
for some
n>0
, in which case the expression simplifies to:

\sup_{n>0}\frac{n\mu}{t_{J(n)}\lambda\mu-J(n)C_{\lambda}}=\sup_{j>0}\frac{(t_{j+1}-1)\mu}{t_{j}\lambda\mu-jC_{\lambda}}.

To minimize the supremum, we would like to choose a batch schedule so that the worst-case relative error is equal to some constant
\rho>1
for all
j
. Asymptotically,
t_{j+1}-1\sim t_{j+1}
, and so we get the following recurrence:

\displaystyle t_{j+1}=\rho\left(\lambda t_{j}-\frac{jC_{\lambda}}{\mu}\right).

We will solve this recurrence using the method of undetermined coefficients. The homogeneous equation
t_{j+1}=\rho\lambda t_{j}
has solution
t_{j+1}=C(\rho\lambda)^{j+1}
, so we’ll guess that the solution to the equation has the form:

t_{j+1}=C(\rho\lambda)^{j+1}+\alpha j+\gamma.

Writing
\beta=\rho C_{\lambda}/\mu
, this then implies that:

\displaystyle\alpha j+\gamma

\displaystyle=\rho\lambda(\alpha(j-1)+\gamma)-\beta j

\displaystyle=(\rho\lambda\alpha-\beta)j+\rho\lambda\gamma-\rho\lambda\alpha,

and thus, equating coefficients, that:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\rho\lambda\alpha-\beta

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{\rho\lambda-1},

and

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\rho\lambda(\gamma-\alpha)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho\lambda\alpha}{\rho\lambda-1}=\frac{\rho\lambda\beta}{(\rho\lambda-1)^{2}}.

Let
R=\rho\lambda
. Then our candidate solution is:

t^{*}_{j+1}=CR^{j+1}+\frac{\beta j}{R-1}+\frac{R\beta}{(R-1)^{2}}.

Plugging this in to our original recurrence to verify, we get:

\displaystyle Rt^{*}_{j}-\beta j

\displaystyle=CR^{j+1}+\frac{R\beta(j-1)}{R-1}+\frac{R^{2}\beta}{(R-1)^{2}}-\beta j

\displaystyle=CR^{j+1}+\left(1+\frac{1}{R-1}\right)\beta(j-1)+\left(1+\frac{1}{R-1}\right)^{2}\beta-\beta j

\displaystyle=CR^{j+1}+\left(\frac{1}{R-1}\right)\beta j-\left(1+\frac{1}{R-1}\right)\beta+\left(1+\frac{1}{R-1}\right)^{2}\beta

\displaystyle=CR^{j+1}+\left(\frac{1}{R-1}\right)\beta j+\left(\frac{1}{R-1}+\frac{1}{(R-1)^{2}}\right)\beta

\displaystyle=CR^{j+1}+\left(\frac{1}{R-1}\right)\beta j+\frac{R\beta}{(R-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=t^{*}_{j+1},

confirming that our solution is valid. Expanding the variables we introduced and setting
C=1
for simplicity, this gives us:

\displaystyle t_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho\lambda+\frac{\rho^{2}\lambda\log(1/(1-c^{2}\lambda^{2}))}{\mu(\rho\lambda-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle t_{j}

\displaystyle=(\rho\lambda)^{j}+\frac{\rho\log(1/(1-c^{2}\lambda^{2}))(j-1)}{\mu(\rho\lambda-1)}+\frac{\rho^{2}\lambda\log(1/(1-c^{2}\lambda^{2}))}{\mu(\rho\lambda-1)^{2}}.

Returning finally to our original stopping time equation, we get that with this batching schedule and for any
N\geq t_{1}
,

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\widetilde{E}_{N}]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda_{N}]}{\rho}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[N]\mu}{\rho}.

Since
\lambda\in(0,\min(1,1/c))
and we require
\rho\lambda>1
for feasibility, this implies that we can achieve any competitive ratio
\rho\in(\max(1,c),\infty)
, but at the cost of a larger initial startup time. To understand this tradeoff, we examine the asymptotic behavior of
t_{1}
as
\rho\to c
for
c\geq 1
. Choosing the (potentially suboptimal) value
\lambda=1/\sqrt{\rho c}\in(1/\rho,1/c)
and letting
\eta=\sqrt{\rho/c}
, we get that:

t_{1}=\eta-\frac{\rho}{\mu}\left(\frac{\eta\log(1-\frac{1}{\eta^{2}})}{(\eta-1)^{2}}\right)=\eta-\frac{\rho}{\mu}\left(\frac{\eta(\log(\eta-1)+\log(\eta+1)-2\log(\eta))}{(\eta-1)^{2}}\right).

Now, letting
\eta=1+\delta
and taking the limit as
\delta\to 0^{+}
, we get:

1-\frac{\rho}{\mu}\left(\frac{\log(\delta)+\log(2)}{\delta^{2}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\rho\log(1/\delta)}{\mu\delta^{2}}\right)=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{c}{\mu(\eta-1)^{2}}\right),

where we used the fact that
\rho\to c
as
\eta\to 1
. Finally, since this upper-bound holds for our fixed choice of
\lambda
, it also holds for the optimal choice.
∎
