A Mean Field Games Perspective on Evolutionary Clustering
Alessio Basti, Fabio CamilliAdriano Festa

Abstract
We propose a control-theoretic framework for evolutionary clustering based on Mean Field Games (MFG). Moving beyond static or heuristic approaches, we formulate the problem as a population dynamics game governed by a coupled Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman and Fokker-Planck system. Driven by a variational cost functional rather than predefined statistical shapes, this continuous-time formulation provides a flexible basis for non-parametric cluster evolution. To validate the framework, we analyze the setting of time-dependent Gaussian mixtures, showing that the MFG dynamics recover the trajectories of the classical Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm while ensuring mass conservation. Furthermore, we introduce time-averaged log-likelihood functionals to regularize temporal fluctuations. Numerical experiments illustrate the stability of our approach and suggest a path toward more general non-parametric clustering applications where traditional EM methods may face limitations.

Introduction

Clustering lies at the heart of unsupervised learning, where the goal is to group data points into coherent subpopulations: points within the same group share high similarity, while those in different groups remain dissimilar. Classical approaches employ finite mixture models, flexible statistical representations that express data as weighted sums of distributions, most commonly Gaussian, and rely, for example, on the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm for parameter estimation. These methods enable soft clustering and probabilistic interpretations (see
[
3
,
4
]
). While these techniques are usually applied in static contexts, many modern applications, spanning from social network dynamics to real-time signal processing, require detecting clusters that evolve over time.

Although diverse discrete evolutionary clustering methods have been proposed, such as repeated EM and temporal regularization, they often lack a unifying theoretical framework in the continuous setting
[
2
,
8
,
11
,
22
]
. Drawing inspiration from recent efforts to reinterpret machine learning via differential geometry, dynamical systems, and optimal control
[
9
,
10
,
13
,
15
,
14
,
18
]
, we establish a common control-theoretic framework that recasts evolutionary clustering through the lens of Mean Field Games (MFG). MFG theory has emerged as a powerful framework to describe the collective behavior of large populations of interacting agents. Originally introduced independently by Jean-Michel Lasry and Pierre-Louis Lions
[
17
]
, and by Minyi Huang, Roland Malhamé and Peter E. Caines
[
16
]
, the theory provides a continuum description of strategic interactions when the number of agents tends to infinity. In this limit, individual decisions are encoded through a Hamilton–Jacobi equation, while the collective evolution of the population is described by a Fokker–Planck equation. Beyond its original applications in economics and control, MFG theory has recently found connections with machine learning, optimal transport, and large-scale optimization problems, where learning dynamics can be interpreted as gradient flows or mean-field limits of interacting particles
[
1
,
7
,
5
,
20
,
23
]
.

In this sense, evolutionary clustering fits naturally within this perspective: it can be viewed as a controlled mean-field evolution in which clusters emerge as stable population patterns driven by an optimality principle. Indeed, clustering can be interpreted as a collective organization process: data points adapt their state according to a cost that balances data fidelity and interaction with the overall distribution. The MFG formalism captures this interplay between microscopic optimization and macroscopic density evolution. Moreover, unlike classical EM-type methods that rely on fixed parametric families, the MFG framework is intrinsically non-parametric, since the unknowns are probability densities evolving in time under variational principles. Hence, a key contribution of this paper is to bridge the gap between heuristic discrete methods and rigorous population dynamics, thereby enabling new extensions in both parametric and non-parametric clustering frameworks.

The remainder of the paper is structured to detail these findings. In Section
2
, we introduce the general non-parametric MFG framework for multi-population dynamics. In Section
3
, we validate this framework by restricting our analysis to time-dependent Gaussian mixtures, providing a rigorous mathematical demonstration of its equivalence with the classic EM algorithm. Finally, we introduce time-averaged log-likelihood functionals to handle short-term fluctuations and, in Section
4
, present numerical experiments that confirm the validity of our approach in the Gaussian case.

2
Non-Parametric Mean Field Games Framework

We represent the dataset at time
t\in[0,T]
as a probability density
f(\cdot,t)
mapping
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
(0,\infty)
. The mixture
m
is composed of
K
components expressed as

m(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}(t)m_{k}(x,t),\qquad\alpha(t)=(\alpha_{1}(t),\dots,\alpha_{K}(t))\in\mathcal{T}_{K},

(2.1)

where
m_{k}
are the cluster densities and
\mathcal{T}_{K}
is the unit simplex. The weights
\alpha_{k}(t)
describe the proportion of the total population associated with the
k
-th component at time
t
.
We highlight that the reference density
f(x,t)
is assumed to be given and represents the observed data distribution at time
t
, while the mixture
m(x,t)
approximates
f
by evolving according to controlled dynamics.

By on Bayes’ theorem, we introduce the probabilities
\gamma_{k}
(also known as
responsibilities
) that each data point belongs to a given latent component
k
of the mixture, indicating how much each component contributes to generating that observation, i.e.

\gamma_{k}(x,t)=\dfrac{\alpha_{k}(t)m_{k}(x,t)}{m(x,t)},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,t\in[0,T],\,k=1,\dots,K.

(2.2)

These responsibilities can be used to divide the dataset into clusters by setting:

C_{k}(t)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;\middle|\;\gamma_{k}(x,t)=\max_{j=1,\dots,K}\gamma_{j}(x,t)\right\}.

To compute the densities
m_{k}
and the weights
\alpha_{k}
, we consider a
quasi-stationary
MFG. In contrast to standard MFGs, where agents anticipate the future evolution of the population, the quasi-stationary setting assumes agents react only to the current population state. This makes the model particularly suitable for online or slowly varying data (see
[
19
]
).
In this setting, each data point is viewed as a controlled stochastic agent. The state
X_{t}
of an agent in the
k
-th cluster evolves according to:

dX_{t}=a(X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,dW_{t},\quad X_{0}=x,

(2.3)

where
a
is the control,
\varepsilon>0
is the diffusion coefficient, and
W_{t}
denotes a standard Wiener process. We assume that, at each time instant
t
, the agent belonging to the
k
-th population optimizes a cost functional
J_{k}(x,a;t)
using the information available at the present time, where

J_{k}(x,a;t)=\lim_{S\to+\infty}\frac{1}{S}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{t}^{S}\left(L(\tilde{X}_{s},a(\tilde{X}_{s}))+F_{k}(\tilde{X}_{s},m(\cdot,t))\right)ds\right],

(2.4)

where
L
is a Lagrangian cost, and
F_{k}
is the interaction term coupling the agent’s state with the distribution of the population,
\tilde{X}
refers to a (fictitious) dynamics of type (
2.3
) starting at time
t
. The optimal control
a
obtained by optimizing this functional is then incorporated into dynamics (
2.3
)
[
19
]
. Note that, in (
2.4
), the distribution
m
is frozen at the current time
t
. Therefore, agents do not anticipate its future evolution, but instead optimize their configuration based solely on the information available at that time.

To bridge the microscopic dynamics with the macroscopic description, we introduce the Hamiltonian
H
via the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian:

H(x,p)=\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{-p\cdot a-L(x,a)\right\}.

Under the quasi-stationary assumption, agents solve a stationary optimization problem at each time
t
, while the densities
m_{k}
evolve according to the optimal drift
-\partial_{p}H(x,Du_{k})
, where the notation
\partial_{p}H
denotes the derivative of
H
with respect to the momentum
p
. The resulting coupled system is given by:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\varepsilon\Delta u_{k}(x,t)+H\big(x,Du_{k}(x,t)\big)+\lambda_{k}(t)=F_{k}\big(x,m(\cdot,t)\big),\\[6.0pt]
\partial_{t}m_{k}(x,t)-\varepsilon\Delta m_{k}(x,t)-\operatorname{div}\!\big(m_{k}(x,t)\,\partial_{p}H(x,Du_{k}(x,t))\big)=0,\\[6.0pt]
\alpha_{k}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,t)\,f(x,t)\,dx,\\[6.0pt]
m_{k}(x,0)=m_{k,0}(x),\qquad\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{k}(x,t)=0,\end{array}\right\}_{\forall\,k=1,\dots,K}

(2.5)

where
u_{k}
is the ergodic value function, and
\lambda_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the ergodic constant. It is worth noticing that the responsibilities
\gamma_{k}
couple the sub-populations to the empirical data density
f
and to the full mixture
m
.

The coupling
F_{k}
determines the qualitative behavior of the clustering dynamics and can encode, for example, entropy regularization, data fidelity of the mixture
m
to the observed data
f
, and repulsion/attraction between components. A meaningful information-theoretic choice is a convex combination of an entropy penalization and a Kullback-Leibler-type fidelity term:

F_{k}(x,m)=\underbrace{\eta_{k}\log\big(m_{k}(x,t)\big)}_{\text{Entropic penalization}}+\underbrace{(1-\eta_{k})\alpha_{k}(t)\log\left(\frac{m(x,t)}{f(x,t)}\right)}_{\text{Data fidelity}},\quad\eta_{k}\in[0,1].

Specifically, the terms in
F_{k}
correspond to the first variational derivative with respect to
m_{k}
of the negative Shannon entropy
\mathcal{H}(m_{k})=\int m_{k}\log(m_{k})dx
and the Kullback-Leibler divergence
\text{KL}(m\|f)=\int m\log(m/f)dx
. These derivatives are considered up to additive constants, which are absorbed by the ergodic constant
\lambda_{k}(t)
in the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation of system (
2.5
).

Remark 2.1
.

We do not address the existence of solutions to the non-
linear system (
2.5
), as our focus is primarily on
computational aspects. Nevertheless, such a result could, in principle, be
established via a fixed-point argument (cf.
[
19
]
).

3
Fokker–Planck Equation and the Gaussian Case

To bridge our MFG perspective with the standard Gaussian EM algorithm, we consider a specialized quadratic coupling term that acts as a dynamic attractor:

F_{k}(x,m)=\frac{1}{2}(x-M_{k}(t))^{\top}V_{k}^{2}(t)(x-M_{k}(t)),

for a suitable positive-definite symmetric matrix
V_{k}(t)
. For the quadratic Hamiltonian
H(x,p)=\tfrac{1}{2}|p|^{2}
, the ansatz

u_{k}(x,t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,(x-M_{k}(t))^{\top}V_{k}(t)\,(x-M_{k}(t))

(3.1)

gives the linear drift

B_{k}[m](x,t):=\partial_{p}H(x,Du_{k})=Du_{k}=V_{k}(t)\big(x-M_{k}(t)\big).

(3.2)

With this drift, the second equation in (
2.5
) becomes a Fokker–Planck (FP) equation with affine drift. The MFG approach thus gives rise to the following system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}m_{k}(x,t)-\varepsilon\Delta m_{k}(x,t)-{\rm div}(m_{k}(x,t)B_{k}[m](x,t))=0,\\[8.0pt]
\alpha_{k}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,t)f(x,t)dx,\\[8.0pt]
m_{k}(x,0)=m_{k,0}(x)\end{array}\right\}_{\forall\,k=1,\dots,K}

(3.3)

for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
. For mathematical consistency, we assume finite first and second moments:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|\,f(x,t)\,dx\leq C_{f},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\,f(x,t)\,dx\leq C_{f},

for a constant
C_{f}>0
. In the following proposition, we prove that a solution of (
3.3
) at time
t
,
m_{k}(\cdot,t)
, is a Gaussian density
{\cal N}(x|\nu_{k}(t),T_{k}(t))
explicating the dependence of the parameter on
M_{k}
,
V_{k}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
m
in (
2.1
) be a mixture with
(m_{k},\alpha_{k})
,
k=1,\dots,K
, given by the system (
3.3
). Then, for each time
t
,
m(\cdot,t)
is a Gaussian mixture with

m_{k}(x,t)=c_{k}(t)\,\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}(x-\nu_{k}(t))^{\top}\,T_{k}(t)^{-1}(x-\nu_{k}(t))\Bigr)

where

\begin{cases}\nu^{\prime}_{k}(t)=-V_{k}(t)\Bigl(\nu_{k}(t)-M_{k}(t)\Bigr),\\[2.84526pt]
{T^{\prime}_{k}(t)=2\varepsilon\,I-T_{k}(t)\,V_{k}(t)-V_{k}(t)\,T_{k}(t)},\\[2.84526pt]
c_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}\det(T_{k}(t))}}.\end{cases}

The proof of the previous proposition is postponed to the appendix.

We define

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(t)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\gamma_{k}(x,t)f(x,t)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,t)f(x,t)\,dx}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad

\displaystyle t\in[0,T],

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}(t)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x-\mu_{k}(t))(x-\mu_{k}(t))^{\top}\gamma_{k}(x,t)f(x,t)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,t)f(x,t)\,dx}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+},

\displaystyle t\in[0,T],

(3.5)

\displaystyle m_{k,0}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_{k}(0),\Sigma_{k}(0)).

(3.6)

Here,
\mu_{k}(t)
and
\Sigma_{k}(t)
are the mean and covariance of the
k
-th component at time
t
, computed as weighted averages of the reference density
f(x,t)
.
In other words,
\mu_{k}(t)
is the expected value of
x
if we focus only on component
k
, and
\Sigma_{k}(t)
measures how spread out the data is around this mean.
The choice of
m_{k}(x,0)
reflects the assumption that the initial distribution
m(0)
is itself a Gaussian mixture approximating the dataset
f(x,0)
.

In the models that follow, the vector
M_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and positive definite symmetric matrix
V_{k}(t)
in (
3.2
) depend on
\gamma_{k}(t)
,
\mu_{k}(t)
, and
\Sigma_{k}(t)
, and therefore on the overall density
m
. They are chosen to ensure that the mixture
m
in (
2.1
) remains a Gaussian mixture that maximizes a log-likelihood functional based on the data.
In the next subsections, we present two models that aim to maximize the log-likelihood while keeping each cluster stable and well-defined over time.

3.1
1^{st}
model: maximizing the instantaneous log-likelihood

In this section, we consider a model that at each time maximizes the Gaussian log-likelihood functional

{\cal L}(\{\beta_{k},\rho_{k},\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\,\big|\,f(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}(x,t)\ln\left(\beta_{k}{\cal N}(x|\rho_{k},\Delta_{k})\right)f(x,t)\,dx,

(3.7)

with respect to the parameters

\beta_{k}>0
,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}=1
,

\rho_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Delta_{k}
(as a positive-definite symmetric matrix), where the responsibilities
\gamma_{k}(x,t)
are given. The previous functional measures how well the mixture model explains the observed data and it is suitable for the application of EM algorithm (cfr.
[
3
]
).

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
t\in[0,T]
, the unique maximizer of
{\cal L}
in (
3.7
) is given by

\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k}(t)
,
\rho_{k}=\mu_{k}(t)
and
\Delta_{k}=\Sigma_{k}(t)
, where
\alpha_{k}
,
\mu_{k}
and
\Sigma_{k}
are defined as in (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).

The proof of the previous proposition is classical and can be found in
[
3
]
since the time plays no role.
In the next proposition we show that, for appropriately chosen
M_{k}(t)
,
V_{k}(t)
, the parameters of the mixture components
m_{k}
of
m
coincide at each time with the maximizer of the functional
{\cal L}
in (
3.7
) (for the proof, see the appendix).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(m_{k},\alpha_{k})
,
k=1,\dots,K
, be a solution of the system (
3.3
) with
M_{k}
,
V_{k}
given by

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}M_{k}(t)=\mu_{k}(t)+V^{-1}_{k}(t)\mu^{\prime}_{k}(t)\\[4.0pt]
V_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}(t)(2\varepsilon I-\Sigma_{k}(t)^{\prime})\end{array}\end{cases}

(3.8)

and assume
[\Sigma_{k}(t),\Sigma_{k}^{\prime}(t)]=0
for all
t
, where the brackets denote the commutator. Then, for
k=1,\dots,K
,

\nu_{k}(t)=\mu_{k}(t),\quad T_{k}(t)=\Sigma_{k}(t)\quad\text{for any $t\in[0,T]$}.

(3.9)

Moreover

\mathbb{E}_{m(\cdot,t)}[X]=\mathbb{E}_{f(\cdot,t)}[X],\quad\operatorname{Cov}_{m(\cdot,t)}[X]=\operatorname{Cov}_{f(\cdot,t)}[X]\quad\text{for any $t\in[0,T]$.}

Remark 3.4
.

The commutativity assumption
[\Sigma_{k}(t),\Sigma_{k}^{\prime}(t)]=0
is only required when defining the second equation of (
3.8
) and it is sufficiently mild since it is automatically satisfied when covariance matrices are diagonal or simultaneously diagonalizable.
If not verified, one may define
V_{k}(t)
directly as the solution of the Sylvester equation

\Sigma_{k}(t)V_{k}(t)+V_{k}(t)\Sigma_{k}(t)=2\varepsilon I-\Sigma_{k}^{\prime}(t).

Remark 3.5
.

The hyperparameter
\varepsilon
serves to regularize the term appearing in the expression for
V_{k}
, thereby guaranteeing the existence of its inverse, which is exploited in the first equation of (
3.8
). Although
\varepsilon
is instrumental in ensuring well-posedness, it does not alter the solutions: all dependencies on
\varepsilon
cancel out algebraically, leaving both the mean and covariance of each
m_{k}
independent of its value.

3.2
2^{nd}
model: maximizing a time average of the log-likelihood

The objective of this section is no longer to select the model parameters that maximize the instantaneous log-likelihood functional. Instead, we introduce a drift term that enables the mixture weights, means, and covariances to maximize a time-averaged log-likelihood over a finite window. This approach smooths out short-term fluctuations in the observations. Specifically, we proceed following two different principles:

•

(Asymmetric regularization) We assign weights to past data using a kernel function
g
, which performs a convolution over the interval
[t-\tau,t]
for a fixed
\tau>0
. The kernel
g:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

g(\tau)=0,\quad g(s)\geq 0\quad\forall\,s\in(0,\tau],\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(s)\,ds=1.

(3.10)

As an explicit example of
g
, we can consider
the following exponential kernel that assigns more weight to recent observations (asymmetric)

g(s)=\frac{e^{-s}-e^{-\tau}}{1-e^{-\tau}(1+\tau)}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\tau]}(s)

(3.11)

•

(Symmetric regularization) We use a standard symmetric mollifier giving the same relevance to the past and the future choosing
g
as

g(s):=\frac{2}{\tau C}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{1-(2s/\tau)^{2}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{[-\tau/2,\tau/2]}(s),\hbox{ with }C=\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-\frac{1}{1-s^{2}}}ds.

(3.12)

in this case we have that

g(-\tau/2)=g(\tau/2)=0,\quad g(s)\geq 0\quad\forall\,s\in(-\tau/2,\tau/2],\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(s)\,ds=1.

Given
g
, we denote by
*
the standard convolution in
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

g*m=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}m(s)g(t-s)ds.

When applied to vector- or matrix-valued quantities, convolution is understood component-wise.
The aim is to find a Gaussian mixture
m(t)
given by the solution of (
3.3
) which maximizes the convolution of the log-likelihood functional (
3.7
) with the kernel
g
, i.e.

\begin{split}&{\cal L}_{g}(\{\beta_{k},\rho_{k},\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\,\big|\,f):=\bigl(g*{\cal L}\bigr)(t)=\\
&\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)\ln\bigl[\beta_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\rho_{k},\Delta_{k})\bigr]\,f(x,s)\,dx\,ds.\end{split}

(3.13)

Note that, when the kernel
g
coincides with the Dirac delta function
\delta_{0}
, then
{\cal L}_{g}
coincides with the functional
{\cal L}
in (
3.7
). To define the convolution of the log-likelihood functional, we extend the dataset by setting
f(x,s)=f(x,0)
for
s\in[-\tau,0]
, and apply the same extension to the initial condition of the FP equation.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any
t\in[\tau,T]
, the unique maximizers of
{\cal L}_{g}
in (
3.13
) with respect to
\beta_{k}
and
\rho_{k}
are:

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=\bigl(g*\alpha_{k}\bigr)(t)=:\tilde{\alpha}_{k}(t),

(3.14)

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bigl(g*(\mu_{k}\alpha_{k})\bigr)(t)}{\bigl(g*\alpha_{k}\bigr)(t)}=:\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t).

(3.15)

Finally, an approximate maximizer for the covariance
\Delta_{k}
is:

\Delta_{k}\approx\frac{\bigl(g*(\alpha_{k}\Sigma_{k})\bigr)(t)}{\bigl(g*\alpha_{k}\bigr)(t)}=:\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}(t).

(3.16)

The proof can be found in the appendix.

Remark 3.7
.

The above maximization assumes a timescale separation where the mixture parameters
\{\beta_{k},\rho_{k},\Delta_{k}\}
evolve much slower than the window length, allowing them to be treated as locally frozen. Consequently, while the exact algebraic maximizer for
\Delta_{k}
coincides with (
A.3
), we can deliberately omit the higher-order contribution and obtain (
3.16
). This approximation preserves the analytical tractability of the optimal control updates while fully retaining the intended temporal smoothing properties of the model.

In the next proposition, we show that with an appropriate choice of the drift terms
M_{k}
and
V_{k}
in (
3.3
), the parameters of
m_{k}
satisfy equations (
3.15
) and (
3.16
). This ensures that the resulting mixture
m
has an expected value and covariance matrix equal to the weighted averages of those of
f
over the interval
[t-\tau,t]
(asymmetric choice) or
[-\tau/2,\tau/2]
(symmetric).

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
(m_{k},\alpha_{k})
,
k=1,\dots,K
, be a solution of the system (
3.3
) with
M_{k}
,
V_{k}
given by

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}M_{k}(t)=\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t)+V^{-1}_{k}(t)\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{k}(t)\\[4.0pt]
V_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(t)(2\varepsilon I-\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}(t)^{\prime}),\end{array}\end{cases}

and assume
[\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}(t),\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}^{\prime}(t)]=0
for all
t
. Then, for
k=1,\dots,K
and for any
t\in[0,T]
, the mean
\nu_{k}
and the covariance
T_{k}
of
m_{k}
are equal to the maximizers described by Proposition
3.6
, i.e.

\nu_{k}(t)=\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t),\quad T_{k}(t)=\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}(t).

Moreover, relying on the
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}
coefficients of the mixture defined in (
3.16
), for any
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m(\cdot,t)}[X]

\displaystyle=\bigl(g*\mathbb{E}_{f}[X]\bigr)(t),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}_{m(\cdot,t)}[X]

\displaystyle=\bigl(g*\mathbb{E}_{f}[XX^{\top}]\bigr)(t)-\bigl(g*\mathbb{E}_{f}[X]\bigr)(t)\bigl(g*\mathbb{E}_{f}[X]\bigr)(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{\alpha}_{k}(t)\,\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t)\,\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t)^{\top}-\Bigl(g*\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mu_{k}\mu_{k}^{\top}\Bigr)(t).

The proof of the proposition can be found in the appendix.

Remark 3.9
.

The first model (subsection
3.1
) is highly sensitive to noise as it relies on the numerical differentiation of the parameters
\mu_{k}
and
\Sigma_{k}
. In contrast, the second model (subsection
3.2
) enhances robustness by exploiting the regularizing effect of convolution. Furthermore, from an application standpoint of the second model, the asymmetric kernel
3.11
is causal, making it suitable for real-time analysis based solely on past observations; the symmetric kernel
3.12
, however, is non-causal and intended for offline post-processing and smoothing tasks.

4
Numerical approximation and examples

In this section, we discuss the numerical implementation for solving the clustering problem based on an iterative approximation of the nonlinear FP formulation (
3.3
). The central goal is to recover the finite mixture model described in (
2.1
), along with the associated responsibility functions
\gamma_{k}(x,t)
from which the clusters are obtained.

The method we propose is inspired by the EM algorithm, but adapted to the structure of the PDE system. Instead of tackling the full problem (
3.3
) simultaneously for all variables
(m_{k},\alpha_{k})
, we decouple the system and solve a sequence of
K
independent problems iteratively. A key advantage of our formulation is that all inter-component dependencies are captured within the E-step. As a result, the M-step reduces to solving
K
separate PDEs, which can be computed in parallel, leading to significant gains in efficiency and scalability. In the first two approaches presented (instantaneous log-likelihood and asymmetric time average) it is possible to alternate the E-step and M-step at each discrete time step, generating a sequential procedure in (discrete) time, as sketched in Algorithm
1
. This is not possible in the last case (symmetric time average) where the dependency between the E and M-step requires to look for an equilibrium
globally in time
. This can be found through a fixed point iteration.

4.1
Computational approach and discretization

In this section we describe in detail the numerical strategy used to approximate the coupled estimation–evolution problem. The algorithm follows an EM-type iteration, where at each step the latent variables are updated (E-step) and the density evolves according to a FP equation (M-step). The complete procedure is outlined below and reported in the sequential scheme of Algorithm
1
.

0.

Discretization.

The time domain
[0,T]
is discretized uniformly with time step
\Delta t
, i.e.
\{t_{n}:=\Delta t\,n;\;n=0,1,...,N_{T}\}
with
T\in[t_{N_{T}},t_{N_{T}+1})
. The spatial domain is discretized uniformly in cartesian cells of measure
h
(i.e.
\sqrt[d]{h}
in each direction) and we call the barycenter of each cell
x_{i}
with
i=1,...,M
, where the data density
f
is going to be computed.

1.

Initialization via soft clustering.

The computation begins with the initialization of both the probability density
m^{0}
and the parameter vector
\alpha^{0}
. Following

[
1
]
, we adopt a
static soft clustering
methodology derived from a MFG formulation. More precisely:

–

The initial parameter vector
\alpha^{0}_{k}
is computed as the weighted average (and covariance) of the data with respect to the soft weights.

–

The discrete parameters
\mu_{k}^{0}
and
\Sigma_{k}^{0}
are computed w.r.t. the values of
\alpha^{0}_{k}
.

–

The Gaussian mixtures are placed accordingly to
\mu_{k}^{0}
and
\Sigma_{k}^{0}
, i.e.
m_{k}^{0}
is a discretization of
{\cal N}(x|\mu_{k}^{0},\Sigma_{k}^{0})
.

These steps are iterated until convergence. This initialization ensures coherence between the discrete representation of the data and the continuous density used in the FP dynamics, while avoiding unstable or degenerate starting profiles.

2.

E-step: Numerical evaluation of the latent parameters.

Given the values at the previous step, the E-step updates the responsibilities and the statistical parameters. This requires computing:

\gamma^{n}_{k}(x_{i}),\quad\alpha^{n}_{k},\quad\mu^{n}_{k},\quad\Sigma^{n}_{k},

which represent mixing proportions, cluster centers, and covariance matrices (as defined in (
2.2
)-(
3.5
)). These integrals are approximated numerically using a first-order multidimensional quadrature formula on the uniform grid, as explicitly displayed in Algorithm
1
. This step provides the necessary quantities to assemble the updated drift field
B_{k}^{n}(x)
for the subsequent M-step.

3.

M-step: Discretization of the Fokker–Planck equation.

The density update is governed by the FP equation:

\partial_{t}m_{k}=\operatorname{div}\big(B_{k}(x,t)m_{k}\big)+\varepsilon\Delta m_{k}.

The numerical approximation is carried out using an explicit semi-Lagrangian scheme, which is well-suited for drift-diffusion equations. After spatial and temporal discretization, the FP update can be written in algebraic form as:

m_{k}^{n}=A[B_{k}^{n}]m_{k}^{n-1},

where
m_{k}^{n}
is the vector approximating
m_{k}(\cdot,t^{n})
, and
A[B_{k}^{n}]
is the discrete evolution operator that accounts for both the advection driven by the drift
B_{k}^{n}
and the diffusion scaled by
\varepsilon
(for details on this specific scheme, see
[
6
]
).

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced a mean field game framework for evolutionary clustering, aimed at describing the time-dependent identification of mixture distributions in a continuous setting. Starting from a variational formulation based on the log-likelihood functional, we derived a coupled system of Fokker–Planck and Hamilton–Jacobi equations governing the evolution of the cluster densities. This approach provides a continuous-time and control-theoretic interpretation of evolutionary clustering, while preserving the probabilistic structure of the mixture throughout the dynamics.

Within this general framework, we then focused on the Gaussian case, showing that the resulting evolution recovers, in continuous time, the trajectories associated with the classical Expectation–Maximization algorithm. This correspondence gives a rigorous link between the proposed mean field game formulation and a standard clustering methodology, and offers a new perspective on dynamic mixture reconstruction.

We further considered temporally regularized variants of the model, based on time-averaged log-likelihood functionals with asymmetric and symmetric kernels. These extensions were introduced to improve temporal coherence and to reduce abrupt variations in the reconstructed trajectories. The numerical experiments in one and two space dimensions show that all the proposed models are able to detect the main cluster features with good accuracy, while the regularized formulations provide smoother evolutions and, in several cases, a more favorable balance between accuracy and stability.

The proposed framework is formulated in a way that is not restricted, at the modeling level, to Gaussian mixtures. Although the analytical and numerical developments presented here are carried out in the Gaussian setting, the underlying mean field game structure suggests possible extensions to more general classes of evolving distributions. Future work will be devoted to these non-Gaussian and genuinely non-parametric settings, as well as to a deeper analytical study of the associated coupled systems and to the development of efficient numerical methods for higher-dimensional applications.

∎

Proof of Prop.
3.2
and
3.6
.

We work with a general kernel
g
to prove
3.6
. The aim is to find the components of the mixture that maximize

{\cal L}_{g}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)\ln\bigl[\beta_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\rho_{k},\Delta_{k})\bigr]\,f(x,s)\,dx\,ds.

We write explicitly

\ln\bigl[\beta_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\rho_{k},\Delta_{k})\bigr]=\ln\beta_{k}-\tfrac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\det\Delta_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\rho_{k})^{T}\Delta_{k}^{-1}(x-\rho_{k}),

and introduce multiplier
\lambda
for
\sum_{k}\beta_{k}=1
:

L={\cal L}_{g}+\lambda\bigl(1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}\bigr),

we have three different optimization steps.

Let us optimize w.r.t.
\beta_{k}
. Differentiating:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_{k}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}f(x,s)\,dx\,ds-\lambda=0

\Longrightarrow\;\beta_{k}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dx\,ds.

Imposing
\sum_{k}\beta_{k}=1
gives

\lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{j}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dx\,ds\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\Biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,s)dx\Biggr)ds=1,

so

\beta_{k}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dx\,ds.

Only the quadratic term depends on
\rho_{k}
. We have

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\rho_{k}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)(x-\rho_{k})^{\top}\Delta_{k}^{-1}(x-\rho_{k})\,f(x,s)\,dx\,ds=

=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)\Delta_{k}^{-1}(x-\rho_{k})f(x,s)\,dx\,ds=0\;

Hence

\rho_{k}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\gamma_{k}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dxds}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dx\,ds}.

Finally, w.r.t.
\Delta_{k}
.
Using matrix derivatives (see
[
21
]
, Eq. 57)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta_{k}}\ln\det\Delta_{k}=\Delta_{k}^{-1},

\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta_{k}}(x-\rho_{k})^{\top}\Delta_{k}^{-1}(x-\rho_{k})=-\Delta_{k}^{-1}(x-\rho_{k})(x-\rho_{k})^{\top}\Delta_{k}^{-1}\quad\textrm{(see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{MatrixCookbook}{}{}]}, Eq. 61)}

we have

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Delta_{k}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)\bigl[\Delta_{k}^{-1}-\Delta_{k}^{-1}(x-\rho_{k})(x-\rho_{k})^{\top}\Delta_{k}^{-1}\bigr]f(x,s)\,dxds=0.

By multiplying on left and right by
\Delta_{k}
,

0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)\bigl[(x-\rho_{k})(x-\rho_{k})^{\top}-\Delta_{k}\bigr]f(x,s)\,dx\,ds\;

so, by adding and subtracting
\mu_{k}(s)
inside the inner integral, we obtain

\begin{split}\Delta_{k}&=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x-\rho_{k})(x-\rho_{k})^{\top}\gamma_{k}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dxds}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{k}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dx\,ds}\\[8.0pt]
&=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\alpha_{k}(s)\Bigl[\Sigma_{k}(s)+\bigl(\mu_{k}(s)-\rho_{k}\bigr)\bigl(\mu_{k}(s)-\rho_{k}\bigr)^{\top}\Bigr]ds}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t-s)\alpha_{k}(s)\,ds}\\[8.0pt]
&=\frac{\bigl(g*(\alpha_{k}\Sigma_{k})\bigr)(t)}{\bigl(g*\alpha_{k}\bigr)(t)}+\frac{\Bigl(g*\bigl(\alpha_{k}(\mu_{k}-\rho_{k})(\mu_{k}-\rho_{k})^{\top}\bigr)\Bigr)(t)}{\bigl(g*\alpha_{k}\bigr)(t)}.\end{split}

(A.3)

As discussed in the Remark
3.7
, the second term in the equation (
A.3
) represents the inter-temporal variability of the centroids. By neglecting this higher-order term under the timescale separation assumption, we obtain the simplified update
\Delta_{k}=\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}(t)
defined in (
3.16
). Since each blockwise optimization is concave, these give the unique maximizers of
{\cal L}_{g}
(up to the aforementioned approximation for
\Delta_{k}
).
∎
