Error bounds for simultaneous Wasserstein contractive adaptive increasingly rare MCMC
Julian Hofstadler, Daniel Rudolf

Abstract
We investigate adaptive increasingly rare Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms and the associated time-average estimator for approximating expectations. Under a simultaneous Wasserstein contraction assumption on the underlying family of Markov kernels we derive explicit bounds for the mean squared error. We illustrate the applicability of our estimate through adaptive stereographic algorithms and Metropolis-Hastings schemes that employ normalizing flows for adaptation. We also consider a generic adaptive algorithm for doubly intractable problems and provide a corresponding cost analysis to achieve a desired precision.



Keywords:
adaptive increasingly rare MCMC; Wasserstein contraction; doubly intractalbe distributions.

Introduction

In statistics and numerical analysis a challenging task is the computation of integrals of the form

\pi(h):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\,\pi({\rm d}x),

(1)

where
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
is a Borel space and
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
a measurable
\pi
-integrable function.
Here
\pi
may be a partially known probability measure with a density that contains an unknown normalization factor as it commonly appears in Bayesian statistics for posterior distributions. Additionally, we either impose or have a natural dependence of
\pi
to an additional characterizing quantity
z^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}
, with
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}})
being an
auxiliary measurable space. This can or should be beneficially used to address the integration problem. We provide two examples to motivate the latter point of view:

Example 1.1

(Normalizing flows)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\mathcal{Z}
be a parametrized set of
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms, i.e., for any
u\in\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
we have a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism
z_{u}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
in
\mathcal{Z}
.
Choose an auxiliary probability measure
\widetilde{\pi}
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
such that realizations of
\widetilde{X}\sim\widetilde{\pi}
can be drawn. Consider
z_{u}(\widetilde{X})\sim\pi_{z_{u}}:=\widetilde{\pi}\circ z_{u}^{-1}
for
z_{u}\in\mathcal{Z}
, where
\widetilde{\pi}\circ z_{u}^{-1}
denotes the pushforward measure of
\widetilde{\pi}
by
z_{u}
.
The idea is to take advantage of the ‘best’ proxy of
\pi
through
\pi_{z_{u}}
s. To this end, define the Kullback-Leibler divergence

\KL(\pi,\pi_{z}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{{\rm d}\pi}{{\rm d}\pi_{z}}(x)\right)\pi({\rm{\rm d}}x),

where
\frac{{\rm d}\pi}{{\rm d}{\pi}_{z}}
denotes the density of
\pi
with respect to (w.r.t.)
\pi_{z}
. Then, let

z^{*}:=\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\KL(\pi,{\pi}_{z})

be well defined (that is, there exists a unique minimizer). Then, we may use
{\pi}_{z^{*}}
as proxy for
\pi
to construct sampling schemes. However, usually we won’t have access to
z^{*}
, such that it needs to be approximated, cf.
[
GRVE22
]
.

Example 1.2

(Doubly-intractable distributions)
.

Let
\bar{y}\in\mathcal{D}
be some measured data, with
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{D}},\mu)
being a probability space.
Assume
\nu
is a reference probability measure on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
serving as prior distribution.
Consider
E\colon\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as an energy function that induces for
x\in\mathcal{X}
a likelihood mapping
y\mapsto\ell(y|x)
of an energy based model as

\ell(y|x)=\frac{\exp(-E(y,x))}{z^{*}(x)},

with
z^{*}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\exp(-E(y,x))\,\mu({\rm d}y)
. Here
z^{*}\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
is interpreted as non-evaluable (partition) function. Now the distribution of interest, denoted by
\pi=\pi_{z^{*}}
, takes the form

\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)=\frac{\ell(\bar{y}|x)\nu({\rm d}x)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell(\bar{y}|\widetilde{x})\nu({\rm d}\widetilde{x})}=\frac{\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))}{z^{*}(x)\,C_{z^{*}}}\nu({\rm d}x),

with normalizing constant
C_{z^{*}}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))\,z^{*}(x)^{-1}\nu({\rm d}x)
. Here
C_{z^{*}}
is unknown and evaluating the function
z^{*}
infeasible, which renders the sampling problem w.r.t.
\pi_{z^{*}}
doubly-intractable, compare to
[
MGM12
]
.
If
\mathcal{Z}
is a set of mappings
z\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)

we may approximate
z^{*}
by
\widehat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
aiming for
\pi_{\widehat{z}}
, where
\widehat{z}
just substitutes
z^{*}
, that is ‘close’ to
\pi_{z^{*}}
.

These examples showcase two different conceptional scenarios. In Example
1.1
the dependence of
\pi
on
z^{*}
is imposed and we typically have
\pi_{z^{*}}\not=\pi
. In contrast to that a natural dependence of
\pi
on
z^{*}
is present in Example
1.2
and we have
\pi_{z^{*}}=\pi
.
In both settings it is reasonable to (try to) exploit this underlying structure in the corresponding sampling problem.

The entire idea is not new and fits well in the classical framework of
adaptive Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). For example, for adaptive Metropolis, cf.
[
HST01
]
, on the fly a parameter
z^{*}
, corresponding to the covariance matrix of
\pi
, is learned during simulation. In each iteration a refined proxy of
z^{*}
is used to change the proposal in the Metropolis scheme improving the speed of convergence. After this, numerous extensions of adaptations in a Metropolis-Hastings framework, e.g. in
[
AM06
,
SV10
,
FMP11
]
, and other Markov chain schemes, e.g. in
[
RR09
]
, have been studied.
Also different adaptation strategies, for example via preconditioning are feasible, we refer to
[
HMN26
]
for recent developments.

We also consider adaptive MCMC, but add restrictions on the schedule of adaptation times where a refined proxy of
z^{*}
is used to change the transition mechanisms.
We follow the adpatively increasingly rare (AIR)
MCMC approach, coined and introduced in
[
CŁR18
]
where
adaptation, as the name suggests, get less frequent over time. The corresponding AIR process

In this work we are primarily concerned with theoretical guarantees of AIR algorithms. Namely, we consider the time-average estimator

Furthermore, we apply our results to the following examples,

1)

For all examples we are able to deduce explicit error bounds for the mean squared error of

Let us comment on how our results fit into the existing literature. AIR algorithms were introduced and studied in

Our analysis is based on a martingale decomposition technique that relies on solutions of Poisson’s equation. This is a standard tool in the adaptive MCMC literature, see e.g.
[

It is worth mentioning that mean squared error bounds are well studied for classical, i.e. non-adaptive, MCMC methods, we refer to
[

Rud12
,

,
Hof25

[

uses a Wasserstein contraction assumption, equivalently stated in terms of Ricci curvature there, to deduce such bounds. Our error estimates can also be considered as generalization of their framework to the adaptive setting.

The doubly intractable setting is investigated in Section

2.1
Adaptive increasingly rare MCMC

The goal of the present paper is to study sequences
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z})
-valued random variables corresponding to AIR MCMC schemes.
For providing an algorithmic description that determines
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in terms of an auxiliary
\mathcal{Z}
-valued sequence of random variables
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
we require

•

a probability measure
p_{0}
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
that serves as initial distribution, i.e.,
p_{0}(A)=\mathbb{P}(X_{0}\in A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
;

•

a transition kernel
Q_{0}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}\to[0,1]
which specifies for
B\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
that

Q_{0}(x,B)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}\in B\mid X_{0}=x);

(2)

•

a transition kernel
K\colon(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to[0,1]
which specifies for any
(z,x)\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
that

K(z,x,A)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A\mid X_{n}=x,Z_{n}=z);

(3)

•

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n={j(j+1)}/{2}
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
a transition kernel

Q_{n}\colon(\mathcal{X}^{n+1}\times\mathcal{Z}^{n})\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}\to[0,1]

which specifies for any
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{z})\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}\times\mathcal{Z}^{n}

and
B\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}
that

Q_{n}(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{z},B)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in B\mid(X_{0},\dots,X_{n})=\widetilde{x},(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n-1})=\widetilde{z}).

Note that, the conditional distribution of
Z_{0}
given
X_{0}
is specified in (
2
) and the conditional distribution of
X_{1}
given

in (

).
Now we are able to provide the transition mechanism, see Algorithm
1

and

Algorithm 1

For

given

If

More generally, in

2.2
Wasserstein distance and contraction

Let
\rho\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
be a lower semi-continuous (w.r.t. the product topology on
\mathcal{X}
) metric. For probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
define

W(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf_{\xi\in C(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\xi({\rm d}x_{1},{\rm d}x_{2}),

where
C(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is the set of couplings of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
. We refer to it as
Wasserstein distance
.
By the well-known Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality formula, cf.
[
Vil09
]
, we have the following identity

W(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\sup_{\|h\|_{\rho}\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\,\mu_{1}({\rm d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\,\mu_{2}({\rm d}x)\right|,

(4)

where
\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}\,;\,x\neq y}\frac{|h(x)-h(y)|}{\rho(x,y)}
is the Lipschitz semi-norm of
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
w.r.t. the metric
\rho
.

For the rest of the section fix
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
For
(x,A)\in(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
set
K_{z}(x,A):=K(z,x,A)
. Note that
K_{z}
is a transition kernel on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
and
recall that
\Pi(z,\cdot)=\pi_{z}
is a probability measure on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
we call

\tau(K_{z}^{k}):=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}\,;\,x_{1}\not=x_{2}}\frac{W(K_{z}^{k}(x_{1},\cdot),K^{k}_{z}(x_{2},\cdot))}{\rho(x_{1},x_{2})}

the
Wasserstein contraction coefficient of
K_{z}^{k}
, with
K_{z}^{k}
being the product of
K_{z}
with itself
k
-times.
By
[
Dob96
, Proposition 14.3 and Proposition 14.4]
the contraction coefficient satisfies

•

a submultiplicativity property, i.e.
\tau(K_{z_{1}}K_{z_{2}})\leq\tau(K_{z_{1}})\tau(K_{z_{2}})
for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
; and

•

a contraction property, i.e.
W(\mu_{1}K^{k}_{z},\mu_{2}K^{k}_{z})\leq\tau(K^{k}_{z})\,W(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
.

Slightly adopting the notation of
[
Hof26
]
we define the following.

Definition 2.2
.

The transition kernel
K_{z}
is called Wasserstein contractive if it admits
\pi_{z}
as invariant distribution, i.e.
\pi_{z}K_{z}=\pi_{z}
, satisfies
\tau(K_{z}^{\ell})<1
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and we have that
\tau(K_{z})<\infty
.

By submultiplicativity and contractivity we have the following:
If
K_{z}
is Wasserstein contractive, then for any probability distribution
\mu
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
we have
W(\mu K_{z}^{k},\pi_{z})\leq C\tau^{k}
for a constant
C<\infty
.

2.3
Poisson’s equation

To prove our main results we intend to use a martingale approximation technique based on solutions of Poisson’s equation.
In this section we present the required background and preliminaries about Poisson’s equation.

For the rest of this section let
z\in\mathcal{Z}
be fixed. Given a
\pi_{z}
-integrable function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, referred to as
forcing function
, we call

u_{z}(x)-K_{z}u_{z}(x)=f(x)-\pi_{z}(f),

(5)

the
Poisson equation
(with forcing function
f
). If

satisfies (
5
), then we say it solves Poisson’s equation.

In the spirit of
[
JO10
]
we define the

, and
coarse diffusion coefficient
of the probability space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\pi_{z})
, respectively, as

{\ecc}_{z}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x^{\prime})\pi_{z}({\rm d}x^{\prime}),

and

\diff(z,x):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})^{2}K_{z}(x,{\rm d}x_{1})K_{z}(x,{\rm d}x_{2}).

The following result guarantees existence and regularity of solutions to Poisson’s equation for Wasserstein contractive
K_{z}
, see also
[

]

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\pi_{z}
-integrable with
\|f\|_{\rho}<\infty
. Assume that there exists an
\widetilde{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
{\ecc}_{z}(\widetilde{x})<\infty
. If
K_{z}
is a Wasserstein contractive kernel, then, the mapping

x\mapsto u_{z}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left((K^{k}_{z}f)(x)-\pi_{z}(f)\right),\quad x\in\mathcal{X},

is a well-defined solution of Poisson’s equation w.r.t. forcing function
f
, and for
\ell=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}\colon\tau(K_{z}^{k})<1\}<\infty
satisfies

(a)

Lipschitz boundedness, that is,
\|u_{z}\|_{\rho}\leq\frac{\tau(K_{z})^{\ell}\|f\|_{\rho}}{1-\tau(K_{z}^{\ell})}
; and

(b)

for any

that

|u_{z}(x)|\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\rho}\tau(K_{z})^{\ell}}{1-\tau(K_{z}^{\ell})}\,{\ecc}_{z}(x)
and

.

Proof.

By
[
Hof26
, Theorem 3.4]
,

solves Poisson’s equation w.r.t. forcing function

and satisfies property (a). Property (b) follows from the proof of
[
Hof26

, see in particular Equation (3) there.
∎

2.4
Assumptions on the AIR algorithm

In this section we formulate our regularity condition on the AIR algorithm which we use to carry out our analysis later. Our assumptions are twofold. Firstly, we require a simultaneous Wasserstein contraction for
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
. Secondly, we assume that the metric
\rho
used in
W
is sufficiently regular when combined with
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that the family of kernels
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
, used in Algorithm
1
, is simultaneous Wasserstein contractive if there exist
C\in(0,\infty)
,
\tau\in[0,1)
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
the invariant distribution of
K_{z}
is
\pi_{z}
,

\tau(K_{z})\leq C\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau(K_{z}^{\ell})\leq\tau.

Remark 2.6
.

We adopt the convention that the number
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
appearing in Definition
2.5
is always the smallest such number.

Remark 2.7
.

Requiring the family
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
to be Wasserstein contractive with constants independent of

is certainly restrictive. However, without adding other assumptions, e.g. on
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, weakening this

is not possible, cf.
[
HŁRR26
, Appendix C]
.
Additionally, by appropriately choosing

[

]
that the simultaneous uniformly ergodic and simultaneous

[

,
BŁR24

]
as well as Sections

In addition to simultaneous Wasserstein contractivity, we also require some sort of regularity connecting the metric

Definition 2.8

Let

(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
metric regular, if

\sup_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\ecc}_{Z_{j}}(X_{k})\right]<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{E}[{\diff}_{Z_{j}}(X_{k})^{2}]<\infty.

Remark 2.9
.

For any bounded metric
\rho
, that is,
\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})<\infty
, the process
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is metric regular by definition of the eccentricity and coarse diffusion coefficient.

The following result provides a sufficient condition for
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to be metric regular in terms of a simultaneous Lyapunov function.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
V\colon\mathcal{X}\to[1,\infty)
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
L>0
such that
for any
(z,x)\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X}
we have
K_{z}V(x)\leq\alpha V(x)+L
, i.e.
V
is a uniform Lyapunov function for
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
.
Assume
\mathbb{E}[V(X_{0})]<\infty
and for
\widetilde{L}>0
let

\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\leq\widetilde{L}\left(V(x_{1})^{1/2}+V(x_{2})^{1/2}\right),

for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Then,
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is metric regular.

Proof.

By
[
Hai18
, Proposition 4.24]
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
we have
\pi_{z}(V)\leq\frac{L}{1-\alpha}
.
As a consequence of the Lyapunov function property

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(X_{k})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}V(x)K(Z_{k-1},X_{k-1},{\rm d}x)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[\alpha V(X_{k-1})+L]

\displaystyle\leq\dots\leq\alpha^{k}\mathbb{E}[V(X_{0})]+L\sum_{j=0}^{k}\alpha^{j}\leq\mathbb{E}[V(X_{0})]+\frac{L}{1-\alpha}.

(6)

Using (
6
) we obtain uniformly for any
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\ecc}_{Z_{\ell}}(X_{k})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{L}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}(V(X_{k})^{1/2}+V(x)^{1/2})^{2}\pi_{Z_{\ell}}({\rm d}x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\widetilde{L}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[V(X_{k})+\pi_{Z_{\ell}}(V)]\leq 2\widetilde{L}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[V(X_{0})]+\frac{4L\widetilde{L}^{2}}{1-\alpha}<\infty.

Furthermore, by
(
6
) and the imposed assumptions, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\diff(Z_{k},X_{k})]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x_{1},x_{2})^{2}K_{Z_{k}}(X_{k},{\rm d}x_{1})K_{Z_{k}}(X_{k},{\rm d}x_{2})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\widetilde{L}^{2}(V(x_{1})^{1/2}+V(x_{2})^{1/2})^{2}K_{Z_{k}}(X_{k},{\rm d}x_{1})K_{Z_{k}}(X_{k},{\rm d}x_{2})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\widetilde{L}^{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(V(x_{1})+V(x_{2}))K_{Z_{k}}(X_{k},{\rm d}x_{1})K_{Z_{k}}(X_{k},{\rm d}x_{2})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 4\widetilde{L}^{2}\mathbb{E}[K_{Z_{k}}V(X_{k})]=4\widetilde{L}^{2}\mathbb{E}[V(X_{k+1})]\underset{\eqref{al: Lyap_est}}{<}\infty.\qed

We rely on a simultaneous Wasserstein contractivity condition and assume that there is a single particular element

For fixed

Thus, for any

\sqrt{n/2}\leq m(n)\leq 2\sqrt{n}.

3.1
Main results

The mean squared error of the time-average estimator based on the AIR MCMC algorithm is stated in Theorem
3.1
. For illustrative purposes additionally, we formulate two consequences, see Corollaries
3.2
and
3.3
, and discuss these results. The proof of Theorem
3.1
is presented in Appendix
A
.

We use the following notation:
For a simultaneously Wasserstein contractive family
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
, set
\Gamma=\frac{C^{\ell}}{1-\tau}<\infty
, with
C,\tau
and
\ell
as in Definition
2.5
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be determined by Algorithm
1
and
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\pi_{z}
-integrable for
z\in\mathcal{Z}\cup\{z^{*}\}
with
\|h\|_{\rho}<\infty
. Assume
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
is simultaneous Wasserstein contractive and
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is metric regular.
Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
m=m(n)
as in (
7
),
we have

\mathbb{E}|\widehat{\pi}_{n}(h)-\pi_{z^{*}}(h)|^{2}\leq\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}}{n}\left(\Gamma^{2}\Lambda+4\Gamma^{2}\kappa+B(m)\right),

with
\Lambda:=\sup_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{E}[{\diff_{Z_{j}}(X_{k})}]<\infty
and
\kappa:=\sup_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{E}({\ecc}_{Z_{j}}(X_{k})^{2})<\infty
as well as
B(m):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(k+1)\sup_{\|g\|_{\rho}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}|\pi_{Z_{t_{k}}}(g)-\pi_{z^{*}}(g)|^{2}
.

In Theorem
3.1
there appears a term
B(m)
, which is interpreted as
bias term
and depends on the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that is used to approximate
z^{*}
. We provide two further results, where, under additional assumptions, we are able to provide more explicit bounds.
The first result covers the case where we have
\pi_{z}=\pi
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}

with vanishing bias term.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let the assumptions and the setting be as in Theorem
3.1
.
Additionally, assume that
\pi_{z}=\pi_{z^{*}}
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}|\widehat{\pi}_{n}(h)-\pi_{z^{*}}(h)|^{2}\leq\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}\Gamma^{2}}{n}\left(\Lambda+4\kappa\right).

We note that the estimates of Corollary
3.2
match the rate one would have for classical MCMC, see
[
JO10
,
Rud12
,
ŁMN13
,
Hof25
]
.

The second corollary keeps the bias term and offers bounds of it under suitable proxy assumptions.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let the assumptions and the setting be as in Theorem
3.1
.
Additionally, assume
\sup_{\|g\|_{\rho}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}|\pi_{Z_{t_{k}}}(g)-\pi_{z^{*}}(g)|^{2}\leq b/(k+1)^{1+\alpha}

for an absolute constant
b<\infty
and
\alpha\geq 0
.
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 3
,

\mathbb{E}|\widehat{\pi}_{n}(h)-\pi_{z^{*}}(h)|^{2}\leq\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}C_{1}}{n}+\frac{6\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}b}{(1-\alpha)\,n^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}&\qquad&\alpha\in[0,1)\\
&\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}C_{1}}{n}+\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}b\log(3\sqrt{n})}{n}&&\alpha=1\\
&\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}C_{1}}{n}+\frac{3\left\|h\right\|_{\rho}^{2}b}{(1-\alpha)n}&&\alpha\in(1,\infty),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

where

C_{1}=\Gamma^{2}\Lambda+4\Gamma^{2}\kappa
.

Proof.

We begin with the first case, i.e.
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Note that by definition of
B(m)
and (
8
) we obtain

B(m)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{b}{(k+1)^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{b}{1-\alpha}\big[(m+1)^{1-\alpha}-1\big]\leq\frac{3\,b}{1-\alpha}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

Combining this with the estimate from Theorem
3.1
implies the claim.
In the case
\alpha=1
, we have
B(m)\leq b\log(m+1)
, such that by (
8
) (and
n\geq 3
) the conclusion of this part follows. In the final case
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
we have

B(m)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{b}{(k+1)^{\alpha}}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{b}{(r+1)^{\alpha}}{\,\rm d}r=\frac{b}{1-\alpha}.

which also by (
8
) yields the bound.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

By the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality the bias part in Theorem

,
i.e.

\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{\|g\|_{\rho}\leq 1}|\pi_{Z_{t_{\ell}}}(g)-\pi_{z^{*}}(g)|^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[W(\pi_{Z_{t_{\ell}}},\pi_{z^{*}})^{2}\big].

.

3.2
Application to classical adaptive MCMC

Next we apply our results to two examples from the adaptive MCMC literature.
Here ‘classical’ refers to the fact that there is no bias term, i.e.
\pi_{z}=\pi
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, meaning each transition kernel
K_{z}
, with
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, has
\pi
as its invariant distribution. Furthermore, the underlying metric
\rho
is the trivial one, i.e. we rely on what is usually coined as ‘uniform ergodicity’ setting.
Adaptation is incorporated by updating, in a suitable sense, the underlying transition kernel.

3.2.1
Adaptive stereographic MCMC

We consider the stereographic MCMC approach, introduced in
[
YŁR24
]
, and further developed in
[
BŁR24
]
to the adaptive setting.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be the corresponding Borel sets.
Assume that the target distribution
\pi
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
admits a strictly positive and continuous density
p\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
. Furthermore, throughout this section, let
[
BŁR24
, Assumption 3.3]
be satisfied, that is, we impose that

\limsup_{\left\|x\right\|\to\infty}\;p(x)\left(\left\|x\right\|^{2}+1\right)^{d}<\infty,

with
\left\|x\right\|
denoting the Euclidean norm of a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The core idea of the stereographic approach is to beneficially exploit the preconditioned stereographic projection. It bijectively transforms
\pi
to the
d
-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
The geometric structure and compactness of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
allow ‘efficient approximate sampling’ w.r.t. the transformed target distribution there, followed by projecting the output back to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Adaptivity is incorporated by updating the preconditioner and different algorithms on the sphere may be used, e.g. Metropolis-Hastings, or slice samplers, cf.
[
BŁR24
]
.

Let
\rho(x,y)
be the trivial metric, that is
\rho(x,y)=1
if
x\neq y
, and
0
otherwise, with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is well known that in this case
W
coincides with the total variation distance (up to a multiplicaitive constant), check for instance
[
RS18
, Remark 2.1]
.
In this example, the space
\mathcal{Z}
is set to be a class of suitable positive definite matrices times a class of eligible mean vectors, that together determine a preconditioner, compare to
[
BŁR24
, Section 2]
.
Here
\pi
is assumed to be independent of
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, that is,
\pi=\pi_{z}
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, although on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
different ‘transformed targets’ are used.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(X_{n},Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of random variables as specified in Algorithm
1
, based either on an AIR stereographic random walk or
on AIR stereographic spherical slice sampling, see
[
BŁR24
, Section 2]
.
Then, there exists a constant
\widetilde{C}<\infty
, such that for any bounded measurable
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mathbb{E}\left|\widehat{\pi}_{n}(h)-\pi(h)\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}\left\|h\right\|_{\infty}}{n},

where
\left\|h\right\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|h(x)|<\infty
.

Proof.

By Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 of
[
BŁR24
]
the corresponding families of kernels
(K_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are simultaneous uniformly ergodic. Hence, by the same arguments
as
in
[
HŁRR26

satisfies the simultaneous Wasserstein contraction. Boundedness of

.
∎

3.2.2
Adaptive Independent Metropolis with normalizing flows

We investigate an AIR version of the independent Metropolis-Hastings (IMH) algorithm.

As in
[
BGBL22
,
GRVE22
]
we adapt the proposal by the use of normalizing flows,
however, also other strategies are feasible, see e.g.
[
PM18
]
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be the corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra and
p\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
be a strictly positive and continuous probability density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, which induces
\pi
. As in Example
1.1
,
let
\mathcal{Z}
be a set of normalizing flows, parametrized by
u\in\mathcal{U}
.
Let
\widetilde{\pi}
be another distribution on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
, with density
\widetilde{p}
, such that sampling
\widetilde{X}\sim\widetilde{\pi}
is feasible.
Given
X_{0}=x_{0},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}
within IMH one first proposes a candidate
\widetilde{X}\sim\widetilde{\pi}
, realized by
\widetilde{x}
, which is then accepted as
x_{n+1}
with probability

a(x_{n},\widetilde{x})=\min\left\{1,\frac{p(\widetilde{x})\,\widetilde{p}(x_{n})}{p({x_{n}})\,\widetilde{p}(\widetilde{x})}\right\},

and
otherwise rejected, such that
x_{n+1}=x_{n}
.

It is known from
[
MT96
,
Wan22
,
BJ24
]
that the ‘efficiency’ of IMH crucially depends on the ratio
w(x)=p(x)/\widetilde{p}(x)
.
Indeed, let

w^{*}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}w(x)<\infty
, then, the total variation distance between the distribution of
X_{n}
and
\pi
is bounded by
2(1-1/w^{*})^{n}
, see for example
[
MT96
, Theorem 2.1]
,
which renders the IMH Markov chain

(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to be uniformly ergodic.
If
z_{u}
, with
u\in\mathcal{U}
, is a normalizing flow, then by the change-of-variables formula
z_{u}(\widetilde{X})
, with
\widetilde{X}\sim\widetilde{\pi}
, has density

p_{z_{u}}(x)=\widetilde{p}(z_{u}^{-1}(x))|\det Dz_{u}^{-1}(x)|,

where
Dz_{u}^{-1}
denotes the Jacobi matrix of
z_{u}^{-1}
.

Hence, for a suitably chosen flow
z_{u}
, proposing
z_{u}({\widetilde{X}})
may improve the speed of convergence if
w_{u}^{*}<w^{*}
, with
w_{u}^{*}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)/p_{z_{u}}(x)
.
Intuitively, if
\pi
and
\pi_{z_{u}}
are ‘close to each other’, then
w_{u}^{*}
should be ‘close to one’.
Thus, we may try to minimize the KL divergence
over
z\in\mathcal{Z}
(or
u\in\mathcal{U}
), that is, we try to find a flow
z_{u}
, in terms of
u\in\mathcal{U}
, that minimizes,

\KL(\pi,\pi_{z_{u}})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{p(x)}{p_{z_{u}}(x)}\right)\pi({\rm d}x)=C_{\pi}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log p_{z_{u}}(x)\,\pi({\rm d}x),

where
C_{\pi}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log p(x)\pi({\rm d}x)
is a constant independent of
z_{u}
.
Also other loss functions are possible; for details we refer to the different settings considered in
[
AKS19
,
NAF+21
,
GRVE22
,
GODMG23
]
.
The integral
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log p_{z_{u}}(x)\pi({\rm d}x)
is usually intractable, therefore, we rely on the idea to minimize the empirical proxy

J_{z_{u}}^{(n)}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log p_{z_{u}}(Y_{j}),

where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
is a finite sequence of random variables that approximately samples
\pi
.
Inspired by
[
GRVE22
]

\nu
on the ‘parameter space’
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
.
A likelihood function
y\mapsto\ell(y|x)
is determined by a mapping
E\colon\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\ell(y|x)=\frac{\exp(-E(y,x))}{z^{*}(x)},

with
z^{*}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\exp(-E(y,x))\,\mu({\rm d}y)\in(0,\infty)
being intractable in the sense of a non-evaluable function
x\mapsto z^{*}(x)
. The mapping
y\mapsto\ell(y|x)
fits into the framework of energy based models, cf.
[
LCH+06
]
.

There is a rich literature on learning of the parameters of such models by optimization methods such as contrastive divergence, maximum pseudo-likelihood or score matching, see e.g.
[
NGS+22
,
SK21
]
. Assessing uncertainty requires sampling of a posterior distribution
\pi=\pi_{z^{*}}
given by

\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)=\frac{\ell(\bar{y}|x)\nu({\rm d}x)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell(\bar{y}|\widetilde{x})\nu({\rm d}\widetilde{x})}=\frac{\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))\nu({\rm d}x)}{z^{*}(x)\,C_{z^{*}}},

with unknown normalizing constant
C_{z^{*}}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))\,z^{*}(x)^{-1}\nu({\rm d}x)
. Additionally,
x\mapsto z^{*}(x)
cannot be evaluated, such that the sampling problem is doubly-intractable.
Examples occur e.g. in statistical mechanics
[
Hab14
]
,
molecular dynamics

[
EHR+23
]
, or exponential random graph models
[
HH06
]
.

To employ classical MCMC algorithms, evaluating
x\mapsto\ell(\bar{y}|x)
, which requires
x\mapsto z^{*}(x)
, is essential, yet not possible in the present setting. To deal with this issue one may use, for example, augmented state space methods, as considered here
[
MPRB06
,
MGM12
]
, noisy MCMC, cf.
[
AFEB16
,
HRS20
]
, or adaptive MCMC as in
[
ALR13
,
LJSL16
]
, see also the survey
[
PH18
]
.
We consider an adaptive MCMC approach approximating
x\mapsto z^{*}(x)
, cf.
[
Hab14
,
EHR+23
]
. To define
\mathcal{Z}
we impose a standing assumption.

Standing assumption.

Let
\rho\colon\mathcal{X}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
be the metric on
\mathcal{X}
that renders
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
a Polish space. For
E\colon\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
we assume for
y\in\mathcal{D}
that the mapping
x\mapsto\exp(-E(y,x))
satisfies the following continuity condition: For all
\varepsilon>0
and for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
there exists a
\delta>0
such that

\rho(x,x^{\prime})<\delta\;\Longrightarrow\;\sup_{y\in\mathcal{D}}|\exp(-E(y,x))-\exp(-E(y,x^{\prime}))|<\varepsilon.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of probability measures on
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{D}})
with
\xi=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{y_{j}}\in\mathcal{P}
for arbitrary
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\in\mathcal{D}
.
We define

\mathcal{Z}=\left\{z\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)\;\bigg|\;x\mapsto z(x)=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\exp(-E(y,x))\,\gamma({\rm d}y),\gamma\in\mathcal{P}\right\}.

The standing assumption yields that
\mathcal{Z}
contains only continuous functions, such that we can equip this set easily with a suitable
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. Now let
(\pi_{z})_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
be the sequence of probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
given by

\pi_{z}({\rm d}x)=\frac{\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))}{C_{z}\,z(x)}\nu({\rm d}x),\quad z\in\mathcal{Z},

with
C_{z}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))z(x)^{-1}\nu({\rm d}x)
.

To use a point-wise proxy for
z^{*}
below we rely on the following.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let

be functions satisfying

for all

For

let

and assume that

R^{(\ell,u)}:=\inf_{x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho(x_{0},x)^{2}+R_{1}(x_{0}))\frac{u(x)^{2}}{\ell(x)^{2}}\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)\right]<\infty.

Using Assumption

Proposition 4.2

\mathbb{E}[W(\pi_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}},\pi_{z^{*}})^{2}]\leq\frac{2R^{(\ell,u)}}{N},

(9)

Next we provide the concrete AIR scheme in the doubly intractable setting. We emphasize here that

4

Lemma A.1
.

Let the assumptions, the setting and
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be as in Theorem
3.1
. Then, for

we have

\mathbb{E}|\widehat{\pi}_{n}(h)-\pi_{z^{*}}(h)|^{2}\leq\frac{3}{n^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}(M_{n}^{2})+\mathbb{E}(A_{n}^{2})+\mathbb{E}(B_{n}^{2})\right],

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1})-K_{Z_{j}}u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})),

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(u_{Z_{t_{j}}}(X_{t_{j}})-u_{Z_{t_{j}}}(X_{t_{j+1}}))+u_{Z_{t_{m}}}(X_{t_{m}})-u_{Z_{t_{m}}}(X_{n+1}),

If not stated otherwise, all steps of the proofs are understood

The definition of

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(h(X_{j})-\pi_{z^{*}}(h))

Since

,

\displaystyle h(X_{j})-\pi_{Z_{j}}(h)

\displaystyle=u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-K_{Z_{j}}u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1})-K_{Z_{j}}u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})+u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1}).

Consequently,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(h(X_{j})-\pi_{Z_{j}}(h)\right)=M_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1})).

Thus, a telescoping argument yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}-1}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1}))=u_{Z_{t_{k}}}(X_{t_{k}})-u_{Z_{t_{k}}}(X_{t_{k+1}}),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t_{m}}^{n}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1}))=u_{Z_{t_{m}}}(X_{t_{m}})-u_{Z_{t_{m}}}(X_{n+1}),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\sum_{j=t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}-1}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j+1}))+\sum_{j=t_{m}}^{n}(u_{Z_{j}}(X_{j})-u_{Z_{j}}(\xi_{j+1}))=A_{n}.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\mathbb{E}|\pi_{Z_{t_{k}}}(h)-\pi_{z^{*}}(h)|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(k+1)\mathbb{E}|\pi_{Z_{t_{k}}}(h)-\pi_{z^{*}}(h)|^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle\leq\|h\|_{\rho}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(k+1)\sup_{\|g\|_{\rho}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}|\pi_{Z_{t_{k}}}(g)-\pi_{z^{*}}(g)|^{2}=\|h\|_{\rho}^{2}B(m),

with
B(m)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(k+1)\sup_{\|g\|_{\rho}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}|\pi_{Z_{t_{k}}}(g)-\pi_{z^{*}}(g)|^{2}
.

Combining these bounds with Lemma
A.1
finishes the proof.

∎

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

For arbitrary
\widehat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, observe that by the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality

W(\pi_{\widehat{z}},\pi_{z^{*}})=\sup_{\|h\|_{\rho}\leq 1,\;h(x_{0})=0}\left|\pi_{z^{*}}(h)-\pi_{\widehat{z}}(h)\right|.

Assume that
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\|h\|_{\rho}\leq 1
and
h(x_{0})=0
. Then

\displaystyle|\pi_{z^{*}}(h)-\pi_{\widehat{z}}(h)|\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})\left|\frac{1}{C_{z^{*}}z^{*}(x)}-\frac{1}{C_{\widehat{z}}\,\widehat{z}(x)}\right|\exp(-E(\bar{y},x))\nu({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})\left|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{z}(x)}-1\right|\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)+\left|\frac{C_{\widehat{z}}}{C_{z^{*}}}-1\right|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})\,\pi_{\widehat{z}}({\rm d}x).

By Jensen’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle W(\pi_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}},\pi_{z^{*}})^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})^{2}\;\Big|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\;\Big|\frac{C_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}}}{C_{z^{*}}}-1\Big|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})^{2}\,\pi_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}}({\rm d}x).

(15)

By Assumption
4.1
we have
\ell(x)\leq\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)\leq u(x)
, such that

C_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}}\geq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp(-E(\bar{y},x^{\prime}))u(x^{\prime})^{-1}\nu({\rm d}x^{\prime})

and

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})^{2}\,\pi_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}}({\rm d}x)\leq R_{1}(x_{0}).

Estimating (
15
) by incorporating
R_{1}(x_{0})
and afterwards taking the expectation yields (with a Fubini argument)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W(\pi_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}},\pi_{z^{*}})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,x_{0})^{2}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\Big|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}\right]\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+2R_{1}(x_{0})\,\mathbb{E}\left[\Big|\frac{C_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}}}{C_{z^{*}}}-1\Big|^{2}\right].

(16)

By Jensen’s inequality and again by a Fubini argument we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Big|\frac{C_{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}}}{C_{z^{*}}}-1\Big|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\Big|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big)\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)\Big|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big)^{2}\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x)\right]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}\pi_{z^{*}}({\rm d}x).

(17)

Using
\ell(x)\leq\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)\leq u(x)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}\Big(1-\frac{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}{z^{*}(x)}\Big)\Big|^{2}\leq\frac{z^{*}(x)^{2}}{\ell(x)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}{z^{*}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}.

The fact that
\frac{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}{z^{*}(x)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\exp(-E(Y_{i},x))}{z^{*}(x)}
with
\mathbb{E}(\frac{\exp(-E(Y_{1},x))}{z^{*}(x)})=1
and the iid property of
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N}
imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}{z^{*}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{\exp(-E(Y_{1},x))}{z^{*}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2E(Y_{1},x))]}{z^{*}(x)^{2}}-1\right)\leq\frac{1}{N}\;\frac{u(x)^{2}}{z^{*}(x)^{2}},

where the last inequality follows by
\exp(-E(Y_{1},x))\leq u(x)
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{z^{*}(x)}{\widehat{Z}^{(N)}(x)}-1\Big|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{N}\frac{u(x)^{2}}{\ell(x)^{2}}
, such that, by (
16
), (
17
) and taking an infimum over
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, the assertion is proven.
∎
