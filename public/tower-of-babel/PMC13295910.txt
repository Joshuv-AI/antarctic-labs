Leave policy after stillbirth in LMICs: how much are we thinking about bereaved mothers? A scoping review
Authors: Anuj Kumar Pandey, Diksha Gautam, Rohan Sachdeva, Nida Sheikh, Prashant Sharma, Prashasti Agarwal, Anvita Pandey, Anju Sehrawat, Benson Thomas M, Rajeeb Kumar Sah, Sutapa Bandyopadhyay Neogi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLow-income and-middle-income countries (LMICs) contribute to the maximum burden of stillbirth globally, which affects parents in various tangible and intangible ways, emphasising the need for having a provision of parental leave to recover from the physical and mental/emotional drain. In response to this, we conducted a scoping review with the objective of identifying provision of leave within maternity benefit policies after stillbirth in LMICs.MethodologyThe study is conducted in accordance with Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines, using the population, concept and context framework to define the research questions. The International Labour Organization (ILO) global care policy portal and documents from selected international United Nations (UN) organisations were reviewed to assess the inclusion of stillbirth within maternity leave policies. Additionally, country-specific maternity leave policies were retrieved from official government sources for LMICs. Fuerther, databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and ProQuest were searched for studies from LMICs to identify literature with recommendations around leave policy. Study selection and data extraction were conducted by two independent reviewers with arbitration by a third reviewer.ResultsThis study noted a stark difference in the leave policy; although all 49 LMICs included in the review had a national maternity leave policy, 67.3% did not align with ILO’s Convention number 183. In contrast, only 10 countries (<20%) have provision of leave after stillbirth. Notably, even among the 10 countries with specific provisions for stillbirth, the average duration of leave after stillbirth was 50.1 days, compared with an average of 107.7 days following a live birth in the same countries. The review also found substantial variation in how stillbirth was defined across policy documents. Furthermore, documents of selected UN organisations revealed a detailed provision of leave post stillbirth for childbearing (12 weeks) and for non-childbearing (2 weeks) women. Further, we could not identify literature with recommendations around leave policy among publications about stillbirth in LMICs.ConclusionLeave grant showed stark disparity. Ignoring the need for physical rest may have long-term health implications. Organisations should have a provision to support women having a stillbirth. Having a provision for flexible leave options allows women to decide when they are emotionally and physically ready to return, rather than forcing a one-size-fits-all solution.

Introduction

WHO defines stillbirth as the ‘birth of a baby with no signs of life at or after 28 weeks of gestation’.1 Stillbirth remains a significant public health challenge2 3 globally, with approximately 2 million cases reported annually, and among them, almost half are thought to be preventable.1 The decline in stillbirth rates (SBR) has been slower than other maternal and child health indicators over recent decades. Between 2000 and 2019, the global annual rate of reduction in SBR was 2.3% (90% CI 1.7 to 2.7), compared with 2.9% (2.5 to 3.2) for the neonatal mortality rate, and 4.3% (3.8 to 4.7) for children aged 1–59 months4. A study reported that 84% share of stillbirth burden is contributed by low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).5 In LMICs, the most common causes of stillbirths are infections; however, in high-income countries (HICs), the majority of stillbirths occur before labour onset with the main causes being related to placental pathology.6

Every newborn action plan, launched in 2014 by WHO, sets a target of ≤12 stillbirths per 1000 total births for every country by 2030. Among high-income and upper-income to middle-income countries, 94 have achieved the target, whereas the majority of sub-Saharan Africa trails far behind the rest of the world.7 In 2016, the publication of The Lancet Ending Preventable Stillbirths Series helped clarify the medical, economic and psychosocial consequences of stillbirth and elevate it to a position of priority in health policy.

Stillbirth affects parents in various tangible and intangible ways. Parents, especially mothers, undergo profound emotional, psychological, social and economic impacts of a stillbirth. They often endure intense grief, compounded by inconsistent bereavement care, societal stigma and conflicting expectations and anxiety over subsequent pregnancies.8 9 These factors may have detrimental effects on strained marital relationships, emotional attachment to the deceased baby and unresolved grief that may affect future children. Mothers also suffer from chronic pain, fatigue, altered body image and a diminished quality of life. These emotions of mothers and families are aggravated by the insensitive behaviour from healthcare providers, friends and the community.10 Beyond the emotional and psychological toll, families also bear economic consequences and financial burdens. It also affects parents’ employment, with over one-third of bereaved mothers reducing their working hours. Those returning to work report a significant decline in productivity, with just 26% of their usual performance after 30 days and only 63% after 6 months.11 Despite the severity of these consequences, stillbirth remains a largely overlooked issue in health systems and policy responses, leaving many mothers without the support they need during one of the most devastating experiences of their lives.

Representation of women in the workforce has been steadily increasing over the past two decades, and they will continue to play a central role in the economy. Therefore, it is essential to recognise the impact of events such as stillbirths on women’s employability. Maternity leave is crucial not only for job security but also for safeguarding the economic and physical well-being of working women. Studies have even reported positive maternal and neonatal outcomes with more extended maternity leave.1215 However, disparities persist between maternity leave entitlements for mothers of live births and those who experience any adverse outcomes, such as abortion, miscarriage and stillbirth. Although mothers with live births are entitled to paid leave through employers to recover and bond with their newborns, many mothers who deliver stillborn babies lose their leave benefits immediately. It is important to emphasise that maternity leave is not solely for childcare but also for the mother’s physical and emotional recovery. According to the International Labour Organization, only 7% of countries have specific provisions for stillbirths in their maternity benefit policies. Such policies exist in 9.8% of African countries, 17.9% of Asian countries, 6.4% of European countries and 11.8% of American countries.11 Although some countries have maternity benefit policies for mothers experiencing stillbirths in place, the duration of leave is often shorter, offering only partial benefits compared with those provided for live births.16 However, the intent of the maternity leave under the law is not only to ensure the bringing up of the child but primarily to protect the health of the women who delivered.17

We conducted a scoping review to identify provisions of leave within maternity benefit policies after stillbirth in LMICs. Through this study, we attempted to answer the following: 1) which LMICs have national policies on maternity leave benefits for mothers who experienced stillbirth; if policy exists, what does the policy state? 3) Do international health organisations advocate for inclusion of stillbirth within maternity leave frameworks? and 4) What leave-related recommendations have been made by the researchers working in the area specific to stillbirths in LMICs? Overall, the study aims to support policymakers in recognising the needs of bereaved mothers and the critical importance of inclusive maternity leave policies. For this review, we have not restricted ourselves to any gestational or weight cutoffs for defining the stillbirth.

Methods

Available literature has information on leave policies for early pregnancy loss18 19; however, the limited evidence around leave policy after stillbirth from LMICs, where the burden of stillbirth is notably higher, highlights the need to generate a stronger evidence base in this context. Our aim is not to catalogue every reference to stillbirth or all related policy interventions. Rather, we intend to explore whether LMICs have national policies that provide leave benefits for mothers who have experienced stillbirth. We also attempted to examine what provisions (with emphasis on stillbirths) are included under the broad ambit of the policy. Additionally, we explored how international health organisations conceptualise stillbirth and whether they advocate for its inclusion within maternity leave frameworks to promote more inclusive policies.

Protocol and registration

A scoping review was conducted following the protocol laid down in a pre-registered protocol20 and in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines. Given the range of data, literature and context that needs to be referred for evidence synthesis, a scoping review approach was chosen.2123 We have used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines for reporting this scoping review.24 Ethical approval was not necessary given the nature of the study design.

Information sources, search strategy and eligibility criteria

We have used the population–concept–context (PCC) framework25 to explain the main concept of our research question, and later, we developed the search strategies using the same framework. This framework enabled us to explain the research questions clearly and helped us check for any potentially missed inclusion/exclusion criteria (table 1). Various sets of information were assessed for evidence synthesis. Primarily, the International Labour Organization (ILO) global care policy portal26 was screened (since inception till 3 July 2025) to look for evidence around the availability of maternal leave policy and if the policy was aligned with the ILO’s Convention number 183. Later, country-wise maternity leave policies were retrieved from official government websites and assessed to determine if stillbirth is included within the maternity leave policies.

A separate document assessment was performed to understand if selected international UN organisations include stillbirths within their maternity leave policy. Although we reviewed the policy documents, we also examined additional materials such as their websites, including any brief statements released by these organisations around stillbirth within their maternity leave policy. This approach helped us understand how global health organisations make the maternity leave policy more inclusive by including leave policies around such adversities.

For the third research question, we searched different databases, namely, PubMed, Web of Science, ProQuest and Scopus. Initially, the search strategy was developed for the PubMed database following the PCC framework as mentioned in table 1. We developed the search terms referring to previously published literature. 27 28We adapted the search terms developed for PubMed for other databases. The search strategy along with the search results are available as online supplemental file 1. Articles around stillbirth, irrespective of the gestational age (if the article self-defined the condition as stillbirth), were considered for inclusion. Another inclusion was for studies from LMICs published since inception till 13 December 2024. We did not apply any filter for the study design or language. Further, a rapid search was conducted in April 2026 and did not yield any additional studies. Across documents, we retained the definitions as reported in the respective documents to preserve contextual and policy-specific interpretations.

Selection of sources of evidence

Data extraction was performed by two independent reviewers. For the first and third research questions, data were extracted by RS and AP, whereas arbitration was done by AKP. For the second research question, data extraction was done by NS and AS, whereas arbitration was done by DG. Before data extraction, dummy tables were created, and pilot testing was performed for 10% of the data. Once agreed on by all the authors, full data extraction was performed. Considering the depth of information and to avoid any error during data extraction for the first two research questions, we requested two more reviewers, PS and PA, to validate a random 10%–20% of the information. All data were extracted using an Excel sheet. For the third research question, information was extracted in the form of an Excel sheet, which was later combined to remove the duplicates. The standard method of title screening, abstract screening and full-text screening was completed by two independent reviewers to exclude articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria for the review. The methodological quality and risk-of-bias assessment were not completed, as per the guidance of the scoping review protocols.21 29

Data extraction, collating, summarising and reporting the results

We used the ‘policy analysis triangle’ framework30 31 to analyse and document the first two research questions. This framework was designed for analysing health sector–related policies. It focused broadly on the content, actors, context and processes. The ‘content’ implied policy objectives, operational policies, regulations, etc., whereas ‘context’ referred to systemic factors. We have considered ‘context’ and ‘content’ as women who all would be eligible for leave entitlement, for how long or any conditions for availing the leave. ‘Actor’ implied individual who would be women who have experienced stillbirth, and ‘process’ referred to the way in which policies were initiated, developed or formulated, negotiated, communicated, etc.

Before data extraction, all documents were collected, and primary keywords were used, such as ‘stillbirth’, ‘still born’, ‘dead fetus in womb’, ‘death of fetus - third trimester’, or ‘babies born with no sign of life’ or ‘dead fetus after 28 weeks of gestation’, etc, for identifying information. Availability of any of these keywords was screened thoroughly in the included documents. In case of language other than English, Google Translator was used for translating. These documents were thoroughly read to ensure adequate immersion in the data, and relevant notes were taken using the pre-piloted data extraction Excel sheet. The data extraction process began after the team became familiar and a common consensus was reached on information that would be extracted from the documents. Following data extraction by both the independent reviewers, the arbitrator reviewed the sheet and finalised the content. To ensure that no information was missed out, external reviewers were asked to validate 10% of the information. JBI guidelines were followed to answer the third research question. Further, PRISMA-ScR guidelines are used for reporting the findings.24

In all the countries (except Lesotho in which information was not available and Bhutan in compensation to casual/sick/annual leave/unpaid), women are entitled to avail paid leave. There are provisions for presenting a medical certificate stating the birth of a dead fetus with no sign of life in Bhutan and Zambia. In India, this policy primarily applies to the central government servant – considering the fact of state being a health subject still needs modifications.

A woman experiences similar physical and hormonal changes over the same time frame of more than 4 00 000 min, regardless of whether the outcome is a stillbirth or a live birth. However, the disparity in leave granted is stark: an average of 50.1 days after stillbirth compared with 107.7 days after a live birth. This raises critical questions about leave policies, which should be recognised as a fundamental right, not a privilege.

Further, as part of the second research question for the document review, we have primarily assessed the UN organisation’s document titled ‘Staff Regulations and Rules, including provisional Staff Rules - United Nations’ which was amended in year 2023.33 The document clearly states having a provision of separate leaves for post-stillbirth for childbearing and non-childbearing women implying a more inclusivity towards these areas.

In case of a non-childbearing woman, there is a provision of 2 weeks of special leave with full pay only after submitting a medical certificate. Further organisation-specific searches yielded that organisations, such as WHO, United Nations Children’s Fund, United Nations Development Programme, etc, fall within a common system laid down by the UN Staff Regulations and Rules policy. Individual policies by these organisations do not explicitly mention entitlements or extensions of leave in the event of stillbirth or neonatal death.

Moreover, United Nations Population Fund within the Division for Human Resources Parental leave policy being effective from 202334 35 states having provision to such leave policies. The document states that in case of a stillborn fetus, the women are entitled to avail a total of 4 week of full paid leave after submitting a medical certificate. This leave will be in lieu of the balance leave under parental leave. The document further elaborated, which was one of its kind, that these leaves are to help women recover from the physical trauma. The document further defined stillbirth as a birth of a dead fetus after 20 full weeks of pregnancy, having no sign of life. This leave must be availed immediately after the delivery of the dead fetus.

Our final research question aimed to explore whether researchers working in the area of stillbirth have discussed the need for inclusivity by advocating for maternity leave policies following a stillbirth. Our keyword search yielded a total of 459 articles from a total of four database searches. Following the scoping review stages of title, abstract and full text review, we retrieved 21 articles for full text screening. On review, none of the articles have a recommendation for having a provision of maternity leave after delivering a stillborn fetus. Studies have discussed various aspects, namely, gaps in data quality, definition of stillbirth, clinical assessment post stillbirth, challenges in implementation, etc. One study protocol36 from India describes the development of a bereavement care package that can be integrated into maternal healthcare services. However, we did not receive any literature providing such recommendations.

Discussion

The review included 49 guidelines and 49 maternity leave policy documents. While 49 LMICs have maternity leave policy, only 20% of the countries include stillbirths (with varying definitions) in the policy. The duration of leave after stillbirths is almost half of that after a live birth. A paid leave for 2–12 weeks is provided after submitting a medical certificate that includes stillbirths, as per UN regulations. There is a dearth of advocacy documents for universalising maternity leave policies following stillbirth.

Maternity leave implies leave after the birth of a baby, which is a critical component of maternity protection wherein through various sources women are compensated with the paid time off during phases of pregnancy, delivery and postnatal period. The provision of such leaves is in response to the physiological demand for the women after birth. The physiology remains the same, and physical changes remain the same; however, the toll of mental and emotional burden is higher among women who experience such adversities.37 Physiological changes that occur during pregnancy typically take 6–8 weeks to return to normal irrespective of the outcome of pregnancy.38

This study is one of its kind, as we have attempted to review policy documents of 49 LMICs on having a provision for leave after stillbirth. One of the other major strengths of this study is the use of different policy documents, information from websites, videos and press-briefs to derive to the conclusion with no language restriction. For each research question, we followed a rigorous procedure for selecting and extracting information. A structured PCC framework was used, which had standardised how we assessed each of the documents included and how reporting was done. Further, we attempted to close the gap between having a policy and what recommendations have been laid down by the researchers. Although one of our strengths lies in conducting an extensive search, we acknowledge that given the volume of documents reviewed, we might have missed some information, particularly in documents provided only in hard copy, and this remains a key limitation of our study. Additionally, the inclusion of countries and documents were dependent on the availability and accessibility of policy information, which may also have led to the exclusion of certain countries or incomplete capture of policies. This may affect the generalisability of the findings; thus, the results should be interpreted within the context of available data. Moreover, this scoping review focuses on the presence of policies and documents rather than their implementation. Thus, future studies should explore the extent and the level of implementation of the policies wherever it is available and associated facilitators and barriers.

Studies have demonstrated an increase in facility-based deliveries, implying improvement in the overall health seeking of the women39 in LMICs. Studies have also shown an increase in female labour force participation in LMICs.40 41 Despite this significant increase in the female labour force participation, >65% of the assessed LMICs do not meet ILO’s Convention number 183, which mandates 14-week maternity to recover and spend time to bond with the baby and newborns overall care. These are for the registered pregnancies; evidence also suggests that a small proportion of pregnancies in LMICs, especially in rural and hard-to-reach settings, remain unregistered and are often not attended by the healthcare providers, and this leads to under-recognition of stillbirths and subsequent formal care to parturient mother. Evidence from HICs like Australia and New Zealand have a structured leave policy after such adversities.42 43 This indicates the availability of more comprehensive and systematic support mechanisms, including immediate psychosocial care, individualised bereavement practices and continuity of care across the postpartum period. Recent years have also seen a dramatic increase in organisations investing in their workers through better health plans, and that is very much evident in the availability of early pregnancy loss leave for miscarriage or abortions in several countries like Ireland and Alberta.18 19 Studies have also suggested to explore the efficacy of such leave policies within the countries. These contrasts help situate LMIC policy responses within a broader global framework and underscore potential gaps, while pointing toward adaptable good practices for strengthening bereavement care.

The need for longer maternity just after such adversities as stillbirth invites a nuanced discussion. Some contend that resuming work will give a purpose to life and can offer a sense of routine, purpose and even emotional relief by returning to familiar environment. These routines might help the traumatised women to cope up with isolation and loneliness. In line to this argument, another study recommended increased physical activity as intervention to cope with mental trauma post stillbirth.44 In contrast to these contentions, physical recovery from pregnancy remains consistent, regardless of the outcome; however, the need for leave and in return rest should be considered a human right instead of privilege. A woman undergoes the same physical and hormonal changes during pregnancy, and the human body does not recognise the outcome as stillbirth or live birth and thus requires a dignified space in time for the body to heal.45 The reason and rationale behind such skewness of having an average leave duration of 50.1 days after stillbirth, compared with 107.7 days after a live birth, need a critical evaluation and policy decision. Our study also evaluated whether researchers from LMICs have made a recommendation on including stillbirth within the maternity leave policy. Nevertheless, such searches did not yield any eligible records, with recommendations by researchers raising questions if this is even considered a priority. However, we identified a considerable breadth of information around positives for maternal leave policies after live birth and newborn and maternal overall outcomes through these processes.14 27 46 47 Ignoring the need for physical rest may have long-term health implications for women’s lives.48 Therefore, organisations/firms should have a provision to support women having stillbirth. Working at the cost of essential physical and emotional recovery should not be endorsed. Further, having a provision for flexible leave options allows women to decide when they are emotionally and physically ready to return, rather than forcing a one-size-fits-all solution.

In conclusion, despite growing recognition of maternal health needs, a significant gap exists within the system in supporting women following non-viable births. LMICs formally acknowledging such events are substantially less compared with those after a live birth. This reflects how systems prioritise reproductive outcomes over the holistic well-being of women. Addressing this gap requires a shift toward more woman-centred approaches that acknowledge the emotional, psychological and social dimensions of pregnancy loss across diverse health system contexts.
