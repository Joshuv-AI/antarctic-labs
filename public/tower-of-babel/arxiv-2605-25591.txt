Nonclassical Weyl Laws and Connes’ Integration for weak Lorentz Ideals, I
Raphaël Ponge, Yongqiang Tian

Abstract
. Motivated by nonclassical Weyl laws arising in various contexts—including Connes’ approach to the Riemann Hypothesis—we develop a systematic theory of Dixmier traces and Connes’ noncommutative integration for weak Lorentz ideals associated with regularly varying functions. A key ingredient is an asymptotic additivity property for eigenvalue partial sums, obtained by combining Karamata’s theorem with results of Kalton and Lord–Sukochev–Zanin. This yields a direct construction of Dixmier traces in terms of eigenvalue sequences and a complete spectral characterization of measurable operators, answering a question of Connes in this general setting. We also extend to weak Lorentz ideals the Birman–Solomyak perturbation theory for eigenvalue and singular-value asymptotics. Weyl operators—those admitting precise asymptotic limits for their rescaled eigenvalue sequences—are shown to form a closed subset of the ideal, stable under compact perturbations, extending classical results of Weyl and Birman–Solomyak. We further study strong measurability (measurability with respect to all positive normalized traces). We prove that every Weyl operator is strongly measurable, so spectral measurability implies strong measurability. The converse does not hold in general; a spectral characterization via Pietsch’s correspondence is obtained in the companion paper [ 51 ] . Finally, as an application, we establish spectral measurability for operators arising from nonclassical Weyl laws: operators associated with the Riemann Hypothesis (assuming RH); Schrödinger operators with anisotropic potentials and Dirichlet Laplacians on infinite-volume domains; Dirac operators on open spin manifolds with conformally cusp metrics; and the operator formed by the Dirac operator of the Podles̀ quantum sphere and the Laplacian on the 2-torus.

1.
Introduction

In the framework of Connes’ noncommutative geometry
[
20
]
, the role of the integral is played by positive normalized traces, and more especially
Dixmier traces
[
27
]
, on the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
. In the approach of
[
43
,
49
,
62
]
, Dixmier traces are obtained by applying an
extended limit

\omega
to the Cesàro mean,

(1.1)

\frac{1}{\log(N+1)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T),\qquad N\geq 0.

where
(\lambda_{j}(T))_{j\geq 0}
is any eigenvalue sequence for
T
(see Section
3
for the precise meaning of eigenvalue sequence).

Following Connes
[
20
]
,
T
is called
measurable
(or
Dixmier-measurable
) if
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)
is independent of the choice of
\omega
; this common value is the
noncommutative integral

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int T
. It can further be shown (see
[
20
,
43
,
49
]
) that

(1.2)

\biggl(\text{$T$ is measurable and}\ \mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int T=L\biggr)\Longleftrightarrow\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L,

giving both a spectral characterization of measurability and a practical way to compute NC integrals without reference to extended limits.

Stronger notions of measurability arise by enlarging the class of traces. From the point of view of noncommutative geometry, it is natural to use all positive normalized traces. The corresponding notion is called
strong measurability
or
positive measurability
. A spectral characterization of strongly measurable operators on
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
was provided by Semenov-Sukochev-Usachev-Zanin
[
56
]
as a consequence of a version of Pietsch’s correspondence for traces on
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
.

Birman-Solomyak
[
6
,
10
]
developed a perturbation theory for eigenvalue and singular-value asymptotics of compact operators in weak Schatten classes
\mathscr{L}_{p,\infty}
,
p>0
, which became a cornerstone of operator ideal techniques in semiclassical analysis. The existence of Weyl laws for operators in
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
implies strong measurability (see, e.g.,
[
49
]
). In particular, the Weyl laws of Birman-Solomyak
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
for negative-order
\Psi
DOs imply a stronger form of Connes’ trace theorem
[
19
]
.

The aim of this paper, and its companion paper
[
51
]
, is to extend this circle of ideas to the larger setting of
weak Lorentz ideals
, with a special emphasis on the spectral aspects. This is motivated by instances of “nonclassical” Weyl laws in various settings, including Connes’ approach to Riemann Hypothesis (see below).

1.1.
Nonclassical Weyl laws and weak Lorentz ideals

Classical Weyl laws
are eigenvalue asymptotics of the form,

\lambda_{j}(T)\sim cj^{-\frac{1}{p}}\qquad\text{as}\ j\rightarrow\infty,

where
\lambda_{0}(T)\geq\lambda_{1}(T)\geq\cdots
are the eigenvalues of
T
(which for simplicity is assumed to be a positive compact operator). As coined by Simon
[
59
]
,
nonclassical Weyl laws
are eigenvalue asymptotics of the form,

\lambda_{j}(T)\sim cj^{-\frac{1}{p}}(\log j)^{q},\qquad q\neq 0.

They appear in numerous settings, including Dirichlet problems on infinite-volume domains, Weyl pseudodifferential operators on Euclidean space, Weyl laws on open manifolds, Weyl laws in sub-Riemannian geometry, Weyl laws for bisingular operators,
etc
. (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
18
,
25
,
36
,
45
,
59
]
and the references therein).

Assuming the Riemann Hypothesis (RH) holds, one of the most striking examples of nonclassical Weyl law occurs for the Dirac operator
\mathscr{D}
whose eigenvalues are
the imaginary parts of the zeros of Riemann’s zeta function (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
22
]
). The Riemann-van Mangoldt formula then implies that

\lambda_{j}\left(|\mathscr{D}|^{-1}\right)\sim cj^{-1}\log j\qquad\text{as}\ j\rightarrow\infty.

Therefore, at least from the point of view of noncommutative geometry and Connes’ approach to RH, it is important to extend the framework of Connes’ NC integral to operators satisfying nonclassical Weyl laws.

The right operator-ideal setting is provided by
weak Lorentz ideals
(in the sense of
[
43
]
). In this paper’s setup they are associated with functions of regular-variations of negative index, i.e., functions that are decreasing near
\infty
and satisfy
\lim_{t\to\infty}g(\lambda t)/g(t)=\lambda^{\rho}
for some
\rho<0
(which is called the index of
g
). The corresponding weak Lorentz ideal is

\mathscr{L}_{g}=\left\{T;\ \mu_{j}(T)=\operatorname{O}(g(j))\right\},

where
\mu_{0}\geq\mu_{1}(T)\geq\cdots
are the singular values of
T
. In particular, for
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1/p}
,
p>0
, we recover the weak Schatten class
\mathscr{L}_{p,\infty}
.

In this paper we develop a systematic theory of Dixmier traces and Connes’ integration in the general setting of weak Lorentz ideals, we extend the Birman–Solomyak perturbation theory, and set up the theory of strong measurability in this setting. We also provide several examples that are either new or refinements of previously known examples. Further results and examples are provided in the companion paper
[
51
]
.
We also refer to
[
34
,
36
,
42
,
55
,
66
]
, and the references therein, for various related results.

1.2.
Measurability and Connes’ integral

Originally, Dixmier traces were defined on the Dixmier-Macaev ideal
\mathscr{M}_{1,\infty}
, which strictly contains the weak trace-class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
(see
[
20
,
27
]
). More recently (see
[
43
,
49
,
62
]
) it was observed that a simpler construction of Dixmier traces was available on the smaller weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
as a direct consequence of the following asymptotic additivity property,

(1.3)

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S+T)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S)+\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{O}(1),\qquad S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}.

The above result was established in the first edition of
[
43
]
. It readily implies that applying any extended limit to the Cesàro mean (
1.1
) defines a positive linear trace on
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
. In particular, the Dixmier trace is defined directly on the whole ideal
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
and the additivity is immediate.

It has been known for some time that the original approach to Dixmier traces of
[
20
,
27
]
extends
mutatis mutandis
to Lorentz ideals (a.k.a. Marcinkiewicz ideals; see, e.g.,
[
28
,
34
,
55
]
and the references therein). A key observation of this paper is that a version of (
1.3
) holds for their weak versions as well.

From now on, we let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{-1}
-function such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)\,dt=\infty
, and set
G(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,ds
.

Proposition A

(see Proposition
3.4
)
.

For all
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

(1.4)

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S+T)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S)+\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right).

This result is a direct consequence of combining results of Kalton
[
38
]
and Lord-Sukochev-Zanin
[
43
]
with Karamata’s theorem for
\textup{RV}_{-1}
-functions. It enables us to extend to weak Lorentz ideals the approach of
[
43
,
49
]
to Dixmier traces and Connes’ integral on
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
.

Proposition B

(see Proposition
3.16
)
.

Given any extended limit
\omega
, the following formula

(1.5)

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T):=\omega\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\Bigr\}_{N\geq 0}\Bigr),\qquad T\in\mathscr{L}_{g},

defines a positive linear trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
which annihilates
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
.

As in the
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
-setting, we say that an operator
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is
measurable
if
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)
takes the same value for all extended limits
\omega
. This common value is then defined to be the
noncommutative integral
of
T
, and is denoted
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T
. The set of measurable operators is a closed subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, which contains both its commutator space
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and the separable ideal
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
(see Proposition
3.25
).

We have the following extension of (
1.2
) to weak Lorentz ideals.

Theorem C

(see Theorem
3.26
for the full statement)
.

For any
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

(1.6)

\biggl(\text{$T$ is measurable and}\ \sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=L\biggr)\Longleftrightarrow\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L.

As with (
1.2
) this provides us with a spectral characterization of measurable operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
and a spectral interpretation of
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}
. This also provides a simple test for measurability and a practical way to compute NC integrals in applications.

In particular, the asymptotic additivity property (
1.4
) gives a direct proof that the r.h.s. of (
1.6
) is a singular trace on its domain, answering a question of Connes
[
49
, Question A]
in the generality of all weak Lorentz ideals (see §§
3.7
).

1.3.
Birman-Solomyak perturbation theory for weak Lorentz ideals

The perturbation theory of Birman-Solomyak
[
6
,
10
]
for asymptotics of eigenvalues and singular values of operators in weak Schatten classes is a cornerstone of the use of operator ideal techniques in semiclassical analysis. We observe that the
RV
-property is the very property that allows us to extend Birman-Solomyak’s theory to weak Lorentz ideals.

Let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
-function with
\rho<0
. We equip the quotient space
\dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}:=\mathscr{L}_{g}/(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
with its natural quotient quasi-norm, which satisfies
\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}=\limsup_{j\to\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)
(see Lemma
2.32
).

If
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
we let
\lambda_{0}^{\pm}(T)\geq\lambda_{1}^{\pm}(T)\geq\cdots
be the positive/negative eigenvalues of
T
. We say that
T
is a
Weyl operator
if

\Lambda^{+}(T):=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{+}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{-}(T):=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-}(T)\quad\text{both exist}.

More generally, if
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is not selfadjoint, then
T
is called a Weyl operator if its real and imaginary parts both are Weyl operators. We then define

\Lambda^{\pm}(T):=\Lambda^{\pm}\left(\Re T\right)+i\Lambda^{\pm}\left(\Im T\right).

In addition, we denote by
\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
the set of Weyl operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

The following perturbation result extends to all weak Lorentz ideals the perturbation result of Birman-Solomyak for eigenvalue asymptotics of operators in weak Schatten classes.

Theorem D

(see Theorem
4.7
)
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
T_{\ell}\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
,
\ell\geq 0
, be such that

\dot{T}_{\ell}\longrightarrow\dot{T}\quad\text{in}\ \dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}.

Then
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell})
exist,
T\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and we have

\Lambda^{+}(T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell}).

In particular, if
T
and the
T_{\ell}
are selfadjoint, then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\big(\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T_{\ell})\big).

This result implies that
\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is a closed subset of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
on which the functionals
\Lambda^{\pm}
are continuous (Corollary
4.8
). It also implies that
\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and the functionals
\Lambda^{\pm}
are invariant under perturbations by operators in
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
(Corollary
4.9
). This extends to weak Lorentz ideals a well-known result of Weyl
[
69
]
.

We also have a version for singular values of Theorem
D
and its above-mentioned consequences (see Theorem
4.14
and its corollaries). In particular, we obtain an extension to weak Lorentz ideals of Ky Fan’s theorem (see Corollary
4.16
).

Finally, as with weak Schatten classes (see, e.g.,
[
10
, §11.6]
) all these perturbation results for eigenvalue asymptotics admit equivalent reformulations in terms of counting functions (see Proposition
4.18
and Proposition
4.19
).

1.4.
Strong measurability

Let
T_{g}
be any positive operator in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
such that
\lambda_{j}(T_{g})=g(j)
for
j
large enough. A trace
\varphi
on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is called
g
-normalized
if
\varphi(T_{g})=1
. For instance, every Dixmier trace is
g
-normalized.

As with the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, we may consider stronger notions of measurability by considering larger classes of traces. From the perspective of noncommutative geometry, it is natural to consider the whole class of positive normalized traces. Accordingly, we shall say that an operator
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is
strongly measurable
(or positively measurable) if
T
takes the same value on all positive normalized traces. In particular, such an operator is measurable. We denote by
\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
the space of strongly measurable operators.

As normalized positive traces span the whole space of continuous traces, it can be shown (see Proposition
5.4
) that

\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})=\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}

Moreover, all the operators in
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
are strongly measurable (see Proposition
5.5
). We also have the following result, which extends to weak Lorentz ideals the corresponding result for
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
in
[
49
]
.

Theorem E

(see Theorem
5.8
)
.

If
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is a Weyl operator, then it is strongly measurable, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=\Lambda^{+}(T)-\Lambda^{-}(T).

In particular, if
T
is selfadjoint, then

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{+}(T)-\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-}(T).

The above result relates Weyl law properties to measurability. It provides a simple way to check strong measurability. The converse does not hold.

This leads us to introduce a further notion of measurability. We shall call an operator
spectrally measurable
if it is a Weyl operator in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. In particular, an operator
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is spectrally measurable if and only if
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)
both exist. Thus, Theorem
E
asserts that spectral measurability implies strong measurability.

We also show that the space
\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
does not depend on the choice of representative function in the equivalence class defining
\mathscr{L}_{g}
(Proposition
5.12
).

In the companion paper
[
51
]
we will prove a spectral characterization of strongly measurable operators on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. It will extend to weak Lorentz ideals the spectral characterization of strongly measurable operators on
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
of Semenov-Sukochev-Usachev-Zanin
[
56
]
.

1.5.
Examples arising from nonclassical Weyl laws

In Section
6
, we present several examples of spectrally measurable operators arising from various settings. Recall that by Theorem
E
spectral measurability implies strong measurability. Therefore, in all the examples below the operators are measurable with respect to the whole set of positive normalized traces.

We use a general paradigm to construct our examples. Namely, suppose that
A
is a selfadjoint operator with non-negative spectrum such that
0
is isolated in the spectrum, and the positive part of the spectrum consists of positive eigenvalues with finite multiplicity. Assume further that the counting function
N(A;\lambda)
of
A
satisfies a (nonclassical) Weyl law of the form,

(1.7)

N(A;\lambda)\sim c\lambda^{p}(\log\lambda)^{q},\qquad c>0,\quad p>0,\quad q\geq-1.

Lemma F

(see Lemma
6.1
)
.

If
A
satisfies the Weyl law (
1.7
), then the operator
A^{-p}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}A^{-p}=cp^{-q}.

The specific examples treated in Section
6
include the following:

•

Riemann Hypothesis.
Assuming (RH), let
\mathscr{D}
be the Dirac operator whose eigenvalues are the imaginary parts of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
on the critical line
\Re s=1/2
(see, e.g.,
[
21
,
22
]
). The Riemann–von Mangoldt formula implies the nonclassical Weyl law,

N(|\mathscr{D}|;\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\lambda\log\lambda\Bigl(1+\operatorname{O}\bigl((\log\lambda)^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr).

Setting
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}\log(t+2)
, the operator
|\mathscr{D}|^{-1}
then is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
(see Proposition
6.2
).

•

Schrödinger operators and Dirichlet Laplacians on unbounded domains.
On
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, consider the Schrödinger operators,

H_{\alpha}:=-\Delta+|x_{1}\cdots x_{n}|^{\alpha},\qquad\alpha>0.

These operators were introduced by Simon
[
58
,
59
]
in 2D and studied in higher dimensions in
[
15
]
. It is shown in
[
15
,
59
]
that we have nonclassical Weyl laws,

N(H_{\alpha};\lambda)\sim c(n,\alpha)\,\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\log\lambda)^{n-1},

where
c(n,\alpha)
is some explicit constant (see Eq. (
6.6
)). It was actually this Weyl law that prompted Simon
[
59
]
to coin the terminology “nonclassical Weyl law”. Thus, if we set
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}(\log(t+2))^{n-1}
, then the operator
H_{\alpha}^{-(n/2+1/\alpha)}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
(see Proposition
6.4
).

As noticed in
[
15
,
59
]
, as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
the spectrum of
H_{\alpha}
is closely related to that of the Dirichlet Laplacian
\Delta_{\Omega}
on the infinite-volume domain,

\Omega:=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\ |x_{1}\cdots x_{n}|<1\}.

In particular, as shown in
[
15
,
59
]
, we have the Weyl law,

N\big(\Delta_{\Omega};\lambda\big)\sim c(n,\infty)\,\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}}(\log\lambda)^{n-1},\qquad c(n,\infty):=\frac{2n^{n}}{n!}(2\pi)^{-n}|\mathbb{B}^{n}|,

This implies that
\Delta_{\Omega}^{-n/2}
is likewise spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
(see Proposition
6.5
). This refines
[
66
, Example 5.5]
, where only Dixmier-measurability was established (see also
[
36
]
).

•

Open manifolds with conformally cusp metrics.
Let
X^{n}
be an open spin manifold with a conformally cusp metric
\bar{g}=x^{2r}g_{0}
(examples include finite-volume hyperbolic manifolds and metric horns). Assuming no harmonic spinors on the boundary, Moroianu
[
45
]
proved that
\not{D}_{\bar{g}}
has discrete spectrum, with Weyl laws,

N(|\not{D}_{\bar{g}}|;\lambda)\sim\begin{cases}\not{c}_{1}(n)\,\operatorname{Vol}_{\bar{g}}(X)\,\lambda^{n}&\text{if}\ r>1/n,\\[4.0pt]
\not{c}_{2}(n)\,\operatorname{Vol}_{h_{0}}(M)\,\lambda^{n}\log\lambda&\text{if}\ r=1/n.\end{cases}

where
\not{c}_{1}(n)
and
\not{c}_{2}(n)
are explicit constants (see Eq. (
6.9
)). This gives spectral measurability of
|\not{D}_{\bar{g}}|^{-n}
in
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
when
r>1/n
, and in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
with
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}\log(t+2)
when
r=1/n
. In the latter case, we further have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}|\not{D}_{\bar{g}}|^{-n}=\not{c}_{2}(n)\operatorname{Vol}_{h_{0}}(M),

This shows that NC integral recaptures the volume of the boundary
M=\partial X
, even though
(X^{n},g)
has infinite volume for
r=1/n
(see Proposition
6.8
).

•

Podles̀ quantum spheres.
Given
q\in(0,1)
, let
(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{q}),\mathscr{H},\mathscr{D}_{q})
be the spectral triple of Dabrowski–Sitarz
[
24
]
on the Podles̀ quantum sphere, which has “dimension zero” in the sense of noncommutative geometry (see
[
31
]
). Following Gayral–Sukochev
[
34
]
, we consider the operator,

A:=|\mathscr{D}_{q}|\otimes 1+1\otimes\Delta,

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}=(\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z}))^{2}
. Using the results of
[
31
,
34
]
it can be shown that we have the nonclassical Weyl law,

N\bigl(A;\lambda\bigr)\sim\frac{\pi}{(\log q)^{2}}\,\lambda(\log\lambda)^{2}.

It follows that
(|\mathscr{D}_{q}|\otimes 1+1\otimes\Delta)^{-1}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
with
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}(\log(t+2))^{2}
(see Proposition
6.11
). This improves the measurability result of
[
34
]
where only Dixmier-measurability in a larger ideal was established (see also
[
36
,
66

).

2.
Singular Values and Weak Lorentz Ideals

In this section, we record the main definitions and properties regarding weak Lorentz ideals.
Throughout this paper we let
\mathscr{H}
be a (separable) Hilbert space with inner product
\left\langle\cdot|\cdot\right\rangle
. The algebra of bounded linear operators on
\mathscr{H}
is denoted
\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H})
. The operator norm is denoted
\|\cdot\|
. We also denote by
\mathscr{K}
the (closed) ideal of compact operators on
\mathscr{H}
.

2.1.
Singular values

Given any operator
T\in\mathscr{K}
, we let
(\mu_{j}(T))_{j\geq 0}
be its sequence of
singular values
, i.e.,
\mu_{j}(T)
is the
(j+1)
-th eigenvalue counted with multiplicity of the absolute value
|T|=\sqrt{T^{*}T}
. By the
min-max principle
we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(T)=\min\left\{\|T_{|E^{\perp}}\|;\ \dim E=j\right\}.

In addition, we have

\mu_{j}(T)=\operatorname{dist}(T,\mathscr{R}_{j}),\qquad\text{where}\ \mathscr{R}_{j}=\{R\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H});\ \operatorname{rk}R\leq j\}.

We record the following properties of singular values (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
35
]
),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(T)=\mu_{j}(T^{*})=\mu_{j}(|T|),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{j}(T)-\mu_{j}(S)\right|\leq\|S-T\|

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(ATB)\leq\|A\|\mu_{j}(T)\|B\|\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(U^{*}TU)=\mu_{j}(T)\qquad\forall U\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),\ \text{$U$ unitary}.

In addition, we have Ky Fan’s inequalities,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{j+k}(S+T)\leq\mu_{j}(S)+\mu_{k}(T),

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(S+T)\leq\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(S)+\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T).

2.2.
Quasi-Banach ideals

We briefly recall basic definitions regarding quasi-Banach ideals.

Definition 2.1
.

A
quasi-norm
on a vector space
E
is a function
\|\cdot\|:E\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with the following properties:

(i)

\|\lambda x\|=|\lambda|\|x\|
for all
x\in E
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

\|0\|=0
and
\|x\|>0
for
x\neq 0
.

(iii)

There exists
C>0
such that

(2.8)

\|x+y\|\leq C\left(\|x\|+\|y\|\right)\qquad\forall x,y\in E.

In other words, a quasi-norm is like a norm where the usual triangular inequality is relaxed into the quasi-triangular inequality. In particular, any norm is a quasi-norm. Similarly to norms any quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|
on a vector space
E
defines a topology in which a basis of the neighborhood system for the origin consists of the balls

B(0,\delta)=\left\{x\in E;\ \|x\|\leq\delta\right\},\qquad\delta>0.

Note this topology need not be locally convex if
\|\cdot\|
is not a norm. Moreover, every point admits a countable neighborhood basis.

We say that a quasi-norm on
E
is
r
-convex
with
r\in(0,1]
if

\|x+y\|^{r}\leq\|x\|^{r}+\|y\|^{r}\qquad\forall x,y\in E.

This property of
r
-convexity implies the quasi-triangular inequality with a specific constant:

\|x+y\|\leq 2^{\frac{1-r}{r}}\big(\|x\|+\|y\|\big),\qquad x,y\in E.

Moreover, the
r
-convexity implies that the function
d(x,y):=\|x-y\|^{r}
,
x,y\in E
, is a metric that defines the same topology as the original quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|
. Conversely, any quasi-norm is equivalent to an
r
-convex quasi-norm for some
r\in(0,1]
(see, e.g.,
[
39
, Theorem 1.3]
). As a result any quasi-norm topology is metrizable.

Definition 2.2
.

A
quasi-Banach space
is a quasi-normed space whose topology is complete, i.e., every Cauchy sequence is convergent.

Remark 2.3
.

The quasi-triangular inequality (
2.8
) implies the inequalities,

\|x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}\|\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}C^{j}\|x_{j}\|,\qquad x_{j}\in E.

Thus, if
E
is a quasi-Banach space, and we have

\sum_{j\geq 0}C^{j}\|x_{j}\|<\infty,\qquad x_{j}\in E,

then the series
\sum x_{j}
converges in
E
(see
[
43
, Lemma 3.2.3]
). Conversely, this property characterizes the completeness of a quasi-norm
(see the proof of
[
43
, Theorem 3.1.2]
; see also
[
33
, Theorem 5.1]
for a proof in the setting of normed vector space).

Definition 2.4
.

A
quasi-Banach ideal
is a two-sided ideal
J
with a complete quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{J}
such that

\|ATB\|_{J}\leq\|A\|\|T\|_{J}\|B\|\qquad\text{for all $T\in J$ and $A,B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H})$}.

If
\|\cdot\|_{J}
is a norm, then we say that
J
is a
Banach ideal.

Example 2.5
.

If
p>0
, then the
Schatten class
,

\mathscr{L}_{p}:=\big\{T\in\mathscr{K};\ \sum\mu_{j}(T)^{p}<\infty\},

is a quasi-Banach ideal with quasi-norm,

\|T\|_{p}:=\big(\sum\mu_{j}(T)^{p}\big)^{1/p},\qquad T\in\mathscr{L}_{p}.

This is actually a Banach ideal for
p\geq 1
.

Example 2.6
.

For
p>0
, the
weak Schatten class
,

\mathscr{L}_{p,\infty}:=\big\{T\in\mathscr{K};\ \mu_{j}(T)=\operatorname{O}(j^{-\frac{1}{p}})\big\},

is a quasi-Banach ideal with quasi-norm,

\|T\|_{p,\infty}:=\sup_{j\geq 0}(j+1)^{\frac{1}{p}}\mu_{j}(T),\qquad T\in\mathscr{L}_{p,\infty}.

Remark 2.7
.

Every two-sided ideal
J
of
\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H})
is contained in
\mathscr{K}
and contains the ideal of finite-rank operators. Moreover, properties of the polar decomposition imply that

\big(T\in J\Longleftrightarrow|T|\in J\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big(T\in J\Longleftrightarrow T^{*}\in J\big).

If in addition
J
is a quasi-Banach ideal, then (
2.2
) implies that

\displaystyle\|T\|_{J}=\|T^{*}\|_{J}=\||T|\|_{J},

\displaystyle\|U^{*}TU\|_{J}=\|T\|_{J}\qquad\forall U\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),\ U\ \text{unitary}.

In fact, if
S,T\in J
, then

\big(\mu_{j}(S)=\mu_{j}(T)\quad\forall j\geq 0\big)\ \Longrightarrow\ \|S\|_{J}=\|T\|_{J}.

More generally, it can be shown that

\big(T\in J\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{j}(S)\leq\mu_{j}(T)\quad\forall j\geq 0\big)\ \Longrightarrow\ \big(S\in J\quad\text{and}\quad\|S\|_{J}\leq\|T\|_{J}\big).

Remark 2.8
.

We refer to
[
35
,
43
,
60
]
for further background on Banach ideals and quasi-Banach ideals.

2.3.
Functions of regular variation

In what follows, we shall say that a real-valued function
g(t)
defined on an interval
[a,\infty)
is
ultimately increasing
(resp.,
ultimately decreasing
) if it is increasing (resp., decreasing) on some interval
[b,\infty)
with
b\geq a
.

Definition 2.9
.

An
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}
-function
g:[a,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is
slowly varying
if

(2.9)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(\lambda t)}{g(t)}=1\qquad\forall\lambda>0.

Remark 2.10
.

If
g(t)
is ultimately increasing or decreasing, then (
2.9
) is equivalent to

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(2t)}{g(t)}=1.

Remark 2.11

(see
[
5
, Proposition 1.3.6]
)
.

If
g(t)
is slowly varying, then, for all
\rho>0
, we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{\rho}g(t)=\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-\rho}g(t)=0.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
g:[a,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be an
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}
-function. For
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, we say that
g(t)
has
regular variation of index

\rho
, or is
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
, if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

g(t)
is ultimately monotonic.

(ii)

We have

(2.10)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(\lambda t)}{g(t)}=\lambda^{\rho}\qquad\forall\lambda>0.

Remark 2.13
.

If
\rho>0
(resp.,
\rho<0
), then (i)–(ii) imply that
g(t)
is ultimately increasing (resp., decreasing).

Remark 2.14
.

In our terminology an
\textup{RV}_{0}
-function is a slowly varying function that is either ultimately increasing or ultimately decreasing.

Remark 2.15
.

An
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}
-function
g:[a,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
if it is ultimately monotonic and
t^{-\rho}g(t)
is slowly varying. In particular, it follows from Remark
2.11
that we have

(2.11)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-\alpha}g(t)=\infty\quad\text{if $\alpha<\rho$},\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-\alpha}g(t)=0\quad\text{if $\alpha>\rho$}.

This implies that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}g(t)=0\quad\text{if $\rho<0$},\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}g(t)=\infty\quad\text{if $\rho>0$}.

In addition, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt<\infty\quad\text{if $\rho<-1$},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt=\infty\quad\text{if $\rho>-1$}.

Remark 2.16
.

By the Uniform Convergence Theorem (UCT) (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Theorem 1.5.2]
) the convergence (
2.10
) holds uniformly with respect to
\lambda
as it varies within compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
. If
\rho>0
(resp.,
\rho<0
), we even have uniform convergence on each interval
(0,a]
(resp.,
[a,\infty)
).

Throughout this paper we will make frequent use of the following consequence of the UCT.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
g:[a,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
,
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

If
t_{\alpha}\rightarrow\infty
and
\lambda_{\alpha}\rightarrow\lambda>0
, then
g(\lambda_{\alpha}t_{\alpha})\sim\lambda^{\rho}g(t_{\alpha})
.

(2)

If
\lim u(s)=\lim v(s)=\infty
and
u(s)\sim v(s)
for
s
large, then
g(u(s))\sim g(v(s))
.

Remark 2.18
.

Part (2) follows from part (1) by taking
t_{\alpha}=u(s)
and
\lambda_{\alpha}=v(s)/u(s)
.

Remark 2.19
.

As a first consequence of Lemma
2.17
, we see that if
g:[a,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
, then

(2.12)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(t+b)}{g(t)}=1\qquad\forall b\in\mathbb{R}.

Example 2.20
.

The following are among the most well-known examples of
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
-functions on
[0,\infty)
.

•

g(t)=t^{\rho}
,
\rho>0
, and more generally
g(t)=(t+a)^{\rho}
,
\rho\neq 0
with
a>0
.

•

g(t)=(\log(t+2))^{q}
,
q\neq 0
(these functions are
\textup{RV}_{0}
).

•

g(t)=t^{\rho}(\log(t+2))^{q}
,
\rho\neq 0
,
q\neq 0
.

Theorem 2.21

(Karamata; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
14
]
)
.

Let
g:[a,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
,
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

If
\rho>-1
, or if
\rho=-1
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt=\infty
, then

(2.13)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{tg(t)}{\int_{0}^{t}g(s)ds}=\rho+1.

(2)

If
\rho<-1
, or if
\rho=-1
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt<\infty
, then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{tg(t)}{\int_{t}^{\infty}g(s)ds}=|\rho+1|.

Remark 2.22
.

We refer to
[
5
,
14
]
for further background on slowly varying functions and functions of regular variation.

Remark 2.23
.

In the rest of the article we will solely consider
continuous

RV
-functions. On the one hand, in practice the main examples of
RV
-functions that show up are continuous and even smooth. On the other hand, in this paper, we are only interested in the values of these functions at integer points. It follows from a result of Adamović (see
[

, Proposition 1.3.4]
) that if
h(t)
is an
RV
-function we always can find an ultimately monotonic
smooth
function
h_{1}(t)
such that
h_{1}(t)\sim h(t)
and
h_{1}=h
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Therefore, there is no loss of generality of restricting ourselves to continuous (or even smooth)
RV
-functions.

2.4.
Weak Lorentz ideals

In what follows, we let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
-function with
\rho<0
. We also define

G(t):=\int_{0}^{t}g(s)ds,\qquad t\geq 0.

In addition, given any operator
T\in\mathscr{K}
, we set

\|T\|_{g}:=\sup_{j\geq 0}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

Lemma 2.24
.

The following hold.

(i)

Let
T\in\mathscr{K}
. We have

\displaystyle\|\lambda T\|_{g}=|\lambda|\,\|T\|_{g}\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\|ATB\|_{g}\leq\|A\|\|T\|_{g}\|B\|\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}).

(ii)

There is
C>0
such that

(2.14)

\|S+T\|_{g}\leq C\big(\|S\|_{g}+\|T\|_{g}\big)\qquad\forall S,T\in\mathscr{K}.

Remark 2.25
.

The first part is a direct consequence of (
2.1
) and (
2.4
). The second part is a standard consequence of Fan’s inequality (
2.6
). For the reader’s convenience a proof of this result is given in Appendix

.

Definition 2.26

(see
[
43
]
)
.

The
weak Lorentz ideal
associated with
g
is

\mathscr{L}_{g}:=\left\{T\in\mathscr{K};\ \mu_{j}(T)=\operatorname{O}\left(g(j)\right)\right\}.

Lemma
2.24
ensures that
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is a two-sided ideal on which
\|\cdot\|_{g}
is a quasi-norm satisfying (
2.14
). We actually have the following result.

Proposition 2.27
.

\mathscr{L}_{g}
is a quasi-Banach ideal.

Remark 2.28
.

The only part that needs to be proved is the completeness of the quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{g}
. This can be deduced from Calkin’s correspondence for quasi-Banach ideals as described in
[

, §3]
. For the reader’s convenience, a direct proof of this property is given in Appendix

Example 2.29
.

If

Remark 2.30

If

3.
Dixmier Traces and Connes’ Integration on Weak Lorentz Ideals

In this section, we extend to weak Lorentz ideals the approach of
[
43
,
49
,
62
]
to Dixmier traces and to Connes’ integral on the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
. This simplifies previous approaches to Dixmier traces on Lorentz ideals and provides full analogues for weak Lorentz ideals of the results of
[
43
,
49
]
.

Throughout this section, we let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{-1}
function such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt=\infty
. We also set
G(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(s)ds
. These assumptions ensure that

(3.1)

\sum_{j<N}g(j)=G(N)+\operatorname{O}(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{j\geq 0}g(j)=\infty.

In particular, this implies that
\mathscr{L}_{1}\subsetneq\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

In addition, by Karamata Theorem (Theorem
2.21
) we have

(3.2)

tg(t)=\operatorname{o}(G(t))\qquad\text{as $t\rightarrow\infty$}.

3.1.
Traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}

By definition, the
commutator space
of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is

\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g}):=\operatorname{Span}\left\{[A,T];\ A\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),\ T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}\right\}.

Using the fact that every operator
A\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H})
is a linear combination of 4 unitary operators (see, e.g.,
[
52
, §VI.6]
), it can be shown that

(3.3)

\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g}):=\operatorname{Span}\left\{U^{*}TU-T;\ T\in\mathscr{L}_{g},\ U\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),\ U\ \text{unitary}\right\}.

We shall say that a linear functional
\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a
trace
if it annihilates the commutator space
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
. That is,

\varphi(AT)=\varphi(TA)\qquad\forall T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}\ \forall A\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}).

In fact, in view of (
3.3
), the above trace property is equivalent to being
unitarily invariant
, i.e.,

(3.4)

\varphi(U^{*}TU)=\varphi(T)\qquad T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}\ \forall U\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),\ U\ \text{unitary}.

More generally, given any subspace
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{g}
containing
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
we shall call a trace any linear functional on
\mathscr{E}
that annihilates

\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
.

We also recall that a linear functional
\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is called
positive
if

\left(T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}\quad\text{and}\quad T\geq 0\right)\ \Longrightarrow\ \varphi(T)\geq 0.

3.2.
Inequalities for eigenvalues

For an arbitrary compact operator
T
, the non-zero part of the spectrum of
T
consists of isolated eigenvalues. For each
\lambda\in\operatorname{Sp}(T)\setminus\{0\}
, the associated root space is
E_{\lambda}(T):=\cup_{j\geq 0}\ker(T-\lambda)^{j}
. This is a finite-dimensional space and its dimension is called the
algebraic multiplicity
of
\lambda
.

Definition 3.1
.

An
eigenvalue sequence
for
T
is any sequence
\lambda(T)=(\lambda_{j}(T))_{j\geq 0}
consisting of all eigenvalues of
T
in such a way that

•

Each eigenvalue is repeated according to its algebraic multiplicity.

•

We have

|\lambda_{0}(T)|\geq|\lambda_{1}(T)|\geq|\lambda_{2}(T)|\geq\cdots\geq 0.

Remark 3.2
.

If
T\geq 0
, then
\mu(T)=(\mu_{j}(T))_{j\geq 0}
is the unique eigenvalue sequence of
T
.

Remark 3.3
.

In general, an eigenvalue sequence need not be unique.

It is immediate that if
\lambda(T)
is an eigenvalue sequence for
T
, then
c\lambda(T)
is an eigenvalue sequence for
cT
for all
c\in\mathbb{C}
. In addition, we have the following Weyl’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
35
,
60
]
),

(3.5)

\sum_{j<N}|\lambda_{j}(T)|\leq\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T).

In what follows, given any operator
T\in\mathscr{K}
, by
\lambda(T)
we shall mean an arbitrary eigenvalue sequence for
T
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the approach of
[
43
,
49
,
62
]
to Dixmier traces on the weak trace
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
relies on the asymptotic additivity property (
1.3
). The key observation in this section is that we have an analogous result for operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

For one thing, it was shown by Kalton
[
38
, Proposition 3.2]
that any quasi-Banach ideal is geometrically stable, and hence is closed under logarithmic submajorization in the sense of
[
64
]
(see
[
64
, Lemma 35]
; see also
[
43
, Proposition 2.4.18]
). In particular, the ideal
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is closed under logarithmic submajorization. We may thus apply
[
43
, Lemma 5.6.4]
to get that, for all
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

(3.6)

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S+T)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S)+\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{O}\left(Ng(N)\right).

On the other hand, by the Karamata asymptotic (
2.13
) we have
Ng(N)=\operatorname{o}(G(N))
. Therefore, we arrive at the following result.

Proposition 3.4

(Asymptotic Additivity)
.

Given any operators
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S+T)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S)+\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right).

We mention a few direct consequences of Proposition
3.4
.

Corollary 3.5
.

If
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and
\lambda(T)
and
\lambda^{\prime}(T)
are eigenvalue sequences for
T
, then

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}^{\prime}(T)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right).

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
3.4
for
S=0
and
\lambda(S+T)=\lambda^{\prime}(T)
.
∎

Corollary 3.6

(Kalton
[
38
]
)
.

If
T\in\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, then

(3.7)

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right).

Proof.

In view of (
3.3
) and Proposition
3.4
it is enough to prove the result for operators of the form,

U^{*}TU-T,\qquad T\in\mathscr{L}_{g},\quad U\in\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{H}),\ U\ \text{unitary}.

We just need to apply Proposition
3.4
to the pair
(U^{*}TU,-T)
with
\lambda(U^{*}TU)=\lambda(T)=-\lambda(-T)
. This is possible, since
U^{*}TU
and
T
have the same eigenvalues with same multiplicity. We thus get

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(U^{*}TU-T)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\sum_{j<N}\left(-\lambda_{j}(T)\right)+\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right)=\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right).

This gives the result.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
T\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, then

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=\operatorname{o}\left(G(N)\right).

Proof.

As
T\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, we have
\mu_{j}(T)=\operatorname{o}(g(j))
. Combining this with (
3.5
) and (
3.7
) gives the result.
∎

Corollary
3.6
is actually a special case of a much more general result, which we mention for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.8

(
[
43
, Theorem 5.1.5]
; see also
[
38
]
)
.

If
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, then

(3.8)

S-T\in\operatorname{Com}\left(\mathscr{L}_{g}\right)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(S)=\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{O}(Ng(N)).

In particular, we have

(3.9)

T\in\operatorname{Com}\left(\mathscr{L}_{g}\right)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=\operatorname{O}(Ng(N)).

Remark 3.9
.

The above result is proved in
[
43
]
for ideals that are closed under logarithmic sub-majorization. The spectral characterization of commutators provided by (
3.9
) was originally proved by Kalton
[
38
]
for geometrically stable ideals.
We also note that we can get (
3.8
) from (
3.9
) by using (
3.6
).

Remark 3.10
.

The above result was proved in
[
30
]
in the general setting of geometrically stable ideals. Every quasi-Banach ideal is geometrically stable (see
[
38
]
). This result is further extended to ideals closed under logarithmic submajorization in
[
43
, Theorem 5.6.1]
.

3.3.
Extended limits

In what follows we let
\ell_{\infty}=\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be the unital
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded complex-valued sequences
a=(a_{j})_{j\geq 0}
with norm,

\|a\|_{\infty}=\sup_{j\geq 0}|a_{j}|,\qquad a=(a_{j})_{j\geq 0}\in\ell_{\infty}.

Let
\mathfrak{c}_{0}
be the (closed) ideal of sequences converging to zero. The quotient algebra
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra with respect to the quotient norm and the induced involution. Moreover, the quotient map
\pi:\ell_{\infty}\rightarrow\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
is a contracting
*
-homomorphism.

Definition 3.11
.

An
extended limit
is a positive linear functional
\omega:\ell_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that:

(i)

\omega(1)=1
, where
1
is the constant sequence whose entries are all equal to
1
.

(ii)

\omega(a)=0
for all sequences
a=(a_{j})_{j\geq 0}\in\mathfrak{c}_{0}
.

We denote by
\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
the set of extended limits.

Remark 3.12
.

Together (i)–(ii) mean that

(3.10)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}a_{j}=L\ \Longrightarrow\ \omega(a)=L.

Thus, an extended limit is precisely a
positive
extension of the limit.

Remark 3.13
.

If
\omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, then its positivity has some consequences. Given any
a=(a_{j})_{j\geq 0}\in\ell_{\infty}
, we have

\omega(\overline{a})=\overline{\omega(a)},\qquad\Re(\omega(a))=\omega\left(\Re a\right),\qquad\Im(\omega(a))=\omega\left(\Im a\right).

Moreover, together with the fact that
\omega(1)=1
positivity implies that

|\omega(a)|\leq\omega\left(|a|\right)\leq\omega(\|a\|_{\infty}\cdot 1)=\|a\|_{\infty}.

Thus,
\omega
is a continuous linear form on
\ell_{\infty}
with
\|\omega\|=1
. Conversely, any continuous linear form
\ell:\ell_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
\ell(1)=1
and
\|\ell\|=1
must be positive (see, e.g.,
[
23
, pp. 83–84]
).

Remark 3.14
.

If
\omega:\ell_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is an extended limit, then its positivity and (i)–(ii) ensure that
\omega
descends to a (positive) state on the unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
. Conversely, any state
\omega:\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
uniquely lifts to a positive linear form on
\ell_{\infty}
satisfying (i)–(ii). We thus have a natural one-to-one correspondence between
\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
and the states on
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
a=(a_{j})\in\ell_{\infty}
, and set
\operatorname{EL}(a):=\left\{\omega(a);\ \omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\right\}
.

(i)

\operatorname{EL}(a)
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
containing all the cluster points of
a
.

(ii)

If
a
is real-valued, then

\operatorname{EL}(a)=\left[\liminf a,\limsup a\right].

(iii)

We have

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}a_{j}=L\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \big(\omega(a)=L\quad\forall\omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\big).

Proof.

The convexity of
\operatorname{EL}(a)
is a direct consequence of the convexity of
\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. Suppose that
\alpha
is a cluster point of
a
. Let
(a_{k_{j}})_{j\geq 0}
be a subsequence converging to
\alpha
. Given
\omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, we define another extended limit
\tilde{\omega}
by

\tilde{\omega}(b):=\omega\left(\{b_{k_{j}}\}\right),\qquad b=(b_{j})\in\ell_{\infty}.

We then have
\tilde{\omega}(a)=\omega(\{a_{k_{j}}\})=\alpha
, and hence
\alpha\in\operatorname{EL}(a)
. This proves (i).

Assume that
a
is real-valued. As
\liminf a
and
\limsup a
are cluster points of
a
, part (i) implies that
[\liminf a,\limsup a]\subseteq\operatorname{EL}(a)
. Moreover, we
have the obvious inequalities,

\inf_{k\geq j}a_{k}\leq a_{j}\leq\sup_{k\geq j}a_{k}\qquad\forall j\geq 0.

Therefore, if
\omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, then its positivity and (
3.10
) imply

\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}a_{j}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\big(\inf_{k\geq j}a_{k}\leq a_{j}\big)\leq\omega(a)\leq\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\big(\sup_{k\geq j}a_{k}\big)=\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}a_{j}.

This proves part (ii).

Part (iii) follows from (ii) and (
3.10
). The proof is complete.
∎

3.4.
Dixmier traces

If
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, then, as in (
2.15
), the Weyl’s inequality (
3.5
) implies that

(3.11)

\bigg|\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\bigg|\leq\sum_{j<N}|\lambda_{j}(T)|\leq\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)\leq G(N)\|T\|_{G}.

In particular, we see that

\bigg\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\bigg\}_{N\geq 0}\in\ell_{\infty}.

(By convention
\sum_{j<0}\lambda_{j}(T)=0
.) Moreover, it follows from Corollary
3.5
that the class

\pi(\lambda(T))\in\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
does not depend on the choice of the eigenvalue sequence
\lambda(T)
for
T
. It follows that, given any extended limit
\omega
, we have a well defined functional
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

(3.12)

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T):=\omega\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\bigg\}_{N\geq 0}\bigg),

where
\lambda(T)
is any eigenvalue sequence for
T
.

Proposition 3.16
.

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
is a normalized positive trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
annihilating
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
. Moreover, we have

(3.13)

\left|\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)\right|\leq\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}\left(|T|\right)\leq\|T\|_{G}\qquad\forall T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}.

Proof.

It is immediate from Proposition
3.4
that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
is additive. If
c\in\mathbb{C}
, then
c\lambda(T)
is an eigenvalue sequence for
\lambda(cT)
, and so we have

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(cT)=\omega\bigg(c\bigg\{G(N)^{-1}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\bigg\}\bigg)=c\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T).

It also follows from Corollary
3.6
and Corollary
3.7
that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
annihilates
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})\cup(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
. In addition, if
T\geq 0
, then
\lambda(T)=\mu(T)
, and so we have

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)=\omega\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)\bigg\}\bigg)\geq 0.

Therefore,
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
is a positive trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
that annihilates
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
. The inequalities (
3.13
) follow from (
3.11
) and (
3.12
). The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

The inequalities (
3.13
) and the continuity of the inclusion of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
into
\mathscr{M}_{G}
implies that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
is a continuous trace. In fact, every positive trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is continuous (see Proposition
5.3
).

Remark 3.18
.

The fact that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
annihilates
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
implies it is a singular trace, i.e., it annihilates finite-rank operators. It is actually a general fact that if
J
is a Banach ideal that does not contain
\mathscr{L}_{1}
, then every continuous trace on
J
is singular (see
[
43
, Theorem 3.5.8]
).

Definition 3.19
.

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
is called the
Dixmier trace
associated with the extended limit
\omega
.

Remark 3.20
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and set
\operatorname{DT}(T):=\{\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T);\ \omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\}
. Given any eigenvalue sequence
\lambda(T)
,
Lemma
3.15
implies the following:

•

\operatorname{DT}(T)
is a convex set containing all the cluster points of the sequence
\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\}
.

•

If
T
is selfadjoint, then

\operatorname{DT}(T)=\bigg[\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T),\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\bigg].

Remark 3.21
.

Dixmier
[
27
]
originally defined his traces on the larger Banach ideal
\mathscr{M}_{G}
in terms of extended limits that are invariant under the dilation
(a_{j})_{j\geq 0}\rightarrow(a_{[j/2]})_{j\geq 0}
. Dixmier observed that, for any dilation-invariant extended limit
\omega
, we define an additive functional on the positive cone of
\mathscr{M}_{G}
by letting

\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)=\omega\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)\bigg\}\bigg),\qquad T\in(\mathscr{M}_{G})_{+}.

Therefore, it uniquely extends to a positive trace on
\mathscr{M}_{G}
, which is called a Dixmier trace as well.

Note that Proposition
3.4
does not hold for operators in
\mathscr{M}_{G}
. In fact, even if the formula (
3.12
) makes sense for operators in
\mathscr{M}_{G}
, this need not define an additive functional on
\mathscr{M}_{G}
(see
[
43
, Example 6.6.1]
). However, as shown by Sedaev-Sukochev
[
55
, Corollary 18]
, given any extended limit
\omega
, there is always a dilation-invariant extended limit
\omega^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
agrees on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
with the Dixmier trace
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega^{\prime}}
(which is defined on
\mathscr{M}_{G}
). Thus, every Dixmier trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
in the sense of (
3.12
) is the restriction of a Dixmier trace on
\mathscr{M}_{G}
in the sense of Dixmier.

3.5.
Measurable operators

Definition 3.22
.

An operator
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is called
measurable
(or
Dixmier-measurable
) if all Dixmier traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
take the same values on
T
.

We denote by
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
the set of measurable operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

Definition 3.23
.

If
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is measurable, then its
noncommutative integral
is defined by

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T:=\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T),

where
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
is any Dixmier trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

Remark 3.24
.

In the case of the weak trace-class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, i.e.,
g(t)=t+1
, we shall denote the NC integral just by
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int
, since this is the usual notation in this case.

Proposition 3.25
.

The following hold.

(1)

\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is closed subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
containing
\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}
. In particular,
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
are subspaces of

\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
.

(2)

The NC integral
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}:\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a continuous positive linear trace that annihilates
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
.

Proof.

By definition,

\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})=\bigcap_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\big\{T\in\mathscr{L}_{g};\ \operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega^{\prime}}(T)\big\},

where
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
range over all states on
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
. As the Dixmier traces
\operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}
are continuous linear maps, it follows that
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is a closed subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. This gives the first part. The second part follows from Proposition
3.16
and the fact that
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}
agrees with any Dixmier trace on
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
.
∎

3.6.
Spectral characterization

For the weak trace
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, the equivalence (
1.2
) provides a spectral characterization of measurable operators and a spectral interpretation of the NC integral (see
[
20
,
43
,
49
]
). The following statement extends this result to all weak Lorentz ideals.

Theorem 3.26
.

Given any
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is measurable and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}=L
.

(ii)

{\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}G(N)^{-1}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L}
for
some
eigenvalue sequence
\lambda(T)
.

(iii)

{\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}G(N)^{-1}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L}
for
every
eigenvalue sequence
\lambda(T)
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.5
, if
\lambda(T)
and
\lambda^{\prime}(T)
are eigenvalue sequences for
T
, then

\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}^{\prime}(T)=\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)+\operatorname{o}(1).

Thus, if a sequence
\big(G(N)^{-1}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\big)_{j\geq 0}
has a limit for
some
eigenvalue sequence, then it has a limit for
every
eigenvalue sequence, and the value of the limit does not depend on the choice of the eigenvalue sequence. This gives the equivalence of (ii) and (iii).

Moreover, if
\lambda(T)
is any eigenvalue sequence for
T
, then it follows from Remark
3.20
that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L\;\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \omega\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\bigg\}\bigg)=L\quad\forall\omega\in\operatorname{EL}(\mathbb{N}_{0})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \operatorname{Tr}_{\omega}(T)=L\quad\forall\omega

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ T\ \text{is measurable and}\ \sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=L.

It follows from this that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) and (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii), and so (i),(ii), and (iii) are equivalent. The proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 3.27
.

Let
g_{1}(t)
,
t\geq 0
, be any
\textup{RV}_{-1}
-function such that
g_{1}(t)\sim g(t)
. Then the spaces
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g_{1}})
and
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
agree, and on there
\sideset{{}^{g_{1}}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}=\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}
.

Proof.

Set
G_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g_{1}(s)ds
. As
g_{1}(t)\sim g(t)
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt=\infty
, we see that
G_{1}(t)\sim G(t)
. Thus, if
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, then

\bigg(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G_{1}(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L\bigg)\Longleftrightarrow\bigg(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=L\bigg)

Combining this with Theorem
3.26
gives the result.
∎

Let
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
be another Hilbert space. In order to distinguish them we shall denote by
\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H})
and
\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H}^{\prime})
the quasi-Banach ideals of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
-operators on
\mathscr{H}
and
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
, respectively.

In what follows, we shall say that two operators
S\in\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H})
and
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H}^{\prime})
have the same
non-zero spectrum up to multiplicity
if they have the same non-zero eigenvalues with the same (algebraic) multiplicities. Equivalently, any eigenvalue sequence for one operator is an eigenvalue sequence for the other operator.

The very fact that Theorem
3.26
provides us with a characterization of measurability and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}
in terms of eigenvalue sequence immediately implies a strong form of spectral invariance. Namely, we have the following result.

Corollary 3.28
.

If
S\in\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H})
and
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H}^{\prime})
have the same non-zero spectrum up to multiplicity, then
S
is measurable if and only if
T
is measurable. Moreover, in this case
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}S=\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T
.

In the special case where
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
is the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
with an equivalent inner product we obtain the following result.

Corollary 3.29
.

\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g}(\mathscr{H}))
and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}
do not depend on the inner product of
\mathscr{H}
.

3.7.
Connes’ question

From the sole perspective of spectral theory, Theorem
3.26
implies the following result.

Proposition 3.30
.

The following formula,

(3.14)

{\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}}^{\prime}T:=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\quad\text{whenever the limit exists},

defines a positive continuous linear form with the following properties:

(i)

Its domain is a closed subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
containing the commutator subspace
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and the separable ideal
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
.

(ii)

It is positive and continuous on its domain.

(iii)

Its annihilates both
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, and so this is a singular trace on its domain.

In the case of the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, Connes asked for a direct proof of Proposition
3.30
that does not involve any use or reference to extended limits (see
[
49
, Question A]
). Extended limits are in one-to-one correspondence with states on the
C^{*}
-algebra
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
. Their existence is then a consequence of applying the Hahn-Banach theorem on
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
. As
\ell_{\infty}/\mathfrak{c}_{0}
is non-separable, this requires assuming the Axiom of Choice. Therefore, one motivation of Connes was to obtain a construction of the NC integral that does not rely on the Axiom of Choice.

A positive answer to Connes’ question was provided in
[
49
]
. This was a consequence of a version of Proposition
3.4
for the weak trace
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
that was established in the first edition of
[
43
]
.

As we shall now proceed to show, the full version of Proposition
3.4
enables us to get a direct proof of Proposition
3.30
. This will extend the scope of applicability of the answer to Connes’ question to all weak Lorentz ideals.

Direct proof of Proposition
3.30
.

The first part of the proof of Theorem
3.26
shows that Proposition
3.4
implies the equivalence of (ii) and (iii). Note that the arguments there do not make any reference to extended limits. Therefore, the domain of
{\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}}^{\prime}
is

\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g}):=\biggl\{T\in\mathscr{L}_{g};\ \lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)\ \textup{exists}\biggr\}.

In the terminology of
[
43
]
the operators in
\mathscr{M}^{g}_{T}
are called Tauberian operators.

The fact that
\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is a subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}
is a linear functional is a direct consequence of Proposition
3.4
. It then is immediate from its definition that
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}
is a positive linear functional. It follows from Corollary
3.6
and Corollary
3.7
that if
T\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}\cup\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, then the r.h.s. of (
3.14
) vanishes. This means that
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})\cup(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
is contained in
\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and annihilated by
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}
. In particular,
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}
is a singular trace on
\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
.

It remains to show that
\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is closed and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}
is continuous. For
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, set

\tau_{N}(T):=\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T),\qquad N\geq 1.

It follows from Proposition
3.4
and the inequalities (
3.11
) that, for all
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{N}(S)-\tau_{N}(T)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\tau_{N}(S-T)|+\operatorname{o}(1)

\displaystyle\leq\|S-T\|_{G}+\operatorname{o}(1).

Using these inequalities it can be shown that, if
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
(T_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}\subseteq\mathscr{M}^{g}_{T}
converges to
T
in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, then the following hold:

(a)

\bigg\{\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}T_{\ell}\bigg\}_{\ell\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence, and hence has a limit.

(b)

We have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{N}(T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}{\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}}^{\prime}T_{\ell}.

That is,
T\in\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}T=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}T_{\ell}
. This shows that
\mathscr{M}_{T}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is closed and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}^{\prime}
is continuous. This completes the direct proof of Proposition
3.30
.
∎

4.
Birman-Solomyak Perturbation Theory for Weak Lorentz Ideals

In this section, we extend to weak Lorentz ideals the perturbation theory of Birman-Solomyak for eigenvalues and singular values of operators in weak-Schatten classes
\mathscr{L}_{p,\infty}
,
p>0
(see, e.g.,
[
6
, §4]
and
[
10
, §11.6]
). The main observation is that the
RV
-property is the very property that allows to extend the original approach of Birman-Solomyak to weak Lorentz ideals.

Throughout this section we let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
function with
\rho<0
.

4.1.
Positive and negative eigenvalues of compact operators

If
T
is a selfadjoint compact operator, then its spectrum consists of real eigenvalues with finite multiplicity. We then denote by
(\pm\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T))
its sequences of positive and negative eigenvalues ordered in such a way that

\lambda_{0}^{\pm}(T)\geq\lambda_{1}^{\pm}(T)\geq\lambda_{2}^{\pm}(T)\geq\cdots\geq 0,

where each eigenvalue is repeated according to multiplicity. Equivalently, we have

\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)=\mu_{j}(T^{\pm}),\qquad j\geq 0,

where
T^{\pm}
are the positive and negative parts of
T
, i.e.,

T^{+}=\frac{1}{2}\big(|T|+T),\qquad T^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\big(|T|-T).

Note that

\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(cT)=c\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T),\quad c>0,\qquad\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(-T)=\lambda_{j}^{\mp}(T).

We have the following version of the min-max principle for positive and negative eigenvalues (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 9.2.4]
).

\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)=\min_{\dim E=j}\max_{\xi\in E\setminus\{0\}}\pm\frac{\left\langle T\xi|\xi\right\rangle}{\left\langle\xi|\xi\right\rangle}.

Comparing this to the min-max principle (
2.1
) shows that

(4.1)

0\leq\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)\leq\mu_{j}(T).

In addition, we have the following Weyl’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 9.2.8]
),

(4.2)

\lambda_{j+k}^{\pm}(S+T)\leq\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(S)+\lambda_{k}^{\pm}(T).

4.2.
Quasi-norm topology on
\mathscr{L}_{g}/(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}

Given any
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we shall denote by
\dot{T}
its class in the quotient space
\dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}:=\mathscr{L}_{g}/(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
. We equip

\dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}
with the topology of the quasi-norm defined by

\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}:=\inf\big\{\|T+S\|_{g};\ S\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}\big\},\qquad T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}.

By definition,
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
is the closure of the ideal
\mathscr{R}
of finite-rank operators. Hence,

\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}:=\inf\big\{\|T+S\|_{g};\ S\in\mathscr{R}\big\}=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathscr{L}_{g}}(T;\mathscr{R}).

Therefore, using Lemma
2.32
, we obtain

(4.3)

\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}=\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

Lemma 4.1
.

The quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{g}}
is
r
-convex with
r:=(|\rho|+1)^{-1}
, i.e.,

(4.4)

\|\dot{S}+\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}\leq\|\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}+\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r},\qquad S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}.

Proof.

Let
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
. Given
j\geq 0
, let
k_{j}
be the unique integer such that
k_{j}\leq j\theta<k_{j}+1
. We thus have

j\theta-1<k_{j}\leq j\theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad j(1-\theta)\leq j-k_{j}<j(1-\theta)+1.

As
k_{j}\sim\theta j
and
g(t)
is
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
, it follows from Lemma
2.17
that

(4.5)

g(k_{j})\sim g(\theta j)\sim\theta^{\rho}g(j).

Likewise, as
j-k_{j}\sim(1-\theta)j
, we have

(4.6)

g(j-k_{j})\sim g\left((1-\theta)j\right)\sim(1-\theta)^{\rho}g(j).

Bearing this in mind, by Fan’s inequality (
2.6
) we have

\mu_{j}(S+T)\leq\mu_{k_{j}}(S)+\mu_{j-k_{j}}(T).

Thus,

g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(S+T)\leq\frac{g(k_{j})}{g(j)}\cdot g(k_{j})^{-1}\mu_{k_{j}}(S)+\frac{g(j-k_{j})}{g(j)}\cdot g(j-k_{j})^{-1}\mu_{j-k_{j}}(T).

Taking
\limsup
with respect to
j
on both sides and using (
4.5
)–(
4.6
) yields

(4.7)

\|\dot{S}+\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}\leq\theta^{\rho}\|\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}+(1-\theta)^{\rho}\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}.

If we set
r:=(|\rho|+1)^{-1}
, then we have

\min_{\theta\in(0,1)}\left\{\theta^{\rho}\|\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}+(1-\theta)^{\rho}\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}\right\}=\big(\|\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}+\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}\big)^{\frac{1}{r}}.

Combining this with (
4.7
) gives (
4.4
). The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

By (
4.4
) the
r
-convexity of
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{g}}
implies the quasi-triangular inequality,

\|\dot{S}+\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}\leq 2^{|\rho|}\big(\|\dot{S}\big\|_{\dot{g}}+\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}\big),\qquad\dot{S},\dot{T}\in\dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}.

Remark 4.3
.

If
F
is closed subspace of quasi-Banach space
E
, then the quotient
E/F
is a quasi-Banach space with respect to the quotient quasi-norm. The completeness of the quotient quasi-norm can be seen by using Remark
4.2
and arguing as in the Banach space setting (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Proposition 11.8]
).
As
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
is a closed subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, it follows that the quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{g}}
is complete. Therefore, we see that
\dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}
is an
r
-convex quasi-Banach space.

4.3.
Quantitative estimates for eigenvalues

For
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, combining the inequality (
4.1
) with the definition of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we obtain

(4.8)

\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)\leq\mu_{j}(T)\leq g(j)\|T\|_{g}\qquad\forall j\geq 0.

We then define

(4.9)

\overline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T):=\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T),\qquad\underline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T):=\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T).

Note that

(4.10)

\big(\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)=c\big)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \big(\underline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T)=\overline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T)=c\big).

Lemma 4.4
.

Set
r=(|\rho|+1)^{-1}
. For all selfadjoint operators
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left|\overline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T)^{r}-\overline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(S)^{r}\right|\leq\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r},

(4.12)

\displaystyle\left|\underline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T)^{r}-\underline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(S)^{r}\right|\leq\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the inequalities for the functionals
\overline{\Lambda}^{+}
and
\underline{\Lambda}^{+}
, since replacing
(S,T)
by
(-S,-T)
gives the
inequalities for
\overline{\Lambda}^{-}
and
\underline{\Lambda}^{-}
. Moreover, by using Weyl’s inequality (
4.2
) and arguing as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
we obtain the inequality,

\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(T)^{r}\leq\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)^{r}+\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(T-S)^{r}.

It follows from (
4.1
) that

\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(T-S)\leq\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T-S)=\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}.

Thus,

\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(T)^{r}-\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)^{r}\leq\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}.

Interchanging the roles of
S
and
T
gives the inequality (
4.11
).

It remains to prove the inequality (
4.12
). It is sufficient to show that

(4.13)

\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(T)^{r}\geq\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)^{r}-\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}.

To prove this inequality we may further assume
\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)^{r}>\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}
, since otherwise the inequality holds trivially.

Let
\theta>1
. As in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, for
j\geq 0
we let
k_{j}
be the unique integer such that
k_{j}\leq j\theta<k_{j}+1
.
The Weyl inequality (
4.2
) gives

\lambda_{k_{j}}^{+}(S)\leq\lambda_{j}(T)+\lambda_{k_{j}-j}(S-T).

Thus,

g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{+}(T)\geq\frac{g(k_{j})}{g(j)}\cdot g(k_{j})^{-1}\lambda_{k_{j}}^{+}(S)-\frac{g(k_{j}-j)}{g(j)}\cdot g(k_{j}-j)^{-1}\lambda_{k_{j}-j}^{+}(S-T).

Taking
\liminf
with respect to
j
on both sides and using (
4.5
)–(
4.6
) gives

\displaystyle\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(T)

\displaystyle\geq\theta^{\rho}\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)-(\theta-1)^{\rho}\overline{\Lambda}^{+}(S-T)

\displaystyle\geq\theta^{\rho}\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)-(\theta-1)^{\rho}\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}.

The inequality (
4.13
) then follows from the fact that

\min_{\theta>1}\left\{\theta^{\rho}\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)-(\theta-1)^{\rho}\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}\right\}=\left(\underline{\Lambda}^{+}(S)^{r}-\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

For weak Schatten classes the quantitative estimates (
4.11
)–(
4.12
) were obtained by Birman-Solomyak (see
[
10
, Corollary 9.6.5]
). Therefore, Lemma
4.4
extends those estimates to all weak Lorentz ideals.

4.4.
Weyl operators

In what follows we shall say that an operator
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is a
Weyl operator
if

\Lambda^{+}(T):=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{+}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{-}(T):=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-}(T)\quad\text{both exist}.

For instance, if
T=T^{*}\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, then it follows from Proposition
2.34
that
T
is a
Weyl operator
with
\Lambda^{\pm}(T)=0
.

More generally, if
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is not selfadjoint, we shall say that
T
is a Weyl operator if its real part
\Re T:=\frac{1}{2}(T+T^{*})
and its imaginary part

\Im T:=\frac{1}{2i}(T-T^{*})
are Weyl operators, in which case we set

\Lambda^{\pm}(T):=\Lambda^{\pm}\big(\Re(T)\big)+i\Lambda^{\pm}\big(\Im(T)\big).

Definition 4.6
.

\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is the set of all Weyl operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

We are now in a position to prove the following perturbation result for eigenvalue asymptotics for operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. This extends to weak Lorentz ideals the perturbation result of Birman-Solomyak
[
6
,
10
]
for operators in weak Schatten classes.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
(T_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}\subseteq\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
be such that

\dot{T}_{\ell}\longrightarrow\dot{T}\ \text{in}\ \dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}.

Then
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell})
both exist,
T\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and we have

\Lambda^{\pm}(T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell}).

In particular, if
T
and the operators
T_{\ell}
are selfadjoint, then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\big(\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T_{\ell})\big).

Proof.

Let us first prove the result in the case where
T
and the operators
T_{\ell}
are selfadjoint. By (
4.11
) we have

\left|\overline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T)^{r}-\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell})^{r}\right|\leq\|\dot{T}-\dot{T}_{\ell}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}.

As
\|\dot{T}-\dot{T}_{\ell}\|_{\dot{g}}\rightarrow 0
as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
, we deduce that

(4.14)

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell})=\overline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T).

Similarly, by using (
4.12
) we get

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(T_{\ell})=\underline{\Lambda}^{\pm}(T).

Combining this with (
4.14
) proves the result in the selfadjoint case.

In general, by Lemma
4.1
we have

\max\left\{\|\Re\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r},\|\Im\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}+\|\dot{T}^{*}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}\right)=\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}.

Therefore, if
\dot{T}_{\ell}\rightarrow\dot{T}
in
\dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}
, then
\Re\dot{T}_{\ell}\rightarrow\Re\dot{T}
and
\Im\dot{T}_{\ell}\rightarrow\Im\dot{T}
. The first part of the proof then ensures that
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(\Re T_{\ell})
and
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(\Im T_{\ell})
both exist,
\Re T
and
\Im T
are Weyl operators, and we have

\Lambda^{\pm}(\Re T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(\Re T_{\ell})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda^{\pm}(\Im T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda^{\pm}(\Im T_{\ell}).

This gives the result in the general case. The proof is complete.
∎

As immediate consequences of Theorem
4.7
we get the following results.

Corollary 4.8
.

\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is a closed subset of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
on which
\Lambda^{\pm}
are continuous functionals.

Corollary 4.9
.

If
T\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and
S\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, then
T+S\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and

\Lambda^{\pm}(T+S)=\Lambda^{\pm}(T).

In particular, if
T
and
S
are selfadjoint, then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T+S)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T).

Remark 4.10
.

For weak Schatten classes the previous result goes back to Weyl
[
69
, Satz V]
(see also
[
10
, §9.6]
). Therefore, Corollary
4.9
extends Weyl’s result to weak Lorentz ideals.

4.5.
Singular values

The perturbation theory for positive and negative eigenvalues extends to singular values as follows.

Definition 4.11
.

|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
consists of operators
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
such that

\Delta(T):=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)\ \text{exists}.

As
\mu_{j}(T)=\lambda_{j}(|T|)=\lambda_{j}^{+}(|T|)
, we see that

\big(\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)\ \text{exists}\big)\Longleftrightarrow|T|\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g}).

That is,
T\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
if and only if
|T|
is a Weyl operator.

In what follows, for
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
we set

\overline{\Delta}(T)=\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underline{\Delta}(T)=\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

In particular, by (
4.3
) we have
\overline{\Delta}(T)=\|\dot{T}\|_{\dot{g}}
. Moreover, as with (
4.10
) we have

\big(T\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})\ \text{and}\ \Delta(T)=c\big)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \big(\overline{\Delta}(T)=\underline{\Delta}(T)=c\big).

We have the following version of Lemma
4.4
for singular values.

Lemma 4.12
.

Set
r=(|\rho|+1)^{-1}
. For all
S,T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\left|\overline{\Delta}(T)^{r}-\overline{\Delta}(S)^{r}\right|\leq\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\left|\underline{\Delta}(T)^{r}-\underline{\Delta}(S)^{r}\right|\leq\|\dot{T}-\dot{S}\|_{\dot{g}}^{r}.

Proof.

The first inequality is a mere restatement of (
4.11
). The second inequality is proved by using Fan’s inequality (
2.6
) and arguing as in the second part of the proof of Lemma
4.4
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

For weak Schatten classes the quantitative estimates (
4.15
)–(
4.16
) were established by Birman-Solomyak (see
[
10
, Corollary 9.6.5]
). Therefore, as with Lemma
4.4
, we see that Lemma
4.12
extends Birman-Solomyak’s estimates to all weak Lorentz ideals.

By using Lemma
4.12
and arguing as in the proof of Theorem
4.7
, we obtain the following perturbation result for asymptotics of singular values for operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. This extends to weak Lorentz ideals the perturbation result of Birman-Solomyak for operators in weak Schatten classes.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
(T_{\ell})\subseteq|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
be such that

\dot{T}_{\ell}\longrightarrow\dot{T}\ \text{in}\ \dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}.

Then
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Delta(T_{\ell})
exists,
T\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and we have

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\Delta(T_{\ell})=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\big(\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T_{\ell})\big).

In particular, we have the following consequences of Theorem
4.14
.

Corollary 4.15
.

|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is a closed subset of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
on which the functional
\Delta
is continuous.

Corollary 4.16
.

If
T\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
and
S\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, then
T+S\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T+S)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

Remark 4.17
.

For weak Schatten classes the previous result goes back to Ky Fan
[
32
, Theorem 3]
(see also
[
35
, Theorem II.2.3]
). It was also conjectured by Gohberg-Krein
[
35
, pp. 32–33]
that Fan’s result holds for weak Lorentz ideals. Therefore, Corollary
4.16
extends Fan’s theorem to all weak Lorentz ideals and gives a positive answer to Gohberg-Krein’s conjecture.

4.6.
Counting functions

The perturbation results of Birman-Solomyak can be equivalently reformulated in terms of counting functions (see, e.g.,
[
10
, §11.6]
). For the sake of completeness, we explain how to reformulate the main results of this section in terms of counting functions.

For
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we set

N^{\pm}(T;\lambda)=\#\left\{j;\ \lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)>\lambda\right\},\qquad\lambda>0.

For
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we also define

\nu(T;\lambda)=\#\left\{j;\ \mu_{j}(T)>\lambda\right\},\qquad\lambda>0.

Set
h(t)=g(t)^{-1}
,
t\geq 0
; this is an
\textup{RV}_{|\rho|}
-function. We also let
h^{\sharp}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be an asymptotic
\textup{RV}_{|\rho|^{-1}}
-inverse, i.e., an
\textup{RV}_{|\rho|^{-1}}
function such that

h(h^{\sharp}(t))\sim t\qquad\text{and}\qquad h^{\sharp}(h(t))\sim t\qquad\text{as}\ t\rightarrow\infty.

Such a function always exists (see Remark
B.2
).

For instance, if
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1/p}
, with
p=-1/\rho>0
, then we may take
h^{\sharp}(t)=(1+t)^{p}
. If
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1/p}(\log(t+2))^{q}
,
q\in\mathbb{R}
, then
h(t)=(t+1)^{1/p}(\log(t+2))^{-q}
, and so we may take
h^{\sharp}(t)=p^{-q/p}t^{1/p}\left(\log(t+2)\right)^{q/p}
,
t\geq 0
(
cf
. Example
B.4
).

By Lemma
B.5
, if
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(T)=c^{\pm}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N^{\pm}(T;\lambda)=\left(c^{\pm}\right)^{\frac{1}{|\rho|}}

In particular, we see that
T\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
if and only if
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N^{\pm}(T;\lambda)
exist. Therefore, Theorem
4.7
implies the following result.

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
T=T^{*}\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
T_{\ell}=T_{\ell}^{*}\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
,
\ell\geq 0
, be such that

\dot{T}_{\ell}\longrightarrow\dot{T}\ \text{in}\ \dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}.

Then
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N^{\pm}(T_{\ell};\lambda))
exist, and we have

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N^{\pm}(T;\lambda)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\big(\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N^{\pm}(T_{\ell};\lambda)\big).

Similarly, for any
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
, Lemma
B.5
also implies that

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)=c\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}\nu(T;\lambda)=c^{\frac{1}{|\rho|}}.

In particular, we see that
T\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
if and only if
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}\nu(T;\lambda)
exists. We thus have the following equivalent reformulation of Proposition
4.18
.

Proposition 4.19
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
and
T_{\ell}\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
,
\ell\geq 0
, be such that

\dot{T}_{\ell}\longrightarrow\dot{T}\ \text{in}\ \dot{\mathscr{L}}_{g}.

Then
\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}\nu(T_{\ell};\lambda))
exist, and we have

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}\nu(T;\lambda)=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\big(\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\sharp}(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}\nu(T_{\ell};\lambda)\big).

5.
Strong Measurability

From the point of view of noncommutative geometry, the NC integral should be a positive trace. Dixmier traces are (normalized) positive traces (see below for the definition of normalized traces). There is a whole wealth of normalized positive traces that are not Dixmier traces (see, e.g.,
[
43
,
68
]
). For this reason, as with the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, it stands to reason to look at a stronger notion of measurability in terms of the whole class of positive normalized traces.

In this section, we investigate this notion and relate it to the spectral asymptotics considered in the previous section. This will lead to a further notion of measurability.

Throughout this section, we let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{-1}
-function such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt=\infty
.

5.1.
Strong measurability

In what follows, we let
T_{g}
be any compact operator such that

(5.1)

T_{g}\xi_{j}=g(j)\xi_{j},\qquad j\geq 0,

where
(\xi_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{H}
. This ensures that
T_{g}
is a positive operator in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
such that

\mu_{j}(T_{g})=\lambda_{j}(T_{g})=g(j)\qquad\text{for}\ j\gg 1.

In what follows, a trace
\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is called
normalized
(or
g
-normalized
) if
\varphi(T_{g})=1
. For instance, every Dixmier trace is normalized.

Note that in view of (
3.4
) this normalization does not depend on the choice of the orthonormal basis
(\xi_{j})_{j\geq 0}
. However, it may depend on the choice of the function
g
to define the weak Lorentz ideal
\mathscr{L}_{g}
(see
[
51
]
). It can be shown, however, that for positive traces (and even continuous traces) the normalization
\varphi(T_{g})=1
does not depend on
g
(see Lemma
5.11
).

Definition 5.1
.

An operator
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is said to be
strongly measurable
(or
positively measurable
) if all normalized positive traces take the same value on
T
.

We shall denote by
\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
the space of strongly measurable operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

Remark 5.2
.

If
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is strongly measurable, then
T
is measurable. Moreover, for every normalized positive trace
\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we have

(5.2)

\varphi(T)=\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T.

The following statement (which is essentially proved in
[
43
]
) gives the relationship between continuous traces and normalized positive traces.

Proposition 5.3
.

The following hold.

(1)

Every positive trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is continuous and is a scalar multiple of a normalized positive trace.

(2)

Every continuous trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is linear combination of four normalized positive traces.

Proof.

By
[
43
, Lemma 2.4.12]
every positive linear functional on a quasi-Banach ideal is continuous. By
[
43
, Theorem 4.1.10]
every continuous trace on a quasi-Banach ideal is a linear combination of 4 positive traces. Moreover, in the same way as in the proof of
[
49
, Lemma 4.3]
, it can be shown that if
\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero positive trace, then
\varphi(T_{g})>0
. Thus,
\psi:=\varphi(T_{g})^{-1}\varphi
is a normalized positive trace such that
\varphi=\varphi(T_{g})\psi
. This shows that every positive trace is a scalar multiple of a normalized positive trace. Combining this with the first part of the proof shows that every continuous trace is a linear combination of four normalized positive traces. The proof is complete.
∎

We have the following description of the space of strongly measurable operators.

Proposition 5.4
.

We have

\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})=\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}\big(\mathscr{L}_{g}\big)}.

In particular,
\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
is a closed subspace of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. Moreover, if
T\in\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, then

(5.3)

\varphi(T)=\bigg(\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T\bigg)\varphi(T_{g})\qquad\text{for every continuous trace $\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}$}.

Proof.

If
T\in\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, then by Remark
5.2
the equality (
5.3
) holds for all normalized positive traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. By Proposition
5.3
the space of continuous traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is spanned by normalized positive traces. Therefore, by linearity the equality (
5.3
) holds for all continuous traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. Thus, if we set
c=\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T
, then
T-cT_{g}
is annihilated by all continuous traces, and hence is contained in
\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}
. Thus,
T\in\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}
. This shows that
\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})\subseteq\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}\big(\mathscr{L}_{g}\big)}
.

It remains to show that
\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}\big(\mathscr{L}_{g}\big)}\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
. Suppose that
T\in\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}
, i.e.,
T=cT_{g}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
. If
\varphi
is a normalized positive trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, then it is continuous by Proposition
5.3
, and so it annihilates
\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}
. As
\varphi(T_{g})=1
, we get

\varphi(T)=\varphi(cT_{g})=c\varphi(T_{g})=c.

This shows that
T
is strongly measurable, and hence
\mathbb{C}T_{g}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Com}\big(\mathscr{L}_{g}\big)}\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
. The proof is complete.
∎

Recall that a trace
\varphi:\mathscr{L}_{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is called
singular
if it annihilates finite-rank operators.

Proposition 5.5
.

Every positive trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
annihilates
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
, and hence is singular. In particular, every operator in
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
is strongly measurable.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.3
every positive trace is a constant multiple of a normalized positive trace. Therefore, it is enough to prove the result for normalized positive traces. Thus, let
\varphi
be a normalized positive trace. If
\xi
and
\eta
are two unit vectors in
\mathscr{H}
and
U
is a unitary operator such that
U\xi=\eta
,
then
|\eta\left\rangle\!\right\langle\eta|=|U\xi\left\rangle\!\right\langle U\xi|=U|\xi\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi|U^{*}
. Thus,

\varphi\big(|\eta\left\rangle\!\right\langle\eta|\big)=\varphi\big(U|\xi\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi|U^{*}\big)=\varphi\big(|\xi\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi|\big).

This shows that
\varphi
takes the same value on all rank 1 orthogonal projections. Denote this value by
c
.

We claim that
c=0
. To see this let
T_{g}=\sum_{j\geq 0}g(j)|\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|
be a Schmidt series representation for
T_{g}
. As
\varphi
is positive and normalized, for all
N\geq 1
, we have

1=\varphi(T_{g})\geq\varphi\big(\sum_{j<N}g(j)|\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|\big)=\sum_{j<N}g(j)\varphi\big(|\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|\big)=c\sum_{j<N}g(j).

This implies that
\sum_{j\geq 0}g(j)\leq c^{-1}<\infty
, which contradicts (
3.1
). Therefore,
\varphi
annihilates all rank 1 orthogonal projections. By linearity it annihilates all finite rank operators. We know from Proposition
5.3
that
\varphi
is continuous. Therefore, it annihilates the closure in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
of finite rank operators, i.e., the ideal
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

More generally, for any ideal
\mathscr{I}
which is not contained in
\mathscr{L}_{1}
, it can be shown that every positive trace is singular (see
[
64
, Lemma 15]
). In fact, the proof given above essentially follows the outline of the proof of
[
64
, Lemma 15]
.

Remark 5.7
.

It follows from Proposition
5.5
and Proposition
5.3
, that
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
is annihilated by all continuous traces. Thus,

(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})}.

5.2.
Strong measurability and Weyl laws

As the following result shows, every Weyl operator in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is strongly measurable. This extends to weak Lorentz ideals a result for the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
(see, e.g.,
[
49
, Proposition 5.12]
).

Theorem 5.8
.

If
T\in\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, then
T
is strongly measurable, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=\Lambda^{+}(T)-\Lambda^{-}(T).

In particular, if
T
is selfadjoint, then

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{+}(T)-\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-}(T).

Proof.

By linearity it is sufficient to prove the result for
T\geq 0
. As
T
is a Weyl operator, we have
\lambda_{j}(T)=\Lambda(T)g(j)+\operatorname{o}(g(j))
. We deduce from (
3.1
) that

\sum_{j<N}g(j)-G(N)=\operatorname{O}(1)=\operatorname{o}(G(N))
. Thus,

\sum_{j<N}\lambda_{j}(T)=\Lambda(T)\sum_{j<N}g(j)+\operatorname{o}\big(\sum_{j<N}g(j)\big)=\Lambda(T)G(N)+\operatorname{o}(G(N)).

Theorem
3.26
then ensures that
T
is measurable and
\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T=\Lambda(T)
.

It remains to show that
T
is strongly measurable. In fact, by proceeding as in the proof of
[
49
, Proposition 5.12]
, it can be shown that
T-\Lambda(T)T_{g}\in(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
. By Proposition
5.5
operators in
(\mathscr{L}_{g})_{0}
are strongly measurable. As
T_{g}
is strongly measurable, it follows that
T
is strongly measurable. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

If
T\in|\mathscr{W}|(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, then
T
is strongly measurable, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}|T|=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

Theorem
5.8
asserts that every Weyl operator in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is strongly measurable. The converse does not hold (see
[
51
]
). This leads us to introduce the following notion of measurability, which then is a stronger property than strong measurability.

Definition 5.10
.

An operator
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
is called
spectrally measurable
, if it is a Weyl operator in the sense of Definition
4.6
, i.e., it belongs to
\mathscr{W}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
.

5.3.
Dependence on the function
g(t)

As a further consequence of Theorem
5.8
, we can show that the space
\mathscr{M}_{s}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
does not depend on the choice of the function
g(t)
to define
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

Let
g_{1}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be some (continuous)
\textup{RV}_{-1}
-function such that
g_{1}(t)\sim g(t)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. In this case the quasi-Banach ideals
\mathscr{L}_{g_{1}}
and
\mathscr{L}_{g}
agree and have equivalent quasi-norms. We denote by
\mathscr{M}_{s}^{g}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
(resp.,
\mathscr{M}_{s}^{g_{1}}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
) the space of strongly measurable operators associated with the normalization
\varphi(T_{g})=1
(resp.,
\varphi(T_{g_{1}})=1
).

We have the following consequence of Theorem
5.8
.

Lemma 5.11
.

For every continuous trace
\varphi
on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
we have
\varphi(T_{g_{1}})=\varphi(T_{g})
.

Proof.

For
j
large enough we have
\lambda_{j}(T_{g_{1}})=g_{1}(j)\sim g(j)
. Thus,
T_{g_{1}}
is a (positive) Weyl operator. Theorem
5.8
then ensures that
T_{g_{1}}
belongs to
\mathscr{M}_{s}^{g}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T_{g_{1}}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\lambda_{j}(T_{g_{1}})=1.

Combining this with Proposition
5.4
we deduce that, for every continuous trace
\varphi
on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, we have

\varphi\left(T_{g_{1}}\right)=\bigg(\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}T_{g_{1}}\bigg)\varphi(T_{g})=\varphi(T_{g}).

The result is proved.
∎

It follows from Lemma
5.11
that, for positive traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, the normalization conditions
\varphi(T_{g})=1
and
\varphi(T_{g_{1}})=1
are equivalent. As a result, the set of
g_{1}
-normalized positive traces agrees with that of
g
-normalized traces. We thus arrive at the following result.

Proposition 5.12
.

We have
\mathscr{M}_{s}^{g_{1}}(\mathscr{L}_{g})=\mathscr{M}_{s}^{g}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
.

This shows that
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
depends only on the quasi-Banach ideal
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, not on the choice of function in the equivalence class of
g(t)
.

Remark 5.13
.

A full spectral characterization of strongly measurable operators in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, analogous to the spectral characterization of measurability in Theorem
3.26
, will be established in the companion paper
[
51
]
via Pietsch’s correspondence for traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

6.
Examples Arising from Nonclassical Weyl Laws

In this section, we describe various concrete examples of spectrally measurable and
g
-hypermeasurable operators, and compute their NC integrals. These examples come from a variety of settings. Most of them arise from nonclassical Weyl Laws in the sense of Simon
[
59
]
. We also describe a general recipe to construct examples from operators satisfying nonclassical Weyl laws.

Further examples can be found in
[
34
,
36
,
42
,
66
]
. We stress that in these references the focus is mostly on Dixmier measurability, while in this section we focus on spectral measurability and
g
-hypermeasurability.

6.1.
General construction of examples

Let
A:\operatorname{dom}(A)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be a selfadjoint operator with non-negative spectrum such that

•

0
is isolated in the spectrum of
A

•

The non-zero part of the spectrum of
A
consists of isolated eigenvalues with finite multiplicity.

We then can arrange the positive eigenvalues of
A
as a non-decreasing sequence,

\lambda_{0}(A)\leq\lambda_{1}(A)\leq\lambda_{2}(A)\leq\cdots

where each eigenvalue is repeated according to multiplicity. We then define the counting function of
A
by

N(A;\lambda)=\#\left\{j;\ \lambda_{j}\left(A\right)<\lambda\right\},\qquad\lambda>0.

For
p>0
we define
A^{-p}
to be
f(A)
with
f(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\infty)}(t)t^{-p}
. Equivalently, we let
(\xi_{j})_{j\geq 0}
be any orthonormal family in
\mathscr{H}
such that
A\xi_{j}=\lambda_{j}(A)\xi_{j}
for all
j\geq 0
, then we have

A^{-p}=0\quad\text{on}\ \ker A\qquad\text{and}\qquad A^{-p}\xi_{j}=\lambda_{j}(A)^{-p}\xi_{j}\quad\text{for all}\ j\geq 0.

In particular,
A^{-p}
is a positive compact operator, and we have

\lambda_{j}(A^{-p})=\lambda_{j}(A)^{-p}\qquad\forall j\geq 0.

Thus, if we define the counting function of
A^{-p}
by

N\left(A^{-p};\lambda\right)=\#\left\{j;\ \lambda_{j}\big(A^{-p}\big)>\lambda\right\},\qquad\lambda>0,

then we have

(6.1)

N\left(A^{-p};\lambda\right)=N\big(A;\lambda^{-\frac{1}{p}}\big)\qquad\forall\lambda>0.

We focus on cases where
A
satisfies a (nonclassical) Weyl law of the form,

(6.2)

N(A;\lambda)\sim c\lambda^{p}(\log\lambda)^{q},\qquad c>0,\quad p>0,\quad q\geq-1.

We then set
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}(\log(t+2))^{q}
,
t\geq 0
. This is a continuous
\textup{RV}_{-1}
-function. Note that for
q=0
the ideal
\mathscr{L}_{g}
just is the weak trace-class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
A
satisfies the Weyl law (
1.7
), then the operator
A^{-p}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and we have

(6.3)

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}A^{-p}=cp^{-q}.

Proof.

It follows from (
6.1
)–(
6.2
) that, as
\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}
we have

N\left(A^{-p};\lambda\right)=N\big(A,\lambda^{-\frac{1}{p}}\big)\sim c\lambda^{-1}\left(\log\big(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{p}}\big)\right)^{q}=cp^{-q}\lambda^{-1}|\log\lambda|^{q}.

Combining this with Lemma
B.5
gives the eigenvalue asymptotic,

\lambda_{j}\big(A^{-p}\big)\sim cp^{-q}j^{-1}(\log j)^{q}\qquad\text{as}\ j\rightarrow\infty.

The result then follows from Theorem
5.8
.
∎

6.2.
Zeros of Riemann’s zeta function (Riemann Hypothesis)

Assume Riemann Hypothesis (RH) holds, and let
\mathscr{D}
be the “Dirac operator” whose spectrum consists of the imaginary parts of non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-s}
(see
[
21
,
22
]
). Thus,
\mathscr{D}
is a selfadjoint unbounded operator on some suitable Hilbert space.

As the zeros of
\zeta(s)
are symmetric with respect to the real axis, we see that the counting function
N(|\mathscr{D}|;\lambda)
,
\lambda>0
, is two times the number of zeros of

\zeta(s)
with imaginary part in
[0,\lambda)
. Therefore, by the Riemann-von Mangoldt formula (see, e.g.,
[
67
]
), as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
we have

\displaystyle N\big(|\mathscr{D}|;\lambda\big)

\displaystyle=2\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\left(\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\right)-1\right)+\operatorname{O}(\log\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\lambda\log\lambda\left(1+\operatorname{O}\left((\log\lambda)^{-1}\right)\right).

Applying Lemma
6.1
then gives the following result.

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume (RH), and set
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}\log(t+2)
,
t\geq 0
. The operator
|\mathscr{D}|^{-1}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}|\mathscr{D}|^{-1}=\frac{1}{\pi}.

Remark 6.3
.

As
|\mathscr{D}|
is a positive operator, the fact that
|\mathscr{D}|^{-1}
is spectrally measurable means that

(6.4)

\lambda_{j}\left(|\mathscr{D}|^{-1}\right)\sim\frac{1}{\pi}j^{-1}(\log j).

The symmetry between positive and negative eigenvalues of
\mathscr{D}
, ensures that
\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(\mathscr{D}^{-1})=\lambda_{2j}(|\mathscr{D}|^{-1})
for all
j\geq 0
. Therefore, the positive and negative eigenvalues satisfy asymptotics of the form (
6.4
). It then follows that
\mathscr{D}^{-1}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. In fact, the symmetry further ensures that
\mathscr{H}
has an orthogonal splitting
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}^{+}\oplus\mathscr{H}^{-}
, and we can find an orthonormal eigenbasis
(\xi_{j}^{\pm})
of
\mathscr{H}^{\pm}
in such a way that
\mathscr{D}^{-1}\xi_{j}^{\pm}=\pm\lambda_{2j}(|\mathscr{D}|)\xi_{j}^{\pm}
. If
J
is the involution on
\mathscr{H}
such that
J\xi_{j}^{\pm}=\xi_{j}^{\mp}
, then we have
J\mathscr{D}^{-1}J=-\mathscr{D}^{-1}
. Equivalently,

\mathscr{D}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathscr{D}^{-1}-J\mathscr{D}^{-1}J\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big[J,J\mathscr{D}^{-1}\big].

This shows that
\mathscr{D}^{-1}
is actually in the commutator space
\operatorname{Com}(\mathscr{L}_{g})
, and so it annihilates
every
trace on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

6.3.
Schrödinger operators on unbounded domains

On
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, consider the family of Schrödinger operators,

H_{\alpha}:=-\Delta+|x_{1}\cdots x_{n}|^{\alpha},\qquad\alpha>0,

where
\Delta=-(\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}+\cdots+\partial_{x_{n}}^{2})
is the (positive) Laplacian.

It was shown by Simon
[
58
]
in dimension
n=2
that
H_{\alpha}
is essentially selfadjoint and its spectrum is positive and discrete. This was extended by Camus-Rautenberg
[
15
]
to dimensions
\geq 3
. It was further shown by Simon
[
59
]
(for
n=2
) and Camus-Rautenberg
[
15
]
(for
n\geq 3
) that as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
we have the Weyl law,

(6.5)

N\big(H_{\alpha};\lambda\big)\sim c(n,\alpha)\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\log\lambda)^{n-1},

where we have set

(6.6)

c(n,\alpha)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\bigg)^{n-1}\frac{\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\big)}.

This type of Weyl law prompted Simon
[
59
]
to coin the terminology “nonclassical Weyl law”.

By combining (
6.5
) with Lemma
6.1
we get the following result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Put
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}(\log(t+2))^{n-1}
,
t\geq 0
. The operator
H_{\alpha}^{-(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha})}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}H_{\alpha}^{-(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha})}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\bigg)^{n-1}\frac{\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\big)}.

We note that in the above result the exponent
\alpha^{-1}+n/2
takes on all positive real values
>n/2
as
\alpha
ranges over
(0,\infty)
. As observed
in
[
59
,
15
]
the behavior of the spectrum of
H_{\alpha}
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
is closely related to the spectrum of the Dirichlet Laplacian

\Delta_{\Omega}
on the infinite-volume open domain,

\Omega:=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\ |x_{1}\cdots x_{n}|<1\right\}.

In particular, we get a Weyl Law for
\Delta_{\Omega}
by letting
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
in the r.h.s. of (
6.5
). Namely, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
we have

N\big(\Delta_{\Omega};\lambda\big)\sim c(n,\infty)\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}}(\log\lambda)^{n-1},

where

c(n,\infty)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)^{n-1}\frac{\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{n}{2}+1\big)}=\frac{2n^{n}}{n!}(2\pi)^{-n}|\mathbb{B}^{n}|.

Combining this with Lemma
6.1
then yields the following result.

Proposition 6.5

(compare
[
36
, Example 2.7]
,
[
66
, Example 5.5]
)
.

The operator
\Delta_{\Omega}^{-n/2}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, with
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}(\log(t+2))^{n-1}
,
t\geq 0
. Moreover, we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}\Delta_{\Omega}^{-\frac{n}{2}}=\frac{2n^{n}}{n!}(2\pi)^{-n}|\mathbb{B}^{n}|.

Remark 6.6
.

The above examples were considered in
[
36
,
66
]
, but there only Dixmier-measurability was considered. The above result thus provides an improvement by establishing spectral measurability. In particular, this ensures that the operators

H_{\alpha}^{-(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha})}
and
\Delta_{\Omega}^{-n/2}
are strongly measurable.

Remark 6.7
.

We also stress the contrast between Proposition
6.5
and the corresponding result in the case
\Omega
is bounded with say Lipschitz boundary. In the latter case we get spectral measurability (and even
(t+1)^{-1}
-hypermeasurability) in the weak trace class
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, since we have the (classical) Weyl’s law,

N\big(\Delta_{\Omega};\lambda\big)\sim c(n)\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}}\left(1+\operatorname{O}\big(\lambda^{-1/2}\big)\right),\qquad c(n)=(2\pi)^{-n}|\mathbb{B}^{n}|\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega).

6.4.
Open manifolds with conformally cusp metrics

In
[
45
]
S. Moroianu established Weyl laws, including nonclassical Weyl laws, for some open manifolds with conformally cusp metrics. More precisely, let

X^{n}
be an open manifold, which is the interior of a compact manifold
\overline{X}
with closed boundary
M^{n-1}=\partial\overline{X}
and boundary defining function
x
. We assume that
X
is endowed with a Riemannian metric of the form,

(6.7)

g=x^{2r}g_{0},

where
g_{0}
is a cusp metric on
\overline{X}
(see
[
45
]
), i.e., a (complete) Riemannian metric which in local coordinates near
M
takes the form,

g_{0}=a_{00}(x,y)x^{-4}dx^{2}+\sum_{1\leq j<n}a_{0j}(x,y)x^{-2}dxdy^{j}+\sum_{1\leq i,j<n}h_{ij}(x,y)dy^{i}dy^{j}.

If in addition
a_{00|M}=1
and
a_{0j|M}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,n
, then (following Melrose
[
44
]
) we say that
g_{0}
is an
exact
cusp metric. Note that the cusp metric
g_{0}
induces on
M
the metric,

(6.8)

h_{0}:=\sum_{1\leq i,j<n}h_{ij}(0,y)dy^{i}dy^{j}.

Note that
(X,g)
has finite volume iff
r>1/n
. It is complete iff
r\geq 1
.

Examples of manifolds with conformally cusp metrics include hyperbolic metrics of finite volume (in which case
r=1
; see
[
45
]
). Another class of examples is provided by metric horns in the sense of Cheeger
[
17
]
(see also
[
41
]
) for which
r>1
.

Assume further that
\overline{X}
is spin. We denote by
\not{D}_{g}
the Dirac operator of
X
acting on the sections of the spinor bundle
\not{S}_{X}
. In addition, we denote by
\not{D}_{h_{0}}
the Dirac operator of
M=\partial X
with respect to the metric (
6.7
). We make the following assumptions:

•

g_{0}
is an exact cusp metric.

•

\ker\not{D}_{h_{0}}=\{0\}
, i.e., there are no (non-zero) harmonic spinors on
M
.

Under these assumptions Moroianu
[
45
]
showed that the Dirac operator
\not{D}_{g}
with domain
C^{\infty}_{c}(X)
is essentially selfadjoint and has pure discrete spectrum. Furthermore, he established Weyl laws for the counting function of
|\not{D}_{g}|
. We get classical Weyl laws for
r\neq 1/n
, but for
r=1/n
we have nonclassical Weyl law. We will restrict ourselves to the case
r\geq 1/n
, since in those cases the leading coefficients in the Weyl laws are computable locally. As
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we have

N\big(|\not{D}_{g}|;\lambda)\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\not{c}_{1}(n)\operatorname{Vol}_{g}(X)\lambda^{n}&\text{if}\ r>\frac{1}{n},\\
\not{c}_{2}(n)\operatorname{Vol}_{h}(M)\lambda^{n}\log\lambda&\text{if}\ r=\frac{1}{n},\end{array}\right.

where we have set

(6.9)

\not{c}_{1}(n):=2^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}|\mathbb{B}^{n}|,\qquad\not{c}_{2}(n):=2^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|.

Combining this with Lemma
6.1
we then obtain the following result.

Proposition 6.8
.

Suppose that
\overline{X}
is spin, and assume that
g_{0}
is an exact cusp metric and
\ker\not{D}_{h_{0}}=\{0\}
.

(1)

If
r>1/n
, then
|\not{D}_{g}|^{-n}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{1,\infty}
, and we have

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int|\not{D}_{g}|^{-n}=\not{c}_{1}(n)\operatorname{Vol}_{g}(X),\qquad\not{c}_{1}(n):=2^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}|\mathbb{B}^{n}|.

(2)

If
r=1/n
, and we put
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}\log(t+2)
,
t\geq 0
, then
|\not{D}_{g}|^{-n}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and we have

(6.10)

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}|\not{D}_{g}|^{-n}=\not{c}_{2}(n)\operatorname{Vol}_{h}(M),\qquad\not{c}_{2}(n):=2^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|.

Remark 6.9
.

The first part is a special case of the version of Connes’ integration formula for open manifolds in
[
50
]
.

Remark 6.10
.

The formula (
6.10
) shows that, for
r=1/n
, the NC integral recaptures the volume of the boundary
M=\partial X
. As mentioned above, if
r=1/n
, then
(X,g)
has infinite volume.

6.5.
Product of quantum spheres with the 2-torus

We revisit an example of Gayral-Sukochev
[
34
, Example 4.9]
. Let
q\in(0,1)
. The Podles̀ quantum sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{q}
is a quantum analogue of the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Its coordinate algebra
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{q})
appears as a
*
-subalgebra of the coordinate Hopf
*
-algebra
\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2))
which is invariant under the action of
\mathscr{U}_{q}(\operatorname{su}(2))
(see
[
48
]
).

The quantum sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{q}
is also a quantum space in the sense of Connes’ noncommutative geometry, since we have a natural spectral triple
(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{q}),\mathscr{H},\mathscr{D}_{q})
, which was constructed by Dabrowski-Sitarz
[
24
]
. Here
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1/2}\oplus\mathscr{H}_{1/2}
is two copies of the same Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{1/2}
, which is actually the representation space of two non-isomorphic
\mathscr{U}_{q}(\operatorname{su}(2))
-equivariant representations of
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{q})
.

The Dirac operator
\mathscr{D}_{q}
is a selfadjoint unbounded operator on
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1/2}\oplus\mathscr{H}_{1/2}
of the form,

\mathscr{D}_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}0&D_{q}\\
D_{q}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad D_{q}\xi_{\ell,m}=\bigg[\ell+\frac{1}{2}\bigg]_{q}\xi_{\ell,m},\qquad[x]_{q}=\frac{q^{x}-q^{-x}}{q-q^{-1}},

where
(\xi_{\ell,m})
is some orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{H}_{1/2}
parametrized by
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+1/2
and
m\in\{-\ell,-\ell+1,\ldots,\ell\}
. Note that
|\mathscr{D}_{q}|=D_{q}\oplus D_{q}
. Moreover, it is shown in
[
31
]
that

(6.11)

\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-t|\mathscr{D}_{q}|}\right]=\bigg(\frac{\log t}{\log q}\bigg)^{2}\big(1+\operatorname{O}\big((\log t)^{-1}\big)\big)\qquad\text{as}\ t\rightarrow 0^{+}.

Let
\Delta=-(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})
be the Laplacian on the (ordinary) torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=(\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z}))^{2}
. This is an essentially selfadjoint operator with non-negative and discrete spectrum. Its eigenvalues are
|k|^{2}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Moreover, as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
we have

(6.12)

\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-t\Delta}\right]=(4\pi t)^{-1}\big(\operatorname{Vol}(\mathbb{T}^{2})+\operatorname{O}(t)\big)=\pi t^{-1}\big(1+\operatorname{O}(t)\big).

We actually can get a much better remainder term by using Poisson summation formula (see, e.g.,
[
50
]
). However, the above remainder term is enough for our purpose.

Following Gayral-Sukochev
[
34
, Example 4.9]
we look at the operator,

A:=|\mathscr{D}_{q}|\otimes 1+1\otimes\Delta.

This is an essentially selfadjoint unbounded operator on
\mathscr{H}\oplus L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Moreover, by using (
6.11
) and (
6.12
) we see that, as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have

\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-tA}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-t|\mathscr{D}_{q}|}\right]\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-t\Delta}\right]=\frac{\pi}{(\log q)^{2}}t^{-1}(\log t)^{2}\big(1+\operatorname{O}\big((\log t)^{-1}\big)\big).

Therefore, we may apply Karamata’s Tauberian theorem in the form, e.g., of
[
40
, Theorem IV.8.1]
to get the Weyl law,

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda^{-1}(\log\lambda)^{-2}N(A;\lambda)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2)}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}t(\log t)^{-2}\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-tA}\right]=\frac{\pi}{(\log q)^{2}}.

By combining this with Lemma
6.1
we then arrive at the following result.

Proposition 6.11

(compare
[
34
, Example 4.9]
)
.

Put
g(t)=(t+1)^{-1}(\log(t+2))^{2}
,
t\geq 0
. The operator
(|\mathscr{D}_{q}|\otimes 1+1\otimes\Delta)^{-1}
is spectrally measurable in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, and we have

\sideset{{}^{g}\!\!}{}{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int}(|\mathscr{D}_{q}|\otimes 1+1\otimes\Delta)^{-1}=\frac{\pi}{(\log q)^{2}}.

Remark 6.12
.

The above result refines
[
34
, Example 4.9]
in two ways (see also
[
36
, Example 2.10]
). First, we obtain measurability in the weak Lorentz ideal
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, whereas in
[
34
]
they work with the larger Lorentz ideal
\mathscr{M}_{G}
, with
G(t)\sim(1/3)[\log(t+2)]^{3}
. Second, the authors in
[
34
]
focus on measurability with respect to Dixmier traces, but Proposition
6.11
yields spectral measurability, which is a much stronger property. In particular, this implies strong measurability with respect to all normalized positive traces on
\mathscr{L}_{g}
.

j=2k
, and so

\frac{g(k)}{g(j)}=\frac{g(j-k)}{g(j)}=\frac{g(k)}{g(2k)}\leq C_{k}.

If
j
is odd, then
j=2k+1
, and so we get

\frac{g(k)}{g(j)}=\frac{g(k)}{g(2k+1)}\leq C_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{g(j-k)}{g(j)}=\frac{g(k+1)}{g(2k+1)}\leq C_{k}.

In any case, we have

g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(S+T)\leq C_{k}\left(g(k)^{-1}\mu_{k}(S)+g(j-k)^{-1}\mu_{j-k}(T)\right).

It then follows that

(A.1)

\|S+T\|_{g}\leq C\left(\|S\|_{g}+\|T\|_{g}\right)\qquad\forall S,T\in\mathscr{K}.

This proves (ii). The proof of Lemma
2.24
is complete.
∎

Remark A.1
.

By using Remark
2.19
and the fact that
g
is
\textup{RV}_{\rho}
it can be shown that
C_{k}\rightarrow 2^{|\rho|}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
It then follows that

C=\sup_{k\geq 0}C_{k}\geq 2^{|\rho|}.

Proof of Proposition
2.27
.

We only need to check that every Cauchy sequence
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is convergent. Note that the inclusion of
\mathscr{L}_{g}
into
\mathscr{K}
is continuous, since we have

\|T\|=\mu_{0}(T)\leq g(0)\|T\|_{g}.

Thus,
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{K}
, and hence it converges to some operator
T
in
\mathscr{K}
.

As
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
, given any
\epsilon>0
, there is
N\geq 0
such that, for all
m,n\geq N
and
j\geq 0
, we have

g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T_{m}-T_{n})\leq\|T_{m}-T_{n}\|_{g}\leq\epsilon.

Letting
m\rightarrow\infty
and using (
2.3
) we get

g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T-T_{n})\leq\epsilon\qquad\forall j\geq 0.

Thus,

(A.2)

\|T-T_{n}\|_{g}\leq\epsilon\qquad\forall n>N.

In particular, by using (
A.1
) we get

\|T\|_{g}\leq C\left(\|T-T_{n}\|_{g}+\|T_{n}\|_{g}\right)<\infty.

That is,
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
. The estimate (
A.2
) then means that
T_{n}\rightarrow T
in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
. This shows that every Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{L}_{g}
is convergent. The
proof of Proposition
2.27
is complete.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.32
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}_{g}
have Schmidt series
T=\sum_{j\geq 0}\mu_{j}(T)|U\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|
, and set
T_{N}=\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)|U\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|
,
N\geq 1
. As
T_{N}
has finite rank, we have
\|T-T_{N}\|_{g}\geq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathscr{L}_{g}}(T;\mathscr{R})
, and hence

\operatorname{dist}_{\mathscr{L}_{g}}(T;\mathscr{R})\leq\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|T-T_{N}\|_{g}.

Let
R\in\mathscr{R}
, and set
N=\operatorname{rk}(R)
. The min-max principle (
2.1
) implies that
\mu_{N}(R)=0
. Combining this with Fan’s inequality (
2.6
) we see that, for
j\geq N
, we have

\mu_{j}(T)\leq\mu_{j-N}(T-R)+\mu_{N}(R)=\mu_{j-N}(T-R).

Thus,

g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)\leq\frac{g(j-N)}{g(j)}g(j-N)^{-1}\mu_{j-N}(T-R)\leq\frac{g(j-N)}{g(j)}\|T-R\|_{g}.

Using Remark
2.19
then gives

\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)\leq\|T-R\|_{g}.

As the above inequality holds for every
R\in\mathscr{R}
, we get

\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathscr{L}_{g}}(T;\mathscr{R}).

To complete the proof it is enough to show that

(A.3)

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|T-T_{N}\|_{g}\leq\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

To see this, set

S_{N}:=\sum_{j\geq N}\mu_{j}(T)|\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|=\sum_{j\geq 0}\mu_{j+N}(T)|\xi_{j+N}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j+N}|\qquad N\geq 1.

As
S_{N}\geq 0
with eigensequence
(\mu_{j+N}(T))_{j\geq 0}
, we see that
\mu_{j}(S_{N})=\mu_{j+N}(T)
for all
j\geq 0
. Note also that

T-T_{N}=\sum_{j\geq N}\mu_{j}(T)|U\xi_{j}\left\rangle\!\right\langle\xi_{j}|=US_{N}.

Here
U^{*}U
is the orthogonal projection onto
\ker T=\ker|T|
. As
\operatorname{ran}S_{N}\subseteq\operatorname{ran}|T|\subseteq(\ker|T|)^{\perp}
we see that
U^{*}US_{N}=S_{N}
. Thus,

(T-T_{N})^{*}(T-T_{N})=S_{N}U^{*}US_{N}=S_{N}^{2}.

It follows that
|T-T_{N}|=S_{N}
, and so we have

\mu_{j}(T-T_{N})=\mu_{j}(S_{N})=\mu_{j+N}(T).

Thus,

g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T-T_{N})=\frac{g(j+N)}{g(j)}g(j+N)^{-1}\mu_{j+N}(T)\leq\rho(N)g(j+N)^{-1}\mu_{j+N}(T),

where we have set

\rho(N):=\sup_{j\geq 0}\frac{g(j+N)}{g(j)}.

It then follows that, for all
N\geq 1
, we have

(A.4)

\|T-T_{N}\|_{g}=\sup_{j\geq 0}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T-T_{N})\leq\rho(N)\sup_{j\geq N}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

By assumption
g(t)
is decreasing on
[t_{0},\infty)
. We then have
\rho(N)=\max\{\rho_{1}(N),\rho_{2}(N)\}
, where

\rho_{1}(N)=\max_{0\leq j\leq t_{0}}\frac{g(j+N)}{g(j)},\qquad\rho_{2}(N)=\sup_{j\geq t_{0}}\frac{g(j+N)}{g(j)}\leq 1.

As
g(t)\rightarrow 0
, we see that
\rho_{1}(N)\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. It follows that
\rho(N)\leq 1
for
N
large enough. Combining this with (
A.4
) we then get

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|T-T_{N}\|_{g}\leq\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{j\geq N}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T)=\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}g(j)^{-1}\mu_{j}(T).

This proves (
A.3
). The proof of Lemma
2.32
is complete.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.36
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{K}
. As
\mu_{j}(T)\leq\|T\|_{g}g(j)
, we have

\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)\leq\frac{\|T\|_{g}}{G(N)}\sum_{j\leq N}g(j).

By assumption
g
is a positive continuous function that is decreasing on
[t_{0},\infty)
. If
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt=\infty
, then
\sum_{j\leq N}g(j)\simeq G(N)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. If
\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)dt<\infty
, then
\sum_{j\geq 0}g(j)
. In either case the sequence
\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}g(j)
is bounded. Therefore, we have

\|T\|_{G}\leq C\|T\|_{g},\qquad\textup{with}\ C:=\sup_{N\geq 1}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j\leq N}g(j)<\infty.

This gives the first part.

Assume
-1<\rho<0
. The inequality
N\mu_{N}(T)\leq\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)
yields

g(N)^{-1}\mu_{N}(T)\leq\frac{G(N)}{Ng(N)}\frac{1}{G(N)}\sum_{j<N}\mu_{j}(T)\leq\frac{G(N)}{Ng(N)}\|T\|_{G}.

As
-1<\rho<0
, it follows from Karamata Theorem (Theorem
2.21
) that
\frac{G(t)}{tg(t)}
is bounded on
[0,\infty)
. Thus,

\|T\|_{g}\leq C^{\prime}\|T\|_{G},\qquad\textup{where}\ C^{\prime}:=\sup_{N\geq 1}\frac{G(N)}{Ng(N)}<\infty.

This gives the second part. The proof of Proposition
2.36
is complete.
∎

Appendix B
Counting Functions and Eigenvalues

In this appendix, we link the asymptotics of non-increasing non-negative null sequences to the asymptotic behaviours of their counting functions when the leading terms are given by functions of regular variations and suitable asymptotic inverses.

B.1.
Asymptotic inverses

If
h:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is function converging to
\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, then by an
asymptotic inverse
we shall mean any function

h^{\sharp}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
converging to
\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
such that

h(h^{\sharp}(t))\sim t\qquad\text{and}\qquad h^{\sharp}(h(t))\sim t\qquad\text{as}\ t\rightarrow\infty.

As the following shows every
RV
-function of positive index admits an asymptotic inverse which is an
RV
-function as well.

Proposition B.1

(see
[
5
, Theorem 5.1.12]
)
.

Let
h:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an
\textup{RV}_{p}
-function with
p>0
.

(1)

h(t)
always admits an asymptotic inverse
h^{\sharp}(t)
which is
\textup{RV}_{1/p}
.

(2)

A function
\tilde{h}(t)
is an asymptotic inverse of
h(t)
if and only if
\tilde{h}(t)\sim h^{\sharp}(t)
.

Remark B.2
.

It follows from Remark
2.23
and Proposition
B.1

that we always can choose the asymptotic inverse to be a continuous
\textup{RV}_{1/p}
-function.
If
h:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is an increasing bijection, then
h
and
h^{-1}
are continuous and are asymptotic inverses of each other.
In general, if
h
is only ultimately increasing, then there is
a>0
such that
h:[a,\infty)\rightarrow[h(a),\infty)
is an increasing bijection. An instance of continuous asymptotic inverse of
h
then is given by any continuous function
h^{\natural}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that
h^{\natural}=(h_{|[a,\infty)})^{-1}
on
[h(a),\infty)
.

Example B.3
.

If
h(t)=t^{p}
,
p>0
, then its inverse
h^{\natural}(t)=t^{1/p}
,
t\geq 0
, is an
\textup{RV}_{1/p}
-continuous asymptotic inverse.

Example B.4
.

If
h(t)=t^{p}(\log(t+2))^{q}
,
p>0
,
q\in\mathbb{R}
, then an
\textup{RV}_{1/p}
-continuous asymptotic inverse is given by

h^{\sharp}(t):=p^{\frac{q}{p}}t^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\log(t+2)\right)^{-\frac{q}{p}},\qquad t\geq 0.

B.2.
Asymptotic behaviour of counting functions

From now on, we let

\lambda_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0

be a non-increasing non-negative sequence converging to
0
. Its counting function is given by

N(\lambda):=\#\{j;\lambda_{j}>\lambda\},\qquad\lambda>0.

In addition, we let
h:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an
\textup{RV}_{p}
function with
p>0
, and let
h^{\sharp}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an

\textup{RV}_{1/p}
asymptotic inverse.

Lemma B.5
.

We have

(B.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}h^{\sharp}(j)\lambda_{j}=\bigg[\limsup_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N(\lambda)\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}},

(B.2)

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}h^{\sharp}(j)\lambda_{j}=\bigg[\liminf_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N(\lambda)\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Thus,

(B.3)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N(\lambda)=c\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}h^{\sharp}(j)\lambda_{j}=c^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Proof.

We only have to prove (
B.1
)–(
B.2
). Define

\displaystyle\underline{N}=\liminf_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h\left(\lambda^{-1}\right)^{-1}N(\lambda),\qquad\overline{N}=\limsup_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}h\left(\lambda^{-1}\right)^{-1}N(\lambda),

\displaystyle\underline{L}=\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}h^{\sharp}(j)\lambda_{j},\qquad\overline{L}=\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}h^{\sharp}(j)\lambda_{j}.

To complete the proof we then only need to show the following two sets of inequalities,

(B.4)

\displaystyle\underline{N}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\underline{L}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\overline{L}\leq\overline{N}^{\frac{1}{p}},

(B.5)

\displaystyle\underline{L}^{p}\leq\underline{N}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\overline{N}\leq\overline{L}^{p}.

Proof of (
B.4
).
We may assume
\underline{N}>0
. Let
a\in(0,1)
. The definition of
N(\lambda)
implies that

N(\lambda_{j})\leq j\leq N(a\lambda_{j})-1\leq N(a\lambda_{j}).

In addition, given any
\epsilon>0
, the definitions of
\underline{N}
and
\overline{N}
implies that, for
\lambda
large enough, we have

(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}\leq h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N(\lambda)\leq\overline{N}+\epsilon.

Take
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
and
\lambda=a\lambda_{j}
, respectively. Then for
j
large enough, we have

(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}h(\lambda_{j}^{-1})\leq N(\lambda_{j})\leq j\leq N(a\lambda_{j})\leq(\overline{N}+\epsilon)h(a^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-1}).

As
h^{\sharp}
is ultimately increasing, for
j
large enough, we get

h^{\sharp}[(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}h(\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\leq h^{\sharp}(j)\leq h^{\sharp}[(\overline{N}+\epsilon)h(a^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-1})].

Thus,

h^{\sharp}[(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}h(\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\lambda_{j}\leq h^{\sharp}(j)\lambda_{j}\leq h^{\sharp}[(\overline{N}+\epsilon)h(a^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\lambda_{j}.

As
h^{\sharp}
is
\textup{RV}_{1/p}
and is an asymptotic inverse of
h
, for
j\to\infty
we have

h^{\sharp}[(\overline{N}+\epsilon)h(a^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\lambda_{j}\sim(\overline{N}+\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{p}}h^{\sharp}[h(a^{-1}\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\lambda_{j}\sim a^{-1}(\overline{N}+\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Similarly, we have

h^{\sharp}[(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}h(\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\lambda_{j}\sim[(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}]^{\frac{1}{p}}h^{\sharp}[h(\lambda_{j}^{-1})]\lambda_{j}\sim[(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

We then get

[(1-\epsilon)\underline{N}]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\underline{L}\leq\overline{L}\leq a^{-1}(\overline{N}+\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
and
a\to 1^{-}
gives the inequalities (
B.4
).

Proof of (
B.5
).
We may assume that
\underline{L}>0
. The proof relies on the inequalities:

\lambda_{N(\lambda)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{N(\lambda)-1}.

Let
\epsilon>0
. For
j
large enough, we have

(1-\epsilon)\underline{L}\leq h^{\sharp}(\lambda_{j})\lambda_{j}\leq\overline{L}+\epsilon.

Then, for
j=N(\lambda)
and
j=N(\lambda)-1
, we get

(1-\epsilon)\underline{L}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda))^{-1}\leq\lambda_{N(\lambda)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{N(\lambda)-1}\leq(\overline{L}+\epsilon)h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda)-1)^{-1}.

As the function
\lambda\to h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}
is ultimately increasing, for
\lambda
large enough, we have

h[((1-\epsilon)\underline{L})^{-1}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda))]^{-1}\leq h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}\leq h[(\overline{L}+\epsilon)^{-1}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda)-1)]^{-1}.

Thus,

(B.6)

h[((1-\epsilon)\underline{L})^{-1}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda))]^{-1}N(\lambda)\leq h(\lambda^{-1})^{-1}N(\lambda)\leq h[(\overline{L}+\epsilon)^{-1}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda)-1)]^{-1}N(\lambda).

As
h
is an
\textup{RV}_{p}
-function and is an asymptotic inverse of
h^{\sharp}
, as
\lambda\to\infty
, we have

(B.7)

h[((1-\epsilon)\underline{L})^{-1}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda))]^{-1}N(\lambda)\sim[(1-\epsilon)\underline{L}]^{p}[h\circ h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda))]^{-1}N(\lambda)\sim[(1-\epsilon)\underline{L}]^{p}.

Similarly, we have

h[(\overline{L}+\epsilon)^{-1}h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda)-1)]^{-1}N(\lambda)\sim(\overline{L}+\epsilon)^{p}[h\circ h^{\sharp}(N(\lambda)-1)]^{-1}N(\lambda)\sim(\overline{L}+\epsilon)^{p}.

Combining this with (
B.6
)–(
B.7
), we then get

[(1-\epsilon)\underline{L}]^{p}\leq\underline{N}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{N}\leq(\overline{L}+\epsilon)^{p}.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
gives (
B.5
). The proof is complete.
∎

Example B.6
.

Suppose that
h(t)=t^{p}
,
p>0
. An exact inverse of
h(t)
then is

h^{\natural}(t):=t^{\frac{1}{p}},\qquad t\geq 0.

This is an
\textup{RV}_{\frac{1}{p}}
-continuous function. Therefore, Lemma
B.5
allows us to recover the well-known equivalence,

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\lambda^{p}N(\lambda)=c\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}j^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda_{j}=c^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Example B.7
.

Suppose that
h(t)=t^{p}(\log(t+2))^{q}
with
p>0
and
q\neq 0
. As mentioned in Example
B.4
an
\textup{RV}_{1/p}
asymptotic inverse is given by

h^{\sharp}(t):=p^{\frac{q}{p}}t^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\log(t+2)\right)^{-\frac{q}{p}},\qquad t\geq 0.

We may thus apply Lemma
B.5
to get the following equivalence,

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\lambda^{p}|\log\lambda|^{q}N(\lambda)=c\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}j^{\frac{1}{p}}(\log j)^{-\frac{q}{p}}\lambda_{j}=\left(p^{-q}c\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.
