Mellin transforms, transfinite diameter and rational approximations of integrals
Francis Brown

Abstract
. We establish a higher-dimensional irrationality criterion for periods which are presented as Mellin integrals depending on many parameters. The criterion is stated as an upper bound on the multi-variate transfinite diameter of the image of the domain of integration under the Mellin arguments. Most of the paper is devoted to studying notions of transfinite diameter relative to very general multivariate Vandermonde matrices. As a proof of principle, we illustrate how this approach works with detailed computations in the case of a 5-parameter family of integrals for ζ ⁡ ( 2 ) \zeta(2) on ℳ 0 , 5 \mathcal{M}_{0,5} , the moduli space of curves of genus 0 with 5 marked points. This yields a ‘higher-dimensional’ proof of the irrationality of ζ ⁡ ( 2 ) \zeta(2) , based on an upper bound for a certain kind of transfinite diameter associated to ℳ 0 , 5 \mathcal{M}_{0,5} .



1.
Introduction

Following Apéry and Beukers, many known irrationality proofs of periods, such as
\zeta(2)
or
\zeta(3)
, involve
constructing families of linear forms which provide rational approximations to the periods in question.
These linear forms commonly arise from integrals which depend on a large number of additional parameters, typically greater than the dimension of the integral, which generate a huge family of additional linear forms. However, the existing criteria for irrationality make no use of these linear forms and their underlying geometric structure.
In this note we introduce a ‘multi-parameter’ irrationality criterion which does exploit them.
Our guiding principle is that one should try to replace ad hoc diophantine arguments with general constructions in algebraic geometry.

A motivating question for this investigation was the following: does there exist a method for proving the irrationality of period integrals which
improves
as their dimension increases?
All known constructions do the exact opposite, and become significantly worse with increasing dimension (or weight). Indeed the quality of known approximations to periods of higher weight, e.g., for the odd zeta values
\zeta(2n+1)
, rapidly become very poor indeed as
n
increases. As long as this situation persists, proving irrationality results for periods by this method is a game where the goalposts are forever moving out of reach.

1.1.
Main example

A fascinating geometric feature of period integrals which becomes
richer
with increasing dimension is the existence of additional parameters.
To illustrate, consider the integrals:

(1.1)

I(h,i,j,k,\ell)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\frac{x^{h}(1-x)^{i}y^{k}(1-y)^{j}}{(1-xy)^{i+j-\ell}}\frac{dxdy}{1-xy}

which depend on five integer parameters
h,i,j,k,\ell\geq 0
. They are canonically associated to
\mathcal{M}_{0,5}
and one shows that
I(h,i,j,k,\ell)
is a linear form in
\mathbb{Z}\zeta(2)+\mathbb{Q}
. The
denominator
of
I(h,i,j,k,l)
is the smallest positive integer
D
such that
DI(h,i,j,k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}\zeta(2)+\mathbb{Z}
. The Apéry family is obtained from the ‘diagonal’

I(n,n,n,n,n)=a_{n}\zeta(2)+b_{n}\ ,

where
a_{n},D_{n}b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since the integrals on the left-hand side tend to zero exponentially fast, and the denominators
D_{n}
are not too large, the approximation
\zeta(2)\sim-b_{n}/a_{n}
is sufficient to prove that
\zeta(2)\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

Rhin and Viola’s remarkable method
[
RV96
,
RV01
]
, based on earlier work by Hata and others, selects a set of fixed integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{5}>0
to construct a better family
I(a_{1}n,a_{2}n,\ldots,a_{5}n)
.
Their preferred choice is
I(12n,12n,14n,14n,13n)
, whose arguments form a line which lies very close to the diagonal. The key point is that changing the direction of this line away from the diagonal will typically compromise the asymptotic behavior of the integrals, but lead to a significant improvement of their denominators. The mechanism for this is the so-called ‘group method’, introduced in
loc. cit.
, which exploits a rich group of symmetries satisfied by the full 5-parameter family of integrals to prove that specific primes do not divide the denominators of certain integrals
I(h,i,j,k,\ell)
. This group structure seems to exist in quite some generality but does not yet have a satisfying explanation from the point of view of algebraic geometry. Quite how to optimise the choice of the parameters
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{5})
is something of an art which is not fully disclosed in the existing literature.

Whilst the group method, which involves selecting a single line of integrals, can lead to spectacular improvements in the irrationality measure, it still does not fully exploit the whole 5-dimensional space of parameters. Furthermore, there is a trade-off between the quality of the denominators of the integrals, and the quality of their asymptotics. Indeed, improving the denominators typically leads to worse asymptotics, and improving the asymptotics to poorer denominator bounds.

These observations were the starting point for the present investigation, as part of a broader study of the fascinating properties of Mellin periods and their associated motives.

1.2.
Mellin periods and the geometry of numbers

We consider an algebraic Mellin integral

(1.2)

I(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})=\int_{\sigma}f_{1}^{s_{1}}\ldots f_{r}^{s_{r}}\omega\qquad\mathrm{Re}(s_{i})\geq 0

on a smooth affine variety
X
of dimension
d
, where
\omega
is a regular differential
d
form on
X
,

\sigma\subset X(\mathbb{C})
is a relative
d
-chain with boundary contained in the points of a divisor
Y\subset X
, and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}

are regular functions on
X
.
We assume that
X,Y,f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
are all defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.

The finite-dimensionality of algebraic de Rham cohomology implies that there exists a finite set of periods
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
such that, for every set of integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\geq 0

(1.3)

I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{Q}\xi_{1}+\ldots+\mathbb{Q}\xi_{m}\ .

By clearing the denominator
D_{\underline{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\underline{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
we have

D_{\underline{n}}\,I(\underline{n})\in\mathbb{Z}\xi_{1}+\ldots+\mathbb{Z}\xi_{m}\ .

Thus a Mellin integral (
1.2
) is a machine for constructing many linear forms in periods. When the
f_{i}
vanish along the boundary of the domain of integration, these linear forms will typically be small.
See
[
Bro16
]
for a systematic study when
X
is a (partial compactification of the) moduli space of curves of genus
0
with marked points, and
\xi_{i}
are multiple zeta values. In all our examples,
r
is at least as large as the dimension
d
. For
\zeta(2)
, which will be our main running example, there are
r=5
parameters, and
m=2
periods, namely
\xi_{1}=\zeta(2)
and
\xi_{2}=1
.

How to make use of the plethora of linear forms (
1.3
)? Consider all such linear forms where the
n_{i}
are bounded above by a fixed integer
M
. We wish to find integers
c_{\underline{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}
so that the linear combination

\sum_{\underline{n}:n_{i}\leq M}c_{\underline{n}}D_{\underline{n}}I(\underline{n})\quad\in\quad\mathbb{Z}\xi_{1}+\ldots+\mathbb{Z}\xi_{m}

is as small as possible, and, importantly, non-zero (this cannot be guaranteed unless the ‘motivic’ versions
[
Bro17
]
of the
\xi_{i}
are linearly independent, which we shall always assume). The geometry of numbers already provides a method for tackling this problem.

Theorem 1.1
.

(Minkowski) Let
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
be a matrix with
\det(A)\neq 0
. Then there exists an integer vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

0<\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}c_{j}\right|\leq|\det(A)|^{\frac{1}{n}}\quad\hbox{ for some }1\leq i\leq n\ .

This theorem suggests a very natural approach for generating small linear forms in the periods
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{r}
. We construct a matrix
Q^{\sigma}
whose entries are integrals
I(\underline{n})
for
n_{i}\leq M
, in such a way that its determinant is non-zero. We will mostly study a particular construction where
Q^{\sigma}
is positive-definite, but there are other possibilities which exploit the existence of Picard-Fuchs recurrence relations (or contiguity relations) between the
I(\underline{n})
. In our situation, the determinant of
Q^{\sigma}
can be bounded above by using a greatly generalized version of the transfinite diameter, which measures the size of the image of the domain of integration
\sigma
under the map
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
(see below).

After clearing denominators, we obtain a matrix
A^{\sigma}_{ij}
which satisfies

A^{\sigma}=A^{(1)}\xi_{1}+\ldots+A^{(m)}\xi_{m}

where
A^{(i)}
are matrices with integer entries
1
1

1

Such
matrix
linear forms in periods merit investigation in their own right: here we consider them to be ‘small’ when
\det(A^{\sigma})
is small, but there are other ways to measure the size of a matrix linear form in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
. Minkowski’s theorem may then be applied to the matrix
A^{\sigma}
to deduce the existence of a non-vanishing linear form.

The denominators occuring in the entries of the matrix
Q^{\sigma}
are related to the orders of the poles of the integral
I
along some suitable compactification of
X
. Whilst a general theorem has not currently been worked out, we expect that denominator bounds can systematically be obtained using overconvergent
p
-adic de Rham cohomology. Our examples suggest, furthermore, that denominator bounds can be significantly improved by exploiting congruences in algebraic de Rham cohomology.

A key point is that the multi-parameter approach described above enables one to decouple the problem of optimising the denominators from the asymptotics of integrals. In fact, examples suggest that a useful strategy is to fill the input matrix
Q^{\sigma}
with integrals with small denominators, irrespective of whether they individually give good approximations or not. Minkowski’s theorem will do the work of extracting a linear combination of these integrals which is small in absolute value.

Remark 1.2
.

In the case when there is a single parameter
r=1
, our method is an effective version of the determinant criterion of Zudilin
[
Zud17
]
, who gave examples of periods (logarithms) to show that significant improvements can already be obtained in a one-dimensional setting. This follows from the fact that the transfinite diameter of the unit interval
[0,1]
is equal to
\frac{1}{4}
, which is significantly smaller than
1/e\sim 0.3678\cdots
. However, he remarks that there are no further gains to be made as the dimension increases, since the transfinite diameter of an
N
-dimensional cube
[0,1]^{N}
is no smaller.

One purpose of this note was to test if this conclusion is indeed the case, since it stands to reason that the larger the space of available linear forms, the better the approximations one might hope to obtain. Indeed
we find that his remark is unduly pessimistic, for at least three reasons. Firstly, our criterion depends on the transfinite diameter of the region
f(\sigma)
, which is typically much smaller than the unit cube. Secondly, the notion of transfinite diameter which is involved is somewhat different from the ones usually found in the literature. Finally, and most importantly, experiments suggest that the denominator estimates can be dramatically improved when one takes into account more parameters.

1.3.
Contents and results

This paper has two main strands. The first is to set up a framework for constructing matrices
Q^{\sigma}
associated to Mellin integrals and bounding their determinant in terms of a suitable generalisation of the notion of transfinite diameter. We believe that this underlies an interesting and very general algebraic structure associated to a family of algebraic Mellin transforms (
1.2
).

The second is to describe some experimental computations exploring the integrals (
1.1
) as a ‘proof of principle’. The main achievement of this paper is that we are able to predict
quite accurately the asymptotics of the matrix determinants using our general theory. Whilst there is (intentionally!) no possible new irrationality result to be gained from such an example, it enables us to probe the hypothesis that the method described here
improves
as more parameters are brought into play, and suggests that more substantial improvements might be gained from considering period integrals in higher dimension.

1.3.1.
Quadratic forms
Q^{\sigma}
and Vandermonde determinants

We now describe a simple construction of matrices
Q^{\sigma}
. Suppose we are given a finite sequence

(1.4)

\mathfrak{f}_{1},\mathfrak{f}_{2},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}

of linearly independent polynomials
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
in
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
on
X
. Consider the symmetric matrix:

Q^{\sigma}=\left(\int_{\sigma}\mathfrak{f}_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{j}\,\omega\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\quad\in\quad M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Q}\xi_{1}+\ldots+\mathbb{Q}\xi_{m})\ .

Its entries are linear combinations of the integrals (
1.3
).
The determinant of
Q^{\sigma}
only depends on the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{M}_{N}=\oplus_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{Z}\mathfrak{f}_{i}
. Consider

t_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(\sigma)=\sup_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\in\sigma}\left|\det(\mathfrak{f}_{i}(z_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\right|\ .

The right-hand side involves a generalised Vandermonde determinant associated to the module
\mathcal{M}_{N}
, and one has the following upper bound

\left|\det(Q^{\sigma})\right|\leq\left(t_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(\sigma)\right)^{2}\ ,

for all sufficiently large
N
. Furthermore, under some mild assumptions on the defining data
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},\omega,\sigma
we may show that the matrix
Q^{\sigma}
is positive-definite and hence has non-vanishing determinant.

Let
D^{\ell},D^{r}\in M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Q})
such that
A^{\sigma}=D^{\ell}Q^{\sigma}D^{r}
has entries which are integer coefficients in the
\xi_{i}
, and denote the ‘denominator’ of this data by
\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}=\det|D^{\ell}D^{r}|
.
Then Minkowski’s theorem, applied to
A^{\sigma}
, guarantees the existence of an integer linear form in
\xi_{i}
satisfying

0\quad<\quad\left|n_{1}\xi_{1}+\ldots+n_{m}\xi_{m}\right|\quad<\quad\left(\left(t_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(\sigma)\right)^{2}\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}\ .

See theorem
7.2
. This gives the following multi-parameter irrationality criterion:

Criterion 1.3
.

Suppose that
m=2
and
\xi_{1}=1,\xi_{2}=\xi
(or
m>2
,
\xi_{1}=1
, and each
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}[\xi]
, for
i>1
). Suppose that there exist sequences (
1.4
) of arbitrary length
N>\!>0
such that

t_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(\sigma)^{2}\,\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}\rightarrow 0\ ,

where
\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}
is defined relative to some choice of matrices
D^{\ell},D^{r}
as above.
Then
\xi
is irrational.

This criterion is suited to performing numerical experiments. The reader may wish to turn directly to §
9
for some detailed computations in the case of the
integrals (
1.1
), where we consider the cases with
r=1
,
r=2
and finally the full set of
r=5
parameters. Experiments suggest, as expected, that the quality of the approximations can improve as the number of parameters increases.

In order to make use of the previous criterion, one needs to have good control over the quantity
t_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(\sigma)
, which is a precursor to the transfinite diameter and is discussed below.
Most of the work presented here is devoting to studying it, and our results are in close agreement with the numerical data presented in §
9
.
The denominator
\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}
, in this case, is related to a possible notion of ‘
p
-adic transfinite diameter’ (see §
8.2.1
, Remark
9.7
).

1.3.2.
Supremal transfinite diameter

The data of a family of Mellin integrals can be encoded by a morphism of varieties

f:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{r}

where
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
. We let
V_{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{r}
denote the Zariski-closure of its image. It is an affine scheme which we call the anciliary image variety.

In order to better understand the quantities
t_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(\sigma)
, we shall replace the data of the functions (
1.4
) with an infinite rank
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^{r})\cong\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
equipped with an increasing filtration
\mathcal{N}_{n}\subset\mathcal{N}
of submodules of finite rank
N_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}
. We assume that the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{M}=f^{*}\mathcal{N}
is free, which amounts to the independence of (
1.4
).
The Mellin transforms define a symmetric bilinear pairing

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma}f^{*}(\mathfrak{m})f^{*}(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})\,\omega\ .

Under some mild assumptions it is non-degenerate, and even positive-definite. If the module
\mathcal{N}_{n}
has basis
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N_{n}}\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathbb{Z}
then the generalised Vandermonde matrix

V_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}=(\mathfrak{m}_{i}(z_{j}))

is well-defined up to permutations of rows and columns. The absolute value of its determinant is the function on
(\mathbb{C}^{r})^{N_{n}}
given by

|\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(z)|=|\det\mathcal{N}_{n}|
.
Given a region
\tau\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}
, we set
t_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}=\sup_{z\in\tau^{N_{n}}}|\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(z)|
. Finally we define the
supremal transfinite diameter
to be

(1.5)

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N},e}(\tau)=\limsup_{n}\left(t_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(\tau)\right)^{1/e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}}

where
e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}
are appropriately chosen integers which we call exponents. For special cases of modules
\mathcal{N}
, and compact
\tau
, the supremal transfinite diameter reduces to various notions of higher-dimensional transfinite diameter which have been studied in the literature. Much previous work has been concerned with the existence of the limits
[
Zah75
]
, but here we only need an upper bound, which is why it is enough to take a limit supremum, which exists unconditionally. Unfortunately, the higher-dimensional transfinite diameters are only known for some quite limited sets
\tau\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, even for
r=2
, but by leveraging known computations we can deduce fairly accurate bounds for the family (
1.1
). A related notion of transfinite diameter on algebraic varieties
was recently studied in
[
Ma‘11
,
CM17
]
.

The quantity
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N},e}(\tau)
measures, in a certain sense, both the size and shape of the region
\tau
. A crude rule of thumb is that it is typically proportional to the volume of
\tau
raised to some power which depends on the dimension of
\tau
and on the exponents
e
.

If we define a limit of denominators (having fixed a choice of period representatives
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
) by

(1.6)

\delta_{\mathcal{N},e}=\limsup_{n}\delta_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}^{1/e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}}

then the irrationality criterion
1.3
, may be rephrased, after taking the limit, as

Criterion 1.4
.

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N},e}(f(\sigma))^{2}\,\delta_{\mathcal{N},e}<1

In other words, the size of the image of the integration domain
f(\sigma)
inside the anciliary image variety
V_{f}(\mathbb{C})
, as measured by the module of functions
\mathcal{N}
, should not be too large relative to denominators.

1.4.
Intuitive example

In practice, the criterion (
1.4
) amounts to establishing an upper bound on the transfinite diameter of
f(\sigma)
.
In order to gain some intuition for the precise relationship, consider a family of examples where
m=2
, i.e., a family of Mellin integrals over
\mathbb{Q}

I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})=\int_{\sigma}f_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots f_{r}^{n_{r}}\omega\ \in\ \mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Q}\xi

where
\omega
is positive on
\sigma
,
f(\sigma)\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}
is Zariski-dense, and

(1.7)

d^{w}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}}I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\xi

for some ‘weight’
w\geq 0
, where
d_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}}=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\{\mathrm{lcm}\{1,\ldots,n_{i}\}\}
. Alternatively, one can assume
m>2
and that every
\xi_{i}
for
i>2
is a polynomial in
\xi
. This kind of denominator bound is fairly typical, but not universally applicable since the precise denominators vary from situation to situation.

In view of the bound (
1.7
), we consider the matrix
Q^{\sigma}
associated to the ‘rectangular’ module

\mathcal{N}_{n}=\bigoplus_{0\leq n_{i}\leq n}\,x_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots x_{r}^{n_{r}}\mathbb{Z}\ .

In other words, we form a symmetric matrix whose entries consists of integrals
I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
with arguments in the range
0\leq n_{i}\leq 2n
. The exponents are asymptotically
e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}\sim\frac{r}{2}n^{r+1}.
The supremal transfinite diameter
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}
is computed using the determinant of a ‘rectangular’ multivariable Vandermonde matrix.

The denominator computed in lemma
7.6
and the irrationality criterion
1.4
reduces via (
7.4
) to

(1.8)

\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))\,\exp\left(\frac{w}{r}\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)<1\

which is implied by the sufficient condition
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))\exp(\frac{2w}{r})<1
.
In the case when
r=1
,
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}
equals the classical 1-dimensional transfinite diameter
\mathrm{tr}
and
we retrieve the criterion of Zudilin:

\mathrm{tr}(f(\sigma))<\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}w\right)\ .

As we increase the number of parameters
r
, the threshold for irrationality in (
1.8
) improves. For instance, when

r=w
, we obtain the criterion for irrationality

\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))<\exp\left(-2+\frac{1}{w+1}\right)\ .

A universal sufficient condition is thus
4\,\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))<4\exp\left(-2\right)=0.5413\ldots

Typically
f(\sigma)\subset[0,1]^{r}
is contained in the unit square, which satisfies
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}([0,1]^{r})=\frac{1}{4}
. So for the criterion to apply when
r=w
,
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))
needs to be (very roughly) less than half that of the unit square.

Example 1.5
.

Consider the
\zeta(2)
integrals (
1.1
), for which
w=2
and
m=2
. In §
9
we study in some detail the two-parameter subfamily
I(n_{2},n_{1},n_{2},n_{1},n_{2})
which amounts to setting
r=2
and letting

f_{1}=\frac{y(1-x)}{1-xy}\quad,\quad f_{2}=x(1-y)\ .

All conditions assumed above hold. The map
f=(f_{1},f_{2})
maps the region
[0,1]^{2}
to a subset of the unit square which lies below the hyperbola
\{(x,y):xy=0.091\}
. Criterion (
1.8
)
requires that

4\,\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))<4\exp(-5/3)=0.7555\ldots

Using the techniques developed in this paper, we can prove that
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(f(\sigma))\sim 0.14
to an accuracy of about
\pm 0.01
, which is amply suffficient for a ‘2-dimensional’ irrationality proof for
\zeta(2)
. In this example, however, we observe that the denominators for
Q^{\sigma}
are considerably smaller than the rather crude bounds used above, and a much weaker inequality is sufficient in practice to establish irrationality (for instance, we can comfortably replace
0.7555\ldots
with
0.81
in the above).

1.5.
Plan and comments

In §
2.1
, we review some background on algebraic Mellin integrals, introduce the anciliary image variety and illustrate with some simple examples. In §
3
we define positive-definite matrices
Q^{\sigma}
and bound their determinants. In §
4
we define a very general notion of Vandermonde determinant associated to a module,
and in §
5
we define the corresponding notion of supremal transfinite diameter. The literature is unfortunately lacking effective techniques for estimating the transfinite diameter as far as we are aware, but in this section we introduce some methods which give some upper bounds for tensor products and direct sums of modules, which are in turn applied in §
6
to a 2-dimensional region bounded by the unit cube and a hyperbola. In §
7
we derive in some more detail the irrationality criterion outlined in the introduction and §
8
discusses generalisations,
p
-adic variants and so on. The reader may wish to turn immediately to §
9
where the family of integrals for
\zeta(2)
are studied in several different ways. These examples are not intended to be anything more than a proof of principle; we did not attempt to optimise the choices for the modules
\mathcal{N}
. Indeed it might be interesting to consider modules whose monomial bases in
r
variables are centered around on one or more of the Rhin-Viola ‘lines’ mentioned earlier.

There are many other families of integrals for which it would be interesting to study the multi-parameter irrationality criterion introduced here. Likewise, it would be very interesting to generalise the irrationality criterion
1.4
to a linear independence criterion, by bounding the numerators in the linear forms obtained from Minkowski’s theorem. This will be postponed to another day.

2.
Mellin transforms and anciliary image variety

We briefly review some properties of Mellin transforms before introducing the anciliary image variety.

2.1.
Algebraic Mellin integrals

Let
X
be an affine algebraic variety over
\mathbb{Q}
equipped with a morphism to affine space of dimension
r>0
:

(2.1)

f:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{r}=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]\ .

Denote the coordinates of
f
by
f_{i}=x_{i}\circ f
, for
1\leq i\leq r
, so that
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
.
Let
\omega\in\Omega^{d}(X)
(the global sections of the sheaf of regular
d
-forms) be a regular differential form of degree
d
and

\sigma\subset X(\mathbb{C})
a relative
d
-chain whose boundary is contained in the complex points of a divisor
Y\subset X
, which we can assume to be normal crossing. The integral

(2.2)

I(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})=\int_{\sigma}f_{1}^{s_{1}}\ldots f_{r}^{s_{r}}\,\omega

defines a function of
s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}
which is holomorphic for
\mathrm{Re}(s_{i})\geq 0
and extends meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}^{r}
. In some examples, the form
\omega
may additionally have poles along the boundary of
\sigma
. One can reduce to the previous situation by performing blow-ups to resolve singularities locally along the boundary of
\sigma
.

2.1.1.
Periods

For non-negative integer values of
s_{i}
, the integrals
I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
are periods of
H^{d}(X,Y)
. In particular, there exist
m
regular differential forms
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}\in\Omega^{d}(X)
, whose relative de Rham cohomology classes are independent in
H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{d}(X,Y)
such that for all
\underline{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
non-negative integers there exist unique rational numbers
a_{1}(\underline{n}),\ldots,a_{m}(\underline{n})
with:

(2.3)

{[}f_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots f_{r}^{n_{r}}\omega]=a_{1}(\underline{n})[\omega_{1}]+\ldots+a_{m}(\underline{n})[\omega_{m}]\qquad\in\quad H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{d}(X,Y)\ .

Note that
m
may be strictly smaller than
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\,H^{d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X,Y)
. If we denote by

\xi_{i}=\int_{\sigma}\omega_{i}\quad\hbox{ for }\quad 1\leq i\leq m

then
\xi_{i}
is a period of
H^{n}(X,Y)
since
\partial\sigma\subset Y(\mathbb{C})
. We deduce the

Lemma 2.1
.

The Mellin integral (
2.2
) at integer values:

I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})=a_{1}(\underline{n})\xi_{1}+\ldots+a_{m}(\underline{n})\xi_{m}\quad\in\quad\mathbb{Q}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}]

is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear form in finitely many periods
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
of
H^{n}(X,Y)
.

2.1.2.
Holonomicity

It is well-known that, as we vary
n_{i}
, the rational numbers (
2.3
) satisfy holonomic recurrence equations (in several variables) (e.g.,
[
LS91
]
). On a more basic level, the cohomology classes
{[}f_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots f_{r}^{n_{r}}\omega]
satisfy linear relations with coefficients which are polynomials in the
n_{i}
, akin to integration-by-parts identities.
From a different perspective, for every
1\leq i\leq r
, there is a shift operator
\tau_{i}
which represents the action of multiplication by
f_{i}
on cohomology classes of degree
d
in the twisted algebraic de Rham cohomology of

X\,\setminus\,V(f_{1}\ldots f_{r})
relative to
Y
, with coefficients in the algebraic vector bundle with connection
(\mathcal{O}_{X},\nabla)
, where
\nabla=d+\sum_{i=1}^{r}s_{i}\frac{df_{i}}{f_{i}}
. In a suitable basis, these may represented by contiguity matrices, from which the matrices
Q^{\sigma}
may often be derived. We will not exploit this structure in the present work, but provide some details in the case of the integrals (
1.1
) in an Appendix.

2.2.
Anciliary image variety

Let
(X,f)
be as above. An important role is played by what we shall call the
anciliary image variety
associated to the data of a Mellin transform.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
V_{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{r}
denote the Zariski closure of
f(X)
.
By abuse, we shall simply write
f:X\rightarrow V_{f}
for the natural map through which (
2.1
) factorises.

The affine coordinate ring of
V_{f}
is the quotient
\mathbb{Q}[f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}]/I
, where
I
is the ideal generated by the algebraic relations satisfied by the functions
f_{i}
. By abuse of notation, we write

(2.4)

f(\sigma)\subset V_{f}(\mathbb{C})\ .

Two key quantities are the
number of parameters

r
, and the
anciliary dimension

\dim V_{f}\leq\dim X
.

2.3.
Some examples

We give two very basic examples to illustrate the role played by the number of parameters and of the ramification locus of the map
f:X\rightarrow V_{f}
.

2.3.1.
Rational numbers

Let
X=\mathbb{A}^{1}
,
Y=\{0,1\}
and
\sigma=[0,1]
.
First let
r=1
, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}
denote the map
f=x(1-x)
, where
x
is the coordinate on
\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
Set
\omega=dx
. Then
the family of integrals

I(n)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(x(1-x)\right)^{n}dx

are periods of
H^{1}(\mathbb{A}^{1},\{0,1\})\cong\mathbb{Q}
and are all rational.
The anciliary image variety is
V_{f}\cong\mathbb{A}^{1}=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Q}[f]
.
The image
f(\sigma)\subset V_{f}(\mathbb{C})
is the interval
[0,\frac{1}{4}]
, as the function
f
is maximised on the interval
[0,1]
at the point
x=\frac{1}{2}.

The boundary points
\{0,\frac{1}{4}\}
of
f(\sigma)
can be
determined algebraically. To see why, note that
the critical locus of
f
is the vanishing locus of
f^{\prime}=1-2x
. Thus the discriminant of
f
is

\mathrm{Disc}(f)=\{\frac{1}{4}\}\subset V_{f}
, and

f:X\rightarrow V_{f}=\mathbb{A}^{1}
is a double covering of affine spaces ramified at
\frac{1}{4}
.
The locus
f(Y)
is the single point
\{0\}\subset V_{f}
.

Now let
r=2
, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{2}
denote the map
f=(x,1-x)
.
Set
\omega=dx
. Then
the following family of integrals generalises the previous example:

I(n_{1},n_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}x^{n_{1}}(1-x)^{n_{2}}dx\ .

They are periods of
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{A}^{1},\{0,1\})\cong\mathbb{Q}
and are again all rational.
The anciliary image variety
V_{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
is defined by the locus
x_{1}+x_{2}=1
and is isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}^{1}
. Then
f:X\rightarrow V_{f}
is an isomorphism and the image of
f(\sigma)
is simply the unit interval in
V_{f}
relative to the coordinate
x_{1}
.

2.3.2.
A logarithm

Let
X=\mathbb{A}^{1}\backslash\{-1\}
,
Y=\{0,1\}
and
\sigma=[0,1]
.
First let
r=1
and define
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}
by

f=\frac{x(1-x)}{(1+x)}
. Let
\omega=\frac{dx}{1+x}
. The associated integrals

I(n)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{x(1-x)}{1+x}\right)^{n}\frac{dx}{1+x}

are periods of
H^{1}(\mathbb{A}^{1}\backslash\{-1\},\{0,1\})
which is a Kummer extension of
\mathbb{Q}(-1)
by
\mathbb{Q}(0)
. It has two periods
\xi_{1}=1
and
\xi_{2}=\log 2
and one may easily deduce that
I(n)\in\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Z}\,\log 2
(the coefficient of
\xi_{1}
is an integer since it is obtained as a residue of the cohomology class of the integrand).
The function
f^{\prime}
vanishes at
x=-1\pm\sqrt{2}
, and the corresponding critical values of
f
are
3\pm 2\sqrt{2}
. Thus the boundary of
f(\sigma)
is contained in
f(Y)\cup\mathrm{Disc}(f)=\{0\}\cup\{3\pm 2\sqrt{2}\}
and indeed

f(\sigma)=[0,3-2\sqrt{2}]\ .

Now let
r=2
, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{2}
denote the map
f=(\frac{x}{1+x},\frac{1-x}{1+x})
.
Set
\omega=\frac{dx}{1+x}
and
\sigma=[0,1]
. Then
the family of integrals

I(n_{1},n_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{n_{1}}(1-x)^{n_{2}}}{(x+1)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\frac{dx}{(x+1)}

are periods of the same cohomology group as before.
Thus
I(n_{1},n_{2})
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of
1
and
\log 2
.
The anciliary image variety
V_{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
is isomorphic to the affine line
2x_{1}+x_{2}=1
, and
f(Y)=\{(0,1),(\frac{1}{2},0)\}
. The region
f(\sigma)
is thus the interval
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
in the coordinate
x_{1}
.

3.
Quadratic forms associated to Mellin transforms

3.1.
A bilinear form

Let
(X,f)
be as in §
2
and let
V_{f}
denote the anciliary image variety.
Consider the bilinear symmetric pairing

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(V_{f})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{O}(V_{f})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle g\otimes h

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma}gh\,\omega\ .

We may restrict this pairing to any free
\mathbb{Z}
-module

\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{O}(V_{f})\ .

If
\mathcal{M}
has finite rank with generators
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
, then this pairing is represented by a symmetric matrix.

Definition 3.1
.

With these notations, define the
N\times N
symmetric matrix

(Q^{\sigma}_{N})_{ij}=\int_{\sigma}\mathfrak{f}_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{j}\,\omega\qquad\hbox{ for }1\leq i,j\leq N\ .

Note that the definition uses the multiplicative (ring) structure on
\mathcal{O}(V_{f})
, but that we
do not
need to assume that
\mathcal{M}
is stable under multiplication.

The determinant
\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})
is well-defined, since
changing the basis of
\mathcal{M}
by
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{Z})
results in the matrix

P^{T}Q^{\sigma}_{N}P
which has the same determinant.

3.2.
Positive-definiteness

We make the following assumptions.

(P1)

The form
\omega
is real and non-negative on
\sigma
.

(P2)

The generators
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
of
\mathcal{M}
are real-valued on
\sigma
.

(P3)

The domain
\sigma
is Zariski-dense in
X
.

In some circumstances these assumptions can be relaxed (see §
8
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
(P1)
-
(P3)
. Then the bilinear pairing (
3.1
) is positive semi-definite.
Let
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
be any elements in
\mathcal{O}(V_{f})
. The following are equivalent:

(i) The elements
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
are linearly independent.

(ii) The matrix
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
is positive-definite.

Proof.

Let
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
define an element
P_{v}\in\mathcal{O}(V_{f})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
by

P_{v}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}v_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{i}
.
By assumption (P1) and (P2), the integral

I(v)=\int_{\sigma}P_{v}^{2}\,\omega\geq 0

is non-negative and vanishes if and only if
P_{v}=0
. Since

P_{v}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{i}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}v_{i}v_{j}\mathfrak{f}_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{j}\ ,

the integral
I(v)

may be written as the quadratic form

I(v)=v^{T}Q^{\sigma}_{N}\,v\ .

By (P3), the restriction
\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\sigma)
is injective; the morphism

f^{*}:\mathcal{O}(V_{f})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
is injective by definition.
Suppose that
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
are linearly independent. Then
P_{v}
vanishes on
\sigma
if and only if
v_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. Equivalently,
I(v)=0
if and only if all
v_{i}=0
vanish, and hence
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
is positive definite. The converse is trivial, since
if
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
are not independent, there exists a non-trivial vector
v
such that
P_{v}=0
, and hence
I(v)=v^{T}Q^{\sigma}_{N}v=0
.
∎

In particular, when
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
are a basis for a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{M}
, the determinant
\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})
only depends on
\mathcal{M}
and under assumptions
(P1)
-
(P3)
we have

\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})>0\ .

3.3.
Vandermonde determinant associated to
\mathcal{M}

Let
X^{N}
denote the
N
-fold product
X\times\ldots\times X
and let
z_{i}:X^{N}\rightarrow X
denote the projection onto the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
component.
Let
\sigma^{N}\subset X^{N}(\mathbb{C})
denote the
N
-fold product of the chains
\sigma
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathbb{Z}\mathfrak{f}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Z}\mathfrak{f}_{N}
be a free
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of
\mathcal{O}(V_{f})
of rank
N
.
For any
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})\in X^{N}(\mathbb{C})
we consider an
N\times N
matrix

V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)=(\mathfrak{f}_{j}(z_{i}))\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N\ ,

whose rows are indexed by the components of
z
and whose columns are indexed by
\mathfrak{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{f}_{N}
, in some chosen order. Its determinant is well-defined up to a sign and hence its square is well-defined (it is independent of the choice of generators of
\mathcal{M}
).

Remark 3.4
.

The natural map
\mathcal{O}(V_{f})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}(X)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\sigma)
, which restricts functions to
\sigma
, induces a map

\textstyle{\bigwedge^{\!\!N}}\mathcal{O}(V_{f})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(\sigma^{N})
.
The function

\left(\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\right)^{2}\ \in\ C^{\infty}(\sigma^{N})

is simply the square of the image of a generator of

\det(\mathcal{M})\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

The matrix
V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)
is a generalised Vandermonde matrix.

3.4.
Calculation of the determinant of
Q^{\sigma}_{N}

The
N
-fold external tensor product of
\omega
on
X^{N}
will be denoted by

\omega^{\boxtimes N}=\underbrace{\omega\boxtimes\omega\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes\omega}_{N}\ \in\ \Omega^{dN}(X^{N})\ .

Many thanks to a referee for pointing out that the following result goes back to the work of Heine and Szëgo in the theory of orthogonal polynomials
[
And83
]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The determinant of
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
is given by the integral:

(3.2)

\det Q^{\sigma}_{N}=\frac{1}{N!}\int_{\sigma^{N}}\left(\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\right)^{2}\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}\ .

Proof.

Let
\Sigma_{N}
be the symmetric group on
N
elements and let
\varepsilon:\Sigma_{N}\rightarrow\{1,-1\}
be the sign representation. If
h\in\mathcal{O}(X)
we write
h(z_{i})
for
z_{i}^{*}(h)\in\mathcal{O}(X^{N})
. Recall that
\mathcal{O}(V_{f})
embeds in
\mathcal{O}(X)
. Then:

\displaystyle\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})=

\displaystyle\det\left(\int_{\sigma}\mathfrak{f}_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{j}\,\omega\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma_{N}}\varepsilon_{\tau}\int_{\sigma}\mathfrak{f}_{1}\mathfrak{f}_{\tau(1)}\omega\,\times\int_{\sigma}\mathfrak{f}_{2}\mathfrak{f}_{\tau(2)}\,\omega\times\ldots\times\int_{\sigma}\mathfrak{f}_{N}\mathfrak{f}_{\tau(N)}\,\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma_{N}}\varepsilon_{\tau}\int_{\sigma^{N}}\mathfrak{f}_{1}(z_{1})\ldots\mathfrak{f}_{N}(z_{N})\mathfrak{f}_{\tau(1)}(z_{1})\ldots\mathfrak{f}_{\tau(N)}(z_{N})\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma^{N}}\mathfrak{f}_{1}(z_{1})\ldots\mathfrak{f}_{N}(z_{N})\det\left(\mathfrak{f}_{j}(z_{i}))\right)\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma^{N}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{f}_{i}(z_{i})\right)\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}\ .

Now consider the action of
\Sigma_{N}
on
X^{N}
by permuting factors. By functoriality of integration, we deduce that for any
\pi\in\Sigma_{N}
,

\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})=\int_{\pi^{-1}(\sigma^{N})}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{f}_{i}(z_{\pi(i)})\right)\pi^{*}\left(\det(V_{\mathcal{M}}(z))\right)\,\pi^{*}(\omega^{\boxtimes N})\ .

The action of
\pi
on the determinant is
\pi^{*}\left(\det(V_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(z))\right)=\varepsilon(\pi)\det(V_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}(z))
. Furthermore, the action of
\pi

results in a
simultaneous change in orientation of both the integration cycle
\sigma^{N}
and the form
\omega^{\boxtimes N}
which cancels out, i.e., there exists
\eta\in\{1,-1\}
such that

\pi^{*}(\omega^{\boxtimes N})=\eta\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}
and
\pi^{-1}(\sigma^{N})=\eta\,\sigma^{N}
. Therefore, for any such
\pi\in\Sigma_{N}
one has the formula

\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})=\int_{\sigma^{N}}\left(\varepsilon(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{f}_{i}(z_{\pi(i)})\right)\det(V_{\mathcal{M}}(z))\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}\ ,

and by summing over all elements
\pi\in\Sigma_{N}
we conclude that

\displaystyle N!\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})=\int_{\sigma^{N}}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{N}}\left(\varepsilon(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{f}_{i}(z_{\pi(i)})\right)\det(V_{\mathcal{M}}(z))\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}=\int_{\sigma^{N}}\left(\det(V_{\mathcal{M}}(z))\right)^{2}\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}\ .

∎

3.5.
An upper bound for the determinant

Finally, the determinant of the matrix
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
may be bounded above in the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{O}(V_{f})
be a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
N
. Define

(3.3)

t_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma)=\sup_{z\in\sigma^{N}}\left|\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\right|\ .

It is well-defined since changing basis of
\mathcal{M}
replaces
V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)
by

P\,V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\,P^{T}
for some
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{Z})
, which has determinant in
\{1,-1\}
.

Under the assumptions
(P1)
,
(P2)
, the right-hand side of (
3.3
) is the non-negative square root of the Vandermonde determinant defined in remark

3.4
.

Corollary 3.7
.

If
N=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}
, is larger than a constant which depends only on
\int_{\sigma}\omega
, then

(3.4)

|\det Q^{\sigma}_{N}|^{\frac{1}{N}}<t_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma)^{\frac{2}{N}}\ .

If, furthermore, the assumptions
(P1)
–
(P3)
hold then
0<(\det Q^{\sigma}_{N})^{\frac{1}{N}}
.

Proof.

By equation (
3.2
) we have

\left|\det Q^{\sigma}_{N}\right|\leq\frac{1}{N!}\left|\int_{\sigma^{N}}\,\omega^{\boxtimes N}\right|\sup_{z\in\sigma^{N}}\left|\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\right|^{2}

and hence

|\det Q^{\sigma}_{N}|\leq\frac{1}{N!}\left|\int_{\sigma}\omega\right|^{N}\,\sup_{z\in\sigma^{N}}\left|\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\right|^{2}\ .

By Stirling’s formula,
(N!)^{\frac{1}{N}}
exceeds
|\int_{\sigma}\omega|
for all sufficiently large
N
, hence

|\det Q^{\sigma}_{N}|^{\frac{1}{N}}<\sup_{z\in\sigma^{N}}\left|\det V_{\mathcal{M}}(z)\right|^{\frac{2}{N}}\ .

Finally, if
(P1)
–
(P3)
hold,
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
is positive definite by lemma
3.2
.
∎

3.6.
Linear algebra interpretation

The following remark can be skipped, but may illuminate proposition
3.5
.
The identity (
3.2
) is a manifestation of a completely general algebraic identity.
Let
V
be a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
N
and embed

\textstyle{\bigwedge^{\!\!N}(\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V)}\quad\subset\quad V^{\otimes 2N}\ .

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
denote a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
V
. The determinant
\det(V)=\bigwedge^{\!\!N}V
is the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
1
generated by

e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{N}
. We presume that the following lemma must be known.

Lemma 3.8
.

There is a natural inclusion

\rho:\mathrm{Sym}^{2}\left(\det(V)\right)\hookrightarrow\textstyle{\bigwedge^{\!N}(\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V)}

which sends

(e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{N})^{2}\mapsto N!\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{N}}\epsilon(\sigma)(e_{1}.e_{\alpha(1)})\otimes\ldots\otimes(e_{N}.e_{\alpha(N)})

where the symmetric group
\Sigma_{N}
acts on
(\mathrm{Sym}^{2})^{\otimes N}
by permuting factors.

Note that the module
\mathrm{Sym}^{2}\left(\det(V)\right)
is the Schur functor
\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}V
where
\lambda
is the rectangular Young diagram with
2
columns and
N
rows.

Proof.

Via the inclusion
\det(V)\hookrightarrow V^{\otimes N}
we may identify

e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{N}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\epsilon(\sigma)\,e_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{\sigma(N)}\ .

Thus the map
\rho
satisfies

\displaystyle\rho\left((e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{N})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\Sigma_{N}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\,(e_{\sigma(1)}.e_{\tau(1)})\otimes\ldots\otimes(e_{\sigma(N)}.e_{\tau(N)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{N}}\epsilon(\alpha)\,(e_{\sigma(1)}.e_{\alpha\sigma(1)})\otimes\ldots\otimes(e_{\sigma(N)}.e_{\alpha\sigma(N)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N!\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{N}}\epsilon(\alpha)\,(e_{1}.e_{\alpha(1)})\otimes\ldots\otimes(e_{N}.e_{\alpha(N)})

where in the first line we view
V^{\otimes 2N}=V^{\otimes N}\otimes V^{\otimes N}
with the natural action of
\Sigma_{N}\times\Sigma_{N}
permuting factors in each component, the notation
a.b
denotes the symmetrised tensor product
a\otimes b+b\otimes a
, and in the third line we write
\alpha=\tau\sigma^{-1}
and use the fact that
\epsilon(\alpha)=\epsilon(\sigma)^{-1}\epsilon(\tau)=\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)
. ∎

If we have a symmetric product
\langle,\rangle:\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V\rightarrow R
to some commutative ring
R
, then the
N
-fold product defines a map

(\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(V))^{\otimes N}\rightarrow R
. It may be applied to both sides to give the well-known formula for a Gram determinant:

\langle e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{N},e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{N}\rangle=N!\det\left(\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\ .

4.
Vandermonde determinants associated to modules

Having already considered Vandermonde determinants on
X^{N}(\mathbb{C})
, we now need to consider generalised Vandermonde determinants on the anciliary image variety
V^{N}_{f}(\mathbb{C})
.

4.1.
Vandermonde matrices

Let
V=\mathrm{Spec}\,R
be an affine algebraic variety of finite type over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a free
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of
R
of rank
N
. Choose a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\mathfrak{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{N}
. Let
z=(z_{1},\ldots z_{N})\in V^{N}(\mathbb{C})
and define the matrix

(4.1)

V_{\mathcal{N}}(z)=\left(\mathfrak{m}_{j}(z_{i})\right)\ .

It is a generalised Vandermonde matrix, and its determinant only depends on the choice of generators
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
by a sign. In particular, the function

\left|\det V_{\mathcal{N}}(z)\right|\quad\hbox{ on }\quad V^{N}(\mathbb{C})

is well-defined and symmetric: it equals
\left|\det\mathcal{N}\right|
, where
\det\mathcal{N}=\bigwedge^{\!\!N}\mathcal{N}
.
For any set
\tau\subset V(\mathbb{C})
, denote its
N
-fold product by
\tau^{N}\subset V^{N}(\mathbb{C})
, and define

(4.2)

t_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)=\sup_{z\in\tau^{N}}\left|\det V_{\mathcal{N}}(z)\right|\ .

It is a non-negative real number, or infinite.

The number
t_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\geq 0
is well-defined and is related to notions of transfinite diameter on an algebraic variety.
Clearly, if
\tau\subset\tau^{\prime}
then
t_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq t_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau^{\prime})
.
The definition makes sense when
\mathcal{N}
is not free, but in this case the determinant function is identically zero, so we will usually assume that
\mathcal{N}
is free.

If
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{N}
is a free submodule of rank equal to that of
\mathcal{N}
then

(4.3)

t_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)=t_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\,C

where
C
is the cardinality of the finite torsion module
\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M}
.

4.1.1.
Morphisms

Consider a morphism

f:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}

between two affine varieties of finite type over
\mathbb{Q}
where
V=\mathrm{Spec}\,R
,
V^{\prime}=\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\prime}
.

Definition 4.2
.

A free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{N}\subset R^{\prime}

will be called
f
-
admissible
if

f^{*}:\mathcal{N}\longrightarrow f^{*}\mathcal{N}

is injective, where
f^{*}\mathcal{N}
, for want of a better notation, denotes the
\mathbb{Z}
-module which is the image of
\mathcal{N}
under the morphism
f^{*}:R^{\prime}\rightarrow R
of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules.

For a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{N}
of rank
N
, we denote a choice of basis by
\mathfrak{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{N}
.
Thus
\mathcal{N}
is
f
-admissible if and only if
\mathfrak{f}_{i}=\mathfrak{m}_{i}\circ f
, for
1\leq i\leq N
, are linearly independent functions on
V(\mathbb{C})
.
When this holds,

(4.4)

t_{f^{*}\mathcal{N}}(\tau)=t_{\mathcal{N}}(f\tau)\ .

4.1.2.
Main example: the anciliary image variety

Recall that the anciliary image variety is

V_{f}=\mathrm{Spec}\left(\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]/I\right)

and the natural inclusion
V_{f}\subset\mathbb{A}^{r}
is denoted by
f^{*}
. We will consider
f
-admissible free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
. Equation
(
4.4
) trades the usually more complicated functions
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
on the set
\sigma\subset X(\mathbb{C})
, which in our applications is simply a hypercube, against simpler functions
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
, which are typically monomials in the coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
, but on the region
f\sigma\subset V_{f}(\mathbb{C})
.

4.2.
Classical examples of Vandermonde determinants

Let
V\cong\mathbb{A}^{r}
and
R=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
.

4.2.1.
One-dimensional Vandermonde matrix

Let
r=1
, and let
\mathcal{N}_{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}]
be the
\mathbb{Z}
-module consisting of polynomials in
x=x_{1}
of degree
<n
. In the basis

\mathfrak{m}_{1}=1,\mathfrak{m}_{2}=x,\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{n}=x^{n-1}
, the matrix

V_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}1&z_{1}&\ldots&z_{1}^{n-1}\\
1&z_{2}&\ldots&z_{2}^{n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&z_{n}&\ldots&z_{n}^{n-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\

is the classical Vandermonde matrix, and hence

t_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(\tau)=\sup_{z\in\tau^{N}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left|z_{i}-z_{j}\right|\

is related to the logarithmic capacity, or transfinite diameter, of
\tau
. There are several possible higher-dimensional generalisations. We consider two special cases, the ‘rectangular’ and ‘homogeneous’ versions.

4.2.2.
‘Rectangular’ Vandermonde matrices

Let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\geq 0
and let

\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})}=\bigoplus_{0\leq i_{1}<n_{1},\ldots,0\leq i_{r}<n_{r}}\mathbb{Z}\,x_{1}^{i_{1}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}\ldots x_{r}^{i_{r}}

be the free module generated by all monomials of degree
<n_{i}
in each variable
x_{i}
.
It is the tensor product

(4.5)

\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})}\cong\mathcal{N}_{n_{1}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{n_{2}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\ldots\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{n_{r}}

where
\mathcal{N}_{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}[x]
consists of polynomials of degree
<n
as in §
4.2.1
. The matrix
V_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})}(z)=V_{\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}}(z)
is called the
rectangular multivariable Vandermonde matrix
.
Its rank and the determinantal degree are

(4.6)

\mathrm{rank}\,\,\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})}=n_{1}\ldots n_{r}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\deg\,\left(\det\,\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}\right)=\frac{n_{1}\ldots n_{r}}{2}(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}-r)\ .

Example 4.3
.

Let
r=2
, and
n_{1}=3,n_{2}=2
. Then if we denote the coordinates on
\mathbb{A}^{2}
by
(x,y)
, we may write
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and we have

\mathcal{V}_{(3,2)}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{6})=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{1}^{2}&y_{1}&x_{1}y_{1}&x_{1}^{2}y_{1}\\
1&x_{2}&x_{2}^{2}&y_{2}&x_{2}y_{2}&x_{2}^{2}y_{2}\\
1&x_{3}&x_{3}^{2}&y_{3}&x_{3}y_{3}&x_{3}^{2}y_{3}\\
1&x_{4}&x_{4}^{2}&y_{4}&x_{4}y_{4}&x_{4}^{2}y_{4}\\
1&x_{5}&x_{5}^{2}&y_{5}&x_{5}y_{5}&x_{5}^{2}y_{5}\\
1&x_{6}&x_{6}^{2}&y_{6}&x_{6}y_{6}&x_{6}^{2}y_{6}\end{pmatrix}

Its determinant has degree
9
.
Its absolute value is a function on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{6}
.

4.2.3.
‘Homogeneous’ Vandermonde matrices

Let
n\geq 0
and let

\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\mathrm{hom}}=\bigoplus_{0\leq k<n}\bigoplus_{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{r}=k}\mathbb{Z}\,x_{1}^{i_{1}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}\ldots x_{r}^{i_{r}}

be the direct sum of the free modules generated by all homogeneous monomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
of degree
k
, for
0\leq k<n
.
When
r=1
it coincides with the rectangular case.
It has rank and determinantal degree

(4.7)

\mathrm{rank}\,\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\mathrm{hom}}=\binom{n+r-1}{r}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\deg\left(\det\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\mathrm{hom}}\right)=r\binom{n+r-1}{r+1}\ .

Example 4.4
.

Let
r=2
and let
\mathbb{A}^{2}
have coordinates
(x,y)
as above. Then

\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{hom}}_{3}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{6})=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&y_{1}&x_{1}^{2}&x_{1}y_{1}&y^{2}_{1}\\
1&x_{2}&y_{2}&x_{2}^{2}&x_{2}y_{2}&y^{2}_{2}\\
1&x_{3}&y_{3}&x_{3}^{2}&x_{3}y_{3}&y^{2}_{3}\\
1&x_{4}&y_{4}&x_{4}^{2}&x_{4}y_{4}&y^{2}_{4}\\
1&x_{5}&y_{5}&x_{5}^{2}&x_{5}y_{5}&y^{2}_{5}\\
1&x_{6}&y_{6}&x_{6}^{2}&x_{6}y_{6}&y^{2}_{6}\end{pmatrix}

The determinant has degree
8
.
Its absolute value is a function on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{6}
.

4.3.
Upper bounds for Vandermonde determinants

We can deduce upper bounds for
t_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)
relative to basic operations on
\mathbb{Z}
-modules by applying formulae for determinants.
Here we consider direct sums (using a formula due to Laplace which essentially computes
\det(\mathcal{N}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2})
) and tensor products (using a formula in Appendix
A
, which computes
\det(\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2})
).

4.3.1.
Tensor products

Let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}
be two free
\mathbb{Z}
-submodules of
R
of finite rank, such that the image of their tensor product
\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}
in
R
via the map
R\otimes R\rightarrow R
is also free.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}
be free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules as above, and let
n_{i}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{i}
, for
i=1,2
.
Let
\tau\subset V(\mathbb{C})
. Then there is an upper bound

t_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau)\leq\frac{(n_{1}n_{2})!}{H_{n_{1},n_{2}}}\,\,t^{n_{2}}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau)t^{n_{1}}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau)\ ,

where the number
H
is defined in (
A.3
).

Proof.

Let
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n_{1}n_{2}})
in
V^{n_{1}n_{2}}(\mathbb{C})
.
For a suitable ordering on the generators, the Vandermonde matrix of the tensor product is an amalgam:

V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z)=A(z)\star B(z)\qquad\hbox{ where }\qquad A(z)=\left(\mathfrak{m}_{j}(z_{i})\right)\quad\hbox{,}\quad B(z)=\left(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{j}(z_{i})\right)

are matrices with
n_{1}n_{2}
rows and
n_{1}
(resp.
n_{2}
) columns,
where
\mathfrak{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}_{n_{1}}
is a basis for
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{n_{2}}
is a basis for
\mathcal{N}_{2}
.
It follows from theorem
A.3
that

\left|\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z)\right|\leq\frac{(n_{1}n_{2})!}{H_{n_{1},n_{2}}}\,\max_{I,J}\left(\left|\det(V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(z_{i})_{i\in I})\right|^{n_{2}}\left|\det(V_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z_{j})_{j\in J})\right|^{n_{1}}\right)

where
I,J
are subsets of
\{1,\ldots,mn\}
with
|I|=n_{1}
and
|J|=n_{2}
. It follows that if
z\in\tau^{n_{1}n_{2}}
, i.e., all components
z_{i}\in\tau
, we have

\left|\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z)\right|\leq\frac{(n_{1}n_{2})!}{H_{n_{1},n_{2}}}t^{n_{2}}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau)t^{n_{1}}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau)\ .

By taking the supremum over all
z\in\tau^{n_{1}n_{2}}
, we obtain the stated inequality.
∎

Note that even if the generators
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{j}
satisfy linear relations (i.e., the image of
\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}
is not free) the left-hand side vanishes and the bound is still valid.
The inequality in the previous proposition can, after taking suitable limits, become an equality in certain situations: see
[
Bro25
]
[Theorem 6.1] for an example in the case when
\tau
is a product. The examples of §
6
show that the further one deviates from such a product situation, the worse the upper bound can become.

4.3.2.
Direct sums

A similar, but more elementary, formula holds for direct sums.
Let
\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2}
be free
\mathbb{Z}
-submodules of
R
of finite rank such that the image of
\mathcal{N}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2}
in
R
via the map
R\oplus R\rightarrow R
is free.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}
be as above, and let
n_{i}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{i}
, for
i=1,2
. Let

\tau\subset V(\mathbb{C})
. Then there is an upper bound

t_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau)\leq\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}}{n_{1}}\,\,t_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau)t_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau)\ .

Proof.

Let
z=(z_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n_{1}+n_{2}}\in V(\mathbb{C})^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
.
Expanding the determinant of
V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z)
via Laplace’s formula as a sum over complementary minors gives

\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z)=\sum_{I,J}\varepsilon_{I,J}\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(z_{I})\det V_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(z_{J})

where the sum is over all subsets
I\subset\{1,\ldots,n_{1}+n_{2}\}
such that
|I|=n_{1}
, and
J
is its complement. The coefficients satisfy
\varepsilon_{I,J}\in\{1,-1\}
.
Now take the absolute value and the supremum over
z\in\tau^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
. ∎

5.
Supremal transfinite diameter

We define a notion of transfinite diameter in which the limit is replaced by a limit supremum, and work in the setting of affine schemes
V
. The limits will be taken with respect to certain exponents
e
, which are introduced to allow additional flexibility. In this section we state and prove some properties of the supremal diameter with respect to natural operations on modules.

In brief, the supremal transfinite diameter provides a pairing between subsets
\tau\subset V(\mathbb{C})
and free modules
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{O}(V)
with some additional data. It produces a number denoted
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.

5.1.
Definition

Throughout, let
V=\mathrm{Spec}\,R
be as in §
4.1
. Consider a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module

\mathcal{M}\subset R
which has a filtration by (necessarily free)
\mathbb{Z}
-submodules of finite rank

\mathcal{M}_{n}\subset\mathcal{M}\qquad\hbox{ for }n\geq 1\ ,

such that
\mathcal{M}_{n}\subset\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
and
\mathcal{M}=\varinjlim\mathcal{M}_{n}.
Consider a sequence of
exponents

e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathbb{N}\ .

The exponents will usually grow a little faster than the rank:

(5.1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}{r_{n}\log r_{n}}=\infty\ \qquad\hbox{ where }r_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}\ .

Definition 5.1
.

Given a filtered
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{M}
, and exponents
e^{\mathcal{M}}
, we define the supremal transfinite diameter of a set
\tau\subset V(\mathbb{C})
to be

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M},e}(\tau)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(t_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}\ ,

where
t_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}(\tau)
is the supremum of the Vandermonde determinant
|\det\mathcal{M}_{n}|
on
\tau^{\mathrm{rank}\mathcal{M}_{n}}
(definition
4.1
).

Note that the limit supremum
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)
may be infinite, but always exists. It follows immediately from the definition that if
\tau\subset\tau^{\prime}
then

(5.2)

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M},e}(\tau)\leq\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M},e}(\tau^{\prime})\ .

Remark 5.2
.

When the exponent function is clear from the context, we shall drop it and simply write
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}
. Note that if
e^{\prime}_{n}=\alpha_{n}e_{n}
, where the
\alpha_{n}
tend to a limit
\alpha
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, one has the identity:

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M},e^{\prime}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M},e}(\tau)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\ .

Examples 5.3
.

Consider the case
V=\mathbb{A}^{r}
. Denote its coordinate ring by
\mathcal{O}(V)=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
.

(1)

If
r=1
we may take
\mathcal{M}_{n}\subset\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{Z}[x]
to be the subspace of polynomials in
x
of degree
<n
, and
e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}=\binom{n}{2}
. Then, as in §
4.2
,

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sup_{z_{i}\in\tau}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|z_{i}-z_{j}|\right)^{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}}\

which equals the transfinite diameter of
\tau\subset\mathbb{C}
whenever it exists (e.g., if
\tau
is compact).

(2)

For general
r
, let
0<a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
equipped with the filtration by submodules
\mathcal{M}_{n}=\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(a_{1}n,\ldots,a_{r}n)}
with exponent function

(5.3)

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{r+1}\frac{a_{1}\ldots a_{r}}{2}(a_{1}+\ldots+a_{r}-\frac{r}{n})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{n^{r+1}}{2}a_{1}\ldots a_{r}(a_{1}+\ldots+a_{r})\qquad\hbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty

as in §
4.2.2
.
Then we define for any
\tau\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}

\mathrm{Sup}_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})}^{\ \mathrm{rec}}(\tau)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(t_{\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(a_{1}n,\ldots,a_{r}n)}}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}\ .

It equals the ‘rectangular’ transfinite diameter of
\tau
if the latter exists, which holds for example when
\tau
is compact. This is a special case of
[
BBL18
]
[Theorem 5.1, Rem. 5.2].

(3)

Let
n\geq 0
and let
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
with the filtration by submodules
\mathcal{M}_{n}=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\mathrm{hom}}
as defined in
§
4.2.3
. The exponent function is

(5.4)

e^{\mathrm{hom}}_{n}=r\,\binom{n+r-1}{r+1}\sim\left(\frac{r}{r+1}\right)\frac{n^{r+1}}{r!}\ .

Then we define for any
\tau\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}

\mathrm{Sup}_{n}^{\hom}(\tau)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(t_{\mathcal{N}^{\hom}_{n}}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}\ .

It is the usual ‘homogeneous’ transfinite diameter of
\tau
when it exists. Similarly, the limit exists when
\tau
is compact as a consequence of
[
BBL18
]
[Theorem 5.1, Rem. 5.2].

These particular examples are special cases of what is called the
C
-transfinite diameter in
[
LW21
]
, which in turn is a special case of the more general definition for an arbitrary module given above.

Remark 5.4
.

When the degree of the Vandermonde determinant makes sense, we define the exponent function to be
\det{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\subset R^{\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}}
, as in the examples above.
More generally, let
\mathbb{G}_{m}\times\mathbb{A}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{r}
be the action which on complex points scales coordinates by
\lambda\in\mathbb{G}_{m}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Then
(1)
-
(3)
satisfy

(5.5)

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda\tau)=\lambda\,\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\ \quad\hbox{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\ .

In general, whenever
V
admits a
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-action of this sort and its affine ring is graded with non-negative degrees and is of finite rank in each graded degree, it is advisable to choose the exponent function to be

e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}=\deg\left(\det\mathcal{M}_{n}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{rank}\mathcal{M}_{n}}\deg(\mathfrak{m}_{i})

where the
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
are any choice of independent homogenous generators for
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.
It is compatible with the grading and guarantees that a scaling law (
5.5
) holds.

5.1.1.
Functoriality and torsion

The supremal diameter is functorial in the following sense.
Let
f:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}=\mathrm{Spec}(R^{\prime})
be a morphism as in §
4.1.1
, and consider a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module

\mathcal{N}\subset R^{\prime}
which is
f
-admissible. Suppose that
e^{\mathcal{N}}
is an exponent function and let
e^{f^{*}\mathcal{N}}_{n}=e^{\mathcal{N}}
. Then by (
4.4
)

(5.6)

\mathrm{Sup}_{f^{*}\mathcal{N}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}}(f\tau)\ .

The supremal diameter is insensitive to ‘small’ modifications of the module
\mathcal{M}
. For example,
suppose that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}
are as above and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{n}\subset\mathcal{M}_{n}
is free of equal rank. Let
C_{n}
denote the cardinality of the torsion module
\mathcal{M}_{n}/\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{n}
. If the corresponding exponent functions are equal:
e^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}_{n}=e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
, then by (
4.3
)

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\tau)\quad\hbox{ whenever }\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}C_{n}^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}=1\ .

The latter condition on the limit holds unless
C_{n}
grows rapidly as a function of the rank of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.
Consequently, the
\mathbb{Z}
-module structure on
\mathcal{M}
does not usually play an important role in the theory.

Example 5.5
.

For example, if
\mathcal{M}
is as in
(1)
above, and

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[1,2x,3x^{2},\ldots,nx^{n-1}]\ ,

then
C_{n}=n!
and
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)
. But if, for example,

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[1,2x,4x^{2},\ldots,2^{n-1}x^{n-1}]\ ,

then
C_{n}\sim 2^{n^{2}/2}
and so
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(2\tau)=2\,\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)
by (
5.6
) where
f
is the map
x\mapsto 2x
.

5.2.
Summary of known results

For our applications, we are interested in upper bounds for the supremal diameter.
We briefly list some known results on the transfinite diameter.

5.2.1.
1
-dimensional case

This situation is classical and well-studied. One knows that

(5.7)

\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}([a,b])=t([a,b])=\frac{(b-a)}{4}

whenever
a<b
. The module and exponent function are as in §
4.2
.

5.2.2.
Rectangular transfinite diameter

Unfortunately, there are few results which establish the precise value of the transfinite diameter in higher dimensions. For the rectangular case §
4.2.2
, very little seems to be known. We show below that the rectangular diameter
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})}
is multiplicative. In particular

\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(1,\ldots,1)}(\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2})=\left(\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(1,\ldots,1)}(\tau_{1})\right)^{\frac{r}{r+s}}\left(\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(1,\ldots,1)}(\tau_{2})\right)^{\frac{s}{r+s}}

for any
\tau_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}
and
\tau_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{s}
.
In the case
r=s=1
we recover the formula
[
LW21
]
[Theorem 5.1].
Since, in one dimension, the rectangular and homogeneous transfinite diameters coincide, we may use (
5.7
) to deduce the rectangular transfinite diameter of a rectangular box:

(5.8)

\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(1,1)}([a,b]\times[c,d])=\frac{\sqrt{(b-a)(d-c)}}{4}\qquad\hbox{ where }a<b\ ,\ c<d\ .

5.2.3.
Homogeneous transfinite diameter

This is the standard notion of transfinite diameter. We shall denote it by
\mathrm{tr}
.
The existence of the limits for compact sets was proven by
[
Zah75
]
, in which case
\mathrm{tr}=\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}
.
The multiplicativity of the homogeneous transfinite diameter was established in
[
SS62
]
,
[
BC99
, Theorem 1]
. Let
\tau_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\tau_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be compact sets. Then

(5.9)

\mathrm{tr}(\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\tau_{1})^{\frac{m}{m+n}}\mathrm{tr}(\tau_{2})^{\frac{n}{m+n}}\ .

Thus
\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2})=\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(\tau_{1})^{\frac{m}{m+n}}\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(\tau_{2})^{\frac{n}{m+n}}
. It may be possible to derive this directly from the formula for the determinant of a matrix amalgam (Appendix §
A
). In particular, the homogeneous supremal diameter for a rectangular box coincides with the rectangular version, and is given by (
5.8
).

Let
\tau\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then for any
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
one has the formula

(5.10)

\mathrm{tr}(P\tau)=|\det(P)|^{\frac{1}{n}}\,\mathrm{tr}(\tau)\ ,

which can be proven from the definition §
4.2.3
using standard properties of the determinant and the scaling law (
5.5
).
Equation (

where

denotes a certain resultant associated to the homogeneous part

of

5.3.
Supremal diameter for tensor products

Let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
be two filtered
\mathbb{Z}
-submodules of
R
and suppose that
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}\subset R
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module equipped with the filtration
(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N})_{n}=\mathcal{M}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{n}
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
be as above with exponent functions
e^{\mathcal{M}}
,
e^{\mathcal{N}}
,
e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}
. Suppose that
e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}
satisfies (
5.1
) and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}}{e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}}=\alpha\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}}{e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}}=\beta\ .

Then we have the upper bound

(5.12)

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq\,\,\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\right)^{\alpha}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\right)^{\beta}\ .

Proof.

For brevity write
r_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}
,
s_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}
. Let
n
be sufficiently large that
r_{n},s_{n}>0
.
By Theorem
4.5
,

t_{(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N})_{n}}(\tau)\leq\frac{(r_{n}s_{n})!}{H_{r_{n},s_{n}}}\,\,t^{s_{n}}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}(\tau)t^{r_{n}}_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(\tau)\ .

By applying the bound
m!\leq m^{m}
for integers
m\geq 0
, we deduce that

\left(\frac{(r_{n}s_{n})!}{H_{r_{n},s_{n}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}}}\leq((r_{n}s_{n})!)^{\frac{1}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}}}\leq\exp\left(\frac{r_{n}s_{n}}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}}\log(r_{n}s_{n})\right)\ .

Since
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N})_{n}=r_{n}s_{n}
, the assumption (
5.1
) implies that the previous expression tends to
1
in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
It follows that

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq\,\,\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\left(t^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}(\tau)\right)^{\alpha_{n}}\left(t^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}}}_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(\tau)\right)^{\beta_{n}}\right)\quad\hbox{ where }\quad\alpha_{n}=\frac{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}}{e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}}\ ,\ \beta_{n}=\frac{e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}}{e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}}\ .

By assumption
\alpha_{n}\rightarrow\alpha
and
\beta_{n}\rightarrow\beta
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The result follows from the definition of the limit supremum, together with the fact that the previous expression only involves positive quantities, and that the exponential and logarithm restricted to the positive real axis are increasing monotone functions.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
be as above, but suppose that
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is stationary, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\mathcal{N}
for all
n
sufficiently large. In particular,
\mathcal{N}
is of finite rank. Suppose we are given exponent functions
e^{\mathcal{M}}
and
e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}

where the latter satisfies (
5.1
), such that the following limit exists:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}}{e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}}=\alpha\ .

Then, if
\tau
is bounded, we have the upper bound:

t^{\sup}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq\,\,\left(t^{\sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\right)^{\alpha}\ .

Proof.

Define an exponent function for
\mathcal{N}_{n}
to be
e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}
. With this definition, the limit
\beta
in theorem
5.6
is zero. Since
\tau
is bounded,
t^{\sup}_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)
is finite, and hence (
5.12
) implies the stated inequality, since

\left(t^{\sup}_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\right)^{\beta}=1
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

A natural definition of
e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}
, which applies in the examples
5.3
, is

(5.13)

e^{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}=e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}+e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}\ .\

When this holds, we have
\alpha+\beta=1
in the setting of theorem
5.6
.

In some cases, (
5.12
) is an equality.
The following corollary is not as general as possible since it assumes the existence of limits, but is related to results in the literature on transfinite diameters
[
LW21
]
.
It proves that the (supremal) transfinite diameter is multiplicative with respect to external products.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subset R_{i}
be free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules filtered by finite rank submodules and equipped with exponent functions
e^{\mathcal{N}_{i}}
. Consider the tensor product
\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}\subset R_{1}\otimes R_{2}
, which is free and equipped with the tensor product filtration.
Suppose that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathcal{N}_{i}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,(\mathcal{N}_{2-i})_{n}}{e^{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}_{n}}=\alpha_{i}\quad\hbox{ for }\quad i=1,2\ .

Write
V_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,R_{i}
, and consider
\tau_{i}\subset V_{i}(\mathbb{C})
for
i=1,2
. Suppose that the limit suprema
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}(\tau_{i})
are actual limits for
i=1,2
. Then

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2})=\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau_{1})\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{2})\right)^{\alpha_{2}}\

and the limit supremum on the left is also a limit.

Proof.

Let
R=R_{1}\otimes R_{2}
. There are canonical embeddings of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules
R_{i}\rightarrow R
.
The inequality
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2})\leq\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau_{1})\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{2})\right)^{\alpha_{2}}

follows from theorem
5.6
.
To obtain a lower bound the idea is as follows. For all sufficiently large
n
, choose a configuration of
r_{i}=\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{N}_{i})_{n}
points
z^{\prime}\in\tau_{1}^{r_{1}}

and
z^{\prime\prime}\in\tau_{2}^{r_{2}}
such that
|\det V_{(\mathcal{N}_{1})_{n}}(z^{\prime})|
and
|\det V_{(\mathcal{N}_{2})_{n}}(z^{\prime\prime})|
are arbitrarily close to
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau_{1})
and
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{2})
, respectively. Consider the configuration of
r_{1}r_{2}
points
w_{ij}=(z^{\prime}_{i},z^{\prime\prime}_{j})
in
\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2}
where
1\leq i\leq r_{1}
,
1\leq j\leq r_{2}
. For a suitable ordering of these points, one may show
[
Bro25
, §3.2,§6]
that the Vandermonde matrix
V_{(\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2})_{n}}(w_{ij})
, expressed as an amalgam of two matrices, is in fact their Kronecker tensor product and hence

|\det V_{(\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2})_{n}}(w_{ij})|=\left|\det V_{(\mathcal{N}_{1})_{n}}(z^{\prime})\right|^{r_{2}}\left|\det V_{(\mathcal{N}_{2})_{n}}(z^{\prime\prime})\right|^{r_{1}}\ .

By raising to the power
(e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}})^{-1}
, we obtain a lower bound for
t_{n}^{\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2})
which is arbitrarily close to
\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\tau_{1})\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\tau_{2})\right)^{\alpha_{2}}
.
∎

5.4.
Supremal diameter for direct sums

Let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}\subset R
be two free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules equipped with filtrations by finite rank submodules. The direct sum

\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}

is given the filtration
(\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N})_{n}=\mathcal{M}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{n}
. We assume that it is free when viewed as a submodule of
R
via the map
R\oplus R\rightarrow R
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Suppose that we are given free
\mathbb{Z}
-submodules
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}
of
R
as above, and exponents
e^{\mathcal{M}}
,
e^{\mathcal{N}}
,
e^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}
, where the latter satisfies (
5.1
).
If

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}}=\alpha\quad\ ,\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}}=\beta

we have the inequality

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\right)^{\alpha}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\right)^{\beta}\ .

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
,
r_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{M}_{n}
, and
s_{n}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}
. By proposition
4.6

t_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{n}}(\tau)\leq\binom{r_{n}+s_{n}}{r_{n}}t_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}(\tau)t_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}(\tau)\ .

Since
\binom{r_{n}+s_{n}}{r_{n}}\leq 2^{r_{n}+s_{n}}
and
\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}}{r_{n}+s_{n}}\rightarrow\infty

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\binom{r_{n}+s_{n}}{r_{n}}^{\frac{1}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}}}\rightarrow 1\quad\hbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty\ ,

and we conclude by raising both sides of the previous inequality to the power
\frac{1}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}}
and letting
n\rightarrow\infty
. This uses similar properties of limit suprema as set out at the end of the proof of theorem
5.6
.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

The canonical exponent function for a direct sum is

e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}=e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}+e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}\ .

With this definition, the exponents in the previous theorem satisfy

\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}+e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}}\qquad,\qquad\beta=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}+e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}}

and in particular,
\alpha+\beta=1
. The proof only requires that

\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{N}}}{\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{M}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{N}_{n})}\rightarrow\infty

which is weaker than (
5.1
).

One may also retrieve corollary
5.7
using the previous proposition by writing
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
as a direct sum

\oplus_{i}\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathcal{M}
where the
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
are a basis of
\mathcal{N}
.

Corollary 5.12
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{k}\subset R
are free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules with a filtration by finite rank submodules such that, for all
n\geq 0
,

\mathcal{M}_{n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}(\mathcal{N}_{i})_{n}\ ,

and exponents
e^{\mathcal{N}_{i}}
and
e^{\mathcal{M}}
, where the latter satisfies (
5.1
).
If

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}_{i}}}{e_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}=\alpha_{i}

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}(\tau)\right)^{\alpha_{i}}\ .

Upper bounds for a square region bounded by a hyperbola

As an application of these techniques, we compute some upper estimates for the rectangular supremal transfinite diameter

as

A key point is that we reduce the
rectangular

homogeneous
diameter (about which much more is known) of a different region.
If formulae for the transfinite diameters of two-dimensional figures in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such as a quadrilateral were available, our estimates could be significantly improved.

Naïve bound

Since
\tau_{\epsilon}\subset[0,1]^{2}

5.2
) that

\mathrm{Sup}_{(1,1)}^{\mathrm{rec}}(\tau_{\epsilon})\leq\mathrm{Sup}_{(1,1)}^{\mathrm{rec}}([0,1]^{2})=(\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}([0,1])^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\ ,

which follows from (

) since

.

6.2.
Use of tensor products: map to a curved rhombus

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}x
. The inclusion

i:\mathbb{Z}[xy,x+y]\otimes\mathcal{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[x,y]

is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules.

Proof.

Let
u=xy
and
v=x+y
. Then one has the relation

x^{2}=xv-u
. Consider the ring structure on
\mathbb{Z}[u,v]\otimes\mathcal{N}
induced by the identification of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules:

\mathbb{Z}[u,v]\otimes\mathcal{N}\cong\left(\mathbb{Z}[u,v]\otimes\mathbb{Z}[x]\right)/I

where
I
is the ideal generated by
x^{2}-xv+u
. Thus the natural map
i
of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules can be promoted to a map of rings for the induced ring structure:

(u,v,x)\mapsto(xy,x+y,x):\left(\mathbb{Z}[u,v]\otimes\mathbb{Z}[x]\right)/I\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[x,y]\ .

Since the ring generators
x,y
of
\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
are in the image of
i
, it is surjective.
To prove injectivity, consider the graded Poincaré series of
\mathbb{Z}[u,v]
, where
u
has degree
2
and
v
has degree
1
. It is

P_{\mathbb{Z}[u,v]}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)(1-t^{2})}

If
x,y
are given degree
1
, we have
P_{\mathcal{N}}(t)=1+t
and hence

P_{\mathbb{Z}[u,v]\otimes\mathcal{N}}(t)=\frac{1+t}{(1-t)(1-t^{2})}=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}

which equals the Poincaré series of
\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
, since it has two generators in degree
1
. This proves that after tensoring with
\mathbb{Q}
, the map
i\otimes\mathbb{Q}
is an isomorphism of graded
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces. Since a submodule of a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module is free,
(\ker i)\otimes\mathbb{Q}=\ker(i\otimes\mathbb{Q})=0
, and hence
i
is injective.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\tau\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a region in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and consider the map

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}

which maps
(x,y)
to
(xy,x+y)
. Then
\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}_{(1,1)}(\tau)\leq(\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(\phi\tau))^{\frac{2}{3}}
.

Proof.

Let us write
\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{Z}\oplus x\mathbb{Z}
and
set
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
,
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{Z}[xy,x+y]
, equipped with the rectangular and homogeneous filtrations, respectively:

\mathcal{M}_{n}=\bigoplus_{0\leq i,j<n}x^{i}y^{j}\mathbb{Z}\ ,\qquad\ ,\qquad\mathcal{P}_{n}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i,j\\
i+j<n\end{subarray}}(xy)^{i}(x+y)^{j}\mathbb{Z}\ .

By lemma
6.1
and the fact that the map
i
respects the filtrations by degree in
x
and
y
, we find that

(6.1)

\mathcal{M}_{n}\cong\left(\mathcal{P}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N}\right)\oplus\mathcal{C}_{n}\ \quad\hbox{ and }\quad\mathcal{P}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{n}

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}
(resp.
\mathcal{K}_{n}
) is the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
n
with generators
x^{i}y^{n-1}
for
0\leq i\leq n-1
(resp.
generated by
x(xy)^{i}(x+y)^{j}
for
i+j=n-1
). Recall from (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) that

e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}=n^{2}(n-1)\sim n^{3}\quad,\quad e^{\mathcal{P}}_{n}=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{3}\sim\frac{n^{3}}{3}\ .

If we define
e_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}=0
then using the convention (
5.13
) we have

e^{\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{N}}_{n}=e^{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\,\mathrm{rank}\,\mathcal{N}_{n}\sim\frac{2}{3}n^{3}\ .

Since the total degrees of the determinants of
\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{K}_{n}
are quadratic in
n
, we have

t_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}(\tau)^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}\rightarrow 1\ \quad\ ,\quad t_{\mathcal{K}_{n}}(\tau)^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}}\rightarrow 1\qquad\hbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty\ .

It follows from proposition
4.6
applied to each direct sum in (
6.1
) that

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\leq\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)^{\frac{2}{3}}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}\

and hence
\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)^{\frac{2}{3}}
.
Now, by the corollary to theorem
5.6
and the asymptotics of the exponent functions given above, we deduce that

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)\leq\ \mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)\ .

We conclude that

\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)=\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{N}}(\tau)^{\frac{2}{3}}\leq\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)^{\frac{2}{3}}\ .

Since
\mathcal{P}=\phi^{*}\mathbb{Z}[u,v]
where
\phi(u,v)=(xy,x+y)
, the statement follows from (
5.6
).
∎

,
(\epsilon,1+\epsilon-2\sqrt{\epsilon})
and therefore we have a lower bound

\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(R_{\epsilon})^{\frac{2}{3}}\geq\left(\frac{\epsilon(1-\sqrt{\epsilon})^{2}}{16}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\ .\

This shows that
\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(R_{\epsilon})\sim\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{4}
to leading order in
\epsilon
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

6.3.
Map to two rectangles (direct sums)

Consider the map

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto(xy,x):\mathbb{C}^{2}\overset{\phi_{x}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{C}^{2}

and similarly let
\phi_{y}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
be defined by
(x,y)\mapsto(xy,y)
.

Proposition 6.5
.

For any bounded region
\tau\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
, we have

\mathrm{Sup}^{\,\mathrm{rec}}(\tau)\ \leq\ \Big(\mathrm{Sup}^{\,\hom}(\phi_{x}(\tau))\Big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\,\Big(\mathrm{Sup}^{\,\hom}(\phi_{y}(\tau))\Big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\ .

Proof.

The
\mathbb{Z}
-module spanned by
x^{i}y^{j}
for
0\leq i,j<n
is a direct sum:

\mathbb{Z}[x,y]^{\mathrm{rec}}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[xy]_{n}\oplus x\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{x}\oplus y\,\mathcal{N}_{n}^{y}\qquad\hbox{ where }\quad\mathcal{N}_{n}^{x}=\mathbb{Z}[xy,x]^{\hom}_{n-1}\quad\ ,\quad\mathcal{N}_{n}^{y}=\mathbb{Z}[xy,y]^{\hom}_{n-1}\ .

Recall that

e^{\mathbb{Z}[x,y]^{\mathrm{rec}}}_{n}=n^{2}(n-1)\quad\ ,\quad\ e^{\mathbb{Z}[xy]}_{n}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\quad\ ,\quad e^{\mathbb{Z}[xy,x]^{\hom}}_{n-1}=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{3}\ \ ,

and similarly for
e^{\mathbb{Z}[y,xy]^{\hom}}
.
It follows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathbb{Z}[xy]}_{n}}{e^{\mathbb{Z}[x,y]^{\mathrm{rec}}}_{n}}=0\quad\ ,\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathbb{Z}[xy,x]^{\hom}}_{n-1}}{e^{\mathbb{Z}[x,y]^{\mathrm{rec}}}_{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{\mathbb{Z}[xy,y]^{\hom}}_{n-1}}{e^{\mathbb{Z}[x,y]^{\mathrm{rec}}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{3}\ .

Since
\det|V_{x\mathcal{N}_{n}^{x}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})|=|x_{1}\ldots x_{N}|\det|V_{x\mathcal{N}_{n}^{x}}|
and since
\tau
is bounded, it follows from an argument detailed previously that we may replace
x\mathcal{N}^{x}_{n}
by
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{n}
, and similarly for
y\mathcal{N}^{y}_{n}
.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathbb{Z}[xy]}(\tau)\right)^{0}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathbb{Z}[xy,x]^{\hom}}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\mathrm{Sup}_{\mathbb{Z}[xy,y]^{\hom}}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(\phi_{x}(\tau))\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}(\phi_{y}(\tau))\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}

where the first line follows from corollary
5.12
and the second by (
5.6
).
∎

Example 6.6
.

Applying the proposition to
\tau_{\epsilon}

\phi_{x}(\tau_{\epsilon})
is contained in the rectangle
[0,\epsilon]\times[0,1]
(more precisely in the slightly smaller region where

), and hence

\mathrm{Sup}^{\mathrm{rec}}(\tau_{\epsilon})\leq\mathrm{Sup}^{\hom}([0,1]\times[0,\epsilon])^{\frac{2}{3}}=\ \left(\frac{\epsilon}{16}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}

which is exactly the same as the first inequality in example
6.4
.

Remark 6.7

There exist various generalisations of this proposition to higher dimensions. The typical shape of the regions arising from moduli space integrals

are

7.
Small linear forms from the geometry of numbers

7.1.
Minkowski’s theorem on linear forms

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
be a matrix with real entries
(A)_{ij}=a_{ij}
. Then there exists a non-zero integer vector
\underline{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}c_{j}\right|\leq\varepsilon_{i}\,\hbox{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\ ,

provided that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\geq|\det(A)|
.

In particular, if
\det(A)\neq 0
there exists some
1\leq i\leq n
such that

(7.1)

0<\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}c_{j}\right|\leq|\det(A)|^{1/n}\

which follows from the theorem on setting
\varepsilon_{i}=|\det(A)|^{1/n}
for all
i
.
By applying this to a matrix
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
of Mellin transforms we can deduce the existence of small linear forms in periods.
One can also use Minkowski’s theorem on short vectors for quadratic forms to obtain a similar bound, but the previous theorem has the advantage that it does not require
A
to be symmetric nor positive definite.

7.2.
Denominators

Let
(X,f)
be a family of Mellin integrals as considered earlier and let us fix once and for all a basis
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
of periods as in lemma
2.1
.
Let
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
denote the matrix of Mellin integrals with respect to

N

\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{A}^{r})

Let us choose invertible matrices

such that

(7.2)

\widetilde{Q}^{\sigma}_{N}=D^{\ell}_{N}Q^{\sigma}_{N}D^{r}_{N}\quad\in\quad M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}])

has entries which are
integral
linear forms in the
\xi_{i}

\delta_{N}=|\det(D^{\ell}_{N})\det(D^{r}_{N})|\ .

Theorem 7.2
.

If
N
is sufficiently large and

, then there exists a non-zero linear form in the
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
, which is a rational linear combination of Mellin integrals

, such that

0<\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}\xi_{i}\right|\leq\left(t_{\mathcal{N}}(f\sigma)^{2}\,\delta_{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}

where
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

was defined in (

).

Proof.

Apply (
7.1
) to

to deduce the existence of a non-zero integer vector
0\neq\underline{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
and an index

such that

0<\left|(\widetilde{Q}^{\sigma}_{N}\underline{c})_{i}\right|<\left|\det(\widetilde{Q}^{\sigma}_{N})\right|^{\frac{1}{N}}=|\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})|^{\frac{1}{N}}\delta_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}

and apply corollary
3.7
which states that
|\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})|^{\frac{1}{N}}<t_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma)^{\frac{2}{N}}
for

4.4
),
we have
t_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma)=t_{\mathcal{N}}(f\sigma)
.
Finally, use the fact that the entries of

are integer linear combinations of
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
to write the

\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}\xi_{i}
for some integers

.
∎

Under the additional assumptions (P1) -(P3),
\det Q_{N}^{\sigma}\neq 0
holds automatically, by lemma
3.2
.
Now consider the set up of §
5.1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}=\varinjlim_{n}\mathcal{N}_{n}
and
e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
is an exponent function. Define

\delta_{\mathcal{N},e}=\limsup_{N}\delta_{N}^{\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{N}}_{n}}}

which we recall depends on the choices of matrices
D^{\ell}_{N},D^{r}_{N}
as above. Henceforth we drop the dependence on

from the subscript (as in remark
5.2
).

7.3.
Irrationality criterion

Corollary 7.3
.

Suppose that
\det\,Q_{N}^{\sigma}\neq 0
for infinitely many values of
N
. If

\mathrm{Sup}^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(f\sigma)\,\delta_{\mathcal{N}}<1

then
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}\xi_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Q}\xi_{r})\geq 2
. In particular, if
r=2
and
\xi_{1}=1
, then we retrieve criterion
1.4
.

The following quantity enables us to measure the quality of approximations independently of any choice of exponent function.

Definition 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be of rank
N
, and
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
,
\delta_{N}
be defined as in §
7.2
.
Define the
threshold
to be:

(7.3)

\vartheta_{N}=-\frac{\log\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})}{\log\delta_{N}}

Note that
\vartheta_{N}>1
if and only if
\det(Q^{\sigma}_{N})\delta_{N}<1
.

7.4.
Denominators

We may always find
D^{\ell}_{N},D^{r}_{N}
satisfying (
7.2
) by taking, for example,
D^{r}_{N}
to be the identity and
D^{\ell}_{N}
to be the diagonal matrix whose
(i,i)
th entry is the least common multiple of the denominators of the elements in the
i
th row of
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
.
This can be improved by taking
D^{r}_{N}
to be the diagonal matrix whose
j^{\mathrm{th}}
entry is the inverse of the greatest common divisor of the coefficients of all entries in the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
column of the matrix
D^{\ell}_{N}Q^{\sigma}_{N}\in M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{i}])
, which has integral entries.

In the examples, we find that certain rows and columns of
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
satisfy congruences modulo primes, which leads to further reductions in the prime factors dividing
\delta_{N}
.
The question of finding optimal denominator bounds is an interesting one which will not be pursued further here.

Example 7.5
.

Consider the example of §
1.4
. The integer
N
implicitly depends on
n
, which we omit from the notation. Let
D_{N}^{\ell}
be the diagonal matrix whose entry in the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
row is the least common multiple of the denominators of the elements in row
i
of the matrix
Q^{\sigma}
, and let
D_{N}^{r}
be the identity.

Lemma 7.6
.

The determinant of
D^{\ell}_{N}
satisfies

\det(D^{\ell}_{N})\sim\exp\left(w\frac{2r+1}{r+1}n^{r+1}\right)\qquad\hbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty\

Proof.

The rows of
Q^{\sigma}
are indexed by tuples
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
where
0\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\leq n
. The least common multiple of the denominators of all elements in that row is:

(\mathrm{lcm}\{1,\ldots,m\})^{w}\quad\hbox{ where }m=\max\{n+n_{1},n+n_{2},\ldots,n+n_{r}\}

The determinant of
D^{\ell}_{N}
is the product over all row-indices. Since
\mathrm{lcm}\{1,\ldots,M\}\sim e^{M}
,

\log D^{\ell}_{N}\sim\sum_{0\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\leq n}w\,\max\{n+n_{1},n+n_{2},\ldots,n+n_{r}\}\sim w\frac{2r+1}{r+1}n^{r+1}\ .

To verify the latter asymptotic, we check that

\int_{[n,2n]^{r}}\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}\,dx_{1}\ldots dx_{r}=r\int_{n}^{2n}x_{r}dx_{r}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\int_{n}^{x_{r}}dx_{i}\right)=r\int_{n}^{2n}x_{r}(x_{r}-n)^{r-1}dx_{r}=\frac{2r+1}{r+1}n^{r+1}\ .

∎

The rectangular exponent function (
5.3
) has the asymptotic
e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}\sim\frac{r}{2}n^{r+1}
, and hence

(7.4)

\delta_{\mathcal{N}}\leq\exp\left(\frac{2w}{r}\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)\ .

Remark 7.7
.

A referee asked the excellent question of whether the matrices
D_{n}
can be designed to make use of the cancellation of primes in the method of Rhin-Viola. I do not know the answer to this question, but the numerical experiments to be discussed later clearly demonstrate a large degree of ‘prime cancellation’ of possibly a different nature. It would be very interesting to investigate this further.

7.5.
Irrationality criterion via the determinant

The irrationality criterion above is effective in the sense that it guarantees the existence of small linear forms in the periods
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
.
Another approach is to note that the determinant
\det(D^{\ell}_{N}Q_{N}^{\sigma}D_{N}^{r})\in\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}]
is a
polynomial form
in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
with integer coefficients.
This argument generalises the one-dimensional result of Zudilin
[
Zud17
]
. In this context, one can simply replace
\delta_{N}=\det(D^{\ell}_{N}D_{N}^{r})
with any
d_{N}
such that
d_{N}\det(Q_{N}^{\sigma})\in\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}]
.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
Q^{\sigma}_{N}\in M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Q}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}])
and let
0<d_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
d_{N}\det(Q_{N}^{\sigma})\in\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}]
.
If
d_{N}|\det(Q_{N}^{\sigma})|\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
and
\det(Q_{N}^{\sigma})\neq 0
for infinitely many
N
, then

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}\langle\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}\rangle>1
.

The proof is obvious, and implies criterion
1.3
when combined with earlier results. In our computations §
9
we write down

as a (typically good) approximation to
\delta_{N}
for ease of computation.

Remark 7.9
.

In our examples the matrices
Q^{\sigma}_{N}
and their determinants are highly structured owing to the presence of large symmetry groups. It would be interesting to exploit this structure systematically.

8.
Comments, variants, generalisations

8.1.
Beyond positivity

To establish the non-vanishing of the determinant of
Q_{N}^{\sigma}
we made some assumptions to ensure that it is positive-definite. However, the assumption
(P1)
, for example, can sometimes be dropped.
For instance, theorem 5.9 in
[
Jed91
]
gives conditions for the homogeneous transfinite diameter to be a determinant of integrals similar to the ones considered here.

The holonomic structure can also be exploited.
Consider the case when there is a single parameter
r=1
. Then the matrix
Q_{N}^{\sigma}
is a Hankel matrix

H_{N}(\underline{a})=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&\ldots&a_{N}\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&\ldots&a_{N+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{N}&a_{N+1}&\ldots&a_{2N}\end{pmatrix}

where
\underline{a}=(a_{n})_{n}
with
a_{n}=\int_{\sigma}f^{n}\omega
. A classical theorem of Kronecker’s implies that the determinant of
H_{N}
vanishes for all
N
sufficiently large if and only if
the generating function
\sum a_{k}x^{-k-1}
is a rational function of
x
. See
[
BB18
, §2]
, for an overview.
The latter can be ruled out on an
ad-hoc
basis from the asymptotics of the integrals in question, or their holonomic recurrence equations.
2
2

2

Incidentally, the fact that the Hankel determinants
\det(H_{N})
are invariant under various operations on the sequence
\underline{a}=(a_{n})
such as the binomial transform may suggest approaches to improve the denominator estimates

In the case when there are more parameters, the matrices
Q_{N}^{\sigma}
are ‘multivariable Hankel forms’ in the sense of
[
Pow82
]
, who generalised some of Kronecker’s results to this setting. These results presumably point to generalisations of the above results with weaker assumptions on the entries of
Q_{N}^{\sigma}
.

8.2.
Cohomological determinants

Suppose, as in §
2
, that
Y\subset X
is simple normal crossing and defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. For any integers
n_{i}\geq 0
, the integrand of
I(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
defines a de Rham class

[f_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots f_{r}^{n_{r}}\omega]\quad\in\quad M_{\mathrm{dR}}=H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{d}(X,Y)

which is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space. In particular, any set
\mathfrak{f}
of linearly independent functions as in (
1.4
) produces a set of cohomology classes in
M_{\mathrm{dR}}
, and we may
consider the symmetric matrix:

(8.1)

Q^{\mathrm{dR}}_{N}=\left([\mathfrak{f}_{i}\mathfrak{f}_{j}\,\omega]\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}

with entries in
M_{\mathrm{dR}}
. They can be paired with any class
[\sigma]\in M_{B}^{\vee}:=H_{d}(X(\mathbb{C}),Y(\mathbb{C}))
to retrieve the matrix

Q^{\sigma}_{N}=\langle\sigma,Q^{\mathrm{dR}}_{N}\rangle
considered earlier. The family of determinants

\det Q^{\mathrm{dR}}_{N}\quad\in\quad\mathrm{Sym}^{\bullet}(M_{\mathrm{dR}})

form a very interesting algebraic object associated to the data of the Mellin integrals (
1.2
). Note that the problem of estimating the denominator of
\det Q^{\sigma}_{N}
, relative to a fixed choice of
\mathbb{Q}
-basis for

Q^{\mathrm{dR}}_{N}
.

8.2.1.

-adic and other periods

We can deduce many other interesting numbers from

. For instance, we can pair it with any other relative homology class
[\sigma^{\prime}]\in M_{B}^{\vee}

p

9.
Main example:
\zeta(2)
and
\mathcal{M}_{0,5}

In this section we study in some detail the family of linear forms in
1,\zeta(2)
arising from the moduli space of curves
\mathcal{M}_{0,5}
of genus
0
with
5
marked points. Although the applications to the irrationality of
\zeta(2)
are not of particular interest, the underlying geometry and Mellin transforms are very well understood and so this provides fertile ground for exploring the
constructions introduced in this paper.

9.1.
A family of Mellin transforms

Consider the family of integrals

(9.1)

I(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}u^{s_{1}}_{1}\ldots u^{s_{5}}_{5}\,\omega\qquad\hbox{ for }s_{1},\ldots,s_{5}\geq 0,

where
\omega=\frac{dxdy}{1-xy}
, and the
u_{i}
are dihedral ‘coordinates’
[
Bro09
]
given by:

(9.2)

u_{1}=x\ ,\ u_{2}=\frac{1-x}{1-xy}\ ,\ u_{3}=\frac{1-y}{1-xy}\ ,\ u_{4}=y\ ,u_{5}=1-xy\ .

It was shown by Dixon in 1905 that the
integrals
I(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})
are invariant under dihedral symmetries:

I(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})=I(s_{\rho(1)},\ldots,s_{\rho(5)})

where
\rho\in D_{10}
is the group of symmetries of a pentagon with edges labelled
1,\ldots,5
in order.
One may show by elementary or geometric means that

I(h,i,j,k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}\zeta(2)+\mathbb{Q}

and more precisely
[
RV96
]
that

(9.3)

d_{m_{1}}d_{m_{2}}I(h,i,j,k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\zeta(2)

where
m_{1},m_{2}
are the two successive maxima of the entries of the pole vector
\underline{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{5})
where

(9.4)

p_{1}=i+j-\ell\quad,\quad p_{2}=j+k-h\quad,\quad p_{3}=k+\ell-i\quad,\quad p_{4}=\ell+h-j\quad,\quad p_{5}=h+i-k\ .

Note that (
9.3
) is not optimal for certain values of the arguments
(h,i,j,k,\ell)
. This is exploited in
[
RV96
]
as a consequence of several things including Gauss’ hypergeometric transformation.

9.1.1.
Geometric interpretation

See
[
Bro09
,
Bro16
]
for further background.
The
u_{1},\ldots,u_{5}
satisfy the following dihedrally-symmetric set of algebraic relations

(9.5)

u_{1}u_{4}+u_{5}=1\ ,\ u_{2}u_{5}+u_{1}=1\ ,\ u_{3}u_{1}+u_{2}=1\ ,\ u_{4}u_{2}+u_{3}=1\ ,\ u_{5}u_{3}+u_{4}=1

which can be interpreted combinatorially via sets of crossing chords in a pentagon
[
Bro09
]
. The ideal
I
of
\mathbb{Z}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{5}]
generated by (
9.5
) defines a smooth affine scheme

\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}=\mathrm{Spec}\left(\mathbb{Z}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{5}]/I\right)
which contains the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,5}
of curves of genus
0
with 5 ordered marked points

\mathcal{M}_{0,5}\cong\mathbb{A}^{2}\ \setminus\ V\left(xy(1-x)(1-y)(1-xy)\right)\ \overset{\eqref{uiasxy}}{\hookrightarrow}\ \mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}\ ,

where
\mathbb{A}^{2}=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Q}[x,y]
.
The vanishing locus

Y=V(u_{1}\ldots u_{5})\subset\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}

is a simple normal crossing divisor with five irreducible components
Y_{i}:\{u_{i}=0\}
and one proves that

M=H^{2}(\mathcal{M}^{\delta}_{0,5},Y)\cong\mathbb{Q}(0)\oplus\mathbb{Q}(-2)

which has two periods
1,\zeta(2)
. The domain

\sigma^{\delta}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{5}\geq 0\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}(\mathbb{R})

defines a relative homology class
[\sigma^{\delta}]\in M_{B}^{\vee}
whose interior is homeomorphic to
(0,1)^{2}
via (
9.2
). One shows that
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(\mathcal{M}^{\delta}_{0,5})
has no zeros or poles along
Y
.

Thus, in our initial set-up, consider
X=\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}
equipped with the function

\underline{u}:\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{5}\

whose components are
u_{1},\ldots,u_{5}
. One may show that

(9.6)

\mathcal{M}_{0,5}=\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}\setminus Y\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\mathcal{M}_{0,5}^{\delta}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,5}\backslash E

where
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,5}
is the Deligne-Mumford-Knudsen compactification of
\mathcal{M}_{0,5}
and
E
is a divisor ‘at infinity’ with five irreducible components.
The parameters
p_{1},\ldots,p_{5}
may be interpreted as (one less than) the order of the poles of the differential form
u_{1}^{h}u_{2}^{i}u_{3}^{j}u_{4}^{k}u_{5}^{\ell}\omega
along each component of
E
.

In the remainder of this section we apply the constructions in this paper to families of integrals obtained by restricting
the most general family (
9.1
) to linear subpaces of the parameter space
s_{1},\ldots,s_{5}\in\mathbb{N}^{5}
.

9.2.
One parameter family

We first consider the family, considered by Apéry and Beukers
[
Ape79
,
Beu79
]
where all parameters are equal
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{5}=n
.

I(n)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}f^{n}\omega\quad\quad\hbox{ where }\quad f=u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}u_{4}u_{5}=\frac{x(1-x)y(1-y)}{1-xy}\ .

It is associated to

The function

\eta=\frac{5\sqrt{5}-11}{2}\sim 0.09017\ ,

and so we deduce that

f([0,1]^{2})\subset[0,\eta]\subset V_{f}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}

By (

9.5.2.
Denominator bounds

Consider the basis of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
given in (
9.14
). A term in the matrix
Q^{\sigma}_{n}
is equivalent, by dihedral symmetry, to an integral with integrand
u_{1}^{a}u_{2}^{b}u_{i}^{c}u_{i+1}^{d}\omega
where
i\in\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}
and
a+b,c+d\leq n
. The pole vector can be computed for each value of
i
, and its maximum is attained when
i=1
, leading to a largest pole of order
a+b+c+d
. The second maximum is attained when
i=2
(or
i=5
), in which case it is
a+c+d
(or
b+c+d
). Thus we may bound the two successive maxima
m_{2}\leq m_{1}
, solely as functions of
a,b
, by

m_{1}\leq a+b+n\quad\ ,\quad m_{2}\leq\max\{a,b\}+n\ .

An elementary computation yields

\sum_{0\leq a,b\leq n}a+b+n\sim\frac{5}{6}n^{3}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\sum_{0\leq a,b\leq n}\max\{a,b\}+n\sim\frac{3}{4}n^{3}\ .

Since (
9.14
) is a direct sum over 5 such copies, the relevant factor is
5(\frac{5}{6}+\frac{3}{4})=\frac{95}{12}
and
in total that the denominator is (crudely) bounded by

(9.19)

\lim_{n}\,\exp\left(\frac{95}{12}n^{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{e_{n}}}=\exp\left(\frac{19}{4}\right)=\exp(4.75)\ ,

since
e^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}\sim\frac{5}{3}n^{3}
.
This bound is based on the least common multiple of the denominators of row entries. Experiments suggest that the actual denominator is considerably smaller.

A.0.1.
Amalgams of matrices

Let
m,n\geq 1

mn

m

(A.1)

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\ldots&a_{1m}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&\ldots&a_{2m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{mn\,1}&a_{mn\,2}&\ldots&a_{mn\,m}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{a}_{1}\\
\underline{a}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\underline{a}_{mn}\\
\end{pmatrix}

with entries
a_{ij}
in a commutative ring
R
, and where
\underline{a}_{i}=(a_{i1}\ldots a_{im})\in R^{m}
denotes the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
row vector.
Likewise, consider the matrix with
mn
rows and
n
columns

(A.2)

B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}&\ldots&b_{1n}\\
b_{21}&b_{22}&\ldots&b_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
b_{mn\,1}&b_{mn\,2}&\ldots&b_{mn\,n}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{b}_{1}\\
\underline{b}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\underline{b}_{mn}\\
\end{pmatrix}

with entries
b_{ij}\in R
, and
\underline{b}_{i}=(b_{i1}\ldots b_{in})\in R^{n}
denotes the
i^{\mathrm{th}}

Definition A.1
.

Define their amalgamated matrix product:

A\star B=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{a}_{1}\otimes\underline{b}_{1}\\
\underline{a}_{2}\otimes\underline{b}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\underline{a}_{mn}\otimes\underline{b}_{mn}\\
\end{pmatrix}

It is a square matrix with
mn
rows and columns, and entries in
R
.

Our convention for the Kronecker tensor product of two row vectors is:

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\otimes(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=(\underbrace{a_{1}b_{1},a_{2}b_{1},\ldots,a_{m}b_{1}}_{\underline{a}\otimes b_{1}},\underbrace{a_{1}b_{2},a_{2}b_{2},\ldots,a_{m}b_{2}}_{\underline{a}\otimes b_{2}},\ldots,\underbrace{a_{1}b_{n},a_{2}b_{n},\ldots,a_{m}b_{n}}_{\underline{a}\otimes b_{n}})

where we write commas between entries for legibility and
\underline{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
. This convention applies to every row in the definition of
A\star B
. The Kronecker product of two matrices is a special case.

.

, and consider the matrices

A.0.2.
A formula for the determinant of an amalgam

Consider the trivial Young tableau
1_{n^{m}}

n^{m}
consisting of
m

n

1,\ldots,m

The symmetric group
\Sigma_{mn}

Consider the tableau
\alpha=\sigma(1_{n^{m}})

A_{\sigma}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\det A_{\mathrm{col}_{i}(\alpha)}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad B_{\sigma}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\det B_{\mathrm{row}_{i}(\alpha)}

where, for any subset

A_{I}

, and similarly for
B
; and
\mathrm{col}_{i}(\alpha)

.

Appendix B
Fast computation of
I(h,i,j,k,l)

The following method leads to very fast computation of the matrices
Q^{\sigma}
discussed in §
9
.

B.1.
Contiguity matrices

Consider the de Rham cohomology group which underlies the family of Mellin integrals
I(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})
defined by (
9.1
), namely

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dR}}=H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{0,5}/k;(\mathcal{O},\nabla))

over the field
k=\mathbb{Q}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})
,
where
\mathcal{O}
is the structure sheaf of
\mathcal{M}_{0,5}
and

\nabla=d+\sum_{i=1}^{5}s_{i}\frac{du_{i}}{u_{i}}

where
u_{i}
are given by (
9.2
). By a method to be explained elsewhere, we can easily compute the space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dR}}
by computing the iterated higher direct images of the algebraic vector bundle with integrable connection
(\mathcal{O},\nabla)
relative to the two maps
(x,y)\mapsto y:\mathcal{M}_{0,5}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0,4}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0,3}\cong\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Q}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}
has the affine coordinate
y
. We verify that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dR}}
has rank 2 with basis given by the cohomology classes of the forms

\omega_{1}=\frac{dxdy}{1-xy}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\omega_{0}=dxdy\ .

For each
1\leq i\leq 5
, define the shift operator
T_{i}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dR}}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dR}}
which is induced by multiplication by
u_{i}
on
\omega_{0},\omega_{1}
. In this basis they are represented explicitly by
2\times 2
matrices we denote by
M_{i}(s)\in M_{2\times 2}(k)
. They are easily computed in Maple and given explicitly below. One checks the relation:

(B.1)

M_{i}(\tau_{i}s)M_{j}(s)=M_{j}(\tau_{j}s)M_{i}(s)

for all
1\leq i,j\leq 5
,
where
s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})
and
\tau_{i}
is the operator which replaces
s_{i}
with
s_{i}+1
.
It follows that for a generic 5-tuple
\underline{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{5})\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
and
\underline{s}^{\prime}=\tau_{5}\underline{s}
such that the integrals converge one has:

(B.2)

\begin{pmatrix}I(\tau_{i}\underline{s})\\
I(\tau_{i}\underline{s}^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}=M_{i}(\underline{s})\begin{pmatrix}I(\underline{s})\\
I(\underline{s}^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}\ .

To compute
I(h,i,j,k,l)
for integer values of
h,i,j,k,l
, we simply compose the matrices
M_{i}
, specialised to integer values of
s_{i}
, in a suitable order, and apply them to the initial vector of periods

v=\left(\int_{\sigma}\omega_{1}\ ,\ \int_{\sigma}\omega_{0}\right)^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta(2)\\
1\end{pmatrix}

Note that since the matrices
M_{i}
have poles in the
s_{i}
, some care must be taken regarding the order in which they are composed (see below), but
(
B.1
) ensures that the answer is independent of the order taken (provided it avoids poles). This is very similar to the approach in
[
WLK+25
]
, where a set of matrices

are derived from hypergeometric functions and is presumably equivalent in this case.
