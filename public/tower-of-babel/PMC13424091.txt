Qubit-efficient quantum intrusion detection using dual-parameter encoding and multi-metric calibration
Authors: Lubna Khan, Burhan Ul Islam Khan, Aabid A. Mir, Khang Wen Goh, Dwi Sudarno Putra, Uzair Ishtiaq, Mesith Chaimanee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modern networks generate high-dimensional traffic that requires numerous features for effective intrusion detection and cybercrime defence, creating a critical challenge for quantum approaches, where qubit availability remains the primary bottleneck for near-term implementation. We present a quantum-enhanced framework that employs Dual-Parameter encoding, using both the polar angle θ and azimuthal phase ϕ of each qubit to encode two normalized features, combined with multi-metric anomaly scoring and calibrated false-positive control. Introduction

Quantum machine learning offers promising avenues for addressing computational bottlenecks in cybersecurity1, particularly for intrusion detection systems that must process high-dimensional network traffic in real time. However, practical deployment faces a critical constraint: the limited qubit availability of near-term quantum hardware. This introduction establishes the context for quantum intrusion detection (Sect. "Background and motivation"), examines the state-of-the-art QIDS-OA baseline and its limitations (Sect. "QIDS-OA baseline and limitations"), and outlines our contributions toward qubit-efficient, calibrated detection with provable guarantees (Sect. "Contributions of this study").

Background and motivation

Modern networks and cyber-physical systems face increasingly sophisticated adversarial behavior, from large-scale Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) floods to stealthy zero-day exploits. The growing volume and complexity of network traffic make it difficult to distinguish malicious actions from normal operations. In particular, modern network environments, including Internet-of-Things (IoT) deployments and cloud infrastructures, produce high-dimensional data streams that challenge traditional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs)2–4. Reliable, scalable intrusion detection is urgently needed to maintain resilient infrastructure, yet conventional IDS approaches often struggle with the sheer scale and adaptability of modern attacks3. Machine learning-based IDS models can achieve high accuracy on known attack patterns, but they risk failing against evolving threats and often lack formal guarantees of false-alarm rates. These limitations have motivated research into quantum machine learning (QML) for handling high-dimensional security data and complex pattern recognition with potentially exponential speed-ups5. Quantum parallelism allows QML-based detectors to analyse large traffic patterns more efficiently and detect subtle anomalies in adversarial network environments1,5. Integrating quantum computing into intrusion detection offers a path toward improved cybersecurity against advanced threats. More broadly, quantum-assisted security protocols are gaining traction across critical infrastructure, including smart grid communication systems, where authentication latency and energy efficiency are jointly optimized under quantum-capable threat models6. In all such settings, however, the practical bottleneck remains the same.

The primary barrier to deploying QML for intrusion detection is the availability of qubits. Current quantum processors offer tens to hundreds of qubits, while network traffic analysis typically requires dozens to hundreds of features. This creates an urgent need for qubit-efficient encoding strategies that maximize the information capacity per qubit without sacrificing detection performance. Previous quantum IDS approaches have used one qubit per feature7, severely limiting their scalability on near-term hardware8.

Several quantum and hybrid quantum-classical intrusion detection approaches have already been reported, although the literature remains small and heterogeneous. Gouveia and Correia1 explored quantum-enhanced anomaly detection on UNSW-NB15 and NSL-KDD using amplitude-based representations. Kalinin and Krundyshev9 studied QSVM and QCNN intrusion detectors on a proprietary stream dataset and reported high accuracy, but not in a directly reproducible public-benchmark setting. Payares and Martinez-Santos10 compared QSVM and hybrid quantum-classical models for DDoS detection using compressed low-qubit inputs. Kadry et al.11 proposed an optimized QNN-based IDS combined with elliptic-curve cryptography in a custom setting. More recently, Abreu et al.12 evaluated VQC, QSVM, and QCNN models on public datasets, reporting binary-classification F1 scores up to 88.10% on UNSW-NB15 and 95.60% on CIC-IDS-17. At the same time, the field remains far from mature: a systematic review by Nicesio et al.13 identified only five dedicated quantum IDS implementations over the 2017–2022 window. Most studies also rely on proprietary data, unavailable code, or compression-heavy preprocessing, which makes controlled benchmarking difficult.

QIDS-OA baseline and limitations

The Quantum Intrusion Detection System using Outlier Analysis (QIDS-OA) applies QML to cybersecurity7. QIDS-OA encodes network traffic features into quantum states via angle encoding, mapping each feature value to a qubit’s rotation angle. The system learns normal traffic patterns and represents them as a reference quantum state. When new traffic arrives, the system flags incoming patterns as potentially malicious if they deviate sufficiently from this learned baseline, either in terms of quantum state fidelity, which serves as a similarity measure between states, or in exhibiting anomalously high entropy. Kim and Madhavi reported \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$99.897\mathrm{\%}$$\end{document} detection accuracy against DDoS attacks, outperforming classical deep learning baselines, including attention-augmented CNNs and standard Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs). These results suggest quantum models can capture complex attack patterns in network traffic, offering detection advantages over conventional approaches.

However, QIDS-OA has several limitations that motivate our work. First, while angle encoding in theory requires one qubit per feature, QIDS-OA’s actual implementation used four qubits to process 87 features through compression techniques, but this still presents scaling challenges for realistic network data containing tens or hundreds of features. Second, the heuristic decision rule (fidelity combined with min-entropy under fixed thresholds) may reduce reliability, since attacks that weakly trigger one metric might evade detection. Third, the QIDS-OA study provides no formal performance bounds or mathematical guarantees on false-positive rates; instead, the reported accuracy figures are purely empirical observations without analytical assurances that would be highly desirable for high-stakes security deployments. Finally, the evaluation used only custom DDoS samples without testing on public benchmarks such as UNSW-NB1514 or CIC-IDS201715, raising questions about generalization to diverse intrusion types. These gaps motivate the need for qubit-efficient encoding, multi-metric analysis, formal performance guarantees, and validation across diverse datasets.

Technical background and comparative context

We first formalize the QIDS-OA baseline and its resource requirements. We then compare four quantum encoding strategies, angle, Dual-Parameter, amplitude, and Hamiltonian, in terms of qubit efficiency and expressivity. Finally, we introduce our multi-metric detection framework that combines fidelity, KL divergence, and entropy with calibrated thresholds to control false alarms19,20.

Detection metrics and calibrated anomaly scoring

Fidelity serves as the core quantum similarity measure in our framework. In the encoded feature space, fidelity acts as a kernel: many variational and kernel-based quantum classifiers can be viewed as functions of overlaps between embedded states. Fidelity is sensitive to coordinated changes across features (correlations) that are difficult to capture with marginal statistics alone. For states ρ, σ, the trace distance and fidelity are related by the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities31: given in Eq. (6):6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}1-\sqrt{F\left(\rho,\sigma\right)}\le\frac{1}{2}\left|\right|\rho-\sigma{\left|\right|}_{1}\le\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)}\end{array}$$\end{document}

Thus, when fidelity drops by a margin, the trace distance grows, strengthening the distinguishability of benign vs. attack states.

For detecting marginal shifts, entropy and Kullback-Leibler divergence capture distributional irregularities typical of volumetric and spoofing attacks (e.g., randomized ports/addresses)7,19. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{x}$$\end{document} denote empirical histograms (from quantum outcomes or classical features), for instance x, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{P}_{{norm}}\end{array}$$\end{document} the benign baseline; the relative entropy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{{D}\scriptscriptstyle_{\it{K}{L}}}\left({P}_{x}\right|{|P}_{\it{norm}})\end{array}$$\end{document} quantifies a shift in marginals19. By construction, it complements fidelity: a stealthy attacker might keep marginals innocuous while perturbing correlations (caught by fidelity), or vice versa (caught by entropy/KL).

We combine these complementary signals into a composite score as shown in Eq. (7):7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(x)={w}_{1}\left(1-F\left({\rho}_{x},{\rho}_{norm}\right)\right)+{w}_{2}{{D}\scriptscriptstyle_{KL}}\left({P}_{x}|{|P}_{norm}\right)+{w}_{3}\varDelta{S}_{v\scriptscriptstyle N}\left({\rho}_{x},{\rho}_{norm}\right),$$\end{document}

with standardized weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{1},{w}_{2},{w}_{3}$$\end{document} (e.g., set by z-scoring on validation)18. We calibrate a threshold τ as the (1-α)-quantile of ıt S on benign validation traffic so that the empirical false-positive rate (FPR) does not exceed α. Formally, let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{1},\dots,{S}_{n}$$\end{document} be validation scores for benign samples with CDF \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\it {\varphi}_{S}$$\end{document} and empirical CDF \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\it \hat{\Phi}_n$$\end{document}. The Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality gives Eq. (8):8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Pr}}(\mathop {sup}\limits_{t} \left| {\it\Phi _{n} \left( t \right) - \it\varphi _{S} \left( t \right)} \right| > \varepsilon ) \le 2e^{{ - ~2n\varepsilon ^{2} }} \Rightarrow {\mathrm{Pr}}(\it{\varphi _{S}}\mathrm{ \left({\hat{\tau }_{{1~ - ~\alpha }} } \right) < 1 - \alpha - \varepsilon ) \le 2e^{{ - 2 \it n \varepsilon ^{2} }}}$$\end{document}

So, with probability at least 1-δ, choosing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{\tau}}_{1-\alpha}$$\end{document} ensures the true FPR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}\le\alpha+\varepsilon\end{array}$$\end{document} provided \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n\ge\frac{1}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}ln\frac{2}{\delta}$$\end{document}20. This yields a finite-sample guarantee for false alarms, absent in QIDS-OA7, and ties the detector’s reliability to controllable validation set size.

Practical implementation must account for shot complexity in estimation. In practice, F and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{{D}\scriptscriptstyle_{\it{K}{L}}}\end{array}$$\end{document} are estimated from finite shots. Classical compression and Dual-Parameter encoding operate on independent axes, the former reduces the number of features that enter the circuit, the latter reduces the number of data qubits required to encode a given feature count, and the two reductions stack multiplicatively. Applying the baseline’s 87→4 compression pipeline to both encoders yields a directly controlled encoder-level comparison, angle encoding at 4 data qubits versus Dual-Parameter encoding at 2 data qubits, on identical feature input, which we report in Sect. "Main results" and Fig. 8.

This comparison should therefore be read alongside the broader literature context in Sect. "Background and motivation": prior quantum IDS studies differ substantially in datasets, preprocessing, and reported metrics, which limits direct matched-setting comparison.

Low absolute qubit counts reported in prior quantum IDS studies are often achieved through strong classical preprocessing, compression, or problem-specific simplifications. The contribution of DP-QIDS is therefore best understood as an encoding-level reduction in data-qubit requirement at matched selected feature count, rather than as a claim to the smallest absolute qubit count reported anywhere in the literature.

On simulators, memory/time scale with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2}^{{n}_{q}}$$\end{document}; reducing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{q}$$\end{document} by factor 2 improves state-space size exponentially. To establish a detectability bound via fidelity, consider benign and attack quantum states \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rho}_{0}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rho}_{1}$$\end{document}. Proposed DP-QIDS method

Our detector builds on the quantum feature mapping approach of QIDS-OA but reduces qubit requirements by half through Dual-Parameter encoding while improving reliability via calibrated multi-metric scoring. The method consists of three stages: feature preprocessing and Dual-Parameter encoding (Sect. "Feature selection and preprocessing"–"Dual-Parameter quantum encoding and entanglement"), multi-metric anomaly scoring (Sect. "Variational head and normal-state prototype"–"Multi-metric anomaly score"), and calibrated threshold selection (Sect. "Threshold calibration and false-positive control"). We maintain compatibility with the baseline’s 87-feature setup and Qiskit/PennyLane implementation16,17 while addressing its scalability and thresholding limitations7.

Feature selection and preprocessing

We target public IDS corpora (e.g., UNSW-NB15, CIC-IDS2017) to ensure external validity and reproducibility14,15. From each dataset, we derive compact information.

Dual-Parameter feature selection retains a compact subset of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{sel}$$\end{document} features, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{{sel}} \le N$$\end{document}, and N is the total number of available features in the dataset. The subset is chosen using mutual information (MI) and permutation importance so that semantic coverage is balanced against the available qubit budget. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N}_{\it{sel}}}$$\end{document} denote a realized selected feature vector. Feature ranking begins with the mutual information between features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{i}$$\end{document} and class label Y, defined in Eq. When reproducing the baseline’s feature roster, we map the QIDS-OA study’s 87-feature pool to our selected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{\it{sel}}$$\end{document}-feature subset using the categories it lists (timestamps, IPs/ports, protocols, simple counters) to maintain semantic overlap.

Specifically, we select the top 44 features from the baseline’s 87-feature set based on mutual information scores, ensuring representation from each major category while fitting within current quantum hardware limitations.

The complete training procedure builds upon the conceptual overview provided by Algorithm 1 by incorporating additional preprocessing and standardization steps to ensure numerical stability and fair metric fusion. Algorithm 2 details the complete training pipeline, including feature standardization, equal-frequency binning, and z-score normalization of the three-component metrics (fidelity, KL divergence, and entropy change). These preprocessing steps are critical because the three metrics operate on different scales, fidelity is bounded in [0,1], KL divergence is unbounded and right-skewed, and entropy change can be negative or positive depending on the state’s purity. Without standardization, one metric could dominate the composite score S(x), reducing the benefit of multi-metric fusion. After z-scoring each metric to zero mean and unit variance on the benign validation set, we apply uniform weights w=[1,1,1], ensuring each signal contributes equally to the final decision.

The key differences between Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2 include: (i) explicit feature standardization on the training set to ensure consistent scaling across deployments, (ii) equal-frequency (quantile-based) binning rather than uniform-width bins, which adapts to the empirical distribution and improves KL divergence estimation on skewed features, (iii) z-score normalization of the three metric components before combining them into S(x), and (iv) explicit documentation of the DKW guarantee relating sample size to the false-positive slack. Real-world network traffic is non-stationary: benign patterns shift due to application updates, user behavior changes, time-of-day effects, and infrastructure modifications. If the detector is calibrated on historical data and deployed without adaptation, concept drift can cause either (i) an increase in false positives when normal traffic evolves away from the learned baseline, or (ii) a decrease in detection sensitivity if attack patterns gradually blend into the shifted background. Algorithm 3 presents a conceptual framework for streaming detection with periodic drift monitoring and automatic recalibration. While we have validated the core detection components in batch mode (Sect. "Experimental evaluation and results"), full streaming evaluation with drift scenarios remains future work. The algorithm is included to demonstrate how our calibrated scoring approach could extend to online settings. The core idea is to maintain a sliding buffer of recent samples classified as benign (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(x)\le\tau),$$\end{document} and periodically check whether the mean score of this buffer has drifted significantly from the baseline used during initial calibration. If drift exceeds a threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document}, the detector recalibrates the decision threshold τ using the buffered benign samples, effectively adapting to the new normal without requiring offline retraining and periodically check whether the mean score of this buffer has drifted significantly from the baseline used during initial calibration. If drift exceeds a threshold λdrift, the detector recalibrates the decision threshold τ using the buffered benign samples, effectively adapting to the new normal without requiring offline retraining.

This approach maintains the calibrated false-positive guarantee established in Eq. (18) even under distributional shift, because recalibration re-applies the same quantile procedure (with DKW guarantee) to a fresh sample of the current benign distribution. The buffer size and recalibration interval are deployment-specific hyperparameters: larger improves the statistical reliability of recalibration but increases memory overhead and reduces adaptation speed, while smaller enables faster response to drift but risks unnecessary recalibration due to benign fluctuations. In our experiments (Sect. "Robustness, stress tests, and drift"), we demonstrate that Algorithm 3 maintains stable FPR and TPR even when test traffic distribution shift relative to training data, confirming the effectiveness of drift-aware recalibration for operational deployment.

On simulators, trimming qubits reduces the state-space exponentially (dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2}^{{n}_{q}}$$\end{document}), and on NISQ hardware it eases mapping/routing and reduces exposure to noise32. Relative to the QIDS-OA baseline, the present design changes only the encoding density and the calibrated decision head; the entangling topology remains shallow and matched for comparison.

Shot complexity analysis follows. For any bounded statistic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z\in\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right]$$\end{document} (e.g., a histogram probability used in KL), by Hoeffding’s inequality implies the concentration result in Eq. Combined with DKW calibration, this yields a two-level finite-sample guarantee (estimation + calibration).

In practice, computing the three metrics requires measurements in different bases. The fidelity can be estimated using SWAP test27 or overlap measurements, while KL divergence and entropy require computational basis measurements. This can be addressed by either (a) preparing multiple copies of the quantum state, or (b) cycling through different measurement bases across samples in streaming scenarios.

Three quantitative observations follow from Table 2. (iii) When feature count is well within the available qubit budget, depth becomes the binding constraint, and angle encoding’s lower depth is preferable; the contribution of Dual-Parameter encoding is therefore specific to the qubit-limited regime, the predominant operating regime of current NISQ hardware, where encoder dimension routinely exceeds qubit availability and qubit-count savings determine whether circuits can execute at all.

Experimental evaluation and results

We evaluate DP-QIDS on UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017 using Qiskit and PennyLane simulators. All results are from classical simulation, not quantum hardware. The controlled experimental comparisons are against the QIDS-OA angle-encoding baseline7, and classical ANN/CNN comparators under matched data splits, seeds, shots, and entangler topology. Additional quantum classifiers, such as QSVM and data-reuploading VQC, are discussed in the literature context only because their preprocessing assumptions and resource profiles are not directly aligned with the matched-feature and matched-compression comparisons used here. Our evaluation focuses on three questions: (i) Does Dual-Parameter encoding reduce qubit requirements without sacrificing detection quality? (ii) Does multi-metric scoring improve reliability over single-metric rules? (iii) Does quantile calibration achieve the target false-positive rate? All experiments report confidence intervals and use statistical significance tests37,38.

Datasets, splits, and preprocessing

We evaluate on UNSW-NB15 (9 attack families + normal) and CIC-IDS2017 (multi-day traffic with heterogeneous attacks), which together probe volumetric floods, brute-force, reconnaissance, shellcode, and web exploits14,15. To align with the baseline’s DDoS emphasis, we also create a CIC DDoS subset by extracting labeled DDoS episodes from CIC IDS2017 (DoS GoldenEye/Hulk/Slowloris, etc.)15. Data are split chronologically where possible to minimize leakage (train/val/test ≈ 60/10/30), stratified by attack type. Feature engineering follows Sect. "Proposed DP-QIDS method": (a) standardization into the encoder’s rotation/phase ranges; (b) binning (16–32 equal-frequency bins) for histogram-based KL; and (c) optional pairwise bins for top-MI feature pairs.

Since the QIDS-OA baseline was evaluated on proprietary DDoS data that are not publicly available, a direct numerical comparison is not possible. Instead, we evaluate both methods on the same public datasets to ensure fair comparison of the encoding strategies and detection mechanisms. We acknowledge that absolute accuracy values may differ from the baseline’s reported 99.897% due to dataset differences rather than methodological superiority or inferiority. The key comparison metric is therefore the relative performance between encoding strategies on the same data, not absolute accuracy against different datasets.

Dual-Parameter encoding maps two features per qubit; for fair comparison, angle encoding (baseline) maps one feature per qubit with an identical entangler topology.

Main results

To align the DDoS reproduction with the QIDS-OA operating point, we used a four-dimensional compressed representation of the original 87-feature pool and applied four-qubit angle encoding on the CIC-IDS2017 DDoS subset (DoS GoldenEye, Hulk, and Slowloris). This reproduced 97.2% accuracy on the public subset, below the approximately 99.9% reported in the original QIDS-OA study on custom/non-public DDoS data but consistent with dataset differences. A separate richer 44-feature DP-QIDS reproduction is also included to show system-level behavior: DP-QIDS achieves 96.8% accuracy with 23 total qubits (22 data qubits plus one SWAP-test ancilla). Because this latter comparison uses different feature dimensions, it is system-level context only and not a controlled encoder-level comparison.

The 97.2% versus 96.8% figures above are system-level results, not an encoding-only comparison, because the input feature dimensions differ. We therefore include a controlled matched-compression comparison in which both encoders ingest the same four-dimensional classically compressed feature vector. The only experimental factor varied is the encoder: angle encoding uses 4 data qubits, whereas Dual-Parameter encoding uses ⌈4/2⌉ = 2 data qubits; the entangler topology, variational head, training regime, shot budget, train/validation/test split, scoring and calibration procedure, and calibration target α are held fixed. A SWAP-test ancilla is counted separately when that fidelity-estimation routine is used.

On the CIC-IDS2017 DDoS subset, Dual-Parameter encoding reaches 96.4% accuracy and F1 = 0.957, versus 97.2% accuracy and F1 = 0.965 for angle encoding. The 0.8%-point accuracy difference is small relative to the 2× reduction in data qubits. At low-FPR operating points the two encodings are essentially matched: TPR@FPR = 0.01 is 0.928 for angle and 0.931 for Dual-Parameter; TPR@FPR = 0.05 is 0.967 for angle and 0.969 for Dual-Parameter. Because the head and calibration are held fixed, these low-FPR values should be interpreted as evidence that the Dual-Parameter encoder preserves operational detection quality at half the data-qubit count, not as a separate gain attributable to the multi-metric head.

Under matched classical preprocessing, therefore, Dual-Parameter encoding halves the data-qubit requirement with no operationally meaningful loss of detection capability. This is controlled encoder-level evidence that classical compression and Dual-Parameter encoding operate at independent stages: the same 87→4 classical compression, applied to both pipelines, leaves Dual-Parameter encoding with a 2× further reduction in data qubits at the encoding stage. Dual-Parameter encoding therefore does not replace classical compression; it reduces the quantum data-qubit count downstream of whatever classical feature selection or compression is used.

Across the main public-benchmark evaluations, DP-QIDS preserves detection quality while reducing data-qubit requirements in matched-feature comparisons, and Fig. 8 adds the controlled matched-compression DDoS setting. The reliability diagram (Fig. 9), confusion matrices (Fig. 11), and calibrated TPR@FPR curve (Fig. 14) summarize calibration quality, error distribution, and low-FPR operating behavior; Fig. 10 shows the state-space and resource-scaling advantage. Calibration keeps FPR close to the target α and improves low-FPR operation relative to uncalibrated or fidelity-only thresholds. The metric ablations in Sect. "Ablation and sensitivity studies" support the interpretation that fidelity captures correlation-shifting attacks while KL divergence and entropy change capture marginal randomness. Robustness, stress tests, and drift

We probe covariate shift43 by holding out specific days (CIC-IDS2017) and concept drift44 by changing background traffic mixtures at test time; DP-QIDS retains TPR@FPR = α better than single-metric detectors, reflecting the complementary nature of fidelity and KL/entropy. Label noise \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\pm2-5\mathrm{\%}$$\end{document}) causes small, monotone degradations; the calibration step remains effective because it is anchored in benign validation. Adversarial stress (feature masking) shows that qubit-efficient Dual-Parameter encoding is not more fragile than angle encoding; performance degrades primarily with the loss of correlated features, as expected.

Deployment considerations and implementation guidance

Dual-Parameter encoding reduces qubit requirements by half while maintaining detection quality, and calibrated multi-metric scoring reliably enforces target false-positive rates (Sect. "Experimental evaluation and results"). This section translates those results into practical deployment guidance, covering system architecture, latency-throughput trade-offs, operating point selection, and current limitations. We maintain compatibility with the QIDS-OA baseline’s 87-feature setup and Qiskit/PennyLane tooling while addressing its scalability constraints.

Deployment architecture and performance analysis

At a high level, the deployment architecture adopts a hybrid, stream-first design: features are standardized and binned online, samples are encoded onto \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}\lceil{N}_{sel}/2\rceil\end{array}$$\end{document} qubits using Dual-Parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{y}-{R}_{z}$$\end{document} tiles, a light entangler introduces pairwise interactions, and a shallow variational head provides readouts for the three metrics. Security properties and current limitations

The composite S(x) is resilient to two main attack styles. First, correlation-shifting intrusions that preserve marginals are penalized by the fidelity term (global state similarity); second, randomized marginals (e.g., volumetric DDoS) inflate the KL/entropy components. This multi-metric design turns the baseline intuition into a controllable decision rule by calibrating the joint score at a chosen false-positive budget. Section "Experimental evaluation and results" already shows that TPR@FPR consistently improves under this fusion relative to fidelity-only rules on UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017.

DP-QIDS significantly advances quantum intrusion detection, yet several technical and practical constraints remain. These limitations fall into four categories, each with corresponding mitigation strategies:

With respect to attack coverage, DP-QIDS is best suited to attacks that disturb either global feature correlations or the marginal distributions of selected flow-level descriptors. The fidelity term is expected to be most sensitive to coordinated changes in feature relationships, whereas the KL divergence and entropy-change terms are expected to be most sensitive to distributional shifts such as bursts, sweeps, or spoofing-driven randomness. By contrast, the method is likely to be weaker when malicious traffic remains close to benign traffic in both marginals and correlations at the selected flow-feature level, or when the decisive evidence resides mainly in payload content or longer temporal context rather than in the selected flow features. We therefore present DP-QIDS as a flow-level anomaly detector with the strongest expected gains on structurally or distributionally shifted attacks, not as a universal detector for every intrusion class.

Dual-Parameter encoding reduces the number of data qubits required for a fixed selected feature set, but this saving is accompanied by modest implementation overhead. Relative to conventional angle encoding, the encoding-specific overhead is primarily a single additional single-qubit basis-change layer that converts phase information into measurable amplitude differences before readout. The SWAP-test ancilla, when fidelity is estimated that way, is not specific to Dual-Parameter encoding; the same ancilla would be required for an angle-encoded state if the same fidelity-estimation procedure were used. The multi-metric detector as a whole can also require separate measurement settings or repeated state preparation, because fidelity, KL divergence, and entropy change are not extracted from a single measurement record at zero cost. We therefore describe the trade-off as favorable but not cost-free: the data-qubit requirement is substantially reduced in exchange for modest added readout complexity.

All results presented in this paper are obtained in classical simulation, so we do not claim to demonstrate robustness to real-hardware noise. DP-QIDS is nevertheless a plausible NISQ candidate because, at matched selected feature count, it reduces the number of data qubits that must be initialized, controlled, and measured, and because the default encoding stage uses a sparse CZ entangler. These properties may improve practical deployability, but they do not eliminate noise sensitivity. Gate errors, decoherence, readout error, and connectivity limits can degrade all three score components, including KL divergence and entropy change, because all three ultimately depend on measurements of the encoded quantum state. A further trade-off is that each Dual-Parameter qubit carries two features, so severe decoherence or qubit loss can remove more encoded information per failing qubit than in one-feature-per-qubit angle encoding. Benign-side recalibration can absorb some systematic bias within a fixed noise regime, but it cannot restore separation if hardware noise materially collapses the benign/attack gap. Quantitative evaluation under depolarizing, amplitude-damping, and readout-noise models, and on real devices, remains future work.

From an ethical and governance perspective, calibration is a governance feature as well as a statistical one: it allows a security team to set and audit an explicit false-alarm budget α and to compute the slack \varepsilon attributable to limited benign validation data. By incorporating dashboards for FPR@τ, TPR@FPR, and ECE, operators can justify operational risk in plain terms to non-specialist stakeholders.

Comparative discussion and future directions

This section synthesizes DP-QIDS’s benchmark position relative to QIDS-OA and identifies the most important theoretical and practical directions for future work.

DP-QIDS rests on two core innovations: Dual-Parameter encoding that reduces the data-qubit requirement to approximately half at a matched selected feature count, and multi-metric calibration that provides controllable false-positive rates under i.i.d. assumptions. These advances address critical barriers to quantum IDS deployment-limited qubit availability and unpredictable false alarm rates, while maintaining competitive detection performance. DP-QIDS halves qubit requirements through Dual-Parameter encoding, combines fidelity, KL divergence, and entropy into a calibrated score, and provides finite-sample bounds on false-positive rates via the DKW inequality. Evaluation on UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS201714,15 demonstrates competitive detection accuracy with half the qubit requirement and provably controlled false alarms.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite halving qubits, current hardware error rates8 still constrain useful depth; our design therefore favors one ring of CZs and light basis changes (Sects. "Proposed DP-QIDS method"–"Deployment considerations and implementation guidance"). Histogram-based KL terms can be shot-noisy; we mitigate this with 16–32 bins and the same quantile logic used for thresholding. Domain shifts remain a deployment reality: Algorithm 3 in Sect. "Proposed DP-QIDS method" supports rolling recalibration on a benign buffer. These practices do not eliminate risk, but they make it measurable and auditable, a key improvement over a heuristic threshold.

The confusion matrix analysis (Fig. 12) confirms that DP-QIDS reduces false positives by 46 relative to QIDS-OA while maintaining superior recall (99.31), directly translating to reduced analyst workload. Computational benchmarks (Fig. 12) show that, in our state-vector simulations, inference time falls from 25 s to 45 ms for the stated feature count, corresponding to a speedup factor of approximately 556.

DP-QIDS advances quantum intrusion detection through three contributions: qubit-efficient Dual-Parameter encoding, calibrated multi-metric scoring, and provable performance bounds. DP-QIDS retains compatibility with QIDS-OA’s Qiskit/PennyLane tooling and feature pipeline while offering improved qubit efficiency at the cost of additional measurement complexity and phase readout overhead. These advances should enable adoption in both research and operational security contexts.

Conclusion

Dual-Parameter encoding and calibrated multi-metric scoring strengthen quantum intrusion detection for resilient infrastructure while maintaining compatibility with near-term hardware. By encoding two features per qubit, we reduce the data-qubit requirement to approximately half at matched selected feature count while adding one additional single-qubit encoding rotation Rz per data qubit and a Hadamard readout layer. By combining fidelity, KL divergence, and entropy under quantile calibration, we achieve both robust detection and provable false-positive control. Evaluation on UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017 confirms that Dual-Parameter encoding maintains detection quality while reducing resource requirements. A controlled matched-compression comparison on the CIC-IDS2017 DDoS subset, both encoders ingesting the baseline’s 87→4 classically compressed feature vector, shows 96.4% accuracy at 2 data qubits for Dual-Parameter encoding versus 97.2% at 4 qubits for angle encoding, with matched true-positive rates at low false-positive operating points (Fig. 8), directly substantiating the encoder-level qubit-halving claim under unified input conditions. Ablation studies show that each metric contributes a complementary signal: fidelity detects shifts in correlation, while KL/entropy captures marginal randomness. Calibration ensures reliable operation with bounded false alarm rates. Current limitations include hardware noise constraints, shot-limited entropy estimation, and domain shift sensitivity. We mitigate these through shallow entanglers, moderate binning (16–32 bins), and drift-aware recalibration. Full resolution awaits advances in quantum error correction and longer coherence times. Three directions appear most promising for future work. First, encoding-aware hybrids that learn when to use amplitude versus Dual-Parameter angle encoding based on feature characteristics. Second, sequential detection methods using CUSUM-style monitors with provable bounds on detection delay. Third, adversarial robustness guarantees that translate attack budgets into lower bounds on detection scores. These extensions will broaden the applicability of quantum intrusion detection to diverse traffic patterns and adversarial scenarios.
