Two Sides of the Same Curve: Targets, Reduction, and LDL-C Burden
Authors: Masatsune Ogura
Published: 2026

See article vol. 33: 1030-1048

The relationship between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) has traditionally been discussed in terms of achieved LDL-C levels. Decades of clinical trials have firmly established that lower LDL-C levels are associated with lower cardiovascular risk, leading to increasingly stringent LDL-C targets in contemporary guidelines. However, from a biological perspective, atherosclerosis develops as a consequence of cumulative exposure to circulating LDL particles 1 , 2) . This concept raises an important question: should clinicians focus primarily on the LDL-C level ultimately achieved, or on the magnitude of LDL-C reduction achieved through treatment?

In recent years, international lipid-management strategies have increasingly incorporated both concepts. The 2019 ESC/EAS guidelines recommend not only achieving LDL-C levels below specified thresholds but also reducing LDL-C by at least 50% from baseline in very high-risk patients 3) . Similarly, the ACC/AHA guidelines emphasize percentage LDL-C reduction as an important therapeutic goal 4) . In contrast, the 2022 Japan Atherosclerosis Society (JAS) Guidelines continue to focus primarily on achieved LDL-C levels, providing clinicians with a relatively simple and practical treat-to-target approach 5) .

Against this background, Morishita et al. examined the association between relative LDL-C reduction and cardiovascular outcomes after PCI in a real-world Japanese cohort 6) . Their study demonstrated a graded association between larger LDL-C reductions and lower cardiovascular risk and further showed that patients achieving both LDL-C ＜55 mg/dL and ≥ 50% LDL-C reduction experienced the most favorable outcomes. These findings provide important real-world support for the dual-target strategy increasingly advocated by international guidelines.

At first glance, the results may appear to challenge the traditional target-based approach. However, the implications for routine clinical practice are less straightforward. One of the major strengths of absolute LDL-C targets is their simplicity. General practitioners receiving patients after PCI from tertiary-care centers can readily determine whether LDL-C remains below a recommended threshold and can adjust therapy accordingly. In contrast, introducing relative LDL-C reduction as an additional treatment goal may complicate decision-making.

Consider a patient whose LDL-C is maintained at 61 mg/dL after PCI. Most clinicians would regard such a patient as well controlled. Yet under a dual-target framework, that same patient may still be considered inadequately treated if LDL-C reduction from baseline is less than 50%. Whether this additional metric improves clinical decision-making or merely adds complexity remains an open question, particularly in community practice, where baseline LDL-C values may not always be readily available.

Importantly, percentage reduction is inherently influenced by baseline LDL-C levels. Patients with relatively low baseline LDL-C concentrations may have difficulty achieving a 50% reduction despite attaining excellent LDL-C levels, whereas patients with markedly elevated baseline LDL-C may achieve substantial percentage reductions while remaining above guideline-recommended thresholds. As the authors themselves acknowledge, relative reduction and achieved LDL-C do not always convey identical clinical information.

The interpretation of relative LDL-C reduction may be particularly challenging in patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome (ACS). LDL-C concentrations decline during the acute phase following myocardial infarction, and the authors appropriately recognize this as a study limitation. Consequently, baseline LDL-C values measured during ACS hospitalization may underestimate true pre-event LDL-C levels, potentially affecting calculations of percentage reduction. Whether the same ≥ 50% reduction threshold should be applied equally to ACS and stable CAD populations therefore remains uncertain.

An alternative way to interpret these findings is through the concept of cumulative LDL-C burden. Atherosclerosis can be viewed as the result of the area under the LDL-C–time curve—that is, cumulative LDL-C exposure throughout life. Within this framework, achieved LDL-C levels and relative LDL-C reduction may not represent competing metrics but rather different perspectives on the same biological process.

Achieved LDL-C reflects the residual burden that remains after treatment, whereas relative LDL-C reduction reflects the amount of burden removed by therapy ( Fig.1 ) . Patients who achieve both a low LDL-C level and a large percentage reduction may therefore be those in whom cumulative LDL-C burden has been most effectively minimized. Conversely, patients who meet only one of these measures may still have a substantial residual burden. From this perspective, the apparent tension between achieved LDL-C and relative reduction becomes less a matter of choosing one metric over the other and more a question of how best to integrate both.

This conceptual framework may also help explain why contemporary European and American guidelines have adopted dual therapeutic goals. Rather than representing two independent treatment targets, achieved LDL-C levels and percentage reduction may be complementary indicators of the extent to which cumulative LDL-C burden has been reduced. Future studies incorporating longitudinal LDL-C trajectories and direct estimation of LDL-C burden may further clarify the biological and clinical significance of this concept.

Thus, the study by Morishita et al. raises a timely and clinically relevant question: should lipid management focus on how low LDL-C becomes or on how much LDL-C is reduced? The answer is likely neither alone. From the perspective of LDL-C burden, both metrics capture different aspects of the same pathogenic process. The challenge for future guidelines may therefore not be choosing between achieved LDL-C levels and relative LDL-C reduction, but determining how best to integrate both measures into a clinically practical framework that more accurately reflects cumulative LDL-C exposure and cardiovascular risk.
