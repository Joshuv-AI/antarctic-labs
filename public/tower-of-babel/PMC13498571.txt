Dislocation density–based constitutive modeling and experimental validation of cold-rolled AA1050 aluminum with a techno-economic assessment for lithium-ion battery applications
Authors: Linzhi Yin, Ali B. M. Ali, Omar J. AlKhatib, Pradeep Kumar Singh, Xin Sun, Fatma Ahmed Hassan, Hamdi Ayed, Fuhaid Alshammari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a numerical–experimental study of microstructure evolution during cold rolling of AA1050 aluminum. For the first time, a dislocation density–based constitutive model used to simulate microstructural evolution during the cold rolling of commercially pure aluminum. The model was implemented within the ABAQUS/Explicit environment via a VUMAT subroutine to directly couple strain hardening behavior with dislocation density evolution and grain refinement mechanisms under varying thickness reductions. Cold rolling reductions of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 80% were simulated, and the resulting fields of dislocation density, equivalent plastic strain, grain size, and stress were analyzed. The results show that increasing thickness reduction intensifies plastic deformation and produces pronounced grain refinement, especially near the surface and edges, trending toward more homogeneous refinement at higher reductions. Dislocation density exhibits a rapid initial rise followed by stabilization, with the highest stabilized values obtained at 80% reduction. Experimental validation using X-ray diffraction line profile analysis for the 20% and 40% reductions demonstrates excellent agreement with simulation predictions, with discrepancies below 2%. The calibrated implementation provides a reliable framework for predicting microstructure–thickness relationships and serves as an effective tool for designing reduction schedules to achieve desired microstructural states and improved mechanical performance. Moreover, the techno-economic assessment shows that selecting Al1050 for lithium-ion components and numerical analysis of cold rolling process generated a 10-year net present value (NPV) gain of approximately USD 2.2–2.5 million compared with conventionally processed Al1060 and Al3003.

Introduction

Aluminum alloys have gained substantial attention owing to their low weight, high strength-to-weight ratio, recyclability, and corrosion resistance, leading to increasing use in automotive, aerospace, and telecommunication applications1–8. Despite these advantages, the broader adoption of wide aluminum sheets is hindered by the complexity of their processing routes and the higher production costs relative to steel1–8. Cold rolling is an important process when rolling wide aluminum alloy sheets. During the rolling process, two important parameters are the rolling reduction and the subsequent annealing temperature9–13. A thermodynamically unstable structure is created during cold deformation, which strengthens metals due to an increase in lattice defects and encourages restoration phenomena later. Metals and alloys’ microstructures as well as their mechanical and physical characteristics can be altered during restoration processes, and the necessary mechanical and physical characteristics can be obtained by modifying the annealing and deformation parameters. As a result, several studies on this topic using various metals and alloys have been conducted14–19. Cold rolling increases the strain hardening coefficient, which leads to higher mechanical strength while promoting a more uniform thickness distribution in the rolled sheet14–17,20. This process ensures that the material becomes stronger and more durable without compromising its uniformity. Plastic deformation is one of the most effective approaches for improving the microstructure and mechanical properties of metallic materials. Controlled deformation refines the internal structure, through mechanisms such as dislocation accumulation and grain refinement, resulting in increased strength, hardness, and enhanced overall performance. The microstructural evolution during multipass rolling processes significantly influences the final mechanical properties of the materials. To meet quality assurance standards, it is essential that materials are processed with well-defined and controlled microstructures21. Hence, understanding and modeling the microstructural evolution during cold rolling, as well as its influence on material behavior, provides crucial insight into the deformation process. By optimizing these conditions, the mechanical properties and overall quality of the final product can be significantly improved22–26. During cold working, the microstructure of metallic materials experiences pronounced evolution. This includes strain hardening that enhances strength, recovery involving dislocation rearrangement, recrystallization that produces new grains with distinct orientations, and grain growth or refinement. These processes are highly dependent on the deformation rate, underscoring their sensitivity to the rate of applied strain27. The formation and distribution of dislocations play a crucial role in determining the plastic behavior of metals. Dislocations are responsible for the characteristic response observed during plastic deformation, and their density is strongly correlated with changes in material hardness. Moreover, their presence correlates strongly with variations in material hardness. The intricate mechanism of dislocation glides over the slip planes governs the phenomenon of plastic deformation in metals. The production, movement, ensuing trapping, and deposition of dislocations all contribute significantly to the microscopic work hardening of metal alloys24,28–33.

Experimental studies have been carried out to quantify the dislocation density evolution in commercially pure aluminum and the AA1050 alloy34–37. Wroński et al.38 examined the long-term microstructural stability of ECAP-processed AA1050 using EBSD and hardness measurements, reporting an initial dislocation density of about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.17\times {10}^{15}\hspace{0.17em}{m}^{-2}$$\end{document} that decreased to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$8.29\times {10}^{14}\hspace{0.17em}{m}^{-2}$$\end{document} after five years of ambient recovery. Sidor et al.28 calculated the dislocation density in Al 1050 cold rolled by using X-ray line profile analysis (XPLA) and micro hardness indentation. Both methods produce similar values for dislocation density. Also, they calculated the dislocation density in different redactions using the Kubin–Estrin model. This model gives a good estimate of the evolutionary pattern of dislocations. It reasonably approximates the dislocation density at small and moderate reduction levels (5–30%). The difference between the measured and simulated values is minimal when the material is subjected to more severe straining (~ 47%). Chakravarty et al.39 further investigated the mid-thickness dislocation distribution in 46.8% cold-rolled 1050 using indentation and numerical models (Kubin–Estrin and a modified version), revealing that model predictions tend to slightly underestimate experimental values, likely due to unmodeled complexities in dislocation interactions and solute effects. While several studies have investigated the evolution of dislocation density in AA1050 aluminum through experimental and numerical simulation, notable discrepancies persist between predicted and experimentally measured dislocation densities, particularly under conditions of severe deformation. The models employed in the studies exhibited significant deviations between simulated and experimental results and were generally limited in scope, as they did not account for key mechanical parameters such as equivalent plastic strain and stress. However, understanding these parameters, alongside dislocation density, is essential for developing a comprehensive picture of microstructural evolution during cold rolling of AA1050 aluminum. Addressing these limitations requires a physically based modeling framework capable of simultaneously predicting the evolution of dislocation density, equivalent plastic strain, and stress fields, thereby providing a more accurate representation of the underlying deformation mechanisms. By incorporating such physics-informed behavior into numerical simulations, the accuracy of both instantaneous and long-term microstructural predictions can be significantly improved, ultimately reducing the reliance on extensive experimental testing and contributing to more cost-efficient and optimized process design.

Dislocation-based models have been successfully employed in various deformation processes to predict dislocation density and grain size evolution40–44. Majid et al.45 conducted research on the evolution of material microstructure during cold spray process using dislocation-based model and focusing on AA1050 aluminum particles and substrates. Their study revealed at the interfaces between the aluminum particles and the substrate, as well as within the particles themselves, the grain size is smaller. This reduction in grain size is attributed to the high dislocation density in these regions and the grain size increases with distance from the interface. As we move away from the particle–substrate boundary, the dislocation density decreases, leading to larger grain sizes. Using a dislocation-based model to simulate a single impact of cold spraying, Lemiale et al.46 found that a recovery cell structure, which is comparable to a recovery grain structure, formed at the particle–substrate interface. They observed an increase in the size of the dislocation cell in certain cases following the primary structure’s recovery. This increase is explained by the formation of dynamic recovery conditions at high temperatures, which cause the rate of dislocation accumulation to be low in the areas where temperature increases are greatest. This behavior raises the initial velocity, which raises the saturation of the final cell size. Moreover, while aluminum alloys are widely used in lithium-ion battery components, existing studies primarily focus on material selection, electrochemical performance, or manufacturing feasibility, with limited consideration of how process-induced microstructural features influence both performance and economic outcomes47,48. In parallel, numerical and experimental investigations of cold rolling—particularly for commercially pure aluminum such as Al1050—have provided valuable insight into deformation behavior, grain refinement, and dislocation density evolution, yet these advances are rarely linked to downstream energy applications49. Moreover, although techno-economic analyses are commonly employed at the battery pack or system level, they typically overlook the role of microstructure-informed manufacturing optimization. As a result, the economic value of numerical process design, which enables targeted control of grain size and dislocation density during rolling, remains largely unexplored50–54.

In the present work, a dislocation density–based constitutive model was implemented within an ABAQUS/Explicit finite element framework to simulate the cold rolling process of AA1050 aluminum. The model incorporates dislocation density as an internal state variable to describe strain hardening and microstructural evolution during plastic deformation. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first implementation and validation of a dislocation density–based constitutive model for cold rolling of commercially pure aluminum. Numerical simulations were conducted for 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 80% thickness reductions, predicting the evolution and spatial distribution of dislocation density, grain size, equivalent plastic strain, and stress. The predicted dislocation densities were compared with experimental values obtained from X-ray diffraction (XRD) line profile analysis for 20% and 40% reductions. The excellent agreement between simulation and experiment (differences within 2%) confirms the accuracy and reliability of the adopted modeling approach in predicting the microstructural changes induced by cold rolling. This validated framework provides a physically based and computationally robust tool for process optimization, enabling precise control of final microstructural features through tailored rolling parameters. On the other hand, this study addresses the economic analysis gaps in this field by coupling numerical and experimental cold-rolling analysis of Al1050 with a quantitative techno-economic evaluation for lithium-ion battery components, thereby establishing a direct connection between process optimization, performance improvement, and long-term economic benefit.

Nowadays, advanced numerical calculation software enables the simulation and prediction of the cold rolling process and its associated thermomechanical effects. The focus of this article is on numerical simulation, and the results were validated using experimental XRD measurements. In this research, the cold rolling process of metallic strips is simulated using ABAQUS/Explicit finite element software (version 6.14). Aluminum 1050 was selected as the workpiece material, while the roll was modeled as rigid body. The workpiece was meshed with CPS4R elements (linear four-node quadrilateral with reduced integration). The model was developed in two dimensions under the plane-stress assumption. Although cold rolling of wide sheets is commonly idealized as a plane-strain process, the present work adopts a plane-stress formulation as a simplified and computationally efficient framework for evaluating the evolution of the main microstructural variables during rolling. In this context, the predicted stress and strain fields are interpreted as effective in-plane quantities used to drive the dislocation-density-based constitutive model, rather than as a full representation of the three-dimensional rolling stress state. The use of this assumption may affect the absolute magnitude of the predicted von Mises stress, equivalent plastic strain, and dislocation density; however, all reduction cases were analyzed using the same formulation, allowing consistent comparison of the relative trends with increasing thickness reduction. Due to the geometric and loading symmetry, only half of the workpiece was modeled to reduce computational cost. Symmetrical boundary conditions were applied, and the bottom edge of the specimen was fixed in the Y-direction to represent the symmetric support conditions. Fully constrained boundary conditions were applied to the rigid rolls in non-rotational degrees of freedom. A mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure mesh-independence of the results. The Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) adaptive meshing technique was employed to minimize element distortion resulting from large deformations. A surface-to-surface contact formulation was applied between the roll and the specimen. A friction coefficient of 0.25 was assigned to represent the interaction between the aluminum plate and the rigid body59. A mesh size of 250 μm was selected, resulting in a total of approximately 16,000 nodes (shown in Fig. 1). In this study, the Cowper–Symonds stiffness-based model was applied in its two-dimensional form45,59. The dislocation density was introduced as a numerical internal variable to describe the strain hardening behavior as a function of strain evolution. To implement the dislocation density evolution law, a VUMAT subroutine was developed. The algorithm in the VUMAT code, shown in Fig. 2, starts by defining material state variables. At each increment, stresses are calculated so that ABAQUS determines the stable time step. For each material point, an elastic trial stress is computed based on the total strain, and the corresponding trial Von Mises stress (Smises) is determined. This value is compared to the current yield stress (Sy) at that point. If the Von Mises stress exceeds the yielding stress, yielding occurs. In such cases, the plastic strain increment is calculated so that the stress returns to the current yield surface. At this stage, dislocation hardening is evaluated according to the dislocation-based constitutive model. Finally, stresses and state variables are updated, and the procedure is repeated for all integration points throughout the simulation.Fig. 2Flowchart summarizing the VUMAT subroutine process implemented in ABAQUS for stress update and dislocation density computation.

Experimental procedure

The cold rolling experimental conditions for AA1050 were performed at room temperature without lubrication under control conditions. The initial thickness of AA1050 was 10 mm. The roll bonding process was performed by total reductions in thickness of 20%, and 40%. The roll diameter was 110 mm, and the rolling speed was 15 rpm. Figure 3 illustrates the schematic representation of the cold rolling process.Fig. 3Schematic illustration of the cold rolling process in the experimental setup.

Schematic illustration of the cold rolling process in the experimental setup.

For optical microstructural characterization, the cold-rolled samples corresponding to 20% and 40% thickness reductions were sectioned along the rolling-direction/thickness-direction plane. The sectioned specimens were prepared using standard metallographic procedures, including mechanical grinding and polishing, to obtain a refined surface suitable for optical observation. The polished surfaces were subsequently etched for 8 s using a modified Keller’s reagent. The microstructural features were examined using a Leitz Metallux 3 optical microscope, and the optically resolved grain morphology was evaluated from the acquired micrographs.

Results and discussion

In the simulation part, the results for dislocation density, grain size, equivalent plastic strain, and stress were obtained. The simulated dislocation density values were then validated against those measured experimentally using XRD analysis.

Dislocation density

Figure 4a–e illustrates the distribution of dislocation density in the material under various thickness reductions (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 80%) during the cold rolling process. At 10% reduction (Fig. 4a), the dislocation density remains relatively low across material, as indicated by the dominant green and blue regions. Slightly higher densities appear near the surfaces and edges, where the deformation is more intense. At 20% reduction (Fig. 4b), a modest increase in dislocation density is observed, particularly in the surface layers. With further deformation (30% reduction, Fig. 4c), the dislocation density rises substantially, extending deeper into the interior, which signifies the enhanced plastic deformation and the onset of strain hardening64. At 40% reduction (Fig. 4d), regions of high dislocation density spread more uniformly across the cross-section, showing that deformation affects not only the near-surface zones but also the bulk of the material. By 80% reduction (Fig. 4e), the contour is dominated by red regions, indicating an almost saturated dislocation network throughout the material thickness. This state corresponds to severe plastic deformation, where the material experiences extensive work hardening and a significant loss of ductility. As the percentage of reduction increases, the dislocation density increases significantly, with a transition from localized regions of high dislocation density near the surface at lower reductions (10%-20%) to a more uniform, high-density distribution throughout the material at higher reductions (40%-80%). This trend is typical in metals undergoing plastic deformation: initially, dislocations are generated and concentrated near the surface, and with increased deformation, dislocation multiplication leads to higher densities throughout the material65–67. The material also becomes progressively harder and stronger (work hardening) but loses ductility as the dislocation density increases. For the 20% reduction case, the maximum dislocation density obtained from the simulation is 6.19 × 1014 m-2 while the corresponding experimental value determined by XRD is 5.97 × 1014 m-2. Although experimental validation was available only for the 20% and 40% thickness reductions, the very close agreement between the simulation results and the XRD measurements confirms the predictive capability of the implemented dislocation density based constitutive model. The relative difference between the numerical predictions and experimental values remains below approximately 7%, which indicates that the model parameters adopted from the literature are suitable for describing the deformation behavior of AA1050 during cold rolling. Therefore, once validated at representative deformation levels, the model can reasonably be used to predict microstructural evolution and dislocation density distributions at other reduction levels such as 10%, 30%, and 80%.

Microhardness evolution and correlation with dislocation density

Figure 12 shows the evolution of Vickers microhardness as a function of rolling reduction for cold-rolled AA1050 aluminum. At low reductions (10–30%), the increase in hardness is more pronounced, with values increasing from 38 to 46 HV, indicating rapid dislocation multiplication. In contrast, at higher reductions (40–80%), the hardness increases more gradually from 48 to 53 HV, demonstrating a clear reduction in the hardening rate and indicating a tendency toward saturation.Fig. 12Variation of dislocation density through the thickness (path) of cold-rolled aluminum sheets for different thickness reductions.

Variation of dislocation density through the thickness (path) of cold-rolled aluminum sheets for different thickness reductions.

The reduced slope of the hardness curve at higher rolling reductions indicates a decrease in the hardening rate rather than a complete saturation of hardness. This behavior is consistent with the Taylor-type relationship between strength-hardness and dislocation density, in which an increase in dislocation density leads to an increase in hardness. However, because the strengthening contribution scales with the square root of dislocation density, and because dynamic recovery becomes more active at higher strains, the rate of hardness increase gradually decreases. Therefore, both the hardness evolution and the dislocation-density results indicate a progressive transition toward a stabilization regime at high rolling reductions, rather than a complete saturation of hardness while dislocation density continues to increase independently. As the rolling reduction increases, dislocation multiplication enhances the resistance to plastic deformation, resulting in higher hardness values, while the gradual reduction in the hardening rate reflects the increasing influence of recovery mechanisms. The hardness trend is also consistent with the increase in equivalent plastic strain and von Mises stress observed at higher reductions. For example, the equivalent plastic strain increases from about 1.5 at 10% reduction to over 7 at 80% reduction, which directly contributes to the observed increase in hardness. This confirms that the progressive microstructural refinement and dislocation accumulation are directly reflected in the mechanical response of the material. Therefore, the hardness evolution provides an additional quantitative mechanical indication supporting the dislocation-density–based interpretation of the cold rolling behavior of AA1050 aluminum.

Techno-economic analysis: application in li-ion battery current collectors

AA1050 aluminum is not only a model material for studying dislocation-density–based plasticity, but also a technologically important alloy in electrochemical energy storage systems. High-purity 1xxx series aluminum foils, including AA1050, AA1060, AA1070 and AA1235, are widely used as cathode current collectors in lithium-ion batteries due to their excellent electrical conductivity, good formability and relatively low cost. From a materials selection perspective, high-purity aluminum is preferred over highly alloyed grades because the absence of significant alloying additions maximizes electrical conductivity and minimizes parasitic interfacial reactions. Industrial and academic studies consistently report that 1xxx series foils are among the standard choices for cathode current collectors, with 1000, 3000 and 8000 series alloys forming the dominant families in commercial LIB production. Recent reviews on current collectors for lithium-ion batteries identify aluminum foil as the state-of-the-art cathode current collector material, highlighting its favorable combination of electrochemical stability, conductivity, low density and cost compared with alternative metals such as Ti, Ni or stainless steel.

By combining the validated dislocation-density–based finite element framework developed in this work with process-based Li-ion battery cost models, a future techno-economic analysis can systematically quantify how numerical analysis of preparing aluminum components such as current-collector foils, tabs, connectors, and thin structural elements in lithium-ion batteries can provide costs benefits in real world applications like car manufacturing companies.

In order to quantify the economic benefit of numerically optimized cold rolling of Al1050 for lithium-ion battery components, a comparative techno-economic analysis was conducted against two baseline materials: Al1060 and Al3003, both processed using conventional trial-and-error manufacturing without numerical optimization. The assessment explicitly accounts for material cost, manufacturing energy, process efficiency, scrap rate, operational energy efficiency, and time horizon, and is evaluated using a Net Present Value (NPV) framework.

Conclusion

The evolution of dislocation density in cold-rolled 1050 aluminum alloy was investigated through a combination of finite element simulations incorporating a dislocation-based hardening VUMAT subroutine and experimental validation using XRD line profile analysis. Simulations were conducted for various thickness reductions (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 80%), while experimental measurements were performed for 20% and 40% reductions. The numerical results included distributions of dislocation density, grain size, equivalent plastic strain, and stress, providing a comprehensive view of the microstructural and mechanical changes induced by cold rolling. A close agreement between simulated and experimental dislocation density values confirmed the reliability of the proposed modeling approach and its capability to capture the underlying deformation mechanisms. At 80% reduction, the microstructure consists predominantly of ultrafine grains across the entire thickness, indicating extensive plastic deformation and pronounced strain hardening.As the reduction percentage increases from 10 to 80%, the plastic strain penetrates deeper into the material and intensifies significantly, reflecting the progressively increasing degree of deformation imposed on the aluminum sheet. As the reduction percentage increases from 10 to 80%, the plastic strain penetrates deeper into the material and intensifies significantly, reflecting the progressively increasing degree of deformation imposed on the aluminum sheet. The maximum equivalent plastic strain rises from approximately 1.5 at 10% reduction to about 7.28 at 80%, representing a substantial increase of nearly 385%. Higher strain reductions lead to higher dislocation densities, with 80% reduction standing out due to its delayed rise and higher final dislocation density. However, the overall dislocation behavior across time shows a typical rapid accumulation followed by a stabilization phase for all strain reductions.

For 20% and 40% reductions, the simulated maximum dislocation densities (6.18 × 1014 m-2 and 6.88 × 1014 m-2, respectively) closely match the experimental XRD measurements (5.98 × 1014 m-2 and 7.4 × 1014 m-2), demonstrating the high accuracy of the numerical model.

For 20% and 40% reductions, the simulated maximum dislocation densities (6.18 × 1014 m-2 and 6.88 × 1014 m-2, respectively) closely match the experimental XRD measurements (5.98 × 1014 m-2 and 7.4 × 1014 m-2), demonstrating the high accuracy of the numerical model.

This strong agreement between simulation and experiment validates the dislocation-based hardening model implemented in the VUMAT subroutine and confirms its capability to predict microstructural evolution under different rolling reductions.

Furthermore, the techno-economic analysis indicates that employing Al1050 for lithium-ion battery components, combined with numerically optimized cold rolling, results in a 10-year net present value increase of approximately USD 2.2–2.5 million relatives to conventionally processed Al1060 and Al3003.
