Comparison of intra-articular steroid injection and pericapsular nerve group block for pain control and functional outcomes in patients undergoing manipulation under anaesthesia for frozen shoulder – A randomised controlled trial
Authors: Sangeeta Dhanger, Pratheeba Natrajan, Bhavani Vaidiyanathan, Agalya Kandavelu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aims:Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA) for frozen shoulder is usually done under intra-articular steroid injection along with local anaesthetic agents. The pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block is also found to be effective for MUA. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of the PENG block with that of intra-articular steroid injections in patients with frozen shoulder.Methods:This randomised controlled trial was conducted on 130 patients aged 18–75 years with stage 2 or stage 3 frozen shoulder scheduled for manipulation under anaesthesia. The participants were randomly assigned into two groups: Group S (intra-articular steroid injection) and Group P (PENG block). Group S received ultrasound-guided intra-articular injection of 40 mg triamcinolone with 2 mL of 2% lignocaine and 2 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine. Group P received ultrasound-guided PENG block using 10 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine and 10 mL of 2% lignocaine. Numerical pain rating scale (NPRS) scores for pain were recorded at 15 min; 30 min; and 1, 6, 12, and 24 hours. Range of Motion (ROM) and Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) were also assessed. Data were analysed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences 19.0, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:NPRS pain scores were significantly lower in the PENG group across all time points (P < 0.001). At 15 minutes, the mean NRPS scores were 5.2 [95% confidence interval (CI) 4.8–5.6] in Group S and 4.1 (95% CI 3.5–4.7) in Group P. ROM and SPADI scores at 24 hours were significantly better in the PENG group. ROM improved across all planes in both groups; however, improvement was significantly greater in the PENG block group.Conclusion:PENG block is effective in providing pain relief and improving shoulder function following MUA in patients with frozen shoulder.

INTRODUCTION

Frozen shoulder is a clinical syndrome which causes functional restriction of the motion of the shoulder characterised by glenohumeral joint discomfort and stiffness.[1] It primarily affects middle-aged women, especially in the non-dominant arm, and is mostly associated with systemic conditions including diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease, with the prevalence rate of 2–5% in the general population.[2]

Numerous methods are available to manage the frozen shoulder, but a single, formally accepted management method is still lacking.[34] A frequently employed interventional approach is manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA), but the outcome is highly dependent on effective analgesia.[5] Intra-articular corticosteroid injections are frequently used as a conservative treatment for frozen shoulder because of the anti-inflammatory properties of corticosteroids.[67] Despite being commonly used, there is conflicting evidence about their efficiency as compared to local anaesthetics.[7]

The pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block is gaining interest in shoulder surgeries as it provides focused analgesia without causing severe motor blockade. The PENG block can be an acceptable substitute for intra-articular injections for MUA of frozen shoulder as it offers good pain relief and facilitates early mobilisation by anaesthetising the articular branches of the nerves feeding the shoulder capsule.[8]

The purpose of this study was to compare the efficacy of the PENG block with intra-articular steroid injections in patients with frozen shoulder scheduled for MUA. The primary objective of our study was to compare the pain relief between the two techniques using numerical pain rating scale (NPRS). Our secondary objectives were to compare the Range of Motion (ROM) and Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) scores[9] before and after MUA and to compare the duration of analgesia provided by both techniques.

METHODS

This randomised controlled trial was conducted after receiving approval from the institutional ethics committee (IEC-42/IGMC and RI/PP-11/2024) and was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2025/07/090431) before starting the enrolment. The study was conducted in a tertiary care centre between August 2025 and December 2025 and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and good clinical practice guidelines and complied with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting standards. After obtaining written informed consent, 130 individuals aged 18 to 75 years, with stage 2 or stage 3 frozen shoulder, and who were scheduled for MUA in the operation theatre were included. Patients who had undergone shoulder surgery or trauma, had neurological or other musculoskeletal conditions that affected shoulder function, were pregnant or nursing, or had contraindications to regional anaesthetic or steroid use (such as infections, allergies, or coagulopathy) were excluded from the study. Participants were randomly allocated into either Group S (intra-articular steroid injection) as the control group or Group P (PENG block) as the intervention group using a computer-generated random number sequence. Allocation concealment was done using the sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelope (SNOSE) technique [Figure 1].

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) chart

A thorough pre-operative assessment was done, and ROM, functional assessment using the SPADI score, and pain assessment using the NPRS were recorded. On the day of procedure, pulse oximeters for peripheral oxygen saturation, non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiogram, and end-tidal carbon dioxide monitors were attached, and baseline parameters were recorded. Using appropriate aseptic measures, an anaesthesiologist with 5 years of experience in ultrasound-guided interventions performed all procedures using a linear probe transducer (frequency 8–12 MHz) of M-Turbo® ultrasound machine (Sonosite Inc., Bothell, WA, USA).

In group S, the patient was positioned in the lateral position with the affected shoulder up. Intra-articular steroid injection was performed under ultrasound guidance, and 40 mg of triamcinolone combined with 2 mL of 2% lignocaine and 2 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine (a total volume of 5 ml) was injected into the shoulder joint’s pericapsular region [Figure 2a-c].

(a-c): Intraarticular steroid injection on left shoulder; (d-f): Left shoulder pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block. LA: Local anaesthetic

In group P, the patient was positioned in the supine position. PENG block was performed under ultrasound guidance focusing on the articular branches that feed the shoulder joint. Ten millilitres of 0.5% bupivacaine and 10 mL of 2% lignocaine were injected [Figure 2d-f].

After the block, intravenous (IV) midazolam 0.05 mg/kg and fentanyl 2 μg/kg were administered for sedation and shoulder manipulation was done under monitored anaesthesia care. Post-procedure NPRS, ROM, and SPADI scores were recorded at 15 min, 30 min, 1 hour, 6 hours, 12 hours, and 24 hours intervals. ROM was assessed using goniometry in flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and internal and external rotation; SPADI was assessed as a separate patient-reported disability index.

Post-procedure, both groups received a standardised post-procedural analgesic regimen consisting of tablet paracetamol 500 mg every 8 hours. Injection tramadol 50 mg intramuscular was administered as rescue analgesia if the NPRS score exceeded 6. All rescue analgesic consumption was recorded at each assessment time point. The total duration of analgesia [time from intervention completion to first rescue analgesia dose (or first NPRS score ≥4)] was also recorded for both groups.

The primary outcome variable, the post-manipulation pain score (NPRS at 24 hours), was used for calculating the sample size. A between-group difference of 1.5 points on the NPRS was considered clinically significant based on the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for NPRS in post-operative and musculoskeletal pain settings.[10] The required number of samples, 58 in each group, was estimated using the following formula, assuming a standard deviation of 2.0, a two-sided α of 0.05, and a power of 80% (β = 0.20): n = 2(Z1−α/2 + Z1−β) 2σ2Δ2n = Δ22(Z1−α/2 + Z1−β) 2σ2. We decided to recruit 65 patients per group, for a total of 130 participants, in order to allow for potential dropouts and protocol deviations (10%).

The Chi-square test was applied for categorical variables including gender distribution and comorbidities (diabetes mellitus and hypertension) between the two independent groups, which is the standard and appropriate approach. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons of NPRS scores at all time points since these are ordinal data expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR), making a non-parametric approach justified. For within-group repeated measures of SPADI scores across three time points (baseline, 15 minutes, and 24 hours), the Friedman test was chosen as the non-parametric equivalent of repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Subsequently, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was correctly applied as a post-hoc pairwise comparison within each group across the three SPADI time points. The Mann–Whitney U test was also appropriately used for between-group SPADI comparisons at each individual time point. All statistical analyses were performed using statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 (International Business Machines Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 130 patients scheduled for MUA were assessed for eligibility, and 127 were included in the study [Figure 1]. Baseline demographic characteristics, including age, weight, height, and duration of symptoms, were comparable between the two groups (P > 0.05). There was no significant difference in the distribution of comorbidities (diabetes mellitus and hypertension) between groups (P = 0.827). The study population showed a female predominance, with 72 out of 127 participants (56.7%) being female, which is consistent with the known epidemiology of frozen shoulder. No complication or procedure-related adverse events (e.g. haematoma, nerve injury) were reported in either group during the 24-hour observation period [Table 1].

Group P demonstrated significantly superior ROM recovery compared to Group S at both 15 minutes and 24 hours post-intervention, across all five shoulder movement planes, while being comparable at baseline [Figure 3b].

Baseline SPADI scores were comparable between the groups (Group P: median 95.4, IQR 93.2–97.8; Group S: median 95.0, IQR 92.6–97.3; (P = 0.491), confirming the absence of significant pre-procedural functional differences [Table 2]. Both groups demonstrated statistically significant improvement in SPADI scores across all three measurement time points (Friedman test: Group P χ2 =130.00, P < 0.001; Group S χ2 =126.00, P < 0.001). Within each group, pairwise comparisons confirmed significant improvement at every successive time point: baseline to 15 minutes, baseline to 24 hours, and 15 minutes to 24 hours (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001 for all comparisons in both groups) [Table 3].

At 15 minutes post-MUA, no significant between-group difference in SPADI scores was observed (Group P: median 48.1, IQR 39.2–55.0; Group S: median 46.7, IQR 44.0–50.2; P = 0.548) as shown in Table 2. At 24 hours, Group P demonstrated nominally but significantly lower SPADI scores compared to Group S (Group P: median 28.2, IQR 19.3–33.2; Group S: median 30.1, IQR 28.1–32.5; Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.022). Both groups achieved clinically meaningful SPADI reductions from baseline (Group P: mean reduction 68.36 ± 8.22 points, 71.7%; Group S: mean reduction 64.71 ± 4.29 points, 68.1%). The duration of the manipulation procedure did not differ significantly between the two groups (Group S: 12.97 ± 1.88 min vs. Group P: 13.11 ± 1.84 min; P = 0.656).

DISCUSSION

Frozen shoulder treatment is primarily intended to diminish pain and restore shoulder movement. Various interventions such as physiotherapy, ultrasound-guided hydro-dissection, subacromial injection, arthroscopic capsule release, and open surgery have been described. MUA is one of the most effective and commonly performed interventions.[1112] Nevertheless, effective peri-procedural analgesia is critical for optimising MUA outcomes in adhesive capsulitis.

The primary objective of this study was to compare both the techniques with respect to pain relief using NPRS scores. In the study, the NPRS scores were consistently and significantly lower in the PENG group across all six time points in 24 hours of MUA (P < 0.001). Both interventions showed clinically significant improvements in pain, ROM, and shoulder disability scores. Though intra-articular corticosteroid injections have been used because of their anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects since a long time, they do not provide immediate relief.[13] In contrast, the PENG block, which was originally described for hip analgesia,[14] has gained interest in shoulder procedures due to its ability to selectively anaesthetise the articular branches of the axillary, suprascapular, and lateral pectoral nerves, which supply the glenohumeral joint capsule.[15] This selective sensory blockade provides immediate effective pain relief without compromising motor function, an essential consideration for early mobilisation following MUA.[16]

The drugs and doses for injection in the present study were selected on the basis of established clinical practice and prior feasibility data for each technique. The Group S participants received total 5 ml (2 mL of 2% lignocaine, 2 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine, and 1 ml of 40 mg of triamcinolone acetonide) with accepted intra-articular injection volumes to avoid capsular overdistension.[7] Group P participants received 10 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine combined with 10 mL of 2% lignocaine (a total volume of 20 mL). Nonetheless, a large volume of drug is recommended for pericapsular nerve block techniques to ensure adequate spread around the targeted articular nerve branches.[10] The total drug volumes differed between the groups; however, this reflects the intrinsic anatomical and technical requirements of each technique.[17]

A significant improvement in ROM across all five planes of shoulder movement at both 15 minutes and 24 hours post-MUA was observed in Group P in comparison with Group S. This difference is possibly because intra-articular injections primarily address inflammatory pain rather than nociceptive input from capsular stretch during manipulation.[1819]

Functional outcomes, measured by the SPADI score, improved significantly in both groups in the present study, with the PENG group showing a slightly greater reduction at 24 hours. This suggests that improved immediate analgesia may translate to early functional recovery. Rigorous mobilisation after hydrodilatation is a treatment for patients suffering from a frozen shoulder.[20] PENG block is primarily an analgesic adjunct and does not directly alter capsular mechanics; therefore, it is best conceptualised as complementary to hydrodilatation or manipulation rather than a competing therapeutic modality.[21] Current literature offers substantive evidence for the effect of hydrodilatation on ROM. There are no high-quality randomised controlled trials that directly compare PENG block as a stand-alone therapy versus hydrodilatation.[2223] The encouraging outcomes observed in our study can lay the groundwork for future well-designed, multicentric randomised controlled trials that will further validate the efficacy of the PENG block and establish its role as a standard peri-procedural analgesic strategy in the management of frozen shoulder. We recommend combined strategies which include motor-sparing regional analgesia such as PENG block, which permit comfortable hydrodilatation or manipulation and structured physiotherapy after MUA. Future designed randomised controlled trials are required to evaluate the additive analgesic benefit, pulmonary safety, and durability of functional outcomes of such combined approaches.

The present study has certain limitations that must be acknowledged. First, due to the fundamentally different nature of the two interventions, an intra-articular injection versus a pericapsular nerve block, complete blinding of the operating anaesthesiologist was not feasible; the techniques differ substantially in patient positioning, procedural approach, and injectate volume. Second, the study was conducted at a single centre with a follow-up period limited to 24 hours, which restricts the ability to evaluate the long-term outcomes such as sustained functional improvement or recurrence of stiffness. Third, the larger injectate volume in the PENG group (20 mL vs 5 mL) introduces a potential volume-related hydrodistension effect that cannot be entirely separated from the nerve block effect itself, and future studies should consider volume-matched control arms to more precisely isolate the contribution of neural blockade from that of capsular distension. Fourth, gender-stratified subgroup analyses were not performed, which is a limitation given that frozen shoulder disproportionately affects middle-aged women and differences in pain perception. Finally, while both groups received a standardised post-procedural analgesic regimen, the study was not primarily powered to detect differences in rescue analgesic consumption, and this outcome should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. Future well-designed multicentric randomised controlled trials incorporating blinded outcome assessment, extended follow-up, volume-matched control arms, gender-stratified analyses, rehabilitation outcome data, and possibly a placebo or combination arm would further clarify the long-term role of the PENG block in the comprehensive management of adhesive capsulitis.

CONCLUSION

PENG block is effective in providing pain relief and improving shoulder function following MUA in patients with frozen shoulder as it produces superior pain control and greater improvement in ROM, with no significant adverse events.
