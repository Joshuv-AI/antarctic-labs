Introduction

Fish gills have developed diverse structures and mechanisms for functioning in aquatic ecosystems. Although designed primarily to exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide with the surrounding water, in addition to their respiratory role the gills are also critically necessary for a range of functions including excretion of nitrogenous wastes, osmoregulation, and protection from pathogens and noxious substances in a highly variable physical environment1. Exposure to different concentrations of dissolved gases, temperature, pH, and contaminants requires an efficient adaptive response that involves several highly specialized gill cell types such as mucous cells, and mitochondrion-rich chloride cells, which provide protection for the epithelial surface and maintenance of ionic balance, respectively. This adaptability is particularly well demonstrated in Crucian carp (Carassius carassius), where ‘reversible gill re-modelling’ has been recognized as a response to changing environmental conditions such as fluctuations in oxygen concentration and temperature2. In these carp, it has been theorized that the gills adapt to hypoxic conditions by increasing the absorptive surface area of the lamella. Alternatively, adverse conditions such as exposure to environmental pollutants can also lead to decreasing gill surface area, via proliferation of pavement cells and increased mucus secretion3, 4. Such changes can be viewed as compensatory mechanisms for preserving the integrity of the fragile lamella tissue; however, overcompensation can also result in respiratory compromise. In addition, it should be recognised that although the basic organisation of teleost gills is consistent among fishes, there may be variations in gross and microscopic structure that are species specific. For example, the cellular proportion of chloride cells varies among freshwater, estuarine, and marine species, and the gills and gill rakers in fishes such as menhaden (Brevoortia spp.) have special structural adaptations for filter feeding5. Unlike the gills, the pseudobranch is not actually considered a respiratory organ per se, as this structure does not typically have direct contact with the external environment. Derived embryologically from the first gill arch, it is thought to have a role in providing oxygenated blood to ocular structures via a counter current system.

Necropsy and Trimming

The gills seem particularly prone to the creation of artifacts subsequent to sub-optimal collection and preservation procedures. For example, lesions such as gill edema and epithelial necrosis may be induced at initial specimen collection if fish are allowed to reside in small static unfiltered containers for extended periods of time (e.g., 30 minutes or more) prior to euthanasia6, or if freshwater fish are exposed to unbuffered acidic euthanasia solutions such as tricaine methanesulfonate; such changes then may be incorrectly attributed to experimental treatment. Similarly, alternative methods of euthanasia, such as submersion in icewater or blows to the head, may induce confounding artifacts such as lamella telangiectasis and vascular congestion. Initial placement of gill specimens in fixatives other than neutral buffered formalin, such as modified Davidson’s solution (see Species Introduction for recommended procedure) is strongly recommended for gill studies, to avoid causing artifacts such as lamella epithelial lifting. Further steps to enhance gill preservation can include special efforts to lavage the gills with fixative, and/or removal of opercula if the entire fish, or their heads, are fixed whole. Although pathological findings may affect all regions of the gill, it is more usual for these to affect individual gill arches or parts thereof. It is therefore recommended that several gill arches from both sides of the head are taken for microscopic evaluation.

Histopathological evaluation of the gill is critically dependent on the orientation of the specimen, since this must include sufficient lamella profiles in the appropriate plane for examination. For small species or juveniles, fish may be fixed whole and the gills will be sectioned in situ, either transversely across the head or longitudinally. For studies in which the assessment of gill changes is considered to be a key component, excision of the gills is recommended as opposed to in situ evaluation in whole body specimens. Gills examined in situ are invariably sectioned along a plane that is oblique to the longitudinal axis of most filaments, which can create artifactual distortion of gill morphology and cellularity. Additionally, if processing is restricted to the cartilaginous filaments, avoiding bony areas of the arches in larger fish, then other artifacts potentially associated with whole body sectioning and acid decalcification procedures may be avoided.

Morphology, Physiology, Nomenclature, Diagnostic Criteria and Differential Diagnosis

1. Gills

Teleost gills are located within paired branchial cavities beneath protective opercular coverings on each side of the buccal cavity. The gills arise embryologically from paired pouches that line the pharynx, from which an additional rudimentary filament becomes the pseudobranch (see below). The bilateral gills are composed of two sets of four arches. Each gill arch is formed by two rows of hemibranchs separated by an interbranchial septum, which together comprise the holobranch7. It should be noted that some fish species lack an interbranchial septum, while in others such as salmonids, the septum may occur some distance from the base of the primary lamella. In the microscopic findings in this publication, the terms filament and lamella are primarily used as locators or modifiers, rather than primary or secondary lamella. The number of gill filaments varies between arches and increases during fish growth8. Gill rakers are anteriomedial projections of the gill arch which protrude into the water flow from the buccal cavity. These structures function to filter particulates, protect the respiratory epithelia, and in some species assist in guiding food items to the esophagus. The rakers are composed of acellular or mixed bone and comprise the hypobranchial, epibranchial and the ceratobranchial bone; the last, which is the largest, extends along most of the ventral portion of the arch7. The rakers are covered by connective tissue, a submucosa that contains small non-respiratory blood vessels, and a mucosal epithelium which may incorporate sensory structures (taste buds), each of which is comprised of a neuromast and associated innervation. In some fishes such as striped bass (Morone saxitilis), pharyngeal teeth may be present9.

The gill filament provides support to the respiratory surface without impeding the flow of oxygenated water across the surface of the gill. Support is achieved via a cartilaginous core that extends from the gill arch to the distal portion of the filament. Refinements, such as the transverse spines present in trout, provide additional rigidity to the filament7. The associated musculature consists of striated adductor muscles that attach the hemibranchs to a smooth muscle band in the interbranchial septum, while a second striated muscle (abductor) connects the cartilage rod in the primary lamella and the gill arch. This system operates to increase efficiency of respiration by allowing the filaments to become erect, thus maximising the surface area available for gas exchange.

Lamella and filament epithelia are comprised of three main cell types: pavement cells, chloride cells, and mucous cells. Pavement cells are the continuous single layer of squamous epithelial cells that line the outer surfaces of filaments and lamella. The apical membrane of these cells is covered with a series of microridges which provide increased surface area for respiration as well as a mechanism for retention of mucous10. A negative aspect of microridges is that they also provide sites for viral and bacterial attachment prior to entry into the host11, 12. Irritation to this fine layer in the form of repeated trauma, chronic infestation with ciliate parasites or cytotoxicity frequently leads to pavement cell hyperplasia, which if severe, can result in respiratory compromise13, 14.

Chloride cells are predominantly distributed at the base of the lamella, but they can also be found in more distal regions. Their function involves systemic osmoregulation via the secretion of sodium chloride. They have a specialised role in the gill lamella epithelium and are sometimes referred to as ionocytes or ‘mitochondria-rich cells’15, 16. This role requires significant amounts of energy, hence the presence of large numbers of mitochondria in the cytoplasm that impart an eosinophilic coloration compared to adjacent cells. They are also characterized by extensive invagination of the basolateral membrane (BM) which forms a labyrinth throughout the cytoplasm. The BM is in close proximity to mitochondria, and is the location of the Na+-K+ ATPase required for the sodium ion transfer that allows salt secretion17, 18. The distinctive eosinophilia of the cytoplasm and the basal location of the nucleus within the cell provides a useful aid for the identification of chloride cells in H&E stained sections. There are immunohistochemistry markers (e.g. ATPase) available that can stain chloride cells for definitive identification19.

Goblet mucous cells are typically sparse in the healthy gills of most teleost species, but they may proliferate rapidly in response to mechanical or chemical irritation. Consequently, the presence of abundant mucous cells is usually considered a pathologic response that should be recorded in toxicologic assessments. Filaments also contain populations of cells associated with cellular and humoral immunity (phagocytes, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and eosinophilic granular cells), which commonly increase in number following infection or toxic insult. In addition, intraepithelial accumulations of lymphoid cells at the base of gill lamella in fish such as Atlantic salmon are thought to have a role in immune surveillance20. Dendritic cells have been observed in gill tissues12, 21, and their functional association with antigen recognition and uptake was more recently confirmed22. Other cell types occasionally present in the gills include neuroepithelial cells, rodlet cells, and neuromasts (taste buds)15. Various changes in mucous cells have been described in the literature. Although decreased mucous cells has been reported, they may appear less numerous due to degranulation or lamella epithelial hyperplasia. Occasionally, measurements of gill mucous cell size appear in published accounts of toxicology studies; however, there is little evidence to suggest that relative changes in mucous cell size alone (i.e., independent of mucous cell number) represent reliable toxicologic effects.

The vascular network of the respiratory gill consists of arteries and arterioles for gas exchange purposes, referred to as the arterioarterial or respiratory route12, and an arteriovenous route for providing nutrition to the gill arch, filaments and lamella7. The major site of respiratory gas exchange with the environment is the central lamella capillary. Lamella capillaries are constructed of endothelial cells, including specialized endothelial cells referred to as pillar cells. Pillar cells are located periodically along the internal walls of capillaries and span the lumen between opposite capillary walls. They therefore function to maintain the structural integrity of these narrow vessels by preventing vascular collapse or dilation. Damage to pillar cells can result in focal aneurysmal dilation of lamella (lamella telangiectasis)23. This may result from targeted or incidental destruction of pillar cells, or the rupture of these cells caused by pressure forces. There is increasing use of fish embryos in research, and it is important to recognise that during embryonic and early larval stages the gills are still developing, and fish are able to obtain sufficient oxygen directly through diffusion across their skin. The more familiar features described above fully form in more mature life stages.

Basic gill functions include respiration, nitrogenous waste excretion, osmoregulation, and acid-base balance. Unlike mammals, the gills are actually the primary site of nitrogenous waste elimination24. Consequently, impairment of gill function can have extensive systemic consequences beyond even hypoxemia and respiratory acidosis. Respiratory efficiency in gills is maximized because the direction of lamella blood flow is opposite to that of water movement, resulting in a counter current system that provides for oxygen extraction of up to 80%25. Ventilation is controlled by proprioreceptors within the body as well as mechanoreceptors which respond to water flow. Chemoreceptors in the gill regulate gill ventilation and heart rate increase. The receptor site is in the pseudobranch which receives blood from the first gill arch and is sensitive to changes in hydrostatic pressure, pH and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2). Other adaptations of gills to normoxic and hypoxic environments have been studied extensively25.

Gill – Non-Proliferative

Non-proliferative lesions in the gill are commonly encountered in fish used in toxicologic studies. The sensitive respiratory epithelium is in direct contact with the surrounding water and is therefore susceptible to toxic chemicals, water quality issues (e.g., excess sediment), as well as a variety of pathogens. As such, most pathological changes encountered involve the epithelium and specialised cells forming part of the epithelium (i.e. mucous and chloride cells).

Other Terms

• Lamella synechia

Pathogenesis

• Biochemical changes to the lamella epithelial surface or patent epithelial necrosis causes adjacent lamella to become “sticky” and adhere to one another focally

• Common with certain types of ectoparasitic infections (e.g. trichodinid ciliates), bacterial gill disease, and certain toxic exposures26

Diagnostic Features

• Focal or multifocal attachments of neighboring lamellae

• Attachments may be proximal or distal

• Distal lamellar adhesions may result in the formation of cyst-like spaces that entrap pathogens, such as bacterial, oomycete, fungal or parasitic agents

• Excessive mucous production may also be present

• Unlike lamella fusion, cell proliferation is not required

Differential Diagnosis

• Fusion, lamella: Fusion requires cell proliferation, and typically occurs from proximal to distal along the length of the lamella

• Tissue preparation artifact: Gills that are poorly positioned, curled, or compressed in the tissue cassette may have lamella that lie smoothly against each other, mimicking adhesions. Gills with excessive surface mucus may be especially prone to this artifact. In true adhesions, the focal attachment sites create slight tension between the affected lamella, which results in angular distortion of the lamella

Comments

Lamellar adhesions are often confused with lamella fusion in the literature and careful review and adherence to the published diagnostic features is recommended.

• Stunted lamella

Pathogenesis

• Can be a consequence of lamella or filament damage caused by prior trauma or inflammatory disease

Other Terms

• Epithelial cell parasitism; A variety of more specific terms are used, often based on the nature of the agent(s) involved e.g. epitheliocystis associated with gamma-proteobacteria

Pathogenesis (Chlamydia-like organisms)

• Infections result in cellular hypertrophy due to proliferation of bacteria or proteobacteria in the cytoplasm of lamella epithelium

Diagnostic Features

• Presence of aggregates of punctate (approximately 1 micrometer diameter) organisms ranging from 5 μm to 1000 μm (1 mm) within the cytoplasm of the gill epithelial cell

• Reticulate and elementary bodies, 1 micrometer diameter, may appear as inclusions in the cell (containing DNA, proteins, ribosomes)

• May be associated with adhesion, necrosis, inflammation or hyperplasia of the gill

Differential Diagnosis

• Viral inclusion bodies, such as Koi herpes virus infections resulting in extensive gill necrosis.

• Microsporidean infections

• Myxozooan infections

• Lymphocystis (Iridovirus infection)

• Opisthokonta infections (e.g. Dermocystidium or other Icthyosporea)

Comments

Generally, the primary pathology process (e.g. necrosis or hyperplasia of the gill epithelium) should be recorded with an additional finding or finding comment of bacteria if present and narrative description in the pathology report, as needed.

Infections with Flavobacterium spp. also frequently infect the skin, imparting a characteristic ‘saddleback’ lesion with associated discoloration. These spread to affect the rest of the body surface resulting in severe disruption of osmoregulation and mortality in affected populations27. Other causative bacterial agents can include Flexibacteria spp and Cytophaga spp.

Confirmatory diagnosis is by culture of the bacterium on selective media and growth of characteristic yellow colonies, biochemical tests and DNA sequencing. See comment under hyperplasia, necrosis and inflammation for additional information about common associated pathology findings.

For intracellular agents, experience, including consultation with publication authors, is critical to differentiation, but use of special stains such as PAS or silver stains, in conjunction with molecular or ultrastructural pathology methods can be used to aid in diagnosis. These agents are uncommon in toxicology studies using laboratory reared fish but wild caught or commercial aquaculture species may have these findings.

Comments

Edema is often accompanied by other changes indicative of noxious agent exposure, such as lamella synechia, lamella epithelial degeneration, necrosis, and/or hyperplasia. Observation of proteinaceous material is helpful in differentiating the pathology finding of edema from the artifactually induced epithelial lifting (Fig. 11.16). Although most often a perimortem artifact6, 28, epithelial lifting is frequently misinterpreted in the literature as a toxicological change. Careful re-examination of untreated control fish will often reveal a similar degree of epithelial lifting in the gills of those animals. It is recommended that epithelial lifting should generally not be recorded as a finding in toxicological studies.

Fungus, gill

Other Terms

• Branchiomycosis; Microsporidiosis, Microsporean infection

Branchiomyces spp

Pathogenesis

• Infections with Branchiomyces spp. can have a rapid onset and unless treated at the early stage of infection can prove fatal. Breach of epithelial surfaces leads to compromised osmoregulation, impaired respiration, lethargy, and death

Diagnostic Features

• Diagnosis is based on histology, intravascular non septating branching hyphae with intrahyphal round basophilic bodies, 4 mm in diameter, with a granulated cytoplasm and a basophilic wall (aplanospores)

• Depending on involved Branchiomyces species, these hyphae penetrate the vessel wall and grow into the surrounding supportive tissue, leading to necrosis, hemorrhage and (granulomatous) inflammation

• Fungal stages can be highlighted by the use of silver stains which demonstrate the hyphal walls

Differential Diagnosis

• Discriminated from infections with oomycetes which are characterized by the presence of filamentous non-septate mycelium

Microsporidia organisms

Pathogenesis

• Infections are caused by the spore form of Microsporidia; the spore is quite resistant to disinfection or removal procedures. The coil filament of the spore injects the parasitic material into the host cell where it then rapidly proliferates

Diagnostic Features

• Intracellular 1–2 μm diameter, oval, smooth organisms with a marginated nucleus and vacuole (parabasal vacuole)

• Generally numerous organisms in each cell

• Microsporidiosis caused by a variety of parasites (e.g. Loma sp.) are also visible by light microscopy using Gram’s or Luna stain29

• The intracellular organisms are acid fast

• Some species can be identified by the use of polarized light upon microscopic evaluation

• Hypertrophy of the infected epithelial cell is common and maybe striking (i.e. xenoma cell)

Differential Diagnosis

• Myxozoa infection – extracellular cyst-like structure

• Ichthyophthirius – 5,000–1,000 μm wide ciliated protozoan organism with a horseshoe-shaped, large macronucleus

• Bacterial infection with Chlamydia-like organisms (i.e. Epitheliocystis disease) – accumulation of finely granular organisms (1 μm diameter)

Comments

Branchiomyces infections are generally only observed in wild-caught fish populations (e.g. largemouth bass) and is very uncommon in hatcheries or laboratory reared animals.

Microsporidia in the gill is common in wild-caught or aquaculture-reared fish but is uncommon in laboratory reared fish. Macroscopic presentation is generally a white cyst or white spot. Microsporidiosis caused by a variety of parasites (e.g. Loma sp.) are also visible by light microscopy using Gram’s or Luna stain29.

Hypertrophy, epithelium, lamella, gill

Other Terms

• Epithelial cell hypertrophy

• Pavement cell hypertrophy

Pathogenesis

• Increased synthesis of structural proteins and intracellular vacuolation associated with swelling of organelles including smooth endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria and cytoplasmic vesicles

• May be observed in response to toxic agent exposures or suboptimal water quality conditions (e.g., elevated ammonia)

Diagnostic Features

• Apparent thickening of the epithelium caused by an increase in cell size

• May be focal or diffuse

• Often associated with pale cytoplasmic eosinophilia and/or vacuolation in respiratory epithelial cells

• Mucous cells may be increased but are often unaffected

• Chloride cells, specialized gill epithelial cells, may be hypertrophic

Differential Diagnosis

• Should be distinguished from cellular hyperplasia which is an increase in the number of cells (see below)

• Some intracellular infections with bacteria, fungi or parasites (e.g. microsporidial and chlamydial) cause cellular hypertrophy but in these cases the agents will be visible by light microscopy and use of Gram staining and Giemsa can highlight their presence

Comments

Lamellar epithelial hypertrophy and hyperplasia often co-occur and the combined term maybe used in toxicity studies as hyperplasia/hypertrophy, epithelia, lamella.

Infiltrate, [insert predominant cell type], gill

Other Terms

• None

Modifiers

• Mononuclear cell; granulocytic; mixed; increased rodlet cells

Pathogenesis

• A wide variety of potential causes, including water quality issues, infectious diseases, and toxic exposures

Diagnostic Features

• Infiltration of gill tissues with inflammatory cells

• Inflammatory cell infiltrates are usually considered excessive when substantial numbers of cells extend into the interstitium and epithelium of the proximal filaments

Differential Diagnosis

• Normal mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue: In especially the proximal third of the gill filament, low to moderate numbers of resident lymphocytes and eosinophilic granular cells may occur independent of disease processes, and exist as part of the innate local immune system

Comments

Minimal to mild inflammatory cell infiltrates are relatively common in captive-reared fish used in toxicology studies. There can be significant variation in the relative abundance of eosinophilic granular cells in the gills, depending on the fish species. Carp species particularly have abundant populations of these granular cells, most predominantly in the underlying connective tissues but also to a lesser extent in the epithelium. Such cells are often considered functionally similar to mammalian mast cells, and therefore their presence does not always indicate inflammation. Fish to fish variation may also be significant.

Oomycete hyphae, gill

Other Terms

• Water mold, Winterkill (catfish) Saprolegniasis, Saprolegnia species

Pathogenesis

• Infections with Saprolegnia spp. can have a rapid onset and unless treated at the early stage of infection can prove fatal. Breach of epithelial surfaces leads to compromised osmoregulation, impaired respiration, lethargy, and death

• Although oomycetes can be primary pathogens, they are more frequently secondary invaders

Diagnostic Features

• Tufts of thin white threadlike hyphae (reminiscent of cotton wool) adherent to body surfaces, including the gills

• Microscopic diagnosis is based on observing extensive necrotic changes affecting the gill tissues associated with the presence of filamentous non-septate mycelia, and depending on the stage of development, the presence of multicellular zoosporangia

• Oomycete stages can be highlighted by the use of silver stains which demonstrate the hyphal walls. Host inflammatory reaction is usually minimal to absent, reflecting the rapid onset of the disease

Differential Diagnosis

• Discriminated from infections with true fungi which usually have septate hyphae and which may be dematiaceous (i.e., have brown or yellow coloration, e.g. Exophiala spp.)

• Post-mortem invasion of abraded tissue or wounds by oomycete organisms may mimic in vivo infection

Comments

Highly prevalent predominantly in freshwater environments. Optimized husbandry and biosecurity procedures may prevent spread of infection. Suppression of T-cell immunity in winter may increase susceptibility to opportunistic infection, such as with Saprolegnia hyphae.

Parasite, gill

(Figs. 11.18, 11.21, 11.22, 11.23, and 11.24)

Other Terms

• Ciliates (white spot or ‘Ich’, trichodinids, Capriniana), amoebae, monogeneans, flagellates, coccidians, ichthyosporeans, myxozoa

Pathogenesis

• Dependent on the agent(s) involved. White spot caused by Ichthyophthirius multifiliis is a significant pathogen affecting farmed fish and captive species in freshwater. The parasite grows immediately beneath the epithelium, and at maturity ruptures the epithelium to exit the host. The epidermis is breached, and epidermal cells become necrotic. In the gills, there may be a hyperplastic response that reduces respiratory capacity. Inflammation and edema may be present to a limited degree

• Amoebae may appear in section as relatively pale staining and vacuolated cells with a mildly basophilic nucleus and in some species a large 2 µm cytoplasmic inclusion which is an intracellular kinetoplast symbiont (Perkinsela sp.), or they may present as basophilic organisms at the proliferative edge of the epithelium that resemble exfoliated epithelial cells (Fig. 11.24Fig. 11.24.Goldfish. Telangiectasia, lamella, gill

(Figs. 11.25 and 11.26Fig. 11.25.Atlantic salmon. Gill. Telangiectasia (arrow). H&E, 200×. Image courtesy of Jeffrey Wolf.)

Atlantic salmon. Gill. Telangiectasia (arrow). H&E, 200×. Image courtesy of Jeffrey Wolf.

Other Terms

• Lamella aneurysm

Pathogenesis

• Dilatation of lamellar capillaries and rupture of pillar cells, with intravascular accumulation of red blood cells. Can occur artifactually during collection of fish or euthanasia. Chilling of fish in ice (one method of sedation prior to sacrifice) can cause this, among other handling procedures

Diagnostic Features

• Dilatation of lamellar capillaries

• Rupture of pillar cells

• Intravascular accumulation of red blood cells

• Most frequently seen the apex of the lamella but can occur along its length

• Thrombosis may be observed in more chronic lesions

Differential Diagnosis

• Lamellar thrombus: Lesion contains fibrin and nuclear debris

Comments

Although telangiectasia could potentially occur as a result of toxic or infectious damage to pillar endothelial cells, it is also a common perimortem artifact that is often seen to a low degree in control fish28. Telangiectasia can be viewed as a continuum with thrombus formation if the fish survives for a period of time. If thrombosis is present, generally recording as such is recommended, but the pathologist may elect to either record both findings under one or the other terminology or to use a “slash” term (telangiectasia/thrombus).

Telangiectasia is frequently over-reported as a treatment effect in toxicology studies and care should be taken to account for tissue orientation in the block and on the slide. If pillar cells are regularly arranged in the section, this would suggest artifactual folding of the filament or lamella. The concurrent presence of lamellar thrombi suggests that at least some degree of telangiectasia was present prior to sacrifice.

(Figs. 11.22 and 11.29)

Modifiers/Qualifiers

• Filament, lamella

Other Terms

• Fusion of primary lamella

Pathogenesis

• Moderate to severe lamella epithelial cell proliferation results in fusion of not only lamella, but adjacent gill filaments

• Causes are the same as for lamellar epithelial hyperplasia

Diagnostic Features

• May affect only two adjacent filaments or may be more widespread

Differential Diagnosis

• Tissue sectioning artifacts: Superimposition of adjacent lamella sectioned obliquely may resemble fused filaments

Fusion, lamella, gill

(Figs. 11.21, 11.22, 11.23, 11.24, 11.29, and 11.30)

Other Terms

• Fusion of secondary lamella

Pathogenesis

• Involves proliferation of pavement cells (lamellar epithelial hyperplasia) to the degree that it partially to completely fills in interlamellar spaces

• Causes are the same as for lamellar epithelial hyperplasia.

• Response to chronic irritation that begins at the interlamellar sulci and extends distally

Diagnostic Features

• Epithelial cells in the interlamellar sulcus proliferate to partially or completely fill the space between secondary lamella

• Fusion is not generally diagnosed until at least 75% of the interlamellar space is filled

• Can be focal (e.g., limited to two or three adjacent lamella), segmental, or diffuse

• Extensive hyperplasia of the epithelium can include squamous and goblet cell metaplasia, such that the epithelium resembles epidermis/skin

Differential Diagnosis

• Lamellar adhesion: Attachment of adjacent lamella that does not require cell proliferation

• Hyperplasia, lamella: Proliferation of the epithelium occupying less than approximately 75% of the interlamella space

• Plane-of-section artifact: Oblique or tangential sections through the filament can give the false impression of lamellar fusion, but in such cases the lamella usually appear shortened. Need to examine areas of the gill in which full-length lamellae are visible

Comments

Gill re-modelling involving moderate to substantial fusion of the secondary lamellae is associated with normal physiological response in carp to seasonal environmental cues2. Lamellar fusion and lamellar epithelial hyperplasia are not mutually exclusive terms, but rather fusion may be added as an additional descriptive term to convey the end stage of the hyperplastic finding.

Hyperplasia, chloride cell, gill

(Fig. 11.30)

Other Terms

• Increased chloride cells or ionocyte

Pathogenesis

Physiological adaptation in fish to maintain ion regulation in fish in marine and freshwater environments. Under conditions where this may be compromised there may be an increase in chloride cell numbers

Diagnostic Features

• In H&E-stained sections, chloride cells are typically plump individualized eosinophilic cells that are present in small numbers along filaments within interlamella sulci (e.g., 1–3 cells within each sulcus)

• When increased, chloride cells may pile up in interlamella sulci and may also be found along lamella, especially proximally

Differential Diagnosis

• Mucous cell hyperplasia: the cytoplasm of mucous cells is often gray-blue and heterogeneous in texture

• Lamellar epithelial hyperplasia: the hyperplastic pavement cells are generally less plump and less pink

• Plane-of-section artifact: oblique sectioned gills can create the perception of increased chloride cells

Comments

It may be difficult to discriminate between physiological adaptation with increased numbers and pathological hyperplasia of chloride cells.

Hyperplasia, epithelium, lamella, gill

(Figs. 11.20, 11.23, 11.29, and 11.30)

Other Terms

• Epithelial cell hyperplasia

• Proliferative branchitis

Pathogenesis

• Increased replication of lamellar epithelial cells occurs rapidly following exposure to almost any noxious agent or irritant and is also rapidly reversible following withdrawal of the stimulus. Lamellar epithelial hyperplasia appears to be an adaptive response designed to protect the fragile gill apparatus from permanent damage, although the trade-off may involve a temporary, albeit potentially fatal, loss of respiratory capacity

Diagnostic Features

• Apparent thickening of the epithelium caused by increase in cell number. May be focal or diffuse

• Low grade hyperplasia tends to first fill in the proximal areas of interlamellar sulci; as this change progresses, more of the interlamellar space becomes filled

• May or may not be accompanied by increases in other lamellar cell types, such as mucous and chloride cells, and inflammatory cell infiltrates

• May be accompanied by low grade necrosis of individual epithelial cells

• In moderate to severe cases may also see fusion of adjacent filaments and/or apparent loss of lamellar capillaries, with or without lamellar thrombosis

• In severe cases, the lamellar epithelial hyperplasia may be accompanied by squamous epithelial metaplasia

Differential Diagnosis

• Should be distinguished from cellular hypertrophy which is an increase in the size of cells (see above)

• Certain parasites (e.g., gill amoeba, Ichthyobodo sp.) may mimic exfoliating epithelial cells on the surface of hyperplastic gill epithelium

• The gill of the marine flatfish, dab (Limanda limanda) infected with Xcellia lamelliphila may appear hypertrophied due to the large numbers of parasites pervading the gill epithelium32

• Squamous cell carcinoma: can resemble severe hyperplasia, but usually creates a mass-like lesion

• Artifact due to tangential or oblique sections of gill or a rostral orientation of the gill; these may cause the normal epithelium to appear artifactually hyperplastic (or inflamed)

Comments

A common finding as a response of exposure to irritants, including chemicals and pathogens, particularly protists including ciliates. As a very general rule, toxic exposures and poor water quality are usually associated with diffuse hyperplasia, whereas infectious agents more often cause focal or nodular lesions. However, diffuse hyperplasia with lamella fusion can be the end stage result of long-term continuous exposure to almost any noxious agent.

Hyperplasia, mucous cell, gill

(Fig. 11.30)

Other Terms

• Increased mucous cells

• Goblet cell hyperplasia

Pathogenesis

• In most fish species, normal healthy gills contain few mucous cells. Mucous cell hyperplasia occurs as an adaptive response following chronic physical or chemical irritation

• It is unknown whether increased mucous cells arise from dedicated stem cells, pavement cells that have undergone metaplastic change, or both

Diagnostic Features

• Increased numbers of goblet mucous cells line the lamellar and interlamellar sulci along filaments

• Mucus within these cells is usually pale grey-blue in H&E sections but may be pale pink in some species. The pH of mucous glycoproteins may vary as determined by PAS and Alcian blue histologic staining33

• In moderate to severe cases, may additionally see thick strands of mucus adjacent to lamellae, or located perpendicularly to the tips of lamellae and/or filaments

• May also see increased mucous cells lining the branchial cavity and within the epidermis

Differential Diagnosis

• Chloride cell hyperplasia: these pale eosinophilic cells have homogeneous cytoplasm, unlike mucous cells

Comments

Increased gill mucous cells is usually a non-specific finding.

Parasitic pseudotumor, gill

Other Terms

• X-cell pseudotumor

• X-cell infection

• X-cell xenoma

Pathogenesis

• Parasitic infection historically mistaken for neoplasia

Diagnostic Features

• Mass-like lesion comprised of large uniform polygonal cells with finely vacuolated cytoplasm, and prominent nuclei and nucleoli

• Lesions observed in the gills, pseudobranch, skin, and/or fins of certain marine fishes (e.g., bottom-dwelling fish such as cod, flatfish, catfish, and salmonids)

• In the gill, can cause lamellar fusion resulting in potential respiratory compromise

• Fibrous connective tissue can surround X-cells in these lesions

Differential Diagnosis

• Chloride cell hyperplasia: these pale eosinophilic cells have homogeneous cytoplasm, unlike mucous cells

• Lymphocystis viral infection: infected cells are much larger and have granular cytoplasmic inclusions that displace the host fibrocyte nucleus

Comments

Typically observed as an incidental finding and not a cause of mortality. Recently, molecular analysis has identified X-cells as phylogenetically divergent parasitic organisms related to perkinsids (Perkinsus) and dinoflagellates32.

Gill – Neoplastic

There are several neoplastic benign and malignant gill lesions described in the literature, including branchioblastoma, chondroma, chondrosarcoma, osteoma, osteosarcoma, squamous cell carcinoma, hemangioma and hemangiosarcoma, most of which have been induced by experimental carcinogenesis studies34, 35. Spontaneous occurrence has rarely been reported, with cases of branchioblastoma being the most frequent36, 37. Gill tumors are commonly observed with carcinogen exposure due to their large surface area.

Pathogenesis

• This benign neoplasm arises from the epithelium of the pseudobranch lamellae

Diagnostic Features

• Non-encapsulated tumor composed of embryologic blast-type cells with strong basophilia

• Fibrous component can be present as well as polygonal/cuboidal epithelial cells which may form rudimentary lamella

• Cells resembling mucous cells and cartilage may also be present

Differential Diagnosis

• Granulomatous lesions associated with bacterial (e.g. mycobacteria), fungal (e.g. Branchiomyces), and parasitic infections (e.g. microsporidians and myxozoans)

Comments

Although these are rare tumors overall, they are more often reported in the gills versus the pseudobranch.
