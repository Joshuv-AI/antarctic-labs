Neuraminidase-Associated Vaginal Dysbiosis Correlates with HPV Infection Across Oncogenic Risk Subtypes
Authors: Jiali Wang, Li Bai, Ning Leng, Yong Yang, Xia Cao, Wenhong Peng, Jingxuan Xu, Erfu Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a major cause of cervical cancer, and increasing evidence suggests that cervicovaginal microbial dysbiosis may influence HPV susceptibility. This study evaluated the association between microbial enzymatic markers and HPV infection across different risk subtypes.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted in 43,426 women undergoing HPV screening. HPV status and subtype classification (high-, possible-, and low-risk) were determined using standardized genotyping. Clinical and laboratory parameters including age, white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), vaginal pH, leukocyte esterase, fungal infection, Trichomonas vaginalis, and microbial enzymatic markers (neuraminidase [NEU], N-acetylglucosaminidase [NAG], β-glucuronidase [GUS], and H2O2), were analysed.ResultsHPV positivity was identified in 20.6% of participants. Neuraminidase emerged as the most consistent independent predictor across all HPV categories (adjusted OR range: 1.437–1.828; p<0.001), indicating a strong association between anaerobic bacterial activity and HPV infection. Age was uniformly associated with increased HPV risk across all models (adjusted OR ≈ 1.014–1.018 per year; p < 0.001). Fungal infection demonstrated a significant protective association (adjusted OR range: 0.636–0.778). Leukocyte esterase and WBC showed selective associations, particularly with high-risk HPV. In contrast, vaginal pH, T. vaginalis, and NAG lost significance after adjustment, suggesting confounding effects. Model explanatory power was modest (pseudo-R2: 0.010–0.016).DiscussionNeuraminidase-associated vaginal dysbiosis is strongly associated with HPV infection across multiple risk subtypes. These findings highlight the potential role of microbial functional biomarkers in understanding cervicovaginal dysbiosis and HPV susceptibility, particularly in populations vulnerable to microbial imbalance and secondary infections.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus infection is the most common sexually transmitted viral infection globally.1 It is also the primary etiological agent underlying cervical cancer, which accounts for approximately 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually worldwide. Approximately 90% of cervical cancer deaths occur in low and middle-income countries.2,3 Despite the high prevalence of HPV exposure, only a subset of infections persists and progress to malignancy, highlighting the importance of host, viral, and microenvironmental determinants in modulating infection dynamics and disease progression.4,5 Emerging evidence indicates that the cervicovaginal microenvironment plays a critical role in HPV acquisition, persistence, and clearance.6 A Lactobacillus-dominant vaginal microbiome is generally associated with mucosal homeostasis and protection, whereas dysbiotic states characterized by increased anaerobic bacterial diversity have been linked to enhanced HPV susceptibility and persistence.7–9 These dysbiotic microbial communities can alter mucosal immunity, disrupt epithelial barrier integrity, and promote a pro-inflammatory milieu conducive to viral infection.10 Beyond compositional alterations, functional microbial activity particularly enzymatic dysbiosis has emerged as an important but underexplored component of the cervicovaginal niche. Enzymes such as neuraminidase, produced by anaerobic bacteria including Gardnerella vaginalis and Prevotella species, degrade sialic acid residues on mucins, thereby compromising the protective mucus barrier and facilitating pathogen adherence and epithelial access.11,12 Similarly, enzymes such as β-glucuronidase and N-acetylglucosaminidase are indicative of microbial metabolic activity and may reflect alterations in microbial ecology and host–microbe interactions.13 Dysbiotic enzymatic profiles may also influence colonization by opportunistic or drug-resistant bacterial and fungal species, posing heightened risks in immunocompromised individuals. However, large-scale clinical evidence linking these functional enzymatic markers to HPV infection across different oncogenic risk subtypes remains limited. Most previous studies have focused either on microbiome composition using sequencing-based approaches or on specific clinical correlates of HPV infection, often in relatively small cohorts.14 Few investigations have integrated routine clinical laboratory parameters with functional microbial biomarkers in a large population, and even fewer have systematically compared these associations across HPV risk stratifications (high-, possible-, and low-risk subtypes). Such stratified analyses are essential, as different HPV subtypes exhibit distinct biological behaviours, immune evasion mechanisms, and oncogenic potential.15

Leveraging large-scale clinical datasets with integrated enzymatic, microbiological, and host parameters provides a unique opportunity to identify reproducible predictors of HPV infection across oncogenic risk subtypes. In particular, functional microbial markers such as neuraminidase (NEU), β-glucuronidase (GUS), and N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG), alongside inflammatory indicators like leukocyte esterase (LE) and white blood cell count (WBC), may reflect vaginal dysbiosis associated with HPV susceptibility. While previous studies have largely focused on microbiome composition or isolated clinical correlates, few have systematically examined these markers in combination within a large population based cohort.7 Understanding these associations may be especially relevant in immunocompromised populations, where microbial imbalance can increase susceptibility to secondary bacterial or fungal colonization and potentially contribute to antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate cervicovaginal clinical, microbiological, and enzymatic determinants associated with HPV infection and to identify independent correlates across high-, possible-, and low-risk HPV subtypes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using electronic medical records obtained from the record Department of Clinical Laboratory, Danyang Maternal and Child Health Hospital, Jiangsu Province, China. A total of 43,426 women (only chinses Population) who underwent routine cervicovaginal screening between January 2020 and December 2025 were included in the present study. Participants were included if they had valid HPV genotyping results and at least one recorded clinical or laboratory parameter relevant to cervicovaginal health assessment. Participants were excluded if they had missing or invalid HPV test results, duplicate records, or completely missing clinical and laboratory data relevant to the study variables. Due to variable missingness across laboratory parameters, the effective sample size differed between analyses. For multivariable regression modelling, a complete-case analysis approach was applied to ensure consistency across included predictors.

HPV Detection and Risk Classification

HPV infection status was determined using standardized molecular diagnostic assays based on nucleic acid amplification techniques. Cervicovaginal samples were collected using sterile swabs and subjected to genomic DNA extraction following the manufacturer’s protocol. HPV genotyping was performed using a validated polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assay with type-specific detection, targeting conserved regions of the L1 gene. Amplified products were subsequently identified using genotype-specific probes through multiplex detection platforms (hybridization-based methods), enabling simultaneous identification of 25 HPV genotypes. Detailed information regarding L1 gene primer sequences and PCR amplification conditions is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S1) to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility. The detected HPV genotypes were categorized according to their established oncogenic potential based on International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classification criteria. High-risk HPV (HR-HPV) types included HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 68, 73, and 82. Possible-risk HPV types included HPV26, 53, and 66, while low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) types included HPV6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 81, and 83.2,16 The primary outcome variable was defined as overall HPV positivity (presence of any HPV genotype). Secondary outcomes included subtype-specific binary classifications comprising high-risk HPV infection, possible-risk HPV infection, and low-risk HPV infection, enabling stratified analyses according to oncogenic risk. Cases involving co-infection with multiple HPV genotypes were classified according to all detected oncogenic risk categories. Accordingly, participants harboring multiple HPV genotypes could be included in more than one subtype-specific HPV risk group analysis. Overall HPV positivity was defined as the presence of at least one detectable HPV genotype irrespective of co-infection status.

Integrated Profiling of Clinical and Cervicovaginal Biomarkers

A total of 13 clinical and laboratory variables were evaluated for their association with HPV infection. Demographic and haematological parameters comprised age (years), recorded at the time of examination, and peripheral blood indices including white blood cell count (WBC, ×109/L) and red blood cell count (RBC, ×1012/L), measured using an automated haematology analyser according to standard laboratory protocols of our hospital. Indicators of the cervicovaginal microenvironment included vaginal pH, measured using calibrated pH indicator strips (range 3.8–5.5), and vaginal cleanliness score, assessed microscopically and graded on a four-point ordinal scale (Grade I–IV) based on epithelial cells, lactobacilli, leukocyte density, and background flora. In the analytical framework, age, WBC, RBC, and vaginal pH were treated as continuous variables, whereas cleanliness was treated as an ordinal variable but excluded from multivariable regression due to high collinearity with WBC (r = 0.91).

Microbiological and enzymatic variables included leukocyte esterase (LE), fungal infection, Trichomonas vaginalis, bacterial vaginosis (BV), neuraminidase (NEU), N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG), β-glucuronidase (GUS), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), all of which were subsequently evaluated in regression models. LE and H2O2 were assessed using reagent strip-based semi-quantitative assays and recorded as binary variables (positive vs negative). Fungal infection and T. vaginalis were identified by microscopic examination (saline/KOH wet mount) and recorded as present or absent. BV was diagnosed using standard clinical criteria (Nugent scoring system); however, it was excluded from multivariable regression analysis due to complete missingness of vaginal pH values among BV-positive cases, rendering the variable non-estimable in the complete-case dataset. Enzymatic activities, including NEU, NAG, and GUS, were measured using standardized biochemical assay kits and categorized as binary variables based on manufacturer-defined thresholds. Within the regression framework, all microbiological and enzymatic variables were treated as binary predictors, enabling estimation of odds ratios for HPV infection. Variable coding and inclusion criteria were maintained consistently across univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses to ensure comparability and robustness of effect estimates.

HPV Risk Group–Specific Modelling

To assess whether associations between predictors and HPV infection varied according to oncogenic risk, four separate multivariable logistic regression models were constructed using identical sets of predictors but different binary outcomes: (i) any HPV infection, (ii) high-risk HPV infection, (iii) possible-risk infection, and (iv) low-risk HPV infection. All models were fitted using the same complete-case dataset (N = 21,008), ensuring direct comparability of effect estimates across outcome categories without variation in sample composition. Missing data were addressed using a complete-case analysis approach. Although this resulted in a reduced sample size for multivariable modelling, it ensured internal consistency across included predictors and avoided biases introduced by variable-specific sample differences. Given the large overall sample size, the impact of missingness on statistical power and model stability was considered minimal.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the association between clinical, microbiological, and enzymatic variables and HPV infection status. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data processing and visualization were performed using standard R packages, while logistic regression analyses were implemented using generalized linear modelling frameworks with L-BFGS optimization algorithms to ensure computational stability for large-scale datasets. Baseline characteristics were compared between HPV-positive and HPV-negative groups. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons across HPV risk categories (high-risk, possible risk, and low-risk), non-parametric methods were applied due to non-normality of several variables. Specifically, the Kruskal–Walli’s test was used for overall group comparisons of continuous variables, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the crude associations between each predictor and HPV infection status (binary outcome: HPV positive vs negative). Each model included a single predictor variable, generating unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). These models reflect marginal associations without adjustment for potential confounders. Due to variable-specific missing data, each univariable model was fitted using complete cases available for that specific predictor-outcome pair, resulting in varying sample sizes across analyses.

To identify independent predictors of HPV infection, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed by simultaneously including all retained variables. The model was fitted using a complete-case dataset (N = 21,008) to ensure consistency across predictors. Two variables were excluded based on methodological considerations: vaginal cleanliness was removed due to strong collinearity with WBC (Pearson correlation coefficient r = 0.91), which could lead to unstable coefficient estimates, and bacterial vaginosis (BV) was excluded because all BV-positive cases had missing vaginal pH values, rendering the variable non-estimable in the complete-case dataset. All logistic regression models were estimated using the L-BFGS optimisation algorithm to ensure computational stability for large-scale data. Model performance and goodness-of-fit were assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and McFadden’s pseudo-R2. Adjusted and unadjusted odds ratios were reported with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. No correction for multiple testing was applied in the regression analyses, as all variables were pre-specified based on clinical relevance rather than exploratory screening.

Ethical Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Danyang Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No.: [0026/2023]). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All data were fully anonymized prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality.

Results

Study Population and HPV Prevalence

During the study period, a total of 43,426 women who underwent routine cervicovaginal screening were included in the analysis (Figure 1). The overall prevalence of HPV infection was n= 8,958 (20.6%), while n=34,468 (79.4%) of individuals were HPV-negative (Figure 2A). The age of participants ranged from 17-to-86 years (mean ± SD: 41.4 ± 10.29; median [IQR]: 41 [33–49]). Stratification by oncogenic risk group demonstrated that high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) was the most prevalent category (n = 7,090; 16.3%), followed by low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) (n = 1,824; 4.2%) and possible-risk HPV (n = 1,445; 3.3%) (Figure 2B). Detailed genotype-specific analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in subtype prevalence. Among high-risk HPV types, the most prevalent subtype was HPV52 (n = 1,936; 4.46%), followed by HPV16 (n = 1,122; 2.58%), HPV58 (n = 1,052; 2.42%), and HPV39 (n = 637; 1.47%). Other notable high-risk subtypes included HPV51 (n = 572; 1.32%), HPV56 (n = 709; 1.63%), HPV68 (n = 596; 1.37%), HPV33 (n = 540; 1.24%), and HPV18 (n = 362; 0.83%). Less prevalent high-risk types included HPV35 (n = 388; 0.89%), HPV45 (n = 108; 0.25%), HPV59 (n = 408; 0.94%), HPV73 (n = 70; 0.16%), HPV82 (n = 73; 0.17%), and HPV26 (n = 36; 0.08%) (Figure 2C). Among possible risk types, HPV53 (n = 1,028; 2.37%) was the most common, followed by HPV66 (n = 422; 0.97%) and HPV26 (n = 36; 0.08%). Among low-risk HPV types, the most prevalent subtype was HPV81 (n = 850; 1.96%), followed by HPV42 (n = 316; 2.01%), HPV43 (n = 183; 1.16%), HPV6 (n = 295; 0.68%), HPV11 (n = 204; 0.47%), HPV40 (n = 108; 0.69%), and HPV83 (n = 21; 0.13%). High-risk HPV subtypes accounted for the majority of infections, with HPV52, HPV16, and HPV58 emerging as the dominant genotypes in this population. Cervicovaginal Microenvironment and Clinical Features Stratified by HPV Status

Significant differences in clinical and laboratory characteristics were observed between HPV-negative (HPV−) and HPV-positive (HPV+) individuals. Baseline demographic and cervicovaginal characteristics of the study population according to HPV status are presented in Table 1. Among demographic and hematological variables, age (years) was significantly higher in HPV-positive individuals (mean: 43.05 vs 41.00 years, p = 2.956 × 10−51). Similarly, white blood cell count (WBC, ×109/L) was elevated in the HPV-positive group (mean: 14.18 vs 12.98 ×109/L, p = 1.869 × 10−11), indicating an increased inflammatory response. In contrast, red blood cell count (RBC, ×1012/L) did not differ significantly between groups (mean: 1.86 vs 1.88 ×1012/L, p = 0.868). Regarding cervicovaginal microenvironment parameters, vaginal pH (unitless) was significantly higher in HPV-positive individuals (mean: 4.92 vs 4.89, p = 5.574 × 10−7). Independent Clinical and Microenvironmental Correlates of HPV Infection

Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between clinical, microbiological, and enzymatic variables and HPV infection. In univariable analysis (Figure 4A), several factors showed significant associations with HPV positivity. Neuraminidase (NEU) exhibited the strongest association (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.42–1.69, p = 2.46 × 10−23). Other significant positive associations included Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) (OR = 1.43, p = 0.016), vaginal pH (OR = 1.23, p = 6.78 × 10−7), leukocyte esterase (LE) (OR = 1.20, p = 1.87 × 10−10), N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG) (OR = 1.18, p = 1.68 × 10−5), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (OR = 1.15, p = 3.47 × 10−6), cleanliness score (OR = 1.11, p = 2.56 × 10−11), and age (OR = 1.02, p = 2.10 × 10−62). White blood cell count (WBC) showed a modest but significant association (OR = 1.01, p = 3.31 × 10−12). In contrast, fungal infection demonstrated a protective association (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.63–0.82, p = 3.53 × 10−7). RBC, GUS, and BV were not significantly associated. In the multivariable model (Figure 4B), which adjusts for confounding and includes all retained predictors (N = 21,008), several associations remained robust. Neuraminidase (NEU) persisted as the strongest independent predictor (aOR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.39–1.71, p = 1.39 × 10−16). Age (aOR = 1.02 per year, p = 8.08 × 10−20) and WBC (aOR = 1.01, p = 4.89 × 10−4) also remained independently associated with increased odds of HPV infection. Leukocyte esterase (LE) retained significance (aOR = 1.16, p = 0.006), indicating a role of local inflammation (Figure 4). Conversely, fungal infection remained significantly protective (aOR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.63–0.86, p = 7.66 × 10−5). β-glucuronidase (GUS) became significantly protective after adjustment (aOR = 0.89, p = 0.003), suggesting a suppression effect. In contrast, variables such as vaginal pH, TV, NAG, and H2O2 lost statistical significance after adjustment, indicating that their univariable associations were largely confounded by other covariates. These findings demonstrate that neuraminidase activity, age, inflammatory markers, and fungal status represent the most robust independent correlates of HPV infection, while several apparent associations in univariable analysis are explained by confounding. Cervicovaginal Biomarker Profiles Across HPV Risk Groups

Clinical and microbiological parameters were compared across four groups: HPV-negative (HPV), low-risk HPV, possible risk, and high-risk HPV. Among demographic variables, age differed significantly across groups (Kruskal–Wallis p = 9.24 × 10−50), with mean values of 41.00 years (HPV), 42.40 (low-risk), 43.17 (possible risk), and 43.12 (high-risk), indicating a progressive increase with HPV risk category. Similarly, WBC showed significant differences (p = 9.24 × 10−12), with higher values observed in HPV-positive groups (12.98, 13.44, 13.59, and 14.35 ×109/L, respectively). In contrast, RBC did not differ significantly across groups (p = 0.840). For cervicovaginal microenvironment parameters, vaginal pH differed significantly (p = 3.94 × 10−6), although mean differences were modest (4.89, 4.89, 4.94, and 4.93). Vaginal cleanliness scores also varied significantly (p = 6.33 × 10−10), with increasing values observed across HPV risk categories (2.15, 2.23, 2.22, and 2.26, respectively).

Microbiological markers demonstrated distinct patterns across groups. Leukocyte esterase (LE) positivity increased progressively (0.578, 0.645, 0.625, 0.618; p = 1.13 × 10−8), indicating enhanced inflammatory activity with HPV risk. In contrast, fungal infection showed a decreasing trend across groups (0.066, 0.045, 0.035, 0.051; p = 2.51 × 10−6), consistent with its inverse association with HPV infection. Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) showed no statistically significant difference across groups (p = 0.115), and bacterial vaginosis (BV) also did not differ significantly (p= 0.484) (Figure 5). Enzymatic biomarkers revealed strong group-specific differences. Neuraminidase (NEU) was significantly elevated across HPV risk groups (0.090, 0.164, 0.134, 0.128; p = 2.05 × 10−23), with the highest levels observed in HPV-positive groups. The progressive increase in age and neuraminidase positivity across HPV oncogenic risk groups suggests that age-related alterations in the cervicovaginal microenvironment may contribute to microbial dysbiosis associated with HPV persistence. N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG) also differed significantly (0.150, 0.216, 0.142, 0.171; p = 4.32 × 10−7), showing variability across risk categories. Similarly, β-glucuronidase (GUS) demonstrated significant differences (0.150, 0.216, 0.142, 0.171; p = 4.32 × 10−7), with higher values in HPV-positive groups. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels also varied significantly (0.649, 0.714, 0.688, 0.676; p = 1.16 × 10−5). These findings demonstrate that age, inflammatory markers, and key enzymatic indicators particularly neuraminidase show consistent variation across HPV risk groups, whereas RBC, T. vaginalis, and BV do not exhibit significant differences. Discussion

In this large-scale cross-sectional analysis of 43,426 women, we demonstrate that the cervicovaginal microenvironment particularly neuraminidase-associated vaginal dysbiosis is strongly and consistently associated with HPV infection across all oncogenic risk categories. Among the evaluated biomarkers, neuraminidase (NEU) emerged as the most robust and reproducible correlate associated with HPV infection in both overall and risk-stratified multivariable models, with effect sizes remaining stable after adjustment for demographic, inflammatory, and microbiological covariates. These findings highlight a potential role for vaginal microbial enzymatic activity in shaping susceptibility to HPV infection, extending beyond individual pathogen presence to broader ecological dysfunction.

A key strength of this study lies in the consistency of neuraminidase associations across all HPV risk groups, including high-risk, possible risk, and low-risk subtypes. The magnitude of association was particularly pronounced in possible-risk HPV (aOR ≈ 1.83), but remained highly significant in all models, suggesting that neuraminidase reflects a general permissive state of the vaginal microenvironment rather than a subtype-specific effect. This finding suggests that neuraminidase activity may reflect a permissive dysbiotic cervicovaginal microenvironment rather than subtype-specific viral effects. Biologically, neuraminidase is produced by anaerobic bacteria such as Gardnerella vaginalis, Prevotella spp., and Mobiluncus spp., which are hallmarks of vaginal dysbiosis and bacterial vaginosis-like states. These enzymes degrade sialic acid residues on mucins, compromising the structural integrity of the cervical mucus barrier and exposing epithelial receptors to viral attachment. This mechanism has been supported by experimental and clinical studies demonstrating that disruption of the mucosal barrier facilitates HPV entry and persistence.14,17 Our findings align with prior microbiome-based studies showing that a Lactobacillus-depleted, anaerobe-dominant vaginal environment is associated with increased HPV acquisition and persistence.6,18,19 Although previous sequencing-based studies were generally conducted in substantially smaller cohorts, the consistency of the present findings across a large clinical population supports the biological relevance of cervicovaginal dysbiosis in HPV-associated disease.20 Unlike sequencing-based microbiome studies, the present analysis utilized clinically accessible enzymatic biomarkers that may offer greater translational scalability in routine screening settings. From a clinical perspective, neuraminidase-associated dysbiosis may potentially serve as an adjunctive functional biomarker for identifying women with altered cervicovaginal microenvironments who may be at increased risk for persistent HPV infection and cervical neoplastic progression. This distinction is critical, as functional outputs of the microbiome may have more direct relevance to host–pathogen interactions than microbial identity alone.21 In addition to neuraminidase, age emerged as a consistent independent predictor across all HPV risk groups, with a modest but highly significant increase in odds (~1–2% per year). While HPV infection is often considered more prevalent in younger women, particularly shortly after sexual debut, our findings likely reflect cumulative exposure risk, cohort effects, and potential differences in immune clearance in this population. Moreover, age-related hormonal changes and alterations in vaginal microbiota composition may contribute to increased susceptibility in older individuals. These findings are consistent with epidemiological studies demonstrating a secondary peak of HPV prevalence in middle-aged and older women in certain populations.22 Importantly, the combined observation that both age and neuraminidase activity remained consistently associated across oncogenic subtypes suggests a potential interaction between age-related microenvironmental alterations and microbial dysbiosis in modulating HPV susceptibility and persistence.23 Markers of inflammation also played a role in HPV association. Leukocyte esterase (LE) and WBC counts were independently associated with HPV infection in the overall and high-risk models, suggesting that both local and systemic inflammatory responses are linked to HPV presence. This relationship is complex and likely bidirectional: inflammation may facilitate viral persistence by disrupting epithelial barriers, while HPV infection itself can induce local immune responses. Notably, WBC was selectively associated with high-risk HPV, which may reflect the greater immunogenicity and oncogenic potential of these subtypes.24

One of the most intriguing findings of this study is the consistent protective association of fungal infection across all models. This inverse relationship has been observed in some prior studies but remains poorly understood. One possible explanation is competitive exclusion within the vaginal microbiome, where fungal overgrowth limits the proliferation of bacterial species associated with dysbiosis and HPV susceptibility.25 Alternatively, symptomatic fungal infections may prompt earlier clinical consultation and treatment, indirectly reducing HPV detection rates. Another possibility is that antifungal-associated immune activation may create an environment less conducive to HPV persistence. While speculative, these hypotheses highlight the need for mechanistic studies exploring microbial interactions in the cervicovaginal niche. The role of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was more complex and appeared to be risk-group-specific. While traditionally considered a marker of Lactobacillus dominance and a healthy vaginal microbiome, H2O2 showed a significant positive association with possible risk and high-risk HPV in multivariable models. This counterintuitive finding may reflect limitations of the assay used, which detects presence rather than functional concentration, or residual confounding by unmeasured variables. Alternatively, it is possible that in this population, H2O2 positivity does not fully capture protective Lactobacillus activity, particularly in transitional microbiome states. Similar inconsistencies have been reported in prior studies, suggesting that H2O2 alone may be an insufficient proxy for vaginal health.26 Other variables, including vaginal pH, Trichomonas vaginalis, and N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG), demonstrated significant associations in univariable analyses but lost significance after adjustment, indicating that their effects are largely mediated by other factors such as age, inflammation, or broader microbial dysbiosis. This underscores the importance of multivariable modeling in distinguishing independent predictors from confounded associations.

Despite the large sample size and comprehensive clinical dataset, the overall explanatory power of the models was modest (pseudo-R2 ≈ 0.01–0.02), which is expected given that key determinants of HPV infection such as sexual behavior, vaccination status, and host genetic factors were not available. The variables analyzed here represent downstream correlates of infection rather than primary causal drivers, and should be interpreted accordingly. Nevertheless, the consistency and strength of neuraminidase associations suggest that the cervicovaginal microenvironment plays a meaningful role in modulating HPV susceptibility. This study has several important strengths. The large sample size provides substantial statistical power and allows for robust subgroup analyses across HPV risk categories. The use of standardized clinical laboratory markers enhances reproducibility and translational relevance. Additionally, the integration of enzymatic, microbiological, and hematological variables offers a comprehensive view of the cervicovaginal ecosystem. While the present study has several limitations, it also provides important opportunities for future advancement and translational application. The cross-sectional design captures associations at a single time point and therefore future longitudinal investigations will be valuable for clarifying temporal dynamics and potential causal relationships between cervicovaginal dysbiosis and HPV acquisition or persistence. In addition, incorporation of behavioural, immunological, and vaccination-related variables in future studies may further strengthen model performance and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of HPV susceptibility and host–microenvironment interactions. The use of clinically accessible enzymatic and microbiological markers in this large-scale cohort represents an important strength with potential scalability in routine clinical settings. Future integration of multi-omics approaches, including microbiome sequencing, metabolomics, and host immune profiling, may further enhance mechanistic understanding of HPV-associated cervicovaginal dysbiosis. Expanding analyses across multicenter and ethnically diverse populations would additionally improve generalizability and support external validation of the observed associations. Importantly, future studies involving high-risk immunocompromised populations, including women living with HIV, transplant recipients, and individuals with severe immune dysfunction, may provide further insight into how altered immune status influences cervicovaginal microbial ecology and HPV persistence. Furthermore, incorporation of HPV vaccination status, sexual behaviour, contraceptive use, smoking status, and antibiotic exposure into future analytical frameworks may help refine interpretation of cervicovaginal microenvironmental associations and improve risk stratification strategies. Collectively, these future directions provide a strong foundation for advancing microbiome-informed precision screening approaches and potential microbiota-targeted interventions for HPV prevention and cervical health management.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that alterations in the cervicovaginal microenvironment, particularly neuraminidase-associated vaginal dysbiosis, are consistently associated with HPV infection across different oncogenic risk subtypes. The findings suggest that functional microbial dysbiosis and inflammatory microenvironmental changes may contribute to HPV susceptibility and persistence beyond traditional clinical indicators alone. Rather than serving as a standalone predictive marker, neuraminidase activity may reflect broader disturbances in the vaginal microbial ecosystem associated with HPV positivity. These findings highlight the potential value of integrating functional microbial biomarkers with existing clinical and HPV screening approaches to improve understanding of cervicovaginal ecological changes associated with HPV infection. Future longitudinal, mechanistic, and multi-center studies incorporating microbiome sequencing, immunological profiling, and diverse high-risk populations will be important to clarify causal relationships and evaluate the translational utility of microbial functional markers in HPV-associated disease risk stratification and prevention strategies.
