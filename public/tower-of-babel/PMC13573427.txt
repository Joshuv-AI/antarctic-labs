Comparative assessment of heavy metal accumulation and associated ecological and health risks in citrus orchards under canal and domestic wastewater irrigation
Authors: Asma Ashfaq, Rimsha Bashir, Zafar Iqbal Khan, Ahlam Khalofah, Suad AL-Ruzayza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSargodha is popularly known as California of Pakistan because of its renowned production of superior quality citrus cultivars. However, the increasing reliance on domestic wastewater for irrigation in this region has raised serious concerns regarding the accumulation of hazardous metals in soil and edible fruit tissues. This may pose a significant risk to food safety and human health. In this study, a field-based comparative sampling strategy was adopted, where irrigation water, soil, and citrus fruit samples were obtained from orchards with canal water irrigation (CW-I) and domestic wastewater irrigation (DWW-II). Samples of water, soil, and fruits were analysed through atomic absorption spectrophotometry to evaluate the accumulation of selected heavy metals in irrigation water, soil, and edible parts of three citrus species grown under CW-I and DWW-II irrigation systems. Zn concentrations ranged from 13.31–51.67 mg L−1 in water, 44–71 mg kg−1 in soil, and 1.08–1.58 mg kg−1 in fruits. Cr levels varied between 1.86–11.66 mg L−1 in water, 9.67–22.70 mg kg−1 in soil, and 0.011–0.10 mg kg−1 in fruits. Ecological risk assessment indicated higher metal accumulation in citrus fruits irrigated with DWW-II compared to CW-I, as indicated by elevated contamination factor (CF) and enrichment factor (EF), with Zn consistently showing the lowest contribution. The pollution load index (PLI) values exceeded 1 under domestic wastewater irrigation (1.53–1.65), suggesting moderate pollution. The estimated daily intake (EDI) values were higher for all metals under domestic wastewater irrigation, particularly for Fe (0.122 mg kg−1day−1) and Zn (0.0604 mg kg−1day−1), yet remained below permissible limits. Health risk assessment revealed that THQ values for all studied metals remained within permissible limits, indicating no significant non-carcinogenic risk associated with the consumption of these citrus fruits. The findings demonstrate that domestic wastewater irrigation significantly increased metal concentrations in soil and fruits. These results highlight the importance of continuous monitoring and the implementation of sustainable irrigation practices to minimize long-term health risks associated with wastewater use in agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09351-7.Keywords: Heavy metals, Canal water, Domestic wastewater, Potential ecological risk, Citrus

Introduction

Citrus represents the leading fruit crop worldwide, both in terms of cultivation area and production volume among all fruit-bearing trees. It ranks as the third most widely traded agricultural commodity in the global market. The cultivation of citrus extends across more than 135 countries and regions [1]. Citrus is the genus of the subfamily Aurantioideae in the family Rutaceae. The Rutaceae constitutes a family of flowering plants comprising approximately 160 genera. Among these, the most economically significant are the members of the genus Citrus, which encompass several major fruit species, including sweet orange (Citrus sinensis), sweet lime (Citrus limetta), grapefruit (Citrus paradisi), lime (Citrus aurantifolia), mandarin (Citrus reticulta) and lemon (Citrus limon) [1, 2]. Citrus fruit belongs to the non climacteric group and can be used as a source of valuable macro- and micronutrients for human diets [3].

The main centers of citrus cultivation are in tropical and subtropical regions located between 35°N and 35°S latitude near the equator [4]. Among citrus-producing countries, Pakistan holds a significant position due to its favourable climate and extensive agricultural practices. Pakistan exports over 50% of its fresh Kinnow to five major importers, namely, Russia, Indonesia, Afghanistan, the Philippines, and the United Arab Emirates [5]. Punjab contributes nearly 95% of Pakistan’s total citrus production, with kinnow accounting for approximately 70% of the total citrus fruit output. The average citrus yield in Pakistan is approximately 11.6 tons per hectare [6]. Sargodha, popularly known as California in Pakistan, is renowned for its production of superior-quality citrus cultivars [7].

Pakistan possesses one of the world’s most extensive canal networks. Water from the Indus River, along with other sources, is collected and redirected for irrigation and hydropower generation quality. Irrigation water quality plays a critical role in determining the productivity and sustainability of agricultural systems, particularly in regions facing water scarcity. In arid and semiarid regions, wastewater is widely used for crop and vegetable irrigation due to limited water availability and its cost-effectiveness. Reusing wastewater for agricultural irrigation is a widespread practice globally, with approximately 20 million hectares irrigated in nearly 50 countries [8]. Wastewater is commonly utilized in developing countries such as Pakistan, where agriculture serves as the primary means of economic livelihood and freshwater resources are limited. It is noteworthy that only approximately 3% of the Earth’s total water supply consists of freshwater. Moreover, domestic wastewater originating from household activities such as laundry, bathing, and dishwashing is increasingly being used in North Africa as a water conservation strategy for irrigating crops [8].

The use of domestic wastewater for irrigation provides certain short-term agronomic benefits, as it is often enriched with organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients that can enhance soil fertility and reduce the need for chemical fertilizers. However, alongside these benefits, domestic wastewater frequently contains potentially toxic heavy metals such as cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), nickel (Ni), and chromium (Cr), which may accumulate in soils and crops over time [3].

The quality of irrigation water plays a crucial role in controlling the accumulation and mobility of metals in agricultural soils and crops. Continuous application of wastewater introduces elevated concentrations of trace metals, dissolved salts, and organic matter into the soil system, which can alter key physicochemical properties such as pH, cation exchange capacity, and organic matter content. These changes enhance metal solubility and bioavailability, facilitating their uptake by plant roots and subsequent translocation to edible plant parts, including fruits. In contrast, irrigation with relatively canal water generally results in lower metal accumulation due to reduced external inputs. Consequently, long-term wastewater irrigation can lead to the progressive buildup of heavy metals in soil‒plant systems, increasing the likelihood of their entry into the food chain and posing significant ecological and human health risks [6–9].

Heavy metal toxicity refers to the accumulation of metals that are naturally present in the Earth’s crust to levels exceeding safe limits, primarily due to anthropogenic activities [9, 10]. Heavy metal contamination in soils is increasing at an alarming rate as a consequence of rapid industrialization, posing a substantial threat not only to human health but also to ecological stability. Heavy metals indirectly influence soil enzymatic activities by modifying the structure and function of microbial communities responsible for enzyme synthesis [11].

Heavy metals naturally originate from sources such as rock weathering, biological activity, airborne soil particles, and volcanic eruptions. Anthropogenic causes include mining operations, wastewater discharge, industrial and agricultural practices, and vehicle and chimney emissions. Insecticides, textile printing, paint manufacturing, hair dyes, and polyvinyl chloride pipes are the primary sources of metals [12]. Among heavy metals, zinc (Zn) is an essential trace element that plays a multifaceted role in supporting macrophage chemotaxis and immune regulation by modulating leukocyte function, thereby enhancing the body’s resistance to infections. Furthermore, it serves as a vital cofactor for the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase, which facilitates the neutralization of reactive oxygen species, thereby reducing oxidative stress [9]. Iron (Fe) plays a crucial role in various enzymatic pathways involved in cell growth, mitochondrial electron transport, and the synthesis of genetic material. Unlike many other minerals, iron lacks a natural excretion mechanism from the body. The normal serum iron level ranges from 60 to 170 µg/dL [13].

Soil contamination by heavy metals constitutes a major environmental challenge with profound consequences for agricultural productivity, food security, and human well-being. The accumulation of these toxic elements in farming soils poses a serious threat to crop yield, food quality, and the long-term sustainability of agricultural systems [14]. Nonetheless, when such fruit are contaminated with hazardous metals, they might have several health impacts, including disruption in cognitive and behavioural function, weakened immune response and loss of important nutrients [15].

Therefore, the present study was conducted to comparatively assess concentration of metals in irrigation water, orchard soils and fruits of selected citrus species under canal water and domestic wastewater irrigation systems. The specific objectives were to: (i) determine the concentrations of selected heavy metals, (ii) evaluate ecological contamination using contamination factors, and (iii) assess potential human health risks associated with fruit consumption. It was hypothesized that domestic wastewater irrigation would increase heavy metal accumulation and associated risks relative to canal water irrigation, with differences among citrus species.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in two citrus-producing regions of District Sargodha, Punjab, Pakistan. The first sampling site (CW-I) was located at Chakiyan Phularwan (Tehsil Bhalwal), where Citrus orchards were irrigated exclusively with canal water. The second sampling site (DWW-II) was Chak No. 79 N.B. (Tehsil Sargodha), where citrus orchards were irrigated with domestic wastewater. Due to time and resource constraints, the study was restricted to two irrigation sites. These sites were selected because they represent major citrus-growing areas of Sargodha and supply substantial quantities of citrus fruits to local markets (Fig. 1).

Samples collection & sampling design

Fruit

The study was conducted as a field based comparative investigation using a hierarchical sampling framework to evaluate the effects of irrigation water quality on heavy metal accumulation in citrus fruits under semiarid conditions. Two irrigation sites were considered as the main experimental factor: The first irrigation site, comprising orchards irrigated with canal water and located in Tehsil Bhalwal, District Sargodha, was termed as CW-I, whereas the second irrigation site, comprising orchards irrigated with domestic wastewater and located in Tehsil Sargodha, District Sargodha, was referred to as DWW-II. Within each irrigation site, three geographically distinct locations were selected to represent spatial variability.

At each irrigation site, sampling was structured across three levels: location, orchards and trees. Specifically, three locations (L= 3) were selected per irrigation regime, within each location three orchards (O= 3) were included, and within each orchard three mature citrus trees (T= 3) were randomly selected. This nested structure resulted in a total of 27 independent biological replicates per irrigation regimes, mathematically expressed as n=L × N × O = 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 trees. Each tree was considered a true biological replicate because it represents an independent physiological unit with distinct root and metal accumulation capacity. Three commercially important citrus varieties, namely C. limetta, C. reticulata, and C. sinensis, were included in the study.

These fruits were manually harvested. Fruits were harvested at the edible ripening stage, approximately 6–8 months after fruit set depending on the citrus variety and local climatic conditions. Sampling was carried out during the typical harvesting season (December-January) when fruits reached full maturity. The maturity stage was determined based on surface morphological characteristics, including uniform peel colour development (yellow to orange), attainment of full fruit size, smooth and glossy peel texture, and slight softness indicating optimal ripeness and high juice content [7].

After harvesting, fruits were washed thoroughly with distilled water and peeled, and the edible fruit segments were separated. These separated segments were allowed to air dry for several days until complete drying was achieved, followed by oven drying at 72 °C for 48 h to ensure the removal of any residual moisture [16]. The fully dried samples were then finely ground to obtain a homogeneous powder and stored in a desiccator at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to wet acid digestion prior to metal determination of heavy metal concentrations.

Soil

Soil samples were collected from the root zone (0–25 cm depth) of mature citrus trees corresponding to those selected for fruit sampling. This soil layer was selected because it represents the active zone for nutrient uptake and metal accumulation in orchard soils [9]. After collection, the soil samples were air-dried at room temperature and then oven dried at 70 °C for 48 h using a hot air oven (Memmert UF110, Memmert GmbH, Germany) to achieve constant weight. The dried samples were finely ground and passed through a 2 mm stainless steel sieve (Endecotts Ltd., UK) to obtain a uniform particle size. The processed samples were stored in clean airtight polythene bags prior to heavy metal analysis.

Water

At each site, three separate irrigation water samples were collected directly from the water channel used for orchard irrigation. Canal water was collected from the irrigation channel in chakiyan Phularwan. Domestic wastewater samples were collected from the wastewater stream entering the orchard at Chak No.79 N.B. For domestic wastewater, samples were collected at three different time intervals (morning, afternoon, and evening) to account for temporal variations in wastewater composition. The collected samples were composited to obtain representative samples. Canal water samples were also collected during the irrigation period from canal outlets supplying the orchards. All water samples were collected in prewashed 500 mL plastic bottles filtered to eliminate suspended particles, stored in an ice box at 5 °C to minimize physical and chemical alterations, and then transported to the laboratory for metal analysis [9].

Sample preparation and digestion

For chemical digestion, 1 g of dried soil or fruit powder and 1 mL of irrigation water sample were treated with 10 mL of concentrated HNO3 and allowed to predigest overnight [17]. The samples were subsequently heated on a hot plate at 70 °C until substantial evaporation occurred. After cooling to ambient temperature, 5 mL of concentrated HClO4 (70%) was added, and the mixtures were reheated at 70 °C until the formation of dense white fumes, confirming complete digestion. The digests were cooled, filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper, transferred to 50 mL volumetric flasks, and diluted to volume with 0.1 N HNO3 [18].

Heavy metal analysis

The concentrations of Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb in the soil, water and fruit samples were quantified using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AA-6300, Shimadzu, Japan). Prior to experimental use, all glassware and laboratory equipment were thoroughly washed and dried to prevent contamination. Accurately measured quantities of the required materials were obtained using an analytical balance and subsequently dissolved in deionized water or an appropriate solvent. To facilitate complete dissolution, the mixtures were gently heated on a hot plate. The resulting solutions were then transferred into volumetric flasks to ensure accuracy. After initial mixing, deionized water was added slowly until the meniscus aligned precisely with the calibration mark on the flask. Proper homogenization was achieved by shaking the flasks, and the meniscus level was carefully inspected at eye level to confirm a final volume of 100 mL. The concentrations of heavy metals, including Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb, were determined using a series of standard solutions to enable accurate quantitative analysis. Analytical quality control

The analytical quality control procedures were strictly followed throughout the study. All glassware was acid-washed with 10% HNO₃ and rinsed three times with ultrapure deionized water prior to use. Reagent blanks and procedural blanks were prepared and analyzed with each batch of samples; blank signals were subtracted from all sample readings and remained below the limit of detection for all four metals. Calibration curves for Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb were constructed using aqueous standard solutions prepared from certified stock standards (1000 mg L−1, Merck, Germany) at five concentration levels. Calibration ranges were 0.05–2.0 mg L−1 for Zn and Pb, 0.10–5.0 mg L−1 for Cr, and 0.50–10.0 mg L−1 for Fe. All calibration curves yielded correlation coefficients (R2) of ≥ 0.999, confirming linearity across the working range. The limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were calculated as LOD = 3σ/m and LOQ = 10σ/m, where σ is the standard deviation of ten replicate blank measurements and m is the slope of the calibration curve. Calculated LOD values were 0.015, 0.030, 0.050, and 0.010 mg/L and LOQ values were 0.050, 0.100, 0.160, and 0.033 mg L−1 for Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb, respectively. All reported concentrations in water (Zn: 18.67–51.67 mg L−1; Cr: 1.86–11.66 mg L−1; Fe: 54.34–195 mg L−1; Pb: 1.75–5.00 mg L−1), soil (Zn: 49–71 mg kg−1; Cr: 9–12 mg kg−1; Fe: 206–300 mg kg−1; Pb: 0.91–3.44 mg kg−1), and citrus fruits (Zn: 1.13–1.50 mg kg−1; Cr: 0.04–0.10 mg kg−1; Fe: 1.96–3.13 mg kg−1; Pb: 0.015–0.03 mg kg−1) exceeded their respective LOQ values, confirming reliable quantification. Method accuracy was assessed by spike-and-recovery experiments at two fortification levels in each matrix; mean recoveries ranged from 85 to 110% for all metals, within the internationally accepted range of 80–120%. Analytical accuracy was further verified using Certified Reference Material NIST SRM 1643f for water and BCR-141R for soil; measured values agreed with certified values within ±5% uncertainty. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate (n = 3) and results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Intra-day precision expressed as percentage relative standard deviation (%RSD) was below 5% for all metals across all matrices, confirming satisfactory repeatability of the AAS measurements.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Prior to statistical analysis, the data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. A two-factor factorial analysis of variance ANOVA was performed in Python (version 3.12) to evaluate the effects of irrigation source (CWI-I and DWW-II), citrus variety (C. limetta, C. reticulata, C. sinensis), and their interaction on heavy metal concentration in water, soil, and fruit samples. Mean comparisons were conducted Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Significance levels were reported as ns = non significant, *= p < 0.05, **= p < 0.01, and ***= p < 0.001. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using SPSS version 23 to evaluate the relationships among heavy metal concentrations in water, soil, and fruit samples under both irrigation systems. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) and heatmap analysis were conducted using Minitab version 19 to assess multivariate relationships and distribution patterns of heavy metals among irrigation sources and citrus varieties.

Results

Heavy metal concentrations in irrigation water

Zn concentrations in irrigation water varied significantly across sites. At CW-I, the mean Zn concentration ranged from 13.33 to 50 mg L−1, while, at DWW-II, it ranged from 18.67 and 51.67 mg L−1. The highest Zn concentration was observed in C. limetta at the DWW-II (Fig. 2). Cr concentrations ranged from 1.86 to 11.66 mg L−1 across both sites, with DWW-II consistently showing higher values. Fe concentrations ranged from 45.5 to 210 mg L−1, with the highest value recorded in C. limetta at DWW-II and the lowest in C. sinensis at CW-I. Pb ranged from 0.93 to 5.2 mg L−1, with maximum accumulation in C. reticulata under DWW-II irrigation. Two-way ANOVA showed that site significantly influenced Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb concentrations (p < 0.001), while varietal and site × variety effects were significant for most metals. These results indicate that irrigation-site conditions were the primary factor affecting heavy metal concentrations in irrigation water. Overall, DWW-II consistently showed higher metal concentrations than CW-I across all metals and varieties (Table 6, Fig. 2).

Concentration of Heavy metals in the Soil Samples

Soil Zn concentrations ranged from 44 to 71 mg kg−1, with the highest value recorded in C. sinensis at DWW-II. Cr ranged from 9.67 to 22.7 mg kg−1, with the highest accumulation in C. limetta under DWW-II. Fe concentrations at DWW-II ranged from 296.67 to 310 mg kg−1, with C. reticulata showing the peak value. Pb under CW-I raged from 0.91 to 1.10 mg kg−1, while DWW-II soils showed substantially higher concentrations of 2.94 to 3.44 mg kg−1. Two-way ANOVA confirmed that site was the dominant source of variation for Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb concentrations (p < 0.001), while varietal effects were significant for Cr and Pb, whereas Zn and Fe were not significantly affected by variety. A significant site × variety interaction was observed only for Zn, indicating differential accumulation among citrus varieties across irrigation-site conditions (Table 7, Fig. 3).

Heavy metals in citrus fruits

The concentration of Zn in fruits of citrus plants was found between 1.08±0.04 mg kg−1 to 1.58±0.07 mg kg−1 and the highest concentration was recorded in C. limetta at DWW-II. The concentrations of Cr ranged from 0.011 to 0.10 mg kg−1, which were the lowest in CW-I and highest in C. sinensis in DWW-II, respectively. However, Fe was in the range of 1.96 to 3.2 mg kg−1 respectively with the highest value observed in C. limetta in DWW-II. The lowest concentration of the all the metals was observed for Pb with C. reticulata under DWW-II having the highest concentration ranging from 0.010 to 0.031 mg kg−1. Two-way ANOVA revealed that site had a significant effects on Zn, Cr, Fe, and Pb concentrations (p < 0.001), whereas varietal differences were significant only for Cr. No significant site × variety interactions were detected for any metal, suggesting a relatively consistent response of citrus varieties across irrigation-site conditions. All concentrations of metal in fruit were found to be below the permissible levels. At the irrigation sites, the fruits contain the highest level of Fe, followed by Zn, Cr and Pb, with Fe > Zn > Cr > Pb (Table 8, Fig. 4).

Contamination factor

CF values indicated that Fe showed the highest contamination (3.48–5.44), particularly in C. reticulata at the DWW-II site, reflecting moderate to considerable pollution. Cr concentration ranged from 1.06–2.50, with the maximum value observed in C. limetta irrigated with DWW-II. Similarly, the highest Zn CF (1.42) was recorded in C. sinensis at DWW-II, whereas Pb exhibited the lowest contamination overall (0.11–0.42), although its maximum value also occurred in C. limetta at DWW-II. In contrast, the lowest CF values for most metals were detected at CW-I, especially in C. reticulata and C. sinensis. Overall, CF values consistently followed the order DWW-II > CW-I, while metal contamination trend was Fe > Cr > Zn > Pb (Fig. 5).

Pollution load index

PLI values showed clear variation between CW-I and DWW-II. At CW-I, all citrus varieties had PLI below 1, indicating low metal pollution and safe environmental conditions. In contrast, all varieties at DWW-II showed PLI values above 1, confirming significant contamination due DWW-II to irrigation. The highest PLI was recorded in C. limetta (1.65), followed by C. sinensis and C. reticulata, indicating greater metal accumulation in C. limetta. Overall, DWW-II was more contaminated than CW-I and may pose environmental and food safety risks (Table 9).

Discussion

Water analysis from citrus field

The present study revealed that the concentration of Zn in both irrigation water sources ranged from 13.33 to 51.67 mg L−1. The observed value of Zn in water was above the permitted limit of 5 mg L−1, as reported by Phetxumphou et al. [21]. The consistently higher Zn at DWW-II compared to CW-I, suggests that DWW-II serves as the primary input pathway, likely driven by household discharge containing galvanized pipe, corrosion products, and agricultural runoff [40]. These values were markedly higher than those reported by Li et al. [30] and Ahmad et al. [29] in comparable irrigation water studies, indicating a relatively greater contamination burden in the present study area. The significant site effect confirmed by ANOVA (p < 0.01) further supports than Zn loading is predominantly site-driven rather than variety-dependent.

The present study revealed that the Cr concentration in water samples varied from 1.86 to 11.66 mg L−1. The chromium concentration in both irrigation sites was beyond the maximum acceptable limit of 0.55 mg L−1, as reported by Chiroma et al. [22]. A comparison of the results with the other research showed that the amount of Cr in the present research was significantly higher than that described by Khan et al. [40], where the Cr concentration in the water sample fell within the range of 0.18–0.686 mg L−1. This is due to the cleaning of clothes by various detergents, utilization of soaps, and leathery items that contain a high content of Cr, which then enter into household waste. The cumulative usage of these products makes DWW-II more contaminated. Moreover, a shortage of pretreatment enables Cr to infiltrate freely in the irrigation system [41]. This demonstrated an elevated amount of Cr in DWW-II.

The Fe content in water at two different locations ranged from 45.5 to 210 mg L−1. When compared with the findings of Ugulu et al. [9], who reported Fe concentrations in fresh and sewage water ranging from 5.45–11.32 mg L−1, the values observed in the present study were considerably higher. The reason behind this is the overuse of old water pipes in households. As water passes through old iron pipes, corrosion occurs, causing rusted iron to mix with the water [42]. The allowable safe range of Fe in water is 5 mg L−1, indicating that the Fe concentration observed in the present study was markedly higher than the permissible level and higher than that reported by Griffiths et al. [23]. The non-significant site × variety interaction for Fe in ANOVA indicates that Fe loading from water is largely site-controlled, with minimal varietal influence on initial water phase concentration.

The Pb concentration in various water samples ranged from 0.93 to 5.2 mg L−1. The current value of Pb in water exceeded the permitted value of 0.065 mg L−1 reported by Chiroma et al. [22]. The current findings are higher than those of Khan et al. [40], who observed lead concentrations from different irrigation sources within the range of 0.062 to 0.128 mg L−1. The higher Pb at DWW-II reflects the cumulative input of battery waste, laundry effluents, vehicle emissions, and cosmetic effluents entering the domestic wastewater stream [41]. The significant site effect (p < 0.001) with a relatively small interaction term indicates that Pb levels in water are primarily determined by irrigation source characteristics rather than citrus variety.

Soil analysis from citrus fields

In the present study, Zn concentrations in soil ranged from 44 to 71 mg kg−1. According to Khan et al. [40], the level of Zn in the soil of C. reticulata (28.7–67.88 mg kg−1) was within the range observed in the present study. Hashem and Qi [43] reported a Zn concentration of 53.03 mg kg−1 in treated wastewater-irrigated soil, which was slightly lower than the upper range observed in the current study. The greater Zn retention capacity of the alluvial loam soil at DWW-II relative to the sandy alluvial soil at CW-I may have independently contributed to higher soil Zn at that site. These elevated Zn concentrations may be attributed to the loamy nature of the soil, which has a moderate Zn-binding capability, along with various agronomic practices. A continuous supply of domestic wastewater saturates the soil, resulting in additional Zn leaching in the topsoil [44]. The significant site × variety interaction effects in ANOVA (p < 0.001 and p < 0.05) confirm that Zn accumulation is associated with the combined influence of irrigation-site conditions and citrus variety.

According to the findings, the average contamination of Cr in soil ranged from 9.67–22.7 mg kg−1. C. limetta contained the maximum level of Cr in DWW-II, while the minimum concentration of Cr was found in the soil of C. reticulata and remained below the permissible limit reported by Chiroma et al. [22]. Duan et al. [45] also indicated a higher concentration of Cr (111.54 mg kg−1) in Taiyuan, China. Continuous irrigation with domestic wastewater results in Cr accumulation in soils due to its absorption onto soil particles and organic matter. Plants generally restrict Cr uptake as a defence mechanism against toxicity; therefore, its translocation to edible plant parts is often limited compared with essential micronutrients. The higher Cr accumulation at DWW-II site suggests that soil at the wastewater irrigated site may be accumulating metals at a comparatively faster rate, though site-specific soil characteristics also contribute to this pattern.

The Fe range in CW-I and DWW-II irrigated soil varied from 198.33 to 310 mg kg−1. When compared with the findings of Ugulu et al. [9], who reported Fe concentrations ranging from 21.93 mg/kg to 26.32 mg kg−1 in soils under C. limetta and C. sinensis, the values observed in the present study were considerably higher. The dominant site effect (p < 0.001) with non-significant variety and interaction terms confirms that Fe accumulation is strongly associated with irrigation-site conditions. Fe dynamics in soil are fundamentally governed by the Fe (II)/Fe (III) redox couple: periodic waterlogging and the high organic load of wastewater create transient reducing conditions that promote Fe3+ reduction to soluble Fe2+ by iron-reducing bacteria (Qui & Primo-millo, 2015) [46].

According to the current study, Pb concentrations in the soil varied between 0.91 and 3.44 mg/kg. As reported by Wahid et al. [47], Pb contamination in soil ranged from 1.17–1.79 mg/kg, which is closely related to the present findings. Pb concentrations in all soil samples remained well below the safe limit of 200 mg kg−1 [19]. These results suggest that the application of domestic wastewater may have enriched soils with heavy metals [48].However, the stronger Pb-binding capacity of the alluvial loam soil at DWW-II relative to sandy alluvial soil at CW-I means that difference in soil texture may also contribute to differential Pb retention between sites.

Comparative analysis across environmental media showed that metal concentrations were highest in soil compared to irrigation water and fruit samples, indicating that soils act as a major reservoir for heavy metals. The elevated levels under domestic wastewater irrigation suggest continuous deposition and accumulation over time.

Heavy metals in fruits

Zn is absorbed by roots through ZIP transporter (ZIP1/ZIP4), but its movement is tightly regulated. When Zn levels are high, the plant sequesters excess Zn into vacuoles in roots and leaves via CDF transporters to prevent toxicity. As a result, only a small portion of Zn is loaded into the phloem and transported to fruits (Xie et al., 2025) [49]. The mean Zn level in citrus fruits varied from 1.08 to 1.58 mg kg−1, with the maximum value found in C. limetta irrigated with domestic wastewater. This level was below the recommended limit of 100 mg kg−1 in fruits as set by Chiroma et al. [22]. The concentration of Zn in the present research was closely related to the study reported by Saleem et al. [50], which was 0.20–1.60 mg kg−1. Although Zn availability in soil and water was elevated, low concentrations were observed in citrus fruits because most Zn is absorbed by nonedible parts, such as leaves. In edible parts, Zn is present due to its abundance in wastewater and because it is an essential mineral for plant growth [51].

The current investigation indicated that the Cr concentration in all citrus varieties fluctuated between 0.011 and 0.10 mg kg−1. Similar observations were reported by Rasool [52], with mean concentrations ranging from 0.063 to 0.15 mg kg−1 in juice of various orange varieties in the Sargodha district. These findings are closely aligned with the current investigation. Rahim et al. [24] suggested a safe limit of 2.3 mg kg−1 for fruits. Likewise, the present concentration was comfortably under the safety limit. In contrast, Abdel-Shafy and El-Khateeb [53] found a Cr level of 0.45 mg kg−1 in C. sinensis, which exceeds the range observed in the present study.

The Fe level in the edible parts of citrus fruits ranged from 1.96 to 3.2 mg kg−1 in the present study. C. limetta showed an elevated Fe level (3.2 mg kg−1) of Fe at domestic wastewater sites; however, this value was lower than the permissible limit of 20 mg kg−1 for fruits given by Shah et al. [25]. Rasool [52] evaluated the level of Fe in citrus cultivars in different tehsils of Sargodha and found that their concentrations varied according to their origin. In the Silanwali region, Fe concentrations were 2.90 mg kg−1 in grapefruit, 2.88 mg/kg in mandarin and highest in sweet lime (5.23 mg kg−1). The dominant site effect (p < 0.001) with non-significant variety and interaction effects confirms that fruit Fe is strongly associated with irrigation-site conditions.

In the current area of study, Pb concentrations at both irrigation sites ranged from 0.010–0.031 mg kg−1. These findings are consistent with Khan et al. [40], who reported Pb concentrations in sewage water ranging from 0.031 to 0.095 mg kg−1, indicating that domestic wastewater is a significant source of heavy metals. The Pb levels observed in the present study were below the acceptable limit of 0.3 mg kg−1 for fruits reported by Rahim et al. [24]. Owing to the low Pb concentrations in citrus fruits, their consumption is unlikely to pose any health risk to humans. The significant site effect (p < 0.001) with non-significant variety interaction terms confirms site-level control over fruit Pb patterns. The low-fruit Pb correlations at DWW-II are consistent with strong Pb immobilization in both soil types, though the greater binding capacity of alluvial loam soil at DWW-II may further restrict Pb bioavailability at that site relative to the sandy alluvial soil at CW-I.

A comparison among citrus species indicated noticeable variation in metal accumulation patterns. C. limetta showed relatively higher concentrations of metals than C. sinensis and C. reticulata, suggesting species-specific differences in uptake and translocation mechanisms. Additionally, metal concentrations in fruits are lower than those observed in soils, reflecting selective uptake and internal regulation by plants. Among the metals, Fe and Zn exhibited comparatively higher accumulation, whereas Cr and Pb remained at lower levels, consistent with their mobility and physiological roles.

Integration of PCA with Metal Accumulation Patterns

The PCA analysis across irrigation water, soil, and fruit compartments revealed consistent irrigation-site associated differentiation across all three matrices, with F1 explaining 96.28–99.62%, and 98.95% of total of variance in water, soil, and fruit samples respectively. Since irrigation regime and sampling site were not independently varied in this comparative field based study, and given the contrasting soil types, sandy alluvial soil at CW-I and alluvial loam at DWW-II. These patterns reflect combined irrigation-site-associated influences rather than irrigation source alone. The F2 components (0.28–3.70% of variance) captured minor varietal differences, with C. sinensis showed a distinct physiological response in the water PCA, consistent with its significantly different Cr accumulation pattern in the ANOVA (varieties significant for Cr: p < 0.01, Table 8). C. limetta exhibited the greatest displacement between sites in the fruit scatter plot (Fig. 14), aligned with its highest PLI (1.6535),

The heatmap visualisations (Figs. 11, 13, 15) revealed that Fe and Zn consistently generated the highest intensity signal across all compartments, while Cr and Pb produced comparatively lower signals. Despite higher Fe and Zn signals, all fruit metal concentrations remained well below permissible limits. Furthermore, THQ values for all metals remained below 1, confirming that citrus fruits from both irrigation-site conditions are safe for human consumption

The concentration factor (CF)

The CF in soil samples was highest for Fe (5.44) in C. reticulata at DWW-II, while the lowest CF (0.12) for Pb was observed in C. sinensis at CW-I. The CF of Cr and Zn ranged from 1.06–2.29 and 0.88–1.42, respectively, indicating moderate soil pollution. According to Khan et al. [40], the CF values of Fe (0.1345–0.7595) were lower and Zn were to the current study. The CF of Pb was less than 1, indicating minimal Pb accumulation in soil and negligible soil pollution. The increasing metal contamination trend is Pb < Zn < Cr < Fe. CF values were consistently higher at DWW-II than CW-I across all metals and varieties, may partly reflect the greater metal retention capacity of loam soil alongside irrigation-site-associated metal input rather than irrigation water quality alone.

Pollution load index

A PLI value less than 1 indicates no pollution or baseline levels, whereas values greater than 1 signify progressive deterioration of soil quality and anthropogenic contamination [54]. PLI values at CW-I ranged from 0.8091 to 0.8802, remaining below unity for all citrus varieties and confirming acceptable soil metal levels under CW-I. At DWW-II, PLI values ranged from 1.5325–1.6535, indicating moderate pollution status. The highest PLI was recorded in C. limetta (1.6535), followed by C. sinensis (1.6155) and C. reticulata (1.5325). Elevated PLI values under wastewater irrigation are consistent with previous studies, however, soil texture differences between sites may independently contribute to differential metal retention and should be considered when interpreting site-level PLI difference.

Enrichment factor (EF)

EF values for Zn (0.0176–0.0215), Cr (0.003–0.020), and Fe (0.026–0.0306) remained below 1 at all sites, indicating predominantly natural geogenic sources for these metals. Pb showed a marginally elevated EF of 1.11 in C. limetta at CW-I, suggesting a minor anthropogenic contribution at CW-I irrigated site that warrants monitoring. The increasing order of the metal enrichment factor followed Cr < Zn < Fe < Pb, consistent with Ali et al. [55], and Khan et al. [40] who reported comparable EF ranges for these metals in similar agricultural soils.

Ecological risk factor

The results showed that the metals had a high level of potential ecological risk in C. limetta compared to C. sinensis and C. reticulata grown under canal and domestic wastewater irrigation. The PER values for Zn, Pb, and Cr spanned from 0.88-1.42, 0.558-2.11 and 2.132-5.005, respectively, indicating a low potential ecological risk of these metals to public safety. Akoto and Anning [33] reported a PER value for Fe of 8.3, which is consistent with the current PER value. The present investigation of ecological risk for Pb (2.11) and Zn (1.373) also agrees with the findings of Saha et al. [56], who reported Pb (2.11) and Zn (1.37) in soils from Shanxi, a province of China, and found very low potential risk. The PER followed the trend Fe > Cr > Pb > Zn. The current results show PER values below 40, indicating that the soil is protected from potential health risks [56].

Estimated daily intake

EDI values were consistently higher at DWW-II than CW-I for all metals following the order Fe > Zn > Cr > Pb. Fe recorded the highest EDI (0.075–0.122 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1), followed by Zn (0.0414–0.0604 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1), Cr (0.0004–0.0038 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1), and Pb the lowest (0.0003–0.0011 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1), with maximum values for all metals recorded at DWW-II. Amjad et al. [57] reported lower EDI values for Fe ranging from 0.0025–0.019 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1 than the current study. This suggests greater iron availability or exposure in our study area. The EDI of Cr (0.0064 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1) reported by Ugulu et al. [9] was higher than that in our study. According to Khan et al. [40], the EDI values of Pb and Cr were 1.1858 × 10−6 and 7.4970 × 10−6 mg kg⁻1 day⁻1), respectively, which were much lower than those in our study. All EDI values remained below internationally recommended tolerable daily intake limits, confirming no immediate dietary hazard from fruit consumption under either irrigation-site condition.

Target hazard quotient

The THQ values of all four metals remained below 1 across all varieties under both irrigation-site conditions, indicating no significant non-carcinogenic health risk associated with fruit consumption. According to USEPA [36], THQ value below 1 suggests that consumers are unlikely to experience any immediate health effects. The THQ levels for Zn and Pb ranged from 0.112–0.163 and 0.109–0.338, respectively, with all values remaining below 1. These values were consistent with Khan et al. [40] and Saleem et al. [50] Although domestic wastewater irrigation resulted in higher metal concentrations in fruits, the corresponding THQ values remained well below the critical limit of 1. The corrected ascending THQ order was Cr < Zn < Pb < Fe.

Study limitations

The present study was designed as comparative field based study examining heavy metal distribution across two irrigation-site condition in Sargodha. It is important to acknowledge that irrigation source and sites were not independently varied in this study, as only one site per irrigation was included. In addition, the two study sites were located approximately 56.6 km apart, which may have introduced spatial variability in soil characteristics, local sources of heavy metals, and background metal concentrations.

Consequently, the observed differences in heavy metal concentration between CW-I and DWW-II reflect not only differences in irrigation water quality but also site-specific factors including differences in soil texture and composition. The sandy alluvial soil at CW-I and the alluvial loam soil at DWW-II differ in their metal binding capacities, cation exchange capacity, and organic matter content, all of which independently influence metal retention, mobility and plant bioavailability. Therefore, all findings in this study are presented as irrigation-site conditions rather than definitive evidence of causation by irrigation source alone.

Conclusion

This comparative field based study showed higher heavy metal accumulation and ecological risk indices (CF, PLI, PERI, and EDI) at DWW-II than at CW-I, with Fe and Zn showing comparatively greater accumulation. However, THQ values for all studied metals remained below 1, indicating no significant non-carcinogenic health risk associated with citrus fruit consumption. Because only one site was examined under each irrigation condition, the observed variations between CW-I and DWW-II may also have been influenced by site-specific characteristics, including differences in soil texture, composition, and metal retention capacity. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as irrigation-site associated patterns rather than definitive effects of irrigation source alone. Overall, the study provides baseline information for citrus growing regions of Sargodha and emphasizes the need for continued monitoring and irrigation management practices in domestic wastewater associated agricultural systems. Furthermore, the study captures inter-compartmental linkages and variability among citrus species. Future research should focus on long-term field assessments, metal speciation, and bioavailability, as well as the development of mitigation strategies such as improved domestic wastewater treatment, controlled irrigation practices, and phytoremediation approaches to reduce metal accumulation in edible crops.
