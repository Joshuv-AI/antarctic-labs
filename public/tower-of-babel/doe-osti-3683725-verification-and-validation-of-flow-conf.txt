ABSTRACT
Blockage or flow-confinement effects in scale wind and water turbine testing in experimental facilities,
including wind and water tunnels and tow tanks, can significantly alter incident aerodynamic and
hydrodynamic loads, turbine performance, and wake dynamics relative to unconfined conditions.
Because scale-model experiments are important sources of OpenFAST validation data, directly
modeling facility confinement can reduce reliance on empirical blockage corrections and enable more
direct comparison with measurements obtained under confined conditions. Herein, we present the
framework and methodology for modeling solid-wall flow confinement in OpenFAST AeroDyn using
the free-vortex-wake method implemented in the OLAF module and three-dimensional source-panel
elements. Model verification is performed through implementation checks and assessments of
temporal and spatial numerical sensitivity. Model validation is performed using rotor performance
coefficients and time-averaged laser Doppler velocimetry velocity profiles from the U.S. Department
of Energy’s (USDOE’s) Marine Hydrokinetic Family 1 (MHKF1) reference axial-flow turbine
experiments conducted in the Garfield-Thomas Water Tunnel (GTWT) at the Applied Research
Laboratory (ARL), Penn State University. The model was verified to enforce the solid-wall no-
penetration condition and to remove the nonphysical global source/sink mode associated with the
open-ended wall-panel geometry. Comparison of OpenFAST predictions with and without flow
confinement demonstrated that the source-panel model predicts systematic changes in rotor
performance and mean velocity profiles consistent with the dominant effects of solid-wall
confinement observed in the GTWT measurements.

1. INTRODUCTION
Blockage or flow confinement effects can significantly influence scale-model wind and water turbine
experiments conducted in controlled facilities such as wind tunnels, water tunnels, and tow tanks.
When a turbine operates in a confined test section, the presence of solid walls and, in some cases, a
nearby free surface modifies the flow field relative to an unconfined environment. These effects can
alter the effective inflow velocity through the rotor, axial and tangential induction, thrust, torque,
power production, and wake development. As a result, turbine performance and load measurements
obtained under confined conditions may differ from those expected in open-water or open-air
operation.
Flow confinement is particularly important for marine hydrokinetic turbine testing because water-
tunnel and tow-tank experiments often involve relatively high rotor loading, finite test-section
dimensions, and interactions with both wall and free-surface boundaries. For this reason, acceptable
blockage ratios in water-tunnel testing are often more restrictive than those typically used in wind-
tunnel testing. If these effects are not properly accounted for, validation of numerical models against
scale-model measurements can be biased by facility-specific confinement effects rather than by
deficiencies in the turbine model itself.
Scale-model experiments are an important source of validation data for OpenFAST simulations of
axial-flow turbines. However, if the experimental measurements are affected by blockage or flow
confinement, direct comparison between an unconfined numerical model and confined experimental
data can lead to misleading conclusions. Empirical blockage correction methods are commonly used
to adjust measured turbine performance quantities, such as thrust and power coefficients, toward
equivalent unconfined conditions. Although useful, these corrections can introduce uncertainty
because they often rely on simplified assumptions and may neglect important effects associated with
rotor-induced swirl, wake rotation, and angular momentum (Ross and Polagye 2020). In addition,
simple blockage corrections are generally not available for wake-flow dynamics or for local distributed
blade-load quantities, such as spanwise flapwise and edgewise loads (Kim and Neary 2025).
An alternative approach is to model the confinement effects directly within the numerical simulation.
High-fidelity computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methods, such as Reynolds averaged Navier-Stokes
(RANS) or large-eddy-simulation (LES) solvers, can resolve wall and free-surface effects with
relatively high physical fidelity, but their computational cost can be prohibitive for design studies, load
analysis, or broad parametric investigations. A mid-fidelity alternative is to represent the solid walls
and free-surface boundaries using a source-panel method and to couple the resulting confinement-
induced velocity field with a free-vortex-wake (FVW) representation of the turbine rotor and wake.
The objective of this report is to document the implementation, verification, and validation of a flow-
confinement model in OpenFAST’s AeroDyn and OLAF FVW framework (Branlard, Brownstein et
al. 2022). The implemented approach uses source panels to represent the facility boundaries and
modifies the local velocity field through the rotor and within its wake to capture confinement-induced
effects, including convective acceleration through the rotor plane and changes in wake evolution. The
study evaluates whether the model can qualitatively reproduce the expected flow-confinement physics
and whether it can improve agreement with experimental quantities of interest, including turbine
performance metrics and, where available, structural-response or load-related measurements.
This report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the flow-confinement modeling approach,
including the source-panel formulation and its coupling with the OLAF FVW method. Section 3
presents the verification and validation (V&V) strategy used to assess the implementation. Section 4
describes the selected V&V test case based on scale-model axial-flow turbine experiments conducted

 10

in the Garfield-Thomas water tunnel (GTWT) at the Applied Research Laboratory (ARL) at Penn
State University. Section 5 presents the results, including temporal and spatial sensitivity studies, wall-
panel dependency, convergence behavior, and comparisons between model predictions and
experimental measurements. Section 6 summarizes the main conclusions and discusses the benefits
and limitations of the mid-fidelity flow-confinement approach for modeling scale-model turbine
experiments in confined facilities.

2. FLOW CONFINEMENT MODELING
2.1. OpenFAST AeroDyn
AeroDyn is a time-domain aerodynamic and hydrodynamic loading module within the OpenFAST
multiphysics framework (Jonkman 2026). When coupled with the structural-dynamics modules in
OpenFAST, AeroDyn provides the fluid-dynamic loads required for aero- or hydro-elastic simulations
of axial-flow turbines, cross-flow turbines, and other lifting-surface assemblies operating in wind or
water currents. The module computes distributed loads on turbine blades and, when applicable, tower
or support-structure members.
The aerodynamic or hydrodynamic loading in AeroDyn is based on a blade-element or lifting-line
representation. In this approach, the 3D flow around each blade is approximated using local 2D flow
at a set of spanwise analysis nodes. At each node, the local relative velocity and angle of attack are
used with prescribed 2D airfoil or hydrofoil polar data to compute sectional lift, drag, and pitching
moment. These sectional quantities approximate the integrated pressure and viscous shear
contributions over the local cross section and are applied as distributed loads per unit length along the
blade. The distributed loads are then integrated along the blade span to obtain the total aerodynamic
or hydrodynamic forces and moments acting on the rotor.
AeroDyn includes blade element momentum (BEM) and FVW options for modeling rotor induction
and wake effects. The BEM approach estimates induction using momentum balance with engineering
corrections, while the FVW approach, implemented through OLAF, explicitly represents the rotor
wake and computes the corresponding induced velocity field in the time domain. The present flow-
confinement implementation is developed for the OLAF-based FVW approach because its spatially
resolved representation of the blades and wake allows the velocity field induced by solid-wall source
panels to be evaluated directly at blade and wake points.

2.2. OpenFAST-AeroDyn-OLAF
OLAF is the FVW capability within AeroDyn used to model the unsteady rotor-induced velocity field
and wake evolution (Branlard, Brownstein et al. 2022). In OLAF, each turbine blade is represented
using a lifting-line formulation, with bound vortex filaments located along the blade aerodynamic
center, commonly approximated by the quarter-chord line. The blades are discretized into spanwise
analysis nodes, and the local sectional loads are computed from the relative velocity, angle of attack,
and prescribed 2D airfoil or hydrofoil polar data at each node.
The FVW method was first applied to model power performance and wake-flow fields of vertical-axis
wind turbines in the VDART3 code (Strickland 1981). The method was further developed over several
decades to expand its capabilities for horizontal-axis turbine geometries and marine turbines operating
in water environments, including implementation in the open-source axial- and cross-flow turbine
simulation code CACTUS (Murray and Barone 2011). Related FVW formulations have also been
adopted in turbine design and analysis tools, including commercial tools such as QBlade (Marten 2020)
and open-source tools such as OpenFAST (Jonkman 2026).
The OLAF/FVW formulation represents the flow field as the superposition of the prescribed inflow,
bound vorticity on the blades, and wake vorticity shed from the rotor. The bound circulation
associated with each blade element is related to the sectional lift through the Kutta–Joukowski
theorem. As the rotor advances in time, changes in bound circulation and blade loading generate

 12

trailing and shed vorticity, which forms a Lagrangian wake composed of convecting vortex filaments.
The wake points are advected with the local flow velocity, allowing the wake to deform naturally in
response to the rotor-induced velocity field and the imposed inflow.
The velocity induced by the bound and wake vortex filaments is evaluated using the Biot–Savart law.
Because the wake is represented explicitly, OLAF can resolve important near-wake features such as
helical tip-vortex structures, wake skew, wake expansion, vortex interaction, vortex merging, and far-
wake evolution. Numerical regularization and wake-diffusion models, such as vortex-core spreading,
are used to avoid singular induced velocities near vortex filaments and to represent the effective
diffusion of the wake vorticity.
In OpenFAST, OLAF provides a mid-fidelity alternative to BEM by resolving the wake geometry and
induced velocity field in the time domain while retaining a blade-element representation of the
sectional loads. Inflow conditions can be prescribed as uniform flow or provided through InflowWind
for more complex inflow fields, including unsteady, three-dimensional, or turbulent inflow over a
specified inflow domain.
The explicit spatial representation of both the blades and wake makes OLAF suitable for coupling
with a source-panel model of facility boundaries. In the present implementation, the confinement-
induced velocity field from solid-wall source panels is added to the local velocity field evaluated at the
blade analysis nodes and wake points. This allows the modeled facility boundaries to influence the
local inflow, blade loading, wake convection, and resulting turbine performance without relying solely
on empirical blockage corrections.

2.3. Panel method
The incorporation of potential-flow source-panel elements into OLAF enables direct modeling of
flow-confinement effects caused by solid wall boundaries. The source-panel method is a numerical
approximation in which the solid boundary surfaces are discretized into a finite number of surface
panels. A constant-strength source distribution is prescribed over each panel, and the unknown source
strengths are solved such that the no-flow-through boundary condition is satisfied at the panel centers.
The present implementation uses three-dimensional quadrilateral constant-strength source panels.
Although the source-panel influence formulation can be introduced using two-dimensional point
sources or two-dimensional constant-strength source segments, the implemented wall model is based
on the corresponding three-dimensional quadrilateral source formulation (Hess and Smith 1962, Birk
2022). Therefore, this section focuses on the three-dimensional panel formulation and its coupling
with OLAF.

2.3.1. Three-dimensional quadrilateral source element
In the three-dimensional formulation, the discretization process involves both the discretization of
the wall geometry and the discretization of the source-strength distribution. The solid walls are
represented by quadrilateral panels, and each panel is assigned a constant source strength. The
formulation is derived in a local panel coordinate system, and the resulting induced velocities are
transformed into the global coordinate system used by OpenFAST.

2.3.5. OLAF Implementation
OLAF implements the three-dimensional constant-strength source-panel formulation described in the
previous sections. The source panels represent the solid wall boundaries of the confined test section,
and the panel-induced velocity field is coupled to the OLAF FVW solution through the velocity
evaluations used for wake convection and lifting-line load calculation.
The source-panel implementation workflow in OpenFAST is shown in Figure 2. At each source-panel
update, OLAF evaluates the normal velocity induced at each wall-panel control point by the freestream,
existing wake and blade vortex filaments. These normal velocity contributions form the right-hand
side of the constrained source-panel system. The source strengths are then solved using the normal

 17

influence matrix and the global mass conservation constraint. The resulting source strengths define
the instantaneous wall-induced potential-flow correction associated with the confined geometry.
The updated source strengths are used to compute the velocity induced by the wall panels at the OLAF
wake points and blade analysis nodes. At the wake points, the wall-induced velocity modifies the
convection velocity of the Lagrangian wake markers, allowing the wake to respond to the presence of
the solid wall boundaries. At the blade analysis nodes, the wall-induced velocity modifies the local
inflow velocity used to compute the lifting-line angle of attack and the corresponding sectional loads.
In principle, the wake and source-panel systems form a coupled interaction within each time step: the
wake-induced velocity affects the wall-panel source strengths, and the resulting wall-induced velocity
affects both wake convection and blade loading. In the present implementation, this interaction is not
iterated to convergence within each time step. Instead, the source-panel strengths are updated at the
prescribed source-panel update interval and then used in the subsequent OLAF velocity evaluations.
This non-iterative coupling reduces computational cost while retaining the leading-order effect of the
solid wall boundaries on rotor loading and wake evolution.
The source-panel geometry is provided to OLAF as a quadrilateral surface mesh in VTK format. The
VTK polygon connectivity follows the source-panel orientation convention implemented in OLAF.
For a given VTK connectivity, the panel normal internally computed by OLAF is opposite to the
conventional right-hand-rule normal associated with the polygon ordering. Therefore, the
conventional right-hand-rule normal must point from the fluid toward the panel so that the
corresponding OLAF internal normal points away from the panel and into the fluid domain,
consistent with the source-panel formulation.
For the present internal cylindrical tunnel wall, this corresponds to a conventional right-hand-rule
normal pointing radially outward and an OLAF internal source-panel normal pointing radially inward
into the tunnel flow domain. When VTK output is enabled, OLAF can also write source-panel-related
visualization fields, including panel geometry, normal vectors, and wall-panel quantities used for
verification and post-processing.
The present implementation models confinement effects from solid wall boundaries only. It does not
include a free-surface treatment, wave-making model, or dynamic free-surface boundary condition.
Additional details regarding input parameters and usage examples may be found in the documentation
available online at: https://openfast.readthedocs.io/en/main/source/user/aerodyn-olaf/.

3. MODEL VERIFICATION AND VALIDATION
The present study applies the model verification and validation methodology of (Thacker, Doebling
et al. 2004), as illustrated in Figure 3. In this framework, verification assesses whether the numerical
model is implemented correctly and whether the selected temporal and spatial resolutions introduce
acceptable levels of numerical error. Validation then assesses the level of agreement between model-
predicted and experimentally derived quantities of interest.

 Figure 3. Model verification and validation for current energy converter (CEC) models adapted
 from (Thacker et al. 2004).

For the present OpenFAST flow-confinement model, verification is focused on three aspects:
implementation verification of the source-panel method, temporal convergence of the OLAF/FVW
solution, and spatial convergence of the wall-panel discretization. Validation is performed by
comparing model predictions against experimental measurements from a confined water-tunnel test
case. The objective of this V&V process is not to demonstrate that the source-panel model resolves
all facility-scale flow physics, but to determine whether the implemented model is adequate for the
intended purpose: qualitatively and quantitatively representing the dominant solid-wall flow-
confinement effects on turbine performance and wake evolution.
The verification methodology follows the Grid Convergence Index (GCI) approach introduced by
(Roache 1997) and standardized by the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME 2009).
This approach was also used in the attached CFD/FSI study to assess temporal and spatial
discretization uncertainty, where solution verification was defined as the process of estimating and
reducing numerical error associated with discretization and iterative convergence. In that study,
temporal convergence was assessed by applying the same GCI methodology used for grid convergence,
replacing the spatial refinement ratio with the time-step refinement ratio.

 20

3.1. Verification approach
Verification is divided into code verification and solution verification. Code verification evaluates
whether the numerical implementation correctly solves the intended mathematical formulation. For
general CFD solvers, the Method of Manufactured Solutions or comparison against analytical
solutions is often recommended. In the present work, the source-panel implementation is verified
through targeted implementation checks because the model is implemented as an extension to the
existing OpenFAST FVW framework rather than as a standalone CFD solver.
The implementation verification checks include confirmation that the VTK polygon connectivity
produces the required OLAF internal panel-normal orientation. For the internal tunnel-wall geometry,
the conventional right-hand-rule normal are required to point radially outward from the fluid domain,
while the corresponding OLAF internal normal point radially inward into the tunnel flow domain.
The pressure coefficient for the potential flow about a sphere was used as an analytical verification of
the method. Additional checks are applied to the global mass-conservation constraint to ensure that
the solved source strengths do not introduce a nonphysical net source or sink over the open-ended
wall-panel representation. Some of these checks are included as part of the automatic unit-testing of
OpenFAST executed for each new version of the software.
Solution verification is then performed to assess the sensitivity of the model predictions to numerical
discretization choices. In the present study, the primary discretization parameters are the OLAF time
step and the wall-panel spatial resolution. The time-step study evaluates whether the temporal
advancement of the baseline OLAF/FVW solution is sufficiently resolved before introduction of the
source-panel wall model. The wall-panel dependency study evaluates whether the discretization of the
solid boundaries is sufficiently refined to represent the confinement-induced velocity field without
introducing significant panel-resolution error.
The quantities of interest for solution verification include turbine performance metrics and flow-field
metrics that are directly influenced by the confinement model. These may include power coefficient,
thrust coefficient, torque coefficient, source-panel normal-velocity residual, area-weighted source
strength, and selected wake-flow quantities. Performance coefficients are used because they provide
integrated measures of the rotor response, while wall-panel residuals and wake-flow quantities are used
to check whether the source-panel model is behaving consistently with its intended boundary-
condition enforcement.

3.2. Validation approach
The validation assessment compares the OpenFAST source-panel predictions with the US
Department of Energy’s (USDOE) reference MHKF1 axial-flow turbine water-tunnel measurements

 23

for the selected quantities of interest: rotor performance coefficients, time-averaged LDV velocity
profiles, and cavitation-potential metrics (Fontaine, Straka et al. 2020). Because the source-panel
model directly represents the confined GTWT wall boundary, the primary validation reference is the
uncorrected in-tunnel experimental data. Blockage-corrected experimental data are not used as the
primary validation target because the blockage correction removes part of the confinement effect that
the present model is intended to predict directly.
For the performance-coefficient validation, discrepancies in model-derived vs. measurement-derived
quantities of interest along power performance curves are evaluated rather than pointwise error
metrics. Although the simulations were performed using uniformly prescribed TSR values, the
experimental TSR values do not exactly coincide with the numerical TSR sweep because the measured
tunnel velocity varied slightly during the tests. Therefore, validation of the model’s accuracy predicting
integrated hydrodynamic performance curves for ‫ܥ‬௉ , ‫ ்ܥ‬, and ‫ܥ‬ொ is interpreted based on the overall
trend, magnitude, and peak-performance region rather than point-by-point percent error.
For the LDV validation, only time-averaged axial and vertical velocity profiles are used as quantitative
validation targets. Phase-averaged LDV and PIV fields are used only for physical interpretation of the
experimental wake structure and are not used for quantitative validation in this report. For the
cavitation comparison, the model output is interpreted as a cavitation-potential assessment rather than
a direct prediction of cavitation inception, because OpenFAST does not resolve cavitation bubble
dynamics or detailed tip-vortex core pressure.

3.3. Acceptance criteria and interpretation
The verification and validation results are interpreted with respect to the intended use of the model.
For verification, the selected temporal and wall-panel spatial resolutions are considered adequate if
further refinement produces small changes in the primary quantities of interest and if the no-flow-
through and global mass-conservation residuals remain acceptably small. Formal GCI values are
reported for quantities that exhibit monotonic convergence, while non-monotonic or oscillatory
convergence is reported using maximum differences between refined cases.
For validation, the model is considered adequate if the predicted turbine performance and flow-field
trends agree with experimental measurements within the combined experimental and numerical
uncertainty, or if the remaining discrepancies can be explained by known model-form limitations.
Since the present source-panel approach models solid-wall confinement using a potential-flow
boundary representation, it is not expected to resolve viscous wall boundary layers, turbulence
production at the walls, or free-surface dynamics. Therefore, validation emphasizes the model’s ability
to reproduce the dominant inviscid confinement effects and their influence on rotor performance and
wake evolution, rather than full high-fidelity reproduction of all facility-scale flow physics.
The V&V process therefore provides a structured basis for assessing whether the implemented
OpenFAST flow-confinement model is sufficiently accurate for mid-fidelity analysis of confined
turbine experiments and for reducing reliance on empirical blockage corrections.

4. VERIFICATION & VALIDATION TEST CASE
The present study uses the 1:8.7-scale water-tunnel test of the 5.0-m-diameter USDOE-MHKF1 axial-
flow hydrokinetic turbine as the primary verification and validation test case detailed in (Fontaine,
Straka et al. 2020). The experiment was conducted in the Garfield Thomas Water Tunnel (GTWT) at
ARL Penn State and was designed to provide a validation-quality dataset for hydrokinetic turbine
modeling as shown in Figure 4. The test campaign included turbine performance, blade loading, near-
wake flow, cavitation, acoustics, and unsteady structural-response measurements. The full dataset also
includes model and tunnel CAD geometry files and inflow data intended to support computational
V&V studies.
This test case is particularly suitable for evaluating the OpenFAST source-panel confinement model
because the turbine was tested in a confined circular water-tunnel test section with a non-negligible
model/tunnel blockage. The blockage ratio, defined as the ratio of the rotor-nacelle-tower frontal area
to the tunnel cross-sectional area, was approximately 5%. This blockage increases the mass flow
through the turbine relative to an unconfined free-field condition and therefore provides a relevant
high-blockage case for assessing whether direct wall modeling can reproduce the dominant flow-
confinement effect.
Because the purpose of the present model is to directly represent solid-wall confinement, the primary
validation comparisons are made against the uncorrected in-tunnel measurements when available. The
blockage-corrected data reported by (Fontaine, Straka et al. 2020) are used only as an auxiliary
reference for interpreting the equivalent unconfined turbine performance. This distinction is
important because comparing a confined-domain model against blockage-corrected data would
remove part of the physical effect that the source-panel model is intended to resolve.

4.1. Experimental setup
The GTWT is a high speed, high-Reynolds-number, closed-loop water tunnel. The test section is 1.22
m in diameter and 4.27 m long. The tunnel’s variable-pitch impeller produces test section inflow
velocities from 0.0 to 16.0 m/s. Freestream turbulence is controlled by honeycombs and screens
upstream of a 16:1 contraction ratio nozzle feeding the test section. The tunnel can accurately control
test section static pressure (from 20.7 kPa to 413.7 kPa) and water dissolved air content (1.0e~20.0 M
ppm).
The test article was a 1:8.7-scale model of the USDOE-MHKF1 turbine rotor mounted on a scaled
nacelle and tower as shown in Figure 5. The scaled rotor had an overall tip diameter of 574.2 mm and

 25

a hub diameter of 47.1 mm. The rotor was mounted to a 76.2-mm-diameter nacelle and a 76.2-mm-
diameter tower. A 63.5-mm-diameter, approximately 2.0-m-long driveshaft connected the turbine-
nacelle-tower assembly to a downstream dynamometer housing containing a motor-generator in a
direct-drive configuration. The model rotor was fabricated from 17-4 PH stainless steel to maximize
strength and minimize deflection during testing. One blade was instrumented with strain-gage rosettes
at the 25% and 50% span locations.

The turbine performance measurements were obtained using custom-designed, strain-gaged, water-
proofed thrust and torque load cells installed inline on the driveshaft. The torque and thrust cells were
arranged as separate single-component bridge units to reduce cross-talk and to allow load-cell capacity
to be matched to the expected operating condition. Turbine rpm was measured with a shaft encoder
and varied systematically while the dynamometer operated in controlled-rpm mode. The tunnel static
pressure was maintained at 310 kPa, the maximum allowable pressure for the test configuration, to
eliminate or minimize cavitation effects during the primary performance measurements.
The experimental coordinate system places the origin at the leading edge of the rotor hub. The X-axis
is positive downstream, the Y-axis is aligned with the tower direction, and the Z-axis is positive
vertically upward. The shaft encoder counts from 0 to 360 degrees in the counter-clockwise direction
when looking upstream, opposite the rotor rotation direction. This coordinate and phase convention
is used when interpreting phase-averaged LDV/PIV data and unsteady loading measurements.

4.2. Test conditions
The turbine was tested over inflow velocities from 2.0 to 7.0 m/s, corresponding to TSR values from
approximately 1.0 to 10.0. The dynamometer was operated in controlled-rpm mode, and the shaft
speed was varied systematically at each tunnel velocity. The maximum chord Reynolds number for
the experiment was approximately 700,000 based on the local blade chord at 95% span. The full-scale

 26

chord Reynolds number for the designed field site was estimated to be approximately 2,000,000; while
performance data indicated that the model became approximately Reynolds-number independent
above a critical chord Reynolds number of about 350,000.
The primary on-design operating condition for detailed flow-field measurements was a tunnel velocity
of 5.0 m/s and a shaft speed of approximately 664 rpm, corresponding to TSR = 4.0. This operating
condition was used for LDV and PIV flow-field mapping, including inflow characterization and near-
wake measurements. The 5.0 m/s case is therefore the main validation condition for comparing
predicted velocity fields and wake structure.
The performance data were collected at multiple tunnel velocities and TSR values. However, not all
operating conditions are equally suitable for validation of the present model. The 2.0 m/s data are
more sensitive to Reynolds-number effects, while the 7.0 m/s data were affected by cavitation
breakdown. Therefore, the present validation emphasizes the non-cavitating, approximately Reynolds-
number-independent operating range, particularly the 3.0–5.0 m/s tunnel-velocity cases and the on-
design condition at 5.0 m/s and TSR = 4.0. Fontaine et al. reported that blockage corrections ranged
from approximately 6% to 9% depending on inflow speed and TSR, and that the USDOE-MHKF1
turbine reached a peak ‫ܥ‬௉ of approximately 0.47 after blockage correction.
For the primary performance tests, the tunnel static pressure was held at 310 kPa to reduce the
likelihood of cavitation. Separate cavitation tests were conducted by lowering the tunnel pressure at
fixed operating conditions, but cavitation modeling is outside the scope of the present OpenFAST
confinement-model validation. Thus, cavitating conditions are not used for the primary validation of
the source-panel wall model.

equivalent phase-binned velocity statistics at the same spatial locations, over enough rotor revolutions,
and with temporal resolution fine enough to resolve the blade-passing and tip-vortex positions within
each narrow phase bin. This requirement is more stringent than the intended validation level of the
present mid-fidelity model. OpenFAST is used here to capture the leading-order effects of solid-wall
confinement on rotor loading and mean wake convection, rather than to reproduce all phase-resolved
coherent structures measured by LDV or PIV. In addition, model choices such as time-step size, wake
discretization, vortex-core regularization, and the simplified potential-flow wall treatment introduce
uncertainty in the exact phase-resolved position and strength of thin blade wakes and tip-vortex cores.
Therefore, the LDV validation in this report is restricted to time-averaged velocity profiles.

4.4. OpenFAST model set up
The OpenFAST model was configured using the AeroDyn driver to simulate the USDOE-MHKF1
turbine operating inside the GTWT test section. The model consists of the USDOE-MHKF1 rotor,
nacelle, tower, OLAF FVW wake model, and a cylindrical source-panel representation of the tunnel
wall. The source-panel model was included to directly represent the solid-wall confinement effect of
the water tunnel.
A representative OpenFAST input case is provided with this report to document the baseline setup.
The setup was established according to the online guidelines of the OLAF documentation. The same
baseline model configuration was used for the validation cases, while the inflow velocity and rotor
speed were adjusted according to the target operating condition. For the performance-coefficient
validation, simulations were performed at ܷ଴ = 5.0 m/s with rotor speed varied to match the selected
TSR values. For the LDV-profile validation, the model was run at the on-design condition of TSR =
4.0. For the cavitation-potential assessment, separate simulations were performed with the AeroDyn
cavitation-check option enabled at the experimental cavitation-test condition, ܷ଴ = 4.0 m/s, TSR =
4.0.
AeroDyn was configured to use OLAF by selecting the FVW wake model. The blade geometry was
prescribed using 20 spanwise blade-analysis nodes. The USDOE-MHKF1 blade chord, twist, and
airfoil assignments were defined in the blade input file, and Reynolds-number-dependent airfoil polar
tables were used for the sectional aerodynamic/hydrodynamic load calculations. The nacelle and tower
geometry were included to represent the experimental turbine support configuration. The tower
influence was represented using AeroDyn’s potential-flow model with the Bak correction. The OLAF
“TwrShadowOnWake” option was also enabled so that the tower-flow disturbance was included in
the convection of the OLAF wake.
The simulations used steady uniform inflow. For the 5.0 m/s performance and LDV validation cases,
the freestream velocity was held constant and rotor speed was changed to obtain each target TSR. For
the cavitation-potential case, the freestream velocity and rotor speed were changed to match the
experimental cavitation-test condition. The OLAF time step and wake-discretization parameters used
in the production simulations were selected from the timestep and wake-resolution dependency
studies described in Section 3.
The tunnel wall was represented by a cylindrical quadrilateral source-panel mesh. The baseline source-
panel surface contained 7200 quadrilateral panels, with 60 panels in the circumferential direction and
120 panels in the streamwise direction. The tunnel-wall radius was ܴ୲୳୬୬ୣ୪ = 0.610 m, corresponding
to a tunnel diameter of ‫୳୲ܦ‬୬୬ୣ୪ = 1.220 m. The source-panel cylinder was modeled as an open-ended

5. RESULTS
The results are organized into verification and validation assessments. The verification results first
assess whether the implemented source-panel confinement model behaves consistently with the
intended numerical formulation and whether the selected numerical settings are sufficiently insensitive
to timestep, wake resolution, and wall-panel resolution. The validation results then compare the
verified OpenFAST source-panel model predictions with the USDOE-MHKF1 water-tunnel
measurements to assess discrepancies for the selected quantities of interest: performance coefficients,
time-averaged LDV velocity profiles, and cavitation-potential metrics.
Because the source-panel model is intended to represent the confined GTWT wall boundary directly,
the primary validation reference for turbine performance is the uncorrected in-tunnel experimental
data. Blockage-corrected data, where shown, are used only as an auxiliary reference for interpreting
the difference between confined and equivalent unconfined operation. For the LDV comparison, only
time-averaged velocity profiles are used as quantitative validation targets, consistent with the validation
scope defined in Section 4.3.

5.1. Verification results
The verification results focus on the numerical behavior of the OpenFAST-OLAF source-panel
implementation before comparison with experimental data. Three aspects are examined:
implementation-level checks of the source-panel boundary condition, timestep and wake-resolution
sensitivity, and wall-panel resolution sensitivity. These studies are used to confirm that the source-
panel model produces a physically consistent wall-induced velocity field and that the selected
production setup is sufficiently insensitive to the numerical parameters considered here.

5.1.1. Source-panel implementation verification
The source-panel implementation was first verified before performing the timestep, wall-panel
resolution, and experimental validation studies. The verification focused on three implementation-
level aspects: panel-normal orientation, global mass conservation of the open-ended source-panel
system, and enforcement of the no-penetration boundary condition on the tunnel wall. We note that
the potential flow about a sphere was also used as verification, as is part of OpenFAST’s unit-testing
framework 1, but this study is not reported here.
The first implementation check concerned the source-panel normal orientation. An initial tunnel-wall
VTK mesh was generated with the conventional right-hand-rule normal directed radially inward
toward the tunnel fluid domain. Although this orientation is intuitive for an internal-flow surface,
OLAF does not use the conventional right-hand-rule polygon normal direction. Instead, OLAF
constructs its internal panel normal using the opposite cross-product orientation, (ܲସ െ ܲଶ ) × (ܲଷ െ
ܲଵ ). Consequently, the initial VTK connectivity resulted in OLAF internal normal pointing radially
outward from the tunnel fluid domain. With this orientation, the source-strength distribution
developed a nonphysical sink-like ring near the rotor plane and produced an unphysical reduction in
the approach-flow velocity. The VTK polygon connectivity was therefore reversed. In the final
configuration, the conventional right-hand-rule normal point radially outward from the tunnel domain,

5.2.1. Performance coefficient validation
The rotor-integrated performance validation was performed by comparing the OpenFAST predictions
with flow confinement against the uncorrected USDOE-MHKF1 water-tunnel measurements at the
nominal ܷ଴ = 5.0 m/s condition. Figure 13 additionally includes the OpenFAST predictions without
flow confinement and the blockage-corrected experimental data as auxiliary references. This
comparison provides a direct indication of how the modeled tunnel-wall confinement shifts the
predicted performance relative to an unconfined condition. The experimental data are plotted at their
measured TSR values, while the numerical results are shown as curves over the prescribed TSR sweep.
Therefore, the comparison is interpreted at the performance-curve level rather than through pointwise
error metrics.
Figure 13 compares the predicted and measured ‫ܥ‬௉ , ‫ ்ܥ‬, and ‫ܥ‬ொ over the tested TSR range. The error
bars shown for ‫ܥ‬௉ , ‫ ்ܥ‬, and ‫ܥ‬ொ represent the reported statistical uncertainty of the binned
experimental mean values from the processed dataset. The source-panel OpenFAST model captures
the overall shape of the ‫ܥ‬௉ curve, including the rapid increase in power coefficient from low TSR to
the peak-performance region and the gradual decrease at higher TSR.
For ‫ܥ‬௉ , the OpenFAST prediction without flow confinement follows the blockage-corrected
experimental data reasonably well, particularly through the peak-performance region and toward
higher TSR. Including the source-panel wall model shifts the predicted ‫ܥ‬௉ upward toward the
uncorrected in-tunnel measurements. The confined prediction reproduces the overall shape of the
measured ‫ܥ‬௉ curve, including the rapid increase at low TSR, the broad peak region, and the
subsequent decrease at higher TSR. This comparison indicates that the source-panel model introduces
the expected confinement-induced increase in power performance and improves consistency with the
directly measured in-tunnel data.
A similar trend is observed for ‫ ்ܥ‬. The prediction without flow confinement remains closer to the
blockage-corrected measurements, while application of the source-panel wall model increases the
predicted thrust coefficient toward the uncorrected in-tunnel data. However, the confined prediction
exceeds the uncorrected measurements over much of the higher-TSR range. The model therefore
captures the direction of the confinement effect on rotor thrust, but appears to overpredict its
magnitude. This overprediction is also consistent with the stronger axial wake deficit predicted by
OpenFAST in the LDV comparison discussed in Section 5.2.2.
For ‫ܥ‬ொ , the no-confinement prediction is generally closer to the blockage-corrected measurements,
while the source-panel model increases the predicted torque coefficient toward the uncorrected
measurements. The confined prediction reproduces the measured peak region and the subsequent
decrease in ‫ܥ‬ொ with increasing TSR, although some discrepancy remains at higher TSR. As with ‫ܥ‬௉ ,
the shift between the unconfined and confined OpenFAST predictions is consistent with the
experimentally observed difference between the blockage-corrected and uncorrected performance
data.
Overall, Figure 13 shows that the source-panel wall model produces a systematic shift in the predicted
performance coefficients consistent with the experimentally observed effect of water-tunnel
confinement. Without the wall model, the OpenFAST predictions generally lie closer to the blockage-

uncorrected in-tunnel data. The agreement is strongest for ‫ܥ‬௉ and ‫ܥ‬ொ , while the confined ‫்ܥ‬

For the mean axial velocity, inclusion of the flow-confinement model systematically increases the

the source-panel wall model produces the expected confinement-induced acceleration of the flow

Despite this improvement, the confined prediction generally remains below the measured axial

effective flow area and may further increase the core and bypass-flow velocities. The absence of this
boundary-layer displacement effect therefore provides one possible explanation for the remaining
underprediction of the outer-region axial velocity.
Within the rotor wake, the flow-confinement model also moves the predicted axial velocity toward
the LDV measurements, but the simulated wake deficit remains stronger than measured over much

where the confined OpenFAST model predicts higher ‫ ்ܥ‬than the uncorrected measurements over
much of the operating range. The remaining axial wake-deficit discrepancy therefore reflects not only
limitations in the representation of confinement but also differences in predicted rotor loading and
subsequent wake evolution.
For the mean vertical velocity, the influence of flow confinement is most apparent at the near- and
intermediate-wake stations. At X = 0.1106‫ܦ‬, 0.1548‫ܦ‬, 0.2344‫ܦ‬, and 0.3760‫ܦ‬, the confined prediction

substantially faster recovery of the mean vertical velocity toward zero than observed in the LDV
measurements. Although the source-panel model modifies the downstream profile, the difference
between the two numerical predictions is small relative to the remaining discrepancy with the
experiment. This indicates that the discrepancy at this downstream location is not primarily
attributable to the flow-confinement model. Instead, it is more likely associated with limitations of the
downstream FVW wake representation and viscous and turbulent wake dynamics that are not fully
represented by the present mid-fidelity model. (Lee, Sengupta et al. 2022) The source-panel tunnel
extends approximately 10.4 ‫ ܦ‬downstream of the rotor, leaving about 9.4 ‫ ܦ‬of modeled wall

primary cause of the discrepancy at this location.
Overall, the radial wake comparisons show that including flow confinement consistently shifts the
OpenFAST predictions toward the measured velocity profiles over the near- and intermediate-wake
region. The source-panel model captures the confinement-induced increase in axial velocity and
improves the predicted mean vertical velocity over much of the rotor wake. Remaining discrepancies

mean vertical velocity at X =0.9997‫( ܦ‬574 mm). Comparison with the no-confinement solution

5.2.3. Cavitation-potential assessment
The final validation quantity considered in the present study is cavitation potential. The assessment
compares the experimentally observed cavitation behavior shown in Figure 23 with the cavitation
potential predicted by OpenFAST at the experimental cavitation-test condition. As discussed
previously, this comparison is interpreted as a validation of the model to assess cavitation-potential
rather than a direct prediction of cavitation, because OpenFAST does not resolve cavitation bubble
dynamics or tip-vortex core pressure. Instead, the model estimates whether local blade operating
conditions approach cavitation-prone conditions.
In OpenFAST, cavitation potential is evaluated by comparing the local cavitation number with the
critical cavitation number inferred from the sectional minimum pressure coefficient. Cavitation is
predicted when the local cavitation condition reaches or exceeds the critical condition. Figure 24

 50

shows the predicted ratio ߪ/ߪୡ୰୧୲ along the blade span for four static-pressure conditions. A ratio
greater than or equal to 1 indicates a predicted cavitating condition.
At the two highest static-pressure conditions, ܲୱ୲ୟ୲୧ୡ = 306 kPa and 202 kPa, the predicted ߪ/ߪୡ୰୧୲
remains below 1 over the entire blade span. Therefore, OpenFAST does not predict blade cavitation
at either of these conditions. This is consistent with the absence of blade cavitation in the experiment,
but it does not reproduce the experimentally observed intermittent and steady tip-vortex cavitation at
306 and 202 kPa, respectively. This discrepancy is expected because the OpenFAST cavitation check
is based on sectional blade loading and minimum pressure coefficient, whereas the measured
cavitation at these conditions is associated with the tip-vortex core. The tip-vortex core pressure is
not resolved directly by the present mid-fidelity model.
As the static pressure is reduced to 133 kPa, the predicted cavitation-potential ratio exceeds 1 near the
outer blade span, beginning at approximately ‫ݎ‬/ܴ ൎ 0.78. This indicates that the model predicts the
onset of blade cavitation in the outboard region. This result is qualitatively consistent with the
experimental observations, which show blade cavitation inception near the cavitation-breakdown
point at the same static-pressure condition.
When the static pressure is further reduced to 115 kPa, the predicted cavitation-prone region expands
substantially. The model predicts ߪ/ߪୡ୰୧୲ ൒ 1 over approximately the outer 25% of the blade span,
beginning near ‫ݎ‬/ܴ ൎ 0.70 and extending to the blade tip. This trend is again consistent with the
experiment, which shows developed blade cavitation under the lowest static-pressure condition. The
OpenFAST prediction therefore reproduces the expected increase in cavitation risk with decreasing
static pressure and the outboard concentration of blade cavitation.
Overall, the cavitation-potential assessment shows mixed agreement with the physical experiment
results. The model does not capture the observed tip-vortex cavitation at the higher static-pressure
conditions, but it does predict blade cavitation at the lower static-pressure conditions where blade
cavitation was observed experimentally. Thus, the OpenFAST cavitation model appears more suitable
for identifying the onset and growth of blade-surface cavitation risk than for predicting tip-vortex
cavitation inception. For the present flow-confinement validation study, this is still a useful result
because it demonstrates that the model reproduces the correct qualitative trend of increasing blade
cavitation susceptibility as the tunnel static pressure is reduced.

6. CONCLUSIONS
This study details the implementation, verification, and validation of a solid-wall flow-confinement
model in OpenFAST using a three-dimensional quadrilateral source-panel formulation. The model
was developed to represent the confined wall boundary of the GTWT directly within the OLAF
FVW framework, allowing the wall-induced velocity field to influence blade inflow, rotor loading,
and wake convection without relying on empirical blockage corrections as the primary modeling
approach.
Implementation verification showed that the source-panel method requires careful treatment of
panel-normal orientation and global mass conservation. OLAF internally defines the source-panel
normal opposite to the conventional right-hand-rule normal associated with the VTK polygon
connectivity. For the internal cylindrical tunnel wall, the VTK connectivity was therefore defined
such that the conventional right-hand-rule normal points radially outward, yielding an OLAF
internal normal that point radially inward from the wall into the fluid domain. Verification of this
orientation was important because the opposite connectivity produced a nonphysical sink-like
source distribution near the rotor plane.
For the open-ended cylindrical wall-panel representation of the tunnel, a Lagrange-multiplier
constraint was required to remove a nonphysical global source/sink mode. Without this constraint,
the source-panel solution produced an excessive source-strength imbalance, suppressed the
approach flow near the rotor plane, and biased the predicted rotor performance coefficients.
Comparison with the no-flow-confinement baseline further showed that the source-panel wall
model increased the predicted rotor performance coefficients over most of the simulated TSR range,
demonstrating a measurable effect of tunnel-wall confinement on rotor loading. With the constraint
applied, the integrated net source strength was reduced from 17.8989 mଷ /‫ ݏ‬to െ3.95334 ×
10ିଵ଴ mଷ /‫ݏ‬, and the wall no-penetration residual was reduced to numerical precision. These checks
confirmed that the constrained source-panel implementation provides a physically consistent
representation of the solid-wall boundary condition.
Baseline OLAF timestep and wake-parameter sensitivity studies were used to select the underlying
FVW numerical settings before applying the source-panel wall model. The timestep study showed
monotonic convergence of the rotor-integrated performance coefficients, and the 2° rotor-azimuth
increment per time step produced sub-1% GCI values for ‫ܥ‬௉ , ‫ ்ܥ‬, and ‫ܥ‬ொ . The wake-parameter
sensitivity studies showed that a freely convected near-wake extent of 2‫ܦ‬, or 2 rotor revolutions,
and a total near-wake extent of 16‫ܦ‬, or 20 rotor revolutions, provided sufficiently insensitive
baseline OLAF performance predictions while maintaining practical computational cost. These
settings were therefore adopted for the source-panel simulations.
The source-panel wall-resolution dependency study showed that the rotor-integrated quantities were
only weakly sensitive to further wall-panel refinement once the Fine panel resolution was reached.
The convergence behavior was oscillatory, so a formal GCI estimate was not applied. Instead, the
Very Fine case was used as an additional refinement check. The Fine case differed from the Very
Fine case by 0.76% in ‫ܥ‬௉ , 0.45% in ‫ ்ܥ‬, and 0.77% in ‫ܥ‬ொ , while the Coarse case showed larger
differences exceeding 1%. Based on this comparison, the Fine wall-panel resolution was selected for
the validation simulations.

 53

Validation against the USDOE-MHKF1 water-tunnel measurements showed that the source-panel
OpenFAST model produced a systematic shift in the predicted rotor-performance coefficients
consistent with the experimentally observed effect of water-tunnel confinement. Without flow
confinement, the OpenFAST predictions generally remained closer to the blockage-corrected
measurements, whereas inclusion of the source-panel wall model shifted ‫ܥ‬௉ , ‫ ்ܥ‬, and ‫ܥ‬ொ toward the
uncorrected in-tunnel measurements. The agreement was strongest for ‫ܥ‬௉ and ‫ܥ‬ொ , while the
confined prediction tended to overpredict ‫ ்ܥ‬, particularly toward higher TSR. These comparisons
indicate that the source-panel model captures the direction and overall magnitude of the
confinement effect on rotor-integrated performance, although discrepancies remain in the predicted
rotor loading. These remaining differences may be influenced by uncertainties in the blade-section
polar data and other model-form assumptions rather than by the flow-confinement treatment alone.
The LDV velocity-profile comparisons further demonstrated the influence of the flow-confinement
model on the predicted mean flow field. Far upstream of the rotor, both confined and unconfined
OpenFAST solutions remained approximately uniform and differed only modestly. Immediately
upstream of the rotor, both solutions predicted stronger axial induction and substantially larger
vertical-velocity variation than measured, indicating that these near-rotor discrepancies are not
primarily associated with the source-panel wall model. In the radial wake, however, inclusion of flow
confinement systematically increased the predicted axial velocity both within the rotor wake and in
the bypass-flow region outside the rotor projected area, generally shifting the OpenFAST
predictions toward the LDV measurements. The confined solution also improved the predicted
mean vertical velocity over much of the near- and intermediate-wake region.
Despite these improvements, remaining discrepancies were observed in the wake profiles. The
confined solution generally underpredicted the measured axial velocity, particularly in the accelerated
outer-flow region. The absence of tunnel-wall boundary-layer displacement in the inviscid source-
panel formulation may contribute to this difference. Within the rotor wake, the stronger predicted
axial-velocity deficit is also consistent with the higher ‫ ்ܥ‬predicted by OpenFAST. At X = 0.9997‫ܦ‬,
both the confined and unconfined solutions showed substantially faster recovery of the mean
vertical velocity toward zero than observed by LDV, indicating that this downstream discrepancy is
not primarily caused by the flow-confinement treatment. The tower-wake comparison likewise
showed that inclusion of flow confinement substantially improved both axial- and vertical-velocity
predictions relative to the unconfined solution, but significant differences from the LDV
measurements remained. Although the AeroDyn tower potential-flow and tower-shadow models
were active and the tower disturbance was applied to OLAF wake convection, the present mid-
fidelity representation does not explicitly resolve the viscous separation and turbulent wake
generated by the support structure.
The cavitation-potential assessment showed mixed agreement with the experiment. OpenFAST did
not predict blade cavitation at the higher static-pressure conditions of 306 kPa and 202 kPa, even
though the experiment observed intermittent and steady tip-vortex cavitation at those pressures.
This discrepancy is expected because the OpenFAST cavitation check is based on the sectional
blade loading and the minimum pressure coefficient and does not resolve tip-vortex core pressure.
At lower static pressures, the model predicted blade cavitation over the outer blade span. At 133
kPa, the predicted cavitation-potential ratio exceeded the critical value near the outboard blade

 54

region, consistent with the experimentally observed blade cavitation inception near breakdown. At
115 kPa, the predicted cavitation-prone region expanded over approximately the outer 25% of the
blade span, consistent with the experimentally observed developed blade cavitation. Thus, the model
appears more suitable for identifying blade-surface cavitation potential than for predicting tip-vortex
cavitation inception.
Overall, the OpenFAST source-panel model was verified to enforce the solid-wall no-penetration
condition and to remove the nonphysical global source/sink mode associated with the open-ended
wall-panel geometry. Comparison of OpenFAST predictions with and without flow confinement
demonstrated that the source-panel model produces systematic changes in rotor performance and
mean velocity profiles consistent with the dominant effects of solid-wall confinement observed in
the GTWT measurements. The confined predictions generally shifted toward the uncorrected in-
tunnel performance data and improved agreement with the LDV measurements over much of the
rotor wake and tower-wake regions. At the same time, the confined-versus-unconfined comparisons
helped distinguish remaining discrepancies that are not primarily attributable to the source-panel
wall model. These discrepancies include overprediction of rotor thrust and near-rotor cross-flow,
underprediction of the measured outer-region axial velocity, excessive downstream recovery of the
vertical velocity, detailed tower-wake discrepancies, and the inability to resolve tip-vortex core
pressure. The principal remaining limitations are associated with blade-section loading uncertainty
and physics not explicitly represented by the present mid-fidelity formulation, including tunnel-wall
boundary layers, viscous and turbulent wake evolution, detailed support-structure wake dynamics,
and tip-vortex cavitation.
Future work should consider improved characterization of blade-section polars, an effective wall-
boundary-layer or displacement-thickness treatment, improved support-structure wake modeling,
source-panel-enabled timestep and wake-parameter sensitivity studies, and a higher-fidelity treatment
of tip-vortex cavitation.
