Extrahepatic biliary obstruction due to ampullary carcinoma in a dog
Authors: Shushi YAMAMOTO, Toshikazu SAKAI, Kenjiro MIYOSHI, Takaharu ITAMI, Mishio DANJOU, Wang HSINCHEN, Yuji HAMAMOTO, Eunryel NAM, Mitsuhiro ISAKA, Shidow TORISU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoplasia-related extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO) is rare in dogs. An 11-year-old spayed mixed-breed dog had intermittent jaundice for several months. Imaging showed a lesion extending from the distal common bile duct to the major duodenal papilla. To relieve EHBO, transduodenal ampullectomy was performed, followed by partial duodenectomy including the papillary orifice and cholecystoduodenostomy. Histopathological examination revealed ampullary adenocarcinoma with invasion into the duodenal muscularis propria; margins were tumor-free. At >2 years after surgery, metastasis was not detected, and the dog remained clinically well. To our knowledge, this is the first report of canine ampullary carcinoma, suggesting that transduodenal ampullectomy can be useful for both palliative biliary decompression and the assessment of invasion depth to guide subsequent definitive surgery.Keywords: ampullary carcinoma, canine, extrahepatic biliary obstruction, fluctuating jaundice, transduodenal ampullectomy

In dogs, the common causes of extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO) include gallbladder mucoceles, cholelithiasis, bacterial or parasitic cholangitis, pancreatitis, and neoplasia [6, 10, 18], while neoplasia-associated EHBO is not frequently reported. The tumors responsible for EHBO arise either extraluminally (e.g., pancreatic neoplasms compressing the common bile duct [CBD]) or intraluminally within the biliary system. Previous reports have shown that EHBO caused by intraluminal tumors accounts for <10% of all cases, making it particularly uncommon compared with other causes [6]. This phenomenon is related to the rarity of primary biliary tract tumors in dogs, with an incidence rate of approximately 0.2% [9, 22]. Further, Hayes et al. reported that with respect to the distribution of primary biliary tract tumors, lesions arising from the intrahepatic bile ducts were the most frequent, whereas extrahepatic sites accounted for approximately one-quarter of the cases [9]. Among extrahepatic biliary tract tumors in dogs, gallbladder tumors are the most commonly reported, followed by CBD tumors [10]. A broadly similar distribution is observed in humans; however, unlike in dogs, tumors of the major duodenal papilla (ampulla of Vater) account for approximately 10–20% of neoplasms within the extrahepatic biliary system [11, 13, 23]. Because ampullary tumors commonly present with jaundice at the initial evaluation, they are an important differential diagnosis for EHBO [23, 26]. To the best of our knowledge, ampullary tumors have not been previously reported in dogs. Herein, we present a dog that developed EHBO secondary to a tumor arising from the major duodenal papilla.

An 11-year-old spayed female mixed-breed dog exhibited intermittent anorexia and vomiting. The dog’s body weight decreased from 3.90 to 3.06 kg over 3 months (body condition score: 2/5). The primary veterinarian made a diagnosis of EHBO secondary to cholelithiasis and initiated medical therapy. However, the serum total bilirubin levels fluctuated repeatedly (0.4 → 1.0 → 0.4 → 0.9 → 0.6 → 0.9 mg/dL) without sustained improvement, prompting referral.

Upon presentation to our institution (day 1 of admission), physical examination revealed no remarkable abnormalities. Complete blood count and coagulation testing were within reference limits. Serum biochemistry showed the following findings: alanine aminotransferase level, 1,084 U/L; aspartate aminotransferase level, 287 U/L; alkaline phosphatase level, 1,782 U/L; gamma-glutamyl transferase level, 28 U/L; total cholesterol level, 619 mg/dL; total bile acid level, 486.9 μmol/L; total bilirubin level, 0.68 mg/dL; and C-reactive protein level, 2.14 mg/dL. No other clinically significant abnormalities in the remaining serum chemistry analytes were identified. The thoracic and abdominal radiographic imaging findings were unremarkable. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed gallbladder sludge (gallbladder volume: 1.1 mL/kg), dilation of the common bile duct to 6.6 mm, and a hyperechoic intraluminal mass in the distal duct near the papillary orifice (Fig. 1). Taken together, the findings were indicative of obstructive jaundice secondary to a structural lesion of the common bile duct. Despite intravenous fluid therapy, the serum bilirubin levels did not decrease on day 2 of hospitalization, and the dog’s clinical status did not improve. Therefore, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan was performed under general anesthesia, confirming the dilation of the intrahepatic ducts, hepatic ducts, and CBD. It also revealed an approximately 15-mm, contrast-enhanced, dome-shaped lesion extending from the distal CBD to the papillary orifice (Fig. 2A and 2B). No pulmonary metastases or regional lymphadenopathy was identified. To further characterize the lesion and distinguish inflammatory disease from neoplasia, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed after CT scan. Endoscopic evaluation of the duodenum revealed an irregular, elevated lesion at the major duodenal papilla with focal erythema (Fig. 3). Cytologic evaluation of impression smears prepared from tissue obtained using endoscopic biopsy forceps from the major duodenal papilla revealed clusters of atypical epithelial cells, making a diagnosis of inflammation or lymphoma unlikely. As biliary decompression was required, the procedure was converted to an exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperatively, after confirming CBD dilation, a longitudinal antimesenteric duodenotomy was performed to expose the papilla. The papillary orifice was gently dilated with mosquito forceps, allowing passage of a 6-Fr catheter and confirming CBD patency. To obtain diagnostic tissues and relieve biliary obstruction, the major papilla, including the dome-shaped protruding lesion, was excised in a fan-shaped manner with scissors, thereby widening the orifice. In addition to the papillary mucosa, a portion of the underlying duodenal wall including the muscular layer was partially resected to reduce the risk of re-obstruction due to mucosal fusion and to allow histologic assessment of deeper tumor invasion. Care was taken to avoid full-thickness perforation of the duodenum. A 5-mm cotton swab was then passed through the orifice to confirm the adequate luminal width and to ensure that duodenal perforation did not occur (Fig. 4). Portions of the intraluminal distal CBD lesion were sampled to increase diagnostic yield. The duodenotomy was closed, and hepatic biopsy was conducted. Bile culture yielded negative results, while histopathological examination of the papillary and intraductal tissues revealed a malignant epithelial neoplasm forming tubular structures, consistent with adenocarcinoma. Although partial resection of the duodenal wall including the muscular layer was performed, histopathology revealed tumor infiltration into the duodenal muscularis propria within the excised tissue, suggesting residual disease in the adjacent duodenal wall. Liver biopsy showed bile plugs compatible with obstructive cholestasis without significant inflammation or necrosis. Postoperatively, the dog’s clinical status improved, and the serum bilirubin, liver enzyme, and C-reactive protein levels normalized. Follow-up ultrasonography showed a small amount of mixed echogenic materials persisting within the distal duct.

Histopathological examination indicated residual disease in the duodenum, posing a risk of recurrent obstruction with progression. Therefore, a definitive second surgery was elected after consultation with the owner. On Day 41 of admission, under general anesthesia, a repeat contrast-enhanced CT scan was performed, followed by extended surgery. CT scan revealed irregular intraluminal attenuation and mural thickening within the distal 1.5 cm of the common bile duct. In contrast to the first CT scan, a discrete protruding lesion at the CBD orifice was no longer apparent. At the second surgery, the common bile duct (CBD) was carefully dissected from the pancreatic body using a combination of sharp and blunt dissection. Dissection was initiated at the duodenal wall and progressed proximally along the CBD. Based on CT findings, approximately 2 cm of proximal margin from the major duodenal papilla was secured to obtain an adequate margin on the CBD side. During dissection along the pancreatic body while paying close attention to the pancreatic ducts, a tubular structure presumed to be the main pancreatic duct was identified within the operative field and was ligated and transected. The mobilized CBD was then ligated with braided nylon and transected. To determine the caudal duodenal margin, the pancreas was further carefully dissected from the duodenum to approximately 2 cm caudal to the major papilla. The accessory pancreatic duct was not directly visualized in this region. However, preoperative endoscopy had confirmed that the minor papilla was located more than 2 cm distal to the major papilla. Based on this information and the expected anatomical relationship between the papillae, the accessory pancreatic duct and its orifice were considered preserved caudal to the dissection field. Therefore, additional dissection was avoided to minimize surgical trauma. This dissection provided sufficient margins around the ampullary carcinoma, after which the duodenum was transected. A partial duodenectomy including the common bile duct orifice was then performed with approximately 1.5-cm cranial and caudal margins (Fig. 5), followed by end-to-end duodenal anastomosis using 4–0 polydioxanone. As a biliary diversion, a cholecystoduodenostomy was created approximately 5 cm distal to the anastomosis. Then, an abdominal drain was placed. Postoperatively, a transient ileus was noted for several days. No bile leakage, pancreatic fistula, or anastomotic dehiscence was detected, and the dog was discharged. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen showed a tumor extending from the papillary mucosal surface into the bile duct and the surrounding duodenal muscular layer, confirming the final diagnosis of ampullary adenocarcinoma (Fig. 6A and 6B). No tumor cells were present at the CBD or duodenal resection margins. A discrete main pancreatic duct was not identified, thereby indicating an underdeveloped main duct in this dog. Although variation in pancreatic ductal anatomy exists in dogs, the accessory pancreatic duct typically serves as the primary drainage pathway. Because the accessory pancreatic duct was preserved in this case, pancreatic exocrine drainage was considered to be adequately maintained, and no clinical evidence of pancreatic complications was observed during long-term follow-up. At the time of writing (>2 years postoperatively), no metastasis has been detected, and the clinical course remained favorable.

Primary tumors of canine CBD are rare; the previously reported types included adenocarcinoma, adenoma, and squamous cell carcinoma [9, 10]. An adenocarcinoma arising from the major duodenal papilla and extending into the distal CBD, as observed in the present case, has not been previously reported in a dog. In humans, ampullary tumors are rare, accounting for approximately 0.2% of all gastrointestinal cancers [23]. However, their incidence has increased in recent decades [17, 23]. Typical symptoms include nonspecific findings such as weight loss, anorexia, and vomiting, and a high frequency of jaundice. Unlike the typically progressive jaundice caused by other gastrointestinal tumors, ampullary tumors in humans may present with intermittent resolution and recurrence of jaundice in up to 30% of cases (“fluctuating jaundice”) [3, 4]. The proposed mechanisms include temporary relief of obstruction due to intratumoral necrosis and sloughing of tumor fragments or intermittent obstruction from tumor fragments, mucus, or blood clots [4, 7]. Considering that the tumor in this dog extended into the distal CBD, fluctuating jaundice was most plausibly explained by the transient relief associated with tumor sloughing. These findings suggest that, even in dogs, an ampullary tumor should be considered when fluctuating obstructive jaundice is present.

To diagnose ampullary tumors, ultrasonography, contrast-enhanced CT scan, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography are suitable screening tools. For definitive diagnosis and local staging in human medicine, endoscopic biopsy, endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), and intraductal ultrasonography (IDUS) have a high diagnostic performance [14, 19, 25]. Endoscopic macroscopic classification into four morphological categories (protruded, mixed, ulcerated, and special types) is correlated with depth of invasion and prognosis; ulcerated lesions generally exhibit deeper invasion, poorer differentiation, and more frequent nodal metastasis [2, 14]. In our case, no overt ulceration was identified endoscopically, and the lesion was most consistent with an exposed protruded type. In humans, exposed protruded lesions often remain between the duodenal mucosa and the sphincter of Oddi. Further, they can have favorable postoperative outcomes (5-year survival rate: 75%). These findings support the role of endoscopic biopsy and papillectomy as standard diagnostic and therapeutic approaches [5, 27]. However, duodenal wall invasion has been reported in approximately 27% of exposed protruded lesions [2], thereby indicating that morphology alone may be insufficient for accurate staging. Moreover, several series have reported that endoscopic biopsy with false-negative rates exceeding 20% has a modest diagnostic accuracy [5, 12, 16]. Based on these data, endoscopic assessment and endoscopic resection alone may underestimate malignancy or depth of invasion. If endoscopic sampling is inconclusive or insufficient for assessing invasion, transduodenal surgical ampullectomy is a reasonable diagnostic and therapeutic option for ampullary lesions [8, 12, 24]. In this case, although endoscopic cytology suggested adenocarcinoma, endoscopic samples are typically superficial and therefore limited in their ability to assess invasion depth into the duodenal wall and surrounding structures. In ampullary tumors, invasion depth directly influences the required surgical margins and extent of resection; thus, definitive surgical planning based solely on endoscopic findings can be difficult. Moreover, the patient had a prolonged clinical course with progressive deterioration and fluctuating jaundice, necessitating prompt biliary decompression. Accordingly, although biliary decompression was the immediate objective, transduodenal surgical ampullectomy—beyond superficial mucosal excision—allowed acquisition of tissue sufficient to confirm muscularis invasion histologically and informed the subsequent decision to proceed with definitive extended resection. In the absence of routine EUS/IDUS for local staging in veterinary practice, transduodenal surgical ampullectomy remains an important option for canine ampullary tumors, thereby allowing an accurate histologic diagnosis, assessment of invasion depth, and palliative biliary decompression.

Human ampullary adenocarcinoma is broadly categorized as intestinal (INT) or pancreatobiliary (PB) type based on histopathology [5, 23]. INT tumors exhibit colorectal-like tubular to villous architectures, often with goblet cell differentiation and mucin production, indicating a duodenal intestinal epithelial origin. PB tumors comprise cuboidal to columnar cells forming small tubular to papillary structures resembling pancreaticobiliary carcinomas, thereby suggesting an origin from the distal pancreatic duct or CBD epithelium [15, 20, 23]. This histologic classification is correlated with invasion and metastatic patterns and has prognostic significance. In the current case, the tumor comprised biliary-like mucosa, and the malignant epithelial component formed tubular-to-papillary architectures. Accordingly, the lesion was confirmed to originate from the distal bile duct or pancreatic duct epithelium. Further, immunohistochemical staining applied according to the human pathology criteria revealed cytokeratin 7 positivity and cytokeratin 20 negativity, thereby confirming the PB type (Fig. 6C and 6D). In humans, compared with INT tumors, the PB-type tumors exhibit a higher frequency of lymph node metastasis and a more invasive course [28], and the PB type is considered an independent adverse prognostic factor [5, 28]. Nevertheless, margin-negative resection with adequate clearance is associated with improved overall survival [1, 5, 21]. The tumor exhibited an invasive behavior consistent with the pancreatobiliary subtype. However, extended resection achieving histologically clear margins of approximately 4–5 mm may have contributed to the favorable outcome. Further, despite invasion into the duodenal muscularis propria, the absence of nodal and distant metastases may have supported durable survival without adjuvant therapy.

This report has a limitation. As only a single case was examined, the extent to which human-based classifications and clinical behavior translate to canine disease remains uncertain.

In conclusion, EHBO attributed to a primary CBD/ampullary tumor is rare in dogs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an ampullary tumor (adenocarcinoma) in a dog. If jaundice alternates between exacerbation and remission rather than showing relentless progression, EHBO due to an ampullary tumor should be considered. This case indicates that transduodenal surgical ampullectomy facilitates an accurate histologic diagnosis and depth assessment. Moreover, if appropriate, definitive surgery with adequate margins can yield favorable long-term outcomes in dogs without metastasis at presentation.
