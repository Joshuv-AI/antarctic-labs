Dynamic Multiform Fat Grafting for Facial Rejuvenation
Authors: Beibei Ren, Tongrong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Traditional fat grafting techniques predominantly rely on static assessment and homogeneous fat filling, yet clinical practice has revealed limitations such as poor adaptability to dynamic expressions and unnatural contour transitions. Recent studies have demonstrated that centrifugally stratified fat layers exhibit distinct biological properties. However, achieving harmonious integration of volumetric restoration and dynamic expression through precise dynamic evaluation combined with layered and progressive grafting of multimorphological fat remains a critical clinical challenge. Building on facial dynamized anatomy, this study proposes a dynamic assessment–multimorphological fat grafting strategy, aiming to establish a personalized rejuvenation protocol aligned with facial biomechanical principles.Methods:A total of 305 patients (19–56 y old) were enrolled from December 2019 to February 2023. Fat harvested from the thighs or abdomen was processed into 3 forms: high-density macrofat, high-density microfat, and stromal vascular fraction gel. These were injected dynamically into multiple facial regions, angles, depths, and layers using specialized cannulas. Follow-up ranged from 6 months to 3 years to assess clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction.Results:All 305 patients showed significant improvement in facial volume loss, skin elasticity, and fine wrinkles, achieving a rounded and youthful facial contour. Patient satisfaction rates were high, with 95.1% reporting satisfaction and 4.9% indicating basic satisfaction. No complications such as nodules or cysts were observed.Conclusions:Dynamic multiform fat grafting demonstrates high efficacy and patient satisfaction, offering natural and harmonious outcomes.

Takeaways

Question: How can limitations of traditional static fat grafting (unnatural contours during facial expressions) be overcome through dynamic assessment and multiform fat application?

Findings: In 305 patients, dynamic preoperative marking during expressions (eg, smiling, gazing) guided layered injection of centrifugally processed fat: high-density macrofat (structural support), microfat (dynamic zones), and stromal vascular fraction gel (skin rejuvenation). This achieved 95.1% patient satisfaction, restored volume/skin quality, and prevented complications (nodules, overfilling).

Meaning: Dynamic multiform fat grafting harmonizes facial restoration with natural movement, offering personalized, biomechanically aligned rejuvenation.

INTRODUCTION

Autologous fat grafting (AFG) is a widely used technique in reconstructive and aesthetic surgery, recognized as an ideal filler material due to its excellent biocompatibility, ease of harvest, natural results, and potential for long-term facial rejuvenation.1 In recent years, the application of AFG has evolved beyond simple volume restoration to a dual function encompassing “structural” grafting for volume and “regenerative” grafting, which utilizes stem cell components within fat to improve skin quality and promote tissue repair.2 However, a significant clinical challenge in AFG is “facial overfilled syndrome” (FOS), characterized by an unnaturally bloated or puffy appearance and restricted facial expression.3 The root cause often lies in practitioners adhering to an outdated static paradigm of volume loss, employing homogeneous filling techniques while neglecting the dynamic nature of facial expression and the influence of gravity on facial anatomy.4 Modern anatomical studies highlight a functional distinction between deep static fat compartments providing structural support and superficial dynamic fat layers that accommodate facial movement.5 Furthermore, postural changes (eg, from upright to supine) cause significant displacement of superficial fat compartments.6 Consequently, fat grafting performed based solely on assessment in the supine position can lead to unnatural results when the patient is upright or animated. To address these limitations, this study proposes a “dynamic assessment–multiform fat grafting” strategy. This approach begins with a dynamic evaluation, considering the effects of expression and gravity on soft tissues. The harvested fat is then processed into three distinct forms: large-particle fat for deep structural support, microparticle fat for buffering and filling in highly mobile areas, and stromal vascular fraction (SVF) gel for skin quality improvement. By precisely transplanting different fat forms according to specific anatomical layers and functional requirements, this strategy aims to achieve more natural and harmonious facial rejuvenation outcomes.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This study included a total of 305 patients from December 2019 to February 2023, comprising 295 women and 10 men, 19 to 56 years of age. All participants were in good general health with no systemic or psychiatric disorders. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Subjects were provided a detailed description of the study and all provided written consent.

Surgical Technique

Under sufficient indoor lighting, the patient was seated and instructed to maintain a forward gaze. The surgeon sat at eye level with the patient to observe facial skeletal and soft tissue morphology. The soft tissue deficits and dynamic interactions with surrounding tissues were meticulously assessed in various positions: frontal, lateral, upward gaze, downward gaze, and during facial expressions (eg, looking upward/downward, laughing, mouth opening). Targeted filling areas and estimated fat volumes were precisely marked using a surgical pen. (See Video 1 [online], which displays dynamic expression-guided preoperative marking).

The patient was placed in the supine position under local anesthesia. The donor site can be the thighs or the abdomen. Tumescent solution (0.9% sodium chloride 1000 mL + 2% lidocaine 20 mL + 0.1% adrenaline 1 mL) was infiltrated into the donor area. Fat was harvested using a 3.0-mm multiporous cannula, with a side hole measuring 2.5 mm long by 1.5 mm wide, under low negative pressure manual suction, collected in 50-mL syringes, and set aside for processing.

The harvested fat was allowed to settle. Fat aspirated with the 3.0-mm cannula was centrifuged at 1200g for 3 minutes. The lower layer of tumescent fluid was discarded, and the lower one-third of the fat was collected as high-density macrofat.7 This macrofat was processed 3 times through a fat extraction device to yield high-density microfat. The upper two-thirds of the fat and oil layer were repeatedly passed through a 1.4-mm adapter for 1 minute, then centrifuged at 1600g for 3 minutes. The viscous substance beneath the oil layer was collected as SVF gel.8

Nerve block anesthesia was administered at the zygomaticotemporal, supraorbital, supratrochlear, infraorbital, and mental nerves, with 1.0–1.5 mL of 0.5% lidocaine (containing 1:200,000 adrenaline) per site. Following nerve blocks, a 27-gauge blunt needle was used to inject tumescent solution subperiosteally and into the superficial subcutaneous layer via fan-shaped infiltration to promote vasoconstriction and tissue separation. Total tumescent solution volume did not exceed 15 mL.

Each fat type was loaded into 1-mL spiral syringes for injection. High-density macrofat was injected using a 1.2-mm single-port blunt cannula, primarily targeting large facial areas such as the frontotemporal region, hairline, anterior cheek (malar region), nasolabial folds, lateral cheeks, buccal area, chin, mandibular border, and nasal dorsum. High-density microfat was delivered via a 0.8 mm cannula for precision in delicate areas, including the tear troughs, upper eyelid sulcus, columella, earlobes, malar festoons, and vermilion lips. SVF gel was administered subdermally using a 1.0-mm blunt cannula to improve skin texture or intradermally with a 27G sharp needle for fine lines (eg, eyelid bags, dark circles, forehead wrinkles, crow’s feet, glabellar lines, nasal dorsum wrinkles, perioral lines, neck lines), lip borders, acne scars, and depressed scars.

The patient is placed in the supine position, and facial disinfection and anesthesia are administered. Initially, approximately 70% to 80% of the estimated total fat volume is injected into the target areas. A critical step follows before injecting the remaining volume: the patient is instructed to perform a series of standardized facial expressions sequentially, such as smiling, puffing the cheeks, and grimacing. The surgeon closely observes the coordination of superficial fat pads and muscles during these movements, assessing for irregularities like dimpling, abnormal bulging, or restricted animation, and marks areas requiring adjustment. The patient is then assisted into a 90-degree upright sitting position. In this posture, the surgeon and patient jointly observe the facial contour lines’ smoothness from multiple angles (frontal, 45-degree, and 90-degree lateral views) using a mirror. The patient repeats key expressions like smiling to evaluate morphological changes in areas like the nasolabial folds and tear troughs under gravity’s influence, precisely identifying points needing supplemental injection or refinement. Upon returning the patient to the supine or semirecumbent position (back elevated 30–45 degrees), the remaining fat is used for precise microadjustments based on the sitting assessment feedback. This final stage focuses on “sculpting” rather than significant volume addition—addressing minor irregularities visible upright, reinforcing support in dynamic areas, and refining key transition zones to ensure natural animation. The principle of “small amounts, multiple times, gradual approximation” is followed throughout. Before the sitting assessment and final injection, the patient is encouraged to use a mirror to express their perceptions and expectations. The surgeon explains observations and planned adjustments professionally. This open communication manages expectations effectively and enhances the patient’s understanding and satisfaction with the outcome and their dynamic aesthetics. All injections are performed using a fanning retrograde technique, with microaliquots of 0.03–0.05 mL per pass to construct a 3-dimensional fat matrix network. The remaining SVF gel is cryopreserved and can be used for subsequent injections 1 to 3 months postoperatively. Specifications: syringes are placed in 5-mL sterile sealable plastic bags labeled with the patient’s name, date, and ID number, then stored frozen in a −20°C freezer.

The liposuction areas were dressed with sterile cotton pads and compression bandages, whereas erythromycin ophthalmic ointment was applied to the injection sites in the filled regions. Patients were prescribed oral antibiotics for 2 days to prevent infection. For the first postoperative month, they were advised to maintain regular daily routines, avoid dieting or fat-reducing medications, and refrain from excessive facial expressions. Pressure or massage on the filled areas was strictly prohibited during this period to protect the newly formed vasculature. Individuals dissatisfied with the initial results could receive supplemental injections of stored SVF gel within 3 months postoperatively. The postoperative follow-up plan is scheduled at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and then once a year thereafter, until 36 months after the surgery. At the last follow-up visit (ie, 6 months after the surgery or later), the patient was required to complete a satisfaction survey questionnaire. The satisfaction survey uses a Likert 5-point scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied), and the satisfaction rate = (number of responses rated 4–5)/total sample size × 100%.

RESULTS

From December 2019 to February 2023, a total of 305 patients (295 women, 10 men) 19 to 56 years of age (mean age: 35 y) were included. The average operative time ranged from 75 to 160 minutes (mean: 110 min), with lipoaspirate volumes of 120–300 mL (mean: 180 mL). The average total single-session facial fat grafting volume was 35.2 ± 8.1 mL, distributed as follows: high-density macrofat: 65.0 ± 5.0%, SVF gel: 25.0 ± 5.0%, and high-density microfat: 12.5 ± 2.5%. The satisfaction assessment conducted at an average follow-up period of 18.5 ± 6.2 months after the surgery showed that the average satisfaction score was 4.5 ± 0.5. Specifically, 95.1% of the patients reported as “satisfied” (4 points) or “very satisfied “(5 points), whereas 4.9% of the patients reported as “basically satisfied” (3 points). No induration, cysts, or complications were observed in the grafted areas.In total, 15% of patients underwent secondary SVF gel injections (12.3 ± 5.4 mL) within 3 months postoperatively, with no complications reported from cryopreserved SVF gel. One male patient experienced suboptimal results due to insufficient autologous fat volume for facial augmentation. In case 2, due to long-term acne on the mid-face skin, there were scars. The skin elasticity and tension were insufficient, and the limited space under the skin prevented the achievement of the expected injection volume, resulting in suboptimal results.

DISCUSSION

The dynamic assessment and multiform fat grafting strategy proposed in this study represents a significant breakthrough beyond traditional static filling. Its core innovation lies in the “synergistic integration” of concepts, aiming to elevate facial rejuvenation from mere “volume restoration” to “functional and dynamic aesthetic reconstruction.” This technique employs a dynamic multiposition assessment and precision injection strategy. By incorporating postural changes and observing real-time dynamic changes in facial tissues under the combined effects of gravity and mimetic muscles, the method addresses the “mismatch” between intrinsic emotional states and external facial expressions caused by aging.9 It shifts the goal of modern facial filling toward “restoring the authenticity of emotional signals,” carrying profound psychotherapeutic significance.

The anatomical root of FOS lies in deep, inappropriate filling anchoring the transverse facial septum, which is connected to the major smiling muscle (zygomaticus major).4 To prevent this, a dynamic filling strategy is essential. This involves repeatedly asking the patient to perform expressions like smiling during the injection process, observing the filler distribution in real-time to ensure harmony with facial dynamics and nonobstruction of the transverse facial septum-zygomaticus major complex movement. Particularly in areas with very thin skin, like the tear trough, placing static structural fat above or below the highly dynamic orbicularis oculi muscle can lead to visible nodules and irregularities when the muscle contracts and compresses the fat.10 Dynamic filling, based on a deep understanding of muscular movement, guides the amount and location of filling to prevent this dynamic conflict. Similarly, assessment of the lower face third must be dynamic. For example, evaluating the ability to pout can reveal structural loss (eg, muscle and fat) due to perioral aging, beyond just skin laxity.

The Coleman centrifugation technique separates fat into layers of different cellular densities. The high-density bottom layer, richest in viable adipocytes, SVF, and adipose-derived stem cells, offers optimal postgrafting volume retention and is suitable for refined facial filling.7 In the multiform fat strategy, based on preoperative dynamic assessment, the face is divided into a “static support zone” and a “dynamic expression zone.” The static support zone (eg, areas for deep volume restoration and bony contour support) primarily uses high-density macrofat (1.5–2 mm diameter) as a structural “scaffold” for volume augmentation in deep fat compartments. The dynamic expression zone (eg, areas with significant movement or fold formation during expression, like smiling or blinking) primarily uses high-density microfat or SVF gel. High-density microfat (0.5–1 mm) serves as a “dynamic buffer layer,” injected into the superficial subcutaneous or intramuscular plane to accommodate muscle movement. SVF gel is injected into the subdermal or dermal layer to improve skin quality and provide soft filling, avoiding the stiffness or nodules associated with traditional large-particle fat. For instance, in the eyelid-cheek junction, microfat is strictly placed in deep layers (eg, suborbicularis oculi fat pad or supraperiosteal plane) to prevent clumping in this thin-skinned area. Although microfat adipocytes are smaller, postgrafting cell structure remains normal, and survival rates are comparable to traditional techniques.11 SVF gel is not a traditional filler but a regenerative biomaterial rich in adipose-derived stem cells and extracellular matrix.12 Its components can induce neoangiogenesis13 and have been proven to significantly increase dermal thickness,14 fundamentally improving skin quality. Comparative clinical studies even found SVF gel’s long-term volume retention rate in the temples exceeds that of Coleman fat,15 suggesting its effects may stem more from “tissue regeneration” than “tissue transplantation.” Therefore, subdermal injection of SVF gel can improve both retention and skin texture. Furthermore, SVF gel can be injected through fine-gauge needles into the dermis to improve fine wrinkles and atrophic scars. Additionally, SVF gel has been reported as an excellent candidate for cryopreservation; surplus gel can be stored frozen for up to 3 months,16 allowing for touch-up procedures without repeated liposuction.

CONCLUSIONS

The dynamic assessment–multi-form fat grafting strategy represents a significant paradigm shift in facial rejuvenation, moving beyond traditional static volume restoration to functional and dynamic aesthetic reconstruction. By integrating postural changes and real-time expression analysis into the surgical plan, this approach effectively addresses the limitations of static filling, such as unnatural contour transitions and FOS. The precise stratification of high-density macrofat for structural support, microfat for dynamic buffering, and SVF gel for skin quality improvement ensures harmonious integration with facial biomechanics. Clinical outcomes in 305 patients demonstrated high efficacy and satisfaction rates (95.1%) with no observed complications, validating the safety and predictability of the technique. Although the study is limited by its single-center design and varying follow-up durations, the findings highlight the critical importance of restoring the authenticity of emotional signals in facial rejuvenation. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized quality of life scales are recommended to further validate these physiological and psychosocial benefits.
