Association between physical activity and sociodemographic and behavioural factors among physical education students at a Brazilian public university
Authors: Jean Carlos de Goveia, Eric Willian Gomes Moreira, Thaiane Moleta Vargas, Bruno Pedroso, Leandro Martinez Vargas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to analyze the prevalence of physical activity and its association with sociodemographic factors and lifestyle indicators among physical education students at a Brazilian public university.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional observational study with a quantitative approach. A total of 190 students enrolled in on-site undergraduate programs (teacher training and bachelor’s degree) participated, with a mean age of 21.2 ± 4.5 years; 52.1% were men and 47.9% were women. Data were collected in person using an electronic form (Google Forms) comprising sociodemographic variables, physical activity level (IPAQ-short version), and the Fantastic Lifestyle questionnaire. The primary outcome was physical activity level, classified as active (≥150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity [MVPA]) or inactive (<150 min/week of MVPA). Sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle-related variables, including overall lifestyle, diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, leisure, sleep, and health perception, were examined as independent variables associated with physical activity level. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0, employing descriptive statistics, chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests, and binary logistic regression (Odds Ratio – OR; 95%CI; p ≤ 0.05).ResultsAmong participants, 94.2% were classified as physically active; 82.6% presented an adequate lifestyle; and 37.9% were classified as overweight or obese according to BMI. No significant associations were observed between physical activity level and sociodemographic or behavioural variables, except for health perception, which was significantly associated with physical inactivity. Negative health perception was the only factor associated with physical inactivity (OR = 14.30; 95%CI: 2.48–82.22; p = 0.003), suggesting that subjective health perception may be more strongly associated with physical inactivity than sociodemographic or behavioural characteristics among future health-promotion professionals.ConclusionsIn summary, physical education students demonstrated a high prevalence of physical activity and a predominantly adequate lifestyle. Negative health perception was the only factor associated with physical inactivity. Future investigations are encouraged to include additional variables to provide a broader analytical perspective.

Introduction

Physical activity is globally recognized as one of the pillars of health promotion and the prevention of non-communicable chronic diseases. It may be defined as any voluntary movement performed by the human body that results in energy expenditure above resting levels, including active transport, household tasks, occupational activities, and leisure pursuits [1]. Despite the widely documented benefits, data from the World Health Organization indicate that 31% of the world’s population does not reach the minimum recommended levels of physical activity (150 min per week of moderate intensity or 75 min of vigorous intensity), thus constituting an important risk factor for the development of non-communicable chronic diseases [2].

In the university context, multiple socioeconomic and demographic factors influence health-related behaviours, such as sex, age group, household income, working hours, and the academic environment itself. The demands of university life, combined with increasing autonomy, changes in daily routines, and rising responsibilities, may contribute to risk behaviours, including physical inactivity, poor diet, substance use, and reduced sleep and leisure time [3]. These behavioural patterns have been observed across different university settings, where factors such as stress, eating habits, and low engagement in physical activity also stand out as recurrent challenges during academic training [4].

Beyond sociodemographic determinants, studies involving Brazilian university students have shown that the motives underlying exercise practice vary according to individual experience and relate differently to adherence and maintenance of physical activity. Initially, extrinsic motives (such as physical fitness, weight control, appearance) tend to be more influential, while intrinsic motives (stress regulation, enjoyment/well-being, affiliation) become more prominent among more experienced practitioners [5].

Among physical education students, however, this relationship may present specific features. A systematic review conducted by Silva et al. [6] found that students in this field generally achieve satisfactory or above-recommended weekly levels of physical activity, largely due to the practical and physically engaging nature of the curriculum. Nevertheless, studies indicate the coexistence of risk behaviours — such as stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms — even in courses oriented towards health promotion [7].

At an international level, few studies have examined the prevalence of physical activity and its associated factors specifically among Physical Education students. Most research involving university populations treats students as a homogeneous group, without considering curricular, contextual, and sociodemographic particularities that may influence health behaviours. Unlike students from other academic disciplines, Physical Education students are continuously exposed to knowledge and experiences related to physical activity, health promotion, and healthy lifestyle behaviours throughout their academic training. Despite this expected exposure, evidence regarding the prevalence of physical activity and associated factors in this population remains limited.

Furthermore, few studies incorporate multivariate analyses that simultaneously consider motivation, lifestyle, sleep patterns, and self-rated health [4, 8, 9]. This gap limits the understanding of determinants of active behaviour within a group that, although exposed to bodily practices through their training, also experiences academic stressors, irregular habits, and psychosocial challenges that exert a complex influence on the adoption and maintenance of healthy behaviours. Therefore, investigating this population may contribute to a better understanding of health behaviours among future health and education professionals and support the development of pedagogical, curricular, and institutional strategies aimed at promoting health in the university setting.

The study was guided by the hypothesis that most Physical Education students would present adequate levels of physical activity — the primary outcome — yet still maintain health risk behaviours that characterise an inadequate lifestyle, such as insufficient sleep, inadequate diet, alcohol consumption, and dissatisfaction with one’s own health.

In light of the above, the present study aimed to analyse the prevalence of physical activity and its association with sociodemographic factors and lifestyle indicators among physical education students from a Brazilian public university.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an observational, cross-sectional study with a quantitative approach, conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [10].

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the State University of Ponta Grossa (CEP-UEPG) under reference number CAE 68362023.5.0000.0105 on 25 October 2024, and all participants signed an informed consent form prior to their participation. All data were handled confidentially and used exclusively for scientific purposes.

Sample and participants

The study was conducted at the State University of Ponta Grossa (UEPG), located in the municipality of Ponta Grossa, Paraná, Brazil, a medium-sized city with 358,371 inhabitants [11]. According to UEPG’s 2023 institutional report, approximately 6,885 students were regularly enrolled in on-site undergraduate programs distributed across 37 courses, of which 180 were enrolled in the bachelor’s degree and 189 in the teacher training program in physical education [12].

A non-probabilistic convenience sampling approach was adopted, comprising students from both Physical Education degree programmes. All students enrolled during the data collection period were considered eligible. Inclusion criteria were: (a) being aged 18 years or older; (b) being enrolled in either the Bachelor’s or Teacher Training programme. Students who did not complete the questionnaire or did not provide consent were excluded from participation.

The minimum sample size was estimated using the Raosoft Sample Size Calculator (Available at: http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html), considering an expected proportion of active students of 70%, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a target population of 369 students. The minimum required sample size was 189 participants. The final sample consisted of 190 students, thus meeting the minimum required sample size.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted online during June 2025 using an electronic questionnaire developed on the Google Forms® platform (https://forms.gle/Nda67xRNYrddwJrG9). The survey link was distributed through class representatives via WhatsApp® groups and remained available throughout the data collection period. Participation was voluntary, and the average completion time was approximately ten minutes.

On the initial page of the electronic form, participants were provided with the informed consent form, which described in detail the study’s objectives, procedures, and characteristics. Only after reading and agreeing to the consent form was the questionnaire made available. The instrument consisted of three distinct sections, each accompanied by a brief introductory explanation to facilitate understanding of the items presented.

The first section included questions related to individual variables such as sex, age, course, year of study, programme modality (bachelor’s or teacher training), body weight (kg), and height (metres). Additional information was collected regarding marital status (single, married, divorced, cohabiting, or widowed) and family income, with all responses presented in a multiple-choice format. Socioeconomic variables followed the economic classification criteria proposed by the Brazilian Association of Research Companies (ABEP) [13].

The second section of the questionnaire employed the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), previously validated in young Brazilian adults [14, 15]. The analysis focused on the frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day) of walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity physical activities performed over the previous seven days.

The final section comprised the “Fantastic Lifestyle” instrument, translated and validated in Brazil by Añes, Petroski and Reis [16], which assesses key elements characterizing a health-promoting lifestyle. The instrument consists of 25 items distributed across nine domains (family and friends, physical activity, nutrition, tobacco and drugs, alcohol, sleep, behaviour type, introspection, and work), with scores ranging from 0 to 100 points.

Instruments and variables

The primary outcome of this study was the level of physical activity, categorized as active or inactive in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines [1]. Total weekly minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) were calculated based on the reported duration and frequency of the activities. Following WHO recommendations, participants were classified as active if they achieved at least 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity (including walking), at least 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of both per week; otherwise, they were classified as inactive.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). According to World Health Organization criteria, participants were classified as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), or obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). For analytical purposes, BMI was subsequently dichotomized as < 25 kg/m² (underweight/normal weight) and ≥ 25 kg/m² (overweight/obesity) [17].

The secondary outcomes included lifestyle and its behavioural components (diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, leisure, and sleep), assessed using the Fantastic Lifestyle instrument [16]. Thus, individuals classified as “Excellent”, “Very good”, and “Good” (≥ 55 points) were categorized as having an adequate lifestyle, whereas those in the “Fair” and “Needs improvement” categories (< 55 points) were considered to have an inadequate lifestyle. This decision aimed to enhance statistical consistency in the association analyses, reduce dispersion across categories with limited representativeness, and facilitate the interpretation of the findings without compromising the validity of the instrument.

The dichotomization strategy adopted in the present study was based on previous epidemiological investigations involving university students that used the Fantastic Lifestyle instrument and grouped the categories “Excellent”, “Very Good”, and “Good” as indicators of a positive lifestyle profile, whereas “Fair” and “Needs Improvement” represented lifestyle patterns requiring behavioural changes. This approach has been employed to facilitate statistical modelling and improve interpretability when some categories present a limited number of observations [18].

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 21.0 (IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which allowed the use of parametric statistics. Continuous variables were described as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies.

Associations between physical activity level (active/inactive) and the independent variables were tested using Pearson’s chi-square test. In cases where one or more cells had fewer than five observations (n < 5). Fisher’s exact test was applied where appropriate to ensure valid statistical comparisons.

Subsequently, associations between physical inactivity (< 150 min/week of MVPA) and the secondary outcomes were examined. Multivariate analysis was conducted using binary logistic regression to estimate the strength and direction of the associations. Estimates were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and a significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was adopted. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in the bivariate analyses were included in the final multiple regression model (forward conditional). The final multivariate model was adjusted for sex, age, workload, satisfaction with health, and sleep quality. The reference category corresponded to the group with the lowest proportion of physical inactivity, according to the criteria described by Vargas et al. [18].

Results

A total of 369 students enrolled in the Physical Education programmes were invited to participate in the study through online dissemination of the questionnaire link. Of these, 190 students completed the survey and were included in the final analysis, corresponding to an overall participation rate of 51.5%. No questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete responses. Participation rates were 62.8% among Bachelor’s degree students (113/180) and 40.7% among Teacher Training students (77/189).

The mean age was 21.2 ± 4.5 years. There was a slight predominance of males (52.1%). Most participants were single/divorced, were enrolled in the bachelor’s program (59.5%), and reported low family income (93.2%). With regard to nutritional status, 37.9% presented a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m².

The other variables analyzed—marital status, study/workload, leisure, and sleep pattern—did not show a significant association with insufficient physical activity after adjustment, indicating an absence of independent effects on the outcome. When examining the results, it was observed that although a study/workload of ≥ 6 h per day was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of physical inactivity in the crude analysis (OR = 2.98; 95% CI: 0.73–12.16; p = 0.045), this relationship did not remain statistically significant in the adjusted multivariate model (OR = 2.31; 95% CI: 0.48–11.16; p = 0.296). This statistical attenuation indicates that the initial unadjusted effect of workload was likely confounded or shared with other behavioral and psychosocial variables simultaneously entered into the regression model.

The findings indicate that negative health perception was the variable most strongly associated with physical inactivity in the studied population. In contrast, the sociodemographic and behavioural variables did not demonstrate any significant influence on the levels of physical activity within the context investigated. Although the magnitude of the association was substantial, the wide confidence interval suggests considerable uncertainty around the estimate, likely due to the low number of inactive participants.

Discussion

This study analyzed the prevalence of physical activity and the association between sociodemographic factors, lifestyle indicators, and failure to meet the physical activity levels recommended by the World Health Organization among students enrolled in Physical Education programs at a Brazilian public university. The results showed a high prevalence of physical activity in the sample; however, they also identified that negative health perception was associated with not meeting the recommended levels. No significant associations were observed with sex, marital status, family income, study/workload, or BMI.

These findings partially support the study hypothesis. As expected, most Physical Education students demonstrated adequate levels of physical activity; however, contrary to our initial expectations, lifestyle-related behaviours such as diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, leisure, and sleep were not significantly associated with physical inactivity. This suggests that, within this relatively homogeneous academic population, subjective health perception may play a more relevant role than other behavioural characteristics in explaining differences in physical activity levels.

Although BMI was not significantly associated with physical activity level, it is noteworthy that 37.9% of the students were classified as overweight or obese. Considering that the sample consisted of future Physical Education professionals, this proportion deserves attention. Similar trends have been observed nationwide, as recent data from the VIGITEL surveillance system indicate that 37.4% of Brazilian young adults aged 18–24 years present with excess body weight [19]. This alignment suggests that engagement in physical activity alone may not be sufficient to prevent excess body weight. Factors such as dietary habits, sedentary behaviour outside exercise sessions, sleep patterns, and metabolic characteristics may also contribute to weight status. However, the interpretation of BMI among physically active populations should be made cautiously, as this index does not distinguish fat mass from lean mass and may therefore overestimate excess weight in individuals with greater muscular development. Future studies incorporating direct measures of body composition may provide a more accurate assessment of nutritional status in this population.

The prevalence of physically active students observed in the present study (94.2%) was higher than that reported in most investigations involving university students and even exceeded estimates reported among physical education students in previous Brazilian studies. One possible explanation is the use of self-reported measures, which are susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. Because participants were enrolled in physical education programmes and were professionally exposed to health-promotion concepts, they may have overestimated their physical activity levels. Therefore, the prevalence reported should be interpreted cautiously and not assumed to reflect objectively measured activity patterns.

With regard to lifestyle, most students (82.6%) were classified as having an “adequate” lifestyle, indicating healthy behaviours not only in terms of physical activity but also in aspects such as diet, sleep, and alcohol consumption. Similar results were reported by Ribeiro et al. [20], who identified that more than half of health-area students presented a “good” lifestyle, and by Mota et al. [21], who highlighted the influence of everyday factors on the adoption of healthy habits.

Among physical education students, Vargas et al. [22] observed that 95.7% displayed an adequate lifestyle, emphasizing the importance of social relationships, regular sleep, stress management, and satisfaction with work. Accordingly, the evidence from the present study confirms that physical education students tend to adopt healthier lifestyles; however, challenges remain, particularly concerning diet and certain risk behaviours, reinforcing the need for educational and health-promotion strategies within the academic environment.

Beyond these findings, studies suggest that internal differences may also exist among physical education students according to the level of physical activity they engage in. Stachoń and Pietraszewska [23] reported that students more engaged in vigorous activities had lower body fat percentages compared with those who performed activities of moderate intensity. These results indicate that, even within programs with a strong practical component, the intensity and pattern of participation may influence several health indicators in different ways.

Regarding the associated factors, no significant differences were found for sex, marital status, study/workload, family income, or body mass index (BMI). These findings partially contrast with the literature, which frequently reports a higher prevalence of physical inactivity among women [24, 25]. This lack of sex-based divergence may be underpinned by the specific pedagogical and practical structure of the Physical Education curriculum. Unlike other academic fields, this graduation presents a highly active matrix, requiring both male and female students to participate equally in extensive practical and sports-based modules. This routine imposes a high baseline of mandatory physical exertion that effectively attenuates the gender gaps typically observed in general university populations, where structural or cultural barriers often disproportionately restrict women’s leisure-time physical activity. Likewise, working more than six hours per day did not prove to be a determinant factor for an inactive lifestyle, a result consistent with the findings of Vargas et al. [22].

Taken together, these findings suggest that, even among future health-promotion professionals who are extensively exposed to physical activity, health education, and exercise-related knowledge throughout their academic training, subjective health perception may be more relevant in explaining physical inactivity than traditional sociodemographic or behavioural characteristics. This observation reinforces the importance of considering psychological and perceptual dimensions when investigating health behaviours in university populations.

The absence of associations identified in this study may be related to the relatively homogeneous profile of the sample — composed predominantly of young, single students within the same field of study. This context tends to reduce differences between groups, which may explain the lack of variables significantly associated with lifestyle. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that, in university populations with similar characteristics, factors such as sex, income, or workload may exert limited influence on health-related behaviour patterns.

A relevant finding was that, even among students classified as physically active, some reported being dissatisfied with their own health. This indicates that regular physical activity, although essential, does not in itself guarantee a positive perception of health. This result is consistent with investigations showing that negative self-rated health is associated with lower engagement in physical activity [26], as well as with systematic reviews highlighting the central role of health perception in maintaining active behaviours [27]. Thus, health perception can be understood as a multidimensional phenomenon, reinforcing the need for strategies that encompass not only physical practice but also the promotion of students’ overall well-being.

This result highlights the relevance of subjective and psychosocial dimensions in understanding differences in physical activity behaviours, as demonstrated by recent studies showing that exercise self-efficacy mediates the relationship between motivation and physical activity level [28, 29]. Individuals reporting negative health perceptions are more likely to present lower confidence regarding engagement in regular physical activity, as well as greater vulnerability to stressors and fatigue, which interfere with the maintenance of an active lifestyle [30].

In line with this, studies highlight that self-rated health functions as an integrative marker of physical and mental well-being and is consistently associated with motivation and adherence to exercise practice [31, 32]. Therefore, strategies to promote physical activity in university settings should include interventions that foster subjective well-being, stress management, and the strengthening of self-efficacy, complementing traditional actions focused solely on observable behaviours.

Another relevant point is that, although most participants reported an adequate lifestyle, maintaining such patterns depends on institutional policies that provide psychosocial support and on extension programs integrating physical activity, mental health, and quality of life within the university environment [8]. These actions are aligned with the recommendations of the World Health Organization and with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, particularly Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 – Good Health and Well-Being and 4 – Quality Education, which advocate the creation of healthy, inclusive and equitable academic environments [2, 33].

In this context, addressing the active profile of future physical educators becomes a strategic imperative. For SDG 3, institutional tracking of physical activity and self-rated health mitigates metabolic and mental health risks early in adult life, fostering healthier campus communities. Concurrently, this interfaces directly with SDG 4, as physically active university students are pedagogically better positioned to act as future multipliers of high-quality health literacy. Upon entering the school system, these healthy professionals will be better equipped to implement sustainable active behaviors and promote equity in well-being within the communities they will eventually lead.

Among the limitations of the study, its cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between the variables analyzed. Additionally, self-reported information may be subject to recall bias and overestimation of physical activity. Another limiting aspect is the absence of psychological variables (such as stress, anxiety, or depressive symptoms) in the analytical model, which could have broadened the understanding of the relationship between health perception and active behaviour. It should also be considered that the sample consisted exclusively of Physical Education students — a relatively homogeneous group, naturally more exposed, through training and professional identity, to content emphasizing the importance of physical activity — which may limit the generalizability of the findings to students from other academic fields.

An important limitation concerns the small number of physically inactive students identified in the sample (n = 11), resulting in a low events-per-variable ratio in the multivariate model. Consequently, the adjusted estimates should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future studies with larger and more heterogeneous samples are needed to confirm these associations.

Although physical activity was assessed using a self-reported instrument and may therefore be susceptible to recall and social desirability biases, the prevalence observed in the present study is consistent with previous investigations involving Physical Education students, which have reported prevalence values ranging from 71.5% to 100% [6]. Therefore, while the possibility of overestimation cannot be completely ruled out, the findings appear compatible with those reported in similar populations evaluated using the same instrument.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the scientific community by providing empirical evidence on sociodemographic and behavioural factors associated with physical activity among physical education students, a group often under-represented in research on university health. The findings broaden understanding of the role of health perception as a mediating variable between behaviour and lifestyle, suggesting that positive self-perception may act as a protective factor against inactivity.

In practical terms, the findings reinforce the need for institutional strategies that integrate actions to promote physical activity, mental health, and social well-being in the university context, such as psychosocial support programs, physical exercise initiatives, the creation of active environments, and continuous educational actions. Such measures may enhance the training of future professionals who are better prepared to promote healthy behaviours across a variety of population contexts.

Collectively, the results indicate that subjective health perception may represent a more sensitive indicator of vulnerability to physical inactivity than demographic or behavioural characteristics among Physical Education students. This finding broadens the understanding of physical activity determinants in future health-promotion professionals and highlights the importance of integrating psychosocial dimensions into university health-promotion strategies.

Conclusion

Physical Education students from this Brazilian public university presented a high prevalence of physical activity and a predominantly adequate lifestyle profile. Sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and most behavioural variables were not significantly associated with physical inactivity.

Among all investigated factors, negative self-rated health was the variable most strongly associated with not meeting the recommended levels of physical activity. This finding suggests that subjective health perception may represent an important correlate of physical inactivity within this population.

From a practical perspective, university administrators and public health professionals should complement physical activity promotion initiatives with actions aimed at strengthening students’ positive health perception and overall well-being, thereby supporting the maintenance of active lifestyles among future health-promotion professionals.
