The complement system in Alzheimer’s disease: evaluating biomarker potential in a complex neuroimmune context
Authors: Heli Soni, Greg T. Sutherland, Markus J. Hofer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammatory processes are increasingly recognised as important modulators of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression, driving interest in immune-related biomarkers beyond classical pathological measures. Among these, the complement system has attracted attention because of its interactions with amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau pathology, genetic associations with AD risk, and evidence of activation within affected brain regions. However, these biological observations do not directly translate into straightforward biomarker signals. Complement activity is highly dynamic, spans multiple activation and regulatory states, and may reflect both central and peripheral immune processes. This complexity limits interpretation when complement markers are assessed in isolation, as age, systemic inflammation, vascular comorbidity, and blood–brain barrier integrity can influence measured levels. Current evidence does not support complement-derived biomarkers as stand-alone diagnostic classifiers comparable to established amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration (AT(N)) measures. Their independent or additive value within multimodal biomarker frameworks remains unclear, partly because of cohort heterogeneity, incomplete assay harmonisation, uncertain tissue-source attribution, and limited longitudinal validation. This review critically evaluates complement-derived measures as biologically informative markers of neuroimmune activity in AD, distinguishing biological plausibility from analytical and clinical utility. We argue that their most defensible current role is within multimodal biomarker frameworks, where they may provide context-specific information on inflammatory state rather than function as independent diagnostic, staging, or treatment-monitoring tools. Progress toward clinical application will require rigorous standardisation, mechanistic clarification, and validation across large, longitudinal, and diverse cohorts.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is biologically defined by the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles in the brain, which remain central to neuropathological diagnosis [1–3]. These pathological features also underpin contemporary biological classification frameworks, including the amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration (AT(N)) framework [4]. Advances in positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assays have enabled in vivo detection and biological classification across the AD continuum [5, 6]. More recently, imaging-based staging frameworks integrating amyloid and tau PET have demonstrated that pathological burden progresses across biologically distinct phases rather than a single uniform trajectory [7].

Despite these advances, AT(N) measures do not fully account for the marked inter-individual variability observed in clinical progression, regional vulnerability, and overall disease trajectory among individuals with comparable pathological burden [8, 9]. To address this gap, there has been increasing interest in fluid-based biomarkers that reflect broader aspects of AD pathobiology beyond core protein aggregation, including neuronal stress, synaptic dysfunction, glial activation, and inflammatory processes. However, many of these candidate biomarkers remain limited by poor disease specificity and interpretability [10–17]. For example, neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels in blood and CSF increase across multiple neurodegenerative conditions [18–20], while circulating glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) largely reflects astrocytic reactivity rather than AD-specific pathology [21, 22]. Consequently, there remains a need to critically evaluate biomarkers that may provide additional biological context beyond established amyloid and tau-based measures.

Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognised as a contributor to AD pathobiology, with glial responses implicated in synaptic integrity, protein clearance, and tissue resilience. However, inflammatory biomarkers measured in blood or CSF often represent convergent downstream signals from multiple upstream pathways, limiting their mechanistic interpretability [23, 24]. For example, a pilot study reported increased matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) activity in AD dementia, but its diagnostic specificity and interpretability require further validation [25]. Similarly, higher CSF chitinase-3-like protein 1 (YKL-40) levels have been associated with tau-related pathology, longitudinal cognitive decline, and regional brain atrophy. Nonetheless, CSF YKL-40 likely reflects broader astrocytic and inflammatory activity rather than a discrete AD-specific mechanism [26].

These limitations highlight the need to assess immune pathways with plausible mechanistic links to AD pathology, while recognising the challenges associated with translating such pathways into reliable biomarkers. Among these, the complement system has emerged as a biologically relevant pathway linking classical AD pathology with neuroimmune responses [27, 28]. Beyond its canonical role in innate immunity, complement components are active within the central nervous system (CNS), where receptor-mediated signalling can influence cellular activation, synaptic homeostasis, and inflammatory states [29–31]. Studies of AD brain tissue have localised C1q in association with pathological features, while recent fluid biomarker evidence links CSF C1q with neuroinflammatory markers and amyloid–tau measures [32, 33]. Experimental work further suggests that activated microglia can induce reactive astrocyte phenotypes through secreted factors, including C1q, supporting a potential role for complement-associated glial crosstalk beyond host defence [34]. Complement activity has also been reported in association with Aβ plaques, tau pathology, and complement-related genetic risk loci, including C1, C1q, and clusterin (CLU) in AD risk [35], further supporting disease relevance [36, 37]. Activation through the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways converges on shared effector molecules, including C3a, C5a, and the membrane attack complex (MAC), which may exert context-dependent cytotoxic or regulatory effects [38–40]. However, the magnitude, temporal dynamics, and functional consequences of complement activation in human AD remain incompletely resolved [41].

Importantly, while complement biology provides a strong mechanistic rationale, its translation into clinically informative biomarkers remains uncertain. Recent reviews by Papavergi et al. and Tenner and Petrisko have provided broad overviews of complement involvement in AD, spanning genetic, mechanistic, pathological, and therapeutic perspectives [28, 42]. Building on these contributions, the present review focuses specifically on the biomarker relevance of complement-derived measures within the contemporary AD landscape, with particular emphasis on interpretability, methodological limitations, and their potential value alongside established measures. We evaluate complement components by examining their biological rationale, methodological constraints, and report performance relative to established imaging and fluid biomarker modalities. We also briefly consider emerging complement-targeted therapeutic strategies in the context of biomarker development and associated translational challenges. Particular emphasis is placed on whether complement measures provide additional biological information beyond existing frameworks, or whether they currently remain biologically informative but translationally constrained signals.

Local complement activation in AD: biological plausibility and biomarker complexity

The complement system was historically regarded as a peripheral immune defence mechanism [43], until key components of this coordinated innate immune network were identified within the CNS [44]. Although the liver is the principal systemic source of circulating complement proteins, accumulating experimental evidence indicates that neurons [45], astrocytes [46], and microglia [47] can locally express or synthesise selected complement-related molecules (Table 1), supporting the capacity for complement activity within the brain parenchyma [66]. Complement receptor 1 (CR1) expression has also been reported in human glial cells and brain tissue, further supporting the presence of complement-associated signalling pathways in the adult CNS [53]. However, whether such expression reflects constitutive physiological activity, reactive induction or both remains incompletely resolved, as much of the evidence derives from model systems [67, 68] or post-mortem tissue [69] in which pathological processes are already established [70–72].

Experimental studies support a physiological role for complement in synaptic refinement and immune surveillance within the developing brain [73–80]. In particular, C1q and C3-deficient mouse models exhibit impaired synapse elimination [77], while C3–CR3 signalling has been implicated in activity-dependent microglial engulfment of weaker synapses [78, 79, 81]. Although these findings establish a developmental function, their relevance to adult human disease remains uncertain. In AD, classical pathway components and terminal complement complexes have been reported in association with Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in various cellular and molecular [82, 83] as well as post-mortem brain tissue [69, 84, 85], a finding that has been consistently interpreted in subsequent reviews of complement biology in AD [48, 86, 87]. While these observations indicate local engagement of the complement cascade within pathological microenvironments, they do not resolve whether such activation is protective, maladaptive, disease-stage dependent, or context specific.

This ambiguity reflects the tightly regulated nature of complement biology. Under physiological conditions, complement activity is controlled by regulatory proteins such as C1-inhibitor and CD59 [88]. However, the presence of complement regulators in AD tissue despite ongoing activation suggests that regulatory mechanisms may be insufficient to restrain local complement activity under pathological conditions fully [89]. Experimental AD models suggest that astrocyte-derived C3 and complement receptor signalling (C3aR) can influence amyloid-related pathology, synaptic integrity, and glial responses, although these findings may not fully translate to human AD [90, 91]. In addition, studies using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived glial cells have suggested that AD-associated variants, such as CR1*2, may alter phagocytic responses to opsonised targets, including Aβ aggregates and synaptic material, despite reduced receptor expression [92]. These findings suggest that complement-related genetic variation may influence disease risk by modulating glial function [92]. These mechanistic and tissue-based findings support the biological plausibility of complement involvement in AD, but they do not directly establish whether complement-derived measures in CSF or blood provide analytically reliable, disease-specific, or clinically additive biomarker information.

From a biomarker perspective, this biological complexity introduces a fundamental challenge. Complement activation does not represent a single uniform process but encompasses overlapping physiological, compensatory, and potentially pathogenic states that may coexist across brain regions and disease stages (Fig. 1). As a result, complement measurements in CSF or blood are unlikely to reflect a discrete pathological signal. Instead, measured concentrations may represent a composite of central synthesis, local activation, peripheral immune activity, blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and systemic inflammatory responses [93, 94]. However, this heterogeneity may also represent biologically meaningful variation rather than non-specific noise. Studies have reported associations between complement-related measures and AD diagnosis, disease progression, or AD-related biomarker profiles in CSF and blood [95, 96]. Nonetheless, these findings remain context-dependent and require further validation across larger, longitudinal, and better-standardised cohorts. In this context, the dynamic and context-dependent nature of complement signalling may capture aspects of neuroimmune activity that are not directly reflected by core amyloid and tau-based measures. Accordingly, complement is best understood not as a static biomarker pathway, but as a context-dependent regulator of neuroimmune processes whose measurable signals may vary across physiological and pathological states [97]. Understanding how this complexity shapes CSF and blood measurements will be critical for determining whether complement-derived measures can be reliably incorporated into multimodal AD biomarker frameworks.

Mechanistic complexity of complement activation and biomarker interpretation

Evidence for complement involvement in AD derives from several sources, including post-mortem human tissue studies, experimental mouse and cellular models, genetic association studies, and CSF or plasma biomarker analyses. These evidence streams collectively support the biological relevance of complement in AD, but they differ substantially in their capacity to establish causality, temporal sequence, and clinical interpretability.

A substantial body of literature has examined complement involvement in AD, particularly its association with core pathological features and neuroinflammatory processes [23, 36, 42, 72, 98]. Experimental and post-mortem studies have reported localisation of complement components, including C1q, C3, C5a and MAC, in association with Aβ plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and dystrophic neurites, supporting local complement engagement within AD [99–102]. In particular, neuritic plaques containing dystrophic neurites have been identified as potent triggers of complement activation via the classical and alternative pathways, particularly through C1q and C3 mechanisms [33, 103–108]. Collectively, these findings indicate that complement activation in AD is not a uniform process but varies across pathological contexts.

In some experimental contexts, complement activation may exert protective or compensatory effects by supporting microglial clearance of misfolded proteins, apoptotic debris, and damaged synapses [109]. Comparable mechanisms have been described in other neurological conditions, such as multiple sclerosis, where complement-mediated opsonisation facilitates phagocytic clearance [110]. For example, C1q has been reported to attenuate Aβ-induced neurotoxicity through induction of the neuronal receptors low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1B (LRP1B) and G-protein-coupled receptor 6 (GPR6), in transgenic AD mouse models [111]. Similarly, C3a/C3aR signalling has been implicated in regulating tau phosphorylation via the glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) pathway and cyclin-dependent kinase5 (p35/CDK5) signalling, with modulation of C3aR activity altering tau-associated toxicity in experimental systems [112, 113]. However, these observations are largely derived from in vitro systems and transgenic models, which may not fully recapitulate cellular complexity, regional heterogeneity, or temporal progression of pathology in the human brain.

Conversely, in settings of greater pathological burden, sustained or dysregulated complement activation has been associated with maladaptive outcomes in experimental and post-mortem studies [100, 114]. Signalling through the anaphylatoxin receptors C3aR and C5aR1 has been implicated in these processes, with prolonged activation linked to microglial reactivity, cytokine production, and synaptic dysfunction in experimental models [90, 115–120]. Pharmacological inhibition of C5aR1 attenuates neuroinflammation and synaptic loss in transgenic mice [68, 121], whereas disruption of C3aR signalling produces context-dependent effects, including reductions in tau pathology and improvements in cognitive performance [57, 112]. These findings suggest that complement-associated pathology may be governed not only by the magnitude of activation, but also by receptor-specific signalling, disease stage, cellular context, and regulatory control.

Together, these observations indicate that complement activity in AD is context-dependent rather than uniformly protective or pathogenic. This variability, shaped by cellular environment, regional pathology, and disease progression, complicates both biomarker interpretation and therapeutic targeting. While such context dependence may reflect underlying biological heterogeneity, it remains unclear whether this variability can be reliably captured or applied for disease stratification in clinical settings [122].

Methodological considerations in interpreting complement biomarkers

Interpretation of complement biomarkers in AD is strongly influenced by methodological context, because different experimental approaches capture distinct and often non-equivalent aspects of complement biology, as reviewed recently [42]. Immunohistochemical studies in post-mortem human tissue provide spatial insight into complement deposition within pathological structures, including plaque-associated complement signal, glial proximity, and regional heterogeneity. However, these studies remain inherently cross-sectional and do not, by themselves, resolve functional activation status or temporal sequence [123–125]. In contrast, biofluid-based studies directly quantified soluble complement proteins and activation fragments in CSF or plasma, with markers such as C1q, C3, FH and CLU showing cohort-dependent associations with disease status and progression, albeit with considerable variability [126, 127]. Broader biomarker reviews further emphasise differences in analytical platforms and clinical interpretation [128]. In addition, experimental models, including chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, demonstrate that complement activation can occur alongside other inflammatory and vascular cascades, underscoring its context-dependent nature and limited disease specificity [129].

Molecular and transcriptomics approaches introduce an additional layer of complexity. Studies examining astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles have reported elevated complement-related proteins, including C1q, C3d, C4b, and factor D, in AD-associated samples [130]. Single-nucleus and spatial transcriptomic analyses have further identified complement biosynthesis and regulatory signatures within the AD brain microenvironment, including distinct expression patterns in microglia and astrocytes, with validation in iPSC-derived microglial models [131]. Other studies have also identified plaque-associated glial niches characterised by distinct gene expression profiles, including enrichment of complement-related pathways (C4b, C5) in microglia and astrocytes, as well as local expression of C1q and C3 surrounding Aβ deposits [132, 133]. Similarly, region-specific analyses have suggested that complement-associated gene expression varies across brain regions (e.g., differential transcriptional responses in hippocampal and cortical regions, including the inferior temporal cortex), reflecting local responses to pathology [134–136]. However, these approaches measure gene expression or compartment-specific protein content rather than proteolytic activation of the complement cascade, limiting inference of functional activity. Thus, molecular evidence of complement expression or pathway enrichment should not be treated as equivalent to evidence of proteolytic complement activation or functional pathway activity. Consequently, the high spatial and cellular specificity observed in tissue-level analyses is not readily captured in bulk CSF or plasma measurements.

Accurate interpretation, therefore, requires careful antibody validation and distinction between total complement proteins, activation fragments, and terminal pathway products, as emphasised by Tenner et al. [28]. Complement proteins are also particularly susceptible to ex vivo activation during sample collection and processing, increasing the risk of artefactual signal generation [137, 138]. Collectively, these factors contribute to substantial heterogeneity across studies, with observed variability reflecting methodological differences as well as underlying disease biology. As such, complement biomarkers should be interpreted as composite and context-dependent signals whose comparability, reproducibility, and mechanistic interpretation depend on assay design, sample handling, biological matrix, and clinical context.

Complement biomarkers in AD: biological relevance and interpretive challenges

Biomarker research in AD has expanded substantially, with numerous candidates proposed to address unresolved gaps in disease characterisation [139]. Although Aβ and tau biomarkers reliably quantify pathological burden, they do not fully explain variability in clinical progression, regional vulnerability, or downstream neurodegeneration [140–142]. Individuals with comparable amyloid and tau burden may show markedly different rates of cognitive decline, reflecting that Aβ and tau biomarkers account for only a modest proportion of clinical variability (~ 20–40%) [143]. Even comprehensive neuropathological assessments explain only ~ 40% of the variance in cognitive decline, leaving a substantial proportion of clinical variability unexplained [144].

This limitation has driven interest in additional biomarker domains, including markers of neurodegeneration, glial activation, vascular injury, and immune signalling, which may capture aspects of disease biology not represented within conventional amyloid and tau-centred frameworks [10, 23, 128, 145]. Within this landscape, complement-derived measures may provide information about immune-regulatory activity that intersects with Aβ pathology, tau pathology, glial responses, vascular dysfunction, and systemic inflammation [42, 95]. The comparison highlights important differences in the biological meaning and interpretive maturity of current biomarker classes. Aβ and tau measures remain central to contemporary AD biological classification because they index core pathological features of the disease. However, their interpretation is still shaped by disease stage, assay modality, mixed pathology, and clinical context [139, 197, 198]. Tau markers, particularly tau PET and selected plasma phosphorylated tau species, generally show closer associations with disease progression and cognitive impairment than amyloid measures alone, although performance varies by marker, platform, and cohort characteristics [169, 178, 189, 198, 199]. In contrast, neuronal injury and glial biomarkers extend the biomarker framework by indexing downstream damage or cellular reactivity, but they lack sufficient disease specificity to function as independent AD diagnostic classifiers [17, 19, 175, 185].

Complement-derived measures occupy a different position within this framework. They do not replace amyloid or tau biomarkers as classifiers of core AD pathology, and current evidence does not support their use as standalone diagnostic, staging, stratification, or treatment-monitoring tools [28, 42, 95, 176]. Similar to glial biomarkers, complement-derived measures are biologically informative but not AD-specific when interpreted in isolation. Their potential value lies in capturing an immune-regulatory dimension of AD pathobiology that is not fully represented by amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration, or glial reactivity markers alone. Selected studies report associations between complement proteins or activation-related measures and AD-related pathological markers, cognitive measures, or prodromal conversion, including multimarker complement panels [32, 41, 81, 95, 96, 137]. However, these findings remain observational, cohort-dependent, and incompletely replicated across larger longitudinal and clinically diverse populations.

Several methodological and biological factors limit interpretation. CSF complement measures are more proximal to CNS processes, whereas plasma measures offer greater scalability. However, both are affected by assay variability, uncertain tissue-source attribution, BBB, and systemic inflammatory or vascular influences [173, 188]. These limitations mean that diagnostic performance should not be conflated with biological relevance. For example, plasma GFAP may show useful associations with preclinical amyloid positivity [11, 157, 195], whereas complement alterations may provide information about immune activity within pathology-positive individuals [139]; however, neither class should be interpreted as AD-specific in isolation.

Collectively, the evidence supports a layered interpretation of AD biomarkers rather than a linear hierarchy of superiority (Fig. 2). In this model, core proteinopathy markers define amyloid and tau status; neurodegeneration markers reflect injury burden; glial markers capture cellular reactivity; and complement-derived measures may provide contextual information about immune-regulatory activity. Disease heterogeneity occurs both within AD and across overlapping neurodegenerative conditions, where mixed pathologies and comorbid vascular or inflammatory processes may influence clinical presentation and progression [48, 200–202]. Importantly, complement may offer pathway-relevant information linked to defined immune mechanisms, including opsonisation, synaptic tagging, anaphylatoxin signalling, and terminal pathway activation [16, 95, 99, 112, 117, 120, 176]. However, pathway relevance does not establish biomarker readiness. At present, complement-derived measures are best interpreted as biologically informative, translationally constrained, and potentially adjunctive markers of neuroimmune activity within multimodal biomarker models, rather than as independent diagnostic or stage-defining measures.

Complement pathways as emerging therapeutic targets

Complement activation has been investigated as a potential therapeutic target in AD because of its involvement in neuroinflammatory signalling, glial responses, and synaptic regulation in experimental disease models [102]. Preclinical studies suggest that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of upstream complement components, including C1q and C3, can alter microglial-mediated synaptic loss and inflammatory signalling [39, 203]. Similarly, blockade of downstream receptors, including C3aR and C5aR1, has been reported to modulate microglial activation and synaptic pathology in models of amyloid or tau-related disease [112, 117, 119, 121, 204]. Antagonists targeting the C5a–C5aR1 axis, including PMX53 and the brain-penetrant PMX205, have shown beneficial effects in experimental systems [28, 68, 109, 203]. However, these findings remain predominantly preclinical and do not establish clinical efficacy in human AD.

Complement signalling also interacts with other inducible innate immune pathways implicated in AD. For example, type-I interferon signalling has been shown to drive microglia-mediated neuroinflammation and synapse loss in AD mouse models [205], and may regulate complement gene expression or amplify inflammatory responses within the CNS under pathological conditions [206]. Baseline transcriptomic datasets from healthy human brain tissue [207–209] indicate low expression of type-I interferon-related genes across most cell types, suggesting that IFN-I signalling is unlikely to represent a constitutive feature of the healthy CNS. Instead, evidence from AD models suggests that type-I interferon pathways are inducible and emerge in response to pathological stimuli, including amyloid deposition and neuroinflammatory activation [210]. These observations support a broader view of complement as part of an inducible neuroimmune network rather than an isolated therapeutic pathway [211].

The therapeutic challenge lies in selectively modulating pathogenic complement activity while preserving its physiological roles in immune surveillance and debris clearance. This challenge is compounded by activation-state heterogeneity, as complement function may vary across disease stages, cellular contexts, regulatory states and brain regions [41, 203]. Evidence from atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, and multiple sclerosis shows that complement can be therapeutically tractable in some disease contexts [110, 212–214]. However, these examples should not be overextended to AD, where pathology is chronic, spatially heterogeneous, and strongly influenced by ageing, mixed pathology, BBB integrity, and peripheral immune status.

Overall, complement-targeted approaches remain biologically plausible and experimentally supported but clinically unresolved in AD. Future work will need to determine which complement targets are most relevant, when intervention is likely to be beneficial, and whether therapeutic effects depend on disease stage, anatomical region, cellular source of complement activation, or preservation of physiological complement functions.

Conclusion and future directions

AD remains biologically anchored in amyloid and tau pathology, but these proteinopathies do not operate in isolation. Disease expression is shaped by a wider network of glial, vascular, immune, metabolic, and genetic influences that contribute to variability in progression and clinical presentation. Within this broader neurobiological landscape, complement signalling provides a mechanistically plausible link between protein aggregation, synaptic vulnerability, and neuroinflammatory activity. Yet, the same dynamic and context-dependent biology that makes complement relevant to AD also limits its translation into clinically interpretable biomarker signals.

This review critically evaluated the evidence for complement-derived measures as candidate biomarkers in AD. Current evidence does not support complement-derived measures as replacements for established AT(N) biomarkers or as primary diagnostic classifiers. Instead, they are best regarded as biologically informative but interpretively constrained indicators of neuroimmune activity. Their potential value lies in providing contextual information about immune-regulatory processes alongside established pathological, neurodegenerative, glial, and vascular markers; however, their independent or additive clinical value remains unresolved.

Resolving this uncertainty will require improved temporal, spatial, and biochemical resolution of complement activity in AD. A major barrier to translation is biological interpretability. Complement components exist in multiple activation and regulatory states, and fluid-based measurements may compress dynamic immune processes into single quantitative readouts. Observed alterations may therefore reflect local complement activation, compensatory immune regulation, systemic inflammatory activity, BBB transfer, or a combination of these processes. Without improved resolution of activation-state heterogeneity across disease stages, tissue compartments, and biological matrices, complement-derived measures remain difficult to interpret as disease-specific biomarker signals.

Future studies should prioritise longitudinal, multimodal, and methodologically harmonised designs. These studies should distinguish total complement proteins from activation fragments and terminal pathway products, account for BBB integrity, systemic inflammation, vascular comorbidity, age, APOE genotype, and assay platform, and test whether complement-derived measures add information beyond established AD biomarkers. Regional human tissue studies and controlled human model systems, including iPSC-derived co-cultures or organoid platforms incorporating glial and vascular components, may also help clarify the anatomical and temporal context of complement activation, although their translational relevance will require careful validation.

In summary, complement-derived measures should not be positioned as competing or standalone diagnostic biomarkers for AD. Their most defensible role at present is as candidate adjunctive markers of neuroimmune activity whose value depends on improved biological resolution, analytical standardisation, longitudinal validation, and demonstration of added information within multimodal biomarker frameworks. As complement-targeted therapeutic strategies continue to be explored, future studies may examine whether complement measures indicate pathway engagement in research settings. However, their use for patient stratification or treatment monitoring in AD remains unproven and should not be assumed without prospective validation.
