Lutein biosynthesis and pigment dynamics in a partially chlorophyll-deficient mutant of Chlorella sp. under autotrophic, mixotrophic, and heterotrophic cultivation
Authors: Manlin Yan, Ting Liu, Xupeng Cao, Quanyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe color of microalgae is related to the components and contents of pigments. Yellow microalgae have a low chlorophyll content, which is helpful for obtaining high-purity lutein during downstream extraction. Chlorophyll and lutein content changes under different culture modes if chlorophyll biosynthesis is not fully destroyed. Low chlorophyll content improved the purity of the final lutein product. It is not fully known about the lutein biosynthesis and pigment dynamics in yellow microalgae under different culture modes.ResultsA yellow algal mutant Chlorella sp. M04 strain was obtained via chemical mutagenesis. The effects of the culture mode (autotrophic, mixotrophic, and heterotrophic) and carbon source were evaluated on the original strain (Chlorella sp. AE10), and the mutant. The ratios of lutein contents to (Chl a + Chl b) contents in the wild type and the mutant were 1:3.5 and 1:0.79, respectively. The highest lutein production observed in the original strain was 3.87 mg/L/d at day 3 under mixotrophic conditions. The lutein productivity of the mutant under heterotrophic conditions was 1.88 mg/L/d on day 2, which was higher than that under autotrophic and mixotrophic conditions. The chlorophyll-a content of the mutant (Chlorella sp. M04) decreased by 88.8% of chlorophyll-a content of the original strain, and chlorophyll b of the mutant was 7.2% of that of the original one under mixotrophic conditions on day 5. Glucose was found to be more suitable than acetic acid for the mutant. Comparative transcriptomic analysis was performed for the original and mutant strains, particularly for the biosynthesis pathways of chlorophyll and lutein.ConclusionThe advantage of this mutant is its low chlorophyll content, making it suitable for downstream processes. This study provides new insights into the metabolic regulation of chlorophyll and lutein production in microalgae.Graphical Abstract

Background

Lutein is an important pigment and natural antioxidant [1]. In the human body, it mainly accumulates in the eye and is an important constituent pigment of the macula, which is very essential for human health [2, 3]. Lutein supplementation can protect brain nerves and prevent various eye and other diseases caused by oxidative damage [4]. There is no natural way to synthesize lutein in the human body; it can only be obtained from vegetables and fruits consumed daily, with more lutein found in egg yolks and vegetables [5, 6]. Lutein is mainly derived from marigold flowers. Microalgae have been found to have the potential to produce lutein with faster growth rates and higher levels of free lutein than marigolds [7]. Microalgal lutein production can improve environmental sustainability [8]. Therefore, lutein production by microalgae has attracted increasing attention.

Screening for suitable algal species and optimizing culture conditions are critical for lutein production by microalgae. Microalgal culture modes are classified as autotrophs, mixotrophs, and heterotrophs [9]. Heterotrophy promotes the growth of microalgae when lutein content is low [10]. Therefore, a two-stage process was developed [11–13]. The first stage is autotrophy or mixotrophy, and the second stage is heterotrophy. Microalgae use fish farming wastewater [14], Monascus fermentation broth [15], and food waste hydrolysate [16–18] to produce lutein via autotrophy, mixotrophy, and heterotrophy. The lutein content was 17.4 mg/g under optimal mixotrophic conditions [19]. Lutein content is species-specific. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the effects of culture mode on lutein content.

Several methods have been developed to improve the metabolic phenotype of the lutein produced by microalgae. Synthetic biology and metabolic engineering are often used to improve lutein production [20–22]. Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) is a biotechnological tool used to develop desirable traits in industrial micro-organisms, including microalgae [23–25]. It is difficult to construct lutein-producing algal strains using synthetic biology methods, because genome information and genome editing tools are limited. ALE is time-consuming and requires a well-designed methodology to improve the lutein content in microalgae. Mutagenesis breeding is a simple and easily manipulated method for obtaining large numbers of mutants in a short period of time. Specifically, cells are exposed to physical or chemical mutagens, and mutants with the desired phenotype are selected [26]. N-methyl-N′-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) was used to increase the lutein content of Chlorella sorokiniana [27]. Atmospheric room-temperature plasma (ARTP) has recently been used to construct several novel yellow microalgal strains [28–30]. High-purity lutein can be more easily extracted/purified from yellow microalgae [30, 31]. The protein content was 44.2–63.2% [31–33]. They have the potential to produce both lutein and protein.

In general, mutagenesis will destroy the pigment biosynthesis [34, 35]. The color of microalgae is related to the content of chlorophyll, lutein, and other pigments. These mutants may have partial chlorophyll activity. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the dynamic processes involved in pigment biosynthesis during cultivation. In a previous study, the Chlorella sp. AE10 was obtained and the lutein content was about 9.58 mg/g combined with 850 μmol/m2/s, 20% CO2, nitrogen starvation and blue light under autotrophic conditions [36]. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the lutein production potential of Chlorella sp. of AE10 under different culture conditions. The second step was the construction of yellow and lutein-producing microalgae. Random mutagenesis may lead to a mutant with a high lutein content, but this is not easy. In this study, we aimed to obtain a yellow strain with low chlorophyll content and not a mutant with higher lutein content than the wild type. Lutein production was investigated under autotrophic, mixotrophic, and heterotrophic conditions for both the original and mutant strains. The third objective was to explore the changes in chlorophyll and lutein content under different culture modes. Comparative transcriptomic analyses were performed to elucidate underlying possible molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Materials

MNNG was purchased from Mackin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chlorella sp. AE10 was obtained via adaptive evolution [25].

Chemical mutagenesis

The initial biomass concentration of the original microalgal strain was 0.15 g/L, the concentration of MNNG was 0.5 g/L, and the mutagenesis time was 1 h. The specific operation was as follows: centrifuging the algal liquid, adjusting the concentration of the algal biomass to 0.15 g/L, adding the mutagen, and mutagenizing under dark conditions; after the end of the mutagenicity, the cells were washed with fresh medium several times to remove the residual mutagen, and cultured in fresh medium under dark conditions for 24 h, and then cultured on BG11 plates containing 0.5% glucose. After 24 h, the plates were inoculated under low-light conditions (< 50 μmol/m2/s). When algal colonies grew on the plates, yellow or yellow–green colonies were screened and cultured. Only one yellow colony was obtained from hundreds of colonies. The isolated mutant was denoted Chlorella sp. M04.

Culture conditions

The original strain (Chlorella sp. AE10), and the mutant (Chlorella sp. M04) were inoculated in 100 mL shake flasks containing 50 mL of BG11 medium, an incubator temperature of 25.00 ± 0.05 °C, a light intensity of 130 μmol/m2/s, and a 24-h light incubation, with a shaking table speed of 120 rpm.

The growth profiles and lutein production of the original and mutant strains under autotrophic, mixotrophic, and heterotrophic conditions were evaluated. The autotrophic conditions were BG11 medium and 24 h continuous light; the mixotrophic conditions were BG11 medium containing 5 g/L glucose and 24 h continuous light; and the heterotrophic conditions were BG11 medium containing 5 g/L glucose and cultivated in the dark. An initial cell concentration of 0.1 g/L. The biomass, lutein, and chlorophyll concentrations were measured daily.

The effects of three different concentrations of acetate and glucose were also investigated under mixotrophic conditions. The glucose concentrations were 5, 10, and 25 g/L, and the acetic acid concentrations were 5.25, 10.50, 26.25 g/L. The pH of the medium was adjusted to neutral, and the initial biomass dry weight was 0.3 g/L. Biomass, lutein, and chlorophyll concentrations were measured daily.

Results

Mutagenesis

Hundreds of colonies were obtained by chemical mutagenesis. Only one yellow colony was isolated from them. The genomes of the original and mutant strains were also sequenced (data not provided). The mutant strain was confirmed to be different from the original strain. The Chlorella sp. M04 existed in two different states during the cultivation process, green and yellow (Fig. 2). This color change was related to the content of pigments, such as chlorophyll and lutein. The contents of lutein, Chl a, and Chl b of Chlorella sp. M04 are 34.41%, 8.63%, and 3.00% of those of Chlorella sp. AE10 at day 0 (Fig. 2). This indicated that the mutant was a partially chlorophyll-deficient strain. The ratios of lutein contents: (Chl a + Chl b) contents in the wild type and the mutant were 1:3.5 and 1:0.79, respectively, on day 0 (Fig. 2). Purification of lutein from the mutant was easier than that from the wild type. Effect of culture mode on original strain and mutant

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the original Chlorella sp. AE10 demonstrated the most instantaneous biomass growth (p < 0.01) under mixotrophic conditions, followed by heterotrophic conditions, and the lowest biomass under autotrophic conditions. However, the lutein content was highest under autotrophic conditions (up to 6.02 mg/g, and there was no significant difference between the lutein contents under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions. The lutein content in Chlorella sp. AE10 declined on day 1, with the lutein content decreasing by 10.8% under autotrophic conditions and by 55.6% and 49.4% under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions, respectively, compared to the lutein content on day 0. The highest lutein productivity of the original strain was 3.87 mg/L/d on day 3 under mixotrophic conditions (Fig. 2B).

The biomass of the Chlorella sp. M04 grew slowly under autotrophic conditions, reaching only 1.5 times the initial biomass on day 5 (Fig. 2A). Conversely, biomass growth under heterotrophic conditions was significant, reaching 27 times the initial biomass on day 4. The biomass concentration under mixotrophic conditions was slightly lower than that under heterotrophic conditions but also reached a 26-fold increase. Under autotrophic conditions, the lutein content in Chlorella sp. M04 exhibited an increasing and then decreasing trend, reaching its highest value on day 2, and then gradually decreasing from 3.14 mg/g to 0.88 mg/g. Under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions, the lutein content decreased slightly at the initial stage of incubation, but then gradually rebounded, with the highest lutein at 1.91 mg/g and 2.03 mg/g. In general, lutein content remained stable under mixotrophic conditions but increased by 25.8% under heterotrophic conditions. The lutein productivity of the mutant under heterotrophic conditions was 1.88 mg/L/d at day 2 (Fig. 2B). Mutagenesis led to a decrease in lutein content, similar to a mutant of Auxenochlorella pyrenoidosa [31]. It should be noted that the lutein content in the mutant may be higher than that in the wild type [28]. Therefore, screening after mutagenesis is important for future studies.

As illustrated in Fig. 2C, the changes in Chlorella sp. AE10 displayed a trend similar to that of lutein. As for chlorophyll b (Fig. 2D), under autotrophic conditions, its content fluctuated, with an overall decreasing trend. However, under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions, chlorophyll-b content first increased and then began to decrease after reaching a peak on the third day, but still portrayed an overall increase. Notably, the chlorophyll content of Chlorella sp. M04 was extremely low, in which chlorophyll b was almost undetectable under mixotrophic conditions, while under heterotrophic conditions, the highest content of 0.62 mg/g was recorded on day 3, which was much lower than that of 7.73 mg/g in Chlorella sp. AE10 (Fig. 2D). Chlorophyll a continued to decrease under autotrophic conditions; under mixotrophic conditions, the highest content was recorded on day 2, and on the last day, the content was the same as that in the initial condition. Under heterotrophic conditions, the highest content of chlorophyll b increased on day 2, and the last day was the same as that in the initial condition (Fig. 2A). Although the lutein content in Chlorella sp. M04 was not high, its overall yellow color may have been caused by the high proportion of lutein in the total pigment.

The color change of the mutant is shown in Fig. 2E–G, where the algal solution was green when the chlorophyll content was high (e.g., on day 3 under heterotrophic conditions). On day 3, the chlorophyll content was the same under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions, but the algal solution was yellow under mixotrophic conditions because of the lower chlorophyll-a content. In addition, the chlorophyll-a content of M04 on day 2 was higher than that on day 1 under heterotrophic conditions (Fig. 2C).

In summary, autotrophy was beneficial for increasing lutein and chlorophyll-a content in Chlorella sp. AE10, whereas mixotrophy and heterotrophy increased the lutein productivity (Table 1). For Chlorella sp. M04, growth was too slow under autotrophic conditions, and the maximal lutein content was similar under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions. Organic carbon sources can yield higher biomass concentrations than CO2, because the solubility of CO2 is very low. The highest lutein production by Chlorella sp. AE10 and Chlorella sp. M04 was under mixotrophic and heterotrophic conditions, respectively. Chlorella sp. AE10 has normal capacity for chlorophyll biosynthesis. Both organic and inorganic carbon sources could be utilized, such that mixotrophy was better than autotrophy and heterotrophy. The chlorophyll contents of the mutants were partially deficient. Heterotrophy was superior to autotrophy and mixotrophy. Effects of acetate and glucose on mutant

As portrayed in Fig. 3, different concentrations of glucose had little effect on the lutein and chlorophyll contents of Chlorella sp. M04. Glucose had little effect on both biomass and lutein concentrations. One possible reason for this is that the culture was performed for only 5 days. Another possible reason is that 5 g/L glucose is sufficient for microalgae. Even if 25 g/L of glucose could not be utilized by the microalgae, it did not lead to much more growth. One objective of this study was to construct a yellow algal strain with low chlorophyll content. This is a good model for investigating the metabolic regulation of pigment biosynthesis, including that of lutein and chlorophyll. Comparative transcriptomic analysis

About 114.1 Gb clean data were obtained. The number of unigenes was 28129. The numbers of the differentially expressed genes (DEG) in different groups are documented in Fig. 4. Interestingly, the total number of M-04-2 vs. M-01–2 was the lowest. The largest number was observed for M-04-4 vs. M-04-1. This indicated that the gene expression of the mutant changed greatly from day 1 to day 4 under mixotrophic conditions.Fig. 4Differentially expressed gene statistics

Differentially expressed gene statistics

The results of the KEGG enrichment analysis are shown in Fig. 5. It should be noted that the DEGs of photosynthesis were seven, and six of them were down-regulated compared with those of the original strain under mixotrophic conditions on day 2. This was because the chlorophyll content of the mutants decreased significantly. Under mixotrophic conditions, there were 32 mutant DEGs in the DNA replication pathway between groups M-04-4 and M-04-1 (Fig. 5C); all 31 of them were down-regulated on day 4 compared to those on day 1. On day 4, growth of the mutant under mixotrophic conditions was slower than on day 1. This is because DNA replication was down-regulated. Conclusion

The original strain exhibits higher lutein productivity under mixotrophic conditions than under autotrophic conditions. Chlorophyll biosynthesis is partially inhibited in the mutant. Therefore, the mutant grows slowly under autotrophic conditions. Lutein productivity under heterotrophic conditions is higher than that under mixotrophic conditions. Lutein production was optimized under mixotrophic conditions for Chlorella sp. AE10 and under heterotrophic conditions for Chlorella sp. M04. Although the lutein productivity of the mutant was low, its advantages were low chlorophyll content and yellow color. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed the possible molecular mechanisms of the mutant. The dynamic pigment profiles are helpful for further study.
