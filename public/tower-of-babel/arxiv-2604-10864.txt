Introduction

The question of how large a disordered system must be before it exhibits a desired ordered subsystem lies at the core of Ramsey theory. This main idea lends itself to many formulations. In the context of graph theory one such formulation is: given a graph
G
and a color set
C
, determine the least
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that any edge-coloring
c:E(K_{\ell})\to C
of the complete graph will accommodate some injection
f:V(G)\to V(K_{\ell})
such that
f
exhibits some desired property under the coloring
c
.

In the classical form
C=\{\text{blue},\text{red}\}
and the goal is for
f
to be monochromatic, that is,
\{f(x),f(y)\}
has the same color for all edges
\{x,y\}\in E(G)
. Ramsey’s theorem
[
16
]
states that the accompanying Ramsey number (the least
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that such an injection into
K_{\ell}
always exists) is finite. We denote the Ramsey number by
R(G)
, and when
C
is expanded to contain
k
colors the
k
-color (classical) Ramsey number is denoted by
R_{k}(G)
. Since the classical formulation, the problem has taken on many variations that change the requirement on
f
(see, for example, Section 3 of the survey by Conlon, Fox, and Sudakov
[
10
]
). In this work we are looking at a variation with an algebraic flavor, known as
zero-sum
Ramsey numbers.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a graph
G
and a finite abelian group
\Gamma
, the
zero-sum Ramsey number
of
G
over
\Gamma
, denoted by
R(G,\Gamma)
, is the minimum
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any edge-coloring
c:E(K_{\ell})\to\Gamma
, there exists a
zero-sum
injection
f:V(G)\to V(K_{\ell})
. That is,
f
satisfies

\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E(G)}c(\{f(x),f(y)\})=0_{\Gamma}.

Ramsey’s theorem has an earlier counterpart outside the context of graphs in the form of Van der Waerden’s theorem
[
17
]
which states that for each
r
and
k
there is some
n=W(r,k)
such that any
r
-coloring of
[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,...,n\}
contains in it a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length
k
. Similarly, the story of zero-sum Ramsey theory on graphs starts outside of graphs in the form of the Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv theorem
[
11
]
, which states that given
2m-1
elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, there is a collection of
m
of them summing to zero. These results inspired the above notion of
R(G,\Gamma)
which was introduced by Bialostocki and Dierker
[
3
]
. It is important to note that although
R_{k}(G)
is finite for all
G
and even any finite size of
C
, the same is not true for
R(G,\Gamma)
. Indeed, recalling that the
exponent

\exp(\Gamma)
is the smallest integer
r>0
such that
g\cdot r=0_{\Gamma}
for all
g\in\Gamma
, we require that
\exp(\Gamma)
divides
e(G)
, the number of edges in
G
. Without this, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we could define the constant coloring
c(\{x,y\})=g
where
g\in\Gamma
has some order
r
that does not divide
e(G)
. Then for any embedding, the sum of the edges would be
g\cdot e(G)\neq 0_{\Gamma}
. On the other hand, it is easy to see that with this requirement,
R(G,\Gamma)\leq R_{|\Gamma|}(G)
and therefore
R(G,\Gamma)
is finite. Furthermore, when
p=e(G)
, we get that
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{p})\geq R(G)
by treating a red-blue coloring as a
0
-
1
coloring. Hence, the zero-sum Ramsey number is fundamentally linked to the classical Ramsey number.

A breakthrough result by Lee
[
15
]
(resolving a long-standing conjecture of Burr and Erdös) states that
R(G)
is linear in
n
for a particular class of graphs, called
d
-degenerate graphs:

Definition 1.2
.

A graph
G
is
d
-degenerate if its vertex set can be ordered
V(G)=\{v_{1},...,v_{n}\}
so that for all
i\in[n]
,
|\{v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G):j>i\}|\leq d.

Preceding the result in
[
15
]
was the work by Chvátal,
Rödl, Szemerédi, and Trotter
[
8
]
which showed that for all
G
with maximum degree
\Delta
,
R(G)
is linear in
n
(here, and for the rest of the paper,
n\coloneqq|V(G)|
and
m\coloneqq e(G)
). Likewise, the current author with Katz, Lian, and Malekshahian
[
13
]
showed that
R(G,\Gamma)
is linear in
n
in the regime where
G
has maximum degree
\Delta
and
|\Gamma|
divides
m
. Hence, mirroring the results on classical Ramsey numbers, it is natural to conjecture (Conjecture 1 in
[
13
]
) that
R(G,\Gamma)
is linear in
n
for all
d
-degenerate graphs (when
|\Gamma|
divides
m
).

In the context of graphs without isolated vertices, the work by Colucci and D’Emidio
[
9
]
took the first step in proving this for
d=1
and cyclic groups
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
where their result implies that for all forests (i.e.
1
-degenerate graphs)
R(G,\Gamma)\leq n+9p-12
under the condition that
m=\Omega(p^{2})
. We improve this to all
d
-degenerate graphs, and significantly improve the constraint on
m
to be
\Omega(p)
. We also offer a slight improvement to the upper bound on
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{p})
when
d=1
. Our main result, then, is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be a
d
-degenerate graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges and let
p\leq m/(2d(d+1)^{2})
be a prime dividing
m
such that
2d<p
. Then

R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{p})\leq n+(3+3d)p.

Notice that unlike the work on bounded degree graphs
[
13
]
, we do not allow
|\Gamma|=|\mathbb{Z}_{p}|=m
. This is because some sort of separation between
p
and
m
is necessary for the constant in front of
p
to remain small (whereas in
[
13
]
they show that for bounded degree graphs,
R(G,\Gamma)\leq C\cdot n
for some large constant
C
super-exponential in
\Delta
). Indeed, even in the case of bipartite graphs with maximal degree
\Delta
, the results due to Graham et al.
[
12
]
tell us that there exists a graph
G
such that
C^{\Delta}n\leq R(G)
. But then recall that when
p=m
,
R(G)\leq R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{p})
.
Combining this with our result shows that if
p_{1}=m
and
p_{2}\leq m/2d(d+1)^{2}
are primes, then there exist
d
-degenerate graphs
G
such that

R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}})=2^{\Omega(d)}\cdot n\text{ \quad\quad while \quad\quad}R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{p_{2}})<2\cdot n.

In general, the strength of the known bounds depends quite heavily on the relative size of
|\Gamma|
and
m
. Notably, in the setting where
\Gamma={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
is a cyclic group, exact results are known for all graphs when
p=2

[
5
]
, and for forests (i.e.
1
-degenerate graphs) when
p=3

[
2
]
. However, as is most often the case in Ramsey theory, most results in this area are not exact and aim to establish asymptotic bounds on
R(G,\Gamma)
. For a broader survey on these and related results see
[
6
]
and also
[
9
,
4
,
1
,
13
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Cauchy-Davenport

)
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
A_{1},...,A_{s}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be non-empty. Then

|A_{1}+...+A_{s}|\geq\min\{p,|A_{1}|+...+|A_{s}|-s+1\}.

2.1
Terminology

Before giving a brief overview of the strategy for the proof of
Theorem

1.3
, we introduce some useful terminology.

Let
R
be a set of vertices. Then given two vertices
x,y\in R
we denote the pair
\{x,y\}
by
xy
. Further, we can treat
R
as the vertex set of a complete graph and write
E(R)\coloneqq\{uv:u\neq v\in R\}
. Given a subset of vertices
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\}\subset R
and an edge coloring
c:E(R)\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we define
c(yX)=c(Xy)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{s}c(yx_{i})
. Further, given an additional vertex
z\in R
we define
c(Xyz)=c(Xy)+c(yz)
.

Likewise, given a vertex coloring
\mathcal{C}:R\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
we define
\mathcal{C}(X)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mathcal{C}(x_{i})
. Lastly, for
G
a graph,
Y\subset V(G)
, and
v\in V(G)\setminus Y
, we can define
d_{G}(v)
to be the degree of
v
in
G
and
d(v,Y)\coloneqq|\{v^{\prime}\in Y:vv^{\prime}\in E(G)\}|
to be the degree of
v
into
Y
. Lastly, we denote
N(v)\coloneqq\{v^{\prime}\in V(G):vv^{\prime}\in E(G)\}
to be the neighborhood of
v
in
G
.

2.2
Proof overview and key lemmas

Let us now go over the main ideas behind the proof of
Theorem

1.3
. For the rest of this section, let
G
be a
d
-degenerate graph with
m
edges and let
p\leq m/(2d(d+1)^{2})
be a prime dividing
m
such that
2d<p
.

Suppose we are given a vertex set
R
and an edge coloring
c:E(R)\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. First, consider some
independent
set
J\subset V(G)
and let
f:V(G)\to R
be an injection with
\sum_{xy\in E(G)}c(f(x)f(y))=g
. Then suppose that for each
v\in J
we could choose to modify
f
into
f_{v}
in such a way that
\sum_{xy\in E(G)}c(f_{v}(x)f_{v}(y))=g+\delta(v)
for some
\delta(v)\neq 0
. Further suppose that any number of these modifications can be made and all are independent. That is, for each
W\subset J
, we have an injective
f_{W}
such that
\sum_{xy\in E(G)}c(f_{W}(x)f_{W}(y))=g+\sum_{v\in W}\delta(v)
. Then as long as
|J|\geq p-1
, by a direct application of
Theorem

2.1
with
A_{v}=\{0,\delta(v)\}
, we get that there is some
W\subset J
such that
\sum_{v\in W}\delta(v)=-g
and so
\sum_{xy\in E(G)}c(f_{W}(x)f_{W}(y))=0
. Thus,
f_{W}
is our desired zero-sum embedding.

Our proof will use
Lemma

2.2
to find an appropriate
J\subset V(G)
while
Lemma

2.3
will use
J
in order to find a suitable
f
along with choices for its modification, or, if this is impossible, find a monochromatic embedding of
V(G)
into
R
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
G
is a
d
-degenerate graph with
m
edges then there is an independent set
J\subset V(G)
of size at least
m/d(d+1)^{2}
where
1\leq d_{G}(v)\leq 2d
for all
v\in J
.

Proof.

Let
G^{\prime}
be the induced subgraph of
G
on non-isolated vertices and say that
|V(G^{\prime})|=n^{\prime}
. We recall that we can order
V(G^{\prime})=\{v_{i},...,v_{n^{\prime}}\}
such that for all
i\leq n^{\prime}
,
|\{j>i:v_{j}v_{i}\in E(G^{\prime})\}|\leq d
. Let
x
be the number of vertices in
V(G^{\prime})
of degree at most
2d
and let
y=n^{\prime}-x
. For each vertex
v_{i}
with degree
d(v_{i})>2d
we have

|\{j<i:v_{j}v_{i}\in E(G^{\prime})\}|\geq d+1.

This gives us that
y(d+1)\leq n^{\prime}d
and hence
x\geq n^{\prime}/(d+1)
. Let
V_{0}
be the set of vertices with degree at most
2d
and observe that we can write
V_{0}=\{w_{1},...,w_{x}\}
such that for all
i\leq x
,
|\{j>i:w_{j}w_{i}\in E(G^{\prime})\}|\leq d
. Then, if we in order extract vertices
w_{i}
from
V_{0}
and place them into
J
and then remove from
V_{0}
the neighborhood
N(w_{i})
, we are guaranteed to at each step shrink
V_{0}
by at most
d+1
vertices and so at the end
|J|\geq|V_{0}|/(d+1)\geq n^{\prime}/(d+1)^{2}>m/d(d+1)^{2}
since
m/n^{\prime}<d
.
∎

Note that it is crucial that these vertices have degree at least 1 since different embeddings of a vertex of degree
0
will never yield different sums. The reason why the vertices need to be of low degree will become evident in the proof of the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
J=\{v_{1},...,v_{s}\}\subset V(G)
be an independent set of
s=2p
vertices so that
1\leq d_{G}(v_{i})\leq 2d
for all
v_{i}\in J
. Further, let
R
be a vertex set of size
n+(3+3d)p
and let
c:E(R)\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
be an edge coloring.

Either there is a monochromatic copy of
G
in
R
or there exist injections
f:V(G)\setminus J\to R
, and
h,h^{\prime}:J\to R
satisfying the following

1.

f(V(G)\setminus J)\cap(h(V)\cup h^{\prime}(V))=\emptyset
,

2.

h(v)\neq h^{\prime}(v^{\prime})
for all
v\neq v^{\prime}\in J
, and

3.

|\{i\in[s]:c(h(v_{i})f(N_{i}))\neq c(h^{\prime}(v_{i})f(N_{i}))\}|\geq p
.

The proof of
Lemma

2.3
will follow in
Section

3
. Note that the modification to
f
we mentioned in the proof overview is realized by condition
3
and the choice of defining
f(v_{i})=h(v_{i})
or
f(v_{i})=h^{\prime}(v_{i})
for any given
v_{i}\in J
. Further, in the proof overview we said that we need
|J|\geq p-1
. However, as it turns out, this would not give us enough flexibility and it is necessary to occasionally allow for some
v\in J
not to provide a choice for a modification of
f
. Hence, we require
|J|\geq 2p
.

We now prove
Theorem

1.3
using

2.2

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
R
be a vertex set of size at least
n+(3+3d)p
and let
c:E(R)\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
be an edge-coloring. We begin by applying

Lemma

2.2
to
G
to get
J=\{v_{1},...,v_{s}\}\subset V(G)
, an independent set of size
s\coloneqq 2p
where each vertex has degree at most
2d
but degree at least
1
. For each
v_{i}\in J
denote
N_{i}\coloneqq N(v_{i})
. We can then define

U\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[s]}N_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad Z\coloneqq V(G)\setminus(J\cup U)

noting that
(U\cup Z)\cap J=\emptyset
. Then we apply
Lemma

2.3
to either get the embedding functions
f:U\cup Z\to R
and
h,h^{\prime}:J\to R
, or a monochromatic copy of
G
in
R
. In the latter case, the monochromatic copy of
G
is a witness to a zero-sum embedding of
G
because
p
divides
m
. Then let us consider the former case.

Consider the set of sequences
\overline{u}=(u_{i})_{i\in[s]}
where each
u_{i}\in\{h(v_{i}),h^{\prime}(v_{i})\}
. For each such
\overline{u}
let us denote
c(\overline{u})\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[s]}c(u_{i}f(N_{i}))
. Applying
Theorem

2.1
with
A_{i}\coloneqq\{c(h(v_{i})f(N_{i})),c(h^{\prime}(v_{i})f(N_{i}))\}
we get

p=\min\{p,s+p-s+1\}\leq\left|\sum_{i\in[s]}A_{i}\right|=|\{c(\overline{u}):\overline{u}\in\{h(v_{1}),h^{\prime}(v_{1})\}\times...\times\{h(v_{s}),h^{\prime}(v_{s})\}\}|

by condition
3
of
Lemma

2.3
.
Thus, for each
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
there is some sequence
\overline{g}\in\{h(v_{1}),h^{\prime}(v_{1})\}\times...\times\{h(v_{s}),h^{\prime}(v_{s})\}
such that
c(\overline{g})=g
. Next define

z\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in U\cup Z\\
xy\in E(G)\end{subarray}}-c(f(x)f(y))

and consider the corresponding sequence
\overline{z}=(z_{i})_{i\in[s]}
for which
c(\overline{z})=z
. We can then extend
f
to
J
with
f(v_{i})\coloneqq z_{i}
. Note that
f
remains injective because of conditions
1
and
2
of
Lemma

2.3
and the fact that
h
and
h^{\prime}
are injections themselves. Further,

\sum_{xy\in E(G)}c(f(x)f(y))=\sum_{i\in[s]}c(z_{i}f(N_{i}))+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in U\cup Z\\
xy\in E(G)\end{subarray}}c(f(x)f(y))=z-z=0.

Thus,
f
is a zero-sum embedding of
G
into
R
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.3

In the rest of this paper we prove
Lemma

2.3
thereby concluding our proof of
Theorem

1.3
. The proof of
Lemma

2.3
can be broken down as follows. First we try to define

,
h
, and
h^{\prime}
into

. We will do this by embedding one element of

at a time and embedding elements

(i.e. defining

is about to be embedded. If this process fails, then the set of unused vertices in

, which we will call

, will exhibit some strong properties under the coloring

of the colorings (for a precise definition of what we mean by strongly regular see

3.4
). Keeping this property in mind, we will again try to define

,
h
, and
h^{\prime}
into

. This time we will do this by embedding all of

from the start into a set of vertices that are all strongly regular in some vertex coloring

. Then we will define

and

one element of

at a time. If this fails, we will be able to immediately argue that on the remaining vertices,

is almost monochromatic (meaning that each vertex sees at most

which finds a monochromatic embedding of

3.1
Embedding a
d
-degenerate graph

Although we will only use the following lemma at the very end, we will present it now because it is self-contained and the second and last time we will use the
d
-degeneracy of
G
(the first being

2.2
where we found a sufficiently large independent set of non-isolated low-degree vertices). This lemma will allow us to later embed

.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a
d
-degenerate graph on
n
vertices, and
H
a graph such that for any collection of
d
vertices
Y\subset V(H)
, their joint neighborhood

N(Y)\coloneqq\bigcap_{y\in Y}N(y)

satisfies

|N(Y)|\geq n-|Y|.
Then there exists a subgraph
H^{\prime}\subset H
that is isomorphic to
G
.

Remark 3.2
.

When
d=1
this lemma is just the fact that any forest on
n
vertices can be embedded into a graph with minimum degree
n-1
.

Proof.

G
is
d
-degenerate, so there is an ordering of vertices
v_{1},...,v_{n}
such that for all
j\in[n]
,
Y_{j}\coloneqq\{v_{\ell}\sim v_{j}:\ell>j\}
has size at most
d
. So, starting with
i=n
, set
f:\emptyset\to\emptyset
and working backwards through to

, let us embed each
v_{i}
into some new
f(v_{i})=w_{i}\in N(Y_{i})\setminus f(\{v_{i+1},...,v_{n}\})\neq\emptyset
(recalling that
|N(Y_{i})|\geq n
). The result of this process is an embedding of
G
into
H
thereby concluding the proof.
∎

3.2
Embedding into
R

We will now prove

2.3
by defining two processes that attempt to construct our desired

,

, and

Proof of Lemma

.

Recall that
J=\{v_{1},...,v_{s}\}\subset V(G)
is an independent set where each

1\leq d_{G}(v_{i})\leq 2d

We start with empty functions

Our process will work by embedding each

and

. Then each such edge
xx^{\prime}
will have either
c(xx^{\prime})\neq C_{x}
or
c(xx^{\prime})\neq C_{x^{\prime}}
(or both). But since each
x
sees at most
k^{\prime}
neighbors in a color other than
C_{x}
, we have a bound on the number of edges between
D
and
D^{\prime}
to be
k^{\prime}(|D|+|D^{\prime}|)
, and so,
|D|\cdot|D^{\prime}|\leq k^{\prime}(|D|+|D^{\prime}|)
and thus,

|D^{\prime}|\leq k^{\prime}(1+|D^{\prime}|/|D|)\leq 2k^{\prime}.

Let
g
be the most common edge color in
E(\mathcal{R})
, and define the graph
H
by
V(H)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{R}:C_{x}=g\}
and
E(H)\coloneqq\{u,u^{\prime}\in V(H):c(uu^{\prime})=g\}
. Then by the above,
|V(H)|\geq|\mathcal{R}|-2k^{\prime}\geq n+dk^{\prime}
and for each
Y=\{y_{1},...,y_{d}\}\subset V(H)
,

N(Y)\supset\bigcap_{y_{i}\in Y}R_{y_{i}}

and so
|N(Y)|\geq n+dk^{\prime}-d(k^{\prime}+1)=n-d
. Thus, we can apply
Lemma

3.1
to
G
and
H
to find a monochromatic embedding of
G
into
H
, thereby concluding the proof.
∎

4
Conclusion

In their paper, the current author along with Katz, Lian, and Malekshahian
[
13
]
proved a linear bound for
R(G,\Gamma)
when
G
has maximum degree
\Delta
but allowed
\Gamma
to be a general abelian group of order up to
|\Gamma|\leq e(G)
.
It would be natural to try to use the techniques from
[
13
]
to extend the results in this paper to all abelian groups
\Gamma
with
|\Gamma|\leq e(G)
(thereby confirming Conjecture 1 in their paper). There are two key techniques that would need to be adapted to our setting. The first deals with the reason behind the restriction
2d<p
. That is, if we allow general abelian groups, we would need to deal with the possibility that there could be
v\in V(G)
and
0\neq g\in\Gamma
such that
d(v)\cdot g=0
. As a result, the argument in
eq.

6
that
|N^{*}|\cdot\mathcal{C}_{i}(w)=|N^{*}|\cdot\mathcal{C}_{i}(w^{\prime})\implies\mathcal{C}_{i}(w)=\mathcal{C}_{i}(w^{\prime})
(recall that
|N^{*}|=|N(v_{i})|
for some
v_{i}\in J
) would no longer hold. Further, it would no longer be possible to use Cauchy-Davenport. Instead, we may replace it with Kneser’s Theorem
[
14
]
but this introduces significant complications in insuring that
\{c(\overline{u}):\overline{u}\in\{h(v_{1}),h^{\prime}(v_{1})\}\times...\times\{h(v_{s}),h^{\prime}(v_{s})\}\}=\Gamma
.

Second, in order to allow
|\Gamma|=e(G)
we could replace
h
and
h^{\prime}
in
Lemma

2.3
with a set of functions
h_{1},...,h_{\alpha}
such that
\sum_{i\in[s]}|\{c(h_{j}(v_{i})f(N_{i})):j\in[\alpha]\}|\geq|\Gamma|+s-1
(this corresponds to the blueprints realized at intensity
\alpha
in
[
13
]
). However, it is unclear how the lack of such an embedding would allow us to argue for the existence of a monochromatic copy of
G
in
R
.
