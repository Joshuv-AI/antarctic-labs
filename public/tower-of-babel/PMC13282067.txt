Normothermic Regional Perfusion in Controlled Donation After Circulatory Death and its Impact on Postreperfusion Syndrome in Liver Transplantation Compared With Brain-dead Donors: A Retrospective Comparative Study
Authors: Hélène Platteeuw, Alexandre Bourgeois, Cédric Cirenei, Charlotte Geay, Anne Bignon, Maxime Leroy, Hélène Béhal, Sébastien Dharancy, Emmanuel Boleslawski, Gilles Lebuffe, Damien Rousseleau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Controlled donation after circulatory death (cDCD) increases graft availability but may carry higher perioperative risk, which in situ normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) may mitigate by reducing ischemia–reperfusion injury. Evidence remains limited regarding NRP impact on PRS during liver transplantation (LT). The objective of this study was to compare the incidence of PRS and early postoperative outcomes between cDCD LT with NRP and donation after brain death (DBD) LT.Methods.This was a retrospective, single-center observational study comparing cDCD LT with NRP versus DBD LT. The primary endpoint was PRS (≥30% mean arterial pressure decrease for ≥1 min within 5 min of reperfusion). Secondary endpoints included early allograft dysfunction, acute kidney injury (AKI), rejection, biliary/vascular complications, and survival. Associations between graft type and outcomes were assessed using logistic regression (binary) and linear regression (continuous), and Cox models (time-to-event), with multivariable adjustment for clinically relevant covariates.Results.Among 106 recipients (cDCD n = 47; DBD n = 59), PRS occurred 27 patients (25.5% versus 25.4%). Graft type was not associated with PRS after adjustment (odds ratio, 1.13; 95% confidence interval, 0.41–3.10; P = 0.81). No significant differences were observed in early allograft dysfunction, acute rejection, AKI, or biliary and vascular complications. There was 1 death in the cDCD group and 5 in the DBD group (2.1% versus 8.5%).Conclusions.In a standardized program using routine NRP, cDCD LT did not increase PRS risk and achieved early outcomes comparable to DBD LT. These findings suggest clinical feasibility and short-term safety of Maastricht III protocols incorporating NRP.

The persistent shortage of suitable donor livers has led to the increasing use of donation after circulatory death (DCD) as a strategy to expand the transplant pool. In France, DCD liver transplantation (LT) was introduced in 2008, and controlled DCD (cDCD), referring to organ donation after planned withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy, procedures accounted for approximately two-thirds of the increase in liver transplants between 2012 and 2019.1

Early outcomes associated with DCD grafts were inferior compared with donation after brain death (DBD), with higher rates of graft dysfunction, retransplantation, and lower graft survival.2 Some studies also reported increased mortality in cDCD recipients.3 These adverse outcomes were driven by ischemia–reperfusion injury, particularly affecting the biliary tract and leading to complications such as ischemic cholangiopathy.4-10 Warm ischemia, occurring after circulatory arrest and before graft preservation, exacerbates hepatocellular injury and promotes the accumulation of ischemic metabolites.11-13 These factors may amplify the inflammatory and hemodynamic responses upon reperfusion, thereby increasing the risk of postreperfusion syndrome (PRS).14

Postreperfusion syndrome was described by Aggarwal et al15 as a >30% drop in mean arterial pressure (MAP) lasting at least 1 min within 5 min of portal vein unclamping and graft reperfusion. PRS has been associated with adverse outcomes such as acute kidney injury (AKI) with potential progression to chronic kidney disease, increased transfusion requirements, primary graft dysfunction, graft loss, elevated liver enzyme release, and perioperative mortality.16-23

To decrease the deleterious effects of warm ischemia in cDCD transplantation, the use of normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) has become mandatory in France. This technique has been associated with improved outcomes, including reduced graft loss, fewer biliary complications, and a lower incidence of ischemic cholangiopathy.24-26 While most studies have focused on graft survival and biliary complications, the potential impact on early intraoperative events particularly PRS remains poorly explored.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to compare the incidence of postreperfusion syndrome between liver transplants from controlled donation after circulatory death using NRP and standard liver transplants from donation after brain death.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design and Regulatory and Ethical Considerations

This study was a retrospective monocentric case–control study conducted at Lille University Hospital. The study population included adult patients who underwent LT between January 2018 and December 2022. Data on recipients were obtained from CRISTAL, the French national transplant registry managed by the Agence de la Biomédecine (ABM). Retrospective perioperative data were extracted from the Lille University Hospital health data warehouse (INCLUDE), which integrates data from the anesthesia information management systems (DIANE, Bow medical, France). Postoperative and follow-up data up to 1 y posttransplant were retrieved from the hospital information system (SILLAGE®, SIB, France).

This study was conducted in accordance with French regulations governing retrospective noninterventional research in accordance with the CNIL Reference Methodology MR-004 for noninterventional health research using routinely collected personal data. Data use was declared to the French Data Protection Authority through Lille University Hospital’s Data Protection Officer (DEC23-079) and received approval from the INCLUDE data warehouse ethics committee. The INCLUDE data warehouse is CNIL-approved (authorization number: 1754053), ensuring full GDPR compliance (https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/cnil/id/CNILTEXT000039292712). All patients were informed by letter about this retrospective study and had the opportunity to object to the reuse of their data. In accordance with the CNIL Reference Methodology MR-004 for noninterventional research using routinely collected health data, written informed consent was not required. The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines.27

Data Collection

We collected preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables. Recipient characteristics included age, sex, BMI, ASA class, MELD and Child-Pugh scores, underlying liver disease, history of alcohol use, viral status (HBV, HCV, HIV), diabetes, chronic renal disease, smoking history, prior transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, and pretransplant clinical status (mechanical ventilation or renal replacement therapy). Donor variables comprised age and graft steatosis or fibrosis. Intraoperative data included surgical duration, blood loss, transfusion requirements, fluid administration, cold ischemia time (CIT), and warm ischemia time (WIT).

Exposure

Inclusion criteria mirrored those for Maastricht III donation in France and included recipients aged 18–65 y undergoing their first LT with a MELD score <26. Exclusion criteria comprised complete portal vein thrombosis, hemodynamic instability, split-liver or super-urgent transplantation, combined organ transplantation, CIT >8 h, use of hypothermic oxygenated perfusion (HOPE), donor age >70 y, macrovesicular steatosis >20%, fibrosis stage ≥F2, AST and ALT levels >160 U/L.

cDCD Protocol and Normothermic Regional Perfusion

All surgical procedures were performed according to institutional standards. Liver recovery in cDCD donors (Maastricht category III) was conducted in accordance with ABM guidelines. During NRP, graft acceptance was determined according to predefined national criteria incorporating donor characteristics, ischemic time thresholds, intraperfusion biochemical parameters, and histological assessment.28

Eligible donors were <71 y of age, with AST and ALT <4× the upper normal limit both before withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) and during NRP. Preimplantary biopsy was systematically performed, and grafts were considered acceptable in the absence of chronic liver disease, defined as macrovascular steatosis ≤20% and fibrosis stage <F2. Ischemic criteria included a functional WIT ≤45 min, time to circulatory arrest ≤30 min, and a CIT below 8 h.

Definitions followed the criteria established by the French ABM. WIT was defined as the interval from WLST to the initiation of NRP. Functional WIT corresponded to the period beginning when MAP fell below 45 mm Hg and ending with the start of NRP. The agonal phase extended from WLST to the cessation of spontaneous ventilation and circulation, while the asystolic phase followed until the confirmation of circulatory death. CIT referred to the duration of hypothermic preservation of the graft.

Reperfusion

Graft reperfusion was performed in a controlled, sequential manner. Following completion of vascular anastomoses, vessels were released in the following order: supra and infrahepatic vena cava first, then the portal vein, and finally the hepatic artery. This sequence allows gradual restoration of blood flow, helping to minimize portal hypertension and hemodynamic instability at the time of reperfusion.

Blood Management

ROTEM and TEG were not routinely available in our center during the study period. Intraoperative transfusions were administered at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist, based on surgical blood loss, observed hemostasia, and preoperative laboratory values including hemoglobin, platelet count, and conventional coagulation tests. In case of significant bleeding, red blood cell transfusion was the primary intervention. Platelet transfusion was used rarely, and fresh frozen plasma or coagulation factor concentrates were not routinely administered during LT at our institution.

Perioperative Management

Perioperative management followed a standardized institutional protocol applied to both groups. Immunosuppression included methylprednisolone (500 mg during surgery, 250 mg on day 1, tapered to 20 mg/d, from day 30) and mycophenolate mofetil (500 mg intraoperatively). Dosing was adjusted for body weight: patients ≥80 kg received 500 mg QID initially, then 1000 mg BID orally; patients <80 kg received 500 mg 3 time daily, then 750 mg twice daily. In patients with impaired renal function, basiliximab (20 mg on day 0 and day 4) was administered and tacrolimus initiation was delayed at day 3. Tacrolimus was started at 0.1 mg/kg/d from day 0 in patients with preserved renal function. Full protocol details are available in Supplementary Material, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A874.

Intraoperatively, all patients underwent continuous hemodynamic monitoring and were treated accordingly. Before reperfusion, all patients received sodium bicarbonate 8.4% at a dose of 1 mL/kg, in accordance with institutional practice. Norepinephrine use at reperfusion was recorded. Doses were recorded for the 5 min before and after reperfusion.

Postoperative surveillance included a CT scan between postoperative day (POD) 5–9 d to screen for early surgical complications. Follow-up after ICU occurred twice weekly during the first 2 wks, then weekly up to 1 mo, biweekly up to 3 mo, and monthly to quarterly until 1 y.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of PRS at portal vein unclamping, defined as a superior of 30% decrease in MAP for >1 min.15 Postreperfusion syndrome (PRS) was identified automatically from the institutional data warehouse based on continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring, with baseline MAP defined as the average value during the 5 min preceding reperfusion.

Secondary outcomes assessed the impact of graft type on postoperative results, including patient and graft survival, graft-related and medical complications, need for interventions, and length of hospital stay. Follow-up at 3 and 12 mo included graft loss, rejection, and complications. Posttransplant AKI was assessed according to KDIGO criteria.29

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentage). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile range (IQR) depending on distribution. Normality was assessed graphically and via Shapiro–Wilk test.

The baseline characteristics of donors and recipients were compared between graft type using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test (in case of nonnormality distribution) for continuous variables; and χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The association between graft type (DBD versus cDCD) and PRS was analyzed using logistic regression model. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) were derived from this model as effect size. Covariates variables were predefined, based on clinical relevance, and included: donor age, receiver age, CIT, and MELD score.

The effect of graft type on perioperative outcomes was assessed using linear regression model for quantitative variables and logistic regression model for binary outcomes. When residuals did not meet the normality assumption in linear regression models, log or rank transformations were applied.

The evolution of postoperative liver function tests (ALT and AST) was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with patient included as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation over time and graft type*time (in days) interaction specified as fixed effect. Because of the non-normal distribution of enzyme levels, data were log-transformed before analysis. Estimated coefficients and their 95% CIs were expressed as effect sizes, with cDCD grafts used as the reference at each postoperative day. A 2-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Postreperfusion syndrome

PRS occurred in 27 patients (25.5%), with similar rates between cDCD and DBD groups (25.5% versus 25.4%, respectively; OR = 1.01, 95% CI, 0.41–2.43; P = 0.99). Results remained not significant after adjustment on confounding factors (OR 1.13; 95% CI, 0.41–3.10; P = 0.81). Pre and postunclamping MAP were similar between groups. In the 5 min before portal vein unclamping, MAP averaged 82 mm Hg (±11) in the cDCD group and 82 mm Hg (±12) in the DBD group (P = 0.81). In the 5 min following unclamping, MAP declined to 77 mm Hg (±14) in the cDCD group versus 75 mmHg (±14) in the DBD group (P = 0.40). The lowest MAP within 5 min postunclamping averaged 64 mm Hg (±14) for cDCD recipients and 61 mm Hg (±14) for DBD recipients, with no significant difference (P = 0.22). Time spent below 70% of baseline MAP also did not differ meaningfully (1.64 ± 1.1 min for cDCD versus 1.5 ± 1.3 min for DBD; P = 0.72; Figure 2).

Perioperative Outcomes

Median operative time was significantly longer in the DBD group (395 [321–470] min versus 341 [305–383] min; P = 0.01). CIT was also longer in the DBD group than in the cDCD LT (371 [299–412] versus 279 [240–341] min; P < 0.001). Portocaval anastomosis was more frequently required in cDCD LT (46.8% versus 30.5%; P = 0.085). Blood loss and transfusion rates were not significantly different between groups. Catecholamine use was comparable between groups. The proportion of patients receiving catecholamines before reperfusion did not differ significantly (72.3% in cDCD versus 86.2% in DBD; P = 0.077), nor did the use of epinephrine (6.4% versus 15.5%; P = 0.14). Norepinephrine doses before reperfusion were low and comparable between groups (0.025 [0.014–0.042] versus 0.035 [0.005–0.064] µg/kg/min for cDCD and DBD, respectively), and increased similarly after reperfusion (0.48 [0.11–1.58] versus 0.53 [0.06–1.64] µg/kg/min). Cumulative norepinephrine and epinephrine doses were also similar between groups (Table 2). No patient received ephedrine, phenylephrine, or methylene blue in this reperfusion period. DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study directly comparing PRS incidence between cDCD–NRP and DBD within a mandatory national NRP framework. In this study, we found that the incidence of postreperfusion syndrome (PRS) was comparable between recipients of cDCD grafts preserved with NRP and those of DBD grafts. Approximately 25% of recipients experienced PRS, a rate consistent with previous reports in LT.18,30 Importantly, despite historical data suggesting a higher risk of PRS in cDCD transplantation,31 our results indicate that NRP may neutralize this difference, supporting its role as a protective strategy against hemodynamic instability at reperfusion.

These findings complement recent evidence from Puttappa et al,32 where both NRP and normothermic machine perfusion significantly reduced the incidence of PRS compared with direct procurement and static cold storage (37% versus 11%–15%). In that study, PRS was not only less frequent with normothermic techniques but also strongly associated with worse graft survival, highlighting its clinical relevance. Our results, comparing cDCD–NRP directly with DBD, suggest that in the French setting, where NRP is mandatory and standardized, PRS risk in cDCD transplantation is no higher than in conventional BDB.

NRP may reduce PRS through attenuation of ischemia–reperfusion injury. Warm ischemia accelerates hepatocellular injury, lactate accumulation, and release of vasoactive mediators, all of which contribute to vasoplegia and hemodynamic collapse at reperfusion.33,30,34,35 NRP restores oxygenated circulation shortly after circulatory arrest, allowing metabolic recovery and clearance of inflammatory mediators before cold storage. This physiological restoration may explain the comparable hemodynamic profile of cDCD–NRP and DBD grafts in our series.

Our data also confirm that the overall incidence and severity of PRS in both groups fall within the range reported in the literature (8%–30%).19,36 Results remained similar when adjusted for known predictors, including CIT, donor age, and operative time, consistent with other reports.16,19,23,30

Biliary complications, particularly ischemic cholangiopathy, are a risk following cDCD LT.1,10,37,38 In our study, biliary complication rate did not differ between cDCD and DBD groups. This replicates the findings from Mastrovangelis et al,39 whose 2024 meta-analysis showed that NRP reduced ischemic cholangiopathy (RR 0.23), primary nunfunction (RR 0.51), and recipient mortality (HR 0,50) compared with cDCD without NRP, providing outcomes comparable to DBD grafts. Earlier reports from Spain and the United Kingdom similarly demonstrated significant reductions in cholangiopathy and early allograft dysfunction using NRP.40,41 A French multicenter study by Savier et al42 also reported lower transaminase peaks and equivalent rates of early allograft dysfunction, AKI, graft loss, and biliary complications between cDCD with NRP and DBD LT.

During follow-up, we observed a lower 6-mo readmission in the cDCD group. The 2023 annual report from the French ABM highlighted excellent short-term survival (at 1 mo and 1 y) in cDCD recipients, attributed to younger donors and lower recipient MELD scores. However, in our cohort, donor age and MELD were comparable between groups. Operative and CITs were longer in the DBD group. These disparities are likely related to recipient selection and allocation logistics rather than surgical technique, as procedures were performed by the same surgical team. Under the French allocation framework, cDCD grafts are preferentially allocated to recipients with lower anticipates surgical complexity, whereas DBD grafts may be implanted in more complex cases. Longer CIT in the DBD group may also reflect broader geographic sharing, while cDCD grafts follow a structured regional NRP pathway.

These results support the use of this technic to decrease graft shortages. In France in 2023, among deceased liver donors, 183 (13.8%) were cDCD donors. LT activity from cDCD donors continued to increase in 2023 (+13.7% compared with 2022), reflecting both a 16.2% rise in organ procurement from this donor category and an improvement in liver retrieval efficiency.1

France is one of the few countries, where NRP during cDCD recovery in mandated by national protocol. The French model enforces strict donor criteria: age <71 y, AST/ALT <4× upper limit of normal, macrovesicular steatosis ≤20%, fibrosis stage <F2, and CIT <8 h. In other countries (such as United Kingdom and Spain), donor age thresholds and tolerable functional WITs differ and NRP is used variably depending on local protocols.37,38,40 These discrepancies in donor selection and graft preparation can impact graft quality and may explain why PRS incidence did not differ between cDCD and DBD in our cohort, likely attributable to the protective effect of our structured NRP protocol.

The strength of this study lies in the data collection. Perioperative data are systematically collected and stored in our data warehouse. The extraction of invasive blood pressure parameters allows for an accurate determination of postreperfusion syndrome. This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design, conducted within a high-volume French transplant programme, introduces the possibility of center-specific clinical practices, although national standards of care were applied. Second, the sample size may limit the ability to detect small differences, even if such differences may not be clinically meaningful. Third, recipients were selected according to the French allocation policy, resulting in relatively low MELD scores and a high proportion of hepatocellular carcinoma cases. This reflects a lower-risk recipient population, which may limit the broader generalizability of our findings, particularly regarding the physiological severity of postreperfusion syndrome. Furthermore, differences in intraoperative management between groups may also have influenced outcomes. Portocaval anastomosis was more frequently performed in the cDCD group, and operative times were slightly shorter in this group. These variations may reflect surgical adaptations aimed at mitigating historical risk factors associated with cDCD grafts. As such, intraoperative strategy could have influenced perioperative hemodynamic and should be considered when interpreting outcomes, including postreperfusion syndrome and early postoperative complications. An additional limitation relates to anesthetic management at reperfusion. Although perioperative care was standardized, the increase in norepinephrine requirements in both groups after unclamping indicates that vasopressor use may have influenced the hemodynamic response and attenuated the expression of postreperfusion syndrome. Consequently, we cannot exclude the impact of differences in anticipatory anesthetic strategies on its hemodynamic expression.

CONCLUSION

In this single-center cohort, LT from controlled donation after circulatory death using postmortem in situ NRP was associated with a similar incidence of postreperfusion syndrome and comparable postoperative outcomes to those observed after LT from donation after brain death. These findings support the clinical feasibility of this approach, although they should be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and event rate.
