Balancing energy, water, and ecology through renewable energy transitions in the Yellow River Basin
Authors: Chuandong Wu, Dawen Yang, Ximing Cai, Deliang Chen, Yuting Yang, Taihua Wang, Yi Zhang, Jianshi Zhao, Bojie Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transition to renewable energy for climate mitigation often involves trade-offs with regional priorities, owning to resource competition and potential ecological impacts. Addressing these challenges requires a holistic strategy. This study examines the potential of Hydropower–Photovoltaic–Wind–Pump renewable energy base (HPWP-base) in the Yellow River Basin in China—a region facing significant sustainability pressures, to balance energy, water, and ecology. Annually, the HPWP-base could replace 23.8% (249.4 tera watt hours) of coal-fired electricity generation. Such a basin-wide transition enables 28.0% reduction in energy-related emissions, 31.5% decline in electricity-sector water consumption, and 9.0% restoration of ecosystem carbon storage. These synergistic outcomes, attained through established technologies (pump and storage hydropower, basin-wide cooperation, and grid expansion), show the practicality of the HPWP-base. Therefore, it can serve as a solution to accelerate regional electricity transformation while advancing sustainable development, and enable policymakers to appreciate how to link renewable energy transition with multiple sustainable goals.

Introduction

Advancing global and regional sustainability lies in resolving the resource–environment–development trilemma (REDT) that socio-economic development conflicts with finite resources and environmental protection1. While mitigating climate change through renewable energy transitions is essential, these pathways often introduce unexpected trade-offs with regional resource competition and ecological protection. Historically, pursuing socio-economic development has been at the expense of natural resources and ecosystems. Unintended global climate change is especially critical, accelerating the Earth’s approach to multiple tipping points2–4 while worsening water scarcity and ecological degradation5–8. Addressing the REDT requires a holistic strategy that acknowledges these interconnections and minimizes trade-offs in policy and decision-making.

This study implements such a holistic approach to the Yellow River Basin (YRB) in China, a region where the REDT is acutely visible and grapples with sustainability challenges. For instance, high coal production, severe water scarcity (7.8% of China’s land but only 2.7% of its freshwater resources), and significant development pressures9 (Fig. 1). The YRB produces 56% of China’s coal (approximately 2422 Mt annually) and contributes roughly 8.6% of national energy-related emissions. Emission reductions enabled by renewable energy transition in energy-intensive regions like the YRB are essential for regional and global sustainability10–13. Although numerous renewable energy transition pathways have been proposed14,15, their implementation could collide with socio-economic and resource constraints. For example, phasing out coal may undermine local revenues in the YRB, while integrating wind-solar power requires flexible generation sources to ensure supply reliability16–21. The YRB is rich in coal resources, accounting for roughly 56% of China’s coal production, 20% of the country’s installed coal-fired power capacity, and 8.6% of its energy-related emissions. Over the past two decades, rapid industrial development has intensified severe water scarcity, especially in areas with dense coal mining and coal-fired power plants.

Environmental costs further complicate renewable energy transitions. Coal mining and utilization degrade land and water resources16,22. Meanwhile, escalating temperatures and shifting precipitation patterns linked to energy-related emissions are pushing ecosystems toward the risk of collapsing key services. Such as freshwater supply and carbon sequestration that are vital for sustainable development23–26. Therefore, these challenges necessitate a sustainable energy transition pathway that simultaneously declines emissions, prevents environmental damage, and mitigates resource competition, while remaining affordable. Affordability is especially critical in regions with economic and resource constraints, where progress toward Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) requires leveraging local resources for energy security while keeping costs manageable.

Prior studies have shown the value of pump and storage hydropower (PSH) as a cornerstone of renewable energy transition pathways, which strengthens system resilience under climate change27. PSH currently accounts for the largest share of global dispatchable capacity (approximately 165 GW), and all energy storage projects with cascades can meet approximately 79% of global electricity demand in 201719. Beyond power generation, PSH supports water security by reducing dependence on conventional hydropower, enabling more allocation of water for human and ecological needs during droughts28.

Based on abundant wind–solar resources, flexible hydropower, and supportive policy initiatives promoting the development of Hydropower–Photovoltaic–Wind–Pump renewable energy base (HPWP-base) in the YRB29–31, this study leverages the HPWP-base to address the REDT challenge through renewable energy transition. This framework aims to cut emissions, alleviate water scarcity, and conserve ecosystems by replacing coal-fired electricity generation and restoring land from coal mining. Through implementing a basin-wide estimation and optimization that is connected to practical conditions of the YRB (Supplementary Fig. 1–3, Supplementary Table 1), the capacity configuration of the HPWP-base is determined based on five scenarios (Table 1 and “Methods”). Reduced emissions, water savings and ecological conservation enabled by HPWP-based are discussed. Results

Electricity sector decarbonization enabled by HPWP-base

Under a separate operation mode, leveraging 33 hydropower plants can integrate 4.5 GW of wind plants and 24.5 GW of solar photovoltaic plants, respectively. HPWP-base achieves annual 73.6 TWh of clean electricity (Fig. 2). After deploying 49.5 GW of PSH, integrated wind plants and solar photovoltaic plants increased by 8.3 GW and 71.2 GW in HPWP-base. Correspondingly, annual electricity generation reaches 178.8 GW, which is 142.9% higher than that of the S-HPW scenario (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 4). The YRB’s coal-fired electricity generation is approximately 1046.4 TWh in 2023. Under the coordinated operation mode, installation capacity of wind and solar power are 5.6 GW and 24.7 GW in the HPW scenario, respectively. Annual electricity generation is 76.4 TWh. PSH (49.05 GW) further facilitates an additional 6.9 GW of wind power and 69.1 GW of solar powers integrated into the HPWP base. Annual electricity generation is 209.2 TWh under C-HPWP scenario, which is 173.8% higher than that of the C-HPW scenario. HPWP-base can thus replace 7.3% and 20.0% of coal-fired electricity under C-HPW and C-HPWP scenarios, respectively.

Compared with the separate operation mode, coordinated operation benefits the deployment of HPWP-based and increases annual electricity generation by 3.8% and 17.0% in C-HPW and C-HPWP scenarios, respectively (Fig. 2). Meanwhile, differences in flexible generation sources shift center of wind-solar power exploitation (Supplementary Fig. 5). For example, under both S-HPW and C-HPW scenarios, as approximately 63% of hydropower plants are located upstream, higher flexibility in upstream makes wind-solar power installation capacity of midstream decline by an average 54.6% compared to that of upstream. In contrast, under S-HPWP and C-HPWP scenarios, wind-solar capacity of midstream is on average 83.8% higher than that of midstream. This shift of wind-solar exploitation center is attributed to the limited ultra-high-voltage (UHV) channels transmission capacity in the upstream, which constrains the construction of PSH and wind-solar power integration. Consequently, the electricity generation of midstream is an average 53.8% and 113.1% of that of upstream under the separate operation mode and the coordinated operation mode, respectively.

Therefore, basin-wide cooperation and deploying PSH facilitate integration of wind-solar power, as well as basin-wide electricity sector decarbonization. The results show that 49.05 GW of PSH plants enable the integration of additional integration of 70–80 GW wind-solar power. Reasonability of this increased integration is verified by detailed analysis results of annual energy balance, hourly power balance, and changes in PSH’s energy storage (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). This improvement is attributed to PSH’s primary role in managing the critical temporal mismatch and surplus power peaks of variable generation. However, basin-wide balanced development is constrained by notable UHV transmission capacity between upstream and midstream.

Harmonizing basin-wide renewable energy transition

Striking contrast between abundant flexible dispatch capability and wind-solar resource, and only one UHV direct-current line (with 6 GW of transmission capacity; Supplementary Table 2) in the upstream, makes HPWP-base deployment necessitates expanding transmission channels. Connected to practical conditions of the YRB, UHV channels in upstream and midstream are expanded by 40.9 GW and 19.5 GW, respectively (Fig. 3a), under the Channal expansion scenario. Compared to the result of the C-HPWP scenario, electricity generation of upstream is increased by 27.7 TWh (Fig. 3b), while the difference in electricity generation between upstream and midstream has declined to 82.3%. Meanwhile, the total capacity of wind-solar power in upstream and midstream is 74.7 GW and 59.4 GW, respectively (Fig. 3c). Under the channel expansion scenario, annual electricity generation of the HPWP-base is 249.4 TWh, which is 19.3% higher than that of the C-HPWP scenario (Fig. 3d). HPWP-base can thus replace 23.8% of coal-fired electricity. Climate, water, and ecology co-benefits

Energy transition in the YRB has far-reaching implications for balancing energy, water, and ecology. After adopting strategies including basin-wide cooperative operation, deploying PSH and expanding UHV channels, the achievable coal-fired electricity replacement is up to 7.0% in S-HPW scenario, 20.0% in C-HPWP scenario, and 23.8% in Channel expansion scenario (Fig. 4a). Replacing coal-fired electricity with clean electricity cuts annual coal consumption by 44.1 Mt, 123.2 Mt, and 147.6 Mt under the S-HPW, C-HPWP, and Channel Expansion scenarios, respectively (Fig. 4b). As a result, maximum CO₂ emissions could decline by 0.29 Pg year⁻¹—equivalent to about 28.0% of the YRB’s energy-related emissions—while methane emissions from coal mining would fall by 0.003 Pg year⁻¹. Together, these reductions contribute to a 2.3% decrease in China’s total CO₂ emissions in 2022 (Fig. 4c). Replacing coal-fired electricity with HPWP-based renewable generation also delivers significant water savings. Based on the empirical relationship between coal replacement and water use (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9), substituting 1 kWh of coal-fired power saves an average of 1.9 × 10−3 m3 of water. This translates to annual water savings of 0.14 km3, 0.39 km3 and 0.47 km3 under the S-HPW, C-HPWP and Channel expansion scenarios, respectively (Fig. 4d). From 2010 to 2021, mean annual water use of agriculture, industry, domestic and ecological conservation is 37.4 km3, 6.7 km3, 5.7 km3 and 2.9 km3, respectively. The maximum savings under the Channel expansion scenario equal 31.5% of electricity-sector water consumption, 1.3% of agricultural use, 7.0% of industrial use, 8.2% of domestic use, and 16.4% of ecological use in the YRB. Sensitivity analyses and life-cycle assessments further confirm the robustness of these estimates (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Beyond water conservation, reduced coal mining through HPWP-base deployment enables large-scale land recovery. Under the Channel Expansion scenario, about 1352.9 km2 of land formerly used for coal mining could be reclaimed for ecological restoration. Restoring this area would increase ecosystem carbon storage by 0.01 Pg C—equivalent to roughly 9% of the carbon storage lost to coal mining activities.

Discussion

The HPWP-base is implemented to address sustainability pressures in the YRB through replacing coal-fired electricity with clean renewable power. Prior studies emphasized the necessity of structural shifts in electricity systems aimed at climate mitigation. However, such advocacy often loses traction when weighed against regional economic priorities. These trade-offs can thus hinder the achievement of sustainable development goals32. This study reframes renewable energy transition not merely as a decarbonization effort, but as a strategic solution to the REDT.

This framework enables policymakers to appreciate how HPWP-base delivers direct co-benefits: reducing CO2 emissions (approximately 2.3% of China’s 2022 total), enhancing carbon sequestration via repurposing coal mine lands for ecological restoration, and saving water of 0.47 km3 each year. Persistent cycle of resource depletion, environmental degradation, and unsustainable development is thus disrupted, while advancing multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) outlined in Agenda 2030 (Fig. 4b–d), including Goal 13 (climate action), Goal 7 (affordable and clean energy), Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation), and Goal 3 (good health and well-being).

In water-scarce and ecologically vulnerable regions like the YRB, these co-benefits align with China’s strategic objectives, including “carbon peaking and carbon neutrality” and “Ecological Conservation and High-Quality Development of the Yellow River Basin”30. Supporting these adequate policies, future electricity transition frameworks focused on maximizing natural resource utilization, can transform trade-offs into synergistic achievements by: (1) advancing regional sustainability alongside global climate goals, and (2) reducing environmental impacts of electricity generation while promoting equitable energy access. These analyses show that incorporating socio-ecological dimensions into energy system design is both necessary and technically achievable.

The feasibility of this incorporation in the YRB rests on three key conditions. First, the national 15th Five-Year Plan for Building a New Energy System33 explicitly prioritizes the development of renewable energy bases in the “Ji Zi Bend” area and the upstream of the YRB. The Plan provides strong and timely policy backing for the collaborative arrangements required for the energy development recommended by this study. Second, the Yellow River Conservancy Commission, as the basin-level authority, possesses the institutional mandate and operational capacity that can effectively mediate potential dispatching conflicts among cascade hydropower stations, thereby ensuring coordinated reservoir operations. Third, the recommended renewable energy transition in the YRB aims to balance energy production, water use, and ecological protection, and is not expected to create water-use conflicts between the energy sector and other sectors, such as irrigation. Complementary generation and coordinated operation of hydropower (especially PSH) with wind and solar power at sub-daily timescales can reduce the need for storage and release regulation by traditional hydropower. This, in turn, can provide greater operational flexibility and leave more capacity to meet irrigation demands, which typically occur continuously over multi-day periods34,35. Moreover, economic benefits of the energy-water-ecology synergy will include both direct energy generation benefits and co-benefits with reduced CO2 emissions, saved water for water supply and irrigation, and mitigated health-related impacts16.

Technologically, unlike approaches dependent on breakthroughs in storage materials or emerging generation technologies, HPWP-base leverages established technologies, offering a solution for resource- and technology-constrained regions. However, HPWP-base could shift hydropower operational paradigms of hydropower plants, as suppressing the volatility of wind-solar power, requiring frequent start-up and shut-down cycles. Coordinated operation of PSH and conventional hydropower plants is thus essential for enhancing supply reliability and reducing operational pressures of traditional hydropower plants. This coordination allows more water to support critical services, including domestic water supply, ecological flows, flood control, shipping, and irrigation27,36–41. Despite this, it should be noted that future hydropower designs should emphasize instantaneous power output to complement variable renewables, rather than relying solely on capacity-focused planning. Through five-scenario analyses, this study acknowledges these real-world barriers (such as comprehensive ownership, lacking PSH) while quantifying the considerable benefits of overcoming them. It underscores the critical need for an innovative coordinated dispatch mechanism and PSH to realize the quantified system-level benefits in the YRB. Furthermore, bridging regional transmission gaps facilitates spatially balanced development (Fig. 3).

Extended modeling over 35 years (1984–2018) indicates that climate variability introduces uncertainties into HPWP-base’s performance under the Channel expansion scenario (Supplementary Fig. 10). Annual electricity generation is an average of 252.8 TWh, 1.4% higher than the planned year (249.4 TWh). Yet, annual electricity generation for the seven years fell below the planned-year level due to hydrological and meteorological fluctuations. These results suggest that annual generation targets are achievable despite climate uncertainty, but performance is sensitive to climate variability—particularly droughts, which could constrain both energy production and ecological flows. Careful site selection and climate-adaptive planning are therefore critical. HPWP-base may be especially effective in high-latitude regions where snow and glacier melt sustain river flows, and seasonal demand patterns favor hydropower support18,25. Attention should also be given to compound events of extreme demand spikes and energy droughts, which can have significant socio-economic consequences. Furthermore, additional sensitivity analyses are conducted to verify the robustness of our water-saving estimates (Supplementary Fig. 4). Results show that variations in regional water consumption coefficients (reflecting different cooling methods) cause minimal variances in total water saved (only 4.08–6.67% across S-HPW and Channel expansion scenarios). Additionally, accounting for the lifecycle water consumption of wind and PV plants adds merely 6–10% to total water use, which is significantly lower than that of coal-fired plants.

Economic and societal implications also warrant consideration. Transitioning away from coal-dependent economies may affect industrial stability and employment. While research indicates overall labor impacts are limited (net job changes of 0.1–1% of the total workforce), effects are concentrated among coal-fired plants and mining workers42,43. Spatial mismatches between these regions and emerging renewable hubs exacerbate relocation challenges; displaced workers may need to travel nearly 200 km for green jobs, and fewer than 15% can conveniently shift sectors44. Strategic siting of renewable projects near coal-fired plants could facilitate these transitions. Moreover, environmental trade-offs associated with PSH, wind, and PV (such as land-use changes or microclimate impacts45–47) require further study. For example, PV installations can improve vegetation growth in arid areas through microclimate moderation47,48, suggesting potential synergies with agrivoltaics49. While this study does not quantify all ecological impacts, it underscores the socio-ecological co-benefits of a well-optimized renewable system.

Beyond the YRB, HPWP-base might offer a transferable model for sustainable renewable transitions in other regions worldwide. Currently, there are 1400 GW of conventional hydropower and 135 GW of PSH globally50 (Supplementary Fig. 11), concentrated in North America, Northern Europe, and East Asia—regions with substantial electricity demand. These extensive infrastructures provide a robust engineering foundation for implementing HPWP-base. Yet, 45% of these facilities are in water-scarce areas, limiting HPWP-base potential. In South and Southeast Asia, water scarcity and cloudy conditions could constrain system performance. In contrast, North America, Latin America, Southern Africa, and parts of East/Southeast Asia could leverage integrated water-energy management to maximize benefits. In these regions, HPWP-base offers a scalable, climate-resilient pathway to address the REDT and advance sustainable development.

Methods

Data description

The meteorological data adopted in this study to estimate wind/solar power generation, including hourly 10 m wind speed, downward surface shortwave radiation and 2 m air temperature, is collected from the ECMWF Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) dataset51. Details of coal plants and coal mines in the YRB are collected from the Global Energy Monitor52, for example, location, capacity and annual CO2 emission. Daily inflow of each hydropower plant used to estimate hydropower output and, more importantly, the potential to integrate wind and PV plants, is extracted from the GRADES dataset53 and further calibrated using in-situ observations. The water scarcity, defined as the ratio of net water withdrawal to the total available water for a region, is collected from a global water scarcity analysis5. Furthermore, other necessary information involved in the site selection, including digital elevation model, slope, and land cover type, is also collected and compiled into a raster dataset with a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km. Carbon intensity for diverse land covers54, is collected to estimate the potential increase in carbon storage, as a result of the retirement of coal mines.

Basin-wide renewable energy base

HPWP-base is defined as the clean electricity supply center with transmission and storage infrastructures, in which hydropower (including PSH) is positioned as the flexible generation source, aimed at facilitating integration of wind and solar power. HPWP-base is designed to resolve the REDT in the YRB by replacing coal-fired electricity. This study optimizes the capacity configuration of HPWP-base and simulates its maximum electricity generation matching with load curve changes at the daily scale.

Optimizing capacity configuration of HPWP-base

There are 33 hydropower plants located on the mainstream of the Yellow River. Specifically, there are 22 and 11 hydropower plants located upstream and midstream, respectively. Optimizing capacity configuration of HPWP-base in basin-wide is thus challenging, as these hydropower plants are owned by 15 companies and there are tremendous differences in available ultra-high-voltage (UHV) channels between upstream and midstream of the YRB (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, the geophysical distribution of wind and solar sources highlights that both wind and solar resources in the upstream are richer than those of other regions (Supplementary Fig. 5). Accordingly, considering differences in resources, especially transmission channels, the YRB is divided into two regions, including the upstream and midstream and the HPWP-base is optimized for two regions, respectively. In other words, cross-region electricity transmission and wind-solar utilization are not allowed between upstream and midstream. Meanwhile, HPWP-base consists of 33 HPWP-units, which consist of wind plants, solar photovoltaic plants, PSH, and a hydropower plant. Each corresponds to a specific hydropower plant. Crucially, the capacity optimization is performed independently for each unit, determining its own dedicated wind and solar PV capacity, rather than applying a single, aggregated solution across the entire basin.

To reflect the true complex conditions of YRB while researching sustainable development pathways through renewable energy transition, achievable capacity configuration and regarding clean electricity generation, are searched for two decision modes:

(1) Separate operation mode. This scenario is directly connected to the real-world conditions of the YRB. Each HPWP unit, as an independent profit-seeking entity, aims to maximize its own power generation. The solution of this mode is a Nash Equilibrium, where no HPWP-units can unilaterally increase their own generation by changing their generation schedule, given the schedules of all other HPWP-units. Socio-ecological benefits of basin-wide energy transition

Coal-fired plants are characterized by high water consumption, high CO2 emissions, and heavy air pollution, and thus, accelerating the retirement of coal-fired plants is usually the top topic of climate change mitigation. Replacing coal-fired plants with the HPWP-base could not only introduce clear electricity, but also achieve tremendous socio-ecological benefits, including water stress mitigation, air quality enhancement, and ecological conservation. Here, these benefits are estimated through the water consumption coefficient and coal consumption of unit generation of coal-fired plants and unit production of coal mines, which are compiled from industry water quota published by eight provinces (Supplementary Tables 3, 4).

Water savings (km3 year−1) are divided into two parts, including saved water from coal-fired electricity replacement (Wele, only cooling water is considered in this study, which accounts for 90% of the total water consumption of coal-fired plants), and saved water from coal mining (Wmining). For the former, water saving potential is estimated as water consumption coefficient of unit generation of coal-fired plants multiplies by the amount of electricity generation of the HPWP-base (as shown by blue dots in Supplementary Fig. 8). Strategies for improving co-benefits through facilitating wind-solar integration

Intrinsic intermittency, fluctuation and randomness hamper direct integration of wind-solar power; hydropower (including pump and storage hydropower) is introduced to suppress the volatility of wind-solar power. However, there are a few pump and storage hydropower plants that have been built in the YRB. Therefore, an optimal wind-solar capacity configuration is first searched through leveraging the flexibility of 33 hydropower plants. This scenario is named Hydropower-PV-Wind (HPW). To facilitate integration of wind-solar power and improve supply reliability, pump and storage hydropower plants are further introduced into the HPWP base, which is named the Hydropower-PV-Wind-Pump scenario (HPWP). Furthermore, additional transmission capacity needed to expand basin-wide co-benefits is further discussed, which is named the Channel expansion scenario (Channel expansion).

Both HPW and HPWP are implemented in separate operation mode and coordinated operation mode, namely, S-HPW, S-HPWP, C-HPW and C-HPWP scenarios. Comparing the results of the HPW and the HPWP aims to analyze the influences of PSH on integrating wind and solar power. Channel expansion is only implemented in coordinated operation mode, which aims to reveal the necessity of expanding the transmission channel in the YRB.
