Determining the utility of radio frequency identification (RFID) technology for disaster victim identification (DVI)
Authors: Makena P. McLean, Vienna C. Lam, Megan Bassendale, Gail S. Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadio frequency identification (RFID) technology was evaluated as a method for electronically tracking human remains and associated identifying information during mass fatality incidents. Conventional tags are prone to physical damage and failure, which can compromise identification processes and hinder the repatriation of bodies. Nine types of uniquely numbered RFID tags were deployed in decompositional and burial environments to assess the survivability of different types of RFID tags buried, inserted, and attached to pig carcasses. The best method of insertion or attachment to keep tags in situ, and whether physical writing in pencil or permanent marker remained on the tags' surfaces. In a series of four studies of whole carcasses, pieces of tissue, as human proxies, and burials in soil, all but two tags were determined to be viable throughout the decomposition process. All tags displayed movement issues, so bone attachment sites are recommended. Handwritten labels on the tags prepared under dry conditions and when protected from decompositional fluids held up better than writing interacting with the fluids. We found the most useful tags were the LF Yellow Ear Tag and the UHF White Tag.

INTRODUCTION

The loss of life and property following disasters is profoundly devastating for both individuals and communities. According to the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC), in 2021 alone, 121.3 million people were affected by disasters, resulting in 14,577 reported deaths [1]. The identification and repatriation of deceased individuals is of paramount importance, providing closure for families and ensuring that dignity and respect are preserved for those who have died. This is a central purpose of Disaster Victim Identification (DVI) [2]. Beyond its humanitarian value, DVI also has critical legal and societal functions, including facilitating insurance claims and contributing to investigations of potential crimes [3, 4]. Given these implications, ensuring that identifications are both accurate and complete is essential.

Disaster environments are highly dynamic, requiring personnel to potentially operate under hazardous and often unpredictable conditions [3]. Recoveries may occur in severely damaged or destroyed structures, as has been documented in Gaza [5, 6]. In some contexts, such as the ongoing armed conflict between Ukraine and Russia, hostilities are still active. Under such circumstances, DVI teams face significant risks and are often compelled to work rapidly to safeguard both themselves and the deceased [5, 7]. However, traditional body‐tracking tags are not fail‐safe; they have been reported to fail or degrade due to environmental factors or chemical exposure, such as contact with embalming fluids [8, 9]. Furthermore, maintaining a reliable chain of custody is critical, as human remains may constitute evidence in legal or forensic proceedings [10].

Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) tags offer the potential to assign unique tracking tags to unidentified human remains, thereby supporting the identification process during disaster victim recovery. RFID technology is already widely applied across diverse fields, including the tracking of companion animals, wildlife, commercial goods, and even individuals at large events [11], suggesting possible applicability in forensic contexts. However, suitability for DVI depends on whether these tags can endure the extreme conditions typically encountered, such as decomposition, insect colonization, and burial [12, 13].

RFID utilizes radio waves and radar technology to send a unique number to an RFID reader from an RFID tag [11, 14]. RFID tags are small objects that come in a variety of sizes and shapes, and transmit radio waves containing a unique identification number [8, 15]. These are read by a scanner that is able to pick up and display the number that the RFID devices transmit [11]. The tags come with varying levels of radio wave frequency from ultra‐high, high, and low [16], with some costing only a few cents [14].

RFID tags could be extremely useful in human identification in disasters but only if they can survive the harsh conditions of decomposition. Human decomposition progresses through five recognized stages: fresh, bloat, active decay, advanced decay, and dry remains [17, 18]. The duration of each stage varies considerably depending on environmental conditions [12]. Decomposition is also influenced by myriad interacting intrinsic and extrinsic variables. Temperature is a highly influential factor, with warmer conditions accelerating decomposition [13]. This process can be further intensified by insect activity, particularly maggot masses, which may elevate localized temperatures to 35°C–50°C or higher [19, 20, 21, 22]. Additional environmental factors, including rainfall, humidity, and sun exposure, also contribute to variation in decomposition rates [13]. In certain contexts, bodies may undergo adipocere formation, a waxy, gray substance that develops under wet, anaerobic conditions when fatty acids are preserved [13]. Conversely, in arid environments, mummification may occur [22]. Any identification tag designed for disaster victim recovery must therefore be durable enough to withstand this full spectrum of postmortem conditions.

The application of RFID technology to DVI has been explored only to a limited extent. One of the earliest documented uses occurred during recovery efforts following Hurricane Katrina in 2005, when the United States Disaster Mortuary Operational Response Team (DMORT) attached RFID tags to the deceased in an effort to expedite identification [23, 24]. In this context, tags were injected into the shoulder of the body or placed within the body bag, and at least one coroner reported that their use accelerated the identification process [24].

Subsequent research has focused on practitioner perspectives and alternative applications. In Australia, interviews with DVI experts provided insight into attitudes toward RFID, with participants expressing concern about potential data loss in the event of technological failure [8]. Importantly, they emphasized the need to retain manual, paper‐based methods to ensure information was not lost if RFID malfunctioned [8]. While valuable, this study did not evaluate whether RFID tags could withstand the environmental challenges of DVI conditions. Other investigations have had some success with embedding RFID chips into teeth [25, 26, 27].

Additional field studies have assessed RFID‐enabled recovery kits. An Australian trial of RFID bracelets and scene marker tags found them to be well received, with practitioners reporting significantly reduced processing times per body, to the extent that the RFID‐based kits were preferred over traditional methods [7]. RFID tags have also demonstrated survivability in freezer storage [10]. In veterinary and animal‐tracking applications, RFID tags designed for pets are hermetically sealed to prevent fluid ingress, allowing them to function reliably within living organisms; however, their resilience during postmortem decomposition remains unverified [27]. During the DVI efforts following the 2004 Boxing Day tsunami, practitioners successfully implanted RFID tags into the nasal cavities of severely decomposed deceased persons, although traditional tagging was still considered more appropriate for less decomposed bodies [9]. Although limited research has been conducted on the feasibility of using RFID tags in DVI, no research has been conducted on whether tags will survive the conditions of decomposition, nor which tags would be best for deployment. If RFID tags could be used, the best attachment or insertion sites have not been explored, nor has the possibility of adding backup data. The primary aim of this research was to evaluate the survivability, readability, convenience, and functionality of nine commercially available RFID tags under conditions simulating disaster victim recovery. Four sequential studies were undertaken: (I) to assess tag survivability during decomposition in whole carcasses, (II) to identify optimal placement positions within tissue, (III) to evaluate survivability during burial, and (IV) to determine whether tags could retain surface annotations made with pencil or permanent marker throughout decomposition and burial. The findings are intended to inform DVI teams on the feasibility of incorporating RFID technology as a reliable tool for cataloguing human remains.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The survivability and readability of nine types of RFID tags were evaluated in outdoor decomposition environments with the aim of identifying the most useful RFID tags and their optimal tag placement location (attached to, vs. inserted into, tissue) for recovery post‐decomposition. Table 1 gives details of each tag. We inserted RFID tags in and around commercially available whole pig carcasses (Sus scrofa domesticus Erxleben) and pig shoulders that were protected from large vertebrate scavengers in bear‐proof, wire‐lined cages. A total of 134 tags were tested, including replacement tags. The research was carried out over four studies: (I) a preliminary study on RFID viability, (II) full experimentation on RFID survivability, which included an assessment of insertion and attachment techniques, (III) RFID survival in a buried context, and (IV) an assessment of analog writing (backup notation) survivability. All field experiments took place at two sites within the Coastal Western Hemlock Biogeoclimatic Zone [28]: (a) British Columbia Institute of Technology (BCIT) Woodlot, Maple Ridge, Canada, and (b) study III (not involving biological components) was conducted at a private residential site nearby. All laboratory activities were conducted at the Simon Fraser University (SFU) Centre for Forensic Research's (CFR) Forensic Entomology Laboratory, Burnaby, BC, Canada. This research received exemption from animal care ethics approval by the SFU University Animal Care Committee. This research adheres to the standards set forth by the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC), and considerable effort was made to ensure that none of the carcasses used for this study were euthanized specifically for this project. Animal products used in this study were redirected from food consumption for this research. Full carcasses were already being utilized in a forensic course at BCIT and were used in this project secondarily, so this work was ruled to be exempt from further ethics approval by the SFU Ethics Board.

RFID tags and scanner specifications

After placement, nine RFID tags, which differed in size, shape, and material composition, were assessed on site, on a weekly basis (Figure 1). The majority of these tags were purchased online, with one being obtained from local retailers. Selection criteria were informed in part by Lovell et al. [8], whose study participants expressed concern about relying solely on technological means of body documentation, highlighting the risk of information loss due to potential errors. Consequently, several tags that allowed for mechanical inscription directly onto their surfaces were included in the assessment.

Summary of the nine commercially available Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) tags used in this research.

Each applicable tag was labeled on both the front and back with the identifiers “Tag One” or “Tag Two,” using both a pencil and a permanent marker. Physical inscription was not feasible for tags with limited surface area. All inscriptions were refreshed as necessary between testing rounds; however, no writing was replaced during the burial study, as it was conducted continuously without distinct experimental phases.

The field assessments of the tags' working condition were evaluated by the first author and second author in the full pig carcass experiment. The field assessments of the tags' working condition for the burials and pig shoulders were evaluated by the first author. The legibility of the writing was evaluated both subjectively in the field by the first author and through analysis of photographic documentation following the experiment by other authors. At each inspection point, inscriptions were categorized as good, moderate, poor, or obfuscated. A rating of good indicated that the writing appeared fresh and consistent with its original state; moderate denoted fading, smudging, chipping, or reduced visibility; poor referred to instances where portions of the writing were missing or significantly degraded, suggesting potential information loss; and obfuscated described writing that was no longer discernible.

A pet chip scanner made by Sonew was used to scan LF tags. It read at a 134.2 kHz frequency, also capturing the 125 kHz frequencies. This reader was wireless and did not require recharging throughout every study. A Fongwah 860–960 MHz USB UHF reader was used to read the UHF tags as they required a higher frequency reader. This UHF reader plugs into a laptop and records the numbers read on an open document, whereas the LF scanner displays the numbers on a screen, which must be recorded separately by hand. The LF scanner measured 135 by 53.1 by 59.95 mm and the UHF tag measured 86.1 by 0.8 by 54.1 mm. Both scanners were manufactured in China and purchased from Amazon.ca.

In the first experiment with pig shoulders, commonly found ribbon string was used to attach the Blue Key Chain Tags to the cage and to ensure they were not lost during the decomposition changes. Embroidery thread was used for burial experiments and for the smaller tags interacting with the pig shoulders or cage. Embroidery thread is composed of six individual strands, and originally, we used just three strands to avoid overwhelming the smaller tags. For example, the Pet Tags were very small, so three strands were used to ensure the most tissue contact. Therefore, this was selected because the thickness of the thread could be altered to properly tie around the small Pet Tags. In addition, its bright red or pink color made it visible throughout the burial and decompositional environments. Zip ties were used to attach the larger tags to the pig shoulders or cage. Plastic zip ties were selected for their durability. The larger tags, such as the Card Tag, Sticky Tag, White Tag, and Yellow Label Tag, could not be inserted all the way into the shoulders due to their large sizes and need for retrieval to assess physical writing. Attachment methods for each of the tags can be seen in Table 1.

Study I: Initial study of RFID viability protocol

The first experiment used full pig carcasses and ran for 5 weeks over the summer of 2023. Four intact juvenile pigs weighing approximately 23 kg were killed by a butcher using a captive bolt gun, then immediately transported to the research site and placed into bear‐proof cages (105 × 45 × 75 cm) consisting of 3 cm thick steel bars spaced 10 cm apart and lined with chicken wire. Two carcasses were deployed on 6 July 2023, and a further two carcasses were deployed on 15 July 2023. The experiments were concluded on 10 August 2023.

RFID tags were positioned in multiple locations, including beneath the carcass, within the ears, the mouth, and inserted into the tissue, in 4 cm deep incised cuts made with a scalpel into the upper thigh/lower back of the pig. Due to the limited number of Yellow Ear Tags, none could be inserted into an incised hole within the carcass. The number and placement of tags in each experiment are detailed in Table 1. Control tags were affixed to the cages. Due to supply chain delays, only the Blue Key Chain Tag model was available for placement during the first carcass deployment. The following week, the Yellow Ear Tags were deployed. In total, 15 Blue Key Chain Tags and 10 Yellow Ear Tags were utilized. The Yellow Ear Tag controls were placed on the ground away from the body so that they were not exposed to decomposition. Internally placed tags were also assessed to determine whether they could be read in situ, inside the carcass.

Study II: Full study on RFID survivability protocol

No teaching carcasses were available for the main experiments; therefore, to prevent the use of animals killed only for an experiment [29], and as whole carcasses were not required to assess decomposition of animal tissue, fresh pork shoulder roasts, with bone and skin, weighing approximately 1.8 kg, were utilized. On 15 June 2024, three pork shoulders were placed in separate cages for each experiment. The same cages used in the preliminary experiment were cleaned and reused. The shoulders were each deployed with a pair of tags so that one acted as a control, attached to the cage. Tags were placed at the same sites in all three shoulders. See Table 1 for details of tag placement and attachment sites.

Two rounds of experiments were conducted so that each tag could be tested in two spots, either inserted into the tissue or attached to it and left outside the tissue. The incisions were made with a scalpel. The depth of the incision was approximately 4 cm. If a tag was inserted into the tissue in the first round, it would be attached to the outside of the tissue in the second, and vice versa. If the tag was attached to the tissue via zip tie or thread, a loop through the skin or tissue would be made with a scalpel to slide the zip tie or thread under and tie around. If the tag was attached to a bone, the thread would be tied around the bone. Therefore, the rounds examined the functionality of attachment locations. Inserting the tags into the tissue in one of the rounds allowed each tag to interact directly with the decomposition process. Due to time constraints, not every possible site on the shoulder could be examined with every tag. Since the tags were being read via radio waves, we thought it was also important to keep them separated, thus reducing potential locations for the pig on the moderately sized shoulder. During decomposition, the Pet Tags and Dot Tags had to be reinserted into incised holes and tied with embroidery thread, as they were at risk of being lost since they were so small. Tags were assessed weekly for 9 weeks, and the first round concluded on 10 August 2024. All control tags remained attached to the cage for the duration of both rounds. Readings of the tags were taken through the tissue, when possible, in both rounds. The second round began on 10 August 2024 and was also assessed once a week. This study was concluded on 5 October 2024, after 9 weeks.

Study III: RFID burial survivability study

Six Blue Key Chain and six Yellow Ear Tags were buried in the ground to determine survivability. They were buried alone and not associated with tissue. Four holes were dug by hand using an auger shovel in a residential yard in Pitt Meadows, BC. The tags were deployed in January 2024. The ground was frozen, so the tags could not be buried deeply and were deployed at depths between 6 and 24 cm. Controls were attached to a fence post and a cinder block nearby, and two of each tag were left inside for true controls. Tags were assessed weekly through the soil and were also removed for scanning and reburied, as they were placed on top of each other in the hole so they could not be read at the same time. This experiment concluded on 15 June 2024.

A second burial experiment was conducted beginning on 15 June 2024 in the Woodlot. Two holes were dug with an auger shovel close to the cages. However, due to time constraints, the tags were deployed on the following day, 16 June 2024. The first hole was approximately 24 cm in diameter and 10 cm deep. The second hole was approximately 24 cm in diameter and 20 cm deep. Two of each tag were placed into each hole. Tags in the 10 cm hole were assessed weekly through the soil and through exhumation. Eventually, embroidery thread was added to tags that were at risk of being lost. Tags in the 20‐cm hole were left undisturbed for the duration of the study until 5 October 2024.

Study IV: Survivability of backup writing on tags

Physical handwriting using a permanent black pen and a pencil was placed on the White Tag, Card Tag, Yellow Label, and Sticky Tag to determine whether the tag would support physical writing as a backup in case a tag failed, as this has previously been mentioned as a concern [8]. The Yellow Ear Tag, Blue Key Chain Tag, Pet Tags, and Dot Tags were too small to be written upon (Figure 1).

RESULTS

Study I. Initial study of RFID viability

The preliminary experiment utilized four pig carcasses. Two acted as control carcasses to observe the decompositional changes. Every tag was successful in that no tags broke, and all tags could be read at any time during the experiment; however, several tags moved from their original position within the remains. Decomposition did not appear to affect the function of the tags, as in both cages, every tag was successfully read each week, despite the carcasses progressing from fresh through dry remains. However, tag mobility was an issue. In the instances where the tags were placed in the ears or the mouth, the tags would routinely fall out of place and required reinsertion, likely due to the presence of maggot masses and scavengers. No tag was ever lost, and each week they were returned to their designated positions as much as possible, even when the carcass was highly decomposed. As hypothesized, the mouths and ears did not consistently offer secure places to leave the tags. Rather, the Yellow Ear tags were so large that it was challenging to fit them within the ears. The ears and the mouth also decayed so quickly that natural holes started to form in the tissue around the head, allowing the tags to fall onto the ground or other portions of the pig. The Blue Key Chain tags that were inserted in the incised muscle tissue, however, performed very well and stayed in situ. The LF scanner was able to read through the pig's skin. Ambient temperature during this experiment ranged from 10.5°C to 32.5°C (Figure 2).

Study II: Full study on RFID survivability and burial survivability study

The meat went through fresh, decay, advanced decay, and most reached the dry remains stage. As the meat was only a shoulder joint, no bloat was observed. Insects colonized the remains, and small maggot masses were observed (Figures 3 and 4). All but two trial tags could be read successfully throughout both rounds of the study. This means all other environmental control, trial, and burial tags could be read by the scanner successfully. The first round consisted of half the tags being deployed inside the tissue while the other half were attached to the tissue (Figure 3). In the second round, these positions were switched, thereby giving each tag an opportunity to come into direct contact with the decomposing tissue in at least one round, apart from the Pet Tags, which were inserted into the tissue in both rounds (Figure 4). None of the control tags, once attached to the cage, were lost or became detached from the cage. Although some were recovered, some small trial tags were permanently lost in the cages. Temperatures ranged from 5.5°C to 33.5°C (Figure 2).

Attachment methods

None of the tags in this study were completely resistant to movement as decomposition progressed. At least one of the tags in each of the cages moved throughout the study and had to be reattached. Any time a tag was removed to read, it would be reinserted into the original position or into a position interacting with the decomposing tissue if the original position no longer existed. This was done to ensure the tags would interact with the decomposing tissue for the entire study. The embroidery thread interaction with the decompositional fluid resulted in the thread becoming brittle and breaking in some cases. Once the full six strands were used to tie the tags, this problem was reduced, though there were a few instances where the knot came undone. The zip ties were never reused.

Tag viability in tissue

The easy‐to‐see Blue Key Chain Tag performed well despite its proximity to other tags, sometimes resulting in other tags reading instead of the desired tag. Therefore, it could be read in situ but did have to be removed on occasion. This issue occurred with a few of the tags but would likely not be an issue in the field, as only one tag would be utilized rather than several as in these experiments. In round one, as the tissue decomposed, the tag fell deeper into the tissue or the cage floor. In the second round, the string attachment to the bone held up well, though it did have to be retied in some cases. A stronger string would eliminate this issue. Since the Blue Key Chain Tag was further away from other tags in round two, it read in situ.

The Yellow Ear Tag also performed well, though its shape made it difficult to retrieve from the incised holes on some occasions in round one when it had to be removed to be read. Its removal was required sometimes when the scanner could not reach deep enough into the incised hole. In the second round, a zip tie attachment on top of the skin often proved very unsuccessful and broke.

The Card Tag is unsuitable for DVI as it was the only tag to break, potentially due to water seeping inside the tag, highlighting the need for a robust encasement material. This tag also fell deeper into the tissue or cage floor in round one, while in round two the zip tie also often broke off from the skin. This tag could be read in situ in both rounds until one of the tags broke.

The adhesive Sticky Tag never stuck to the tissue and, on occasion, had to be lifted to the scanner to be captured in the scans, thereby not reading consistently in situ in either round. The adhesive issues also meant the tag eventually ended up deeper inside the tissue and on the cage floor as decomposition occurred. As described before, as the tissue decayed, the tag fell further within it.

The Blue and Purple Pet tags remained within the tissue consistently throughout both rounds. However, once the tissue had decayed, the tags became exposed, highlighting a potential risk for these small tags to be lost if not tied down. Both Tags could be read through the tissue, however, similarly to the Blue Key Chain Tag and Yellow Ear Tag, their proximity to each other and other tags often meant one had to be removed to read both.

The Dot Tag would not read in situ and had to be removed from the tissue to be read on nearly every scan in both rounds. Its small size meant it would be lost if not tied down. One, however, was recovered. This tag also bent, broke, and became unreadable when stepped on, a real possibility in the field.

The White Tag performed very well in the first round, remaining very securely attached once the skin had dried out. In cage two, however, once in the dry remains stage, there was nothing to which to attach the tag. During the second round, it frequently had to be removed from inside the tissue to be read, whereas in the first round, it was read in situ very easily.

The Yellow Label Tag had to be removed from the tissue on occasion to capture the reading. In the first round, the tag eventually moved deeper into the tissue or to the cage floor, while in the second, it often leaned against the tissue or the cage floor as decomposition occurred and did require reattachment on occasions.

Every tag would, depending on its placement in a specific round (whether it was interacting with the tissue or not), shift as decomposition took place, resulting in the tags falling deeper into the remaining tissue or even to the cage floor.

Study III: Effect of burial on viability

All buried tags could be read successfully throughout the duration of the first burial study. This was the only study to take place in the winter. Therefore, the Blue Key Chain Tag and Yellow Ear Tag each experienced cold temperatures and snowfall, with both surviving throughout the entire winter period until June, when they were reused in the main experiment. The minimum temperature recorded during this time was −8.5°C. Though operating temperatures for these tags were not given, the tags successfully survived the outdoor environment in the Pacific Northwest for several months.

The very shallowly buried tags routinely become exposed throughout the week, perhaps due to small animal activity, wind, and/or weather changes. Therefore, at the end of each assessment, the tags would be reburied. The shallow tags could be read through the minimal amount of soil that was on top of them. In the final assessment on 15 June 2024, one of the buried Blue Key Chain Tags could not be found.

The more deeply buried tags were never naturally exposed. The auger's diameter only allowed for a hole large enough to fit both tags if they were stacked on top of one another. It was difficult to read the tags through the soil and over top of one another, so they had to be uncovered and reburied each week. No tags were lost.

In the second burial study, the Sticky Tag was the most reliable tag at both depths, whereas the Blue Key Chain, Blue and Purple Pet and Dot Tags could not be read underground. The Yellow Ear, Card, White, and Yellow Label Tags could be read infrequently. The Sticky Tag was the only tag to be read at both depths. Pet Tags lost underground were never recovered in the 10‐cm hole before string was tied to the tags. A Purple Pet Tag was also permanently lost in the 20‐cm hole.

Study IV: Survivability of backup writing on tags

All the physical writing on the trial, control, and buried tags could have benefited from a protective sealant, as although there were a few cases where the writing held up well, often the physical writing on every tag was obfuscated. The writing on the White Tag faded when in direct contact with decomposition fluids. The QR code on the White Tag surface was untested. The Sticky Tags' dark and rough surface made it very difficult to see any writing and cannot be recommended for backup writing. Additionally, the writing on the Yellow Label Tags could not be properly photographed after burial and retrieval due to the material of the tag. The permanent marker, though visible in real life, did not show up in the photograph. Consequently, permanent marker is probably not suitable for the field. The Blue Key Chain, Yellow Ear, Blue and Purple Pet, and Dot Tags never received writing.

RFID scanner usability

The UHF and LF scanners both performed well in the field. The LF scanner did not require recharging throughout any of the three experiments, meaning that its battery lasted at least 16 months. Its small screen displayed the tag's number, making this a very easy‐to‐use handheld device. The UHF scanner was very sensitive; whenever a tag got within its reading distance, it would be repeatedly set off. At times when a tag was nearby, it would stop reading, but removing the tag from the reading range for a moment and bringing it back would allow the scanner to read it successfully. In the field, the scanner could be briefly removed and then reintroduced rather than removing the tag. Every time the UHF scanner read a tag, it made a loud sound to notify that it was successful. The most challenging issue related to the UHF scanner was that it was required to be plugged into a laptop to function, meaning that it was only useful as long as the laptop battery lasted, which is usually only a few hours. Laptops need regular recharging, which may be difficult in a DVI situation. Additionally, every tag would be read together as one line of code, and the individual numbers would have to be separated and verified by hand in the field. Even after verifying each number, there were times when a tag was not read, and this mistake was not noticed, meaning the tag was not removed to test. This could be a major problem in the field. It is important to note that only one UHF scanner was used because the scanners can be quite expensive. A different scanner or a more expensive option may yield different results.

DISCUSSION

In disaster situations, the scene must be cleared as safe before any DVI personnel enter [3] but this may not be possible in active hostile situations [7] or disasters where hazards such as fires, earthquake aftershocks, flooding, and many other threats to safety exist. Disasters can also involve weapons, unexploded ordnance, chemicals, and broken infrastructure that can all be challenging for DVI professionals who must enter and work within the environment [3]. The environment can also be disturbed by the disaster, meaning roads may not be accessible, and power, water, and other amenities may be lacking [3]. The unique job of disaster recovery involves navigating these challenges. This means a system that accurately and quickly tags the bodies is required to aid the practitioners in their work [7, 8]. Although almost all tags survived decomposition, some were considered more valuable for DVI than others.

Recommended tags

Most of the tags survived the effects of decomposition, but all moved within the tissues as decomposition progressed, and physical writing degraded in all situations. Therefore, the tag best suited to DVI may be situational and depends on the needs of the practitioners and the environment. Secure attachment is vital. Based on the results of the experiments, recommendations for future use are made according to the different categories: the two tags that we found the most useful were the LF Yellow Ear Tag and the UHF White Tag. The LF Yellow Ear Tag survived all three experiments without ever being misplaced, even when falling into the soil, and despite the fact that it was too small to add physical writing, it may have been better than the Blue Key Chain Tag and Pet Tags, as it was picked up on more scans, and it could be read through the soil. Additionally, the LF scanner is recommended, which will read the Yellow Ear Tag as opposed to the second recommended tag.

The second tag we recommend is the UHF White Tag, which remained securely attached and in position throughout both rounds until its attachment site entirely decomposed. It contained a bar code and could hold a large amount of physical writing. It is very light and easy to transport, and provided a strong signal in the scans, as it was picked up more often than the other UHF tags. However, the writing became illegible if the tag was in direct contact with the tissue, and could not be read as frequently in a scan if within the tissue. The White Tag could also be read through the soil and, as with the Yellow Ear Tag, it may be useful in burials. Both tags were frequently picked up and were very easy to capture by their respective scanners.

Tags unsuitable or not recommended for DVI

This research demonstrates that not all RFID tags are suitable for DVI, and careful selection of tags is required. Tags with any potential for liquids to seep inside, or tags that are easy to break, are not recommended. The Card Tag should not be deployed in a DVI scenario since it was the only tag to break due to being breached by decompositional fluids. The Dot Tag required removal from the tissue and cage nearly every week, as it could not be read through tissue. Additionally, this tag broke when stepped on; meaning its field use is limited. Therefore, we do not recommend the Card Tag or the Dot Tag for DVI in any scenario.

The Blue Key Chain Tag could be used for DVI, but since the metal ring broke in the burial experiment, there is a risk of the tag being lost if that were to happen in the field. The surface also cannot be written on, and therefore, the Blue Key Chain Tag would not be recommended for use in the field. The Sticky Tag did not retain all the physical writing well enough throughout the experiments, nor did the sticky adhesive backing ever work. It was frequently read in the scans in both rounds; however, it performed very well underground in the scans through the soil, so could be viable if written information is not needed. The Pet Tags are not recommended unless in a scenario where the body will not be expected to decompose heavily; otherwise, they may be lost [31]. Finally, although some writing could be seen on the Yellow Label Tag, writing did not survive on many of the trial tags. Further, it was not possible to read the writing based on photographs. This is an important concern since photographs are important to forensic investigations. Though the Yellow Label Tag had to be removed to be read, it was often read in the scans. Therefore, if a method of physical writing on the tags could be read on camera and survive through burial and decomposition, this tag may be deployable in DVI; otherwise, it is not recommended.

RFID tag robusticity

Almost all the RFID tags survived all stages of decomposition from fresh to skeletonization. One study using RFID for tagging the dead after a disaster suggested that RFID tags should only be used to tag the dead when the remains are highly decomposed [9], when decompositional fluids are no longer present; however, these studies demonstrate that RFID can potentially withstand any stage of decomposition since all but one RFID tag survived every stage. Temperature and rainfall had an effect on the decomposition of the tissue, breaking down the tissue, and so impacting whether the tags remained attached. Therefore, the weather during the aftermath of a disaster should be a consideration when choosing the best tag and attachment method. The presence of wounds on the body can also speed up decomposition [32]. That means any incision made within the tissue to facilitate a tag's attachment could result in faster decomposition, which may not be ideal when the individual is not yet identified [32]. As well, incising a deceased person may not be ethically acceptable.

Many tags moved in the tissue during decomposition and even dropped out of the tissue. Insects also colonized the tissue, with maggot masses routinely noticed, as insect activity has been shown to disarrange clothing [33], the potential effects on RFID tags should also be considered. Therefore, the tags must remain securely attached to prevent movement during decomposition, which was shown to be difficult in these experiments. Maggot masses can also reach extreme temperatures of 35°C–50°C [19, 20, 21], yet in the experiments described in this paper, the tags survived within maggot masses. The decomposition and maggot mass activity produced a foam of metabolic fluids that appeared to degrade most or all writing on the White Tag. It may have affected the other tags, but only the White Tag in the second round was found within the foam of the metabolic fluids. The foam had to be wiped off the surface to assess the writing. This was not seen to the same extent on the other tags. However, pieces of tissue had to be removed from all the tags, often to assess the physical writing, which resulted in writing degradation. These experiments demonstrated that RFID can, in most cases, survive the harsh decompositional environment created by decomposition. It is important to note that these experiments were not conducted on human remains. Therefore, it is unknown whether the tags will survive these conditions within human remains. However, it is probable they will, as pig carcasses are commonly used as proxies for human remains in forensic taphonomy research [14, 34, 35, 36].

It is important to note that the cage protected the tissue and tags from large scavengers, but tags might have been lost if scavengers were able to access the body. Steel bear‐proof cage environments also do not accurately reflect the realities of various disaster scenarios, as bodies would not be protected from the environment as securely. Weather conditions affected which attachment method was best suited, as in the first round, the tissue dried out, so the White Tag looped through the skin remained securely attached. In the second round, the tissue was exposed to more rainfall (Figure 2), so several of the attachment sites developed a paste‐like texture, allowing the tags to break off. It is also important to note that in the first round, the pork shoulders had more skin covering the tissue than in the second. Zip ties proved to be durable, but due to their rigidity, it often meant the tags would break away from the fragile skin during the movement process. When assessing the front and back of the tags for writing, the zip ties also broke off from the skin in some instances. Attaching tags to the skin via zip ties is only recommended in dry environments, as that made the skin less fragile. As stated, the embroidery thread was too thin and became brittle and would not be suitable for the field. Though there were times the string broke or became untied due to the fluid presence, when tugged on, the thread in many instances could withstand force while remaining attached. The thread did allow for greater movement, suggesting something like fishing wire may be more useful due to its durability. A robust and bright colored attachment method is also recommended, however, as it is easier to spot the tags, especially when some are very small. Attaching tags to the skin via zip ties is only recommended in dry environments.

Long‐term storage applications

Long‐term storage of the dead resulting from disasters requires careful planning to preserve remains and enable later identification. Bodies should be placed in body bags or plastic wrap and, when possible, stored in refrigerated facilities [37]. In the absence of refrigeration, temporary burial—such as that used in Thailand in 2004—is recommended to slow decomposition [37]. If planning is inadequate, permanent loss of identity can occur during storage, as many victims of the 2004 Boxing Day Tsunami remain unidentified as of 2024 [38]. Though RFID tags were not tested over years underground in this study, they did all survive the burial environment for up to 21 weeks in the first preliminary experiment and 17 weeks in the third experiment, at several depths and throughout winter. This supports other studies which have shown RFID durability in soil environments [39, 40, 41]. This suggests potential for long‐term use in DVI contexts, though challenges remain in reading tags through soil and ensuring physical label durability over time. The effects of decomposition on tag performance in burial settings also remain unknown.

Ethical considerations

Maintaining the dignity of the dead is a fundamental priority in DVI [2]. Practitioners must treat remains with respect, even when their identities or the families of the deceased are unknown, and when possible, consider cultural and religious beliefs [2, 42]. This can be particularly difficult in international contexts where local cultural representatives are unavailable to guide respectful practices [43]. Determining whether an individual's culture would support technologies such as RFID implants prior to deployment presents a challenge [44]. While incising bodies for identification purposes, such as DNA sampling or autopsies, is standard and sometimes legally required in DVI [45], making incisions solely to anchor RFID tags may be perceived as intrusive by families or cultures that oppose bodily disturbance. In such cases, less invasive alternatives that can be externally attached to the limbs, such as around bones should be considered. Public openness to RFID implantation in humans has been explored, and some bereaved individuals have expressed willingness to support its use if it helps in the return of their loved ones.

Transporting RFID materials in a DVI environment

When access to communities is limited, or the disaster makes approach difficult, equipment may have to be transported by the DVI practitioners themselves [3], making the quantity, weight, volume, and transportability of equipment an important consideration [8]. The lightest weight and smallest tags tested in this study were the White Tag, Yellow Ear Tag, Blue Key Chain Tag, Pet Tags, and Dot Tag. It has been recommended that nations and organizations plan and prepare for disasters ahead of time [3] and these tags would be easy to stockpile as they are expected to last several years. If finances are a concern, some of these tags can be purchased in bulk for a discounted price online, such as from the RFID4U Store [3]. Both scanners were lightweight, but the LF scanner was smaller. The UHF scanners can be expensive at a few hundred dollars each. A laptop was also required when using the UHF scanner, whereas the LF scanner was less than fifty dollars CAD and displayed the tag's unique number on its own without a laptop. It also had an excellent battery life, not requiring recharging throughout the entire set of experiments, so it was viable over 16 months. The UHF scanner cannot be charged and draws its power from the laptop, which needs regular recharging, making it much less practical in the field.

DVI personnel and the adoption of the RFID system

DVI efforts can be led by many organizations depending on the context, such as the local government or the International Committee of the Red Cross [3]. Consequently, individuals with no DVI training can find themselves handling remains and tagging even when they may not be aware that everybody needs a tag [3, 46]. In this study, the primary researcher (MPM) had no DVI training and had never deployed any body tags on any decedent. However, they found this technology easy to grasp, so it is probable that it could be taught to DVI practitioners and other civilians quickly. This is important as it has been stated that since the background of those working on a disaster scene can be very diverse, creating a set of policies for them can be difficult [46]. The importance of creating a system that everyone involved can understand has been noted [8]. Though the adoption of RFID technology was met with apprehension by some participants in Lovell et al.'s study [8], another study reported that participants preferred the RFIDs for body tagging [7]. This suggests that DVI practitioners are open to the technology. Ensuring that the tags are robust enough to survive, quick to deploy, and easy to use may make this technology appealing to DVI practitioners [7, 8].

Physical writing on tags in a DVI environment

Recording information on the traditional waterproof paper tags in the field was preferred by those interviewed by Lovell et al. [8]. Our study demonstrated that it is possible, though not certain, for physical writing on the tags to also survive those conditions, as the permanent marker and pencil writing did survive on some of the tags in both rounds. However, the final state of the writing was not ideal. Tags that were not regularly handled showed that writing can also survive soil burial and remain in good condition. Regardless, since most of the tags survived, if anything were to happen to the information, barcode, or printed numbers on the tag, the RFID would still provide the number associated with the individual. Using the printed number, the scanner, and physical writing also allows for the technology to help verify what the human recorded on the tag in case one method fails [8].

Since every tag saw degradation of the physical writing, we would not recommend relying on physical writing on any tag that is in direct contact with decompositional fluid. The control tags did retain the writing more often, but in some cases, it was still difficult to see, so a method to protect their surfaces from the environment may be needed. Tags that can maintain physical writing that can be accurately photographed should also be selected, as demonstrated by the Yellow Label Tag in the burial experiments; not all surfaces will allow for this. In the first round, the physical writing was deployed in the rain, which made writing with the permanent marker difficult. This meant the permanent marker writing was not retained. The second round, however, was deployed under dry conditions and survived more often. We do not think that the fact that some pencils and permanent markers degraded or disappeared in these studies is fatal to their use in DVI. However, a method to deploy the writing and protect it should be created. Therefore, if possible, deployments of physical writings should occur under dry conditions. Additionally, in current DVI practices, written tags are fitted with a plastic covering to protect them [10]. If the tags in these studies were similarly protected, the physical writing may have survived better.

Scanners in a DVI environment

Although practitioners have mentioned that they did not want to carry additional supplies, a scanner would be needed to read the tags [8]. Disasters can, however, make dealing with power sources difficult. The LF scanner may be more useful since it has a very long‐lasting battery that lasted throughout all three experiments (16 months), whereas the UHF scanner required a laptop, which is an added piece of equipment that requires regular charging. In a scenario where there is no power in the disaster area, keeping the laptop charged could be difficult [3]. Additionally, some RFID tags' numbers can be rewritten to change the number stored inside. This has been tested in other work [10]. Both scanners would display letters, so it may be possible to customize the tags with words as well. However, the LF scanner has a smaller screen and may not be able to display long numbers if rewritten. In an earlier study, the more powerful scanner was entirely successful after issues were found with the portable and less expensive scanner [9]. The opposite was true in this research, and we would recommend an LF scanner to any DVI practitioner. It is smaller, faster, and easier to operate. The application of RFID tags that can be read with cell phones should be explored.

Limitations

Since the main purpose of the experiments was to determine whether the tags would survive the decomposition process, the tags needed to be inserted into the tissue to be in direct contact. As a result, many of the attachment methods used in the study may not be ethically permissible for use on the deceased in mass disaster contexts due to ethical concerns, as the fundamental DVI priority is to maintain the dignity of the deceased. This means in the field, the size, attachment method, and placement of the tags may need to be modified. The size of the tissue used in these experiments also limited the possibility of attaching the tags to the tissue. The vast number of tags available in the world also means not every tag could be studied; therefore, though many RFID tags are encased in similar materials, this study cannot speak for every RFID tag's performance. Though the goal of these experiments was not to replicate the circumstances of a disaster, naturally, there are elements that were not studied, such as shorter time frames, dangerous scenarios, and extreme weather conditions. The experiments were only conducted in one regional area, and all tissue experiments were conducted only in summer and fall. We did look at the survivability of the tags in winter, but this was not in connection with decomposing tissue. The tissue was also not wrapped in any sort of clothing, which may affect the rate of decomposition [47, 48]. The buried tags were also buried without any tissue to decompose with them, which could have influenced the tags and their physical writing. As well, for ethical reasons, most of the experiments were carried out on pieces of meat rather than complete bodies, which provided fewer and different attachment sites. Even in the first experiment in which complete pig carcasses were used, there are still differences between pigs and humans, although pigs have been used as human proxies in many taphonomic studies [34, 35, 36, 49].

Future research

The opportunities for further development of this research are vast. The largest limitation of the main experiment is the fact that these tags are read by radio waves, and the UHF tags were all competing to be read at the same time. If this experiment could be repeated by testing each tag alone in these environments, it may be possible to capture them more often. However, those conditions may not reflect the realities of the world, as several bodies could be buried within one grave, resulting in several tags of one type in one location [31]. The same could occur when several bodies are being housed in a freezer [9]. Therefore, future experiments should be done to deploy several of one tag type to see if they are all read. Additionally, we could not secure full carcasses for the main experiment, which limited the sites that could be used to attach the tags. Therefore, with full carcasses, more sites and less invasive locations, such as bones, should be explored in future research. The burial depth was also not at the recommended depth of 1.5–3 meters deep, and this should be tested [37, 50].

CONCLUSIONS

Tagging methods for the deceased in disaster contexts must be both robust and reliable [8]. Disasters are already devastating, and when victims remain unidentified, the suffering of families and communities is compounded. Honoring the dignity of those who have died requires that they be identified and, where possible, returned to their loved ones [2]. This, in turn, demands that all identifying information be collected accurately and underpinned by sound scientific principles [4, 51]. For practitioners to reach defensible conclusions about the deceased's identity, they must be able to maintain accurate and consistent records for each individual [8]. Consequently, tagging systems must be capable of withstanding the dynamic and often extreme conditions of disaster environments [8]. Reports of conventional tags becoming damaged and unusable under such conditions are therefore unacceptable [8]. The present study is the first to demonstrate that certain RFID tags can withstand the harsh conditions of decomposition, representing an important advance for disaster victim identification [37, 50].
