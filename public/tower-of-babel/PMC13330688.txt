Copper peptide activated cascade catalysis for glucose regulation and hypoxia reversing in infected diabetic wound healing
Authors: Zeng-Jin Huang, Rui-Feng Huang, Ping-Ping Jiao, Shuai Zheng, Meng Wang, Fan-Wen Teng, Tuo Chen, Zhi-Sen Zhou, Guan-Hai Wang, Gen-Long Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStarvation therapy has emerged as a promising strategy in diabetic wounds treating by regulating glucose level to deplete microbial nutrients without inducing antimicrobial resistance. However, this process consumes large amounts of oxygen, exacerbating wound hypoxia and compromising therapeutic efficacy. In this study, we designed a GOX-loaded hydrogel incorporated with copper peptide (GHK-Cu) to construct a copper peptide-activated cascade catalysis system for concurrent glucose regulation and hypoxia reversing. GOX initiates the cascade by catalyzing glucose oxidation, which reduces local hyperglycemia levels and generates hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Subsequently, copper ions in GHK-Cu activate the subsequent step by mediating the decomposition of H2O2 through a catalase(CAT)-like reaction, releasing local oxygen to effectively alleviate the hypoxic state of the wound, and its own biological activity can further promote skin repair. The research results show that the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel can efficiently facilitates the decomposition of glucose into oxygen via the cascade reaction. Moreover, this hydrogel has been confirmed to have multiple therapeutic effects, including antibacterial activity, tissue repair promotion, antioxidant capacity, and angiogenesis stimulation. In conclusion, the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel provides an effective approach for chronic diabetic wound therapy. Its multifunctional synergistic mechanism offers novel insights for addressing clinical challenges in refractory diabetic wound healing.Keywords: Chronic diabetic wounds, Starvation therapy, GHK-Cu, Catalase-like reaction

Introduction

Chronic diabetic wounds formed by persistent hyperglycemia accelerate bacterial proliferation, inhibit cell activity and angiogenesis, and exacerbate oxidative stress damage, collectively leading to stagnant wound repair [1,2]. In recent years, wound regulation strategies based on bioactive enzymes have attracted much attention, glucose oxidase (GOX), a typical biocatalyst, can specifically catalyze the oxidation of glucose to gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide [3]. In the high-glucose microenvironment of diabetic wounds, GOX can continuously consume excess local glucose, not only reducing high-glucose toxicity, but also achieving starvation therapy by depriving bacteria of carbon sources, which is independent of antibiotics and helps alleviate clinical drug resistance problems [4]. Nevertheless, the sole application of GOX has significant bottlenecks. The massive oxygen consumption during glucose oxidation further aggravates the inherent tissue hypoxia of wounds, which inhibits the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells and hinders neovascularization [5]. Meanwhile, excessive H2O2 causes oxidative damage to normal tissues, stimulates the overexpression of inflammatory factors, and exacerbates the wound inflammatory response.

As an essential trace element in organisms, copper is widely involved in key biochemical processes such as redox reactions and energy metabolism in the body, playing an indispensable role in maintaining normal growth and development, regulating iron absorption and utilization, participating in hematopoiesis, and sustaining skin homeostasis [6,7]. Copper-based functional materials, relying on the variable valence conversion characteristics of copper ions, exhibit unique enzyme-like catalytic and redox regulatory capabilities, which can effectively resist oxidative stress damage and reduce the risk of inflammation-related diseases [8,9]. Compared with traditional copper-based nanomaterials, GHK-Cu is a highly biocompatible complex formed by chelation of copper ions with tripeptide-1, whose core advantage lies in the synergistic catalytic effect and multiple biological functions between copper ions and the peptide backbone [10]. Its chelated copper ions possess unique CAT-like catalytic activity, which can efficiently catalyze the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide to produce oxygen in an acidic microenvironment, while precisely regulating local H2O2 concentration to avoid oxidative stress and tissue damage caused by its excessive accumulation, thus providing a new idea for constructing a stable and controllable catalytic therapy system. This catalase-like behavior of copper-peptide complexes is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that di-copper peptidyl systems can mimic catalase activity by efficiently decomposing H2O2 into O2 and H2O in both solution and cellular environments, highlighting the intrinsic biomimetic enzymatic nature of copper-peptide coordination systems [11,12]. The GHK tripeptide ligand not only enhances the biosafety and delivery efficiency of copper ions, but also activates downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK by binding to the cell membrane copper transporter CTR1, promotes fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis and keratinocyte migration, and upregulates the expression of growth factors such as VEGF and bFGF to accelerate angiogenesis [13,14]. Furthermore, it can disrupt bacterial membrane potential and interfere with DNA replication, with a bacteriostatic efficiency against common pathogenic bacteria in wounds such as Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and is not prone to inducing drug resistance [15].

Given these limitations, we propose a strategy that combines GOX with GHK-Cu to construct a copper peptide-activated cascade catalysis system. We hypothesize that integrating GOX with GHK-Cu will allow for the concurrent realization of glucose regulation and hypoxia reversing: GOX initiates the process by consume glucose, while GHK-Cu subsequently acts on the product H2O2. This cascade not only mitigates H2O2 toxicity through efficient decomposition but also replenishes oxygen to counteract the hypoxia induced by GOX. To achieve sustained and efficient codelivery of these active components, we designed Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX injectable hydrogel. In this system, GOX continuously consumes glucose and generates H2O2, while creating a local acidic environment. Under these conditions, the copper ions in GHK-Cu efficiently catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into oxygen, alleviating tissue hypoxia and reducing oxidative damage. Simultaneously, GHK-Cu exerts multifaceted biological functions including antibacterial, antioxidant, proangiogenic, and prorepair effects. This hydrogel system establishes a closed loop pathological microenvironment responsive therapeutic mechanism and provides a novel technical strategy for the precise treatment of diabetic chronic wounds and holds significant potential for clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) and sodium alginate were obtained from Maiklin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Glucose oxidase (GOX, EnzymoPure™, 200 U/mg) was supplied by Acmec Company, while GHK-Cu was provided by Qiangyao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China). The GOX activity analysis kit from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. and Beijing Punadi Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), respectively. The Calcein-AM/PI double staining kit was supplied by Baisha Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China). The experimental strain, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC 43300), was provided by the Guangdong Institute of Microbiology (Guangzhou, China). Deionized water was used for all experiments, and all other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel

Oxidized sodium alginate was prepared via chemical oxidation [16]. Briefly, 3 g of sodium alginate was dispersed in 50 mL of Distilled water under magnetic agitation for 30 min. Sodium periodate was then added at 1:1 M ratio relative to sodium alginate, followed by 10 min of stirring until complete dissolution. The mixture was kept in a dark environment and agitated nonstop at 300 rpm for 6 h. When this step finished, the liquid was moved into a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 3,000 Da, then dialyzed in deionized water for 72 h. The dialyzed solution obtained was then gathered and freeze-dried to prepare oxidized sodium alginate (OSA), which was preserved for later experiments.

A hydrogel formulation was formulated by combining 3% (w/v) OSA and 1% (w/v) CMCS at a 2:1 vol ratio. Following this, GHK-Cu stock solution (10 mg/mL initial concentration, adjusted to 100 μg/mL final concentration) and GOX stock solution (10 mg/mL initially, with a final concentration of 200 μg/mL) were incorporated. The resultant mixture was transferred to an electric stirrer and homogenized at 200 rpm, so as to achieve uniform dispersion of GHK-Cu and GOX throughout the Prepared hydrogel solution. Gelation was initiated via incubation of the system at 37°C for 30 min, forming the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel. Concurrently, control hydrogels loaded solely with GHK-Cu or only with GOX were prepared.

Material characterization

The Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel was lyophilized prior to microstructural analysis. Microstructural characterization with elemental mapping was performed using scanning electron microscopy SEM (JSM-IT300). The TA ARES G2 rheometer was used for rheological assessment. A frequency scan was conducted in a dynamic oscillation mode with a strain of 1% and a range from 0.1 to 100 radians per second. The dependence relationship of the viscoelastic moduli G' and G'' was determined. The 0.1 - 100% strain amplitude scan at a fixed strain/fixed temperature revealed the shear thinning behavior. Additionally, the water absorption capacity was measured to quantify the equilibrium expansion rate.

Glucose consumption and detection of hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid

The Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel was immersed in a simulated wound solution containing 10 mM of glucose. It was incubated in a 37°C constant temperature incubator. Small samples of supernatant were collected at regular intervals and the glucose levels were determined using a glucose detection kit. The total amount of H2O2 generated during the reaction was quantitatively analyzed using an H2O2 detection kit. Meanwhile, the pH changes of the solution at each time point were monitored in real time using a pH meter to evaluate the regulatory effect of glucose oxidation to gluconic acid on the acid-base environment of the system. Additionally, a free GOX group and a hydrogel group without GOX were set up as controls.

Dissolved oxygen measurement

To evaluate the hydrogen peroxide decomposition capability of the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel, 4 mL of 10 mM glucose solution containing 2 μM hydrogen peroxide was mixed with 4 mL of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel solution in a sealed volumetric flask. The dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration in each system was continuously monitored for up to 60 min using a portable dissolved oxygen meter.

Cytotoxicity analysis

HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells were plated in 96-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/well (100 μL medium) and cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 24 h. Test sample-supplemented medium was applied to experimental wells, and plain medium to controls. After 24 h, 10 μL MTT (0.5 mg/mL final) was added for 4 h of dark incubation. Medium was discarded, 150 μL DMSO was added, and plates were shaken for 10 min before measuring absorbance at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Intracellular ROS clearance assay

The ROS quenching activity of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX was evaluated via live cell imaging. HUVECs (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 6 well plates for 24 h, followed by 100 μM/L H2O2 induced oxidative stress 6 h and subsequent 24 h treatment with Gel@GOX, Gel@GHK-Cu, or Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX. After PBS washing, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min, and fluorescence signals were quantified using the imaging system (Ex = 488 nm, Em = 500-540 nm).

Determination of malondialdehyde (MDA) content

After 24 h of treatment on HUVECs (5.0 × 103 cells/well) subjected to 100 μM/L H2O2 induced oxidative stress with the hydrogel formulations: Gel@GOX, Gel@GHK-Cu or Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX, the MDA levels were quantitatively analyzed. After PBS washing, the cell lysates obtained through 5 min of ultrasonic treatment were reacted with 0.3 mL thiobarbituric acid reagent at 95°C for 30 min. The resulting colored supernatant was transferred to a microplate for absorbance measurement at 532 nm.

Determination of superoxide dismutase (SOD) content

To determine SOD content, HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates at 5.0 × 103 cells/well. After 6 h of oxidative stress induction with 100 μM H2O2, cells were treated with Gel@GOX, Gel@GHK-Cu, or Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX for 24 h. Cells were then harvested and lysed in 1 mL extraction buffer. Lysates were mixed with reagents per manufacturer's instructions, incubated at 37°C for 30 min to facilitate the SOD substrate reaction, and absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a Dynatech microplate reader.

Cell scratch assay

Confluent HUVECs monolayers (1 × 105 cells/well) underwent scratch-wound assays using sterile pipette tips. Following PBS washes, cells were treated with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel extract in serum-free medium, blank control included. Time-lapse imaging under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) captured wound closure at 0/12/24 h. Migration rates were quantified via ImageJ's scratch assay tools.

In vitro angiogenesis

The angiogenic potential of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel was evaluated through HUVEC tubulogenesis assays. Corning Matrigel 50 μL per well coated 96 well plates were polymerized for 1 h. HUVECs (3 × 104 cells/well) treated with the hydrogel were cultured under typical conditions for 6 h. Tubulogenesis was assessed using an inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with angiogenesis parameters quantified via ImageJ analysis tools. Tube formation was observed using an inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under identical magnification, exposure settings, field positions, and focal planes. Images from multiple parallel groups were captured, and quantitative parameters of tube formation were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Anti-inflammatory test

To evaluate the hydrogels anti-inflammatory ability, RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded into 6 well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) and cultured under standard conditions for 24 h. Inflammation was then induced by 12 h treatment with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Cells were divided into treated with different Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel formulations, followed by another 24 h of incubation. LPS-stimulated group was used as a positive control. Afterward, cell supernatants were collected and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C) to clear cellular debris. ELISA kits (used per manufacturer guidelines) were applied to quantify IL-6 and TNF-α levels in the supernatants, absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader. The results were used to assess the hydrogels in vitro anti-inflammatory activity.

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

Log-phase RAW264.7 cells and HUVECs were seeded on confocal dishes and co-cultured with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX for 24 h. After medium removal, cells were rinsed twice with cold PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), and washed three times. For intracellular staining (CD86, CD206, VEGF, CD31), cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (15 min) and washed. Primary antibodies (5% BSA dilution) were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies (1 h, RT, dark). After PBS rinsing, cells were mounted with DAPI-containing medium and imaged by confocal microscopy. Image J was used for quantitative analysis of fluorescence signals to assess anti-inflammatory and angiogenic profiles.

In vitro antibacterial assays

The antibacterial activity of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel against MRSA was evaluated using a multimodal strategy. Gel@GOX, Gel@GHK-Cu, and Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogels were placed on MRSA-inoculated agar plates (107 CFU/mL) and incubated for 24 h, after which inhibition zone diameters were measured to assess diffusion-based antimicrobial effects. Concurrently, time-dependent bactericidal activity was quantified via colony counting following 24 h co-culture with 2 mL of bacterial suspension (107 CFU/mL). Complementary bacterial viability analysis was performed using Calcein-AM/PI double staining: after 24 h hydrogel treatment, bacteria were washed with PBS, incubated with dual fluorescent dyes (5 μL Calcein-AM, 15 μL PI, 5 mL PBS), and visualized under a 3D confocal laser scanning microscope (3D-CLSM).

Bacterial biofilm elimination test

The antibiofilm efficacy of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel against MRSA biofilms (107 CFU/mL) was quantitatively and qualitatively assessed through crystal violet/Calcein-AM dual staining. Biofilms co-cultured with hydrogels in TSB medium (2 mL, 72 h, 37°C) underwent sequential staining protocols. Crystal violet binding (30 min) with ethanol extraction for OD540 quantification, and Calcein-AM fluorescent labeling (0.1%, 30 min, 37°C) followed by PBS washes and 3D-CLSM imaging. Triplicate PBS rinses preceded both colorimetric and fluorescence analyses to minimize residual interference.

In vivo wound healing studies

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the US National Research Council. Male ICR mice (8-10 weeks old, ∼40 g) were used to establish a diabetic model. After 4 weeks of high-sugar, high-fat diet feeding, diabetes was induced via intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 50 mg/kg). The model was considered successful when two consecutive fasting blood glucose measurements exceeded 13.9 mmol/L. A 9 mm full-thickness skin wound was created surgically on the depilated dorsal area, and 100 μL of 1 × 108 CFU/mL methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) suspension was inoculated to establish an infected wound model. Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence, with allocation concealment performed by an independent investigator not involved in animal handling or outcome assessment. After 24 h of infection, eligible mice were randomly assigned to five groups: 0.9% sodium chloride solution, Tegaderm, Gel@GOX, Gel@GHK-Cu, and Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX. In addition, a non-diabetic (ND) infected wound group treated with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX was included as a normoglycemic control. Corresponding hydrogel formulations were topically applied to wounds on days 1, 4, 7, and 11. Wound morphology was recorded at each time point, and wound healing rates, body weight changes, and granulation tissue formation were systematically evaluated throughout the study. All wound imaging and quantitative analyses were performed in a blinded manner by investigators unaware of group allocation.

After treatment completion, wound tissues were harvested for serial dilution and culture to quantitatively assess in vivo antibacterial efficacy. Following euthanasia, blood and major tissue specimens were collected. Serum concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α were determined using ELISA kits, while SOD activity and MDA levels in wound margin homogenates were analyzed to evaluate the hydrogel's antioxidant potential. Major organs and peri-wound tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then processed via paraffin embedding, sectioning, and H&E staining. Histopathological evaluation and immunofluorescence quantification (CD31, VEGF, TNF-α, and IL-6) were performed in a blinded manner by independent investigators. Simultaneous immunofluorescence labeling for CD31, VEGF, TNF-α, and IL-6 was performed to comprehensively assess tissue repair, blood vessel formation, and inflammatory status.

Statistical analysis

All experimental studies were conducted with at least three independent replicate experiments, and the final results were presented in the form of “mean ± standard deviation”. When evaluating the statistical significance of differences between groups, one-way analysis of variance and t-test were used. Among them, the criteria for determining statistical significance were: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***) and p < 0.0001 (****).

Results and discussion

Preparation of injectable hydrogels (Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX)

A Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX composite hydrogel was successfully synthesized for chronic diabetic wound therapy, with its preparation protocol detailed in Scheme 1 and Fig. 1A. The hydrogel matrix consists of CMCS and OSA, two well-characterized natural carbohydrate polysaccharides. CMCS retains a linear chitosan backbone with distinct β-1,4 glycosidic bonds, abundant amino (-NH2) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups, and a characteristic monosaccharide composition (rich in D-glucosamine). OSA, on the other hand, is oxidized by sodium periodate to selectively introduce active aldehyde (-CHO) groups at the C2/C3 positions of the glucuronic acid residues (G units) with α-1,4 glycosidic bonds. The molecular weight distribution and chain conformation of OSA have been thoroughly validated through structural characterization. In this system, the amino groups of CMCS undergo Schiff base reactions with the aldehyde groups of OSA, forming dynamic covalent bonds(Fig. 1B). These bonds were further stabilized by electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding involving carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on the polymer chains, resulting in a robust three-dimensional network structure. [17,18]. The porous architecture and surface functional groups (-COOH and -NH2) collectively facilitate the stable immobilization of GHK-Cu and GOX [19]. To optimize the catalytic efficiency of the cascade system, different mass ratios of GHK-Cu to GOX were systematically evaluated, and the mass ratio was determined to be 2:1 (GOX: GHK-Cu), which achieved the most balanced glucose consumption, H2O2 conversion, and O2 generation performance (Fig. S1B). GOX-catalyzed glucose oxidation generates gluconic acid, which may induce local acidification and potentially compromise enzymatic activity. In the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel, the CMCS matrix contains abundant amino groups that can be protonated under acidic conditions, providing partial buffering capacity. Experimentally, under physiological conditions (37°C, pH 7.4), GOX maintained sustained catalytic activity over the evaluation period, while GHK-Cu exhibited gradual and controlled release (Fig. S1C–D), indicating that the acidic products did not significantly impair enzyme function. These results collectively demonstrate the long-term operational stability of the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX cascade system. In addition, this formulation exhibits excellent injectability and can produce self-supporting filaments when extruded (Fig. 1C). It is worth noting that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel exhibits strong adhesion to various substrates, including skin, polymers, wood, glass, and metals (Fig. 1D), highlighting its excellent fit and wide applicability. Overall, the research results indicate that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX has excellent physicochemical properties, biological functions, and translational application value in wound healing applications.

SEM characterization showed that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX had a highly porous and interconnected microstructure. This structure facilitated efficient drug loading and controlled release, promoted gas exchange and oxygen diffusion, and relieved hypoxia in diabetic wound microenvironments (Fig. 1E). The hydrogel also exhibited excellent wound exudate absorption capacity. Importantly, it reached swelling equilibrium within 24 h in physiological saline, with a swelling ratio of 337.42 ± 22.45% (Fig. 1I). This suitable swelling allowed rapid exudate uptake without structural degradation from over-swelling. Matching its porous structure, the hydrogel conformed well to irregular wound surfaces and maintained close interfacial contact. It regulated local humidity, reduced exudate accumulation and infection risk, and provided a favorable microenvironment for the sustained release of functional components [20]. Elemental analysis via energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) revealed the characteristic signals of copper (4.1%), carbon (21.8%), hydrogen (27.3%), oxygen (16.2%), and nitrogen (12.6%) in the synthesized Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel (Fig. 1E). These results offered straightforward evidence that the functional components were successfully immobilized within the polymeric hydrogel matrix.

To assess the viscoelastic properties of the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel, dynamic mechanical analysis was performed within the linear viscoelastic (LVE) region. As shown in Fig. 1F, the storage modulus G′ was consistently higher than the loss modulus G″ across the tested frequency range, indicating a dominant elastic response. At 1.0 rad/s, G' remained much higher than G″, and the hydrogel displayed stable quasi-solid rheological behavior under physiological conditions without obvious flow. Such mechanical stability guarantees the hydrogel's structural integrity and firm adhesion to the wound bed. Strain sweep tests showed a critical strain threshold of 10%. Above this threshold, G′ dropped sharply while G″ increased, and the two moduli intersected at 251% strain (Fig. 1G), indicating hydrogel structural collapse and sol-gel phase transition. Frequency dependent viscosity measurements confirmed obvious shear-thinning behavior (Fig. 1H), with viscosity gradually decreasing as shear rate increased, a typical feature of non-Newtonian fluids.Together, these rheological properties provide the hydrogel with excellent injectability and shape adaptability, supporting minimally invasive administration and tight fitting to complex wound shapes.

Under the catalysis of loaded GOX in the presence of oxygen, glucose is oxidized to produce gluconic acid and H2O2 [21,22]. The glucose consumption efficiency of the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX system was similar to free GOX (Fig. 1J), confirming the hydrogel effectively uses wound glucose for catalysis and supports subsequent catalytic cycles. Meanwhile, H2O2 was generated during glucose consumption (Fig. 1K). pH monitoring (Fig. S1A) showed glucose oxidation produced gluconic acid, lowering the hydrogel pH from 7.32 to 6.10 within 60 min. This acidic environment disrupts partial internal cross-links (e.g., amide bonds and hydrogen bonds), thereby regulating hydrogel degradation, enabling controlled GHK-Cu release, and optimizing the wound microenvironment. To further elucidate the catalytic behavior of GHK-Cu and quantify its oxygen generation efficiency, dissolved oxygen concentration was continuously monitored in a sealed system. As shown in Fig. 1L, the dissolved oxygen concentration in the Gel@GOX group gradually decreased due to continuous oxygen consumption during glucose oxidation, whereas the Gel@GHK-Cu group exhibited a marked increase in dissolved oxygen levels, confirming its oxygen generating capability. Notably, the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group maintained a relatively high dissolved oxygen level throughout the monitoring period, with an oxygen-generation efficiency of 54.22%, which was significantly higher than that of the Gel@GHK-Cu group (32.13%) and the Gel@GOX group (−71.84%). These results indicate that GHK-Cu can effectively utilize GOX-generated H2O2 to produce O2, thereby alleviating oxygen depletion and sustaining the cascade catalytic process. To further investigate the catalytic mechanism of GHK-Cu, Gel@FeSO4/GOX was employed as a positive control for a typical Fenton-like reaction, and hydroxyl radical (·OH) generation was evaluated using the DPBF assay. As shown in Fig. 1M, the Gel@FeSO4/GOX group produced the strongest ·OH signal, whereas a moderate level of ·OH generation was also detected in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group, although its intensity was significantly lower than that of the Gel@FeSO4/GOX group. Combined with the dissolved oxygen results, these findings suggest that GHK-Cu possesses both catalase-like and Fenton-like activities. Among them, the catalase-like pathway plays a dominant role by efficiently catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2 into O2 and H2O, thereby providing a sustained oxygen supply and alleviating local hypoxia. Meanwhile, the limited amount of ·OH generated through the Fenton-like pathway may contribute to the antibacterial effect. Therefore, the therapeutic benefits of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX are primarily attributed to its efficient oxygen-generation capability and regulation of the oxygen microenvironment, accompanied by a moderate ROS-mediated synergistic effect.

In vitro biocompatibility and antioxidant capacity of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX

Biocompatibility of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX was assessed using live/dead cell staining and CCK-8 assays with HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells. Quantitative CCK-8 analysis (Fig. 2A and B) revealed minimal cytotoxicity across all experimental groups toward both cell types, with survival rates exceeding 80% even at the highest tested concentration. As shown in Fig. 2C, no obvious red fluorescence was detected in the fields of view of hydrogels with different components, only uniformly distributed green fluorescence was observed, indicated the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX has relatively low toxicity. These quantitative and qualitative results further validate the excellent biocompatibility of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX.

Chronic oxidative stress in the microenvironment of diabetic wounds mainly results from mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of NADPH oxidase, and abnormal glucose metabolism, which leads to excessive generation of ROS, thereby causing oxidative stress-mediated damage to pancreatic β cells and insulin resistance [[23], [24], [25]]. Initially, the antioxidant capacity of the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel was evaluated via DPPH radical scavenging assays and total oxygen radical scavenging capacity (TOSC) assessments. As shown in Fig. S2, when the hydrogel concentration increased from 0 mg/mL to 100 mg/mL, the DPPH scavenging rate elevated from 0% to 84.61 ± 5.48%. Its TOSC also exhibited a consistent trend, reaching 7.83 mM at 100 mg/mL. Owing to the copper ions in GHK-Cu acting as the catalytic center, electron transfer occurs, which enables the reduction of DPPH free radicals to stable non-free radical forms [26]. The research has fully demonstrated that the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel exhibits excellent in vitro free radical scavenging performance. Following the establishment of an H2O2 induced oxidative stress model, the ROS level in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX treatment group decreased by more than 87.06%, approaching that of the normal control group(Fig. 2D and E). Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 2F, the relevant antioxidant effects were further verified through the MDA experiment. Additionally, GHK-Cu can regulate its redox activity by chelating copper ions, reduce the generation of superoxide anions, and indirectly inhibit the production of hydroxyl radicals in the Haber-Weiss reaction [27]. Combined with these effects, Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX possesses excellent antioxidant activity and intracellular reactive oxygen species scavenging ability, which helps it play a protective role in resisting oxidative damage in chronic diabetic wounds.

Furthermore, the multi-enzyme mimetic capabilities of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX were systematically evaluated. SOD is a crucial intracellular antioxidant enzyme, catalyzes the dismutation of O2− into O2 and H2O2. As shown in Fig. 2G, SOD activity was significantly increased following treatment with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX, indicating that the hydrogel not only enhances O2− scavenging but also possesses SOD mimetic functionality. Catalase (CAT) is well known to catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into water and O2, thereby preventing H2O2 accumulation and protecting biological systems from peroxide induced oxidative damage. As illustrated in Fig. 2H, treatment with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX resulted in a CAT activity rate of 79.24%, demonstrating its effective catalase-like activity. Enhanced CAT activity has been reported to alleviate oxidative stress and promote diabetic wound healing [28]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the synthesized Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX possesses multifunctional enzyme-mimicking properties and is capable of efficiently scavenging diverse ROS.

Anti-inflammatory ability of hydrogels in vitro

In diabetic patients, long-term hyperglycemia creates a high-glucose microenvironment that not only induces persistent inflammation and disrupts tissue repair, thereby establishing a prolonged inflammation delayed healing cycle, but also promotes the accumulation of pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages, which contributes to impaired wound healing [29,30]. Precise regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment and effective intervention in high-glucose wounds are therefore critical for therapeutic success. To address this challenge, we evaluated the regulatory effect of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX on macrophage polarization, a key determinant of the inflammatory response (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B, macrophage polarization was first assessed by immunofluorescence staining under LPS stimulation. In the LPS treated group, red fluorescence for M1 macrophage marker CD86 was significantly increased, indicating elevated M1 macrophages and enhanced pro-inflammatory activity. After Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX treatment, the red signal nearly vanished, with fluorescence intensity dropping from (55.78 ± 9.43) AU to (2.93 ± 1.73) AU. Meanwhile, green fluorescence for M2 marker CD206 rose sharply from (1.24 ± 0.37) AU to (61.05 ± 5.72) AU, indicating a higher M2 ratio (Fig. S3A and B). Flow cytometry further verified that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX decreased M1 macrophages (F4/80+ CD86+) from 27.33% to 2.43%, increased M2 macrophages (F4/80+ CD206+) from 1.66% to 23.37%, and reduced the M1/M2 ratio (Fig. 4C–E). These results confirm that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX effectively promotes macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.

Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway serves as a master regulator of macrophage polarization dynamics. Pathological hyperactivation of this pathway accelerates M1 differentiation and inflammatory mediator production, whereas its suppression enables M2 polarization to facilitate tissue repair [31,32]. ELISA profiling demonstrated Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel dual regulatory capacity. In LPS challenged RAW264.7 macrophages, treatment significantly attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations TNF-α decreased from 61.65 ± 11.68 pg/mL to 17.82 ± 6.35 pg/mL (Fig. 3J), IL-1β from 25.79 ± 2.68 pg/mL to 10.86 ± 1.12 pg/mL (Fig. 3K), and iNOS from 40.96 ± 6.31 pg/mL to 19.99 ± 2.43 pg/mL (Fig. 3L). Concurrently, anti-inflammatory factors were upregulated, with IL-10 secretion increasing from 14.87 ± 1.61 pg/mL to 40.65 ± 7.20 pg/mL (Fig. 3M) and TGF-β from 10.05 ± 0.73 pg/mL to 31.37 ± 1.90 pg/mL (Fig. 3N). These results indicate that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX effectively suppresses inflammatory responses and promotes macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. To further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate key proteins involved in the NF-κB signaling pathway and M2-associated signaling. The results showed that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX significantly inhibited p65 nuclear translocation and reduced IκBα phosphorylation/degradation, indicating effective suppression of NF-κB signaling. Meanwhile, STAT6 phosphorylation was markedly upregulated, suggesting activation of M2-associated signaling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX regulates macrophage polarization through coordinated modulation of the NF-κB/STAT6 signaling axis. From an upstream mechanistic perspective, this immunomodulatory effect may be attributed to GHK-Cu mediated intracellular copper signaling regulation and GOX-induced metabolic reprogramming via glucose depletion. The synergistic action of these two processes reshapes inflammatory signaling networks, thereby promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype.

In vitro antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities of hydrogels

Chronic wound infections in diabetic patients are a clinical challenge, with the core issue being that the hyperglycemic environment not only weakens the immune function and hinders blood supply but also significantly reduces the wound healing capacity [33]. Such wounds are particularly prone to infections by drug-resistant bacteria like MRSA, and bacteria tend to form biofilms, further exacerbating the infection. The antibacterial ability was conducted through the disk diffusion test, dilution plating method, and live/dead staining experiments. As shown in Fig. 4A and D, Gel@GOX alone had no obvious direct bactericidal effect, and Gel@GHK-Cu only produced an inhibition zone of (3.91 ± 0.53) mm (with limited antibacterial effect), while the experimental group Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX significantly expanded the inhibition zone to (6.52 ± 0.40) mm (p < 0.01), visually demonstrating the excellent antibacterial performance under the synergistic effect of GHK-Cu and GOX. Notably, the dilution plating method further quantified the antibacterial effect, with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX achieving an in vitro antibacterial rate of 93.26% against MRSA (Fig. 4B). As shown in Fig. 4C and E, the control group showed dense green fluorescence (live bacteria) in the live/dead staining pictures, while the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group had almost negligible green fluorescence, with a significant increase in red fluorescence (dead bacteria), reducing the number of live bacteria by 94.83% (p < 0.001), confirming its highly efficient bactericidal effect. To elucidate the antibacterial mechanism, CAT was introduced into the system to investigate the contribution of ROS to bacterial killing. The results showed that the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX (CAT) group exhibited antibacterial activity comparable to that of the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group, with no significant difference observed, indicating that ROS-mediated oxidative killing was not the primary antibacterial pathway. Furthermore, intracellular ROS levels in bacteria were evaluated using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Fig. S6A–B). Although Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX treatment induced a certain degree of ROS accumulation, the fluorescence intensity was lower than that observed in the Gel@GOX group, suggesting that GHK-Cu could partially consume GOX-generated H2O2 through its catalase-like activity, thereby reducing excessive ROS accumulation. Therefore, the superior antibacterial efficacy of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX is mainly attributed to the disruption of bacterial membrane integrity by GHK-Cu and the glucose-depletion effect mediated by GOX [34,35].

To gain further insight into the antibacterial mechanism, SEM was employed to observe the morphological changes of MRSA after coincubation with hydrogels of different components. As shown in Fig. 4F, MRSA in the Gel@GOX group maintained intact cell membranes and regular morphology, indicating that GOX alone was insufficient to induce substantial membrane damage [4]. Only a small number of bacteria in the Gel@GHK-Cu group exhibited slight membrane damage. The underlying mechanism is that GHK-Cu carries a positive charge and can bind electrostatically to the bacterial cell membrane, disrupting the structural integrity and permeability of the membrane, leading to the leakage of intracellular substances and osmotic imbalance, thereby weakening bacterial viability [36]. Notably, bacteria in the synergistic Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group displayed severe structural damage, such as obvious membrane rupture and massive leakage of intracellular contents. To further quantify the degree of bacterial injury, a nucleic acid analyzer and a BCA kit were used to measure the leakage levels of extracellular nucleic acids and proteins, respectively (Fig. 4K–M). The results showed that the leakage amounts of DNA, RNA and protein in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group were 127.54 ng/μL, 149.56 ng/μL and 0.46 mg/mL, respectively, all of which were significantly higher than those in the single-component groups, indicating a much stronger destructive effect on bacterial membrane integrity. These results demonstrate a pronounced synergistic antibacterial effect between GHK-Cu and GOX. Specifically, GHK-Cu compromises bacterial membrane integrity, while GOX suppresses bacterial growth through glucose depletion. The combination of membrane disruption and metabolic inhibition ultimately leads to more severe cellular damage and intracellular component leakage, thereby significantly enhancing the antibacterial activity of the hydrogel against MRSA [37].

Biofilms, which hinder the penetration of antibacterial substances and enhance bacterial resistance through EPS matrix, are a key pathological factor for the non-healing of diabetic wounds [38,39]. The study evaluated the elimination of biofilms through crystal violet staining and 3D-CLSM. As shown in Fig. 4G and I, the quantitative analysis by crystal violet staining indicated that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX inhibited MRSA biofilm formation by 85.02%, far superior to GHK-Cu alone (53.92%) and GOX alone (34.68%). Notably, Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX could decompose the EPS matrix structure and disperse bacteria, further verifying its ability to disrupt biofilms (Fig. 4H and J). The biofilm-dispersing effect of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX is closely associated with GHK-Cu penetrating the EPS barrier and the glucose-depletion effect mediated by GOX. Specifically, GHK-Cu compromises bacterial cell membrane integrity, while GOX continuously consumes glucose and suppresses bacterial metabolic activity. The synergistic action of membrane disruption and metabolic inhibition facilitates biofilm destruction and bacterial dispersion [40]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX exerts a synergistic antibacterial effect through multiple mechanisms, including bacterial membrane disruption, glucose depletion, and biofilm eradication. Such a strategy effectively interrupts the vicious cycle of infection, biofilm formation, and delayed healing in diabetic wounds, providing a promising therapeutic approach for chronic wound infections.

Diabetic wounds healing in vivo

To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy under physiological conditions, we established an in vivo model using MRSA infected diabetic mice. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, wound treatments were administered over a 14 day period. The control group (without hydrogel) exhibited markedly delayed wound healing, attributable to the combined inhibitory effects of hyperglycemic microenvironment and bacterial infection, achieving only 58.55 ± 2.82% wound closure by day 14 (Fig. 5B–C).The Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX(ND) group exhibited a faster healing process than the diabetic control group, further demonstrating the impaired wound repair associated with the diabetic microenvironment. Although Tegaderm treatment moderately improved wound closure compared with the untreated diabetic group, its therapeutic efficacy remained inferior to that of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX. Notably, the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group achieved nearly complete wound closure by day 14 (92.58 ± 2.89%), approaching the healing performance observed in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX(ND) group and significantly outperforming the Tegaderm-treated group. Severe intrinsic hypoxia exists in diabetic wound tissues. Tissue oxygen partial pressure (pO2) was quantitatively detected by oxygen microelectrodes (Fig. 5E). The baseline pO2 of untreated diabetic wounds was merely 12.57 mmHg. After Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel treatment, the tissue pO2 reached 26.89 mmHg, an increase of 113.92%. It is verified that the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX hydrogel is capable of effectively reversing tissue hypoxia and substantially ameliorating the hypoxic microenvironment of diabetic wounds. No significant body weight differences were observed among groups (Fig. 5D), confirming the hydrogel's good biocompatibility and systemic biosafety. As shown in Fig. S4C, CRP analysis of vital organs showed no significant differences between the experimental and control groups. The study demonstrated that wound MRSA infection did not spread. Furthermore, H&E staining observations of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidneys) and skin tissue on days 14 and 28 (Fig. S4A–B) revealed intact organ structures with no apparent pathological damage. Additionally, no degradation residues of the hydrogel were observed in the skin tissue, further confirming that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX did not cause systemic organ damage and exhibited excellent biocompatibility.

To explore the anti-MRSA mechanism, wound exudate sampled on Day 3, 7 and 14 was quantitatively analyzed using gradient dilution and colony culture assays (Fig. 5F–G). The Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group maintained strong inhibition against MRSA during the whole treatment. Its antibacterial rate was as high as 94.51 ± 1.02% on Day 14, reflecting durable antibacterial performance. Compared with Tegaderm, which mainly acts as a passive physical barrier, Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX exhibited substantially stronger and more sustained antibacterial activity against MRSA throughout the treatment period. The antibacterial activities of hydrogels without GHK-Cu or GOX decreased significantly to 36.82 ± 4.02% and 57.10 ± 3.60%. It is verified that the combination of GHK-Cu and GOX works synergistically to modulate local glucose levels, disrupt bacterial cell membranes, alleviate tissue hypoxia, and improve antibacterial efficacy, which facilitates accelerated wound closure and improved early tissue remodeling. Moreover, the superior therapeutic outcomes observed in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group compared with the commercial Tegaderm dressing further highlight the advantages of its multifunctional therapeutic mechanism, including glucose consumption, oxygen generation, antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory regulation, and pro-angiogenic effects.

Following treatment, wound tissues were subjected to histopathological evaluation via H&E staining (Fig. 6A). Langerhans cells, as critical antigen-presenting cells, exhibit abnormal proliferation during the inflammatory phase [41]. In the control group, numerous spindle-shaped Langerhans cells (black arrows) were observed in the epidermal layer of the wound bed, while the dermis was densely infiltrated with round inflammatory cells predominantly composed of neutrophils (red arrows). Additionally, the epidermis at the wound margins displayed discontinuity and delayed regeneration, indicating the severe infection and impaired healing. In contrast, both single-component groups Gel@GHK-Cu and Gel@GOX exhibited markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. Notably, the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group showed excellent efficacy in removing inflammatory cells. This effect stems from the synergism between GHK-Cu and GOX, which inhibits NF-κB inflammatory pathway activation and blocks inflammation progression [42]. This synergistic system greatly improved infected wound histology and accelerated healing. Overall, this combined strategy provides a new therapeutic approach and research paradigm for clinical management of infected wounds.

TNF-α and IL-6 are major pro-inflammatory cytokines in acute wound inflammation. Immunofluorescence revealed diffuse red fluorescence in the control group, mainly in dermal inflammatory infiltrates. Quantitative analysis showed TNF-α at (50.34 ± 6.09) AU and IL-6 at (30.04 ± 5.03) AU (Fig. 6B–E), indicating strong local inflammation. The Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX (ND) group exhibited the lowest TNF-α and IL-6 expression levels, reflecting the relatively mild inflammatory response under normoglycemic conditions. Tegaderm treatment partially reduced local inflammatory cytokine expression compared with the untreated diabetic control group; however, the reduction was less pronounced than that achieved by Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX. In the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX group, sparse fluorescence was observed only at wound edges, with TNF-α and IL-6 levels sharply reduced to (22.31 ± 6.67) AU and (7.56 ± 2.63) AU, approaching the levels observed in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX (ND) group, similar to normal skin. These results confirm that the hydrogel effectively inhibits pro-inflammatory mediators and alleviates inflammatory injury to wound regenerative cells. ELISA was further used to quantify serum inflammatory cytokines for systemic immunomodulatory evaluation(Fig. 6F–I). In the control group, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β concentrations were elevated at (175.06 ± 11.78) pg/mL, (44.94 ± 3.21) pg/mL, and (46.39 ± 2.57) pg/mL, respectively, whereas the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 remained low at (34.77 ± 5.26) pg/mL, indicating a pronounced pro-inflammatory dominance and anti-inflammatory deficiency imbalance. Consistent with the local inflammatory analysis, Tegaderm treatment moderately improved the systemic inflammatory profile but failed to restore cytokine levels toward those observed in the normoglycemic group. Following Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX treatment, serum levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were substantially reduced to (43.74 ± 15.92) pg/mL, (23.72 ± 3.08) pg/mL, and (16.85 ± 1.70) pg/mL, respectively values comparable to those in healthy mice with no statistically significant differences. Concurrently, IL-10 increased from (34.77 ± 5.26) pg/mL to (79.15 ± 4.71) pg/mL, representing a 304% elevation. The research results indicate that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX exerts a potent anti-inflammatory effect by regulating macrophage polarization. The specific mechanism involves promoting the transformation of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [10,43]. This phenotypic switch is conducive to resolving inflammation and facilitating tissue regeneration. In addition, GHK-Cu and GOX in the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX composite can effectively further contributes to controlling the inflammatory response and thereby accelerating wound healing.

The hyperglycemic environment of diabetic wounds is prone to excessive accumulation of ROS, leading to oxidative damage. MDA is a product of lipid oxidation caused by ROS, and SOD is a key enzyme for removing ROS [[44], [45], [46]]. In the wound tissue of the control group, the MDA content was as high as (51.91 ± 6.71) nmol/mg prot (Fig. 6K), and the SOD activity was only (34.49 ± 3.38) U/mg prot (Fig. 6J), indicating severe oxidative damage. After treatment with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX, the MDA content significantly decreased to (15.27 ± 8.05) nmol/mg prot, and the SOD activity increased to (109.82 ± 6.97) U/mg prot, approaching the level of normal skin. Furthermore, the activity of SOD was highly consistent with the nodes of wound inflammation regression during the treatment period, confirming that this hydrogel can enhance antioxidant capacity and reduce oxidative damage. Among them, GHK-Cu can activate the expression and activity of SOD, thereby effectively scavenging superoxide anion free radicals [10]. Meanwhile, GOX-mediated glucose consumption alleviates hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, thereby reducing ROS generation at the wound site. The synergistic effects of GHK-Cu and GOX restore redox homeostasis in diabetic wounds, protecting resident cells from oxidative injury and maintaining a microenvironment for tissue regeneration. These research findings demonstrate that Gel@GHK-Cu can significantly inhibit the production of oxidative components and enhance antioxidant effects. Consequently, it improves the in vitro antioxidant capacity and prevents secondary damage induced by ROS.

Hydrogel-mediated cell proliferation and angiogenesis effects

Diabetic patients frequently experience reduced lower extremity blood flow and compromised wound oxygen energy supply due to atherosclerosis, microvascular dysfunction, and hyperglycemia mediated endothelial damage pathophysiological hallmarks underlying chronic wound healing impairment [47,48]. GOX is capable of ameliorating the hyperglycemic microenvironment of diabetic wounds, and this regulatory effect might serve as a crucial determinant in promoting wound healing [49]. To validate this conjecture, scratch wound assay and EdU incorporation assay were further employed to examine the migration and proliferation capacities of human umbilical vein endothelial cells. As shown in Fig. 7A&S5A, quantitative EdU DNA binding analysis revealed significantly greater EdU positivity in HUVECs treated with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX compared to controls. After 24 h incubation, fluorescence intensity measurements showed a 2.8-fold elevation in the treatment group (68.29 ± 4.84 AU vs 25.03 ± 3.03 AU, p < 0.001), confirming the hydrogel ability to stimulate endothelial cell cycle progression and establish proliferative reservoirs for angiogenesis. Subsequent scratch assay analysis (Fig. 7B and E) demonstrated superior migratory capacity, with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX achieving 77.35 ± 4.69% wound closure within 24 h markedly higher than single component formulations (Gel@GHK-Cu: 66.56 ± 7.14%, Gel@GOX: 59.67 ± 3.87%, p < 0.001), accompanied by denser migration patterns, evidence of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX synergy in facilitating endothelial motility for vascular repair. Furthermore, Transwell assay results further demonstrated that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX possessed a significant ability to promote cell migration(Fig. 7C). Crucially, Matrigel based tubulogenesis assessment (Fig. 7D) showed the composite hydrogel induced mature vascular mimetics, with quantitative metrics including nodal density (735 ± 149 per field), junction counts (233 ± 16), and total branching length (35,254 ± 8,276 μm) demonstrating 2.3-fold, 2.7-fold, and 2.5-fold enhancements respectively over controls (p < 0.001). These findings collectively validate Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX multifunctional capacity to promote endothelial differentiation and generate functional microvascular networks closely mimicking physiological angiogenesis. It is reported that GOX can reduce the local glucose concentration at the wound site, alleviate the inhibitory effect of a high glucose environment on cells, and decrease the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and their inhibitory effect on cell functions [50]. Notably, the research results have confirmed that GOX regulates the local microenvironment at the wound site(such as AGEs), promoting cell migration and proliferation, thereby creating a more favorable microenvironment for these cellular processes.

Furthermore, cell proliferation and tube formation assays demonstrated that the Gel@GHK-Cu group exhibited a significantly superior effect compared to the Gel@GOX medium group. We postulate that GHK-Cu incorporated within the hydrogel exerts a pivotal function in facilitating angiogenesis and tissue reconstruction [26]. To validate this postulation, the expression of vascular endothelial-specific markers CD31 (an intercellular adhesion molecule that specifically labels vascular endothelial cells) and vascular endothelial growth factor VEGF (a pivotal regulator of angiogenesis) was systematically evaluated [51,52]. The pro-angiogenic efficacy of the hydrogel was morphologically assessed via dual immunofluorescence staining for CD31 and VEGF. As shown in Fig. 8A & S5B-C, Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX treated HUVECs exhibited markedly enhanced fluorescence signals for both CD31 (green) and VEGF (red), with significantly improved signal intensity and spatial uniformity, indicating that the hydrogel upregulates the expression of key angiogenic markers at the cellular level and provides molecular support for endothelial cell functional activation. Notably, following 14 days of in vivo treatment with Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX in diabetic mice, immunofluorescence analysis of wound tissue (Fig. 8B & S5D-E) revealed weak and disorganized CD31 and VEGF signals in the control group, whereas the treatment group demonstrated robust fluorescence intensities CD31 reached 161.21 ± 15.14 AU (5.93-fold increase vs. control) and VEGF reached 142.34 ± 11.69 AU (6.67-fold increase vs. control) with positive signals aligned along the trajectory of newly formed blood vessels. These research results indicate that this hydrogel can effectively increase the levels of vascular promoting markers. Previous studies have demonstrated that enhanced production of growth factors is a critical driver of angiogenesis and accelerated wound repair [53]. Consistent with this concept, the marked upregulation of VEGF and CD31 observed in the present study was accompanied by rapid wound closure in vivo, suggesting that Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX promotes tissue regeneration through enhancement of angiogenic signaling. Meanwhile, GHK-Cu still exhibits excellent angiogenic promoting capabilities at the molecular level.

Given that GHK-Cu has the property of promoting the high expression of characteristic molecules related to angiogenesis. To clarify its molecular mechanism, we used an ELISA system to quantitatively analyze the relevant markers in the wound tissue homogenates of diabetic mice and evaluate its regulatory effect on key signaling pathways during the wound proliferation phase. As shown in Fig. 8C, after intervention with the hydrogel, the expression level of EGFR2 protein in the wound tissue was significantly upregulated to 2.52 times that of the control group. Notably, the increase in EGFR2 can regulate cell fate by activating the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway [54,55]. Further detection showed that the hydrogel could significantly upregulate the expression of phosphorylated AKT (p-Akt) protein (Fig. 8D), suggesting that it promotes diabetic wound healing by activating the EGFR/Akt pathway. In addition, Akt can regulate the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A, Fig. 8F) and hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α (HIF-1α, Fig. 8E) by phosphorylating the serine/threonine protein kinase (mTOR) [56]. Among them, HIF-1α as a key upstream transcription factor of VEGF-A, can directly activate its transcription by binding to the hypoxia response element (HRE) in the VEGF-A gene promoter region under hypoxic conditions. Meanwhile, VEGF-A can feedback-stabilize HIF-1α through downstream signaling pathways to form a positive feedback regulatory loop. Treatment with the hydrogel significantly upregulated the expression of both, further confirming its pro-angiogenic effect. Based on the above findings, we propose that the Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX regulates the EGFR/Akt pathway (Fig. 8G), and the integration of GHK-Cu endows it with excellent pro-angiogenic ability, enhancing its therapeutic efficacy in diabetic wound healing.

Conclusion

In summary, this study verified the therapeutic effect of Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX composite hydrogel on chronic diabetic wound healing through in vitro and in vivo experiments. The in vitro experiments of the hydrogel demonstrated that it could effectively consume glucose and generate controllable H2O2 and acidic by-products, while GHK-Cu catalyzed the decomposition of H2O2 into oxygen, endowing it with strong antibacterial properties and promoting cell proliferation and migration. Meanwhile, the hydrogel significantly accelerated wound closure in diabetic mice, reduced inflammation, promoted granulation tissue remodeling and angiogenesis, and decreased oxidative stress and bacterial load. Additionally, Gel@GHK-Cu/GOX could activate the EGFR/Akt pathway thereby up-regulating the expression of VEGF, and inhibit NF-κB signaling to reduce TNF-α levels and increase IL-10 levels. As a promising advanced wound dressing, this hydrogel provides an innovative strategy for the comprehensive management of chronic diabetic wounds.
