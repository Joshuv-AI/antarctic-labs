Identification and functional characterization of a novel mutation in the NEUROD1 gene in a Chinese family with maturity-onset diabetes of the young
Authors: Jianhua Li, Juan Zhang, Shuxin Ren, Yanxia Liu, Cong Wu, Jie Dong, Chaofeng Zhu, Sufang Chen, Huijuan Zhang, Tianyi Li, Yanyan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsFew families with maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) caused by neurogenic differentiation factor 1 (NEUROD1) mutations have been identified. The aim of this study is to identify the affected gene in a Chinese family with MODY using whole exome sequencing (WES) and explore the potential pathogenicity of the identified mutation.MethodsWES was performed in patients with clinically suspected MODY, and candidate variants were verified with Sanger sequencing for family co-segregation. Structure-function alterations in the newly identified mutant protein were analyzed using three-dimensional modeling and dual luciferase reporter gene assays to assess pathogenicity.ResultsA novel heterozygous missense mutation, Y297D (c.889T > G, p.Tyr297Asp), in the NEUROD1 gene (NM_002500.5) was identified in a MODY proband and his affected relatives. Y297D mutation disrupted a C-H conjugated interaction and introduced a new hydrogen bond, altering the protein’s local structure. These alterations reduced transcriptional activity in the transactivation domain of the Y297D mutant compared with that of the wild-type protein (P < .05).ConclusionsA novel NEUROD1 mutation has been identified in Chinese MODY, and the abnormal conformation of NEUROD1-Y297D reduced insulin transcription activity, impairing pancreatic β-cell function in patients with MODY6. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RAs) may be a precision hypoglycemic strategy to consider in diabetic patients with NEUROD1 mutations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00592-026-02731-3.

Introduction

Neurogenic differentiation factor 1 (NEUROD1), known as BETA2, encodes a basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor expressed exclusively in neuronal, intestinal, and pancreatic endocrine cells. The human NEUROD1 gene, mapped to chromosome 2q32, contains two exons. Exon 2 encodes the entire NEUROD1 protein, including the bHLH domain (100 amino acids (aa)–156aa), and a carboxy-terminal transcriptional activation structural domain (AD) comprising subdomains AD1 (189aa-299aa) and AD2 (300aa-355aa) [1]. NEUROD1 attaches to the E-box on the insulin gene (INS) promoter and heterodimerizes with the widely distributed HLH protein E47 to stimulate INS expression [2]. Mice with a specific NEUROD1 disruption exhibited a substantial reduction in pancreatic β cells, failed to form mature islets, developed severe diabetes, and died perinatally, highlighting the critical role of NEUROD1 in normal islet development [3]. Recent studies have provided new insights into NEUROD1, in which NEUROD1 inactivation in human embryonic stem cells (HESCs) disrupts differentiation from pancreatic progenitor cells to insulin-expressing cells (HESC-β) [4]. Subsequent research has revealed that NEUROD1 reprograms the epigenetic landscapes and transcription factor to drive endocrine cell differentiation and the production of β cells that produce insulin [5] and is essential for proper transactivation and functional α cell development, in addition to its vital role in the maturation and differentiation of islet β cells [6].

Heterozygous mutations in NEUROD1 in humans result in a milder phenotype of maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), whereas homozygous mutations cause permanent neonatal diabetes, often with neurological issues [7, 8]. Owing to the incomplete penetrance of the MODY6 subtype caused by NEUROD1 mutations and the focus of genetic sequencing on common MODY-related genes (hepatocyte nuclear factor [HNF]4 A, glucokinase, and HNF1A), MODY6 may be underdiagnosed [2, 9–12]. With next-generation sequencing, more families with MODY have been identified with heterozygous mutations in NEUROD1, primarily in its transactivation or bHLH domains [13–18]. Furthermore, apart from monogenic inherited diabetes, the NEUROD1 Ala45Thr polymorphism has been substantially linked to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), particularly in Asian populations [19].

To avoid misdiagnosis of low-penetrance cases, we incorporated patients clinically suspected with MODY using relaxed family history criteria, i.e., a family history of diabetes in at least two generations. In the present study, we aimed to identify a novel heterozygous missense mutation in the NEUROD1 gene from a family with MODY. Furthermore, we performed family co-segregation analysis, mutant three-dimensional modeling, and transcriptional activity assays to support evidence of the pathogenicity of this mutation.

Materials and methods

Participants

Unrelated individuals with clinically suspected MODY were recruited or referred to The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University between January 2022 and December 2023 for genetic sequencing and clinical evaluation. Inclusion criteria included onset age of diabetes ≤ 25 years, a family history spanning at least two generations, and negative results for islet autoantibodies, including anti-GAD and insulinoma-associated antigen 2 (IA2) antibodies. Individuals with insulin resistance syndrome, secondary diabetes, or type 1 diabetes were excluded from this study. For probands with suspected pathogenic mutations, family members were further recruited for genotype-phenotype co-segregation studies. In addition, we selected 200 unrelated non-diabetic individuals as controls based on the following criteria: age 60 years or older, normal glucose tolerance, no family history of diabetes, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level < 5.6%. The research was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, and all subjects signed informed consent forms.

Whole exome sequencing (WES)

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes of participants using commercially available kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to standard protocols. Targeted exon enrichment was conducted with the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V8 kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and library preparation was completed using the SureSelect XT Reagent kit (Agilent Technologies). Exon-enriched DNA libraries were sequenced on the Hiseq4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end sequencing. We aligned the sequencing reads to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37) with Burrows-Wheeler Aligner, and variants were called using the genome analysis toolkit and ExomeDepth software. ANNOtate VARiation detection software was employed for variant annotation, and pathogenicity was assessed using the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria [20].

Sanger sequencing

Sanger sequencing was performed to confirm family co-segregation and WES results. Primer sequences were designed online using the Primer 5.0 program. Target fragments were amplified by polymerase chain reaction, and the ABI3730XL automated sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) performed bidirectional sequencing on the purified products.

Construction of the three-dimensional model

We used a de novo modeling approach to construct the three-dimensional structure of the NEUROD1 protein with I-TASSER software (http://zhanglab.ccmb.med.umich.edu/I-TASSER) [21]. Energy optimization was applied to improve the protein model and provide a more logical protein structure. The optimized protein model was assessed using the PROCHECK tool (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/PROCHECK/). Newly identified mutations were incorporated to assess their impact on the protein structure. PyMOL software was used to map the overall and local three-dimensional structures of wild-type (WT) and mutant NEUROD1 proteins.

Molecular docking of NeuroD1 and p300

Molecular docking of WT and mutant NEUROD1 with the local region of p300 was conducted using HDOCK. The three-dimensional structure of p300 was obtained from the Alphafold Protein Database, and a de novo modeling approach was used to construct the three-dimensional structure of the NEUROD1 protein with I-TASSER software. Protein interaction interfaces were analyzed using the Discovery Studio interface module, and interacting amino acid residues between the two proteins were mapped with PyMOL.

Cell culture and maintenance

The murine pancreatic beta-cell line, Beta-TC-6, (originally purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) were preserved and maintained in our laboratory. Cells were routinely cultured and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco]), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100ug/mL streptomycin. Cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37℃ with 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and cells were subcultured upon reaching 80–90% confluency using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA.

Plasmid construction and mutagenesis

A luciferase reporter vector was constructed by amplifying the human INS promoter region ([insert 1572 bp) and cloning it into the transcriptional region of the pGL3-basic plasmid (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The human wild-type NEUROD1 cDNA was inserted into the pcDNA3.1(+) mammalian expression vector to generate pcDNA3.1-hNeuroD1-WT. The Y297D mutation was subsequently introduced into the human NEUROD1 cDNA template using the QuikChange Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The successful generation of both the wild-type construct and the Y297D mutation was confirmed by Sanger DNA sequencing.

Transfection and dual-luciferase reporter assay

For transient transfection assays, Beta-TC-6 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10^5 cells/well. After 24 h of incubation, when the cells reached approximately 70–80% confluency, co-transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

In each well, cells were co-transfected with a total of 4010 ng of plasmid DNA, consisting of 2000 ng of the pGL3-INS-promoter reporter vector, 2000 ng of the respective expression vector (empty pcDNA3.1(+), pcDNA3.1-hNeuroD1-WT, or pcDNA3.1-hNeuroD1-Y297D), and 10 ng of the pRL-TK vector (encoding Renilla luciferase under the HSV-thymidine kinase promoter; Promega) to serve as an internal control for transfection efficiency normalization.

The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 6 h post-transfection. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection, washed with cold PBS, and lysed using Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega). Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of Firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity. Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate independent wells, and the entire assay was independently repeated at least three times. To ensure equal protein expression, the expression levels of the transfected WT-NeuroD1 and the Y297D mutant were verified in parallel by Western blotting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data in the figures are presented as mean ± standard deviation. To evaluate mean differences, the Dunnett T3 multiple comparisons test and Brown-Forsythe analysis of variance test were used. statistically significant differences are denoted as P < .05.

Results

Identification of the Y297D mutation in the NEUROD1 gene through whole exome and targeted sequencing

We identified a heterozygous missense mutation, Y297D (c.889T > G, p.Tyr297Asp), in the NEUROD1 gene (NM_002500.5) in a MODY proband using WES (Fig. 1). This mutation has not been reported in the 1000 Genomes Project, Exome Aggregation Consortium, Genome Aggregation Database, or Human Gene Mutation Database. Y297D mutation affected the highly conserved amino acid in the transactivation domain, and this substitution might not be tolerated (Fig. 2). Multiple bioinformatics software predicted this mutation was deleterious, and the Y297D mutation was absent in 200 non-diabetic controls, supporting its potential pathogenicity.

Genotype-phenotype characteristics of the family with the NEUROD1-Y297D mutation

Genotype-phenotype analysis demonstrated that NEUROD1-Y297D mutation co-segregated with the diabetes phenotype in this family. The mutation was present in the proband and the proband’s brother, mother, and aunt, who were diagnosed with diabetes at ages 18, 32, 38, and 35, respectively (Fig. 1A). The clinical characteristics of the mutation carriers are summarized in Table 1. The proband (III-1) was diagnosed with diabetes following a physical examination that revealed elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels without classic hyperglycemic symptoms. Given that the proband was younger than 25 years of age at diagnosis and negative for islet autoantibodies (GAD antibodies, insulin autoantibodies, and islet cell antibodies), exome sequencing was performed, leading to identification of the NEUROD1-Y297D mutation. His advanced glycation end-products, which are used to evaluate vascular complications, were within normal limits, and he did not currently experience any diabetes-related issues. Owing to his preserved insulin reserve (homeostasis model assessment of β-cell function, 38.0) and signs of severe insulin resistance (homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, 3.36; body mass index [BMI], 31.1 kg/m2), the proband was prescribed a combination of pioglitazone/metformin tablets with liraglutide. A follow-up 3 months later showed satisfactory glycemic control (FPG level, 5.40 mmol/l; HbA1c level, 6.80%). The proband’s brother, with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, was newly diagnosed with diabetes at 32 years of age and was also identified as a carrier of the Y297D mutation. He had diabetic nephropathy and was on a diet to achieve stable glycemic control at the time of the examination. The brother inherited the Y297D mutation from the mother, who was diagnosed with diabetes at 38 years of age, whereas their father, who had normal glucose tolerance, did not carry the mutation. The proband’s mother was receiving pioglitazone/metformin therapy at the time of the examination, which was switched to dapagliflozin combined with metformin due to poor glycemic control (HbA1c level, 7.8%) at the 3-month follow-up, and glycemic control has been acceptable (HbA1c level, 6.5%) over the past year. The proband’s maternal aunt showed improved HbA1c levels of 6.9% after using sitagliptin/metformin for 3 months. In addition to the proband’s mother having mild depression, the other three mutation carriers presented no neurological abnormalities. All affected mutation carriers in this family were negative for GAD and IA2 antibodies.

Construction of a three-dimensional model of the Y297D mutation

The 3D structure of the human NEUROD1 protein was constructed to investigate the molecular defect, and the Y297D mutation was mapped using I-TASSER software. The Y297 mutation was located in the transactivation domain at the C-terminal, buried deeply within the inner core (Fig. 3A). The Y297D mutation substituted the uncharged polar residue tyrosine (Tyr) with the negatively charged polar residue aspartic acid (Asp).

The carbonyl in the main chain of WT Y297 formed hydrogen bonds with Thr300 and Met301, while the phenol group of the side chain formed conjugated interaction with Met301 (Fig. 3B). In the mutation, the carboxyl group in the side chain of D297 formed a new hydrogen bond with Thr300, and the conjugated interaction between the phenol group and Met301 was lost (Fig. 3C).

Molecular docking of NEUROD1 and p300

To further analyze the effect of the Y297D mutation on protein–protein interactions, we performed molecular docking of WT and mutant NEUROD1 with specific regions of p300 using HDOCK (Fig. 4A and D). Tyr297 of the WT-NEUROD1 protein interacted with Ser2095 of p300 through hydrogen bonding, and Gly310, His314, Ala306, and Tyr303 of NEUROD1 engaged in hydrophobic interactions with Leu2081 and Ile2085 of p300, (Fig. 4B and C). However, the Y297D mutation disrupted the hydrogen bonding interaction at residue 297 with P300 and Lys126, Val127, and His217 of NEUROD1 formed new hydrogen bonds with Arg2088 and Ala2076 of p300, thus induced new binding interface (Fig. 4E and F).

Attenuation of transactivation by the NEUROD1-Y297D mutant

Compared with plasmid vector pcDNA3.1 containing human NEUROD1 WT (pcDNA3.1-hNeuroD1-WT) expression levels, NEUROD1 expression levels in transfected cells were comparable (Fig. 5A); however, the relative luciferase activity stimulated by pcDNA3.1-hNeuroD1-Y297D was reduced by 32.6% (P < .05) (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that the Y297D mutation resulted in the attenuation of transactivation activity.

Discussion

WES analysis of the proband in a two-generation MODY pedigree identified a novel heterozygous missense mutation, Y297D, in the NEUROD1 gene (Fig. 1). Additionally, this is the third NEUROD1/MODY6 mutation we have found in Chinese families, following reports of S159P and E59Q mutations [22, 23]. The MODY6 subtype is rare despite advancements in next-generation sequencing technologies facilitating the identification of NEUROD1 mutations. To our knowledge, approximately 46 cases of NEUROD1 mutations have been documented in families with MODY, including 33 missense mutations, 3 nonsense mutations, 1 splicing mutation, 1 regulatory mutations, and 8 frameshift mutation, and most of the mutations are present in the bHLH domain or the transactivation domain (Table 2) [2, 13–18, 24, 25]. Our results from family co-segregation analysis, genotyping of non-diabetic controls, and structural-function analysis of the Y297D mutation supported a causal relationship between the Y297D mutation and the development of diabetes in this family.

Summary of the reported NEUROD1/MODY6 mutations

The NEUROD1/MODY6 exhibited considerable clinical variability and differed from classic MODY criteria. For instance, 20% of families experienced incomplete penetrance, and affected mutation carriers had a higher BMI than their counterparts [2]. Incomplete penetrance, typical in patients with MODY6, may be associated with a lower BMI or younger age [8, 26]. The higher BMI of the proband in this Y297D family may have contributed to the earlier onset of diabetes despite his younger age (Table 1). However, we cannot rule out the possibility of incomplete penetrance in this family since we could not obtain samples from the proband’s maternal grandparents for three-generation co-segregation analysis. Moreover, some individuals with NEUROD1 mutations are diagnosed with diabetes later in life; for example, a patient with the NEUROD1-H206PfsTer38 mutation developed diabetes at 76 years of age in Japan [15]. Furthermore, as observed in the proband, obesity may be more prevalent among individuals with MODY6 [8, 14, 26, 27]. The TODAY clinical trial reported that 4.5% of youth with diabetes in the overweight/obese cohort had genetic variants causing monogenic diabetes [28]. A recent study indicated that obesity-induced microribonucleic acid (miR)-802 overexpression disrupted insulin transcription and secretion, with NeuroD1 identified as a target of miR-802-dependent silencing [29]. Therefore, NEUROD1 inactivation mutations and down-regulated NEUROD1 expression due to obesity-induced miR-802 overexpression may synergistically lead to impaired insulin secretion and the diabetic phenotype observed in the proband. Given the incomplete penetrance and obesity feature in some MODY6 cases, the “relaxed” criteria for MODY, i.e., a family history of diabetes in at least two generations, regardless of obesity, could facilitate the identification of more patients with MODY6.

The severity of diabetic microangiopathy in patients with NEUROD1/MODY ranged from mild to severe, including renal failure [9]. The proband with the NEUROD1-H241Q mutation died at 44 years of renal failure [26]. In a Japanese study, two affected mothers carrying NEUROD1 mutations also had renal failure from diabetic nephropathy and intellectual disability, both dying in their 50s. The proband’s 32-year-old brother also had diabetic nephropathy; however, no neurological abnormalities were observed in the three affected patients, except for the proband’s mother, who had depression [15]. We further investigated the transmission pattern of NEUROD1 mutations. Statistically, 85.7% of individuals with MODY6 with a known parent of origin inherited NEUROD1 heterozygous mutations from their mother [2]. The Y297D mutation in the proband was also inherited from his mother, which partly explained his earlier diabetic diagnosis since the intrauterine environment of his affected mother may have contributed to the proband’s earlier onset of diabetes [15].

The treatment of NEUROD1/MODY varied among patients, with oral hypoglycemic agents (OHA) being the most commonly used, followed by insulin. Patients with MODY6 carrying frameshift mutations often require insulin therapy [16]. The proband in our study was treated with a combination of pioglitazone/metformin and liraglutide due to a high BMI, as liraglutide supports glycemic control and weight loss [30]. The proband presented improved HbA1c levels (6.8%) and a decreased BMI from 31.3 to 27.0 kg/m2 after 1 year. The three affected relatives with NEUROD1 mutations were treated with OHA and/or dietary therapy to achieve acceptable glycemic control (Supplementary Table 1). The 5 patients carrying the same mutation of NEUROD1-P197H in a Chinese family also displayed considerable differences in therapy, and insulin, OHA, and diet were prescribed for 2, 1, and 2 patients, respectively, and similar situations also existed among Caucasian families [17, 24]. Additionally, exendin-4, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA), promotes NEUROD1 expression in insulin-producing cells derived from mouse embryonic stem cells [31]. Moreover, exendin-4 can ameliorate cognitive impairment of T2DM through Wnt/β-catenin/NEUROD1 signaling [32]. Therefore, GLP-1RAs may be precise hypoglycemic strategies for patients with NEUROD1 mutations, especially those with cognitive impairment.

The novel Y297D mutation identified here was located in the carboxy-terminal transactivation domain (156-355aa) of the NEUROD1 protein, a region associated with the co-activator p300 [33]. p300 functionally interacted with the sequences spanning the transactivation domain of NEUROD1 (156-355aa) to activate INS transcription, and mutations removing the C-terminal glutamine-rich region also diminished the capacity of p300 to activate INS E-box-directed transcription [33]. The Y297D mutation substituted the uncharged Tyr residue with the negatively charged residue Asp, and the introduction of a positive charge altered the local conformation of the transactivation domain (Fig. 3), which further disrupted the interaction between NEUROD1 and p300 (Fig. 4), thereby attenuating the transactivation of the NEUROD1-Y297D mutant.

The attenuation of transactivation caused by the Y297D mutant was confirmed via in vitro experiments (Fig. 5). A previous study reported that NEUROD1 retained 37% of its activity when AD2 was absent, whereas only 10% of NEUROD1 activity was observed in mutants lacking AD1 and AD2 [1]. The frameshift mutation 206 + C in AD1 caused a severe decrease in transcription activity, especially in the presence of p300 [8]. The reduction of transcription activity from the Y297D missense mutation, also located in the AD1, was not as significant as that from the 206 + C mutation; however, it was more severe than that caused by the S159P mutation in the non-functional region [22]. However, assessing the transcriptional activity of the Y297D mutant in the presence of p300 will help simulate its functional defect under physiological conditions. Moreover, Chandra et al. reported that mutations in the transactivation domain of the transcription factor HNF4A can disrupt long-distance DNA binding by affecting domain-domain interactions [34]. The NEUROD1-Y297D mutation also influences its DNA affinity through a similar mechanism, further attenuating the transcription activity of INS; however, this requires confirmation through further electrophoretic mobility shift assays.

In summary, we identified a novel missense mutation, Y297D, in the NEUROD1 gene from a Chinese family with MODY. This mutation altered the molecular structure of the carboxy-terminal transactivation domain, resulting in an abnormal conformation of NEUROD1 that attenuated insulin transcription activity. Consequently, this led to defective pancreatic β-cell function in patients with MODY6 in this family. Our study findings support use of the “relaxed” MODY criteria and WES to identify more families with NEUROD1/MODY, and they suggest that GLP-1RA administration may be a precise hypoglycemic strategy to consider in patients with diabetes with NEUROD1 mutations.
