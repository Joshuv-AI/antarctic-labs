Scaling Laws from Sequential Feature Recovery: A Solvable Hierarchical Model
Arie Wortsman Zurich, Hugo Tabanelli, Yatin Dandi, Florent Krzakala, Bruno Loureiro

Abstract
We propose a simple mechanism by which scaling laws emerge from feature learning in multi-layer networks. We study a high-dimensional hierarchical target that is a globally high-degree function, but that can be represented by a combination of latent compositional features whose weights decrease as a power law. We show that a layer-wise spectral algorithm adapted to this compositional structure achieves improved scaling relative to shallow, non-adaptive methods, and recovers the latent directions sequentially: strong features become detectable at small sample sizes, while weaker features require more data. We prove sharp feature-wise recovery thresholds and show that aggregating these transitions yields an explicit power-law decay of the prediction error. Technically, the analysis relies on random matrix methods and a resolvent-based perturbation argument, which gives matching upper and lower bounds for individual eigenvector recovery beyond what standard gap-based perturbation bounds provide. Numerical experiments confirm the predicted sequential recovery, finite-size smoothing of the thresholds, and separation from non-hierarchical kernel baselines. Together, these results show how smooth scaling laws can emerge from a cascade of sharp feature-learning transitions.

Introduction

Despite the empirical success of neural networks, we still lack a predictive theory answering a deceptively simple question: given a structured learning problem, which features are learned first, and how does their sequential discovery translate into statistical efficiency? This question lies at the intersection of three active lines of research. First, neural scaling laws suggest that the performance of large models follows power laws in data, compute, or model size

(

This paper asks whether scaling laws can arise not from a fixed spectral bias, but from the progressive uncovering of the relevant features in the data, as can happen in deep neural networks. We investigate a mathematically tractable high-dimensional task that requires recovering hidden features across multiple layers. These latent features are combined through weights with a power-law profile. Statistically detecting an individual feature requires a sample size proportional to the inverse squared feature strength: strong features are learned first, weaker features later, and the prediction error is governed by the tail of the hidden spectrum that has not yet been recovered. Solving the task efficiently requires untangling the compositional structure. This combination of hierarchy and anisotropy leads to different scaling laws for predictors that are adapted, or not adapted, to the task geometry.

We also compare the hierarchical spectral method with non-hierarchical kernel baselines. These comparisons illustrate the role of compositional structure: while the target is a high-degree function of the input, exploiting its hierarchy allows the learner to recover the relevant latent representation at a lower sample scale.

Our
main contributions
are as follows:

•

We introduce a high-dimensional task combining hierarchical and compositional structure, providing a tractable setting for studying scaling laws in a setting where both depth and feature learning are required for efficient learning.

•

We prove sharp sample-complexity thresholds for the recovery of individual latent directions by a spectral algorithm in the high-dimensional limit. Our result is based on an eigenvector perturbation analysis of the resolvent that goes beyond the standard Davis–Kahan bounds for this problem, a technique we believe can be of independent interest.

•

We show that the emergence of scaling laws in this setting can be understood from the aggregated spectral transitions, with the error controlled by the unlearned spectral tail.

•

We provide experiments confirming the predicted recovery transitions, finite-size effects, and separation from shallow kernel methods.

Overall, our results show that power-law learning curves can arise from a simple and interpretable mechanism: hierarchical learners recover latent features one at a time, and a power-law spectrum of feature strengths converts these sharp spectral transitions into smooth scaling laws.

2.1
The spectral algorithm

We now show that exploiting the compositional structure in

To formalize the recovery of individual hidden directions by the spectral estimator, we use the following notion of weak recovery.

Definition 2.2

(Weak Recovery)
.

We say that a sequence of estimators
(u_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
u_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
weakly recovers
a sequence of unit vectors
(v_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
v_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, if there exists a constant
c>0
, independent of
N
, such that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\,\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\langle u_{N},v_{N}\rangle\right|\geq c\right)=1,

i.e.
|\langle u_{N},v_{N}\rangle|\geq c
with high probability as
N\to\infty
.

Given the recovered eigenvectors
\widehat{A}^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{A}^{(1)}_{d_{1}}
, we define the learned first-layer representation by

\widehat{h}^{(1)}_{\mu,i}=\langle\widehat{A}^{(1)}_{i},F_{\mu}\rangle,\qquad(\mu,i)\in[n]\times[d_{1}].

(2.11)

Thus the first spectral step produces a low-dimensional representation
\widehat{h}^{(1)}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
of the input.

2.2
Second-layer and readout estimation

Once the first-layer representation has been estimated, the rest of the pipeline operates in the low-dimensional latent space
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
.

\widehat{A}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\mu}\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{2}\left(\widehat{h}^{(1)}_{\mu}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}.

(2.12)

In contrast with the first layer, this step is performed in dimension
d_{1}\ll D
and is not responsible for the high-dimensional spectral transition analyzed below. In the diagonal teacher considered here, the population version of
\widehat{A}^{(2)}
is aligned with the second-layer matrix
A^{(2)}
defined in
Equation

2.4
. As in the hierarchical spectral pipeline of
(
58
)
, the statistical bottleneck is the recovery of the first-layer directions in the degree-
q
Hermite feature space. Once these directions have been recovered, estimating the second layer only involves the learned representation in dimension
d_{1}
, and therefore does not generate the feature-wise high-dimensional transitions studied in
Section

3
. We then define the learned second latent feature by

\widehat{h}^{(2)}_{\mu}=\left\langle\widehat{A}^{(2)},\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{2}\left(\widehat{h}^{(1)}_{\mu}\right)\right\rangle.

(2.13)

It remains to fit the one-dimensional readout
g
from the learned scalar features
\widehat{h}^{(2)}_{\mu}
. In the experiments, we use ridge regression on a fixed feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\widehat{a}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\left(y_{\mu}-\langle a,\phi(\widehat{h}^{(2)}_{\mu})\rangle\right)^{2}+\rho\|a\|^{2}.

(2.14)

The resulting predictor is denoted by
\widehat{f}
. The full estimation error will be measured by the test or generalization mean-squared error.

Definition 2.3

(Mean-squared error)
.

Let
\widehat{f}
be the predictor returned by the algorithm trained on the dataset
\{x_{\mu},y_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in[n]}
. We define its mean-squared error, or equivalently its squared-loss generalization error, as

\mathrm{MSE}(n)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\left(\widehat{f}(x)-f_{\star}(x)\right)^{2}\right],

where the expectation is over an independent test point
x
. Equivalently, if
y=f_{\star}(x)
and
\widehat{y}=\widehat{f}(x)
, then
\mathrm{MSE}(n)=\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{y}-y)^{2}]
.

By standard results on kernel ridge regression
(
16
)
, the error introduced by the ridge regression step in Equation
2.14
is
\simeq\frac{1}{n}
which we show to be subdominant compared to the error in the estimation of
\widehat{h}^{(2)}
.
Hence, the error is governed by which first-layer directions have been recovered. Directions with larger
|\lambda_{i}|
contribute more strongly to the label, so failing to recover them has a larger effect on the MSE. This leads to the unrecovered-tail estimate below.

The preceding signal-to-noise heuristic gives the scaling picture that the main results make precise. Write
a_{i}=|\lambda_{i}|
for the strength of the
i
-th latent feature, so that
a_{i}\propto i^{-\gamma}
. The
i
-th direction becomes recoverable when its spike separates from the empirical noise, which as we have discussed occurs at the sample scale
n_{i}\asymp d^{q}/a_{i}^{2}
,
and remains hidden below this threshold. If
m(n)
denotes the number of directions recovered at sample size
n
(see
Fig.

1
for an illustration), the prediction error is then controlled by the unrecovered tail,
\mathrm{MSE}(n)\simeq\sum_{i>m(n)}a_{i}^{2}
. In particular, for summable spectra
2\gamma>1
,

\mathrm{MSE}(n)\asymp(\nicefrac{{n}}{{d^{q}}})^{-1+1/(2\gamma)}.

(2.15)

Thus, the model predicts smooth power-law generalization as the aggregate effect of many sharp spectral recovery transitions. The next section proves this prediction.

.

Our layer-wise spectral estimator is closely aligned with the learning strategy of

Further Related work

Hierarchical and compositional models.

Depth is often argued to be effective because it allows to exploit hierarchical or compositional structure in the data. This intuition has motivated depth-separation results and compositional target models from both approximation-theoretic and statistical viewpoints
(

;
44
;
53
;

Multi-index and spectral methods.

A related line studies multi-index models, where the target depends on a low-dimensional projection of the input. These have been used to characterize statistical-computational gaps, weak-recovery thresholds, and the limitations of kernel methods

3.1
Recovery of the First Layer

Our first set of results concerns the recovery of the different directions

Theorem 3.1

(Weak Recovery)
.

Consider the setting described in
Section

2
, and assume that one of the two regimes in
3.1
holds. Assume moreover that

under the technical small-

1.

Sufficient Sample Complexity:
The
k

\hat{C}
, denoted by

satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\dfrac{u_{k}}{\|u_{k}\|_{2}},\dfrac{A^{(1)}_{k}}{\|A^{(1)}_{k}\|_{2}}\right\rangle\right|=1-O_{d}\left(\frac{d^{q}\,k^{2\gamma}}{nZ_{\gamma}^{2}}\right).

(3.1)

In particular, if
n=\omega_{d}\left(d^{q}k^{2\gamma}Z_{\gamma}^{-2}\right)
, with high probability the direction
A^{(1)}_{k}
is recovered by

. This gives a
1/n
rate for the decay of the overlap error.

2.

Necessary Sample Complexity:
Let
\delta>0
be independent of
d
. If
n=\Theta\left(d^{q}k^{2\gamma}Z_{\gamma}^{-2}d^{-\delta}\right)
, then, with probability at least
1-o_{d}(1)
,

is not recovered by

.

The complete proof of
Theorem

3.1
is discussed in

, but we highlight below the main idea.

A natural first attempt to prove this result would invoke the classical Davis–Kahan bound
(
24
)
. Unfortunately, this does not provide the tight sample complexity due to the lack of a sufficiently large spectral gap in our power-law setting. The main technical challenge is therefore to have a finer control the eigenvectors of
\hat{C}
. In order to avoid using Davis–Kahan, we use the Neumann expansion of the resolvent (see, e.g
(

33
)
) to prove:

\hat{u}_{k}=u_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{\top}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}),

(3.2)

where
P_{\mathrm{Ker}}
denotes the projection into
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])
, and

.

Equation

∎

5
Discussion and Future Directions

We have introduced a model in which scaling laws arise from sequential feature recovery. The central message is that hierarchy and anisotropy work together: depth exposes the relevant intermediate representation, while the power-law structure of the signal spreads the recovery of its components across sample sizes. As a result, a smooth power-law learning curve can emerge from the aggregate effect of many sharp feature-learning transitions. This gives a mechanism by which power laws are generated by representation learning, rather than inherited from a fixed kernel spectrum.

The main limitations of our analysis are also what make this mechanism transparent: the hierarchy is specified in advance, inputs are Gaussian, and learning is performed by a layer-wise procedure. These assumptions enable sharp recovery and non-recovery guarantees, while pointing to natural next questions: extending the mechanism to more generic data, richer nonlinearities, and higher information exponents. More broadly, our results suggest that scaling laws in deep learning may reflect not only spectral bias at a fixed representation, but the progressive organization of representations across depth.

A.1
Metrics and evaluation protocol

We use three complementary diagnostics in the numerical experiments. The MSE measures end-to-end prediction performance, the first-layer feature overlap measures recovery of the latent representation as a subspace, and the direction-wise cosines isolate the individual spectral transitions predicted by
Theorem

3.1
. These quantities answer different questions: the MSE mixes all recovered and unrecovered directions through their weights, the aggregate overlap summarizes representation recovery, and the direction-wise overlaps reveal which latent directions have crossed their spectral threshold.

Generalization error.

The MSE reported in the figures is the empirical test estimate of the generalization error in
Definition

2.3
. Given an independent test set
\{(x_{\mu}^{\rm test},y_{\mu}^{\rm test})\}_{\mu=1}^{n_{\rm test}}
, we compute

\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{1}{n_{\rm test}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n_{\rm test}}\left(\widehat{f}(x_{\mu}^{\rm test})-y_{\mu}^{\rm test}\right)^{2}.

(A.1)

This is the final performance metric of the algorithm. The overlap quantities below are diagnostic measures tied to the teacher-student setting. They are used to identify whether changes in MSE are caused by first-layer feature recovery or else.

First-layer feature overlap.

Let
H^{(1)},\widehat{H}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rm test}\times d_{1}}
denote the true and learned first-layer feature matrices evaluated on the same independent test set. Let
Q
and
\widehat{Q}
be orthonormal bases for the column spaces of
H^{(1)}
and
\widehat{H}^{(1)}
, respectively. We define

q_{h}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\|Q^{\top}\widehat{Q}\|_{F}^{2}.

(A.2)

Equivalently,
q_{h}^{(1)}
is the average squared canonical correlation between the true and learned latent feature spaces. It belongs to
[0,1]
, more precisely, it is equal to one when the learned features span the same subspace as the true features, and close to zero when the two subspaces are nearly orthogonal.

This subspace definition is intentional. The learned coordinates are only meaningful up to signs, permutations, and possible finite-size rotations inside the recovered eigenspace. These transformations can be absorbed by the second-layer fit and should not be counted as representation error.

Direction-wise alignment.

The aggregate overlap
q_{h}^{(1)}
does not show which individual directions have been recovered. To test the feature-wise prediction of
Theorem

3.1
, we also measure single direction alignments. Let
u_{i}=A_{i}^{(1)}/\|A_{i}^{(1)}\|
be the normalized
i
-th teacher direction, and let
\widehat{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d_{1}}
be an orthonormal basis of the top eigenspace of
\widehat{C}
(returned by the first spectral step). We define:

\cos^{2}(\theta_{i})=\|\widehat{U}^{\top}u_{i}\|^{2}=u_{i}^{\top}\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}u_{i}.

(A.3)

This is a projector overlap. It is invariant to the sign of
u_{i}
and to the choice of basis inside the recovered eigenspace. The value
\cos^{2}(\theta_{i})\simeq 0
means that direction
i
is absent from the recovered subspace, while
\cos^{2}(\theta_{i})\simeq 1
means that it has been recovered. This is the quantity shown in the left panel of
Figure

4
, where the different curves turn on sequentially according to the spike sizes
|\lambda_{i}|=Z_{\gamma}i^{-\gamma}
.

After a direction has been recovered, we are interested not only in whether it is present, but also in how fast its alignment improves with
n

\cos(\theta_i)=\|\widehat{U}^{\top}u_{i}\|
and plot the angular error
1-\cos(\theta_i)
. This is the quantity shown in the center panel of
Figure

4
. We use
1-\cos(\theta_i)
rather than
1-\cos^{2}(\theta_{i})

3.1
is stated directly in terms of the absolute eigenvector overlap and predicts

1-\cos(\theta_i)=O_{d}\left(\frac{d^{q}i^{2\gamma}}{nZ_{\gamma}^{2}}\right)

(A.4)

after recovery.

Finally, when the test features are well conditioned, the aggregate feature overlap can be viewed as a smoothed average of the direction-wise overlaps. Heuristically,

Appendix C
Detection of outliers

Recall that the algorithm described in
Algorithm

1
works by first computing the matrix:

\hat{C}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\mu}\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{2}(\mathcal{F}[\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{q}(x_{\mu})]).

(C.1)

To simplify the our notation, let
F_{\mu}=\mathcal{F}[\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{q}(x_{\mu})]\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where
D=B(d,k)=\binom{d+k-1}{k-1}=\Theta_{d}(d^{q})
. With this notation,

\hat{C}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\mu}\left(F_{\mu}F_{\mu}^{T}-I_{D}\right).

We want to study the eigenvectors of this random matrix. For this, we first write:

\hat{C}=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]+\left(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]\right).

(C.2)

The reader should think of the first term as the signal and the second one as the noise. We begin this section by studying the signal part.

C.1
Studying
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]

As noted in
58
, (and previously in
63
;
65
, among others) when computing the expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]
, the vectors
F_{\mu}
behave as if they were isotropic Gaussians in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For this reason, we have:

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\gamma>0
, and denote
\lambda_{j}=Z_{\gamma}j^{-\gamma}
and
u_{j}=A^{(1)}_{j}
. Then:

\mathbb{E}\left[C^{(1)}\right]=\frac{\nu_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}A^{(1)}D_{\gamma}(A^{(1)})^{T}+\Delta,

where
\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}=o_{d}(1)
,
A^{(1)}=[u_{1},\dots u_{d_{1}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d_{1}}
,
D_{\gamma}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
and
\nu_{1}
denotes the first Hermite coefficient of
g
.

We postpone the Proof of
Lemma

C.1
to
Section

E.5
.

C.2
Eigenvector Perturbation Formula

As noted before, it will be useful to write:

\hat{C}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]+\left(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}\left[C^{(1)}\right]\right)=A^{(1)}D_{\gamma}(A^{(1)})^{T}+\left(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}\left[C^{(1)}\right]\right)+\Delta.

(C.3)

Since
\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}=o_{d}(1)
, we only care about the first two terms of the decomposition above.

Now, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, let

R_{\bar{C}}(z)=(zI_{D}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])^{-1},R_{\hat{C}}(z)=(zI_{D}-\hat{C})^{-1}.

(C.4)

Then:

R_{\hat{C}}(z)-R_{\bar{C}}(z)=R_{\hat{C}}(z)(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])R_{\bar{C}}(z).

(C.5)

Denote
\Delta=\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]
. Then:

zI_{D}-\hat{C}=zI_{D}-\mathbb{E}[C]-\Delta=(zI_{D}-\mathbb{E}[C])(I_{D}-R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta).

(C.6)

Therefore:

R_{\hat{C}}(z)=(I_{D}-R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta)^{-1}R_{\bar{C}}(z).

(C.7)

If
\|R_{\bar{C}}\Delta\|_{\rm op}\leq 1
, then we can expand
(I_{D}-R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta)^{-1}
into its Neumann series:

(I_{D}-R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta)^{-1}=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}(R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta)^{\ell}.

(C.8)

Then, going back to
Equation

C.7
, we can write:

R_{\hat{C}}(z)=R_{\bar{C}}(z)+R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta R_{\bar{C}}(z)+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

(C.9)

Let
u_{k},\hat{u}_{k}
be isolated eigenvectors of
\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}],\hat{C}
, respectively. Let
\gamma
be a contour around
\lambda_{k}
and
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
. Then, we can write the projectors
\Pi_{k}=u_{k}u_{k}^{T}
and
\hat{\Pi}_{k}=\hat{u}_{k}\hat{u}_{k}
as:

\Pi_{k}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}R_{\bar{C}}(z)dz,\quad\hat{\Pi}_{k}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}R_{\hat{C}}(z)dz.

(C.10)

Then, we can integrate
Equation

C.9
to get:

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{k}\Pi_{k}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{k}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}R_{\bar{C}}\Delta R_{\bar{C}}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

(C.11)

We can re-write the resolvent
R_{\bar{C}}
in the following way:

R_{\bar{C}}(z)=(zI-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{z}P_{\mathrm{Ker}},

(C.12)

where
P_{\mathrm{Ker}}=P_{\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])}
denotes the projection into the kernel of
\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]
. Then:

\displaystyle R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta R_{\bar{C}}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{z}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\right)\Delta\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{z}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\right)

(C.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\dfrac{1}{(z-\lambda_{j_{1}})(z-\lambda_{j_{2}})}u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{1}}^{T}\Delta u_{j_{2}}u_{j_{2}}^{T}+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}

(C.14)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}+\dfrac{1}{z^{2}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}.

(C.15)

Integrating, we get:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}R_{\bar{C}}(z)\Delta R_{\bar{C}}(z)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\dfrac{1}{(z-\lambda_{j_{1}})(z-\lambda_{j_{2}})}u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{1}}^{T}\Delta u_{j_{2}}u_{j_{2}}^{T}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}

(C.16)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}+\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\dfrac{1}{z^{2}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}}_{=0}

(C.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}\left(u_{j}u_{k}^{T}+u_{k}u_{j}^{T}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}u_{k}u_{k}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}u_{k}^{T}.

(C.18)

Then, replacing this in
Equation

C.11
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{k}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}\left(u_{j}u_{k}^{T}+u_{k}u_{j}^{T}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}u_{k}u_{k}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}u_{k}^{T}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

(C.19)

In order to get eigenvectors, we apply this projection to
u_{k}
and obtain:

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{k}u_{k}

\displaystyle=u_{k}+\left(\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}\left(u_{j}u_{k}^{T}+u_{k}u_{j}^{T}\right)+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}u_{k}u_{k}^{T}\Delta P_{\mathrm{Ker}}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}u_{k}^{T}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2})\right)u_{k}

(C.20)

\displaystyle=u_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

(C.21)

Note that the second and third terms are orthogonal to
u_{k}
, so

\|\hat{\Pi}_{k}u_{k}\|^{2}=1+\left\|\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}\right\|^{2}+o(\|\Delta\|^{2}_{\rm op}),

(C.22)

and since the term in the middle is bounded by
C\|\Delta\|^{2}
, we conclude that:

\|\hat{\Pi}_{k}u_{k}\|^{2}=1+o(\|\Delta\|^{2}).

(C.23)

Then, we can normalize
Equation

C.21
and we will have:

\hat{u}_{k}=u_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}\Delta u_{j}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

(C.24)

At last, we replace the eigenvectors
u_{k}
by
A^{(1)}_{k}
, by using

Lemma

C.1
, plus the fact that
A^{(1)}
are almost the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]
. To see this, note that by applying a covariance concentration bound
61
for
\hat{A}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{A}^{(1)}
, the condition
\varepsilon<(k-2\gamma)
gives that

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}}u_{i}u^{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}}A^{(1)}_{i}(A^{(1)})^{T}\right|\leq d_{1}^{\gamma}\sqrt{\dfrac{d_{1}}{d^{q}}}=d^{\varepsilon(\frac{1}{2}+2\gamma)-q},

(C.25)

which is small by our assumption that
\varepsilon<\nicefrac{{q}}{{(1-2\gamma)}}
to get:

\hat{u}_{k}=A^{(1)}_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{(A^{(1)}_{k})^{T}\Delta(A^{(1)}_{j})}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}(A^{(1)}_{j})+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

(C.26)

C.3
Analysis of Outliers - Sufficient Sample Complexity

Having
Equation

C.24
, we can study the eigenvectors of
\hat{C}
. In order for the expansion to be valid, we need
\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}=\|\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]\|_{\rm op}
to be small. In the following Lemma, we show that this is indeed the case when
n\gg d^{q}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Consider the estimator
\hat{C}
in
Algorithm

1
computed for the Hermite tensor of degree
q
. Then with high probability

\left\|\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]\right\|_{\rm op}\lesssim\sqrt{\dfrac{d^{q}}{n}}.

(C.27)

The proof proceed the same way as
58
and
65
. By Lemma F.4 in
65
, with probability at least
1-e^{-cd}
,

\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}_{2}\leq Cd^{q},

(C.28)

for a universal constant
C
. By truncating the matrix
\hat{C}
with indicators
\mathbf{1}_{\|\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}_{2}\leq Cd^{q}}
, and applying Bernstein’s inequality, we get the desired results.
∎

Then, by
Equation

C.27
, we can apply the expansion
Equation

C.24
and we can conclude

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\hat{u}_{k}
denote the
k
-th eigenvector of
\hat{C}
, and
u_{k}
the
k
-th eigenvector of
\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]
. Denote
\Delta:=\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]
. Then,

\hat{u}_{k}=A^{(1)}_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{(A^{(1)}_{k})^{T}\Delta(A^{(1)}_{j})}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}(A^{(1)}_{j})+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}+o_{d}(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}^{2}).

where
\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}=O\left(\max\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{d^{q}\log(d)}{n}},\dfrac{d_{1}^{-\gamma}}{Z_{\gamma}}\sqrt{\dfrac{d_{1}}{d}}\right)\right)
.

Lemma

C.3
tells us that we can write the
k
-th eigenvector of
\hat{C}
as:

\hat{u}_{k}=A^{(1)}_{k}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{(A^{(1)}_{j})^{T}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])A^{(1)}_{k}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}}_{(I)}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[C^{(1)}])u_{k}+\Delta,

(C.29)

for
\|\Delta\|_{2}=o_{d}(1)
. Let’s focus on
(I)
. We have:

\displaystyle(I)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])u_{k}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}

(C.30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}(\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\mu}(F_{\mu}F_{\mu}^{T}-I_{D})-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])u_{k}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}

(C.31)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\mu}h_{\mu,j}h_{\mu,k}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}.

(C.32)

From Lemma F.4 in
65
, there exists a universal constant
C
such that with high probability
\|F_{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}\leq Cd^{q}
. Then, assuming the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]
are de-localized (in the sense that
\|u_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq Cd^{-q}
, we will have

|h_{\mu,j}|=\langle u_{j},F_{\mu}\rangle\leq C.

(C.33)

Then:

\displaystyle\|(I)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{1}{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\mu}h_{\mu,j}h_{\mu,k}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(C.34)

By
Lemma

F.1
, with high probability:

\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}}{n}y_{\mu}h_{\mu,k}h_{\mu,j}\right)^{2}\lesssim\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}{d^{q}}\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}.

(C.36)

Then:

\displaystyle\|(I)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}{d^{q}}\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}}{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(C.37)

We now focus on the inner sum. Recall that
\lambda_{j}=z_{j}Z_{\gamma}j^{-\gamma}
, and that
z_{j}\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})
. Then the inner sum is upper bounded by the case where
z_{j}\not=z_{k}
, that is:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}}{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}\leq\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}}{(k^{-\gamma}-j^{-\gamma})^{2}}.

(C.38)

By separating the sum according to
j<k
and
j>k
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}}{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j<k}\dfrac{j^{-2\gamma}}{(k^{-\gamma}-j^{-\gamma})^{2}}+\sum_{j>k}\dfrac{k^{-2\gamma}}{(k^{-\gamma}-j^{-\gamma})^{2}}\right)

(C.39)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j<k}\dfrac{1}{\left(\left(\dfrac{k}{j}\right)^{-\gamma}-1\right)^{2}}+\sum_{j>k}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\dfrac{j}{k}^{-\gamma}\right)^{2}}\right)

(C.40)

Note that for
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the function
u\to u^{\gamma}
is concave. Then,
u^{\gamma}\leq 1+\gamma(u-1)
, and therefore
1-u^{\gamma}>\gamma(1-u)
. On the other hand, for
\gamma>1
if
u\in(0,1)
, then
u^{\gamma}<u
and hence
1-u^{\gamma}>1-u
. Either way, we get the following upper-bound:

\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}}{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}\lesssim\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j<k}\dfrac{1}{\left(\left(\dfrac{j}{k}\right)^{\gamma}-1\right)^{2}}+\sum_{j>k}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\left(\dfrac{k}{j}\right)^{\gamma}\right)^{2}}\right)

(C.41)

Using this fact:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not=k}\dfrac{\min(k,j)^{-2\gamma}}{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j<k}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\dfrac{j}{k}\right)^{2}}+\sum_{j>k}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\dfrac{k}{j}\right)^{2}}\right)

(C.42)

\displaystyle\lesssim\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j<k}\dfrac{k^{2}}{\left(k-j\right)^{2}}+\sum_{j>k}\dfrac{j^{2}}{\left(j-k\right)^{2}}\right)

(C.43)

\displaystyle\lesssim\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\left(k^{2}+d_{1}\right)\lesssim\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}d_{1}^{2}.

(C.44)

Then, going back to
Equation

C.37
:

\displaystyle\|(I)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}{d^{q}}\dfrac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}d_{1}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\dfrac{d_{1}^{2}}{d^{q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(C.45)

Since
d_{1}=d^{\varepsilon}
, and
\varepsilon<\frac{q}{2}
, we conclude that
\|I\|_{2}=o_{d}(1)
. From this, we can go back to
Equation

C.29
, and applying inner product with
A^{(1)}_{k}
, we will get:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{u}_{k},A^{(1)}_{k}\rangle=\|A^{(1)}_{k}\|^{2}+\langle(I),A^{(1)}_{k}\rangle+o_{d}(1),

(C.46)

and by Cauchy-Schwarz and
Equation

C.45
:

\left|\langle(I),A^{(1)}_{k}\rangle\right|\leq\|\hat{A}^{(1)}_{k}\|_{2}\|(I)\|_{2}=o_{d}(1).

(C.47)

where we used the fact that the norm of
\hat{A}^{(1)}_{k}
concentrates around
1
by Hanson-Wright Inequality (
62
, Theorem 6.2.2). Then, we conclude:

\langle\hat{u}_{k},A^{(1)}_{k}\rangle=1+o_{d}(1).

(C.48)

Denote

\mathrm{Overlap}(a,b)=\dfrac{\langle a,b\rangle}{\|a\|\|b\|}.

(C.49)

After normalizing in
Equation

C.48
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Overlap}(\hat{u}_{k},A^{(1)}_{k})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\|I\|^{2}+\left\|\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[C^{(1)}])u_{k}\right\|^{2}}}

(C.50)

By applying Taylor expansion to the function
u\to\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}
, we get:

\displaystyle 1-\mathrm{Overlap}(\hat{u}_{k},A^{(1)}_{k})=1-O\left(\|I\|^{2}+\|II\|^{2}\right)+O((\|I\|^{2}+\|II\|^{2})^{2}).

(C.51)

By
Equation

C.45
, we know that
\|I\|^{2}=o_{d}(1)
, with a bound independent of
n
. Thus, the only thing left to conclude is to show that
\|(II)\|
goes to zero with
n
, at a rate
\dfrac{1}{n}
, and we can conclude the first part of
Theorem

3.1
. Computing
\|(II)\|^{2}
, we get:

\|(II)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\|\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]\|_{\rm op}^{2}.

(C.52)

By
Equation

C.27
, with high probability:

\|\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}]\|_{\rm op}\lesssim\sqrt{\dfrac{d^{q}\mathrm{poly}\log(d)}{n}}.

(C.53)

Then:

\|(II)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\dfrac{i^{2\gamma}d^{q}\mathrm{poly}\log(d)}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}n}.

(C.54)

Then we conclude that, if
n=\Theta(Z_{\gamma}^{2}i^{2\gamma}d^{k+\delta+\varepsilon})
,

\mathrm{Overlap}(\hat{u}_{k},\hat{A}^{(1)}_{k}\rangle=1-o_{d}(1),

(C.55)

thus, we recover the direction
\hat{A}^{(1)}_{k}
with
n=\Theta(Z_{\gamma}^{2}i^{2\gamma}d^{k+\delta+\varepsilon})
. As for the rate,
Equation

C.54
tells us that it is controlled by the second term, and decays as
\frac{-1}{n}
, which concludes the first part of
Theorem

3.1
.

C.4
Analysis of Outliers - Necessary Sample Complexity

Recall that from
Lemma

C.3
:

\hat{u}_{k}=A^{(1)}_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{(A^{(1)}_{j})^{T}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])A^{(1)}_{k}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}A^{(1)}_{j}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[C^{(1)}])u_{k}+o(\|\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[C^{(1)}]\|^{2}).

(C.56)

We will work in the regime where
n\gg d^{k}
, so the residual term is vanishing by
Equation

C.27
.In this section, we will prove that the sample complexity we found in the last section is in fact, necessary. For this, we will focus on the last term:

w=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[C^{(1)}])u_{k}=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\hat{C}u_{k}.

(C.57)

We will prove that with high probability,
\|w\|=\Theta_{d}(d^{\frac{\delta}{2}})
. For this, we will use to preliminary results.

Lemma C.4

(Paley-Zigmund Inequality, (
13
, Exercise 2.4))
.

Let
Y
be real, positive random variable, and
\theta\in(0,1)
. Then:

\mathbb{P}\left(Y>\theta\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}}.

(C.58)

The Paley-Zigmund inequality will give us a lower bound as long as we can bound the moments of a particular random variable. In our case, the random variables will be polynomials of Gaussians, so we will use Gaussian hypercontractivity.

Lemma C.5

(Gaussian Hypercontractivity,
36
Theorem 5.8)
.

Let
X
be a
N
degree polynomial of
m
Gaussian random variables. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C(p,N)\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

for all
1<p<\infty
.

Having
Equation

C.58
and
Lemma

C.5
, we are ready to proceed with the proof of the second part
Theorem

3.1
. First, we identify the random variable to which we will apply Paley-Zigmund inequality, which is
\|w\|
.

Step 1: Defining the key-quantities
By using the definition of
\hat{C}
we have:

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\dfrac{y_{\mu}}{\lambda_{k}}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu}\rangle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu}+\Delta

(C.59)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}Z_{\mu}=\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}(Z_{\mu}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu}])}_{S_{n}}+\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu}],

(C.60)

for
\|\Delta\|=o_{d}(1)
. On the other hand:

\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\mu}\right]=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{Ker}}\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\mu}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu}\rangle F_{\mu}\right]=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]u_{k}.

(C.61)

Applying
Lemma

C.1
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]=\nu_{1}A^{(1)}D(A^{(1)})^{T}+\Delta
, for
\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}\lesssim\frac{1}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}
. Then:

\|\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\mu}\right]\|=\left\|\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\left(\nu_{1}A^{(1)}D(A^{(1)})^{T}+\Delta\right)u_{k}\right\|=\left\|\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}}\Delta u_{k}\right\|\leq\dfrac{\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}}{|\lambda_{k}|}=o_{d}(1),

(C.62)

since for
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
, we get
\dfrac{Z_{\gamma^{2}}}{Z_{\gamma}k^{-\gamma}}=\dfrac{k^{-\gamma}}{\sqrt{d^{1-2\gamma}}}=o_{d}(1)
. Then:

\mathbb{E}[\|w\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu}]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]+o_{d}(1),

(C.63)

and also:

\mathbb{E}[\|w\|^{4}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]+o_{d}(1).

(C.64)

Then, by applying
C.58
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|w\|^{2}>\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{2}\right]\right)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{2}\right]}

(C.65)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|w\|^{2}>\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{2}\right]\right)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}+o_{d}(1).

(C.66)

Step 2: Controlling the quotient
\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}
Let’s focus on the term
\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}
. Denote
\bar{Z}_{\mu}=Z_{\mu}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu}]
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{1}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{2}}\rangle\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{3}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{4}}\rangle\right].

(C.67)

By independence, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{4}\right]+\dfrac{n-1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}]^{2}+\dfrac{2(n-1)}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{1}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right],

(C.68)

where in the last expectation
\mu_{1}\not=\mu_{2}
. On the other hand:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}.

(C.69)

Then:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}+\dfrac{n-1}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}+\dfrac{2(n-1)}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z_{\mu_{1}},Z_{\mu_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}

(C.70)

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}+\dfrac{2(n-1)}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{1}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}

(C.71)

Since
\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}
is the
L^{2}
of a real polynomial, we can apply Gaussian hypercontractivity
Lemma

C.5
to get::

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{4}\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2},

(C.72)

so we have:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{2(n-1)}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{1}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}.

(C.73)

We now focus on the last term. We first note that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{1}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z_{\mu_{1}}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu_{1}}],Z_{\mu_{2}}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu_{2}}]\rangle^{2}\right]

(C.74)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}\left[\left(\langle Z_{\mu_{1}},Z_{\mu_{2}}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu_{2}}]\rangle-\langle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu_{1}}],Z_{\mu_{2}}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu_{2}}]\rangle\right)^{2}\right]

(C.75)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}\left[\left(\langle Z_{\mu_{1}},Z_{\mu_{2}}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mu_{2}}]\rangle\right)^{2}\right]

(C.76)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\langle Z_{\mu_{1}},Z_{\mu_{2}}\rangle\right)^{2}\right],

(C.77)

where in the last two lines we bounded the conditional expectations (which are in fact conditional variances) by the second moment. To bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\langle Z_{\mu_{1}},Z_{\mu_{2}}\rangle\right)^{2}\right]
, we proceed by first conditioning in
\mu_{2}
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z,Z\rangle^{2}|Z_{\mu_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\|Z_{\mu_{2}}\|^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\dfrac{y_{\mu_{1}}}{\lambda_{k}}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},\dfrac{Z_{\mu_{2}}}{\|Z_{\mu_{2}}\|}\right\rangle^{2}|Z_{\mu_{2}}\right]

(C.78)

\displaystyle=\|Z_{\mu_{2}}\|^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\dfrac{y_{\mu_{1}}}{\lambda_{k}}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},\dfrac{P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{2}}}{\|P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{2}}\|}\right\rangle^{2}|Z_{\mu_{2}}\right].

(C.79)

Denote
a=\dfrac{P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{2}}}{\|P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{2}}\|}
. Note that for this expectation,
a
is a fixed norm
1
vector in
\mathrm{Ker}(\hat{C}^{(1)})
. Then,, by applying Holder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\dfrac{y_{\mu_{1}}}{\lambda_{k}}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{y_{\mu_{1}}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle^{2}\left\langle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{2}\right]

(C.80)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[y_{\mu_{1}}^{6}]^{\frac{1}{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle^{6}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{6}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(C.81)

By
Lemma

C.5
: since
y_{\mu}
has finite variance, we have
\mathbb{E}[y_{\mu_{1}}^{6}]^{\frac{1}{3}}=\Theta_{d}(1)
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle^{6}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle^{2}\right]=C\|u_{k}\|^{2}=C.

(C.82)

Then:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\dfrac{y_{\mu_{1}}}{\lambda_{k}}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu_{1}}\rangle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{2}\right]\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{6}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(C.83)

Now, since
P_{\mathrm{Ker}}a=a
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{6}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{6}\right],

(C.84)

and by
Equation

C.82
we conclude
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu_{1}},a\right\rangle^{6}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim 1
. Replacing in
Equation

C.79
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z,Z\rangle^{2}|Z_{\mu_{2}}\right]\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\|Z_{\mu_{2}}\|^{2},

(C.85)

and therefore:

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Z,Z\rangle^{2}\right]\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z_{\mu_{2}}\|^{2}\right].

(C.86)

Going back to
Equation

C.71
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{C}{n}+\dfrac{2(n-1)}{n}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bar{Z}_{\mu_{1}},\bar{Z}_{\mu_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}

(C.87)

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{C}{n}+\dfrac{2(n-1)}{n}\dfrac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]}.

(C.88)

Step 3: Control of the norm:

The only thing left is to compute
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]
. First, by
Equation

C.62
:

\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{\mu}\|^{2}]+o_{d}(1).

(C.89)

Then, we can just compute
\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{\mu}\|^{2}]
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{Z}_{\mu}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\mu}^{2}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu}\rangle^{2}\|P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right].

(C.90)

Denote
G_{\mu}=y_{\mu}^{2}\langle u_{k},F_{\mu}\rangle^{2}
. Then:

\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{\mu}\|^{2}]=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|P_{\mathrm{Ker}}F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right].

(C.91)

Note that
\mathbb{E}[G_{\mu}]=\Theta(1)
, and we can write
P_{\mathrm{Ker}}=I_{D}-P_{U}
, for
P_{U}
the projection into the space spanned by
u_{1},\dots,u_{d_{1}}
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{\mu}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]-\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|P_{U}F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]

(C.92)

Now, define the event
\mathcal{A}:=\{\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}\geq\dfrac{D}{2}\}
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\right]

(C.93)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{D}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\right]=\dfrac{D}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}\right]\right)

(C.94)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{D}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\right]+o_{d}(1),

(C.95)

where the last line follows from
Lemma

B.2
. Doing the same for the term
\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|P_{U}F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\mu}\|F_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]\geq\dfrac{D}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}+\dfrac{d_{1}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}

(C.96)

Then, going back to
Equation

C.88
:

\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}=1-o_{d}(1).

(C.97)

Replacing this in our original Paley-Zigmund inequality,
Equation

C.66
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|w\|^{2}>\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{2}\right]\right)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{4}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]^{2}}+o_{d}(1)

(C.98)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|w\|^{2}>\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{2}\right]\right)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}+o_{d}(1).

(C.99)

Then, with probability at least
(1-\theta)^{2}+o_{d}(1)
:

\|w\|^{2}>\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|w\|^{2}\right]

(C.100)

From
Equation

C.63
, this implies that with probability at least
(1-\theta)^{2}+o_{d}(1)
:

\displaystyle\|w\|^{2}\geq\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]+o_{d}(1)

(C.101)

and since:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|S_{n}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z_{\mu}\|^{2}\right]+o_{d}(1)

(C.102)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{n}\left(\dfrac{D}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}+\dfrac{d_{1}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)+o_{d}(1)

(C.103)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{d}\left(\dfrac{d^{q}}{n\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right),

(C.104)

we have that with probability at least
(1-\theta)^{2}+o_{d}(1)
:

\displaystyle\|w\|^{2}\geq\theta\Theta_{d}\left(\dfrac{d^{q}}{n\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)+o_{d}(1).

(C.105)

Replacing
n=\Theta_{d}\left(\frac{d^{q}i^{2\gamma}d^{-\delta}}{Z_{\gamma}}\right)
, and taking
\theta=d^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}
, we conclude that with probability at least
1-o_{d}(1)
:

\|w\|\geq\theta\sqrt{\dfrac{d^{q}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}n}}=\Theta_{d}(d^{\frac{\delta}{2}}).

(C.106)

To conclude that the overlap is negligible, recall we had:

\hat{u}_{k}=u_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\not=k}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{u_{j}^{T}(\hat{C}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}])u_{k}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}u_{j}+w+o(\|\Delta\|_{\rm op}),

(C.107)

so by the same reasoning of
Equation

C.50
:

\mathrm{Overlap}(\hat{u}_{k},A^{(1)}_{k})=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left\|w\right\|^{2}+o_{d}(1)}}.

(C.108)

Then, with high probability, since
\|w\|^{2}
grows to infinity with
d
:

\mathrm{Overlap}(\hat{u}_{k},A^{(1)}_{k})=o_{d}(1),

(C.109)

which concludes the second part of
Theorem

3.1
.

Appendix D
Derivation of the rates

In this section, we build on
Theorem

3.1
and prove
Corollary

3.1
in order to then derive rates for the MSE between the teacher and the predictor in
Equation

3.4
.
From these two results, we now that
Algorithm

1
either learns a direction
A_{i}^{(1)}
or not at all.

By assumption of
Equation

3.4
, the function
g
is a polynomial and at this stage the features
\hat{h}^{2}_{\mu}
in
Algorithm

1
are one-dimensional and correspond to a predictor of the second layer of the teacher model.
Algorithm

1
now proceeds with an independent sample
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}=\{x^{\prime}_{\mu},y^{\prime}_{\mu}\}
. We first transform:

x^{\prime}_{\mu}\to\hat{h}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{\prime}_{\mu}),

(D.1)

and then doing KRR on
\{\hat{h}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{\prime}_{\mu}),y_{\mu}\}
. We used the notation
h^{(2)}_{\mathcal{D}}
to highlight the fact that this predictor is independent of the new data sample. Denote
\hat{f}
denote the predictor obtained by doing KRR on
\{\hat{h}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{\prime}_{\mu}),y_{\mu}\}
. Denote by
\hat{L}(\hat{f})
the empirical risk, and by
L(\hat{f})
the population risk. Then, by Theorem 1 in
16
, using the fact that the features are one-dimensional:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D^{\prime}}}\left(L(\hat{f})-\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}L(f)>\dfrac{\tau}{n}\right),

(D.2)

goes to
0
with
n
for a fixed first sample
\mathcal{D}
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\left(L(\hat{f})-\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}L(f)>\dfrac{\tau}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D^{\prime}}}\left(L(\hat{f})-\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}L(f)>\dfrac{\tau}{n}\right)\right),

(D.3)

which goes to
0
by
Equation

D.2
. Then, we conclude that with high probability

L(\hat{f})-\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}L(f)=O(\dfrac{1}{n}),

(D.4)

and

L(\hat{f})\leq\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}L(f)=O(\dfrac{1}{n}).

(D.5)

Assuming the kernel is universal, we have
g\in\mathcal{H}
. By further assuming that the KRR regularization parameter
\lambda
is optimal, we get:

\displaystyle\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{L(f)\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(g(h^{2}_{\mu})-g(\hat{h}^{2}_{\mu}))^{2}\right]

(D.6)

Then, we conclude:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}(n)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g(h^{2}_{\mu})-g(\hat{h}^{2}_{\mu})\right)^{2}\right]+O\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\right).

(D.7)

Now, we focus on the linear term of the subtraction. We have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{linear}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{j\geq i^{\star}}j^{-\gamma}\left((h_{j}^{(1)})^{2}-1)\right)\right\|^{2}\right],

(D.8)

where
i^{\star}
is the number of learned directions. By
Theorem

3.1
, we learn the
i-th
direction if

n\asymp\dfrac{d^{q}i^{2\gamma}}{Z_{\gamma}^{2}}\implies i\asymp\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}^{2}n}{d^{q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}.

(D.9)

Since directions are learned sequentially, the number of learn directions ( or the last direction that was recovered) at sample complexity
n
is:

i^{\star}=\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}^{2}n}{d^{q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}.

(D.10)

This proves
Corollary

3.1
. We will now prove
Equation

3.4
. By
Equation

D.8
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{linear}}(n)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(Z_{\gamma}^{2}\sum_{i\geq i^{\star}}j^{-2\gamma}\right).

(D.11)

We now study this sum according to the value of
\gamma
. If
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
, then
Z_{\gamma}=(d_{1}^{1-2\gamma})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, and we get:

\displaystyle Z_{\gamma}^{2}\sum_{i\geq i^{\star}}j^{-2\gamma}

\displaystyle=Z_{\gamma}^{2}(Z_{\gamma}^{-2}-\sum_{i\leq i^{\star}}i^{-2\gamma})

(D.12)

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{1}{d_{1}^{1-2\gamma}}(i^{\star})^{1-2\gamma}

(D.13)

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{1}{d_{1}^{1-2\gamma}}\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}^{2}n}{d^{q}}\right)^{-1+\frac{1}{2\gamma}}

(D.14)

\displaystyle=1-\left(\dfrac{n}{d_{1}d^{q}}\right)^{(-1+\frac{1}{2\gamma})},

(D.15)

which gives the rate for the case where
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
and
d^{q}\ll n\ll d^{q}d_{1}
.

On the other hand, for
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
,
Z_{\gamma}=\Theta_{d}(1)
and

\sum_{i\geq i^{\star}}i^{-2\gamma}=\Theta_{d}((i^{\star})^{1-2\gamma}).

(D.16)

Then:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{linear}}(n)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{d}\left(\sum_{i\geq i^{\star}}i^{-2\gamma}\right)

(D.17)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{d}((i^{\star})^{1-2\gamma})

(D.18)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{n}{d^{q}}\right)^{\frac{1-2\gamma}{2\gamma}}

(D.19)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{n}{d^{q}}\right)^{-1+\frac{1}{2\gamma}},

(D.20)

and we conclude
Equation

3.4
for the linear case. For the non-linear case, we note that all the non-linear terms of
Equation

D.7
will be sub-leading with respect to the linear part when
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
, so we conclude
Equation

3.4
for the non-linear case as well.

Appendix E
Explicit computations with Weiner Chaos

E.1
Wiener Chaos properties

This section will only overview the necessary concepts we need from Wiener chaos expansions. This results are based on
52
,
49
and
65
.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{B(d,q)}
, we define

\displaystyle I_{q}(A)=\langle A,\mathcal{F}(\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{q}(x))\rangle\,,

(E.1)

where for
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
with
|\beta|=q

(\mathrm{He}_{q}(x))_{\beta}=\mathord{\mathrm{He}}_{\beta}(x).

(E.2)

This way, the first layer coefficients can be written as:

h^{(1)}_{\mu,i}=I_{q}(A^{(1)}_{i}).

(E.3)

We will extensively use the orthogonality of Hermite polynomials.

Lemma E.1

(Orthogonality of Different Chaos)
.

Let
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
,
A\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot q},B\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot q^{\prime}}
. Then:

\mathbb{E}\left[I_{q}(A)I_{q^{\prime}}(B)\right]=\mathbf{1}_{q=q^{\prime}}\langle A,B\rangle.

The space spanned by random variables in the
k
-th Wiener chaos is denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. We will also need the orthogonal projection into the
k
-Wiener chaos, which we denote by
J_{k}:L^{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
.

We define the Malliavin derivative
D:\mathrm{dom}(D)\to(L^{2})^{d}
by

DF=(\partial_{x_{1}}F,\ldots,\partial_{x_{d}}F),\qquad\mathrm{dom}(D)=\left\{F\in L^{2}:\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k\|J_{k}(F)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}^{2}<\infty\right\}

where, for smooth functions
F
with compact support,
\partial_{x_{j}}
is the usual partial derivative of
F(\bm{x})=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
in the variable
x_{j}
, and this is extended by completion to
\mathrm{dom}(D)
. We also define the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck infinitesimal generator
L:\mathrm{dom}(L)\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
by

LF=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}-k\,J_{k}(F),\qquad\mathrm{dom}(L)=\left\{F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}):\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^{2}\|J_{k}(F)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}^{2}<\infty\right\}.

and we define its inverse
L^{-1}F=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{k}J_{k}(F)
whenever
J_{0}(F)=\mathbb{E}F(x)=0
.

We will need the following rules to compute the different terms that appears.

Lemma E.2

(Product formula,
49
)
.

For any
k,\ell\geq 1
,
S\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot k}
, and
T\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot\ell}
,

I_{k}(S)I_{\ell}(T)=\sum_{r=0}^{\min(k,\ell)}r!\binom{k}{r}\binom{\ell}{r}I_{k+\ell-2r}(S\tilde{\otimes}_{r}T).

We will also need the following rule for computing product of derivatives.

Lemma E.3
.

For any
k,\ell\geq 1
,
S\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot k},T\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot\ell}
,

(DI_{k}(S))^{\mathrm{T}}(DI_{\ell}(T))=k\ell\sum_{r=1}^{\min(k,\ell)}(r-1)!\binom{k-1}{r-1}\binom{\ell-1}{r-1}I_{k+\ell-2r}(S\tilde{\otimes}_{r}T)

For some functions, specially polynomials, the following Gaussian Integration by Parts Lemma will be very useful.

Lemma E.4

(Theorem 2.9.1 in
49
, Gaussian Integration by Parts)
.

Let
F,G\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}
function having a bounded derivative. Then

\mathbb{E}[Fg(G)]=\mathbb{E}[F]\mathbb{E}[g(G)]+\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(G)\langle DG,-DL^{-1}F\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}].\quad

E.2
Computing Expectations with Malliavin Calculus

To compute expectations, we will need:

Remark E.1
.

As the discussion in
49
, Page 31 notes, the conditions under which
Lemma

E.4
hold are not optimal. In particular, it remains true if
g
is a polynomial.

Denote the second layer by
h^{(2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1)
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial.
In this section, we will study how to compute expectations of the form:

\mathbb{E}\left[g(h^{(2)})I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right],

(E.4)

for
j\not=k
. We are interested in how this quantity scales with
d
, as sharp as possible. In particular, we will avoid using Gaussian Approximations when is not absolutely necessary.

E.3
Linear Case

To get some intuition, we will first study the case
g(u)=u
. We want to compute:

E_{\mathrm{lin}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[SI_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1)I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right].

(E.5)

By
Lemma

E.2
:

\displaystyle I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{q}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})-1

(E.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})+(\|A_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-1),

(E.7)

where
c_{q,r}=r!\binom{q}{r}^{2}

Analogously

\displaystyle I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})=\sum_{r=0}^{q}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{k})=\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{k})+q!\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle.

(E.8)

Then, by the orthogonality of different chaos:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{lin}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1)I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right]

(E.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})+(\|A_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-1)\right)\left(\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{k})+q!\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle\right)\right]

(E.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}^{2}\langle A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i},A_{j}\tilde{\otimes_{r}}A_{k}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda c_{q,q}^{2}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1)\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle

(E.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,i\not\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}c_{q,r}^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\langle A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i},A_{j}\tilde{\otimes_{r}}A_{k}\rangle+\sum_{i\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}^{2}\langle A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i},A_{j}\tilde{\otimes_{r}}A_{k}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda c_{q,q}^{2}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1)\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle,

(E.12)

were in the last line we split the sum according to wether
i\in\{j,k\}
or not. Recall we assume that
A_{i}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot q}
have independent, centered gaussian entries with variance
\frac{1}{d^{q}}
. Let:

T^{r}_{i,j,k}=\langle A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i},A_{j}\tilde{\otimes_{r}}A_{k}\rangle.

(E.13)

For
u,v\in[d]^{q-r}
and
r\in\{0\}\cup[q-1]
we have:

(A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})_{u,v}=\sum_{\ell\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell]A_{i}[v,\ell],\quad\text{ and }(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{k})_{u,v}=\sum_{\ell\in[d]^{r}}A_{j}[u,\ell]A_{k}[v,\ell].

(E.14)

Then:

\displaystyle T^{r}_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}(A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})_{u,v}(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{k})_{u,v}

(E.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}A_{j}[u,\ell_{2}]A_{k}[v,\ell_{2}]

(E.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)A_{j}[u,\ell_{2}]A_{k}[v,\ell_{2}].

(E.17)

If
i\not=j\not=k
, then we have:

\mathbb{E}_{A_{j}}\left[T_{r}|A_{i},A_{k}\right]=0.

(E.18)

On the other hand, the conditional variance equals:

\mathrm{Var}\left(T_{r}|A_{i},A_{k}\right)=\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)A_{k}[u,\ell_{2}]\right)^{2}.

(E.19)

Taking expectation with respect to
A_{k}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{j}}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(T_{r}|A_{i},A_{k}\right)\right]=\dfrac{d^{r}}{d^{2q}}\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)^{2}=\dfrac{d^{r}}{d^{2q}}\|A_{i}\otimes_{r}A_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(E.20)

And taking expectation with respect to
A_{i}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\|A_{i}\otimes_{r}A_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)^{2}\right]

(E.21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)^{2}\right]

(E.22)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}\frac{1}{d^{q}}\right)=O_{d}(d^{-r}).

(E.23)

Applying the Law of total variance, using the fact that for distinct
i,j,k
,
T_{i,j,k}
is centered:

\mathrm{Var}_{A_{i},A_{j},A_{k}}(T_{i,j,k})=O\left(\dfrac{d^{r}}{d^{2q}d^{r}}\right)=O_{d}\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{2q}}\right).

(E.24)

Then, by applying Chebyshev Inequality we get that with high probability with respect to
A_{i},A_{j}
and
A_{k}
:

\left|T^{r}_{i,j,k}\right|=O_{d}\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\right),\text{ when }i\not=j\not=k.

(E.25)

We now move to the harder case where
i=j
( the case
i=k
is analogous). First, by definition:

T^{r}_{i,i,j}=\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]A_{j}[v,\ell_{2}].

(E.26)

Fixing
A_{i}
:

\mathbb{E}_{A_{j}}[T^{r}_{i,i,j}]=0.

(E.27)

The conditional variance given
A_{i}
equals:

\mathrm{Var}(T^{r}_{i,i,j}|A_{i})=\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\left(\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]\right)^{2}.

(E.28)

Denote
w(u,v)=\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]
. Then:

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\left(\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}

(E.29)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right),

(E.30)

where in the last line we applied Cauchy-Schwarz. Now, taking expectation and then applying Cauchy-Schwarz again:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\mathrm{Var}(T^{r}_{i,i,j}|A_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right)\right]

(E.31)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(E.32)

By the equivalence of norms for polynomials (
36
, Theorem 3.50):

\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)\right]^{2},

(E.33)

and the same holds for
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right)^{2}\right]
. Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\mathrm{Var}(T^{r}_{i,i,j}|A_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right)\right]

(E.34)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\left(\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right)\right]

(E.35)

By
Equation

E.23
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in[d]^{q-r}}\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}w(u,v)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in[d]^{r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{1}]A_{i}[v,\ell_{1}]\right)^{2}

(E.36)

\displaystyle=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{r}}\right),

(E.37)

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in[d]^{q-r}}A_{i}[u,\ell_{2}]^{2}\right)\right]=O\left(\dfrac{d^{q-r}}{d^{q}}\right)=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{r}}\right).

(E.38)

Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{i}}\left[\mathrm{Var}(T^{r}_{i,i,j}|A_{i})\right]\leq\dfrac{C}{d^{q}}\sum_{\ell_{2}\in[d]^{r}}\dfrac{1}{d^{r}}\dfrac{1}{d^{r}}=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{q+r}}\right)

(E.39)

Hence, by the Law of total variance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{A_{i},A_{j}}\left(T^{r}_{i,i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{q+r}}\right),

(E.40)

and applying Chebyshev Inequality we get that with high probability over
A_{i},A_{j}
:

\left|T^{r}_{i,i,j}\right|=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{\frac{{q+r}}{2}}}\right).

(E.41)

Replacing
Equation

E.25
and
Equation

E.41
in

Equation

E.12
:

\displaystyle\left|E_{\mathrm{lin}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,i\not\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}^{2}T^{r}_{i,j,k}+\sum_{i\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}^{2}T^{r}_{i,j,k}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}c_{q,q}^{2}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1)\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle

(E.42)

\displaystyle=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\sum_{i=1,i\not\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\right)+O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\sum_{i\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}c_{q,q}^{2}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1)\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle.

(E.43)

For the last term, applying Bernstein’s inequality (
62
, Theorem 2.9.1) for the cross inner product and Hanson-Wright (
62
, Theorem 6.2.2) for the norm, we get that with high probability:

(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1)\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\right).

(E.44)

Finally, recalling that
\lambda_{i}=Z_{\gamma}z_{i}i^{-\gamma}
, with
z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})
, we can apply Bernstein’s inequality over the Radamacher variables. We get that, with high probability over the
z_{i}
:

\displaystyle\left|E_{\mathrm{lin}}\right|=O\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}}{d^{q}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1,i\not\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}i^{-2\gamma}}\right)+O\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}}{d^{\frac{q}{2}}}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in\{j,k\}}^{d_{1}}i^{-2\gamma}}\right)+\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}}{d^{q}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}i^{-2\gamma}}.

(E.45)

By definition
Z_{\gamma}=(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}i^{-2\gamma})
, so we finally conclude that with very high probability:

\left|E_{\mathrm{lin}}\right|=O\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}}{d^{q}}(j^{\gamma}+k^{\gamma}))\right).

(E.46)

Thus, we have proved the following Lemma.

Lemma E.5
.

Let
S=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1)
. Then, given
i,j\in[d_{1}]
with
i\not=j
:

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[SI_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right]\right|=O\left(\dfrac{Z_{\gamma}}{d^{\frac{q}{2}}}(j^{\gamma}+k^{\gamma}))\right).

E.4
The non-linear case

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial of degree
m
. Following
Theorem

3.1
, we now assume
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
.

We will now study the order in terms of
d
of the expectation:

\mathbb{E}\left[g(h^{(2)})I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right],

(E.47)

where we recall that we denote
S=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1)
. Since
g
is a polynomial, we can no longer apply the orthogonality of different chaos in the same way we did for the linear case in
Equation

E.12
. To compute this, we will use
Lemma

E.4
.

E.4.1
First Step: Applying Gaussian Integration by parts

This section follows the construction made in Chapter 8 in
49
, tailored to our setting. The objective is to derive
Lemma

E.6
. The reader may skip this subsection on a first reading.

Denote
F_{1}=S
,
F_{2}=I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})
, for
j\not=k
. Then, our expectation has the form:

\mathbb{E}\left[g(F_{1})F_{2}\right].

(E.48)

Applying
Lemma

E.4
once, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g(F_{2})F_{1}\right]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(F_{1})\langle DF_{1},-DL^{-1}(F_{2}-\mathbb{E}[F_{2}]\rangle)\right]}_{M:=}+\mathbb{E}[g(F_{1})]\mathbb{E}[F_{2}].

(E.49)

We focus on the first term. Denote

V_{1}=\langle DF_{1},-DL^{-1}F_{2}\rangle.

(E.50)

Let
v_{1}=\mathbb{E}[V_{1}]
, and denote
\bar{V}_{1}=V_{1}-v_{1}
. Then:

\displaystyle M=\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(F_{1})\bar{V}_{1}\right]+\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(F_{1})]v_{1}.

(E.51)

Then, applying
Lemma

E.4
again:

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(2)}(F_{1})\underbrace{\langle DF_{1},DL^{-1}\bar{V}_{1}\rangle}_{V_{2}}\right]+\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(F_{1})]v_{1}.

(E.52)

We now denote
v_{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[V_{2}\right]
, and
\bar{V}_{2}=V_{2}-v_{2}
. Applying
Lemma

E.4
again:

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(2)}(F_{1})\bar{V}_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(2)}(F_{1})\right]v_{2}+\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(F_{1})]v_{1}

(E.53)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(3)}(F_{1})\langle DF_{1},DL^{-1}\bar{V}_{2}\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}[g^{(2)}(F_{1})]v_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}v_{1}.

(E.54)

Iterating
\mathrm{deg}(g)-1
times, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g(h^{(2)})I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(g)-1}\mathbb{E}[g^{(r)}(F_{1})]v_{r}

(E.55)

where we inductively defined

V_{r+1}=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle DF_{1},DL^{-1}(V_{r}-v_{r}))\rangle\right],\quad v_{r}=\mathbb{E}\left[V_{r}\right],

(E.56)

and
V_{0}=I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{j})
. Note that the objects
V_{1},\dots V_{\mathrm{deg}(g)}
are exactly the ones that appear in
Definition

G.1
. This can be made precise. We actually have:

V_{r}=\Gamma_{F_{2},\underbrace{F_{1},\dots,F_{1}}_{r\text{ times}}}.

(E.57)

By
Lemma

G.1
, one can relate the expectation of this variables to cumulants. To be precise, we have that for
r\in\mathbb{N}
:

\kappa_{r}(F_{2},\underbrace{F_{1},\dots,F_{1}}_{r\text{times}})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\{2,\ldots,r\}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma_{F_{2},F_{1},\ldots,F_{1}}(F)\right],

and since the expectation is repeated,
Equation

E.57
allows us to conclude the relation:

\kappa_{r}(F_{2},\underbrace{F_{1},\dots,F_{1}}_{r\text{times}})=r!\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma_{F_{2},F_{1},\ldots,F_{1}}(F)\right]=r!\mathbb{E}\left[V_{r}\right]=r!v_{r}.

(E.58)

Then, we conclude:

Lemma E.6
.

Let
S=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(h^{(2)})^{2}-1)
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial. Then, given
P,Q\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot q}

\mathbb{E}\left[g(h^{(2)})I_{q}(P)I_{q}(Q)\right]=\sum_{r=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(g)}\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(r)}(h^{(2)})\right]\dfrac{1}{r!}\kappa_{r}(I_{q}(P)I_{q}(Q),\underbrace{S,\dots,S}_{r\text{times}}).

Remark E.2
.

This type of result is a generalization of Eq. 8.5.1 in
49
to the multi-variate case. Note that it works for any symmetric tensor.

E.4.2
Step 2: Computing the order of the Cumulants

By
Lemma

E.6
, our problem is reduced to computing cumulants. In particular, we want to derive bounds for

v_{r}=\dfrac{1}{r!}\kappa\left(\underbrace{S,S,\dots,S}_{r},(I_{q}(A_{k})I_{q}(A_{j})-q!\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle)\right).

(E.59)

A key property of cumulants is that they are multi-linear. Since the variable
S=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-1)
, this allows us to exchange this sums with the cumulants. Denote

Y_{i_{\ell}}=(I_{q}(A_{i_{\ell}})^{2}-1),G_{i,j}=I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})-\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle.

By the multi-linearity of the cumulants:

v_{r}=\dfrac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{r}}\kappa\left(G_{j,k},Y_{i_{1}},Y_{i_{2}},\dots,Y_{i_{r}}\right).

(E.60)

By expanding each
Y_{\ell}
into its chaos decomposition:

Y_{i_{\ell}}=I_{q}(A_{i_{\ell}})^{2}-1=\sum_{L=0}^{q-1}c_{q,L}I_{2q-2L}(A_{i}\otimes_{L}A_{i})+(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1),

(E.61)

and for
G_{j,k}
:

I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})-\langle A_{j},A_{k}\rangle=\sum_{L=0}^{q-1}c_{q,L}I_{2q-2L}(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{L}A_{k}).

(E.62)

With this, we can go further with the multi-linearity of the cumulants to obtain:

v_{r}=\dfrac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{r}}\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}c_{q,s,t}\kappa\left(I_{2q-2s_{1}}(A_{i_{1}}\tilde{\otimes}_{s_{1}}A_{i_{1}}),\dots,I_{2q-2s_{r}}(A_{i_{r}}\tilde{\otimes}_{s_{r}}A_{i_{r}}),I_{2q-2t}(A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{t}A_{k})\right),

(E.63)

where we ignored the expectation terms in
Equation

E.61
since they become negligible. Denote
f^{s}_{i}=A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{s}A_{i},i\leq{r}
, and
f^{s}_{r+1}=A_{j}\tilde{\otimes}_{s}A_{k}
so that:

v_{r}=\dfrac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{r}}\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}c_{q,s,t}\kappa\left(I_{2q-2s_{1}}(f^{(s_{1})}_{i_{1}}),\dots,I_{2q-2s_{r}}(f^{(s_{r})}_{i_{r}}),I_{2q-2t}(f^{t}_{r+1})\right).

(E.64)

Now, by
Lemma

G.2
, we can write:

\displaystyle\kappa\left(I_{2q-2s_{1}}(f^{(s_{1})}_{i_{1}}),\dots,I_{2q-2s_{r}}(f^{(s_{r})}_{i_{r}}),I_{2q-2t}(f^{t}_{r+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\{2,\ldots,|m|\}}}(q_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|)})!\sum_{*}c_{q,l,\sigma}(a_{2},\ldots,a_{|m|-1})

(E.65)

\displaystyle\langle(\ldots((f_{i_{\lambda(1)}}\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{2}}f_{i_{\lambda_{\sigma}(2)}})\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{3}}f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(3)})\ldots)\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{|m|-1}}f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|-1)};f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|)}\rangle,

(E.66)

where the second sum runs over combinations of indices having technical conditions (all specified in
Lemma

G.2
. We ignored the upper-indices to avoid overloading the notation. The important message of

E.66
is there is a finite set of possible contractions (in particular, a set of size
O_{d}(1)
), and we are summing over all of them and all possible permutations of indices. Denote

T_{\lambda}=\langle(\ldots((f_{i_{\lambda(1)}}\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{2}}f_{i_{\lambda_{\sigma}(2)}})\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{3}}f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(3)})\ldots)\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{|m|-1}}f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|-1)};f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|)}\rangle.

(E.67)

Thus, we have concluded:

Lemma E.7
.

Let
h^{(2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(h^{(2)})^{2}-1)
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial. Then:

\mathbb{E}\left[g(h^{(2)})I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right]=\sum_{r=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(g)}\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(r)}(h^{(2)})\right]\dfrac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{r}}\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}c_{q,s,t}\sum_{\lambda}T_{\lambda}.

We now make two observations: The first is that

is exactly what we computed for

E.5
. The second one is that, so far, everything has been exact (i.e we have no error terms in our formula). Since we aim only to compute the order of the expectation, we will now proceed to bound each term, beginning with
T_{\lambda}

E.4.3
Bounding
T_{\lambda}

Recall that we defined:

T_{\lambda}=\langle(\ldots((f_{i_{\lambda(1)}}\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{2}}f_{i_{\lambda_{\sigma}(2)}})\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{3}}f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(3)})\ldots)\widetilde{\otimes}_{r_{|m|-1}}f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|-1)};f_{\lambda_{\sigma}(|m|)}\rangle.

(E.68)

In particular, the term
T_{\lambda}
is computed from a particular set of appearances of
A_{1},\dots A_{d_{1}}
. Let
A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{r}}
denote the tensors involved in the computation of a particular
T_{\lambda}
.
We claim the following:

Claim E.1
.

If
\{i_{1},\dots i_{r}\}\cap\{j,k\}=\emptyset
, then with high probability with respect to
A_{1},\dots A_{d_{1}}

\left|T_{\lambda}\right|\lesssim\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}.

Note that symmetrization only change the expectation up to constants, so we can compute the terms without symmetrization. If
\{i_{1},\dots i_{r}\}\cap\{j,k\}=\emptyset
, then the contraction in
Equation

E.68
is linear in
A_{j}
and
A_{k}
, and therefore we can write (without symmetrization):

T_{\lambda}=\sum_{a,b\in[d]^{q}}w(a,b)A_{j,a}A_{k,b},

(E.69)

where
w(a,b)
sums over all the other contractions.
Then, fixing
A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{r}}
, we note that the expectation of
T_{\lambda((i_{1},s_{1}),\dots,(i_{r+1},t))}
w.r.t all
A
is zero. We can also compute the conditional variance to get:

\mathrm{Var}(T_{\lambda}|A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{r}})=\dfrac{1}{d^{2q}}\sum_{a,b\in[d]^{q}}w(a,b)^{2}=\dfrac{1}{d^{2q}}\|w(a,b)\|^{2}.

(E.70)

Since
\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\|w(a,b)\|^{2}\right]=O_{d}(1)
, we can proceed as with did in the linear case and conclude the lemma by the law of total variance. Having this, we get concentration by applying Chebyshev Inequality and Gaussian Hypercontractivity
Lemma

B.3
).
∎

Having dealt with the disjoint case in
E.1
, we now proceed with the harder case where indices
i_{1},\dots i_{r}
may have a non-empty intersection with
\{j,k\}
. Note that we don’t want an sharp bound, but rather a bound that shows that this terms are negligible with respect to the linear part.

Let
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r}
be such that
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\cap\{j,k\}\not=
. Assume that the tensor
A_{j}
appears
m_{j}
times in the sequence
f_{i_{1}},\dots,f_{i_{r+1}}
. Then, necessarily,
m_{j}
has to be odd: It appears once in
f_{i_{r+1}}
, and all other appearances will be tensor product of
A_{j}
with itself. By the same argument,
m_{k}
is also odd. Let
\mathcal{G}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
A_{a_{1}},\dots,A_{a_{l}\ell}
. Then from this observation we conclude:

\mathbb{E}_{A}[T_{\lambda}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[T_{\lambda}|\mathcal{G}_{[r]\setminus\{j,k\}}]]=0.

(E.71)

By computing the conditional variance of
T_{\lambda}
, we will have:

\mathrm{Var}(T_{\lambda}|\mathcal{G}_{[r]\setminus\{j,k\}})=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\lambda}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{[r]\setminus\{j,k\}}\right].

(E.72)

Let
F(A_{j})=T_{\lambda}
. Then, by
Lemma

B.4
:

\mathrm{Var}(T_{\lambda}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{[r]\setminus\{j\}})\leq\dfrac{C}{d^{q}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{j}}\left[\|\nabla_{A_{j}}T_{\lambda}\|^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{[r]\setminus\{j\}}\right].

(E.73)

Now, note that
T_{\lambda}
has the following form:

T_{\lambda}=\mathrm{Contraction}(A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{r}},A_{j},A_{k}).

(E.74)

In particular, it is multi-linear in all of this arguments. Let
D_{H}
be a directional derivative in direction
H
. If we denote by
\mathrm{Pos}(j)
the set of positions such that
A_{i_{1}}=A_{j}
, then:

D_{H}T_{\lambda}=\sum_{p\in\mathrm{Pos}(j)}\mathrm{Contraction}(A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{p-1}},H,A_{i_{p+1}},\dots).

(E.75)

Then, by applying Cauchy-Schwarz:

\left|D_{H}T_{\lambda}\right|\leq C\sum_{p\in\mathrm{Pos}(j)}\|H\|_{2}\prod_{\ell\not=p}\|K_{\ell}\|_{2},

(E.76)

where
K_{\ell}
are all other tensors. Since all these tensors are already contractions, we can apply Cauchy-Schwarz again to get:

\left|D_{H}T_{\lambda}\right|\leq C\sum_{p\in\mathrm{Pos}(j)}\|H\|_{2}\|A_{j}\|^{m_{j}-1}\prod_{\ell\not=j}\|A_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq Cm_{j}\|H\|_{2}\|A_{j}\|^{m_{j}-1}\prod_{\ell\not=j}\|A_{\ell}\|_{2}.

(E.77)

Taking supremum over the sphere, we conclude:

\|\nabla_{A_{j}}T_{\lambda}\|\leq C\|A_{j}\|^{m_{j}-1}\prod_{\ell\not=j}\|A_{\ell}\|_{2}^{m_{\ell}},

(E.78)

and taking the square:

\|\nabla_{A_{j}}T_{\lambda}\|\leq C\|A_{j}\|^{2(m_{j}-1)}\prod_{\ell\not=j}\|A_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2m_{\ell}}.

(E.79)

Finally, by Hanson-Wright we know that
\|A_{j}\|_{2}
is
\Theta_{d}(1)
with high probability. Therefore:

\|\nabla_{A_{j}}T_{\lambda}\|\leq C\prod_{\ell\not=j}\|A_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2m_{\ell}},

(E.80)

and in particular:

\mathrm{Var}(T_{\lambda}^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{[r]\setminus\{j\}})\lesssim\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}\prod_{\ell\not=j}\|A_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2m_{\ell}}.

(E.81)

By the Law of total variance, we can take expectation again to conclude:

\mathrm{Var}(T_{\lambda)})\leq\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}.

(E.82)

Then, by applying Chebyshev Inequality and hypercontractivity, we conclude that with high probability over
A_{1},\dots,A_{d_{1}}

|T_{\lambda}|\lesssim\dfrac{1}{d^{\frac{q}{2}}}.

(E.83)

Let’s write all of this in a Lemma.

Lemma E.8
.

Let
T_{\lambda}
be defined as in
Equation

E.68
, for a
set of tensors
A_{i_{1}},\dots A_{i_{r}}
. Then:

1.

If
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\cap\{j,k\}=\emptyset
, then with high probability
|T_{\lambda}|\lesssim\dfrac{1}{d^{q}}
.

2.

If
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\cap\{j,k\}\not=\emptyset
, then with high probability
|T_{\lambda}|\lesssim\dfrac{1}{d^{\frac{q}{2}}}
.

E.4.4
Conclusion

From
Lemma

E.7

\mathbb{E}\left[g(h^{(2)})I_{q}(A_{j})I_{q}(A_{k})\right]=\sum_{r=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(g)}\mathbb{E}\left[g^{(r)}(h^{(2)})\right]\dfrac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{r}}\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}c_{q,s,t}\sum_{\lambda}T_{\lambda}.

We will separate the linear part from the rest. We write:

Since the sum of

E.5
Studying

The objective of this section is to prove the following Lemma:

Under the assumptions of

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]-\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(h^{2})]}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}A^{(1)}_{i}(A_{i}^{(1)})^{T}\|_{\rm op}=\max_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{B(d,q)},\|B\|_{2}=1}\left|B^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{C}\right]B-\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(h^{2})]}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\langle A_{i},B\rangle^{2}\right|.

Then, going back to

E.5.1
Step 1: Integration by Parts

We will first try to write the term

E.4

Now we apply integration by parts again and obtain:

Now, by the definition of

Step 2: Computation of the Kernels

Then, by Cauchy Schwartz:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{B(d,q)},\|B\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle Dh^{(2)},D\left(\langle Dh^{(2)},DL^{-1}Y_{B}\rangle\right)\right\rangle^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}+o_{d}(1)

denote the

-Wasserstein distance on

. That is, for

,

defines the metric:

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{h:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R},\mathrm{Lip}(h)\leq 1}\left|\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\mu}[h(G)]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\nu}[h(Z)]\right|.

Lemma F.2

(Theorem 5.1.3 in

)
.

with

and

Lemma F.3
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
A^{(1)}_{1},\dots,A^{(1)}_{r}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes q}
be symmetric tensors of order
q
such as the ones specified in
Section

2
. Let
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, and let
H_{k}(x)
denote the degree
k
Hermite tensor of
x
. Define
h^{(2)}
as in
Section

2
. Then, then there exists a constant
C_{k}<\infty
, depending only on
k
, such that:

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(h^{(2)},N\right)\leq C_{k}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}},

(F.14)

where
N\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{E}[h^{(2)}],\mathrm{Var(h^{(2)})})
.

The proof is similar to Lemma A.1 in
58
, but instead of using the multi-variate Gaussian approximation Lemma, we use the one-dimensional version
Lemma

F.2
. We begin by computing the Wiener expansion of
h^{(2)}
. We have:

\displaystyle h^{(2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\left(I_{q}(A^{(1)}_{i})^{2}-1\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}(I_{q}(A_{i})^{2}-\|A_{i}\|^{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1).

(F.15)

By the product formula
Lemma

E.2
, and the fact that the first term is centered:

\displaystyle h^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}I_{2q-2r}(A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1)

(F.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}I_{2q-2r}(c_{q,r}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1).

(F.17)

From here, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[h^{(2)}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\|A_{i}\|^{2}-1),\text{ and}\mathbb{E}\left[(h^{(2)})^{2}\right]=\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}c_{q,r}^{2}\|\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i}\|^{2}_{F}.

(F.18)

Now, we want to apply
Lemma

F.2
. By centering, we have to control
\langle D\bar{h^{(2)}},DL^{-1}\bar{h^{(2)}}\rangle
, for
\bar{h^{(2)}}=h^{(2)}-\mathbb{E}[h^{(2)}]
. Denote
B_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}c_{q,r}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i}
. By the linearity of the derivative:

\displaystyle\langle D\bar{h^{(2)}},DL^{-1}\bar{h^{(2)}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\langle DI_{2q-2r}(B_{r}),DI_{2q-2r^{\prime}}(B_{r^{\prime}})\rangle,

(F.19)

and by applying
Lemma

E.3
:

\displaystyle\langle D\bar{h^{(2)}},DL^{-1}\bar{h^{(2)}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})}c_{q,r,r^{\prime},p}I_{4q-2r-2r^{\prime}-2p}(B_{r}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}B_{r^{\prime}}).

(F.20)

Now, let

K_{s}=\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})}c_{q,r,r^{\prime},p}\mathbf{1}_{4q-2r-2r^{\prime}-2p=s}B_{r}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}B_{r^{\prime}}.

(F.21)

Then:

\langle D\bar{h^{(2)}},DL^{-1}\bar{h^{(2)}}\rangle=\sum_{s\geq 0}I_{s}(K_{s}).

(F.22)

Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathrm{Var}(h^{(2)})-\langle Dh^{(2)},DL^{-1}h^{(2)}\rangle\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 1}\|K_{s}\|^{2}_{F}.

(F.23)

Then to conclude, we need to show that this norms are negligible for large
d
. Let
s\geq 0
. We have:

\displaystyle\|K_{s}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})}c_{q,r,r^{\prime},p}\mathbf{1}_{4q-2r-2r^{\prime}-2p=s}\|B_{r}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}B_{r^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}

(F.24)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathbf{1}_{4q-2r-2r^{\prime}-2p=s}\left\|B_{r}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}B_{r^{\prime}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(F.25)

Recall
B_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}c_{q,r}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{r}A_{i}
. Denote
T^{r}_{i}=A_{i}\tilde{\otimes}{r}A_{i}
. Then:

\displaystyle B_{r}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}B_{r^{\prime}}=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}T^{r}_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}T^{r}_{j},

(F.26)

and:

\displaystyle\|K_{s}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathbf{1}_{4q-2r-2r^{\prime}-2p=s}\sum_{i,j}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\|T^{r}_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}T^{r}_{j}\|_{F}^{2},

(F.27)

so everything reduces to estimating the norms
\|T^{r}_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}T^{r}_{j}\|_{F}
.

Ignoring symmetrization, which only changes things up to constants, given
a,b\in[d]^{q-r}
, we can apply
Lemma

G.4
for
i=j
and
Lemma

G.5
for
i\not=j
. From which we will obtain:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|T^{r}_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}T^{r}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}\right]=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d}\right)\forall i,j\in[d_{1}]

(F.28)

Since
\|T^{r}_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}T^{r}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}
is a polynomial of Gaussians, we can use
Lemma

B.3
to control its moments, and applying Chebyshev we will get:

\|T^{r}_{i}\tilde{\otimes}_{p}T^{r}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}},

(F.29)

with high probability over
A_{1}^{(1)},\dots,A^{(1)}_{d_{1}}
. Replacing in
Equation

F.27
:

\displaystyle\|K_{s}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}C\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathbf{1}_{4q-2r-2r^{\prime}-2p=s}\sum_{i,j}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

(F.30)

Note that, since
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}

\sum_{i,j}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}=Z_{\gamma}^{4}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}i^{-2\alpha}\right)^{2}=\Theta_{d}\left(\dfrac{d^{2(1-2\alpha)}}{d^{4\frac{(1-2\alpha)}{2}}}\right)=\Theta_{d}(1).

(F.31)

From this, we can conclude:

\|K_{s}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\forall s,

(F.32)

with high probability. Finally, this allows us to conclude:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathrm{Var}(h^{(2)})-\langle Dh^{(2)},DL^{-1}h^{(2)}\rangle\right)^{2}\right]=O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(F.33)

with high probability over
A_{1}^{(1)},\dots,A^{(1)}_{d_{1}}
.
∎

Lemma G.4
.

Let
A\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\odot q}
be a symmetric tensor with independent gaussian centered entries with variance
d^{-q}
. Let
r,r^{\prime}\in[q-1]\cup\{0\}
, and let
p\in[2q-2\max(r_{1},r_{2})]
.
Then:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|(A\otimes_{r_{1}}A)\otimes_{r_{3}}(A\otimes_{r_{2}}A)\|^{2}\right]=O\left(\dfrac{1}{d}\right).

Let
M=(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3})
, and
N=4q-2(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3})=4q-2M
. Let
F:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes q}\to(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes N}
be defines by:

F(A)_{x}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}A_{I_{\ell}(x,z)},

(G.1)

where each
I_{\ell}(x,z)\in[d]^{q}
is a
q
-tuple. Given a pair
(x,z)
, let
\pi(x,z)
define a partition of set
[4]
by

a\sim_{\pi(x,z)}b\iff I_{a}(x,z)=I_{b}(x,z).

(G.2)

Denote by
\hat{\pi}
the minimal partition, that is
\hat{\pi}=\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4\}\}
. In this partition, we have
I_{1}(x,z)=I_{2}(x,z)=I_{3}(x,z)=I_{4}(x,z)
. Let

F^{\mathrm{distinct}}(A)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\pi(x,z)=\hat{\pi}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}A_{I_{\ell}(x,z)},

(G.3)

and let

F^{\mathrm{equal}}(A)=F(A)-F^{\mathrm{distinct}}(A)=\sum_{z\in[d]^{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\pi(x,z)\not=\hat{\pi}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}A_{I_{\ell}(x,z)}.

(G.4)

Let
\Phi(x)=\|x\|^{2}
. Then we have:

\phi(F(A))\leq\Phi(F^{\mathrm{equal}}(A))+\phi(F^{\mathrm{distinct}}(A))

(G.5)

Since
\Phi
is convex, we can apply
Lemma

G.3
to obtain:

\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(F^{\mathrm{distinct}}(A))\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(F^{\mathrm{distinct}}(A^{1},A^{2},A^{3},A^{4}))\right],

(G.6)

where
A^{\ell},\ell\in[4]
are independent copies of
A
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(F^{\mathrm{distinct}}(A,B,C,D))\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[d]^{M}}\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in[d]^{N}}\prod_{a=1}^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{I_{a}(x,z)}^{(a)}A_{I_{a}(x,z^{\prime})}^{(a)}\right]

(G.7)

\displaystyle=\lesssim\dfrac{1}{d^{4q}}d^{M+N}=\dfrac{1}{d^{r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}}}.

(G.8)

Since
r_{3}>0
, this term is at most
O(\frac{1}{d})
.
We now move to
F^{\mathrm{equal}}
. Given
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in[4]
, define the sets:

J_{ab}=\left\{(x,z):I_{\ell}(x,z)=I_{\ell^{\prime}}(x,z)\right\},

(G.9)

and

J_{\mathrm{equal}}=\left\{(x,z):\pi(x,z)\not=\hat{\pi}\right\}.

(G.10)

Then, by definition of
\hat{\pi}
:

J_{\mathrm{equal}}\subseteq\bigcup_{a<b}J_{ab}.

(G.11)

We claim that
|J_{a,b}|\leq Cd^{M+N-1}
. To see this, note that a pair
(x,z)
has
M+N
degrees of freedom (
M
from
x\in[d]^{M}
, and
N
from
z\in[d]^{N}
).
Moreover, denote by
m_{a,b}
the number of shared coordinates between
I_{a}(x,z)
and
I_{b}(x,z)
. Then, we have
M+N-m_{a,b}
degrees of freedom. Let’s bound this quantity.

If we look at the function
F
as
F(A^{1},A^{2},A^{3},A^{4})
, then we have that
m_{1,2}=r_{1}
and
m_{3,4}=r_{2}
. The final
r_{3}
contraction further identifies coordinates as a partition:

r_{3}=s_{13}+s_{14}+s_{23}+s_{24},

(G.12)

where
s_{ab}
denotes the number of coordinates that are shared after the contraction
r_{3}
. Since
r_{3}>1
, we have that one of this terms has to be at least
1
.

Note that, since initially the different blocks of the
r_{3}
contraction were not sharing coordinates, we have that
m_{13}=s_{1,3}
,
m_{1,4}=s_{1,4}
,
m_{2,3}=s_{2,3}
and
m_{2,4}=s_{2,4}
. Since

, we have that
\max(m_{12},m_{23})\leq q-1
. Moreover, a cross contraction

cannot be more than the indices that were already contracted in

or

s_{a,b}\leq q-\min(r_{1},r_{2})\leq q-1.

(G.13)

Then we have that

for all

, and consequently,
q-m_{a,b}\geq 1
, and therefore, we cannot have more that

degrees of freedom, which by definition is at most
4q-1
degrees of freedom. As a consequence,
|J_{a,b}|\leq Cd^{M+N-1}
.

. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|F^{\mathrm{equal}}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[d]^{M}}\sum_{z,z\in[d]^{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\pi(x,z)\not=\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}A_{I_{\ell}(x,z)}\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}A_{I_{\ell}(x,z^{\prime})}\right]

(G.14)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{d^{4q}}\left|J_{\mathrm{equal}}\right|

(G.15)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{d^{4q}}\sum_{a<b,a,b\in[4]}|J_{a,b}|,

(G.16)

but as we just saw,

, so we conclude:

EE\left[\|F^{\mathrm{equal}}\|^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{C}{d},

(G.17)

and we conclude the proof.
