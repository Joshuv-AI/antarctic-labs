Access to WASH facilities and health implications of period poverty among in-school girls in rural areas of Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Adeolu J Alabi, Olufunmilayo I Fawole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMenstrual health is essential to adolescent well-being, yet many girls experience period poverty, an inadequate access to sanitary products, water, sanitation facilities, and menstrual education. This scoping review synthesised existing evidence on access to menstrual products, commonly described barriers and reported associations between poor health and period poverty among in-school girls in rural areas of Africa.MethodsThis study was developed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute recommendations and reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist. PubMed, Web of Science and Google Scholar were searched for literature published in English, between 2015 and 2025. The literature must meet the inclusion criterion of being conducted among in-school girls in rural communities in Africa. Extracted data were summarised in an Excel spreadsheet, and descriptive analyses were conducted.ResultsTwenty-one (21) relevant studies were identified, of which 14 were quantitative, four were qualitative, and three were mixed methods studies. The studies were conducted across six countries, with the largest proportion from Kenya (5/21; 23%). Many of the girls were between 10 and 19 years. Period poverty was common among in-school menstruating girls in rural areas of Africa, and it negatively influenced their physical, mental, social and sexual health. Period poverty was linked to body odour, body burns, headaches, fatigue, urogenital symptoms, experience of fear, discomfort, shame, and stress. It can also lead to vulnerability to sexual exploitation, disrupted schooling, poor academic performance and school dropout.ConclusionsPeriod poverty was widespread among in-school girls in rural Africa and linked to negative implications on their health. Integrated interventions that reduce girls’ vulnerability to stigma and sexual exploitation, such as free or subsidised menstrual products, gender-responsive water, sanitation and hygiene facilities, and menstrual education for boys and girls, are urgently required.Keywords: Women, Menstrual hygiene management, Rural areas, Africa, Period poverty, Health implications

Introduction

Menstrual health is the state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being in relation to the menstrual cycle, and not merely the absence of disease or discomfort [1]. Good menstrual health practices include the use of clean menstrual absorbents (sanitary pads, menstrual cups) and hygienic disposal of the menstrual absorbents. It also includes regular changing of materials (ideally every 4–8 h) [2, 3]. Good menstrual health practices ensure that menstruation can be managed with dignity and safety. Schools with female-friendly facilities such as clean menstrual absorbents, privacy, soap, water, hygienic disposal methods, sanitation facilities, supportive environment (including sensitised teachers) and information on menstruation for both girls and boys, promote good menstrual health [4], thereby contributing to better education and health outcomes of young girls [4].

Menstrual health is essential for the well-being of women [4]. As at 2025, more than 500 million women and girls worldwide experience period poverty [5, 6]. Period poverty includes inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities: water, latrine, handwashing facilities, safe disposal or waste management system; poor menstrual health education and services [5]. Globally, the WHO and UNICEF reported that 2.3 billion people lack basic sanitation, and only 27% have handwashing facilities with soap and water [7]. In low-middle-income countries (LMICs), about 50% of the schools lack adequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities, which are crucial for safe menstruation management [4]. Consequently, this results in poor menstrual hygiene management during the menstrual cycle [5]. More than 50% of girls in LMICs have practised poor menstrual hygiene management, with the majority being from rural areas [8]. In many rural African schools, menstrual hygiene management is a critical issue for girls because of cultural, economic, and infrastructural challenges [9]. Period poverty is exacerbated by financial constraints and traditional cultural norms prevalent in the region. The high cost of sanitary pads prevents many rural girls from accessing, purchasing, or consistently using appropriate, hygienic, and comfortable materials [10]. This may lead to the use of unsafe alternatives [10]. Due to cultural norms and beliefs, many girls in the region also feel isolated or impure during menstruation [11].

Poor menstrual hygiene management has substantial implications for the health of girls [8]. Studies have shown that it results in both physical and psychological distress among young girls [1, 12, 13]. Unhygienic menstrual practices increase girls’ susceptibility to infections such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), reproductive tract infections (RTIs), and bacterial vaginosis. These infections are common when absorbents are reused or worn for too long without being changed or washed [13]. The UNICEF & WHO also reported the socio-economic implications of poor menstruation management on girls [7]. Poor access to affordable menstrual products and facilities affects girls’ education. School girls miss school days during menstruation due to shameful feelings from visible stains on clothing, and a lack of private space for menstrual health management [14]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, 50 to 70% of girls miss an average of 1.6–2.1 days of school every month related to menstrual issues [15]. School absenteeism is a pervasive problem with detrimental consequences for girls’ academic achievement [16]. Students who are frequently absent from school are less connected to their classmates, experience reduced participation in classroom activities and interact less with their teachers and peers [17]. Studies have shown that school absenteeism affects girls’ academic development, which negatively influences their educational attainment [18, 19].

There is a dearth of scoping reviews on the accessibility of menstrual products to in-school girls in rural communities of Africa, especially on the health implications of period poverty on these young girls. This scoping review addressed the evidence gap on period poverty and its health implications [10]. The study aimed to synthesise the existing evidence on the accessibility of menstrual products and the health implications linked to period poverty among menstruating girls in rural areas of Africa.

Design and methods

A scoping review was conducted to explore evidence on the accessibility of menstrual products and the health implications of period poverty. This review was developed according to the recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA ScR) checklist [20]. JBI framework outlined the systematic approach to map the existing literature and included the following steps: (1) Determination of review question, (2) Identification of study population, (3) Search strategy for literature, (4) Selection of literature, (5) Data identification, and (6) Data extraction and collation.

Definition of terms

For the purpose of this review, (1) Period Poverty was defined as the lack of access to affordable menstrual products, adequate sanitation facilities, menstrual health education, and supportive social environments needed to manage menstruation safely and with dignity [5]; (2) Menstrual Health was defined as the state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being in relation to the menstrual cycle [4]; (3) Menstrual Hygiene Management was defined as the use of clean menstrual materials to absorb or collect menstrual blood, which can be changed in privacy as often as necessary during menstruation [14]; (4) WASH Access was defined as access to adequate water, sanitation, and hygiene services, including clean water, safe and private toilet facilities, handwashing facilities with soap, and proper waste disposal systems necessary for maintaining menstrual hygiene management [5] and (5) Rural Setting was contextualised based on how the included studies described their study area. Studies were classified as rural if they were conducted in communities, schools, or districts that the authors identified as such.

Step 3: Search strategy for literature

Results

Collation of Studies

Collation of Studies

During the data the identification phase, a total of 3,812 records were retrieved through database searches and reference listing. Database search identified 1,313 records, comprising 1,296 records from PubMed and 17 records from Web of Science. Additional sources retrieved 2,499 records, which included 2,470 records from Google Scholar and 29 records through reference list screening. For PubMed, the search results were sorted by relevance, and the first 130 pages of results were screened. For Google Scholar, results were also sorted by relevance, and the 247 pages of results were screened.

More than half (2,523) records remained after duplicates were removed. In the screening phase, titles and abstracts of all 2,523 records were assessed independently by two reviewers, based on predetermined relevance and eligibility criteria, of which 2,490 records were excluded. Consequently, 33 full-text records were reviewed for eligibility; however, 22 were excluded for various reasons, including being irrelevant (n = 11) and ongoing study (n = 1). In the final inclusion phase, 21 studies were selected for data extraction. Selected studies included 15 quantitative, four qualitative and three mixed-methods studies. There were no disagreements during the title/abstract screening or full-text review stages. Summary of the reviewed literature

As regards, data collection methods, the majority (14/21; 67%) of the studies used quantitative methods. Qualitative methods, including interviews and focus group discussions, were used in 4 studies (19%), while mixed-method approaches were used in 3 studies (14%). Surveys and questionnaires were the most commonly used data collection tools across the studies. The majority (14/21; 67%) of the studies adopted a cross-sectional study design, including analytical cross-sectional studies. Other study designs included randomised controlled trials (2/21; 10%), quasi-experimental, explorative, qualitative narrative, critical emancipatory, and qualitative designs.

The primary population across most studies was adolescent school girls in primary and secondary schools. A few studies also included boys, mixed student populations, and rural school communities. The reported age range of participants varied across studies, with most participants being adolescents, although some (4/21; 19%) studies did not specify participant ages.

Access of menstruating in-school girls in rural Africa to menstrual products

Thirteen of the reviewed papers showed limited access to menstrual products among in-school girls in rural Africa [21–33]. These are cross-sectional and mixed-methods studies conducted among in-school girls in Ethiopia, Tanzania, Kenya, Ghana, Uganda, and South Africa, which reported that a large proportion of girls lacked adequate sanitary products during menstruation [21–33]. For example, a study in Ethiopia showed that girls in some of the schools lacked menstrual hygiene management facilities, including sanitary napkins, a private place to change napkins and an ample water supply [21]. Another study in Ghana showed that, although most of the schools had toilet facilities, they lacked clean water, soap, privacy and dustbins, which were necessary for their menstrual hygiene management [16]. Also, a study in Kenya showed that there was no running water and soap near the latrines for hand washing after using or changing the menstrual materials [23]. It was reported that even where menstrual products were available in schools, access was often unreliable and mediated through school authorities, which discouraged the use of menstrual products [23]. Sanitary pads were sometimes kept in school offices rather than made freely accessible [23, 31].

Physical implications

Four studies reported that period poverty was associated with physical discomfort such as body odour, body burns, fatigue, and urogenital symptoms [24, 32, 34, 35]. For example, a study in Zambia reported that girls complained of friction burns on their inner thighs during their long journey to school due to chaffing of wet non-absorbent material used to make menstrual cloth [32]. Another study in Tanzania showed that during menstruation, some girls had physical: discomfort as a result of soiling from using improper MHM absorbent materials [34], while another study in Gambia found that heavy menstrual bleeding and extreme pain were associated with symptoms of urogenital infection [35].

Social and mental implications

Five studies showed how school girls were emotionally and socially affected [24, 25, 32–34]. These girls experienced fear, anxiety, shame, and stress during menstruation, especially when their menstrual status was known to male peers or teachers [24, 32]. The study in Tanzania showed that girls also experienced psychological disturbances such as anxiety and embarrassment from period poverty. Findings showed that teasing and bullying by boys affected the girls’ emotional well-being, and sometimes resulted in girls’ avoidance of social interactions during menstruation [28, 32]. According to the study in South Africa, the onset of menstruation results in gender inequities that disproportionately affected younger girls, especially those who are menstruating for the first time [36].

Sexual implications

Only one study identified sexual risks associated with period poverty [37]. The study among menstruating girls in rural Kenya found that two-thirds of pad users received the sanitary product from sexual partners. Also, some relied on transactional sex for menstrual products, which was also associated with having multiple partners [37].

Economic implications

Thirteen studies consistently associated period poverty with disrupted schooling, poor academic performance, and increased risk of school dropout [11, 21, 24–26, 28, 32, 33, 36, 38–40]. For example, the study in Zambia reported that girls who suffered from poor menstrual hygiene had reduced school attendance, poor academic performance, and even dropouts [25]. Similarly, Kumbeni study in South Africa reported recurrent absenteeism, reduced concentration in class, and avoidance of participation in class as a result of period poverty [26]. The study in Rwanda showed that more than 50% of girls reported having been absent from school during their menstruation. and that school dropout was common among girls who had experienced teasing and humiliation by classmates when their clothes were stained with blood [33].

Discussion

This scoping review synthesised evidence from 21 primary studies that explored the accessibility to menstrual products and the health implications of period poverty among in-school girls in rural Africa. These primary studies employed qualitative, quantitative, and experimental designs and was conducted across Eastern, Western, and Southern Africa. Evidence showed that period poverty was a challenge among in-school menstruating girls in rural Africa [21, 28], and linked with negative implications for their health [15].

Findings from this review showed that period poverty was highly prevalent among in-school girls in rural areas of Africa, with reports of inadequate access to menstrual products, lack of privacy and unsafe sanitation facilities [21, 26, 38]. This is similar to the findings of Crankshaw (2020) in South Africa, which identified period poverty as a major problem among school girls. In the Northwest of England, a HIC, period poverty was often contextualised around affordability, homelessness, and stigma [41]. This is unsurprisingly due to differences in gross income power relative to the geographical context of this review. In rural areas of Africa, lack of menstrual products often overlaps with poor WASH infrastructure, limited access to pain management, long distances to school, and minimal school support [22, 31]. Consequently, the health and dignity of young girls are compromised.

This review found that period poverty among rural schoolgirls was linked with physical, mental, sexual, and economic health outcomes simultaneously [24, 34, 35]. The negative physical health outcomes included inner thigh burns, headaches, fatigue, heavy bleeding, and symptoms of urogenital infection, which were exacerbated by a lack of water, soap, and private changing spaces [22, 26]. Mental health outcomes such as anxiety, stress, shame, and depressive symptoms were related to fear of leakage/stain, teasing, and menstrual stigma [15]. When girls did not have access to period products during menstruation, they experienced fear and harassment [24, 32]. This corroborates UNICEF global reports on the experience of fear and harassment by girls who had period poverty [42]. Similarly, a study conducted in Nepal reported negative implications of period poverty on physical health. Emotional distress was also linked with period poverty in this region [43]. The pronounced poor physical health of girls observed in rural areas of Africa may be due to structural challenges, which include inadequate WASH facilities, poverty, and limited access to menstrual care or services. These findings suggest that period poverty is pervasive, and consequential where material, infrastructures, and social supports are weak. Addressing period poverty requires a multi-dimensional approach that goes beyond the provision of menstrual products to include gender-responsive WASH infrastructure, comprehensive menstrual health education, pain management, and broader poverty-alleviation strategies.

Beyond the physical and mental distress, this review also highlights the inter-connection between mental, social and economic consequences of period poverty that are experienced by in-school girls. Our reviews reported girls withdrawing from social interaction during menstruation due to fear of being teased, embarrassment from possible leakage, and lack of privacy in school sanitation facilities [23, 29, 32, 34]. The absence of doors, locks, water, soap, clean toilets and disposal bins in school latrines created an environment where girls felt exposed and unsafe while managing their menstruation. As a result, many preferred to remain in solitude, avoid participation in classroom discussions, physical activities, or peer engagement, and be absent from school. The social withdrawal negatively affected their confidence and educational engagement [25, 44]. These findings is similar to the results of a systematic review of qualitative studies in HICs, which found that menstrual stigma and negative social norms caused embarrassment and avoidance of social activities among menstruating adolescents [45]. Another study by Akerman (2024) in Sweden, an HIC, showed how menstrual stigma limited young women’s everyday lives and social relationships, including participation in social activities [46]. Policymakers and practitioners must recognise menstrual health as both a public health and gender equity priority, essential to safeguarding girls’ wellbeing, educational continuity, and human rights.

Sexually high-risk behaviours were associated with period poverty. The sexual dimension of period poverty that is not often discussed. Dependence on male partners for menstrual products increases girls’ vulnerability to coercion, exploitation, multiple sexual partnerships, and exposure to sexually transmitted infections and teenage pregnancy [47]. Therefore, period poverty is not only a hygiene or educational issue, but also a sexual and reproductive health risk concern. The implication of this is that a lack of access to basic menstrual products can promote risky sexual behaviour [47, 48].

A major strength of this scoping review is the comprehensive mapping of evidence across many African countries, different study designs, and multiple outcome domains. The review described the complexity of menstrual health challenges among rural in-school girls by including qualitative, quantitative, and intervention studies. It not only captured access of school girls to menstrual products, but also the interconnected physical, mental, social, economic, and sexual health implications. However, there exist some limitations. The heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures limits direct comparability across studies. Many of the studies relied on self-reported data, which may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias. Also, although the primary population was adolescent school girls, a few studies included boys, teachers, parents, school administrators, policymakers, and wider school communities as study participants. This limitation was addressed by including only studies in which girl-specific findings related to menstrual health, menstrual hygiene management, period poverty, or WASH access were available. Also, the synthesis and interpretation of the findings were primarily focused on adolescent girls. Despite these limitations, the review provides valuable insights for research, policy, and practice. It highlights the multidimensional nature of period poverty in rural contexts and underscores the need for interventions that address access to menstrual products.

Conclusion

Period poverty is widespread among in-school girls in rural areas of Africa and has been linked to negative implications for health, education, dignity, and safety. Yet, there exists limited data on access to menstrual products and the health outcomes of period poverty among girls from areas of Africa. This gap underscores the need for future research to explore access to WASH and long-term outcomes among rural girls in Africa.
