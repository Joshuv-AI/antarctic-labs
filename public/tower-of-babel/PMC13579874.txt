The impact of schema therapy and its basic and mode treatment models for depressive disorders: a systematic review
Authors: Vicky Xu, Robert Brockman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh depression rates persist despite the availability of gold-standard psychological treatments. There is growing research evidence for schema therapy’s effectiveness for depression. However, the adoption of schema therapy in clinical practice has outstripped the pace of research. The most recent model of schema therapy (the mode model) is increasingly being used over the basic model because of its experiential techniques and expansion beyond early maladaptive schemas. This systematic review aimed to explore the impact of schema therapy and its treatment models (basic and mode) on symptom reduction in depressive disorders.MethodA systematic literature search was conducted in PsycINFO, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library on 23 November 2023 and updated on 1 June 2024. Studies were excluded if they used combined therapies, child samples, or lacked a formal depression diagnosis, depressive outcome data, or an English translation. The review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines and study quality was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklists.ResultsSeven studies (N = 580) with a range of methodological designs, outcomes measures, and diagnoses were identified. All studies found that schema therapy had a significantly positive impact on depressive outcomes and large effect sizes pre-post treatment were observed. The studies suggest that schema therapy was non-inferior to established treatments such as CBT and had similar efficacy to short-term psychoanalytic supportive psychotherapy and individual supportive therapy. Included studies also indicated that the dosage of sessions may be important in ensuring significantly large treatment effects.ConclusionsThe literature indicates that schema therapy is effective for reducing depressive symptoms in depressive disorders. The strength of the findings are limited by the modest number of studies and sample sizes therein. However, current evidence lends more support to the mode model’s effectiveness in depressive symptom reduction. Higher schema therapy dosage may elicit pronounced treatment benefits for depressive disorders, even with chronic and comorbid psychopathologies. Comparative RCTs of the different schema therapy models and between schema therapy and CBT are required to advance the evidence base.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO (CRD42023480067).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-03103-2.Keywords: Schema therapy, Depression, Depressive, Systematic review

Depression prevalence and recurrence rates remain high despite the use of first-line psychological treatments. Given the widespread negative implications of depression for individual and societal welfare (e.g., emotional distress, heightened morbidity rates, and absenteeism; 1), exploring other evidence-based psychotherapies is vital [2]. Hence, there has been burgeoning research and clinical interest in schema therapy as a promising avenue for depression treatment. This review focuses on the impact of schema therapy on unipolar depression, which is characterised by low mood, impaired general functioning, and can sometimes have a chronic presentation [3, 4].

Depression has micro and macro level implications on individuals’ quality of life and societal welfare [2], with the combined global economic burden of depression and anxiety accruing to US$1 trillion annually [5]. It has wide reaching ramifications including emotional distress, increased cardiovascular and stroke risks, heightened morbidity rates, rising absenteeism, and declining economic participation [1, 6]. Furthermore, depression can evoke feelings of worthlessness, increased suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts [7, 8]. This is evidenced through 800,000 deaths by suicide annually [5].

Depression remains a leading cause of global health loss affecting 280 million people worldwide, despite the availability of gold-standard treatments such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT; 9, 10). In fact, meta-analytic evidence indicates CBT’s effect sizes for depression have gradually reduced since its inception in the 1960s [11] and that individuals diagnosed with depression in CBT treatment had the highest dropout rates (36%) and relapse rates (31.6%; 12, 13). For front-line psychopharmacology, the past four decades of research indicates that antidepressants are superior to placebo for treating depression, but its small effect size (~ 0.30) is below the threshold for clinically important benefits [14, 15].

Consequently, there has been growing interest in a new wave of therapy models that build upon the established efficacy of CBT, but which aim to better accommodate clients with complexity, chronicity, comorbidity, or are non-responsive to established treatments [16]. One such model that has gained attention in the literature of late is schema therapy [17]. Schema therapy stems from CBT principles and targets chronic problems by focusing on early life experiences that may hinder one’s ability to fulfil valid needs in an adaptive manner [18]. Recent studies highlight its generalisability across diverse demographics from children [19], adults [20], to older adults [21]. It has also demonstrated efficacy for various psychopathologies including eating disorders [22], social anxiety disorder [23], and post-traumatic stress disorder [24]. Furthermore, schema therapy has demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of personality disorders (PD; 25) and psychological comorbidity, whereas CBT has been shown to be less effective for chronic and comorbid psychological presentations [26].

Schema therapy provides two treatment models: basic and mode. The basic model is predicated on altering early maladaptive schemas (EMS), which are entrenched and inflexible belief systems that typically stem from childhood adversity and unmet needs. These EMS can underlie psychopathologies including depression [27]. EMS can lead to an over-anticipation of adverse events (such as rejection and abandonment), which in turn elicits maladaptive coping behaviours including avoidance [28]. Research has demonstrated that depression severity is associated with dysfunctional schemas [29]. Basic model studies for depression have found significant reductions in self-reported suicidal thoughts and depressive symptoms [30, 31]. Clinicians may utilise the basic model in simpler cases, when focused on 1–2 schemas, coping styles, and core emotional needs [32].

The newer ‘mode model’ focuses on schema modes. Modes are transient states consisting of elicited schema clusters and their corresponding emotions, cognitions, and behaviours [33]. Schema modes are categorised into four categories: healthy modes (healthy adult and happy child), maladaptive child modes, maladaptive parent modes and maladaptive coping modes [33, 34]. Individuals may attempt to reduce distress associated with unmet needs via rigid coping strategies, which is posited to maintain depression [35]. There is no singular mode model for depression given that clients with depression have a non-homogeneous presentation of symptomology. Instead, each individual has a specific combination of modes that precipitates and maintains their depression [32]. Clinicians may adopt the mode model for clients with multiple elevated schemas, strong coping styles, multiple therapy goals, or complex presentations [32, 36]. In essence, it is a ‘parts’ model that can account for presentations of multiplicity [32]. Like the basic model, the mode model is predicated upon experimental techniques including guided imagery, limited reparenting, chair work, and imagery rescripting [37]. Schema therapy is reportedly effective for remission from major depressive disorder (MDD) samples with comorbid PD [38].

Early research evidence was predicated upon the basic model wherein EMS were targeted for depressive symptom reduction [30]. The scarcity of mode model research is reflected in a recent bibliometric analysis of 704 quantitative schema therapy studies where approximately half examined EMS whereas only 7% investigated modes [17]. Furthermore, Pilkington and Karantzas [17] identified the most researched mental health outcome was for depression. Therefore, the present review serves as a necessary overview of the fragmented extant literature.

To our knowledge, there has only been one past review with a similar research scope. A meta-analysis by Körük and Özabacı [39] reviewed seven studies and concluded that schema therapy was highly effective for depressive symptom reduction. They also found no significant differences in treatment effects across session dosage and duration, individualist or collectivistic countries, and individual or group treatment. However, the study’s search was limited to a narrow year range spanning 2007–2017. Given Young founded schema therapy in 1990, the current review will perform a wider literature search from inception to comprehensively account for relevant studies. Furthermore, they included mixed treatment studies which integrated neurorehabilitation [40] or motivational interviewing [41], obscuring schema therapy treatment effects. Despite their aim of evaluating schema therapy, one of their included studies [42] did not use this modality and adopted integrative psychodynamic therapy instead. These cumulative limitations undermine their methodological rigour and statistical analyses. Consequently, the literature necessitates a rigorous updated review.

A recent network meta-analysis that investigated third-wave therapies for depression included only one schema therapy paper [43]. This underscores the scarcity of high-quality schema therapy research. A recent Delphi study identified that establishing schema therapy’s effectiveness across different clinical presentations was a priority [44]. Increased schema therapy adoption and particularly mode work in clinical practice appears to be outstripping its research pace [17]. There is currently no known comparison of the efficacy of schema therapy models for depression [17].

The purpose of the present review is to investigate if schema therapy is effective at reducing depressive symptoms for people diagnosed with a depressive disorder and whether this differs across the basic or mode model. The current review is situated to be the most rigorous and updated review of this domain. This review also aimed to identify practical insights and research implications to further advance effective psychological treatments targeting depression.

Method

Study protocol

The protocol was registered and published in PROSPERO (CRD42023480067); 4 changes were made, and their justifications are recorded. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) was adopted to uphold the validity and integrity of research conducted and reported. Ethics exemption on the basis of a systematic review with no data collection was obtained from the University of Technology Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee.

Literature search

Searches were undertaken in the electronic databases (PsycINFO, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library) for papers published in peer-reviewed journals on the impact of schema therapy on depression outcomes in depressive disorders. The literature search was conducted on the 23rd of November 2023 and an updated search done on the 1st of June 2024. Search strategies were constructed for each database in consultation with university research librarians (see Appendix A).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The search was conducted from inception with no restrictions on publication dates. Studies were only included if they investigated the effect of schema therapy on a diagnosed depressive disorder (ICD or DSM) in adult populations and had quantitative data to measure treatment outcomes. Studies were excluded if: participants were children (under 18-years-old) or had no formal diagnosis of a depressive disorder (DSM or ICD criteria); there was no measure of depressive outcomes or quantitative data to indicate treatment change; emotional schema therapy or schema therapy was used concurrently with another psychotherapy; and no English translation was available.

Screening

The search generated 598 studies with 282 duplicates being automatically removed within Endnote and Covidence. Another 58 duplicates were identified manually. The remaining 258 studies were independently screened by two reviewers at the title and abstract stage; there was substantial interrater reliability (κ = 0.64). The remaining 38 studies at full text stage were screened independently by a reviewer. Each study was then discussed together by the reviewers to confirm the screening decision. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved between both reviewers. Backward citation searching of prominent full text studies was conducted and produced no additional results.

Data extraction and study quality assessment

The first author extracted the following information from included studies: 1) author, 2), year, 3) journal, 4) demographics, 5) diagnostic criteria, 6) treatment characteristics, 7) schema therapy model, 8) depressive symptom measures, and 9) quantitative outcomes. The quality of the studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools [45].

Quality assessment

Overall, the general quality of studies was moderately high as assessed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools [45]; see Table 1. The studies ranged from moderate (6/10) to high quality (10/10). When averaged, the case series (9.5/10), case report (6/8), and quasi-experiment (7/8) studies were of high quality. Based on the quality assessment, improvements could include adding control groups for quasi-experiments, consecutive participant inclusion for case series reliability, and clearer diagnostic proces for case reports. RCTs were assessed as moderate quality (M = 7.33/10) since they all measured outcomes uniformly for treatment groups and follow-up and had either no non-differential drop-out rates across conditions, no attrition, or intention-to-treat analysis.

There were a total of n = 580 participants, ranging from 1 to 229 (M = 82.86; SD = 91.06). Reported ages spanned 18–65 years. Most studies had mixed-sex samples, aside from two being solely women. Ethnicity and nationality reporting was inconsistent; however, all studies were conducted in Western countries. All included studies had psychological comorbidities, including personality disorders (n = 5) and anxiety disorders (n = 2) or clinically significant anxiety symptoms (n = 2). Most included participants that were taking antidepressants during treatment (n = 4).

Schema treatment characteristics

One study [18] adopted the basic model and the remaining six studies utilised the mode model. Comparative treatments included CBT (n = 2), individual supportive therapy (IST; n = 1), and short-term psychoanalytic supportive psychotherapy (SPSP; n = 1). Treatment duration ranged from 22 to 75 sessions. Most treatments were conducted in outpatient individual settings (n = 5) with others in predominately group outpatient format (n = 1) or mixed inpatient and day clinic with combined individual and group sessions (n = 1).

Outcome measures

Depressive outcomes were measured using widely-adopted psychometric tools including the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II; n = 5) [59], Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS; n = 1) [60], Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory (MCMI-III; n = 1) [61], and Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD24; n = 1) [62].

Diagnoses

Diagnoses included MDD based on DSM-IV [63] and DSM-IV TR criteria (n = 2) [64], Chronic Depression (DSM-IV; n = 2), Depression (ICD-10 or DSM-IV; n = 2) [65], Persistent Depressive Disorder (DSM-V; n = 1) [66] and dysthymia (DSM-IV; n = 1). Most studies (n = 5) used the gold standard diagnostic method of structured clinical assessment [67].

Impact of schema therapy on depression symptoms

Despite methodological variability, all seven studies unanimously found that schema therapy produced a beneficial effect on depressive outcomes; while comparative studies demonstrated its non-inferiority or comparability to alternative psychotherapies.

Three studies reported large effect sizes pre-post treatment, concluding that schema therapy led to significant decreases in depressive symptoms (d = 1.30–2.91) [48, 50, 51]. Three comparative RCTs found schema therapy either produced outcomes that were not significantly different from (d = 0.08) [18] or non-inferior to the gold-standard CBT (B = 0.21; p = 0.73) [47]; was not statistically different to short-term psychoanalytic supportive psychotherapy (SPSP; B = − 2.18 × 10− 3, p = 0.686) [46]; and non-superior to individual supportive therapy (IST; B = 0.35; p = 0.57) [47].

Schema therapy models for depression outcomes

Basic model

Carter et al.’s [18] RCT with an outpatient MDD sample was the only basic model study. Despite higher PD comorbidity in the schema therapy group than CBT post-randomisation; the increased complexity of presentations did not hinder its efficacy. Treatment was based upon Young’s treatment manuals and workshops; a maximum of 30 sessions were delivered over 12 months. Both treatments successfully achieved similar recovery rates (average percentage change of 50% on the MADRS; 38% CBT and 34% schema therapy), and similar remission rates (34% schema therapy; 28% CBT). However, it found no significant differences in depressive outcomes between modalities (between treatment effect sizes of 0.064 and 0.08 respectively for MADRS and BDI-II). Follow-up data on sustained treatment gains between therapies was lacking.

Mode model

Since Carter et al.’s [18] publication, all research which satisfied selection criteria examined the mode model (n = 6) and had mostly high-quality assessment ratings across various methodologies spanning case studies to RCTs. Their contributions across time to the schema therapy evidence base for depressive outcomes are detailed below in chronological order due to a lack of study subgrouping and pattern emergence.

Malogiannis et al.’s [50] single case-series examined 12 outpatient women with chronic depression (DSM-IV), antidepressant usage, and high PD comorbidity (58.33%). They conducted 60 individual therapy sessions over 20 months, with a focus on limited reparenting and experiential work. Using mixed regression analysis, results indicated large effect sizes for reduced depression symptoms post-treatment (d = 2.01; HRSD24), which were sustained at follow-up (d = 2.24). No significant symptom reduction was observed during the baseline 8-week wait-period, suggesting symptom changes were attributable to treatment. The results of Malogiannis et al.’s [50] study is limited by its small sample size and comorbidity in presentations as this does not isolate schema therapy’s impact on depression. Nonetheless, the results may provide clinical value because comorbidity is more prevalent in practice than depicted in research [68].

A naturalistic pre-post study by Skewes et al. [48] provided both quantitative and qualitative insights. All 6 outpatients had comorbid MDD (DSM-IV TR) and PD. They found 20 weekly group sessions across 5 months and 5 individual sessions produced large effect sizes for depression symptoms (d = 2.91 pre-post; d = 2.44 pretreatment to follow-up). All participants no longer met criteria for MDD post-treatment, this was sustained at follow-up. Qualitative feedback revealed treatment normalised and actively challenged schemas, facilitated self-expression and self-disclosure, behavioural change motivation, and reduced inhibition.

Renner et al. [51] conducted a multiple-baseline single case series with 25 outpatient adults with chronic MDD (for at least 2 years via DSM-IV; 80% female sample). Their design advanced upon prior studies including Malogiannis et al. [50], by increasing their sample size and number of active sessions. Study outcomes indicated that schema therapy produced a significantly large effect on depressive symptoms when compared to the 6–24 weeks control no-treatment stage and 12-week schema exploration phases (d = 1.30; BDI-II), hence outcomes were attributed to the active therapy phase (p = 0.003) rather than the effect of time. Treatment benefits included a 40% positive treatment response, 35% remission rate, and 67% of participants no longer met diagnostic criteria for depression.

A moderate quality case study conducted by Herts and Evans [49] was also included in the review. Although the statistical significance for outcomes was not calculated, they discovered that 22 sessions contributed to significant reductions in depressive symptoms (73% decrease on BDI-II) in an older Caucasian female with PDD for 40 years. Self-reported qualitative outcomes included reduced depressive rumination, decreased low mood and increased hope and self-efficacy to manage psychological health. Recent case studies demonstrated the application of schema therapy across various depressive disorder presentations, although these involved small sample sizes.

A recent RCT by Kopf-Beck et al. [47] found schema therapy was clinically non-inferior to CBT and non-superior to IST for clients with a primary diagnosis of depression, high level of antidepressant usage (94.52%), and high anxiety disorders comorbidity (60%). A maximum of 28 sessions saw improved outcomes on depressive symptoms across all 3 treatments (p < 0.001; BDI-II). Treatment was a time-intensive seven-week course of bi-weekly day clinic and inpatient treatment that included both individual and group sessions. Notably, there was an interaction effect of schema therapy and CBT differences scores across weeks (B = 0.54, p = 0.04), and BDI-II scores post-treatment but not follow-up. The RCT used multimodal symptom assessment to compare the efficacy of schema therapy with established treatments such as CBT. This suggests a short-term advantage for CBT in time-intensive treatments.

Another recent pragmatic RCT by Kool et al. [46] compared the mode model against SPSP for dosage effects in adult outpatients with depression or dysthymia (DSM-IV) and PD comorbidity. Compared to the 25-session condition, 50-sessions produced significantly reduced depression scores (p = 0.004; d = 0.44; 4.7 points lower on BDI-II) with a further reduction a year later (mean difference of 5.6 BDI points lower; d = -0.53, p < 0.003). This dosage effect was also observed for SPSP (p = 0.004); however, schema therapy and SPSP did not differ in their effects for reduced depression outcomes (p = 0.686). The 50-session arm also led to increased remission from depression (p = 0.025), PD (p = 0.010), and from both PD and depression (p = 0.003) than 25-sessions. Insufficient statistical power prevented an examination of the standalone efficacy of schema therapy or its equivalence to SPSP.

Discussion

This systematic review sought to evaluate the impact of schema therapy in reducing depressive symptoms among individuals diagnosed with a depressive disorder, and to explore whether the impact was different between the basic and mode models. Seven studies met the inclusion criteria and were methodologically heterogenous in terms of study design, sample size and demographic characteristics, and statistical methods. The present review also aimed to identify clinical insights and research implications to inform and progress effective psychological treatments for depressive disorders.

All studies were in accordance with schema therapy’s effectiveness on reducing depressive symptoms in depressive disorders; with some studies establishing its efficacy being similar to other modalities such as SPSP and IST, alongside non-inferiority to the gold-standard CBT. Only one study assessed the basic model of schema therapy. The lack of basic model studies which met the study selection criteria hinders a fair and comprehensive comparison of the differential efficacy between models. Both models appear effective in reducing depression outcomes although future research is required delineate differential efficacy. However, the mode model is the most supported by empirical evidence currently. The following section discusses this review’s findings on the effects of schema therapy on depression and outlines potential clinical and research implications.

Schema therapy impacts on depressive symptoms in depressive disorders

The present review concludes there is a good level of evidence for schema therapy effectiveness for depressive disorders across various contexts (e.g., inpatient and outpatient settings) and a similar efficacy to other psychotherapies, albeit not necessarily superior. There were also large effect sizes in highly comorbid and chronic depressive samples. The literature points to several mechanisms that may explain why schema therapy reduces symptoms of depression, such as schema therapy’s ability to target psychopathologies at the deepest cognitive level with EMS and modes [34]. Through cognitive techniques, individuals learn to prove their schemas false on a logical level [35]. However, schema therapy extends upon cognitive therapy principles and provides experiential techniques such as imagery rescripting and chair work; this enables individuals to have dialogue with schema modes to challenge and alter them on an emotional level and have corrective and adaptive experiences to fulfil basic emotional needs [41, 69]. Originally developed to treat chronic and treatment-resistant psychopathologies, schema therapy may be effective for chronic depression presentations. This may be due to its ability to address early childhood adversity and higher levels of EMS associated with chronic depression, via experiential techniques such as imagery rescripting to target dysfunctional schemas and emotionally process events in a safe space [35]. Furthermore, limited reparenting within the therapeutic relationship is an active treatment component that facilitates corrective emotional experiences, nurtures schema and mode change, and builds adaptive coping behaviours [33]. These mechanisms might explain schema therapy’s unique effectiveness.

Dosage effect

Kool et al.’s [46] recent RCT findings provide evidence of a dosage effect, even when controlling for initial depression severity. Although 25 sessions may produce symptom reduction, 50 sessions may be ideal to maximise recovery. This dosage effect is exhibited in Renner et al.’s [51] average number of 53 sessions and Malogiannis et al.’s [50] 60 session protocol. The other selected studies ranged from 22 to 30 sessions. The pragmatic nature of Kool et al.’s [46] RCT promotes generalisability via its broader eligibility criteria for comorbidity and antidepressant usage. However, the largest effect size was identified in Skewes et al. [48] which had 20 weekly group sessions and 5 individual sessions for a comorbid PD and depression sample. This can also be explained by the group therapy format being more effective than individual therapy only for PD samples [70]. Hence it is inappropriate to compare this unique group therapy structure with the individual therapy format of other studies. More RCTs that compare the number of sessions across different settings (such as individual or group therapy formats) are needed to further understand the impact of session dosage on depressive symptom reduction.

Surprisingly, Körük and Özabacı [39] did not find a dosage effect within their meta-analysis but noted they had insufficient data on sustained depressive outcomes to establish long-term benefits. The present review may have discovered a dosage effect in light of updated studies (predominately with the mode model) with moderately-high quality (JBI quality assessment), and a more rigorous selection criteria with an inclusive timeframe. Whereas Körük and Özabacı [39] lacked a quality assessment and their aforementioned methodological limitations impede the strength of their statistical conclusions.

The dosage effect may partly explain why schema therapy was observed to be non-superior to CBT in reducing depressive outcomes. Notably, the two RCTs by Kopf-Beck et al. [47] and Carter et al. [18] had a maximum of 28 [47] and 30 sessions [18], respectively. This is below the 50 session ideal for significant depressive symptom reduction [46]. Carter et al. [18] did not use the strongly researched mode model and treatment dosage likely needed to be higher for its majority chronic and/or recurrent depression sample, as chronic depression is associated with poorer psychotherapy response [71]. Kopf-Beck et al. [47] found schema therapy was non-inferior to CBT and similar but non-superior to IST. However, treatment was conducted in an atypical, compressed 7-week timeframe. This is not conducive to emotional ventilation and deeper schema change, which has been shown to take time due to ingrained EMS and modes [72].

Basic vs. Mode model for depressive disorders

Despite the basic model being the first model, only one was captured post-selection. Hence, there is currently insufficient high-quality basic model research to draw a fair and comprehensive conclusion on the differential efficacy between models. Notwithstanding this, Carter et al. [18] presents promising evidence for the basic model by finding schema therapy produced comparative effects to the gold-standard CBT for depressive symptom reduction. Assumptions regarding more research being available for the basic model may be countered by schema therapy practice outstripping research [17] and an increased attention in third-wave psychotherapies recently which coincided with the mode model’s introduction [73]. Within the selected studies, the oldest study sourced is Carter et al. [18] which remains relatively recent considering schema therapy research began growing in 1990s [32]. Whereas the remaining selected studies were published around the past decade, which reflects the adoption of the newer mode model within clinical practice [17].

Another reason for the lack of basic model papers may be due to older schema therapy studies having poorer research rigour, below the methodological standards of a systematic review and therefore not meeting inclusion criteria for this review or past reviews. Carter et al. [18] had a moderate quality rating which was the lowest amongst selected studies; due to factors including the treatment groups being different (there was a higher PD rate in the schema therapy group). Similarly, Körük and Özabacı’s [39] meta-analysis also found only one basic model study conducted by Rashidi and Rasooli [30] which had limitations of different baseline symptom severity across conditions and lacked methodological detail to establish internal validity. Hence, this study was not detected by the current review as it was not indexed in selected peer-review journals (a further quality indicator). There appears to be a pattern of lower quality methodology in these older research articles which coincides with the basic model being the first model of schema therapy.

Conversely, the mode model was the most researched and exhibited strong effect sizes for depressive symptom reduction, alongside a range of methodological designs including two recent high-quality RCTs [46, 47]. The results suggest that both the basic and mode model are effective for depressive symptom reduction even when compared to other gold standard therapies, however there is a stronger evidence base for the mode model and further higher quality research is required for basic model studies to conduct a fair comparison.

Dodo bird effect

The similarities in improved depressive outcomes across schema therapy, CBT, IST, and SPSP is reminiscent of the Dodo bird effect within psychology [74]; wherein all psychotherapies generate similar outcomes due to shared factors such as therapeutic rapport [75]. This finding mirrors Carter et al.’s [18] conclusions and past comparative studies on different psychotherapies for depression [76–78]. Moreover, there are similarities between schema therapy, IST, and SPSP regarding a focus on childhood experiences, emotional needs, the therapeutic alliance, and emotional ventilation [47]. On the other hand, a range of studies that assess the outcomes of various psychotherapies for PD have found schema therapy is superior to transference-focused psychotherapy, clarification-oriented psychotherapy, and treatment-as-usual for PD symptoms and secondary outcomes of depressive symptom reductions [25, 79]. Hence there may be an insufficient number of studies examining third wave therapies such as schema therapy with a primary depression focus in comparison to the plethora of CBT research [80]. Future research to remedy identified methodological shortcomings are detailed below.

Comorbidity with personality disorders

Majority (5 of 7) of the selected studies featured PD comorbidity. This mirrors the 50% comorbidity rate observed for PD and MDD [81]. Given both PDs and depression are highly comorbid and exhibit rigid coping modes that maintain psychopathology, these commonalities can be effectively targeted by schema therapy [32]. There has been substantial research demonstrating schema therapy’s effectiveness for PDs [82–84] and Level I evidence for BPD [85]; Level I evidence is the highest quality evidence derived from high-quality RCTs, prospective studies, or systematic reviews of such studies [86]. RCT evidence suggests depressive outcomes also improve in samples with comorbid PD and depression receiving schema therapy [25]; however these presentations are associated with higher psychosocial impairments, therefore a higher session dosage is recommended [87]. Hence schema therapy may be a promising option for PD and depression comorbidity.

Clinical implications

This review bolsters clinician confidence in using schema therapy as an evidence-based modality for depression treatment. Clinicians may consider this modality, especially when working with individuals that are non-responsive to first-line treatments, have chronicity, and/or comorbidity [49]. For clinicians seeking to adopt schema therapy as a treatment modality they should consider interactive schema training as studies have found that roleplaying and skills practice produced positive benefits for treatment effects, when compared to traditional learning methods including lectures [18, 87]. Hence an interactive approach should be considered for continued psychology professional development and coursework, especially to facilitate proficiency in experiential exercises underpinning the mode model.

The dosage effect suggests the Australian Medicare Model of Mental Health Care Plans (MHCP) provision of 10 sessions may be insufficient for schema therapy to generate meaningful clinical change [46, 88, 89]. All selected studies exceeded 10 sessions, highlighting treatment effects are produced with a higher dosage currently supported by MHCPs. Policymakers should consider increasing the flexibility and number of rebated sessions to improve access to evidence-based psychotherapy for clients with chronic and complex psychopathologies and those non-responsive CBT [90].

Limitations and future research directions

A key limitation was the small number of included studies, and within them a scarcity of effect size computations and heterogeneity of designs and measures, which rendered a meta-analysis unfeasible. An updated and rigorous meta-analysis would provide the highest level of evidence. During screening, there were 10 ongoing schema therapy empirical studies (see Fig. 1); many were pre-registered RCTs examining depressive disorders (e.g., 91) or comparative studies to other modalities (e.g., 92). Therefore an updated review is recommended to capture this flourishing research.

The lack of a separate control group hinders schema therapy from advancing beyond Level II evidence for depression to Level 1 evidence, as defined by the National Health and Medical Research Council and adopted by the Australian Psychological Society [85, 86]. Large-scale RCTs with control groups and long-term follow-up are required to demonstrate sustained efficacy. Replication studies, comparative effectiveness trials, acceptability and cost-effectiveness should be evaluated to support the growing evidence base demonstrating schema therapy’s utility for depression. A future meta-analysis should then capture these developments.

Small sample sizes in selected studies, including the three RCTs, were insufficiently powered to detect comparative treatment differences to establish superiority (i.e., 46). A future comparative RCT needs to be adequately powered to detect superiority with methodology appropriate for schema therapy. This entails adopting the well-researched mode model and longer-term treatment to ascertain an appropriate dosage [46]. Given the knowledge gap in differential effectiveness of schema therapy models, it is not possible to conclude with certainty the mode model’s effectiveness over the basic model to a strong degree. Hence, further research involving well-designed RCTs comparing the basic and mode model for depressive disorders would yield high-quality evidence to guide treatment.

Although large effect sizes were reported in studies using the mode model, two of the three studies that had effect size computations had high treatment dosage, ranging from 60 to 75 sessions [50, 51]. Therefore, further research is needed to disentangle the effects of treatment model and dosage to clarify their respective contributions to treatment outcomes. Most of the literature and included studies did not specify their adopted model. Only two included studies explicitly referred to the model used in their treatment approach within the study methodology [48, 50]. Standardised reporting would facilitate secondary research and differentiate model efficacy across psychopathologies.

Notably, all selected studies were conducted in Western and developed countries; which is a common methodological shortfall within psychological research [93]. Emergent qualitative research suggests cultural adjustments must be considered to deliver schema therapy in a culturally sensitive manner [94]. Future research should explore the cross-cultural effectiveness of schema therapy in diverse populations.

There is an overrepresentation of women in psychological research generally [38, 49–51]. This suggests that males, who often experience higher mental health stigma and lower health literacy, may be less likely to seek treatment [41]. Similarly, in the present review of seven selected studies, the samples of the identified studies were disproportionately female as five studies had a majority of female participants. This disparity hinders our understanding of whether differences in treatment modalities affect male populations’ responses to therapy.

Moreover, future research should consider measuring schema change outcomes by using tools including the Young Schema Questionnaire [95] and the Schema Mode Inventory [96] alongside depressive outcomes to gauge treatment benefits specific to schema processes. Since early maladaptive schemas are found to contribute to chronic depression [50], regularly assessing client schema and mode activation and changes could assist in a stronger understanding of the common schemas and mode underlying depression and how they respond to various cognitive and experiential techniques.

This review has identified common methodological deficiency in prior research concerning not conducting or reporting a formal clinical diagnosis (e.g., 97, 98). This is essential for accurately assessing efficacy in clinical samples versus subclinical populations. There’s a pressing need for empirical designs to recruit participants diagnosed with depressive disorders based on widely accepted criteria (such as the DSM and ICD) to establish baseline severity. This standardisation will enhance the interpretation and generalisability of findings and support secondary research.

There are studies indicating schema therapy techniques are effective for PTSD samples non-responsive to CBT approaches such as prolonged exposure [99]. Hence, it would be of clinical interest to then conduct comparative therapies across different depression diagnoses (e.g. MDD, dysthymia) and samples (e.g. have relapsed after CBT or are resistant to CBT) to differentiate efficacy across diagnoses.

Conclusion

There is comprehensive and burgeoning support for schema therapy as an effective alternative treatment for depression, and one that is comparably noninferior to gold standard and established psychotherapies. This is reflected in the variety of research methodologies revealing high-quality evidence for the mode model and emerging dosage effects. Considering depression’s prevalence, quality of life impediments, relapse rates, impacts at the individual and societal level, it is essential to consider schema therapy as part of a diverse therapeutic toolkit for evidence-based depression interventions. Schema therapy offers a distinctive approach and strength by targeting long-standing core schemas which may provide longer-term outcomes than CBT. Additionally, it facilitates the exploration of the root and underpinnings of challenges faced by clients, as opposed to a short-term present-focused therapy modality such as CBT. Continued investment into future clinical, research, and mental health policy initiatives are warranted to further evaluate and refine schema therapy’s standing as a strong modality for depression treatment.
