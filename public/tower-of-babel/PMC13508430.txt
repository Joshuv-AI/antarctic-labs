Traditional wisdom unveiled: an ethnobotanical exploration of medicinal bath plants for postpartum and infant healthcare among the Hakka people in Guangdong, China
Authors: Donglin Li, Zhicheng Wu, Shuying Lv, Yuelan Meng, Zhendong Xie, Chongjian Ma, Xilong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedicinal baths are an important component of traditional Chinese medicine and are widely used for postpartum recovery, neonatal care, and general health maintenance. Although medicinal bathing practices among ethnic groups such as the Yao and Tibetan have been relatively well documented, ethnobotanical research on Hakka medicinal baths in Guangdong remains limited, despite their widespread use among postpartum women and newborns.MethodsField investigations were conducted in 19 Hakka settlements in northern Guangdong from 2023 to 2024. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, individual consultations, and field observations involving 138 Hakka informants. Fidelity Level (FL) and Informant Consensus Factor (FIC) were used to evaluate the cultural importance of medicinal bath plants and the degree of agreement among informants.ResultsA total of 108 medicinal bath plant species were documented, most of which were herbaceous. The most frequently used plant parts were the whole plant, leaves, and stems. Knowledge of medicinal bath practices was mainly concentrated among older women, who play a central role in postpartum and neonatal care. These plants were reported to be used for the prevention and management of more than 30 health conditions. FIC values ranged from 0.61 to 0.87, with the highest agreement observed for postpartum recovery, respiratory diseases, and skin diseases. Ten plant species had a Fidelity Level (FL) of 100%, indicating high cultural recognition and importance. Compared with the medicinal bath practices of neighboring Yao communities, Hakka practices are characterized by milder remedies, simpler formulations, and a stronger emphasis on family-based healthcare, particularly for mothers and infants.ConclusionHakka medicinal baths reflect intergenerational knowledge transmission, practical healthcare strategies, and culturally embedded healing practices. This study systematically documents the medicinal bath plants used by the Hakka in Guangdong and provides a basis for the conservation of traditional knowledge and the sustainable use of medicinal plant resources. The findings also highlight the relevance of Hakka medicinal bath practices in postpartum care, infant healthcare, and community-based complementary healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05432-0.Keywords: Hakka, Medicinal bath, Ethnobotany, Traditional knowledge

Introduction

Chinese herbal bath therapy is an important component of traditional Chinese medicine and has a long history as a form of complementary and alternative medical practice [1]. In medicinal bath practices, selected herbal materials are added to water, decocted for an appropriate duration, and the resulting solution is used for bathing at a suitable temperature [2]. Medicinal baths have been widely used in postpartum recovery [3], neonatal care [4], and management of conditions such as colds [5], rheumatism [6], and skin-related conditions [7]. It represents a distinctive healthcare practice closely associated with the properties of medicinal plants and is used for disease prevention, treatment, and general health maintenance [8, 9].

Medicinal baths are considered to offer several advantages. First, the active components of medicinal materials may act directly on affected external areas, which may be particularly relevant in the treatment of skin diseases [10]. Second, during bathing, the herbal solution and the thermal stimulation of water may help promote blood circulation and support general physiological functions [2, 11–14]. Third, as a topical practice, medicinal bathing avoids oral administration and may therefore reduce gastrointestinal irritation associated with some other treatment routes. These characteristics are often regarded as making medicinal baths a relatively safe and acceptable therapeutic option compared with some other treatment methods [15–17]. In recent years, a series of ethnobotanical studies have examined medicinal bath plants used by Yao and Tibetan communities [2, 9, 18, 19].

The term “Hakka” literally means “guest families”. The Hakka people are generally regarded as a subgroup of the Han Chinese, with a long history of southward migration and settlement in southern China [20]. At present, more than 45 million Hakka people are estimated to live in China, mainly distributed across provinces such as Guangdong, Fujian, Jiangxi, Guangxi, Hainan, Taiwan, Sichuan, and Hunan [10], with Guangdong representing one of the most important settlement areas. The Hakka people have developed distinct cultural traditions, including their own dialect, customs, and healthcare practices [21–24]. Living predominantly in mountainous and hilly environments, they have maintained close interactions with local plant resources and gradually accumulated practical knowledge of medicinal plant use [25]. This knowledge is deeply embedded in daily family life, particularly in preventive care, postpartum recovery, and infant healthcare. Herbal teas, medicinal baths, and medicinal soups constitute the main forms of medicinal plant use among the Hakka, reflecting a household-based healthcare system that differs from those of other regional groups such as Cantonese and Hoklos communities.

Current ethnobotanical research on the Hakka has focused mainly on medicinal soups. For example, Luo et al. [26] documented 42 medicinal plant species used for soup making among the Hakka in western Fujian. Ding et al. [27] documented 83 soup-making plants used by the Hakka in Guangdong, while 97 Hakka medicinal plants were reported in Ganzhou [28]. However, ethnobotanical studies focusing on medicinal bath practices among the Hakka remain very limited, despite the fact that medicinal baths are regarded as a fundamental form of care for postpartum women and newborns. Medicinal baths are widely practiced among Hakka postpartum women and newborns, often for up to one month after childbirth. These practices are traditionally believed to support neonatal health and alleviate conditions such as jaundice, while also helping mothers recover from postpartum discomfort. In local cultural understanding, this includes the removal of “wind,” a traditional concept referring to pathological factors associated with weakness or imbalance after childbirth, which is believed to adversely affect maternal health if not properly managed [10].

The Hakka people have accumulated rich and valuable practical experience in medicinal bath practices for postpartum women and newborns, yet systematic documentation of this knowledge remains limited. Despite the richness of this traditional knowledge, ethnobotanical studies focusing specifically on Hakka medicinal bath practices remain limited. Moreover, with ongoing modernization and lifestyle changes, this traditional knowledge is at risk of gradual erosion. Therefore, there is a clear need for the systematic documentation of medicinal bath plants used for postpartum and infant healthcare among the Hakka in northern Guangdong. Accordingly, the present study aimed to document the medicinal bath plants used by the Hakka in Guangdong in order to preserve and transmit this valuable traditional knowledge. In addition, this study seeks to provide ethnobotanical evidence for understanding the role of traditional medicinal baths in complementary postpartum and infant healthcare.

Methods

Study area

Guangdong Province is known for its rich plant diversity, with more than 6,000 native species and approximately 5,000 introduced species recorded [29]. Of these, approximately 800 species are reported to have medicinal uses [30]. Guangdong has a humid subtropical climate (tropical in the far south), characterized by short, mild, and relatively dry winters and long, hot, and humid summers [10, 27, 31]. The study was conducted in northern Guangdong, one of the main settlement regions of the Hakka people (Fig. 1). A total of 19 sites were selected across five prefecture-level cities: Shaoguan (Nanxiong, Shixing, Lechang, Renhua), Qingyuan (Yingde, Qingxin, Fogang, Yangshan), Heyuan (Yuancheng, Heping, Longchuan), Meizhou (Dapu, Meixian, Pingyuan), and Huizhou (Huicheng, Huiyang, Huidong, Boluo, Longmen). This region is characterized not only by high plant diversity but also by the long-term preservation of traditional knowledge among Hakka communities. The combination of abundant plant resources and the continuity of local healthcare practices makes northern Guangdong a particularly suitable area for ethnobotanical research on Hakka medicinal bath plants.

The location of the study

Data collection

Field surveys were conducted from 2023 to 2024. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, personal conversations, and field observations [32, 33]. A total of 138 informants were interviewed. All informants were Hakka, and individuals familiar with medicinal plants were selected using the snowball sampling method [34]. The names, genders, occupations, and ages of the informants, as well as the local names of the plants, plant parts used, methods of preparation, and medicinal uses, were recorded. The interview questions included the following: (1) What plants are used for herbal baths for postpartum women and newborns? (2) Which parts of the plants are used? (3) What are the methods of use and general dosage patterns? (4) Why is this species used? (5) What are the Hakka names of these plants? (6) How was this knowledge of herbal remedies acquired? (7) Where and when are these plants collected? Photographs of each target plant were taken, and voucher specimens were collected. All plant species were identified according to the Flora of Guangdong [35] and the Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae [36]. All voucher specimens were deposited at Shaoguan University.

Data analysis

Ethnobotanical indices, including Fidelity Level (FL) and Informant Consensus Factor (FIC), were calculated to analyze the data. FL and FIC were used to quantify the cultural importance of plant species for specific ailment categories and the degree of agreement among informants, respectively [37, 38]. FL and FIC were calculated using the following formulas [39–42]:

Fidelity Level: FL (%) =(Np/N)*100.

Np is the number of use reports for a given species in a particular ailment category, and N is the total number of use reports recorded for that species.

Informant Consensus Factor: FIC = (Nur-Nt) / (Nur-1).

Nur is the number of use citations in each category, and Nt is the number of species reported in that category.

Results and discussion

Demographic features of the informants

Hakka medicinal baths represent a widely practiced form of community-based complementary healthcare, particularly for postpartum women and newborns in southern China. A total of 138 informants were interviewed in this study (Table 1), and key socioeconomic factors—including age, gender, educational background, and occupation—were recorded, as these factors are known to influence the acquisition of medicinal plant knowledge [43–45]. The informants mainly consisted of herbal medicine vendors and experienced elders; as a result, their educational levels tended to be relatively low. Similar findings were also reported by Huang et al. [46], who suggested that respondents with lower educational levels may have greater interest in traditional medicine and more frequent contact with plants [47, 48]. The informants ranged in age from 41 to 85 years and were categorized into four age groups: 40–50, 51–60, 61–70, and ≥ 71 years. Their average age was 63 years, and informants aged over 71 years accounted for only 13.04% of the sample. Knowledge regarding medicinal bath plants was mainly concentrated among experienced elders, consistent with findings reported in previous studies [49–53]. In our study, individuals around the age of 40 demonstrated relatively limited knowledge in this regard, mentioning an average of five medicinal bath plants per person. These plants were frequently cited in the survey and are traditional Chinese herbs such as Acorus calamus L., Artemisia argyi Lévl. et Van. and Lonicera japonica Thunb. In contrast, individuals aged 50 or older demonstrate a more comprehensive understanding, with an average of 12 medicinal bath plants described per person.

Among the informants, 48 were male and 90 were female. The proportion of female informants (65.22%) was nearly twice that of male informants (34.78%). This suggests that women tend to possess more extensive knowledge of medicinal bath plants. This pattern is closely associated with the widespread use of medicinal bath plants among women in the Hakka region of northern Guangdong for postpartum care and infant healthcare. Women play a crucial role in local postpartum and infant care, which makes them more familiar with the use of medicinal bath plants. They acquire knowledge of medicinal bath plants through familial transmission from elder relatives, guidance from experienced caregivers, and long-term practical experience. In addition, occupational background was associated with knowledge level, as individuals engaged in professions related to traditional Chinese medicine generally possessed a deeper understanding. Such individuals likely acquired more knowledge of medicinal bath plants through specialized training and practical experience in the field.

Plant species and life form

A total of 108 plant species belonging to 51 families and 99 genera were recorded as medicinal bath plants used by Hakka informants in northern Guangdong (see Appendix 1). Three species were pteridophytes, and the remaining species were spermatophytes. Asteraceae was the most represented family among the surveyed plants, with 11 species, followed by Lamiaceae with 7 species (Fig. 2A). Other relatively well-represented families included Rubiaceae (6 species), Euphorbiaceae (5 species), Phyllanthaceae (5 species), Rutaceae (5 species), Malvaceae (4 species), Amaranthaceae (4 species), Acanthaceae (3 species), and Polygonaceae (3 species). Similar findings were reported in other ethnobotanical studies [10, 27, 28, 33, 54–56]. Asteraceae is one of the largest flowering plant families and includes many species with recognized medicinal importance [57]. The frequent use of Asteraceae species in traditional medicine may be related to their aromatic compounds and essential oils [58, 59]. At the generic level, 90 genera were represented by only one species, while 9 genera included two species, reflecting the diversity of medicinal bath plant assemblages.

A Proportions of plant families. B Proportions of life forms

In terms of life form (Fig. 2B), herbs accounted for the largest proportion of species (46.29%), followed by shrubs (29.63%), trees (15.74%), and lianas (8.33%). This pattern is consistent with previous ethnobotanical studies in China [33, 54, 60] and may be related to the common practice of collecting medicinal plants from nearby areas for immediate use. From an ecological perspective, herbaceous plants have a broader and more abundant distribution in the wild compared to tree and shrub species, making herbaceous medicinal herbs easier to obtain and process during wild collection.

In Hakka communities, medicinal bath plants for postpartum women are usually prepared in advance by the mother or mother-in-law. These plants are typically collected from nearby forests, hillsides, roadsides, or uncultivated land around villages. After collection, they are dried and tied into small bundles for storage. Alternatively, they may be purchased from local markets or herbal medicine shops. Because of the hot and humid climate in Guangdong, most medicinal bath plants are dried before use to facilitate long-term storage.

Parts used

The plant parts used in medicinal baths are shown in Fig. 4C. Whole plants (54 species) were the most frequently used plant part, followed by stems and leaves (46 species) and roots (8 species). Fruits (2 species), flowers (2 species) and seeds (1 species) were used less frequently. In general, whole plants were more commonly used in herbaceous species, whereas branches and leaves were more frequently used in woody species. Different plant parts may contain distinct compounds with medicinal properties. Leaves are commonly used because they are easy to collect and often contain abundant secondary metabolites. This preference for leaves has also been reported in previous ethnobotanical and pharmacological studies [61–66].

For the same plant species, preferences for specific parts may vary among individuals. Some informants believed that stems and leaves were sufficient for mild symptoms, whereas roots were preferred in more severe cases. For example, Gardenia jasminoides Ellis is used in medicinal baths for newborn jaundice. In Huizhou, Hakka informants preferred its fruits and considered the fruits and roots to be more effective than the stems and leaves; when the plant was not in fruit, the roots were used instead. By contrast, Hakka informants in Shaoguan more commonly used the stems of Gardenia because they were easier to harvest. This illustrates regional and individual variation in the selection of plant parts for medicinal baths.

Medicinal uses

A total of 108 medicinal bath plant species were reported to be used for managing more than 30 health conditions (Fig. 3). Among these, 72 species were used for multiple conditions, whereas 36 species were reported exclusively for a single condition. For example, Hedyotis hedyotidea (DC.) Merr. was reported to be used for colds, fever, cough, neonatal conditions, and eczema. In contrast, Alchornea trewioides (Benth.) Müll. Arg. was reported only for medicinal baths used to manage eczema.

To ensure consistency in reporting medicinal plant uses, all reported health conditions were categorized into five groups based on the International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC-2) [67], with localized adjustments informed by our findings: postpartum recovery, skin diseases, digestive disorders, respiratory diseases, and others. FIC values ranged from 0.61 to 0.87 (Table 2), with the highest values observed for postpartum recovery, respiratory diseases, and skin diseases.

The category of postpartum recovery mainly concerned postpartum women. Commonly used bath plants in this category included Liquidambar formosana Hance, Zingiber officinale Roscoe, Artemisia argyi H. Lév. & Vaniot, Sambucus javanica Reinw. ex Blume, and Clerodendrum chinense (Osbeck) Mabb. Traditionally, Hakka women observe a postpartum confinement period of approximately one month after childbirth. During this period, they refrain from physical labor and recover through bed rest, dietary nourishment, and medicinal baths, in which herbs are regarded as important tonic and therapeutic agents. In local tradition, herbal bathing is believed to dispel “wind” generated during or after delivery, which is considered harmful to maternal health [10]. After childbirth, the woman’s mother or mother-in-law typically collects and prepares the required medicinal plants. The postpartum medicinal bath is typically prepared by boiling 3–5 kinds of herbs, most commonly leaves and stems, in a large container until the decoction turns dark. After cooling, the decoction is used for bathing or full-body immersion, with each session lasting approximately 10–15 min.

Respiratory diseases were cited 159 times and mainly included colds, fever, cough, and nasal discharge. Frequently used plants in this category included Melicope pteleifolia (Champ. ex Benth.) Hartley, Eleutherococcus trifoliatus (L.) S. Y. Hu, Lygodium japonicum (Thunb.) Sw., Ficus hispida L. f., and Glycosmis pentaphylla (Retz.) Corrêa. Skin diseases were cited 131 times and mainly included neonatal jaundice and dermatitis. Commonly used plants for skin conditions included Lonicera japonica Thunb., Gardenia jasminoides J. Ellis, Impatiens balsamina L., Polygonum nepalense (Meisn.) H. Gross, and Ludwigia adscendens (L.) Hara.

Respiratory and skin diseases are commonly among newborns. In Guangdong Province, the hot and humid climate [68] may contribute to common skin problems in newborns, such as jaundice, eczema, dermatitis, and prickly heat. As infants grow older, they may become more susceptible to respiratory conditions, mainly manifested as colds, fever, cough, and nasal discharge. The practice of bathing infants with herbal mixtures is widespread across many cultures [69, 70], although its specific purposes vary. In traditional Chinese society, herbal baths are regarded not only as a means of treating illness but also as an important practice for supporting infant health and growth [2]. Similar practices have also been documented in ethnobotanical studies from Suriname and French Guiana [71, 72]. In a broader regional context, ethnobotanical studies in Southeast Asia, particularly in northern Thailand [73, 74], have also documented the use of medicinal plants in women’s healthcare and postpartum recovery. These studies likewise highlight the importance of traditional knowledge and its transmission, as well as the challenges posed by modernization and cultural change. These findings are consistent with the patterns observed among the Hakka in northern Guangdong. Furthermore, Thomas [75] described how the Trinitario Amerindians of Trinidad used herbal baths to help children learn to walk more quickly. These cross-cultural practices suggest that infant herbal bathing is not only a healthcare measure but also reflects shared experiential knowledge and cultural responses to infant vulnerability across different ethnic groups.

In addition, certain species, such as Acorus calamus L., Cinnamomum burmanni (Nees & T. Nees) Blume, Palhinhaea cernua (L.) Vasc. & Franco, Citrus maxima (Burm.) Merr., Platycladus orientalis (L.) Franco are traditionally used in New Year’s Eve baths. Among Hakka communities in Guangdong, this custom symbolizes the removal of bad luck and the welcoming of the New Year. It is also believed to promote general well-being and relieve physical discomfort. Accordingly, medicinal bath herb packets are commonly sold in local markets before the Chinese New Year.

Analysis of FL

Ten plant species had an FL value of 100: Artemisia argyi, Zingiber officinale, Liquidambar formosana, Vitex negundo, Eleutherococcus trifoliatus, Gardenia jasminoides, Cardiospermum halicacabum, Impatiens chinensis, Acorus calamus and Cinnamomum burmanni. A. argyi, Z. officinale, L. formosana, and V. negundo were mainly used for postpartum care, particularly for dispelling “wind” and eliminating dampness in traditional understanding, whereas E. trifoliatus was more frequently used for common colds. G. jasminoides and I. chinensis were mainly used for newborn-related conditions (see Table 3). These species were among the most widely recognized plants used in medicinal bath practices, and several studies have investigated their phytochemical and pharmacological properties. For example, Du et al. systematically reviewed the major chemical constituents, pharmacological activities, and health-related functions of G. jasminoides and its related products [76]. Previous studies have reported that G. jasminoides exhibits a range of pharmacological activities, including hepatoprotective, antimicrobial, choleretic, neuroprotective, and metabolic regulatory effects [77]. Xiu et al. [78] reported that E. trifoliatus has both medicinal and edible value. In traditional use, it is associated with functions such as clearing heat, detoxification, dispelling wind and dampness, and relieving cough and asthma. Its tender leaves and shoots are edible and aromatic, suggesting potential for development as a medicinal and edible resource. Meanwhile, L. formosana also plays an important role in the daily life of the Zhuang people. Its resin is an important raw material for the traditional “Fengxiang” dye, while its leaves are used to produce the black color in five-colored glutinous rice, reflecting distinctive regional and cultural characteristics [79].

Herbal formulation

Hakka medicinal bath plants were predominantly herbaceous, and the use of whole plants was a defining feature. There was no fixed formula for these baths; instead, 3–5 plants with similar medicinal properties were typically combined. Medicinal baths were administered at specific times to support the health of postpartum women and newborns and to prevent common illnesses. In many Hakka communities, women and their newborns underwent continuous medicinal bathing during the postpartum confinement period, which lasted from one week to one month depending on local customs. When postpartum women experienced discomfort associated with wind chill or cold exposure, medicinal bath plants traditionally used to dispel “wind,” remove dampness, and alleviate cold were commonly selected. Similarly, when newborns presented with jaundice, convulsions, or indigestion, targeted combinations of plants were applied, with more cautious selection and lower dosages than those used for adults. In practice, these plants were usually decocted together, and the resulting liquid was cooled to approximately 40–50 °C before use, without the addition of cold water. Alternatively, different formulations could be applied in rotation, with each used for 2–3 days. Postpartum Hakka medicinal baths could be broadly categorized into three types: those used to dispel “wind” and alleviate cold; those referred to as “sha medicine,” which are intended to prevent major illnesses; and those known as “warm medicine,” which are believed to eliminate toxins and replenish the body. Comparison with medicinal bath of Yao people

Yao medicinal bath traditions in China have been widely documented in previous ethnobotanical studies, particularly in southern regions such as Hunan Province [2, 9, 73, 80]. In this study, the comparison was based on published data on Yao medicinal bath plants [9], which provided a well-established reference for analysis. This comparison was undertaken because Yao and Hakka communities are geographically close and share similar environmental conditions, which may influence the availability and selection of medicinal plants. Comparing these two groups therefore helps to identify both shared ethnobotanical knowledge and region-specific adaptations in medicinal bath practices.

The comparison revealed notable differences between Yao and Hakka practices in terms of plant species composition, usage patterns, medicinal functions, and cultural context (Fig. 4). Yao medicinal baths involved a larger number of plant species (152 species), with a predominance of woody plants and frequent use of roots, stems, and bark. These practices are associated with a broader range of applications, including postpartum care, general health maintenance, and culturally embedded activities such as festivals and rituals. In contrast, Hakka medicinal bathing involved 108 recorded plant species, of which 18 overlapped with Yao practices. Herbaceous species were more commonly used, and plant parts such as whole plants, leaves, and stems were frequently utilized. These practices were primarily focused on postpartum women and newborns, emphasizing postpartum recovery and the management of common infant conditions. Hakka medicinal baths were closely linked to the “zuo yuezi” (postpartum confinement) tradition and were typically embedded in family-based healthcare practices.

Overall, these differences reflect both shared ethnobotanical knowledge and region-specific adaptations shaped by ecological conditions and cultural practices. While Yao medicinal bathing showed greater diversity and stronger integration with ritual and community life, Hakka practices tended to emphasize practicality, safety, and intergenerational knowledge transmission. These findings highlight the cultural diversity of traditional ethnomedicinal systems and provide a basis for the preservation and sustainable use of local healthcare knowledge.

Conclusion

This study documented 108 Hakka medicinal bath plant species used primarily for postpartum care and infant healthcare, revealing clear patterns in plant selection, usage methods, and cultural practices. Herbs were the most commonly used life form, with whole plants, leaves, and stems frequently used, and baths typically prepared using combinations of 3–5 plants with similar properties. Hakka medicinal bath practices emphasize practicality, safety, and family-based healthcare, in contrast to neighboring Yao practices, which involve a greater diversity of plant species and stronger integration with ritual and ceremonial contexts. Knowledge of medicinal bath plants among the Hakka is primarily transmitted through oral traditions and everyday family practices, particularly among older women and caregivers. However, this knowledge is increasingly threatened by modernization, changing lifestyles, and the gradual decline of intergenerational transmission. Despite these pressures, medicinal bath practices remain an important component of daily household healthcare, especially in postpartum recovery and infant care. These findings reflect both the regional adaptability and the cultural specificity of traditional medicinal knowledge. Future research may focus on the pharmacological investigation of key species, more detailed documentation of preparation methods, and broader comparative studies across different ethnic groups. Overall, this study contributes to the documentation and understanding of traditional medicinal knowledge and may provide a basis for its preservation and sustainable use.
