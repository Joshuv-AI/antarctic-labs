Interacting and joint impact of oxidative balance score and insulin resistance on cardiovascular and metabolic diseases risks: a cross-sectional, population-based study
Authors: Leilei Du, Yangli Liu, Kun Zhu, Zhiheng Lin, Bing Hua, Jia Peng, Chun-Mei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground‌Oxidative stress and insulin resistance (IR) are risk factors for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases (CVMDs). However, the interaction between the oxidative balance score (OBS, marker of oxidative stress), and the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG, IR surrogate), on CVMDs risk, remains largely unexplored.MethodsThis cross-sectional, population-based study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999–2018), examining OBS and TyG. All exposures and outcomes were ascertained at the same time point; therefore, only associational relationships are reported. The primary outcome was the prevalence of four major CVMDs—cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and hyperlipidemia.ResultsAmong 17,265 participants, the prevalence rates were 8.5% for CVD, 36.9% for hypertension, 13.6% for T2DM, and 71.4% for hyperlipidemia. TyG significantly mediated the relationship between OBS and CVMD risk, with mediation proportions of 1.8% for CVD, 7.8% for hypertension, and 10.5% for T2DM. No statistically significant multiplicative or additive interactions between OBS and TyG in influencing CVMDs risk were observed. Participants with low OBS and high TyG levels had significantly increased risks for CVD, hypertension, T2DM, and hyperlipidemia, with adjusted odds ratios of 1.52 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.21-1.92), 1.62 (95%CI: 1.38-1.91), 2.94 (95%CI: 2.13-4.04), and 9.52 (95%CI: 7.51-12.1), respectively, compared to those with high OBS and low TyG levels. Adding OBS and TyG to traditional CVMDs risk models resulted in very small improvements in the C-statistic (ΔC <0.003 for most outcomes). ConclusionsThis study suggests that TyG is associated with a mediation effect between oxidative stress on CVMDs risk and underscores the co-exposure effects of OBS and TyG on CVMDs risk, highlighting the importance of a combined OBS and TyG assessment for improved CVMDs risk stratification. Because of the cross-sectional design and the mathematical overlap between TyG and the hyperlipidemia definition, causal inferences cannot be made, and the hyperlipidemia findings require independent validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06075-2.Keywords: Triglyceride, Glucose, Oxidative stress, Cardiovascular disease, Metabolic disease, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Cardiovascular and metabolic diseases (CVMDs) are a leading cause of global mortality, imposing significant socio-economic burdens and necessitating a deeper understanding of their pathogenic mechanisms [1–3]. Given their high incidence and mortality rates, it is crucial to identify new risk factors to improve prevention and treatment strategies. Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of oxidative stress in the etiology and progression of CVMDs [4]. Oxidative stress levels exhibit multifactorial regulation in body, with the oxidative balance score (OBS) serving as a comprehensive biomarker integrating 20 distinct components (16 dietary and 4 lifestyle factors) - comprising 15 antioxidant and 5 pro-oxidant parameters [5]. This composite score quantitatively reflects an individual’s oxidative/antioxidative homeostasis, where elevated OBS levels indicate predominant antioxidant capacity, while diminished scores suggest pro-oxidant predominance [6]. The OBS is negatively linked to the risk of mortality and incidence associated with a range of chronic diseases [5, 7, 8]. Insulin resistance (IR) is recognized as a central factor in the development of a spectrum of cardiovascular and metabolic disorders [9]. The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), derived from fasting plasma levels of triglycerides and glucose, has been posited as a reliable indicator of IR [10]. Accumulating clinical evidence has established the TyG as a robust predictor of disease severity and adverse clinical outcomes in CVMDs [11, 12], making it a potentially valuable tool for understanding and predicting CVMDs.

Recent studies have further extended these findings. A 2025 NHANES-based study demonstrated that OBS was robustly associated with Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome progression, with each OBS increment reducing the odds of CKM stages 1–4 [13]. Another 2024 study reported that oxidative stress biomarkers, including gamma-glutamyl transferase and uric acid, collectively mediated approximately 10.5% of the association between TyG and CVD mortality, and 8.4% of the association with all-cause mortality, in patients with metabolic syndrome [14].

Recent evidence demonstrates that oxidative stress accelerates endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and end-organ damage, perpetuating the progression of hypertension and diabetes [15]. OBS reflects the net oxidative balance, while TyG is a surrogate for insulin resistance; these two markers may interact through shared pathways involving endothelial dysfunction and metabolic stress. However, the interaction between the OBS and TyG on CVMDs risk remains underexplored.

Notably, oxidative stress is intimately linked to IR through insulin sensitivity in insulin-responsive tissues, ultimately influencing disease progression and clinical outcomes‌ [16, 17]. However, the interaction between the OBS and TyG on CVMDs risk remains underexplored. A recent NHANES-based study by Zhang et al. examined the mediating role of TyG in the OBS–mortality relationship, finding that TyG mediated part of OBS’s protective effect on all-cause mortality. Building on this work, our study specifically investigates: (1) the interactive and joint effects of OBS and TyG on the prevalence of four major CVMDs (CVD, hypertension, T2DM, hyperlipidemia), rather than mortality; and (2) the mediating role of TyG across multiple metabolic outcomes, thereby extending the evidence from mortality to broader CVMD outcomes [18]. Thus, this study aims to investigate the extent of interaction or combined association of two indicators with CVMDs. Elucidating the mediating role of TyG could provide valuable insights into the mechanisms by which is associated with the risk of CVMDs, potentially revealing new avenues for intervention. Moreover, understanding whether abnormal levels of OBS and TyG exert synergistic, additive, or antagonistic effects on CVMDs risk and prognosis is crucial, which may inform more personalized and effective preventive strategies.

This study aims to fill a critical gap in current literature by examining the joint impact of OBS and TyG on the risks of four CVMDs [cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and hyperlipidemia] and the potential mediating role of TyG using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018 data.

Methods

Study population

This study analyzed decadal data (1999–2018) comprising ten consecutive cycles of NHANES, a nationally representative surveillance program administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Employing a rigorous stratified multistage probability sampling methodology, NHANES systematically collects comprehensive health metrics through standardized mobile examinations and structured interviews. The survey protocol encompasses multidimensional health assessments, including clinical evaluations, nutritional biomarker profiling, and quantification of modifiable risk factors among non-institutionalized US residents. All study procedures received ethical approval from the NCHS Institutional Review Board in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from each participant prior to data collection.

Based on the NHANES documentation for each cycle, the total number of participants enrolled in each individual NHANES cycle from 1999 to 2018 was as follows: 1999–2000 (n = 9,965), 2001–2002 (n = 11,039), 2003–2004 (n = 10,122), 2005–2006 (n = 10,348), 2007–2008 (n = 10,149), 2009–2010 (n = 10,537), 2011–2012 (n = 9,756), 2013–2014 (n = 10,175), 2015–2016 (n = 9,971), and 2017–2018 (n = 9,254). The total screened population consisted of 101,316 participants.

From the initial NHANES cohort, we implemented rigorous exclusion criteria to ensure data quality (Figure S1). The exclusion process comprised: (1) age-related exclusion (participants < 20 years, n = 46,235); (2) pregnant status exclusion (n = 1,547); (3) missing essential biochemical parameters [indicators used for calculating OBS, fasting glucose and triglyceride (TG) n = 30,598]; (4) unavailable serum biomarkers (hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), n = 840; (5)incomplete baseline CVMDs information (CVD, hypertension, T2DM and hyperlipidemia, n = 6); (6) absent socioeconomic and lifestyle covariates [education level and poverty income ratio (PIR), n = 4,069]; (7) missing medication records (lipid-lowering, antidiabetic, and antihypertensive agents, n = 9) and (8) excluding participants without sample weights(n = 747). After applying these exclusion criteria, the final analytical cohort consisted of 17,265 eligible participants.

Exposure measurement and outcome definition

The OBS was constructed by integrating 16 dietary components and 4 lifestyle parameters. Dietary assessment was performed using the mean values derived from two standardized 24-hour dietary recall interviews, capturing both dietary intake and supplemental sources. The nutritional components comprised: (1) dietary fiber; (2) carotenoids (retinol equivalents); (3) riboflavin; (4) niacin; (5) vitamin B6; (6) total folate; (7) vitamin B12; (8) vitamin C; (9) vitamin E (α-tocopherol equivalents); (10) calcium; (11) magnesium; (12) zinc; (13) copper; (14) selenium; (15) total fat; and (16) iron. Concurrent lifestyle assessments included: (1) physical activity levels; (2) body mass index (BMI); (3) alcohol consumption; and (4) smoking status (quantified through serum cotinine measurements). Among these 20 components, five demonstrated pro-oxidative properties (total fat, iron, BMI, alcohol consumption, and cotinine), while the remaining fifteen exhibited antioxidant characteristics [5].

The TyG served as a reliable surrogate marker for assessing IR. During the baseline examination, venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast for standardized measurement of fasting glucose and TG levels by using an autoanalyzer. The formula of TyG = Ln[fasting TG (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2], as previously described in epidemiological studies [19].

The outcome analyzed in this study mainly include the following four CVMDs. CVD ascertainment was performed through physician-validated, structured interviews utilizing standardized questionnaires. Participants were specifically queried regarding prior clinical diagnoses of coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, or stroke by healthcare professionals, with affirmative responses to any condition constituting confirmed CVD cases. T2DM was defined according to established criteria: self-reported physician diagnosis, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour postprandial glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL during oral glucose tolerance testing, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or current use of glucose-lowering medications (oral agents or insulin). Hypertension classification followed contemporary clinical guidelines [20], incorporating either measured blood pressure (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg) or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication. Hyperlipidemia was characterized per National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III guidelines [21]: TC ≥ 200 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, gender-specific HDL-C thresholds (< 40 mg/dL for males, < 50 mg/dL for females), LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL, or self-reported lipid-lowering pharmacotherapy. Of note, because TyG is mathematically derived from triglyceride levels, and elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL) are themselves part of the hyperlipidemia definition, any observed association between TyG and hyperlipidemia is partly tautological and should not be interpreted as biologically independent.

Covariates assessment

Sociodemographic data collection was performed using validated questionnaires, capturing key variables including educational attainment, PIR, and comprehensive medical history. Educational levels were stratified into three categories: (1) less than high school diploma; (2) high school graduate or equivalent; and (3) college degree or higher. The PIR was categorized into tertiles (≤ 1.3, 1.3–3.5, and > 3.5) to reflect socioeconomic status. Current smoking status was operationally defined as self-reported consumption of ≥ 100 cigarettes during lifetime with current use. Alcohol consumption thresholds were sex-specific: ≥2 standard drinks/day for males and ≥ 1 drink/day for females. Biochemical parameters including HbA1c, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C were obtained from the NHANES database, with all measurements performed using standardized laboratory protocols.

Statistical analysis

A complete case analysis approach was used. Participants with missing data on any variable required for the analysis (including OBS components, TyG, outcomes, and covariates) were excluded, as detailed in Figure S1. No imputation was performed.

Consistent with NHANES analytic guidelines, all statistical analyses incorporated sampling weights and accounted for the complex survey design through adjustment for clustering and stratification variables. Baseline participant characteristics were summarized using median of OBS. Continuous variables were presented as either mean ± standard deviation (SD, for normally distributed data) or median with interquartile range [IQR] (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency counts with corresponding percentages. Continuous variables were evaluated using either one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square tests. In the absence of established clinical thresholds, we operationalized the median of OBS and TyG as cut-off points (OBS median = 19, TyG median = 8.594). The study population was stratified into four mutually exclusive groups based on median values of OBS and TyG, with the reference group defined as participants exhibiting OBS ≥median and TyG < median. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cardiovascular and metabolic disease risks. Two models were fitted: Model 1 adjusted for age and gender; Model 2 further adjusted for ethnicity, education status, PIR, smoking, drinking, lipid-lowering drugs, antidiabetic drugs, antihypertensive drugs, BMI, LDL-C and HbA1c. Then, we evaluated the effect of the TyG on CVMDs stratified by OBS level, and vice versa. Although antidiabetic, antihypertensive, and lipid‑lowering medications are part of the diagnostic criteria for T2DM, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, we included them as covariates in Model 2 to adjust for potential confounding by disease severity and treatment status. This approach follows standard practice in NHANES‑based observational studies to avoid residual confounding by indication.

Mediation analyses were systematically performed to assess the potential mediating role of the TyG in the association of OBS and CVMDs. The analytical framework was constructed as follows: (1) OBS (per SD increase) served as the independent variable (X), (2) TyG (per SD increase) as the mediator (M), and (3) CVMDs as the dependent variable (Y). This standardized approach allowed for quantification of both direct (X→Y) and indirect (X→M→Y) effects, with the latter representing the mediation effect through the TyG. Mediation analyses used OBS and TyG as continuous variables (per SD increase) to preserve full distributional information for estimating indirect effects.

The incremental discriminative value of novel biomarkers (including OBS, TyG, and their combined effect) over traditional risk factors was systematically evaluated using two statistical approaches: Harrell’s concordance statistic (C-statistic) to quantify model discrimination improvement, and likelihood ratio (LR) tests to assess the significance of nested model comparisons.

To comprehensively assess both additive and multiplicative interaction effects between OBS (median) and TyG (median) on CVMDs risk, we incorporated their product term into the multivariable regression model. The OR with 95%CI for this product term served as the metric for multiplicative-scale interaction. For additive interaction analysis, we employed three established epidemiological measures: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion (AP), and the synergy index (SI). The interpretation of these indices follows biological interaction theory: null interaction: RERI = 0, AP = 0, SI = 1; synergistic interaction: RERI > 0, AP > 0, SI > 1; antagonistic interaction: RERI < 0, AP < 0, SI < 1. We utilized the delta method to estimate the 95%CI for these three interaction indicators, thereby providing a robust assessment of the interaction effects. Interaction analyses used median‑dichotomized OBS and TyG to facilitate clinical interpretation of combined exposure groups (e.g., ‘low OBS + high TyG’) and to enable calculation of additive interaction measures.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.4.3). The threshold for statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p < 0.05 for all hypothesis tests.

Results

Population characteristics

The study cohort comprised 17,265 eligible participants. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were stratified according to the median OBS and presented in Table 1 for comparative analysis. The study population exhibited a mean age of 46.9 ± 16.7 years, with a slight female predominance (50.9%). The median OBS was 19 (IQR: 12–25), while the median TyG was 8.59 (IQR: 8.19–9.02). Participants with lower OBS showed distinct characteristics compared to higher OBS individuals. The low OBS group had higher proportions of non-Hispanic Black males, smokers, and less educated individuals. They also exhibited lower socioeconomic status, higher BMI, and greater prevalence of CVMDs. Metabolic parameters were significantly worse in the low OBS group, with elevated TG, LDL-C, glucose, HbA1c, and TyG levels, along with reduced HDL-C (all p < 0.01). Baseline characteristics of the four groups defined by combined OBS and TyG median stratification (high TyG + high OBS, high TyG + low OBS, low TyG + high OBS, low TyG + low OBS, and Low OBS + High TyG) are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Joint association of OBS and TyG with CVMDs

The prevalence rates of CVMDs were 8.5% for CVD, 36.9% for hypertension, 13.6% for T2DM, and 71.4% for hyperlipidemia in this study. When compared with the reference group, the incidence of CVMDs exhibited a progressive increase in the low TyG + low OBS group, high TyG + high OBS group, and high TyG + low OBS group. Notably, the high TyG + low OBS group demonstrated the highest incidence of CVMDs among the four groups [CVD: 4.80% vs. 6.90% vs. 9.60% vs. 13.60%, p < 0.001; hypertension: 25.10% vs. 30.40% vs. 46.30% vs. 48.50%, p < 0.001; T2DM: 4.30% vs. 6.80% vs. 20.80% vs. 24.60%, p < 0.001; hyperlipidemia: 52.30% vs. 55.30% vs. 90.40% vs. 91.20%, p < 0.001, Table 1; Fig. 1]. Supplementary Table S2 demonstrated significant and positive associations of low OBS and high TyG with CVMD risk in multivariable adjusted models. Table 2 presented the associations between the OBS and TyG with the risks of CVMDs. After adjusting for multiple confounders (Model 2), individuals with elevated OBS and elevated TyG, decreased OBS and decreased TyG, and decreased OBS and elevated TyG were associated with significantly increased risks of hypertension compared to those with elevated OBS and decreased TyG. Specifically, the adjusted ORs were 1.54 (95% CI: 1.30–1.83), 1.32 (95% CI: 1.11–1.57), and 1.62 (95%CI: 1.38–1.91), respectively. Similar trends were observed for T2DM. Compared with the reference group, the high OBS + high TyG group was not significantly associated with CVD (OR 1.18; 95% CI 0.94–1.48, p = 0.150).In contrast, patients in low OBS and low TyG group, and low OBS and high TyG group had a 1.28-fold (adjusted OR: 1.28; 95%CI: 1.03–1.59), and 1.52-fold (adjusted OR: 1.52; 95%CI: 1.21–1.92) increased risk of CVD, respectively. Notably, participants with elevated TyG were independently associated with a significantly increased risk of hyperlipidemia compared to the reference group, with this association persisting across all OBS levels.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings. First, excluding participants taking lipid‑lowering, antidiabetic, or antihypertensive medications (Supplementary Table S4) yielded results consistent with the main analysis. Second, excluding participants with prior CVD (Supplementary Table S5) showed that the associations for hypertension, T2DM, and hyperlipidemia persisted (CVD was not analyzed because all remaining participants were free of CVD by design). Third, using HOMA‑IR as an alternative surrogate for insulin resistance instead of TyG (Supplementary Table S6) produced comparable findings.

Finally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses confirmed dose‑response relationships for OBS and TyG with most CVMD outcomes, with some nonlinear patterns (e.g., OBS with CVD, TyG with hyperlipidemia; Supplementary Figures S2–S3). Specifically, OBS was significantly associated with all four CVMD outcomes (p for overall < 0.05 for all), with a nonlinear pattern for CVD (p for nonlinear = 0.048). TyG was significantly associated with hypertension, T2DM, and hyperlipidemia (p for overall < 0.001 for all), but not with CVD (p for overall = 0.125).

Mediation and interaction analyses of TyG of OBS with CVMDs risk

Figure 2 illustrated statistically significant mediation effects of TyG in the association between OBS and different CVMD. In the fully adjusted model, the mediation efficacy of TyG was 1.8% (p = 0.004, βindirect = -0.0002) of the relationship between OBS and CVD, 7.8% (p < 0.001, βindirect = -0.001) of the relationship between OBS and hypertension, 10.5% (p < 0.001, βindirect = -0.001) of the relationship between OBS and T2DM.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study systematically investigated the combined, synergistic and mediating effects of TyG and OBS on the risk of four major CVMDs. Our results have elucidated the intricate interplay among these two factors and their association with the assessment of CVMDs risk, potentially paving the way for the development of more tailored and efficacious preventive strategies.

Our results reaffirmed the significant association between the OBS and risks of multiple CVMDs, corroborating previous research findings [5, 7, 22]. OBS, as a comprehensive indicator of oxidative homeostasis, integrates both pro-oxidant and antioxidant factors, providing a more holistic reflection of the overall oxidative stress burden on the body than any single oxidative stress marker. Oxidative stress is associated with vascular endothelial homeostasis through the augmentation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby exacerbating arterial stiffness and subsequently accelerating the progression of CVDs [23, 24]. Moreover, oxidative stress is recognized as a detrimental factor associated with IR [25]. Under pathological conditions, an imbalance occurs where ROS production surpasses the antioxidant system’s neutralizing capacity, contributing to oxidative stress [25]. This stress impairs insulin signaling and decreases insulin sensitivity, contributing to dyslipidemia and glucose metabolism disorders, which are linked to the development and progression of CVMDs [19, 23]. Therefore, IR may serve as a potential significant mediating factor in the association between oxidative stress and CVMDs risk.

This study further investigated the relationship between TyG as a validated measure of IR and the risk of CVMDs and explored its mediating role in the association of OBS with CVMDs risk. TyG has been proven to be an alternative indicator of IR through simple formula calculations [26]. This study demonstrated that TyG played a significantly mediating role in the relationship between OBS and CVMDs risk, albeit with a modest mediating contribution to the risk of CVD. Recognizing TyG as a valid indicator of IR—a critical pathophysiological process in CVMDs—our findings underscored its significant mediating role. These insights suggest that TyG may be a factor in the pathways through which oxidative stress links to the development and progression of CVMDs. The study thus highlights its potential as a therapeutic target for individuals exhibiting elevated oxidative stress, with interventions to lower TyG levels, including lifestyle modifications or pharmacological approaches, possibly reducing their risk of CVMDs.

Our study further investigated the potential interaction between OBS, TyG, and the risk of CVMDs. The findings indicated that OBS and TyG did not exhibit significant synergistic or interactive effects in influencing CVMDs risk. Notably, although both OBS and TyG independently associated with the risk of CVMDs, as supported by our results and previous research [19, 22], their combined impact does not appear to exceed the sum of their individual effects. That added complexity to understanding how OBS and TyG are jointly associated with the risk of CVMDs. It is well established that the etiology of CVMDs is multifactorial, encompassing elements such as inflammation, environmental influences, and genetic factors [1, 3, 27]. The presence of these various factors may partially attenuate the interaction between OBS and TyG, potentially explaining the absence of a pronounced synergistic effect. Alternatively, OBS and TyG might be associated with CVMDs through distinct pathways. Therefore, more research is warranted to elucidate the precise pathways and mechanisms through which OBS and TyG are linked to CVMDs.

While our study did not identify a synergistic interaction between OBS and TyG, it underscored the importance of considering these factors together in assessing CVMDs risk. Our findings suggested that the combined assessment of OBS and TyG may more effectively assess CVMDs risk than either factor alone, indicating that individuals with low OBS and elevated TyG may be at greater risk than those with abnormalities in only one of these variables. Moreover, the combination of both OBS and TyG into risk assessment models significantly enhanced their ability to forecast CVMDs risk, highlighting the potential clinical importance of jointly assessing these two factors for CVMDs risk evaluation.

The incremental improvements in the C-statistic were very small (ΔC < 0.003 for CVD, hypertension, and T2DM). While statistically significant due to the large sample size, improvements of this magnitude are generally considered to be of limited clinical relevance for risk discrimination. For hyperlipidemia, the larger ΔC (0.0259) should be interpreted with caution, as it is partially inflated by the mathematical overlap between TyG and the hyperlipidemia definition.

The lack of a significant association between the high OBS + high TyG group and CVD in the fully adjusted model (OR 1.18, p = 0.150) suggests that the protective effect of high OBS may offset the risk associated with high TyG. Alternatively, the association between TyG and CVD may be largely explained by other metabolic risk factors (e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia) that were adjusted for in Model 2. In contrast, TyG remained strongly associated with hypertension, T2DM, and hyperlipidemia even after full adjustment, reflecting their closer biological links to insulin resistance.

The findings of this study hold practical implications for clinical practice and risk stratification. Comprehensive assessment of OBS and TyG levels can help identify high-risk groups, allowing for more targeted prevention strategies. These may include dietary modifications, such as increasing intake of dietary antioxidants and polyunsaturated fatty acids while reducing calorie intake, along with lifestyle changes like enhancing physical activity and minimizing smoking and alcohol consumption. However, because this is a cross‑sectional study, causal inferences cannot be made. Such interventions have been previously associated with improved oxidative stress levels, improved insulin sensitivity, and lower CVMDs risk [28–30].

Consistent with recent literature, recent studies have reported that TyG‑derived composite indices incorporating obesity measures (e.g., TyG‑BMI, TyG‑WC, TyG‑WHtR) may outperform standard TyG in predicting CVD and mortality [31, 32]. For example, TyG‑WHtR was found to be a strong predictor of CVD mortality [31], and TyG‑WC and TyG‑WHtR enhanced CVD prediction beyond standard TyG alone [32]. While our study focused on the interaction between OBS and standard TyG, we acknowledge that these more complex TyG‑derived indices might have performed differently in our analyses. Future studies should investigate whether OBS interacts differently with TyG‑BMI, TyG‑WC, or TyG‑WHtR.

We conducted a comprehensive analysis using representative NHANES data to examine the interactive, combined effects of OBS and TyG on the risk of four major CVMDs, as well as the mediating role of TyG in this relationship. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of NHANES data precluded definitive causal interpretation of observed associations between OBS, TyG, and clinical outcomes. Due to the cross‑sectional design, reverse causation cannot be excluded, and all associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. While our findings indicate associations and interactions, establishing causality requires further investigation, including randomized controlled trials and experimental designs. Secondly, the study population, comprising representative US participants, exhibits population heterogeneity. Thirdly, CVMD diagnoses relied on self-reported data, introducing potential recall and verification biases. Fourthly, the analysis focused on four major CVMD and further evidence is needed to support the consistency of our findings across other CVMDs. Moreover, OBS, TyG, and CVMDs data were derived from baseline assessments, preventing us from evaluating the impact of their dynamic changes on both short-term and long-term CVMDs risk. Lastly, despite comprehensive adjustment for known confounders, unmeasured factors may persist as potential sources of residual confounding.

Additionally, the hyperlipidemia findings (OR 9.52) are influenced by mathematical overlap, as TyG is derived from triglyceride (TG) levels and elevated TG (≥ 150 mg/dL) is itself a diagnostic criterion for hyperlipidemia in this study. Consequently, these findings are not interpretable as independent biological evidence. The remaining CVMD outcomes (CVD, hypertension, T2DM) do not share this limitation, as their definitions do not rely on TG levels.

Sixth, adjusting for antidiabetic, antihypertensive, and lipid‑lowering medications while these drugs are also used to define T2DM, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia may introduce collider bias. However, a sensitivity analysis excluding medication users (Supplementary Table S4) yielded results consistent with the main findings, suggesting that any potential collider bias is unlikely to affect our conclusions.

Seventh, we used complete case analysis and did not perform imputation for missing data. Although the proportion of missingness was relatively small after exclusions, we cannot rule out the possibility that missing data could have introduced some bias.

Eighth, TyG is a simplified surrogate for insulin resistance that does not directly measure insulin levels. It may overestimate IR in individuals with hypertriglyceridemia due to other causes (e.g., familial hypertriglyceridemia). HOMA-IR or gold‑standard euglycemic hyperinsulinemic clamp data were not available in this study.

Ninth, OBS, TyG, and CVMDs were derived from a single time point assessment. The dietary components of OBS were based on two 24‑hour dietary recalls, which are subject to within‑person variability and may not accurately reflect habitual dietary patterns. Repeated measurements would be needed to assess the reliability of OBS and the stability of TyG over time.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cross-sectional, population-based study of US adults suggests that TyG is associated with a mediation effect between oxidative stress and CVMDs risk. These findings underscore the co-exposure effects of OBS and TyG on CVMDs risk, suggesting that a joint assessment of OBS and TyG may be a useful consideration for risk assessment, but further prospective studies are needed.
