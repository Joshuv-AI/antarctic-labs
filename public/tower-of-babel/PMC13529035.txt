Insights into the clinical presentation, imaging, and management of infiltrative keratitis following accelerated corneal collagen cross-linking
Authors: Aafreen Bari, Nandyala Sushma, Tushar Agarwal, Chetan Shakkarwal, Prafulla K Maharana, Tanuj Dada, Namrata Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To analyze the risk factors, clinical features, visual outcomes, and in-vivo imaging characteristics in cases of keratoconus developing keratitis following accelerated collagen cross-linking (CXL).Methods:A retrospective observational case series at a tertiary eye center in India evaluating eyes that underwent accelerated CXL from January 2021 to July 2024.Results:Of 723 eyes undergoing CXL, 14 eyes developed infiltrative keratitis (1.93%). The majority belonged to the upper middle socioeconomic class and presented in summer and rainy seasons. The relative risk of developing infiltrates in cases ≤16 years was 2.38, and in cases with history of vernal keratoconjunctivitis, it was 0.7. The median area of epithelial defect was 12 mm2, and the average resolution time was 18.4 ± 12.9 days. The most common organism that was isolated was coagulase-negative Staphylococcus. The majority of the cases on anterior segment optical coherence tomography showed involvement anterior to the demarcation line. Scheimpflug imaging showed three keratometric patterns: flattening and thinning, flattening and thinning with transient steepening, and minimal to no change.Conclusion:Infiltrative keratitis following accelerated CXL is a rare clinical entity. Early presentation and timely management including use of topical steroids are associated with good visual outcomes.

CXL offers solution to a wide range of patients with progressive keratoconus.[1] However, it carries certain risks associated with procedural complications. The conventional and accelerated CXL begin with epithelial debridement and impregnation of corneal stroma with riboflavin. This is followed by exposure to ultraviolet-A light for induction of biochemical reaction. The corneal epithelium is a major anatomical barrier and one of the first defence mechanisms of the eye. The corneal epithelium features four types of junctions: tight junctions (zonula occludens), desmosomes (macula adherens), adherens junctions (zonula adherens), and gap junctions. These junctions create a robust barrier, further strengthened by the secretion of heavily O-glycosylated transmembrane mucins like MUC1, MUC4, and MUC16. Additionally, the corneal epithelium plays a role in regulating the immune response. When the epithelium is compromised, cell surface glycosylation is disrupted and galectin-3 expression increases, which can increase susceptibility to infections. In response, corneal epithelial cells produce antimicrobial peptides, such as β-defensins, to help protect against potential pathogens.[2] Thus, any disruption of corneal epithelium, inadvertently multiplies the risks of infection.

Additionally, a bandage contact lens (BCL) is placed to minimize pain and photophobia. After CXL, following removal of the corneal epithelium, the patient experiences pain and photophobia due to exposed corneal neural plexus. A BCL facilitates to decrease these symptoms. However, in the early postoperative period, the pain could also be associated with infiltrative keratitis. In a retrospective study by Tzamalis et al.,[3] BCL and use of topical steroids were identified as major risk factors for development of microbial keratitis. Contrarily, a study by Zhu et al.[4] concluded that though the incidence of infectious keratitis following use of BCL is low, it is associated with significant higher risks in post-keratoplasty cases and post-pterygium cases and not in post-CXL cases.

There are limited studies in the literature discussing the risk factors and outcomes of infiltrative keratitis following collagen cross-linking. To understand the predisposing factors, clinical symptoms and signs of presentation, and underlying etiopathogenesis, a study was planned to analyze cases which presented with infiltrative keratitis following CXL.

Methods

This was a retrospective observational study analyzing medical records of cases undergoing accelerated CXL from January 2021 to July 2024. It was conducted at a tertiary eye center in India for progressive keratoconus. It was approved by the institutional ethics committee board and was conducted adhering to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The preoperative and follow-up data of patients who underwent CXL and those who developed infiltrative keratitis on follow-up in the duration were collected from medical records. Data and clinical records of all cases who underwent CXL and belonged to any age and gender were included in the study. Their demographic details including age and gender, history of allergic eye disease, date of procedure, duration of onset of symptoms and clinical presentation, visual acuity, and treatment and resolution time were included in the study. Patients with a known history of allergic ocular disease and documented progressive keratoconus were initially managed with topical antihistaminic agents and lubricants. CXL was undertaken only after achieving ocular surface stabilization and ensuring the allergic condition was in a quiescent stage. The microbiological reports including scraping, culture, and antibiotic sensitivity reports were collected and analyzed. Their clinical images, anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT), and Scheimpflug images in the preoperative phase and postoperative phase (during infiltrative keratitis and after resolution) were collected and studied.

Accelerated CXL protocol

Eyes diagnosed with Modified Amsler–Krumeich Stage II and Stage III keratoconus exhibiting documented progression, defined by a significant increase in maximum keratometry (Kmax) and/or progressive reduction in minimal corneal pachymetry on serial tomography, were included in the study. Patients were assigned iso-osmolar riboflavin (Peschke D Single Use Isotonic eye drops, 3 mL, riboflavin ≥0.1%, dextran 500 20%) or hypo-osmolar riboflavin (for cases with thinnest pachymetry less than 400 µm) based on corneal thickness. In patients aged <16 years, the procedure was performed under general anesthesia; in those ≥16 years, corneal collagen cross-linking was conducted under topical anesthetic agents. An eyelid speculum was positioned, and a central 7-mm epithelial debridement was performed using 20% alcohol. Riboflavin drops were then applied according to the osmolarity needed, ensuring complete saturation of the debrided area over a 30-minute period with applications every 2 minutes. Subsequently, ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure at 9 mW/cm² was delivered for 10 minutes, adhering to an accelerated protocol.[5] At the end of the procedure, a topical antibiotic was instilled, and a bandage contact lens was placed.

Following the procedure, patients received topical moxifloxacin hydrochloride 0.5% three times daily for 2 weeks and preservative-free carboxymethylcellulose 0.5% six times daily for 8 weeks. In eyes with clinically quiescent VKC, topical olopatadine 0.2% once-daily was prescribed as adjunctive therapy.

All patients were followed postoperatively at day 1 and week 1 and at 1, 3, and 6 months. At the 1-week follow-up visit, the BCL was removed upon confirmation of complete corneal epithelial healing. A low-potency topical corticosteroid, fluorometholone 0.1%, was initiated at a dosage of three times daily and subsequently tapered over a period of 3 weeks. No cases reported BCL dislodgement or exposure to tap water (e.g., face washing) during the immediate postoperative period.

In cases where infectious keratitis was clinically suspected during follow-up, the BCL was removed in accordance with a standard institutional protocol and sent for microbiological culture and sensitivity testing, including evaluation for bacterial, fungal, and Acanthamoeba pathogens. Corneal scrapings were obtained using a sterile Kimura spatula and submitted for comprehensive microbiological workup, including smear examination (Gram stain, 10% potassium hydroxide [KOH] wet mount, and Calcofluor white staining), culture on blood agar, Sabouraud dextrose agar, and non-nutrient agar with Escherichia coli overlay, followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Topical corticosteroids were withheld in eyes with suspected fungal or atypical infectious keratitis (n = 3/14). In the remaining eyes (n = 11/14), adjunctive topical corticosteroid therapy was initiated after 48 hours of fortified antibiotic treatment (cefazolin 5% and tobramycin 1.3% administered one hourly), with prednisolone phosphate 1% prescribed four times daily. In cases with a positive potassium hydroxide (KOH) smear suggestive of fungal infection, topical natamycin 5% was initiated at an hourly dosing frequency.

Statistical analysis

Data of all subjects were entered in a computer database, and analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) with an advanced statistics program. The continuous data were summarized using descriptive statistics (number of eyes (n), mean, standard deviation (SD), median, minimum, and maximum). Categorical data were summarized using frequency count (n) and percentages (%). Relative risk was calculated for enumerated risk factors.

Results

The records of 723 eyes with keratoconus that underwent CXL from January 2021 to July 2024 for disease progression were included in the study. Fourteen of these eyes (1.93%) developed corneal infiltrates in the postoperative period. No participant had a documented history of systemic disease. Their medical records including microbiology reports, clinical pictures, AS-OCT images, and Scheimpflug images were included in the study.

Demographics

The mean age of patients who developed microbial keratitis after CXL was 16.92 ± 4.53 years, while the median age was 18 years. The number of pediatric cases (≤ 16 years) developing infiltrative keratitis following CXL was six (6/194). The relative risk of developing infiltrative keratitis in pediatric cases was 2.38. The number of females and males developing infiltrates was 5 and 9, respectively. All cases belonged to North India. According to modified Kuppuswamy scale, the total mean score was 19.4, which caters to upper middle socioeconomic class. Hypo-osmolar CXL was done in four cases, while iso-osmolar CXL was done in 10 cases. The number of cases which underwent CXL and developed infiltrative keratitis was highest in summer and rainy seasons (March to July, n = 6, 42.9%).

Clinical features

All cases underwent accelerated CXL with placement of BCL. The mean duration of presentation from procedure to onset of symptoms was from 3.7 ± 1.5 days (range 1 to 7 days). The mean duration from onset of symptoms to presentation was 2.7 ± 1.5 days (range 1 to 5 days). Among these cases, four had previous vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC), while 373 of those without infiltrates also had a history of VKC. The relative risk of developing infiltrates in a case undergoing CXL with history of VKC was 0.7. At presentation, the mean visual acuity was log MAR 1.4 ± 0.6, and the median visual acuity was log MAR 1.3. The median area of epithelial defect at presentation was 12 mm2. Twelve of 14 cases (85.7%) presented with multifocal anterior stromal infiltrates [Fig. 1]. Four cases (28.5%) had infiltrate size <2 mm. Four cases (28.5%) had hypopyon at presentation. Three cases (21.4%) required one anti-glaucoma medications in addition to topical antibiotic therapy. Topical steroids were given in 11 of 14 (78.5%) cases. All cases healed with medical management only. The average resolution time for infiltrates was 18.4 ± 12.9 days. One case had reinfection 2 weeks after resolution of primary keratitis. However, it was treated meticulously and healed in 2 weeks. The mean visual acuity after complete healing was log MAR 0.59 ± 0.32, and the median visual acuity was log MAR 0.54. Twelve of 14 (85.7%) eyes were visually rehabilitated using glasses or contact lens, while two eyes (14.2%) were planned for optical keratoplasty.

Microbiology results

Corneal scraping was done in all cases except four due to the small size of infiltrates (<2 mm). The overall most common agent causing keratitis in these cases was coagulase negative Staphylococcus (CoNS). Four cases (40%) were positive for Gram-positive cocci, while one was positive on KOH mount for yeast-like microorganisms. On bacterial culture, the positivity was 40% (4/10, one case of Staphylococcus aureus and three cases of CoNS). On fungal culture, there was growth of Candida sp. in only one case (10%). One case was positive for Acanthameba sp. The resistance to fourth-generation fluoroquinolones (moxifloxacin) was observed in three of five (60%) culture reports. BCL yielded Escherichia coli in one case and CoNS in another case. In the case where Escherichia coli was isolated from the contact lens culture, CoNS was identified from the corneal scraping culture.

Imaging

AS-OCT (MS 39, Hanson Instruments Ltd, Worcestershire) records of six cases were studied. Inflammatory reaction on the endothelium was observed as hyper-reflective irregular deposits [Fig. 2a]. Demarcation line was observed in three cases (complete in one case and partially in two cases). The case with complete demarcation line showed stromal scarring anterior to it [Fig. 2b]. One case had deeper focal involvement [Fig. 2c] which healed with formation of full thickness scar [Fig. 2d]. In a case with anterior stromal infiltrates on presentation [Fig. 2e], corneal ectasia of anterior and posterior layers was observed on subsequent follow-ups [Fig. 2f].

The records of Scheimpflug imaging of eight cases were collected and analyzed. Records of five cases were complete pre- and postprocedure. Among these, there was gradual flattening of keratometry and corneal thinning [Fig. 3] among four cases, of which one case had transient steepening with an episode of keratitis which gradually flattened [Fig. 4]. Discussion

Infiltrative keratitis following CXL is a significant clinical concern. This condition often occurs after an elective corneal procedure in eyes that generally have good visual acuity with contact lenses. The procedure introduces factors that can increase the risk of infectious keratitis, such as the debridement of the corneal epithelium and the placement of a BCL. CXL itself triggers an inflammatory response in the stromal keratocytes, and the introduction of an infectious agent can place additional stress on these cells. Furthermore, this infection tends to arise in eyes that are already being treated with topical antibiotics, complicating the clinical picture.

Keratoconus classically affects patients in early teens and progresses to third decade.[6] The mean age of patients affected was 16.92 ± 4.53 years. A significant number of cases (42.9%) belonged to the pediatric age group. It may be due to an association with altered microbiome seen in eyes with naïve keratoconus as reported in a multicentric validation study. It was concluded that Pelomonas and Ralstonia were two genera uniquely observed in these cases.[7] Similarly, an association of vernal keratoconjunctivitis was observed in 28.5% cases. Previous studies have shown dysbiosis of ocular surface microbiota in allergic eye diseases.[8910] They observed abundant numbers of genera Blautia and Morganella in association with allergic eye disease as compared to controls.[8] Similar studies concluded that Staphylococcus sp. load is much higher in addition to Streptococcus sp. and Acinetobacter sp. load in cases of vernal keratoconjunctivitis.[910] These microorganisms may play a role in altering the ocular environment and create pro-infectious state synergistically with the stromal inflammation associated with CXL.

Most of the cases belonged to the upper middle socioeconomic class, presented within 5 days of onset of symptoms, and resolved with medical management only. This may be due to early presentation and timely management of infiltrative keratitis. None of the cases lead to corneal perforation or nonhealing microbial keratitis. This is contrary to other studies in the literature where therapeutic keratoplasty was done for management of keratitis after CXL.[111213] The incidence of keratitis is reported to be higher in the people with poorer environmental and personal hygiene, lower socioeconomic status, and lower level of education.[14] The socioeconomic status of cases with CXL keratitis was evaluated to assess if it was prevalent in the cases with a lower socioeconomic background. However, in this study, the majority of the cases belonged to the upper middle class (according to modified Kuppuswamy grading). This suggests that the risk of infiltrative keratitis after CXL is uniform across all, irrespective of the socioeconomic background. However, a prospective study is required to validate the findings.

The clinical presentation of these cases typically involves multiple stromal infiltrates which were observed in 85.7% cases and 28.5% cases presented with hypopyon. The complex clinical feature may be explained by various mechanisms like pre-existing stromal inflammation due to CXL, contact lens wear, altered microbiome, and association with vernal keratoconjunctivitis.[15] The case which presented with reinfection had undergone CXL elsewhere and at presentation had active vernal keratoconjunctivitis. On follow-up, a longer course of topical steroids and anti-histaminic drops were given to ensure optimization of the ocular surface.

In the majority of the studies in the literature, the reported microorganisms included are CoNS, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus sp., Staphylococcus hominis, Micrococcus luteus, Moraxella, Serratia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acanthameba, and Herpes simplex virus.[161718192021]

However, in this study, the chief causes of infection were CoNS, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Candida sp., and Acanthameba sp. Microbiological assessment in all cases except very small ulcers (<1–2 mm) is vital to isolate the underlying infectious agent and provide targeted treatment. BCL should also be sent for microbiological assessment to ensure good yield. Two of 13 cases (15.4%) yielded positive results from BCL, contrary to 42.8% observed by Maharana et al.[20] It may be associated with the time of presentation, virulence, and load of microorganism and technique of BCL culture. A previous study by Martins et al.[22] examining the correlation between corneal and contact lens cultures reported that concordance of isolated microorganisms from both sources was highest in cases of fungal infections. In contrast, bacterial keratitis demonstrated greater variability in the microorganisms identified. These findings underscore the importance of submitting contact lenses for culture as this practice aids in accurately determining the spectrum of infectious agents involved. Topical fluoroquinolones (most commonly moxifloxacin 0.5%) were routinely prescribed as prophylactic antibiotic therapy in post-CXL cases. Though culture positivity was low in the study, 60% resistance to moxifloxacin suggests a trend of emerging resistance pattern and a need to optimize the use of antibiotic therapy.

The stromal keratocytes which undergo cross-linking are under pre-existing stress, additionally infectious agent incites inflammation related to it. To combat it, topical steroids would theoretically be extremely beneficial. After ruling out fungus and atypical microorganisms, topical steroids may be added early in the disease course. They may decrease the local inflammation and reduce scarring providing early visual rehabilitation. Topical steroids were prescribed in 78.5% cases in the study. It could have contributed to better post-treatment visual acuity in our study.

Scheimpflug imaging helped to analyze the clinical response of activated keratocytes (after CXL) to infection in terms of effect on keratoconus progression or regression. The initial response in terms mild corneal steepening within the first month post-CXL is well reported. It is followed by average flattening of about two diopters at 6 months follow-up.[26] In cases of infiltrative keratitis following CXL, there was a marked steepening (4 D) observed in one case in the early phase which later flattened (one diopter from pre-CXL status) as observed on AS-OCT. The flattening in post-CXL cases was significant (up to 11.4 D). It may be due to fibroblast activation during the inflammatory phase of infection and scar formation. This infers that severe corneal ulcers may lead to greater flattening of keratometry. Early initiation of topical steroids in these cases may help in decreasing the severity of corneal stromal melt, associated neovascularization, and subsequent scarring. The corneal infiltrates were observed at both superficial and deeper stromal levels. The majority of cases involved anterior stroma and were limited anterior to demarcation line. Scheimpflug imaging in these cases was consistent with pre-CXL readings, suggesting that anterior corneal scars following CXL did not affect the anterior and posterior curvature.

Children undergoing CXL are at higher risk of developing infiltrates. This could be due to hyperactive immune response and ocular trauma due to self-infliction. In this study, history of VKC was not observed to have an association with development of infiltrative keratitis. This could likely be due to the absence of active allergy at the time of procedure. Keratitis presenting with infiltrates should be preferably considered as infective to avoid subsequent complications. Scraping can be avoided in cases with an infiltrate size less than two millimeters. These can be managed using topical antibiotic monotherapy and steroids. In cases where the infiltrate size is greater than two millimeters, microbiological assessment is essential. After ruling out fungi and atypical microorganisms, topical fortified antibiotics and steroids should be initiated. These cases should preferably be admitted for close follow-up and to assess the clinical response.

The strengths of the study include that it is one of the largest case series in the literature comprehensively evaluating demography, socioeconomic features, clinical presentations, microbiological results, and emerging antibiotic resistance against fluoroquinolones along with in-vivo histological analysis using AS-OCT. Scheimpflug imaging aids in evaluating the effects of infiltrative keratitis in eyes with keratoconus in the proinflammatory stage. No study in the literature has analyzed these cases using imaging modalities. However, the study has its limitations as well. The study design is retrospective observational type. The imaging data are available for a limited number of patients. A prospective study with inclusion of all pre-existing risk factors and through clinical design would provide a more detailed insight to cases of CXL keratitis. However, it may be a challenging since the incidence of the entity varies from <0.1% to up to 2%. A longer study duration may also be required to comprehensively analyze all risk factors and outcomes. Future studies which prospectively study the ocular microbiome in cases undergoing CXL in the preoperative phase and postoperative phase will further guide on the role on ocular surface environment in keratitis.

Conclusion

To conclude, CXL keratitis is a complex clinical entity distinct from other cases of keratitis. The pediatric age group is at higher risk of developing infiltrates. The etiological agent may vary from bacteria (Gram positive cocci) and fungi to Acanthamoeba. Multifocal stromal infiltrates are classically observed in these cases. Early presentation, timely treatment, and use of topical steroids are generally associated with improved visual outcomes.
