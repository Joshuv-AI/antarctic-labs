Electric field–induced ferromagnetic domain change by ferroelectric topological domain switching in Co-substituted BiFeO3 nanodots
Authors: Koomok Lee, Peter Meisenheimer, Paul Stevenson, Yasuhito Nagase, Kei Shigematsu, Ramamoorthy Ramesh, Masaki Azuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electric field–induced magnetization reversal accompanying polarization switching is promising for low–power consumption, nonvolatile, voltage-write, magnetic-read memory applications. Perovskite BiFe0.9Co0.1O3 is a room-temperature multiferroic material in which both ferroelectric and weakly ferromagnetic orders coexist, with spontaneous magnetization coupled to the ferroelectric polarization. Here, we report electric field–induced ferroelectric and ferromagnetic domain changes in BiFe0.9Co0.1O3 nanodots using a combination of piezoresponse microscopy and scanning nitrogen-vacancy center magnetometry assisted by image analysis techniques to directly observe both ferroic orders on the nanometer scale. The complex ferroelectric domains present in a 190-nanometer structure which can be switched from a net-down to a net-up polarization by scanning with a biased cantilever, accompanied by reversal of both in-plane and out-of-plane components of the magnetization. This directly demonstrates electric field–induced magnetization reversal accompanying 180° polarization switching in a complex structure of a scale relevant to the semiconductor industry, creating a potential path for next generation memory devices.

INTRODUCTION

Information technology is fast developing with the advent of artificial intelligence and diffusion of data centers, which require huge amounts of data capacity and high-speed processing (1, 2). In such a situation, optimization of overall device performance is of great importance maintaining high energy efficiency. Application of multiferroic materials with coupled ferroelectric polarization and ferromagnetic moments have great potential to minimize the current-generated joule heating, while achieving nonvolatile, large data storage (3–7). Perovskite BiFeO3 (BFO) is a room-temperature multiferroics with coupled ferroelectric polarization (P ~ 100 μC/cm2) (8, 9) and spin-canted G-type antiferromagnetism with long-range spin cycloidal modulation (~63 nm) propagating orthogonal to the polarization (10). Electric field switching of a ferromagnetic moment coupled to the antiferromagnetic spin structure of BFO thin films through exchange bias has demonstrated a reduction in energy consumption down to ~100 μJ/cm2, an order of magnitude lower than spin transfer torque magnetoresistive random access memory devices (4, 11). Recent studies have also shown electrically switchable magnon transport associated with the spin cycloid and electrical detection through a spin Hall layer in BFO thin films (12–14). This simplifies magnetic readout without ferromagnet enabling ultrafast terahertz switching operation.

As the dimension of structures is decreased from two-dimensional (2D) to scale down devices, complex ferroelectric topologies such as vortices or quadrant center–type structures confined in nanoscale regions have been reported in BFO (15–21). At device scales relevant for electronic applications, these interface-dependent textures become dominant, and electric field manipulation of abovementioned ferroelectric topological structures is attracting attention for showing reversible switching behavior. However, it remains unclear how the magnetic domains are correlated with these ferroelectric topologies and are reconfigured under an electric field. Moreover, micrometer-scale magnon transport readout schemes would need to be scaled down within a nanometer scale–confined geometry to detect the variation or even disappearance of spin cycloid. Therefore, from the viewpoint of scaling with semiconductor electronics, it would be beneficial to introduce a directly observable net magnetization coupled to the ferroelectric polarization.

For this purpose, Co substitution on BFO has been shown to be an effective approach by breaking the spin cycloid, resulting in a G-type canted collinear spin structure with spontaneous magnetization of ~0.03 bohr magneton (μB)/formula unit (f.u.) at room temperature (Fig. 1) (22–26). From density functional theory (27) and Mössbauer spectroscopy (28), the weakly ferromagnetic moment induced by spin canting has six possible directions within a magnetic easy plane orthogonal to the polarization. By applying an electric field along [001]pc (pc indicates pseudocubic) direction in BiFe0.9Co0.1O3 (BFCO) single crystals, reorientation of the magnetic easy plane was demonstrated in connection with 71° polarization switching (29). Furthermore, by combined piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) and magnetic force microscopy, the direct observation of out-of-plane magnetization reversal was confirmed accompanying 71° (109°) polarization switching in (001)pc ((110)pc)–oriented BFCO thin films grown by pulsed laser deposition (PLD) (28, 30–34). If this can be extended to electrical manipulation of net magnetization at scales relevant to electronic devices, it offers a pathway to multivalued magnetic memory applications with low power consumption per switching.

In this work, we demonstrate electric field–induced local magnetoelectric switching behavior and direct observation of magnetic domain reconfiguration correlated with ferroelectric topological domains in (001)-oriented BFCO nanodot arrays. Each nanodot, with an average of 190 nm in diameter, was fabricated by PLD using an anodized porous alumina oxide (AAO) mask on Nb:SrTiO3 (STO) (001), as described previously (35, 36). From PFM measurements, ferroelectric topological center–type domains were shown to reconfigure from an initial downward center-convergent structure to an upward center-divergent structure by applying an electric field. The correlated magnetic domain was then mapped by highly sensitive scanning diamond nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center magnetometry (SNVM) (34, 37, 38) assisted by an image processing technique. By applying an out-of-plane electric field, direct observation of both in-plane and out-of-plane magnetization reversal was demonstrated. This proceeds through reorientation of the magnetization accompanying polarization switching in the topological quadrant center domain in multiple dots.

RESULTS

Ferroelectric domain in BFCO nanodots

BFCO nanodot arrays with a diameter of 190 nm were deposited through porous AAO mask on Nb:STO (001) substrate. The pore size was tuned by two-step anodization in malonic solution and the epitaxy of rhombohedrally distorted nanodots was confirmed by x-ray diffraction, as described in (35). The epitaxy of rhombohedrally distorted nanodots was confirmed by x-ray diffraction with out-of-plane lattice constants of 4.022 Å (not shown). Local mapping of round-shaped nanodots was performed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Fig. 2B). Ferroelectric properties were examined in a single dot by landing the PFM tip and sweeping the tip-bias voltage to observe clear butterfly-like amplitude and saturated phase hysteresis loops (fig. S1). Downward polarization was confirmed in the as-grown state from the hysteresis shift toward the negative voltage side, indicating the interfacial built-in field pointing downward. Initially, ferroelectric domains were investigated by PFM on the as-grown BFCO nanodots.

Figure 2 (C to H) shows the vertical and two lateral (x and y components) PFM images on multiple nanodots within a 2 μm–by–2 μm area. Lateral PFM (L-PFMx, L-PFMy) images were mapped with the slow scanning of cantilever directed either along x || [100]pc and y || [010]pc by rotating the sample 90° with respect to the microscope (fig. S3). Obtained amplitude and phase signals were then converted as Rcosθ (R and θ denote the amplitude and phase angle, respectively). Vertical PFM (V-PFM) images show a uniform, downward polarization state, whereas in L-PFMs, domain boundaries divide the nanodots into left/right (Fig. 2D) and up/down (Fig. 2E) halves. These indicate the formation of topological center–type domain structures where four-quadrant polarization components are separated by charged domain walls (16, 20). Next, out-of-plane polarization switching was performed by scanning with a cantilever biased at −26 V along −y || [01¯0]pc slow scan direction within 1 μm–by–1 μm area (note S1). It should be noted that the switching of out-of-plane polarization components was observed from downward to upward even in the region slightly out of the poled area (Fig. 2F). As the out-of-plane polarization is reversed, the in-plane polarization components were also switched from inward to outward near the poled region (Fig. 2, G and H), indicating topological domain reconstruction from center-convergent structure to center-divergent one. A 180° polarization switching occurs in 190-nm (001)-oriented BFCO nanodots, which is distinct from BFCO (001) thin films where 71° switching is observed while preserving the stripe pattern domain (28). Note that the locations of domain boundaries in poled dots were shifted leftward/upward affected by the cantilever motion during the poling scan. In addition to the fast scan direction, the effective in-plane electric field, called the trailing field (28), is known to be applied during poling along the slow scan direction, suppressing the northward polarization variants. Figure 2 (I and J) shows the 3D polarization maps of selected dots (labeled as I and II) inside the poled region. Vector PFM was used to reconstruct the 3D polarization map in a single dot by combining V-PFM, L-PFMx, and L-PFMy images. This demonstrates the reconstruction from topological downward center-convergent to upward center-divergent structures by electric field poling.

Ferromagnetic domain and magnetization reversal by polarization switching in BFCO nanodots

Next, the ferromagnetic domains in the same region as in Fig. 2 were mapped using SNVM. Figure 3 (B to E) shows the topography and stray magnetic field along the NV quantization axis (BNV) of both the as-grown state and after electrical poling. Since the location of nanodots in BNV image is shifted with respect to AFM because the location of a single NV defect is deviated away from the tip center or perturbed by surface morphology, we detect the location of dots shown in dashed circle by comparative analysis–based image processing (detailed methods are given in note S2). Before and after electrical poling, approximately the same magnetic contrast was detected in several dots located outside the poled region surrounded by green dashed circle as shown in fig. S5. It is worth mentioning that the magnitude of the observed stray field corresponds to a magnetic moment of ~0.2 μB/f.u. This increased moment is close to the values in BFO films with mosaic-like ferroelectric domains (39), while an order of magnitude larger than that of BFO films with ferroelectric topological center–type domain and canted collinear antiferromagnetism (21). In our previous density functional theory calculation, the enhancement of net moment as large as 0.4 μB/f.u. was expected in BFCO depending on the canting angle between the Co and Fe (40). We consider that stray magnetic field emanating from nanodots with different surface geometries from thin film could have led to the overestimation of BNV (41, 42).

Figure 3F shows the ferromagnetic domains in selected dots (labeled as I and II) inside the poled area. Initially, ferromagnetic multidomain structures were revealed in which several blue and red magnetic contrasts were separated by domain boundaries in a single dot. After electric field poling, magnetic contrast change was observed where reversal of red domains to blue domains and vice versa preserves the geometry of the variants. This change in the magnetic contrast indicates a reversal of magnetization. As shown in Fig. 1C, spontaneous magnetization has one of six possible directions perpendicular to the electric polarization along <111>pc directions. The present nanodot sample has one of the 64 possible magnetization orientations associated with total four polarization variants. Considering the 180° polarization switching from the initial downward center-convergent to upward center-divergent domains, the magnetic easy plane is not required to change. This is illustrated in Fig. 3G. Therefore, the corresponding four magnetic easy planes can be drawn perpendicular to the polarization components. Given the complex magnetoelectric coupling relations in a nanodot, reconstruction of nontrivial magnetization from current stray field measurement is extremely challenging. Nevertheless, by hypothesizing that magnetizations constrained within the four easy planes would reflect the magnetization components viewed along x, y, and z directions, we project magnetizations of Mi (i = x, y, z) from stray field BNV (note S3) and evaluate magnetization reversal behavior after poling. Figure 3H shows Mi in dot I. By dividing each Mi into four domain variants, out-of-plane magnetization reversal was confirmed in four quadrants from Mz, while in-plane magnetization reversal was also demonstrated in the second, third, and fourth quadrants from Mx and the first and fourth quadrants in My, respectively. We can then deduce the magnetization orientation in a nanodot (Mdot) by allocating one magnetic easy axis where three possible directions within the same quadrant from each Mi coincide or narrowing down to at most three based on the orientations in Mz. As a result of electric poling, we can conclude that magnetization in dot I is reversed by reorienting 120° in the second and third quadrants and 180° the fourth quadrant, respectively, while the first quadrant is considered to be reoriented between 60° and 180°. Undetermined orientation in the first quadrant before poling could be attributed to several reasons including uncompensated spins from edge surface or domain boundaries, as well as measurement artifacts. Although complete reconstruction including magnitude and distinct orientation of nontrivial magnetization is insufficient in our current measurement, we would like to highlight that this method can effectively visualize the magnetization rotation by narrowing down the orientation of magnetization into one of six within the sixfold magnetic easy planes in each quadrant. Therefore, complex magnetoelectric coupling is uncovered by comparing the magnetic textures before and after the ferroelectric switching in multiple dots.

Confirmation of coupling of magnetization and polarization switching in BFCO nanodots

Last, direct coupling of magnetization and polarization switching is further confirmed in other selected dots I and II (Fig. 4). Figure 4 (A to D) compares the images of vector PFM and BNV of nanodots in as-grown and poled states. Figure 4 (E and F) shows the polarization switching and magnetization reversal in dots I and II. For a ferroelectric domain, the reconstruction from downward center-convergent to upward center-divergent states occurs, while its corresponding ferromagnetic domain is changed by reorienting magnetizations within the easy planes, which are not altered by 180° polarization switching. This is the first direct observation of electric field–induced multiferroic magnetization reversal in both in-plane and out-of-plane directions in nanodots. Magnetoelectric switching behaviors in dots other than I and II are demonstrated in note S4.

Electric field control of bistable domain wall conductance in topological center domain structures in BFO nanoscale geometries has been drawing attention for nonvolatile memory applications (18, 43). When the lateral size shrinks to 10-nm scale, topological center domains become energetically unfavorable (43), and even a ferroelectric single-domain structure can be stabilized and observed in our previously fabricated 60-nm-sized BFCO nanodots (35). At this intermediate 190-nm length scale, the presence of conductive domain walls can contribute to an additional functionality. In BFCO, the magnetoelectric coupling behavior appears to be independent to domain boundary conditions, making this promising for single-phase, atomic-scale magnetic memory media.

In the present study, electric field–induced magnetization reversal was demonstrated by scanning a voltage-biased cantilever. Further investigation is required to achieve deterministic control of magnetization reversal by polarization switching such as the application of uniformly distributed electric field using ring-shape electrode to reproduce equivalent size of both quadrant ferroelectric and ferromagnetic domain changes (17, 18, 21). Moreover, reduction into fourfold magnetic anisotropy within the easy plane through epitaxial tensile strain could simplify the detection and control of magnetization reversal behavior. The storage density of 60-nm single-domain BFCO nanodots was estimated to be as high as 10 Gbit/cm2 (35). Given the complex magnetoelectric switching behavior in BFCO nanodots, deterministic control of multiple ferromagnetic orientations could maximize the storage density up to six times reaching 6 × 10 Gbit/cm2 BFCO nanodots of 190 nm consist of four quadrant domains potentially have 64 magnetization orientations enabling ultrahigh areal density. Although deterministic control of total 64 would be unrealistically challenging and complicated for practical device applications, this study provides an experimental platform to design next-generation, high-density magnetic memory by combing multiple magnetic orientations from each quadrant domain as a memory bit.

DISCUSSION

In this work, we demonstrate magnetization reversal by polarization switching in BFCO nanodots with 190 nm in diameter deposited on Nb:STO (001) substrates. We observe electric field–induced ferroelectric topological domain reconstruction from downward center-convergent to upward center-divergent polarizations by PFM measurements. In addition, clear stray field mapping from weakly ferromagnetic domains is enabled by SNVM measurements, confirming ferromagnetic multidomain structure. After 180° polarization switching, magnetization reversal of both in-plane and out-of-plane directions was demonstrated. This proceeds through the reorientation of magnetization within the sixfold magnetic easy plane in magnetic domain variants and is promising for low–power consumption, single-phase, voltage-write, magnetic-read memory devices. We hypothesize that the deterministic, electrical control of multiple magnetization orientations in BFCO nanodots could provide a framework for designing atomic-scale, ultrahigh-density, multivalued magnetic memory.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

The BFCO nanodot sample was prepared on the basis of our previously reported paper (35). The AAO mask was fabricated by two-step anodization of Al foil in malonic acid (36) and transferred to the Nb:STO (001) substrate. The BFCO nanodots were grown by PLD. KrF laser fluence of 0.8 to 1.0 J/cm2 was used with frequency of 2 Hz. Substrate temperature was set to 600°C, and oxygen partial pressure was kept to 1.4 Pa during the deposition time of 5 hours. After the deposition, the AAO mask was mechanically eliminated with the aid of sodium hydroxide.

PFM measurement

Topography was measured by AFM in contact mode. Total out-of-plane and in-plane polarization components were mapped by PFM in an asylum MFP-3D. Vertical and two L-PFM signals were collected using dual ac resonance tracking mode. Both topography and PFM images before and after poling are shown in fig. S3. In V-PFM images, topographic artifacts attributed to abrupt morphology change depending on the scanning orientation, which appear at the bottom of dot edge, were corrected to either upward or downward. After physically rotating the sample position, L-PFM images were scanned while longer axis of cantilever directed either along x || [100]pc and y || [010]pc. Both amplitude and phase angle were converted to Rcosθ (R and θ denote the amplitude and phase angle, respectively) and combined to reconstruct vector PFM images using Python. To enable polarization switching, −26 V was applied through cantilever tip-bias voltage combined with external dc power source (Agilent, E3611A).

SNVM measurement

Both topography and stray magnetic field were mapped by commercial scanning NV microscopy (Qnami ProteusQ). A Qnami Quantilever probe tip was used containing single NV center implemented into a diamond with (100) surface orientation. Magnetic field was quantitatively measured in full B mode by sweeping the electron spin resonance frequency within 2.87 to 3.10 GHz in optically detected magnetic resonance spectra using a bias magnet of 3 mT. The signal was denoised by removing high-frequency components from the NV signal in Fourier space to achieve clear resolution of SNVM images.

Detection of nanodots in SNVM image

The location of nanodots in AFM and SNVM is slightly different because of differences in tip geometry. Location shift in both images originated from the deviation of a single NV defect away from the probe tip center. The circular shape of dot edges was visible in SNVM images by applying Laplacian or high-frequency filters. Location and shape of dots found in filtered images was found to be reasonably overlapped with respect to topography by entirely shifting the position of dots (detailed methods are shown in note S2).
