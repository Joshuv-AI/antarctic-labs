Excess EEG beta-band oscillations in Dup15q syndrome correlate with clinical severity
Authors: Joerg F Hipp, Stormy Chamberlain, Charlotte DiStefano, Joel Frohlich, Maria-Clemencia Hernandez, Vidya Saravanapandian, Vanessa Vogel-Farley, Celia Goeldner, Shafali S Jeste
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDup15q syndrome is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder caused by copy number gains of 15q11.2-q13.1, which include UBE3A and three GABA-A receptor subunit genes among several other genes. The contribution of the different genes to etiology remains elusive. Excess EEG beta-band power is a characteristic electrophysiological phenotype of this syndrome and a candidate biomarker of increased GABA-A receptor activity. Here we investigate the link between clinical and EEG phenotypes. We implemented a method to isolate oscillatory activity in the beta-band (16–32 Hz) from the background of the EEG power spectrum, i.e., locally referenced power (LRPβ) and applied it to EEGs from children with Dup15q syndrome (n = 52) and age-matched typically developing children (n = 14). We then investigated the relationship of LRPβ with cognitive ability (assessed with the Mullen Scales of Early Learning or the Differential Ability Scale), and adaptive behaviors (assessed with the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales). LRPβ was strongly elevated in individuals with Dup15q syndrome compared to typically developing children (effect size, Hedges’ g = +1.84). LRPβ significantly correlated with both cognitive ability (developmental quotient; r = −0.48, p = 0.008) and adaptive functioning (Adaptive behavior composite score; r = −0.56, p = 0.002) in individuals without a history of epilepsy. Our results suggest that excess GABA-A receptor function is a relevant component of Dup15q syndrome pathophysiology and further substantiates the relevance of EEG beta-band power as a biomarker in Dup15q syndrome.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Neuroscience, Biomarkers, Physiology

Introduction

Dup15q syndrome is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder with a birth prevalence of 1 in 10,000–20,000 (see Supplementary Information). Hypotonia and feeding difficulties in infancy and global developmental delay, including motor, cognitive and language impairments, and intellectual disability are the most frequent features [1–4]. Autism spectrum disorder and behavioral challenges are also common in Dup15q syndrome. More than half of individuals with Dup15q syndrome will experience seizures throughout their lifetime. The age at onset and severity of epilepsy are strongly associated with the degree of impairment across cognitive, language, motor, and adaptive domains [1, 5].

Genetics of Dup15q syndrome (see Supplementary Introduction for more details)

Dup15q syndrome results from additional maternal copies of the 15q11.2-q13.1 region [3]. The majority ( ~ 60%–80%) and most severely affected individuals present with an isodicentric (IDIC) supernumerary chromosome of maternal origin, resulting in triplication of the chromosomal segment (tetrasomy). Most of the remaining 20%–40% population have interstitial tandem duplications (INT), resulting in three total copies (trisomy) of the 15q11.2-q13.1 region [3, 6]. The deletion of maternal 15q11.2-q13.1 causes a different neurodevelopmental disorder, Angelman syndrome (AS) [7], further illustrating the importance of expression levels of genes from this region. Paternally derived duplications are not fully penetrant and are associated with a milder and different neurodevelopmental phenotype [3, 8, 9].

The specific role of the maternal allele strongly implies UBE3A, the only gene that is paternally imprinted in the 15q11.2-q13.1 region, in the etiology of Dup15q syndrome. UBE3A is also the causative gene in AS, where pathogenic variants of the maternal copy are sufficient to cause the syndrome. Furthermore, there is evidence that non-imprinted, bi-allelically expressed genes additionally contribute to the etiology of the syndrome. A maternal duplication restricted to UBE3A has been associated with mild developmental delay as well as neuropsychiatric phenotypes in one family but did not show core features of Dup15q syndrome [10]. Studies with Ube3a overexpressing mice led to conflicting results, with most recent findings indicating indistinguishable molecular and behavioral phenotypes compared to wild-type controls, despite increased mortality when challenged with seizures [11]. Furthermore, genes beyond UBE3A are also relevant in AS, where maternal deletions of 15q11.2-13.1 result in a substantially more severe phenotype than pathogenic variants of UBE3A [12, 13].

Of the bi-allelically expressed genes in 15q11.2-13.1, the GABA-A receptor subunit genes (GABRB3, GABRA5, GABRG3) are considered as key genes of interest, given the central role of GABA-A receptors in brain development and function [14, 15] and the link to neurodevelopmental phenotypes of gain of function pathogenic variants of any of the three genes [16–21]. The three genes code for the β3, α5, and γ3 subunits of GABA-A receptors and can co-assemble to form functional pentameric GABA-A α5-containing receptors.

Increased oscillatory EEG activity in the beta-frequency range is a candidate biomarker of excess GABA-A receptor activity in Dup15q syndrome

EEG beta-band power is an established pharmacodynamic biomarker for GABA-A receptor modulating drugs. In particular, beta-band power increases with GABA-A enhancing drugs such as benzodiazepines [22, 23] and reduces with negative modulation of GABA-A α5 receptors [24, 25].

Individuals with Dup15 syndrome have a distinctive EEG phenotype characterized by excess beta-band (12–30 Hz) oscillations [3, 9, 26] with more than 100% excess power compared to typically developing controls [27]. Excess beta-band oscillations are also present in patients with paternal 15q duplications [27], and conversely, a directionally opposite EEG phenotype (i.e., decreased beta-band power) is found in individuals with AS caused by 15q11.2-q13.1 deletion compared to individuals with AS caused by UBE3A pathogenic variants [28].

Consequently, beta-band power has been proposed as a biomarker of Dup15q syndrome reflecting enhanced, perhaps excessive, GABA-A activity resulting from the GABA-A receptor subunit gene cluster copy number gains [29].

Is there a link between EEG beta-band oscillations and clinical symptom severity in Dup15q syndrome?

A key question to understand the etiology of Dup15q syndrome is whether this EEG biomarker of GABA-A activity relates to clinical severity and thus stratifies individuals with Dup15q syndrome. An association between beta power and the level of developmental disability would suggest that excess GABA-A function is an important part of the pathophysiology of the syndrome that would then serve as a promising target of pharmacotherapy, with beta oscillations then serving as a measure of drug target engagement of medications that target GABA neurotransmission.

Prior studies have failed to establish such a relationship [29], which may be explained by the use of absolute beta power, which captures a mixture of various electrophysiological signals and may lack sensitivity to signals specifically related to GABA-A dysfunction.

In this study, we implemented a method to isolate EEG beta-band oscillations from the background signal to derive a sensitive measure of beta oscillations, i.e., locally referenced beta power (LRPβ). We hypothesized that absolute beta power (ABSβ) is rather nonspecific and, thus, likely insensitive to subtle but meaningful individual differences in GABA-A function in Dup15q syndrome. Here, we show that LRPβ is strongly elevated in individuals with Dup15q syndrome compared to typically developing children and, moreover, is significantly correlated with symptom severity in individuals with Dup15q syndrome without a history of epilepsy.

Methods and materials

See the Supplemental Methods and Materials for an extended description of the methods.

Inclusion criteria

Individuals with Dup15q syndrome and deletion AS had a clinical and genetic diagnosis (copy number gains of 15q11.2-q13.1 / deletion of 15q11.2-13.1). Typically developing controls had no known genetic, psychiatric or neurological abnormalities.

Dataset 1: EEG and clinical measures

Subsets of dataset 1 were analyzed in previous publications [5, 26, 27, 29]. Participants were assessed under the supervision of a licensed clinical psychologist. Assessments were performed in accordance with the UCLA Institutional Review Board (IRB). Informed written consent was obtained from parents of participants prior to the start of all study activities. Assessments included awake EEG recordings and clinical measures. EEG data and clinical scales were considered contemporaneous, and thus included in the correlation analysis, if they were recorded within 14 days of each other.

EEG recordings

EEG data were collected in the awake state from children with Dup15q syndrome and age matched typically developing children, see [27] for details. We excluded data from participants with benzodiazepines as antiseizure medication (ASM). See Table S1 for the sample size of EEG recordings.

Clinical measures

Cognitive development was assessed with either the Differential Abilities Scale – Second Edition (DAS-II) [30] or the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL) [31]. Ratio scores for full-scale developmental quotient (FSDQ), non-verbal developmental quotient (NVDQ) and verbal developmental quotient (VDQ) were calculated for each [32].

Adaptive behavior was assessed via a simi-structured interview with the caregiver, using the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales-2ndEdition (VABS-II) [33] or 3rdEdition (VABS-3) [34]. See Table S2 summarizing the sample size for clinical scores, including a breakdown by scale, genotype, and history of epilepsy.

Dataset 2: EEG recordings for test-retest reliability analyses

The second dataset was used to estimate the test-retest reliability of LRPβ. Overnight clinical EEG recordings from 12 individuals with Dup15q syndrome, collected at the Pediatric Neurophysiology Laboratory at the UCLA Ronald Reagan Medical Center, were included in the study (7 females, 10 IDIC, 2 INT, age: 59 ± 41 months, range 9–137 months). Within these long-term recordings we identified for each participant three 10-min sections in the awake state with good data quality.

Dataset 3: EEG recordings from individuals with AS and controls

The third dataset was used to test the hypothesis that LRPβ is decreased in the EEG of individuals with AS, who have only one copy of 15q11.2-q13.1 that includes the GABA-A receptor subunit gene cluster. Overnight EEG recordings were performed at the patients home within the FREESIAS observational study [35]. 10 min awake EEG data was analyzed from the first visit for all available participants (28 Del AS, 9 Mut, 13 Controls).

EEG preprocessing and spectral analysis

EEG data were bandpass filtered (0.5–45 Hz, FIR filter, 2 sec), carefully cleaned from artifacts (interpolation of artifactual channels; exclusion of sections with strong artifacts; removal of independent components that captured eye movement, facial muscle activity or technical artifacts) and re-referenced to average across all channels for analysis. This careful pre-processing was done to ensure that signals analyzed reflect brain activity rather than technical or physiological artifacts. Accounting for muscle activity is of particular importance for studying EEG signals at high frequencies like the beta-band range [36]. Power spectral estimates were derived using Morlet Wavelets (https://roche.github.io/neuro-meeglet/; Parameter: bw = 1/3 oct, corresponding to f/σf = 10.23) [37].

Locally referenced power (LRP)

We implemented a method to isolate and quantify oscillatory neuronal activity from electrophysiological signals, LRP. This approach entails the estimation of the background activity for the frequency range of interest, which may comprise non-oscillatory signals and broader-band phenomena that extend beyond the frequency range of interest and the superposition of weaker oscillatory signals extending across neighboring frequencies and appearing without a clear oscillatory pattern. The approach then removes this estimated background signal from the absolute power in the frequency range of interest. Here we applied this approach to isolate oscillatory activity in the beta frequency range (defined as 16–32 Hz) from the EEG, which we refer to as LRPβ. The background power in the frequency range of interest is derived by linear interpolation of log-log-transformed power and frequency using two points at a lower and higher frequency neighboring the frequency range of interest (i.e., reference supports, here 12 and 40 Hz, Fig. 1B–D). The background signal for the frequency range of interest is then estimated as the integral of this background power spectral density over the beta frequency range (16–32 Hz). LRPβ is then defined as the logarithm of the ratio between the beta power (integral over the beta frequency range) and the estimate of the background power. We also investigated a variant, LRPβ_Max, where we used the maximal value in the beta frequency range of the log-transformed ratio of the power spectral density and the estimated background power spectral density (Fig. 1B).

A method that is gaining growing interest, models the power spectral density as superposition of a global aperiodic 1/fα component (aperiodic signal) and oscillatory signals (periodic signals) [38]. To link the LRP metric to this body of literature, we investigated analogous measures by using the global 1/fα component (aperiodic signal) as the background signal estimate (“globally referenced power”, GRP; GRPβ and GRPβ_Max; Figs. S1A, B). To derive the aperiodic 1/fα signal, we used the python implementation of fooof / specparam (https://specparam-tools.github.io/).

We also compared the above approaches metrics to absolute beta power (ABSβ), i.e., EEG signal power in the beta frequency range (16–32 Hz).

Statistical analyses

Statistical comparisons of EEG metrics between groups were performed using unpaired t-test allowing unequal variance. Effect sizes were reported using Hedges’ g.

To ensure that possible age-dependences of EEG metrics and clinical scores did not lead to trivial correlations between the two, we removed the effect of age using a linear regression of log-age (see Supplementary Information).

To analyze cross-sectional relationships between EEG metrics and clinical parameters, we used Pearson correlation coefficients (Spearman rank correlations for Supplementary analyses). We derived p-values and confidence intervals exploiting that the Fisher z-transformed correlation coefficients are approximately normally distributed with a standard error of 1/√(n-3) (1.06/√(n-3) for Spearman rank correlations). Given our directional hypothesis (greater EEG abnormality with lower clinical scores), we report 1-sided tests and show 90% confidence intervals.

Results

Visual inspection of the EEG power spectra of individuals with Dup15q syndrome revealed clear beta-band peaks – reflecting excess GABA-A receptor activity ([27], see Introduction) – for almost all individuals (Fig. 1A). Beta-band peaks are superimposed on a background that substantially differs between individuals, which likely reflects other neuronal processes that may not be directly related to GABA-A receptor signaling of interest. Thus, absolute beta power (ABSβ), as used in prior studies, quantifies a mixture of the beta-band oscillations and the background signal. We proposed that ABSβ is therefore a rather unspecific and possibly insensitive proxy of the excess GABA-A function in Dup15q syndrome.

Isolating EEG beta oscillations using LRP

We implemented a method to isolate beta-band activity from the background signal (see Methods and Materials for details; integrated over 16 to 32 Hz; Fig. 1A–D). We estimated the background signal in the beta-band by interpolation from power at lower and higher neighboring frequencies (12 and 40 Hz, respectively). We then removed the estimated background from the beta power. The extraction of LRPβ is illustrated for power spectra from six individuals with Dup15q syndrome and two typical developing (TD) controls in Fig. 1E.

Differences in LRPβ between children with Dup15q syndrome and TD children

LRPβ was largely independent of age (Table S2) and was strongly elevated in children with Dup15q syndrome compared to TD children (LRPβ: ES = 1.84, p = 7.4 10−13; Fig. 2A, B; Table 1, S1). The difference was found relatively consistently across the scalp, though the effect was least pronounced at electrodes C3 and C4 (Fig. 2C). The difference between groups for LRPβ was much stronger compared to ABSβ (ES = 0.99, p = 2.8·10−5).

Differences in LRPβ in Dup15q syndrome subgroups

LRPβ was numerically higher in IDIC (triplication of maternal 15q11.2-q13.1) compared to INT (duplication of maternal 15q11.2-q13.1), i.e., the expected direction of a putative gene-dose effect, but the distributions were largely overlapping, and differences were not significant (Table 1; ES = 0.25, p = 0.21; Fig. 2D). A topographic analysis of the difference suggested that there may be higher LRPβ at temporal electrodes in IDIC compared to INT but this needs further investigation with more data (Fig. 2C). A diagnosis of epilepsy was not significantly reflected in the magnitude of LRPβ (Fig. 2D).

Test-retest reliability of LRPβ in Dup15q syndrome

A useful metric should not only separate cases from controls but should also be consistent when re-measured. We investigated the test-retest reliability of LRPβ using three 10 min awake EEG sections extracted from overnight recordings from n = 12 individuals with Dup15q syndrome (see Methods and Materials; two 10 min signals from the afternoon of the first day 10 min signal from the next morning). Test-retest reliability quantified as intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was excellent (0.89; Table 2) [39].

Investigating the relationship between LRPβ and clinical symptom severity

The substantially better separation of children with Dup15q syndrome and TD children using LRPβ compared to ABSβ (used in previous studies) suggests improved quantification of GABA-A related beta oscillations. Furthermore, there was substantial spread in LRPβ across the Dup15q syndrome population and a high test-retest reliability. Together these characteristics strongly support using LRPβ to test for an association with clinical symptom severity.

About half of the individuals with Dup15q syndrome develop epilepsy. Individuals with Dup15q syndrome and epilepsy are on average more severely impaired than individuals without epilepsy [5] (see also Fig. S4), reflected in a shift to a lower range of scores on the outcome measures, and with less dynamic range and risk of flooring effects. Epilepsy and more severe clinical phenotypes are both manifestations of a more deviant neurodevelopmental trajectory, which may be caused by factors that are independent or more distant from GABA-A Receptor function, and thus weaken the correlation with GABA-A specific beta-band power [40]. We therefore decided to test for a relationship between LRPβ and symptom severity in individuals with and without epilepsy separately with the hypothesis that a correlation would be stronger and therefore easier to identify in individuals without epilepsy.

The correlations between LRPβ and clinical scores in children with Dup15q syndrome without epilepsy were significant for all scores investigated (all p ≤ 0.02, r: −0.56 to −0.40, Table 3, Fig. 3). Correlations were spatially broad but weakest at temporal electrodes (Fig. S5). The negative sign of the correlation coefficients is in the expected direction, i.e., higher EEG beta-band oscillations are associated with greater impairments in adaptive functioning and cognitive development. In line with our previous work [29], the correlation was much weaker and only partially significant with ABSβ (Table 3).

Alternative methods to isolate EEG beta oscillations from background activity

As a sensitivity analysis and to facilitate linking our work to other work, we investigated another approach that differed in the estimation of the background signal by using the 1/fα component of a global fit of the power spectrum comprising a superposition of oscillatory signals and an aperiodic 1/fα component (method: specparam / fooof [38]; Fig. S1). We refer to this measure as global relative beta-band power (GRPβ). We also analyzed variants that used the maximum of the normalized power spectral density in the beta frequency range instead of the power in the beta frequency range (LRPβ_Max, GRPβ_Max; Fig. 1B, S2). All variants were highly correlated with each other and showed very similar characteristics in terms of separation between Dup15q syndrome and TD children and test-retest reliability (Tables 1, 2, S2; Fig. S3). The correlation between all metrics and clinical scores in children with Dup15q syndrome without epilepsy were significant (all p < 0.05, r: −0.59 to −0.40, Table 3).

Investigating LRPβ in AS with 15q11.2-13.1 deletions

Most individuals with AS have a deletion of 15q11.2-13.1 (Del-AS), i.e. the genetic converse of Dup15q syndrome (see Introduction). Motivated by the results in Dup15q syndrome, we wondered if LRPβ would also be of utility to isolate beta-band abnormalities in Del-AS that may relate to the deletion of the GABA-A receptor subunit genes on the maternal allele. Mirroring the genetics, we indeed found that LRPβ was significantly reduced in Del-AS compared to TD controls, i.e. beta power dropped below the interpolated baseline (TD controls: 0.99 ± 0.915, Del-AS: −1.11 ± 1.070 (mean ± SD); Difference ES = −2.11, p = 3.0 10−7; Fig. S6). The other LRPβ variants (LRPβ_Max, GRPβ, GRPβ_Max) do not conceptually align with a decrease of spectral power below the background (the maximum of the normalized power; specparam / fooof: superposition of oscillatory signals and an aperiodic 1/fα component) and were not analyzed for the Del-AS.

Discussion

Increased EEG beta-band power has been proposed as a biomarker of excess GABA neurotransmission, resulting from overexpression of several GABA-A receptor subunit genes in Dup15q syndrome [3, 9, 26, 27, 29]. Our work extends prior work by introducing a more sensitive metric of beta-band oscillations, LRPβ, that provides a substantially greater effect size for the comparison with TD children and reveals a correlation with overall clinical symptom severity across multiple developmental domains. These results suggest that excess GABA-A receptor function may play a role in the pathophysiology of Dup15q syndrome and therefore represent a valid target for therapeutic intervention, and that the EEG beta-band power may serve as a biomarker to guide drug development in this syndrome.

The spontaneous or resting state EEG signals analyzed here are a superposition of various oscillatory (or periodic) and non-oscillatory (or aperiodic) signals reflecting various different neuronal processes [38, 41, 42]. The spectral composition of these signals is a highly characteristic “fingerprint” of an individual [43]. Thus, oscillatory activity reflecting a specific neuronal process of interest, like the beta-band oscillations related to GABA-A function studied here, are hidden in a background of activity of various other neuronal processes with substantial intra-individual variability. LRP isolates oscillatory signals at specific frequencies from the background activity and, where applicable, should provide a more specific and sensitive representation of signals of interest. Our results support these general considerations by showing substantially larger effect sizes in case control differences and a correlation with symptom severity for LRPβ compared to traditionally used absolute beta-band power. Furthermore, LRPβ also revealed a decrease of beta power below the background in Del-AS, the genetic converse of Dup15q syndrome.

The topographies of the LRPβ of Dup15q syndrome to TD control differences and the correlation with clinical scores were spatially extended across the scalp, suggesting that the GABA-A abnormalities are a phenomenon spread across many cortical areas (though we did not perform source analyses in the absence of individual structural MRI scans and low channel counts).

LRP has potential applications beyond Dup15q syndrome and AS, in particular in other genetic neurodevelopmental disorders with characteristic EEG phenotypes, such as e.g. Fragile X syndrome and Rett syndrome [44, 45] and genetic developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (e.g. STXBP1, SCN1A, SYNGAP1) [46, 47]. Of note, the background signal, in particular the 1/f slope, has been shown to also contain relevant information in such disorders that may provide complementary information [48–50]. A detailed investigation of the 1/f slope in Dup15q syndrome is beyond the scope of this work.

We explored a popular alternative to LRP that differed in estimation of the background signal (local interpolation vs. global 1/fα fit, i.e. aperiodic signal) [38], which performed relatively similarly in terms of group separation and correlation with symptom severity but may differ in performance and utility in other putative applications. For example, the decrease of beta power below the background signal that we found for Del-AS is incompatible with describing the EEG as a superposition of oscillatory signals and an aperiodic 1/fα component (see Supplementary Discussion for a more detailed account).

Our results show that beta-band oscillations, quantified as LRPβ, correlated with both global developmental quotients and the level of adaptive functioning of patients with Dup15q syndrome without a history of epilepsy. Correlations were comparable in magnitude and statistically significant across language (VABS communication and VDQs), social (VABS socialization), motor domains (VABS motor) and nonverbal cognition (NVDQ). This suggests that altered neuronal network activity reflected in beta-band oscillations in Dup15q syndrome contributes to the syndrome broadly, rather than to specific symptom domains.

We did not find an association between LRPβ and symptom severity in individuals with Dup15q syndrome with epilepsy. Individuals with a history of epilepsy have more neurodevelopmental impairment and delays ([5]; see also Fig. S4), which may be caused by factors that are independent or more distant from GABAA Receptor function, and thus weaken the correlation with GABA-A specific EEG beta-band power [40]. Consequently, a weaker relationship between clinical scores and the EEG beta-band oscillations would be expected. Furthermore, a shift to a lower range of scores on the outcome measures, with less dynamic range and risk of flooring effects, is more pronounced for individuals with a history of epilepsy and may further preclude the detection of significant correlations using these metrics (see Fig. S4).

Our results add to the body of work suggesting that EEG beta-band oscillations can serve as a biomarker of GABA-A receptor dysfunction in Dup15q syndrome [9, 26, 27, 29]. We found that relevant EEG endpoints had a high to excellent test-retest reliability. Showing a link to clinical symptom severity suggests that EEG beta-band power and, therefore, excess GABA-A receptor function is not just an epiphenomenon but a relevant part of the Dup15q pathophysiology.

These results open the perspective to developing a biomarker based on EEG beta-band oscillations that has several potential contexts of use, including as a (I) surrogate endpoint for therapeutics targeting the GABA-A pathophysiology in Dup15q syndrome, (II) stratification or inclusion of patients in a clinical trial, (III) demonstrate drug target engagement and PK/PD modeling for dose finding and (IV) to support diagnosis since clinical features, including hypotonia, epilepsy, and feeding difficulties have limited diagnostic sensitivity [4], though genetic testing is becoming widely available.

Our work suggests that the increased GABA-A receptors are a relevant component of Dup15q syndrome pathophysiology. Dup15q syndrome is a multigene syndrome, and duplication of UBE3A alone may not recapitulate the disorder (see Introduction). The differential contributions of the GABA-A receptor genes, UBE3A, and possibly other genes remain an open question. It should be noted that their contributions might also be highly non-additive, i.e., dependent on each other. For example, it is conceivable that addressing either problem will dramatically improve symptoms, while alternatively, it may be the case that both need to be addressed jointly to have a meaningful effect.

Our work has some limitations. Dup15q syndrome, like many other genetic NDDs, is a rare condition, yielding smaller sample sizes than in studies of non-syndromic NDDs. The p-values (uncorrected for multiple testing) from our exploratory analyses should be interpreted as indicative of evidence strength rather than statistical conclusions. Future studies need to confirm these results with larger sample size. We also did not conduct detailed characterization of epilepsy (including age of onset and burden / severity, which are incomplete for this dataset), and these variables might have provided more insight into subset of children in which we did not find an association of clinical severity and EEG. Finally, source analyses of LRPβ may reveal spatial specificity. Using e.g. ASMs and seizure severity as covariates or as stratification factors may reveal also correlations or LRPβ and clinical scores in individuals with epilepsy.

As we move into an era of precision health in genetic NDDs, GABA-A receptors composed of the subunits encoded by the duplicated GABRB3, GABRA5, and/or GABRG3 genes represent a therapeutic approach to normalize the altered EEG beta-band power by restoring GABA signaling and consequently alleviating symptoms in Dup15q syndrome.
