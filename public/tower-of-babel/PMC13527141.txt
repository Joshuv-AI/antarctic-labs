Precise estimation of rice leaf macro and micro nutrients from multi-spectral images using neural architecture search with polynomial approximation functions
Authors: Diksha Arora, Jhilik Bhattacharya, Chinmaya Panigrahy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Estimating the nutritional status of rice leaves is crucial for efficient nutrient management and yield enhancement. Traditional wet lab analyses are time-consuming and labor-intensive. This study presents a novel deep learning-based approach utilizing multispectral images captured by unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to estimate macro/micro nutrients in rice leaves. The proposed framework integrates a differentiable neural search technique using polynomial function approximators and an adaptive activation mechanism, which not only provides improved predictive performance but also deals efficiently with limited training data. The model performance is evaluated across different treatments and crop growth stages using mean absolute error (MAE) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values. Experiments were conducted at the Punjab Agricultural University. The results demonstrate that the proposed model achieves MAE values in the range of 0.06–0.11 for SAS-I and 0.06–0.16 for SAS-II across eleven leaf macro/micro nutrients. To further evaluate the reliability of the predicted nutrients beyond the prediction error analysis, uncertainty estimation of nutrients is also performed. Comparative analysis shows that the proposed framework outperforms conventional deep learning baselines and machine learning methods in terms of accuracy and robustness. Furthermore, the t-SNE visualization of learned feature representations effectively clusters similar nutrient values while separating dissimilar ones. The robustness of the proposed framework is further validated through ablation studies, treatment-wise and plot-wise cross-validation, highlighting the contribution of individual components and their performance under varying field conditions. These findings highlight the proposed NAS-based framework for precise and reliable nutrient assessment in precision agriculture.

Introduction

Rice serves as the staple food crop for the majority of the population and is the primary crop grown in the trans-Indo-Gangetic Plains, including Punjab1. Smart farming with artificial intelligence (AI) serves as an efficient mode for enabling data-driven crop monitoring and decision-making by integrating data from sensors, remote sensing platforms, and predictive models to continuously monitor crop conditions and optimize management decisions2. Among various crop management practices, nutrient management is one of the important factors, as macro/micro nutrients are the essential elements that facilitate the optimal growth and development of crops3. There are sixteen fundamental nutrients for rice crops where nitrogen (N), potassium (K), and phosphorus (P) are primary macro-nutrients; sulfur (S), calcium (Ca), and magnesium (Mg) are secondary macro nutrients; copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), chlorine (Cl), and molybdenum (Mo) are micro nutrients4. Deficiency or toxicity of any of these nutrients can restrict typical plant growth5,6. For example, boron deficiency reduces rice yield and degrades grain quality7. Consequently, diagnosis of crop nutritional status enables early detection and prevention of diseases, ensuring the precise application of fertilizers or nutritional supplements8–12.

Fertilization plays a crucial role in plant growth and yield by supplying essential mineral nutrients. However, nutrient absorption varies significantly across spatial and temporal scales. Despite these variations, farmers often apply fertilizers uniformly based on intuitive and visual assessments, leading to their overuse13. Excessive fertilization contributes to environmental degradation through increased greenhouse gas emissions, water and soil pollution, ecosystem disruptions, and high input costs14. Therefore, frequent analysis of leaf nutrient concentration is crucial for accurately assessing crop nutritional status and addressing potential deficiencies. Traditional agronomic methods rely on regular leaf sampling for chemical analysis, which is a time-consuming, labor-intensive, and environmentally harmful process15,16. These limitations have motivated the development of non-destructive, UAV–based nutrient estimation techniques using AI and multispectral remote sensing data.

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) or drones represent a promising approach for monitoring and assessing vegetation status in crop management17. The use of drones mounted with high-tech cameras and sensors (such as RGB, thermal, multispectral, and hyperspectral) offers adequate control over several features, such as flight altitude, geographical coverage, etc., which lead to high spatial resolution data, resulting in precise crop monitoring and crop health analysis18–20. Vegetation indices have been widely explored for estimating plant nutrient concentration21,22. These were derived from spectral bands and provide interpretable features by capturing relationships between reflectance values across different wavelengths. These reflectance values are associated with plant biochemical and structural properties. They capture diverse crop characteristics, including chlorophyll content, canopy structure, and stress conditions, which are indirectly related to nutrient concentrations23,24. NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) is the most commonly used VI, effective for assessing vegetation health and crop growth. Similarly, RVI (Ratio Vegetation Index), GNDVI (Green Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), and NDRE (Normalized Difference Red Edge) have shown strong correlations with leaf nitrogen status25. Previous studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of UAV-based multispectral imagery combined with vegetation indices and feature-selection techniques for estimating both macro/micro nutrient concentrations in crop leaves26,27. Furthermore, integrating multiple vegetation indices, including NDVI, NDRE, GNDVI, and RVI, can provide a more comprehensive assessment of crop nutrient status by capturing complementary spectral information, thereby improving the robustness and reliability of nutrient estimation.

In this study, we design a neural search space using polynomial approximation functions to estimate nutrients using UAV-based multispectral images of crops at various growth stages. It is theoretically evident that using deep spectral features from optimal neural architectures will provide better estimates of crop nutrients compared to traditional machine learning techniques. Earlier reliance on traditional machine learning techniques like linear regression, random forests, and support vector machine for canopy nitrogen weight28; gradient boosting regression tree for estimating N, P, K, Mg, Ca and S29; partial least squares regression, artificial neural network (ANN), support vector regression and Gaussian process regression for N, P, and K leaf content13; multiple regression analysis to estimate N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Cu, Fe, Mn, and Zn26 was replaced with deep learning–based approaches employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs). However, selecting an optimal architecture that balances the feature dimensionality as well as the data availability is important. A study27particularly reported cases where multilayer perceptron (MLP) models performed better compared to CNN architectures in citrus leaves nutrient estimation tasks. This motivates the fact that a careful search and design of network architecture is crucial for convergence. It was further observed that many existing deep learning–based studies primarily focus on nutrient deficiency classification of N, P, and K using RGB leaf images rather than quantitative estimation. For example30, proposed an S-RiceNet-D-LightGBM model for early diagnosis of N, P, and K deficiencies, where it outperformed several pretrained CNN architectures like VGG16, ResNet101, DenseNet169, InceptionNetV3, and MobileNetV2, achieving an accuracy of 95.01%. Similarly31, addressed the identification of Ca, Fe, K, Mg, and N deficiencies using CNN. In another study32, evaluated CNN, VGG-16, and Inception-V3 architectures for classifying N, P, and K deficiencies with highest accuracy of approximately 93%, while33 proposed a customised CNN with transfer learning for multi-nutrient deficiency detection, primarily focusing on N, P, and K in paddy leaves, achieving high classification performance, with an accuracy of 98.2%. In contrast, only limited work has explored regression-based non-destructive nutrient estimation of multiple macro/micro nutrients using multispectral imagery34.

The proposed Neural Architecture Search (NAS) framework incorporates learnable polynomial approximators with adaptive activations to transform multispectral inputs into a nonlinear feature space, facilitating faster convergence and improved adaptability across varying data distributions. The hypothesis driving this study is that multispectral images possess unique spectral features that correspond to a particular nutrient amount of the crop. These features, when appropriately learnt via sequential filter transforms, can provide some mapping values for nutrient assessment. However, it is emphasized that nutrient estimation models are crop and region-specific due to variations in soil quality, climate, and cultivation practices, and it is often difficult to obtain a large dataset for local regions to train the estimation model. We hence introduce learnable polynomial approximators with adaptive activations in the network architecture to improve predictive performance with smaller dataset sizes. This work emphasizes the importance of designing and validating models on a crop-wise and region-wise basis. Although the proposed model is validated only for paddy crops in the northern region of India, the proposed search-space design may be explored for adaptation to other crops and geographical regions in future studies. The research is conducted in paddy crop fields of Punjab Agricultural University (PAU), located in Ludhiana, Punjab, India. Data is collected over a span of two years, covering two consecutive seasons of paddy, precisely during the years 2022 and 2023. Data collection

In this research, an Agribot mounted with a Micasense RedEdge-MX camera is employed to capture multispectral images of the experimental field at PAU. It is equipped with five narrow-band multispectral sensors with a default spatial resolution of 8 cm/pixel at an altitude of 400 ft (120 m). The choice of system is suitable in this case, as the geographic region allows the Agribot to fly very close to the rice fields. For the current experiment, the drone height was maintained at 15 m, giving a resolution of approximately 1.042 cm/pixel. The captured images encompass five different bands, namely, green, red, blue, near-infrared, and red-edge, with a resolution of 1280× 960, while the resulting image after orthomosaicing achieves an approximate resolution of 4500× 6500. The main goal of the study is to estimate leaf macro/micro nutrients through multispectral images to verify whether a deep learning algorithm is well-suited for the task. To achieve this objective, diverse fertilizer treatments were applied in different stages of crop development. The soil assimilated these fertilizers and subsequently reflected them in the crops. Data collection, like capturing multispectral images and leaf sample collection, was scheduled after 2–3 days of fertilizer treatment. This provides ample time for fertilizer absorption in the leaves before the commencement of actual data collection.

Leaf samples of paddy crops have been collected from the fields and utilized as ground truth data for the prediction of various macro/micro nutrients. These samples are collected on the same day as the drone flying activity. Sample collection from each field is done from 8–10 distinct points. The collected leaf samples are analyzed for eleven different macro/micro nutrients in the soil science testing lab in PAU, Ludhiana. These nutrients are measured in mg/kg and include Zinc (Zn), sulfur (S), Phosphorus (P), Sodium (Na), Manganese (Mg), Magnesium (Mn), Potassium (K), Iron (Fe), Copper (Cu), Calcium (Ca), and Boron (B).

Data preparation

After capturing multispectral images, orthomosaicing was performed for each day of data collection. Orthomosaic images of the field were generated for all five spectral bands, namely Red, Green, Blue, RedEdge, and Near-Infrared (NIR). From each orthomosaic image, regions of interest (ROIs) were extracted per field, each containing all five spectral bands. Subsequently, each ROI was subdivided into non-overlapping 25 × 25 pixel patches, corresponding to an approximate ground coverage of 26 cm2 per patch. In total, 20,988 and 63,737 image patches were obtained for SAS-I and SAS-II, respectively. Such large variations in magnitude can lead to unstable gradient updates and slow convergence during model training. To mitigate this issue, normalization was applied to all ground truth nutrient values before training, facilitating faster convergence and reduced training loss. Using these normalized target values, all models were trained and evaluated using a spatially consistent splitting strategy, wherein 75% of the data from each field (upper region) was used for training and the remaining 25% (lower region) for testing, for both Study Area–Season I and Season II. This ensures the minimal spatial autocorrelation between training and testing samples, which enables a fair and consistent comparison across all learning approaches.

Nutrient estimation model

Given that robust predictive performance across varying conditions often requires both a large-scale dataset and a larger network, the proposed neural architecture is designed to be flexible and capable of converging efficiently with minimal parameters. To enable these, we introduce a neural architecture search space with Fourier and polynomial approximators, which will learn a transformed nonlinear feature space that will not only help the model converge faster but also adapt itself according to the type of data. We use four different types of search spaces, namely function approximation search (FAS), adaptive activation search (AAS), layer connection search (LCS), and feature weight search (FWS). To adaptively select the most suitable transformations and architectural components, we employ a differentiable neural architecture search strategy inspired by DARTS using a continuous search space. Specifically, each search space, i.e., FAS (function approximation search), AAS (adaptive activation search), LCS (layer connection search), and FWS (feature weight search), consists of multiple operations. Learnable architecture parameters are associated with each operation, and a softmax relaxation is applied to enable continuous optimization during training. As weights are shared across all candidate operations using a joint optimization of network weights as well as architecture pathways, this provides a cost-effective solution compared to other evolution-based strategies and reinforcement strategies. The final architecture is obtained by selecting the operations with the highest learned importance. Together, these search space components enable the network to adaptively modify its nonlinear feature space, activation behavior, information flow, and feature selection strategy, which enable the network to effectively capture complex spectral nutrient relationships.

FAS introduces polynomial basis expansions used to capture complex nonlinear spectral relationships that standard convolution layers may fail to capture efficiently. It includes learnable polynomial functions (e.g., Chebyshev and Fourier bases), which provide an orthogonal representation of spectral signals that is particularly suitable for multispectral data whose reflectance responses exhibit smooth yet nonlinear variations across wavelengths. Unlike standard convolutional filters, these polynomial functions explicitly model higher-order interactions and frequency components, enabling compact yet expressive feature representations under limited data. The input data is represented over an interval [−1,1] to form an orthogonal basis with Legendre or Chebyshev polynomials, ensuring non-redundant and decorrelated non-linearities and compact information with fewer coefficients, such that spectral patterns can be efficiently captured, giving multiscale spatial sensitivity and promoting faster convergence during training.

LCS enables adaptive selection between skip and dense connectivity patterns, improving gradient flow and feature reuse across layers to learn optimal information flow instead of fixing skip or dense connections. For smaller datasets, skip connections prevent overfitting, while dense connections enhance feature reuse and learning efficiency. The NAS framework adaptively discovers the most appropriate connection to a layer from previous layers, as shown in Fig. 4. FWS assigns adaptive importance to feature maps, enabling the model to focus on informative spectral features while suppressing noise. It is used to emphasize important spectral channels and reduce irrelevant ones. FWS searches the optimal weights of a feature map. These can include using equal weights for features, using partial feature weights, or using entire feature weights. In partial feature weights, half of the features in a layer are used as they are, while feature attention is computed over the other half. Together, these search space components enable the network to adaptively modify its nonlinear feature space, activation behavior, information flow, and feature selection strategy, which enable the network to effectively capture complex spectral nutrient relationships.

Results

This section details the various experiments performed with different models, data preprocessing, and splits. All experiments are performed with a learning rate = 0.01 and epochs = 100. The experiments include: (i) estimation of leaf macro/micro nutrients using conventional machine learning models (Section "Estimating Leaf Macro/Micro Nutrients Using Conventional Machine Learning Techniques"); (ii) estimation using deep learning models for both study areas in Season I and Season II (Section "Estimating Leaf Macro/Micro Nutrients using Deep Learning Baseline Models for SAS-I and SAS-II"); (iii) estimation using neural architecture search with polynomial approximation functions for Season I and Season II (Section "Estimating Leaf Macro/Micro Nutrients using Neural Architecture Search with Polynomial Approximation Functions for SAS-I and SAS-II"); (iv) growth stage–wise and treatment-wise estimation of leaf macro/micro nutrients to analyze observed variations (Section "Growth Stage-wise and Treatment-wise Estimation of Eleven Leaf Nutrients"); (v) cross validation and generalization analysis of the proposed NAS-based architecture (Section "Cross Validation and Generalization Analysis of the proposed NAS-based Architecture"); (vi) estimating leaf macro/micro nutrients through vegetation indices (Section "Estimating Leaf Macro/Micro Nutrients using Vegetation Indices"); and (vii) ablation analysis of the proposed model (Section "Ablation Analysis of the Proposed Model").

Estimating leaf macro/micro nutrients using deep learning baseline models for SAS-I and SAS-II

This study also analyzed several convolutional neural network (CNN) backbone architectures, including ResNet18, ResNet34, GoogleNet, LeNet5, AlexNet, VGG16, Swin Transformer, ConvNeXt, DenseNet121, MobileNet, Inception, and EfficientNet for estimating eleven leaf macro/micro nutrients. To ensure a fair comparison, all networks were trained using identical hyperparameters of 100 epochs, batch size of 32, learning rate of 0.01, and weight decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1e^{-4}$$\end{document}, ensuring consistency in optimization conditions. The hyperparameters were selected based on preliminary experiments exploring different learning rates and regularization strengths. All models were adapted to accept five spectral input channels while producing eleven continuous output values corresponding to nutrient concentrations. Model performance was assessed using MAE and the coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}). The results obtained for SAS-I and SAS-II are summarized in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. For SAS-I, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} performance ranking is as follows: EfficientNet > DenseNet121 > MobileNet > ResNet34 > GoogleNet > ResNet18 > Alexnet > Inception, accompanied by relatively lower MAE values for all parameters, indicating stronger agreement between predicted and measured nutrient concentrations. A similar trend is observed for SAS-II, with the performance order: DenseNet121 > EfficientNet > ResNet34 > Inception > ResNet18 > Alexnet > MobileNet > GoogleNet. However, the overall performance of SAS-II is lower than that of SAS-I, indicating greater variability and reduced model generalization under the second seasonal condition.

These results suggest that EfficientNet, DenseNet, and residual learning mechanisms are performing better in capturing the complex, nonlinear relationships between multispectral features and nutrient concentrations. In contrast, VGG16 and Swin Transformer, ConvNeXt exhibit poor performance with negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values for both SAS-I and SAS-II, which may be attributed to the fully-connected layer size of VGG, attention collapse for SWIN, and GeLU miscalibration for ConvNeXt, given the dataset size and configuration.

In this experiment, the SAS-I and SAS-II datasets were trained and evaluated independently using the proposed NAS–based framework augmented with polynomial approximation functions. These results highlight the advantage of automatically learning task-specific network architectures that are more relevant to capture complex spectral features in multispectral agricultural imagery. The incorporation of polynomial approximation functions further enhances the model’s ability to represent nonlinear feature transformations, leading to improved regression accuracy.

For instance, the model was trained using samples with T1, T2, and T3 and evaluated on the control field samples. Estimating leaf macro/micro nutrients using vegetation indices

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of vegetation indices (VIs) for estimating leaf macro/micro nutrient concentrations. Specifically, three aspects are explored: (i) the impact of using VIs as standalone inputs, (ii) their effectiveness when incorporated as complementary features alongside five spectral bands, and (iii) the impact of feature selection in identifying the most informative vegetation indices and spectral bands for nutrient estimation.

Initially, VIs were incorporated as standalone inputs to the best-performing deep learning baseline model, namely EfficientNet, for our dataset to investigate their impact on estimating different macro/micro nutrients. Several widely used indices such as normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), green normalized difference vegetation index (GNDVI), Chlorophyll Index (CI), normalized difference water index (NDWI), modified chlorophyll absorption in reflectance index (MCARI), normalized difference red-edge (NDRE), optimized soil adjusted vegetation index (OSAVI), soil adjusted vegetation index (SAVI), ratio spectral index (RESI), were employed in our study for analyzing macro/micro nutrients. The corresponding results are shown in Table 11, indicating poor performance with a mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} of 0.18 across all nutrients, including negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values observed for certain nutrients such as B and Zn. Subsequently, VIs were incorporated as complementary features alongside the five spectral bands. This combination resulted in improved performance compared to using VIs alone, attaining a mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} of 0.39.

Ablation analysis of the proposed model

The proposed NAS architecture consists of four key search space components, i.e., FAS, FWS, AAS, and LCS. To analyze the contribution of each component in the proposed NAS architecture, an ablation study was conducted by systematically removing each component from the complete architecture. The performance of each variant was evaluated using MAE, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} as shown in Table 13. The results indicate that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} provides a clearer distinction among different variants, as MAE exhibits relatively smaller variations across most configurations. The results demonstrate that the removal of AAS leads to the most significant degradation in performance among all components, with several macro/micro leaf nutrients showing negative or zero \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values. This degradation in performance without AAS suggests that adaptive activation plays an important role in capturing non-linear relationships among spectral features, which are essential for the precise estimation of macro/micro leaf nutrients. While removing FWS results in comparatively smaller performance drops, indicating its contribution is less dominant than that of other search spaces.

Furthermore, the exclusion of LCS also leads to notable performance degradation, particularly for some nutrients such as B, K, Mn, and P, emphasizing the importance of layer connection in predicting leaf macro/micro nutrients through multispectral images. Similarly, removing FAS reduces the model’s ability to effectively extract complex spectral features, leading to moderate declines in performance of the NAS-based model. Discussion

The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed NAS-based framework in estimating leaf macro/micro nutrients with improved accuracy compared to conventional machine learning techniques as well as predefined deep learning baseline techniques. Both growth stage-wise and treatment-wise analyses show that the model consistently maintains low mean absolute errors across multiple nutrients, treatments, and seasons, indicating reasonable robustness within the evaluated experimental setting.

Although the proposed model demonstrates robust performance in the estimation of macro/micro nutrients across different growth stages, treatments, and seasons, its practical applicability also requires an understanding of the confidence associated with its predictions. Additionally, the relative uncertainty (RU=CI range/mean prediction) was computed and expressed as a percentage to assess the reliability of the uncertainty estimates. Table 14 reports the average CI range, relative uncertainty, and coefficient of variation (CV) for each nutrient. The results indicate that nutrients such as K, Mn, P, and Zn exhibit low relative uncertainty (≤ ±5%), making them suitable for automated field mapping. Nutrients such as Ca, Cu, Fe, Mg, and S show moderate uncertainty (±5% to ±8%), indicating reliable predictions for agronomic decision-making. In contrast, higher uncertainty (> ±8%) is observed for B, suggesting that these predictions should be interpreted with caution. The alarmingly high RU for Na depicts that even though Na estimation achieves competitive predictive accuracy, the uncertainty analysis indicates lower confidence and stability in Na predictions relative to several other nutrients.

Furthermore, the coefficient of variation is used to assess prediction stability. Nutrients with both low CV (<5%) and low RU (≤ ±5%), such as K, P, Zn, and Mn, demonstrate consistent and reliable predictions. In contrast, Na exhibits the highest variability, indicating unstable predictions across stochastic passes.

These observations highlight the model’s potential to learn meaningful patterns related to target variations. Future work should focus on extending the model’s validation across multiple seasons and study areas to further enhance its generalizability and accuracy. Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of estimating eleven leaf macro/micro nutrients (B, Ca, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Na, P, S, and Zn) using multispectral drone imagery and a Neural Architecture Search (NAS)-based framework with polynomial approximation functions. Comparative experiments were conducted using predefined deep learning baseline architectures as well as conventional machine learning techniques. Among the conventional machine learning models, XGB, RF, and MLP demonstrated competitive performance by outperforming DL baselines in some cases. Among deep learning models, DenseNet and EfficientNet consistently achieved better performance across both SAS-I and SAS-II. Although overall performance on SAS-II was slightly lower, achieving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values ranging from 0.34 to 0.86 as compared to that of SAS-I, whose \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values are in the range of 0.14 to 0.71. Despite this, these models still outperformed other DL baselines, which exhibit lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values up to 0.15 for SAS-I and 0.09 for SAS-II with the best performing networks. Furthermore, the proposed NAS-based model surpasses both predefined DL architectures and traditional CML methods, achieving superior accuracy and demonstrating robust performance across different treatments and crop growth stages.

A series of experiments was conducted, including growth stage-wise and treatment-wise analyses, season-specific training/testing, treatment-wise cross-validation, and an ablation study for the proposed network. In addition, a Monte Carlo dropout-based confidence interval analysis was performed to assess predictive uncertainty. The results demonstrate that the model effectively captures nutrient variations under diverse conditions.

Furthermore, statistical analyses of channel-wise mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, and Gaussian distribution of pixel-level features confirmed that the model can differentiate learned features, exhibiting higher similarity within the same growth stage and treatment, and greater variability across different conditions. Overall, the proposed NAS-based approach provides precise and uncertainty-aware nutrient estimation, demonstrating strong potential for precision agriculture applications. We observe that intra-season testing loss on SAS-I ranging from 0.06–0.11 across different parameters previously, has now increased to 0.08–0.22. This indicates that a larger variation in the number of fields is required to further improve cross-season estimates. Such variations can alter spectral reflectance characteristics and influence nutrient estimation accuracy. Future work will focus on enhancing generalizability by incorporating data from additional study areas and seasons, further improving the model’s reliability for broader crop monitoring and nutrient management.
