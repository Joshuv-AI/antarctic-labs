The Hochschild cohomlogy ring of a self-injective Nakayama algebra is a Batalin-Vilkovisky algebra
Xiuli Bian, Tomohiro Itagaki, Wen Kou, Weiguo Lyu and Guodong Zhou

Abstract
. Lambre, Zhou and Zimmermann showed that the Hochschild cohomology ring of a Frobenius algebra with semisimple Nakayama automorphism is a Batalin-Vilkovisky algebra. They asked whether the semisimplicity condition is necessary. In this paper, we show that for a self-injective Nakayama algebra, the Hochschild cohomology ring is always a Batalin-Vilkovisky algebra. In course of proofs, we correct some inaccuracies in the literature, hoping not to introduce new errors.

Introduction

Hochschild cohomology plays a central role in the study of associative algebras, it carries a rich algebraic structures: it is a graded commutative algebra structure via the cup product(also known as the Yoneda product), a graded Lie algebra structure via a Lie bracket of degree
-1
, and thus a Gerstenhaber algebra structure
[
6
]
, among others.

In recent decades, a new algebraic structure - known as a Batalin-Vilkovisky (BV) structure - on Hochschild cohomology has been studied. Roughly speaking a BV structure consists of a degree
-1
operator that squares to zero and interacts with the cup product, to recover the Lie bracket. Such a structure is known to exist only for certain special classes of algebras.

Tradler first showed that the Hochschild cohomology ring of a finite dimensional symmetric algebra is a BV algebra
[
23
]
. Later, Lambre, Zhou and Zimmermann
[
14
]
, and independently Volkov
[
25
]
discovered that Hochschild cohomology ring of a Frobenius algebra with semisimple Nakayama automorphism has a BV algebra structure.
Lambre, Zhou and Zimmermann further asked whether this result holds for Frobenius algebras with nonsemisimple Nakayama automorphisms. Due to the computational complexity of Hochschild cohomology, this question is rather hard.

When this paper was in course of preparation, Herscovich and Li showed that for the Fomin-Kirillov algebra with three generators, which is a Frobenious algebra, its Hochschild cohomology ring is NOT a BV algebra
[
9
]
.
Hence, the answer to the question of Lambre-Zhou-Zimmermann is negative in general.

In this paper, we consider BV structure on the Hochschild cohomology ring of a self-injective Nakayama algebra.
In terms of quiver with relations, basic self-injective Nakayama algebras over an algebraically closed field are given as truncated basic cycle algebras, which are truncated quotients of path algebras where the underlying graph is a single oriented cycle.

Our method is computational, one byproduct of which is that the BV algebra structure is determined explicitly in the case that the Nakayama automorphism is not semisimple. 1.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall the definitions and the notations for Hochschild cohomology, Gerstenhaber algebras and Batalin-Vilkovisky (BV for short) algebras. Moreover, following
[

, we recall the BV differential on Hochschild cohomology of a Frobenius algebra with semisimple Nakayama automorphism.

1.1.
Hochschild (co)homology and Gerstenhaber algebras

We begin by recalling the definition of a Gerstenhaber algebra.

Definition 1.1
.

A
Gerstenhaber algebra
over
{\mathbb{K}}
is a graded
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space
V^{\bullet}=\bigoplus\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}V^{n}
equipped with two linear maps: a cup product

\cup\colon\,V^{n}\times V^{m}\rightarrow V^{n+m},\quad(a,b)\mapsto a\cup b

and a Lie bracket of degree
-1

[-,-]\colon\,V^{n}\times V^{m}\rightarrow V^{n+m-1},\quad(a,b)\mapsto[a,b]

such that

(1)

(V^{\bullet},\cup)
is a graded commutative algebra, that is,
a\cup b=(-1)^{|a|\cdot|b|}b\cup a
;

(2)

(V^{\bullet},[-,-])
is a graded Lie algebra of degree
-1
, i.e., we have that

[a,b]=-(-1)^{(|a|-1)(|b|-1)}[b,a]

and a graded Jacobi-identity

(-1)^{(|a|-1)(|c|-1)}[[a,b],c]+(-1)^{(|b|-1)(|a|-1)}[[b,c],a]+(-1)^{(|c|-1)(|b|-1)}[[c,a],b]=0;

(3)

the cup product and the Lie bracket satisfy the compatible condition called the
Poisson rule, i.e.,

[a\cup b,c]=a\cup[b,c]+(-1)^{|b|(|c|-1)}[a,c]\cup b,

where
a,b,c
are arbitrary homogeneous elements in
V^{\bullet}
, and
|a|
denotes the degree of
a
.

One classic example of a Gerstenhaber algebra is the Hochschild cohomology of a
{\mathbb{K}}
-algebra. The cohomology theory of associative algebras was first introduced by Hochschild in
[
10
]
. Latter, Gerstenhaber showed in
[
6
]
that the Hochschild cohomology ring of a
{\mathbb{K}}
-algebra carries a Gerstenhaber algebra structure.

Given a
{\mathbb{K}}
-algebra
A
, its
Hochschild cohomology groups
are defined as

\mathrm{HH}^{n}(A)\cong\mathrm{Ext}^{n}_{A^{e}}(A,A),\quad n\geq 0,

where
A^{e}=A\otimes A^{op}
is the enveloping algebra of
A
. There exists a projective resolution of
A
as
A^{e}
-module, the so called
standard bar resolution

\mathrm{Bar}(A)
which is given by
\mathrm{Bar}_{r}(A)=A\otimes{A}^{\otimes r}\otimes A
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bar}(A)\colon\cdots\to A\otimes{A}^{\otimes r}\otimes A\xrightarrow{b_{r}}A\otimes{A}^{\otimes r-1}\otimes A\to\cdots\to A\otimes{A}\otimes A\xrightarrow{b_{1}}A^{\otimes 2}(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\to}}A),

where the map
\mu:A\otimes A\to A
is the multiplication of
A
, and

\displaystyle b_{r}(a_{0}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\otimes a_{r+1})=

\displaystyle a_{0}a_{1}\otimes{a_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{a_{r}}\otimes a_{r+1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(-1)^{i}a_{0}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i-1}\otimes a_{i}a_{i+1}\otimes a_{i+2}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\otimes a_{r+1}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{r}a_{0}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r-1}\otimes a_{r}a_{r+1}.

The
Hochschild cohomology complex
is defined to be
C^{*}(A)=\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}({\mathrm{Bar}}(A),A)
. Note that for each
r\geq 0
,

C^{r}(A)=\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A,A)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A^{\otimes r},A),

in particular,
C^{0}(A)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}({\mathbb{K}},A)\cong A
. Thus
C^{*}(A)
has the following form:

C^{*}(A)\colon\,0\to A\xrightarrow{b^{0}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A,A)\to\cdots\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A^{\otimes r},A)\xrightarrow{b^{r}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A^{\otimes(r+1)},A)\to\cdots,

where the differential
b^{*}
is given as follows: for
r\geq 0
, a linear map
f:A^{\otimes r}\to A
, and
a_{1},\dots,a_{r+1}\in A
,

\displaystyle b^{r}(f)(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r+1})=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r+1}a_{1}f(a_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(-1)^{r-i+1}f(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i-1}\otimes a_{i}a_{i+1}\otimes a_{i+2}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r+1})

\displaystyle+f(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r})a_{r+1}.

Note that the differential of
b^{r}
here includes an additional sign
(-1)^{r+1}
compared to the original definition. This modification is made to ensure that the definition of the morphism complex
C^{*}(A,A)=\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}({\mathrm{Bar}}(A),A)
is compatible with the differential graded (dg) setting. Specially, if we regard the algebra
A
as a dg algebra with trivial differential, and the complex
{\mathrm{Bar}}(A)
as a dg
A
-module, then the differential
b^{*}
on
C^{*}(A,A)
is defined as follows:

b^{r}(f)=d_{A}\mathop{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}f-(-1)^{|f|}f\mathop{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}b_{r}=(-1)^{r+1}f\mathop{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}b_{r},

where we write
|f|:=r
. Due to this modification, the definitions of the cup product and the Gerstenhaber bracket are also adjusted accordingly.

The
cup product

f\cup g\in C^{n+m}(A)=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A^{\otimes(n+m)},A)
for
f\in C^{m}(A)
and
g\in C^{n}(A)
is given by

(f\cup g)(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n+m}):=(-1)^{mn}f(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n})\cdot g(a_{n+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n+m}).

It is ready to see that the cup product is associative. By
[
6
, Page 278, (20)]
, the differential is a derivation with respect to the cup product, that is,

b(f\cup g)=b(f)\cup g+(-1)^{|f|}f\cup b(g),\;\forall f,g\in C^{*}(A),

so
(C^{*}(A),\,\cup,\,b)
is a dg algebra. This also shows this cup product induces a well-defined product on Hochschild cohomology,

\cup\colon\,\mathrm{HH}^{m}(A)\times\mathrm{HH}^{n}(A,A)\longrightarrow\mathrm{HH}^{n+m}(A),

which is still called cup product. Moreover, by
[
6
, Theorem 3]
, this cup product is graded commutative up to homotopy on the cochain level. As a consequence, the graded
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space
\mathrm{HH}^{*}(A)=\bigoplus\limits_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{HH}^{n}(A)
is a graded commutative algebra.

Furthermore, the
Lie bracket
is defined as follows. Let
f\in C^{m}(A)
and
g\in C^{n}(A)
.
If
m,n\geq 1
, then for
1\leq i\leq m
, define
f\circ_{i}g\in C^{n+m-1}(A)
by

(f\circ_{i}g)(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n+m-1}):=f(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i-1}\otimes g(a_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i+n-1})\otimes a_{i+n}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n+m-1}),

if
m\geq 1
and
n=0
, then
g\in A
and define

(f\circ_{i}g)(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{m-1}):=f(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i-1}\otimes g\otimes a_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{m-1}),

for any other case, set
f\circ_{i}g
to be zero. Now we define

f\circ g:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{(n-1)(i-1)}f\circ_{i}g,

and

[f,g]:=f\circ g-(-1)^{(n-1)(m-1)}g\circ f.

It is a long routine verification that the shift
sC^{*}(A)
(with
sC^{*}(A)^{n}=C^{n+1}(A)
) of the Hochschild cochain complex is a graded Lie algebra. By
[
6
, Page 280]
, the differential is also a derivation with respect to the Gerstenhaber Lie bracket, that is,

b([f,g])=[b(f),g]+(-1)^{|f|-1}[f,b(g)],\;\forall f,\,g\in C^{*}(A).

As a consequence,

(sC^{*}(A),\,[-,-],\,b)
is a dg Lie algebra.

There exists a compatibility condition between the cup product and the Lie bracket given by
[
6
, Corollary 1 to Theorem 5]
. Altogether, Gerstenhaber proved the following important result.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
6
, Page 267]
)
.

The Hochschild cohomology ring
(\mathrm{HH}^{*}(A),\,\cup,\,[-,-])
is a Gerstenhaber algebra.

1.2.
Hochschild cohomology and BV algebras.

With the development of Hochschild theory and homological algebra, it has been observed that in many situations a Gerstenhaber algebra often carries an additional operator structure, which leads to the notion of a Batalin-Vilkovisky algebra.

Definition 1.3
.

A
Batalin–Vilkovisky algebra
(BV algebra) is a Gerstenhaber algebra
(\mathrm{H}^{\bullet},\,\cup,\,[-,-])
together with an operator

\Delta\colon\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{\bullet-1}

of degree
-1
such that
\Delta\mathop{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}\Delta=0
and

[f,\,g]=(-1)^{|f|}(\Delta(f\cup g)-\Delta(f)\cup g-(-1)^{|f|}f\cup\Delta(g))

for homogeneous elements
f,g\in\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}
.

Remark 1.4
.

Our sign convention for Gerstenhaber algebras and BV algebras follows
[
8
, Proposition 1.2]
and
[
17
, Paragraphe 5.1]
and are different those in
[
6
]
and
[
23
]
. For the equivalence between these two sign convention, see
[
15
, Remark 2.5]
.

Tradler showed that the Hochschild cohomology algebra of a finite dimensional symmetric algebra is a BV algebra
[
23
]
; a different proof was given by Menichi
[
18
]
. For a symmetric algebra
A
, the
\Delta
-operator on the Hochschild cohomology corresponds to the Connes’
\mathfrak{B}
-operator on the Hochschild homology via the duality between the Hochschild
cohomology and the Hochschild homology.

Lambre, Zhou and Zimmermann
[
14
]
proved the following result generalizing Tradler-Menichi’s result.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
14
]
)
.

The Hochschild cohomology ring of a Frobenius algebra with semisimple Nakayama automorphism is a BV algebra.

Let us recall some details about the above mentioned result of Lambre, Zhou and Zimmermann
[
14
]
, as we shall need them in the sequel.

Recall (cf e.g.
[
28
, Section 1.10.1]
or
[
26
]
) that a finite dimensional
{\mathbb{K}}
-algebra
A
is called a Frobenius algebra, if there is a non-degenerate associative bilinear form

\langle-,-\rangle:\,A\times A\to{\mathbb{K}}
.
Here the associativity means that

\langle ab,c\rangle=\langle a,bc\rangle

for all
a,\,b
and
c
in
A
. Endow
D(A)=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A,{\mathbb{K}})
, the
{\mathbb{K}}
-dual of
A
, with the canonical bimodule structure

(afb)(c)=f(bca),\text{ for }f\in D(A),\text{ and }a,b,c\in A.

The property of being Frobenius is equivalent to saying that
D(A)=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A,{\mathbb{K}})
is isomorphic to
A
as left or as right modules. It is readily seen that the map

a\mapsto\langle a,-\rangle

for
a\in A
gives an isomorphism of right modules between
A
and
D(A)
, while the map
a\mapsto\langle-,a\rangle
gives the isomorphism of left modules.

For
a\in A
, there exists a unique
\sigma(a)\in A
such that
\langle a,\ -\rangle=\langle-,\ \sigma(a)\rangle\in D(A)
.
It is easy to see that
\sigma:A\to A
is an algebra isomorphism and we call it the
Nakayama automorphism of
A
(associated to the bilinear form
\langle-,-\rangle
).
We denote by
A_{\sigma}
the
A
-bimodule which is
A
as
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space with the
A
-bimodule defined by

b\cdot c\cdot a:=bc\sigma(a),\text{ for }b,c\in A,\text{ and }a\in A^{op}.

To define the operator
\Delta
in
[
14
]
, we need the definition of
\mathrm{H}_{*}(A,A_{\sigma})
. The complex used to compute the Hochschild homology
\mathrm{H}^{*}(A,A_{\sigma})
is
C_{*}(A,A_{\sigma})=A_{\sigma}\otimes_{A^{e}}\mathrm{Bar}(A)
. For
r\geq 0
,
C_{r}(A,A_{\sigma})=A_{\sigma}\otimes_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A)\simeq A_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\otimes r}
and the differential

d_{r}:\;C_{r}(A,A_{\sigma})=A_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\otimes r}\to C_{r-1}(A,A_{\sigma})=A_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\otimes r-1}

sends
x\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}
to

x\sigma(a_{1})\otimes a_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(-1)^{i}x\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i-1}\otimes a_{i}a_{i+1}\otimes a_{i+2}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}+(-1)^{r}a_{r}x\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r-1}.

In
[
13
]
, Kowalzig and Krähmer defined a
{\mathbb{K}}
-linear map

B_{\sigma}\colon\,C_{r}(A,A_{\sigma})\longrightarrow C_{r+1}(A,A_{\sigma})

by

B_{\sigma}(a_{0}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r+1}(-1)^{ir}1\otimes a_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\otimes a_{0}\otimes\sigma(a_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma(a_{i-1}).

It is easy to check that
B_{\sigma}
is a chain map satisfying
B_{\sigma}\mathop{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}B_{\sigma}=0
, which induces an operator
B_{\sigma}:\;\mathrm{H}_{r}(A,A_{\sigma})\to\mathrm{H}_{r+1}(A,A_{\sigma})
.

The isomorphism of
A
-bimodules
A_{\sigma}\rightarrow D(A),\;a\mapsto\langle-,a\rangle
induces an isomorphism of complexes

\partial:\ D(C_{*}(A,A_{\sigma}))\longrightarrow C^{*}(A),

which is given by

\displaystyle D(C_{r}(A,A_{\sigma}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Hom}_{{\mathbb{K}}}(A_{\sigma}\otimes_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A),{\mathbb{K}})

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A,\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A_{\sigma},{\mathbb{K}}))

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A,A)

\displaystyle=C^{r}(A),

for
r\geq 0
, which induces an isomorphism
D(\mathrm{H}_{*}(A,A_{\sigma}))\cong\mathrm{HH}^{*}(A)
on homology group level. In fact, we can make
\partial
and its inverse
\partial^{-1}
explicit as follows:

\displaystyle\partial\colon\,\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\otimes r},{\mathbb{K}})

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A,A)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\partial(f):\,1\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\otimes 1\mapsto\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}f(y_{j}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r})x_{j},

\displaystyle\partial^{-1}\colon\,\mathrm{Hom}_{A^{e}}(A\otimes A^{\otimes r}\otimes A,A)

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(A_{\sigma}\otimes A^{\otimes r},{\mathbb{K}})

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto\partial^{-1}(g):\,a_{0}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\mapsto\langle g(1\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\otimes 1),a_{0}\rangle,

where
\{x_{i}{\;|\;}1\leq i\leq N\}
is a basis of
A
and
\{y_{j}{\;|\;}1\leq j\leq N\}
the dual basis such that
\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
.

Recall that for a finite dimensional vector space
V
, a
{\mathbb{K}}
-linear transformation
\varphi\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{K}}(V)
is called semisimple, if extended to the algebraic closure
\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
of

\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
-linear transformation
\varphi\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}\in\mathrm{End}_{\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}}(V\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}\overline{{\mathbb{K}}})
is diagonalizable. When talking about semisimplicity of the Nakayama automorphism of a Frobenius algebra, we always mean this sense.

In the sequel, we could always assume that
{\mathbb{K}}
is algebraically closed
. In fact, once the proofs and computations have been done over an algebraically closed field, then one can return to a general field by using the argument in Section 4 of
[
14
]
.

In the following, let
A
be a Frobenius algebra with bilinear
\langle-,-\rangle:\,A\times A\to{\mathbb{K}}
and the semisimple Nakayama automorphism
\sigma
. Following
[
14
]
, we recall the BV differential on
\mathrm{HH}^{*}(A)\simeq\mathrm{HH}_{(1)}^{*}(A)
.

Let
I_{A}
be the set of eigenvalues of the automorphism
\sigma
. Then
I_{A}\subset{\mathbb{K}}
because
\sigma
is semisimple. Fix an eigenvalue
\lambda\in I_{A}
and let
A_{\lambda}
denote the eigenspace associated with
\lambda
. It is straightforward to verify that for
\lambda,\mu\in I_{A}
, one has
A_{\lambda}\cdot A_{\mu}\subseteq A_{\lambda\mu}
. When
\lambda\mu\not\in I_{A}

2.
Minimal resolution for truncated quiver algebras and comparison morphisms

In this section, we recall the minimal projective bimodule resolutions for truncated quiver algebras following Bardzell
[
2
]
which in fact dealt with general monomial algebras.
Moreover, we recall the comparison morphisms between the minimal resolution and the reduced bar resolution as constructed in
[
1
]
.

Let
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},o,t)
be a
quiver
. That is,
Q_{0}
is the set of vertices,
Q_{1}
the set of arrows, the map
o:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
gives the origin of an arrow and the map
t:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
is the target of an arrow. We always assume that
Q
is a finite quiver, i.e.,
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
are finite sets. The path
p=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{r}
of
length

l(p)=r
is defined by the condition that
t(\alpha_{i})=o(\alpha_{i+1})
for all
1\leq i\leq r-1
, and vertices are viewed as paths of length
0
. For
n\geq 0
,
Q_{n}
denotes the set of all paths of length
n
and
Q_{\geq n}
is the set of all paths with length at least
n
. Denote by
{\mathbb{K}}Q
the path algebra of
Q
, that is the space generated by all paths of finite length and endowed with the multiplication given by concatenation of paths.

For an algebra given by a bound quiver
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Q/I
with
I\subseteq Q_{\geq 1}
, one usually uses a variant of the normalized bar resolution, which is the
reduced bar resolution
introduced by Gerstenhaber and Schack
[
7
]
and popularized by Cibils
[
4
]
. Let
{\Lambda_{+}}=\Lambda/(\mathbb{K}Q_{0})
. Since
I
is asked to be contained in
Q_{\geq 1}
, the spaces
KQ_{n}\;(n\geq 0),\;\Lambda,\;\Lambda_{+}
etc are all
{\mathbb{K}}Q_{0}
-bimodules. The reduced bar resolution of
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Q/I
has the form:

{\mathbb{B}}_{*}\colon\;\cdots\to\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}^{\otimes r}\otimes\Lambda\xrightarrow{b_{r}}\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}^{\otimes r-1}\otimes\Lambda\to\cdots\to\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}\otimes\Lambda\xrightarrow{b_{1}}\Lambda\otimes\Lambda(\xrightarrow{b_{0}}\Lambda),

where
b_{0}:\Lambda\otimes\Lambda\to\Lambda
is (induced by) the multiplication of
\Lambda
, and the differential
b_{r}
with
r\geq 1
is given by

\displaystyle b_{r}(a_{0}[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r}]a_{r+1})

\displaystyle=a_{0}a_{1}[a_{2}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r}]a_{r+1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(-1)^{i}a_{0}[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{i-1}\mid a_{i}a_{i+1}\mid a_{i+2}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r}]a_{r+1}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{r}a_{0}[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r-1}]a_{r}a_{r+1},

where we use the bar notation
a_{0}[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r}]a_{r+1}=a_{0}\otimes a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{r}\otimes a_{r+1}
for
a_{0},\;a_{r+1}\in\Lambda
and
a_{1},\cdots,a_{r}\in\Lambda_{+}
.

It is not difficult to verify that the constructions in Section
1
carry over verbatim from the bar resolution to the reduced bar resolution; see, for example,
[
21
]
.

For an integer
N\geq 2
and a finite quiver
Q
, let
\Lambda={{\mathbb{K}}Q}/J^{N}
be a truncated quiver algebra. The set

{\mathcal{B}}=\bigcup\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}Q_{n}

is a
{\mathbb{K}}
-basis of
\Lambda
. We shall make no distinction between an element
p\in\bigoplus\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}{\mathbb{K}}Q_{n}
and its quotient in
\Lambda
. Let us introduce some useful notations. For
n\geq 0
, define

\chi(n)=\begin{cases}kN,&\text{if }n=2k,\\
kN+1,&\text{if }n=2k+1.\end{cases}

For composable arrows
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in Q_{1}
, we write

\alpha_{1,\;n}:=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}.

Bardzell constructed a minimal projective bimodule resolution
({\mathbb{P}}_{*},d_{*})
for monomial algebras in
[
2
]
. More explicitly, for truncated quiver algebra
\Lambda
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{*}\colon\;\cdots\longrightarrow P_{r}\xrightarrow{d_{r}}P_{r-1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow P_{1}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}P_{0}(\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}\Lambda),

where
P_{r}=\Lambda\otimes{\mathbb{K}}Q_{\chi(r)}\otimes\Lambda
and the differential
d_{*}
is given by

\displaystyle d_{2k}(1\otimes\alpha_{1,\;kN}\otimes 1)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{1,\;i}\otimes\alpha_{i+1,\;(k-1)N+i+1}\otimes\alpha_{(k-1)N+i+2,\;kN},

\displaystyle d_{2k+1}(1\otimes\alpha_{1,\;kN+1}\otimes 1)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\otimes\alpha_{2,\;kN+1}\otimes 1-1\otimes\alpha_{1,\;kN}\otimes\alpha_{kN+1},

where all
\alpha_{i}
are arrows.

Ames, Cagliero and Tirao constructed the comparisons morphisms between
{\mathbb{P}}_{*}
and
{\mathbb{B}}_{*}

[
1
]
. Let us recall their construction.

The chain map

\mu_{*}\colon{\mathbb{P}}_{*}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}_{*}
is defined as follows. Let
\mu_{0}\colon{\mathbb{P}}_{0}=\Lambda\otimes{\mathbb{K}}Q_{0}\otimes\Lambda\longrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}_{0}=\Lambda\otimes\Lambda
be the identity map.
If
n=2k
with
k\geq 1
and
p=1\otimes\alpha_{1,kN}\otimes 1
with all
\alpha_{i}\in Q_{1}
, define

\displaystyle\mu_{2k}(p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leq x_{i}\leq N-1\end{subarray}}1\bigl[

\displaystyle\underbrace{\alpha_{1,\;x_{1}}}_{x_{1}}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{x_{1}+1}}_{1}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{x_{1}+2,\;x_{1}+x_{2}+1}}_{x_{2}}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{x_{1}+x_{2}+2}}_{1}\mid\cdots\mid

\displaystyle\underbrace{\alpha_{k+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}x_{j},\;k-1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}}_{x_{k}}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}}_{1}\bigr]\,\underbrace{\alpha_{k+1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j},\;kN}}_{k(N-1)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}.

If
n=2k+1
with
k\geq 0
and
p=1\otimes\alpha_{1,\;kN+1}\otimes 1
with all
\alpha_{i}\in Q_{1}
, define

\displaystyle\mu_{2k+1}(p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leq x_{i}\leq N-1\end{subarray}}1\bigl[

\displaystyle\underbrace{\alpha_{1}}_{1}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{2,\;x_{1}+1}}_{x_{1}}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{x_{1}+2}}_{1}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{x_{1}+3,\;x_{1}+x_{2}+2}}_{x_{2}}\mid\cdots\mid

\displaystyle\underbrace{\alpha_{k+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}x_{j}}}_{1}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{k+1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}x_{j},\;k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}}_{x_{k}}\mid\underbrace{\alpha_{k+1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}}_{1}\bigr]\underbrace{\alpha_{k+2+\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j},\;kN+1}}_{k(N-1)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}.

The chain map
\omega_{*}\colon{\mathbb{B}}_{*}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_{*}
is defined by
\omega_{0}:=\mathrm{Id}
. For
n\geq 1
and any

p=1[p_{1}\mid\cdots\mid p_{n}]1\in\Lambda\otimes\Lambda_{+}^{n}\otimes\Lambda,

write
l(p)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}l(p_{i})
, and let
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}=\alpha_{1,l(p)}\in{\mathbb{K}}Q_{l(p)}
. When
n=2k
with
k\geq 0
, define

\omega_{2k}(p)=\begin{cases}1\otimes\underbrace{\alpha_{1,\;kN}}_{kN}\otimes\underbrace{\alpha_{kN+1,\;l(p)}}_{l(p)-kN},&\text{if }p_{2i-1}p_{2i}=0\in\Lambda\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

when
n=2k+1
with
k\geq 0
, define

\omega_{2k+1}(p)=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=0}^{l(p_{1})-1}\underbrace{\alpha_{1,\;j}}_{j}\otimes\underbrace{\alpha_{j+1,\;kN+j+1}}_{kN+1}\otimes\underbrace{\alpha_{kN+j+2,\;l(p)}}_{l(p)-kN-j-1},&\text{if }p_{2i}p_{2i+1}=0\in\Lambda\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.1
.

The authors of
[
1
]
proved the comparison morphisms
\mu_{*}:\;{\mathbb{P}}_{*}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}_{*}
and
\omega_{*}:\;{\mathbb{B}}_{*}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_{*}
by direct inspection. In 2018 Redondo and Román constructed comparison morphisms for general monomial algebras and based on these comparison morphisms
[
19
]
, Redondo and Rossi Bertone investigated
L_{\infty}
-structures
[
20
]
. In a forthcoming work, we will give another construction of comparison morphisms for monomial algebras by using algebraic Morse theory and also investigate higher structures.

These comparison morphisms can also be constructed by using the method of contracting homotopies (see, for instance,
[
12
, Section 2]
).
Notice that a contracting homotopy on the reduced bar resolution
{\mathbb{B}}_{*}
can be found in Section 3.1 in
[
1
]
, and a contracting homotopy on
{\mathbb{P}}_{*}
, was constructed by Sköldberg
[
22
]

Applying the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(-,\Lambda)
to the reduced bar resolution
{\mathbb{B}}_{*}
,
we obtain the
reduced Hochschild cochain complex
(see Cibils
[
4

), denoted by

{\mathbb{B}}^{*}\colon 0\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\xrightarrow{b^{0}}\cdots\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}^{\otimes r}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\xrightarrow{b^{r}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}^{\otimes r+1}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\longrightarrow\cdots,

and for any

, the differential
b^{r}
is given by

\displaystyle b^{r}(f)(1[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r+1}]1)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r+1}f(a_{1}[a_{2}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r+1}]1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(-1)^{r-i+1}f(1[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{i-1}\mid a_{i}a_{i+1}\mid a_{i+2}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r+1}]1)

\displaystyle+f(1[a_{1}\mid\cdots\mid a_{r}]a_{r+1}).

Applying the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(-,\Lambda)
to the minimal projective bimodule resolution
{\mathbb{P}}_{*}
, we obtain the cochain complex
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}

{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\colon 0\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\xrightarrow{d^{0}}\cdots\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{\mathbb{K}}Q_{\chi(r)}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\xrightarrow{d^{r}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{\mathbb{K}}Q_{\chi(r+1)}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\longrightarrow\cdots,

with

, and

for

The comparison morphisms
\mu_{*}
and
\omega_{*}
, induce the corresponding morphisms
\mu^{*}
and
\omega^{*}
between
{\mathbb{B}}^{*}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}

\mu^{r}:\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}^{\otimes r}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{{\mathbb{K}}Q_{\chi(r)}}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda),\ f\mapsto f\circ\mu_{r},

and

\omega^{r}:\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{{\mathbb{K}}Q_{\chi(r)}}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(\Lambda\otimes{\Lambda_{+}}^{\otimes r}\otimes\Lambda,\Lambda),\ g\mapsto g\circ\omega_{r}.

In the following sections, we shall use these morphisms to compute the cup product, the Lie bracket and the BV structure on the complex
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}
.

3.
The Hochschild cohomology spaces

Let
\Lambda={{\mathbb{K}}Q}/J^{N}
be a truncated quiver algebra with
N\geq 2
. Truncated basic cycle algebra is a special case of truncated quiver algebra. In this section, we give a new description of the complex
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}
and provide an explicit
{\mathbb{K}}
-bases of equivalence classes for Hochschild cohomology groups of truncated basic cycle algebras, following Locateli
[
16
]
, and Bardzell, Locateli and Marcos
[
3
]
. To keep the notation simple, a representation of each equivalence class will be used to denote the class itself.

3.1.
The Hochschild cohomology spaces for truncated quiver algebras

The notion of
parallel paths
, introduced by Cibils
[
4
]
, provide a convenient description of the spaces
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(P_{*},\Lambda)
. Recall that two paths
p
and
q
are called
parallel
if they have the same origin and target; we write
p\parallel q
. For subsets
X
and
Y
of paths in
Q
, set

(X\parallel Y):=\{(p,q)\in X\times Y{\;|\;}\ p\parallel q\},

and denote by
{\mathbb{K}}(X\parallel Y)
the
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space with basis
(X\parallel Y)
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
4
]
,
[
16
, Lemma 1]
)
.

For every
r\geq 0
, there is an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector spaces

{\mathbb{P}}^{r}=\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{e}}(P_{r},\Lambda)\cong{\mathbb{K}}(Q_{\chi(r)}\parallel{\mathcal{B}}),

where for each
(\gamma,b)\in(Q_{\chi(r)}\parallel\mathcal{B})
, the map

f_{(\gamma,b)}\colon P_{r}=\Lambda\otimes{\mathbb{K}}Q_{\chi(r)}\otimes\Lambda\to\Lambda

is given on basis elements by sending
p\otimes\gamma^{\prime}\otimes q\in P_{r}
to
\delta_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}pbq
, where
p,\;q\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in Q_{\chi(r)}
.

Locateli described the differential of the complex
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}
of a truncated quiver algebra
\Lambda
in terms of parallel paths.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
16

)
.

The differential in the complex

3.2.
The case

for truncated basic cycle algebras

In this subsection, we briefly discuss here the well known case

\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Z_{e}/J^{N}
without proof for completeness.
In the main text of this paper, we will consider only the case

,

The truncated basic cycle algebra
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Z_{1}/J^{N}
with

is given by the bounded quiver
Z_{1}
with exactly one vertex

and one loop
\alpha
, and we have the relation
\alpha^{N}=0
.

The Hochschild cohomology groups of

are given as follows:

(1)

\mathrm{HH}^{0}(\Lambda)={\mathbb{K}}\{(e_{1},\alpha^{i}){\;|\;}0\leq i\leq N-1\},

(2)

(3)

From now on, when considering the truncated basic cycle algebra

Remark 6.5

Let

\displaystyle=d^{*}\mu^{*}_{m+n-1}[\omega^{*}_{m}f,\omega^{*}_{n}g]_{{\mathbb{B}}}=\mu^{*}_{m+n}{b^{*}}[\omega^{*}_{m}f,\omega^{*}_{n}g]_{{\mathbb{B}}}

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
N\geq 2
be the integer such that
\gcd(N,e)=d\geq 1
. Write
N=dN_{1}
and
e=de_{1}
. For fixed integer
s
, if
d{\;|\;}p-s
, assume that
x_{p}\in[1,e_{1}]
and
x_{p}^{\prime}\in[2,e_{1}+1]
and be the unique solution of the congruence
(x-1)N_{1}\equiv\frac{p-s}{d}\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e_{1})
, respectively. Then

(1)

\{x_{p}{\;|\;}1\leq p\leq e,\text{ and }d{\;|\;}p-s\}=\{1,\ldots,e_{1}\}
,

(1’)

\{x_{p}^{\prime}{\;|\;}1\leq p\leq e,\text{ and }d{\;|\;}p-s\}=\{2,\ldots,e_{1}+1\}
,

(2)

for any
k\geq 0
,
\sum\limits_{x=1}^{e_{1}}[\frac{k+e_{1}-x}{e_{1}}]=k=\sum\limits_{x=1}^{e}[\frac{k+e-x}{e}]
.

We only prove the cases (1)(iv)(a) and (4)(i), while the others are similar or follow directly from Lemma
6.11
by straightward computation.

Case (1)(iv)(a):
By assumption,
N\equiv 1\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
, which implies
\gcd(N,e)=1
. For
1\leq l\leq e
let
y_{p}\in[1,e]
(resp.
y_{p}^{\prime}\in[1,e]
) be the unique solution of the congruence
(x-1)N\equiv l-p-1\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
(resp.
(x-1)N\equiv l-p\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
). Following from Proposition
6.7
(1), we have

\displaystyle\big[u_{l;\;0,N-1},\,x_{kN,j}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{p=1}\limits^{e}\Big(\Big[\frac{k+e-y_{p}^{\prime}}{e}\Big]-\Big[\frac{k+e-y_{p}}{e}\Big]\Big)(\alpha_{p}^{(k-1)N+1},\alpha_{p}^{j}).

If
j=0
, then
e{\;|\;}k
, we have

\Big[\frac{k+e-y_{p}^{\prime}}{e}\Big]=\Big[\frac{k+e-y_{p}}{e}\Big]=\frac{k}{e},\text{ for all }p\in[1,e],

then
\big[u_{l;\;0,N-1},\,x_{kN,j}\big]=0
. If
1\leq j\leq N-1
, it is obvious that
y_{p}=y_{\underline{p+1}}^{\prime}
for
1\leq p\leq e
, then

\displaystyle\big[u_{l;\;0,N-1},\,x_{kN,j}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{p=1}\limits^{e}\Big[\frac{k+e-y_{p}^{\prime}}{e}\Big]\big[(\alpha_{p}^{(k-1)N+1},\alpha_{p}^{j})-(\alpha_{\underline{p-1}}^{(k-1)N+1},\alpha_{\underline{p-1}}^{j})\big]

\displaystyle=d_{j-1}^{2k-2}\big(\sum\limits_{p=1}\limits^{e}\Big[\frac{k+e-y_{p}^{\prime}}{e}\Big](\alpha_{p}^{(k-1)N},\alpha_{p}^{j-1})\big)

\displaystyle\equiv 0.

Case (4)(i):
Following from Proposition
6.7
(2), if
\gcd(N,e)=1
, then

\displaystyle\big[x_{hN,i},\,v_{1;\,kN+1,j}\big]=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=1}^{e}\Big(\sum\limits_{s=0}^{N-1}\Big[\frac{h+e-x_{s^{l}}}{e}\Big]-\Big[\frac{i-1+e-\overline{1-l}}{e}\Big]\Big)(\alpha_{l}^{(h+k)N},\alpha_{l}^{i+j-1}),

where
x_{s^{l}}\in[1,e]
is the unique solution of the congruence
(x-1)N+l+s\equiv 1\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
for each
1\leq l\leq e
. Set

\lambda_{l}=\sum\limits_{s=0}^{N-1}\Big[\frac{h+e-x_{s^{l}}}{e}\Big]-\Big[\frac{i-1+e-\overline{1-l}}{e}\Big].

We now prove that
\lambda_{l}=\lambda_{l+1}
for all
l\in[1,e-1]
. For
l=1
, we have

\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}=\Big[\frac{h+e-1}{e}\Big]-\Big[\frac{h}{e}\Big]+\Big[\frac{i}{e}\Big]-\Big[\frac{i+e-1}{e}\Big].

We distinguish two cases:

•

if
i\equiv 0\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
, then also
h\equiv 0\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
. In this case,

\Big[\frac{h+e-1}{e}\Big]=\Big[\frac{h}{e}\Big]\quad\text{and}\quad\Big[\frac{i}{e}\Big]=\Big[\frac{i+e-1}{e}\Big],

and therefore
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.

•

if
i\equiv 0\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
, then
h\equiv 0\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
. In this case,

\Big[\frac{h+e-1}{e}\Big]=\Big[\frac{h}{e}\Big]+1\quad\text{and}\quad\Big[\frac{i}{e}\Big]+1=\Big[\frac{i+e-1}{e}\Big],

so again
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.

In the same way, we obtain
\lambda_{l}=\lambda_{l+1}
for
2\leq l\leq e-1
. By Lemma
6.11
we have
\sum\limits_{l=1}^{e}\lambda_{l}=hN-i
, and hence
\lambda_{l}=h_{1}
for each
l\in[1,e]
, where
hN-i=h_{1}e
.

If
\gcd(N,e)=d>1
, then by Proposition
6.9
(2), the proof proceeds in the same way.

7.
BV structure

In this section, we determine the BV algebra structure on the Hochschild cohomology of a selfinjective Nakayama algebra.

Let
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Z_{e}/J^{N}
be the truncated basic cycle algebra with
N,\,e\geq 2
. Then
\Lambda
is a Frobenius algebra, endowed with the associative nondegenerate
{\mathbb{K}}
-bilinear form
\langle\ ,\ \rangle\colon\Lambda\times\Lambda\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}
:

\langle\alpha_{l}^{i},\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{j}\rangle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\,i+j=N-1\text{ and }\,l^{\prime}\equiv l+i\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e);\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for
\alpha_{l}^{i},\,\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{j}\in\Lambda
with
1\leq l,\,l^{\prime}\leq e,\;0\leq i,\,j\leq N-1
. The Nakayama automorphism
\sigma\colon\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda
of
\Lambda
associated to
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
is defined by, for each
\alpha_{l}^{i}\in\Lambda
with
1\leq l\leq e,\;0\leq i\leq N-1
, one has

\sigma(\alpha_{l}^{i})=\alpha_{\underline{l+N-1}}^{i}.

We begin by analyzing the conditions under which
\sigma
is semisimple. This consideration yields the following criterion.

Criterion 7.1
.

Let
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Z_{e}/J^{N}
be the truncated basic cycle algebra.
\sigma
is the Nakayama automorphism of
\Lambda
described as above. Then
\sigma
is semisimple if and only if it satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\nmid e
;

(2)

2\leq\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\leq e
,
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}}){\;|\;}e
,
\gcd(N-1,e)=c>1
and
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\nmid\frac{e}{c}
.

Assume that
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\nmid e
, if
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})=0
or
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})>e
, from
[
14
, Criterion 5.1]
we know that
\sigma
is semisimple.

Take a
{\mathbb{K}}
-basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{e},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e},\ldots,\alpha_{1}^{N-1},\ldots,\alpha_{e}^{N-1}\}
of
\Lambda
. By the definition of
\sigma
we can write
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2}\times\cdots\times\sigma_{N}
, where
\sigma_{i}=\sigma\mid_{{\mathbb{K}}\{\alpha_{1}^{i-1},\ldots,\alpha_{e}^{i-1}\}}
for
1\leq i\leq N
(with convention
\alpha_{l}^{0}=e_{l}
). For all
1\leq i\leq N
, the representative matrix of the automorphism
\sigma_{i}
has the form

\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
1&\cdots&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&1&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}_{e}

In the matrix above, the entry in the
j
-th row and
\underline{j+N-1}
-th column is 1 for
1\leq j\leq e
, and all other entries are 0. It is straightforward to verify that this matrix can be written as the product of
N-1
matrices
M
of the following from

M=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}_{e}

\mathrm{i.e.}
, the matrix of
\sigma_{i}
is
M^{N-1}
.

Assume that
2\leq\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\leq e
and
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\nmid e
. To prove
\sigma
is semisimple, it suffice to prove that
M
is semisimple over the field
{\mathbb{K}}
. By simple calculation, we know that the characteristic polynomial of matrix
M
is
\lambda^{e}-1
, which has
e
different roots in
{\mathbb{K}}
because
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\nmid e
. This means
M
is semisimple over
{\mathbb{K}}
, then we have proved that if condition (1) is satisfied,
\sigma
is semisimple.

Assume that
2\leq\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\leq e
and
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\mid e
.

If
\gcd(N-1,e)=1
, there exist
1\leq a\leq e-1
such that
M^{(N-1)a}=M
because
M^{e}=E
. So in order to prove
M^{N-1}
is not semisimple, we just need to prove that
M
is not semisimple. Since
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\mid e
, we have
(\lambda-1)^{2}\mid\lambda^{e}-1
, then the algebraic multiplicity (AM for short) of the eigenvalue
1
is greater than or equal to
2
,
\mathrm{i.e.}\ AM(1)\geq 2
. And it is easy to see the geometric multiplicity (GM for short) of
1
is
1
, then
1=GM(1)<AM(1)
, which means that
M
is not semisimple over
{\mathbb{K}}
.

If
\gcd(N-1,e)=c>1
, there exist
1\leq a\leq e-1
such that
M^{(N-1)a}=M^{c}
. The characteristic polynomial of matrix
M^{c}
is
(\lambda^{\frac{e}{c}}-1)^{c}
. If
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\nmid\frac{e}{c}
, let
w
be one of the roots of the polynomial
\lambda^{\frac{e}{c}-1}+\cdots+\lambda+1
, it’s not difficult to verify that
GM(w^{l})=c=AM(w^{l})
for

. Then
M^{c}
is semisimple over
{\mathbb{K}}
. If
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\mid\frac{e}{c}
,
AM(1)\geq 2c>GM(1)=c
. Then
M^{c}
is not semisimple over
{\mathbb{K}}
.
This completes the proof of the criterion.

Remark 7.6
.

In case of semisimple Nakayama automorphism, our formula for the
\Delta
-operator coincides with that in
[
11
, Theorem 4.6]
, except that in the statement of
[
11
, Theorem 4.6]
, there is a typos that “
Ni+N-j-1
” should read “
\frac{Ni+N-j-1}{g_{0}}
”, as the correct formula is given in the proof.

Now we turn to the case where the Nakayama automorphism
\sigma
is not semisimple, that is, when
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}}){\;|\;}\mathrm{ord}(\sigma)
. In this case, the Hochschild cohomology
\mathrm{HH}^{*}(\Lambda)
remains a BV algebra.

Before presenting the explicit construction of the BV differential, we first state a more general criterion.

Criterion 7.7
.

Let
(\mathrm{H}^{\bullet},\vee,[-,-])
be a Gerstenhaber algebra with
(\mathrm{H}^{\bullet},\vee)
finitely generated and
\mathcal{G}
a minimal set of homogeneous generators of
(\mathrm{H}^{\bullet},\vee)
. Assume that the following assumptions hold:

(1)

\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}\subset\mathrm{H}^{1}
, and for any
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}
,
y\vee y^{\prime}=0
and
[y,\,y^{\prime}]=0
.

(2)

Given
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}
,
y_{i}\in\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}
, and
f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}
being products of some elements in
(\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet})\cup\{1\}
, whenever
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee y_{i}=0
holds in
\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\,[f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}},\,y_{i}]=0.

(3)

For any
f,\,g\in\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet}
,

[f,\,g]=0.

Then
(\mathrm{H}^{\bullet},\vee,[-,-])
becomes a BV algebra endowed with the operator
\Delta:\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}\to\mathrm{H}^{\bullet-1}
: for
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{2n}=0,

\displaystyle\Delta_{2n+1}(f^{\mathrm{even}}\vee y)=[f^{\mathrm{even}},\,y],

where
y\in\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}
,
f^{\mathrm{even}}
is a product of some elements in
(\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet})\cup\{1\}
.

By Assumption (2),
\Delta
is well-defined.

It is clear that
\Delta\circ\Delta=0
by definition.

For
f,\,g\in\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet}
, by the definition of
\Delta
and Assumption (3), we have

[f,\,g]=0=(-1)^{|f|}(\Delta(f\vee g)-\Delta(f)\vee g-(-1)^{|f|}f\vee\Delta(g)).

Let us consider
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\in\mathrm{H}^{2n}
and
g=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee z_{j}\in\mathrm{H}^{2m+1}
,
where
\lambda_{i},\mu_{j}\in{\mathbb{K}}
,
f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}
,
g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}
are products of some elements from
(\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet})\cup\{1\}
and
z_{j}\in\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}
. By the definition of
\Delta
and Assumption (1), we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{|f|}\big(\Delta(f\vee g)-\Delta(f)\vee g-(-1)^{|f|}f\vee\Delta(g)\big)

\displaystyle=\Delta(f\vee g)-f\vee\Delta(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,\big(\Delta(f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee z_{j})-f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee\Delta(g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee z_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,\big([f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}},z_{j}]-f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee[g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}},z_{j}]\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,z_{j}]\vee g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\quad(\text{by Poisson rule for $\vee$ and $[-,-]$})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee[z_{j},f]

\displaystyle=[f,g]\quad(\text{by Poisson rule and Assumption (3)}).

It is obvious by Assumption (1) that for any
f,g\in\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}
, we have

f\vee g=0.

Let us consider
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee y_{i}\in\mathrm{H}^{2n+1}
and
g=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee z_{j}\in\mathrm{H}^{2m+1}
,
where
\lambda_{i},\mu_{j}\in{\mathbb{K}}
,
f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}
,
g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}
are products of elements from
(\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet})\cup\{1\}
and
y_{i},z_{j}\in\mathcal{G}\cap\mathrm{H}^{2\bullet-1}
. By the definition of
\Delta
and Assumption (1), we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{|f|}\big(\Delta(f\vee g)-\Delta(f)\vee g-(-1)^{|f|}f\vee\Delta(g)\big)

\displaystyle=\Delta(f)\vee g-f\vee\Delta(g)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,[y_{i},f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee g+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,f\vee[z_{j},g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,y_{i}\vee[g,f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee z_{j}\quad(\text{by Poisson rule and Assumption (1)})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,y_{i}\vee[g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee z_{j},f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee z_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,y_{i}\vee g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee[z_{j},f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}}]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee z_{j}\quad(\text{by Poisson rule and Assumption (3)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee y_{i}\vee[f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}},z_{j}]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee z_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee[y_{i}\vee f_{i}^{\mathrm{even}},z_{j}]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee z_{j}\quad(\text{by Poisson rule and Assumption (1)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}\vee[f,z_{j}]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}\,[f,g_{j}^{\mathrm{even}}]\vee z_{j}

\displaystyle=[f,g]\quad(\text{by Poisson rule}).

Therefore,
\Delta
is a BV differential on
(\mathrm{H}^{\bullet},\vee,[-,-])
.

By Theorem
3.4
, Proposition
4.4
, and Proposition
6.10
, when the Nakayama automorphism
\sigma
is not semisimple (i.e.,
\mathrm{char}({\mathbb{K}})\mid\operatorname{ord}\,(\sigma)
), the Gerstenhaber algebra
(\mathrm{HH}^{*}(\Lambda),\vee,[-,-])
satisfies the assumptions of Criterion
7.7
. We therefore obtain the following result.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Z_{e}/J^{N}
be the truncated basic cycle algebra, and the Nakayama automorphism
\sigma
is not semisimple. Then,
(\mathrm{HH}^{*}(\Lambda),\vee,[-,-])
is a BV algebra. More precisely, the
\Delta
-operator is given as follows:

(1)

Let
n=2h\geq 2
. For any
x\in\mathrm{HH}^{n}(\Lambda)
,
\Delta_{n}(x)=0
.

(2)

Let
n=2h+1\geq 1
. For
v_{1;\;hN+1,j}
with
1\leq j\leq N-1
and
j\equiv hN+1\;(\mathrm{mod}\;e)
, assume that
hN-j+1=h_{1}e
, we have

\Delta_{n}\big(v_{1;\;hN+1,j}\big)=h_{1}\,x_{hN,j-1}.

Remark 7.9
.

Note that the BV operator
\Delta
in Theorem
7.8
is not unique. For instance, in case (2), for any
0\neq a\in{\mathbb{K}}
, if we define

\Delta_{n}\big(v_{1;\;hN+1,j}\big)=(h_{1}+a)\,x_{hN,j-1}

and keep values of
\Delta
on even-degree elements unchanged, then this defines another BV algebra structure on
(\mathrm{HH}^{*}(\Lambda),\vee,[-,-])
.

The authors of
[
14
]
suggested the following example of a self-injective Nakayama algebra with non diagonalisable Nakayama automorphism, which may be a possible example of a Frobenius algebra whose Hochschild cohomology ring is NOT a BV algebra. However, this is not the case.

.

Combining Theorems

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
\Lambda={\mathbb{K}}Z_{e}/J^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, be a truncated basic cycle algebra. The the Hochschild cohomology ring
\mathrm{HH}^{*}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is a BV algebra.
