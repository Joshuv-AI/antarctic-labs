An intelligent drug supply chain management and recommendation framework using blockchain and TRPO-driven multi-agent learning
Authors: Shahrzad Bastani Alahabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pharmaceutical companies increasingly face difficulties in tracking products across the supply chain, enabling counterfeiters to introduce fake medicines that cause substantial economic losses and serious health risks. A mechanism capable of tracing and monitoring drug movement at every stage is therefore essential. Blockchain offers a promising foundation for secure and transparent supply chain tracking. This paper introduces a two-module framework. It integrates a blockchain-based drug supply chain management (DSCM) system with a multi-agent recommendation model driven by trust region policy optimization (TRPO). The first module employs a customized blockchain to continuously record, monitor, and verify drug movement within a simulated smart pharmaceutical environment. The second module is a sentiment analysis (SA) that operates with two TRPO agents in a blockchain-secured setting. To enhance policy performance, the TRPO agents incorporate entropy regularization. This setup specifically addresses key SA challenges, including handling unlabeled data, feature selection, and class imbalance mitigation. The first agent applies semi-supervised learning (SSL) with pseudo-labels on high-confidence unlabeled samples to expand the training set. The second agent performs SA, applies Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) for feature ranking, and uses reward mechanisms to improve performance on underrepresented classes. The framework was evaluated on two large real-world drug review datasets, Drugs.com and Druglib.com. For Drugs.com, the blockchain module achieved 3.015-second latency and 172.322 tps throughput, while the SA model reached 93.250% accuracy and 94.329% F-measure. For Druglib.com, latency was 2.930 s, throughput was 189.538 tps, accuracy was 95.192%, and F-measure was 96.257%. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the framework in analyzing patient reviews. It successfully provides secure supply chain recording and sentiment-based insights within controlled experimental conditions.

Introduction

Counterfeit medications pose a severe threat to global health and economic stability, particularly in developing regions, where they account for nearly 30% of the market1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), substandard or falsified drugs cause over 200,000 preventable deaths annually, while the pharmaceutical industry faces economic losses exceeding $200 billion2. Although security investments account for nearly 25% of healthcare supply chain expenditures, traditional detection systems still fail to address the inherent complexity and opacity of global distribution networks3. This persistent vulnerability necessitates a transition to secure, transparent, and intelligent pharmaceutical supply chains that ensure drug authenticity and patient safety in real time.

Beyond securing the physical distribution of pharmaceuticals, ensuring that medications align with individual patient needs is equally vital for therapeutic success4. In this context, SA-driven drug recommendation systems (DRSs) serve as a strategic bridge between supply chain integrity and clinical outcomes. By integrating patient feedback into supply chain analytics, these systems facilitate a feedback-driven architecture where real-world efficacy and safety data directly inform demand forecasting. This integration enables the detection of underperforming products or counterfeit anomalies at the consumer level, thereby validating both the pharmaceutical authenticity and the clinical utility of the medication5.

To address these supply chain vulnerabilities, this study integrates blockchain technology as the foundational layer for a secure and intelligent DSCM framework. Unlike centralized databases, the decentralized and immutable nature of blockchain ensures end-to-end traceability and data integrity throughout the pharmaceutical distribution process6. Through peer-to-peer validation and consensus protocols, the proposed system eliminates data silos and prevents fraudulent record entry, thereby establishing a unified, immutable ledger for all stakeholders. Beyond enhancing transparency, this decentralized architecture provides the fault tolerance and systemic resilience required to maintain operational continuity within multi-actor logistics environments7.

For the recommendation framework, various neural architectures, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), and transformers such as generative pretrained transformer (GPT), have been extensively explored for SA8. However, their performance in specialized clinical domains is often constrained by a reliance on large-scale labeled datasets, which are both costly and scarce9. While SSL leverages unlabeled data through techniques such as pseudo-labeling and consistency training, most existing frameworks employ static sampling strategies10. These fixed criteria fail to adapt to the evolving state of the model, limiting the selection of the most informative samples11. To overcome these limitations, this study introduces a dynamic reinforcement learning (RL) approach, based on TRPO, to guide the SSL sample selection process. Integrating entropy regularization into the TRPO framework stabilizes policy updates, promotes exploration, and addresses class imbalance, ultimately yielding more robust sentiment classification for drug recommendations.

Effective feature selection remains a cornerstone of high-performance SA, yet traditional strategies often struggle with the high dimensionality and non-linear complexity of clinical text12. Conventional approaches, ranging from wrapper-based techniques such as recursive feature elimination (RFE) to embedded models such as L1-penalized logistic regression (Lasso), often rely on static evaluation metrics that fail to adapt to evolving data patterns during training13. While attention-based deep learning models offer improvements, they often struggle to distinguish between genuine feature importance and mere correlation14. A primary limitation of these methods is their rigidity; they lack a dynamic feedback loop to refine feature prioritization as the model matures15. To address this, our framework embeds SHAP-based feature ranking within an entropy-regularized TRPO loop. This integration facilitates a dynamic, interpretable selection process that adapts to evolving feature relevance, enhancing both engine accuracy and transparency.

Addressing class imbalance is critical for sentiment-driven recommendation systems, as models trained on skewed data often marginalize minority yet significant user sentiments, leading to biased predictions16. While traditional data-level solutions like synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) and NearMiss aim to balance distributions, their static nature often leads to overfitting or the loss of crucial context-specific patterns17. Similarly, algorithmic strategies, such as cost-sensitive learning and threshold tuning, rely on fixed penalty structures that lack the flexibility to adapt during training. Over-reliance on these rigid rules often results in a trade-off where gains in minority class recognition compromise overall model stability18. To overcome these challenges, our framework employs entropy-regularized TRPO coupled with a custom reward function. This mechanism adaptively allocates higher rewards and penalties to the minority class, ensuring a balanced optimization that preserves the nuances of rare sentiments without sacrificing global accuracy.

The aim of this paper is to propose two modules for medical systems: a DSCM module and a DR module. The DSCM module employs a custom blockchain system to ensure traceability and secure transactions across the supply chain. The second module uses a dual-agent TRPO structure to address drug recommendation through sentiment analysis. The dual-agent TRPO structure is designed to reduce reliance on labeled data, improve feature interpretability, and improve performance for underrepresented sentiment classes. The novelty of this work lies in the synergistic interaction of these modules: blockchain, entropy-regularized TRPO training, SSL, and SHAP-based feature selection. Together, these components form an integrated framework that has not, to the best of our knowledge, been used in prior research.

Related works

This section reviews the literature in two main categories: (1) approaches focused on DSCM, and (2) techniques developed for intelligent DRS.

DSCM

This section groups DSCM strategies into three categories: traditional systems, AI-driven models, and blockchain-based solutions. This classification helps clarify how different approaches contribute to the evolution of the field.

Traditional methods

Traditional approaches in DSCM relied heavily on operations research (OR), manual planning, and scenario-based modeling. Techniques such as bi-objective optimization, system dynamics modeling, and risk mapping formed the foundation of resilience planning before the emergence of AI-driven or blockchain-enabled systems. Goswami et al.19 introduced a framework for PSCs that combines supply chain mapping with disruption modeling. The approach incorporates input from subject matter experts (SMEs) to support resilience strategy analysis. However, framework complexity may limit real-time scalability in the face of diverse disruptions. Rajabi et al.20 proposed a bi-objective model for resilient PSC design during pandemics. The approach uses scenario-based discrete programming and backup inventory allocation to minimize both drug shortages and operational costs. Yet, scenario explosion increases model runtime and the burden of parameter calibration. Hong et al.21 used system dynamics (SD) modeling to simulate the resilience of the PSC. The model integrates a digital control tower (DCT) to improve coordination, visibility, and adaptive responses to disruptions. However, the SD model lacks granularity for discrete logistics node behavior.

AI methods

Traditional approaches in DSCM suffer from poor traceability, limited responsiveness, and low resilience to disruptions or the entry of counterfeit drugs. AI techniques address these limitations by enabling real-time data processing and predictive analytics for delivery patterns. They also support dynamic inventory management and automated quality control. AI-based models enhance risk-aware decision-making and anomaly detection throughout the supply chain. These developments have encouraged extensive research into data-driven frameworks that improve adaptability and efficiency in pharmaceutical logistics.

Recent advances in AI have introduced a wide array of techniques to optimize various aspects of DSCM. Broadly, AI-driven approaches in this field can be categorized into two thematic groups based on their methodological focus and target application. The first group applies machine learning (ML), DL, and hybrid optimization techniques to predict deliveries, classify supply patterns, and improve decision-making. These models collectively build adaptive and resilient data-driven logistics networks. Hezam et al.22 used three ML models, including gradient boosting (GB), histogram-based gradient boosting (HGB), and cat boosting (CB), to classify delivery status in healthcare SCM (HSCM). They applied feature selection methods and data preprocessing to improve model input. Interpretability remains limited due to the ensemble structure. Anand et al.23 developed a hybrid DL architecture that combines DenseNet 121, a deep CNN (DCNN), with a one-dimensional CNN (1D CNN) to analyze sequential data in HSCM. The combined complexity increases both training and inference time. Shen et al.24 introduced an AI plus vendor-managed inventory (AI + VMI) framework that integrates enterprise resource planning (ERP) and supply processing and distribution (SPD) systems. This setup enables real-time decision-making in hospital drug logistics. However, its adaptation to low-digital settings is still unresolved. Li25 proposed a dual-objective model for vaccine delivery that incorporates analysis of fuel consumption and delay penalties. The model uses a multi-population evolutionary algorithm with simulated annealing and a custom crossover operator. Simultaneous convergence for both objectives remains computationally intensive. Camur et al.26 tested several regression methods, including linear regression, lasso, ridge, elastic net, random forests (RF), gradient boosting machines (GBM), and neural networks, to predict delivery availability using shipment features. Model accuracy is sensitive to missing data and imbalanced inputs under real-time conditions.

The second group applies deep RL (DRL) frameworks to manage inventory policies that must adapt to uncertain demand and supply conditions. These studies often model the supply chain as a Markov decision process (MDP), where agents learn optimal restocking actions through interaction with a simulated supply environment. Kaur et al.27 used DRL to optimize pharmaceutical inventory replenishment modeled as an MDP. The system applied real-time variables and a continuous action space to minimize shortages and expirations. However, model generalization may suffer as supply chain dynamics shift. Mohamadi et al.28 applied a DRL framework using the advantage actor-critic (A2C) algorithm to allocate perishable inventory in a two-echelon VMI system affected by random demand and supply changes. Yet, learning stability declines when retail node behavior becomes highly volatile.

Blockchain-based methods

Centralized drug supply chain systems typically do not ensure transparency or shared oversight. This allows powerful parties to modify records without alerting other involved stakeholders. In contrast, blockchain-based models preserve data integrity through cryptographic techniques, consensus protocols, and tamper-resistant ledgers. These systems enhance visibility, traceability, and secure information exchange among all participants. An online survey found that most users had a positive attitude toward adopting blockchain technology for pharmaceutical logistics.

Recent advancements in blockchain applications for DSCM can be categorized into three thematic groups. Each reflects a distinct technical focus, architectural design, and use case. The first group involves blockchain–Internet of things (IoT) integration for real-time tracking, environmental monitoring (e.g., temperature), and secure data logging to enhance traceability, cold chain management, and fraud prevention. In 2023, Hu et al.29 designed an intelligent vaccine supply chain (VSC) by combining blockchain for tamper-proof records, IoT sensors for real-time tracking, and ML for demand prediction. Yet, synchronizing these technologies in real time introduces integration complexity and risks data inconsistency. Nanda et al.6 formulated an architecture linking IoT sensors with blockchain ledgers to enable secure authentication and transparent tracking throughout the medical supply chain. Still, network jitter challenges real-time ledger updates, reducing operational stability. Abdallah et al.30 built a decentralized pharmaceutical sales system using Ethereum smart contracts and IoT monitoring to manage delivery status, ensure compliance, and automate refunds. A drawback is the decline in contract execution reliability under high transaction volumes. Chen et al.7 combined systematic review and expert interviews to develop a blockchain–IoT PSC model focused on post-pandemic resilience. Yet, hardware diversity across hospitals and logistics systems limits interoperability. Turki et al.31 introduced a decentralized drug traceability framework that leverages IoT devices, smart contracts, off-chain storage, and non-fungible tokens (NFTs) to ensure data integrity. Nonetheless, constrained IoT nodes face bandwidth and processing challenges related to NFT integration. Tahmasbzadeh et al.32 developed a traceable supply chain system using Hyperledger Besu and the interplanetary file system (IPFS), in which smart contracts and off-chain hash storage ensure data authenticity. Yet enabling privacy-preserving interoperability across siloed systems remains unresolved. In 2024, Mangala33 presented a layered architecture incorporating IoT, cloud computing, and blockchain via the IoT–mist–edge–fog–cloud (IMEFC) model to enhance responsiveness and security. Despite this, reliance on Filecoin may delay data retrieval under constrained bandwidth. Rizzardi et al.34 implemented a blockchain–IoT framework based on Hyperledger Fabric (HLF) to automate transactions, ensure secure onboarding, and maintain tamper-resistant logs. A limitation remains in scaling secure device registration across large networks. In 2025, Nagargoje et al.35 proposed a blockchain-based framework that integrates fifth-generation (5G) networks and IoT devices. The framework aims to enhance communication reliability, automate transactions using smart contracts, and ensure data integrity, confidentiality, and traceability within healthcare supply chains. However, scalability and interoperability remain challenging for large-scale deployment. Liu et al.36 developed a healthcare supply chain (HSC) framework enabled by IoT devices. The framework combines edge computing, cloud computing, AI-based predictive analytics, and blockchain technology to improve traceability, security, and operational resilience. However, energy efficiency and protocol interoperability still pose implementation challenges.

The second group centers on blockchain-only architectures that automate verification and enforce drug provenance through smart contracts and cryptographic protocols, without relying on IoT sensors. In 2023, Bandhu et al.37 created a decentralized HSC on Ethereum, where smart contracts and cryptographic hash functions ensured data integrity while eliminating intermediaries. A key issue is the proof-of-work mechanism of Ethereum, which consumes significant energy during peak loads. Gomasta et al.38 developed a HLF-based platform that uses smart contracts, the elliptic curve digital signature algorithm (ECDSA), and hash-based encryption to secure drug provenance. Yet, integrating with legacy systems presents interoperability challenges. In 2024, Panda et al.39 built a blockchain-based medical supply chain (MSC) system using smart contracts and Web3.js to ensure traceability and dual-party verification, excluding third-party intermediaries. Still, the absence of IoT restricts automated real-time traceability. Datta et al.40 introduced a blockchain-enabled SCM model leveraging the interplanetary file system (IPFS) for off-chain data and smart contracts, with validation via proof-of-work (PoW). PoW-based consensus mechanisms remain computationally expensive. Sharma et al.41 designed a multi-layer authentication architecture on HLF, incorporating smart contracts and quick response (QR) code watermarking to combat counterfeit drugs. Nonetheless, combining cryptographic QR checks with high-throughput supply chain operations adds complexity. Mishra et al.42 implemented a traceable PSC framework using smart contracts, parallel search strategies, and data chunk replication. Formal security analysis confirms tamper resistance, though synchronizing distributed nodes may hinder timely access to data. In 2025, Famous et al.43 developed a blockchain-based framework that uses polymorphic encryption and cloud storage to secure pharmaceutical logistics. The system integrates a decentralized supply chain application (SCMapp) on the Ethereum blockchain for encrypted data management and transparent supplier authentication. However, implementation complexity may hinder scalability across global networks. Kutybayeva et al.44 created the efficient blockchain-enhanced transparent PSC management (EBETPSCM) model. This model integrates blockchain and big data analytics to improve traceability, security, and transparency. It uses HLF to support decentralized consensus and cryptographic validation. However, regulatory alignment and interoperability still require further optimization.

The final category incorporates ML or AI into blockchain-based DSCM systems to detect anomalies, predict delays, and improve security. In 2023, Gruchmann et al.45 applied transaction cost economics (TCE) and conducted 25 semi-structured interviews to explore blockchain integration within the Egyptian pharmaceutical ecosystem. Resistance from traditional stakeholders and a limited understanding of blockchain concepts slowed adoption. Yazdinejad46 developed a cyber-physical system (CPS) driven by ML, using bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) and security analytics to identify malicious activity. A challenge remains in the computational demands and frequent retraining required by transformer-based models. Shah et al.47 designed a hybrid DSCM framework by integrating blockchain with role-based permissions and predictive ML analytics to enable proactive logistics management. Synchronization across distributed nodes and inconsistent data standards limit its scalability. Tanvir et al.48 created a dual-blockchain setup using HLF for confidential data and Quorum for public checks, linked through burn-to-claim and governed via stakeholder reputation. Yet, latency and mismatched governance policies across chains complicate coordination. In 2024, Trautmann et al.49 conducted expert interviews and developed a combined blockchain–IoT–AI architecture to improve traceability and coordination across PSC. Nonetheless, varying levels of digitization and incompatible data formats hinder seamless adoption. In 2025, Rupa et al.50 proposed an Ethereum-based blockchain system for DSCM. The system integrates a decentralized application (DApp) to ensure authorization, confidentiality, and immutability. It uses the Sepolia test network and Truffle Suite for secure transaction tracking. However, deployment scalability and interoperability across heterogeneous systems remain challenging. Mirdad51 developed a blockchain-based pharmaceutical management framework that integrates ML models for demand forecasting and reducing drug waste. It uses a decentralized distributed ledger to provide traceability and transparency. However, optimizing model training and blockchain synchronization remains a challenge.

DRS

Early models in DRS used classical ML methods such as decision trees (DTs), RFs, and support vector machines (SVMs)52–55. This offers fast, interpretable solutions but struggles with feature engineering and generalization. Hybrid techniques later combined collaborative filtering and content-based filtering56,57 to reduce cold-start issues and personalize suggestions, though they often lacked contextual depth. Graph-based and causality-aware models58–61 capture relationships among diseases, drugs, and patient states. These models improve safety and interpretability but often struggle with scalability and high computational requirements. DL models such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs), LSTMs, graph neural networks (GNNs), and graph convolutional networks (GCNs)62–65 have become prominent with the availability of electronic health records. These models handle sequential data and drug-drug interactions but are computationally intensive and complex to train. Recent methods combine natural language processing (NLP) and SA66–68 to process patient feedback and reviews. While they improve personalization, they are sensitive to noisy inputs and limited supervision. These traditional models have laid foundational groundwork, but often fall short in adaptability, interpretability, or robustness, limitations that newer RL-based methods aim to overcome.

Transformer-based models have gained significant traction in DRS for their ability to capture semantic context and model complex relationships in clinical text. Early works used BERT-based models for SA and prescription classification from patient reviews69,70. These models improved contextual understanding, but had limited interpretability and required significant computational resources. More advanced systems utilize multimodal inputs, including text, audio, visuals, and structured clinical data, by employing complex architectures such as Bayesian functional with Gaussian tanh-based deep bidirectional LSTM (BFGT-DBi-LSTM)71 and vision transformers72. While these approaches significantly enhance adaptability, they also result in a substantial increase in overall model complexity. Later hybrid designs combined entity recognition transformers (ERT), large language models (LLMs), and mixtures of experts (MOE)73,74. These models enabled personalization using multimodal patient data, though they struggled with integration and scalability. Recent efforts have integrated GPT-4 and similar LLMs with medical knowledge graphs to enrich RSs75–78. These models use contextual embeddings and structured knowledge to improve drug suggestions and clinical trial recommendations. However, they require curated data and face regulatory barriers when deployed in real-world settings.

Positioning of the proposed model within existing research

While recent blockchain-based frameworks in DSCM have significantly enhanced data integrity and counterfeit detection, they remain primarily focused on the logistics of authenticity. These systems lack the analytical intelligence required for real-time decision-making and personalized DRS. In modern pharmaceutical ecosystems, ensuring a drug is genuine is only half the challenge; ensuring it is the right fit for the evolving feedback of the patient is equally critical. Our work addresses this gap by combining immutable blockchain records with adaptive AI-based recommendations. This creates a unified system for both supply chain security and clinical intelligence.

Furthermore, existing DRS models often operate in a logistical vacuum, ignoring the complexities of the pharmaceutical supply chain, such as drug authenticity and delivery reliability. Technically, many deep learning and transformer-based models struggle with sparse, unlabeled, and imbalanced clinical datasets. As a result, they often produce black-box predictions with limited interpretability. These limitations hinder their adoption in industry and clinical settings, where transparency and evidence-based reasoning are mandatory.

By integrating a dual-agent TRPO architecture within a secure blockchain environment, this study shifts the focus from isolated optimization to an integrated ecosystem. Unlike prior studies that treat traceability and SA as disparate domains, our framework positions them as interdependent layers. This allows the system to use supply chain transparency to validate the recommendation context. At the same time, advanced RL addresses data scarcity, feature selection, and class imbalance. Consequently, this research provides a holistic solution that aligns pharmaceutical supply integrity with high-precision, interpretable patient-centric feedback.

Materials and methods

The architecture of the proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 1. It consists of two main modules. The first module is a blockchain-enabled DSCM that provides secure, immutable, and traceable recording of pharmaceutical transactions. The second module is a multi-agent based on TRPO that performs DRS.

Blockchain-enabled DSCM infrastructure

The system uses a decentralized blockchain network to secure data, improve traceability, and maintain transaction integrity across the PSC. Figure 1 shows the architecture of the DSC framework, in which each participant interacts with the blockchain via a dedicated client interface. This interface supports real-time tracking and management of supply activities. The blockchain records information from all major participants, suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, pharmacies, hospitals, and patients, ensuring transparency and protection from data tampering. Core system assets include medicines, raw materials, purchase orders, and transaction logs. Users access lightweight applications that allow them to initiate operations, verify deliveries, and track product movements. A connected off-chain data repository supports large-scale analysis and feeds the recommendation module. The ML engine processes this data to generate customized drug suggestions, trained on historical sentiment and feedback patterns. Users can also access detailed product information such as cost, shelf life, manufacturing date, and ingredients, helping maintain accuracy and informed decision-making throughout the supply process.

To maintain synchronization of the distributed ledger, peer nodes run the consensus protocol. The process begins when the main supplier sends raw materials to the pharmaceutical company. Each participant interacts with the system through a dedicated interface that allows them to log in, start transactions, and track their activities in real time across the blockchain network. For example, when a manufacturer places an order for raw materials, any peer node can validate the request. Once confirmed, the supplier receives the order. When the materials are ready for shipment, the supplier triggers a confirmation event in the system. When a patient or regulator requests detailed information about a drug, the built-in permissions of the blockchain determine which data can be viewed. Likewise, suppose a doctor places an order with a manufacturer. In that case, the workflow follows a consistent sequence: the doctor is authenticated, a transaction request is submitted, the manufacturer verifies it, and a confirmation message is issued once the transaction is approved and logged on the ledger.

Multi-agent RL for DRS

Figure 2 shows the structure of the proposed DRS. The proposed DRS addresses three primary challenges: data scarcity, class imbalance, and low interpretability of models. The first agent employs a semi-supervised protocol, assigning pseudo-labels to high-confidence unlabeled reviews to expand the effective training dataset. The second agent performs sentiment classification and utilizes SHAP values to rank influential features for clinical interpretation. To mitigate class imbalance, this agent uses a weighted reward mechanism integrated into the TRPO loop that prioritizes underrepresented categories, such as adverse drug reactions. Both agents are optimized using entropy-regularized TRPO.

The SSL agent

The SA agent

The second component functions as an SA engine. It classifies each input sentence into one of several sentiment categories using the labeled dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{L}$$\end{document}. During each training iteration, a sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{{L}_{t}}$$\end{document} is randomly drawn from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{L}$$\end{document}. As shown in Fig. 2, each sentence is passed through GPT and LSTM modules to extract semantic and contextual features. The encoded representation is then passed to a multilayer perceptron (MLP). The MLP performs two key tasks: determining the relative importance of each word and predicting the sentiment label of the sequence. The model outputs a composite vector of size n+m, where n corresponds to the number of words in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{{L}_{t}}$$\end{document} and captures word importance, while m represents sentiment category probabilities. These attention weights improve the interpretability of model predictions. SHAP metrics originate from the Shapley framework in collaborative game theory. The expression used to calculate the SHAP attribution for a specific input feature iii, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\varnothing}}_{i}$$\end{document}, is as Eq. 8:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varnothing\:}_{i}=\sum\:_{S\subseteq\:F\backslash\:\left\{i\right\}}\frac{\left|S\right|!\left(\left|F\right|-\left|S\right|-1\right)!}{\left|F\right|!}\left(v\right(S\cup\:\left\{i\right\})-v(S\left)\right)$$\end{document}

In this formulation, F is the complete set of features, and S is any subset that excludes feature i. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left|S\right|$$\end{document} shows the size of subset S, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left|F\right|$$\end{document} is the total number of features. The model output when only features in S are used is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:v\left(S\right)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:v(S\cup\:\left\{i\right\})$$\end{document} gives the output when feature i is added. SHAP computes the average change in the prediction of the model when feature i is added across all subsets, capturing its overall contribution.

Figure 4 shows how SHAP values are calculated using the feature set {A, B, C}. It explains how each feature affects the model prediction. The process begins by selecting the features of interest, A, B, and C, and forming all possible subsets. To measure the impact of a feature like A, the model compares predictions with and without A across different subsets such as {}, {B}, {C}, and {B, C}. This shows how much A contributes in different situations. These contributions are averaged across all possible feature orders to ensure a fair result. This prevents any feature from being unfairly favored or penalized due to its position. SHAP then assigns a share of the prediction to each feature based on these averages. A positive SHAP value indicates that the feature increases the prediction, while a negative value indicates that it decreases it. This method clearly breaks down the prediction and shows how much each feature matters.

Overal algorithm

Figure 5 illustrates the complete workflow of the proposed two-module framework. The process begins with a blockchain layer. This layer logs each transaction and checks data integrity at all stages of the supply chain. The next step is text preprocessing. This step helps reduce irrelevant content and improves the quality of input data for the SA task. Two key operations are performed. The first is stop-word removal, which eliminates frequent words like “the”, “is”, and “and” that convey little meaning. Removing them reduces the input size and focuses learning on important tokens. The second is stemming, which shortens words to their base forms, such as turning “running” into “run”. The terms in this group are similar, which improves the generalization of the model.

After preprocessing, the system uses the available labeled data to train Agent 2, which acts as the main sentiment classifier. At the same time, Agent 1 generates pseudo-labels by selecting high-confidence unlabeled samples. These selections rely on an entropy threshold and margin-based rules. If the selection criteria are met, the pseudo-labeled samples are added to the labeled set. The system then applies the TRPO algorithm to update Agent 1 and enhance its future decisions. Afterward, Agent 2 is retrained using the expanded labeled dataset. This interaction between both agents continues until a termination condition is met. Finally, the framework evaluates performance on the test set, completing the training cycle.

Experimental validation

This section provides a detailed summary of the dataset, outlining its main characteristics and the role it played during experimentation. It then explains the metrics used to evaluate the performance of the proposed framework. After that, the configuration parameters and experimental setup of the model are described. The section concludes with an analysis of the results, highlighting key observations and their relevance to the study goals.

Datasets

To assess the performance of the proposed framework, two major online platforms, Drugs.com and Druglib.com, were utilized as data sources. Drugs.com is widely recognized as a large and frequently used online resource that provides drug information for both patients and healthcare professionals. The platform allows users to share personal experiences with specific treatments and to assign a 10-point satisfaction rating that reflects their overall perception of the effectiveness of a drug. Likewise, Druglib.com functions as an informational hub for healthcare providers and the general public. Although the number of reviews hosted on Druglib.com is smaller, the content is more structured, with user feedback divided into benefits, adverse effects, and general observations. Each review also includes separate five-level ratings for side effects and treatment effectiveness, ranging from no effect to highly effective or severe. After automated data extraction, two separate datasets were created: 215,063 reviews from Drugs.com and 3,551 reviews from Druglib.com. The Drugs.com dataset contains 6,345 distinct medications, whereas Druglib.com contains 541. Despite the difference in scope, the average number of comments per medication is significantly higher on Drugs.com (approximately 58.86) than on Druglib.com (7.66). Druglib.com includes a broader set of medical conditions because users define the condition labels themselves, without a standard vocabulary. As a result, Druglib.com data contains many spelling differences, synonyms, and combined condition names that must be standardized during preprocessing.

Table 1 outlines the characteristics of the datasets employed in this study. Although each entry included a rating score, explicit sentiment labels were not provided. The ratings were converted into three sentiment categories. Scores greater than 7 were labeled as positive (+ 1), ratings below 4 as negative (-1), and scores between 4 and 7 as neutral (0). These thresholds follow prior studies on mapping numeric satisfaction ratings to sentiment categories in healthcare review datasets79. In these studies, scores of 8–10 usually indicate clear satisfaction, 1–3 indicate strong dissatisfaction, and intermediate scores represent mixed experiences.

To sanity-check the label construction, we manually inspected a random sample of 150 reviews and compared the sentiment labels derived from ratings with human judgments. In the high-confidence regions (ratings ≥ 8 and ≤ 3), the agreement between human labels and threshold-based labels exceeded 90%. Most disagreements occurred for boundary ratings (4–7). These ratings were intentionally grouped into the neutral class to reduce misclassification due to polarity. This analysis suggests that the adopted thresholds provide a reliable supervision signal for coarse-grained sentiment classification. However, some noise near the rating boundaries is unavoidable.

It is important to note that while these datasets primarily provide patient review content and satisfaction ratings, they serve as the foundational workload for evaluating the proposed framework. In our integrated model, each review and its associated metadata are treated as distinct events within the pharmaceutical supply chain. These records are encoded into the blockchain module to simulate and track the drug distribution lifecycle from manufacturers to patients. This process enables the evaluation of the traceability, integrity, and performance of the system across different data volumes.

To ensure the robustness and reproducibility of our framework, we adopted a rigorous data splitting strategy. The unified dataset included both blockchain-derived features and sentiment scores. It was divided into two components: an 85% Training/Validation pool and a 15% final test set. The Test set was strictly reserved and remained completely unseen throughout all training and hyperparameter optimization phases. Within the 85% pool, 5-fold cross-validation was used to split the data into training and validation subsets. This process was applied during model training and hyperparameter optimization. Partitioning was performed synchronously across both data domains to prevent data leakage. This also ensured consistent multi-modal integration between blockchain metrics and user-generated sentiment. This strategy provides a reliable and unbiased basis for evaluating the predictive accuracy of the system in a simulated pharmaceutical context.

Evaluation metrics

We evaluate the proposed model in two stages: the blockchain component and the SA module. For the blockchain evaluation, we focus on five key performance metrics: latency, throughput, transaction delay time (TDT), consensus delay (CD), and malicious node detection rate (MNDR). Latency refers to the time it takes for a transaction to be confirmed and committed to the blockchain. It shows how quickly the system responds to user actions. Low latency is crucial for real-time visibility in PSCs. Throughput refers to the number of transactions the system can handle per second (TPS). A higher throughput indicates better support for large-scale drug distribution networks. TDT measures the total time a transaction spends in the system, from submission to confirmation. This metric shows how quickly the system processes a full transaction, which is critical when minimizing delays. CD is the time needed for nodes to reach agreement on new data. A shorter consensus delay helps the system update blocks quickly. This becomes especially important when the network is under heavy use or under attack. MNDR measures how effectively the system identifies and isolates compromised nodes. A high MNDR indicates strong protection against insider threats and network-level attacks. Together, these five metrics help evaluate whether the blockchain can deliver fast, secure, and reliable drug tracking in practice.

To comprehensively evaluate our SA model, we selected five performance metrics: accuracy, F-measure, balanced accuracy (BA), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and area under the curve (AUC). Accuracy provides a general measure of correct predictions but can be misleading in imbalanced settings. Therefore, we included the F-measure, which balances precision and recall, making it more informative for classes with fewer samples. BA addresses class imbalance by calculating the average recall for each class. This prevents most classes from dominating the overall performance score. MCC is a robust metric for multi-class evaluation. It uses all elements of the confusion matrix and produces a single score that remains reliable even when class sizes are unequal. Finally, AUC measures the ability of the model to discriminate between classes across different thresholds, providing insights into its ranking performance and sensitivity to class probabilities.

Setting

The study was conducted on a 64-bit Windows 11 system equipped with an Intel Core i9-12900 K processor, 64 gigabytes (GB) of Double Data Rate 5 (DDR5) random access memory (RAM), and an NVIDIA Ray Tracing Texel eXtreme (RTX) 3090 graphics processing unit (GPU) with 24 GB of video RAM (VRAM). We executed all training, inference, blockchain simulations, and analysis on this system to maintain consistency and ensure reproducible performance. The blockchain component of the DSCM module was implemented using Hyperledger Fabric v2.4. Smart contracts (chaincode) were written in Go v1.19. We used Docker v24.0.2 and Docker Compose v2.17 to orchestrate the blockchain nodes and orderers. These containers were deployed on a local Kubernetes cluster managed by Minikube v1.31. The multi-agent SA recommendation module was built using Python 3.10.6. We implemented the RL agents using the Stable-Baselines3 v1.7.0 library. PyTorch v1.13.1 + cu117 served as the DL backend, enabling full GPU acceleration. Entropy-regularized TRPO was customized on top of the native TRPO implementation provided by Stable-Baselines3 (SB3). Text preprocessing and feature extraction, including SHAP-based feature ranking, were handled using scikit-learn v1.3.0, Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) v3.8.1, and SHAP v0.42.1. SSL relied on pseudo-labeling logic built with NumPy v1.24.3 and Pandas v1.5.3. Visualizations were created using Matplotlib v3.7.1 and Seaborn v0.12.2.

To set the hyperparameters of the proposed SA model, we used the Bayesian optimization with HyperBand (BOHB) algorithm. Table 2 shows the hyperparameters optimized using BOHB. We selected the BOHB method because it can explore large hyperparameter spaces effectively without sacrificing computational efficiency. Traditional grid and random search methods often waste resources on poor configurations. In contrast, BOHB combines Bayesian optimization with the early-stopping mechanism of HyperBand to improve efficiency. The combination of Bayesian optimization and early stopping helps the search converge faster with fewer iterations. BOHB also adapts dynamically to the search landscape, balancing exploration and exploitation based on observed performance. It is particularly effective in DRL settings, where training costs are high and reward signals can be noisy. Thus, BOHB was ideal for optimizing parameters across TRPO, SSL, and SHAP-based modules in a stable and resource-efficient manner.

To ensure unbiased optimization, BOHB was executed exclusively on the 85% training/validation pool described in Sects. 4 − 1. A 5-fold cross-validation framework was used to guide the search process. Within this framework, each fold further partitioned the pool into internal training and validation subsets. Specifically, the model was trained on the training subset. Meanwhile, the BOHB algorithm used the validation subset to iteratively evaluate hyperparameter configurations. This process prevents data leakage and ensures that hyperparameters are selected based on their generalization performance within the training/validation pool. Final evaluation was then performed on the isolated test set. The selected hyperparameter ranges in this table were based on earlier empirical studies in SA, which suggest that similar parameter boundaries provide stable convergence and reliable generalization across datasets.

Drugs.com and Druglib.com are review-based datasets rather than native supply-chain transaction logs. Therefore, blockchain transactions were constructed from the extracted review records and their associated metadata, including drug ID, rating, timestamp, and sentiment label. Each review instance was encoded as a transaction event within a simulated private blockchain network to approximate pharmaceutical supply-chain recording processes. The blockchain experiments were conducted in a controlled environment to generate transactional workloads proportional to the size of each dataset. This approach enables the evaluation of system-level performance under different transaction volumes. At the same time, it preserves the structural characteristics of drug-related records.

The results reported in the tables were obtained using a fixed 15% hold-out test set. This test set remained entirely unseen during the model selection and hyperparameter optimization phases. After determining the optimal configuration via BOHB and cross-validation on the training/validation split, the model was trained 10 times with different random seeds and the optimized hyperparameters. The final performance metrics are reported as the mean value ± standard deviation across these ten independent runs. This approach accounts for performance fluctuations caused by stochastic initialization. It also ensures that the reported results reflect the actual predictive capability of the model rather than a specific random seed. This rigorous evaluation protocol was applied uniformly to both the blockchain-derived feature extraction and the SA modules.

A steady improvement in performance can be seen across models from IMEFC to ML-DPSC as AI and multi-chain mechanisms become more sophisticated. For example, compared to 5GBC-IoT, ML-DPSC reduces latency by 47.1% and increases MNDR by 12.2%. These gains highlight the effectiveness of integrating AI modules such as ML and BERT. ML-DPSC achieves the best overall results among all baseline models. It records 3.704 s latency, 167.432 tps throughput, 5.200 s TDT, and 90.991% MNDR, due to its combination of ML for decision-making and optimized blockchain operations. However, it still shows moderate delay in CD (2.854 s), indicating partial bottlenecks in certificate distribution. Similarly, on the Druglib.com dataset, ML-DPSC remains the top performer with slightly lower latency (3.345 s) and CD (1.323 s). Older hybrid designs, such as ECA-BC and 5GBC-IoT, achieve a moderate balance in performance but still lag in CD and MNDR, showing limited adaptability under complex workloads. AI-augmented and dual-chain models such as CPS-BERT-SD, RBML-SCM, and DBC-BTCQ show progressive improvement in MNDR (up to ~ 90.4%) and TDT. This confirms that security-driven and role-based mechanisms significantly enhance system reliability.

Our proposed model achieves superior performance over all baseline models and consistently leads in all five evaluation metrics on both datasets. On Drugs.com, it reduces latency by 18.6% and increases throughput by 10.1% compared to ML-DPSC. The TDT drops by 28.4%, while CD is reduced by 48.7%. Additionally, the MNDR increases from 90.991% to 94.719%, representing a relative gain of 4.1%. These results are enabled by the two-module design. The first module uses a TRPO-based recommendation agent to optimize transaction prioritization. The second module employs a blockchain protocol that incorporates efficient consensus and streamlined node communication to reduce overhead. On the Druglib.com dataset, latency decreases by 12.4%, throughput rises by 10.0%, and CD is minimized by 15.1% relative to ML-DPSC. The MNDR also improves by 3.0%, indicating better drug traceability. These improvements confirm that the model performs well in terms of scalability, ensures data integrity throughout the supply chain, and accelerates the verification process. The consistent gains across both datasets further demonstrate the generalizability and robustness of the model under different workload conditions.

The observed differences in blockchain performance between Drugs.com and Druglib.com are primarily attributable to the disparity in dataset size and transaction volume. Drugs.com contains substantially more review records and therefore generates a significantly higher transactional workload on the blockchain network. This increased load affects latency, throughput, and consensus-related metrics. As a result, performance differs across the two datasets.

To ensure the statistical significance of our findings, we compared performance across 10 independent runs with different random seeds. For each metric, paired samples were constructed by matching the score of the proposed model to the baseline score obtained with the same random seed. This process reduces bias caused by stochastic variations in data shuffling and training. We employed paired t-tests to evaluate the statistical significance of the per-run performance differences. This test is suitable for paired measurements in repeated-run experimental settings. The proposed model was compared separately against all baseline methods on the Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets. Each test was run separately for the five evaluation metrics: latency, throughput, TDT, CD, and MNDR. Results show consistent and significant improvements over even the most competitive baselines. For the Drugs.com dataset, the proposed model outperformed ML-DPSC, the strongest baseline across several metrics. It achieved a lower latency (mean difference 0.689 s, p < 0.001, 95% confidence interval (CI) [0.601, 0.775], Cohen’s d 2.21), higher throughput (mean improvement 17.106 tps, p < 0.001, 95% CI [15.883, 18.329], Cohen’s d 1.93), and an MNDR increase of 3.73% (p = 0.004, 95% CI [1.52, 5.93], Cohen’s d 1.63). Similar significant improvements were observed when comparing against DApp and IBA-PSC, with p-values consistently below 0.005 and large effect sizes (d > 1.4). For the Druglib.com dataset, the proposed model again outperformed ML-DPSC significantly in all five metrics. It reduced latency by 0.415 s (p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.382, 0.447], d = 2.03) and increased throughput by 4.89 tps (p < 0.001, 95% CI [4.11, 5.67], d = 1.96). For MNDR, the difference between the proposed model and ML-DPSC was 2.8%, also statistically significant (p = 0.002, 95% CI [1.18, 4.42], d = 1.45). Overall, across all comparisons, the proposed model demonstrated p-values below 0.005, 95% confidence intervals that excluded zero, and Cohen’s d values consistently above 1.2, indicating strong to powerful effects. These statistical findings confirm that the observed improvements are not caused by random variation. They reflect genuine performance gains and strengthen the evidence for the robustness and superiority of the proposed blockchain framework in supply chain applications.

Figure 6 shows the relationship between the number of blockchain nodes and system performance across three metrics: latency, throughput, and central processing unit (CPU) usage for Drugs.com and Druglib.com. In blockchain systems, a node is a participant (e.g., a hospital, pharmacy, or distributor) that maintains a copy of the ledger and helps validate transactions. When the number of nodes increases from 10 to 100, latency rises because consensus among nodes becomes more demanding. The effect is more pronounced in the Drugs.com dataset, which shows higher delays across all node counts. However, throughput also improves steadily, reflecting enhanced parallelism in transaction processing. CPU usage scales predictably, highlighting the computational cost of maintaining consensus across nodes. The Drugs.com dataset consistently consumes more resources due to its more complex transaction patterns. These trends confirm that the proposed blockchain framework is scalable and robust under realistic supply chain conditions. Even under heavier loads, performance continues to scale efficiently. This result validates the effectiveness of blockchain in DSCM for maintaining traceability, resilience, and trust across multi-party interactions.

To evaluate the security performance of the blockchain component in our DSCM model, we implemented a controlled simulation of four well-known attack types: Sybil, denial-of-service (DoS), double-spending, and Eclipse attacks. Assessing these attack vectors is important because they target key properties of decentralized systems. These properties include trust, data integrity, and availability. Vulnerability to these attacks raises concerns about the potential applicability of the system in practical environments, especially in safety-critical domains such as the pharmaceutical industry. For each attack, we computed a resistance score, an interpretable metric ranging from 0% to 100%. This score quantifies how effectively the system resists each attack. For example, in Sybil attacks, the score was computed as one minus the ratio of malicious nodes successfully injected to the total number of injected nodes. Figure 7 shows the resistance scores across the four attack types for the Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets. The blockchain model consistently maintained high resistance, particularly on Drugs.com, with scores exceeding 90% in all scenarios. For Druglib.com, the system also performed robustly, with scores ranging from 87% to 90%. These findings indicate that the proposed blockchain framework demonstrates strong resilience against major attack scenarios while maintaining efficient performance under the simulated experimental conditions.

This evaluation is important since decentralized systems need to maintain data consistency and recover automatically without any central control. Analysis of SA

In this section, we compare our SA model with six traditional models and eight transformer models. The traditional models include: HyDRA (hybrid DR architecture)57, RNN-CA-DR (RNN and cellular automata for DR)62, NLP-RNN-DR (NLP and RNN for DR)63, NRC-DRS (NLP, RNN, and cellular automata-based DRS)66, SVR‑MedRec (sentiment-based Vader and RNN medication RS)67, FAERS-DR (food and drug administration adverse event reporting system-based drug repurposing framework)68.

The transformer models includes: DLSA-Rec (DR-based SA for DR)69, PBERT-Rx (PharmaBERT-based RS using patient feedback and sentiment classification)70, BFGT-PSC (Bayes functional and gaussian Tanh-enhanced deep bidirectional LSTM for personalized sentiment-aware clustering-based DR)71, ViT-MEDREC (vision transformer-based medication RS)72, LLM-KG-CTR (LLM and knowledge graph-based clinical trial recommendation)75, LLM‑MG-CN (LLM-based medication guidance system for Chinese)76, CT-PASMR (convolution + transformer for personalized and adverse-safe safe medication recommendation)77, and MTrans-KG (multi-pathway transformer and knowledge graph-based model for drug–drug interactions prediction)78.

To ensure a fair comparison, all hyperparameters of the baseline models were optimized using the BOHB method, like the proposed model. The evaluation results using the Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets are presented in Tables 5 and 6. FAERS-DR showed consistent strength across both datasets. It outperformed HyDRA and RNN-CA-DR by notable margins because it relies on structured regulatory data and adverse event reporting systems. On the Drugs.com dataset, FAERS-DR achieved an Accuracy of 76.665%, which is 4.9% higher than HyDRA. Similarly, it surpassed NRC-DRS by 4.2% in F-measure and 3.5% in BA, benefiting from its ability to integrate structured regulatory signals. Among Transformer-based models, MTrans-KG had the best performance. It achieved 88.919% Accuracy and 89.977% F-measure on Drugs.com. These results reflect its use of multi-pathway Transformer encodings and drug–drug interaction awareness. CT-PASMR closely followed with 86.318% accuracy, reflecting its strong design, which focuses on improving recommendation stability based on adverse event information. BFGT-PSC underperformed compared to MTrans-KG. Although it uses a probabilistic clustering mechanism, its limited explainability and reliance on Gaussian-Tanh assumptions reduce its effectiveness. ViT-MEDREC showed moderate performance with 82.180% Accuracy. Its reliance on visual embeddings reduced effectiveness in text-centric domains. On Druglib.com, the trend remained consistent: MTrans-KG again topped performance (89.321% Accuracy), while models like PBERT-Rx (82.038%) and DLSA-Rec (80.516%) lagged, likely due to their simplified sentiment pipelines or shallow BERT tuning.

Our proposed SA model outperformed all compared approaches across every metric on both datasets. On Drugs.com, it improved over MTrans-KG by + 4.86% in Accuracy, + 4.83% in F-measure, + 4.97% in BA, + 11.0% in MCC, and + 8.6% in AUC. Similar improvements were observed on Druglib.com, where our model achieved + 5.55% higher Accuracy, + 5.27% higher F-measure, + 5.74% higher BA, and + 9.6% higher MCC than MTrans-KG. Notably, our model also surpassed CT-PASMR by + 8.87% in Accuracy and + 8.74% in F-measure, demonstrating stronger generalization and robustness. The substantial MCC improvements across both datasets (+ 13.4% on Drugs.com and + 6.0% on Druglib.com compared with LLM-KG-CTR) show that the model achieves a better balance between sentiment classes, especially for the underrepresented ones. In terms of AUC, the proposed model achieved 0.899 on Drugs.com and 0.908 on Druglib.com. These results confirm its stronger ranking and probability-discrimination abilities.

Following the statistical methodology described in Sects. 4–4, we assessed statistical significance using paired t-tests over 10 independent runs for each metric and dataset. We evaluated whether the observed improvements of the proposed SA model were due to random variation. To do this, we used paired t-tests on five performance metrics (accuracy, F-measure, BA, MCC, and AUC) presented in Tables 5 and 6. On the Drugs.com dataset, when comparing our model to the best-performing transformer baselines such as MTrans-KG, CT-PASMR, and LLM-MG-CN, the p-values across all metrics were consistently below 0.003, indicating high statistical significance. For instance, the AUC difference between our model and MTrans-KG yielded a p-value of 0.0014 and a 95% CI of [0.066, 0.094]. The corresponding Cohen’s d value of 1.21 indicates a large effect size. Similarly, compared with CT-PASMR on F-measure, we observed a p-value of 0.0027, a 95% CI of [0.981, 1.681], and a Cohen’s d of 1.07. On the Druglib.com dataset, the results followed a similar pattern. Comparing our model to MTrans-KG yielded p-values below 0.002 across all metrics, with the AUC improvement yielding a p-value of 0.0011, a 95% CI of [0.057, 0.083], and a Cohen’s d of 1.18. When compared with ViT-MEDREC and LLM-KG-CTR, the differences also reached statistical significance. The p-values were below 0.005, the 95% CIs excluded zero, and Cohen’s d values were above 0.95, indicating strong practical significance. Across both datasets and all comparison models, p-values were below 0.005, 95% CIs did not exclude zero, and Cohen’s d values ranged from 0.88 to 1.25, confirming large effect sizes. These consistent statistical results strongly support that the performance advantages of our SA model are not only empirically meaningful but also statistically robust. This confirms the model is significantly superior in both effectiveness and generalizability compared to existing state-of-the-art solutions.

Performance comparisons across the Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets are shown in Tables 8 and 9. Starting with blockchain, removing it led to moderate performance drops (Drugs.com: −3.06% AUC, Druglib.com: −3.96% AUC), confirming its importance in preserving trust and transparency in parameter exchange. Excluding the SSL module caused a significant degradation across all metrics. On the Drugs.com dataset, AUC dropped by 24.1%. On Druglib.com, it fell by 23.7%. This shows that SSL contributes heavily to extracting semantically rich representations. The SHAP module also played a key role in interpretability and feature weighting. Its removal led to a 28.2% drop in AUC on Drugs.com and a 25.9% drop on Druglib.com, indicating the utility of SHAP in guiding model focus toward sentiment-critical words. Removing the IC module led to a further decline in AUC (− 34.2% on Drugs.com and − 32.8% on Druglib.com). This shows that addressing class imbalance is critical for maintaining precision and recall, especially when negative reviews are sparse.

Removing TRPO, the tailored RL policy optimizer, led to steep declines in MCC (Drugs.com: −94.9%; Druglib.com: −61.9%) and AUC (Drugs.com: −27.5%; Druglib.com: −25%). Interestingly, excluding ER also degraded MCC (Drugs.com: −68.0%; Druglib.com: −42.9%), indicating that ER is crucial for stable exploration. Removing ER reduced AUC by 24.3% on Drugs.com and 24% on Druglib.com. This indicates that ER indirectly improves generalization by reducing overfitting during RL training. The impact of BOHB was also considerable, with AUC declines of 18.4% (Drugs.com) and 18.3% (Druglib.com), indicating that precise tuning greatly enhances model convergence. SCV also had a non-trivial impact (AUC drops of 10.7% and 10.9%, respectively), reinforcing the importance of balanced data splits in highly imbalanced settings.

When TRPO or IC were removed together with SSL or SHAP, the model performance declined further. This indicates that these modules reinforce one another. For instance, while TRPO optimizes policy selection, SHAP ensures input relevance, and their combination yields stronger, more generalizable learning. In summary, the best performance emerges when all modules are active. Each component contributes uniquely: SHAP for explainability, IC for fairness, TRPO + ER for efficient learning, BOHB for tuning, SCV for robustness, and blockchain for trust. These results confirm that the synergistic integration of all modules drives the superiority of the model. Removing any single component caused consistent performance degradation, confirming that the framework works effectively only when all parts function together.

Following the statistical methodology described in Sects. 4–4, we assessed the performance of our full model against its critical ablation variants using paired t-tests to evaluate statistical significance. We evaluated five performance indicators: accuracy, F-measure, BA, MCC, and AUC, using the results reported in Tables 8 and 9. In each case, the full model showed statistically significant improvements over every ablation variant. On the Drugs.com dataset, the removal of SHAP, SSL, and IC had the most pronounced statistical impact. For example, removing SHAP resulted in a p-value < 0.001 for AUC. The 95% CI was [0.213, 0.267], and the effect size (Cohen’s d) was 2.91, indicating a huge difference. Similarly, for the SSL ablation, the p-value for BA was < 0.001, the CI was [0.155, 0.201], and Cohen’s d reached 2.44. The IC ablation showed a p-value < 0.0005 for MCC with a CI of [0.974, 1.017] and a d of 2.87. On the Druglib.com dataset, the SHAP and SSL ablations again exhibited the largest statistical gaps. For instance, AUC differences between the full model and SHAP-free version yielded p < 0.001, CI [0.214, 0.269], and d = 2.73. The TRPO ablation also showed moderate but consistent significance (e.g., MCC p = 0.003, CI [0.502, 0.569], d = 1.29). In contrast, the absence of BOHB and SCV led to less severe but still statistically significant degradation, with AUC p-values ranging from 0.005 to 0.01 and effect sizes of 0.9–1.1. Across all comparisons, p-values were consistently below 0.01. CIs never crossed zero. Cohen’s d values ranged from 0.9 to 2.9, reflecting moderate to substantial effects. Overall, these statistical findings strongly validate that the performance improvements introduced by the full model are not random artifacts. Instead, they confirm the critical role of each subsystem and reinforce that the proposed architecture is most effective when all components operate jointly in an integrated manner.

Analysis of the SSL model

This part of the analysis evaluates the performance of the SSL model. The agent is trained using TRPO policy optimization. It is then compared against several established SSL strategies. To maintain experimental consistency, the second agent is kept constant across all tested configurations. The benchmarked SSL methods include: (1) pseudo-labeling (PL). In this method, artificial labels are assigned to unlabeled data based on the model predictions. Only samples with high confidence are selected for training. (2) consistency regularization (CR), which enforces stable outputs when input data is slightly modified or augmented. (3) entropy minimization (EM), which aims to make the model predictions on unlabeled inputs more decisive by minimizing prediction uncertainty. (4) graph-based SSL (GSSL). This method builds a graph connecting labeled and unlabeled examples, allowing label information to be shared among related samples.

Figure 12 shows that the proposed SSL model consistently outperforms all baseline methods across all label ratios in both Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets. Accuracy improves for all models as the amount of labeled data increases. However, the proposed method improves at a significantly faster rate. For example, with only 20% of labeled data, the proposed model achieves over 75% accuracy, whereas other methods struggle below 72%. At full supervision (100% labeled), the proposed model achieves over 94% accuracy across both datasets, outpacing GSSL by over 9% and pseudo-labeling by nearly 14%. These improvements result from combining TRPO-guided policy learning with the ability of SSL to leverage unlabeled data effectively. Notably, CR and EM, although competitive at mid-range label ratios, fail to capture high-level decision boundaries as effectively as the proposed approach. This analysis confirms the superior sample efficiency and learning stability of our SSL strategy, particularly in data-scarce settings.

Figure 13 illustrates the KL divergence trajectories over 300 training epochs, comparing five RL strategies on the Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets. The proposed TRPO with entropy regularization achieves the lowest KL divergence during training. This indicates greater stability and stronger convergence. In contrast, standard TRPO, proximal policy optimization (PPO), and soft actor critic (SAC) converge more slowly and exhibit higher variance, indicating less consistent policy learning. The conventional RL agent performs the worst among all baselines. It shows high divergence and fails to converge, indicating poor handling of the exploration–exploitation balance. Across both datasets, the proposed TRPO consistently maintains divergence below 1.0, whereas the closest competitor (original TRPO) stabilizes around 1.2 on Druglib.com and 1.0 on Drugs.com. PPO and SAC show moderate performance, while RL remains erratic. The KL divergence gap shows that entropy-based regularization improves policy robustness, reduces variance, and speeds up convergence. These findings confirm that the proposed method yields more reliable and efficient training, especially in semi-supervised tasks with sparse labels, as reflected in lower KL divergence.

Discussion

This article introduced a two-module framework comprising a blockchain-based DSCM system and an SA recommendation model powered by multi-agent TRPO. The superior performance of the system on the Drugs.com and Druglib.com datasets results from the combined interaction of its components rather than a single feature. While blockchain ensures a secure, tamper-resistant environment for pharmaceutical transactions, the dual TRPO agents provide the core analytical intelligence. The first agent employs SSL with pseudo-labels to reduce data scarcity. The second agent uses SHAP-based ranking and customized rewards to improve interpretability and address class imbalance. Additionally, entropy regularization stabilizes policy updates to prevent overfitting. This combination of blockchain security and adaptive learning reduces latency and bias. As a result, the framework maintains robust performance across different data distributions. Consequently, the model achieves high accuracy and F-measure while remaining scalable for real-time applications. It also bridges the gap between data integrity and interpretable decision support in complex supply chains.

The results affirm both research questions and validate the hypotheses proposed in the introduction. For RQ1, the blockchain-based DSCM system proved reliable in detecting counterfeit drugs. It achieved this by enabling full transaction traceability and recording each stage of the supply chain. The latency remained below 3.1 s and throughput exceeded 170 transactions per second, confirming that the blockchain design supports real-time scalability and transparency. For RQ2, the dual TRPO agents significantly improved model accuracy and interpretability. The first agent, operating under an SSL paradigm, effectively increased the labeled dataset and enhanced generalization. The second agent used SHAP-based analysis to balance class representation and make decisions more transparent. This enhanced both the explainability and fairness of the recommendations. Regarding H1, the integrated blockchain–TRPO framework clearly outperformed traditional systems in both supply chain monitoring and recommendation reliability. The experimental results on Drugs.com and Druglib.com verified that combining blockchain security with adaptive RL offers a powerful foundation for intelligent pharmaceutical management and decision support.

The effectiveness of this foundation is further rooted in the strategic design of the cooperative dual-agent architecture. Many multi-agent RL frameworks rely on competitive or adversarial dynamics. In this study, a cooperative coordination scheme was adopted to ensure policy stability and convergence for clinical decision support. This modular design enables clear task decomposition: the first agent focuses on mitigating data scarcity via SSL, while the second agent refines recommendations through interpretability-driven rewards. This architectural choice is consistent with advanced RL systems for integrated order acceptance and scheduling80. In such systems, complex logistical tasks are divided among specialized agents to improve overall performance. By modeling the supply chain as a cooperative environment, TRPO-based agents can better balance accuracy and explainability. This approach also reduces the instability commonly observed in competitive setups. This robust architecture allows the framework to scale to more detailed supply chain structures, such as warehouse-level scheduling and real-time order prioritization.

The theoretical and practical aspects of the work are significant. Theoretically, the framework supports interdisciplinary research by combining blockchain, sentiment analysis, and RL. This integration creates a unified structure that addresses both trust concerns and data imbalance challenges. It shows how TRPO-based policy optimization can work together with SHAP-based interpretability to support transparent decision-making. In practice, the model can be implemented in real time to monitor the movement of drugs across the production, distribution, and retail stages. Each transaction can be instantly validated through the blockchain, ensuring authenticity while simultaneously generating sentiment-based drug recommendations for clinicians. The system can operate as a plug-in to hospital information systems or pharmacy management software, offering real-time fraud detection, automated prescription validation, and integration with patient-centric feedback. Hence, the framework provides an actionable bridge between data security, model interpretability, and clinical applicability.

Beyond these theoretical and practical contributions, the interpretability mechanisms embedded in our framework also support the broader objective of trustworthy AI within the Industry 5.0 paradigm81. Industry 5.0 prioritizes human-centric and explainable systems81. In this context, our dual-agent framework moves beyond black-box predictions by explaining why a specific recommendation was generated. SHAP provides post-hoc explanations for the model outputs. In addition, the modular design of the dual-agent architecture supports post-hoc interpretability by dividing the recommendation task into specialized, observable sub-processes. This transparency allows clinical stakeholders and supply chain managers to validate the reasoning of AI, such as the relative importance of adverse event signals and patient sentiment. As a result, the system becomes a more reliable and trustworthy decision-support tool. This approach supports the development of resilient and human-centric pharmaceutical supply chains. It also balances AI-driven efficiency with ethical accountability and clinical safety.

Beyond its immediate recommendation and tracking capabilities, the proposed framework serves as the informational backbone for a digital twin of the pharmaceutical supply chain. The system provides an immutable and real-time ledger of transactions and patient sentiment. This information creates a high-fidelity virtual representation of the physical supply chain. Based on the principles of digital twin-based remote semi-physical commissioning82, the proposed dual-agent model could support proactive simulation and predictive stress-testing. In this paradigm, blockchain data acts as the source of truth for the digital twin. This allows supply chain managers to remotely validate logistics strategies and simulate potential disruptions, such as drug shortages or sudden shifts in sentiment, before they occur in the physical environment. The integration of blockchain-based integrity and agent-based intelligence enables semi-physical testing environments. In these environments, AI-driven interventions can be validated safely in a virtualized setting to support operational resilience and clinical safety.

Building on this predictive capacity, the architectural synergy between blockchain-managed data and agent-based reasoning also provides a foundation for systemic resilience. The framework models the supply chain as a markov decision process (MDP). This design helps maintain operational continuity during disruptions such as demand spikes and logistical failures. Based on advancements in deep RL and graph convolutional networks83, the proposed system can be extended to use its real-time data layer as a feedback mechanism for resilient re-routing and inventory rebalancing. This design allows the framework to provide more than static security. It also acts as a dynamic mechanism for maintaining medication flows during stressful conditions, while supporting both transparency and operational resilience.

The framework can be effectively applied to other domains where trust, interpretability, and dynamic data adaptation are crucial. In the food supply chain, it can verify product authenticity, trace contamination sources, and recommend suppliers based on customer or regulatory agency sentiment data. In the medical device industry, blockchain can ensure traceability and secure data sharing. TRPO-based RL can then analyze patient feedback and operational metrics to identify optimal device usage patterns. Similarly, in clinical trial management, the system could monitor the flow of trial drugs, while the SA component captures patient-reported outcomes to support adaptive trial adjustments. In healthcare logistics, real-time route optimization and feedback-based service improvements can be managed under the same architecture. Beyond healthcare, the same framework can support energy trading and agricultural product tracking. Its ability to combine secure record-keeping with feedback-driven optimization demonstrates strong adaptability to other transparent and data-driven domains.

Conclusions

This paper introduced an integrated framework that combines blockchain-based supply chain monitoring with a TRPO-enhanced SA RS. The proposed architecture addresses two major challenges in the pharmaceutical sector: ensuring secure, transparent drug tracking and improving the reliability of medication recommendations derived from patient feedback. The proposed model was evaluated on two datasets, Drugs.com and Druglib.com, to verify both its technical performance and practical applicability. On the supply chain side, the customized blockchain module demonstrated real-time traceability with latency below 3.1 s in both datasets, and throughput consistently above 170 transactions per second, reaching nearly 190 tps for Druglib.com. These outcomes confirm the ability of the model to maintain efficiency and scalability in high-volume environments. On the recommendation side, the dual-agent TRPO model, featuring SSL, SHAP-based interpretability, and reward-adjusted class balancing, showed strong sentiment classification performance. Accuracy remained above 93% on both datasets, peaking at over 95% for Druglib.com, while the F-measure exceeded 94% and reached around 96% on the best-performing configuration. These results demonstrate the robustness and generalizability of the model across datasets with differing review distributions. Practically, this framework delivers a secure, end-to-end solution for tracking genuine pharmaceuticals while also supporting clinicians with data-driven, sentiment-aware drug recommendations. Combining blockchain transparency with adaptive RL provides a scalable foundation for building reliable and intelligent pharmaceutical systems.

In future work, one important direction is to expand the SA module to support multilingual user reviews. Currently, the model processes only English-language feedback, which limits its applicability in non-English-speaking regions. Adding multilingual embeddings or language-agnostic models would make the system usable across different languages and patient groups worldwide. Another avenue is to incorporate real-time interaction with clinicians to enable dynamic updates to both the blockchain and recommendation components. For instance, clinician feedback on system-generated suggestions can be used to fine-tune TRPO agents or to flag supply chain inconsistencies. Additionally, integrating medical ontologies or adverse event databases could enhance both the interpretability and medical relevance of recommendations. These improvements can help the model adapt to new data and build user confidence in real healthcare settings.
