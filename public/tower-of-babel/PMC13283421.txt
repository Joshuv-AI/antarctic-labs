A case of bilateral calcified subdural hematomas with midline shift in a patient with a chronic Lumboperitoneal shunt
Authors: Aasim Ali, Anousha Tanveer, Danish Yousuf, Labeeba Abdul Ghafoor, Mukesh Kumar Sharma
Published: 2026

Description

A 35-year-old Asian male presented with a 20-day history of persistent, mild, non-localizing headache. His medical history was significant for a lumboperitoneal shunt placed during childhood for idiopathic intracranial hypertension, which had remained in situ for many years. Neurological examination was unremarkable, with no papilledema, focal neurological deficits, or clinical signs of raised intracranial pressure. Because of the new onset headache in a patient with a long-standing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shunt, neuroimaging was performed to evaluate for shunt-related complications.

Non-contrast CT of the brain (Fig. 1) demonstrated bilateral crescent-shaped subdural collections along the cerebral convexities with a thick, hyperdense calcified outer membrane producing the classic ‘armoured brain’ or ‘eggshell’ appearance consistent with calcified chronic subdural hematoma (CCSDH). The collections were predominantly iso- to hypodense, compatible with chronic organized hematoma, although a small hyperdense component on the left suggested a recent acute-on-chronic hemorrhage, which may have precipitated the recent onset of headache marked by red arrow and red crosses showing calcified subdural hematomas. MRI (Fig. 2) confirmed homogeneous signal intensity within the collections, reduced ventricular size likely related to chronic mass effect, and a mild rightward midline shift marked by yellow arrow.

CCSDH is a rare variant of chronic subdural hematoma, accounting for approximately 0.3–2.7% of cases [1]. It has been reported in association with long-standing CSF shunts due to chronic alterations in intracranial pressure dynamics and low-pressure states predisposing to subdural collections [2]. Despite the patient’s relatively mild symptoms, the presence of midline shift indicated a clinically significant space-occupying lesion. Because the calcified membranes form a rigid shell that limits brain re-expansion, the patient underwent craniotomy with evacuation of the hematoma and removal of the lumboperitoneal shunt.

The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of headache and radiological improvement, addressing the clinical outcome.

This case highlights that heavily calcified chronic subdural hematomas may remain clinically silent for years but can become symptomatic following acute-on-chronic bleeding or exhaustion of intracranial compensatory mechanisms. Careful radiological evaluation for mass effect is essential, and craniotomy may be required when the rigid calcified membranes prevent adequate decompression with burr-hole drainage.
