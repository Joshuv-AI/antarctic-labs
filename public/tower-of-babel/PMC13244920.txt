Factors Predicting Depression Among Thai Adolescents During the Transition From the COVID–19 Pandemic to the Endemic Phase
Authors: Vacharanan Pong‐isawaranan, Tusana Thaweekoon, Wilai Napa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdolescent depression is a major public health issue in Thailand, with post‐pandemic rates at 27%–37%. While perceived stress, coping, and social support are linked to depressive symptoms, their combined predictive contributions during the COVID‐19 pandemic‐to‐endemic transition are underexplored among Southeast Asian adolescents.PurposeThis study examined the effects of perceived stress, approach coping, avoidance coping, and social support on depressive symptoms among Thai adolescents during this transition. Methodology: A predictive cross‐sectional design was used with 605 Thai high school students via multistage sampling from two central Thailand schools (September 2023–March 2024). Measures included the CES‐D, Perceived Stress Scale, Self‐Report Coping Measure, and revised Thai Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support. Hierarchical multiple regression assessed the variance explained by each predictor.ResultsDepressive symptoms were found in 27.8% of participants. Perceived stress was the strongest predictor (β = 0.664, p < 0.001), accounting for 54.3% of the variance. Avoidance coping was a significant secondary predictor (β = 0.133, p < 0.001; ΔR2 = 0.012). Approach coping and social support did not reach independent significance in the final model after perceived stress and avoidance coping were accounted for.ConclusionsPerceived stress and avoidance coping are key modifiable predictors of adolescent depression during Thailand's pandemic‐to‐endemic transition. School‐based psychiatric nurses should integrate stress screening, adaptive coping skills training, and family‐inclusive psychoeducation to address the mental health burden among Thai youth.

Introduction

Adolescent depression constitutes a significant global public health concern, with a recent systematic review reporting a worldwide prevalence of elevated depressive symptoms of 34% among adolescents aged 10–19 years and identifying Asia as one of the highest‐burden regions (Shorey et al. 2022). These trends are mirrored in Thailand, where pre‐pandemic surveys reported prevalence rates of 17.5%–27.7% among adolescents (Choychoda et al. 2023; Panyawong et al. 2020); rates rose to 20%–43.4% across Thai student populations during the acute pandemic phase (Chutipattana et al. 2022; Lamaisaard et al. 2022; Puttakiaw et al. 2022) and have remained elevated at 31.4%–37.5% in the post‐pandemic period (Chautrakarn et al. 2024; Liangruenrom et al. 2025; Pratummanee et al. 2025). National surveillance further indicates that adolescents aged 10–19 years now exhibit the highest suicide‐attempt rate in Thailand (224.34 per 100,000), with students comprising 44.6% of attempters (Pratummanee et al. 2025), suggesting that Thai youth mental health has not returned to pre‐pandemic baselines.

The clinical urgency of these epidemiological trends derives from the broad and enduring developmental consequences of adolescent depression. In the short term, depressive symptomatology disrupts academic engagement and achievement, impairs social and family functioning, and constitutes one of the strongest proximal risk factors for adolescent suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, and suicide death (Pratummanee et al. 2025; Shorey et al. 2022). Longitudinally, adolescent‐onset depression predicts the persistence and recurrence of depressive symptoms into young adulthood (Keyes et al. 2024) and is associated with elevated rates of comorbid anxiety and substance use disorders (Copeland et al. 2021; Psychogiou et al. 2024), lower educational attainment (Wickersham et al. 2021), and adverse psychosocial outcomes in adulthood, including unemployment, reduced income, and labor‐market marginalization (Alaie et al. 2022; Copeland et al. 2021). Adolescent depression is therefore most appropriately conceptualized as a developmental condition with cascading life course implications, underscoring the need to identify modifiable predictors that can inform early prevention—particularly within evolving public‐health contexts such as the COVID‐19 pandemic‐to‐endemic transition.

Among the wide range of biological, psychological, and contextual determinants of adolescent depression, three psychosocial constructs—perceived stress, coping strategies, and perceived social support—form a theoretically coherent and empirically robust set of modifiable predictors. The transactional stress model (Lazarus and Folkman 1984) conceptualizes psychological distress as the outcome of a dynamic appraisal process in which environmental demands are evaluated against perceived coping resources; depressive vulnerability rises when demands are appraised as exceeding those resources. Within this framework, coping strategies (Roth and Cohen 1986) and perceived social support (Cohen and Wills 1985) function as proximal regulators of the stress–depression pathway, either by buffering stress appraisal (the stress‐buffering hypothesis; Cohen and Wills 1985) or by shaping the emotional and behavioral response to it. These three constructs are particularly relevant to adolescence, a developmental period marked by heightened stress reactivity, evolving but still‐maturing coping repertoires, and reorganization of social‐support networks toward peers (Zimmer‐Gembeck and Skinner 2016). They are also amenable to school‐based screening and intervention, distinguishing them from non‐modifiable risk factors such as genetic loading or early adversity. Despite this conceptual coherence, prior research has typically examined these constructs in isolation rather than as an integrated set, limiting understanding of their relative and combined contributions to adolescent depression.

Cumulative evidence consistently identifies perceived stress as a robust proximal predictor of adolescent depression across pre‐pandemic (Felton et al. 2017), pandemic (Gotlib et al. 2020; Liu and Wang 2021; van Loon et al. 2022; Verlenden et al. 2022), and post‐pandemic contexts (Costa et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2025), with meta‐analytic data confirming that depressive symptoms in youth approximately doubled during the acute pandemic phase relative to pre‐pandemic baselines (Racine et al. 2021). The literature on coping strategies converges on a consistent pattern: avoidance‐oriented strategies such as denial, disengagement, and self‐blame predict elevated depressive symptoms, whereas approach‐oriented strategies such as problem‐solving and cognitive reappraisal exert protective effects. This pattern has been observed in pre‐pandemic (Richardson et al. 2021), pandemic (Hussong et al. 2021; Theberath et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2020), and post‐pandemic samples (Karadapanddy et al. 2025; Lampraki et al. 2025; Miola et al. 2023). Evidence on perceived social support follows the same trajectory: lower perceived support consistently predicts greater depressive symptomatology, both before COVID‐19 (Rueger et al. 2016) and during and after the pandemic (Cao et al. 2022; Grey et al. 2020; Hou et al. 2021; Layman et al. 2023; Qi et al. 2020), with family support generally exerting the strongest protective effect.

Despite this evidence, the pandemic‐to‐endemic transition represents a psychosocial context that is theoretically and empirically distinct from both the pre‐pandemic baseline and the acute pandemic phase. In Thailand, COVID‐19 was downgraded from a dangerous communicable disease to a surveillance disease on October 1, 2022, accompanied by the dissolution of the Center for COVID‐19 Situation Administration, the full resumption of in‐person schooling, and a national policy shift toward “living with COVID‐19” (Jitanan et al. 2023; Tangcharoensathien et al. 2023). The data collection window of the present study (September 2023–March 2024), therefore, corresponds to approximately 11 to 18 months after this official policy transition, situating the investigation within the early‐to‐mid endemic phase rather than the acute pandemic period or the immediate post‐transition window. Whereas the acute pandemic phase was characterized by acute, externally imposed stressors—school closures, lockdowns, and infection fears—the endemic transition is characterized by normalized but persistent disruption, including the academic and social reintegration burden following extended remote learning, residual fears of reinfection in the absence of public mitigation, and the withdrawal of pandemic‐era educational, social, and financial supports (Cao et al. 2022; Finch et al. 2025; Matsumoto et al. 2023). Critically, adolescent depressive symptoms have not returned to pre‐pandemic baselines in this phase (Finch et al. 2025; Matsumoto et al. 2023; Pratummanee et al. 2025), suggesting that the relative contributions of stress appraisal, coping repertoires, and social support to depression may differ from those observed during the acute pandemic phase. Empirical examination of these predictors specifically within the endemic transition is therefore needed, rather than extrapolation from earlier evidence.

Geographically and culturally, the existing evidence based on perceived stress, coping, social support, and adolescent depression in Asia has been disproportionately drawn from East Asian samples (e.g., Chinese, South Korean, and Japanese), with relatively little attention to Southeast Asian and especially Thai adolescents. This distinction is consequential because Southeast Asian, and specifically Thai, sociocultural contexts shape each of the three predictors in ways that limit the direct generalizability of evidence from other Asian regions. Thai society is characterized by strong collectivist values, hierarchical family structures, and Buddhist‐influenced norms emphasizing emotional restraint, harmony, and acceptance of adversity—norms that intensify stress appraisal by valuing academic achievement and family obligation (Reangsing et al. 2024; Syafitri et al. 2026). Thai adolescents also exhibit a distinctive coping pattern in which family‐oriented and emotion‐suppressive strategies are normatively reinforced, potentially elevating reliance on avoidance‐oriented coping such as distancing and internalizing (Reangsing et al. 2024). Patterns of perceived social support similarly diverge from those documented in Western and East Asian populations: family is the dominant culturally sanctioned source of support, peer support is secondary, and professional help‐seeking remains markedly low across ASEAN countries owing to mental health stigma, low mental health literacy, and structural service gaps (Dessauvagie et al. 2022; Syafitri et al. 2026). These cultural specificities mean that the magnitude and configuration of the relationships among perceived stress, coping, social support, and depression in Thai adolescents cannot be assumed to mirror those reported in non‐Southeast Asian samples and require direct empirical examination.

Considering these considerations, the present study examined the independent and combined effects of perceived stress, approach coping, avoidance coping, and perceived social support on depressive symptoms among Thai high school students during the COVID‐19 pandemic‐to‐endemic transition. By integrating these three theoretically related constructs within a single hierarchical regression framework, the study extends prior research that has examined them in isolation and provides culturally and contextually situated evidence to inform school‐based psychiatric nursing assessment, prevention, and early intervention for adolescent depression in Thailand and the broader Southeast Asian region.

Methods

Study Design

This predictive cross‐sectional study examined the influence of perceived stress, coping strategies, and social support on depressive symptoms among high school students during the COVID‐19 pandemic‐to‐endemic transition period.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was determined using G*Power (Faul et al. 2009), version 3.1. A literature review identified three independent variables: perceived stress, coping, and social support. Because few studies explored these factors in the Thai context during the transition from the COVID‐19 pandemic to the endemic phase, a small effect size was used for the sample size calculation. Using the software, we set statistical power at 0.80 and a significance level of 0.05, along with a small effect size (0.02) and four predictors for a multiple regression analysis. This resulted in a calculated sample size of 597 participants. Ultimately, the study included 605 participants.

Sampling Procedure

The sample comprised male and female students enrolled in Grades 10–12 (Mathayom 4–6) during the 2023 academic year at secondary schools under the Secondary Educational Service Area Office Region 9 (SESAO 9). SESAO 9 is located in a province in central Thailand and oversees 10 districts, encompassing 32 schools organized into four educational clusters. Each educational cluster is responsible for two to three districts, and each district contains between two and five schools under its jurisdiction. The four educational clusters under SESAO 9 are administrative groupings formed primarily based on geographic proximity to facilitate inter‐school coordination, resource sharing, and teacher professional development; they are not constituted based on student demographics, academic performance, or socioeconomic indicators. Each cluster, therefore, contains a heterogeneous mix of school sizes and community types, and stratification by size and urbanicity at the third sampling stage was used to capture this internal variation within the selected cluster. The multistage sampling procedure consisted of the following six steps: 1.The four educational clusters under SESAO 9 were identified, and one cluster was selected through purposive sampling based on accessibility and representativeness.2.The districts within the selected educational cluster were identified, and one district was chosen through purposive sampling.3.The schools located within the selected district were stratified into two groups: (a) extra‐large and large schools situated in urban communities, and (b) small and medium‐sized schools situated in semi‐urban/semi‐rural communities. One school was then selected from each stratum, yielding a total of two schools.4.The classrooms in each grade level were enumerated. The large school comprised 36 upper‐secondary classrooms (12 per grade level), whereas the medium‐sized school comprised 9 upper‐secondary classrooms (3 per grade level).5.Classrooms in Grades 10–12 were randomly selected. In the large school, 5 classrooms were randomly selected from each grade level, for a total of 15 classrooms. In the medium‐sized school, 2 classrooms were randomly selected from each grade level, for a total of 6 classrooms.6.Students were sampled from each school using proportional allocation, resulting in 476 participants from the large school and 129 participants from the medium‐sized school, for a total analytic sample of 605 participants.

Data Collection

Data collection took place between September 2023 and March 2024 at two schools in central Thailand. This window commenced approximately 11 months after Thailand's official transition to endemic status on 1 October 2022—the date on which the Ministry of Public Health reclassified COVID‐19 from a ‘dangerous communicable disease’ to a ‘communicable disease under surveillance’—and concluded approximately 18 months after that transition (Jitanan et al. 2023). At the time of data collection, the state of emergency had been lifted, the Centre for COVID‐19 Situation Administration had been dissolved, in‐person schooling had fully resumed, and the country was operating under a ‘living with COVID‐19’ surveillance framework (Tangcharoensathien et al. 2023). The study, therefore, reflects adolescent psychosocial functioning during the early‐to‐mid endemic phase. After receiving ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee at Mahidol University (Reference Number: COA.MURA 2023/382), permission was secured from the school principals. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and parental consent was required for those under 18. Data collection took place during guidance periods, during which participants independently completed questionnaires that took approximately 25 to 30 min.

Ethical Considerations

The study prioritized participants' welfare throughout the process. Participation was entirely voluntary, with no coercion, and individuals retained the right to withdraw at any time without facing academic consequences. We ensured data confidentiality by anonymizing all information and reporting findings solely in aggregate form. The research team remained vigilant for participants who might need mental health support and had established referral protocols in collaboration with school guidance counselors.

Instruments

General Information Questionnaire

The authors comprise questions regarding gender, age, educational level, grade point average, parental marital status, number of full siblings, household composition, relationships with parents and peers, number of close friends, COVID‐19 infection in family members, sources of distress during the past month, and consultation sources during periods of distress.

Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D)

The scale was effectively utilized to evaluate depressive symptoms in adolescents. Originally developed by Radloff (1977), this instrument is designed to assess depression symptoms over the preceding week, encompassing six core symptoms: depressed mood, feelings of guilt and worthlessness, feelings of helplessness and hopelessness, psychomotor retardation, loss of appetite, and sleep disturbances. The scale was translated into Thai and adapted by Trangkasombat et al. (1997). It consists of 20 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 60; higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. A cutoff score of 22 or higher signifies the presence of depressive symptoms. This scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.87 in our sample.

Perceived Stress Scale

Originally developed by Cohen et al. (1983), this self‐report instrument was constructed to evaluate perceived stress levels based on Lazarus's theoretical framework. It was later translated into Thai by Wongpakaran and Wongpakaran (2010) to ensure cultural relevance within the Thai context. The scale consists of 10 items, with responses recorded on a 5‐point Likert scale. Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. These scores are categorized into three levels: 0–13 points show low stress, 14–26 points indicate moderate stress, and 27–40 points represent high stress. Application of the instrument to the study sample yielded a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.81, reflecting good internal consistency.

Self‐Report Coping Measure (SCRM)

Developed by Causey and Dubow (1992), this instrument is based on the theoretical framework of Roth and Cohen (1986) and was translated into Thai by Bootjun and Thaweekoon (2019). The SRCM comprises 34 items assessed on a 5‐point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 4 = regularly) across five subscales: self‐reliance/problem‐solving (8 items), distancing (7 items), seeking social support (8 items), internalizing (7 items), and externalizing (4 items). In this study, items were combined into two composite scales: approach coping (self‐reliance/problem‐solving and seeking social support) and avoidance coping (distancing, internalizing, and externalizing). Higher subscale scores reflect more frequent use of the respective coping strategy. The instrument showed acceptable reliability for approach coping (Cronbach's α = 0.82) and avoidance coping (Cronbach's α = 0.77).

The revised Thai Multi‐Dimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (r‐T‐MSPSS) , initially developed by Zimet et al. (1988), was translated into Thai and adapted by Wongpakaran and Wongpakaran (2012). This scale evaluates perceived social support from three distinct sources: family, friends, and significant others. It consists of 12 items, with four items allocated to each source of support. Responses are measured on a 7‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 12 to 84 points, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. The scale demonstrates strong overall internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.84, and subscale coefficients ranging from 0.82 to 0.88.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software, version 29. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize participant demographics and study variables. Prior to conducting the hierarchical multiple regression analysis, the underlying statistical assumptions were systematically evaluated. Linearity between the predictors and the outcome variable was examined through a scatterplot, which revealed linear relationships among perceived stress, coping strategies, social support, and depressive symptoms. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. Although individual variables, with the exception of avoidance coping (Z = 0.036, p = 0.060), did not meet the assumption of univariate normality (perceived stress: Z = 0.056, p < 0.001; social support: Z = 0.071, p < 0.001; approach coping: Z = 0.057, p < 0.001; depression: Z = 0.079, p < 0.001), the standardized residuals were normally distributed (Z = 0.031, p = 0.200), satisfying the regression‐specific normality requirement (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019). Homoscedasticity was confirmed by visual inspection of residual scatterplots, which showed residuals were uniformly distributed above and below the zero line within a narrow range, indicating constant error variance across predicted values. Independence of residuals was evaluated using the Durbin‐Watson statistic, which yielded a value of 1.853, falling within the acceptable range of 1.5 to 2.5 and indicating no autocorrelation. Multicollinearity was assessed through Tolerance and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values. Tolerance values ranged from 0.701 to 0.757, and VIF values for perceived stress, approach coping, avoidance coping, and social support were 1.320, 1.427, 1.356, and 1.374, respectively—all well within acceptable thresholds—indicating no concerns regarding multicollinearity among the predictors.

Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was employed to explore predictive relationships, with variables entered sequentially. In Model 1, perceived stress was entered as the primary predictor, in line with stress‐diathesis models that suggest stress acts as a proximal risk factor for depression (Gniewosz 2024). Model 2 added avoidance coping, identified as a maladaptive strategy that is theoretically associated with the amplification of stress‐related depressive risk. Model 3 introduced an approach to evaluating the protective effects of coping strategies while controlling risk factors. Finally, Model 4 included social support to assess its added contribution beyond the individual's coping resources.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 605 adolescents participated in this study. As presented in Table 1, the average age was 16.59 years (SD = 0.97, range: 15–19 years). The sample showed a slight female majority, comprising 63.8% compared to 36.2% males. Regarding family structure, most participants reported their parents were married and living together (64.3%). Most participants reported residing with both parents (62.3%). Concerning social relationships, most participants characterized their relationship with parents (51.1%) and friends (56.6%) as very good. The majority indicated having five or more close friends (51.2%), and 77.5% reported consulting friends first when experiencing worry or distress, followed by parents (58.0%) and siblings (29.4%). Only 1.8% reported not consulting anyone when distressed, and a mere 1.7% sought support from healthcare professionals.

Descriptive Statistics and Bivariate Correlations

Table 3 presents the means, standard deviations, and bivariate correlation matrix for all study variables. The correlation matrix revealed several noteworthy patterns. A strong positive correlation was observed between depression and perceived stress (r = 0.74, p < 0.001), indicating substantial comorbidity between these indicators of psychological distress. Depression showed significant negative correlations with approach coping strategies (r = −0.11, p < 0.01) and social support (r = −0.31, p < 0.001), while it was positively associated with avoidance strategies (r = 0.38, p < 0.001). Particularly notable was the strong positive correlation between depression and internalizing coping (r = 0.44, p < 0.001).

Hierarchical Regression Analysis

Preliminary diagnostic testing confirmed that all assumptions for hierarchical multiple regression were satisfied, including linearity, normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, independence of errors, and the absence of multicollinearity (see Statistical Analysis section). A four‐step hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed to explore the relative contributions of perceived stress, coping strategies, and social support to adolescent depression. This analytical method enables a systematic examination of the incremental variance explained by theoretically relevant variable sets, while controlling for previously entered predictors (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019).

The results of the hierarchical regression analysis are presented in Table 4. In Model 1, perceived stress was evaluated as the primary predictor. Perceived stress alone accounted for a significant amount of variance in depression scores (R2 = 0.543, F(1, 603) = 717.31, p < 0.001), with a standardized coefficient of β = 0.737 (p < 0.001). This finding underscores the strong relationship between stress perception and depressive symptoms.

In Model 2, the inclusion of avoidance coping strategies notably improved the model fit (ΔR2 = 0.012, p < 0.001). The overall model explained 55.5% of the variance (F(2, 602) = 375.96, p < 0.001). While perceived stress remained the strongest predictor (β = 0.693, p < 0.001), avoidance strategies also contributed significantly (β = 0.118, p < 0.001), indicating that avoidance coping was independently associated with depressive symptoms after controlling for perceived stress, adding unique incremental variance beyond that explained by stress.

Model 3 introduced approach coping strategies, leading to a modest yet statistically significant enhancement (ΔR2 = 0.003, p < 0.05). This model accounted for 55.8% of the variance (F(3, 601) = 253.19, p < 0.001). Approach strategies demonstrated a slight protective effect (β = −0.058, p = 0.047), while perceived stress (β = 0.677, p < 0.001) and avoidance strategies (β = 0.141, p < 0.001) continued to make significant contributions.

In Model 4, social support was added as the final predictor. Although the overall model remained significant (F(4, 600) = 191.31, p < 0.001), the addition of social support did not enhance the model's explanatory power (ΔR2 = 0.002, p = 0.080). In the final model, perceived stress was the most influential predictor (β = 0.664, p < 0.001), followed by avoidance strategies (β = 0.133, p < 0.001). Neither approach strategy (β = −0.034, p = 0.289) nor social support (β = −0.056, p = 0.080) reached statistical significance.

Discussion

This study examined the independent and combined effects of perceived stress, coping strategies, and perceived social support on depressive symptoms among Thai high school students during the COVID‐19 pandemic‐to‐endemic transition. Three principal findings emerged. First, perceived stress was the strongest predictor of depressive symptoms, accounting for 54.3% of the variance and remaining significant across all hierarchical regression models. Second, avoidance coping was independently associated with depressive symptoms, accounting for additional incremental variance after perceived stress was entered. Third, neither approach coping nor perceived social support reached statistical significance in the fully adjusted model. Because mediation was not formally tested, the attenuation of their direct effects upon entry of perceived stress and avoidance coping is consistent with but does not demonstrate indirect pathways; this interpretation is offered as a theoretically plausible hypothesis to be examined in future mediation analyses. Each finding is interpreted below in relation to evidence from other regions to identify cross‐cultural convergence and clarify what may be distinctive about Thai upper‐secondary adolescents during the endemic transition.

Perceived Stress: Cross‐Regional Comparison

The dominant role of perceived stress in predicting depressive symptoms (β = .664, p < 0.001) is consistent with evidence from Western, East Asian, and other Southeast Asian samples, indicating that the centrality of stress appraisal to adolescent depression is a robust, cross‐culturally generalizable phenomenon. Comparable patterns have been reported among Swiss adolescents (Foster et al. 2023), Chinese adolescents during the acute pandemic (Liu and Wang 2021), Chinese university students during normalization (Sun et al. 2025), and US adolescents exposed to pandemic‐related adversity (Verlenden et al. 2022). However, the magnitude of the stress–depression association in the present sample appears at the upper end of reported estimates. This may reflect a stressor profile relatively specific to Thai upper‐secondary students during the endemic transition: simultaneous exposure to academic reintegration, intensified preparation for university entrance examinations, and accumulated family economic strain from the pandemic. In contrast to Western samples in which pandemic stressors have eased substantially, Thai adolescents continue to navigate compounded stressor exposure, which may amplify the appraisal of demands as exceeding coping resources, consistent with Lazarus and Folkman's (1984) transactional model.

Avoidance Coping: Cross‐Regional Comparison

Avoidance coping emerged as a significant secondary predictor (β = 0.133, p < 0.001), in line with Roth and Cohen's (1986) conceptualization of avoidance‐oriented strategies as impeding emotional processing and sustaining distress. The same pattern has been observed in Chinese adolescents during the acute pandemic (Zhang et al. 2020), Italian samples during a later pandemic wave (Miola et al. 2023), and Malaysian post‐COVID‐19 patients (Karadapanddy et al. 2025), supporting the cross‐cultural robustness of avoidance coping as a maladaptive pathway to depression. A potentially distinctive feature of the Thai sample, however, is the relative weight of avoidance over approach coping in the final model. This pattern is congruent with Thai cultural norms emphasizing emotional restraint, harmony preservation, and self‐reliance, which may favor distancing and internalizing strategies over open expression or external help‐seeking. The convergence with findings from other Southeast Asian samples (Karadapanddy et al. 2025) suggests that avoidance coping may carry weight as a depression risk factor in collectivist cultural contexts in which emotional disclosure is socially constrained.

Approach Coping: Cross‐Regional Comparison

The non‐significance of approach coping in the fully adjusted model (β = −.034, p = 0.289), despite a modest bivariate association (r = −0.11, p < 0.01) and marginal significance in Model 3, indicates that its protective role attenuates substantially once perceived stress and avoidance coping are controlled. This pattern parallels findings from Western adolescent samples in which the protective effect of problem‐focused coping has been shown to depend on the severity of co‐occurring stressors (Hussong et al. 2021) and from international evidence indicating that younger adolescents have less developed capacity for cognitive reappraisal than older counterparts (Lampraki et al. 2025). Together, these observations suggest that approach coping in this age group may operate through indirect pathways involving stress severity and developmental capacity for cognitive reappraisal, rather than as an independent direct predictor. Because moderation and mediation were not formally tested in the present analysis, this interpretation should be regarded as a theoretically plausible hypothesis to be examined in future research rather than as a demonstrated mechanism.

Social Support: Cross‐Regional Comparison and Thai‐Specific Patterns

The non‐significance of perceived social support in the fully adjusted model (β = −.056, p = 0.080) contrasts with the substantial inverse bivariate association observed (r = −0.31, p < 0.001), suggesting that the protective influence of social support observed at the bivariate level is largely accounted for by its overlap with perceived stress and coping in the multivariable model. This pattern is theoretically compatible with stress‐buffering and coping‐mediated mechanisms (Cohen and Wills 1985), but because formal mediation analyses were not conducted, we present this interpretation as a hypothesis warranting confirmation rather than as an empirical finding. This statistical pattern parallels findings from Appalachian early adolescents, in whom family and school support moderated rather than directly determined depressive outcomes (Layman et al. 2023). A more distinctive pattern, however, is the differential weight of support sources within the Thai sample: family support showed the strongest bivariate protective association (r = −0.36), substantially exceeding friend support (r = −0.22) and significant‐other support (r = −0.09). This family‐centric ordering diverges from patterns commonly reported in Western adolescent samples, in which peer support typically rivals or surpasses family support as a protective factor, and aligns more closely with East Asian collectivist findings (Cao et al. 2022; Hou et al. 2021; Qi et al. 2020). The pronounced family‐centeredness of social support in this sample is congruent with Thai cultural emphases on filial obligation and family‐based interpersonal anchoring, supporting the contextual relevance of family‐inclusive approaches in this population.

Study Limitation

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference; the observed associations may reflect bidirectional or third‐variable influences. Second, all constructs were assessed via self‐report, introducing potential shared‐method variance and response bias. Third, data collection between September 2023 and March 2024 spanned heterogeneous phases of the Thai upper‐secondary academic calendar, including end‐of‐semester examinations and the inter‐semester break. Because student stress varies substantially across these phases, temporal variation in stressor exposure may have introduced unmeasured heterogeneity into perceived stress and, consequently, depression scores; time‐stratified or repeated assessment within fixed academic windows would address this in future work. Fourth, the sample was drawn from only two schools within a single educational cluster in central Thailand, which limits generalizability; findings should not be extrapolated to other Thai regions (northern, north‐eastern, or southern), to lower‐grade or out‐of‐school adolescents, to populations with substantially different sociocultural or socioeconomic profiles, or to clinical samples. Future research employing longitudinal designs, multi‐informant assessment, and broader geographic and demographic representation would strengthen both causal inference and external validity.

Implications for Psychiatric and Mental Health Nurses

The present findings carry implications for psychiatric and mental health nurses in school‐based and clinical settings, though clinical inferences must be drawn cautiously, given the cross‐sectional design. The robust association between perceived stress and depressive symptoms supports incorporating validated stress screening (e.g., the Perceived Stress Scale) into routine adolescent assessments to identify students who may benefit from early evaluation. Avoidance coping emerges as a clinically informative variable; however, whether modifying coping patterns reduces depressive symptoms cannot be established from cross‐sectional data and must be tested through longitudinal designs and randomized controlled trials. Existing evidence on cognitive‐behavioral approaches targeting problem‐solving and cognitive reappraisal provides a rationale for prioritizing such designs in future intervention research within the Thai endemic‐transition context. The family‐centered pattern of perceived social support, together with the low rate of professional help‐seeking, further supports family‐inclusive psychoeducation, proactive outreach, and stigma‐reduction efforts as culturally congruent components of school‐based mental health services. Interventions should be developed collaboratively with guidance counselors and teachers, embedded within universal school‐based mental health promotion frameworks, and culturally tailored to the Thai context.

Conclusion

This study identifies perceived stress and avoidance coping as the principal predictors of depressive symptoms among Thai high school students during the COVID‐19 pandemic‐to‐endemic transition. With 27.8% of participants meeting clinical depression thresholds and perceived stress explaining 54.3% of variance, the adolescent mental health burden has persisted post‐pandemic. Approach coping and social support did not contribute independently once perceived stress and avoidance coping were accounted for; whether this attenuation reflects indirect, stress‐buffering, or coping‐mediated pathways remains a theoretically plausible hypothesis warranting future designs capable of testing mediation and moderation. Multi‐level interventions targeting both proximal stress appraisal and adaptive coping are nevertheless warranted. These findings extend evidence to Southeast Asian endemic‐transition contexts and underscore the need for routine stress assessment, coping skills development, and family‐inclusive psychoeducation within school‐based mental health services.
