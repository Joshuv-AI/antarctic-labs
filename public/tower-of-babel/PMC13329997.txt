Sulfur-Induced Shape Compliance Modulates Fe Catalyst Agglomeration in Carbon Nanotube Growth
Authors: Evgeni S. Penev, Ksenia V. Bets, Jincheng Lei, Boris I. Yakobson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large-scale carbon nanotube (CNT) synthesis based on floating catalyst chemical vapor deposition (FC-CVD), unlike conventional CVD, utilizes a growth promoter, commonly a sulfur-containing species, whose role in the overall growth process is still poorly understood, hindering more efficient reactor design and a better quality CNT product. By developing a machine-learning interatomic potential here, we conduct atomistic molecular dynamics collision simulations for pure Fe and Fe–S clusters that allow us to directly quantify their sticking probability. Sulfur is found to reduce the intrinsic sticking for small clusters, instead strongly enhancing it for larger sizes. We demonstrate that this crossover is driven by S-induced shape compliance, a mechanism where surface passivation leads to large shape fluctuations that efficiently absorb collision energy. These insights may help rationalize the diverse and sometimes conflicting experimental outcomes reported for S-assisted FC-CVD synthesis of CNTs.

Currently, floating catalyst chemical vapor deposition (FC-CVD) − is the most promising method with a potential for mass production of carbon nanotubes (CNTs). In essence, it is a CVD variant where both the carbon feedstock and Fe catalyst precursor (usually ferrocene − ) are continuously supplied, along with a carrier gas, into a reactor allowing for uninterrupted CNT production. For all practical purposes, however, a growth promoter is used as well, although not required in principle and virtually never used in the “conventional” supported catalyst CVD synthesis of CNTs. Sulfur is a commonly used growth promoter (e.g., from S powder or thiophene), but other chalcogens (Se and Te) have been demonstrated to be effective too.

However, quantifying and understanding the effect of sulfur in the early stages of catalytic particle formation has been a vexing problem. The challenge, at least in part, can be framed in terms of how S affects the sticking coefficient of Fe clusters as a crucial factor for optimizing the FC-CVD growth of CNTs. Indeed, the size distribution of catalytic particles directly affects the main structural parameters, such as helicity (n, m) for single-walled CNTs or wall number for multi-walled CNTs, and the overall quality of the CNT product. Quantitative estimates for the sticking probabilities are important for the modeling of CNT growth kinetics and yield and, thus, can be considered as key input in designing scalable reactors. The problem is additionally compounded by the dual role S plays, reminiscent of heterogeneous catalysis, with too little sulfur being ineffective for the catalyst formation and too much leading to catalyst poisoning. Experimentally, a clear consensus is lacking, with some findings even conflicting. While some studies , have indicated that S prevents the fast agglomeration of Fe clusters, thus extending catalyst lifetime, enriching productive catalyst population and producing smaller CNTs with fewer walls, others suggested that higher levels of S can lead to larger particles and nanotubes alike. , Theoretical efforts are scarce, with some density functional theory (DFT)-based studies considering model systems (e.g., graphene on a S-covered iron surface) with focus on the S effect of CNT nucleation and growth stage rather than early Fe catalyst formation.

Here, we adopt an atomistic approach to directly compute sticking probabilities of select pure Fe and Fe–S particles based on multi-threaded molecular dynamics (MD) simulations employing a machine-learning potential. This allows us to uncover a complex/versatile cluster-size-dependent role of sulfur: from an agglomeration inhibitor for small clusters to a promoter for large clusters.

The effect of the sulfur presence at the early stages of catalyst particle formation can be probed through ab initio molecular simulations (AIMD), in a similar manner as recently employed to study the hydrogen environment effect on ferrocene decomposition in the same context of FC-CVD synthesis of CNTs. We have performed a set of short (4 ps) constant-temperature AIMD simulations using VASP to probe the initial agglomeration of individual Fe and S atoms. The protocol is based on plane-wave DFT, employing the PBE exchange–correlation functional, and includes spin polarization (for details, see section S1 of the Supporting Information). These revealed a reduced clustering rate in the presence of sulfur, pointing to a possible promoter effect on the Fe catalyst size evolution. However, the inherent computational demand of first-principles computational schemes makes AIMD simulations impractical for any systematic study of stochastic processes, such as the outcomes of cluster collisions under FC-CVD conditions. On the other hand, available reactive force fields, such as ReaxFF, , may not be readily transferable to or simply not intended and unsuitable for modeling of gas-phase interactions of Fe–S clusters.

This led us to develop a DeePMD potential for the Fe–S system, as described in section S2 of the Supporting Information, which is used for all simulations discussed below. The data set used for training includes about a hundred separate subsets (spanning the entire range of compositions, from pure Fe to Fe–S and pure S), comprising in total ≃23 000 atomic structures, which are graphically summarized in Figure . This set is obtained in a gradual iterative manner (akin to “active learning”), by expanding an initial set derived from the AIMD trajectories mentioned above. The Fe catalyst formation kinetics can be described in terms of the evolution dynamics of particle sizes or number density N i of i atom clusters, which can be written in a more general form as 1 dNidt=∑k<iβk,i−kNkNi−k−Li where the first term on the right-hand side is the “gain” due to agglomeration of smaller clusters and β k,i–k is a sticking rate coefficient, whose dependence on S atomic fraction c is of primary interest, β = β­(c). The latter is determined by the conditional probability of clusters sticking, P s (v, b) ≡ P(stick|v, b), given a relative velocity v and impact parameter b. The corresponding “loss” term in eq , denoted Li , represents all loss processes, e.g., further agglomeration with other clusters, transport, and conversion into subsequent growth products, which are not explicitly modeled here. Additionally, within the present Fe–S model, the sticking rate coefficients β­(T) derived from the computed P s (v, b) should be understood as describing the pre- or weakly carburized catalyst agglomeration limit, before carbon-mediated nucleation and growth processes are explicitly included.

Here, we determine P s from a “nanocollider” approach, based on atomistic MD simulations, as described in detail in the Supporting Information (for a schematic illustration, see the inset in Figure a and Figure S5 of the Supporting Information). In brief, for a given impact parameter b and relative velocity v, N tot MD trajectories are initiated with initial cluster velocities v/2. A set of v values to sample is chosen assuming for simplicity that, at a given temperature T, velocities obey a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution f(v) (neglecting flow as for a typical FC-CVD reactor design, it is ∼0.01–1 slpm ∼ 10–4–10–2 m/s; ref ). The sticking probability P s is then determined as the fraction of successful “computer experiments” N s in which a sticking event is detected, P s = N s /N tot. The main advantage of such an approach is that it allows for ideal multi-threading by initiating all N tot trajectories simultaneously. This nanocollider setup is realized in LAMMPS, and all simulations are carried out using N tot ∼ 200, where, for each trajectory, the initial orientation of the clusters is randomized. This direct atomistic approach is applied here to the symmetric collisions of two particles of the same size and composition, pure Fe32 and Fe17S15 clusters, with the latter representing a sulfur-rich Fe/S ≃ 1:1 (c ≃ 0.5) composition. This composition is used as a limiting case to enhance the sulfur contribution and expose the size-dependent sticking response. Lower sulfur contents are considered below in the mixed-size composition scans and in section S6 of the Supporting Information.

The comparison between clusters of different masses at T = 1500 K is carried for a set of equiprobable velocities (Figure c), i.e., such that satisfy f(v)/f(ṽ) = {0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.9, 1} for both Fe32 and Fe17S15, with ṽ = (2k B T/m)1/2, the most probable velocity, with m being the respective cluster mass. For the Fe32 cluster, for example, ṽ ≃ 120 m/s. The outcome of an individual trajectory is then classified based on a simple operative criterion for a “sticking event” using the late-time center-of-mass distance d com. Specifically, a trajectory is counted as sticking if the average ⟨d com⟩ over the final observation window after collision remains below an ensemble-specific cutoff δ, ⟨d com⟩ ≤ δ (section S3 of the Supporting Information).

The computed P s (v, b) probabilities are shown in Figure , showing a noticeable effect of the S presence, particularly evident for higher velocities and b ∼ 9–11 Å. For example, at v ≃ 640 m/s and b = 11 Å, while the pure Fe clusters always agglomerate, P s ≡ 1, the chance is only ≃70% for Fe17S15. Inspection of individual trajectories in that parameter domain for the Fe17S15 cluster provides a more detailed picture of the underlying cluster interactions that lead to reduced P s . A representative case, (v, b) = (730 m/s, 11.5 Å) where P s ∼ 1/3, is summarized in Figure d that shows the evolution of the center-of-mass distance d com for all N tot trajectories. Clearly, in most of the ∼2/3 N tot “unsuccessful” scattering events, the clusters fly by as reflected by the fast increase of d com immediately after the closest approach at t ≃ 8 ps. In a few instances, however, the clusters eventually form a transient complex on a time scale of up to ∼15 ps, via just a few-atom “bridges”, which subsequently break (image insets in Figure d). A representative trajectory is highlighted in Figure d, with respective center-of-mass planar coordinate projections of the two clusters shown in the inset. The images in Figure a and d are also representative of the S atom spatial distribution, remaining on the cluster surface (essentially, a S-decorated Fe cluster), which is consistently observed in all trajectories in the present work. A more rigorous, quantitative analysis shows that the Fe–S radial pair distribution function g Fe–S(r) displays a strong peak at r ≃ 2.3 Å (see Figure S4 of the Supporting Information), which allows us to estimate the respective S coordination number to be ≃3, representative of an undercoordinated surface atom [akin to a S adatom on the close-packed (111) surface of a fcc metal].

The computed σ­(v) values are summarized in Figure , revealing a distinct size-dependent reversal in cluster collision outcome; while S inhibits sticking for the N = 22 clusters, it substantially enhances it for N = 100, with the crossover between these two regimes occurring for the intermediate cluster sizes. Notably, however, the sticking rate coefficient β in eq , obtained as a thermal average of the respective cross section 3 β=⟨σ(v)·v⟩≡∫0∞σ(v)vf(v)dv using the reduced mass μ = m/2 in f(v), does display a net enhancement. Indeed, as shown in the inset of Figure , we find that the sticking rate coefficients of Fe–S clusters exceed those of pure Fe for all sizes, βFe–S/βFe > 1. Because the composition is fixed, c = 0.5, the reduced cluster mass due to sulfur provides a constant kinematic enhancement in collision frequency, ⟨v⟩Fe–S/⟨v⟩Fe ≃ 1.13 (inset in Figure ). However, for the smaller clusters (N = 22, 32), β increases by only 3–9%. Factoring out kinematic effects reveals that sulfur does not uniformly influence cluster aggregation. Instead, the effective cross-section ratio σeff Fe–S/σeff Fe ∼ 0.92–1.14, where σeff = β/⟨v⟩ provides a measure of the relative sticking efficiency (Figure , inset), demonstrates a pronounced size dependence: sulfur hinders sticking for the small clusters, while noticeably enhancing it for the larger clusters, with a crossover at N ∼ 30–50.

Whether this crossover originates from S-modified equilibrium interactions or from non-equilibrium aspects of the collision process is a key question. This is addressed by computing the free energy as a function of the center-of-mass distance, F(d com), at T = 1000 K, using umbrella sampling (following a similar protocol as in ref ; for details, see section S4 of the Supporting Information) of two 32 and 100 atom clusters, with both pure Fe and Fe–S (Figure ). Notably, the F(d com) profiles for the Fe–S clusters are substantially shallower compared to those for Fe and only weakly dependent on N. This is in stark contrast to the enhancement of sticking observed for the large Fe–S clusters, especially pronounced for Fe50S50 (Figure ). This distinction is important, because surface S can passivate Fe-rich contact sites without making the cluster mechanically rigid. Thus, the enhanced sticking of larger Fe–S clusters cannot be explained by a stronger thermodynamic binding alone, as indicated by Figure . Instead, it points to a non-equilibrium shape-response mechanism governing agglomeration.

We therefore turn to the internal dynamical response of the clusters, focusing on their ability to undergo and sustain shape deformations. To this end, we analyze fluctuations of the shape anisotropy parameter , k=32∑iλi2/(∑iλi)2−12 , where λ i are the eigenvalues of the gyration tensor and define an effective shape compliance χ k to quantify the amplitude of thermally driven shape fluctuations. Here, these fluctuations are extracted from non-collisional flyby trajectories, allowing us to probe the intrinsic shape dynamics of isolated clusters, avoiding coalescence or post-impact restructuring.

Accordingly, we define the shape compliance χ k as the ensemble variance of shape anisotropy k 4 χk(t)≡⟨[δk(t)]2⟩,⁣δk(t)≡k(t)−⟨k(t)⟩ where ⟨...⟩ denotes an average over flyby trajectories at time t (see section S3 of the Supporting Information). More generally, fluctuation-based measures of mechanical compliance are widely used across nanoscale systems, ranging from atomically thin materials, such as graphene, to lipid bilayers. A larger χ k indicates that, under an identical initialization protocol, the cluster can access a broader range of transient shapes, consistent with a more deformable shape. In this sense, χ k characterizes the intrinsic capacity of a cluster to absorb and redistribute collision energy.

The dynamics of the shape anisotropy parameter is shown in Figure for the same clusters as in Figure obtained from select flyby (non-collision) trajectories. For the smaller clusters (N = 32), both Fe and Fe–S compositions exhibit comparable time mean values of k and moderate fluctuations indicative of limited shape compliance. The behavior of the 100 atom clusters is drastically different: while the Fe100 clusters remain essentially “spherical”, ⟨k⟩ ≃ 0.015 ≡ constant, the Fe50S50 clusters display very large-amplitude long-lived fluctuations that do not appear to saturate over the entire trajectory timespan. Such a dramatic increase in χ k reveals a highly compliant shape of the Fe–S clusters that provides a very efficient route for absorbing and redirecting the translational kinetic energy upon collision. Indeed, post-impact analysis of selected collision trajectories for N = 100 near the sticking–flyby crossover (P s ≃ 1/2) shows that the sticking events involve almost complete loss of separating center-of-mass motion, while the Fe50S50 ensemble displays a much larger post-impact shape response than Fe100 (section S5 and Figure S6 of the Supporting Information).

The above observations (Figure ) reveal the sulfur-induced shape compliance as the main dynamical mechanism driving the size-dependent sticking enhancements (Figure ). We recall that these results are based on the symmetric collision limit of eq (identical cluster sizes and compositions; therefore, β i,j → β i,i ≡ β). As an expedient step beyond this limit, we have probed collisions of mixed size, 32 and 100 atom clusters at a representative relative thermal velocity v rms ≡ ⟨v 2⟩1/2 = (3k B T/μ)1/2 (for details, see section S6 of the Supporting Information), determined by the reduced mass μ of each collision pair. As such, these calculations determine a mixed-size sticking cross section σ i,j (v rms) and not a full thermally averaged kernel β i,j (T) = ⟨σ i,j (v)·v⟩, but they show that the sulfur effect is not restricted to identical-cluster collisions. The largest mixed-size sticking enhancement occurs when the larger cluster is sulfur-rich and the smaller one is sulfur-poor; once sulfur is also present in the smaller cluster, the enhancement is much weaker (Figure S7 of the Supporting Information). Thus, sulfur’s net effect depends not only on the cluster size but also on the sulfur content of both collision partners. In reactor terms, this provides a simple route by which sulfur can bias the evolving catalyst size distribution: it may slow early agglomeration in a small-cluster-dominated population yet enhance sticking once sulfur-containing larger clusters begin to appear.

The continuity of the CNT synthesis in FC-CVD, with simultaneous “on-the-fly” catalyst formation and addition of growth promoters, leads to complex and sometimes contradictory sulfur effects. Indeed, experimental studies of sulfur-assisted FC-CVD, as already outlined in the introduction, report a broad range of outcomes, from reduced agglomeration to enhanced catalytic particle growth. Sulfur addition has been found, for instance, to maintain smaller particles, producing high-quality single-walled CNTs, a regime that may possibly correspond to the early stages, dominated by sulfur-induced passivation or catalyst rigidity. In contrast, other studies , have reported shifts toward large particle sizes, broader CNT diameter distributions, multi-walled CNTs at a higher sulfur content, and extended growth times.

Here, within the framework of our atomistic simulations, these seemingly conflicting observations may be reconciled by a size-dependent competition between (i) sulfur’s surface-mediated chemical effect for small clusters, which reduces direct Fe–Fe contact formation (cf. the reduced σeff at N = 22; Figure , inset) and (ii) its dynamical softening of larger clusters, which redirects translational collision energy into internal shape fluctuations (Figure ), ultimately increasing sticking by enhanced shape compliance. Thus, our results suggest that the reported variations of S impact in experiments may actually reflect differences in the dominant catalyst size regimes rather than contradictory underlying mechanisms. Indeed, because catalyst nucleation and promoter delivery occur concurrently in FC-CVD, modest differences in growth history can bias the catalyst population toward different size regimes. As a result, experimental parameters, such as promoter dosing, precursor chemistry, and residence time, can shift whether sulfur acts predominantly to hinder or enhance aggregation.

Last but not least, we emphasize that, although carbon is omnipresent in FC-CVD (through C feedstock and Fe and S precursors), it was not included here in order to isolate the intrinsic influence of sulfur on Fe/Fe–S agglomeration in the pre-carburized or weakly carburized limit, before subsequent carbon-mediated nucleation and growth processes are treated. This complements recent simulations of catalyst formation from ferrocene , and singles out the sulfur-induced shape compliance as a distinct dynamical contribution at the earliest stages of FC-CVD.
