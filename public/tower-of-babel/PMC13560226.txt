Purple catheter syndrome (PUBS): a mystery in a post stroke patient with Klebsiella - should we be concerned?
Authors: Induja k, Ravi Gaur, Nitesh Manohar Gonnade, Vibhor Tak, Kuntal Kumar Sinha, Nagma Sheenam, Abins T K
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPurple urine bag syndrome (PUBS) is an unusual but recognizable manifestation characterized by a striking purple hue in the urinary collection system. This phenomenon results from bacterial enzymatic breakdown of tryptophan in the presence of alkaline urine. While often benign, PUBS can indicate an underlying infection, particularly in individuals with long-term catheterization and other risk factors, such as immobility and constipation.Case presentationWe describe the case of a woman in her 40s with a prior cerebrovascular accident who was bedbound and dependent on a long-term indwelling catheter. She developed discoloration of her urine bag without systemic symptoms. Urine analysis revealed an alkaline pH and significant pyuria, and culture confirmed infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Management involves replacing the catheter, initiating culture-directed antibiotic therapy with levofloxacin, optimizing bowel habits, and promoting hydration. These interventions led to a full resolution of the discoloration and infection.ConclusionAlthough visually alarming, PUBS often serves as a subtle clinical indicator of underlying urinary tract pathology. This case highlights the necessity of a multifactorial management approach, including microbiological evaluation, catheter hygiene, bowel regulation, and patient education. Early recognition of PUBS may help prevent escalation to more severe complications in vulnerable, catheter-dependent patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02089-2.Keywords: Purple urine, Urinary catheter, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Urinary tract infection, Case report, Catheter-associated complications

Introduction

Purple urine bag syndrome (PUBS) is a rare but visually striking condition characterized by the discoloration of urine and urine collection bags into a deep purple hue. Although usually benign, PUBS often serves as a marker for an underlying urinary tract infection (UTI). It predominantly affects elderly, debilitated patients, particularly females with alkaline urine, multiple comorbidities, and chronic indwelling plastic urinary catheters [1, 2]. In such patients, PUBS may indicate a UTI that, if left untreated, can lead to severe complications like Pyelonephritis, urosepsis / septic shock, acute kidney injury, perinephric abscess, bacteremia, catheter blockage due to biofilm, bladder stones, Delirium (in elderly), increased mortality. Moreover, its occurrence often reflects inadequate catheter care and hygiene, highlighting the need for regular catheter maintenance and monitoring [3]. Here, we present the case of a post-stroke elderly female who developed PUBS due to a UTI caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae. This case is particularly relevant as PUBS, typically reported in elderly catheterized patients, occurred in a relatively younger post-stroke individual, highlighting the need for awareness across broader age groups.

Case report

A right-handed patient in her 40s from North India, presented with left-sided weakness and involuntary movements of the left upper limb. The patient had a history of stroke, a cerebrovascular accident (CVA) with left hemiparesis and was known to be hypertensive, not receiving regular medication. The patient had been bedridden and dependent for mobility and self-care for the past 2 years, with preserved cognitive and sphincter function. Associated symptoms included constipation, impaired trunk balance, and reduced sensation upon the left side. The patient had been catheterized continuously for 2 years with a change of catheter every 20–25 days because of urinary retention, and the catheter was changed 20 days prior. For the past 10 days, the urine bag demonstrated purple discoloration. There was no fever or abdominal pain (Table 1). On examination, the patient’s vital signs were within normal limits, with stable blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature. The patient experienced involuntary head and left upper limb movements, left facial palsy, and decreased power on the left side (> 3/5). The reflexes were exaggerated, and the plantar response was extensor upon the left. The urinary bag appeared purple (Fig. 1). The patient was evaluated and diagnosed with PUBS.

Investigations

Bacterial culture report

A urine culture was performed via the standard loop technique on UTI chrome agar (High Media Mumbai), identification was performed via MALDI TOF VITEK MS (Biomerieux France), and Klebsiella pneumoniae was grown in culture, with a colony count of ≥ 105 CFU/ml, indicating significant bacterial infection. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed via VITEK 2 (Biomerieux france). Her complete blood count (CBC) shows mild anemia, with hemoglobin at 10.7 g/dL, low MCV (77.9 fL), low MCH (23.3 pg), and low MCHC (30 g/dL), suggesting microcytic hypochromic anemia, most likely due to iron deficiency. The total white blood cell count is 11.47 × 10³/µL, which is slightly elevated, and the neutrophil count is also high at 66.7% (absolute neutrophils 7.67 × 10³/µL), indicating an ongoing infection or inflammation. and the inflammatory markers such as the c-reactive protein (CRP) was 7 and the ESR was raised to 25.

First-line antibiotics: The bacteria were sensitive (S) to ceftriaxone, cotrimoxazole, levofloxacin, and norfloxacin, indicating that these antibiotics may be effective for treatment. Piperacillin‒tazobactam showed susceptible dose‒dependent (SDD) activity, indicating that it might be effective at relatively high doses. However, the bacteria were resistant (R) to nitrofurantoin, making it unsuitable for treatment.

Second-line antibiotics: The bacteria were sensitive to tetracycline, meropenem, and imipenem, suggesting that these may be alternative treatment options. However, amikacin showed intermediate resistance (I), indicating limited effectiveness. Cefepime is associated with SDD, meaning that its effectiveness depends on the dosage used.

Management

The management of PUBS is multifaceted and aims at addressing the underlying infection, removing the predisposing factors, and providing supportive care.

Patient was assessed on the FIM (score-of 58) and BARTHEL (score-of 25). All routine investigations were sent.

For our patient catheter replacement, i.e., removal of the biofilm-laden catheter and replacement with a sterile catheter, was crucial in halting the cycle of infection and pigment production. We started the patient on antibiotic therapy based on the urine culture sensitivity. In this case, the patient responded to an antibiotic-levofloxacin 500 mg OD dose for 7 days, which was effective against Klebsiella pneumoniae. The patient’s functional status remained unchanged from baseline, with continued dependence on mobility and self-care due to chronic post-stroke deficits.

Supportive measures addressing contributing factors such as constipation, encouraging adequate hydration, and maintaining catheter hygiene are essential components of care.

Preventive Strategies: Assurance and education of caregivers on catheter care and recognition of signs of UTI were emphasized to reduce the risk of recurrence. The patient was also advised that patients with asymptomatic bacteriuria need acidification of urine and proper hydration and perineal hygiene.

Our management approach aligns with the 2025 IDSA Guidelines for Complicated UTI, which recommend catheter replacement and culture‑directed antimicrobial therapy in patients with catheter‑associated urinary tract infection presenting with systemic features. The guidelines also emphasize selecting empiric antibiotics guided by severity of illness, patient risk factors for resistance, and de‑escalating to targeted therapy once susceptibility results are available. In this case, catheter exchange and targeted therapy with levofloxacin were performed, consistent with these recommendations [4].

As the patient was admitted under the care of our Physiatry team, initial evaluation and management of PUBS were undertaken by the (Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation) PMR team in accordance with established protocols.

The caregiver was initially alarmed upon noticing the sudden purple discoloration of the urine bag, fearing it indicated a serious complication. After receiving a clear explanation of the benign nature of PUBS and observing the positive response to treatment as the color returned to normal, the caregiver expressed relief and satisfaction. They also appreciated the guidance on catheter care, which increased their confidence in managing the patient’s condition at home.

Discussion

PUBS is rooted in the metabolic activity of certain uropathogens capable of producing the enzymes indole-sulphatase and phosphatase [5]. Dietary tryptophan is metabolized to indoles and converted to indicants by the liver [6]. The degradation of indoxyl sulfate by enzymes leads to the formation of indigo (blue pigment) and indirubin (red pigment) (Fig. 2). When these pigments mix in an alkaline urinary environment, they form the characteristic purple discoloration seen in PUBS. The absence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) in this patient indicated a localized infection, making its management straightforward. A similar case of Purple Urine Bag Syndrome has been reported in a neurologically impaired patient with long-term indwelling catheterization due to neurogenic bladder. The patient developed characteristic purple discoloration of the urine bag secondary to urinary tract infection with gram-negative organisms, including Klebsiella species. The authors emphasized the role of chronic catheterization, alkaline urine, and impaired mobility as major predisposing factors, with resolution following catheter replacement and appropriate antimicrobial therapy [3].

The formation of indigo and indirubin from tryptophan and the formation of purple urine bags [7–9]

The most common causative uropathogens include, Klebsiella pneumoniae, as in this case. Other organisms are group B streptococci, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococci, Proteus mirabilis, Escherichia coli, Providencia stuartii, Providencia rettgeri, Morganella morganii and Citrobacter [10, 11].

In this patient, a combination of these factors was likely predisposed to PUBS, with chronic catheter use and constipation serving as the most significant contributors. Ileal diversion, intussusception, and chronic constipation are all associated with colonic bacterial overgrowth. In addition to increasing tryptophan levels, excessive bacteria also increase indole synthesis [10]. Chronic kidney disease can also predispose individuals to this phenomenon because it decreases the excretion of indole and thereby increases the indole concentration [12]. Other risk factors (Fig. 3) include female sex, advanced age and recurrent UTIs [3].

Several predisposing factors contribute to the development of PUBS [2, 9, 13, 14]

Despite its unusual appearance, PUBS is often asymptomatic and self-limiting. Management typically involves improving catheter hygiene, replacing the catheter and urine bag, and treating underlying UTI when present. Regular monitoring, urine cultures, and antibiotic use based on sensitivity reports are important for preventing serious complications such as urosepsis, catheter-related infections, and renal dysfunction [3].

Recent studies suggest that catheter and bag replacement alone can resolve PUBS in many cases without the need for aggressive antibiotic therapy unless an active infection exists. A holistic approach—including medical treatment, preventive care, patient and caregiver education, and addressing host factors such as gut motility, immune status, chronic constipation, alkaline urine, and prolonged catheter use—is essential to reduce recurrence and improve outcomes, especially in vulnerable patients [1].

In addition to UTIs, blue diaper syndrome, a genetic abnormality of tryptophan metabolism, can also cause PUBS [12]. Urine color can vary due to several factors [15]. Green urine may result from a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection or the use of medications or chemicals such as methylene blue, amitriptyline, indomethacin, and doxorubicin. A deep orange hue is often observed in patients taking drugs such as rifampicin or warfarin [9, 16].

PUBS is a rare condition marked by purple discoloration of the urine bag, as observed in chronically catheterized patients with UTIs; Pillai et al. (2009) reported PUBS in a 68-year-old diabetic woman; Pandey et al. (2018) reported it in a 70-year-old man with BPH; and Tirtayasa et al. (2023) reported it in a 27-year-old woman with a neurogenic bladder, all involving bacteria such as E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis and Pseudomonas aeruginosa that metabolize tryptophan into indigo and indirubin, with PUBS signaling serious infections and highlighting the need for catheter care, timely replacement, and targeted antibiotics.

Conclusion

The occurrence of PUBS in this post-stroke elderly female highlights the interplay between host factors, chronic medical devices, and microbial activity. While the purple discoloration of urine is a striking and rare presentation, it serves as a diagnostic clue for underlying UTIs in catheterized patients. Timely recognition and management, including targeted antibiotics, catheter replacement, and supportive measures, ensure favorable outcomes and prevent recurrence. This case reinforces the need for vigilance, especially in managing chronically ill, debilitated, or elderly patients, to provide optimal care and improve quality of life.
