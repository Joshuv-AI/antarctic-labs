Maximum Matching and Related Problems in Catalytic Logspace
Srijan Chakraborty, Samir Datta, Aryan Kusre, Partha Mukhopadhyay

Abstract
Understanding the power of space-bounded computation with access to catalytic space has been an important theme in complexity theory over the recent years. One of the key algorithmic results in this area is that bipartite maximum matching can be computed in catalytic logspace ( 𝖢𝖫 {\mathsf{CL}} ) with a polynomial-time bound ( 𝖢𝖫𝖯 {\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P} ) [ 2 ] . In this paper, we show that we can construct a maximum matching in general graphs in 𝖢𝖫 {\mathsf{CL}} , and, in fact, in 𝖢𝖫𝖯 {\mathsf{CLP}} . We first show that the size of a maximum matching in general graphs can be determined in 𝖢𝖫 {\mathsf{CL}} . Our algorithm is based on the linear-algebraic algorithm for maximum matching by Geelen [ 14 ] . We then show that this algorithm, along with some new ideas, can be used to find a maximum matching in general graphs. Using a similar algorithm of Geelen [ 13 ] , we also solve the maximum rank completion problem in 𝖢𝖫𝖯 {\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P} , which was previously known to be solvable in deterministic polynomial time [ 13 ] . This problem turns out to be equivalent to the linear matroid intersection problem [ 19 ] which has been shown to be in 𝖢𝖫𝖯 {\mathsf{CLP}} by [ 1 ] . Finally, using a PTAS algorithm [ 4 ] for approximating the rank in Edmond’s problem, we derive a 𝖢𝖫𝖯 {\mathsf{CLP}} algorithm that can approximate the rank given by any instance of the Edmond’s problem upto a factor of ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) for any ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \varepsilon\in(0,1) . An application of this is a 𝖢𝖫𝖯 {\mathsf{CLP}} bound for approximating the maximum independent matching in the linear matroid matching problem.

Introduction

The study of space-bounded computation is a central theme in complexity theory, with a long line of work investigating the power and limitations of logarithmic-space algorithms. A recent direction in this area focuses on models that augment classical space bounds with auxiliary resources, such as catalytic space, where a large workspace is available but must be returned to its initial state at the end of the computation. This model, introduced by Buhrman, Cleve, Koucký, Loff, and Speelman
[
5
]
, has led to surprising algorithmic developments and a refined understanding of the role of reversibility and space reuse in computation.

A major goal in this line of research is to identify natural problems that can be solved efficiently within catalytic logspace (
{\mathsf{CL}}
), particularly with polynomial-time bounds (
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
). While several foundational results have established the basic properties of this model, progress on natural combinatorial problems has been more recent. Notably, the breakthrough result of
[
2
]
shows that bipartite maximum matching can be computed in catalytic logspace with polynomial time, providing one of the first nontrivial examples of a classical graph problem lying in
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
. Over the recent years, there have been many results surrounding catalytic computation such as
[
5
,
21
,
8
,
1
,
7
,
3
,
10
,
6
]
. A flowchart for various complexity classes would be:
{\mathsf{L}}\subseteq{\mathsf{NL}}\subseteq{\mathsf{TC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{NC}}\subseteq\mathsf{P}\subseteq{\mathsf{ZPP}}
where the only known bounds for catalytic logspace are
{\mathsf{TC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{CL}}\subseteq{\mathsf{ZPP}}
.

Matching problems occupy a central position in combinatorial optimization and theoretical computer science. The maximum matching problem, in both bipartite and general graphs, has been extensively studied, with classical polynomial-time algorithms based on augmenting paths, combinatorial structures such as blossoms, and algebraic formulations
[
24
,
11
,
15
,
17
]
. In particular, algebraic approaches based on the Tutte matrix and rank computations, such as those developed by Jim Geelen, provide a powerful framework for reasoning about matchings and their generalizations
[
13
,
14
]
.

In this paper, we extend the reach of catalytic logspace algorithms for matching problems. We show that a maximum matching in general graphs can be computed in
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
, thereby generalizing the bipartite case. To show that bipartite matching is in
{\mathsf{CLP}}
,
[
2
]
uses isolating weights which they sample from the catalytic tape. They can solve the problem in one shot if a given weight assignment is isolating, and otherwise they find a threshold edge by finding appropriate augmenting paths and can free up space. This technique breaks down in general graphs, since finding augmenting paths becomes much more difficult, as one might come across blossoms while doing so. To avoid these issues, we build on the algebraic techniques of Geelen
[
14
]
and adapt them to the catalytic setting, carefully managing space reuse. To search for a maximum matching, we again use isolating weights, but not in the conventional sense, in particular, we do not isolate a min-weight matching. As a further application, we show that the
maximum rank matrix completion
problem can also be solved in
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}

[

. This problem, previously known to admit deterministic polynomial-time algorithms, captures a common generalization of linear matroid intersection and bipartite matching
[

Both maximum matching, and the matrix completion problems are special cases of

In

Extending the ideas in

{\mathsf{CLP}}
algorithm for approximating the maximum independent matching in the linear matroid matching problem.

Our results provide new evidence for the power of catalytic space in enabling efficient algorithms for algebraic and combinatorial problems. More broadly, they suggest that linear-algebraic techniques may play a fundamental role in understanding the capabilities of space-bounded computation with reversible auxiliary memory. This also gives a
{\mathsf{CL}}

{\mathsf{NC}}

(1-\varepsilon)

Our results

The maximum matching problem in general graphs admits a well-known algebraic formulation via the Tutte matrix
[
24
]
. Given a graph
G
, the Tutte matrix is a symbolic skew-symmetric matrix in which each edge
(i,j)
is associated with an indeterminate
x_{i,j}
(with a sign), and non-edges correspond to zero entries.

The fundamental connection shows that the rank of this matrix (over a suitable field) is twice the size of a maximum matching in
G
. This characterization enables the use of linear-algebraic techniques for computing matchings, including randomized algorithms based on substituting indeterminates with field elements. In particular, combining this approach with fast parallel algorithms for matrix rank yields an
{\mathsf{RNC}}
upper bound for maximum matching in general graphs
[
18
]
, placing the problem among the central examples of efficiently parallelizable combinatorial problems. From the perspective of derandomization and pinning down the parallel complexity, putting matching in NC has been an outstanding question.

The last decade saw advances in understanding the parallel complexity of matching. In particular, building on the breakthrough quasi-
{\mathsf{NC}}
algorithm for bipartite matching due to Fenner, Gurjar, and Thierauf
[
12
]
, follow-up work by Svensson and Tarnawski
[
23
]
obtained quasi-
{\mathsf{NC}}
algorithms for maximum matching in general graphs.

Despite these advances, a
{\mathsf{CL}}
algorithm to find a perfect matching in general graphs has remained elusive. We settle this question by proving the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Maximum matchings in a general graph
G
can be constructed in
{\mathsf{CLP}}
.

The maximum rank matrix completion problem asks, given a matrix with some entries fixed and others unspecified, to assign values to the unspecified entries so as to maximize the rank of the resulting matrix. This problem admits a natural algebraic interpretation and captures several combinatorial optimization problems as special cases. In particular, Geelen showed that the problem can be solved in deterministic polynomial time via a greedy algorithm that incrementally assigns values while preserving rank growth
[
13
]
. The problem generalizes classical questions such as bipartite matching and linear matroid intersection, and plays a central role in algebraic approaches to matching and related problems. Extending the techniques used to prove Theorem
1.1
, we show the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

The maximum rank completion problem can be solved in
{\mathsf{CLP}}
.

In
[
4
]
, Bläser, Jindal, and Pandey study the problem of computing the commutative rank of a matrix space, a fundamental quantity that captures the maximum rank achievable by linear combinations of given matrices
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}
. More precisely, the rank of a matrix space
\mathcal{A}=\langle A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}\rangle
over a sufficiently large field

is the maximum rank of a matrix in
\mathcal{A}
. If we consider the formal linear combination
A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}x_{i}
, then we can compute a matrix of maximum rank by substituting random values to
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
from
\mathbb{F}
by applying the following Polynomial Identity Testing Lemma
[
25
,
22
,
9
]
:

Lemma 1.3
.

Let

P\in R\left[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\right]

be a non-zero polynomial of total degree
d\geq 0
over an integral domain
R
. Let
S
be a finite subset of
R
and let
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n}
be selected at random independently and uniformly from

\operatorname{Pr}\left[P\left(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n}\right)=0\right]\leq\frac{d}{|S|}

As already mentioned, exact computation of the commutative rank of a given matrix space in deterministic polynomial time is hard
[
16
]
.
Nevertheless, Bläser, Jindal, and Pandey
[
4
]
could show a deterministic polynomial-time
approximation scheme
(PTAS) that computes a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation of the rank for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Their approach combines algebraic and combinatorial techniques to bypass randomness. Our main result in this context is the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given a matrix space
\mathcal{A}=\langle A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}\rangle
by a set of matrices
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we can compute a matrix
A\in\mathcal{A}
whose rank is at least
1-\varepsilon
times the rank of
\mathcal{A}
, in
{\mathsf{CLP}}
.

Since our results use ideas from Geelen’s algebraic matching algorithm
[
14
]
, we provide a brief overview of his result. Let
T
be the Tutte matrix of
G=(V,E)
where
|V|=n
. For any evaluation
of
T^{\prime}
, let
\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime})
denote its rank.
Let
D(T^{\prime})
denote the corresponding set of deficiency, i.e., the set of vertices
x
such that
\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}\setminus\{x\})=\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime})
. Intuitively, the set of vertices corresponds to the Gallai–Edmonds decomposition induced by
T^{\prime}
in a linear algebraic sense. Then, Geelen
[
14
]
defines a preorder
T^{\prime}_{1}\preceq T^{\prime}_{2}
either

\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}_{1})<\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}_{2})\quad\text{or}\quad(\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}_{2})=\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad D(T^{\prime}_{1})\subseteq D(T^{\prime}_{2})).

If both
\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}_{1})=\operatorname{rank}(T^{\prime}_{2})
and
D(T^{\prime}_{1})=D(T^{\prime}_{2})
then
T^{\prime}_{1}=T^{\prime}_{2}
.
The algorithm incrementally assigns values to the indeterminates corresponding to the edges and constructs a sequence
T^{\prime}_{1}\prec T^{\prime}_{2}\prec\cdots\prec T^{\prime}_{m}
where the final matrix achieves the rank of the Tutte matrix.

More precisely, to go from
T^{\prime}_{i}
to
T^{\prime}_{i+1}
, the algorithm selects an edge
(i,j)
and replaces its value by
a\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that
T^{\prime}_{i}\prec T^{\prime}_{i+1}
. If such an assignment does not exist then the algorithm already has found the evaluated matrix achieving the true rank. After finding the size of the maximum matching, they use self-reducibility of matching to search for one. But under our space bounded constraints, the search for a maximum matching in the same way as above, does not work out. Notice that
[
3
]
shows a
{\mathsf{CL}}
reduction from search to weighted decision, that works in the matching context as well, but it is also not clear how to solve the weighted decision using our ideas. To construct a maximum matching of the graph, we need some substantially new ideas. We again sample weight assignments from the catalytic tape, as
[
2
]
, but we are not able to find min-isolating weights. In stead, we have a non-zero polynomial, the least degree of which turns out to be the weight of the matching that attempt to isolate. Smaller weighted matchings may disappear in this polynomial. Finally, if we are not able to find a matching even in this way, we show that we are able to save enough space to construct a matching in polynomial space and time, and later restore the catalytic tape. We heavily use the
‘compress or compute’
paradigm.

Preliminaries

Matchings

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple undirected graph. A
matching

M\subseteq E
is a set of pairwise vertex-disjoint edges. A matching is said to be
maximum
if it has the largest possible cardinality among all matchings in
G
.

We denote by
\nu(G)
the size of a maximum matching in
G
, i.e.,

\nu(G)=\max\{|M|:M\subseteq E\text{ is a matching}\}.

Gallai–Edmonds Decomposition

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph. Define:

1.

D(G)
: the set of vertices that are unmatched in at least one maximum matching of
G
,

2.

A(G)
: the set of vertices in
V\setminus D(G)
that have a neighbor in
D(G)
,

3.

C(G):=V\setminus(A(G)\cup D(G))
.

The partition
(D(G),A(G),C(G))
is called the
Gallai–Edmonds decomposition
of
G
.

This decomposition satisfies the following properties:

1.

Each connected component of
G[D(G)]
is factor-critical.

2.

G[C(G)]
has a perfect matching.

3.

In every maximum matching:

(a)

all vertices of
A(G)
are matched to vertices in
D(G)
,

(b)

each component of
G[D(G)]
has exactly one unmatched vertex.

Linear Matroids

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field. A
linear matroid
is a matroid
M=(E,\mathcal{I})
where
E
is a finite set and there exists a matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times|E|}
such that a subset
I\subseteq E
is independent (i.e.,
I\in\mathcal{I}
) if and only if the corresponding set of columns of
A
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
.

Linear Matroid Intersection

Let
M_{1}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{1})
and
M_{2}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{2})
be two linear matroids represented over the same ground set
E
. The
linear matroid intersection problem
asks to find a set

I\subseteq E\quad\text{such that }I\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\cap\mathcal{I}_{2}

of maximum cardinality.

Linear Matroid Parity

Let
M=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a linear matroid, and suppose the ground set
E
is partitioned into disjoint pairs:

E=\{e_{1},f_{1},e_{2},f_{2},\dots,e_{m},f_{m}\}.

The
linear matroid parity problem
asks to find a maximum-size collection of pairs such that the union of the selected elements is independent in
M
. Formally, find an index set
S\subseteq[m]
maximizing
|S|
such that

\{e_{i},f_{i}:i\in S\}\in\mathcal{I}.

Linear Matroid Matching

Let
M=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a linear matroid, and let
G=(E,F)
be a graph whose vertex set is the ground set of the matroid.

A set of edges
S\subseteq F
is a
matroid matching
if:

1.

S
is a matching in the graph
G
, and

2.

the set of vertices covered by
S
is independent in the matroid
M
.

The
linear matroid matching problem
asks to find a matroid matching of maximum cardinality.

Determinant

For a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
, the determinant is defined as:

\operatorname{\mathsf{Det}}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,\sigma(i)}

where
S_{n}
is the symmetric group and
\text{sgn}(\sigma)
is the parity of the permutation.

Pfaffian

For a skew-symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{2n\times 2n}
where
A^{T}=-A
, the Pfaffian is defined as:

{\mathsf{Pf}}(A)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2n}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{\sigma(2i-1),\sigma(2i)}

It holds that
\operatorname{\mathsf{Det}}(A)={\mathsf{Pf}}(A)^{2}
.

Catalytic Computation

Definition 2.1
.

A catalytic turing machine
M
with space
s(n)
and catalytic space
c(n)
is a turring machine that has a read-only input tape of length
n
, write-only output tape, and
s(n)
space bounded read-write work tape, and a catalytic tape of size
c(n)
.
We say that
M
computes a function

if for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{c(n)}
, the result of executing
M
on input
x
with initial catalytic tape
\tau
i.e.

satisfies:

1.

M
halts with

on the output tape.

2.

M
halts with the catalytic tape consisting of
\tau
.

{\mathsf{CSPACE}}[s(n),c(n)]
is the family of functions computable by such a turing machine. Similarly,
{\mathsf{CTISP}}[t(n),s(n),c(n)]
is the family of functions computable by such a turing machine with the further restriction that the machine simultaneously runs in time
t(n)
.

Definition 2.2

(Catalytic Logspace)
.

We define catalytic logspace as

{\mathsf{CL}}=\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathsf{CSPACE}}[k\log n,n^{k}]

Moreover,
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
is defined as the set of functions computable by a
{\mathsf{CL}}
machine that simultaneously runs in polynomial time.

In particular, we know that
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}={\mathsf{CL}}\cap\mathsf{P}

[
8
]
.
To compute the rank of various matrices, we shall use the fact that
{\mathsf{TC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{CL}}

[
5
]
. It is well known that Determinants and Pfaffians are computable in
{\mathsf{TC}}^{1}
and thus in

.

4
Hunt for a Maximum Matching

Finally we show how to search for a maximum matching in
{\mathsf{CL}}
. We break it down into two steps, the second step being a standard reduction, but the first step is fairly technical and uses new ideas:

1.

First, we show how to search for a perfect matching in a general graphs, given that the graph contains a perfect matching. We shall crucially use the fact that we can find an assignment to the Tutte matrix that achieves full rank,
Corollary

3.8.2
.

2.

Next we give a procedure to find a maximum matching in general graphs, using the Gallai-Edmonds decomposition.

Throughout this section, we assume that we are given a simple graph

\forall i\in[\ell]:c_{w_{0}}^{\bar{e}_{i}}=0,\forall j>\ell:c_{w_{0}}^{e_{j}}=0

and therefore,

\forall i\in[\ell]:c_{w_{0}}^{\bar{e}_{i}}=\sum_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}c_{w_{0}}^{e_{j}}=0.

Summing the above for all
i\in[\ell]
, we get

\sum_{i\in[\ell]}c_{w_{0}}^{\bar{e}_{i}}=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\sum_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}c_{w_{0}}^{e_{j}}=(\ell-1)\sum_{i\in[\ell]}{c_{w_{0}}^{e_{i}}}=0.

But since
\ell>1
, we have:

c_{w_{0}}=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}c_{w_{0}}^{e_{i}}=0.

Again this gives a contradiction since
c_{w_{0}}\neq 0
.

By the above argument, we have that every vertex has degree exactly
1
in
G^{\prime}
. This happens precisely when
M
is a perfect matching of
G
, and we are done.

∎

Finally, we come to the following algorithm:

Lemma 4.2
.

There is a
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
procedure given the following:

1.

A simple undirected graph
G
on
n
vertices and
m
edges with that contains a perfect matching.

2.

A
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that given any graph with a perfect matching, finds an assignment to its Tutte matrix

that is full rank.

returns a perfect matching of
G
.

Proof.

Assume that we are given the catalytic tape as
n^{3}
weight assignments
(W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n^{3}})
where each
W_{r}:E\to[n^{10}]
is given as
(W_{i}(e_{1}),\ldots,W_{i}(e_{m}))
. We are also given

by
\mathcal{A}
. Then we do the following, by processing
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n^{3}}
sequentially:

1.

Suppose, we are processing
W_{r}
. Set
A=T^{\prime}\circ W_{r}
. For all
e\in E
check whether
W_{r}(e)=w_{\bar{e}}-w_{e}
where
z^{w_{\bar{e}}}
is the least degree monomial with non-zero coefficient (can be found using interpolating) in
P_{0}
and
z^{w_{e}}
is the least degree monomial with non-zero coefficient in
P_{1}
. (recall that
A_{e}
is the matrix obtained from

by multiplying

by the variable

{\mathsf{Pf}}(A_{e})=P_{0}+yP_{1}

(i.e. the

edge), then reset the current catalytic tape string to
(i,W_{r}(e_{1}),\ldots,W_{r}(e_{i-1}),W_{r}(e_{i+1}),\ldots,e_{m})
. This frees up

bits of space. On the other hand, if we did not find such an edge, using
Lemma

4.1
, we find a perfect matching as follows:

Compute
w_{0}
to be the minimum degree such that

{\mathsf{Pf}}(A)

P_{0}
does not contain the monomial

4.1
. After finding this matching and returning it, we move to step
3
(to restore the previous catalytic strings) with the temporary variable

2.

Suppose that we just processed

\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log n)

[

3.

We restore all the weights

∎

In this section, we prove

5.1
Mixed matrices

Definition 5.1
.

Let

be an

matrix where each entry of

is either an indeterminate, or an element of
\mathbb{Q}
, where the set of variables is

Mixed matrices can also be written as

where

is a scalar matrix over
\mathbb{Q}
and

is an indeterminate matrix (where no two indeterminates in two coordinates are the same, in the same sense as

For any

matrix

whose rows are indexed by

and columns by

. Let
E=\{(i,j):A_{ij}\in X\}
, further index these tuples such that
E=\{i:i=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),A_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\in X\}
.

Theorem 5.1

(Geelen,
[
13
]
)
.

Let
A
be a mixed matrix over the variable set
X
where
|X|=m
, and let
A^{\prime}
be an evaluation of
A
. Then either
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}=\operatorname{rank}A
and
D(A^{\prime})=D(A)
, or one of the following two statements hold:

•

\exists i\in E,a\in[n^{10}]
such that
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}+1
.

•

\exists i\in E,a\in[n^{10}]

\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}\wedge D(A^{\prime})\subsetneq D(A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a})

Analogously to

3.5
and
3.6
(
2A,2B

Lemma 5.2

(
2A^{\prime}
)
.

Let

be an evaluation of

and

\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}+1
, and in

is

. Then
\forall\tilde{a}\neq a_{i}

\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}+1

Proof.

Let

.
Let
X\subseteq L_{r},Y\subseteq L_{c},|X|=|Y|=k
be a minor of
A
that is non-singular in

. Notice that
L(y)=\operatorname{\mathsf{Det}}(A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow y}[X,Y])
is linear in

. Since
L(a)\neq 0,L(a_{i})=0
, clearly
\forall\tilde{a}\neq a_{i},L(\tilde{a})\neq 0

A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}[X,Y]

Furthermore, on changing one entries of

\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}+1

\forall\tilde{a}\neq a
.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(
2B^{\prime}
)
.

Let
A^{\prime}
be an evaluation of
A
and
i\in E,a\in[n^{10}]
such that
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}=k
where in
A^{\prime}
,
x_{i}
is set to

. Suppose
\exists u\in D(A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a})\setminus D(A^{\prime})
. Then
\forall\tilde{a}\neq a_{i}
, we have
u\in D(A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}})

From the given assumptions, we have a

such that

is non-singular and

. Since

,

is singular.

Claim 5.4
.

\forall\tilde{a}\neq a_{i}
,
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}\leq k
.

Proof.

Suppose
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}>k
for some
\tilde{a}
. Therefore,
\tilde{a}\notin\{a,a_{i}\}
. Therefore, we can find

\widetilde{L}(a)=\widetilde{L}(a_{i})=0
. Therefore,
\widetilde{L}(\tilde{a})=0
gives a contradiction.
∎

Again
L(y)=\operatorname{\mathsf{Det}}(A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow y}[X_{u},Y_{u}])
is linear in
y
, such that
L(a)\neq 0,L(a_{i})=0
.
Therefore,
\forall\tilde{a}\neq a_{i}
we have
L(\tilde{a})\neq 0
i.e
A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}[X_{u}]
is non-singular and by the above claim

is of maximum possible size (i.e.
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow\tilde{a}}=|X_{u}|=|Y_{u}|=k

.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that we are given an evaluation of a mixed matrix
A
, namely
A^{\prime}
. Then we can check, in
{\mathsf{CL}}
if
\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}

Proof.

The proof is very similar to

check if the rank increases by

.
∎

5.2
A catalytic rank algorithm

In this section, we show that the rank of mixed matrices can be computed in
{\mathsf{CL}}
. Let
A
be a mixed matrix with variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
. The algorithm is essentially the same as the matching algorithm described in
Section

3
. We sample an assignment
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
for these variables from the catalytic tape. Either this assignment maximizes the rank of the evaluated matrix
A^{\prime}
, or it does not. We can verify this using
Lemma

5.5
. If the rank is indeed maximized, we proceed to restore the modified catalytic tape.

Otherwise, if the rank is not maximized, we consider two cases:

(a)

Suppose that by perturbing one of the variables
x_{i}
, the rank of
A^{\prime}
can be increased. In this case, by
Lemma

5.2
, for all values other than
a_{i}
, the rank increases. Therefore, we discard
a_{i}
from the catalytic tape and store the index
i
together with the rank. Later, we can recover
a_{i}
as the unique value for which the rank remains unchanged.

(b)

Otherwise, perturbing some variable
x_{i}
does not increase the rank, but enlarges the set
D(A^{\prime})
, i.e., there exists a vertex
u
that gets added to
D(A^{\prime})
. By
Lemma

5.3
,
a_{i}
is the unique value for which
u\notin D(A^{\prime})
. Thus, we again discard
a_{i}
and store its index
i
together with
u
. As before,
a_{i}
can be recovered when needed using this characterization.

Finally, if the sampled assignment already yields the maximum rank, we are done. Otherwise, we have freed enough space to recompute the rank from scratch using
[
13
]
. A formal proof follows.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
A
be an
r\times c
dimensional mixed matrix on variables
X
indexed over the set
E
of size
m\leq n^{2}
, and
n=r+c
. Then, there is a
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
procedure that finds
\operatorname{rank}A
, and an assignment to
X
(and thus an evaluation of
A
, namely
A^{\prime}
) that is of the maximum possible rank, i.e.,
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}=\operatorname{rank}A
.

Proof.

The algorithm mirrors the proof of
Theorem

3.8
.
We assume that the catalytic tape is divided into blocks
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{N},\mathcal{B})
where
\mathcal{C}_{t}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\forall t\in[N]
with each
a_{i}\in[n^{10}]
is
10\log n
bits long, and
\mathcal{B}
is for rank computations;
N=n^{3}
. The algorithm proceeds as follows: We iterate over all
\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{N}
as follows, suppose we are processing
\mathcal{C}_{t}
:

1.

Let
A^{\prime}
be the evaluation of
A
given by
\mathcal{C}_{t}
i.e. by putting
x_{i}\leftarrow a_{i}\forall i\in[m]
. We first check if
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}=\operatorname{rank}A
or not, using
Lemma

5.5
. If yes, then we have found a required assignment, and we proceed to restore all previous catalytic blocks, i.e. jump to Step
4
with the temporary variable
index\leftarrow t-1
. Now suppose we did not get the required assignment. Therefore, from
Theorem

5.1
, the only two possibilities are:

(a)

\exists i\in E,a\in[n^{10}]
such that
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}+1
.

(b)

\exists i\in E,a\in[n^{10}]
such that
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}\wedge D(A^{\prime})\subsetneq D(A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a})
.

Consider the two cases
2A^{\prime},2B^{\prime}
that deals with
(a)
and
(b)
respectively, as follows–

2A^{\prime}
.

In this case, after finding an
i
(we enumerate over all

to find such an

) as in
(a)
, from
Lemma

5.2
, we know that for all substitutions
x_{i}\leftarrow a
and

, we have
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}_{x_{i}\leftarrow a}\neq\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}
. Therefore, we replace
\mathcal{C}_{t}
with
(2A^{\prime},i,\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime},a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})
. This saves
6\log n
.

2B^{\prime}
.

Again, first we find an index
i
, and value

. Now, we pick a

a_{i}
is the unique value for which

. Hence we replace
\mathcal{C}_{r}
with
(2B^{\prime},i,u,a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a,a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})

bits.

3.

Suppose we just processed
\mathcal{C}_{N}
and still did not find a required assignment. In this case, we have freed up at least

space for

.

In either case, we now recompute the modified catalytic strings

\mathcal{C}_{t}=(case,j,\beta,a_{1},\ldots,a_{j-1},a_{j+1},\ldots,a_{m})

This is when

In this section we prove

1.4

Given a matrix space
\mathcal{A}=\langle A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\rangle

of size greater that some polynomial in

for a carefully chosen

that depends inversely on

can attain. Consider the indeterminates

\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}x_{i}=:\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{A})

Theorem 6.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\langle A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\rangle\leq\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a fixed parameter with

a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
for

and

be any subset of

of size

\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}a_{i}\Big)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}x_{i}\Big)
.

2.

or,
\exists I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}\in\binom{[m]}{\ell}
and
\lambda_{i_{1}}\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}}\in S
such that

\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus I}A_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}\lambda_{i}\Big)>\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}a_{i}\Big)

Henceforth, we fix
S
as the first
n^{c}
elements of
\mathbb{F}
in the canonical order. Even though we do not have any uniqueness lemma like
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.6
, we show the following which is essentially an application of the Schwartz-Zippel lemma, and shall be crucial for our purposes.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
\vec{a}=a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{F}
and
\exists I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}\in\binom{[m]}{\ell}
and
\lambda_{i_{1}}\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}}\in S
such that

\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus I}A_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}\lambda_{i}\Big)>\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}a_{i}\Big).

Let

\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}=\Big\{\lambda^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}\in S:\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus I}A_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\Big)=\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}a_{i}\Big)\Big\}.

Then
|\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}|\leq n^{c\ell-c+1}
.

Proof.

Let
A^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{i}a_{i},A^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus I}A_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}\lambda_{i}
.
Let
X,Y\subseteq[n]
be of maximum possible size such that
A^{\prime\prime}[X,Y]
is a square non-singular matrix, but
A^{\prime}[X,Y]
is singular. Let

p(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}})=\operatorname{\mathsf{Det}}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus I}A_{i}a_{i}[X,Y]+\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}x_{i}[X,Y]\Big)

be a polynomial in the indeterminates
x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}
. Clearly
p
is of degree at most
n
(to be precise, the degree is at most
|X|\leq n
), and
p(\lambda_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}})\neq 0
. Therefore, from
Lemma

1.3
, the number of roots of
p
in
S^{\ell}
is upper bounded by
|S^{\ell}|.(n/|S|)=n^{c\ell-c+1}
. Moreover, for any
(\lambda^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
, we have
p(\lambda^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}})=0
. Therefore,
|\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}|\leq n^{c\ell-c+1}
.
∎

We shall approximate
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{A})
upto a factor of
(1-\varepsilon)
in
{\mathsf{CL}}
. We sample
x_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\leftarrow a_{m}
from the catalytic tape. Either we already have landed up at an approximately good assignment
A^{\prime}
in which case we begin reconstructing the modified catalytic strings. Otherwise, we know that we can find
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
and a perturbation to
x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}
for which the rank increases. But then, the above lemma implies that
|\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}|
is smaller than
|S|^{\ell}
by a polynomially large factor. Notice that
I\in\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
, and therefore
I
is the
j^{th}
tuple in
\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
for some
j
that requires a small number of bits to represent. Therefore, we forget
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{\ell}}
from
\vec{a}
and remember instead
I
,
j
and the rank of
A^{\prime}
. We can later reconstruct
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{\ell}}
by finding the
j^{th}
tuple of
\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
. Again, if we never find a suitable catalytic tape, then we have saved enough space to run
[
4
]
and find a
(1-\varepsilon)
approximation of the rank, and a required assignment. We now present the proof in detail:

Theorem 6.3
.

For any constant
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and large enough field
\mathbb{F}
such that
|\mathbb{F}|\geq n^{\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)}
, suppose we are given an instance of Edmond’s problem,
\mathcal{A}=\langle A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\rangle
. Then, we have a
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
algorithm that returns a matrix
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\operatorname{rank}A\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Let
S
be the first
n^{c}
elements of
\mathbb{F}
that therefore require
c\log n
bits to store. We shall throughout the algorithm, work with
S
. Fix
\ell=\lceil 1/\varepsilon-1\rceil,N=n^{3}
and
c=2\ell+3
. Assume that the catalytic tape is divided into blocks
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{N},\mathcal{B})
where
C_{t}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\forall t\in[N]
and each
a_{i}\in S
takes up
c\log n
bits of storage.
\mathcal{B}
is again for rank computations. Let
A=A_{1}x_{1}+\ldots A_{m}x_{m}
where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
are variables. The algorithm proceeds as follows: We iterate over all
\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{N}
as follows, suppose that we are processing
\mathcal{C}_{t}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
:

1.

We Execute
Algorithm

4
. Let
A^{\prime}
be the evaluation of
A
given by
\mathcal{C}_{t}
and
k
be its rank i.e. by putting
x_{i}\leftarrow a_{i}\forall i\in[m]
. If for no perturbation in
S
for any
\ell
variables, gives a matrix of larger rank than
A^{\prime}
then we have found
A^{\prime}
which approximates the rank of
A
upto a factor of
(1-\varepsilon)
by
Theorem

6.1
. Thus we jump to Step
4
to reconstruct the previous catalytic blocks by setting
index=t-1
. On the other hand, suppose for some
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})\in\binom{[m]}{\ell}
, and some perturbation
\lambda_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}}
, the rank of
A^{\prime}
increases. Then, from
Lemma

6.2
we know that
\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
is of size at most
n^{c\ell-c+1}
. Therefore, we enumerate over all
\lambda_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}}\in S
and check if
(\lambda_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
. This way, we find
j
such that
(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{\ell}})
is the
j^{th}
tuple in
\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
. Finally, we reset
\mathcal{C}\leftarrow(i_{1}\ldots,i_{\ell},j,k,\vec{a}^{-I})
where
\vec{a}^{-I}
is
\{a_{1}\ldots,a_{m}\}\setminus\{a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{\ell}}\}
. Notice that the index
j
can be written with memory only
(c\ell-c+1)\log n
. Therefore, in this process, we have freed up
cm\log n-\ell\log n-(c\ell-c+1)\log n-\log n-c(m-\ell)\log n=(c\ell-\ell-c\ell+c-1-1)\log n=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
since we have chosen
c=2\ell+3
.

2.

Suppose we just processed
\mathcal{C}_{N}
and still did not find a required assignment. In this case, we have freed up at least
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log n)
space for
N=n^{3}
. We shift the catalytic strings to make this space contiguous, and run
[
4
]
algorithm to find an assignment
A^{\prime}
achieves the
(1-\varepsilon)
approximation for
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{A})
. Next set
index=N
and go to step
4
.

3.

In either case, we now recompute the modified catalytic strings
\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{index}
. For each
t\in[index]
, we execute
Algorithm

5
with
\mathcal{C}_{t}=(i_{1}\ldots,i_{\ell},j,k,\vec{a}^{-I})
as follows:

We wish to compute the
j^{th}
tuple from the set
\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
. Notice that

\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}=\Big\{\lambda_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}}\in S:\operatorname{rank}\Big(\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus I}A_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}\lambda_{i}\Big)=k\Big\}.

Therefore we enumerate over all
\lambda_{i_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{i_{\ell}}\in S
until we find the
j^{th}
tuple
(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{\ell}})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\vec{a},I}
. Finally we reset
\mathcal{C}\leftarrow(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
.

The above algorithm clearly runs in polynomial time
{\mathsf{poly}}(n^{\ell})
, and the catalytic space used is
\mathcal{O}(c\cdot mn^{2}\log n+|\mathcal{B}|)={\mathsf{poly}}(n,\ell)
with work space
\mathcal{O}(\ell\log n)
, and the catalytic tape is always restored when the algorithm halts. Thus the above gives the required
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
algorithm that finds a matrix
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\operatorname{rank}A\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Corollary 6.3.1
.

Given an instance of Linear Matroid Matching
M
, we have a
{\mathsf{CL}}\mathsf{P}
algorithm that approximates the size of a maximum independent matching of
M
upto a factor of
(1-\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Let
V
be the ground set of the matroid and
T
be the indeterminate Tutte matrix corresponding to the underlying graph on
V
given by the instance
M
. Let
A
be the linear representation matrix of
M
. Then
[
20
]
shows that the size of the maximum independent matching in
M
is exactly
1/2
times
\operatorname{rank}(ATA^{T})
. Therefore,
Theorem

6.3
gives the desired result.
∎
