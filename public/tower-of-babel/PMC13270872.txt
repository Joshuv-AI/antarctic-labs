Shikonin suppresses diffuse large B-cell lymphoma progression by inducing ferritinophagy and ferroptosis via lncRNA ADPGK-AS1 downregulation
Authors: Can Chen, Xiaofei Chen, Xilian Huang, Yaping Xie, Pengfei Shi, Shenxian Qian, Zhenzhen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShikonin, a natural compound, exhibits antitumor effects in DLBCL, but its mechanism remains unclear. Shikonin’s cytotoxicity in DLBCL cells was assessed by MTT assays. Ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation markers were analysed via flow cytometry and biochemical kits. Mechanisms were explored using gene knockdown/overexpression, dual-luciferase assays, RNA pull-down, proteomics, RIP, FISH, and ChIP. Key proteins and genes involved in ferritinophagy and ferroptosis were examined by Western blot, RT-qPCR, and immunofluorescence, with findings validated in a nude mouse xenograft model. Shikonin triggers ferritinophagy and ferroptosis by increasing iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation. LncRNA ADPGK-AS1 protects against ferroptosis by regulating MT2A via eIF4G1 interaction. Shikonin triggers ferritinophagy and ferroptosis by disrupting iron metabolism and redox balance. The eIF4G1–ADPGK-AS1/MT2A axis mitigates these effects, revealing shikonin’s antitumor mechanism and therapeutic potential.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is one of the most aggressive and prevalent subtypes of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for ∼30–40% of all cases1. It is characterised by rapid clinical progression. The standard treatment regimen R-CHOP (Rituximab, Cyclophosphamide, Hydroxydaunorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone) achieves a cure rate of 50–70%2. However, a significant proportion of patients experience relapse or develop resistance to therapy, resulting in poor prognosis3. Thus, there is an urgent need for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

In searching for novel therapeutic approaches, regulating cell death pathways has gained significant attention. One such process, ferritinophagy, is closely linked to ferroptosis. Ferritinophagy begins with the cargo receptor nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), which selectively recognises and binds ferritin, the primary intracellular iron storage protein, and delivers it to autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation. This degradation process releases a large amount of free iron ions (Fe2+) from ferritin, leading to intracellular iron accumulation. The liberated Fe2+ then generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction, which subsequently trigger robust lipid peroxidation and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)4. Under normal conditions, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and its cofactor system Xc- (composed of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2) function as key antioxidant defences that detoxify lipid peroxides and protect cellular membranes. However, when ferritinophagy-mediated iron overload produces excessive lipid peroxides that overwhelm the GPX4/SLC7A11 axis, the cellular membrane integrity is compromised, ultimately inducing ferroptosis5,6. This form of cell death differs from apoptosis, necrosis, and other programmed cell death modalities by its hallmark accumulation of lipid peroxidation products. In DLBCL, ferroptosis is tightly regulated by a complex interplay of oxidative stress, intracellular iron metabolism, and the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including GPX47. Given that NCOA4 is identified as a key mediator of ferritinophagy, its activation facilitates the release of iron from ferritin, thereby promoting ferroptosis8. Thus, targeting the ferritinophagy-ferroptosis axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for DLBCL.

Given the crucial roles of ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in DLBCL and their therapeutic potential, exploring more options is reasonable. In pursuing novel therapeutic strategies for DLBCL, natural products have garnered increasing attention for their potential to regulate cancer cell death pathways. Shikonin, a naturally occurring naphthoquinone compound extracted from the root of Lithospermum erythrorhizon9, has demonstrated potent antitumor activity across various cancer types10. The chemical structure of shikonin features a naphthoquinone core, which is essential for its biological activities. The naphthoquinone moiety can undergo redox cycling, generating ROS and contributing to its pro-oxidant and anticancer properties11. Additionally, the chiral side chain at the C-1 position of shikonin influences its interaction with cellular targets and its enantiomer Alkannin exhibits distinct bioactivity profiles12. This naphthoquinone scaffold is shared by several bioactive natural products and has been recognised as a pharmacophore for the development of novel antitumor agents. Our previous studies have indicated that shikonin exerts antitumor effects in Burkitt lymphoma by inhibiting c-Myc expression and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling axis13. Additionally, shikonin induces ferroptosis in osteosarcoma cells by modulating Nrf2 ubiquitination and suppressing the xCT/GPX4 regulatory axis, thus inhibiting cell proliferation14. Further evidence suggests that shikonin’s ability to induce ferroptosis extends across multiple cancer types, including promoting ferroptosis in multiple myeloma via GOT1-mediated ferritinophagy15.

A recent study reported that Acetylshikonin, an acetylated derivative of shikonin, suppresses DLBCL growth by targeting the TOPK signalling pathway and regulating cell cycle and apoptosis16. However, whether shikonin itself exerts anti-DLBCL activity and through what mechanism remains to be elucidated. Shikonin’s mechanism of ferritinophagy and ferroptosis induction has been explored in other cancers, but its specific role in DLBCL remains under investigation. Over the past decade, one proposed mechanism by which shikonin regulates ferroptosis in small-cell lung cancer cells involves several key steps. Firstly, shikonin increases ROS levels and enhances lipid peroxidation. Secondly, it decreases glutathione (GSH) levels, an important antioxidant that normally helps to counteract ROS. These actions result in the accumulation of peroxidized lipids within the cells. Subsequently, this accumulation triggers a process that involves the induction of ATF3-mediated ferroptosis. Ultimately, this sequence of events promotes further ROS accumulation, leading to cell death17. Additionally, shikonin induces ferroptosis in osteosarcomas by modulating the mitochondrial ROS-regulated hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α)/haem oxygenase-1 (HO-1) axis. The activation of ROS initiates the upregulation of HIF-1α, which subsequently enhances the expression of HO-1, ultimately resulting in iron accumulation and the promotion of lipid peroxidation18. Furthermore, the combination of Shikonin and cisplatin synergistically enhances the sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells to ferroptosis. This occurs through the haem oxygenase-1 (HMOX1) upregulation, which promotes Fe2+ accumulation essential for initiating and sustaining ferroptosis. Ultimately, this combinatorial approach offers a promising strategy to combat cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer by fostering an environment conducive to ferroptosis cell death19. More relevant to the present study, the core signalling cascade of ferritinophagy and ferroptosis can be delineated as follows: the cargo receptor NCOA4 selectively recognises and binds ferritin, the primary intracellular iron storage protein, and delivers it to autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation4,8. This degradation process releases a large amount of free iron ions (Fe2+), which then generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction, subsequently triggering robust lipid peroxidation4. Under normal conditions, GPX4 and its cofactor system Xc- (composed of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2) function as key antioxidant defences that detoxify lipid peroxides. However, when ferritinophagy-mediated iron overload produces excessive lipid peroxides that overwhelm the GPX4/SLC7A11 axis, cellular membrane integrity is compromised, ultimately inducing ferroptosis6,9. Based on these insights, this study aims to comprehensively explore the molecular mechanisms underlying shikonin’s effects on DLBCL cells, with a particular focus on whether and how shikonin regulates the NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy-GPX4/ferroptosis axis to hinder DLBCL progression.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The cell lines utilised in this study were OCI-LY3 (YDT-0504, ATCC: CRL-2465), U2932 (YDT-0681, ECACC: 05062001), GM12878 (YDT-0745, IMR: GM12878), and HEK-293T (YDT-0019, ATCC: CRL-3216). All cell lines were sourced from INDIT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). OCI-LY3, U2932, and GM12878 cells were cultivated in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA, 12633020). HEK-293T cells were grown in a MEM medium (BDBIO, China, C11095500BT). The media were supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, SERANA, Shanghai, China, s-FBS-x-015) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA, 15140–122). The cells were incubated under conditions of 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Cell transfection

To generate transient overexpressing cell lines of ADPGK-AS1 (NR_040107), PHACTR2-AS1 (NR_027114), CFLAR-AS1 (NR_040030), MT2A (NM_005953), and c-Myc (NM_002467) in DLBCL cell lines (i.e. previously cultured OCI-LY3, U2932, GM12878 cells), the corresponding overexpression plasmids were purchased from Youbio (Hunan, China). The human CDS sequences were synthesised, digested with restriction enzymes, and inserted into the empty vectors (pCDH and pcDNA3.1). The resulting plasmids were transfected into DLBCL cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and incubated for 24 h.

To construct ADPGK-AS1-, or eIF4G1- knockdown DLBCL cell lines, siRNAs targeting ADPGK-AS1- (siR-ADPGK-AS1), eIF4G1 (siR-eIF4G1), and a negative control siRNA (siR-NC) were purchased from Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The above siRNAs were transfected into DLBCL cells using Lipofectamine 2000 for 24 h. The sequences of these siRNAs are provided in Table 1.

To establish stable DLBCL cell lines with overexpression or knockdown of ADPGK-AS1, the reconstructed lentiviral vector plasmids containing the target gene (Lenti-ADPGK-AS1) and negative control (Lenti-control) were obtained from Youbio and underwent sequence verification. Additionally, shRNA was designed based on the siR-ADPGK-AS1 sequence (sh-ADPGK-AS1) or negative control (sh-NC). These plasmids were subsequently co-transfected with packaging plasmids into HEK-293T cells to produce lentiviral particles (Lenti-sh-ADPGK-AS1) or (Lenti-sh-NC). After 48 h of transfection, the viral supernatant was collected, filtered, and transfected into DLBCL cells at 60%-70% confluency using Lipofectamine 2000 for 24 h. After transfection, the medium was replaced, and puromycin (2.5 µg/mL, Yuanye, China, S17055) was added for 7–10 days to select positive cells. Ultimately, the transfection efficiency of the cells was confirmed by Western blot.

Methyl thiazolyl diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

DLBCL cells were plated into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well. After incubation periods of 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively, 50 µL of MTT solution (Sigma, USA, M2128) was added to each well. The cells were then incubated for an additional 3 h in the dark. After incubation, 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution (HUSHI, China, 30072418) was dispensed into each well. Absorbance values were measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA, 51119570). Shikonin (HY-N0822, 10 nM, 24 h), Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1, HY-12726), and Cisplatin (HY-17394) were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA). Erastin (S7242) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (USA).

Flow cytometry assay

DLBCL cells were collected during logarithmic growth and washed with PBS. For ROS detection, the cells were centrifuged in a serum-free medium, stained with a 1:2000 diluted ROS probe (Beyotime, China, S0033S), incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, resuspended in a serum-free medium, and analysed by flow cytometry (BD, USA, LSR Fortessa SORP). For lipid ROS detection, the Lipid ROS Assay Kit was used (abclonal, China, RM02821). The cells were resuspended in PBS, stained with 10 μM C11-BODIPY dye at 37 °C for 30 min, washed with PBS, and analysed by flow cytometry using 590 and 510 nm channels. For mitochondrial membrane potential detection, a mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit was used (Beyotime, China, C2006). The cells were resuspended in PBS, stained with 2 μM JC-1 dye at 37 °C for 30 min, washed with PBS, and analysed by flow cytometry with excitation/emission wavelengths set at 510/527 nm for green fluorescence and 585/590 nm for red fluorescence.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Fresh frozen tumour tissues (derived from xenograft tumour tissue constructed in the “Construction of animal models in vivo” section) were thawed, minced, and homogenised in lysis buffer supplemented with 1 mM protease inhibitors. Proteins were extracted from pre-treated samples on ice using RIPA lysis buffer, and their total concentration was assayed using the BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China, PC0020). Subsequently, 20 μg of each protein sample and protein marker (Thermo, USA, 26617)underwent SDS-PAGE separation and were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Thermo Fisher, USA, 88585). After blocking non-specific binding with a blocking buffer and incubating for specific binding with primary and secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualised observed using an ECL kit (Biosharp, China, BL520B). Determination of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), GSH, and iron ion/total iron levels

The DLBCL cell suspension was centrifugated at 1000 rpm for 10 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed twice with PBS, followed by another round of centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The cells were resuspended in PBS and lysed using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (JIERUIAN, China, JRA-150X). The resulting supernatant was collected for further analysis. The MDA, SOD, and GSH reagent kits were sourced from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (China). MDA content was measured using the MDA assay kit (A003-2-2), SOD content was assessed using the SOD assay kit (A001-3-1), GSH levels were determined with the GSH assay kit (A006-2-1), and iron ion/total iron levels were measured using the Iron Assay Kit (Dojindo, Japan, I291), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Results were quantified using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA; 51119570). Intracellular GSH levels were quantified using a commercial Glutathione Assay Kit (A006-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) based on the 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) method. Briefly, reduced GSH reacts with DTNB to generate yellow 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB), measurable at 412 nm absorbance. Samples were deproteinized and analysed in triplicate. GSH concentrations were calculated from a standard curve (0–20 μM) and normalised to total protein content. Total iron and ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were determined using the Iron Assay Kit (I291, Dojindo Laboratories, China), which employs a chromogenic reaction with ferrozine. For total iron, samples were pre-treated with a reducing agent to convert Fe³+ to Fe2+. Ferrozine chelates Fe2+ to form a stable magenta complex (absorbance at 562 nm). Iron concentrations were derived from a standard curve (0–100 μM) and normalised to sample weight/protein.

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

DLBCL cells were plated into glass-bottom culture dishes and allowed to adhere to the surface. Following adherence, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were blocked using 5% BSA for 2 h. The primary antibodies specific for Ferritin, heavy polypeptide 1 (FTH1; Proteintech, China, 11682-1-AP), and Lysosome-Associated Membrane Glycoprotein 1 (LAMP1; Abcam, UK, ab289548) were diluted as per the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated with the cells overnight at an appropriate temperature. Subsequently, unbound primary antibodies were removed, and the cells were incubated with a fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody (Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG, Invitrogen, A21207; Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, Cohesion Biosciences, CSA3208) at room temperature for 1 h. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Roche, USA, 10236276001) for 10 min. Finally, fluorescent signals were visualised and captured using a laser confocal scanning microscope (Zeiss, GER, LSM880).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

A Trizol reagent (Ambion, China, 15596018) was employed to isolate total RNA, followed by a separation process using chloroform and precipitation with isopropanol (Acros Organics, USA, 327270010). The precipitated RNA was then cleaned with 75% ethanol, air-dried, dissolved in RNase-free water, and subjected to quantification. For cDNA synthesis, the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TAKARA, Japan, RR037Q) was utilised, and the resultant cDNA was stored at −80 °C for future applications. RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using the TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (TAKARA, Japan, RR820S), with β-actin acting as the internal reference gene. The relative gene expression levels were determined by applying the 2−ΔΔCT method. The specific primer sequences utilised are presented in Table 3.

Nuclear-cytoplasmic separation experiment

Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation was performed using the Norgen Biotek kit (Norgen Biotek, Canada, NGB-21000). Cells were centrifuged, resuspended in 200 µL of PMSF-supplemented Cytoplasmic Reagent A (1 mM PMSF) per 20 µL pellet, vortexed, and incubated on ice for 15 min. Then, 10 µL of Cytoplasmic Reagent B was added, vortexed, and incubated on ice for 1 min. After centrifugation at 12 000× g for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected as cytoplasmic protein. The remaining supernatant was removed for nuclear protein extraction, and a PMSF-supplemented nuclear reagent was added. After vortexing and 30 min incubation on ice, the sample was centrifuged at 12 000× g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as a nuclear protein. Subsequent steps followed the qPCR protocol.

Construction of animal models in vivo

Nude mice were employed due to their T-cell deficiency, which prevents graft rejection and enables successful engraftment of human tumour xenografts for preclinical studies. 40 of female BALB/c nude mice (Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Jiaxing, China) were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and temperature of 22 °C for a 7-day acclimatisation period. The mice were assigned to eight groups: Control (n = 5), shikonin (n = 5), shikonin + Lip-1 (n = 5), Control + Lenti-control (n = 5), shikonin + Lenti-control (n = 5), shikonin + Lenti-ADPGK-AS1 (n = 5), Lenti-sh-NC (n = 5), and Lenti-sh-ADPGK-AS1 (n = 5). Xenograft tumour models were established by subcutaneously injecting with OCI-LY3 cells stably transfected with Lenti-ADPGK-AS1, Lenti-ADPGK-AS1, or Lenti-sh-NC (1 × 107/200 μL, n = 5). The treatment group of mice received intraperitoneal injections of shikonin (2 mg/kg, every other day) or Lip-1 (10 mg/kg, once daily), while the control groups were administered an equivalent volume of saline intraperitoneally, over 12 days. Tumour growth was closely monitored, and once the control group tumours reached a volume of 1500 mm³, nude mice were intraperitoneally injected with 50 mg/kg of sodium pentobarbital, and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation after anaesthesia. The skin along the edge of the tumour was cut open, and the tumour was completely dissected using ophthalmic scissors and forceps, and their volumes and weights were measured. The animal experiments were conducted by the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals and approved by the Zhejiang University Experimental Animal Centre (Approval Number: 19645).

Proteomic analysis

Proteinomic analysis was conducted on OCI-LY3 cells stably transfected with sh-ADPGK-AS1 or sh-NC control. Cell lysates were prepared using a buffer containing 8 M urea and 1% protease inhibitors, followed by sonication and centrifugation at 15 000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were precipitated with 20% Trichloroacetic acid (TCA, Aladdin, Shanghai, A291704), washed with acetone, and resuspended in TEAB buffer (MERCK, China, T7408). Overnight trypsin digestion was performed at 37 °C, followed by reduction with 5 mM DTT and alkylation with 15 mM Iodoacetamide (IAA, MERCK, China, I6125) under light-protected conditions. The processed samples were then subjected to proteomic sequencing analysis by Hangzhou COSMOSWISDOM Technology Co., Ltd.

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP)

OCI-LY3 cells transfected with either Lenti-control or Lenti-ADPGK-AS1 were harvested and lysed using a lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The eIF4G1 antibody (Proteintech, China, 15704-1-AP) or anti-IgG (Abcam, UK, ab200699) was then conjugated to Protein A Dynabeads (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA, 10001D), and the antibody-bead complexes were incubated with the cell lysate at 4 °C for 1 h to capture protein-RNA complexes. The beads were collected by centrifugation and subsequently washed several times with high salt buffer followed by PBS buffer. RNA was then extracted from the beads, and PCR analysis was performed to assess the enrichment.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH)

ADPGK-AS1-targeting probe was ordered from Hippo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells grown on glass-bottom culture dishes were fixed in 4% PFA for 7 min, washed with 1× PBS, and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 with RNase inhibitor for 10 min on ice. After washing with 1× PBS and 2× SSC buffer, hybridisation with the fluorescence probe was performed overnight at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed 4 times with 2× SSC buffer containing 50% formamide at 37 °C. DAPI was added as a nucleus counterstain. The image was acquired using laser confocal microscopy (Zeiss, GER, LSM880).

RNA pull down assay

The Ribo™ RNAmax-T7 Biotin-Labeled Transcription Kit (RIBOBIO, China, C11002-1) was employed to detect the RNA-RNA interaction between the 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) of MT2A mRNA and ADPGK-AS1 RNA. A 5′UTR-targeting probe was ordered from Hippo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo, USA, 88802) were washed and incubated with ADPGK-AS1 RNA for 1 h. After washing, the beads were resuspended in an incubation buffer and incubated with 5 pmol of 5′UTR RNA at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were washed six times and then digested with proteinase K buffer (containing RIP Wash Buffer, 1% SDS, and 1.2 µg/µL proteinase K) to release the bound RNA. RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Kit (Qiagen, GER, 217004), and cDNA was synthesised with Superscript III (Invitrogen, USA, 18080051-50Rxns). RNA-RNA interactions were quantified by qPCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

OCI-LY3 cells transfected with either Lenti-ADPGK-AS1, Lenti-control, siR-EIF4G1, or siR-NC were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured until they reached 80% confluence. Luciferase reporter plasmids containing either an empty vector (pGLO), the wild-type MT2A (MT2A-WT), or the mutant MT2A (MT2A-MUT) promoter region were purchased from Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol, the above plasmids were respectively transfected into the treated OCI-LY3 cells. After a 24 h incubation period post-transfection, OCI-LY3 cells were washed twice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any residual transfection reagents and serum components that could interfere with the luciferase assay. Subsequently, 100 µL of the Luciferase Assay Reagent (Merck, Germany, LUC1-1) was added directly to each well of a 96-well plate. The reagent was allowed to lyse the cells and initiate the luciferase reaction. To ensure complete lysis and optimal reaction conditions, the plates were gently rocked for 15 min at room temperature. After lysis, the plates were transferred to a luminometer (Promega GloMax 96) for measurement. Luminescence intensity in each well was recorded in relative light units (RLU), and the RLU values from experimental groups were normalised against those of control groups (pGLO plasmid-transfected cells).

Chromatin precipitation (ChIP) assay

Cells were lysed on ice using ChIP lysis buffer. The extracted chromatin was sonicated to 250–500 bp. Chromatin extracts were diluted with IP buffer (1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl of pH 8.1, 150 mM NaCl, supplemented with complete protease inhibitors) and incubated overnight with anti-c-Myc antibody or IgG at 4 °C. Protein A/G sepharose beads pre-coated with IP buffer and BSA were added to reduce non-specific binding. After 2 h, beads were washed, and eluted chromatin DNA was analysed by qPCR for ADPGK-AS1 gene promoter enrichment. Enrichment was normalised to input samples and expressed as fold enrichment over IgG control.

Transcriptome sequencing

The RNA’s concentration, purity, and integrity were assessed using a Nucleic Acid Protein Detector (NanoDrop, USA, ND-1000) and a Cell Analyser (Agilent, USA, Bioanalyzer 2100). mRNA was purified using oligo (dT) magnetic beads (Dynabeads Oligo (dT), Thermo Fisher, USA, 25-61005) and subsequently fragmented. cDNA was synthesised using a reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, USA, 1896649) and amplified by PCR to construct a library. Finally, the obtained samples were subjected to transcriptome sequencing at Hangzhou LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd.

Bioinformatics

RNA was extracted from OCI-LY3 or GM12878 cells, OCI-LY3 cells treated with or without shikonin, and subjected to lncRNA sequencing (n = 3). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “limma” package in R, with selection criteria of p-values < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1. Using the “pheatmap” package in R, a heatmap was generated to visualise the differential gene expression patterns in OCI-LY3 cells treated with shikonin. For OCI-LY3 cells transfected with siR-ADPGK-AS1 or siR-NC, proteomic sequencing was performed, and differential proteins (DEPs) were identified using the “limma” package in R, with the criteria of p-values < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ log2(1.2). A volcano plot was created using R’s “ggplot2” package to visualise the DEPs.

To investigate interactions between lncRNAs and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), the RNA-protein interaction prediction tool (http://pridb.gdcb.iastate.edu/RPISeq/) was employed. The results obtained were evaluated using Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers to assign prediction scores, with scores >0.5 considered indicative of positive interactions. Finally, the potential interaction between c-Myc and the lncRNA ADPGK-AS1 was predicted using the JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) database.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 6.0 (USA, SCR_002798) was used to analyse differences between the two groups using a t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated through one-way ANOVA. Data were derived from at least three independent experiments and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance level was set at 0.05; * corresponds to a p-value < 0.05; ** corresponds to a p-value < 0.01. Figures were created using Photoshop CC 2019 (Adobe, USA).

Results

Shikonin exhibits cytotoxicity against DLBCL cells by inducing ferritinophagy and ferroptosis

Based on previous studies, we identified shikonin as exhibiting antitumor activity in Burkitt lymphoma by inhibiting C-Myc and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways and acting synergically with adriamycin13. Therefore, we wonder whether shikonin has antitumor activity against another lymphoma, DLBCL. To evaluate the toxicity of shikonin to DLBCL in vitro, we treated DLBCL cell lines (OCI-LY3 and U2932) with different concentrations of shikonin (0, 1, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 60 nM) and revealed a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability, with significant toxicity observed at concentrations ≥40 nM (Figure 1(A)). For comparative purposes, parallel experiments were conducted using Erastin (ferroptosis inducer) and Cisplatin (chemotherapeutic agent). Both agents also suppressed cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 1(B,C)). Considering the currently known significant role of shikonin in ferroptosis-mediated cancer17,19, we assessed the impact of combining shikonin with Lip-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor20, in DLBCL cells. Interestingly, the results showed that Lip-1 partially rescued cell viability in both cell lines treated with shikonin, suggesting that shikonin-induced cytotoxicity involves ferroptosis mechanisms in DLBCL cells. Similarly, Lip-1 significantly reversed the cytotoxicity caused by Erastin and Cisplatin (Figure 1(D)). These results suggest that Shikonin exerts its antitumor effects primarily via ferroptosis. Expectedly, shikonin treatment significantly increased total ROS and lipid ROS levels, the markers of ferroptosis-associated oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, key markers of ferroptosis21,22, in OCI-LY3 and U2932 cells. The addition of Lip-1 significantly attenuated these effects, supporting the involvement of shikonin in ferroptosis within the context of DLBCL. Notably, Erastin or Cisplatin treatment produced a similar induction of ROS and lipid ROS that was also sensitive to Lip-1 inhibition (Figures 1(E,F) and S1(A,B)). The results demonstrate that Shikonin exhibits comparable cytotoxicity and pro-ferroptotic effects to cisplatin in DLBCL models, indicating its potential as a therapeutic agent for DLBCL. Furthermore, the administration of shikonin led to an elevation in MDA levels, an indicator of lipid peroxidation23. Concurrently, there was a notable increase in the protein expression of NCOA4, a key ferritinophagy receptor, and a significant decrease in the protein expression of FTH124. In contrast, shikonin treatment decreased cellular SOD and GSH levels, which are indicators of antioxidant capacity25. Additionally, there was a protein downregulation of GPX4 and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), which are crucial ferroptosis inhibitors26. Lip-1 co-treatment mitigated these changes (Figures 1(G,H) and S1(C,D)). More convincingly, treatment with shikonin increased iron ion/total iron levels in DLBCL cells, which Lip-1 co-treated could effectively reverse (Figures 1(I) and S1(E)). These findings confirmed that shikonin induces ferroptosis and ferritinophagy in DLBCL through intracellular iron metabolism and oxidative stress.

Subsequently, mitochondrial membrane potential was measured by flow cytometry, which is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction induced by oxidative stress during ferroptosis27. Not surprisingly, shikonin reduced this potential, shown by increased JC-1 green fluorescence in DLBCL cells after shikonin treatment, indicating dysfunction. To validate our findings in vivo, we established a xenograft tumour model of OCI-LY3 cells treated with or without shikonin. Identification of shikonin-induced downregulation of lncRNA ADPGK-AS1 in DLBCL cells

Given the existing research reports that shikonin mediates cancer progression through lncRNA28,29, we conducted transcriptome sequencing to analyse the differentially expressed lncRNA. A volcano plot was generated from the sequencing result to visualise the differential expression of lncRNAs in OCI-LY3 cells compared to control cells (GM12878 cells), revealing 452 upregulated differentially expressed lncRNAs and 424 downregulated lncRNAs (Figure 3(A)). Subsequent analysis of OCI-LY3 cells treated with or without shikonin showed 178 upregulated lncRNAs and 130 downregulated lncRNAs, indicating the potential involvement of these lncRNAs in the cellular response to the shikonin (Figure 3(B)). Eight most significantly differentially expressed lncRNAs (SOCS2-AS1, ADPGK-AS1, CSRP1-AS1, EIF1B-AS1, CXXC4-AS1, PHACTR2-AS1, BHLHE22-AS1, and CFLAR-AS1) identified from the volcano plot analysis were validated by qPCR in DLBCL cells. Notably, those lncRNAs were significantly upregulated in DLBCL cells, with ADPGK-AS1 showing the largest upregulation (Figure 3(C)). For the downregulated lncRNAs, we selected the most significantly downregulated candidates, including TRPC7-AS1, XXYLT1-AS2, NEXN-AS1, ITGB1-DT, TFAP2E-AS1, IKBKE-AS1, POLR2J4, and KDM7A-DT, and confirmed their reduced expression levels in OCI-LY3 and U2932 cells compared to GM12878 cells (Figure S2(A)). qPCR analysis revealed that ADPGK-AS1, PHACTR2-AS1, and CFLAR-AS1 were significantly upregulated in DLBCL cells, but were markedly downregulated upon shikonin treatment (Figure 3(D)). Since the downregulated lncRNAs did not exhibit an opposing effect to shikonin treatment (Figure S2(B)), we focused our subsequent mechanistic studies on the three upregulated lncRNAs. Overexpression of the three lncRNAs in OCI-LY3 and U2932 cells was achieved (Figure 3(E)), and we found that all three lncRNAs overexpression showed a partial reversal of shikonin’s inhibitory effect on cell viability, with ADPGK-AS1 exhibiting the most significant recovery (Figure 3(F)). Biochemical analysis further revealed that only ADPGK-AS1 overexpression significantly reversed shikonin’s stimulatory effect on MDA content in DLBCL cells (Figure 3(G)). These findings suggest that ADPGK-AS1 may play an important role in shikonin-mediated DLBCL progression. Therefore, we selected ADPGK‐AS1 for further characterisation.

ADPGK-AS1 negatively regulates shikonin-induced ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in DLBCL

To explore ADPGK‐AS1‐mediated regulation of DLBCL, stable knockdown of ADPGK-AS1 in OCI-LY3 cells was achieved using sh-ADPGK-AS1 constructs, with sh-ADPGK-AS1-3 selected for subsequent experiments due to its high silencing efficiency (Figure 4(A)). MTT assays demonstrated a reduction in cell viability of OCI-LY3 cells upon ADPGK-AS1 silencing (Figure 4(B)), accompanied by increased lipid ROS levels as detected by flow cytometry (Figure 4(C)). WB analysis showed decreased expression of GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1, with increased NCOA4 protein level in ADPGK-AS1 silenced OCI-LY3 cells (Figure 4(D)), suggesting the involvement of ADPGK-AS1 in regulating these proteins. Additionally, Lentivirus-encased stable knockdown and overexpression of ADPGK-AS1 in OCI-LY3 cells were confirmed by qPCR (Figure 4(E)), followed by the establishment of xenograft tumour models. Tumour volume and weight measurements revealed a significant reduction in the Lenti-sh-ADPGK-AS1 groups, indicating a stimulative role for ADPGK-AS1 in tumour growth (Figure 4(F)). Using an assay kit, we found that serum Fe2+ levels were significantly elevated in the Lenti-sh-ADPGK-AS1 group (Figure 4(G)). Consistently, WB analysis of xenograft tissues revealed decreased GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 proteins, with increased NCOA4 protein levels (Figure 4(H)), corroborating the in vitro findings and highlighting the functional significance of ADPGK-AS1 in DLBCL tumorigenesis. More importantly, tumours derived from the shikonin-treated group exhibited reduced volume and weight compared to the control group, indicating shikonin’s antitumor activity; however, tumours from the co-treatment with Lenti-ADPGK-AS1 showed a partial reversal of this effect (Figure 4(I)), suggesting that ADPGK-AS1 overexpression effectively attenuates shikonin’s antitumor effects. Lenti-ADPGK-AS1 largely reduced the Shikonin-induced increase in serum Fe2+ (Figure 4(J)). This rescue effect provides compelling functional evidence that ADPGK-AS1 is an essential mediator through which Shikonin modulates iron homeostasis to trigger ferroptosis. Notably, shikonin treatment led to significantly downregulated ferroptosis inhibitors (GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1) and upregulated the ferroptosis promoter NCOA4 in the xenograft tissues. Lenti-ADPGK-AS1 co-treatment reversed these protein expression changes (Figure 4(K)). These findings suggest a protective role for ADPGK-AS1 against shikonin-induced ferroptosis in DLBCL.

MT2A is identified as a therapeutic target for ADPGK-AS1-mediated shikonin in DLBCL cells

To investigate the potential mechanism of ADPGK-AS1 regulation in DLBCL cells, we performed a proteomic analysis to compare the differently expressed proteins between OCI-LY3 cells with ADPGK-AS1 knockdown (sh-ADPGK-AS1) and control cells (sh-NC). The volcano plot of proteomic results showed 452 upregulated and 424 downregulated proteins (Figure 5(A)). Among the various proteins identified, metallothionein 2A (MT2A) stood out due to its significant differential expression and its established function in metal binding and oxidative stress response30 (Figure 5(A)), prompting further investigation. Our data further validated proteomic findings and investigated MT2A’s functional implications in DLBCL cells. Specifically, MT2A expression levels were downregulated in DLBCL cells with ADPGK-AS1 knockdown or shikonin treatment. In contrast, its protein level was upregulated upon ADPGK-AS1 overexpression (Figure 5(B)). These results consistently support the proteomic data and suggest a positive regulatory link between ADPGK-AS1 and MT2A expression responding to the shikonin treatment in DLBCL cells. Next, we conducted a series of experiments to clarify MT2A’s role in DLBCL cells, based on its successful overexpression in OCI-LY3 cells (Figure 5(C)). Firstly, we observed that MT2A overexpression in sh-ADPGK-AS1 cells or shikonin-treated cells slightly restored their inhibitory effect on cell proliferation (Figure 5(D)), indicating its involvement in ADPGK-AS1-mediated cell survival and shikonin’s effects on DLBCL progression. Additionally, the stimulatory effect of sh-ADPGK-AS1 or shikonin on cellular MDA levels was notably reversed by the overexpression of MT2A (Figure 5(E)). Expectedly, MT2A overexpression reversed the increase in lipid ROS caused by sh-ADPGK-AS1, highlighting its role in ADPGK-AS1-regulated antioxidant defense (Figure 5(F)). It also reversed the reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential induced by sh-ADPGK-AS1 (Figure 5(G)), counteracting the rise in intracellular iron ion/total iron levels caused by sh-ADPGK-AS1 (Figure 5(H)). Finally, it reversed the expression of ferroptosis/ferritinophagy markers (GPX4, SLC7A11, NCOA4, and FTH1) modulated by sh-ADPGK-AS1, demonstrating its impact on regulating ferroptosis pathways (Figure 5(I)).

Given that the specific subcellular localisations lncRNAs are closely associated with gene regulation and cell function31, FISH and qPCR analyses further investigated and revealed predominant cytoplasmic localisation of ADPGK‐AS1 in OCI-LY3 cells transfected with ADPGK-AS1 overexpression (Figures 6(A,B)), indicating that ADPGK-AS1 regulates gene expression in a trans-acting manner at the post-transcriptional level and then affects cell function. We confirmed that ADPGK-AS1 regulates MT2A protein expression, but qPCR showed no significant change in MT2A mRNA levels with ADPGK-AS1 overexpression in OCI-LY3 cells (Figure 6(C)), further supporting post-transcriptional regulation of ADPGK-AS1 on MT2A. Non-coding RNAs have been reported to engage in direct or indirect RNA-RNA interactions to target mRNAs32–34. To identify MT2A as a potential interactor with ADPGK-AS1, we conducted an unbiased RNA-RNA interaction prediction using IntraRNA35. As expected, the 5′UTR of the MT2A transcript emerged as a target for ADPGK-AS1, which was substantiated through an in vitro RNA pull down assay between the 5′UTR of MT2A and ADPGK-AS1 (Figure 6(D)). To pinpoint the specific interaction sites, a luciferase reporter assay was designed using OCI-LY3 cells transfected with luciferase constructs containing MT2A mRNA 5′UTR (wild-type or mutant). Results showed increased luciferase activity in MT2A wild-type cells with ADPGK-AS1-overexpressing. No difference was observed with the mutant construct, confirming specific interaction at predicted sites (Figure 6(E)). Additionally, qPCR quantification of luciferase mRNA expression revealed no significant changes either in the mutant construct or wild-type cells, further emphasising the post-transcriptional regulation results (Figure 6(F)). These findings imply that ADPGK-AS1 regulates MT2A expression at the post-transcriptional level through direct interaction with its mRNA 5′UTR to mediate shikonin-regulated DLBCL progression.

eIF4G1-ADPGK-AS1 interaction regulates MT2A translational level in OCI-LY3 cells

To gain a deeper understanding of the post-transcriptional regulatory function of ADPGK-AS1, we employed the RNA-protein interaction prediction tool (http://pridb.gdcb.iastate.edu/RPISeq/) to analyse of interactions between lncRNAs and RBPs, an essential step for elucidating the mechanisms through which lncRNAs exert their regulatory effects36. In our analysis, the predicted scores obtained from the RF and SVM classifiers were 0.8 and 0.9, respectively, between ADPGK-AS1 and the eIF4G1 complex, a well-known RBP involved in translation initiation37. High predicted scores (>0.5 are considered positive) suggest a potential interaction, indicating ADPGK-AS1’s role in translation regulation. Subsequently, RIP assays using an eIF4G1-specific antibody demonstrated significant enrichment of ADPGK-AS1 in the Lenti-ADPGK-AS1 cells compared to the Lenti-control cells, confirming a direct interaction between eIF4G1 and ADPGK-AS1 (Figure 7(A)). To investigate further, WB analysis confirmed significant eIF4G1 expression reduction after eIF4G1-specific siRNA transfection, validating its silencing efficiency (Figure 7(B)). Interestingly, the knockdown of eIF4G1 in ADPGK-AS1-overexpressing cells led to a decrease in ADPGK-AS1-upregulated MT2A protein levels without altering its mRNA levels, suggesting an MT2A translational regulatory mechanism involving eIF4G1 and ADPGK-AS1 (Figures 7(C,D)). Luciferase activity was significantly enhanced in the MT2A wild-type cells with ADPGK-AS1-overexpressing compared to the control cells; however, this enhancement was effectively abrogated in the presence of eIF4G1 knockdown, further suggesting that eIF4G1 is required for the ADPGK-AS1-mediated MT2A upregulation (Figure 7(E)). RNA pull down assay was performed using biotin-labeled 5′UTR of MT2A probes, followed by PCR to detect ADPGK-AS1 enrichment and WB to confirm eIF4G1 expression enrichment. PCR analysis revealed a higher enrichment of ADPGK-AS1 in the RNA pull down fractions from cells overexpressing ADPGK-AS1 compared to controls (Figure 7(F)). Concurrently, WB analysis confirmed the presence of eIF4G1 in these RNA pull down fractions, with its higher expression in overexpressed ADPGK-AS1 cells (Figure 7(G)). These results provide compelling evidence for a direct interaction between eIF4G1 and ADPGK-AS1 in OCI-LY3 cells and demonstrate that this interaction is functionally important for regulating MT2A expression at the translational level.

c-Myc is downregulated in shikonin-treated DLBCL cells and is involved in the regulation of ADPGK-AS1

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying shikonin’s antitumor effects in DLBCL, we conducted mRNA sequencing on OCI-LY3 cells treated with and without shikonin to identify the DEGs (Figure 8(A)). Among these, the significant downregulation of c-Myc, a transcription factor that regulates gene expression38, emerged as a key finding worthy of further investigation. Subsequent PCR experiments on OCI-LY3 and U2932 cells consistently confirmed the downregulation of c-Myc mRNA in shikonin-treated cells (Figure 8(B)). The potential interaction between c-Myc and the lncRNA ADPGK-AS1 was predicted using the JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) database, suggesting a regulatory relationship (Figure S2(C)). Additional WB experiments validated the downregulation of c-Myc in shikonin-treated OCI-LY3 and U2932 cells (Figure 8(C)). Furthermore, overexpression of c-Myc in OCI-LY3 cells, confirmed by WB analysis (Figure 8(D)), revealed a positive regulatory effect on ADPGK-AS1 expression, as evidenced by the increased expression of ADPGK-AS1 in c-Myc-overexpressed OCI-LY3 cells (Figure 8(E)). More importantly, c-Myc overexpression reversed the shikonin-mediated downregulation of ADPGK-AS1 (Figure 8(F)), suggesting a complex interplay between c-Myc, shikonin, and ADPGK-AS1 expression. A ChIP experiment on OCI-LY3 cells, followed by qPCR analysis, provided direct evidence for the binding of c-Myc to the ADPGK-AS1 promoter region (Figure 8(G)). To further investigate whether the Shikonin–c-Myc–ADPGK-AS1 axis ultimately regulates the expression of MT2A, a key regulator of ferroptosis, we examined the protein level of MT2A upon c-Myc overexpression under Shikonin treatment. WB analysis revealed that Shikonin treatment significantly reduced MT2A protein expression. Importantly, overexpression of c-Myc effectively rescued the Shikonin-induced downregulation of MT2A protein (Figure 8(H)). This result, consistent with our previous finding that ADPGK-AS1 promotes the translation of MT2A mRNA, provides direct evidence that Shikonin modulates the MT2A-mediated ferroptosis pathway through the transcriptional regulation of c-Myc on ADPGK-AS1. Our findings demonstrated that shikonin treatment of OCI-LY3 cells leads to significant downregulation of c-Myc and we provided evidence for a potential regulatory interaction between c-Myc and ADPGK-AS1.

(F) qPCR assays were conducted to detect the expression levels of ADPGK-AS1 in OCI-LY3 cells with c-Myc overexpression and shikonin-treated (10 nM, 24 h). (G) ChIP assays were conducted to detect the binding sites of c-Myc and ADPGK-AS1 in OCI-LY3 cells with c-Myc overexpression. qPCR assays were subsequently employed to assess the enrichment of the ADPGK-AS1 promoter region in the ChIP products. Discussion

The present study unveils the intricate mechanisms underlying shikonin’s antitumor activity in DLBCL cells, highlighting its role in inducing ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. Additionally, we identify ADPGK-AS1 as a target lncRNA that is downregulated by shikonin and plays a protective role against shikonin-induced ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. The regulatory networks involving eIF4G1, ADPGK-AS1, and MT2A, as well as the interaction between c-Myc and ADPGK-AS1, provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying shikonin’s antitumor effects in DLBCL. Owing to the phenotypic and genetic heterogeneity observed in DLBCL, it is urgent to develop novel treatment modalities tailored to address this disease39. Shikonin, a natural compound from Chinese herbal medicine, has garnered significant interest among scientists due to its pharmacological properties10. Known for safety in traditional use, it’s appealing as a potential therapy. Recent research highlights its diverse biological activities, especially anti-tumour effects across various cancers, including ovarian cancer19, colorectal cancer40, small cell lung cancer17, pancreatic cancer41, and leukemia42,43. However, its investigation in lymphoma is relatively limited, with only a few reports44–46 and no reports in DLBCL. Importantly, our previous study has identified shikonin’s antitumor activity in Burkitt lymphoma by inhibiting C-Myc and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways and synergistic with doxorubicin13. Consistent with a recent report showing that Acetylshikonin targets the TOPK pathway in DLBCL16, our study further demonstrates that shikonin itself also exhibits anti-DLBCL activity. However, unlike the apoptotic mechanism of Acetylshikonin, we reveal that shikonin induces ferritinophagy and ferroptosis through a distinct lncRNA-mediated regulatory network. The chemical structure of shikonin features a naphthoquinone core (Figure S2(D)), which is known to undergo redox cycling and generate ROS11. This structural property aligns well with our observation that Shikonin induces lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in DLBCL cells. The naphthoquinone moiety is shared by several ferroptosis-inducing agents, suggesting that this scaffold may serve as a pharmacophore for targeting ferroptosis-related pathways. Additionally, the chiral side chain at the C-1 position may influence its interaction with key regulators such as NCOA4 and GPX4, although further structure-activity relationship studies are warranted. The present study provides comprehensive insights into shikonin’s antitumor mechanisms in DLBCL cells, focusing on the ferritinophagy and ferroptosis pathway. We demonstrated that shikonin significantly decreased cell viability in DLBCL cell lines OCI-LY3 and U2932 in a concentration-dependent manner, with notable toxicity observed at concentrations ≥40 nM. Lip-1, a known ferroptosis inhibitor, partially rescued this cytotoxicity, suggesting that shikonin-induced cell death involves ferroptosis mechanisms.

Ferroptosis, a distinctive iron-dependent mode of regulated cell death, has emerged as a novel therapeutic target in cancer treatment47. It is worth mentioning that ferritinophagy, mediated by NCOA4, selectively degrades ferritin to release iron, regulating intracellular iron metabolism48. It closely interacts with ferroptosis and their interplay is vital for iron homeostasis and cell survival, offering potential therapeutic targets for iron-related diseases24. Despite its crucial biological significance, excessive iron accumulation can become toxic due to its propensity to generate ROS and induce cell death49. This toxicity is primarily driven by small pools of iron, predominantly Fe2+, collectively known as the labile iron pool. These Fe2+ ions can directly oxidise to Fe3+ through the Fenton reaction, resulting in the generation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. These radicals are potent inducers of lipid peroxidation49, which, in turn, exacerbates cellular damage leading to ferroptosis. Therefore, heightened levels of iron, ROS, and lipid peroxidation are critical hallmarks of cellular ferroptosis. Recently, research has begun to explore the potential link between shikonin and ferroptosis in cancer cells. Preliminary studies suggest that shikonin may induce ferroptosis in cancer cells by targeting specific pathways involved in this process17–19. Herein, we observed a significant elevation in ROS and lipid ROS levels in DLBCL cells treated with shikonin. Shikonin administration increased MDA levels (an indicator of lipid peroxidation), NCOA4 protein expression (promoting ferroptosis), and iron ion/total iron levels, while decreasing FTH1 protein expression (ferritinophagy marker) and antioxidant proteins SOD and GSH levels. GPX4 and SLC7A11 protein expression (ferroptosis inhibitors) and mitochondrial membrane potential were downregulated. Furthermore, the co-localisation of FTH1 and LAMP1 was increased by treatment with shikonin in DLBCL cells, indicating ferritinophagy at lysosomes. Notably, these ferroptosis pathways were effectively reversed by administering ferroptosis inhibitors, namely Lip-1. These findings underscore that shikonin potently activates the ferroptosis machinery in DLBCL cells, revealing a promising therapeutic strategy against such phenotypes. The results were also verified by constructing the xenograft tumour models of DLBCL cells in vivo.

In addition to its direct effects on ferroptosis, shikonin may also interact with other signalling pathways to regulate cancer cell death. For example, shikonin has been shown to inhibit the Akt signalling and the Long non-coding RNA Nuclear Enriched Abundant Transcript1 (NEAT1) in treating paclitaxel-resistant non-small cell lung cancer29. Recent years have witnessed increasing attention on the progress of lncRNA research in traditional Chinese medicine50–53, as they play crucial roles in tumorigenesis through post-transcriptional regulation of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes54. Shikonin has been reported to reduce tamoxifen resistance in breast cancer by modulating the lncRNA uc. 57 and to suppress lncRNA NEAT1 and Akt signalling pathways in treating paclitaxel-resistant non-small cell lung cancer28,29. However, the mysteries surrounding its interaction with lncRNAs in DLBCL remain unveiled. Beyond ferroptosis, our study also revealed shikonin’s regulation of the lncRNA ADPGK-AS1 identified as a key lncRNA downregulated in shikonin-treated DLBCL cells. Overexpression of ADPGK-AS1 mitigated shikonin’s effects on cell viability, MDA content, and ferroptosis-related protein expression, indicating a protective role against shikonin-induced ferroptosis in DLBCL. This was confirmed in vivo, where ADPGK-AS1 overexpression partially reversed the tumour-suppressing effects of shikonin-mediated ferroptosis. The expression of ADPGK-AS1 exhibits tissue and cell type specificity, and its dysregulation has been implicated in various diseases, particularly cancers. ADPGK-AS1 has been reported to have high expression in multiple cancer types and cell lines, including lung, colon, and breast cancers55–58. Mechanistically, it functions as a cytoplasmic microRNA sponge, particularly for miR-525 and miR-3196, which subsequently inhibits tumour cell migration by modulating the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. However, specific studies on the role of ADPGK-AS1 in diseases are relatively scarce. Importantly, our data provides the first evidence that ADPGK‐AS1 is involved in ferroptosis-regulated DLBCL tumorigenesis and is linked with the treatment efficacy of shikonin.

Interestingly, our findings also revealed a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism between ADPGK-AS1 and MT2A, an oncogene that plays an important role in metal homeostasis, heavy metal toxicity, DNA damage, and oxidative stress protection59. The altered expression of MT2A genes has been observed across multiple cancer types, exhibiting a correlation with tumorigenesis, metastasis, drug resistance, and an unfavourable prognosis60,61. Here, we further discovered that ADPGK-AS1 was predominantly localised in the cytoplasm, and it regulated MT2A protein expression without altering its mRNA levels and protein stability to participate in shikonin-induced ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in DLBCL cells. This regulation was mediated through direct interaction between ADPGK-AS1 and the 5′ UTR of MT2A mRNA. Further analysis identified eIF4G1, a component of the eIF4G1 complex involved in translation initiation62, as a key interactor with ADPGK-AS1. Moreover, our mRNA sequencing data revealed significant downregulation of c-Myc in shikonin-treated DLBCL cells. Bioinformatics analysis predicted and experimentally validated a regulatory interaction between c-Myc and ADPGK-AS1. Overexpression of c-Myc reversed the shikonin-mediated downregulation of ADPGK-AS1, suggesting a complex interplay between these molecules. This finding adds to the growing body of evidence implicating c-Myc in cancer progression and lncRNA regulation. Despite these promising findings, our study has limitations. For instance, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the interaction between shikonin, c-Myc, and ADPGK-AS1 remain incompletely understood. Further studies are needed to elucidate these interactions and their functional consequences in DLBCL progression. Additionally, while our in vivo studies using xenograft tumour models demonstrate the antitumor activity of shikonin and the protective role of ADPGK-AS1, clinical validation in patients with DLBCL is required before these findings can be translated into therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that shikonin induces ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in DLBCL cells by regulating intracellular iron metabolism and oxidative stress. Additionally, we identify ADPGK-AS1 as a target lncRNA that is downregulated by shikonin and plays a protective role against shikonin-induced ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. The regulatory network involving eIF4G1, ADPGK-AS1, and MT2A, as well as the interaction between c-Myc and ADPGK-AS1, provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying shikonin’s antitumor effects in DLBCL. These findings enhance our understanding of DLBCL pathogenesis and suggest potential therapeutic targets for treating this aggressive lymphoma.
