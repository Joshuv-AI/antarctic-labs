Demand Response Under Stochastic, Price-Dependent User Behavior
Guido Cavraro, Andrey Bernstein, Emiliano Dall’Anese

Abstract
This paper focuses on price-based residential demand response (DR) implemented through dynamic adjustments of electricity prices during DR events. It extends existing DR models to a stochastic framework in which customer response is represented by price-dependent random variables, leveraging models and tools from the theory of stochastic optimization with decision-dependent distributions. The inherent epistemic uncertainty in the customers’ responses renders open-loop, model-based DR strategies impractical. To address this challenge, the paper proposes to employ stochastic, feedback-based pricing strategies to compensate for estimation errors and uncertainty in customer response. The paper then establishes theoretical results demonstrating the stability and near-optimality of the proposed approach and validates its effectiveness through numerical simulations.



I
Introduction

With the growing integration of diverse energy resources across the power grid and the rapid electrification of multiple sectors, maintaining a reliable electricity supply has become increasingly challenging for utilities
[
1
]
. Residential demand response (DR) has long been a key instrument for system operators (SOs) to manage extreme operating conditions, including peak load events and voltage violations
[
11
]
.

DR strategies vary in both temporal resolution and control mechanisms, and are generally categorized into
direct
and
indirect
load control. In direct load control, SOs assume full authority over consumer loads during specified periods, modifying consumption patterns in real time to address system-level contingencies. In contrast, indirect load control leverages economic incentives—either through dynamic pricing or predefined monetary rewards—to encourage consumers to voluntarily adjust their electricity usage. See, e.g.,
[
26
]
, for a comprehensive survey.

Despite their potential, the real-world effectiveness of DR programs remains limited. Direct control schemes often face low participation rates, as they may compromise user comfort or convenience, requiring customers to accept reduced autonomy over their energy use
[
27
]
. Indirect control strategies, on the other hand, rely heavily on accurate modeling of consumer behavior in response to pricing or incentives. Inadequately designed price signals can inadvertently cause adverse effects, such as load synchronization or system instability, undermining the reliability they are intended to support
[
20
]
.

This paper focuses on indirect load control, precisely on
real-time price-based
DR. Specifically, the SO dynamically modifies electricity prices during DR events. The classical approach to modeling user response assumes a deterministic relationship between price and consumption, often grounded in the assumption of rational user behavior (e.g.,
[
6
,
21
]
). However, such assumptions are overly simplistic and fail to capture the inherent variability and uncertainty in human decision-making.

A common extension of deterministic models introduces stochasticity into the user response—typically by adding fixed noise terms
[
25
]
. Yet, this approach does not account for the fact that consumer responsiveness is inherently price-dependent: as prices increase, a stronger reduction in demand is generally expected. To address this limitation, we propose a novel framework that models user behavior using price-dependent random variables, drawing on tools from the theory of stochastic optimization with decision-dependent distributions; see the models and algorithms in
[
24
,
9
,
17
,
19
,
29
,
30
]
, the more recent works in
[
31
,
13
]
, and the survey
[
12
]
.

One of the key challenges in implementing indirect, price-based DR strategies lies in the epistemic uncertainty of user response. Specifically, accurately estimating residential price elasticity is difficult because behavioral responses are small, confounded by many factors, and the data (price variation and the corresponding consumption change) are limited
[
2
,
25
]
. Moreover, factors such as number of household members, income, and overall financial situation affect the sensitivity to prices
[
23
]
. Finally, different studies provide different sensitivity results
[
4
]
. This inherent epistemic uncertainty makes traditional open-loop, model-based approaches infeasible in practice. Therefore, this paper advocates a
stochastic
feedback-based
pricing strategies to compensate for the uncertainty in user response and ensure robust performance.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
, we present the power grid and DR models. In Section
III
, we formulate the corresponding stochastic optimization problem. In Section
IV
, we propose our feedback-based DR strategy and
establish theoretical results demonstrating its stability and near-optimality. In Section
V
, we validate its practical effectiveness through numerical simulations. Finally, we conclude in Section
VI
.

II
Model and Demand Response Events

The paper focuses on aggregations of electricity customers participating in DR programs. While the proposed methods are applicable across different geographical scales (e.g., one or multiple distribution circuits), for clarity and concreteness we develop the technical narrative around a specific case involving a feeder. Consider then a distribution feeder with
N+1
nodes, collected in the set
\mathcal{N}=\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. The substation is labeled as
0
.
Each of the nodes
n=1,\ldots,N
serves a group of electricity users located in close geographical proximity, such as those within a specific neighborhood (and served by the same distribution transformer or connected to the same lateral). The aggregate load at node
n
is denoted as
d_{n}
; on the other hand, distributed generation, assume to be uncontrollable, at the same node is denoted as
r_{n}
. Users are billed for their electricity consumption under a net energy metering (NEM) tariff structure
[
6
,
3
]
. Accordingly, we model the total payment collected from all users served at node
n
as

\gamma_{n}(d_{n})\;=\;\pi\,(d_{n}-r_{n})\;+\;\omega_{n},

(1)

where
\pi>0
denotes the retail electricity rate and
\omega_{n}
represents non-volumetric surcharges, such as fixed connection or service fees. Here, we emphasize the dependence of
\gamma_{n}(d_{n})
on
d_{n}
, since we will decouple
d_{n}
into non-dispatchable and flexible loads.

The utility may initiate a
demand response event
(DRE), during which the electricity price is adjusted at each node for users enrolled in the program. Before the DRE, the demand
d_{n}^{\text{pre}}
(where the superscript
pre
indicates that we are considering
d_{n}
prior to the DRE) is partitioned into an inflexible demand
\hat{d}_{n}
(e.g., loads from users that will not be adjusted during the DRE, such as refrigerators, etc.) and a pre-event flexible load, whose aggregate consumption is denoted by
\delta_{n}
(e.g., dispatchable loads such as washing machines, dryers, EV chargers, or HVAC systems). Overall, before the event,

d_{n}^{\text{pre}}=\hat{d}_{n}+\delta_{n}-r_{n}.

Turning now to the DRE, let
x_{n}
denote the price adjustment at node
n
introduced to induce users to lower their power consumption. Accordingly, the cost incurred by a user at node
n
participating in the DR program is given by

\displaystyle c_{n}(w_{n})

\displaystyle=(\pi+x_{n})\big(d_{n}-r_{n}\big)+\omega

\displaystyle=(\pi+x_{n})\big(\hat{d}_{n}+w_{n}-r_{n}\big)+\omega,

where
d_{n}
has been expressed as

d_{n}=\hat{d}_{n}+w_{n},

(2)

with
w_{n}\in[0,\delta_{n}]
representing the deviation from the baseline
\hat{d}_{n}
implemented by users
in response to the price adjustment
.

A key question pertains to how to model
w_{n}
. Despite being the classic approach in the literature, modeling the load response to price changes deterministically—for example, by relying on user rationality
[
6
]
—is an oversimplification. In practice, the responsiveness of users to price signals may be influenced by external factors such as weather conditions, occupancy patterns, appliance availability, or individual behavioral preferences. Therefore, the approach proposed in this paper is to model
w_{n}
as a random variable whose distribution depends on the price signal
x_{n}
. That is,

w_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}(x_{n}),

where
\mathcal{D}_{n}(x_{n})
is a distribution inducing map,
and
w_{n}
is supported on a complete and separable metric space, with support
[0,\delta_{n}]
. This formulation naturally fits within the framework of stochastic optimization with decision-dependent distributions
[
9
,
19
,
29
,
30
]
, and it effectively models a stochastic, uncertain response of the users to a price
x_{n}
.
To ground our model, similarly to
[
29
,
30
]
, we consider the set of Radon probability measures on a complete and separable metric space
M
with finite first moment, denoted as
\mathcal{P}(M)
; when computing expected values, we utilize the probability
measures
m_{(x_{n})}\in\mathcal{P}(M)
that is given as the output of the distributional map
\mathcal{D}_{n}(x_{n})
for each
x_{n}
; for example,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{w_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x}_{n})}[w_{n}]=\int_{M}w~m_{(x_{n})}(dw)
.

Let

\mu_{n}(x_{n}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{w_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}(x_{n})}[w_{n}].

denote the mean of
w_{n}
. In this paper, we assume a
linearized
model for
\mu_{n}(x_{n})
as follows.

Assumption 1

For small enough
x_{n}
,
\mu_{n}(x_{n})
is a linearly decreasing function of the energy price change:

\mu_{n}(x_{n})=\delta_{n}-\beta_{n}x_{n},

(3)

where
\beta_{n}>0
is the price change sensitivity of bus
n
.
\Box

Collect all the quantities discussed above in the vectors
\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}},\hat{\mathbf{d}},\boldsymbol{\delta},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
A couple of remarks are presented next.

Remark 1

We note that Assumption
1
is satisfied, for example, if the distributional map
\mathcal{D}_{n}(x_{n})
induces
location-scale family

[
28
]
:

\displaystyle w_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\delta_{n}-\beta_{n}x_{n}+\varrho_{n}

(4)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
means “in distribution,”
\beta_{n}>0
models an (unknown) parameter, and
\varrho_{n}
is some stationary zero-mean random variable; see also
[
19
,
30
]
. This is a model that captures the sensitivity to small price variations of customers at node
n
via the distributional shift
\beta_{n}x_{n}
, plus an additional element of randomness via
\varrho_{n}
to model external factors. The supports of both
\varrho_{n}
and
w_{n}
are assumed compact, modeling limited total load flexibility. However, Assumption
1
is more general, and captures also other (possibly non-linear) distribution models, wherein the mean is approximately linear for small
x_{n}
.
\Box

Remark 2

Assumption
1
is justified as
x_{n}
is the deviation from the nominal electricity price
\pi
, and it would be small to satisfy price regulation requirements; c.f. the set
\mathcal{X}_{n}
in Section
III
. Moreover, implicitly, we assume that
x_{n}\leq\delta_{n}/\beta_{n}
so that
\mu_{n}(x_{n})\geq 0
. In general, linear models have been often used in the literature to capture price elasticities, e.g., see
[
4
,
18
,
16
,
10
]
when load adjustment to price changes is fast. Notably, linear elasticities arise when the interactions between the price makers and energy users is modeled as a Stackelberg game in which user utility of consumption functions are quadratic and the energy prices are linear
[
6
,
10
]
.
\Box

III
A Demand Response Problem Formulation

In this section, we formulate a stochastic optimization problem whose solution yields the optimal energy price adjustment to be implemented by the utility. Without loss of generality, we consider the case in which the pre-event power flow through the substation exceeds the desired upper bound. Therefore, during the DRE, the system operator aims to lower the total power demand across the feeder to match a reference
p_{\text{ref}}
.

Let
p_{0}(\mathbf{w})
denote the total power across the feeder to be controlled by the utility company. This can be, for example, either the power flowing through the distribution substation or the aggregation of the powers at the customers’ meters. Either way, we use a model of the form:

p_{0}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{s}^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf{d}}+\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{r}),

with
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In particular, if
p_{0}(\mathbf{w})
represents the power flowing through the distribution substation, then
\mathbf{s}^{\top}
are the coefficients of a linear approximation of the AC power flow equations
[
7
]
; on the other hand, if one measures the power at the distribution transformers or directly at the customers’ meters, then the expression for the total power of interest can be readily written with
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
. The goal is to steer
p_{0}
below a desired upper bound
p_{\text{ref}}
. Equivalently, by introducing the target variable

\xi(\mathbf{w})=p_{0}(\mathbf{w})-p_{\text{ref}},

(5)

we aim to enforce
\xi(\mathbf{w})\leq 0
. Before proceeding, a couple of remarks are in order.

Remark 3

When
p_{0}(\mathbf{w})
represents the power at the substation, we utilize a linearized model to design the pricing strategy presented in the ensuing section. However, the deployed algorithm will rely on actual measurements of the power at the substation, thereby capturing the nonlinearities of the power flows.
\Box

Remark 4

While we consider DREs with a demand reduction, the case in which the pre-event power flow through the substation is below a minimum desired value
p_{\text{ref}}
can be treated analogously. This situation may arise, for example, when there is excess generation in the distribution network.
\Box

During the DRE, we propose to minimize a cost given by the weighted sum of two components.
The first represents the cost incurred by the utility during the DRE, and corresponds to the difference between the generation cost and the total payments collected from users, defined as
c_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}):=\pi_{0}p_{0}(\mathbf{w})-\sum_{n}c_{n}(w_{n})
. Then,
c_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
can be further written as:

\displaystyle c_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(\pi_{0}s_{n}-\pi-x_{n})(\hat{d}_{n}+w_{n}-r_{n})-\omega

\displaystyle=(\pi_{0}\mathbf{s}-\pi\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf{d}}+\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{r})-N\omega

(6)

where
\pi_{0}
is the energy cost for the utility.

The second component penalizes the squared norm of
\mathbf{x}
, reflecting the objective of keeping the energy price as close as possible to the pre-event price
\pi
. In addition, the price adjustment at each node,
x_{n}
, is constrained to lie within a predefined interval
\mathcal{X}_{n}=[x_{\min},x_{\max}]
. This constraint protects users from excessive bill fluctuations and captures regulatory limits on price variations. The optimization problem posed to design the price adjustments
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}^{\top}]
results in:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\;\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}[c_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})]+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}

(7a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}[\xi(\mathbf{w})]\leq 0.

(7b)

where
\mathcal{X}=\times_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{X}_{n}
, and where
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})
is a short-hand notation for
w_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{n})
,
\forall\,n=1,\ldots,N
.

Using Assumption
1
, and noticing that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}d(\mathbf{w})=\hat{\mathbf{d}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}
and

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}\xi(\mathbf{w})=-\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{p}_{0}

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}c_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{B}(\pi\mathbf{1}-\pi_{0}\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}\big)+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(\pi_{0}\mathbf{s}^{\top}-\pi\mathbf{1}^{\top})\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}-N\omega

(8b)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{N}]^{\top}
and
\mathbf{B}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and with
\tilde{p}_{0}=\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}-p_{\text{ref}}
for brevity, problem (
7
) can be re-written as the following convex linearly constrained quadratic program (LCQC):

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\;\mathbf{x}^{\top}\Big(\mathbf{B}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{B}(\pi\mathbf{1}-\pi_{0}\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}\big)

(9a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{p}_{0}\leq 0.

(9b)

Unfortunately, solving (
9
) is often impractical – if not infeasible – for the system operator for two main reasons: (i) the price sensitivities
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are unknown and difficult to estimate accurately
[
17
,
5
,
31
]
; and (ii) instead of having full knowledge of all loads (both flexible and inflexible) at the customer side in real time, the system operator typically only has access to real-time measurements of
p_{0}(\mathbf{w}(t))
. These challenges are particularly critical for enforcing the constraint (
7b
), since inaccurate estimates of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
may lead to violations, and achieving real-time full load observability would require pervasive metering infrastructure.

IV
Online Feedback Optimization Algorithm

In this section, we introduce a feedback-based stochastic algorithm to steer the price toward an approximated solution of (
7
). Our proposed approach combines tools from performative optimization
[
12
,
30
]
, regularized Lagrangian methods
[
15
]
, and feedback-based primal-dual algorithms
[
8
]
.

IV-A

Online stochastic algorithm

To begin, the Lagrangian function associated with (
7
) is:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}\Big[c_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})\Big]+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}+\lambda\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}\Big[\xi(\mathbf{w})\Big]

with
\lambda\geq 0
the dual variable associated with (
7b
). Under the current setup and assumptions,
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
is strongly convex in
\mathbf{x}
for any
\lambda\geq 0
, and concave in
\lambda
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
(in fact, linear). A common approach to ensure linear convergence of primal-dual methods is to leverage a regularized Lagrangian
[
15
]
:

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)-\frac{\eta}{2}\lambda^{2}

(10)

with
\eta>0
a regularization coefficient. Hereafter, the
unique
saddle point of (
10
) is referred to as
optimal point
of the regularized Lagrangian, is denoted as
\mathbf{z}^{\star}:=[(\mathbf{x}^{\star})^{\top},\lambda^{\star}]^{\top}
, and it is defined as

\mathbf{x}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal{Y}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\quad\lambda^{\star}=\arg\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x},\lambda).

where
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a finite interval that contains
\lambda^{\star}
(built as discussed in, e.g.,
[
15
]
).
It is known that
\mathbf{z}^{\star}
deviates from the saddle points of the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
when the constraint is active at optimality; fortunately, a bound on the distance between
\mathbf{z}^{\star}
and saddle points of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
as a function of
\eta
is provided in
[
15
, Prop. 3.1]
. A standard primal-dual methods applied to (
10
) then amounts to the following iterative steps (where
t\in\mathbb{N}
is the iteration index):

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\big[\mathbf{x}(t)-\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}(t),\lambda(t))\big]_{\mathcal{X}}

(11a)

\displaystyle\lambda(t+1)

\displaystyle=\big[(1+\epsilon\eta)\lambda(t)+\epsilon\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}[\xi(\mathbf{w})]\big]_{\mathcal{Y}}

(11b)

where
\epsilon>0
is a step size and

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\!=\!2\Big(\mathbf{B}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{B}(\pi\mathbf{1}-\pi_{0}\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}.\hskip-2.84544pt

(12)

We will refer to (
11
) as
Performative Optimum
(PO)
algorithm, borrowing the terminology from
[
17
,
30
]
. As discussed in Section
II
, it is infeasible to perform the updates (
11
) in practice without pervasive metering. The proposed strategy is then to modify (
11
) by: (i) leveraging estimates

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}

[
17
,
5
,
31
]
in (
11a
), and leveraging a single-measurement stochastic approximation of
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x})}[\xi(\mathbf{w})]
in (
11b
). Accordingly, the proposed online algorithm amounts to the following iterations:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)=\big[\mathbf{x}(t)-\epsilon\,\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}(t),\lambda(t))\big]_{\mathcal{X}}

(13a)

\displaystyle\lambda(t+1)=\big[(1+\epsilon\eta)\lambda(t)+\epsilon(p_{0}(t)-p_{\text{ref}})\big]_{\mathcal{Y}}

(13b)

where
p_{0}(t)
is a
measurement
of
p_{0}(\mathbf{w}(t))
at time
t
(and, thus,
p_{0}(t)-p_{\text{ref}}
is a measurement of the regulation error), and where

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\lambda):=2\Big(\hat{\mathbf{B}}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{x}+\hat{\mathbf{B}}(\pi\mathbf{1}-\pi_{0}\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}-\lambda\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,

(14)

where
\hat{\mathbf{B}}=\text{diag}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
We will refer to (
13
) as the
Stochastic Inexact Performative Optimum
(Stochastic InPO) algorithm, which is summarized as Algorithm
1
and illustrated in Figure
1
.

. This is a scenario where

IV-B

Discussion

In this section, we discuss limitations of alternative approaches for the design of pricing strategies.

Consider again the PO algorithm (
11
), which is a standard primal-dual strategy for identifying the saddle point of (
10
). By using the expectations (
8
), the PO is rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\big[\big((1-\epsilon\kappa)\mathbf{I}-2\epsilon\mathbf{B}\big)\mathbf{x}(t)+\epsilon\boldsymbol{\beta}\lambda(t)+

\displaystyle\qquad-\epsilon\mathbf{B}(\pi\mathbf{1}-\pi_{0}\mathbf{s})+\epsilon\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}})\big]_{\mathcal{X}}

(15a)

\displaystyle\lambda(t+1)

\displaystyle=\Big[(1-\epsilon\eta)\lambda(t)-\epsilon\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}(t)+\epsilon\tilde{p}_{0}\Big]_{\mathcal{Y}}

(15b)

where
t
is again the iteration index. If the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are available, the system operator could run (
15
) offline to convergence without the need of feedback from the system to calculate the prices. However, the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are not usually known. Therefore, instead of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, the system operator may rely on an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and use it to run the following inexact version of (
15
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\Big[\big((1-\epsilon\kappa)\mathbf{I}-2\epsilon\hat{\mathbf{B}}\big)\mathbf{x}(t)+

\displaystyle\qquad+\epsilon\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\lambda(t)-\epsilon(\pi-\pi_{0})\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\epsilon\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}})\big]_{\mathcal{X}}

(16a)

\displaystyle\lambda(t+1)

\displaystyle=\Big[(1-\epsilon\eta)\lambda(t)-\epsilon\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}(t)+\epsilon\tilde{p}_{0}\Big]_{\mathcal{Y}}.

(16b)

We will refer to (
16
) as the
Inexact Performative Optimum
(InPO) algorithm. Algorithm (
16
) can be run to convergence offline, too. The main limitation of (
16
) is that its performance depends on the precision of the estimated sensitivities (and we will elaborate on this point in the experiments in Section
V
).

A common approach when dealing with decision dependent probability distributions is to seek
stable points

(\mathbf{x}_{\text{eq}},\lambda_{\text{eq}})
rather than optimal points
[
30
,
24
]
. Stable points (also termed equilibrium points in some prior works) are optimal with respect to the distribution they induce; that is
[
30
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\text{eq}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{eq}})}\phi(\mathbf{x},\lambda,\mathbf{w})

The following

(PS) algorithm, developed based on

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)=\Big[\big((1-\epsilon\kappa)\mathbf{I}-\epsilon\mathbf{B}\big)\mathbf{x}(t)+\epsilon\mathbf{d}^{\text{pre}}\Big]_{\mathcal{X}}

(17a)

\displaystyle\lambda(t+1)=\Big[(1-\epsilon\eta)\lambda(t)-\epsilon\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}(t)+\epsilon\tilde{p}_{0}\Big]_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Algorithm (

The three algorithms presented in this subsections will be used as a comparison with the proposed Stochastic InPO in Section

IV-C

Convergence

In this section, we analyze the convergence of the online stochastic algorithm (
13
). We start with the static setup, where the non-dispatchable loads are fixed during the DRE.

To state the main convergence result, we set
\mathbf{z}:=[\mathbf{x}^{\top},\lambda,]^{\top}
and define
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Also, define the following map:

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{z}):=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}(t),\lambda(t))\\
-\nabla_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}(t),\lambda(t))\end{bmatrix}

(18)

Using (
18
), the algorithm (
15
) for finding the PO can be re-written as
\mathbf{z}(t+1)=\left[\mathbf{z}(t)-\epsilon G(\mathbf{z}(t))\right]_{\mathcal{Z}}
. The map
\mathbf{z}\mapsto G(\mathbf{z})
is
\nu
-strongly monotone and
L
-Lipchitz over
\mathcal{Z}
, where
\nu=\min\{\kappa+2\min{\beta_{n}},\eta\}
and where
L
can be computed as in
[
15
, Lemma 3.4.]
. Note that
\mathbf{z}^{\star}
, by definition, satisfies the fixed-point equation
\mathbf{z}^{\star}=\text{proj}_{\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\mathbf{z}^{\star}-\epsilon G(\mathbf{z}^{\star})\right\}
. Regarding the stochastic iteration (
13
), we have the following assumption.

Assumption 2

There exists
\bar{e}_{\xi}<+\infty
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x}(t))}\Big[\Big|\xi(\mathbf{w}(t))-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x}(t))}[\xi(\mathbf{w})]\Big|\Big]\leq\bar{e}_{\xi}

for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
\Box

The assumption implies that the error in incurred by approximating the expected value of
\xi(\mathbf{w})
(which is computable if the distribution is known and all the loads can be measured) with the single-observation estimate
\xi(\mathbf{w}(t))
is bounded. Then, we have the following result.

Proposition 1
(Convergence with static nonflexible loads)

Consider the stochastic algorithm (
13
), and let
\mathbf{z}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
, be the sequence of prices and dual variables generated by (
13
), starting from
\mathbf{z}(0)\in\mathcal{Z}
. Let Assumption
2
hold. Let
\epsilon
be such that
\epsilon<2\nu/L^{2}
.
Then, the error
e(t):=\|\mathbf{z}(t)-\mathbf{z}^{\star}\|
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[e(t)]\leq

\displaystyle~c(\epsilon)^{t}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[e(0)]+\frac{\epsilon(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|B+\bar{e}_{\xi})}{1-c(\epsilon)},\,\,t\in\mathbb{N}

(19)

where
c(\epsilon):=(1-2\epsilon\nu+\epsilon^{2}L^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}<1
and

.
\Box

The proof Proposition
1
can be found in the appendix. As expected,
e(t)
exhibits, on average, a decaying trend up to an error floor that depends on the mismatch between the true sensitivities
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and their estimates, as well as on the stochastic error arising from approximating the expectation of the power
p_{0}
with a single measurement.

We now turn the attention to a dynamic case, where
\hat{d}_{n}(t)
may now be time-varying to reflect the case where customers switch on and off non-dispatchable loads during the DRE event. In this case, (

Proposition 2

Consider the stochastic algorithm (

\mathbf{z}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
, be the sequence of prices and dual variables generated by (

Again, the operational cost resulting from the PS is the lowest, as it fails to regulate

Unlike in the static case, here the InPO over-satisfies the power constraint, yielding

The numerical simulations show the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed real-time pricing strategy. The Stochastic InPO consistently meets the exchanged power constraints under uncertain user sensitivities and varying non-flexible loads. Enforcing network-level constraints without full model knowledge highlights the potential of feedback-based pricing mechanisms to enhance robustness in DR mechanisms.

Further, the comparison among the different algorithms provides key insights into the trade-offs between model-based and feedback-based coordination. While the PO achieves near-optimal performance when perfect information is available, its practicality is limited in realistic scenarios where such information is hard to obtain. In contrast, the Stochastic InPO offers an alternative that can adapt to changing conditions and uncertainty in user behavior.

VI
Conclusion

This paper proposed a real-time price-based DR mechanism for regulating power exchanges in distribution networks under uncertain consumer behaviors. We proposed a solution that integrates real-time feedback from the grid to ensure convergence and constraint satisfaction even when user sensitivity estimates affected by errors.
We demonstrated the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method through detailed case studies on the IEEE 37-bus feeder and comparison with model-based open-loop alternatives.
The results suggest that integrating feedback control and stochastic optimization represents a promising pathway for implementing responsive, grid-interactive energy systems.
