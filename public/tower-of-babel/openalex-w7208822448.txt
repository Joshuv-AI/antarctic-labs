Abstract
Schizophrenia and psychotic disorders continue to challenge modern psychiatry due to their extreme clinical
heterogeneity. This integrative review proposes a dimensional interpretive matrix for understanding the “anatomy of
schizophrenic delusion,” grounded in clinical observations and corroborated by a systematic review of recent literature.
Using PRISMA methodology and the PICO strategy, we analyzed literature from the past five years on the primary and
secondary dimensionality of psychosis, the role of first-rank symptoms, the influence of substance use (cannabis and
nicotine) on schizophrenization, intersections with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and the impact of sleep
disturbances on psychotic vulnerability. The proposed model suggests that schizophrenia operates along a continuum
in which the central vulnerability resides in the weakness of the secondary ego, the psychic structure responsible for
pragmatic mediation with reality, narrative self-coherence, and resilience against affective oscillations. This fragility,
whether of neurodevelopmental origin (ASD traits) or acquired through substance use and affective instability (bipolar
disorder, BD), facilitates ego evisceration and the emergence of pathognomonic symptoms, particularly Schneider’s
first-rank symptoms. Thirty-four studies published between 2017 and 2026 were included in the qualitative synthesis.
This integrative review offers an innovative clinical hypothesis that harmonizes classical psychopathology with recent
neurobiological findings, with direct therapeutic implications centered on laboralization, sleep hygiene, family
expressed emotion reduction, and substance cessation.
Keywords: Schizophrenia; Delusion; Autism Spectrum Disorder; Cannabis; Dopamine.

1. Introduction
Historically, Kraepelin’s dichotomy between dementia praecox and manic-depressive illness has guided psychiatric
classification for over a century (Jablensky, 2021). This binary division, while useful as a starting point, generated a diagnostic
tradition that tends to treat schizophrenia as a discrete and homogeneous entity, separated by clear boundaries from other mental
disorders. However, the validity of rigid diagnostic categories has been widely questioned in recent decades in favor of
dimensional approaches, such as the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) and the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology
(HiTOP), Sieler et al. (2025), in a study published in Schizophrenia, demonstrated that a dimensional approach to psychosis,
identifying cognition, depression, and thought disorder factors in a clinical sample, captures the heterogeneity of psychotic
disorders with greater fidelity than traditional diagnostic categories (Sieler et al., 2025). This evidence reinforces what daily
clinical practice already suggests: schizophrenic delusion rarely arises in isolation, but rather as the final stage of a
schizophrenization process involving multiple interrelated risk factors accumulated over time.
Schizophrenia is a disorder with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 0.4%, typically manifesting in late adolescence
or early adulthood, and is associated with high functional disability and reduced life expectancy (Leucht et al., 2025). The first
psychotic episode is frequently preceded by a long prodromal phase, which can last several years, during which subclinical
affective, cognitive, perceptual, and identity disturbances progressively accumulate (Leucht et al., 2025). During this period,
aberrant salience, defined as the inappropriate attribution of meaning to neutral stimuli, emerges as a transdiagnostic marker of
psychotic vulnerability, present from the earliest stages of the continuum, following the sequence from anomalous selfexperiences and basic symptoms, through attenuated psychotic syndrome, and finally to full-blown psychosis (Marano et al,
2025). Marano et al. (2025) demonstrated that, in youths with an ultra-high risk (UHR) mental state, abnormal salience
processing can be identified before the first psychotic episode, configuring a priority target for early intervention.
The neurobiology underlying this process involves, in its final common pathway, mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia and
dysfunction in associative cortico-striatal circuits. Corlett & Fraser (2025), in a critical review published in the American Journal
of Psychiatry, revisited two decades of the aberrant salience hypothesis and demonstrated that the primary defect in psychosis
does not simply reside in excess dopamine in the ventral striatum, but in the failure of belief formation and updating circuits
under uncertainty, preferentially located in the head of the caudate, the associative striatum, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.
This neurobiological refinement is of paramount importance: it explains why Schneider’s first-rank symptoms (FRS), including
thought insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting, and somatic passivity, represent not merely delusions of content, but a structural

Concurrently, recent literature has consolidated the role of two environmental catalysts of enormous clinical relevance:
autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and psychoactive substance use. Individuals with ASD have a significantly increased risk of
developing psychosis, with comorbidity rates between 9% and 34%, and differentiating between autistic and psychotic symptoms
remains a major diagnostic challenge (Jutla, Foss-Feig & Veenstra-VanderWeele, 2022). Cannabis use, in turn, amplifies
vulnerability to psychosis through the activation of CB1 receptors, GABAergic disinhibition in the ventral tegmental area, and
consequent mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia (Tseng & Molla, 2025; Weinstein, 2023). Bello et al. (2026), in a prospective study
from the NAPLS-2 consortium, demonstrated that the co-use of cannabis and tobacco increases the risk of conversion to
psychosis in high-risk individuals with a hazard ratio of 2.93 (95% CI: 1.23–6.97). These data show that schizophrenization is
not a spontaneous process, but a trajectory that can be accelerated, decelerated, or even reversed depending on environmental
exposure.
The concept of the “Anatomy of Schizophrenic Delusion” proposed in this article does not refer to neuroanatomical
structures, but to the psychopathological dissection of delusion formation. It is proposed that the schizophrenic process operates
along a dimensional spectrum, ranging from primary forms, strongly anchored in neurodevelopment and genetics, to secondary
forms, driven by environmental stressors and substances upon a vulnerable baseline. The common link between these dimensions
is the depletion or failure in the consolidation of the “secondary ego”: the psychic structure responsible for pragmatic mediation
with reality, for resilience against affective oscillations, and for maintaining the individual’s narrative coherence over time. When
this secondary ego collapses, the neurobiological substrate responds with the aberrant attribution of salience that culminates in
the evisceration of ego boundaries and the emergence of first-rank symptoms. Delusion, in this sense, is not the beginning of the
story, but its final chapter. Understanding the preceding chapters is what allows for intervention before it is written.
This integrative review proposes a dimensional interpretive matrix for understanding the “anatomy of schizophrenic
delusion,” grounded in clinical observations and corroborated by a systematic review of recent literature. Using PRISMA
methodology and the PICO strategy, we analyzed literature from the past five years on the primary and secondary dimensionality
of psychosis, the role of first-rank symptoms, the influence of substance use (cannabis and nicotine) on schizophrenization,
intersections with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and the impact of sleep disturbances on psychotic vulnerability.

2. Metodology
This integrative review was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews
and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020), adapted for integrative reviews that synthesize heterogeneous evidence with the aim of
building or testing theoretical models. The integrative review design was chosen, rather than a strict systematic review, due to
the nature of the research question, which involves integrating findings from distinct study designs (observational studies, clinical
trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and neuroimaging studies) around a dimensional clinical hypothesis. This type of
review is recognized in methodological literature as the most appropriate for synthesizing knowledge in areas where the
heterogeneity of investigated phenomena precludes quantitative meta-analyses (Page et al., 2021).
2.1 Research Question and PICO Strategy
The research question guiding this review was: What evidence from recent literature supports a dimensional model of
schizophrenia centered on the failure of the secondary ego as a common mechanism for both primary and secondary forms of
schizophrenic delusion? The structured formulation followed the PICO strategy, detailed in Table 1.

Inclusion criteria: (1) original articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, and umbrella reviews; (2)

Exclusion criteria: (1) articles dealing exclusively with pharmacological treatment without addressing psychopathological or
neurobiological mechanisms; (2) isolated case reports without an associated literature review; (3) opinion pieces without
empirical basis; (4) animal studies without explicit clinical translation to humans; (5) publications without peer review.

3.1 The dimensionality of schizophrenia: primary vs. secondary
Recent literature progressively moves away from watertight diagnostic categories towards dimensional and
transdiagnostic models that more faithfully capture the clinical heterogeneity of psychotic disorders. Stein & Kircher (2025), in
a qualitative review published in the Journal of Affective Disorders that synthesized genetic, neuroimaging, and
psychopathological findings in major depression, bipolar disorder (BD), and schizophrenia, demonstrated that genetic, cognitive,
and neuroimaging overlaps among these three disorders challenge traditional categorical distinctions and support the hypothesis
of a continuum of major psychoses (Stein & Kircher, 2025). The authors argue that dimensional approaches, such as the Research
Domain Criteria (RDoC), the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP), and clinical staging, offer superior
conceptual frameworks for understanding these disorders along a spectrum, with direct implications for personalized diagnostic
and therapeutic strategies (Stein & Kircher, 2025). Sieler et al. (2025) empirically confirmed this perspective by identifying
latent dimensional factors that cross psychotic disorders, including cognition, depression/negative symptoms, and thought
disorder. The model proposed here aligns with this view, suggesting that there is no rigid categorical cutoff between primary and
secondary schizophrenia, but rather a continuum where the central vulnerability resides in the strength or weakness of the
secondary ego.
Understanding the dimensionality of schizophrenia requires a developmental perspective that considers psychosis as
the possible outcome of vulnerabilities accumulating across different stages of brain maturation. In their 2026 narrative review,
O'Brien et al. (2026) synthesized findings from genetics, developmental neuroscience, early-life adversity, and resilience
research, emphasizing that susceptibility to psychotic disorders may originate before birth and subsequently be shaped by the
interaction between biological predisposition and environmental experience (O’Brien et al., 2026). Rather than representing a
single period of vulnerability, neurodevelopment appears to involve successive windows during which environmental influences
may have particularly important consequences. The prenatal period is especially relevant for the generation and initial
organization of cortical networks, whereas adolescence is characterized by extensive remodeling of previously established neural
systems, including changes in synaptic connectivity, myelination, and circuits involved in executive functioning, reward, and
social cognition (O’Brien et al., 2026).
Within the proposed interpretive model, adolescence therefore represents a particularly relevant developmental context
in which ASD-related traits, affective instability associated with BD, and exposure to psychoactive substances may converge.
Their interaction is hypothesized to interfere with the progressive consolidation of what is here termed the secondary ego,
increasing susceptibility to maladaptive patterns of adaptation. This formulation extends the developmental framework proposed
by O'Brien et al. rather than being directly derived from their review (O’Brien et al., 2026).
In primary schizophrenia, a pre-existing neurodevelopmental vulnerability may be clinically expressed through
characteristics such as impaired social cognition, behavioral rigidity, and difficulties in interpreting emotional information.
Within the present model, these features may interact during adolescence with affective dysregulation and substance exposure,
a developmental period in which substantial biological and psychosocial changes occur. Importantly, O'Brien et al. report that
only a proportion of individuals considered at clinical high risk (CHR) for psychosis subsequently develop a threshold psychotic
disorder, with transition estimates generally ranging from 20% to 30%; moreover, some individuals experience complete
remission of psychotic symptoms (O’Brien et al., 2026). These findings support a fundamental distinction between vulnerability

The proposed model distinguishes between primary and secondary forms of schizophrenization. Individuals with fewer
baseline ASD-related traits fall into the secondary category. This model characterizes them phenomenologically as naive, jovial,
or pragmatically immature. Such individuals are conceptualized as more dependent on environmental determinants of clinical
trajectory. Conversely, insufficient occupational structuring removes this protective effect. In such cases, psychoactive substance
exposure and impulsive hedonism are hypothesized to play a more prominent role. Both factors are thought to contribute to the
progressive schizophrenization process (Murrie et al., 2020).
At both poles of the spectrum, pathogenesis converges on the failure of the “secondary ego” reserve. The secondary
ego, a central concept of the proposed interpretive matrix, can be understood as the psychic structure responsible for pragmatic
mediation with reality, for maintaining the narrative cohesion of the self, and for resilience against affective oscillations and
environmental intrusions. The weaker the secondary ego. Whether rooted in neurodevelopmental constitution (ASD), chronic
affective destabilization (BD), or toxic erosion by cannabis and nicotine, the more the individual is dominated by external stimuli
and internal oscillations, progressively losing the capacity to distinguish what is their own from what is alien, what is perception
from what is projection, and what is memory from what is delusion.

3.2 The intersection between ASD and psychosis in delusion formation
The overlap between autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and psychosis is one of the most intriguing findings in
contemporary psychiatry and, at the same time, one of the most underestimated in clinical practice. Recent meta-analyses indicate
that adults with ASD have rates of psychotic disorders up to ten times higher than the general population, with psychosis
prevalence ranging between 9% and 34% depending on the methodology used (Jutla, Foss-Feig & Veenstra-VanderWeele,

2022). Gesi et al. (2024), in a review published in the Journal of Psychiatric Research, highlight that autistic symptoms
frequently cause “diagnostic overshadowing”: the tendency to attribute all unusual behaviors to the preexisting ASD diagnosis,
neglecting the emergence of a true psychosis that requires specific treatment (Jutla, Foss-Feig & Veenstra-VanderWeele, 2022)..
This phenomenon has serious clinical consequences, as it delays the initiation of antipsychotic treatment at a phase when early
intervention is determinant for long-term prognosis.
A deeper understanding of this overlap requires a return to conceptual origins. Jeong & Son (2026), in a historical and
phenomenological review published in the Journal of the Korean Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, demonstrated
that the very concept of “autism” has dual and divergent roots in the history of psychiatry: Bleuler’s “Autismus” (1911)
designated the active withdrawal from reality and the construction of a fantasy internal world as a cardinal symptom of
schizophrenia, while Kanner’s “infantile autism” (1943) described an innate deficit in the formation of affective bonds (Jeong &
Son, 2026). This conceptual divergence, which persisted for decades, obscured the profound neurobiological and
phenomenological similarities between the two disorders. The phenomenological analysis by Jeong and Son (2026) revealed
that, while patients with schizophrenia exhibit an ipseity disturbance characterized by instability of the minimal self,
hyperreflexivity, and dissolution of ego boundaries, while patients with ASD present a preserved minimal self, but with a
profound deficit in intersubjective attunement (Jeong & Son, 2026). This distinction is clinically fundamental: in ASD, the
secondary ego is not structurally dissolved, but chronically under-exercised due to a lack of adequate social feedback, making it
vulnerable to collapse when subjected to intense affective pressure.
This neurodevelopmental perspective allows for a precise dissection of how schizophrenic delusion forms. Take the
clinical example of the “strange mother delusion.” Individuals with ASD traits have intrinsic difficulty reading emotional and
social cues, a consequence of impaired theory of mind and dysfunction in mirror neurons that support automatic affective
empathy. When placed in a high expressed emotion environment, such as a dependent relationship with an overwhelmed, critical,
or emotionally unpredictable maternal figure, the inability to decode maternal affect generates growing existential anguish. The
patient fills this cognitive gap with obsessive ideation which, combined with the poverty of pragmatic repertoire and cognitive
rigidity characteristic of ASD, crystallizes into a projective elucubration. What classical phenomenology summarily classifies as
persecutory delusion is, in fact, the collapse of social cognition under affective pressure: not a primary break with reality, but a
desperate attempt to make sense of a relational world that remains opaque.
Ribolsi et al. (2022) corroborate this observation, demonstrating that psychosis in individuals with ASD frequently
emerges in contexts of relational overload and that interventions focused on reducing family expressed emotion produce faster
symptomatic remission (Canitano & Pallagrosi, 2017). Keegan et al. (2025), in a systematic review published in Early
Intervention in Psychiatry that analyzed 15 studies with patients in first-episode psychosis, confirmed that high family expressed
emotion, characterized by critical comments, hostility, and emotional over-involvement, is consistently associated with greater
symptom severity, poorer social functioning, and shorter time to relapse (Keegan, Brown & Gayno, 2025). Notably, the authors
identified that family burden and emotional over-involvement are independent predictors of negative outcomes, even after
controlling for patient clinical variables. This finding has direct implications for the anatomy of delusion in ASD: maternal
emotional over-involvement, which frequently emerges as an adaptive response to the dependence of the child with ASD,
paradoxically creates the high expressed emotion environment that precipitates psychotic decompensation.

3.3 The catalytic role of substances: cannabis, nicotine, and amotivational syndrome
The use of psychoactive substances acts as one of the most potent vectors of schizophrenization, especially in secondary
forms. Understanding this role requires, however, moving beyond epidemiology and delving into the neurobiological
mechanisms by which cannabis and nicotine progressively compromise the integrity of the secondary ego.
3.3.1 The endocannabinoid system and the final common pathway to psychosis
The endocannabinoid system (ECS) is one of the most abundant neurotransmission systems in the central nervous
system, composed of G protein-coupled receptors, primarily cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1R) and type 2 (CB2R), and
endogenous ligands (endocannabinoids). CB1Rs are highly expressed in brain areas critical for cognition, memory, reward, and
emotional regulation, including the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, and basal ganglia.
Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main psychoactive compound in cannabis, acts as a partial agonist at CB1R. Unlike
endogenous endocannabinoids, which function as retrograde messengers released by the postsynaptic neuron to modulate
neurotransmitter release at the presynaptic terminal, THC exerts a tonic, persistent, and unregulated activation of CB1R,
subverting the physiological homeostatic mechanism. Tseng & Molla (2025), in an expert review published in Molecular
Psychiatry, demonstrated that CB1R expression peaks in early adolescence and progressively declines into young adulthood,
both in humans and animal models (Tseng & Molla, 2025). This developmental pattern explains the specific vulnerability
window of adolescence: exposure to THC during this sensitive period encounters an endocannabinoid system undergoing major
reorganization, with high CB1R density and intense synaptic plasticity, making the neurobiological consequences potentially
permanent.
The mechanism by which THC induces psychosis involves a cascade of events converging on the mesolimbic
dopaminergic pathway. At the synaptic level, CB1R is predominantly located on presynaptic terminals of GABAergic
interneurons. GABA, the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain, exerts tonic control over dopaminergic neurons in the
ventral tegmental area (VTA). When THC activates CB1Rs on these GABAergic interneurons, it suppresses GABA release,

resulting in disinhibition of VTA dopaminergic neurons. The result is an unregulated increase in dopamine release in the ventral
striatum and prefrontal cortex, mimicking the pattern of striatal hyperdopaminergia described in the dopamine hypothesis of
schizophrenia. Concurrently, CB1R activation in cortical astrocytes promotes astrocytic glutamate release, amplifying cortical
excitation and contributing to the state of aberrant salience attribution.
A high-impact neuroimaging study published in JAMA Psychiatry by Ahrens et al. (2025) provided the most direct
evidence to date that cannabis and psychosis converge on the same dopaminergic pathway (Ahrens et al., 2025). Using
neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (NM-MRI), an indirect marker of long-term dopaminergic function in the
substantia nigra/VTA (SN/VTA), the authors investigated 61 individuals with and without cannabis use disorder (CUD), with
and without a diagnosis of first-episode schizophrenia. The results were remarkable: CUD was associated with elevated NMMRI signal in a specific subregion of the ventral SN/VTA that had previously been identified as a correlate of untreated psychosis
severity. More importantly, this association followed a dose-dependent pattern: the greater the burden of CUD symptoms, the
higher the NM-MRI signal in the psychosis-related region of interest. This finding suggests that cannabis activates the same
dopaminergic pathway that constitutes the final common pathway for the clinical expression of psychotic symptoms, regardless
of whether the individual has a formal diagnosis of schizophrenia (Ahrens et al., 2025).
A SPECT neuroimaging study published in Frontiers in Psychiatry by Weinstein (2023) complemented this picture by
investigating dopamine D₂ receptor availability in the striatum of abstinent cannabis users who had recovered from cannabisinduced psychosis (Weinstein, 2023). The findings indicated that, after four months of abstinence and treatment, D₂ availability
in the caudate returned to levels comparable to those of healthy controls, suggesting that cannabis-induced dopaminergic
dysregulation is partially reversible. However, a non-significant trend of reduced D₂ availability was observed in the right
putamen, possibly related to residual effects of antipsychotic medication, indicating that complete recovery may not be universal
(Weinstein, 2023).
3.3.2 Chronic use, CB1R downregulation, and amotivational syndrome
Acute and chronic cannabis use exert opposing and complementary effects on the dopaminergic system. While acute
THC exposure produces a peak of mesolimbic dopaminergic release, chronic and repeated use leads to adaptive downregulation
of CB1Rs, particularly in the striatum and prefrontal cortex. This desensitization reduces the responsiveness of the reward system
to natural stimuli, creating a state of anhedonia and avolition that is the neurobiological signature of amotivational syndrome.
Amotivational syndrome has been historically debated, with initial studies showing conflicting results. Skumlien et al.
(2024), in a comprehensive review published in Current Behavioral Neuroscience Reports, clarified this apparent paradox by
precisely distinguishing the acute from the non-acute effects of cannabis on motivation (Skumlien, Langley & Sahakian, 2024).
The authors reviewed studies using validated effort-reward behavioral tasks, notably the Effort Expenditure for Reward Task
(EEfRT), in which participants choose between low- and high-effort tasks to obtain monetary rewards of different magnitudes.
The results were surprising: in the non-acute state (after at least 12 hours of abstinence), three independent studies found that
cannabis users chose more high-effort tasks than controls, with medium to large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.54 to ηp² = 0.104).
Two other studies found no differences. No non-acute study found lower motivation in cannabis users (Skumlien, Langley &
Sahakian, 2024).
However, the scenario changes radically during acute intoxication. Wardle et al. (cited in Skumlien et al., 2024)
demonstrated that 15 mg of ingested THC reduced the choice of high-effort tasks by 55% compared to placebo in young adult
women, with a specific effect on high expected value choices (Skumlien, Langley & Sahakian, 2024). This contrast is
fundamental for clinical understanding: the chronic cannabis user is not intrinsically amotivated at baseline, but becomes

profoundly amotivated during periods of intoxication, which in daily use correspond to most waking hours. The sum of these
repeated episodes of acute amotivation, over months and years, produces the clinical picture of amotivational syndrome.
More critically, Skumlien et al. (2024) found a consistent association between cannabis use disorder (CUD) and apathy
in self-report measures, even in the absence of differences in behavioral tasks (Skumlien, Langley & Sahakian, 2024). This
suggests that CUD produces a form of subjective and experiential apathy that is not captured by laboratory effort-reward tasks,
but which is clinically relevant and manifests in daily life as reduced initiative, persistence, and social engagement. This
distinction between subjective apathy and laboratory task performance is crucial: the secondary ego is not just a behavioral entity,
but an experiential and narrative structure that can be eroded even when performance on isolated tasks remains preserved.
A fundamental link between cannabis-induced amotivational syndrome and vulnerability to psychosis is reward
processing. Nusslock, Mittal, & Alloy (2025), in a review published in the “Annual Review of Clinical Psychology”, proposed
a neurodevelopmental framework suggesting that alterations in reward processing may arise through distinct mechanisms in
mood disorders and schizophrenia (Nusslock, Mittal, & Alloy, 2025). In bipolar disorder (BD), disturbances in reward-related
behavior are primarily associated with abnormal regulation of motivational and hedonic responses, with increased sensitivity to
rewarding cues during mania and reduced responsiveness during depressive episodes (Nusslock, Mittal, & Alloy, 2025).
Schizophrenia, in contrast, appears to involve a different pattern in which difficulties in evaluating reward information across
time and in selectively directing attention toward relevant stimuli may contribute to impaired reward processing, without
necessarily implying a primary deficit in motivation or hedonic capacity (Nusslock, Mittal, & Alloy, 2025). This distinction is
particularly relevant to the proposed interpretive matrix, as chronic cannabis exposure may alter reward-related cognition through
interactions between prefrontal CB1R signaling and striatal dopaminergic pathways, potentially generating a functional state that
overlaps with, and may exacerbate, reward-processing abnormalities observed in schizophrenia.
In primary schizophrenia, avolition and apathy are recognized as core negative symptoms and determinants of functional
outcome. Shao et al. (2024), in a study published in the European Archives of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience, developed
a novel effort-based decision-making task in social scenarios (mock job interview) and applied it to 45 patients with
schizophrenia and 49 controls (Shao et al., 2024). The results demonstrated that patients with schizophrenia were significantly
less likely to choose high-effort tasks with demanding interviewers, especially when the reward was of medium to high value.
More importantly, the severity of apathy symptoms correlated directly with the reduction in willingness to exert effort, and
patients were especially reluctant to exert effort for jobs requiring greater social engagement (Shao et al., 2024). This finding
expands the concept of amotivational syndrome beyond the monetary domain: avolition in schizophrenia is especially
pronounced in the social domain, which aligns directly with the difficulty in laboralization observed clinically and with the
tendency toward isolation and passivity.
In the context of the proposed model, amotivational syndrome represents the neurochemical and experiential erosion of
the secondary ego. The individual becomes progressively passive, with a loosening of pragmatic ties to reality, reduced social
initiative, and increasing vulnerability to affective oscillations and delusional intrusions. Laboralization, therefore, is not just a
generic psychosocial intervention: it is a strategy for restoring prefrontal dopaminergic integrity and the cognitive representation
of rewards, directly countering the state of avolition induced by chronic cannabis use and primary schizophrenic processes.
3.3.3 Nicotine, dopaminergic dysregulation, and the self-medication paradox
Nicotine occupies a peculiar place in this equation. The prevalence of smoking in patients with schizophrenia is up to
62%, three times higher than in the general population (Bello et al., 2026). This strong association led to the self-medication
hypothesis: nicotine, by activating nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), particularly α7 and α4β2 subtypes, increases

dopamine release in the striatum and prefrontal cortex, which could transiently attenuate negative symptoms and cognitive
deficits of schizophrenia.
Mu et al. (2024), in a prospective longitudinal study published in Schizophrenia (Nature), investigated the impact of
smoking on symptom improvement in 145 medication-naive male patients with first-episode schizophrenia (Mu et al., 2024).
After 12 weeks of antipsychotic monotherapy treatment, smokers demonstrated greater improvement in negative symptoms
compared to non-smokers, after controlling for confounding variables. This finding corroborates the self-medication hypothesis,
but at the same time exposes its trap: nicotine offers short-term symptomatic relief at the cost of progressive, long-term
subcortical dopaminergic dysregulation, which perpetuates and aggravates vulnerability to psychosis (Mu et al., 2024).
The mechanism by which nicotine contributes to schizophrenization is distinct from, but complementary to, that of
cannabis. While THC acts primarily via GABAergic disinhibition, nicotine acts directly on presynaptic nAChRs of VTA
dopaminergic neurons, stimulating dopamine release in the striatum. Chronic use leads to desensitization of nAChRs, creating a
state of cholinergic deficiency that paradoxically amplifies dopaminergic reactivity to external stimuli, which constitutes the
neurobiological substrate of aberrant salience attribution.
3.3.4 The synergistic effect of co-use: a devastating dopaminergic convergence
The combination of cannabis and tobacco represents a risk that exceeds the sum of its individual parts. Bello et al.
(2026), in a multisite prospective study published in Nature Mental Health, used data from the North American Prodrome
Longitudinal Study-2 (NAPLS-2), with 1,012 clinical high risk (CHR) participants and healthy controls (Bello et al., 2026). The
results were unequivocal: heavy co-use of cannabis with light tobacco was associated with a hazard ratio of 2.93 (95% CI: 1.236.97; p = 0.015) for conversion to psychosis in survival analyses, compared to non-users. Notably, co-use was not associated
with greater baseline psychiatric symptom severity, but specifically predicted conversion to psychosis, suggesting that its effect
is on disease progression rather than immediate symptom expression (Bello et al., 2026).
The synergistic mechanism can be understood on two levels. At the pharmacological level, THC and nicotine activate
distinct dopaminergic pathways which, when combined, produce mesolimbic dopaminergic stimulation of greater magnitude
than either substance alone. At the behavioral level, co-use, frequently in the form of spliffs (mixed cannabis and tobacco
cigarettes), creates a pattern of more frequent use with greater cumulative exposure to THC, amplifying both acute psychotogenic
effects and chronic adaptations of CB1R downregulation and nAChR desensitization.
In the proposed model, co-use represents the convergence of two vectors of secondary ego erosion: cannabis-induced
amotivational syndrome and subcortical dopaminergic dysregulation amplified by nicotine. The result is an accelerated collapse
of the individual’s ability to maintain pragmatic contact with reality, precipitating schizophrenization in individuals with prior
vulnerability, whether of a neurodevelopmental basis (ASD traits) or an affective one (BD).

3.4 First-rank symptoms: the evisceration of the self
In classical psychopathology, Kurt Schneider defined a set of first-rank symptoms (FRS) as pathognomonic of
schizophrenia, unless there was evidence of organic brain disease. These symptoms include audible thoughts, voices arguing or
discussing, voices commenting on one’s actions, somatic passivity, thought insertion, thought withdrawal, thought broadcasting,
and made feelings, impulses, or volitional acts.
The proposed model offers a profound reinterpretation of FRS. They are not merely exotic perceptual distortions, but
the direct clinical translation of the evisceration of the secondary ego. When the protective barrier that separates the internal
world from external reality collapses, whether due to neurodevelopmental fragility, affective instability, or toxic erosion, the
individual loses the capacity for “ipseity” (the sense of ownership of one’s own experiences). A thought is no longer experienced
as my thought, but as an insertion; an impulse is no longer my impulse, but a remote control.
This conceptualization is robustly supported by recent literature. Magrangeas et al. (2022), using natural language
processing in 9,323 clinical records, demonstrated that specific FRS have distinct prognostic values (Magrangeas et al., 2022).
Somatic passivity (OR 1.61) and thought insertion (OR 1.24) were the strongest predictors of negative outcomes. Thought
broadcasting alone was not an independent predictor, suggesting that it represents an earlier stage of ego boundary dissolution,
which only becomes prognostically ominous when it evolves into full loss of agency (insertion and passivity).
Corlett & Fraser (2025) provided the neurobiological substrate for this phenomenological progression. By locating the
primary defect of aberrant salience in the associative striatum and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), circuits responsible

(corollary discharge) translates into the experience of alien control. When the brain cannot correctly predict that a thought or
internal speech was self-generated, it attributes this event to an external agent.
López-Silva et al. (2022), in a 20-year prospective study, confirmed that thought insertion and withdrawal are strongly
associated with auditory verbal hallucinations and delusions of control, forming a cohesive cluster of “ego boundary
disturbances”. Crucially, they found that thought withdrawal is specifically associated with auditory verbal hallucinations,
suggesting that the experience of having one’s thoughts removed creates a cognitive vacuum that is filled by hallucinatory voices.
The clinical relevance of this distinction is immense. Delusions secondary to intense affective oscillations, such as the
delusion of ruin in severe depression or the delusion of grandeur in mania, generally preserve the structure of the self. The patient
believes they are guilty or omnipotent, but they are still themselves feeling and thinking. These are delusions with low specificity
for schizophrenia and generally remit with the stabilization of the underlying mood disorder. In contrast, FRS represent the
rupture of the self’s container. The presence of FRS, therefore, marks the point of no return in the schizophrenizing process: the
moment when the secondary ego has been completely eviscerated.

3.5 Sleep disturbances: the fifth axis of vulnerability
The fifth and final axis of the interpretive matrix concerns the role of sleep disturbances in the genesis and maintenance
of psychosis. Historically considered a mere epiphenomenon or secondary symptom of psychotic disorders, sleep architecture is
now recognized as a fundamental pillar of neurobiological integrity and, consequently, of the secondary ego.
Azargoonjahromi & Bahrampour (2025), in a comprehensive umbrella review published in Sleep Medicine, synthesized
data from 20 systematic reviews and meta-analyses, confirming a strong and bidirectional association between sleep disturbances
and schizophrenia. Patients with schizophrenia exhibit profound alterations in sleep architecture: reduced total sleep time,
increased sleep latency, decreased sleep efficiency, and, crucially, significant reductions in slow-wave sleep and REM sleep.

These alterations are not benign; they correlate directly with the worsening of hallucinations and delusions, cognitive impairment,
and increased suicidality (Azargoonjahromi & Bahrampour, 2025).
The impact of sleep disturbances on the clinical trajectory is devastating. Geoffroy et al. (2025), in a large-scale study
of 13,913 psychiatric inpatients published in European Psychiatry, demonstrated that 81% of patients presented with chronic
sleep disturbances (Geoffroy et al., 2025). More importantly, the presence of these disturbances was associated with a
significantly higher number of hospitalizations (1.84 vs. 1.33), greater use of seclusion (17.6% vs. 13.3%), and physical restraint
(6.6% vs. 5.3%), indicating a more severe and treatment-resistant clinical course (Geoffroy et al., 2025).
The neurobiological link between sleep and psychosis involves the thalamocortical circuits responsible for sleep
spindles. Torun Yazihan (2025), in a narrative review published in Applied Neuropsychology: Adult, demonstrated that sleep
spindle density (11-16 Hz oscillations during NREM sleep) is significantly reduced in schizophrenia, and that this reduction is
already present in the prodromal period (Torun Yazihan, 2025). Sleep spindles are essential for memory consolidation, relational
learning, and synaptic plasticity. Their deficit compromises the brain’s ability to integrate new information into coherent existing
schemas, which is precisely the cognitive failure that underlies aberrant salience and delusion formation. Without the restorative
function of sleep spindles, the secondary ego loses its capacity to maintain narrative coherence.
Furthermore, parasomnias, particularly REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), have gained prominence as potential early
markers of psychiatric vulnerability. Wang et al. (2022), in a study published in the Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery, and
Psychiatry and discussed by Wang et al. (2022) in Psychiatric Times, demonstrated that RBD, characterized by the loss of normal
muscle atonia during REM sleep and the consequent enactment of dreams, can be present in up to 7% of patients with major
depression, and that certain antidepressants can trigger iatrogenic RBD (Wang et al., 2022). In the context of psychosis, the
intrusion of dream mentation (REM sleep) into wakefulness has been proposed as a neurobiological model for hallucinations.
The fragmentation of the sleep-wake boundary parallels the fragmentation of the ego-world boundary seen in first-rank
symptoms.
In the proposed interpretive matrix, sleep disturbances act as a potent amplifier of vulnerability. Chronic insomnia and
architectural sleep deficits erode the cognitive reserve of the secondary ego, making it more susceptible to the impact of substance
use (cannabis/nicotine) and affective oscillations (BD). The clinical implication is clear: the systematic evaluation and treatment
of sleep disturbances, whether through sleep hygiene, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), or targeted
pharmacological interventions, must be considered a primary strategy for neuroprotection and stabilization of the secondary ego
in patients at risk for or suffering from psychosis.
3.6 Practical clinical implications
The dimensional model proposed here has direct and profound implications for clinical practice:
1.

Laboralization as an Existential Prosthesis: In patients with a weak secondary ego (especially those with ASD
traits or amotivational syndrome), structured work acts as an external scaffolding that organizes time, demands
pragmatic contact with reality, and provides predictable social feedback. Laboralization is not just occupational
therapy; it is an antipsychotic intervention.

2.

Targeted Substance Cessation: The co-use of cannabis and nicotine must be treated as a psychiatric emergency
in individuals at high clinical risk, as it represents the most potent vector for the rapid collapse of the secondary
ego.

3.

Management of Expressed Emotion: Particularly in patients with ASD traits, family psychoeducation to reduce
emotional over-involvement and criticism is as crucial as pharmacological treatment.

Aggressive Sleep Management: Sleep architecture must be actively protected and restored, recognizing insomnia

4. Limitations
This integrative review has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the methodological heterogeneity of
the included studies, ranging from neuroimaging studies to natural language processing of clinical records, precludes quantitative
meta-analysis, limiting the synthesis to a narrative and interpretive approach. Second, the concept of the “secondary ego,” while
phenomenologically robust and clinically useful, lacks a direct and exclusive neurobiological correlate, making its objective
measurement difficult. Third, the literature search, although comprehensive, may have missed relevant studies published in
languages other than English and Portuguese or indexed in databases not consulted. Finally, the proposed dimensional model,
while supported by recent evidence, requires prospective validation through longitudinal studies designed specifically to test its
predictive value.

5. Future Perspectives
The dimensional matrix proposed in this review opens several avenues for future research. Longitudinal studies are
needed to track the trajectory of the “secondary ego” from childhood to early adulthood, particularly in high-risk populations
(ASD, BD, heavy substance users), using validated instruments for phenomenological assessment (e.g., EASE - Examination of
Anomalous Self-Experience). Neuroimaging studies should investigate how the functional connectivity of the associative
striatum and DLPFC correlates with the severity of first-rank symptoms and the degree of amotivational syndrome. Furthermore,
randomized clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of specific interventions, such as early laboralization programs and intensive
sleep management, in preventing conversion to psychosis in UHR individuals are essential to translate this theoretical model
into concrete preventive strategies.

6. Conclusion
The anatomy of schizophrenic delusion is not a static lesion, but a dynamic process of structural collapse. This
integrative review demonstrates that the rigid categorical division between primary and secondary schizophrenia is insufficient
to capture the complexity of psychotic disorders. Instead, the evidence supports a dimensional continuum where the central
vulnerability resides in the fragility of the secondary ego, the psychic structure responsible for pragmatic mediation with reality
and narrative self-coherence.
The five axes investigated in this review converge precisely on this mechanism. The neurodevelopmental basis (ASD
traits) provides an initially fragile or under-exercised secondary ego due to deficits in intersubjective attunement. Affective
instability (BD) subjects this fragile structure to chronic stress. Psychoactive substances (cannabis and nicotine), particularly in
co-use, act as potent toxic catalysts, inducing amotivational syndrome and dopaminergic dysregulation that rapidly erode the
remaining cognitive reserve. Sleep disturbances amplify this vulnerability by compromising the synaptic plasticity and memory
consolidation essential for narrative coherence.
When the secondary ego finally collapses under the weight of these interacting factors, the neurobiological substrate,
characterized by aberrant salience and dysfunction in belief-updating circuits, translates phenomenologically into the
evisceration of the self. It is at this point of structural rupture that Schneider’s first-rank symptoms emerge, not merely as
perceptual errors, but as the pathognomonic signature of the loss of ipseity and agency.
