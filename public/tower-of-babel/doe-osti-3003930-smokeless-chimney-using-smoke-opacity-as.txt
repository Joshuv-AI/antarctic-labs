Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
If operated poorly, wood burning heaters can produce large amounts of particulate matter
(PM). Although several instruments are available to monitor PM, most require dilution air and
are unable to sample directly from a flue due to the high PM concentrations and temperatures.
Opacity sensors can measure color, opacity and smoke density from wood heater stacks. They
operate by transmitting a light beam through the emission source and measuring the amount
of light intensity lost (opacity) and light not reflected (color). Although opacity sensors are used
in several areas, none have been thoroughly evaluated or optimized for measuring smoke from
wood heaters.

The goal of this project was to optimize and validate the “Octoring,” a smoke sensing
technology that includes opacity, developed by Acumentor LLC. Over 18-months, we evaluated
the effectiveness of three Octorings when measuring PM from cordwood and pellet heaters
over a wide range of operating conditions.

Our research advances knowledge in this area by providing one of the first thorough
evaluations of an opacity sensor for direct wood smoke measurement. We identified the
conditions in which the Octoring performs well and where accuracy and repeatability must be
improved. This will help guide future sensor design and operation for residential wood heating.
Overall, the Octoring shows promise for monitoring PM from cord wood heaters and pellet
heaters. However, more R&D is needed to improve accuracy, reliability, and repeatability.
Guidelines for how the sensor measurements could be used to optimize combustion through
air-supply controls were not developed since more R&D is required to improve the
measurement technology.

Summary of Research Results:
Overview
The central hypothesis underlying this project was that an opacity sensor, optimized for wood
heater applications, could accurately measure smoke in real time and provide information that
could inform the operator and/or combustion air adjustments to reduce smoke or particulate
matter (PM). The “Octoring”, developed by Acumentor LLC, is the only opacity sensor on the
market designed for residential wood heaters (see Figure 1).

The primary objective of this project was to optimize and validate the Octoring for cordwood
and pellet heaters, compare its measurements against laboratory-grade PM instruments, and
develop operational guidelines for integrating sensor outputs into air-supply control strategies
if the sensor proved it could accurately measure high PM events.

Approach and Activities Over the Period of Performance
The 18-month project was conducted following the Statement of Work between Acumentor
LLC and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), and included a project management
task and five technical tasks, as follows:

 1. Project management and monthly meetings (Task 1)
 2. Build Octorings with Mac address matched receiver unit (Task 2)
 a. Three Octoring prototypes were assembled and shipped to LBNL with
 interchangeable light sources (UV, IR, visible spectrum wavelengths) and
 configured with replaceable modules enabling varied spectral measurements.
 b. Pre-shipment bench testing confirmed all Octorings were functional.
 3. Integrate Octoring into LBNL’s wood heater test facility (Task 3)
 a. LBNL procured the necessary components to install the Octoring at their facility.
 b. LBNL conducted preliminary and baseline tests to ensure all Octorings operated
 as expected within the LBNL facility.
 c. LBNL also integrated data streaming from the Octoring into their data collection
 software so output from the Octoring were logged simultaneously with other
 LBNL particle instruments.
 4. Compared Octoring measurements with laboratory grade PM measurements of
 cordwood heater emission (Task 4)
 a. Recorded Octoring measurements (opacity, lux, and red, green, blue, and clear
 counts) under defined burn cycles from a catalytic and non-catalytic cordwood
 heater: start-up, high-power, low-power, and reload
 b. Compared Octoring measurements to gravimetric PM, and laboratory grade
 optical and size resolved PM instruments (TSI Optical Particle Sizer, TSI
 Aerodynamic Particle Sizer, and TSI Fast Mobility Particle Sizer).
 c. Iterative modifications were made to the light source – evaluated white, green,
 and blue LEDs – and algorithms for postprocessing opacity and lux using color
 measurements.

 3

 5. Compare Octoring measurements with laboratory grade PM measurements of pellet
 heater emission (Task 5)
 a. Recorded Octoring measurements from a pellet heater, which typically present
 lower opacities and finer particulate distributions.
 b. Adjustments identified during cordwood testing were carried forward; further
 tuning was applied in an attempt to improve measurements.
 6. Identify correlations between Octoring and combustion air controls (Task 6)
 a. Opacity and PM data collected from heater testing were analyzed to identify the
 accuracy of the opacity sensor measurements and its ability to consistently and
 reliably detect high PM events.
 b. Statistical analysis was conducted comparing Octoring measurements with
 gravimetric PM and estimated total PM measured by laboratory-grade
 equipment.
 c. Early results indicate that additional R&D is needed to ensure the Octoring
 measurements do not drift with time and temperature. Although rudimentary
 temperature corrections were applied, a more rigorous analysis should be
 conducted. If a repeatable signal can be obtained, then a control logic algorithm
 could be developed to correlate Octoring measurements with high PM events.

Overall, the milestones and deliverables for the five technical tasks were completed.
Comparative datasets for cordwood and pellet heaters were successfully collected, but
unresolved issues with measurement reliability, sensor QA/QC, and durability limited the
development of a robust control algorithm and final operational guidelines. It is recommended
that future work address these limitations (described in detail in the next section) prior to
developing a control algorithm.

Across each experiment, we observed several critical insights regarding the performance of the
Octoring sensor, as well as opportunities for future improvements:

 • Measurement and Reliability
 Throughout testing, we observed instances where the Octoring measurements tracked
 the PM measurements well (see Figure 2), and we observed instances when the
 Octoring provided inconsistent measurements for similar combustion events (see Figure
 3). As a result, the project shifted focus away from developing a preliminary control
 algorithm and toward identifying opportunities to improve sensor measurement
 reliability. This includes implementing a more robust data handling capability,
 addressing measurement drift with changes in sensor temperature (see Figure 4), and
 incorporating a zeroing function that ensures all readings start consistently from zero,
 thereby improving repeatability and reliability in datasets.

 • Light Sensor Quality Assurance, Calibration, and Robustness
 Measurement errors among individual light sensors highlighted the need for improved
 quality control measures. The Octoring requires further calibration efforts because each
 sensor and channel requires its own temperature correction curve and zeroing
 algorithm. Without these corrections, the Octoring may report negative opacities (see
 Figure 5). Additionally, two light sensors experienced failures after less than 100 hours
 of operation, underscoring the need for a more durable sensor. Based on these findings,
 future development should include streamlined calibration protocols and the
 integration of calibration coefficients into the sensor's software and a more robust
 sensor and/or design to address temperature drift and durability concerns. Additionally,
 conducting tests over extended operational hours will provide vital data for improving
 the Octoring design and ensuring it operates over thousands of hours without failure.

 Figure 5: Sample data showing decrease from positive to
 negative opacity 30 minutes after starting a pellet heater.

 • LED Reliability Improvements
 Variability in LED performance initially impacted measurement accuracy. Transitioning
 to narrowband green LEDs enhanced reliability of the measurement by providing a more
 stable light output. However, light information may be lost using a narrowband LED.
 Future research should further explore optimal light wavelengths to improve
 measurement precision.

The challenges documented during the evaluations of the Octoring sensor provide a compelling
roadmap for future research and development, reinforcing the promising foundational
technology created. Addressing these issues will improve the Octoring's reliability and
effectiveness and offers the potential to improve residential wood heater performance and
combustion strategies through PM monitoring and air control automation.

Products, Applications, and Technology Transfer
Through the successful execution of this CRADA project, we demonstrated that an opacity
sensor (the “Octoring”) has significant potential for monitoring real-time PM from residential
 6

wood heaters. The Octoring sensor represents an innovative product designed specifically for
monitoring PM without requiring dilution. This technology is unique, as it optimally operates in
conjunction with existing residential wood heating systems to enhance performance. The
Octoring’s design, including its adaptable light sources and data handling capabilities, can be
further developed for commercial viability, making it suitable for retrofitting into existing
systems or integrating into new heater designs.

The primary application of the Octoring is to improve air supply control strategies in residential
wood heating by providing real-time monitoring of PM. This technology has the potential to
offer actionable data to homeowners and manufacturers, resulting in improved wood heater
performance.

This partnership has fostered a stronger relationship with the residential wood heating
manufacturers and technology developers. It facilitated the development and testing of the
Octoring sensor and paved the way for future research on monitoring technologies for wood
combustion systems.

The data and experiences acquired during testing provide potential pathways for further
development. Identified improvements, such as enhanced calibration methods, more advanced
light sensors, durability testing protocols, and optimized LED technology, position the Octoring
for future iterations that will help create a more robust and accurate product. If optimized, the
Octoring could also support in-situ combustion monitoring and enable automation of wood
heaters. Technology transfer activities will focus on disseminating results through detailed
reports and presentations.

In summary, this project has established a foundation for utilizing opacity measurements to
monitor particulate matter (PM). While further research and development are necessary to
create a robust commercial product, the insights gained from this work serve as a crucial step
toward developing opacity sensors that could enhance the performance of residential wood
heaters. By addressing the technical issues identified during testing, future work will refine the
Octoring's capabilities for effective practical applications.
