Bicyclic graphs with the smallest and largest numbers of connected sets
Audace A. V. Dossou-Olory

Abstract
. For a graph G G with vertex set V V , let N ⁡ ( G ) \N(G) denote the number of nonempty subsets of V V that induce a connected graph in G G . In this paper, we focus on determining N ⁡ ( G ) \N(G) for G G in the family 𝔹 n \mathbb{B}_{n} of n n -vertex bicyclic graphs. We find in 𝔹 n \mathbb{B}_{n} the structures of those graphs that possess the smallest, the largest, as well as the second-largest values of N ⁡ ( G ) \N(G) . Moreover, we compute the extreme values of N ⁡ ( G ) \N(G) over 𝔹 n \mathbb{B}_{n} .

1.
Introduction

All graphs considered in this paper are undirected, simple (no loop and no parallel edge), and of finite order (i.e. the number of vertices). A connected set in a graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
is a nonempty set
S\subseteq V(G)
that induces a connected subgraph in
G
. The edge set of the graph induced by
S
consists of those edges of
G
whose both endvertices belong to
S
. In this paper, we are interested in the overall number of connected sets in a connected graph
G
, henceforth denoted by
\N(G)
. Subgraphs constitute fundamental components of a graph, offering key insights into its intrinsic structure. For example, they can serve to identify essential building blocks and to uncover the functional characteristics of complex networks
[
11
]
.

Over the past, much work has been devoted to determining the maximum and the minimum numbers of connected sets in different families of
n
-vertex graphs (i.e. graphs of order
n
), and also to characterising graphs attaining the extreme values. As a trivial example, precisely the complete graph achieves the maximum number of connected sets among all graphs on
n
vertices. The literature contains a substantial number of research papers studying the extreme numbers of connected sets in acyclic graphs (i.e. in trees). As far as we know, this line of study on trees goes back to Székely and Wang
[
12
,
13
]
; trees with given degree sequence were considered in
[
6
,
8
,
14
,
15
]
; paper
[
7
]
studies trees with given segment sequence; trees with given diameter appeared in
[
9
]
; paper
[
10
]
focuses on trees with given eccentric sequence; and many more, just to mention a few.

We know that every
n
-vertex connected graph possesses
n-1
edges or more, with the bound reached by trees only. A natural family of graphs after that of trees is perhaps the family of unicyclic graphs – connected
n
-vertex graphs having exactly
n
edges. In
[
2
]
, the structures of
n
-vertex unicyclic graphs that reach the extreme numbers of connected sets are completed derived, among other things. One of the questions at the end of paper
[
2
]
asks for constructive characterisation of the graphs that extremise the number of connected sets, over all connected graphs with prescribed order and number of cycles. In general, this
problem can appear out of reach due to the various ways in which the
cycles may intersect. However, special cases of so-called
bicyclic
graphs
are still of interest. A bicyclic graph is a connected graph whose number of edges equals one more the number of vertices. Such a graph necessarily contains two or three different cycles. Unicyclic and bicyclic graphs are among the most popular tree-like structures, and various graph invariants and parameters have been studied in these families.

In this paper, we shall determine close formulas, as a function of
n
, for the smallest and largest possible numbers of connected sets in a
n
-vertex bicyclic graph, and further derive all extreme graph structures. Some known extremal results on trees and unicyclic graphs will play an important role in our current context of determining the number of connected sets of a
n
-vertex bicyclic graph. Our main underlying trick relies on certain graph transformations that modify the number of connected sets, while preserving both the numbers of vertices and edges.

Let us also mention other work related to the topic. We studied in
[
3
]
the two problems of maximising the number of connected sets of a unicyclic graph with prescribed order and girth, or prescribed order, girth, and number of pendant vertices. We determined in
[
4
]
the maximum number of connected sets in a connected graph with given order and number of cut vertices, the minimum number among all cut vertex-free connected graphs, and the extreme numbers as a function of both order and number of pendant vertices. However, the result on the maximum, given
n
vertices of which
p
are pendant, first appeared in
[
1
]
as a Nordhaus-Gaddum type. Paper
[
2
]
extended
[
4
]
to the maximum number of connected sets in unicyclic graphs with given order and number of cut vertices. In
[
5
]
, we investigated the maximum of
\N(G)
where
G
is a unicyclic graph with
n
vertices of which
c
are cut vertices, and found that there are generally two extremal structures. For each of the above mentionned graph families, all extremal graph structures were characterised.

The current paper strictly focuses on
n
-vertex bicyclic graphs. We define our first notation and state some initial results in Section
2
; our main finding are covered in Section
3
.

2.
Preliminary

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a connected graph. For
u,v\in V(G)
, we denote by
\N(G)_{v}
and
\N(G)_{u,v}
the number of connected sets in
G
that involve
v
, and those that contain both
u
and
v
, respectively. On the other hand,
G-v
denotes the graph that remains upon deleting vertex
v
in
G
; more generally,
G-S
represents the graph obtained from
G
by deleting all elements of
S
.

A
u-v
path in
G
is a path between vertices
u
and
v
, i.e.
(u=)w_{0}w_{1}\ldots w_{m-1}w_{m}(=v)
such that
w_{i}w_{i+1}\in E(G)
for all
0\leq i\leq m-1
. As usual, the
n
-vertex path will be denoted by
P_{n}
. A
pendant vertex
(or a leaf) in
G
is a vertex of degree

; an edge incident with a pendant vertex is called a

.

By
C_{n}=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{n-1}
we mean the cycle whose vertices are
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n-1}
in this order, i.e.
v_{n-1}
is adjacent to
v_{0}
, and
v_{j}v_{j+1}
is an edge for all
0\leq j\leq n-2
.

We mention some known results that are relevant to us.

Lemma 1

(
[

]
)
.

For the path
P_{n}
and
u
a leaf of
P_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\N(P_{n})=\binom{n+1}{2}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\N(P_{n})_{u}=n\,.

Lemma 2

(
[

,

]
)
.

For the cycle

and

, we have

\displaystyle\N(C_{n})_{v}=1+\binom{n}{2}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\N(C_{n})=n^{2}-n+1\,.

At several occasions, we employ these two lemmas, without further reference.

A graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
is said to be
bicyclic
if
|E(G)|=|V(G)|+1
and
G
is connected. Every such graph must have at least four vertices. We denote by
\mathbb{B}_{n}
the collection of all bicyclic graphs on

, and to also characterise the extremal graphs.

3.
The extremal graph structures

Bicyclic graphs are tree-like structures in the sense that they can be decomposed as a single ‘block’ with trees attached to the vertices of this block.

Lemma 3
.

Let
G
be a bicyclic graph. Then there is a unique maximal (w.r.t number of vertices) induced subgraph of
G
, denoted by
G^{*}
, such that
G^{*}
is also bicyclic but contains no pendant vertex.

Proof.

If
G
does not contain a pendant vertex, then
G=G^{*}
. Otherwise, let
u
be a pendant vertex of
G
and
H=G-u
. We have
|V(H)|=|V(G)|-1
and
|E(H)|=|E(G)|-1
. Thus
H
is also a bicyclic graph. If
H
does not contain a pendant vertex, then
G^{*}=H
. Otherwise,
H
contains a pendant vertex
v
and
H-v
is also a bicyclic graph. We can repeatedly delete a pendant vertex until we either obtain a bicyclic graph having no pendant vertex, or a bicyclic graph on four vertices. In the latter case, we get the complete graph on four vertices missing a single edge.
∎

As a first consequence of Lemma
3
, we can construct any bicyclic graph
G
from its subgraph
G^{*}
by attaching trees rooted at the vertices of
G^{*}
. Given a bicyclic graph
G
, its subgraph
G^{*}
can only be of the following types:

:

Two cycles with no common vertex, and thus with a single path joining them; we denote it by

, obtained from the disjoint union of
C_{p}
and
C_{q}
by adding a path

connecting one vertex in
C_{p}
and one vertex in
C_{q}
(see Figure

).

Two cycles with only one common vertex. In this case,

and

We continue with some general lemmas that will simplify the computation of the number of connected sets in certain categories of graphs.

Lemma 4
.

Let
H
be obtained from the disjoint union of graphs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
by identifying a vertex
u_{1}
in
H_{1}
and a vertex
u_{2}
in
H_{2}
(similar to Figure
2
). We have

\displaystyle\N(H)=\N(H_{1})+\N(H_{2})-1+(\N(H_{1})_{u_{1}}-1)(\N(H_{2})_{u_{2}}-1)\,.

Proof.

Let
u
be the only common vertex of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
in
H
. We have

\displaystyle\N(H-u)=\N(H_{1}-u_{1})+\N(H_{2}-u_{2})=\N(H_{1})-\N(H_{1})_{u_{1}}+\N(H_{2})-\N(H_{2})_{u_{2}}

and

\displaystyle\N(H)_{u}=\N(H_{1})_{u_{1}}\cdot\N(H_{2})_{u_{2}}\,.

From these two identities, we get

\displaystyle\N(H)

\displaystyle=\N(H-u)+\N(H)_{u}

\displaystyle=\N(H_{1})-\N(H_{1})_{u_{1}}+\N(H_{2})-\N(H_{2})_{u_{2}}+\N(H_{1})_{u_{1}}\cdot\N(H_{2})_{u_{2}}

\displaystyle=\N(H_{1})+\N(H_{2})-1+(\N(H_{1})_{u_{1}}-1)(\N(H_{2})_{u_{2}}-1)\,,

which proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
H
be a graph,
v
a pendant vertex and
v^{\prime}
its neighbour in
H
. Then

\displaystyle\N(H)=\N(H-v)+1+\N(H-v)_{v^{\prime}}\,.

Proof.

The graph
H
decomposes as in Lemma
4
, where
H_{1}=vv^{\prime}
and
H_{2}=H-v
. Thus

\displaystyle\N(H)=3+\N(H-v)-1+(2-1)(\N(H-v)_{v^{\prime}}-1)=\N(H-v)+1+\N(H-v)_{v^{\prime}}\,.

∎

For an integer
m\geq 4
, we define the
tadpole
graph
D_{m}
to be the unicyclic graph obtained by identifying one vertex of the cycle
C_{3}
with one leaf of the path
P_{m-2}
. Furthermore, we define
L_{n}
to be the graph
L_{n}:=G[3,3,n-4]
; see Figure
1
.

Lemma 6
.

We have

\displaystyle\N(L_{n})=\frac{(n+6)(n-1)}{2}

for all
n\geq 5
.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\N(L_{5})=22
. Let
n>5
,
v
be the common vertex of a fixed
C_{3}
and
P_{n-4}
in
L_{n}
. The graph
L_{n}-v
has
D_{n-3}
as a connected component. It is proved in
[
2
]
that

\displaystyle\N(D_{m})=\frac{(m-1)(m+4)}{2}

for all
m\geq 3
. Thus

\displaystyle\N(L_{n}-v)=\N(P_{2})+\N(D_{n-3})=3+\frac{(n-4)(n+1)}{2}\,.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\N(L_{n})_{v}=\N(C_{3})_{v}\cdot\N(D_{n-2})_{w}\,,

where
w
is the unique pendant vertex of
D_{n-2}
. Since

\displaystyle\N(D_{n-2})_{w}=n-1\,,\quad\N(C_{3})_{v}=4\,,

we deduce that
\N(L_{n})_{v}=4(n-1)
and that

\displaystyle\N(L_{n})

\displaystyle=\N(L_{n}-v)+\N(L_{n})_{v}

\displaystyle=3+\frac{(n-4)(n+1)}{2}+4(n-1)=\frac{(n+6)(n-1)}{2}\,,

as needed.
∎

Denote by
A_{4}
the unique bicyclic graph on four vertices. For
n\geq 5
, we identify an endvertex of a
P_{n-3}
with one vertex of degree two in
A_{4}
to obtain
A_{n}\in\mathbb{B}_{n}
.

Lemma 7
.

We have

\displaystyle\N(A_{n})=\frac{n^{2}+7n-16}{2}

for all
n\geq 4
. In particular,

\displaystyle\N(L_{5})=\N(A_{5})=22\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\N(L_{n})<\N(A_{n})\quad\text{holds for all}~~~n>5\,.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\N(A_{4})=14
. For
n\geq 5
, denote by
u_{1}
in
P_{n-3}
and by
u_{2}
in
A_{4}
the two vertices that are identified to obtain
A_{n}
. We employ Lemma
4
to get

\displaystyle\N(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\N(P_{n-3})+\N(A_{4})-1+(\N(P_{n-3})_{u_{1}}-1)(\N(A_{4})_{u_{2}}-1)

\displaystyle=\binom{n-2}{2}+14-1+(n-3-1)(7-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}{2}+13+6(n-4)=\frac{n^{2}+7n-16}{2}\,.

Now Lemma
6
gives us

\displaystyle\N(A_{n})-\N(L_{n})=\frac{n^{2}+7n-16}{2}-\frac{(n+6)(n-1)}{2}=n-5\geq 0

for all
n\geq 5
, which proves the lemma.
∎

As a next step, we shall estimate the smallest number of connected sets containing a given vertex in a
n
-vertex bicyclic graph.

Proposition 8
.

If
G\in\mathbb{B}_{n}
and
v\in V(G)
, then

\displaystyle\N(G)_{v}\geq n+3\,.

Proof.

It is clear that
\N(A_{4})_{v}=7
if
v
is of degree two, and
\N(A_{4})_{v}=8
if
v
is of degree three. So the proposition holds (with equality) for
n=4
. Henceforth, assume
n\geq 5
. We distinguish between three scenarios:

•

v
is a pendant vertex of
G
, and
v^{\prime}
is the neighbour of
v
.

Since
G-v
is also a bicyclic graph, we have

\displaystyle\N(G)_{v}=1+\N(G-v)_{v^{\prime}}\geq 1+(n-1+3)=n+3\,,

where the inequality follows by induction on
n
.

•

G
contains a pendant vertex
u\neq v
.

Since
G-u
is also a bicyclic graph, we have

\displaystyle\N(G)_{v}=\N(G-u)_{v}+\N(G)_{u,v}\geq(n-1+3)+\N(G)_{u,v}\,,

where the inequality follows by induction on
n
. The count for
\N(G)_{u,v}
includes a shortest path between
u
and
v
, thus
\N(G)_{u,v}\geq 1
. In particular,

\displaystyle\N(G)_{v}\geq(n-1+3)+1=n+3\,.

•

G
does not have a pendant vertex.

All shortest paths starting at
v
in
G
contribute to the count
\N(G)_{v}
. Thus

\N(G)_{v}\geq n\,.

Vertex
v
together with any two of its neighbours also induces a connected graph in which
v
is not pendant. We deduce the following:

–

If
v
has at least three neighbours in
G
, then
\N(G)_{v}\geq n+3+1
, where the final
1
accounts for
G
itself.

–

If
v
has only two neighbours, say
v_{1},v_{2}
in
G
, then either
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
must have a neighbour, say
w
not belonging to
\{v,v_{1},v_{2}\}
. Thus, each of the graphs induced by
\{v,v_{1},v_{2}\}
and
\{v,v_{1},v_{2},w\}
is connected and none of them has
v
as a pendant vertex. Hence
\N(G)_{v}\geq n+2+1
, where the final
1
accounts for the whole
G
.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove our first theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Let
n>4
and
G\in\mathbb{B}_{n}
. It holds that

\N(G)\geq\N(L_{n})=\frac{(n+6)(n-1)}{2}\,.

Equality happens in the case where
n>5
if and only if
G
is isomorphic to
L_{n}
.

Proof.

The formula for
\N(L_{n})
is given in Lemma
6
. If
n=5
, then
\N(G)\geq 22
by Figure
4
. Equality holds if and only if
G=L_{5}
or
G=A_{5}
. Let
n>5
, and assume that
G
has a pendant vertex, say
v
. Then
G-v\in\mathbb{B}_{n-1}
and the induction hypothesis on
n
yields

\displaystyle\N(G-v)\geq\frac{(n-1+6)(n-1-1)}{2}\,.

This implies that

\displaystyle\N(G)=\N(G-v)+\N(G)_{v}\geq\frac{(n+5)(n-2)}{2}+(n+3)\,,

now using Proposition
8
. Hence

\displaystyle\N(G)\geq\frac{(n+5)(n-2)}{2}+(n+3)>\frac{(n+5)(n-2)}{2}+(n+2)=\frac{(n+6)(n-1)}{2}\,,

and we are done in this case. If
G
does not have a pendant vertex, then
G=G^{*}
is one of the three types in Figures
1
,
2
,
3
. Henceforth, we assume that
G\in\mathbb{B}_{n}
has the smallest number of connected sets.

Claim 1
:
G
cannot be of type II.

Suppose not. Replace the cycle
C_{q}
(
q\geq p
) in
G=G^{*}=G[p,q,n+2-p-q]
with the tadpole graph
D_{q}
, as shown in Figure
5
, to obtain a graph
G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{n}
. By

, we necessarily have

.

Figure 5
.
From Type II to a Type I.

It follows from Lemma
4
that

\displaystyle\N(G^{\prime})-\N(G)=\N(D_{q})-\N(C_{q})+(\N(D_{q})_{u_{1}}-\N(C_{q})_{u_{2}})(\N(C_{p})_{u_{2}}-1)\,,

where
u_{1}
is the unique pendant vertex in
D_{q}
. It is shown in
[
2
]
that
D_{m}
uniquely minimises the number of connected sets over the set of all
m
-vertex unicyclic graphs. Therefore,

\displaystyle\N(G^{\prime})-\N(G)<(\N(D_{q})_{u_{1}}-\N(C_{q})_{u_{2}})(\N(C_{p})_{u_{2}}-1)\,.

Since
\N(D_{q})_{u_{1}}=q+1
, while
\N(C_{q})_{u_{2}}=1+\binom{q}{2}
, we deduce that

\N(D_{q})_{u_{1}}-\N(C_{q})_{u_{2}}<0\,,

and that
\N(G^{\prime})-\N(G)<0
. This contradicts the minimality of

with respect to the number of connected sets, thus proving the claim.

Claim 2
:
G
cannot be of type III.

Suppose not. By construction (see Figure
3
),
G=G^{*}
has no cut vertex (i.e.
G-u
is still connected for every
u\in\V(G)
). We proved in
[

]
that
C_{n}
is the unique graph having the smallest number of connected sets among all

-vertex connected graphs with no cut vertex. Thus
\N(G)>\N(C_{n})
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\N(C_{n})-\N(L_{n})=n^{2}-n+1-\frac{(n+6)(n-1)}{2}=\frac{n^{2}-7n+8}{2}\,,

which is positive for all

. Therefore,
\N(G)>\N(C_{n})>\N(L_{n})
. In particular,

cannot reach the smallest number of connected sets over

. This proves Claim 2.

As a consequence of the above two Claims, a graph

that minimises the number of connected sets must be of type I.

Claim 3
:
G
is isomorphic to
L_{n}
.

Suppose not. Replace the cycle
C_{q}
(
q\geq p
) in
G=G^{*}
(see Figure

) with the tadpole graph

to obtain the same shape

, as shown in Figure

. Using Lemma

, we obtain

\displaystyle\N(G^{\prime})-\N(G)=\N(D_{q})-\N(C_{q})+(\N(D_{q})_{u_{1}}-\N(C_{q})_{u_{2}})\big(\N(G-(V(C_{q})\backslash\{u_{2}\}))_{u_{2}}-1\big)\,,

where

is the unique pendant vertex of

. Since
\N(D_{q})_{u_{1}}-\N(C_{q})_{u_{2}}\leq 0
and

is the only

\N(G^{\prime})-\N(G)<0
.

If

then

is isomorphic to

. Otherwise, we apply the same graph transformation to

(replacing

. Hence

Let

C_{3}

Similarly, we define

As usual, the

Lemma 10
.

We have

\displaystyle\N(R_{n})

\displaystyle=n+1+2^{n-1}

\displaystyle\N(B_{n})

\displaystyle=n+2+2^{n-1}=\N(R_{n})+1

for all

.

Proof.

The graph

consists of two copies of

and

, while

is adjacent to all other vertices of

\displaystyle\N(R_{n})=\N(R_{n})_{w}+\N(R_{n}-w)=2^{n-1}+2\N(P_{2})+n-5=2^{n-1}+n+1\,.

By Lemma

,

\displaystyle\N(B_{n})=\N(A_{4})+\N(S_{n-3})-1+(\N(A_{4})_{w}-1)(\N(S_{n-3})_{w}-1)\,,

where

is also the center of the star. Thus

\displaystyle\N(B_{n})=14+(2^{n-4}+n-4)-1+(8-1)(2^{n-4}-1)=2^{n-1}+n+2\,,

as stated.
∎

We shall prove that

uniquely maximises the number of connected sets among all

-vertex bicyclic graphs, provided

Theorem 13
