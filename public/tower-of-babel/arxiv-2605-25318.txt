A Sequential Quadratic Programming Perspective on Optimal Control
Abhijeet, Suman Chakravorty

Abstract
This paper investigates the performance of Newton’s method, iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR), and Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP) in solving discrete-time optimal control problems. We offer a unified perspective on these approaches, centered on the understanding that each method ultimately solves a sequence of quadratic programs. Building upon previous comparative works, this paper contributes additional mathematical explanations and results to the analysis. In particular, it is shown that iLQR is a principled Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) approach, rather than merely an approximation of DDP that neglects Hessian terms. This characteristic guarantees that iLQR will always produce a cost-descent direction and converge to an optimum, under some mild assumptions. In contrast, Newton’s method and DDP lack these guarantees, especially when initialized far from an optimum. A series of numerical examples are presented to corroborate the mathematical reasoning and analysis developed in the paper.

2
Background

We address the general class of discrete-time, finite-horizon optimal control problems. Let the state of a system at time step
t
be
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the control input be
\mathbf{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The system evolves over a finite time horizon of
T
steps according to the nonlinear state-transition function
f
:

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}),\qquad t=0,1,\cdots,T-1,

(1)

starting from a given initial state
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

The objective is to find a sequence of control inputs
\mathbf{U}=\{\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\}
that minimizes a total cost functional,
\mathcal{J}
. The optimal control problem can be formally stated as:

\min_{\mathbf{U}}\mathcal{J}(U)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}c(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+C_{T}(\mathbf{x}_{T}),

(2)

subject to the dynamics in (
1
). To ensure the problem is well-posed for the optimization methods used in this work, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The system dynamics function
\mathbf{f}
, stage cost
c
, and terminal cost
C_{T}
are twice continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{2}
) functions with respect to their arguments.

The prominent trajectory optimization methods that we analyze in this paper, including Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), the iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR), and Stagewise Newton methods, all rely on a common underlying principle. They iteratively refine a nominal trajectory by solving a local, second-order approximation of the original optimal control problem. Constructing this approximation requires the derivatives of the dynamics and cost functions evaluated along the nominal trajectory.

Let
(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})
for
t=0,\dots,T-1
denote such a nominal trajectory, which is a dynamically feasible sequence satisfying (
1
). We define the state and control perturbations, or variations, from this nominal trajectory as:

\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}:=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}:=\mathbf{u}_{t}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t}.

(3)

For notational brevity, we adopt a compact subscript notation for the partial derivatives. The first-order derivatives (Jacobians) and second-order derivatives (Hessians) of the dynamics and cost functions are written as:

\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}},\quad\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}},\quad\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}},\quad\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}},\quad\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}},\quad c_{\mathbf{x}},\quad c_{\mathbf{u}},\quad c_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}},\quad c_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}},\quad c_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}.

Derivatives of the terminal cost are denoted similarly, e.g.,
C_{T,\mathbf{x}}
and
C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
. Unless stated otherwise, all such derivatives are evaluated at the corresponding time step along the nominal trajectory, i.e.,
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}\equiv\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})
. Finally, we denote the transpose of a vector or matrix
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
.

A unified perspective for understanding and comparing the trajectory optimization algorithms in this paper is offered by the framework of Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP). We therefore begin by developing the fundamental concepts of the SQP approach as applied to the general optimal control problem described in eq. (
2
).

2.1
Sequential Quadratic Programming

In this section, we outline the basic SQP program for future references. Consider the generic optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}}g(\mathbf{z}),

subject to:

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{z})=\boldsymbol{0}.

This constrained optimization can be converted to an unconstrained optimization using an augmented Lagrangian as:

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{z})+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{z})=0.

This gives the necessary conditions as:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{z}}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}=\boldsymbol{0},

yielding:

g_{\mathbf{z}}+\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0},\;\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{z}^{*})=\boldsymbol{0},

at a minimum (stationary point)
(\mathbf{z}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{*})
.

To solve the problem, the second-order Newton method suggests to start with a nominal trajectory:
(\bar{\mathbf{z}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
and then expand the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}
to second order around the nominal.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=g+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{h}=\big(\bar{g}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}\big)+(g_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{z}^{\top}\big(g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\big)\delta\mathbf{z}+\delta\mathbf{z}^{\top}\,\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\bar{\mathcal{L}}+\Delta\mathcal{L},

where
\bar{g}=g(\bar{\mathbf{z}})
,
\bar{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{h}(\bar{\mathbf{z}})
,
\bar{\mathcal{L}}=\bar{g}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}
and
\otimes
denote a tensor product.

In a standard SQP approach, one follows iterative Newton’s method and optimizes the
(\Delta\mathcal{L})
part in terms of the perturbations
(\delta\mathbf{z},\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
and updates the solution iteratively as:

\mathbf{z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\delta\mathbf{z},\quad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}.

The necessary conditions for obtaining the optimum of
\Delta\mathcal{L}
in terms of
\delta x,\delta\lambda
are obtained by setting

\frac{\partial(\Delta\mathcal{L})}{\partial(\delta\mathbf{z})}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial(\Delta\mathcal{L})}{\partial(\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}=0,

yielding:

\begin{bmatrix}g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}&&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}&&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{z}\\[6.0pt]
\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-g_{\mathbf{z}}-\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\\[6.0pt]
-\bar{\mathbf{h}}\end{bmatrix}

However, taking the
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
term to the LHS gives:

\begin{bmatrix}g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}&&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}&&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{z}\\[6.0pt]
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-g_{\mathbf{z}}\\[6.0pt]
-\bar{\mathbf{h}}\end{bmatrix}

Here, note that
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is simply the new
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
(the updated
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
at new iteration).

However, the necessary conditions above are simply the solution to the QP problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\delta\mathbf{z}}\;g_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{z}^{\top}\big(g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\big)\delta\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\bar{\mathbf{h}}=0.

(4)

Hence, the Newton step can be accomplished by solving the above QP for the solution
(\delta\mathbf{z}^{q},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q})
and updating the solution as:

\mathbf{z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\delta\mathbf{z}^{q},\quad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q}.

Thus, the original constrained optimization can be solved by solving a sequence of QP problems above, and this will be termed as the Newton SQP approach.

However, in general, it is not advisable to use the “true" Hessian of the Lagrangian
\big(g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\big)
in the QP unless one is close to an optimum.

To see this, note that in the constrained case, the descent direction

\delta\mathbf{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta\mathbf{z}\\
\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\end{pmatrix}

is given by:

\delta\mathbf{Z}=(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{L},

and

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}&&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}&&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\quad\nsucc 0

if the current solution is far from an optimum.

If the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}\succ 0
, the Newton direction can be assured to be a descent direction, since then:

\delta\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\nabla\mathcal{L}=\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{L}>0.

However, if
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}\nsucc 0
, there is no such guarantee.

Thus, in order to be able to use the SQP approach, it becomes critical that one be able to ensure that the QP solution is a descent direction for the cost function.

Consider now a modified problem (QP):

\displaystyle\min_{\delta x}\;g_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta\mathbf{z}^{\top}g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\bar{\mathbf{h}}=0.

(5)

We make the assumption that
\mathbf{h}(\bar{\mathbf{z}})=\bar{\mathbf{h}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, i.e., our current estimate is feasible.

The necessary conditions for this QP are:

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{z}}+g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(6)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{h}}+\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}.

(7)

Since
g(\mathbf{z})
is a cost function by design, it is a reasonable assumption that
g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\succ 0
for
\mathbf{z}
.

Hence, the solution to the modified QP (
5
) is always a descent direction for the cost function.
Furthermore,

\mathbf{h}(\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\delta\mathbf{z})\approx\mathbf{h}(\bar{\mathbf{z}})+\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{0}

if
\delta\mathbf{z}
is small, and thus the update
\mathbf{z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\delta\mathbf{z}
also satisfies the constraint. If
\delta\mathbf{z}
is not small enough, we can do a line search using a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that

\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{h}(\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\alpha\delta\mathbf{z})\approx\mathbf{h}(\bar{\mathbf{z}})+\alpha\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{z}}\delta\mathbf{z}=0,

and thus
\mathbf{z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\alpha\delta\mathbf{z}
is feasible.

Hence, if we have a feasible solution, the modified QP always gives a descent direction for the cost while ensuring that the solution remains feasible if allied with a suitable line search approach.
The development above can be summarized as the following result:

Proposition 1
.

Consider the modified QP (
5
). Given
g_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\succ 0
, the solution to the QP,
\delta\mathbf{z}
, is always a descent direction for the cost function
g(\mathbf{z})
. Furthermore, the new solution
\mathbf{z}=\bar{\mathbf{z}}+\alpha\delta\mathbf{z}
can be made feasible, i.e.,
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{z})=\boldsymbol{0}
, by a suitable choice of the line-search parameter,
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

2.2
Background of iterative perturbation feedback methods

The motivation for introducing SQP in the preceding section was to establish a link to the algorithms compared in this work. Before presenting that comparison, we first develop the necessary background for these algorithms. In the following, we reiterate how Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), stagewise Newton methods, and the iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR) utilize the sparsity structure inherent in the optimal control problem described by eq. (
2
) and eq. (
1
). This exploitation of sparsity leads to significantly reduced computational complexity compared to conventional Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP).

To begin with, consider a feasible control sequence and a corresponding trajectory
(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})
can be constructed by evolving the dynamics.
Next, the augmented cost (using eqs. (
2
) and (
1
)) can be written as:

\min_{\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Big(c(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}^{\top}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\Big)+C_{T}(\mathbf{x}_{T})

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the Lagrange multipliers/costates.

In addition, let
(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t},\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t})
be the current guess to the problem. This augmented cost can be expanded about
(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t},\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t})
to get

\bar{\mathcal{J}}+\delta\mathcal{J}+\delta^{2}\mathcal{J}=\min_{\delta\mathbf{x}_{t},\;\delta\mathbf{u}_{t},\;\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}}\Bigg(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Big(c(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})+\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}\big)\Big)+C_{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{T})+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Big(c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}^{\top}\big(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})\big)+\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}-\delta\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\big)+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\top}c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}^{\top}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}f_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\top}f_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}\Big)+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}-\delta\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\big)\Big)+C_{T,\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\prime}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}\Bigg).

(9)

The necessary conditions for the above QP are:

\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}-\delta\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{0}

(10a)

c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})=\boldsymbol{0}

(10b)

c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1})=\boldsymbol{0}

(10c)

with the boundary/transversality condition:

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T},

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}.

(11)

Conventional SQP solves this problem by posing this problem as:

\mathbf{H}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}\\
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}\\
\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{g}=\boldsymbol{0}\Rightarrow\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}\\
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}\\
\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=-\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{g},

where
\mathbf{H}
is the hessian and
\mathbf{g}
is the gradient respectively. It gives the cushion of adding additional constraints, other than the dynamics in eq. (
1
).
However, the matrix
\mathbf{H}
is of the order
(m+2n)T
, and hence, inverting this matrix is computationally very heavy.

A class of algorithms (DDP/Stagewise Newton/iLQR), referred to as “Iterative perturbation feedback methods" in this paper, use the sparsity of this optimization problem. These algorithm use a forward-backward structure to exploit the sparsity and solve this problem with a time complexity
\mathcal{O}(T)
. There is a forward and a backward pass composing these algorithms. In the forward pass, the dynamics eq. (
1
) is propagated while computing the update to control. While in the backward pass, one implicitly solves for the multipliers and a linear feedback update to the control. The solution is made feasible using a suitable line search parameter
\alpha
.

We give an overview of these algorithms starting with “DDP". First, it starts with an initial guess of
\{\mathbf{u}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
and initializes the trajecotry using the dynamics
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})
in the forward pass. Following this, the control update
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}
is solved by leveraging the sparsity of variables in the backward pass. The backward pass begins at the last time step with the transversality condition in eq. (
11
) and then eq. (
10c
) is used to find the control update. Furthermore,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}
is replaced by linearized dynamics using eq. (
10a
) in terms of
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1}
and
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
. The equation which follows from (
10c
) at time-step
T-1
is

c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=\boldsymbol{0}.

Using the transversality condition in eq. (
11
),

c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1})^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}(C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T})=\boldsymbol{0}.

Furthermore, using the linearized dynamics in eq. (
10a
) and replacing
\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}
in the above equation, we get

c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1})^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\prime}(C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}))=\boldsymbol{0}.

The above equation is linear in terms of
\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}
and the solution to it is:

\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}=-\Big(c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\Big)^{-1}\Bigg(c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,x}+\Big(c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\Big)\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\Bigg).

(12)

The control update in the above equation can be written in short as

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}=-(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}+Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}).

(13)

The above term is the linear feedback for the DDP. The terms
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}
,
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}
and
Q_{\mathbf{u}}
are

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}.

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}.

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}.

Hence, eq. (
10c
) is solved to get a linearized feedback control update at the terminal step,
\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}=\mathbf{k}_{T-1}+\mathbf{K}_{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}
. This update is used in eq. (
10b
), along with linearized dynamics from eq. (
10a
), to get a backward pass on
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}
(represented as
\mathbf{v}_{T}
) and
\mathbf{S}_{T}
, where
\delta\lambda_{T}=\mathbf{S}_{T}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}
.
It comes from eq. (
10b
):

c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T-1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\boldsymbol{0}.

Substituting the value of control
\delta\mathbf{u}_{T-1}
from eq. (
13
) and using the transversality condition one gets

c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(-Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}+Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}))+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(-Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}+Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1})))^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}(C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T-1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\boldsymbol{0}.

Using linearized dynamics for
\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}
from eq. (
10a
) and replacing the control from eq. (
13
), the above expression becomes

c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(-(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}+Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}))+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1},x_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(-(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}+Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1})))^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}(C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(-(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}+Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}))))-(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T-1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\boldsymbol{0}.

It is evident from the above equation that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T-1}
and
\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}
can be separated using the form
\mathbf{v}_{T-1}+\mathbf{S}_{T-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}
. Separating out the constant terms gives

c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}-c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}-(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}-\mathbf{v}_{T-1}=\boldsymbol{0}.

Rearranging the terms give,

c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}-(c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}})(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}=\mathbf{v}_{T-1},

or in short,

\mathbf{v}_{T-1}=Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}-Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{T-1},

(14)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}}.

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+C_{T,\mathbf{x}}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}.

Similarly, the terms involving
\delta\mathbf{x}_{T-1}
in eq. (
2.2
) are:

c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}-c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}})-(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}})+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}-\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{S}_{T-1},

or in short,

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{T-1}=Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}-Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}

(15)

where

Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\otimes C_{T,\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}

Now, this can be generalized for every step to get
\mathbf{v}_{t}
and
\mathbf{S}_{t}
and these can be used to calculate
\mathbf{u}_{t}=\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
.
In a nutshell, eq. (
10b
) becomes an equation which can be separated into constants and linear terms in
\delta x_{t}
. This is then used to generate the backward pass on
\mathbf{v}_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t})
and
\mathbf{S}_{t}
. This process is repeated to obtain all
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}
and update the trajectory. The general equations for backward pass are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{t}=Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{t}-(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{\top}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{t}=Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t}-Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{t}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t},

(17)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{v}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{v}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{v}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}

with the terminal conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{v}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}}.

The term
\mathbf{k}_{t}=-(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t}
and
\mathbf{K}_{t}=-(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}
. DDP computes
\mathbf{k}_{t}
and
\mathbf{K}_{t}
for all time steps and then updates the control
\mathbf{u}_{t}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t}+\alpha\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
where
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
is the perturbation given by the system after applying
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1}
which is
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+\delta\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t-1}+\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1})-\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t-1})
, with
\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}=0
as the initial state,
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, is given.

The perturbed cost can be simplified to find the expected reduction in cost by substituting
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=\alpha\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
and the backward pass equations given in eq. (
17
) to get

\delta\mathcal{J}^{*}+\delta^{2}\mathcal{J}^{*}=-\Big(\alpha-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big)\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{\top}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t}

(18)

which is the expected optimal reduction in cost. One can see that
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}
has to be positive definite for a guarantee in cost reduction at each time step
t
.

Remark 1
.

The matrix
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}
in DDP is often not positive definite due to the second order terms
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}},\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}
, which depends on both the system dynamics and the current nominal trajectory. This poses the same problems as the QP in eq. (
4
). As a result, DDP typically requires substantial regularization. This issue also arises in the “Stagewise Newton" approach, as will be discussed next.

The method “Stagewise Newton" solves the same set of necessary conditions as in eqs. (
10a
)-(
10c
), like DDP. However, there are two key differences. In the forward pass, it uses linearized dynamics to update the control. So,
\mathbf{u}_{t}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t}+\alpha\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, where
\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t-1}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1}
.

In addition to this, one replaces
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
to a new variable
\mathbf{l}_{t}
in eqs. (
10b
) and (
10c
).
\mathbf{l}_{t}
takes the form
\mathbf{S}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{v}_{t}
if the necessary conditions are solved in this manner. So, the necessary conditions become:

\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}-\delta\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(19a)

c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}

+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{l}_{t+1}-\mathbf{l}_{t}=0

(19b)

c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}

+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\mathbf{f}_{u_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{l}_{t+1}=0

(19c)

Additionally, rather than using
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}
from the previous iteration (which is done in a typical SQP solver), it enforces linearity of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
in terms of
\delta\mathbf{x}
and
\delta\mathbf{u}
. The backward pass on
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
comes from eq. (
19b
). Given that
\mathbf{l}_{t}^{\text{iter}-1}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}^{\text{iter}}
and
\mathbf{l}_{t+1}^{\text{iter}-1}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}^{\text{iter}}
, where “iter" denotes the current iteration number. This along with the expansions

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}-1}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\text{iter}-1}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\text{H.O.T.}

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}-1}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\text{iter}-1}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\text{H.O.T.},

and using eq. (
19b
), one can write

\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}^{\text{iter}}=\mathbf{l}_{t}^{\text{iter}-1}=\underbrace{c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{l}_{t+1}}_{\text{all derivatives at (iter - 1)}},

which under linearity assumption gives:

\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}^{\text{iter}}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}}+(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\text{iter}})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}^{\text{iter}}.

(20)

However, this will have errors even under linearity assumption of derivatives as the term
(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}
should have been
(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t})^{\top}\mathbf{l}_{t+1}
for eq. (
20
) to hold even in a linear sense. But, “Stagewise Newton" makes this approximation.
So, the backward pass for “Stagewise Newton" becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{t}=Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{t}-(Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{\top}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{t}=Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t}-Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{t}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t},

(22)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1},

(23)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{x_{t}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}

with the terminal conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}},\quad\mathbf{v}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}},\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{T}=C_{T,\mathbf{x}}.

We reiterate that the forward pass for Stagewise Newton updates
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
where
\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t-1}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1}
, is the linearized dynamics, unlike DDP, which takes the full
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+\delta\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t-1}+\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1})-\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t-1})
.

Remark 2
.

In reference
[
23
]
, the author proposes the aforementioned stagewise Newton procedure to find the Newton direction for the optimal control problem posed in this paper. A careful look at these equations clearly shows that they are not the same as the equations we have for Newton LQR above. In particular, rather than the variables
\bar{\lambda}_{t}
being the multipliers from the previous iteration, they are approximated to be linear as in eq. (
23
). To begin with, this is not a good approximation as explained in the aforementioned section. Along with this, not using full perturbation in the forward pass, like DDP also leads to slower convergence
[
26
]
.

We describe the iLQR algorithm next. iLQR uses the modified QP as mentioned in eq. (
5
). For the optimal control problem, this means that the optimization problem in eq. (
9
) becomes

\min_{\delta\mathbf{x}_{t},\delta\mathbf{u}_{t},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Big(c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\top}c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}+(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t+1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1})^{\top}(\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t})+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}-\delta\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\Big)+C_{T,\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}.

(24)

It has the same forward and backward pass as DDP except we drop the tensor products of second order terms in dynamics. This gives:

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{u_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}

Proposition 2
.

The solution to the iLQR problem is always a descent direction for the cost
c(x,u)
and the next solution can be made feasible via a suitable line-search procedure. In contrast, it is not necessary that the solution to Newton LQR be a descent direction.

This makes the tensor products drop from the
Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t},Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}
and
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}
and now the net cost decrease can be guaranteed to be positive definite under the following mild assumptions.

Assumption 2
.

c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}

\succ 0
.
c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}
is always positive definite for all time-steps,
t
.

Assumption 3
.

C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}

\succeq 0
.
C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
is positive semi-definite.

Assumption 4
.

\begin{bmatrix}c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}&&c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\\
c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}&&c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\end{bmatrix}

\succeq 0
. The hessian matrix of incremental cost,
c(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
, is positive semidefinite for all time-steps,
t
.

Proof.

If we have
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}
to be positive definite for all time-steps then we are guaranteed a reduction in cost. At the terminal, we have

Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}

which is positive definite following from assumptions 1 and 2.
In addition to this, the hessian of iLQR is

\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}&Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}\\
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}&Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}&c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\\
c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}&c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\\
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}&C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\\
C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}&C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}\\
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\end{bmatrix}.

(25)

In the above equation, it is evident that the matrix on left hand side is positive definite. This follows from assumptions 2 and 3, which imply:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}&Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}\\
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}&Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\succeq 0,\quad\text{since}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}&c_{\mathbf{x}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\\
c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{x}_{T-1}}&c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-1}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\succeq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{bmatrix}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}&C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\\
C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}&C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

In addition to this,
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}
is already shown to be positive definite. So, the Schur complement
[
6
]
of
\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}&Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}\\
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}&Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}\end{bmatrix}
has to be positive semidefinite, which gives

Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}-Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}\succeq 0.

(26)

But the above equation is nothing but the backward pass on
\mathbf{S}
:

\mathbf{S}_{T-1}=Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}-Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1}(Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-1})^{-1}Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{T-1}\succeq 0

Now, one can write

Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{T-2}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-2}\mathbf{u}_{T-2}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-2}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{T-1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{T-2}}

which is always positive definite as
c_{\mathbf{u}_{T-1}\mathbf{u}_{T-2}}
is positive definite following from assumption 1 and
\mathbf{S}_{T-1}\succeq 0
. By induction, it follows that
Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}\succ 0
for all time-step,
t
.
∎

Thus, this implies that the iLQR guaranties a decent direction under these mild assumptions. In contrast, DDP often requires heavy regularization.

3.1
The impact of Forward-Pass

A critical distinction between the Stagewise-Newton method and the DDP/iLQR family of algorithms lies in the forward pass, specifically in how state perturbations are propagated to compute the control update. In DDP and iLQR, the feedback term depends on the full nonlinear state perturbation,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
. The control update is

\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=\alpha\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
,
where
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
is the difference between the new and previous (nominal) trajectories:

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{t}).

In contrast, the Stagewise-Newton method employs a control update,
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=\alpha\mathbf{k}_{t}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, that relies on a linearized propagation of the state perturbation,
\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t},

\displaystyle\text{where}~~\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t-1}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t-1}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t-1}.

The performance implications of this choice were highlighted by Liao and Shoemaker
[
15
]
, who observed that the linearized forward pass of the Stagewise-Newton method leads to significantly slower convergence compared to DDP. To isolate the effect of the forward pass, they introduced a “Mixed" algorithm combining a DDP-style forward pass with a Stagewise-Newton backward pass, confirming that using the full nonlinear state perturbation,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
, accelerates convergence.

To analyze this difference quantitatively, we examine the Taylor series expansion of the state perturbation. Since the initial state is fixed,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the first control perturbation is purely feedforward:
\delta\mathbf{u}_{0}=\alpha\mathbf{k}_{0}
. The resulting state perturbation at the next step,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{1}
, can be expanded as:

\delta\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{u}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{u}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}\mathbf{u}_{0}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{0}+\alpha^{3}(\text{third order terms})+\alpha^{4}(\text{fourth order terms})+\cdots\cdots

Substituting the initial conditions yields an expansion in powers of the line search parameter
\alpha
:

\delta\mathbf{x}_{1}=\alpha\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}\mathbf{k}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\mathbf{k}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{0}\mathbf{u}_{0}}\mathbf{k}_{0}++\alpha^{3}(\text{third order terms})+\alpha^{4}(\text{fourth order terms})+\cdots\cdots

This recursive structure propagates through time. The state perturbation
\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
at any time step
t
is an expansion in powers of
\alpha
, where the coefficients depend on the system Jacobians and the feedforward control modifications
\{\mathbf{k}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{t-1}
. The general form of the first-order term in
\alpha
is:

\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}=\alpha\left[\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t-1}}\mathbf{k}_{t-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{t-2}\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{t-1}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\mathbf{K}_{i})\right)\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{j}}\mathbf{k}_{j}\right]+\alpha^{2}(\text{second order terms})+\alpha^{3}(\text{third order terms})+\cdots\cdots

Remark 3
.

In the perturbation expansion of the dynamics, each
k
-th order term is scaled by a factor of
\alpha^{k}
. Consequently, one can diminish the influence of higher-order terms and recover the linearized dynamics by reducing
\alpha
.

The Stagewise-Newton method, by design, retains only the first-order terms in
\alpha
from this expansion for its feedback correction. This corresponds to an implicit assumption that higher-order dynamics are negligible, which can be a poor approximation. In contrast, DDP and iLQR use a line search on
\alpha
to actively manage the influence of these higher-order terms, reducing
\alpha
only when necessary to ensure a sufficient decrease in the cost function. This adaptive approach avoids prematurely discarding valuable nonlinear information.

This theoretical difference is demonstrated empirically in Fig.
1
for the pendulum swing-up task. The figure compares four methods of propagating the state perturbation
\delta\theta
:
1) the ground truth (full nonlinear difference), 2) a local linear approximation, 3) a local quadratic approximation, and 4) the recursive linear propagation used in Stagewise-Newton. For a given iteration, the local linear and quadratic models are nearly indistinguishable from the ground truth, indicating the dynamics are well-behaved locally. However, the Stagewise-Newton propagation (“Linear Prop.") significantly deviates from the ground truth, especially for larger
\alpha
. This error reduces as
\alpha
is reduced (e.g.,
\alpha=0.01
), confirming that the method’s validity is restricted to small step sizes.

The need for regularization in DDP

3.3
Cooling of the line-search parameter

Building on the previous observation that iLQR converges more efficiently than DDP, this section investigates a key contributing factor: the quality of the descent direction computed by DDP. Far from the optimal trajectory, the inclusion of the full dynamics Hessian can degrade the DDP descent direction. This degradation has two primary consequences. First, it provides an explanation for the superior convergence speed of iLQR observed previously, even in regularized settings. Second, it often forces the line search to select conservatively small step sizes, thereby hindering overall progress.

The observed slower convergence of DDP can be traced to a methodological inconsistency within the algorithm. The backward pass minimizes a quadratic cost model, but to achieve local quadratic convergence, it asymmetrically applies a second-order dynamic model to the linear value term
\mathbf{v}_{t}
and a first-order model to the quadratic term
\mathbf{S}_{t}

[
26
]
. This creates a descent direction based on a composite, approximated state perturbation. However, the forward pass uses the true nonlinear perturbation. This discrepancy between the model used to generate the search direction and the model used to evaluate it can degrade the direction’s quality. As a result, the algorithm’s progress is hindered, manifesting as both slower overall convergence and a frequent need to reduce the line search parameter
\alpha
.
The quadratic convergence can be written mathematically as:

\displaystyle\left|\left|\{\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\text{iter}+1}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}-\{\mathbf{u}_{t}^{*}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}\right|\right|_{2}\leq c\left|\left|\{\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\text{iter}}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}-\{\mathbf{u}_{t}^{*}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}\right|\right|_{2}^{2},

(27)

where iteration denotes the current iteration number,
c
is a constant and
\{\mathbf{u}_{t}^{*}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
denotes the optimal control trajectory.

In this subsection, we show that the corrupt descent direction in DDP leads to reduction in line search parameter and is one of the reasons for small values which makes
c
in eq. (
27
). In this work, the line search parameter,
\alpha
is reduced, if the ratio
\frac{\Delta J_{\text{actual}}}{\Delta J_{\text{pred}}}<0
. It is a common belief that DDP accepts larger steps than iLQR; owing to its quadratic convergence. Equivalently, for a given decrease in cost, the line-search parameter
\alpha
is often higher under DDP than under iLQR, motivated by the idea that DDP forms a better local model by including a second-order expansion of the dynamics. In this section, we show empirical evidence that DDP can lead to excessive cooling of the line-search parameter,
\alpha
, and hence, can take a much smaller step than iLQR.

Neighboring Extremal Paths

If we solve the DDP equations at the optimal solution
\mathbf{u}^{*}_{t}
, we will get the control update to be
\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}
as the term
\alpha_{t}
will be zero at the optimal. This feedback obtained by solving DDP equations is the optimal first order feedback. In this section, we show that this is the same as solving for neighboring extremal paths
[
3
]
.

To begin with let us start with the neighboring extremal equations. At the optimal, the first order perturbations of the cost are zero i.e,
\delta J=0
. So, the optimization is over the second order terms. Formally,

\displaystyle\min_{\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta^{2}\mathcal{J}=\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}C_{T,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{T}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}\\
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}H_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}&&H_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\\
H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}&&H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}\\
\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}:\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=\delta\mathbf{x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}=0

(28)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system given by

H=c(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}^{\top}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}))

The solution to the above LQR problem is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{t}=H_{\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}-

\displaystyle(H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}})^{\top}(H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}})^{-1}(H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}})

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}=(H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}})^{-1}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}})

(30)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=-\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}

(31)

In the above expression, we have

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}=c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}=H_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}=c_{u_{t},x_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}=c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}

This gives the backward pass in eq. (
29
) to be:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{t}=\underbrace{c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}}_{Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{t}}-\underbrace{(c_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}})}_{Q_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}^{t}}

\displaystyle{\underbrace{(c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}})}_{Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}}}^{-1}\underbrace{(c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}})}_{Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}}.

This backward pass is the same as the backward pass given in the equation (
17
). In addition to this, we have

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}_{t}=-\mathbf{K}_{t}\delta\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}={\underbrace{(c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}})}_{Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{t}}}^{-1}\underbrace{(c_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{t+1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}})}_{Q_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}^{t}}.

This is the same expression as obtained for the feedback
\mathbf{K}_{t}
for DDP. This is also the “optimal first-order feedback."

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}\otimes\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}}
, which are absent in iLQR.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the solution to discrete-time optimal control problems by analyzing Newton’s method, Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), and the iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR) through a unified constrained Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) framework. The critical insight is that each of these methods solves a Quadratic Program (QP)—and consequently, an LQR problem—at every iteration.

Our analysis reveals that although DDP can be construed as an approximation to the Newton step near an optimum, both it and Newton’s method can lead to inconsistent cost predictions and unreliable updates, especially when initialized far from the optimal solution. In contrast, iLQR provides consistent descent directions and admits theoretical convergence guarantees, making it far more robust in practice.

Experiments on multiple tasks confirm that iLQR delivers stable performance, whereas DDP may produce unreliable solutions unless heavily regularized. This regularization, however, removes any guarantee of convergence when far from an optimum. Indeed, iLQR demonstrated superior convergence over a regularized DDP implementation in one specific case ("Test Problem - 4"), while in other problems, the assumptions for iLQR to guarantee descent were satisfied, and it required no regularization.

These results establish iLQR as a principled SQP approach to optimal control rather than a mere approximation of DDP that neglects second-order terms. Our theoretical and empirical findings highlight the importance of consistency over second-order information; in fact, we show that omitting this second-order information is critical to performance, demonstrating an instance where less is more.

Additionally, we explored the neighboring optimal solution, showing it to be equivalent to expanding the perturbed Bellman equation around the optimum. This equivalence yields the DDP feedback solution near the optimum, which can be used to suppress noise and stabilize the system in the presence of uncertainty.
