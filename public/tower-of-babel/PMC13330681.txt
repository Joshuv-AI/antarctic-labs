Hospital based motor cognitive rehabilitation training. What after? An intervention aimed at patients' support between admissions
Authors: Anna Carollo, Chiara Siri, Greta Manoni, Margherita Canesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe letter reports on a teleconsultation program for patients with Parkinson's Disease after hospital-based rehabilitation. PwPD and their caregivers were contacted with regular video/phone calls assessing therapy adherence and addressing emerging difficulties. Results showed high satisfaction, continuity of care, and feasibility, confirming telemedicine as a valuable extension of clinical practice.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, Neurorehabilitation, Telemedicine, Neuropsychology, Caregiver

Dear Editor,

A few years ago, our neuropsychology service (U.O.C. Parkinson's Disease and Movement Disorders Rehabilitation, Moriggia Pelascini Hospital, Gravedona ed. Uniti, Italy) began using virtual communication platforms to address a key question: “What happens to patients between admissions?”

Rehabilitation therapies are recognized as an integral component of Parkinson's Disease (PD) management. Occupational, speech, and motor-cognitive therapies delivered in specialized units have proven effective [1]. However, structured follow-up between hospital-based treatments is generally lacking.

The aim of our project was to design an intervention to support patients once they return home, addressing difficulties they experience after discharge, and to assess adherence to prescribed training. Teleconsultation was selected as a valuable means of extending hospital-based care into home care. Caregivers were actively involved, both to facilitate the understanding of patients' clinical needs and to provide them with an opportunity to share their own experiences, feelings and coping strategies. This letter reports on a clinical initiative that aligns with and reinforces current evidence on telemedicine and teleconsultation.

Neurological disorders are a leading cause of disability worldwide, with PD being the fastest-growing neurological disorder, sometimes described as a “pandemic” [2]. The rising demand for neurological services, combined with rapid technological advances and the recent experience of COVID-19 pandemic, has accelerated the development of telemedicine [3].

According to the MDS Telemedicine Study Group survey [3], this trend extended to individuals with movement disorders, with post-pandemic reviews confirming the high effectiveness and satisfaction of telemedicine approaches [5]. Current applications in PD include remote neurological consultations, telemonitoring with wearable sensors or smartphone-based apps to support clinical decisions, and telerehabilitation (particularly in speech and physical therapy). Psychotherapy via telemedicine has also shown feasibility in small-scale studies [5].

Teleconsultation refers specifically to clinician–patient interactions via video or telephone. In PD, teleconsultation can play a decisive role in expanding access to specialized care, particularly for patients living in remote areas or those with severe disability, while also reducing travel burden and associated costs [4]. Nevertheless, limitations exist, including patients' technological skills, concerns about reduced personal connection, and the inappropriateness of teleconsultation for complex cases or diagnostic assessments [5].

In our project, a trained neuropsychologist enrolled 51 people with PD (PwPD) (mean age: 68.6 ± 7.8; disease duration: 13.6 ± 7.5 years) with heterogeneous cognitive status, who had completed multidisciplinary aerobic motor-cognitive rehabilitation at our unit. Participants came from different regions across Italy (up to 1000 km away), with diverse living conditions and cognitive profiles. After discharge, patients were contacted every 6–8 weeks via either video call or telephone, depending on their technological skills, familiarity with the platform, and internet connection. Approximately half preferred video calls.

At each contact, a semi-structured interview explored patients' impressions regarding cognition, mood and anxiety, adherence to speech and physical therapy exercises, and main concerns (e.g., falls, dysphagia, activities of daily living). Results showed that 90% of PwPD continued physical therapy at home (about half at least three times per week), while 43% performed speech therapy exercises. These findings remained stable across follow-ups of up to 6 months. No differences in clinical, cognitive, or mood and anxiety outcomes were observed between patients adhering and not adhering to physical therapy (see Table 1).

Clinically, the intervention was effective: 13 PwPD received targeted support for therapy-related or neuropsychiatric difficulties from the neurologist, the interviewing psychologist was able to address psychological, psychoeducational, and referral needs, while most patients were stable after rehabilitation and did not need further assistance until their subsequent hospitalization. Compliance was high, with most patients and caregivers maintaining consistent follow-ups. Only a few withdrew due to severe health issues interfering with communication.

One year after discharge, patients were asked to complete a satisfaction questionnaire. Of those who responded, 94% of patients and 83% of caregivers reported benefit from the intervention and expressed interest in continuing. We are still in contact with these patients and the enrolling of new participants is ongoing, with the goal of fostering a more patient-centered health system and bringing healthcare closer to home.

Consistent with existing literature, our experience indicates that teleconsultation is effective in enhancing collaboration among healthcare providers, improving satisfaction and well-being for both PwPD and caregivers. Teleconsultation ensures continuity of care after hospital discharge and thereby promoting the patient's sustained engagement in the management of their condition and overcoming geographical barriers in a neurodegenerative disease that progressively leads to motor disability. Importantly, we observed that personal connection was preserved, as ongoing monitoring allowed patients to feel supported in the comfort of their own homes.

Challenges remain, particularly regarding patients' ability to use technology and the impact of cognitive or motor-behavioral complications, which often require caregivers' assistance. However, as digital literacy improves and technology evolves, these barriers are likely to diminish. Further research is needed to optimize strategies for addressing such limitations. Other limitations include the heterogeneity of the study population in terms of disease duration and cognitive status, which may have influenced satisfaction with the intervention, as well as the absence of a control group.

In conclusion, our experience with teleconsultation has been highly encouraging. We believe that telemedicine should increasingly be integrated into routine clinical practice for PD, fostering a tailored experience of high-quality care. Future studies should be addressed to standardize remotely administered clinical tests, integrate continuous digital monitoring, and extend telemedicine to personalized remote rehabilitation programs, in order to promote equitable access to care. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence highlighting telemedicine as a viable and effective tool for managing Parkinson's Disease, offering enhanced accessibility and patients' comfort.
