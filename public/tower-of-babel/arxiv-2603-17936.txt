Exploration of Fluxonium Parameters for Capacitive Cross-Resonance Gates
Eugene Y. Huang, Christian Kraglund Andersen

Abstract
We study the cross-resonance effect in capacitively-coupled fluxonium qubits and devise a simple formula for their maximum ZX interaction strength. By going beyond the perturbative regime, we find that a CNOT gate can generally be realized in under 200 ns with residual ZZ limited to 50 kHz, for fluxonium qubits with frequencies below 1 GHz. Our analysis relies on a semi-analytical method: we first numerically diagonalize the Floquet Hamiltonian of the strongly-driven control qubit and then perturbatively incorporate the weak qubit-qubit coupling to obtain an effective Hamiltonian. We also derive frequency collision windows around harmful control–target and control–spectator transitions. For large fluxonium devices, we predict a collision-free yield that is considerably less sensitive to junction variability compared to transmons in the same layout. These results support the viability of an all-fluxonium cross-resonance architecture with only capacitive couplings.

II.2
Drive-induced decoherence

Maximizing the
ZX
interaction during the CR gate
requires strongly driving the control qubit,
which can significantly alter its coherence properties from its undriven behavior

78
;
45
;
83
;
115
.
Let
\rho
be the density matrix of the two-fluxonium system.
When weakly coupled to an environment, the density matrix evolves according to the generic master equation

45

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\sum_{n}\Gamma_{n}\mathbb{D}[\hat{L}_{n}]\rho

(16)

where

\mathbb{D}[\hat{L}]\rho=\hat{L}\rho\hat{L}^{\dagger}-(\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\rho+\rho\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L})/2

is the Lindblad superoperator and
\hat{L}_{n}
are the jump operators through which the system couples with the environment, with corresponding rates
\Gamma_{n}
.

In the uncoupled case,
J=0
, we consider decay, excitation and dephasing of the target qubit,
described by the jump operators

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{t}}^{1\to 0}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{|}{|}0><1|\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{t}}^{0\to 1}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{|}{|}1><0|\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{t}}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\outerproduct{|}{|}0><0|-\outerproduct{|}{|}1><1|}{2}\,,

(19)

with associated rates

\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}^{1\to 0}
,

\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}^{0\to 1}

and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}^{\phi}
respectively.
Heating into higher excited states is negligible at millikelvin temperatures.
For the control qubit, its jump operators are expressed in terms of the Floquet modes (evaluated at
t=0
) as

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{c}}^{i\to j}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{|}{|}\Phi_{j}(\Omega)><\Phi_{i}(\Omega)|\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\phi}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\outerproduct{|}{|}\Phi_{0}(\Omega)><\Phi_{0}(\Omega)|-\outerproduct{|}{|}\Phi_{1}(\Omega)><\Phi_{1}(\Omega)|}{2}\,,

(21)

with associated rates

\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{i\to j}(\Omega)

and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{\phi}(\Omega)
,
respectively.
Because the control qubit is strongly driven, heating into higher excited states
cannot be ignored even in a zero-temperature environment.

A CNOT gate implemented using a square pulse with amplitude

\Omega

has the duration

t_{g}(\Omega)=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi/|\mu_{ZX}(\Omega)|
;
therefore,
its average incoherent gate infidelity

\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}(\Omega)

can be approximated by

2

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}_{\mathrm{c}}+\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}_{\mathrm{t}}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{5}\bigl(\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{1}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{\phi}\bigr)t_{g}+\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{\ell}t_{g}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}_{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{5}\bigl(\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}^{1}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}^{\phi}\bigr)t_{g}\,,

(24)

where

\Gamma_{q}^{1}\coloneqq\Gamma_{q}^{0\to 1}+\Gamma_{q}^{1\to 0}

is qubit

and

In a very simple model where the control qubit is susceptible only to dielectric loss

its dissipation rates are given by (
\Omega
-dependence omitted)

78
;
45

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{i\to j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigl|n_{\mathrm{c}}^{[k]ji}\bigr|^{2}S(k\omega_{\mathrm{d}}+\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{i})\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{1}{2}\bigl|n_{\mathrm{c}}^{[k]11}-n_{\mathrm{c}}^{[k]00}\bigr|^{2}S(k\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\,,

(26)

where

0&\text{for }\omega\leq 0\end{cases}

(27)

is the noise spectral density
representing the lossy capacitor
with loss tangent
\tan\delta
at
zero temperature
.
We use this idealized noise model to regularize the problem of finding an optimal drive amplitude
since

can become very large when the Floquet qubit modes hybridize with the plasmon modes.
These modes are highly susceptible to dielectric losses
and, hence, should be avoided in a real implementation.
Minimizing
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{incoh}}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)

rather than maximizing
|\mu_{ZX}(\Omega)|

is thus the better suited objective.
Nevertheless, both goals are equivalent for small drive amplitudes.
We emphasize that the errors estimated with this simple model
should
not
be interpreted as a realistic incoherent error budget.

IV
Frequency collisions

Driving the control qubit at the amplitude needed to saturate the
ZX
rate
can induce undesired multiphoton transitions,
as anticipated in

Fig.

7
.
These transitions stem from resonances between Floquet quasienergies and should be avoided.
In this section,
we establish general guidelines for choosing fluxonium parameters
that circumvent unwanted resonances
and ensure that fast CNOT gates between qubit pairs remain feasible,
even in the presence of random fabrication disorder.

Using the insights from

Section

III
,
we fix the control-qubit parameters to

E_{J,{\mathrm{c}}}/2\pi=\quantity{4.0}{}
,

E_{C,{\mathrm{c}}}/2\pi=\quantity{1.2}{}

and
E_{L,{\mathrm{c}}}/2\pi=\quantity{0.4}{}
.
These parameters produce a low-frequency control qubit with a high anharmonicity,
providing a wide range for positioning the target-qubit frequencies.
Moreover, a small
E_{L,{\mathrm{c}}}/E_{J,{\mathrm{c}}}
ratio minimizes relaxation via inductive losses,
see
Appendix

C
.
To analyze potential frequency collisions, we use an effective energy spectral density description of the driven control qubit’s charge operator.
Throughout this section,
we consider a frequency collision to be an unwanted resonant interaction
inducing a coherent error exceeding
{10}^{-4}
.
This threshold reflects the approximate incoherent error
expected from a

{

100

}

gate
applied between qubits with a

{

1

}

lifetime.

IV.2
Effective energy spectral density

In this section, we briefly introduce the
effective energy spectral density
(effective ESD)
of the control fluxonium’s charge operator,
which will allow us to visualize
the frequency bands that may drive undesired two-body transitions.
The effective ESD arises naturally in the interaction picture
of the individual Hamiltonians, whose time-evolution unitaries are

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\mathrm{c}}(t_{1},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\exp(-i\!\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega(t),t)\,dt)\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\mathrm{t}}(t_{1},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl({-i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{t}}\times(t_{1}-t_{0})\bigr)\,.

(33)

Recall,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{t}}

are the individual Hamiltonians of the control and target qubits, respectively.
If we assume
\Omega(t)
drives the CR gate from the time

t_{\mathrm{i}}
to the time
t_{\mathrm{f}}
,
the change-of-basis unitary into the interaction picture is

\hat{A}(t)=\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)\otimes\hat{A}_{\mathrm{t}}(t)

where

\hat{A}_{q}(t)=\hat{U}_{q}^{\dagger}(t,t_{\mathrm{i}})
.
The
coupled
Hamiltonian in the interaction picture then becomes

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(t)=J\,\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}(t)\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{t}},{\mathrm{I}}}(t)

(34)

where

\hat{n}_{q,{\mathrm{I}}}(t)\coloneqq\hat{A}_{q}(t)\hat{n}_{q}\hat{A}_{q}^{\dagger}(t)

are the interaction-picture charge operators.

From the interaction-picture Hamiltonian,
it is clear that transitions occur due to the coupling

\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}(t)\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{t}},{\mathrm{I}}}(t)
.
Therefore, we can calculate the transition probability from the product state

\ket{|}ki>
to
\ket{|}\ell j>

within perturbation theory as

P^{ki\to\ell j}=J^{2}\bigl|n_{{\mathrm{t}}}^{ji}\bigr|^{2}\biggl|\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\!\!n_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{\ell k}(t)e^{i\omega^{ji}_{\mathrm{t}}t}\,dt\biggr|^{2},

(35)

where

\omega_{q}^{ji}\coloneqq E^{j}_{q}-E^{i}_{q}
.
Given that
\Omega(t)
is explicitly defined to be zero outside the integration domain,
we “can” extend the integration limits and simply obtain

P^{ki\to\ell j}=J^{2}\bigl|n_{{\mathrm{t}}}^{ji}\bigr|^{2}\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}\{n_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{\ell k}\}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{ji})\bigr|^{\mathrlap{2}}\,,

(36)

using the Fourier transform convention

{\mathcal{F}}\{f\}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt
.
We can simplify

Eq.

36

further by averaging (summing) over initial (final) control states:

\displaystyle P^{i\to j}_{\mathrm{t}}={}

\displaystyle J^{2}\bigl|n_{{\mathrm{t}}}^{ji}\bigr|^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{ji})\,,

(37)

where

{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{1}\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}\{n_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{\ell k}\}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}

(38)

is the effective energy spectral density of the control fluxonium’s charge operator.

Equation

37

is useful since we have fixed the control-fluxonium parameters,
so the effective ESD needs to be computed only once,
but the allocation of target-fluxonium parameters remain to be determined.
The extension of the integration limits in going from

Eq.

35

to

Eq.

36

requires careful justification since the integrand does not always decay at long times.
For a more rigorous derivation of

Eq.

37

using Floquet theory,
see
Appendix

G
.
To be precise, the ESD we calculate
is averaged over all carrier phases
as opposed to a fixed sinusoidal carrier.

Zero-collision yield

An important consideration in fixed-frequency architectures with microwave activated gates
is the onset of frequency collisions in larger systems.
The case of transmons in a CR architecture has previously been studied using

must be less than

{

}

Here, we show the results of similar Monte Carlo simulations to assess whether frequency collisions
ultimately limit the scalability of the fluxonium CR architecture, when targeting a gate error of
{10}^{-4}

The probability of having zero frequency collisions (the zero-collision yield)
is calculated for three different qubit connectivities:
square, hexagonal and heavy-hexagonal.
Their geometric arrangements and qubit frequency allocation patterns are shown in

Fig.

The qubits alternate between control and target roles,
with only a single low frequency control-qubit species.

during parallel operation of CNOT gates,

not analyzed in this work.

from the previous sections

a control qubit and any of its neighbors by comparing only their
(Stark-shifted)

V
Conclusion

In this work,
we have shown that an all-fluxonium cross-resonance (CR) architecture
leveraging only direct capacitive coupling provides a practical and easy to implement path toward larger multi-fluxonium processors.
Despite the characteristically small charge dipole moment
associated with low-frequency fluxonium qubits,
our exploration indicates that a CR-based CNOT gate is generally achievable
in

{

200

}

with a residual
ZZ
rate of

{

50

}

.
Meanwhile,
the large anharmonicity of the fluxonium complements the microwave-activated CR architecture by mitigating frequency collisions.
Assuming a Josephson energy disorder of

{

1

}

(achievable with junction annealing

38
;
123
;
87
),
we find that zero-collision yields remain above
0.5

for square lattices supporting up to a distance-21 rotated surface code.
This represents a substantial improvement over
an all-transmon square lattice,
where, for the same variation in Josephson energies,
Ref.
38

calculated a yield below
0.01

already for the much smaller distance-3 layout.
Beyond the scalability of the all-fluxonium CR architecture,
calibrating the CR-based CNOT gate is also straightforward

100
;
88
;
our time-domain simulations of a two-fluxonium system
show coherent errors comfortably below
{10}^{-4}

when using only soft square pulses.

Our study of the CR effect in the strong driving regime further
revealed a universal saturation characteristic in the effective
ZX
strength,
slightly exceeding the bare qubit-qubit exchange coupling rate

J|n_{\mathrm{c}}^{10}n_{\mathrm{t}}^{10}|
.
This holds across a wide range of qubit-qubit detunings,
provided the lower-frequency qubit acts as the control,
in sharp contrast to the narrow straddling regime
typically needed for fast CR gates between transmons

111
;
63
.
However,
the drive amplitude that saturates the
ZX
interaction
can be up to five times larger than
what is used in resonant single-qubit driving.
This can lead to non-negligible heating and a degradation in qubit coherence

131
;
78
.
Such strong driving can also enable multiphoton resonances,
which may induce leakage despite the large anharmonicity of the fluxonium.
Nevertheless, we still find that the majority of frequency collisions arise from
first-order resonances between closely spaced qubit frequencies,
which could be effectively minimized by using spectral pulse shaping techniques

77
;
57
;
47
.

As a blueprint for future experiments,
we propose a lattice of alternating control and target qubits,
where the higher-frequency targets serve as auxiliary qubits for parity measurements in a surface code or similar error-correction framework.
The measurement fidelity of these higher-frequency auxiliary qubits can then be enhanced
by using flux-pulse-assisted readout
while avoiding errors from swapping with temporarily resonant neighbors

107
.

Appendix A
Capacitive–inductive drive symmetry

To argue that the results of the main text
are independent of whether the control fluxonium is capacitively or inductively driven,
we show the charge dipole moments
between Floquet modes
are
mostly
identical in both cases.
The calculations here are carried out in the extended Hilbert space,
where the time-periodic Hamiltonian,

Eq.

2
,
is made time independent, see also

Eq.

57
,
by promoting the time parameter
t

to an operator
\hat{\tau}
.
Its conjugate pair is the frequency operator

-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}
.
Together, they satisfy the canonical commutation relations (CCR) just like

\hat{\varphi}
and
\hat{n}
:

[\hat{\tau},-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}]=[\hat{\varphi},\hat{n}]=i.

Only the control fluxonium
in isolation
is analyzed
in this section,
so the ‘
{\mathrm{c}}
’ subscripts are dropped for simplicity.

The drive term of

Eq.

57

can be absorbed into the charge operator by redefining it as

\hat{n}^{\prime}\coloneqq\hat{n}+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{cap}}}{8E_{C}}\sin(\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)\,,

(41)

where we add the subscript ‘
{\mathrm{cap}}

to distinguish this amplitude from the inductive drive amplitude introduced later.
The Floquet Hamiltonian in terms of

\hat{{H}}=\hat{H}_{0}^{\prime}-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{cap}}^{2}}{32E_{C}}\cos(2\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)-\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{cap}}^{2}}{32E_{C}}\,,

(42)

where
\hat{H}_{0}^{\prime}

is the fluxonium Hamiltonian
from

Eq.

4

but with
\hat{n}
replaced by

\hat{n}^{\prime}
.
Because the term absorbed into
\hat{n}^{\prime}

commutes with
\hat{\varphi}
,
we preserve their CCR:

[\hat{\varphi},\hat{n}^{\prime}]=i
.
However,
the frequency operator should be redefined to

such that it commutes with

\hat{n}^{\prime}
.

The Floquet Hamiltonian in terms of

\hat{{H}}=\hat{H}_{0}^{\prime}-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}^{\prime}+\Omega_{\mathrm{ind}}\cos(\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)\hat{\varphi}-2E_{C}\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{ind}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\right)^{2}\!,

(\hat{\varphi},\hat{n}^{\prime})

that there exists

\hat{U}
such that

36

\displaystyle\hat{U}\hat{\varphi}\,\hat{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\hat{\varphi}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{U}\hat{\tau}\,\hat{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\hat{\tau}\,,

(45a)

\displaystyle\hat{U}\hat{n}^{\prime}\hat{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\hat{U}(-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}^{\prime})\hat{U}^{\dagger}

\hat{U}=\exp\!\biggl(\frac{i\Omega_{\mathrm{cap}}}{8E_{C}}\sin(\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)\hat{\varphi}+\frac{i\Omega_{\mathrm{cap}}^{2}}{64E_{C}\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\sin(2\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)\!\biggr)\,\mathrlap{.}

(46)

in ket notation.
The overlap of two states

|\alpha\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}},|\beta\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

in

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{ext}}

is inherited from its tensor product factors, so it is naturally

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\alpha|\beta\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}}\langle\alpha(\tau)|\beta(\tau)\rangle\,d\tau\,.

(54)

In the extended Hilbert space,
the two-time equation,

Eq.

51
,
is promoted to a genuine Schrödinger equation,

i\partial_{s}|\Psi(s)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\hat{{H}}(J,\Omega(s))|\Psi(s)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\,,

(55)

by defining the Floquet Hamiltonian

\hat{{H}}\coloneqq\hat{H}-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}

where

\hat{\partial}_{\tau}\coloneqq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}ik\omega_{\mathrm{d}}|e_{k}\rangle\langle e_{k}|

(56)

acts on
\mathcal{T}
.
The advantage of the extended space representation is that
time-
dependent
Floquet modes
become time-
independent
eigenstates
of the Floquet Hamiltonian.
Further,
because

\hat{{H}}(J,\Omega)

has no explicit time-dependence,
the evolution is manifestly adiabatic
if
\Omega
is slowly varying.
As only the control qubit is driven,
we can instead decompose the extended space as

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{ext}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{ext}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{t}}

where

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{ext}}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}
.
The control qubit is then endowed with its own Floquet Hamiltonian,

\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)\coloneqq\hat{H}_{{\mathrm{c}}}^{\mathrm{idle}}-i\hat{\partial}_{\tau}+\Omega\sin(\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,

(57)

where

\sin(
\omega_\drivelabel\hat\tau)=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigl(|e_{k+1}\rangle\langle e_{k}|-|e_{k}\rangle\langle e_{k+1}|\bigr)

(58)

also acts on
\mathcal{T}
,
and the total Floquet Hamiltonian can be reorganized as

\hat{{H}}=\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}+\hat{H}_{{\mathrm{t}}}+J\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{n}_{\mathrm{t}}\,.

(59)

This form lends itself to the direct application of time-independent perturbation theory.
Once the eigensystems of

\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}

and

\hat{H}_{{\mathrm{t}}}

are known,
we can perturbatively incorporate the weak
J
-coupling
to block-diagonalize
\hat{{H}}
.

We adopt the conventional basis where

\langle i|\hat{n}_{q}|j\rangle
are purely imaginary.
Consequently, the sinusoid in

Eq.

57

makes the Floquet Hamiltonian
possess only real matrix elements
in the
bare basis
,

\{|e_{k}\rangle\otimes|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle\,|\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\,,

(60)

i.e., it is manifestly time-reversal symmetric.

B.2
Semi-analytical block diagonalization

Let

\{|\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

be instantaneous eigenstates of

\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)

with eigenvalues
\epsilon_{j}(\Omega)+k\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
.
The index
j
is defined such that
the modes with
k=0
are adiabatically connected to the undriven eigenstates:

\displaystyle\ket{|}\Phi_{j,k=0}(\Omega=0,\tau)>\coloneqq\ket{|}j>\,,

(61)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}(\Omega=0)=E_{\mathrm{c}}^{j}\,.

(62)

The phases of the
\Omega\neq 0
modes
are chosen so that their Fourier coefficients
in the bare basis remain real.
Further,
the
k\neq 0
modes are all defined to be in phase
with the
k=0
mode:

\ket{|}\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega,\tau)>\coloneqq e^{ik\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\ket{|}\Phi_{j,0}(\Omega,\tau)>\,.

(63)

Equations

61
and
63

uniquely fixes the Floquet eigenbasis,
with the states automatically satisfying parallel transport,

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)|\partial_{\Omega}|\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=0
,
due to our phase choice
12
.

The Floquet Hamiltonian in

Eq.

59

must be perturbatively block diagonalized
because the target qubit is resonantly driven by the CR pulse,

\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\approx E_{\mathrm{t}}^{1}-E_{\mathrm{t}}^{0}
.
The eigensystem of

\hat{{H}}(J=0,\Omega)

therefore possesses a ladder of nearly-degenerate doublets:

\displaystyle|{\uparrow}_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrlap{|\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}}\phantom{|\Phi_{j,k-1}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}}\otimes\ket{|}0>\,,

(64a)

\displaystyle|{\downarrow}_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq|\Phi_{j,k-1}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\otimes\ket{|}1>\,.

(64b)

These doublets also satisfy the parallel transport criteria within the subspace they span, i.e.,

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\sigma}_{j,k}|\partial_{\Omega}|{\varsigma}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=0

for
\sigma,\varsigma\in\{{\uparrow},{\downarrow}\}
.
This is because the diagonal entries,

\sigma=\varsigma
,
are always zero by our phase convention and
the off-diagonal entries are also zero because

|0\rangle

and
|1\rangle

are orthogonal.

A finite
J
coupling splits the near-degeneracies, mixing the bare doublets.
Let

\hat{S}(J,\Omega)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J^{n}\hat{S}^{(n)}(\Omega)

be the Schrieffer–Wolff (SW) generator that rotates the bare doublets into the dressed doublets:

|\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}/\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\coloneqq e^{-\hat{S}(J,\Omega)}|{\uparrow}/{\downarrow}_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\,,

(65)

such that

\mathcal{V}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)=\Span\{|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}},|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\}

(66)

are invariant subspaces of
\hat{{H}}(J,\Omega)
.
Each
(j,k)
-block of the Hamiltonian is generally of the form

\hat{{H}}_{j,k}=(\epsilon_{j}+\delta\epsilon_{j}+k\omega_{\mathrm{d}})+g_{j}\hat{X}_{j,k}+\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{d}}+\zeta_{j}}{2}\hat{Z}_{j,k}\,,

(67)

where

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\hat{X}_{j,k}$}]{\hat{X}_{j,k}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\phantom{i}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}|\mathmakebox[\widthof{$i-i$}]{+}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}|\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\hat{X}_{j,k}$}]{\hat{Y}_{j,k}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq i|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}|\mathmakebox[\widthof{$i-i$}]{\phantom{i}-i}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}|\,,

(68b)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\hat{X}_{j,k}$}]{\hat{Z}_{j,k}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\phantom{i}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}|\mathmakebox[\widthof{$i-i$}]{-}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}|

(68c)

are the Pauli matrices in the dressed doublet basis
(their dependence on
J
and
\Omega
have been omitted).
Recall that
\hat{{H}}
has only real matrix elements,
which explains why
\hat{Y}_{j,k}
does not appear
in the general form of each block,

Eq.

67
.
The interpretation of the coefficients
are as follows.
The parenthesized constant
is the energy of the
(j,k)
-Floquet mode
with a correction
\delta\epsilon_{j}=O(J^{2})
.
The induced Rabi rate
on the target qubit is
2g_{j}=O(J)
,
which may be positive or negative.
The drive detuning from the bare target-qubit frequency is

\Delta_{\mathrm{d}}\coloneqq\omega_{\mathrm{d}}-(E_{\mathrm{t}}^{1}-E_{\mathrm{t}}^{0})
.
The control-dependent shift to the target-qubit frequency
is
\zeta_{j}=O(J^{2})
.
Only
g_{j}
represents a resonant interaction
and thus appears at first order.
The non-resonant corrections

\delta\epsilon_{j}
and
\zeta_{j}

arise at second order.

The leading order contribution to
g_{j}(J,\Omega)
is simply

\displaystyle g_{j}(J,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,0}(J,\Omega)|\hat{{H}}(J,\Omega)|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,0}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(69)

\displaystyle=Jn_{\mathrm{c}}^{10}n_{\mathrm{t}}^{10}p_{j}(\Omega)+O(J^{3})\,,

(70)

where

n_{q}^{10}\coloneqq\innerproduct{<}{<}1|\hat{n}_{q}|0>

and

\displaystyle p_{j}(\Omega)\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{10}_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\Phi_{j,-1}(\Omega)|\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}|\Phi_{j,0}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(71)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{10}_{\mathrm{c}}}\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}}\bra{<}\Phi_{j,0}(\Omega,\tau)|\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}\ket{|}\Phi_{j,0}(\Omega,\tau)>e^{i\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\,d\tau

is the charge expectation of the
j
th Floquet mode,
oscillating at the frequency
-\omega_{\mathrm{d}}

and normalized by
n^{10}_{\mathrm{c}}
.
Parity selection rules in

\hat{n}_{\mathrm{t}}

forbid even powers of
J
from entering the perturbative expansion of
g_{j}
.

B.3
Parallel-transported basis

The dressed doublets satisfy parallel transport to first order in the
J
-direction.
That is,
for

\sigma,\varsigma\in\{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow},\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}\}
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\sigma_{j,k}(J,\Omega)|\partial_{J}|\varsigma_{j,k}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(72)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\sigma_{j,k}(0,\Omega)|e^{\hat{S}(J,\Omega)}\partial_{J}e^{-\hat{S}(J,\Omega)}|\varsigma_{j,k}(0,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(73)

\displaystyle\quad=\underbrace{\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\sigma_{j,k}(0,\Omega)|[-\hat{S}^{(1)}(J,\Omega)-2J\hat{S}^{(2)}(J,\Omega)]|\varsigma_{j,k}(0,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}}_{=\,0}

\displaystyle\phantom{\quad={}}{}+O(J^{2})\,.

(74)

The matrix element vanishes
by virtue of the SW transformation being a direct rotation between degenerate subspaces,
i.e., the bare doublets are in the kernel of
\hat{S}^{(n)}
for all orders

16
.
Hence, the dressed doublets can alternatively be characterized as

|\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}/\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\hat{\Gamma}^{A}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)|\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}/\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}_{j,k}(0,0)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}+O(J^{3})\,,

(75)

where

\hat{\Gamma}^{A}_{j,k}(J,\Omega):\mathcal{V}_{j,k}(0,0)\to\mathcal{V}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)

is an isomorphism of subspaces which parallel transports its input vector
along a curve
in the control space
\{(J,\Omega)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\}
,
defined as the polyline from
(0,0)
to
(0,\Omega)
to
(J,\Omega)
.
This is shown pictorially as the curve
A
in
Fig.

16
.
Since
Eq.

72
vanishes up to
O(J^{2})
,
the discrepancy becomes
O(J^{3})

in
Eq.

75

after integration over a length of
J
.

Another gauge choice performs the parallel transport

\hat{\Gamma}^{B}_{j,k}

along
the other sides of the rectangle:

(0,0)
to
(J,0)
and then
(J,\Omega)
,
see curve
B
in
Fig.

16
.
We distinguish these states as the
primed
doublets,

|\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}^{\prime}/\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}_{j,k}^{\prime}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\coloneqq\hat{\Gamma}^{B}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)|\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}/\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}_{j,k}(0,0)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\,.

(76)

They satisfy, by construction, parallel transport along
\Omega
:

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\sigma^{\prime}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)|\partial_{\Omega}|\varsigma^{\prime}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=0\,.

(77)

The naturalness of the primed doublets is apparent
when considering the change of basis

\hat{P}
,
whose
(j,k)
-blocks are

\hat{P}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)=\ \sum_{\mathclap{\sigma\in\text{\scriptsize$\{\raise 0.60275pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.90417pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow},\raise 0.60275pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}\}$}}}\ |\sigma_{j,k}(J,\Omega=0)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\sigma^{\prime}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)|\,.

(78)

The inertial terms,

i\dot{\Omega}\hat{P}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\partial_{\Omega}\hat{P}^{\dagger}\vphantom{P}_{j_{2},k_{2}}
,
introduced into the transformed Hamiltonian
when
\Omega
is time-dependent
vanish when
(j_{1},k_{1})=(j_{2},k_{2})
by virtue of

Eq.

77
.
The block-off-diagonal terms
(
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
or
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
)
can be neglected by the adiabatic theorem
provided they are much smaller than the inter-block energy gaps.
The upshot is, in the adiabatic limit,

\hat{P}

may be regarded as a time-independent transformation.

(83)

Its naive solution is

\hat{\Gamma}^{C}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\hat{Y}_{j,k}\Bigr)+O(J^{3})\,,

(84)

which represents
a rotation of
\gamma_{j}
around the
Y

Geometrically,

\hat{\Gamma}^{C}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)

is the holonomy acting on

\mathcal{V}_{j,k}(J,\Omega)

obtained by parallel transport around the rectangular loop,
curve
C
in

16
,
with vertices, and in order:

(J,\Omega)
,

(0,\Omega)
,

(0,0)
,

(J,0)
.
This is also known as the Wilczek–Zee phase

121

The loop collapses
when
\Omega=0

and we have, accordingly,
\gamma_{j}=0

and
\hat{\Gamma}^{C}_{j,k}
acts trivially as expected.

The naive guess turns out to be correct
despite

\hat{Y}_{j,k}
varying with
\Omega

This is because

\partial_{\Omega}\hat{Y}_{j,k}

vanishes on

\mathcal{V}_{j,k}

for the following reasons.
The operator

is anti-Hermitian and has real matrix elements

Since the dressed doublets
are purely real

the diagonal of

must be identically zero.
A quick computation shows the off-diagonal also vanishes:

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}|\bigl(i\partial_{\Omega}\hat{Y}_{j,k}\bigr)|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle\ \quad=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}|\partial_{\Omega}\bigl(i\hat{Y}_{j,k}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\bigr)-\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.29167pt\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\uparrow}\vphantom{\uparrow}}_{j,k}|i\hat{Y}_{j,k}\partial_{\Omega}|{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hss}\phantom{\downarrow}\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{j,k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(90)

Appendix C
Double well renormalization

The potential energy for a fluxonium with
inductive energy
E_{L}

and Josephson energy
E_{J}

at half-flux bias is

V_{E_{L},E_{J}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}E_{L}\varphi^{2}+E_{J}\cos\varphi-E_{J}\,.

(98)

Contrary to convention,
the extra
-E_{J}
is included to zero the potential energy at
\varphi=0
.
Instead of providing the inductive and Josephson energies,
the double-well potential can alternatively be parameterized in terms of the interwell distance and barrier height.
Let
0\leq a<\pi
be the position of the first minima of the potential.
It is related to the inductive-to-Josephson energy ratio transcendentally through

\sinc a=\frac{E_{L}}{E_{J}}\,,

(99)

where
\sinc x\coloneqq\sin x/x
.
For the existence of at least two minima,
E_{L}
must be smaller than
E_{J}
.
Typical fluxonium
E_{L}/E_{J}
ratios vary between
0.1
and
0.5

84
.
This corresponds to minimum positions in the range
\tfrac{1}{2}\pi<a<\tfrac{11}{12}\pi
.
The depth
b>0
of the two minima relative to zero is

b=-V_{E_{L},E_{J}}(a)=E_{J}(1-\cos a-\frac{a}{2}\sin a)\,.

(100)

We can therefore reparameterize the fluxonium potential at half-flux bias
in terms of
a
and
b
as

W_{a,b}(\varphi)\coloneqq b[\frac{\frac{1}{2}(\sinc a)\varphi^{2}+\cos\varphi-1}{1-\cos a-\frac{a}{2}\sin a}]\,,

(101)

which equals
V_{E_{L},E_{J}}(\varphi)

when

Eqs.

99
and
100

hold.

For
a\ll\pi
,
the wavefunction is confined to a region where the cosine is quartic.
In this limit, the double-well potential is self-similar
under simultaneous rescaling of
a
and
\varphi

since

W_{a,b}(\varphi)\approx b[(\varphi/a)^{4}-2(\varphi/a)^{2}]\,.

(102)

For the typical
inductive-to-Josephson energy ratios,
the quartic approximation is crude.

Appendix D
Time-glide-reflection symmetry

The fluxonium Hamiltonian,

Eq.

4
,
is invariant under parity inversion

\hat{\mathcal{P}}
,
which maps the coordinate
\varphi
to
-\varphi
,
when biased at half-flux quantum,

\varphi_{\mathrm{ext}}=\pi
.
We will only analyze the control fluxonium
in this appendix,
so we omit the ‘
{\mathrm{c}}
’ subscripts for simplicity.
The Hamiltonian with a time-periodic drive,

Eq.

2
,
breaks parity symmetry because

\hat{n}
is odd under parity.
However,
the Hamiltonian is invariant under the combined transformation
of a half-period translation in time followed by parity inversion:

\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{H}(t+\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}=\hat{H}(t)
.
This is also known as time-glide-reflection symmetry,
in analogy with spatial glide reflection symmetry

74
.
In the extended Hilbert space,
time-glide-reflection is represented by the unitary transformation

\hat{\mathcal{M}}=\exp(\pi\hat\partial_\tau/\omega_\drivelabel)\hat{\mathcal{P}}
.
Likewise, the Floquet Hamiltonian,

Eq.

57
,
is invariant under

\hat{\mathcal{M}}
; that is,

\hat{\mathcal{M}}\hat{{H}}\hat{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}=\hat{{H}}
.
Since time-glide-reflection is self-inverse,

\hat{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\hat{I}
,
its eigenvalues are either
+1
or
-1
.
The two eigenspaces of
\hat{\mathcal{M}}

are invariant subspaces of

\hat{{H}}
,
so it suffices to restrict our attention to only
those eigenstates that are even under
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
.

To numerically obtain Floquet modes,
one generally first computes the time-evolution operator

\hat{U}(t_{1},t_{0})={\mathcal{T}}\exp\biggl(\!-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\hat{H}(t)\,dt\biggr),

(103)

where
{\mathcal{T}}
indicates time ordering,
over one full period,
t_{1}-t_{0}=2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
.
Then, solving the eigenequation

\hat{U}(t_{0}+2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}},t_{0})|\Phi_{j}(t_{0})\rangle=e^{-i\epsilon_{j}(2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})}|\Phi_{j}(t_{0})\rangle

(104)

produces the Floquet modes
|\Phi_{j}(t)\rangle
at
t=t_{0}
and their quasienergies
\epsilon_{j}
.
The modes at later times can be reconstructed using

|\Phi_{j}(t_{0}+t)\rangle=e^{i\epsilon_{j}t}\hat{U}(t_{0}+t,t_{0})|\Phi_{j}(t_{0})\rangle\,.

(105)

Time-glide-reflection symmetric modes further satisfy

|\Phi_{j}(t_{0})\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{P}}|\Phi_{j}(t_{0}+\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\rangle\,.

(106)

Applying

Eq.

106

to

Eq.

105

when
t=\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}

gives

\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{U}(t_{0}+\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}},t_{0})|\Phi_{j}(t_{0})\rangle=e^{-i\epsilon_{j}(\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})}|\Phi_{j}(t_{0})\rangle\,.

(107)

Solutions of this eigenequation are time-glide-reflection symmetric Floquet modes.
Two benefits arise from this.
First,
the time-evolution operator only needs to be solved over half a period
which is more computationally efficient.
Second,
accidental degeneracies between opposite parity modes are prevented because
the quasienergies now wrap over
2\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
.
One drawback, however, is that

|\Phi_{j}(t)\rangle

can no longer connect to

|j\rangle
at zero drive amplitude since the odd
excited states change sign under time-glide-reflection.
In our calculations,
we use an alternative convention where

\epsilon_{j}+\omega_{\mathrm{d}}

for odd
j

correspond to the bare energy at zero drive.

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CR}}(\chi,\theta,\phi)\coloneqq\exp[-i(\!\frac{\chi}{2}\hat{Z}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{X}_{\mathrm{t}}+\frac{\theta}{2}\hat{X}_{\mathrm{t}}+\frac{\phi}{2}\hat{Z}_{\mathrm{c}}\!)\!]\,.

(108)

For the purpose of this appendix, we set aside the distinction between dressed and bare states.
The case
\chi=\tfrac{\pi}{2}
is locally equivalent to the CNOT gate,
differing by only control-qubit
Z
and target-qubit
X
rotations.
We view this to be a trivial difference,
and therefore define a family of CNOT unitaries considered to be equivalent,

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CNOT}}(\theta,\phi)\coloneqq\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CR}}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\theta-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\phi-\tfrac{\pi}{2})\,,

(109)

with the standard CNOT matrix given by
\theta=\phi=0
, up to an overall phase.
The average CNOT fidelity is then understood to mean the maximal average gate fidelity to

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CNOT}}(\theta,\phi)
,
optimized over
\theta
and
\phi
:

F_{\mathrm{CNOT}}(\hat{M})\coloneqq\max_{(\theta,\phi)}F(\hat{M},\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CNOT}}(\theta,\phi))\,,

(110)

where

F(\hat{M},\hat{U}_{0})=\frac{1}{20}\bigl[\Tr(\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M})+|\Tr(\hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{M})|^{2}\bigr]\,,

(111)

is a formula for calculating the average gate fidelity
to an ideal two-qubit unitary
\hat{U}_{0}

89
.
The imperfect gate
\hat{M}
need not be unitary since it is usually obtained by
truncating a unitary acting on the full Hilbert space to the computational subspace.

It follows from
Eq.

111

that the optimal angles
\theta^{\star}
and
\phi^{\star}

of
Eq.

110

satisfy

(\theta^{\star},\phi^{\star})=\argmax_{(\theta,\phi)}\bigl|\Tr\bigl(\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CNOT}}^{\dagger}(\theta,\phi)\hat{M}\bigr)\bigr|^{2}.

(112)

By decomposing the trace as

\bigl|\Tr\bigl(\hat{U}_{\mathrm{CNOT}}^{\dagger}(\theta,\phi)\hat{M}\bigr)\bigr|=\bigl|z_{+}(\phi)e^{i\theta/2}+z_{-}(\phi)e^{-i\theta/2}\bigr|

(113)

where

\displaystyle z_{\pm}(\phi)\coloneqq r_{0\pm}e^{i(\gamma_{0\pm}+\phi/2)}\pm r_{1\pm}e^{i(\gamma_{1\pm}-\phi/2)}\,,

(114)

\displaystyle r_{j\pm}e^{i\gamma_{j\pm}}\coloneqq\langle{j\pm}|\hat{M}|{j\pm}\rangle\,,

(115)

we deduce the constraint

\theta^{\star}=\angle z_{-}(\phi^{\star})-\angle z_{+}(\phi^{\star})
,
which reduces

Eq.

112

to a one-dimensional maximization,

\phi^{\star}=\argmax_{\phi}\bigl\{|z_{+}(\phi)|+|z_{-}(\phi)|\bigr\}\,.

(116)

The magnitudes in

Eq.

116

in terms of
r_{j\pm}
and
\gamma_{j\pm}
are

|z_{\pm}(\phi)|=\sqrt{r_{0\pm}^{2}+r_{1\pm}^{2}\pm 2r_{0\pm}r_{1\pm}\cos(
\phi-
\delta_\pm)}\,,

(117)

where

\delta_{\pm}\coloneqq\gamma_{1\pm}-\gamma_{0\pm}
.

Equation

116
,
in general, has no closed-form solution.
However,
if

|j\pm\rangle

are approximate eigenvectors of
\hat{M}
,
we have
r_{j\pm}\approx 1

and this leads to

\displaystyle|z_{+}(\phi)|

\displaystyle\approx 2\Bigl|\cos\Bigl(\frac{\phi-\delta_{+}}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|\,,

(118)

\displaystyle|z_{-}(\phi)|

\displaystyle\approx 2\Bigl|\sin\Bigl(\frac{\phi-\delta_{-}}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|\,,

(119)

whose sum is maximized by

\phi^{\star}\approx\delta_{-}+\frac{1}{2}\bmod\!(\delta_{+}-\delta_{-},2\pi)+\frac{\pi}{2}\,,

(120)

where
\bmod(x,y)
is the modulo operator that wraps
x
within
[0,y)
.
This approximation can then be used as an initial guess
for solving
Eq.

116
iteratively.

In the presence of small
\mu_{IZ}
or
\mu_{ZZ}
,
the final unitary will contain the Pauli products

\hat{I}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{Z}_{\mathrm{t}}
,

\hat{Z}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{Z}_{\mathrm{t}}
,

\hat{I}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{t}}

and

\hat{Z}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{t}}
.
It is not possible to distinguish whether these errors originate from

\mu_{IZ}
or
\mu_{ZZ}

by examining the final unitary alone,
as their effects are mixed by the dominant Hamiltonian terms.
Hence, we refer to both contributions collectively as “phase error” in

Fig.

7
.

Appendix G
Effective energy spectral density derivation

The effective Hamiltonian of

Eq.

11

is valid when only the target-qubit transition is resonantly driven.
The adiabatic theorem implies that
off-resonant transitions do not induce population transfer
in the instantaneous eigenbasis.
Adiabaticity is violated when the
instantaneous energy gaps narrow
during the CR drive.
This violation can occur in two ways.
In the first case,
ramping the drive too quickly
leads to diabatic transitions between control-fluxonium eigenstates,
as shown in
Fig.

8
.
In the second case,
the control fluxonium
would
evolve adiabatically
in the absence of coupling (
J=0
),
but finite coupling permits
joint transitions between control-fluxonium Floquet eigenstates
and target-fluxonium eigenstates.
Diabatic errors in the second case
scale as
O(J^{2})

and can be analyzed
by building upon the semi-analytic framework developed in

Appendix

B
.

G.1
Drive-activated scattering

As defined in
Section

B.2
,
the Floquet Hamiltonian

\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)

has eigenstates

\{|\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

with eigenvalues

\epsilon_{j}(\Omega)+k\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
.
Further, their phases are defined such that

\operatorname{Im}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)|\partial_{\Omega}|\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=0
.
If the drive envelope
\Omega(s)
varies sufficiently slowly,
the evolution of the driven control qubit
in isolation
,
follows the adiabatic trajectory

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\mathrm{c}}(s_{1},s_{0})\coloneq\!\smash{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathrlap{0}}\\
k\in\mathrlap{{\mathbb{Z}}}\phantom{{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathrlap{0}}}\end{subarray}}}\,\biggl\{\!

\displaystyle\exp\biggl({-i}\!\int_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\!\!\epsilon_{j}(\Omega(s))+k\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\,ds\biggr)

(121)

\displaystyle\times|\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega(s_{1}))\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega(s_{0}))|\biggr\}.

For the isolated target qubit, its evolution is simply

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{t}}(s_{1},s_{0})\coloneqq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\exp\bigl({-i}E_{\mathrm{t}}^{j}\times(s_{1}-s_{0})\bigr)|j\rangle\langle j|\,.

(122)

Our goal is to perturbatively study how the interaction

J\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{n}_{\mathrm{t}}

affects the joint evolution of the two qubits.
To facilitate this,
we move into an interaction picture
where the isolated evolutions,

Eqs.

121
and
122
,
are rendered static by transforming states
from the Schrödinger picture by
the unitary

\hat{A}(s)\coloneqq\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}(s)\otimes\hat{A}_{\mathrm{t}}(s)

where

\hat{A}_{q}(s)\coloneqq\hat{U}_{q}^{\dagger}(s,s_{\mathrm{ref}})

(123)

and
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
is an arbitrary reference time
when the drive is off,
\Omega(s_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
.
The full Hamiltonian,

Eq.

59
,
in this interaction picture is

\displaystyle\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{I}}(s)\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\hat{A}(s)\bigl[\hat{{H}}(\Omega(s))-i\partial_{s}\bigr]\hat{A}^{\dagger}(s)

(124)

\displaystyle\approx{}

\displaystyle J\bigl[\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}(s)\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}(s)\bigr]\bigl[\hat{A}_{\mathrm{t}}(s)\hat{n}_{\mathrm{t}}\hat{A}_{\mathrm{t}}^{\dagger}(s)\bigr]\,.

(125)

The approximation in the last line follows from the adiabatic theorem,
which states that

i\partial_{s}\hat{A}^{\dagger}(s)\approx\hat{{H}}_{0}(\Omega(s))\hat{A}^{\dagger}(s)\,,

(126)

where
\hat{{H}}_{0}
is the full Hamiltonian,

Eq.

59
,
with
J=0
.

The true adiabatic limit is generally unattainable
since small avoided crossings often appear in the quasienergy spectrum
(see, for example,
Fig.

2
)

120
;
40
.
Very narrow gaps
(e.g., less than
2\pi\times\quantity{10}{}
),
are assumed to be traversed completely diabatically at the cost of
introducing
small discontinuities
in the unitary of

Eq.

121
.
However,
for “intermediate” gaps,
the adiabatic approximation breaks down.

Let the evolution unitary of the coupled system
in the interaction picture be

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{I}}(s_{1},s_{0})={\mathcal{T}}\exp(\!-i\int_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{I}}(\Omega(s))\,ds)\,,

(127)

where
{\mathcal{T}}
indicates time ordering.
Given two eigenstates
of the idle Hamiltonian

\hat{{H}}(\Omega=0)
,
say

|\widetilde{\text{\char 16\relax n}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

and

|\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-1.93747pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{out}}$}\hss}{\mathrm{out}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
,
the probability

|\widetilde{\text{\char 16\relax n}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

is driven to

|\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-1.93747pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{out}}$}\hss}{\mathrm{out}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

is

P^{{\mathrm{in}}\to{\mathrm{out}}}\coloneqq\bigl|\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-1.93747pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{out}}$}\hss}{\mathrm{out}}|\hat{U}_{\mathrm{I}}(c,-c)|\widetilde{\text{\char 16\relax n}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\bigr|^{2},

(128)

where
c>0
is some arbitrary cutoff such that
\Omega(s)=0
for
|s|>c
.
The transition probability does not depend on the value of
c

because

|\widetilde{\text{\char 16\relax n}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

and

|\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-1.93747pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{out}}$}\hss}{\mathrm{out}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

are eigenstates of

\hat{{H}}(\Omega=0)
.
To express

Eq.

128

in terms of bare eigenstates,
we suppose the coupling strength
J
is adiabatically switched on
by making the substitution
J\mapsto w_{b,c}(s)J

where
w_{b,c}(s)
is zero for
|s|>c
and ramps to unity for
|s|

smaller than another cutoff
b<c
.
The new cutoff is still large enough such that

\Omega(s)=0
for
|s|>b
.
This ensures the fictitious tuning of
J
does not influence the drive-activated scattering process.
The dressed states
in
Eq.

128

are then replaced by their bare counterparts,

|{\mathrm{in}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

and

|{\mathrm{out}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
,
which are eigenstates of the
uncoupled
idle Hamiltonian

\hat{{H}}_{0}(\Omega=0)
.
By expanding
Eq.

127

as a Dyson series
and
assuming that

|{\mathrm{in}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

and

|{\mathrm{out}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

are orthogonal,
we can compute the scattering probability to leading order as

P^{{\mathrm{in}}\to{\mathrm{out}}}\!=\!J^{2}\biggl|\int_{\mathrlap{-c}}^{c}\!w_{b,c}(s)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\mathrm{out}}|\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}(s)\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{t}},{\mathrm{I}}}(s)|{\mathrm{in}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}ds\biggr|^{2}\!\!\mathrlap{,}

(129)

where

\hat{n}_{q,{\mathrm{I}}}\coloneqq\hat{A}_{q}\hat{n}_{q}\hat{A}_{q}^{\dagger}

are the interaction-picture charge operators.
(The equality in

Eq.

129
,
as well as in subsequent equations,
should be understood to hold only to leading order in
J
.)
In the limit
b,c\to\infty
,
the integral can be replaced by a Fourier transform:

P^{{\mathrm{in}}\to{\mathrm{out}}}=J^{2}\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\mathrm{out}}|\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}(s)\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{t}},{\mathrm{I}}}(s)|{\mathrm{in}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}](0)\bigr|^{2}\!\!,

(130)

where the Fourier transform is defined as

{\mathcal{F}}[f(s)](\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(s)e^{-i\omega s}ds

for convergent improper integrals

59
.

To utilize

Eq.

130

semi-analytically,
suppose

|{\mathrm{in}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

and
|{\mathrm{out}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

are simple product states of the form

|ij^{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\coloneqq|e_{k}\rangle\otimes|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle
.
Since the Floquet Hamiltonian
is invariant up to a global energy shift

|e_{k}\rangle\mapsto|e_{k+\ell}\rangle
,
it suffices to consider ‘in’ states of the form

|ij^{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

and ‘out’ states of the form

|fg^{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
.

Equation

130

then becomes

P^{ij^{0}\to fg^{k}}=J^{2}\bigl|n_{\mathrm{t}}^{gj}\bigr|^{2}\bigl|\tilde{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k]fi}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{gj})\bigr|^{2}\!\!,

(131)

where

\tilde{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k]fi}(\omega)\coloneqq{\mathcal{F}}[n_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k]fi}(s)](\omega)

n_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k]fi}\!\coloneqq\Tr(\hat{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}|e_{0}\rangle\langle e_{k}|\otimes|i\rangle\langle f|)

can be simplified
by summing over the output indices:

\displaystyle P^{ij\to*}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{f,g\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}P^{ij^{0}\to fg^{k}}

(132)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle J^{2}\smash{\sum_{g\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}}\bigl|n_{\mathrm{t}}^{gj}\bigr|^{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{gj})\,,

(133)

where

{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{f\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\bigl|\tilde{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k]fi}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}

(134)

is the
effective energy spectral density

for the control-qubit charge operator,
when the qubit is
in the
i
state.
The summand
in
Eq.

132

is zero when

i=f
,

j=g

and
k=0
,
so
P^{ij\to*}
equals
the probability
for an initial

final state.

Since the target is resonantly driven,
the non-separable states

|i\pm^{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\coloneqq(|i0^{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\pm|i1^{k-1}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}})/\sqrt{2}

However, there is a minor subtlety in
the calculation of

the scattering probability
for a target qubit in the ‘
+

If the final state is an orthogonal product state,
this probability is simply

P^{i+^{0}\to fg^{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(P^{i0^{0}\to fg^{k}}+P^{i1^{0}\to fg^{k}}\bigr).

(135)

However,
if the final state is
|f\pm^{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

the probability is

\displaystyle P^{i+^{0}\to f\pm^{k}}=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(P^{i0^{0}\to f1^{k-1}}+P^{i1^{0}\to f0^{k+1}}\bigr)

(136)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\pm\frac{J^{2}|n_{\mathrm{t}}^{10}|^{2}}{2}\operatorname{Re}\Bigl\{\overline{\tilde{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k+1]fi}(\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{10})}\tilde{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[k-1]fi}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{10})\Bigr\}.

For the special case

i=f
and
k=0
,
we have

P^{i+^{0}\to i\pm^{0}}=\frac{1}{2}P^{i0^{0}\to i1^{-1}}\!\!\pm\frac{J^{2}|n_{\mathrm{t}}^{10}|^{2}}{2}\operatorname{Re}\bigl\{\tilde{n}_{{\mathrm{c}},{\mathrm{I}}}^{[-1]ii}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{10})^{2}\bigr\}.

(137)

This can be simplified by noting that the reference time

in

123

P^{i+^{0}\to i+^{0}}=P^{i0^{0}\to i1^{-1}}

P^{i+\to*}=\frac{J^{2}}{2}\sum_{g=2}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{1}\bigl|n_{\mathrm{t}}^{gj}\bigr|^{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(-\omega_{\mathrm{t}}^{gj})\,.

Appendix H
Infinite soft square pulse spectrum

The definition of the effective ESD

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{j}(\Omega)-\epsilon_{i}(\Omega)-k\omega_{\mathrm{d}}

and the Stark-shifted transition frequency,
respectively.
As we ultimately only care about the spectral density,
we set
s_{\mathrm{ref}}=0
,
since this only affects the overall phase of
f(s)
.
Let the drive envelope be a symmetric soft square pulse
with plateau duration
2\tau
and midpoint amplitude
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
,
we express this as

\Omega(s)=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\bigl[{\mathcal{R}}(s-\tau)+{\mathcal{R}}(-s-\tau)-1\bigr]\,,

(146)

where
{\mathcal{R}}(s)
is a ramp function
that smoothly interpolates between

{\mathcal{R}}(-\infty)=1

and
{\mathcal{R}}(+\infty)=0

over a characteristic ramp duration;
e.g.,
Eq.

31
.
We seek to compute the ESD
of
f(s)

averaged over long
\tau
,
such that the
\sinc
ripples in the spectrum are also averaged away.
This is not straightforward to directly calculate numerically,
so we first perform a series of analytic transformations to relate
the
\tau
-averaged spectrum to the spectrum of a compactly supported signal.

In the long
\tau
limit, we have the pointwise convergence

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\Omega(s+\tau)=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}{\mathcal{R}}(s)\,,

(147)

which implies

\displaystyle b(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{\tau\to\infty}a(s+\tau)\,,

(148)

\displaystyle\theta(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\bigl\{\phi(s+\tau)-\phi(\tau)\bigr\}

(149)

are both well defined and given by

\displaystyle b(s)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\Phi_{j,k}(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}{\mathcal{R}}(s))|\hat{n}_{\mathrm{c}}|\Phi_{i,0}(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}{\mathcal{R}}(s))\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\,,

(150)

\displaystyle\theta(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}{\mathcal{R}}(s^{\prime}))\,ds^{\prime}\,.

(151)

Define

g(s)\coloneqq b(s)e^{i\theta(s)}
.
For long
\tau
, we have

f(s+\tau)\approx g(s)e^{i\phi(\tau)}\approx g(s)e^{i[\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\tau+\delta]}\,,

(152)

where

\delta\coloneqq\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\{\phi(\tau)-\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\tau\}
.
At first sight,
it appears
this equation
establishes a relation between

{\mathcal{F}}[f(s)](\omega)

and

{\mathcal{F}}[g(s)](\omega)
,
however, it only holds for
s
greater than a finite lower bound
when
\tau
is fixed.
To address this,
we perform the transformation

f_{1}(s)\coloneqq\biggl(\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})+i\frac{d}{ds}\biggr)f(s)\,,

(153)

which corresponds to multiplication by

\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})-\omega

in frequency space.
Define
g_{1}(s)
analogously by replacing
f
with
g
.
The benefit of doing this is that the following relation now holds for all
s
,

f_{1}(s)\approx g_{1}(s-\tau)e^{i[\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\tau+\delta]}+g_{1}^{*}(-s-\tau)e^{-i[\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\tau+\delta]}\,.

(154)

The underlying idea is that

f_{1}(s)
is constructed to vanish on the pulse interior
and
g_{1}(s)
corresponds to the positive half of
f_{1}(s)
,
modulo the dynamical phase.
It follows from

Eq.

154

that

\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[f_{1}(s)](\omega)\bigr|^{2}=2\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[g_{1}(s)](\omega)\bigr|^{2}+\text{oscillatory}\,,

(155)

where the omitted terms are oscillatory in
\tau

and will be averaged away,
except when
\omega=\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})
.
By using this equation and dropping the oscillatory terms,
the ESD of the original function
f(s)
can be expressed as

\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[f(s)](\omega)\bigr|^{2}=\frac{2\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[g_{1}(s)](\omega)\bigr|^{2}}{\bigl[\omega-\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\bigr]^{2}}\,.

(156)

The contribution of the dropped terms
when
\omega=\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})

is irrelevant since
the spectrum
anyway diverges there.

We may factor
g_{1}(s)
into
b_{1}(s)e^{i\theta(s)}
where

\displaystyle b_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl[\nu(s)-\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\bigr]b(s)+i\frac{db(s)}{ds}\,,

(157)

\displaystyle\nu(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{d\theta(s)}{ds}=\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}{\mathcal{R}}(s))\,.

(158)

Notice that

b_{1}(-\infty)=0

because
the first term of

Eq.

157

vanishes due to

\nu(-\infty)=\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})

and the second term vanishes in either limit
s\to\pm\infty
.
This is consistent with the interpretation
that
g_{1}(s)
corresponds to the positive half of
f_{1}(s)
.
Further,
if the matrix element is zero in the bare basis,
the second term also vanishes as
s\to\infty

because
b(\infty)=0
.
When this is the case,

b_{1}(s)
acts as a finite window for the chirp

e^{i\theta(s)}
,
and we refer to
g_{1}(s)
as the
first ramp chirplet
.
Because the support of
g_{1}(s)
is effectively finite,
its Fourier transform is readily computable numerically.

Otherwise,
if the matrix element is nonzero in the bare basis,
e.g. when
i=0
,
j=1
and
k=0
,
then

g_{1}(s)
remains finite for positive
s
.
We can play the same trick again and define

f_{2}(s)\coloneqq\biggl(\Delta(0)+i\frac{d}{ds}\biggr)f_{1}(s)\,,

(159)

likewise for
g_{2}(s)
.
Factoring
g_{2}(s)
into
b_{2}(s)e^{i\theta(s)}

yields

\displaystyle b_{2}(s)\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\bigl[\nu(s)-\Delta(0)\bigr]b_{1}(s)+i\frac{db_{1}(s)}{ds}

(160)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle C_{0}(s)b(s)+C_{1}(s)\frac{db(s)}{ds}-\frac{d^{2}b(s)}{ds^{2}}\,,

(161)

where

\displaystyle C_{0}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl[\nu(s)-\Delta(0)\bigr]\!\bigl[\nu(s)-\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\bigr]+i\frac{d\nu(s)}{ds},

(162)

\displaystyle C_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq i\bigl[2\nu(s)-\Delta(0)-\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\bigr]\,.

(163)

The window
b_{2}(s)
is effectively finite since

C_{0}(\pm\infty)=0
,
so we call
g_{2}(s)
the
second ramp chirplet
.
The ESD of
f(s)
is then recovered as

\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[f(s)](\omega)\bigr|^{2}=\frac{2\bigl|{\mathcal{F}}[g_{2}(s)](\omega)\bigr|^{2}}{\bigl[\omega-\Delta(0)\bigr]^{2}\bigl[\omega-\Delta(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\bigr]^{2}}\,.

(164)

As expected,
if the matrix element does not asymptotically vanish,
its ESD has poles at both the bare and Stark-shifted transition frequencies.

Target-qubit frequency allocation

The target-qubit parameters of

Table

4

are determined by minimizing the collision probability
in one vertex neighborhood of a control qubit.
We fix the nominal control-qubit parameters at

E_{J,{\mathrm{c}}}/2\pi=\quantity{4.0}{}
,

E_{C,{\mathrm{c}}}/2\pi=\quantity{1.2}{}

and
E_{L,{\mathrm{c}}}/2\pi=\quantity{0.4}{}
,
and the nominal target-qubit charging and inductive energies
at
E_{C,{\mathrm{t}}}/2\pi=E_{L,{\mathrm{t}}}/2\pi=\quantity{1}{}
.
Hence,
we optimize over only the
nominal target-qubit Josephson energies.
The charging energies of the target qubits are chosen to be lower than that of the control qubit
since we expect the capacitive loading on the targets to be greater
in the heavy-hex lattice due to their higher neighbor count.
The nominal inductive energy of the target qubits is set to ensure
their qubit frequencies span

{

0.4

}

to

{

1.0

}

as
E_{J,{\mathrm{t}}}/2\pi

varies from

{

3

}

to

{

5

}

.

When calculating the collision probability of one vertex neighborhood,
we consider only collisions involving the qubit frequencies,
corresponding to
types 1, 8 and 9 of

Table

3
.
Additionally,
any realized qubit frequency over

{

1

}

is also considered unacceptable.
In this simplified model,
we represent
the frequency detunings of the collisions
as correlated Gaussian random variables,
with their standard deviations calculated from first-order perturbation theory.
Accordingly, the probability of
simultaneous
collisions
is given by the integral of a multivariate Gaussian over a hyperrectangle.
This can be computed with high numerical accuracy
using the Genz algorithm, implemented in SciPy as

(available since version 1.0.0)

31
;
112
.
The probability of
one or more
collisions is then computed using the
inclusion–exclusion principle,
which is tractable for up to roughly 10 random variables

34
.
Compared to
Section

,
we use a more precise method here
to calculate the zero-collision yield
such that the objective function
remains smooth even in the low-disorder regime.
