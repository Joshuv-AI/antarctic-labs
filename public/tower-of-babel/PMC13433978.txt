Tremelimumab with or without durvalumab in combination with paclitaxel in metastatic urothelial cancer: phase I/II ICRA trial
Authors: Sarah M H Einerhand, Hamza Ali, Stephen Q Wong, Jeroen van Dorp, Tatum van Maanen, Jeantine M de Feijter, Maurits L van Montfoort, Thierry N Boellaard, Linde M Braaf, Wim Brugman, Daniel J Vis, Cindy M J Hollander, Antonios Daletzakis, Helga A Schrijver, Karen Snapper, Sjoukje F Oosting, Cheryl P Bruijnen, Lodewyk F A Wessels, Keith S Chan, Britt B M Suelmann, Michiel S van der Heijden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew therapeutic strategies for patients with therapy-refractory metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) are still needed. In the phase I-II ICRA trial, a multicenter, open-label, phase Ib-II clinical trial (NCT03871036), we evaluated whether paclitaxel plus tremelimumab, with or without durvalumab, could induce a tumor response in mUC following platinum chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI). Patients were randomized between three arms: A (n = 20) paclitaxel plus T750mg; B (n = 12) paclitaxel plus T300mg plus D1500mg; C (n = 12) T750mg. The primary outcome, objective response rate, was met with 26% in arm A (88% CI = [14,36%]) versus 17% (88% CI = [3,42%]) in arm B and 8% (88% CI = [0.3,33%]) in arm C. Secondary outcomes included safety, duration of response and survival. Grade 3-4 treatment-related adverse events occurred in (A) 45%, (B) 75%, and (C) 25% of patients. Murine experiments showed attenuated tumor outgrowth for paclitaxel plus anti-CTLA-4 compared to monotherapy with either agent. Transcriptomic analyses showed upregulation of inflammation signatures in on-treatment tumor tissue biopsies. This study demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity in therapy-refractory mUC treated with paclitaxel plus high-dose anti-CTLA-4 and suggests immune induction may still be possible after failure to anti-PD-(L)1 therapy.Subject terms: Urological cancer, Medical research, Cancer therapy

Introduction

For decades, platinum-based chemotherapy has been the standard of care in the metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) first-line setting1. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) targeting the programmed cell death protein-1 checkpoint (anti-PD-1) are generally used in the second line or maintenance setting1. Recently, combination therapies of anti-PD-1 with either enfortumab vedotin (EV) or gemcitabine-cisplatin have shown superior overall survival (OS) rates compared to platinum-based chemotherapy alone2,3. However, a high clinical need for new therapeutic strategies in patients with therapy-refractory disease remains. Importantly, it is unclear whether patients who fail anti-PD-1 or treatment targeting its ligand (anti-PD-L1) remain amenable to other immunotherapeutic treatments.

ICI targeting the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 checkpoint (anti-CTLA-4) may induce response in therapy-refractory mUC by broadening of peripheral T-cell priming4. Anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy has demonstrated clinical activity and durable responses in platinum-refractory mUC, and dual targeting of the PD-(L)1 and CTLA-4 checkpoints may lead to additive or synergistic activity by leveraging non-redundant pathways to improve the antitumor immune response5–9. In UC, phase Ib-II trials suggest higher efficacy of high-dose compared to low-dose anti-CTLA-4 in combination with anti-PD-16,7. Whereas the phase III DANUBE trial in mUC did not show superior OS of durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) plus low-dose tremelimumab 75 mg (anti-CTLA-4), the phase III HIMALAYA trial in metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma did demonstrate that durvalumab plus a single high priming dose of tremelimumab 300 mg improved OS compared to chemotherapy8,10. These results indicate that the clinical activity of anti-CTLA-4 may not only be contingent on the combination with other agents but also on appropriate dosing.

The efficacy of chemo-immunotherapy has been established in the front-line setting of several cancer types, including mUC3,11,12. In the context of post-platinum mUC, chemo-immunotherapy of ICI with taxanes may offer distinct advantages. Taxanes could attenuate immune suppression in the tumor micro-environment (TME) through induction of immunogenic cell death and tumor (neo-)antigen release and by stimulating non-canonical T-cell cytotoxicity13,14. Moreover, taxane-induced disease control facilitates an interval in which (delayed) response to ICI may occur15. Whereas taxanes plus anti-PD-1 have shown activity in single-arm studies in post-platinum mUC, the combination with anti-CTLA-4 (±anti-PD-L1) has not been tested16,17.

We conducted a signal-finding, two-stage, phase Ib-II trial to evaluate whether anti-CTLA-4 (+/− anti-PD-L1) combined with paclitaxel chemotherapy could induce tumor response in anti-PD-(L)1 refractory mUC patients, the ICRA trial.

Results

ICRA study design and patients

The ICRA-trial enrolled patients with mUC, pre-treated with platinum chemotherapy and anti-PD-(L)1. ICRA had a dose-escalation safety phase followed by a randomized two-stage main-phase testing the target combination doses of tremelimumab, ±durvalumab, ±paclitaxel. In the dose-escalation phase, patients were treated with paclitaxel and incremental doses of tremelimumab with or without durvalumab (Fig. 1). Arms R3a (paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 750 mg) and R4 (paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 300 mg plus durvalumab) were tested in the main phase of the trial (arms A and B, respectively); patients in these arms were migrated to the corresponding main phase arms for efficacy analysis. In the first stage of the main phase, patients were randomized to three arms (n = 12 per arm): (A) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 750 mg; (B) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 300 mg plus durvalumab 1500 mg; (C) tremelimumab 750 mg. The primary endpoint was the confirmed objective response rate (ORR) per RECIST1.1 in arms A and B. In case of at least two responses in a main phase arm, this arm would be expanded with 8 additional patients (n = 20 total). With a (one-sided) type I error rate of 0.12 and power of 85%, the null hypothesis (ORR = 10%) would be rejected if at least four patients in the extended arm achieved a confirmed response.

Between May 2019 and May 2023, 53 patients with therapy-refractory mUC were enrolled in three centers in the Netherlands (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were similar between treatment arms (Table 1). At the cut-off date (December 20, 2024), two patients were still on study (both in follow-up). Thirty-five patients (66%) discontinued treatment due to disease progression, and foureen patients (26%) discontinued due to an adverse event. Of the remaining four patients, two patients (4%) completed the treatment regimen and two (4%) elected to discontinue treatment (Fig. 1).

Efficacy

All patients received at least one dose of anti-CTLA-4 and were therefore included in the analysis of the endpoints of the trial. Two confirmed partial responses (PR) were observed in arm A (P + T750) after conclusion of the first stage of the main phase of the trial. Two confirmed responses were also observed in arm B (P + T300 + D). However, the second response occurred in a patient with prolonged SD after the conclusion of the main phase of the trial. Hence, arm A (P + T750) was expanded to include an additional eight patients.

After the extension phase, a confirmed objective response was achieved in five of twenty patients (26%; 88% confidence interval (CI) = [14,36]) in arm A (P + T750) (Table 2). Note that the objective response rate in arm A was calculated at 26% due to the two-stage statistical design (see “Methods”). The trial met the primary endpoint in arm A as the lower bound of the 88% CI exceeded 10%. As mentioned above, two out of twelve patients (17%) in arm B (P + T300 + D) achieved a confirmed objective response (88% CI = [3,42]). One out of twelve patients (8%) in arm C (T750) achieved a confirmed objective response (88% CI = [0.5,33]). A confirmed complete response (CR) was observed in two (3.8%) patients, one in arm A (P + T750) and one in arm C (T750). Clinical benefit (CB), defined as confirmed complete response/partial response/stable disease ≥24 weeks, was achieved in six patients (32%) in arm A (P + T750), four patients (33%) in arm B (P + T300 + D), and two patients (17%) in arm C (T750). In total, ten out of twenty patients (50%) in arm A (P + T750) and eight out of twelve patients (67%) in arm B (P + T300 + D) had a reduction in target lesion size from baseline versus two out of twelve patients (17%) in arm C (T750) (Fig. 2).

Safety

All treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) in the run-in phase and main phase plus extension are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, respectively. No dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were observed in the run-in safety phase, and no new safety concerns arose from the combination of paclitaxel plus anti-CTLA-4 (±anti-PD-L1). In the run-in phase, one grade 3 immune-related adverse event (irAE) occurred in R2 (P + T225), specifically an increase in aspartate aminotransferase. This irAE resolved to grade 1 within fourteen days upon corticosteroid treatment and therefore did not meet the criteria for a DLT. In the main study phase, grade 3-4 TRAEs occurred in nine (45%) patients in arm A (P + T750) versus nine (75%) patients in arm B (P + T300 + D) and four (33%) patients in arm C (T750). IrAEs, regardless of grade, were mostly gastrointestinal, dermatological or endocrine in nature. Grade 3-4 irAEs resolved with corticosteroid treatment and without sequelae. The most common grade 3–4 chemo-related AEs were anemia and decreased neutrophil count. No grade 5 TRAEs occurred.

Exploratory outcomes

Translational analyses

Baseline PD-L1 assessment was obtained in 48 (91%) patients (Fig. 2), of whom 20 (42%) had received treatment with PD-(L)1 inhibitors and platinum-chemotherapy before tissue acquisition and PD-L1 assessment. PD-L1 positivity on neither tumor nor immune cells was associated with OS (log rank p = 0.89 and p = 0.083, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Next, we performed baseline transcriptomic analysis of patients in the main phase arms. We were primarily interested in identifying biomarkers of response that could potentially be attributed to activation of anti-tumor immunity and able to distinguish patients with durable clinical benefit (i.e., progression-free ≥1 year) from patients with short-lived or non-response. As our trial was conducted in the therapy-refractory disease setting and previous systemic treatment may have altered the TME, we restricted our analysis to samples collected after at least one line of systemic treatment (PFS ≥ 1 year n = 3; PFS < 1 year n = 22). Baseline tumor tissue from patients with durable response exhibited a relatively high level of inflammation, although an inflamed transcriptomic profile was not limited to patients with response (Supplementary Fig. 3). Next, we focused our analysis on expression signatures indicative of T-cell immunity and previously shown to correlate with immunotherapy response in UC18–20. We found increased baseline gene expression of CD8+ T-effector and IFN-γ signatures in durable responders, compared to non-responders (p = 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively) (Fig. 5A). This difference in baseline inflammation between durable responders and non-responders was more pronounced when the analysis was restricted to patients who had a baseline biopsy after treatment with both platinum-chemotherapy and anti-PD-(L)1 therapy (Supplementary Fig. 4). We performed survival analyses using baseline ssGSEA scores from tumor tissue of patients in the main phase arms to categorize patients in tertiles based on high, medium, or low values of the score. Median OS was significantly longer in patients with high expression of IFN-γ (24.0 months) than in patients with medium (17.1 months) or low expression (9.4 months) of IFN-γ (log rank p = 0.033). Similarly, significantly longer mOS was observed in patients with high CD8+ T-effector signature scores (24.0 months) compared to patients with medium and low CD8+ T-effector signature scores (15.0 and 11.4 months, respectively) (log rank p = 0.047) (Fig. 5B).

We additionally analyzed pre-treatment versus on-treatment tissue (tumor biopsy 4 weeks after the first dose of tremelimumab) to assess potential immune induction and treatment dynamics of anti-CTLA-4 in anti-PD-(L)1-refractory patients. We observed increased expression of CD8+ T-effector- and IFN-γ signatures upon treatment in both the paired (p = 0.02 and p = 0.01, respectively) and unpaired (p = 0.01 and p = 0.001, respectively) analyses (Fig. 6). When examining broader transcriptomic changes in the TME, a TGF-β signature—previously associated with non-response in the metastatic setting18—did not increase upon treatment. In contrast, several Hallmark inflammatory signatures were increased upon treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5). Taken together, our results suggest that in anti-PD-(L)1 refractory mUC, baseline inflammation may be needed for a response to anti-CTLA-4, and induction of a more inflamed microenvironment could still be attainable using anti-CTLA-4 ± paclitaxel.

Murine experiments

Our clinical results showed an encouraging response rate for combination regimens of anti-CTLA-4-based ICI with paclitaxel in treatment-refractory mUC. However, as the disease at the therapy-refractory stage is heterogeneous in terms of localization of metastases and previous therapy and as the trial was not designed to demonstrate additional benefit of the combination, we were unable to unequivocally assess whether combination therapy provided added benefit. To investigate possible synergy between anti-CTLA-4 and taxanes in a more controlled setting, we used a G69 murine bladder cancer model21. This serially transplantable model was derived from a classical BBN carcinogen-induced tumor model. G69 has previously been shown to be unresponsive to ICI and, therefore, relevant to this study, since patients within the ICRA trial were treated for urothelial carcinoma with prior unresponsiveness to ICI. In order to assess if paclitaxel elicits an immunogenic form of cell death (ICD), we employed the “gold-standard” vaccination assay22. We first treated G69 cells with paclitaxel in vitro using the IC50 dose for 24 h. Dying cells were then injected into the left subcutaneous flank as “vaccines”. One week later, mice were injected with untreated G69 cells on the right flank. If the drug can induce ICD, it will prime an effective immune response to reject the “challenge”, resulting in a significantly reduced incidence of tumor outgrowth. Gemcitabine was used as a negative control, as previous research has shown it does not induce ICD23. We observed a significant delay in tumor-free survival in mice vaccinated with paclitaxel-treated G69 cells as compared to gemcitabine and vehicle-vaccinated groups. These results suggest that paclitaxel treatment can induce ICD in the murine G69 bladder cancer model.

To assess whether paclitaxel treatment could improve tumor response to anti-CTLA-4 in a tumor-bearing model, we proceeded to investigate potential synergy by tumor growth inhibition studies. After establishment of G69 xenografts, mice were treated with vehicle, anti-CTLA-4, paclitaxel, or their combination (n = 5 per treatment arm) at multiple time points (Fig. 7). The combination treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to vehicle control and monotherapy arms (p = 0.021). These results suggest a superior effect of paclitaxel and anti-CTLA-4 combination treatment in a preclinical tumor-bearing mouse model.

Discussion

In this phase I-II ICRA study, we showed that weekly paclitaxel plus high-dose tremelimumab (P + T750) is associated with confirmed objective response in 26% of mUC patients who progressed on or after platinum-based chemotherapy and anti-PD-(L)1 treatment. Responses were observed in all main phase arms regardless of PD-L1 status or metastasis location. The median OS of the tremelimumab monotherapy arm was similar to other control arms tested in this post-anti-PD-(L)1 setting24–26, whilst the paclitaxel combination arms showed encouraging efficacy, especially in terms of median OS (16.1 and 14.5 months). Paclitaxel plus tremelimumab (± durvalumab) had an overall acceptable safety profile, which shows that a duplet and even triplet chemo-immunotherapy regimen is feasible in heavily pretreated patients with therapy-refractory mUC.

An unanswered question in mUC clinical research is whether patients progressing on anti-PD-(L)1 could still benefit from an alternative ICI. Keeping in mind the inherent bias of cross-trial comparisons, the median OS of the paclitaxel combination arms compared favorably to the historical median OS of paclitaxel monotherapy in the therapy-refractory setting (8–9 months24–26). Although the response rate in the tremelimumab monotherapy arm was modest (8.3%), there were durable responses: one patient had an ongoing complete response of ~45 months, and another patient had stable disease for ~20 months after 4 cycles (discontinued due to irAE). Our transcriptomic data further contributed to the hypothesis that anti-CTLA-4 is biologically active in the post-anti-PD-(L)1 setting, as both the IFN-γ and CD8+ T cell effector signatures were increased upon treatment. These elevations in bulk transcriptomic T-cell related signatures suggest intratumoral and stromal infiltration of T-cells upon systemic treatment, which has been correlated with improved patient outcomes in several studies27–29. Still, a pharmacodynamic effect, not necessarily linked to clinical benefit, could not be dismissed30. Anti-CTLA-4 may have anti-tumor effects that improve OS but are insufficiently represented by short-term outcome measures such as response rate and PFS, a feature previously observed with ipilimumab monotherapy in metastatic melanoma31.

In a recent study, tremelimumab 750 mg monotherapy resulted in a similarly low ORR (8.3%) in the third-line (i.e., post-anti-PD-(L)1) setting, compared to 18.8% in a study in the second-line (i.e., post-platinum) setting5,32. Combination treatment with agents that can reduce tumor load in the short-term may be a necessary adjunct to anti-CTLA-4 in the post-anti-PD-(L)1 setting to optimize the antitumor immune response. Although this study was not powered for formal statistical comparison between arms, the higher proportion of patients with tumor burden reduction and the numerically longer OS suggest the combination arms with paclitaxel were more active than tremelimumab monotherapy. These observations align with phase II trials that investigated potential synergy between ICI and paclitaxel in ICI-naïve patients and reported relatively high response rates for second-line treatment (3317–38.616%). Mechanistically, previous research has shown that paclitaxel releases immunostimulatory damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to prime the CD8+ T cell response, implicating ICD as one of the primary mechanisms causing increased tumor-free incidence33. We further explored this mechanism in the G69 in vivo model and demonstrated that injection of paclitaxel-treated cells led to delayed outgrowth of untreated G69 cells in the contralateral flank, compared to gemcitabine and vehicle. Since chemotherapy was only used to treat cells in vitro and the mice themselves never received systemic chemotherapy, these results seem attributable to the immunogenicity of cell death induced by paclitaxel rather than cytotoxic effects associated with systemic treatment. However, we cannot exclude that cancer cells became more susceptible due to other treatment-associated mechanisms and factors. Additional in vivo experiments showed suppressed tumor outgrowth upon the combination of paclitaxel and anti-CTLA-4 compared to either drug as monotherapy, further suggesting potential synergy of this combination.

The incidence and nature of grade 3–4 irAEs were consistent with previous reports of durvalumab plus tremelimumab and tremelimumab monotherapy5,8,10. No new safety signals or signs of synergistic toxicity emerged. The irAEs in this trial were likely driven by tremelimumab since patients with grade ≥3 irAEs during prior anti-PD-(L)1 treatment were excluded, and the rate of irAEs in the three arms was similar. Of note, several patients had continued clinical benefit after discontinuation of treatment due to AEs.

This study is limited by the small sample size per arm and the non-comparative design. The long-term survival outcomes may have been influenced by subsequent therapy, which became available during the course of the trial and has evolved even further after the conclusion of the trial. Furthermore, translational analyses were limited by the anti-PD-(L)1 pre-treated baseline tissue sample size and the small number of paired pre- and on-treatment samples.

Our data suggest that anti-CTLA-4 can exhibit anti-tumor activity, and the combination with taxanes may improve clinical outcome in anti-PD-(L)1 refractory mUC. Novel therapeutic agents currently in development, e.g., antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), may be better suited as combinatorial drugs with high-dose anti-CTLA-4 to induce an anti-cancer immune response in this setting, as has been observed for EV plus pembrolizumab in the front-line setting2. Other treatments combining ADCs with ICI are ongoing (NCT06524544). In-depth translational analysis of the tumor-immune micro-environment in such trials could help establish optimal combination strategies to provide both an initial tumor response and immunotherapeutic durability.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was reviewed and approved by the NedMec Ethical Review Committee. Subsequent amendments to the protocol were also reviewed and approved by NedMec. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines as put forth by the International Conference on Harmonization. All patients provided written informed consent. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03871036).

Improve Checkpoint-blockade Response in Advanced urothelial cancer (ICRA) was a multicenter, open-label, phase Ib-II clinical trial of paclitaxel plus tremelimumab with or without durvalumab, or tremelimumab monotherapy in mUC. The full study protocol is available as a Supplementary Information file. This adaptive trial consisted of several sequential phases: a run-in phase, a main phase, and an extension phase (Fig. 1). In the run-in phase (n = 3 per arm), patients were sequentially enrolled to assess the safety and tolerability of tremelimumab and paclitaxel, as these have not been combined in this patient population before. The run-in phase consisted of (R1) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 75 mg; (R2) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 225 mg; (R3a) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 750 mg; (R3b) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 75 mg and durvalumab 1500 mg, and (R4) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 300 mg and durvalumab 1500 mg (added in protocol amendment V4 on 20-8-2020 after promising results were observed in hepatocellular carcinoma10). In case of dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) in the run-in phase, the experimental arms of the main phase would be adjusted to the highest dose level deemed to have acceptable toxicity. DLTs were assessed in the run-in phase and in the first six patients in the main phase from the first administration of tremelimumab until the last patient in the relevant arm had been treated with tremelimumab for 30 days. A DLT was defined as any grade ≥3 non-hematological toxicity during the evaluation period, excluding those clearly related to the primary disease or other causes. Specific DLTs include grade 4 immune-related adverse events (irAEs), grade 3-4 noninfectious pneumonitis or grade 2 pneumonitis that does no resolve to grade ≤1 within 3 days, any-grade irAE other than colitis and pneumonitis that does not resolve to grade ≤1 within 14 days, liver transaminase elevation of >8 times upper normal limit or total bilirubine >5 times upper normal limit, and any grade ≥3 non-irAEs (exceptions allowed, see protocol). The run-in phase was followed by a randomized main phase (n = 12 per arm). The main phase consisted of (A) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 750 mg (P + T750); (B) paclitaxel plus tremelimumab 300 mg plus durvalumab (P + T300 + D); (C) tremelimumab 750 mg monotherapy (T750). Patients in arms R3a and R4 received the optimal therapeutic dose and were therefore analyzed in arms A and B, respectively. The monotherapy arm was not included for statistical comparison, but to assess the effects of the tremelimumab monotherapy component. Finally, one experimental arm in the main phase was to be expanded to include twenty patients contingent upon achieving predetermined response criteria—that is, the arm with the most, but at least two, confirmed responses.

Eligible patients were 18 years or older with histologically confirmed mUC. At the conception and throughout most of the enrollment period of this trial, mUC standard of care treatment consisted of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy and second-line anti-PD-(L)1, as EV was not yet available. Eligible patients had (1) progressed on anti-PD-(L)1 treatment; (2) progressed on (cis)platinum-based chemotherapy, administered in either the peri-operative or advanced setting, or were ineligible to receive cisplatin-based chemotherapy. All patients had at least one measurable lesion according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1), had a World Health Organization performance status of 0 or 1, and had adequate renal (creatinine clearance >30 ml/min), hematological, and liver function. Key exclusion criteria were grade ≥3 irAEs during previous treatment with anti-PD-(L)1 or any grade irAE that led to discontinuation of anti-PD-(L)1, active metastases of the central nervous system, anticancer therapy ≤28 days prior to the first dose of study drug or concurrent anticancer therapy, autoimmune disease (exceptions allowed, see protocol), and previous treatment with anti-CTLA-4. Patients were tapered off immunosuppressive treatment within fourteen days before the first dose, if applicable. Female patients who were pregnant or breastfeeding, or male or female patients of reproductive potential who were not willing to employ effective birth control from screening to 180 days after the last treatment dose, were excluded. Sex and/or gender were not considered specifically in the study design. Patients were included regardless of sex or gender. Data on sex, as it appeared in patients’ medical records, have been collected and reported.

Except for patients who rolled over from the run-in phase (arms 3A and 4) into main-phase arms A and B, respectively, patients enrolled in the main phase were randomized. Randomization was performed electronically by the NKI trial bureau using block randomization with randomly varying block sizes to minimize predictability and to maintain balanced allocation across the three study arms. Allocation was disclosed to the patient and the investigator after registration and randomization were completed.

Procedures

Patients in all run-in arms and in the main phase arms A and B received weekly intravenous paclitaxel 70 mg/m2 on days 1, 8, and 15 of a 28-day cycle for a maximum of six cycles. In run-in arms 1 and 2 and main phase arms A and C, intravenous tremelimumab (75, 225, 750, and 750 mg, respectively) started during cycle two of paclitaxel and was administered once every 4 weeks for five cycles, followed by once every 12 weeks for two cycles. Patients in arm R3b received intravenous tremelimumab 75 mg every 4 weeks for four cycles. Finally, patients in the main phase arm B received intravenous tremelimumab 300 mg once. In addition, in arms R3b and B, intravenous durvalumab 1500 mg was started during cycle two of paclitaxel and was administered every 4 weeks for 12 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 1).

All patients treated with paclitaxel received premedication (dexamethasone) during the first cycle to prevent hypersensitivity reactions or fluid retention. Initiation of ICIs in the combination arms was deferred to the second cycle of paclitaxel to mitigate the immunosuppressive effects of dexamethasone. Furthermore, some evidence suggests that phased rather than concurrent administration of anti-CTLA-4 during chemotherapy enhances the antitumor immune response34.

For paclitaxel, dose modifications and interruptions were permitted to manage adverse events. For ICIs, dose modifications were not permitted, whilst interruptions to manage adverse events were permitted. Treatment continued until RECIST1.1-defined disease progression, clinical deterioration due to progression, development of an unacceptable level of toxicity, (ir)AEs that met the requirements for discontinuation as outlined in the toxicity management guidelines, withdrawal of consent, or completion of the treatment regimen. Upon response evaluation, clinically stable patients with unconfirmed progressive disease could remain on treatment until progression was confirmed on a confirmatory scan.

Response assessments were performed via contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest/abdomen/pelvis at 12 weeks (±1 week) after treatment initiation, then every 8 weeks for 12 months, and subsequently every 12 weeks until disease progression, withdrawal of consent, or death. Objective responses were confirmed per RECIST1.1, i.e., responses were to be confirmed on a consecutive scan at least 4 weeks after initial response.

Archival tissue from any point in pre-trial clinical treatment or biopsy at baseline demonstrating muscle-invasive or metastatic UC was required for trial participation. Elective on-treatment biopsies were acquired 4 weeks after the start of tremelimumab to assess treatment effect on the tumor micro-environment.

Adverse events were monitored throughout the treatment period and for 90 days after the end of treatment and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5 (CTCAEv5). A data safety monitoring board was established to evaluate toxicities before and after initiation of the new run-in arm (R4), after completion of the run-in phase, after the main phase, and after the extension phase.

End points

The primary end point consisted of the confirmed objective response rate (ORR) per RECIST1.1 by investigator assessment in the paclitaxel plus tremelimumab arm and the paclitaxel plus tremelimumab plus durvalumab arms (i.e., arms A and B, respectively). Secondary objectives included safety (CTCAEv5) of paclitaxel plus anti-CTLA-4, duration of response, and survival. Exploratory objectives included obtaining an overview of changes in the TME as a result of treatment.

Statistical analysis

The trial used Simon’s two-stage optimal design. In this design, the null hypothesis proportion (P0) representing the probability of an inactive drug was set at 0.1 and tested against a one-sided alternative of 0.3. Based on these parameters, we calculated that twelve patients per arm were to be accrued during the main phase. If fewer than two patients in each arm responded, the study would be stopped. The arm with the most, but at least two, confirmed responses was to accrue a total of 20 patients. With a (one-sided) type I error rate of 0.12 and power of 85%, the null hypothesis would be rejected if at least four patients in the extended arm achieved a confirmed response. The full analysis set included all patients who had received at least one dose of anti-CTLA-4.

Due to the two-stage design of the study, the ORR in the extended arm was an estimation using the uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimate (UMVUE) method35; the corresponding 88% CIs were calculated as proposed by Koyama and Chen36. The ORR in all other arms was represented by the sample proportion; the 88% CIs around the sample proportions were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method.

Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. OS was defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause. Patients alive at the data cut were censored at that date. PFS was defined as the time from randomization to progression or death, whichever came first. Patients without progression at the data cut-off were censored at the last tumor assessment.

Categorical variables were compared with the Fisher’s exact test or the Chi2 test, depending on the sample size. Post-hoc analyses were performed to identify specific pairs of categories that contributed to the overall chi-square statistic by calculating standardized residuals and applying multiple comparisons correction with the Bonferroni method. Continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on whether two or more groups were compared. The safety analyses were performed in all evaluable patients, defined as those who received at least one administration of tremelimumab. Given the limited cohort size, no subanalyses based on sex or gender were performed.

In murine experiments, tumor-free survival among treatment groups was compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. For the anti-CTLA-4 plus paclitaxel experiment, data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with Geisser-Greenhouse correction, followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test.

Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.4.0).

Translational experiments

PD-L1 immunohistochemistry and assessment

PD-L1 immunohistochemistry was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections by a PD-L1 IHC 22C3 1/40 dilution PharmDx qualitative immunohistochemical assay on a DAKO Autostainer 48 system. An experienced uro-pathologist scored PD-L1 membrane staining on immune cells and tumor cells as a fraction of the overall immune and tumor cell populations, respectively. High PD-L1 expression was defined as at least 5% of immune cells as a percentage of tumor area (IC+) and at least 1% of tumor cells (TC+) with membrane staining. Tissues were collected prior to trial admission.

RNA isolation and sequencing

RNA was isolated from FFPE sections or, if not available, fresh frozen (FF) material. If available, baseline samples were collected prior to trial enrolment after at least one line of systemic therapy and on-treatment samples were collected 4 weeks after administration of the first dose of anti-CTLA-4.

For isolation of RNA from FFPE, the pathologist scored tumor percentage and indicated the most tumor-dense region for isolation on a H&E slide using Slidescore (www.slidescore.com). Between 5 and 10 (depending on tumor size) FFPE 10 µm slides were used. For isolation from FF, the pathologist scored tumor percentage and indicated the tumor region, relevant for isolation, on a consecutive H&E-stained cryostat frozen section (5 µm) slide. DNA and RNA were isolated simultaneously with the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE isolation kit (Qiagen, #80234) by using the QIAcube, according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quality and quantity of the total RNA were assessed on the 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument using an Agilent RNA 6000 Nano chip (G2938-90034, Agilent Technologies) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The region method analysis was used according to the manufacturer’s manual (technical-note-470-2014-001, Illumina, Inc.) to determine the percentage of RNA fragments with >200 nt fragment distribution values (DV200). The TruSeq RNA Exome Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc.) was used to generate strand-specific libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions (# 1000000039582v01, Illumina, Inc.) with the following adaptation. Samples that contained intact total RNA were subjected to the optional heat fragmentation step (94 °C for 8 min, 4 °C hold). The generated cDNA fragments were ligated IDT xGen UDI(10 bp)-UMI(9 bp) paired-end sequencing adapters (Integrated DNA Technologies) and amplified by 15 cycles of PCR. The libraries were validated on a 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument following the manufacturer’s protocol “Agilent DNA 7500 kit” (G2938-90025, Agilent Technologies), followed by a 1–4 plex library pooling containing up to 200 ng of each sample. The pooled cDNA libraries were enriched for target regions using the probe Coding Exome Oligos set (CEX, 45MB, Illumina, Inc.) and 10 cycles of PCR. The libraries were analyzed on a 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument following the manufacturer’s protocol “Agilent DNA 7500 kit” (G2938-90025, Agilent Technologies), diluted to 10 nM and pooled equimolar into multiplex sequencing pools. The libraries were sequenced with 54 cycles for Read 1, 19 cycles for Read i7, 10 cycles for Read i5 and 54 cycles for Read 2 on a NovaSeq6000 using a Reagent Kit v1.5 (100cycles) (Illumina, Inc.).

Analysis of RNA sequencing

Kallisto (version 0.50.1) was run in the R environment (version 4.4) and was used to pseudo-align raw reads to the human reference transcriptome GRCh38 and to quantify gene expression using a forward-stranded library with paired-end sequencing. Downstream analyses were run in RStudio (version 2022.02.1 + 461). DESeq2 (version 1.42.1) was used to create normalized read counts from raw read counts. GSVA (version 1.50.5) was used to calculate single-sample gene set enrichment scores for gene signatures. Gene signatures from the Hallmarks gene sets19 and specific gene sets for TGFβ and CD8 effector T cell signature were used18. All applied signatures were added separately using the GSVA function and combined in a data matrix after the ssGSEA scores were calculated per sample per signature. The data was scaled column-wise and then row-wise, principal component analysis was performed, and only the first two principal components were used to reconstruct the data.

The transcriptomic analyses were performed on tissues acquired after at least one line of systemic treatment, at least platinum-based chemotherapy, prior to participation in the trial and on the on-treatment biopsies. A Heatmap was generated using the pheatmap package (1.0.12) to visualize the gene signature expression patterns across samples using the ssGSEA scores. In the Heatmap, samples were ordered based on the duration of PFS and clustered together based on signature expression within the same cluster. Boxplots of ssGSEA scores were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.5.1). Statistics inside boxplots were calculated using the ggpubr package (version 0.6.0.999). The survminer (0.4.9) and survival (3.7.0) packages were used to calculate survival statistics and create Kaplan–Meier plots.

Murine experiments

Cell culture

All cells were cultured under standard cell culture conditions (i.e., 37 °C, 5% CO2). The murine bladder cancer model G69 cell line was generated as previously described21 and maintained in DMEM/F12 media (Gibco; 11320033) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% 100 g/L D-Glucose (Gibco; 15023021), 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 160070), 1% NEAA (Gibco, 11140050), 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 18.5 μg/L of carrier-free recombinant mouse EGF (Biolegend; 585608).

Animals

Six to ten-week-old male FVB mice (FVB/NJ) purchased from The Jackson Laboratory were used in all animal studies. All animal studies were performed in accordance with procedures approved by the IACUC under protocols IS00007158 (Houston Methodist Research Institute). Animals were group-housed in individually ventilated cages under a 12-h light/dark cycle, following the recommendations of the National Advisory Committee for Laboratory Animal Research (NACLAR). An irradiated diet and autoclaved water were provided ad libitum. All animal care and maintenance were conducted in accordance with the American Association of Laboratory Animal Care (AALAC) guidelines. Approved humane endpoints for tumor burden were defined as a maximum volume of 2000 mm³ or a diameter of 2000 mm in any single axis. No animals exceeded the approved tumor burden limits in any study.

In-vivo gold-standard vaccination assay

G69 cells were seeded at 4.7 × 104 cells per mm2 in a 10 cm2 dish and were treated with IC50 concentrations of paclitaxel for 24 h in vitro. After 24 h, adherent cells were washed with DPBS (Sigma, D8537) thoroughly, dissociated with TrypLE express enzyme, and pelleted by centrifugation (1300 × g for 5 min at room temperature). Cell pellets were washed with DPBS once more to ensure clearance of residual enzyme and pharmacological agent(s). 1 × 106 cells were suspended in 50 μL of DPBS and were injected into the left flank of mice as vaccines. Seven days later, mice were challenged with 5 × 105 untreated WT G69 cells suspended in 50 μL of DPBS in the right flank. Tumor growth was recorded at least twice a week using calipers, and volume was calculated using the formula (L × W2)/2, where L is the longest axis, and W is the perpendicular axis.

Treatment of G69 xenografts with paclitaxel and anti-mouse CTLA4 in vivo

Mice were injected with 1.5 × 106 G69 cells in the left subcutaneous flank. Tumor growth was tracked weekly. Mice with an established G69 bladder cancer cell line tumor (i.e., tumor volume ~50 mm3) were randomized into four treatment groups. The treatments used were: vehicle control (PBS injected intravenously (IV) with intraperitoneal (IP) isotype IgG), anti-mouse CTLA4 (BioXCell; #BE0032; Clone UC10-4F10-11) IP with paclitaxel (MedChemExpress; HY-B0015, CAS# 33069-62-4) IV, anti-mouse CTLA IP with PBS, and paclitaxel IV with isotype IgG (BioXCell; #BE0091). Paclitaxel was first dissolved in ethanol, and then diluted 1:1 in Cremophor EL (Millipore; 238470, CAS# 61791-12-6) and administered IV at a dose of 25 mg/kg. Paclitaxel was injected on days 0, 7, and 14. Anti-mouse CTLA4 was given at 200 μg/mouse and injected on days 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, and 21.
