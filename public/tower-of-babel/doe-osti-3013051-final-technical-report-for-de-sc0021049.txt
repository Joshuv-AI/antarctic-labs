Summary

Over five years of support, this DOE Early Career Research Program project has established moiré
superlattice materials as a highly tunable platform for controlling heterogeneous electron transfer
(ET) kinetics at solid-liquid interfaces. By precisely engineering van der Waals heterostructures
of atomically thin two-dimensional (2D) materials, particularly bilayer and trilayer graphene with
controlled twist angles, we have demonstrated systematic control of interfacial ET rates spanning
three orders of magnitude. This research addresses fundamental questions about how electronic
structure, localization, and layer-dependent properties govern charge transfer at
electrochemical interfaces, with broad implications for energy conversion, electrocatalysis, and
next-generation electrochemical devices.

The four-year research trajectory progressed through four major scientific directions:

 1. Moiré-enhanced electron transfer in twisted bilayer graphene: We discovered and
 characterized order-of-magnitude enhancements in rate constants (k⁰) near magic angles,
 with ET rates approaching those of bulk graphite. These results establish moiré flat bands
 as a distinctively tunable platform for mediating electrochemical transformations.
 2. Twisted trilayer graphene systems: By controlling interlayer twists in trilayer graphene,
 we showed that ET rates are highly sensitive to electronic localization in individual layers,
 rather than the overall density of states (DOS). Tunability across three atomic layers spans
 three orders of magnitude, even exceeding bulk metal rates. This work demonstrated that
 layer-specific electronic localization is critical for interfacial ET, providing insight into the
 high reactivity often associated with defects in traditional electrode surfaces.
 3. Orthogonal control via electrostatic gating of 2D MoS₂: Using few-layer MoS₂, we
 evaluated how charge carrier concentration influences heterogeneous ET. Spatially
 resolved electrochemical measurements combined with field-effect modulation reveal
 strong control of outer-sphere charge transfer through band alignment and demonstrate the
 governing role of in-plane charge transport under low carrier densities.
 4. Solid-state doping and intercalation chemistry: We demonstrated control over
 interfacial reactivity and lithium intercalation in graphene/α-RuCl3 heterostructures,
 achieving >2.5 V shifts in intercalation onset. This work extends the tunability paradigm

 2

 to ion transport and intercalation chemistry in 2D materials, relevant for batteries and
 energy storage, and provides a new lever for engineering layered electrodes.

Throughout the grant period, we have published in high-impact peer-reviewed journals (Nature
Chemistry, ACS Central Science, ACS Nanoscience Au, Nano Letters) and developed novel
experimental platforms combining scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM), operando
spectroscopy, and advanced computational methods.

1. Motivation
Tailoring electron transfer dynamics across solid-liquid interfaces is fundamental to the
interconversion of electrical and chemical energy.1─5 This challenge underpins technologies
ranging from batteries and fuel cells to electrochemical sensors and catalytic devices. The Marcus-
Hush-Chidsey (MHC) theory framework traditionally predicts that heterogeneous electron transfer
rates depend on three primary factors: the density of electronic states available at the electrode
surface, the energetic alignment between these states and the redox couple in solution, and the
electronic coupling strength between the electrode and the redox molecules. Conventional bulk
electrodes provide limited tunability of these parameters once the material is synthesized.

Our approach leverages the unique structural and electronic properties of van der Waals
heterostructures—layered materials that can be stacked with atomically precise control. When
two-dimensional materials are stacked with a small azimuthal misorientation, they form moiré
superlattices: periodic superstructures with modulated electronic properties. Near specific “magic
angles,” moiré superlattices produce remarkably flat electronic bands that concentrate electronic
density of states into narrow energy windows and localized spatial regions.6─8 This provides an
unprecedented opportunity to engineer interfacial properties at the atomic scale.

The core hypothesis of this project is that moiré superlattice engineering enables precise,
systematic tuning of heterogeneous electron transfer kinetics. By controlling twist angle and
stacking geometry, we can move beyond the traditional limitations of bulk electrode materials and
create bespoke electrochemical interfaces with designer electronic properties. This research tests
and extends the applicability of MHC theory at nanoscale interfaces and demonstrates new
paradigms for interfacial reactivity control.

2. Tunable angle-dependent electrochemistry at twisted bilayer graphene with
moiré flat bands (in Nature Chem 2022)9

We discovered a strong twist angle dependence of heterogeneous charge transfer kinetics at twisted
bilayer graphene electrodes with the greatest enhancement observed near the ‘magic angle’ (~1.1º).
This effect is driven by the angle-dependent tuning of moiré-derived flat bands that modulate
electron transfer processes with the solution-phase redox couple. Combined experimental and
computational analysis reveals that the variation in electrochemical activity with moiré angle is
controlled by atomic reconstruction of the moiré superlattice at twist angles <2º, and topological
defects localized at AA stacking regions produce a large anomalous local electrochemical
enhancement that cannot be accounted for by the elevated local density of states alone. Adding
more layers creates additional possibilities for creating flat bands through a combination of
stacking order and twist angle. We have therefore begun to explore interfacial charge transfer
mediated by moiré superlattices of trilayer graphene, which show even stronger enhancements in
electrochemical rates than those observed in twisted bilayer graphene.

2.3 Results and discussion

Figure 2.2a shows the resultant dependence of k0 on θm extracted from the experimental
voltammograms (red markers). We find the k0 extracted from TBG for 1° < θm < 2° is strongly

 7

enhanced over that of Bernal (AB)-stacked bilayer graphene (θm = 0°). By comparison to Bernal
bilayer graphene, there is minimal enhancement of k0 for θm > 4°, and for θm < 1°, k0 decreases
monotonically with θm. We attribute the enhancement of the k0 between θm = 1° and 2° to the
elevated DOS arising from the flattened bands that are energetically matched with the formal
potential of Ru(NH3)63+/2+. For θm > 2°, the position of the elevated DOS becomes further apart
from the standard potential (E0), the magnitude of DOS gradually decays, and k0 diminishes as a
result. While the theoretical dependence of k0 on θm (blue markers) calculated from the Gerischer–
Marcus model manifesting a qualitatively consistent trend, these calculations underestimate the
enhancement due to flattened bands in the range 0° < θm < 3°. To explain the anomalous
enhancement of k0 at low twist angles as well as the observed monotonic decrease in k0 with
decreasing θm for θm < 1°, we evaluated the experimental and theoretical real-space variations in
k0 throughout the moiré superlattice. The electronic flat band formed in TBG moiré superlattices
is strongly localized in real space. The localization effects can be visualized in the calculated real
space LDOS maps shown in Figure 2.3. The prominent enhancement of DOS near charge
neutrality is strongly localized at AA sites for TBG of magic angle (θm =1.1°), while very weak
real-space partitioning of the overall DOS in TBG with θm = 3° where flat bands are largely absent.
Similarly, these calculations predict an enhancement of the electroreduction rate constant, k0, at
AA sites near zero overpotential. The massive LDOS at the matched energy results in significant
augmentation of the integral between the electronic states of TBG and the redox molecules.

Although local electron transfer kinetics at isolated AA stacking regions are not directly
measurable owing to the spatial resolution limit (ca. 100 nm) in our SECCM setup, we extract k0
values for AA stacking regions from the apparent k0 measured in the meniscus covering multiple
moiré unit cells that is weighted by the area fraction of each domain. The experimental values for
k0(AA) as a function of θm are shown in Figure 2.2b. Interestingly, we find that k0(AA) is
insensitive to twist angle for θm < 1.5° showing a near-constant value around 0.2 cm/s. This trend
is remarkably consistent with our previous structural measurements that unveiled the geometric
effects of lattice reconstruction. At θm < 1.5°, positive local rotations centered on the AA stacking
sites cause the atomic geometry in these regions to be nearly insensitive to the global θm, ‘pinning’
the local rotation to ~1.2°. On the other hand, localized negative rotation in AB/BA regions
produces a local atomic geometry that closely matches Bernal stacked bilayer graphene. Thus, our
measurements show that for θm < 1.5°, the primary effect that drives the θm-dependent ensemble

 8

k0 shown in Figure 2.2a is the change in the moiré wavelength and attendant AA stacking area
fraction, whereas the local electrochemical activities remain effectively constant.

While this local θm dependence is qualitatively supported by the theoretical calculations, we find
a large discrepancy between the experimental k0(AA) values and those calculated using the
Gerischer–Marcus model alone. This observation may be explained by modifications to the
electronic coupling strength with θm. The highly localized flat bands in small angle TBG would
result in significantly larger real-space overlap of the electronic wave functions of the TBG and
solution-phase redox complexes, inducing an additional augmentation of the measured rate.

2.4 Conclusion

The intriguing interfacial electron transfer behavior of TBG demonstrated here suggests that in
addition to low-temperature correlated electronic phases, moiré-derived flat bands in vdW
architectures present a distinctively tunable material platform to systematically manipulate and
fundamentally probe interfacial charge transfer and (electro)chemical transformations at well-
defined surfaces. In lieu of introducing foreign dopants or structural defects that can be difficult to
control, we have demonstrated the feasibility of activating the pristine graphene basal plane surface
with topological defects that originate from the structural symmetry breaking in twisted bilayers27.
We show that by the precise control of the interlayer twist angle, flat bands that are intrinsically
localized to nanoscale domains in real space may be exploited to tailor the rate constant for an
outer-sphere charge transfer reaction. We anticipate that flat-band engineering with moiré
architectures will become a general strategy to tune the chemical reactivity of a variety of 2D
surfaces beyond graphene.

These findings were published in Nature Chemistry (Yu et al., 2022, 14, 267–273), featured on
the journal cover, and established moiré materials as a new experimental platform for studying
interfacial electron transfer.

Trilayer graphene has garnered significant research interest. Rhombohedral stacked graphene has
shown Mott insulating behavior , superconductivity , and ferromagnetic behaviors . Magic angle
“A-twist-A” trilayer graphene exhibits magnetism-protected superconductivity, and twisted
monolayer on bilayer graphene demonstrates domino-like binary reconstruction behavior. These
exotic properties stem from the unique stacking arrangements possible in trilayer graphene.

The naturally occurring rhombohedral stacking trilayer graphene, often referred to as ABC
graphene, is fundamentally different from the more prevalent Bernal stacking (or ABA stacking)
since the third layer is further displaced from the second layer by half a unit cell. This shift in unit
cell structure results in a remarkably different electronic band structure especially near the Dirac
point, resulting in an intrinsic flat band.11─13

More pronounced flat bands are produced in twisted trilayer graphene (TTG) structures. The
resulting flattened electronic bands, which manifest as large DOS that are localized on AAB and
AAA sites in M-t-B and A-t-A TTG, respectively (Figs. 3.1), now introduce distinctive
possibilities for systematically probing the dependence of interfacial ET on electronic structure
generally and the effects of electronic localization. For example, even within the TTG family,
larger DOS are found in A-t-A as compared to M-t-B near their respective magic angles (Figs.
3.1c,d), properties that naively might be expected to correlate with interfacial ET rates, based on
Marcus–Hush–Chidsey theory.

3.2 Sample preparation
We fabricated samples of twisted-trilayer graphene (TTG) ranging from 0.14° to 3.00° by stacking
monolayer on bilayer graphene with a relative twist. Figure 1D shows a representative optical
image such a sample. They present new variability in stacking domains. Like in twisted bilayer
graphene, the top two layers will generate periodic regions of AA, AB, and BA. However, relative
to the bottom layer, the indistinguishable AB and BA regions will form the distinctive ABC and
ABA stacking domains (see Figure 3.2).

Scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM) with ca. 200 nm spatial resolution allows us
to obtain electrochemical measurements exclusively at the basal plane of the samples of natural
trilayer graphene as well as TTG samples.

3.4 Conclusion
These results provide a powerful demonstration of the sensitivity of interfacial ET kinetics to the
three-dimensional localization of electronic states at electrochemical surfaces and raise the
question of whether traditional measurements of ET rates at macroscopic electrodes might severely
underestimate the true local rate constant, which may be mediated by atomic defects that strongly
localize electronic DOS, at these interfaces. In turn, SECCM measurements are shown to be
powerful tools for probing layer-dependent electronic localization in atomic heterostructure
electrodes.

Future experimental and theoretical work is needed to shed more light on the microscopic origin
of these electron-transfer modulations in the context of reorganization energy, electronic coupling,
and even electric double-layer structure. This work also heralds the use of moiré materials as a
versatile and systematically tunable experimental platform for theoretical adaptations of the MHC
framework applied to interfaces with localized electronic states, which are representative of
defective surfaces that are ubiquitous to nearly all real electrochemical systems. In an applied
context, twistronics is shown to be a powerful pathway for engineering pristine 2D material
surfaces to execute charge transfer processes with facile kinetics, holding implications for
electrocatalysis and other energy conversion device schemes that could benefit from ultrathin,
flexible, and/or transparent electrodes that retain high electron-transfer kinetics.

4. Electrostatic gate control over interfacial kinetics of MoS2 layers (in ACS
nanoscience Au, 2023)14
4.1 Motivation
The twist angle dependence of heterogeneous charge transfer kinetics at twisted bilayer and trilayer
graphene electrodes produces the greatest effects near the magic-angles. These effects are driven
by the angle-dependent tuning of moiré-derived flat bands that modulate electron transfer
processes with the solution-phase redox couple. For redox couples with E0 poorly aligned with
the charge neutrality point of graphene (e.g. Co(Phen)32+/3+), the enhancement is significantly
quenched due to the shift of Fermi level away from the flatbands at the electrochemical bias to
drive this reaction. In this work, we aim at exploiting field-effect as an orthogonal approach to
tune the electronic band alignment and tailor the interfacial electrochemical activities for a broader
range of redox molecules.

4.4 Conclusion
This study showed how electrostatic gating of semiconducting electrodes results in the modulation
of intrinsic electrochemical kinetics as well as electronic transport properties. The FET scheme
provides an orthogonal knob to control interfacial charge transfer, distinct from the applied
electrochemical bias, but the effects of in-plane transport must be considered. This work
demonstrates that vdW heterostructures of few-layer MoS2, graphite, and hBN provide a
distinctive platform for interrogating charge transfer kinetics and electronic transport effects when
coupled with SECCM. SECCM enables the exclusion of electrochemical gating effects on in-plane
transport, allowing electronic transport to be controlled exclusively by the solid-state gate.
Combined experimental measurements and finite-element simulations show how in-plane charge
transport has a pronounced effect on the apparent electron transfer kinetics at semiconducting
electrodes, especially at lower charge carrier densities.

5.2 Methodology

To compare the intercalation of lithium ions into different graphene interfaces, heterostructures of
hBN, graphene, and α-RuCl3 were constructed by mechanical exfoliation of each vdW material
and subsequent stacking of the thinned crystals using a standard dry transfer method. on-chip

electrochemical device shown in Figure 5.1b showed a doped graphene region with α-RuCl3 and

5.3 Results and discussion
Raman spectroscopy was used to follow the vibrational modes of the graphene (G and 2D peaks)
as the electrochemical bias was varied. Figures 5.1d–f display the changes in the G and 2D peak
positions and heights of these spectra as a function of the electrochemical bias, Vwe. For the GrRu
region, there is a noticeable discontinuity in the G peak position, the 2D peak position, and the
2D/G intensity ratio between −2.5 and −3 V that is consistent with a significant change in doping.
We interpret this as a large influx of lithium ions. For the Gr region, the changes seen in the G and
2D peak positions and the 2D/G peak height ratio are consistent with merely electrostatic gating
from the electrolyte or dilute intercalation until about −4.5 V.11,12 At −5 V, there is a sudden blue
shift in the position of the G and 2D peaks, and a relatively large drop in the 2D/G height ratio,
which is consistent with the Gr region undergoing an intercalation event with a large influx of
lithium ions.11,12 Taken together, this analysis of the Raman spectra suggests that the onset
potential for intercalation has shifted by at least +2.5 V (compared to a +0.5 V shift for
graphene/MoCh2 interfaces)12,13 with the introduction of α-RuCl3 into the graphene
heterostructure.

5.4 Conclusion
These results demonstrate that the strong hole doping induced from interfacing graphene with α-
RuCl3 significantly reduces the magnitude of external electrochemical bias (Vwe) needed to drive
the intercalation of lithium ions, with the intercalation onset shifted by >2.5 V relative to the
intercalation of undoped graphene that is only encapsulated by hBN. This decrease in potential
needed for intercalation is shown to be tunable by inserting hBN spacers between the graphene
and α-RuCl3 layers. Computations show that intercalation results in strong electron doping of both
α-RuCl3 and graphene, but experimentally, intercalation appears to be initiated only when charge
transfer to graphene becomes spontaneous. In this study, a distinctive level of control for electronic
and ionic charge accumulation in vdW heterostructures is realized, which may spur future studies
on tailoring intercalation (electro)chemistry in 2D materials.

6. Conclusion
This research establishes moiré superlattices as a programmable platform for manipulating
heterogeneous electron transfer (ET) kinetics at the solid–liquid interface, effectively bridging the
gap between quantum-confined electronic structure and macroscopic electrochemical
performance. By exploiting the twist-angle-dependent electronic states of van der Waals
heterostructures, specifically twisted bilayer and trilayer graphene, we demonstrated a systematic
modulation of interfacial ET rates spanning three orders of magnitude. A pivotal finding was the
translation of "magic angle" physics into room-temperature reactivity, where flat electronic bands
act as potent drivers for enhanced charge transfer. Furthermore, investigations into twisted trilayer
graphene overturned conventional density-of-states arguments, revealing that the spatial
localization of electronic wavefunctions within specific atomic layers, rather than the total density
of states, dictates the kinetic facility of the interface.

Beyond graphene, this work elucidated the critical coupling between in-plane charge transport and
surface reactivity at semiconducting MoS2 interfaces and demonstrated extreme tunability of ion
intercalation potentials in graphene/α-RuCl3 heterostructures. Collectively, these results define a
new paradigm of "flat-band engineering" for electrochemistry, where topological defects and
electronic correlations are harnessed to create "bespoke" active sites on pristine basal planes
without reliance on extrinsic chemical dopants. This strategy not only challenges existing
theoretical frameworks, suggesting that standard models must be re-evaluated to account for
microscopic electronic localization, but also provides a versatile blueprint for the rational design
of next-generation energy conversion and storage architectures with atomically precise kinetic
control.
