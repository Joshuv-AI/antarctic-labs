Is socioeconomic position associated with hospital admissions? findings from a population-based cross-sectional study among middle-aged and older Malaysians
Authors: Suhana Jawahir, Nur Zahirah Balqis, Shaun Wen Huey Lee, Pui San Saw, Mohamad Fadli Kharie, Weng Hong Fun, Yamunah Devi Apalasamy, Norma Mansor, Halima Awang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGuided by the Andersen Behavioural Model of Healthcare Utilisation, this study investigated the relationship between socioeconomic status, enabling factors and need factors in predicting hospital admission.Design and settingData were analysed from the first wave of the Malaysia Ageing and Retirement Survey (MARS), a longitudinal cohort study.Participants5612 Malaysian adults aged 40 and older participated in MARS.Outcome measuresA Generalised Structural Equation Modelling was applied to test associations between socioeconomic status (financial well-being and education levels), enablers and need factors with hospital admission. The weighted coefficients, SEs and 95% CIs were reported. The role of the enablers as mediators was tested. Analysis was performed using STATA v.18.ResultsSocioeconomic status did not affect hospital admission. Better financial well-being lowered functional impairment (β=−0.006, 95% CI −0.009 to −0.003) and better cognitive status (β=0.130, 95% CI 0.109 to 0.152) with no significant effect on the number of chronic diseases. Higher education improved cognitive function (β=2.387, 95% CI 2.081 to 2.693) but was not associated with functional impairment or the number of chronic diseases. Worsening functional impairment and a higher number of chronic conditions predicted higher hospital admissions. A better cognitive status reduces it. Outlook on life mediated the effect between financial well-being and education levels with hospital admission (β=−0.002, 95% CI −0.004 to −0.001 for financial well-being and β=−0.019, 95% CI −0.036 to −0.006 for education).ConclusionHospital admissions were driven by health needs, regardless of socioeconomic status, highlighting the importance of promoting primary prevention of diseases.

Introduction

High hospital admissions, often seen as overcrowded and congested emergency departments, are a significant challenge for health systems worldwide. This problem can lead to adverse effects, such as worse health outcomes, low patient satisfaction and delays in receiving timely care.1 2 A similar issue was observed in Malaysia, whereby overcrowded emergencies were associated with an increase in mortality among patients presenting with sepsis.3 The prolonged waiting time for admission from the emergency department to the ward highlights a significant issue stemming from the high demand for inpatient care.4 Despite numerous efforts at disease prevention, hospital admissions in Malaysia have stagnated, showing no significant decline over the past few decades. A trend analysis between 1996 and 2015 reveals the prevalence of hospital admission among older Malaysians slowly rising from 10.3% (95% CI 9.3% to 11.4%) to 11.1% (95% CI 9.5% to 13.0%).5 In 2019, out of MYR 34 million of Malaysia’s public sector health expenditure, approximately 68% was allocated to curative care. Notably, MYR 20 million was explicitly dedicated to hospital utilisation. The cost of supporting hospital services has also increased from MYR 9.4 billion in 2011 to MYR 20 billion in 2021, highlighting the burden on health systems and the challenge of channelling resources towards preventive care.6

The consistent demand for hospital admissions in Malaysia has been significantly influenced by shifts in the disease demographic. The increasing prevalence of non-communicable and chronic diseases has directly contributed to this trend, leading to a greater need for hospital care.5 7 Diseases of the circulatory systems, which were significantly an outcome of non-communicable diseases, were among the top reasons for hospital admissions in Malaysia in 2022.8 A population-based study conducted in Malaysia showed a rising trend of chronic diseases where the prevalence of Diabetes Mellitus among the older population increased from 14.8% (95% CI 13.6% to 16.2%) to 38.1% (95% CI 35.7% to 40.4%), and the prevalence of Hypertension rose from 55.5% (95% CI 53.8% to 57.3%) to 69.2% (95% CI 67.1% to 71.2%) between 1996 and 2015.5 The prevalence of these chronic diseases was notably higher among individuals aged 40 and above compared with other age groups, underscoring the need for targeted assessments in this subgroup.9 Furthermore, the growing population with functional limitations has also contributed to hospital admissions due to poorer overall health status and a greater need to treat their health conditions.10 A recent study found that functional capacity in the ability to self-care significantly correlated with healthcare utilisation in Malaysia.11

Evidence shows that adults from lower socioeconomic status tend to have higher risks of contracting various illnesses and shorter life expectancy due to disadvantages in multiple aspects of life.12 This includes lower work satisfaction, health literacy and the ability to pursue holistic well-being through preferred care modalities, physical activity and a nutritious diet, among others.13 14 In response, the government introduced several initiatives to improve the well-being of those from lower socioeconomic status. PeKa B40, initiated by the Ministry of Health (MOH) in 2019, is a primary prevention programme designed to offer free health screenings for non-communicable diseases to individuals within the bottom 40% of household income quintile (B40).15 It also covers part of the costs of receiving treatments, such as purchasing health aids, cancer treatments and transportation to healthcare facilities. In addition to this effort, the government introduced Skim Perubatan Madani in 2023. This scheme subsidises some treatment costs for diseases obtained from private general practitioners, explicitly targeting the B40 group.16

While recognising the influence of socioeconomic disparities on the overall health and well-being of the population, the specific impact on healthcare utilisation, particularly hospital admissions, remains inconclusive in Malaysia. Previous studies have indicated that income and educational levels were not consistently predictive of hospital admission in Malaysia.7 17 Although these studies provided valuable insights, they did not address the scarcity of research on the complex and interrelated pathways between various factors in predicting hospital admissions. As pursued in the current study, updating this analysis with recent data while addressing these complexities is crucial to assessing how differing socioeconomic statuses affect hospital admissions.

As efforts to tackle the issue progress, there is a growing focus on identifying additional factors that may significantly influence hospital admissions across various socioeconomic statuses. Among these factors, the impact of psychosocial elements on health and healthcare utilisation is crucial but warrants further investigation, particularly within the Malaysian context. Evidence regarding the impact of psychosocial factors on healthcare utilisation suggests a mixed effect, with some factors increasing utilisation by boosting motivation for better health and enabling better access to care. In contrast, others decrease utilisation through negative perceptions and experiences about the service.1820 In addition, private health insurance ownership is another important factor in changing the path between socioeconomic status and hospital admission. In Malaysia, more affluent people are more likely to own private health insurance.21 However, due to Malaysia’s dual health system, private health insurance provides access exclusively to the private health sector, offering timelier and preference-based healthcare options.22 Private health insurance’s overall impact and role on hospital admissions in Malaysia require further exploration.

The Andersen Behavioural Model of Healthcare Utilisation is a foundational framework for comprehensively understanding the diverse determinants of hospital admissions, providing a structured basis for hypothesis testing in the current study. In the context of Malaysia and the current study’s boundary, financial well-being and education serve as predisposing factors, representing the population with various socioeconomic positions. The inclusion of financial well-being as a predisposing factor diverges from Andersen’s original conceptualisation, where financial status primarily functioned as an enabler for accessing healthcare services in the model. In the current study, financial well-being represents unique demographics within the population (socioeconomic status) rather than as an enabling factor for access. Education level, on the other hand, has always been represented as a predisposing factor in the model.23

This study aims to investigate the relationship between diverse socioeconomic status (predisposing factors), outlook on life, non-monetary support, private health insurance (enabling factors), functional impairment, cognitive status, chronic diseases (need factors) and hospital admission (health behaviour). Understanding these complex relationships would offer invaluable insights into potential interventions and highlight critical areas for targeted efforts in Malaysia’s healthcare system. Method

Study design and sampling population

The study used data from the Malaysia Ageing and Retirement Survey (MARS), a longitudinal cohort study among community-dwelling Malaysian adults aged 40 and over.24 25 MARS is a nationwide study involving respondents from Peninsular and East Malaysia. The face-to-face interviews were conducted using a computer-assisted personal interviewing method. The data obtained during the first wave, between August 2018 and May 2019, were used for the current analysis. MARS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design based on the sampling frame provided by the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) from the 2010 Population and Housing Census. The sample size was determined to ensure national representativeness across states and urban–rural strata. The primary stratification was by state, which was further divided into urban and rural areas. Malaysia was divided into approximately 75 000 enumeration blocks (EBs), each comprising 80–120 living quarters and averaging 500–600 individuals. A total of 900 EBs were selected using proportionate allocation based on state population size, ensuring representation across all states and strata. Within each selected EB, 10–12 households were randomly selected, resulting in 9542 households included in the survey. Eligible respondents were household members aged 40 years and older who had lived in the household most of the time, with up to three of the oldest eligible members per household selected for interview. Detailed information on the study’s design and findings can be found in the publicly available report.25

Measures

The main components of the MARS survey instrument were constructed with guidance from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and the Japanese Study of Ageing and Retirement (JSTAR).2628 The HRS is an ongoing longitudinal study of a representative sample of non-institutionalised USA residents, starting in 1992 with biennial follow-ups of adults aged 51–61 at baseline, later including more cohorts.26 The JSTAR is another longitudinal study among middle-aged and older Japanese aged 50–75, gathering data on their economic, social and health conditions since 2007.27 The study design, development and validation process of both studies are described in detail elsewhere.2628

Independent variables

Predisposing factors

The selected predisposing factor was socioeconomic status, represented by respondents’ financial well-being and education levels.

Financial well-being

The financial well-being of respondents was assessed using the Financial Well-Being Composite Score, developed based on the indicators of financial security outlined in the United Nations’ 2018 Active Ageing Report.29 The score comprises four elements: individual monthly income, amount of savings, ability to manage monthly expenditures and perception of financial independence. Each element was categorised, and each category was scored from 0 to 100. The scores from each element for each respondent were then summed and converted into a composite score, with a higher score indicating better financial well-being. Details of the four elements and the score formulation can be found in online supplemental data 1.

Education level

The education level refers to the highest level attained by the respondents, grouped into three categories: (1) No schooling or up to primary, (2) Secondary and (3) Tertiary.

Covariates

Age was found to be significant in predicting hospital admission in the univariate logistic regression and, thus, was included as a covariate in the path analysis to control for its varying effects on the relationship being tested. In this study, age was retained as a continuous variable. Other variables (sex, ethnicity and marital status) were not significant in the univariate logistic regression and thus were excluded from the path analysis.

Enabling factors

Outlook on life

The MARS questionnaire on perceptions and attitudes towards life was based on the HRS psychosocial and lifestyle content. Outlook on life refers to the respondents’ perceptions and attitudes towards their lives. It consisted of 17 items that required respondents to assess various facets of their lives. For each item, respondents were asked to rate the frequency of these experiences on a scale of 1 to 5, with one indicating ‘never’, 2 for ‘rarely’, 3 for ‘sometimes’, 4 for ‘often’ and 5 for ‘always’. Negative statements were scored using reverse scoring. A Malaysian study reported a high internal consistency among the 17 items (Cronbach alpha: 0.851).30 The possible total score ranged from 17 to a maximum of 85, with a higher score indicating a more positive outlook on life.

Non-monetary support

This variable refers to non-monetary support from children, parents or siblings. The support was categorised into two groups: absence of non-monetary support or receiving any form of non-monetary support. This includes assistance such as looking after children, purchasing household items, medications or supplements, helping with house chores and other similar activities.

Private health insurance coverage

This variable examined whether respondents owned private health insurance, including voluntarily purchased and employer-sponsored plans. Life insurance was not included in this category. Respondents answered “Yes” or “No” based on ownership.

Need factors

The need factors were categorised as perceived (functional impairment and cognitive status) and evaluated (number of chronic diseases).

Functional impairment

The first perceived need factor was functional impairment, represented by the Activities of Daily Living (ADL). MARS’ ADL questions were constructed based on the HRS questions about physical functioning, referring to the ability to perform nine basic self-care activities: bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting, transferring from bed to chair and vice versa, walking, climbing, mouth care and eating. Respondents reported their ability for each activity based on one of these answer options: (a) Able to do it all by themselves (scored as zero), (b) Sometimes need some help (scored as one), (c) Always need some help (scored as two) or (d) Always need help (scored as three). A higher score thus indicated an increasing functional impairment, while a score of zero indicated an absence of any impairment. An exploratory factor analysis was performed, and one item, ‘climbing stairs’, was found to have low factor loading (0.501) and high uniqueness variance (0.681) and thus was removed from predicting functional impairment.

Cognitive status

The second perceived need factor was cognitive status, adapted by MARS from the HRS study. The HRS tool on cognitive status was validated and reported to have high internal consistency (Cronbach alpha >0.6 across several HRS versions) and a highly accurate classification of cognitive impairment (>80%).31 32 The factor was measured using multiple tests, including word recall, simple counting arithmetic, date, object, people and animal naming. Each respondent’s total score for all questions testing cognitive function across 15 items was summed. The possible total score ranged from 0 to a maximum of 83, with a higher score signifying a higher cognitive status. Although some studies using HRS-based measures have applied thresholds such as the lowest 25th percentile to indicate cognitive impairment, there is no universally established cut-off for this composite score. Therefore, in the current study, the cognitive score was retained as a continuous variable, allowing higher values to reflect better overall cognitive performance.

Number of chronic diseases

The evaluated need factor of interest was chronic disease, represented by the number of chronic diseases respondents reported based on formal diagnosis by healthcare professionals. Respondents were asked whether they had any of the 17 chronic diseases, and this comprehensive list can be found in online supplemental data 2. The number of chronic diseases was categorised into four groups: (a) None, (b) One, (c) Two and (d) Three or more.

Outcome measure

Hospital admission

We examined factors influencing hospital admission among respondents, defined as any episode of hospital admission for any medical condition. Respondents were asked whether they had been hospitalised in the past 12 months before the survey, to which they answered “Yes “or “No”. The outcome remained coded as a binary outcome in the subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

The attributes of community-dwelling adults aged 40 and older were described based on the predisposing, enabling and need factors selected for this study. The prevalence of hospital admission was also described. The sample was weighted to adjust for varying selection probabilities, non-response rate and post-stratification adjustment factor calculated by strata, age and gender, based on the Malaysian population data by the DOSM. We presented continuous data by stating their weighted mean (M) and SE, and categorical data were expressed using weighted frequency (n) and percentage (%). Missing data were initially considered when describing the respondents’ characteristics but were subsequently excluded, and a complete-case analysis was used for the inferential analysis. In this study, one respondent (0.02%) was excluded due to missing weight and related variables necessary for generating the weight.

The associations between the selected predisposing, enabling and need factors with hospital admission were explored. The factors were entered into a path analysis to examine the interrelation between factors and outcomes. A Generalised Structural Equation Model (GSEM) approach was employed as the factors encompassed numerical and categorical variables. GSEM estimators are maximum likelihood estimators from the linear, exponential family density optimal for nonlinear regression models with homoscedastic additive errors.33 The outcome and predictors were assigned to relevant families and links. The outcome, hospital admission, was a Bernoulli family, with a link logit. Education levels and the number of chronic diseases were assigned to ordinal families, with link logit. Private health insurance and non-monetary support had a Bernoulli family distribution with a link logit. The financial well-being composite score, outlook on life, functional impairment and cognitive status were numerical factors; therefore, they were assigned Gaussian distributions. The relationship between the predisposing, enabling and need factors influencing the outcome was drawn based on the framework illustrated in figure 1. Survey weights were incorporated into the GSEM analysis, and associations were estimated using weighted coefficients with robust SEs and 95% CIs.

Simultaneously, we explored whether the enabling factors mediate the relationship between the predisposing factors with need and outcome factors in the path analysis. We calculated each relationship’s total, direct and indirect effects, along with the robust SEs and 95% CI, to determine the presence of a mediation effect. These values helped gauge the extent of change in the outcomes when we kept the independent variable (socioeconomic status) constant while allowing the mediators to vary in a manner consistent with what would happen if the independent variable increased by one unit. A mediation effect was confirmed when the predisposing factor (socioeconomic status, financial well-being or education level) affected the hospital utilisation (hospital admission) through the enablers, with significant indirect effects. The indirect effects were bootstrapped with 2000 replications to obtain more accurate SEs and a bias-corrected 95% bootstrap percentile CI by avoiding violation of the normality assumption of the sampling distribution.34 Financial well-being and education levels were tested in separate GSEM models to prevent potential interactions. A sensitivity analysis was conducted in which both the financial well-being and education models were additionally adjusted for body mass index (BMI) and physical activity to account for lifestyle factors. All relationships were considered significant at a p value of <0.05. All analyses accounted for survey weights, clustering and stratification using complex survey procedures and were performed through STATA v.18 (Stata, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Overall, 5612 respondents were included in this study, representing nearly 10 million adults aged above 40 in Malaysia (table 1). The weighted mean of financial well-being among respondents was 46.79 (SE: 19.89). The mean scores for functional impairment and cognitive status reflect variation across adults aged 40 years and older rather than the prevalence of impairment. The largest education level group was those who had up to the secondary level of education (48.05%; 95% CI 45.95% to 50.16%), and the largest age group was those aged between 40 and 49 (35.62%; 95% CI 32.95% to 38.38%). Most respondents were of Malay ethnicity (47.60%; 95% CI 43.49% to 51.74%) and married (77.53%; 95% CI 75.43% to 79.49%). About three-quarters of the respondents were not covered by private health insurance (74.71%; 95% CI 72.06% to 77.19%), while slightly more than half received some non-monetary support (55.11%; 95% CI 52.15% to 58.03%). In the last 12 months before the study, 9.93% of respondents had at least one episode of hospital admission (95% CI 8.92% to 11.04%).

The model is represented in three distinct diagrams in figure 2, where need factors (functional limitation, cognitive status and the number of chronic diseases) are illustrated separately for better clarity, even though they were analysed simultaneously. The direct effect of financial well-being on hospital admission was insignificant, indicating the absence of an association between financial well-being and the possibility of hospital admission in the past year (β=0.004, 95% CI −0.003 to 0.011). Functional impairment and number of chronic diseases had a direct effect on hospital admission, where higher functional impairment and number of chronic diseases predicted a greater likelihood of hospital admission in the past year (β=0.085, 95% CI 0.039 to 0.131, and β=0.551, 95% CI 0.455 to 0.647, respectively). Improved financial well-being leads to a better outlook on life (β=0.129, 95% CI 0.110 to 0.149), ownership of private health insurance (β=0.043, 95% CI 0.035 to 0.050), functional impairment (β=−0.006, 95% CI −0.009 to −0.003) and cognitive status (β=0.130, 95% CI 0.109 to 0.152). Financial well-being did not affect the number of chronic diseases. A better outlook on life lowered functional impairment (β=−0.021, 95% CI −0.034 to −0.009), improved cognitive status (β=0.059, 95% CI 0.015 to 0.102) and predicted fewer chronic diseases (β=−0.011, 95% CI −0.021 to −0.002). Those with better financial well-being had increased ownership of private health insurance (β=0.043, 95% CI 0.035 to 0.050). Private health insurance predicted better cognitive status (β=2.763, 95% CI 1.583 to 3.943) but worsening functional impairment (β=0.173, 95% CI 0.010 to 0.335). Non-monetary support predicted fewer chronic diseases (β=0.207, 95% CI 0.039 to 0.375).

Table 2 summarises the coefficients, SEs, and CIs, highlighting the total, direct and indirect effects between variables in the model. The total indirect effect was not significant in affecting the relationship between financial well-being and hospital admissions. However, only one indirect effect significantly affected the path between financial well-being and hospital admission. Outlook on life significantly mediated the relationship between financial well-being and hospital admission (β=−0.002, 95% CI −0.004 to −0.001).

In the model examining the relationship between education and hospital admission (see figure 3 and table 3), education levels were not significantly linked to the likelihood of hospital admission in the past year (β=0.065, 95% CI −0.035 to 0.165). However, all need factors significantly predicted hospital admission. Increased functional impairment and a higher number of chronic diseases increased the likelihood of hospital admission (β=0.082, 95% CI 0.037 to 0.128 and β=0.550, 95% CI 0.454 to 0.645), while better cognitive status reduced it (β=−0.016, 95% CI −0.030 to −0.002). Outlook on life was the only enabler significantly affecting hospital admission (β=−0.018, 95% CI −0.030 to −0.005).

Higher education levels were associated with an improved outlook on life (β=1.094, 95% CI 0.781 to 1.408), ownership of private health insurance (β=0.625, 95% CI 0.536 to 0.714) and cognitive status (β=2.387, 95% CI 2.081 to 2.693). A better outlook on life predicted lower functional impairment (β=−0.024 95% CI −0.036 to −0.012), better cognitive status (β=0.076, 95% CI 0.054 to 0.098) and fewer chronic diseases (β=−0.012, 95% CI −0.021 to −0.003). Ownership of private health insurance predicted higher cognitive status (β=2.182, 95% CI 1.066 to 3.298). Non-monetary support predicted more chronic diseases (β=0.209, 95% CI 0.041 to 0.376).

Table 3 summarises the coefficients, SEs and CIs of all paths between education level and hospital admission. The total indirect effect of education level on hospital admission was not statistically significant. However, several indirect paths were found to be significant. Outlook on life and non-monetary support mediated the relationship between education level and hospital admission (β=−0.019, 95% CI −0.036 to −0.006 and β=−0.038, 95% CI −0.072 to −0.005, consecutively). There were two significant serial mediation paths: outlook on life and functional impairment (β=−0.007, 95% CI −0.013 to −0.003) and private health insurance and functional impairment (β=−0.022, 95% CI −0.043 to −0.003), both reducing hospital admission.

In the sensitivity analysis, additional adjustment for BMI and physical activity did not alter the main findings, supporting the robustness of the main models. The direct associations of financial well-being and education level with hospital admission remained consistent in terms of magnitude, direction and statistical significance. However, the association between functional impairment and hospital admission became non-significant in both models. All other relationships were unchanged.

Discussion

The study applies the Andersen Behavioural Model of Healthcare Utilisation to examine the complex relationship between socioeconomic status and hospital admissions, exploring various enablers and health needs associated with different socioeconomic levels. The GSEM path analysis revealed that financial well-being and education were not associated with hospital admission. This contradicts the theory that individuals with lower socioeconomic status are more likely to be admitted. These findings align with a Malaysian study that examined inequities in access to inpatient care from 1986 to 2015 using population-based data. This study found that lower wealth quintiles generally did not impact hospital admission, with exceptions at two specific points in time (1996 and 2005).17 Similarly, a 2010 study among community-dwelling older adults found no significant association between education level and inpatient utilisation.7 Both studies targeted different segments of the Malaysian population; in contrast to this study, which focused explicitly on adults over 40.

Such findings need to be interpreted in the context of the Malaysian health system. The universal access to essential healthcare services, including inpatient care, in Malaysia is considerable, with comprehensive services provided almost free of charge by the public sector.35 36 Such a system ensures that the population can access care regardless of socioeconomic status, highlighting that access barriers do not impede inpatient care in Malaysia. Alternatively, if higher hospital admissions are expected among those of lower socioeconomic status due to more significant health needs, the lack of association raises concerns about potential care disparities.37 This suggests that individuals in need may not receive adequate care, while timely care for those of higher socioeconomic status could mask any link between socioeconomic status and hospital admission.

The associations between socioeconomic statuses (financial well-being and education) in predicting the various need factors (functional limitations, cognitive status and the number of chronic diseases) incorporated into the model had contradicting findings. Better financial well-being predicted better functioning and cognitive status but did not significantly affect the number of chronic diseases. Higher education level was associated with better cognitive function, but had no significant impact on functional impairment and the number of chronic diseases. This mixed pattern of associations between socioeconomic status and health need factors potentially explains why the expected association of increased hospital admissions among lower socioeconomic status was not observed. The driving theory was that socioeconomic status only affected several health conditions (the need factors) and did not pervasively influence hospital admission.

Evidence from other Malaysian studies examining the link between socioeconomic status and health needs has been inconsistent. A study among rural-dwelling older adults found that poorer financial status was associated with a higher need for assistance due to functional limitations.38 Several studies among older Malaysians aged 60 years and above found that education did not affect functional limitation.39 40 Another study involving older rural dwellers found no association linking income with cognitive impairment.41 As for the relationship between socioeconomic status and the number of chronic diseases, a population-based study among adults above 60 found no association between income and education with multiple morbidities in the adjusted model.42 However, another study conducted among adults aged 18 and older in the Segamat district of Malaysia found that education significantly predicted multimorbidity.43 It is important to note that the studies used different methodological methods, targeted distinct populations, and were conducted at various time points, which may have contributed to the observed differences. Nevertheless, the existing evidence thus far indicates a mixed association between socioeconomic status and Malaysians’ health status (needs).

Nonetheless, this study found that all need factors, functional impairment, cognitive status and chronic diseases of those over 40 were significantly associated with a higher possibility of hospital admissions in both financial well-being and education models. Unequivocally, when an individual is ill or has a chronic condition, the need for hospital admission increases, as confirmed by various Malaysian studies.5 7 11 The findings highlight the necessity of intensifying efforts to reduce modifiable risk factors associated with developing health morbidities.44 45

Another notable finding was that, among the three enablers tested in the models, outlook on life consistently influenced hospital admission and all health needs. Specifically, we found that individuals with a more positive perception and interpretation of their lives had lower health needs (or better health outcomes) and a reduced likelihood of hospital admission. This corresponds with various studies exploring the effect of a positive outlook.4649 For instance, a systematic review found that psychological factors, including a positive outlook, improved the quality of life post-stroke incidents.49 A positive outlook also predicts better cognitive function, reduced physical disability and better perceived general well-being.47 48 50 Additionally, higher financial well-being and education levels improved their outlook on life.

The outlook on life was identified as a significant mediator in several pathways. For example, it mediated the effect between financial well-being and education levels with hospital admission. In these models, the outlook on life consistently reduced all health needs. The study explored respondents’ perceptions of their lives, including satisfaction, control, autonomy and social support. The significant mediating role of outlook on life with health needs and hospital admission revealed that how people perceive their lives is essential and affects their health and well-being. This finding aligns with similar research conducted among community-dwelling older persons in Malaysia, which found that higher perceived social support mediated the relationship between functional limitations by reducing depressive symptoms and improving quality of life.51 This finding highlights the importance of outlook on life in mediating the effects of socioeconomic status on health needs and hospital admission. The implication of these findings offers valuable insights for policymakers to improve the health of Malaysians. Malaysia may benefit from developing environments, platforms and programmes that empower individuals, providing them with support and opportunities for meaningful contributions. Such platforms should facilitate social interaction and foster a sense of connectedness, strongly emphasising community-based approaches to promote overall well-being.

In this analysis, private health insurance did not directly affect hospital admission. However, only a quarter of the respondents had private health insurance, which may have influenced the results. Additionally, a limitation of this study is the lack of distinction between public and private healthcare usage, which is essential since private health insurance is used solely in private healthcare facilities in Malaysia.22 Analysing these sectors separately could reveal different patterns. Despite the limitation, the study found that private health insurance mediated the relationship between education level and hospital admission, reducing functional impairment and, consequently, the need for hospitalisation.

The current evidence suggests that rather than concentrating solely on health improvement policies for individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, it may be more beneficial for the health system to enhance efforts to reduce modifiable risk factors for chronic health conditions among all Malaysians, regardless of socioeconomic status. The Malaysian Ministry of Health’s current strategic planning aligns with this approach, focusing on strengthening illness prevention and early detection across the entire Malaysian population.52 Furthermore, the MOH emphasises the crucial role of primary care in improving population health. In 2017, enhanced primary healthcare was introduced to assign populations seeking care at primary health facilities to dedicated health teams. This approach ensures individuals receive comprehensive and coordinated care, including health awareness and empowerment initiatives.53 This aligns with the key strategies outlined in the Malaysian Health White Paper 2023 to combat chronic diseases, which emphasises systemic and structural improvements for health promotion and disease prevention through public health strengthening and intersectoral collaborations.54

Another initiative Malaysia may consider to strengthen its health systems is emphasising enhancing patient empowerment for self-efficacy. This approach recognises the importance of the individual in the healthcare system, making them feel valued and integral to the process.55 This underscores the need for patient empowerment in the context of national health improvement. Strategies such as leveraging social media, telemedicine and gaming can significantly increase health awareness and support this empowerment process.55 Additionally, patient empowerment also requires a genuine approach to person-centred care, where patients are actively involved in their health management.56 The chronic care model offers a comprehensive approach to improving the management of chronic diseases. It aims to transform healthcare delivery systems through enhanced health system organisation and leadership, redesigning delivery systems to provide team-based care, supporting patients in self-management of their conditions and linking patients to community resources. If implemented effectively, this model has the potential to significantly improve the management of chronic diseases in Malaysia.57

This study has several strengths. It used nationally representative secondary data, enabling generalisation of the findings to Malaysian adults. Guided by the Andersen Behavioural Model of Healthcare Utilisation, the study provides a theoretically grounded examination of factors associated with hospital admissions. The use of path analysis enabled a systematic assessment of complex interrelationships between variables. In addition, mediation analysis was employed to examine both direct and indirect effects of socioeconomic factors on hospital admissions, strengthening the robustness of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and all variables were based on self-reported and perceived measures, which may be subject to recall bias and individual reporting bias. Only a small proportion of respondents reported having private health insurance, which may have limited the ability to detect its direct effects.

Conclusion

The study investigated various factors predicting hospital admission among Malaysians aged 40 and older. The findings revealed that lower socioeconomic statuses (financial well-being and education) did not directly predict a higher likelihood of hospital admission. However, health needs, such as worsening functional limitations, poorer cognitive status and a higher number of chronic diseases, were significant predictors of hospital admission. The study suggests that Malaysia should continue to strengthen the primary prevention of various health diseases and conditions for the whole population, ensuring inclusivity regardless of socioeconomic background. Additionally, shifting the focus from disease prevention and management alone to improving populations’ perceptions and outlooks on their lives could be beneficial. This could be achieved by introducing interventions to enhance social participation and support in their lives, which could be helpful in the general population. Enhancement towards empowering and improving self-efficacy through a person-centred approach among Malaysians to look after their health is potentially the most crucial strategy for consideration. Although socioeconomic status had no direct link to hospital admissions and showed mixed effects on health needs in this study, its overall influence on well-being remains a crucial topic due to its widespread and complex effects on various aspects of life. Moving forward, examining the impact of socioeconomic status on health from different perspectives, particularly regarding the quality and timeliness of care, is an important recommendation.
