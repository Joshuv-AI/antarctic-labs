Introduction to the Special Section on Improving Health Care Access
Authors: Scott Young
Published: 2026

Access to health care is not merely a feature of high-performing health care systems; it is foundational to health itself. The ability to obtain timely, appropriate, and culturally responsive care can shape and effect outcomes as profoundly as the treatments delivered within clinical walls. Still, despite continued advances in medicine, meaningful gaps in access persist across populations, geographies, and life stages. This special section of The Permanente Journal is dedicated to examining those gaps, understanding their root causes, and highlighting innovations and system-level strategies that can move us toward a more equitable and effective model of care.

Access must be understood as a dynamic, lifelong need, which, although always important, is particularly critical at key junctures. Early childhood, pregnancy, and mental health are but a few examples in which access to preventive, diagnostic, therapeutic, and supportive services can alter and vastly impact outcomes. Delayed or fragmented care during these moments carries consequences that extend well beyond the immediate episode, reinforcing poorer outcomes over time. Ensuring reliable access at these critical points requires understanding the core issues to undertake intentional design and coordination.

Cultural considerations have been and remain central to this work. Health beliefs, language, historical experiences, and trust in institutions all influence how individuals seek and engage with care. Approaches that incorporate cultural humility, community partnership, and tailored communication are essential to making access meaningful rather than merely available.

The rapid expansion of technology introduces both opportunity and complexity. E-visits, digital platforms, secure messaging, and now AI have extended the reach of care into homes and other venues, enabling more continuous and proactive engagement. It has transformed how patients seek information, navigate care options, and make decisions. In some instances, it has led to confusion and unintended delays in care. Ensuring the accuracy, accessibility, and usability of these resources is now a core component of access itself.

High-quality insurance coverage continues to exert a powerful influence on access. Coverage alone is insufficient if costs, administrative complexity, or limited networks create barriers to use. Efforts to simplify coverage, align incentives with preventive and longitudinal care, and reduce friction in navigating the system are essential complements to coverage expansion.

Effective communication (clear, consistent, and bidirectional) remains a cornerstone of accessible care. Innovations such as multilingual digital tools, patient portals, and the integration of community health workers are helping to bridge gaps between patients and care teams. These approaches, when thoughtfully implemented, can strengthen trust, improve adherence, and enhance the overall care experience.

At the system level, integrated health care models play a pivotal role. By aligning clinical services, care processes, and coordination, as well as community partnerships, integrated systems are uniquely positioned to address patient-centered access to care.

The contributions in this special section reflect the breadth and urgency of this topic, from the importance of naloxone availability to a mismatch in online health care usage as examples of access issues. Together, these contributions underscore that improving access is not a single intervention but an ongoing commitment that must be embedded across all aspects of health care delivery.

It is our hope that this special section not only informs but also broadens readers’ perspectives on access, through challenging assumptions, highlighting new possibilities, and inspiring continued efforts to ensure that access to care is recognized and realized as a fundamental determinant of health.
