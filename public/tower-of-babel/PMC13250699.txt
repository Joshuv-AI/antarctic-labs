Assessing Intraurban Variation in Heat‐Attributable Health Burden to Inform Local Interventions
Authors: K Burley Farr, E M McInroe, W L Tsai, A Hsu, A G Rappold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtreme heat exposure leads to excess cardiovascular morbidity and mortality among older adults, but misalignment in the geographic scales of health data and intraurban heat exposure make it challenging to inform local interventions. We introduce an adaptable framework for developing health burden estimates at finer geographic scales by connecting small area analysis (SAA) techniques with an epidemiologic model of heat related health risks in three steps: (a) estimating an exposure response function using individual level cardiovascular disease (CVD) hospitalizations for adults aged 65 and over; (b) downscaling daily CVD hospitalization incidence rates at the ZCTA‐level to census block groups (CBG) using SAA, adjusting for individual‐ and community‐level demographic factors, and (c) linking exposure‐response with daily incidence rates to estimate heat‐attributable burden at the CBG scale. Using the data for a metropolitan area in the southeastern United States for the summer of 2018, we estimated 1.8 to 22.0 excess hospitalizations per 10,000 people across CBG. We demonstrate the utility of this 3‐step approach to help inform localized intervention strategies by classifying neighborhoods as one of four risk groups: Low, Health‐driven, Heat‐driven, and Dual‐channel (driven by both heat and health). In comparison, using coarse‐resolution temperature resulted in a significantly smaller heat‐attributable health burden with different geographic distribution across the CBGs, highlighting the importance of using appropriately scaled exposure data. The findings from this study can support more effective heat interventions to address heat‐health related outcomes, rather than exposure alone.

Introduction

Extreme heat exposure is associated with a range of detrimental health outcomes and can exacerbate existing health issues (Kovats & Hajat, 2008). Epidemiological studies have found an overall significant and positive association between heat and cardiovascular illness, which accounts for the largest portion of overall healthcare expenditure (Bunker et al., 2016; Cheng et al., 2019; Ebi et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Song et al., 2017). Studies have also indicated that the level of heat‐attributable health burden experienced in communities depends on external factors, such as weather and climate patterns, acclimatization, and access to adaptive capacity resources (Bobb et al., 2014; Cheng et al., 2019; Cleland et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2021). Some of the external factors that determine the level of burden are potentially modifiable through physical, lifestyle, and behavioral interventions, communication strategies, and various policies (Crank et al., 2024; Maragno et al., 2020; O’Lenick et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2012).

Reducing heat exposure by modifying characteristics of the built environment is one channel through which reduction in heat‐attributable health burden can be achieved. The built environment is known to contribute to higher surface and air temperatures and account for significant variation in temperatures within urbanized areas (Hsu et al., 2021; Rahmani & Sharifi, 2025; Rizwan et al., 2008), in which the urban heat island adversely affects a range of health outcomes. Local climate zones with high building density, low vegetation, low surface albedo, and high percentage of paved surfaces tend to be hotter within urban areas (Rahmani & Sharifi, 2025). Most urban heat mitigation strategies are implemented at small scales to combat these effects, such as planting street trees, rain gardens, and parks, with localized cooling effects (Kumar et al., 2024). Various methods and modeling approaches have been used to develop higher‐resolution temperature data that can detect this variation using ground measurements, remote sensing data, or a combination of the two (Kianmehr et al., 2023; Shandas et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023).

In contrast to advances in measurement of temperature, lack of health data at small geographic scales is a major challenge to understanding intraurban heat‐attributable health burden variation and the modifying effects of built environment (U.S. EPA, 2018). In the U.S., health data for the wider population are mostly available at aggregated geographic levels, such as postal codes and counties, due to the cost of data collection and privacy concerns (Greenlund et al., 2022; Ozonoff et al., 2007). Some studies have evaluated heat‐attributable health burden based on records aggregated to the postal code (ZIP or Zip Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA)), which is insufficient to capture neighborhood‐level patterns that reflect intraurban variations in temperature metrics that are more relevant to human health, such as air temperature and thermal comfort measures (Fuhrmann et al., 2024; Grubesic & Matisziw, 2006; Guirguis et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022). Fewer studies have used health data available at finer geographic units, such as census tracts (Chuang & Gober, 2015), locally defined communities (Klein Rosenthal et al., 2014), or patient addresses (Gillespie et al., 2024). The geographic resolution of data used in these studies is not available from most U.S. health departments or for all relevant health outcomes, making it difficult to generalize findings to broader areas. Measures of health burden at the appropriate geographic scales that align more closely with temperature variations are needed to enhance planning, prioritization, and evaluations of effective local interventions with respect to the heat‐related health impacts.

To address the need of local‐level health measures, we provided a framework to measure intraurban variability in heat‐health impacts by connecting heat‐health risk estimation and air temperature measurements collected at different spatial scales using small area analysis (SAA). SAA techniques use a range of statistical approaches to predict small area estimates of outcomes from data only available at higher geographic levels, such as surveys or administrative data (Rahman, 2008). We demonstrated the framework by estimating census‐block group (CBG) level heat‐attributable cardiovascular hospitalization burdens and identifying neighborhood level hotspots, using medium‐resolution air temperature data derived by Zhang et al. (2022). Health burden was estimated using epidemiologic model with individual level records of hospitalizations among the aged 65 and over population within the Raleigh‐Cary and Durham‐Chapel Hill metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) located in the southeastern United States.

While the focus in this analysis was on health assessment, we acknowledge that alternative air temperature data sets can yield different results, and these sensitivities are important to continue evaluating. The robustness of the burden estimates was compared to those estimated with lower resolution, more commonly used air temperature data interpolated from weather stations. Finally, we classified CBGs into four risk groups (low risk, heat driven risk, health driven risk, and dual channel driven risk) based on the local‐level burden characteristics of each CBG in relation to the overall burden characteristics of the metropolitan area to exemplify how the framework can be used to inform appropriate interventions at the CBG level. While previous studies have been limited by the geographic scale of health data, this study shows a flexible framework for estimating heat‐attributable health burdens at finer geographical scales matching relevant temperature exposure metrics, which can be extended to other urban areas, health outcomes, and administrative health data sources.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

We focus our analysis on two MSAs in North Carolina, as defined by the US Office of Management and Budget (OMB): the Raleigh‐Cary MSA (Raleigh hereafter) and the Durham‐Chapel Hill MSA (Durham hereafter). Together, these MSAs are more commonly known as the Research Triangle (RT). The area covers three of the top ten largest cities in the state of North Carolina, including the state capitol city of Raleigh (US Census Bureau, 2024b). According to the US Census Bureau, the combined population of the two metropolitan areas in 2020, was just over 2 million people, with an estimated 5.7% growth in population between 2020 and 2023 (US Census Bureau, 2024a). The total land area is 4,409 square miles (11,418 km2), with an overall population density of 454 people per square mile (175 people per square kilometer) in 2020 (US Census Bureau, 2022; US Census Bureau, 2024a). The RT is in the Piedmont physiographic province of North Carolina, characterized by gently rolling hills, and the warm‐humid International Energy Conservation Code climate zone (Antonopoulos et al., 2022). At the Raleigh‐Durham International Airport National Weather Service station, located at the center of the metropolitan areas, the average daily air temperature in the summer of 2018 was 76.6 F (63.5 F, 85.5 F) and the average daily dewpoint was 68.4 F (41.6 F, 74.6 F) (NCEI, Global Surface Summary of the Day).

Data

Air Temperature Data

We relied on 1 km gridded daily near‐surface air temperature data to represent heat exposures (Zhang et al., 2022). This data is available globally from 2003 to 2020 and represents the medium‐scale resolution estimates of air temperature (1 × 1 km) (Zhang et al., 2022). This data source integrates ground‐based air temperature measurements with satellite‐derived digital elevation models and land surface temperature using a Spatially Varying Coefficient Models with Sign Preservation algorithm (Zhang et al., 2022). The authors demonstrated that this data had comparable accuracy to other gridded daily air temperature data sources available at the time, including 1 km Daymet, and represents the UHI phenomenon and intraurban spatial variation in air temperature to a higher degree of resolution (Zhang et al., 2022). Additionally, we performed validation of this data set against ground‐based weather station data and 800 m resolution daily air temperature data from the PRISM Group, which was not publicly available at the time of the analysis, for this geographic area and time period. We found that the gridded 1 km air temperature data showed good agreement with both weather stations and an alternative medium‐scale gridded air temperature data source. The results of the temperature validation exercises are available in the Supporting Information S1. Although the 1 km data is medium‐scale within the context of other environmental variables, it is relatively high resolution compared to other gridded air temperature data sources, which currently range from 800 m to 4 km resolution (Isaak et al., 2024).

For the analysis, the medium‐resolution temperature data was aggregated in several ways. First, we used summertime daily temperature data (May 1st–September 30th) from 2010 to 2019, aggregated to the ZCTA‐level for estimation of exposure‐response function in an epidemiologic study (Figure 1, Step 1) and summertime daily average air temperature data from 2018 aggregated to the CBG level for the attributable burden analysis (Figure 1, Step3). We also compared the medium resolution, gridded data to interpolated temperature surfaces based on the data from NWS stations, which represented a lower‐resolution temperature data source that does not account for intraurban variation in temperature (NOAA, 2025). To interpolate weather station data, we used thin plate spline regressions.

We then demonstrated an example of how gained information can be used to guide prioritization of interventions and discuss other potential use cases.

Medicare Data

We relied on administrative health records from billing claims from short‐stay, inpatient hospitalizations among Medicare beneficiaries in the Triangle area of North Carolina. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) hospitalizations were identified from the first three diagnostic codes based on International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9: 390–438) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10: I00‐I69). The data included a beneficiary‐day indicator of CVD‐related hospitalizations, mailing ZIP code of residence, and beneficiary characteristics; sex, race, and age group (65–74, 75–84, 85+). ZIP codes of residence were mapped to geographical boundaries of a corresponding ZCTA. All hospitalizations and incidence rates in the analysis referred to cardiovascular outcomes as defined here.

Although Medicare data does not include information on all individuals aged 65 and older, we considered this data to be a representative sample of the population of interest. In 2022, the Census Bureau estimated that 93.6% of adults aged 65 and older receive their health insurance coverage fully or partially through Medicare (Lindstrom et al., 2024). In 2022, approximately 5.3% of all adults over the age of 65 used private insurance alone (1.7% of nonworkers aged 65 and up), while the remaining 1.1% are uninsured (Lindstrom et al., 2024). Thus, older adults without Medicare coverage likely include some individuals with similar characteristics to the Medicare population who are covered by private insurance through an employer, nonworking older adults with the resources to rely exclusively on private insurance outside of employment, and older individuals who are ineligible or otherwise unable to access Medicare. For the uninsured subset, one study found that individuals without insurance over the age of 65 were more likely to be Hispanic, but similar in other characteristics to the insured population, possibly due to barriers to access or immigration status (Huguet et al., 2023).

Demographic Data

For population data by demographic subgroup, we relied on decennial population counts by sex, age, and race for all CBGs within the area of interest from the 2020 Census and accessed through IPUMS National Historical Geographic Information System (NHGIS) (Schroeder et al., 2025). To align with the Medicare data, we included “Hispanic or Latino” (Census Table P12H) as a race category and used non‐Hispanic population counts for all other race categories (Census Tables P12I, P12J, P12K, P12L, P12M, P12O). To represent ZCTA‐ and CBG‐level education and poverty, we used percent of population aged 25 and over with a bachelor's degree and percent of households below the poverty threshold, respectively, both derived from the American Community Survey 5‐year estimate, also accessed through IPUMS NHGIS.

Analytical Approach

We followed a three‐step process to estimate heat‐attributable health burden at the CBG level (Figure 1); (a) estimating exposure response function using CVD hospitalizations aggregated as daily counts at the level of ZCTA of residence, (b) estimating daily incidence rate at the CBG level, and (c) combining steps 1 and 2 to estimate heat‐attributable burden at the CBG level. The final section demonstrates an application of the framework to decompose the heat‐attributable burden into four risk groups based on the relative contributions of baseline CVD incidence and temperature exposure to the heat‐attributable CBG‐level burden.

Step 1: Estimating Exposure‐Response Function

In the first step, we estimated the measure of risk (rate ratio) associated with heat exposure (exposure‐response function) by conducting a retrospective study of cardiovascular morbidity in the 65+ year‐old Medicare beneficiaries' population. We used ZCTA level data for both daily hospitalizations and temperature, which were aggregated as described above, during the summers between 2010 and 2019 and applied distributed lag non‐linear model (natural splines with 5 degrees of freedom in time domain and cubic spline with 2 internal knots in temperature domain) to estimate the 7‐day cumulative effect of heat exposure on the risk of CVD‐related hospitalizations. The 7‐day distributed lag window (lags 0–6) was selected based on prior evidence that heat‐related cardiovascular outcomes typically exhibit short lags (≤3 days; Huang et al., 2014), with some studies reporting stronger associations around lag day 6 among older adults during summer (Li et al., 2019). We then fitted models using this 7‐day lag structure, varying the lag‐spline degrees of freedom from 3 to 5. Because estimated relative risks differed by less than 0.4% across specifications, we selected the model with the smallest dispersion parameter (5 degrees of freedom) for this analysis.

The rate ratio was estimated using a conditional Poisson model where beneficiaries served as their own controls matched on the day of the week and year, thus controlling for potential confounding from non‐time‐varying factors (e.g., age, sex, race, comorbidities, environmental setting) and long‐term trends. The model was also adjusted for time varying factors including federal holidays, daily humidity (natural spline with 4 degrees of freedom), within season trend (days since start of the season as natural spline with 4 degrees of freedom), and log count of beneficiaries in ZCTA‐Year as an offset term.

Results

From May 1st to September 30th of 2018, there were 13,370 total hospitalizations among older adults. The CVD hospitalizations at the 95th percentile of the temperature, corresponding to 28.47° Celsius, were 4.1% higher than the hospitalizations at the median temperature (24.03° Celsius) (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1). The corresponding cumulative rate ratio associated with lagged exposures at times t‐l l‐0, L was 1.04 (1.01, 1.07) and the strongest effect was observed on the day of exposure. We estimated 163 hospitalizations (1.2%) were attributed to exposures above 25° Celsus (77° Fahrenheit) which corresponded to an overall burden rate of 5.8 heat attributable hospitalizations per 10,000 adults aged 65 and above. With the average CVD‐related hospitalization (obtained in‐house from the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review files) cost of $45K in the study area, the excess heat‐attributable CVD burden resulted in healthcare costs of $7.4M.

We performed internal benchmarking by comparing annualized, aggregated, model‐based small area estimates of daily incidence rate with direct ZCTA‐level Medicare data. Model‐based estimates agreed well with the observed CVD hospitalization counts. Because CBGs do not always nest within ZCTA, we aggregated CBG‐level estimates to the ZCTA‐level in two ways: simple assignment of CBGs to a single ZCTA and population‐weighted allocation. The correlation coefficients between the observed data and modeled estimates were 0.93 and 0.98 for unweighted and population‐weighted estimates, respectively. The mean absolute percentage errors between the observed data and modeled estimates were 23.3% and 14.8% for unweighted and weighted estimates, respectively. Additional results from the validation in Step 2 are available in the Supporting Information S1.

Differences in baseline incidence rate played an important role in accounting for differences in heat burden between groups on aggregate. We observed that heat‐attributable hospitalization burden rates aligned closely with the variation in daily incidence rates as calculated in Step 2. The highest rates of heat‐attributable burden were observed among adults 85 and over (12.9 CVD hospitalizations per 10,000), adults aged 75–84 (7.8), and Black individuals (7.8), the groups that have the highest incidence rates as seen in the Odds Ratio column in Table 2.

The role of group specific incidence rate differences was also evident in considering geographic distribution of CBG‐level burden which varied substantially, ranging from 1.8 to 22.0 summertime heat‐related hospitalizations per 10,000 older adults. We found that census block groups located in the southeastern part of the metropolitan areas and portions of the urban centers of Raleigh and Durham had the highest rates of heat‐attributable hospitalizations (Figure 2). These CBGs also differed in their racial makeup, income, and education levels compared to the rest of the RT. Specifically, CBGs in the top 10% of heat‐attributable hospitalization rates have more Black residents, more households below the poverty level, and fewer residents with at least a bachelor's degree compared to all other CBGs. Even though age was the most significant risk factor for all‐cause and heat‐attributable hospitalizations (Table 2), CBGs with heat‐attributable hospitalization rates in the top 10% did not differ substantially in terms of sex and age–these characteristics were more evenly geographically distributed across the RT metropolitan areas.

Comparison of Temperature Data Sources

In addition to the main analysis based on medium‐resolution 1 km temperature data, we also estimated heat‐attributable burden using interpolated weather station air temperature data. We estimated 105 CVD hospitalizations attributed to temperatures above 25° Celsius (77° Fahrenheit) using interpolated weather station temperature data, which was 35% lower than the 163 estimated based on 1 km temperature exposure data. Although the higher‐resolution data is slightly warmer across all CBGs compared to the interpolated weather station data, with temperature differences ranging from 0.2 to 3.1°F (Mean: 1.47°F, Standard Deviation: 0.63°F), there are also two distinct spatial patterns between the “low‐resolution” weather station data and “medium‐resolution” 1 km gridded data, as shown in Figure 3, panel a. While the intraurban gradient from the northwest to southeast appears in both data sources, the weather station data estimated a larger difference between these areas, resulting in an inverse relationship between temperature and temperature difference (medium resolution–low resolution), as shown in Figure 3, panel c.

Differences in air temperature (Panel a.) and relative burden (Panel b.) estimated by medium‐ and low‐resolution temperature data, and correlations (Panel c.) between medium‐resolution temperature data and temperature differences between medium‐ and low‐resolution data.

The medium‐resolution data, but not the weather station data, captured warmer temperatures within the urban centers of Durham and Raleigh, likely due to characteristics of the built environment. As a result of these differences between temperature data sources, we observed that lower resolution temperature data resulted in a lower total heat‐attributable hospitalization burden and lower rates in the northernmost CBGs and in the urban centers of Durham and Raleigh cities by 3 to nearly 11 heat‐attributable hospitalization per 10,000 adults aged 65+ (Figure 3, Panel b).

Application of Framework: Rate Decomposition and Risk Groups

By quantifying the relative contribution of heat and baseline risk to heat‐attributable burden at the CBG level, the attributable rate decomposition is used to classify CBGs within the RT area into four distinct risk groups. The risk group classification could assist policymakers in identifying areas to prioritize for heat policy interventions and selecting the appropriate interventions for a specific area based on the dominant risk channel at a certain location, for example, selecting locations for tree planting under limited resources. CBGs with negative heat contribution and negative health contributions to the health burden (“Low Risk”) were found in the wealthier, more suburban parts of Raleigh, Durham, and Chapel Hill (Figure 4). Low Risk CBGs included around 25% of the population of adults aged 65 and higher and have the lowest proportion of non‐white residents, higher proportions of residents with college degrees, and higher rates of home ownership compared to the other three groups. CBGs with positive Health‐Contribution and negative Heat‐Contribution (“Health Driven Risk”) were predominantly rural, located mostly in the northern and western parts of the area (15.7% of population aged 65+).

Classifications of four risk groups (Low, Health‐Driven, Heat‐Driven, and Dual‐Channel) at the census block group level (left panel), and radar plots characterized by demographic variables (percent non‐white population, percent population with a bachelor's degree or higher, percent population living below the poverty level, percent renter, and percent rural) for each risk group (right panel).

CBGs in the “Heat Driven Risk” group, those with positive Heat‐Contribution and negative Health‐Contribution, were in the city centers and suburban areas of south Durham and southwest Raleigh. Heat Driven Risk CBGs had the largest population coverage of all risk groups, including 34.1% of the population of adults aged 65+ in the RT metropolitan areas. This grouping of CBGs had more urban residents compared to the other three groups and the highest proportion of residents with at least a college degree. Census block groups in the “Dual Channel Risk” group, those with positive Heat‐Contribution and Health‐Contribution, included 26.1% of adults aged 65+ and appeared in two distinct parts of the region–in portions of the urban cores of Durham and Raleigh and the more rural, southeastern portion of the metropolitan areas. This group of CBGs had the greatest proportion of non‐white residents of all four groups, as well as the highest percent of households below the poverty line and renter‐owned housing units.

Discussion

In this study, we presented a framework to estimate heat‐attributable health burden at the scale of neighborhoods and classify communities into discernible risk groups: “Low Risk”, “Heat Driven Risk”, “Health Driven Risk”, and “Dual Channel Risk.” By connecting SAA techniques with attributable burden estimation, we provided information that can help align heat alert systems and action plans with health risks experienced in communities. We demonstrated this framework by estimating census block group‐level heat‐attributable CVD burdens among older adults in the RT metropolitan areas of North Carolina, finding 163 cardiovascular hospitalizations attributable to high heat during the summer of 2018. As such, this approach not only helps community planners and policy makers prioritize areas but also tailor strategies to target the neighborhood‐specific drivers of heat‐attributable health burdens. By aligning heat alert systems, actions, and plans with local needs, society may be able to prioritize interventions and reduce heat‐attributable health burden.

The rate decomposition elucidates drivers of the heat‐attributable health burden across neighborhoods by identifying differences between those where baseline health risk, temperature exposure or both factors contribute to increased burden. Research Triangle areas neighborhoods with only positive Health‐Contribution (“Health Driven Risk”) were more rural, while those with only positive Heat‐Contribution (“Heat Driven Risk”) were more urban with high rates of post‐secondary education. CBGs with both positive Heat‐Contribution and Health‐Contribution (“Dual Channel Risk”) had more non‐white residents, higher poverty rates, and more renter‐occupied housing compared to other CBGs. Approximately 34% of the population aged 65+ reside in Heat Driven Risk CBGs and 26% of the population is located in Dual Channel Risk CBGs, indicating that around 60% of the older adult population lives in CBGs with a positive Heat‐Contribution, where CBG‐specific daily temperatures result in higher heat‐attributable health burdens compared to the area average temperatures. This aligns with previous research reporting significant associations between heat exposure and population density (Huang et al., 2025). The contribution of each risk channel to CBG‐specific rates compared to the MSA's area average heat‐attributable CVD rate (5.8 hospitalizations per 10,000) can be viewed online in the accompanying R Shiny dashboard, available at: https://heat‐cvd‐dashboard‐rtp‐nc.apps.cloudapps.unc.edu/.

The risk group categorization is one example of how quantifying health burden can support policymakers and planners in prioritizing interventions, identifying what interventions may be appropriate to address the dominant risk channel at that location, and evaluating the benefit. The results in this analysis highlight the importance of recognizing both heat exposures and heat‐sensitive disease incidence as channels through which burdens can be addressed. For example, approaches to reduce or avoid heat exposure such as tree planting, cool building and paving materials, and cooling centers may be more impactful in the “Heat Driven Risk” CBGs and other CBGs where heat exposure is the dominant risk channel. For the “Health Driven Risk” CBGs, heat education approaches emphasizing the relationship between heat and CVD may be more effective.

Heat‐health education interventions have generally received less attention in the heat policy literature and local planning compared to heat mitigation approaches or heat alerts and warnings issued during extreme heat events (Gabbe et al., 2024; Keith et al., 2023). When heat literacy interventions are implemented, they tend to prioritize knowledge of preventative measures during extreme heat events rather than promoting an understanding of heat risk factors and disease mechanisms (Johar et al., 2025). By separating these two distinct channels of risk, planners can gain insights needed to address neighborhood‐level risk factors and develop suitable approaches that address risk channels that are not currently emphasized in local heat planning.

We compared the health burden estimated by a finer‐resolution data set, 1 km gridded air temperature (main result), with a coarser‐resolution data set of interpolated weather station data. Though it is expected that analytical outcomes can vary by spatial resolution (Fotheringham & Wong, 1991), we observed spatial variations in CVD burden estimations by these two resolutions across the RT area, whereby the interpolated weather station data produced lower heat attributable CVD burden particularly in urban centers and northernmost rural parts of the metropolitan areas.

As “neighborhood scale data” is increasingly demanded for public health applications for its ability to capture environmental health determinants (Thomson et al., 2019), finer‐scale data is particularly relevant for heat‐related health outcomes, due to the intraurban variability of temperature that depends on characteristics of the built environment, human activity, and meteorological factors (Wong et al., 2016). The results of this study not only demonstrate that estimated health burdens vary by the scale of exposure data, but also that relying on exposure data alone is insufficient for identifying areas with the greatest heat‐related health burdens. By connecting medium‐scale exposure data with health burden estimation, the framework introduced in this study can be used to prioritize resource allocation during extreme heat events and quantify the health impacts of heat mitigation measures, which are often implemented at small scales (Kumar et al., 2024; Wondmagegn et al., 2019).

Although the approach used in this study greatly expands the applicability of SAA for mapping heat‐attributable health burden, there are several important limitations to note. One limitation is the inability to validate the results against external data sources. Small area estimation techniques, like MRP, are typically used in cases where population‐level small area data does not exist or is not publicly available, making external validation of small area estimates infeasible. Instead, internal benchmarking of the model‐based estimates with direct estimates from the original data source is the standard validation practice for small area estimation (Bell et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014). However, we were able to perform internal benchmarking as part of Step 2 to validate the small area estimation of CVD incidence rates, which are presented in the main results and Supporting Information S1. Because we are not able to externally validate the CBG‐level CVD hospitalization data, we are also not able to verify the assumption used in Step 2 that the estimated relationships between CVD hospitalizations, poverty, and education at the ZCTA‐level are appropriate to use for representing these relationships at the CBG‐level.

While the gridded 1 km data set used in the main results for this study represents intraurban air temperature variation at a higher degree of resolution compared to more common approaches for interpolating weather station data, this data set is not able to fully account for all factors that may contribute to air temperature variation within urban areas (Zhang et al., 2022). While the main focus of this study is to enable health analyses at the resolution of exposures, the estimates from this study and future extensions of this framework relying on air temperature data will reflect any inaccuracies and biases of the air temperature exposure data sources used. In general, air temperature data sets that rely on weather‐station measurements may be affected by rural biases in the spatial distribution of weather stations, and those that rely on remote‐sensing based land surface temperature may be biased toward surface‐temperature driven urban patterns which vary from air temperature depending on land cover and other climate and atmospheric conditions (Lee, 2025; Sakthivel & Sengupta, 2025). However, modeling approaches for air temperature data are continually improving to address these issues and certain products may be better suited for certain regions.

This study also does not fully account for all the risk factors that may be relevant for heat‐related cardiovascular hospitalizations among elderly adults. In the MRP for all‐cause cardiovascular hospitalizations, we used only age group, sex, and race as individual‐level covariates, although other factors such as pre‐existing medical conditions and lifestyle factors are also known to play a role (Rodgers et al., 2019; Sazlina et al., 2020). Here we are only able to use demographic variables that are included in the Medicare data, which is also the case for many administrative health data sources (Ehrenstein et al., 2019). Similarly, the decomposition exercise enables us to assess the relative contribution of baseline CVD rates and heat exposures, but this only captures two possible channels of variation in small‐area heat‐attributable CVD rates. Another important potential channel is subgroup‐specific rate ratio, to account for variation in heat‐health susceptibility by age, race, sex, and other factors such as chronic kidney disease and diabetes (Cleland et al., 2023). For example, female individuals are hypothesized to have slightly higher risk of heat‐related CVD outcomes due to physiological differences from males which would not be captured by overall CVD hospitalization risk or population distribution across the metropolitan area (Zhou et al., 2025).

It is known that timely intervention strategies are critical (Brimicombe et al., 2024). Real‐time temperature data often can provide prompt actions for public health interventions. Though this analysis was not able to investigate the health burden using real‐time data due to data availability, there is increasing effort in developing real‐time, higher resolution temperature data, such as air temperature sensor networks, heat mapping campaigns and crowdsourced data. This approach could be combined with other frameworks to support real‐time and anticipatory analyses and forecasts for heat warning systems and other short‐term responses to extreme heat. For example, one recent framework proposes combining short‐term meteorological forecasts with temperature‐mortality rate ratio and baseline mortality rates to map the expected excess deaths associated with forecasted weather conditions (Mistry & Gasparrini, 2024). It is worthy to mention that developing high spatial and temporal resolution temperature data can encounter several challenges, including installation and maintenance costs (Scolio et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2021), and data quality and durability (Chapman et al., 2023; Meier et al., 2017) due to the designs of mapping days, routes, and data collection methods (Shi et al., 2021). Nonetheless, future research should take advantage of relatively higher‐resolution, real‐time temperature data for investigating heat‐attributable health burden when possible.

The three‐step methodology introduced in this study is flexible to a range of applications for SAA of temperature‐related health burden. Several recent studies have demonstrated the potential for applying SAA techniques to epidemiological analyses, such as the use of the case time series design, although these analyses are still limited by the scale of health data reporting in the area of interest (Gasparrini, 2022; Ryan et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025). Recent work has also demonstrated that SAA techniques can be applied to other data sources beyond survey data, such as administrative data and electronic health records (Chen et al., 2022; Nielsen et al., 2024). The use of multi‐level regression and poststratification to downscale health data demonstrated in this analysis makes this approach flexible to accommodate new data sources and more appropriate spatial scales for the health outcomes and areas of interest. In particular, this framework may be useful to apply in urban areas or other geographic areas where it is not possible to implement heat policy interventions throughout the entire area due to resource constraints, or in contexts where health outcome data is not available at the same scale as intraurban temperature exposure variation or heat policy interventions. When extending to other contexts, future users should modify the framework to use the appropriate spatial scale and environmental exposure metric for their specific context and the health outcome of interest. Additionally, users may use rate ratio estimates from prior studies or sources, as long as these estimates are available and relevant for their application of the framework.

The methodology used in this study also presents an alternative approach to heat health risk mapping that is directly connected to health outcomes. Existing heat risk indices and mapping approaches are limited in their usefulness, comparability, and accuracy (Cheng et al., 2021). Heat risk indices are generally intended to express the differential risks of experiencing heat‐related health impacts between groups or spatial units, however, many are not connected to specific health conditions or validated against health data. Two separate reviews of heat vulnerability studies found that only about a quarter of studies validated their heat vulnerability indices against health outcomes data (Cheng et al., 2021; Niu et al., 2021). Studies that did validate their heat risk measures only found a weak association with health outcomes data (Niu et al., 2021). Heat risk indices and maps are also not universally available–a survey of city planning officials conducted in 2019 found that over half of respondents reported that heat vulnerability maps are not available to them (Meerow & Keith, 2022). Variation in local data availability and the lack of a unified conceptualization of vulnerability makes existing heat vulnerability indices and maps inconsistent and difficult to compare across urban areas (Cheng et al., 2021; Karanja & Kiage, 2021).

The approach implemented in this study improves on some of the limitations of existing heat risk assessments. In this analysis, estimated heat‐attributable health burdens were directly derived from observed health outcomes data which was used to develop baseline rates and relative risk relationships. The approach also overcomes variation in data availability of neighborhood‐level health outcomes by downscaling data through multi‐level regression and poststratification. In addition to identifying areas with high heat‐health risks like other heat vulnerability approaches, the rate decomposition and risk group categorization enabled by this methodology provides an indication of how the heat‐health risk can be addressed more effectively at a given location.

Furthermore, the results of this analysis indicate several potential future research directions which could extend the range of applications. For instance, the rate ratio for the MRP process do not need to be derived from the same data sources and can be obtained from other sources that are relevant and appropriate for the context (i.e., rate ratio for the same study area published in previous studies). Future studies could perform validation against external data sources by selecting focus areas where health outcome data is available at multiple small area scales. Future research should also apply the framework to develop rate ratio estimates for additional health outcomes, populations (beyond elderly adults) and relevant temperature metrics and creating a public database of these sources would be beneficial for scaling up applications of this approach. Additionally, future studies could assess the contribution of subgroup‐specific rate ratio compared to other channels of heat‐health susceptibility and the potential bias from excluding health risk factors outside of age, sex, and race. While the results from this analysis reveal interesting spatial patterns within the Raleigh‐Durham metropolitan area that spans county and city jurisdictions, metropolitan areas are often statistical units and do not represent the boundaries of decision‐making government entities or agencies. In future work, the methodology could be implemented within jurisdictional boundaries such as cities, counties, and school districts to help decision‐makers identify areas and populations with greater risk.

Conclusion

In summary, this study introduced a framework for estimating health burdens at finer geographic scales by connecting SAA techniques with epidemiologic attributable burden analysis. We demonstrated the framework by estimating heat‐attributable health burdens at the census block group (CBG) level for the RT metropolitan areas in the southeastern United States. We estimated 163 heat‐attributable CVD related hospitalizations among older adults during the summer of 2018, which range from 1.8 to 22.0 hospitalizations per 10,000 people across CBGs. We found that the spatial distribution of health burdens across the metropolitan areas varies under different geographic scales of temperature data, emphasizing the importance of using appropriately scaled exposure data when conducting small area attributable burden analyses. Finally, instead of using common assessment approaches for heat vulnerability, we classified CBGs into four risk groups based on the contribution of neighborhood‐level CVD incidence rates and temperature exposures to health burdens to inform the selection and placement of localized heat‐health interventions.
