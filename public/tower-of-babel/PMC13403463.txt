Exploring experiences of loneliness among stroke survivors living in the community: a secondary qualitative analysis
Authors: Mimi Wai Man Chan, Janet Wing Hung Sit, Suzanne Hoi Shan Lo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo explore experiences of loneliness among community-dwelling stroke survivors through a secondary qualitative analysis.MethodA secondary analysis of a qualitative dataset was conducted, including 26 transcripts from semi-structured interviews that originally aimed to explore psychosocial experience after stroke. Data relevant to loneliness were finally extracted from 20 transcripts. Thematic analysis was adopted for data analysis.ResultsThree themes and six subthemes were identified: (1) Disconnection from social belonging, including difficulty in engaging in social activities and perceived sense of difference from others. (2) Lack of closeness to familiar people or loved ones, including decline in meaningful interactions or relationships with friends and family members, and lack of understanding and empathy from social networks. (3) Loss of direction, hope, and meaning, including perceived sense of learned helplessness, and diminished expectations and purposes in life.ConclusionLoneliness after stroke is a complex experience. There is a clear need for designing tailored interventions to address loneliness following stroke. Addressing loneliness should be considered as a core issue of stroke recovery within both policy frameworks and practical guidelines. In addition, further research is needed to explore the pathways leading to social, emotional, and existential loneliness following stroke.KEYWORDS: Stroke, community, loneliness, qualitative research, thematic analysis, secondary analysis

Introduction

Stroke is a significant threat to global public health, with approximately 94 million people having experienced stroke worldwide (Feigin et al., 2025). While advances in medical treatments have extended the life expectancy of stroke survivors, most of them continue to live with physical dysfunctions and depend on family members or healthcare providers for ongoing care (Broderick, 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Mei et al., 2018). Owing to profound changes in life after stroke, stroke survivors are prone to depression, anxiety, loneliness, or other psychosocial issues, which further complicate the trajectory of stroke recovery (Byrne et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2025).

Loneliness is recognized as one of the serious psychosocial issues following stroke. It is a subjective, unpleasant, and complex experience that occurs when the social relations and networks of individuals are deficient quantitatively or qualitatively (Perlman & Peplau, 1981). Loneliness can be described in three multidimensional aspects, including social, emotional, and existential loneliness (de Jong Gierveld, 1987; Mansfield et al., 2019). Social loneliness is summarized as a sense of lacking social networks or a desire to engage with broader groups of social contacts (Perlman & Peplau, 1998; Weiss, 1973). Emotional loneliness refers to a lonely feeling despite being surrounded by people or being able to share thoughts with others (Weiss, 1973). Existential loneliness is described as an intolerable emptiness and sadness that results from an individual’s awareness of disconnection from others and the world (Ettema et al., 2010). Through these three multidimensional aspects, they can help deepen the understanding of the diverse experiences of loneliness among stroke survivors.

In recent years, loneliness has drawn growing attention in stroke research (He et al., 2025). A United Kingdom national study finds that loneliness is present in approximately 44% of stroke survivors (Byrne et al., 2022). A 16-year longitudinal study from the United States further indicates that loneliness tends to increase over time after stroke, with a particularly marked increase during older adulthood (Willroth et al., 2024). In addition, loneliness is recognized not only as a distressing feeling but also as a significant risk factor for stroke recurrence. It is also associated with psychological distress, poor social engagement, and increased mortality after stroke (Cené et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026). This evidence consistently emerges that loneliness contributes to significant impacts on the stroke recovery journey and the health conditions of stroke survivors. Therefore, it is crucial to comprehensively understand loneliness among stroke survivors to manage better and address it.

Loneliness is a critical issue in stroke recovery, yet it remains inadequately addressed in both practice and research (He et al., 2025). Current evidence also highlights that the understanding of how stroke survivors experience and conceptualise loneliness in daily life is limited (Muir-Little et al., 2025). Developing a rich understanding of how stroke survivors make sense of loneliness is warranted to inform the design and implementation of effective interventions to mitigate loneliness after stroke. Accordingly, this study explored loneliness as subjectively narrated by stroke survivors residing in the community. It aimed to explore the experiences of loneliness among stroke survivors residing in the community. The specific objectives include: (1) to understand how stroke survivors describe and experience loneliness, and (2) to categorize the types of loneliness described by stroke survivors. The findings are expected to highlight how loneliness may be mitigated or exacerbated, providing insights to inform practice improvement, research advancement, and policy development for addressing loneliness after stroke.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study reported on the secondary analysis of a set of qualitative data in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) (Tong et al., 2007) (see Supplementary material 1). In particular, this study presented the findings from a secondary analysis of stroke survivors’ interviews from a previous qualitative study exploring post-stroke psychosocial experiences in the community (Chan et al., 2024). Secondary analysis of qualitative data is a research strategy that uses existing data to investigate a new question or apply a new perspective on the existing research (Long-Sutehall et al., 2011). However, the primary study differed in focus from the current study (Chan et al., 2024). The current study thus focused on an additional in-depth and subset secondary analysis of stroke survivors’ interviews mentioning loneliness, to explore loneliness after stroke, which was not central to the original study (Chan et al., 2024; Heaton, 2008).

Participants

In the original study, 26 community-dwelling stroke survivors were recruited from various community centres and support groups in Macao, using convenience sampling (Chan et al., 2024). The inclusion criteria for the original study were stroke survivors aged over 18 years who resided in the community and had sufficient cognitive ability, as indicated by Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores above the 2nd percentile (Yeung et al., 2014).

Data collection

The original data were collected between September 2022 and April 2023 in Macao via face-to-face semi-structured individual interviews. The interviews were conducted in community centres, support groups, or participants’ homes by the first author (MWMC). All interviews lasted around 30–90 min. The interviews followed an interview guide that asked stroke survivors about their psychosocial experiences after returning to the community (see Supplementary material 2).

Before conducting the interviews, eligible participants were asked to offer their demographic and clinical information, such as gender, age, and duration of stroke. In addition, each participant was requested to provide signed informed consent to confirm their willingness to participate in the interviews. All interviews were audio-recorded. Field notes were also recorded during all interviews.

Data analysis

The interview recordings were transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were entered into MAXQDA, a software programme for the management of qualitative data and following analysis (VERBI Software, 2021). Each transcript was assessed for the data quality, ensuring coherent information that aligned with the aim of this study.

The data from the transcripts were further thematically re-analysed using Braun and Clarke’s framework by the authors who are experienced in conducting qualitative research (MWMC, JWHS, and SHSL) (Braun & Clarke, 2019). First, all transcripts were read by the authors repeatedly and independently. After that, the interview segments in the transcripts were selected and coded initially according to the aim of this study. A set of code lists was then generated to summarise the interview segments. Themes and subthemes were then identified across the code lists. Finally, within each theme, the selected quotes from the transcripts were used to illustrate the experiences of loneliness among stroke survivors.

Notably, the in-depth secondary analysis was limited to the transcripts mentioning “loneliness”, “lonely”, or the descriptions reflecting relevant inferred experiences, such as isolation, exclusion, or disconnection. In addition, the three multidimensional aspects of loneliness, including social, emotional, and existential loneliness, were subsequently adopted to provide a sensitising concept to guide the coding and interpretation of the data (de Jong Gierveld, 1987; Mansfield et al., 2019).

Rigour and trustworthiness

To ensure rigour and trustworthiness of the secondary analysis findings, credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were considered (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Specifically, the emerging codes, subthemes, and themes were consistently reviewed and discussed by the authors. This iterative process between the transcripts and the coding frames helped the authors ensure that analytic sufficiency had been achieved (Sherif, 2018). These reviews and discussions also contributed to the dependability and confirmability of the findings (Amin et al., 2020). Considering that the research team had prior familiarity with the data, which could affect interpretation, reflexive journals were adopted during data analysis to ensure the credibility and confirmability of the findings (Amin et al., 2020). Specifically, the research team adopted reflexive journals to record the coding process and decision, document uncertainties or discrepancies when interpreting the data, and highlight contextual information that might affect interpretation (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). In addition, thick descriptions provided by interviews and observations also increased the credibility of the findings (Adler, 2022). Particularly, the accounts of loneliness experiences from stroke survivors and the variations across stroke survivors describing loneliness would be presented in detail. These rich data provided sufficient information to achieve the study aims and further supported the analytic sufficiency and adequacy of data (Amin et al., 2020). Finally, the attention to describing the participants, contexts, and issues faced by stroke survivors potentially contributed to the transferability of the findings to comparable settings (Amin et al., 2020; Haq et al., 2023).

Ethical considerations

The original study has received ethical approval from [redacted]. All participants involved in the interviews were assigned an identifier, e.g., P01, P02, and P03, to ensure their anonymity and confidentiality.

Themes and subthemes

Three key themes and six subthemes about loneliness after stroke were revealed. They are “disconnection from social belonging”, “lack of closeness to familiar people or loved ones”, and “loss of direction, hope, and meaning”. The findings revealed that loneliness among community-dwelling stroke survivors was a complex and multifaceted feeling, which was shaped by subjective post-stroke experiences, stroke consequences, attitudes from the public, and inclusion in society. Details of the themes and subthemes are shown below.

Theme one: disconnection from social belonging

Experiences of disconnection from social connections and being excluded from society could lead to social loneliness among stroke survivors. This disconnection and exclusion were largely related to the limited physical functioning of stroke survivors and environmental barriers.

Difficulty in engaging in social activities.

Some stroke survivors highlighted that the impacts of stroke, such as limitations in mobility, restricted their ability to participate in group activities. On the other hand, life changes after a stroke, such as the need to pay attention to dietary habits, also led them to withdraw from their previous social activities that had been central to their daily lives. As a result, stroke survivors reported feelings of social loneliness since they were unable to continue to engage in valued social activities and hobbies that they used to enjoy. One male stroke survivor described that:

“Stroke completely wiped out my social life. Currently, apart from visiting the centre for physiotherapy every Saturday, I do not engage in other social activities. Before experiencing stroke, I used to meet friends to have a drink and go running together. All those activities are gone.” (P04, male, 52y/o, 3 years post-stroke)

Similarly, another female stroke survivor described how the limited mobility made it challenging for her to join outdoor social activities. She desired to participate in activities such as others in her neighbourhood, but she remained at her home due to her physical limitations. As a result, she was only able to observe others enjoying their social lives from a distance. This experience left her feeling socially disconnected, deepening her sense of social loneliness.

“One Sunday, some people were singing in the park. I stood on my apartment balcony, listening and watching so many people enjoying themselves. But I could not go downstairs to join them. I could only watch from my apartment.” (P09, female, 69y/o, 6.8 years post-stroke)

Stroke survivors also indicated that environmental barriers were one of the key factors that hindered their abilities to engage in social activities. Stroke survivors commented that the surrounding physical environments are often non-inclusive, particularly due to inaccessible transportation and the lack of wheelchair-friendly facilities. These barriers made it difficult for them to leave their homes, thereby reducing the frequency of going out with others and deepening their feelings of social loneliness. One male stroke survivor recalled that:

“My friends have asked me to go out with them. I told them: ‘I am in a wheelchair now. If I could get onto the transportation, I would join you. However, I cannot get on with my wheelchair.’…… I cannot join them outdoors.” (P15, male, 76 y/o, 15 years post-stroke)

Perceived a sense of difference from others.

For some stroke survivors, their sense of loneliness was attributed to being labelled as “disabled” or “unable”. Stroke survivors perceived that such labels created social boundaries that might limit their opportunities or rights to participate in social life. One female stroke survivor shared her experience of being unable to join social activities due to safety concerns related to physical disability. Such safety concerns unintentionally reinforced her feelings of disconnection and social loneliness.

“There are some outdoor activities that we (people with physical disabilities) are not allowed to participate in…… I understand that the staff may be concerned about our safety. But it makes me feel like I am being labelled and different from other service users and being excluded.” (P10, female, 56y/o, 16 years post-stroke)

Another female stroke survivor revealed that she suffered from being unwelcome by others. Although she participated in group activities at the centre, she perceived a subtle form of rejection from others, which left her with a persistent feeling of not belonging in group activities. These experiences reinforced her sense of social marginalisation, deepening her feelings of isolation. She shared that:

“I sometimes went to the centre to join group activities. Some people were visiting the centre and playing board games every day…… When I sat down to play poker cards with them, they kept silent…… They even dealt cards to everyone in the group, except me. What did this mean? This meant that they did not want me to join them.” (P13, female, 73 y/o, 1.3 years post-stroke)

Theme two: lack of closeness to familiar people or loved ones

Undergoing negative experiences with friends and family members emerged as a key cause of emotional loneliness after a stroke. Among those experiences, perceived insufficient support and a lack of communication and contact with both friends and family members were highly associated with increasing the feeling of emotional loneliness among stroke survivors.

Decline in meaningful interactions or relationships with friends and family members.

Before experiencing a stroke, many stroke survivors have established their own social connections with their peers. However, the post-stroke experiences changed their interpersonal relationships. One male stroke survivor described that experiencing a stroke led to postponing his original study plans. As a result, he lost contact with his former classmates, noting that the differences in life stages created a significant distance between him and his peers. Such a lag and delay in his study plan caused him to lose his cherished friendships, which in turn deepened his sense of emotional loneliness, sadness, and lack of emotional support following the stroke.

“I had experienced stroke when I was a fourth-year undergraduate student. So, I had to suspend my study progress. My friends and I went through those several tough years together, but they finally graduated without me. They became my seniors and moved on ahead of me, leaving me behind...... Over time, they also built their new social networks. They do not have time for me anymore.” (P19, male, 32 y/o, 7.5 years post-stroke)

The same male stroke survivor also presented how stroke negatively affected engagements in his previous hobby of singing. He recalled the experiences of being actively distanced by his singing peers after he could no longer sing. Such a change led him to lose touch with those individuals who mean so much in his interpersonal relationships, which contributed to feelings of emotional loneliness.

“I was an amateur singer. I would not say I was famous, but some people and peers knew me. However, after the stroke onset, I cannot sing anymore. My friends, peers, and fans who once liked me gradually forgot about me. I understand that they have their own lives. They can also choose to drift away if they want…… But it is so hard to accept being forgotten. I sometimes sit quietly at night. I feel unhappy and lonely.” (P19, male, 32 y/o, 7.5 years post-stroke)

Some stroke survivors eagerly look forward to visits from family members. One female stroke survivor expressed a longing for social and emotional connections with family members as she lived alone. Limited companionship from her family members contributed to emotional loneliness, leading her to rely on some passive alternative approaches for companionship, such as turning the television on, to gain a sense of emotional comfort.

“My daughter visits me once a week. There is no one to keep me company here. So, I turn on the television all the time. The sounds from the television make me feel like someone is here with me.” (P09, female, 69y/o, 6.8 years post-stroke)

Lack of understanding and empathy from social networks.

Although some people tried to understand and empathize with the situations of stroke survivors, the majority of stroke survivors felt that it was difficult for others to understand what life after stroke was like fully. Stroke survivors also reported experiencing unintended judgments of stroke from others, such as being viewed with pity or being perceived as unfortunate, which they interpreted as indicators of misunderstanding of stroke-related experiences from others. Such misunderstandings would generate a perceived experiential distance between stroke survivors and those people around them. This gap in understanding appeared to hinder emotional support and connections with others, which further caused feelings of emotional loneliness over time. One male stroke survivor shared that:

“Some friends who are kind might consider that I am poor and unfortunate because of the impacts of stroke. I do not need their pity. I just want to be treated as normal…… I feel like there is something, like a barrier, between my friends and me. I do not know how to cross it.” (P04, male, 52y/o, 3 years post-stroke)

On the other hand, stroke survivors reported that some individuals showed concerns about their situations and reached out through text messages. However, stroke survivors considered these actions and interactions superficial. Instead of offering genuine understanding and concern about their post-stroke lives, such interactions and responses were perceived as generalized and focused on greeting. As a result, stroke survivors feel uncomfortable and find it difficult to receive emotional support and connections from others, which in turn intensifies the experience of emotional loneliness. One male stroke survivor illustrated that:

“Some friends would send me a text message with typing like: ‘How are you?’, ‘Are you okay right now?’, ‘We are worried about you.’. I have told them about my post-stroke experiences and challenges in daily living activities; they replied: ‘Frightening. We support you.’ Then, they would gradually fade out…… It seems that a lot of people care about you, but their concerns are limited to simple and superficial greetings.” (P19, male, 32y/o, 7.5 years post-stroke)

Theme three: loss of direction, hope, and meaning

When stroke survivors experienced repeated failures, unmet expectations, or inability to resume their valued relationships or activities, such experiences reinforce their feelings of loss. In particular, the loss of direction, hope, and meaning in life deepened their sense of existential loneliness.

Perceived a sense of learned helplessness.

Some stroke survivors reported experiencing loneliness that stemmed from their awareness of difficulties in managing everyday activities. One male stroke survivor described himself as useless because of repeated failures related to his physical limitations performing routine tasks, such as shopping in the supermarket or moving freely at home. Over time, these accumulated frustrations and failures intensified his emotional distress and deepened his sense of existential loneliness.

“I have tried to go to the supermarket for shopping. For others, it is only about a minute of walking. For me, I need to take a rest two or three times on the way. Even then, I still cannot reach the supermarket. Now, I almost lie on my bed. My legs go numb. I do not know how to place them comfortably. I feel that I am useless… I can only stay here. What are the meanings of such a life?” (P08, male, 78y/o, 9.9 years post-stroke)

Another male stroke survivor also expressed that his loneliness was rooted in repeated failures related to communicating with others. He described how difficulties in speech and expression created obstacles to maintaining social relationships. These communication challenges extended beyond practical barriers to communication and touched the deeper human needs for recognition and connection, thereby generating a sense of disconnection and existential loneliness.

“There are some people who sit and chat together in the park. Most of the time, they do not understand what I am talking about when I communicate with them. I cannot speak fluently like them…… So, I seldom express my own opinions.” (P16, male, 75 y/o, 1.8 years post-stroke)

Diminished expectations and purposes in life.

Many stroke survivors lamented the loss of previous interests and freedoms after a stroke. Social activities that had once brought them joy, fostered connection with others, and contributed to a sense of identity became inaccessible. Such inaccessible activities also served as a reminder of how their lives had changed. The inability to engage in these social activities left stroke survivors feeling a sense of emptiness, even loss of purpose in life, thereby leading to feelings of existential loneliness.

“I used to be so active. I loved talking to others, exercising, and having coffee with others. Now all those interests are gone. I cannot even keep birds anymore. What else can I do now? I just sit here and ‘wait for the end day of my life to come’.” (P15, male, 76 y/o, 15 years post-stroke)

One male stroke survivor described how reliance on daily routine care services led to a sense of losing control over his relationships with the world. His daily life became increasingly structured around care schedules instead of his personal choice. He questioned his capabilities to make meaningful contributions to his life after a stroke, reflecting a diminished sense of purpose in life and an increased sense of existential loneliness.

“I cannot walk and do housework by myself. It is no big deal. I eventually got used to staying at home alone and waiting for the meal services. In the morning, they deliver breakfast to me, then I wait for my lunch delivery. At night, they bring the dinner to me again… It is the same every day. I need to face reality. Days will pass anyway.” (P16, male, 75y/o, 1.8 years post-stroke)

Existential loneliness was further compounded for some stroke survivors by losing the meaning in life, which led to reflections on mortality and the perceived futility of daily existence. One male stroke survivor illustrated a sense of futility in his post‑stroke life. He highlighted how stroke-related loneliness was intertwined with reflections on the purpose of living and perspectives on the end stage of his life. He expressed a sense of resignation and perceived a lack of agency over survival and death.

“Some people always say: ‘I am waiting for the days to pass.’ But for me, it is no longer about passing the days; I am waiting for the day of my passing away. It finally happens once, right? Survival or death is not up to you or me. There is nothing I can do.” (P08, male, 78y/o, 9.9 years post-stroke)

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of loneliness among community-dwelling stroke survivors through a secondary qualitative analysis. Three themes related to loneliness after stroke were identified from the existing dataset. They include disconnection from social belonging, lack of closeness to familiar people or loved ones, as well as loss of direction, hope, and meaning. These findings show that loneliness after stroke is complex and stems from various contextual factors. Our findings also reflect three types of loneliness, encompassing social, emotional, and existential aspects, which align with previous evidence that loneliness is multidimensional (de Jong Gierveld, 1987; Mansfield et al., 2019) (see Figure 1).

Experiences of loneliness among community-dwelling stroke survivors.

For stroke survivors, limited physical functioning and decreased mobility are the key factors contributing to social loneliness, as they directly diminish the quantity of social connections. These findings align with those from a review on older adults with chronic illnesses (Jiang et al., 2025). This review indicates that individuals experience active or passive social isolation due to embarrassing or deteriorating health conditions associated with their illnesses, which can lead to feelings of social loneliness (Jiang et al., 2025). It is evident that improving physical functioning can contribute to addressing social loneliness among older adults (Cecen-Celik & Deniz, 2026). However, despite advances in stroke rehabilitation programmes aimed at improving functional status, attention to supporting social loneliness caused by physical impairments is limited (He et al., 2025; Kwakkel et al., 2023). Most existing support prioritizes promoting social participation or reducing social isolation through improving functional performance, while a primary focus on social loneliness related to limited mobility remains largely unaddressed (He et al., 2025). Although our data did not directly highlight the potential interventions, the consistent findings about the pathways leading to social loneliness among both older adults and stroke survivors highlight a key recommendation. These findings suggest that it is worth developing and evaluating relevant interventions to alleviate social loneliness by improving functional independence after stroke.

According to our findings, inadequate inclusiveness and environmental constraints can exacerbate social loneliness by limiting opportunities for social engagement and reinforcing experiences of social exclusion among stroke survivors, which is supported by the existing evidence (Pande, 2022). These findings highlight the importance of promoting inclusiveness in society through improving inaccessible environments to address social loneliness after stroke. The latest report on loneliness by the World Health Organisation also supports our arguments. As recommended by this report, corresponding strategies to address loneliness caused by social contexts should extend beyond individual-level interventions to involve broader societal-level support (World Health Organisation, 2025). Specifically, it is necessary to establish an inclusive society through creating accessible physical environments, particularly by adding barrier-free facilities in the community to reduce environmental constraints that restrict the mobility of stroke survivors (Desai et al., 2024; Goldman et al., 2026; World Health Organisation, 2025). However, such implementations require collaboration with various stakeholders, including policymakers, healthcare services, non-governmental organisations, and advocacy groups, to facilitate inclusive policies, allocate resources effectively, and design sustainable support for stroke survivors (Goldman et al., 2026). Therefore, additional efforts to address social loneliness should take place in creating accessible and sustainable support systems through cross-sectoral collaboration to ensure social inclusion and re-engage stroke survivors in social activities.

Our findings also suggest that emotional loneliness among stroke survivors appears to be related to limited support from their meaningful and cherished relationships. As further reported in this study, emotional loneliness may persist even in stroke survivors who have social relationships if those relationships do not provide adequate emotional support. Current evidence indicates that some negative responses, such as experiencing discomfort, embarrassment, social withdrawal, and loneliness, may emerge when the need for emotional resonance among stroke survivors is not met, particularly being understood and emotionally supported (Guo et al., 2021). Furthermore, differences in life stages between stroke survivors and their peers may widen the gap in their relationships and intensify feelings of emotional loneliness. These findings are similar to those of previous qualitative reviews, which suggest that estrangement from family members and friends can aggravate loneliness among adolescents and older adults (Miao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). These findings consistently underscore the importance of establishing new connections with individuals who share similar experiences after stroke to receive emotional support, helping them address emotional loneliness.

Current evidence identifies that peer support can be an effective approach to relieve loneliness by establishing social connections and emotional support among individuals with common experiences (He et al., 2025). This support can be delivered in person or through the internet to offer a safe and comfortable environment, where stroke survivors can feel understood by others who have similar experiences (Bravata et al., 2023; Christensen et al., 2019; May et al., 2023). Such similar experiences can contribute to establishing and maintaining connections, as well as provide and receive emotional support, thereby addressing emotional loneliness (He et al., 2025; Kessler et al., 2014).

However, considering that emotional loneliness can be caused by limited close or familiar relationships, peer support may not fully address loneliness related to existing relationship issues among stroke survivors. Therefore, alleviating emotional loneliness may further require approaches or strategies that extend beyond promoting social connection with peers. A scoping review on older adults suggests interactions occurring through telephone, email, or video-calling to maintain familiar relationships with family and friends (O’Rourke et al., 2018). Another randomized controlled trial identifies that the technology-based psychoeducational intervention is effective in improving family relationships and connectedness between older adults and their family members (Müller et al., 2024). Although such technology-based approaches were primarily suggested for addressing psychosocial issues among older adults, they may also consider for adopting among stroke survivors. Particularly, these approaches may serve as potential strategies to maintain existing relationships and further address emotional loneliness for stroke survivors, because their limited mobility may hinder opportunities for them to interact with others in person.

In addition, a limited understanding of stroke among friends and family members is one of the factors that causes post-stroke emotional loneliness. It is therefore necessary to enhance public awareness and understanding of stroke recovery through education and voluntary activities (Cao et al., 2021; Hafsteinsdóttir et al., 2011). Thus, family members and friends are better equipped to understand the complex needs, challenges, and recovery trajectories of stroke survivors, thereby providing appropriate emotional support and reducing emotional loneliness (Cao et al., 2021; Hafsteinsdóttir et al., 2011).

The suggestions about establishing new connections and maintaining interactions with friends and family members consistently show that the quality and quantity of interpersonal relationships are equally important to address emotional loneliness. However, emotional loneliness has not been the primary outcome in those previous studies on examining such potential interventions and strategies either among stroke survivors or other populations (Hafsteinsdóttir et al., 2011; He et al., 2025; O’Rourke et al., 2018). This gap in existing evidence suggests the need for more research that specifically explores the effectiveness of those interventions and strategies in addressing emotional loneliness.

It is important to note from our findings that stroke survivors experience feelings of failure, unworthiness, and hopelessness, leading to existential loneliness (da Silva et al., 2016; Palstam et al., 2019; Rago et al., 2025; Shipley et al., 2018). These negative experiences may place their lives under the control of others, leading them to diminish their life expectations and question their future (Kitzmüller et al., 2018; Sjöberg et al., 2018). More importantly, existential loneliness appears to shape how individuals understand social connections and interactions (McKenna-Plumley et al., 2023). When individuals feel hopeless or purposeless about their future lives, engagement with others may not relieve these feelings and may instead generate feelings of futility or emotional dissatisfaction (McKenna-Plumley et al., 2023). It is further evident that existential loneliness is a complex and subjective experience. Existential loneliness not only occurs on its own but also co-exists with emotional and social loneliness and may reinforce these experiences as well. These findings consistently highlight loneliness after stroke as an experience characterized by increasing spiritual, relational, and physical distance, instead of simply being alone or experiencing a reduction in social connectedness (Yang et al., 2022).

Notably, our findings suggest an integrated process experience of loneliness after stroke. This study pointed out a coherent pathway of loneliness, in which physical limitations following stroke restrict opportunities for social participation and lead to social exclusion, thereby giving rise to social loneliness. While social exclusion erodes meaningful connections and reduces the availability of emotional support, resulting in emotional loneliness. Over time, such sustained social isolation and disruptions in connections and support undermine the sense of purpose and identity of stroke survivors, culminating in existential loneliness. These observations from our findings indicate that the three multidimensional aspects of loneliness are interrelated and potentially overlapping, which differs from previous evidence on clearly defined categorical distinctions between the three types of loneliness (de Jong Gierveld, 1987; Mansfield et al., 2019). Similar to our findings, a recent systematic review on loneliness in people with acquired brain injury also found that social isolation and a lack of emotional support after injury, as well as physical disability and low self-identity caused by brain injury, can lead to loneliness experiences (Muir-Little et al., 2025). Although both the current study and previous review shared a similar aim to understand loneliness following brain injury, the previous review primarily summarized its findings by grouping them into individual, interpersonal, and systemic factors influencing loneliness, rather than conceptually integrating those findings to develop a pathway leading to loneliness (Muir-Little et al., 2025). On the other hand, our proposed pathway about post-stroke loneliness can contribute to a deeper and better understanding of social, emotional, and existential loneliness after stroke. However, the explanations of this pathway remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Despite this limitation, such a pathway can inform the development of interventions targeting loneliness after stroke in the future, particularly existential loneliness, for which no specific interventions have been identified to address this type of loneliness among stroke survivors (He et al., 2025).

Implication

This qualitative study revealed several concerns about loneliness among community-dwelling stroke survivors that contributed to important implications for research, practice, and policy.

As for the research level, future research is recommended to focus on developing tailored interventions for mitigating loneliness based on the needs of stroke survivors. The role of partnerships between healthcare services, non-governmental organisations, support groups, and family member involvement in improving loneliness is also worth exploring in the future. It is also necessary to conduct additional research to explore and identify the pathway of social, emotional, and existential loneliness after stroke.

At the practice level, it is suggested to integrate the early assessment of loneliness into the routine post-stroke care, thereby enabling timely implementations to reduce the risk of worsening loneliness after stroke. On the other hand, support that only focuses on increasing social connection may not adequately address the emotional and existential loneliness of stroke survivors. Thus, healthcare professionals may consider adopting various interactive strategies and supportive approaches to foster relational closeness and empathetic understanding. In addition, providing opportunities to reconstruct self-identity, self-worth, and purpose in life may be essential for alleviating loneliness after a stroke.

At the policy level, the findings highlight the importance of promoting social inclusion for stroke survivors. Therefore, policymakers should consider paying more attention to building inclusive environments to reduce barriers to social participation among stroke survivors. In particular, adding barrier-free facilities and enhancing public awareness of stroke through education and voluntary activities are considered a priority. In addition, it is suggested that policymakers promote cross-sectoral collaboration, particularly in medical–social service collaboration, to address loneliness among stroke survivors. This can include developing a referral pathway from healthcare services to community support groups to ensure that stroke survivors receive timely and continuous support beyond clinical treatment and reduce loneliness during the stroke recovery journey.

Strengths and limitations

The current study has its own strengths. This study used an existing dataset to conduct secondary qualitative analysis to generate evidence about the experiences of loneliness among community-dwelling stroke survivors. Such a method increases the transferability of the existing data about the lived experiences of stroke survivors. In particular, this secondary analysis study showed how post-stroke psychosocial experiences can be transferred and applied to illustrate experiences of loneliness after stroke through thick descriptions and methodological transparency. Moreover, this study only selected transcripts containing the richest data about loneliness after stroke that are relevant to the research aims, to ensure the credibility of the data. In addition, the involvement of the researchers from the original study with the existing dataset could further ensure that the extracted data were used appropriately and resulted in a more robust data analysis in this study.

However, the design of secondary analysis also has limitations. As this study was conducted as a secondary analysis of a current dataset from a previous study, the previous study's aims were not focused on loneliness after stroke, but instead aimed to understand the psychosocial experience of community-dwelling stroke survivors and to explore the strategies adopted for managing psychosocial challenges. Therefore, differences in research aims between the previous and current study resulted in richer data in some aspects of the transcripts analysed, particularly regarding psychosocial experiences. Furthermore, the original data collection and sampling method were not explicitly focused on loneliness after stroke. The researchers of this study could not control what questions were asked during interviews and could not adopt a more specific sampling method to recruit participants for interviews. As a result, nuanced perspectives on loneliness after stroke might be missing, particularly in understanding different types of loneliness and how they contribute tointervention development.

Conclusion

The current study adopted a qualitative approach to conduct a secondary analysis of existing data, which aimed to explore how community-dwelling stroke survivors experience loneliness. The findings of this study suggested that loneliness among stroke survivors was centred around limited physical functioning, difficulties in navigating interactions with friends and family members, and inclusion in society, instead of being related to deficits in their social skills. Therefore, interventions to address loneliness after stroke should focus on fostering functional independence, maintaining and establishing social relationships, and enhancing self-worth and self-identity. In addition, efforts should be made to promote an inclusive society that can reduce feelings of disconnection from society.
