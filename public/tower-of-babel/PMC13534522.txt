Associations of modifiable risk factors with depression and anxiety in women and men: evidence from the UK Biobank
Authors: Zeyu Luo, Weidi Sun, Shiyi Shan, Jing Wu, Yajie Zhu, Peige Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depression and anxiety are common mental disorders with substantial public health burden, yet the relative contributions of potentially modifiable physiological, psychosocial, and female-specific reproductive factors to their development remain incompletely understood, particularly across sex and age groups. We aimed to examine the associations of these factors with incident depression and anxiety in women and men and to estimate their population attributable fractions (PAFs). This prospective cohort study included 87,648 participants from the UK Biobank (44,383 women and 43,265 men) recruited between 2006 and 2010 and followed for a median of 13.7 years. Incident depression and anxiety were defined as the first recorded ICD-10 diagnosis or new-onset symptom positivity during follow-up among participants free of the respective condition at baseline. Sex-stratified Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and individual and combined PAFs were calculated separately in women and men. During follow-up, 5.65% of women and 4.21% of men developed depression, while 6.02% and 3.60% developed anxiety, respectively. Psychosocial factors contributed the largest population burden in both sexes. Neuroticism symptoms had the largest PAFs for depression (women: HR 2.61, 95% CI: 2.36, 2.88; PAF 48.61%, 95% CI: 44.71%, 52.68%; men: HR 3.50, 95% CI: 3.07, 3.98; PAF 59.93%, 95% CI: 55.41%, 64.13%) and anxiety (women: HR 2.82, 95% CI: 2.56, 3.11; PAF 51.75%, 95% CI: 47.93%, 55.68%; men: HR 2.94, 95% CI: 2.58, 3.36; PAF 53.80%, 95% CI: 48.89%, 58.62%). The additive combined PAF of psychosocial factors was 61.17% in women and 66.90% in men for depression, and 59.98% and 56.80% for anxiety. Among physiological factors, obesity contributed the largest PAF for depression in both sexes, while chronic inflammation was associated with both outcomes in both sexes. In women, reproductive factors provided additional contributions, with hormone replacement therapy showing the largest PAFs for depression (HR 1.48, 95% CI: 1.34, 1.62; PAF 13.43%, 95% CI: 10.15%, 16.84%) and anxiety (HR 1.32, 95% CI: 1.20, 1.44; PAF 9.43%, 95% CI: 6.38%, 12.80%). Age-stratified analyses showed that psychosocial contributions remained substantial across age groups, whereas physiological and reproductive contributions were more pronounced in later life. When all factors were considered jointly, the overall additive combined PAFs were 67.54% in women and 69.71% in men for depression, and 61.23% and 57.82% for anxiety. These findings support sex-informed and life course-oriented prevention strategies for depression and anxiety.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are among the most prevalent mental disorders globally. According to recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease, approximately 332 million individuals were living with depressive disorders and 359 million with anxiety disorders in 2021, respectively [1]. These conditions are prevalent across all age groups and represent a substantial share of mental health diagnoses [2, 3]. Both disorders typically emerge early in life, often follow a recurrent or chronic course, and are associated with profound impairments in psychosocial functioning, occupational performance, and overall quality of life [4, 5]. Beyond the individual, depression and anxiety contribute to increased healthcare utilisation, lost productivity, and wider societal costs [6]. Despite their high prevalence and substantial impact, depression and anxiety are often underdiagnosed and undertreated [5]. Importantly, they are also among the most treatable mental health conditions when identified early, underscoring the need to strengthen prevention strategies through a better understanding of modifiable risk factors [7].

A broad range of physiological, metabolic, and psychosocial exposures have been implicated in the aetiology of these disorders. Cardiometabolic conditions such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and inflammation have been associated with elevated risk through shared biological mechanisms, including neuroendocrine and immune dysregulation [8–10]. Psychosocial factors, such as low socioeconomic position, high neuroticism, and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), have consistently been linked to both disorders across diverse populations [11–13]. However, most epidemiological studies have focused on individual risk factors, often in isolation and within specific domains, limiting the ability to assess their relative contributions across the broader risk landscape. Life-course factors, such as early-life adversity, are also seldom considered alongside adult exposures, despite their long-term influence on mental health trajectories [11].

Furthermore, the extent to which these associations differ by sex remains inadequately characterised. Women are nearly twice as likely as men to be diagnosed with depression and anxiety, with higher symptom severity and earlier age of onset [14]. Yet, many epidemiological studies have analysed men and women jointly, assuming homogeneity in risk pathways. This approach may obscure important sex-specific patterns in exposure, vulnerability, and outcome. Biological, social, and behavioural differences between the sexes, including disparities in hormonal regulation, exposure to psychosocial stressors, and coping mechanisms, may contribute to divergent pathways of risk [15]. In women specifically, reproductive events such as menarche, contraceptive use, parity, and menopause represent key periods of hormonal change that may influence vulnerability to mental disorders [14, 16, 17]. However, these reproductive factors are rarely incorporated alongside general physiological and psychosocial exposures, limiting our understanding of their relative and combined impact.

In addition to identifying risk factors, quantifying their population-level impact is essential to guide prioritization in public health policy. Population Attributable Fractions (PAFs) estimate the proportion of incident cases that could be prevented if a given exposure were eliminated, under the assumption of causality [18]. PAFs are widely used in non-communicable disease research to inform prevention priorities, but their application in mental health has been limited [19]. Few studies have estimated PAFs for depression and anxiety across multiple domains of risk, and even fewer have done so separately for women and men.

In this study, we used data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort, to characterise the risk landscape for depression and anxiety in middle to older adulthood. Our objectives were: (1) to investigate associations between a comprehensive set of physiological, psychosocial, and reproductive risk factors and incident depression and anxiety; (2) to compare these associations between women and men; and (3) to estimate sex-specific PAFs for individual and grouped exposures.

Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large population-based prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 people aged 37–73, from 22 sites across England, Scotland, and Wales between March 2006 and October 2010 [20]. Individuals were invited to attend one of the 22 centers for baseline assessment, which included touchscreen questionnaires, physical and functional measurements. Written informed consent was obtained for all participants electronically. In this study, mental health status and modifiable risk factors were assessed at baseline. Incident depression and anxiety during follow-up were identified using linked hospital inpatient records, first-occurrence data, and the 2016–2017 online mental health survey. Participants were followed from baseline assessment until the first occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, or censoring (16 December 2022), whichever came first. The present study excluded participants with prevalent depression or anxiety at baseline (N = 39,208 for depression and N = 40,438 for anxiety in women; N = 23,754 for depression and N = 25,298 for anxiety in men), leaving 87,648 participants with available data on mental health status and risk factors at baseline for analysis. Measurement of modifiable risk factors

According to previous studies, the modifiable risk factors of depression and anxiety could be generally classified into two domains: (1) physiological risk factors, including physiological and metabolic factors, and (2) psychosocial risk factors, including behavioral, psychological, and social environmental risk factors [22]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that specific reproductive factors may play a role in women’s lifetime mental health [16, 17, 23]. Measurement of outcome

The main outcomes were incident depression and anxiety, identified using hospital records, self-reported diagnoses, and validated symptom questionnaires in the UK Biobank [24]. Depression and anxiety were defined as the presence of either a clinical diagnosis or symptom positivity. Clinical cases were ascertained from hospital inpatient records and first-occurrence data based on ICD-10 codes F32-F33 for depression and F40-F41 for anxiety, or self-reported diagnoses at baseline assessment. Symptoms were assessed at baseline using the corresponding components of the four-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4; subscale score > = 3) and at follow-up (2016–2017 mental health survey) using the PHQ-9 (score > = 10) for depression and the seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) (score > = 10) for anxiety. Prevalent cases at baseline were defined as having a prior ICD-10 diagnosis, self-reported diagnosis, or baseline symptom positivity and were excluded from incident analyses. Incident cases were defined as the first recorded ICD-10 diagnosis or new-onset symptom positivity during follow-up among participants free of the respective condition at baseline. Detailed definitions of the outcomes and their corresponding UK Biobank Field IDs are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics for women and men were presented as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables, and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables with non-normal distributions. Differences between women and men were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables. These baseline summaries were reported for the overall study population and stratified by age groups (< 50, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, and > = 65 years).

Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for depression and anxiety across the three categories of risk factors were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals [25]. Follow-up time was defined from baseline age to the date of outcome onset, death, or censoring (16 December 2022), whichever occurred first. A stepwise modeling approach was applied. Initially, each category of risk factors was examined separately in relation to incident depression and anxiety, adjusting for age and ethnicity (Table S2). Subsequently, all three categories were entered simultaneously into a multivariable model to assess their independent associations; p values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [26]. Interaction with age was assessed by including cross-product terms between each risk factor and continuous age in the fully adjusted model, followed by age-stratified analyses. Sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding continuous age from the multivariable model.

To further quantify sex differences, we estimated the women-to-men ratio of HRs and the women-to-men difference of risk differences (RDs) [27]. The women-to-men ratio of HRs was calculated by dividing the HR in women by that in men, with 95% CIs derived on the log scale and back-transformed to the original scale. The women-to-men difference of RDs was based on 10-year cumulative incidence estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods, with 95% CIs obtained using Greenwood standard errors and normal approximation.

PAFs with 95% CIs were calculated for each risk factor, each category of risk factors, as well as overall risk factors. The PAF reflects the proportion of disease cases that could theoretically be prevented if the exposure were eliminated [18]. Results

Baseline characteristics

Table S3 shows baseline characteristics of participants. A total of 87,648 participants were included, including 44,383 women and 43,265 men. Overall, men were slightly older than women (median 58 vs. 56 years) and had a higher burden of cardiometabolic conditions, including diabetes, stroke, hypertension, higher BMI, and a greater proportion of overweight/obesity. Women showed a higher prevalence of chronic inflammation and slightly higher neuroticism symptoms scores. Most physiological, lifestyle, and psychosocial variables differed between sexes (generally p < 0.001), whereas ACE and high deprivation were comparable. Tables S4 and S5 present age-specific distributions of baseline characteristics in women and men. Across age groups, cardiometabolic conditions, chronic inflammation, and low SES were more common in older participants, whereas smoking, unhealthy diet, and neuroticism symptoms were more prevalent in younger age groups.

Incidence of depression and anxiety

Over a median follow-up period of 13.72 (IQR: 12.93, 14.41) years for women and 13.73 (IQR: 13.00, 14.42) for men, 5.65% of women and 4.21% of men developed depression, while 6.02% of women and 3.60% of men developed anxiety, with a higher incidence of both depression and anxiety among women (p < 0.001).

Associations and PAFs of physiological factors with depression and anxiety

Among physiological factors (Fig. 3A, Table S6), obesity contributed the largest PAF of depression burden in both sexes, with HRs of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.22, 1.45) in women and 1.25 (95% CI: 1.12, 1.40) in men, corresponding to PAFs of 14.85% (95% CI: 10.27%, 19.18%) and 15.32% (95% CI: 9.01%, 22.08%), respectively. Chronic inflammation was also associated with a higher incidence of depression (women: HR 1.44, PAF 7.33%; men: HR 1.47, PAF 6.19%). Diabetes (HR 1.55 in women; 1.52 in men) and stroke (HR 1.58 in women; 1.72 in men) were also associated with incident depression, although their corresponding PAFs were smaller (< 3% in both sexes). The additive combined PAF of physiological factors for depression was 21.16% in women and 21.87% in men. Women-to-men ratios of HRs for depression were generally close to 1.0, and differences in RDs were generally close to 0 (Table S7). Age-stratified analyses for depression (Fig. 4A, Tables S8-S9) showed that in both women and men, obesity contributed most in those aged <65, while chronic inflammation contributed across almost all age groups. For anxiety (Fig. 3B, Table S6), chronic inflammation was associated with a higher incidence in both sexes (women: HR 1.19, PAF 3.31%; men: HR 1.36, PAF 4.80%). Hypertension was additionally associated with anxiety in women (HR 1.11, PAF 4.33%), whereas stroke showed an association with anxiety in men (HR 1.53, PAF 0.54%). The additive combined PAF of physiological factors for anxiety was 7.37% in women and 5.29% in men. Age-stratified analyses for anxiety (Fig. 4B, Tables S10-S11) showed that physiological contributions were modest at younger ages but increased in later life, with the highest additive combined PAF observed in women aged > = 65 and in men aged 60–64 (Fig. 5B).

Associations and PAFs of psychosocial factors with depression and anxiety

In the psychosocial domain, neuroticism symptoms contributed the largest PAF of depression burden in both sexes, with HRs of 2.61 (95% CI: 2.36, 2.88) in women and 3.50 (95% CI: 3.07, 3.98) in men, corresponding to PAFs of 48.61% (95% CI: 44.71%, 52.68%) and 59.93% (95% CI: 55.41%, 64.13%), respectively. ACE (women: HR 1.49, PAF 17.58%; men: HR 1.60, PAF 20.54%) and AAE (women: HR 1.55, PAF 25.31%; men: HR 1.43, PAF 19.11%) were also associated with incident depression. Unhealthy sleep and sedentary behavior showed moderate PAFs for depression, whereas other psychosocial factors contributed smaller proportions. Women-to-men ratios of HRs for depression were generally close to 1.0, although neuroticism symptoms (0.75 [95% CI: 0.63, 0.88]) and low SES (0.78 [95% CI: 0.65, 0.94]) showed relatively stronger associations in men. The additive combined PAF of psychosocial factors for depression was 61.17% in women and 66.90% in men. Age-stratified analyses for depression showed that neuroticism symptoms remained the predominant contributor across all age groups; additive combined PAFs exceeded 60% in most age strata for both women and men.

For anxiety, neuroticism symptoms also contributed the largest PAF (women: HR 2.82 [95% CI: 2.56, 3.11], PAF 51.75% [95% CI: 47.93%, 55.68%]; men: HR 2.94 [95% CI: 2.58, 3.36], PAF 53.80% [95% CI: 48.89%, 58.62%]). ACE (women: HR 1.29, PAF 11.28%; men: HR 1.38, PAF 14.20%) and AAE (women: HR 1.26, PAF 13.72%; men: HR 1.27, PAF 13.12%) were also associated. The additive combined PAF of psychosocial factors for anxiety was 59.98% in women and 56.80% in men. Age-stratified analyses for anxiety showed consistently substantial psychosocial contributions across age groups, with neuroticism symptoms remaining dominant at all ages; additive combined PAFs were generally stable across strata, with modestly higher values in younger and middle-aged adults.

Associations and PAFs of reproductive factors with depression and anxiety

Among female-specific reproductive factors, use of HRT contributed the largest PAF of depression burden (HR 1.48 [95% CI: 1.34, 1.62]; PAF 13.43% [95% CI: 10.15, 16.84%]). Early age at menarche (PAF 2.93%), history of pregnancy termination (PAF 3.34%), and early age at first birth (PAF 1.74%) also showed positive associations with depression. The additive combined PAF of reproductive factors for depression was 19.19%. Age-stratified analyses for depression showed that reproductive contributions were more pronounced in later life, with higher additive combined PAFs observed among women aged > = 60 years.

For anxiety, HRT remained the leading contributor (HR 1.32 [95% CI: 1.20, 1.44], PAF 9.43% [95% CI: 6.38%, 12.80%]), while history of pregnancy termination contributed modestly (PAF 2.82%). The additive combined PAF of reproductive factors for anxiety was 0.13%. Age-stratified analyses for anxiety suggested that reproductive contributions remained modest across age groups, with slightly higher PAFs in older women, particularly those aged 60 years and above.

Overall modifiable risk factors

When all modifiable risk factors were considered jointly, the overall additive combined PAF for depression was 67.54% in women and 69.71% in men; corresponding values for anxiety were 61.23% and 57.82%. Age-stratified analyses showed that additive combined PAFs generally exceeded 50% across nearly all age groups in both sexes.

Sensitivity analyses excluding age from the Cox models (Table S12) produced HRs comparable to the main analyses. Results after Benjamini-Hochberg correction are shown in Table S13. Figures S1-S4 show Schoenfeld residual plots testing the proportional hazards assumption and Figure S5 presents multiplicative combined PAFs.

Discussion

In this large-scale cohort study based on the UK Biobank, we investigated associations between a wide range of physiological, psychosocial, and female-specific reproductive factors and the risk of depression and anxiety in both women and men. Psychosocial factors contributed the largest PAFs, with neuroticism symptoms alone accounting for approximately 50%, and overall psychosocial additive combined PAFs exceeding 60% for depression in both sexes. Physiological factors showed more modest contributions, with obesity and chronic inflammation being the main drivers. Reproductive factors contributed additionally to depression and anxiety in women. When all modifiable factors were considered jointly, the combined PAFs generally exceeded 60%. The specific risk factors and the magnitude of their PAFs varied to some extent between women and men. These findings emphasize the importance of adopting a sex-specific and life course-oriented approach in understanding and addressing mental health risks.

Risk factors in physiological domain demonstrated associations with depression, and to a lesser extent, with anxiety. Obesity contributed the largest PAF for depression in both sexes, which may reflect its dual role as a metabolic and psychosocial stressor. Excess adiposity is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and leptin dysregulation, all of which may contribute to depression risk through immune, neuroendocrine, and neuroplasticity-related pathways, while weight-related stigma and body image concerns may further exacerbate depressive vulnerability [8, 31]. Chronic inflammation was associated with elevated risks of both depression and anxiety in women and men across most age groups. These findings align with previous evidence linking systemic inflammation to dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, altered monoamine neurotransmission, and neuroimmune interactions that may underlie depressive or anxiety symptomatology [32–34]. Hypertension was linked to anxiety only among women, suggesting potential cardiovascular-autonomic mechanisms contributing to anxiety symptoms in women, or perhaps differential awareness and health perception in response to chronic conditions [35]. Taken together, these results highlighted the need to integrate mental health screening into chronic disease management, with attention to sex-specific physiological vulnerabilities and life stage.

Risk factors in psychosocial domain contributed the largest share of risk for both depression and anxiety, with complex sex-specific patterns. Neuroticism symptoms, reflecting a stable personality trait characterized by emotional instability, negative affectivity, and vulnerability to stress, emerged as the single strongest determinant in both sexes [36]. Despite women reporting higher neuroticism scores, the PAF for neuroticism associated with depression and anxiety was greater in men, possibly indicating a lower psychological threshold or reduced emotional regulation capacity among men when characterized by elevated neuroticism [37]. Consistently, women-to-men ratios of HRs of neuroticism symptoms showed relatively stronger associations with depression in men than women. ACE and AAE were associated with both depression and anxiety; AAE showed a higher prevalence among women. Exposure to ACE and AAE can disrupt stress response systems, impair emotional regulation, and increase long-term vulnerability to mood disorders [11]. The higher prevalence of AAE in women may stem from both greater exposure to certain interpersonal adversities, such as domestic violence and caregiving burdens, and greater internalization of traumatic stress [38, 39]. Socioeconomic disadvantage, unhealthy sleep, smoking, and sedentary behavior were independently associated with elevated risks, reinforcing the multifactorial nature of psychological distress. For depression, men generally showed higher PAFs for these factors, and for low SES in particular, women-to-men ratios of HRs indicated a relatively stronger association in men. These patterns may reflect a combination of behavioral clustering, such as co-occurring smoking and inactivity, and lower likelihood of engaging in stress-reducing or help-seeking behaviors [40, 41]. Additionally, men may be less likely to perceive or report early psychological symptoms, resulting in more advanced distress by the time these risk factors accumulate [42]. These findings underscore the importance of addressing emotional traits and psychosocial adversities as central components of sex-sensitive mental health prevention strategies.

Female-specific reproductive factors contributed substantially to the risk of depression and anxiety among women, particularly in older age groups. Ever use of HRT showed the largest PAFs. Given that HRT in this study was defined as self-reported ever use and no information was available on formulation, route, dose, duration, or timing, this association should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect underlying menopausal symptoms, healthcare-seeking patterns, or broader hormone-related vulnerability, rather than the effect of a specific HRT regimen itself [23, 43]. Early menarche and history of pregnancy termination were also linked to depression and anxiety. These findings highlight the importance of considering reproductive history when assessing mental health risks in women. Moreover, the cumulative PAF of reproductive factors rose sharply with age, emphasizing the enduring influence of reproductive transitions on mental well-being later in life.

Our findings have important implications for public health practice and clinical care, underscoring the necessity of sex-specific, multifactorial strategies in mental health prevention and intervention. The substantial contribution of modifiable psychosocial risk factors suggests that large portions of mental health burden could be reduced through targeted health promotion, trauma-informed care, and social support interventions. Integrating mental health screening into chronic disease management, particularly for individuals with diabetes or chronic inflammation, may help address comorbid risks early and effectively [44, 45]. For women, mental health screening may be particularly relevant during the menopausal transition and in later life. Reproductive factors may therefore be considered alongside psychosocial and physiological factors to help identify women at higher risk of depression and anxiety. In men, the pronounced effect of neuroticism highlights the need to address underrecognized emotional distress and increase access to psychological resources. Gendered stigma surrounding help-seeking and emotional expression may contribute to delayed identification and treatment of depression and anxiety in men, despite comparable or greater risk factor burden [41, 42]. Policies should aim to reduce structural barriers, improve mental health literacy, and promote equitable access to care across the life course and across sexes.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it used data from the large-scale, population-based UK Biobank cohort and included over 87,000 participants with available information on mental health status and risk factors, enabling robust sex-specific and age-stratified analyses [20]. Second, the study incorporated a wide range of modifiable risk factors spanning physiological, psychosocial, and female-specific reproductive domains, allowing for a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of mental health risk profiles. This approach moves beyond single-risk-factor models and reflects the complex, layered etiology of depression and anxiety. Third, the application of PAFs alongside HRs enhances the public health relevance of the findings by quantifying the population-level impact of each risk factor. By estimating the proportion of cases theoretically preventable through risk factor modification, this study provides actionable insight into mental health prevention priorities. Lastly, the use of stratified analyses by sex and age allowed for the identification of vulnerable subgroups, such as older women with reproductive risk exposure, offering important direction for future targeted interventions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the assessment of depression and anxiety was based on hospital records, self-reported data, and validated symptom questionnaires, which may lead to underdiagnosis or misclassification, particularly among individuals less likely to seek formal care, such as men or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Second, key psychosocial exposures, such as ACE and AAE, relied on retrospective self-reporting, which is susceptible to recall bias and may underestimate or distort actual exposure. Third, missing data were present for some variables, and analyses were based on a complete-case approach, resulting in the exclusion of a substantial proportion of participants. If included and excluded participants differed systematically, selection bias may have been introduced, which could have affected both the estimated associations and the generalizability of the findings. In addition, several continuous variables were dichotomized to enhance interpretability and facilitate PAF estimation, which may have resulted in some loss of information and attenuation of dose-response relationships. Fourth, although the temporal sequence of exposure and outcome was preserved through the prospective design, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive causal inference. In particular, the PAF approach assumes causality, yet several investigated risk factors may simultaneously act as contributors to, correlates of, or outcomes of depression and anxiety. Thus, the estimated PAFs should be interpreted as indicative of potential population-level relevance rather than as precise causal effects. Fifth, unmeasured confounding (e.g., genetic vulnerability, interpersonal factors) may still influence the observed associations despite adjustment for multiple covariates. Sixth, the large number of comparisons increases the risk of false-positive findings. Analyses were based on a priori, theoretically justified domains and risk factors, and Schoenfeld residual diagnostics suggested that the proportional hazards assumption was generally met. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Nevertheless, individual associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the UK Biobank cohort is not fully representative of the general population, since the participants tend to be healthier and of higher socioeconomic status than the broader UK public [46]. Therefore, caution is warranted in generalizing these findings to other populations, especially those with different sociocultural or healthcare contexts. Future studies using more diverse samples and causal inference methods, as well as explicit measures of social gender identity, are needed to validate and extend these results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large prospective study based on the UK Biobank highlights the substantial and multifaceted contribution of modifiable physiological, psychosocial, and reproductive factors to the risk of depression and anxiety in middle to older adulthood. Psychosocial factors, particularly neuroticism symptoms, accounted for the greatest population-level mental health burden in both sexes, while physiological and reproductive factors provided additional, sex- and age-specific contributions. The overall combined PAFs, generally around or above 60%, underscore the considerable potential of effectively addressing these risk factors for the prevention of depression and anxiety. These findings support the adoption of sex-sensitive, life course-oriented strategies that integrate psychological, metabolic, and reproductive health considerations into mental health prevention and clinical practice.
