Assessing the impact of student-led advocacy initiatives on Canadian undergraduate students’ knowledge of and attitudes toward organ donation: a cross-sectional survey from Western University
Authors: Armita Hamedi Mobarra, Thomas Liang, Alp Sener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Organ donation remains a critical public health concern in Canada, with persistently low registration rates despite widespread support. We sought to evaluate the efficacy of student-created educational materials in enhancing Canadian undergraduate students’ knowledge of and attitudes toward organ donation.Methods:We conducted a pretest–posttest cross-sectional survey at Western University, in which participants engaged with educational content developed by the student-led Organ Advocacy Initiative. We assessed the proportions of students with and without changed opinions toward organ donation using simple percentage analysis.Results:Ninety responses were available for analysis; of these, 62 were fully completed. Postintervention analyses revealed a significant increase in knowledge scores (p = 0.007), with students in health care–related majors demonstrating higher baseline knowledge scores than students in non–health care–related majors (p = 0.02). Whereas pre-existing attitudes toward organ donation were largely positive, concerns regarding organ misuse were more prevalent among non–health care students but showed a measurable decline following the intervention. Willingness to register as an organ donor remained stable among health care students but exhibited a marginal decrease among non–health care students postintervention.Conclusion:Student-led organ advocacy has the potential to improve knowledge of and attitudes toward organ donation. Future research should maximize the impact of student-led educational materials by targeting diverse populations and exploring the long-term effects through longitudinal studies.

Organ donation is commonly defined as the voluntary and altruistic act of giving organs, tissues, or cells to those in need, typically, though not exclusively, after death. In Canada, the donation process is governed by various national and provincial laws and regulations to ensure ethical and transparent practices, such as Canadian Blood Services and Ontario’s Trillium Gift of Life Network. The demand for organ transplants greatly surpasses the available supply, leading to extensive wait times for patients in need.1 Despite overwhelming public support of organ donation (> 90%), only 25% of the Canadian and 35% of the Ontario public have registered their decision to donate.2,3 As a result, many government initiatives have emphasized the need for increasing rates of donation through multisectoral activities, such as identifying decision-making mechanisms and improving professional education and general awareness.4

There has been much research assessing university students’ attitudes, knowledge, and willingness regarding organ donation.5–8 This body of research has highlighted the positive impact of educational interventions on undergraduates’ perspectives of organ donation, particularly among nursing and medical students in the United States9 and around the world.10–13

However, few studies have investigated the attitudes of Canadian undergraduate (baccalaureate) students, with most studies focusing on medical students.14,15 Additionally, there have been limited studies conducted on Canadian students that have investigated the effects of educational interventions on the knowledge of organ donation. As such, this study aims to address this research gap by surveying undergraduate baccalaureate students of all programs at Western University, London, Ontario (hereinafter, “Western”). This study will help shape future research on educational interventions to improve Canadian undergraduates’ views on organ donation. Understanding their attitudes is key, as they can register as donors and may later influence organ-donation policy through careers in health care or politics.

Importantly, most educational materials in previous studies were developed by medical professionals and experts.9,10,16 However, this study is unique in that the educational materials are drawn from Western’s Organ Advocacy Initiative (OAI) chapter, a student-run organization dedicated toward spreading awareness about ethical organ donation using short informative articles and interviews with key partners (e.g., health care professionals, academics, organ donors, and recipients).17,18 Findings from this study will reveal whether student-led advocacy effectively raises awareness about Canadian organ donation among undergraduates.

The purpose of the present study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a student-led educational intervention in influencing Canadian undergraduates’ knowledge of and attitudes toward organ donation. We aimed to explore possible correlations between students’ programs, year of study, and political affiliations, as well as their opinions of and receptivity to educational content surrounding organ donation.

Methods

Study design

We used a pretest–posttest design.19–21 The design of this study was informed by both validated and nonvalidated instruments previously used on undergraduate students in other countries.9 Participants first completed a pretest survey of multiple-choice questions in true/false, Likert scale, and multiselect format. The pretest survey included demographic questions regarding program major, age, year of study, sex, gender, religion, ethnicity, and political stance, as well as questions relating to current knowledge, attitudes, and behaviour regarding organ donation in Canada.22 For screening and confirmation purposes, no identifying features were requested. After completing the pretest, participants read 3 OAI articles (Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.012725/tab-related-content), compiled into a single PDF, each approximately a 10-minute read. The first article, “The Current State of Organ Donations in Canada,”23 covered basic information surrounding organ donation in Canada. The second article, “Debunking Six Common Organ Donation Myths,”24 dispelled common myths surrounding organ donation, and the third, “The Organ Shortage: What We Can Do to Increase Organ Donation in Canada,”25 examined a popular topic in the field of organ donation. Following exposure to the educational materials, participants completed a posttest survey assessing their opinions on organ donation and evaluating whether their attitudes had changed. The posttest also asked participants if they would engage with OAI’s educational content in the future to assess the enjoyability of our educational materials and ask for feedback to make our articles more engaging. The survey opened on July 4, 2024, and remained open for 3 months until closing on Oct. 4, 2024. For financial incentive, participants had the opportunity to enter an online draw for the chance to win 1 of 2 $25 Amazon gift cards on completing the study.

Participant recruitment

An email was sent to all Western undergraduate students via a mass email request system. The email explained the research study and included the link to the Qualtrics survey. After clicking on the link, participants were greeted with the formal invitation-to-participate letter, which detailed the purpose of the study, inclusion and exclusion criteria, study procedures, possible risks and harms, possible benefits, compensation, and ensured confidentiality.

For inclusion criteria, students were eligible to participate in the study if they were currently enrolled in any undergraduate baccalaureate program as a part-time or full-time student at Western (or any of the affiliate colleges); able to provide informed consent; and competent in reading in the English language. Current members and executives of the Western OAI club were excluded, along with students who were part of professional undergraduate programs, including medicine, dentistry, law, and education.

Questionnaire and survey measures

We used Qualtrics XM as the survey platform. Students first completed a short screening survey to confirm whether they were proficient in reading English, had no prior connection to OAI, were a part-time or full-time student, and were not students in a professional undergraduate program. If a student was eligible to participate, they were prompted to begin the study. Participants then completed the pretest survey, which asked for demographic information before collecting data about knowledge levels and attitudes about organ donation, willingness to consider signing up as an organ donor, and current registered organ donor status. Students then read the 3 OAI articles mentioned in the Study design section, which were all approximately a 10-minute read and written by a Western University undergraduate journalist with OAI. These 3 articles were chosen with an intention to provide students with a variety of articles typically written by OAI journalists, including general information on organ donation in Canada, addressing misinformation, and suggested solutions for the organ shortage. Students were instructed to complete the pretest, read the articles, and then immediately complete the posttest. We designed this approach to simulate how the students would naturally process the information presented in the OAI articles and to determine whether the articles alone were sufficient to change their knowledge and perspectives on organ donation, without the influence of other educational materials or external factors.

Our survey measures were as follows:

Knowledge: This section consisted of 10 true/false questions about general organ donation information in Canada. We adapted the items from Fan and colleagues26 to reflect the Canadian context of our educational materials (e.g., “Organ donors cannot claim any monetary compensation.”).

Data analysis

We analyzed data to assess the proportions of students with and without changed opinions toward organ donation using simple percentage analysis. We calculated the correlation between students’ political stance, sex, gender, religion, program major, and opinions toward organ donation and receptivity to educational content through t tests and Pearson correlations. We conducted analyses using Microsoft Excel version 16.80 and Stats iQ. We reviewed qualitative feedback and suggestions using thematic analysis. We set statistical significance at a p value of 0.05 or less.

To assess the effectiveness of OAI articles in informing the public, we aimed to determine whether the intervention articles led to an increase in students’ test scores on the organ donation quiz from the pretest to the posttest. First, we checked our data set for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and found that we had a nonparametric distribution (W = 0.923, p < 0.01). Thus, we used the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test to determine whether there was a significant difference between the students’ pretest and posttest scores.

To assess attitudes, 5-point Likert scale questionnaires were presented during both the pre- and posttest, along with a question on the perceived benefits of organ donation. The 5-point Likert scale used to assess attitudes was collapsed into 3 categories (agree, neither, and disagree) during data analysis owing to the small sample.

To assess undergraduate students’ behaviours toward organ donation, organ donor status was used as a proxy for behaviour and was asked in the pretest.

Ethics approval

We obtained ethics approval from Western’s Health Science Research Ethics Board (project ID: 124685).

Results

A total of 24 871 students were recruited for the study, and 132 students consented. Some responses were entirely blank and others were submitted by participants who did not engage with the intervention (e.g., filling in the sociodemographic info, but leaving both the pretest and posttest sections blank). After we excluded these incomplete responses, 90 responses remained for analysis. Of these, 28 were partially completed (i.e., the participants completed only the pretest portion of the survey) and 62 were fully completed (i.e., the participants completed both the pretest and posttest portions). Willingness

For health care students who fully completed the survey and answered the willingness question (n = 30), a majority (77%, n = 23) answered yes to considering becoming registered organ donors in both the pre- and posttest. This trend was similar among the non–health care students who fully completed the survey and answered this question (n = 29), where a majority answered yes in the pretest (62%, n = 18) and posttest (59%, n = 17). Among the students who partially completed the survey, a majority (65%, n = 13) of the health care students (n = 20) answered yes in the pretest to considering becoming registered organ donors, whereas a majority (57%, n = 4) of the non–health care students (n = 7) answered no.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effect of student-led educational initiatives on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of undergraduate students in Canada. Overall, our findings indicate that health care and non–health care students are knowledgeable and generally hold positive attitudes toward organ donation, although there are notable differences between the groups. Our data also suggest that attitudes and willingness to donate organs remained relatively stable from pre- to posttest, with some minor, insignificant shifts toward greater agreement with organ donation.

Knowledge about organ donation

In line with previous studies on this topic,27–30 we found that the OAI’s educational intervention increased both health care and non–health care students’ knowledge relative to their pretest. However, a 0.5 increase in knowledge, although statistically significant, may not translate to significance in the real world. Some of these differences in knowledge gain might be attributed to the type of educational intervention used, such as through student-written articles (as in our study), videos,9 a peer-learning approach,11 or professional presentations.31

Attitudes toward organ donation

One of the most notable findings is the overwhelming disagreement with the misconception that doctors may act before a patient dies if the patient signed a donor card. This trend was observed across all student groups and increased slightly in the posttest, suggesting that participation in the study may have reinforced this correct understanding. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that mistrust in the medical system is a common barrier to organ donation but can be mitigated through education.32,33 Interestingly, concerns about potential misuse of organs after death were more prevalent among non–health care students. In the pretest, nearly half of the non–health care students who fully completed the survey (48%) felt that the possibility of organ misuse made them less supportive of organ donation. This contrasts with the perceptions of health care students, most of whom (58%) disagreed with this concern. This discrepancy may be due to differences in exposure to medical ethics and trust in health care institutions between the student groups. However, posttest results indicated a decrease in this concern among non–health care students, suggesting that exposure to educational materials may have alleviated some fears. Another key finding is the support for financial incentives for donor families. A majority of both health care and non–health care students agreed that the government should provide such incentives, with a slight increase in agreement in the posttest survey. This reflects an ongoing ethical and policy debate on whether financial incentives can boost donation rates without undermining altruistic motivations.

Behaviours and willingness to donate

Although attitudes toward organ donation were generally positive, actual donor registration rates varied. Among health care students who fully completed the survey, a slight majority (55%) were registered organ donors. In contrast, of the non–health care students who fully completed the survey, a majority (64%) were not registered donors. This disparity may be owing to greater exposure to discussions about organ donation in health care–related programs, reinforcing the importance of registration. Willingness to consider becoming an organ donor was high among health care students, with 77% expressing willingness in both the pre- and posttest survey. Among non–health care students, willingness was slightly lower, with a decrease from 62% in the pretest to 59% in the posttest. The slight decline in willingness among non–health care students could be attributed to individual reflection following the pretest, potentially leading some students to reassess their stance.

Perceived benefits of organ donation

A strong majority of both health care and non–health care students recognized all 4 presented benefits of organ donation, with an increase in recognition in the posttest survey. This suggests that even among those initially supportive of organ donation, increased awareness of its benefits can reinforce positive attitudes. Health care students in particular demonstrated a higher initial awareness of these benefits, which is expected given their education.

Future directions

Interventions to promote postmortem organ donation rates have been discussed in the literature.34 Two popular classes of interventions include the public-education approach and the social-marketing approach. With the rising usage of social media in public health initiatives, the social-marketing approach has shown great promise in encouraging members of the public to sign up as organ donors,35–38 especially given the speed and scale at which information can be disseminated. Nevertheless, the public-education approach also has notable merits for encouraging donor registration and has been discussed more broadly in public health,39 especially before the rise of social media.40–42 From the literature, we noticed that one popular avenue for the public-education approach is through schooling, or school education. 43,44 These interventions targeted both adolescents (middle and high school students) and young adults (university and college students), which is appropriate, as these are formative years during which students develop their views on important topics such as organ donation.

Although much of the literature emphasizes the benefits of social marketing in public health advocacy, one potential advantage of the public-education approach is that it is, at least in principle, less intrusive. This stems from public education’s focus on education and knowledge, whereas social-marketing campaigns may often aim to market ideas and influence decisions on donor registration. However, we believe that the public-education and social-marketing approaches need not be adversarial. As Tontus suggests,45 both mass media campaigns and educational initiatives may be necessary to increase organ-donor registration. To this end, since the initiatives of our present study were primarily education-based, we offer suggestions on how future studies can improve on the public-education approach reflected here.

With this background in mind, our study highlights key areas where OAI at Western University, as well as other related university clubs and educators, can refine their materials to better engage students from diverse personal and academic backgrounds. In line with Molmenti and colleagues’ 32 findings, presenting comprehensive educational content to university students from different backgrounds may be crucial in equipping them with the necessary knowledge to make informed decisions about organ donation.

Therefore, to improve future OAI educational articles, the need for quality-improvement investigations is crucial. As student-led advocacy initiatives can help educate students on important topics, it is essential to ensure that they are engaging students, disseminating accurate information, and targeting as many student groups as possible. Moreover, university student councils should encourage student-led clubs to assess their educational materials and measure their impacts, either by conducting similar quality-improvement studies or by establishing formal goals with appropriate tracking systems.

To enhance the effectiveness of student-led educational articles on organ donation, particularly after targeted improvements are implemented, a follow-up study is crucial to reassess changes in undergraduate students’ KAP. Future pretest–posttest studies should consider a slightly longer interval between the initial test, the reinforcement, and the posttest. Although we cannot provide specific recommendations, the timing should be long enough to ensure that differences in scores reflect actual gains rather than chance (e.g., a participant simply guessing one more answer correctly), but not so long that other external factors could confound the results. Future longitudinal studies could also evaluate the lasting impacts of OAI’s articles on students after initial exposure to help provide valuable insights into whether student-created educational materials lead to sustained changes in KAP. In addition to quantitative data, future research should place greater emphasis on qualitative approaches to better understand students’ beliefs, motivations, and barriers to engaging with organ donation educational materials.

Limitations

There is a potential risk of sampling and nonresponse bias. The survey was distributed via Western’s mass email system, and the 132 students who completed it represented less than 1% of the eligible undergraduate university population. Respondents may have been more polarized or motivated to participate compared with the general student body. Although an incentive was offered to help mitigate this and recruit all eligible students, this factor could still have affected the generalizability of the findings. To avoid this limitation in the future, increasing broadcasting of the study through additional methods, such as social media and professor email networks, could help increase the sample size.

Additionally, owing to the design of the study, participants were not required to fully complete the survey to be part of the optional gift card draw as a means to increase participation and data. As a result, some students only partially completed the survey, which led to a small sample, with some student groups’ sample being as small as 7. This resulted in difficult assumption-making for certain associations and student groups, as well as extrapolation to the larger undergraduate population because of a lower statistical power.46 As a consequence, the findings of this study should be interpreted as descriptive.

Another consequence of the study’s small sample is the data aggregation of the attitude answer choices, specifically strongly agree with somewhat agree into agree, and strongly disagree with somewhat disagree into disagree. These aggregations were performed after data collection to increase the statistical power; however, if the aggregated answer choices were presented in the survey instead of the nonaggregated answer choices, this may have influenced students’ initial answers. Therefore, collapsing answer choices may have misrepresented the responses.

In addition, certain prompts, such as “By signing a donor card, doctors might do something to me before I am really dead” presented in the pretest and posttest survey were not elaborated on. This may have confused how to interpret certain prompts, which may have contributed to variability among respondents. To avoid this confusion, prompts could be worded more clearly to avoid multiple understandings.

Finally, although the pre- and posttest results provide insight into changes after the intervention, the extent to which these measures accurately captured students’ KAP was not formally established through construct validity testing. It is recommended that future studies conduct pilot testing of their measurement tools to establish construct validity in similar student populations, and also assess internal reliability, possibly through the Cronbach α measure.

Conclusion

This pretest–posttest study provides insight into students’ perceptions of the current organ-donation landscape and may inform future educational initiatives. However, the findings are exploratory and warrant replication in larger samples at Western University and other institutions. More broadly, we hope this study and the works of OAI encourage greater student registration and advocacy for organ donation, with important public health implications, particularly as many undergraduates go on to careers in health care or law, where they may influence organ-donation policy.
