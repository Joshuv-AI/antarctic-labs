Oral manifestations of endocrine disorders among patients attending the paediatric endocrinology clinic in the lagos university teaching hospital: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Abimbola Yomi Adeloye, Elizabeth Eberechi Oyenusi, Ifeoluwa Bridget Falokun, Ifeoma Linda Utomi, Veronica Nneoma Ibenye, Mariam Oluwadamilola Alayo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaediatric endocrine disorders represent a spectrum of conditions that affect hormone synthesis, regulation and function in childhood and adolescence. They present with intraoral features that may compromise the long-term well-being and oral health of affected individuals, potentially persisting into their adult years when appropriate diagnosis and therapeutic intervention are delayed or inadequate. This study aims to determine the prevalent oral manifestations of endocrine disorders among children attending the Paediatric Endocrinology Clinic of the Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH).MethodsA cross-sectional study was done among 50 paediatric patients aged (6–18 years) with endocrine disorders, recruited via consecutive sampling. Data collection employed interviewer-administered questionnaires covering medical and dental histories, followed by standardized intraoral examinations of soft and hard tissues. Ethical approval, informed consent, and participant assent were obtained. Data was analysed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics included frequencies and percentages. Inferential analysis was performed using Chi-square test to evaluate associations between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsType 1 diabetes mellitus was the predominant condition among the participants (n = 27, 54.0%), followed by hypothyroidism (n = 7, 14.0%). Other endocrine disorders, including congenital adrenal hyperplasia (n = 6, 12.0%), Turner syndrome, Cushing syndrome, adrenal insufficiency, and metabolic syndrome, were each represented by single cases (2.0% each). Oral dryness emerged as the leading soft tissue manifestation (n = 27, 54.0%), accompanied by oral pigmentation (n = 20, 40.0%), gingivitis (n = 7, 14.0%), and oral ulceration (n = 6, 12.0%). Enamel defects, crossbite, dental crowding, open bite, and dental caries were the primary hard tissue findings, each occurring in eight participants (n = 8, 19.0%).ConclusionsThere is a significant prevalence of oral pathologies among paediatric patients with endocrine disorders. Findings support incorporating dental evaluations into endocrinological care protocols and highlight the need for coordinated inter-professional management involving endocrinologists, paediatric dentists, and orthodontists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07210-w.

Background

The endocrine system plays a crucial role in regulating various bodily functions through the secretion of hormones by its ductless glands [1]. Pediatric endocrine disorders encompass a wide range of conditions that arise due to hormonal imbalances or tissue insensitivity during childhood and adolescence [2]. These disorders include diabetes, disorders of growth and puberty, disorders of thyroid function and sexual development, disorders of the adrenals, calcium/bone metabolism and obesity [3]. Collectively, they contribute significantly to the global disease burden [4]. Notably, certain endocrine disorders can present with oral manifestations, which, if left undiagnosed or inadequately treated, may compromise the long-term well-being and oral health of affected individuals, potentially extending into their adulthood [5].

When hyperpituitarism occurs during the developmental stages of bone and tooth formation in childhood, the excessive activity of pituitary hormones can lead to gigantism and oral manifestations such as macrodontia (enlarged teeth), micrognathia (small jaw), and macroglossia (enlarged tongue) [5]. Conversely, if a patient exhibits small teeth with delayed eruption, a small tongue, stunted growth, and affected facial height, these oral changes may indicate the presence of hypopituitarism (underactive pituitary gland) [6]. Diabetic patients often exhibit multiple oral signs and symptoms, including reduced salivary flow rate, increased susceptibility to dental caries and gingival/periodontal diseases, oral lesions like candidiasis and lichen planus, burning sensation, altered taste perception, geographic and fissured tongue, and impaired wound healing [7]. Among thyroid disorders, hypothyroidism, characterized by decreased serum thyroid hormone levels, is the most commonly encountered condition in the paediatric age group [8]. Investigations have revealed an increased prevalence of dental caries, periodontal disease, enamel defects, macroglossia, anterior open bite, delayed tooth eruption, and delayed exfoliation of primary teeth in these patients [9].

The prevalence of oral manifestations among children with endocrine disorders remains an understudied area, with limited data available. However, a previous study conducted among an adult population of diabetic patients in Nigeria revealed a staggering 78.9% prevalence of periodontal disease in those with uncontrolled diabetes, significantly impacting their quality of life [10]. This finding highlights the urgent need for early detection and management of oral manifestations, particularly in children, to mitigate the potential long-term physical and psychological consequences that can persist into adulthood. Recognizing this gap, the present study aims to determine the prevalent oral manifestations of endocrine disorders among children attending the Paediatric Endocrinology Clinic of the Lagos University Teaching Hospital. By addressing this critical issue, the study seeks to contribute to the understanding and management of oral health in children with endocrine disorders, ultimately improving their overall well-being and quality of life.

Methods

Site and population

An institution based descriptive cross-sectional study was done among children aged 6–18 years attending the paediatric endocrinology clinic of a tertiary health institution in Lagos, Nigeria: Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH), Idi-araba, Lagos, Nigeria.

Sample size determination

To achieve a study power of 90% (Zβ = 1.28), the initial calculation yielded a minimum requirement of 37 participants. To account for a potential 10% attrition rate due to incomplete questionnaires or non-compliance during the clinical examination, the sample size was adjusted to a final total of 50 participants.

Sampling technique

A consecutive sampling method was used to recruit eligible participants that fulfilled the inclusion criteria, until the desired sample size was obtained. This size was considered sufficient to capture the relevant oral manifestations in this specific clinical cohort at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval (ADM/DSCST/HREC/APP/7375) for this study was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of the Lagos University Teaching Hospital on 12th May, 2025, before questionnaires were administered to the respondents.

Data collection and instrument validation

Participants were recruited via consecutive sampling between June and September 2025 at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH). Data were gathered using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire specifically developed for this study (see Appendix I). To ensure content validity, the instrument was reviewed by a multidisciplinary panel of experts, including a paediatric endocrinologist and an oral medicine specialist. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted among 10 paediatric patients (representing 20% of the target sample) to assess the clarity and flow of the questions. The validated tool assessed sociodemographic details, endocrine diagnosis, and duration of illness, pharmacological history, and history of dental visits. A detailed oral soft and hard tissue examination was also done and findings adequately documented in the questionnaire.

Clinical examinations were performed by a single dental officer from the Oral Diagnosis Unit at LUTH. Before the commencement of the study, the examiner underwent rigorous training and calibration under the supervision of specialists in Oral Medicine and Orthodontics. The calibration involved the clinical assessment of 10 paediatric patients, with repeated examinations conducted at a one-week interval to determine consistency.

During the primary study, intra-examiner reliability was maintained by re-examining 10% of the participants after a one-week interval. Reliability was quantified using Cohen’s Kappa (κ) coefficient. The examiner demonstrated substantial to almost perfect agreement, with κ = 0.88 for oral dryness and κ = 0.70 for gingivitis assessment, ensuring high reproducibility of the clinical findings.

Standardized indices were employed to minimize subjectivity and ensure reproducibility of clinical findings. Gingival health assessment was performed using the Loe and Silness Gingival Index. The gingiva was scored on a scale of 0 to 3 based on the severity of inflammation (0 = normal; 1 = mild inflammation; 2 = moderate inflammation; 3 = severe inflammation). For the purpose of analysis, these findings were dichotomized: a score of 0 was categorized as ‘gingivitis absent’, while any score from 1 to 3 was categorized as ‘gingivitis present’. Oral dryness was evaluated objectively using a modified Challacombe Scale. Clinical indicators included dry or sticky mucosa, a fissured tongue, and the “spatula sign” (the adhesion of a wooden tongue depressor to the buccal mucosa or tongue). Oral dryness was recorded as present if these objective clinical signs were observed during the examination. Oral pigmentation was measured using the Dummett-Gupta Oral Pigmentation Index (DOPI). Scores were assigned based on the intensity of pigmentation, ranging from 0 (no pigmentation) to 3 (heavy pigmentation). Results were categorized into ‘absent’ (score of 0) or ‘present’ (scores of 1–3).

The study included children aged 6–18 years with confirmed endocrine disorders whose caregivers provided informed consent and participants provided assent. Children with known syndromic conditions affecting the craniofacial or oral structures were excluded to eliminate confounding variables and ensure that observed manifestations could be more accurately attributed to the primary endocrine pathology.

Data management and analysis

Data were cleaned, coded, and analysed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize sex, caregiver educational level, type of endocrine disorder, and the presence of soft and hard tissue manifestations (e.g., gingivitis, oral dryness, and pigmentation). Age and duration of diagnosis were presented as means and standard deviations (± SD) if normally distributed. The Pearson’s Chi-square test was employed to determine associations between categorical clinical findings (oral dryness, gingivitis, and hard tissue lesions) and sociodemographic or medical characteristics. For analysis involving small cell counts (< 5), Fisher’s Exact Test was applied. Statistical significance for all tests was established at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. Data were presented using frequency tables and bar charts for visual clarity.

Discussion

The prominence of Type 1 DM (56.0%) and the emerging frequency of hypothyroidism (12.0%) within this cohort highlight a shifting epidemiological landscape in paediatric endocrinology across Nigeria. Historically, pancreatic dysfunction has been the most commonly documented endocrine pathology in West African paediatric tertiary centers [1, 3, 11, 12], yet the increasing identification of thyroid disorders signals a dual-pronged evolution in disease presentation. This emerging secondary burden aligns with more recent tertiary-level data from the region [2, 13]. Such trends likely reflect evolving diagnostic vigilance and the increasing availability of hormonal assays in urban centres. Furthermore, the observed male-to-female ratio of 1:1.17 shows strong concordance with data from Northern Nigeria, which reported a ratio of 1:1.5 [14]. This near-parity suggests that, in this demographic, endocrine vulnerability is not heavily skewed by sex-linked factors, but is likely driven by systemic metabolic and autoimmune triggers affecting both genders.

Beyond simple frequency distributions, our analysis revealed a significant association between the type of endocrine abnormality and the prevalence of soft tissue manifestations (p = 0.002). Participants with Type 1 DM and Type 2 DM exhibited the highest frequency of soft tissue involvement. This suggests that the oral mucosal environment is acutely sensitive to current metabolic instability, particularly the glycaemic fluctuations inherent in diabetic pathologies [7]. By contrast, no significant association was observed between the type of endocrine disorder and hard tissue manifestations (p = 0.491). This divergence underscores that while soft tissue health reflects current metabolic status, dental hard tissue anomalies likely stem from a complex interplay of genetic, nutritional, and developmental factors that are not tethered to a single diagnostic classification [15].

Oral dryness was the most prevalent finding (54.0%), likely stemming from the microvascular and neurological sequelae of Type 1 DM. Chronic hyperglycaemia induces the thickening of the basement membrane in salivary gland capillaries alongside autonomic neuropathy, which impairs the stimulus-secretion coupling required for adequate saliva production [7]. In hypothyroid patients, this is compounded by a generalized reduction in metabolic rate, leading to decreased acinar activity and impaired electrolyte transport [8]. Saliva acts as the primary defence for dental hard tissues through its remineralizing potential and acid-neutralizing capacity [16]. Consequently, this salivary deficit removes a vital buffering mechanism, directly predisposing this cohort to the oral ulcers (12.0%) and dental caries (16.0%) identified in our study.

The 14.0% prevalence of gingivitis reflects a critical intersection between endocrine dysregulation and the host immune response. Diabetes-associated hyperglycaemia induces a state of metabolic stress that activates the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End-products (RAGE) pathway [17]. This activation triggers an exaggerated inflammatory cascade, characterized by the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α within the gingival crevicular fluid [10]. This mechanism explains why even paediatric patients with relatively good oral hygiene may still present with persistent gingival inflammation—their systemic state has significantly lowered the threshold for the body’s inflammatory response to dental plaque [18].

Enamel defects (16.0%) underscore the vulnerability of the developing dentition to systemic endocrine insults. Amelogenesis is highly sensitive to calcium and phosphorus homeostasis; consequently, hormonal dysregulation—particularly in thyroid or parathyroid dysfunction—interferes with the enzymatic mineralization of the enamel matrix, leaving permanent structural irregularities [8]. These enamel anomalies often serve as a diagnostic marker for the underlying metabolic comorbidity burden, similar to the pigmentation changes observed in conditions such as Addison’s disease [19].

The concurrent 16.0% prevalence of anterior open bite and crowding suggests that endocrine imbalances during critical growth phases significantly alter craniofacial trajectories. The aetiology of these malocclusions is likely multifactorial, involving the influence of growth and thyroid hormones on craniofacial sutural growth [6] and tooth eruption timing [8]. For instance, hypothyroidism is known to delay dental eruption and promote macroglossia, which alters the equilibrium of the orofacial musculature and leads to vertical growth disturbances like open bites [9]. Furthermore, the 16.0% crossbite prevalence, significantly higher than the 3% reported in similar Indian cohorts [8], may suggest a unique interaction between the severity of endocrine-related growth retardation and the genetic skeletal patterns of West African children.

Ultimately, the clustering of these manifestations, such as the co-occurrence of oral dryness and gingivitis, illustrates a synergistic failure of oral defence mechanisms. Without the cleansing and antimicrobial properties of saliva, the periodontium is rendered more susceptible to inflammatory markers [10]. The presence of oral pigmentation (40.0%), likely linked to ACTH-related melanocyte stimulation [20] in conditions like Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia, further underscores the oral cavity’s role as a clinical mirror for systemic endocrine health.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that children with endocrine disorders attending the Lagos University Teaching Hospital paediatric endocrinology clinic exhibit a substantial burden of oral manifestations. Type 1 DM emerged as the predominant endocrine disorder, affecting 56% of participants who presented with oral dryness followed by hypothyroidism constituting the second most common endocrine condition. These findings advocate for the integration of routine oral health assessments within paediatric endocrinology practice and emphasise the necessity for multidisciplinary collaboration between endocrinologists and dentists. Establishing comprehensive oral health protocols for children with endocrine disorders, including preventive strategies, regular dental surveillance, and timely therapeutic interventions, is essential to mitigate the long-term oral health consequences and improve overall quality of life in this vulnerable population. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to elucidate the bidirectional relationship between endocrine disorders and oral health outcomes, ultimately informing the development of evidence-based, culturally-tailored dental protocols for Nigerian children with endocrine disorders.

Limitations

The following limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design provides only a clinical “snapshot,” precluding the establishment of definitive causal relationships between specific endocrine pathologies and oral manifestations. Second, as a hospital-based study conducted at a single tertiary centre with a relatively small sample size, the findings may have limited generalizability to the broader paediatric population. However, this work serves as an important exploratory foundation for future large-scale, multicentre longitudinal studies.

Additionally, the use of consecutive sampling may have introduced potential selection bias, although this was mitigated by the consistent recruitment of all eligible participants throughout the study period. Finally, children with syndromic conditions were excluded to ensure a homogenous study population and a clearer attribution of oral findings to isolated endocrine disorders. While this sharpened the study’s focus, future research involving syndromic cohorts is necessary to document the full spectrum of manifestations in these complex populations.
