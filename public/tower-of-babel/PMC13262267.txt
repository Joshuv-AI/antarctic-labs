To the Editor:

COPD is characterised by heterogeneous airway inflammation and a variable clinical course [1]. Active airway inflammation can drive progressive structural and functional damage [1]. The concept of disease activity has therefore emerged as a framework to monitor disease progression and stability, and to define attainable therapy targets [2]. However, quantifiable markers of disease activity beyond symptom burden, exacerbations, or lung function decline remain limited, particularly computed tomography (CT) biomarkers of structural airway changes that are applicable across the full spectrum of smoking-related airway disease, including smokers at risk.

Airway wall thickness (AWT) represents a promising structural marker of disease activity in smoking-related airway disease. AWT increases in smokers before the development of emphysema or airway loss, predicts accelerated lung function decline and mortality, and may improve under targeted therapy [3, 4], suggesting that it can capture both disease progression and improvement over time. The expanding implementation of annual low-dose CT for lung cancer screening provides a timely opportunity for repeated structural assessment. However, the absence of an established threshold for clinically relevant change in AWT limits its interpretability. We therefore aimed to derive a clinically relevant change for AWT and to validate its clinical relevance using longitudinal changes in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and health-related quality of life.

This analysis included participants from the HANSE study, a multicentre, population-based lung cancer screening trial conducted by the German Center for Lung Research. The study design has been described previously [5]. We included current and former smokers aged 55–79 years with a smoking history of ≥10 pack-years and available baseline and follow-up CT scans. The study was approved by the local ethics committee and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04913155). All subjects provided written informed consent.

Low-dose chest CT scans were acquired using a mobile 128-slice scanner (SOMATOM go.Top, Siemens Healthineers) across three study centres to ensure standardised image acquisition. Scans were performed during a single inspiratory breath-hold using low-dose parameters with tin prefiltration and iterative reconstruction. Images were reconstructed as 1-mm axial slices using a medium–soft tissue kernel (Br35). Quantitative image analysis was performed using a validated artificial intelligence-based software (AVIEW, Coreline Soft) [6, 7]. AWT was quantified as the standardised square root wall area of airways with a theoretical internal perimeter of 10 mm (Pi10), averaged across the bronchial tree.

Pre-bronchodilator spirometry was performed and quality-controlled according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society recommendations [8]. Airflow obstruction was defined by an FEV1 to forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <0.70. COPD was defined by airflow obstruction. Accordingly, the study cohort comprised both subjects with COPD and smokers at risk without airflow obstruction [7].

Herein, the clinically relevant change for AWT-Pi10 represents the smallest change distinguishing meaningful structural change from technical and biological variability. We applied a two-step approach combining a distribution-based method with clinical validation as we previously described [9]. First, a distribution-based effect size approach was used to estimate the minimal detectable change beyond random error. Effect size was calculated as the annual change in AWT-Pi10 divided by its baseline standard deviation. As effect sizes between 0.30 and 0.50 are commonly used as surrogates for clinically relevant change [10], we used a threshold of 0.40 to approximate the clinically relevant change for changes in AWT-Pi10 [9]. Subjects were then categorised as structural improvement (effect size ≤−0.40), structural worsening (effect size ≥0.40), or structural stability (effect size between −0.39 and 0.39). Second, the clinical relevance of the proposed clinically relevant change in AWT-Pi10 was evaluated by comparing annual changes in FEV1 and disease-specific symptom burden assessed by the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) across AWT-Pi10-defined categories, with CAT analyses restricted to the subset of subjects with available CAT data. Multivariable linear models were used to assess whether clinically relevant change in AWT-Pi10 independently predicted longitudinal changes in FEV1.

This analysis included 4346 subjects with available baseline and follow-up low-dose CT scans (mean±sd age 64.4±6.3 years; 58% male; body mass index 27.1±4.6 kg·m−2; 54% current smokers; 35% with COPD; FEV1 81.9±21.4% predicted; FEV1/FVC 0.71±0.10; smoking exposure 43.1±18.8 pack-years; baseline AWT-Pi10 3.77±0.74 mm; baseline CAT 13.8±6.8).

The median interval between baseline and follow-up CT scans was 12.6 months (interquartile range 11.3–13.0 months). ΔAWT-Pi10 was symmetrically distributed (mean±sd 0.01±0.44 mm). Utilising a baseline standard deviation of 0.74 mm and a predefined effect size threshold of 0.40, the clinically relevant change for AWT-Pi10 was determined to be an absolute change of 0.30 mm, corresponding to an 8% change from baseline. Stratification by this threshold revealed distinct patterns of structural change. Subjects categorised with structural improvement (n=818) demonstrated a mean±sd ΔAWT-Pi10 of −0.60±0.35 mm, with decrease of at least −0.30 mm. Conversely, those with structural worsening (n=848) showed a mean±sd ΔAWT-Pi10 of 0.59±0.30 mm, with an increase of at least 0.30 mm. Subjects with structural stability (n=2680) exhibited ΔAWT-Pi10 changes constrained between −0.30 and 0.30 mm (mean±sd 0.01±0.15 mm).

Further, a sub-stratification of the cohort (n=1522 with COPD versus n=2824 smokers without COPD) confirmed these distinct patterns with remarkable consistency. For instance, structural improvement showed comparable mean ΔAWT-Pi10 (−0.59±0.33 mm in COPD versus −0.61±0.36 mm in smokers without COPD), as did structural stability (0.01±0.15 mm in both cohorts) and worsening (0.59±0.34 mm in COPD versus 0.59±0.28 mm in smokers without COPD).

As shown in figure 1, annual FEV1 change significantly differed across the Pi10 structural categories (p<0.001). Specifically, structural improvement was associated with FEV1 gain, while worsening was associated with FEV1 decline, with the greatest differences in annual FEV1 change observed in subjects with COPD. In a multivariable linear model, ΔAWT-Pi10 was independently associated with annual FEV1 change (β=−0.035 L per year per mm ΔAWT-Pi10, 95% CI −0.043 to −0.027 L per year; p<0.001), after adjusting for age, body mass index, gender, baseline FEV1, smoking status, pack-years and COPD status.

In a subset with known COPD and available CAT data (n=285), CAT scores worsened overall during follow-up (mean±sd ΔCAT +4.8±4.6 points). Subjects with improved AWT-Pi10 showed a significantly lower mean change compared with those with stable AWT-Pi10 (mean difference −1.94 points, 95% CI −3.66 to −0.22 points; p=0.023). The difference between improved and worsened AWT-Pi10 was −2.01 points (95% CI −4.03 to 0.00; p=0.05).

In this population-based study, we define a clinically relevant change in AWT-Pi10 of 0.30 mm, corresponding to an 8% change from baseline. This magnitude is comparable to reproducibility estimates reported in the NELSON lung cancer screening study (n=315; limits of agreement −0.38 to +0.37 mm; ICC=0.94) [11] and exceeds scan–rescan variability reported in an independent cohort using a comparable artificial intelligence-based approach (n=376; limits of agreement ±0.14 mm) [12], indicating that changes of this size are distinguishable from measurement noise. Notably, this magnitude is also comparable to the stepwise differences observed between adjacent GOLD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease) severity grades, supporting its clinical interpretability in the context of COPD disease progression [4].

Across both COPD subjects and smokers without COPD, structural worsening of AWT-Pi10 was associated with mean annual FEV1 declines exceeding 40 mL, a magnitude generally considered clinically relevant [13]. Furthermore, the rate of FEV1 decline observed in smokers without COPD was comparable to that reported in populations with pre-COPD [14]. In contrast, structural improvement was associated with heterogeneous FEV1 responses, with the largest mean increase observed in the COPD subgroup (∼90 mL per year), approximating commonly used anchors for clinically meaningful improvement in COPD [15]. This effect was less pronounced in smokers without COPD, who typically had better baseline lung function and were less likely to receive disease-modifying respiratory treatment, potentially limiting the magnitude of observable improvement. Interpretation of CAT changes is limited by the small proportion of subjects with available data (6% of the cohort) and by selection bias, as CAT assessments were primarily offered to symptomatic patients with diagnosed COPD, probably contributing to the overall worsening observed during follow-up.

Study limitations include the lack of anchoring of clinical relevance to patient-reported outcome measures and potential variability between commercially available quantitative CT software, underscoring the need for further validation in interventional trials and independent cohorts. Furthermore, COPD was defined using pre-bronchodilator spirometry. Although this may overestimate post-bronchodilator obstruction, it misses only a small proportion of cases [16]. However, this study includes a large sample size encompassing both COPD patients and smokers without airflow obstruction, enabling the definition of a clinically relevant structural change as a quantifiable marker of disease activity across the spectrum of smoking-related airway disease.
