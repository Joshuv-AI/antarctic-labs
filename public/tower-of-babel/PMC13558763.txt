How internet use and policy awareness affect village cadres’ pro-environmental behaviors in China: a social cognitive theory perspective
Authors: Yiren Wu, Dan Qiao, Liuyang Yao, Tao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pro-environmental behavior (PEB) of village cadres, who are the primary executives of rural grassroots governance in China, directly influences the effectiveness of rural environmental management. A deeper understanding of the drivers shaping their PEB is therefore crucial for informing interventions that promote the transition to sustainable agriculture. Based on Social Cognitive Theory and utilizing micro-level survey data from 298 village cadres in Hainan Province, this study empirically examines the association between internet use and their PEB. The results demonstrate that: (1) Internet use is significantly positively associated with village cadres’ PEB. (2) Internet use is positively associated with PEB by elevating their ecological policy awareness; furthermore, social networks are associated with a stronger effect of internet use on improving policy awareness. (3) Perceived environmental degradation is found to positively moderate the relationship between internet use and lifestyle-oriented PEB, whereas no significant moderating effect exists for production-oriented PEB. (4) Heterogeneity analysis shows that internet use is significantly positively associated with PEB among agriculture-dominant cadres, small‑scale farmers, and higher‑educated cadres, but not among their counterparts. This study provides important insights for enhancing the environmental governance efficacy of village cadres and promoting sustainable agricultural and rural environmental development.Keywords: Pro-environmental behavior, Internet use, Ecological policy awareness, Village cadres, Social cognitive theorySubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies

Introduction

To advance the objective requirement of promoting harmonious coexistence between humans and nature, the Chinese government has proposed to accelerate the comprehensive green transformation of agricultural development and foster rural ecological revitalization. However, environmental pollution in China’s agriculture and rural areas remains severe, with persistent challenges including low rates of ecologically sound domestic waste treatment, excessive application of chemical fertilizers and pesticides, and serious soil and water contamination1,2. In response, the government has issued a series of relevant documents. For instance, the Battle Against Agricultural and Rural Pollution Action Plan (2021–2025) explicitly calls for continuous improvement in the rural living environment and the prevention and control of agricultural non-point source pollution. Nevertheless, a gap persists between policy design and actual implementation outcomes, partly because farmers, as key agents in implementing environmental policies in rural areas, often exhibit low willingness to participate in environmental governance and possess weak environmental awareness3. Such challenges are widely observed in rural environmental governance globally, making this issue of international relevance4. In this context, village cadres, as the core of grassroots governance, are the key to bridging the “last mile” of policy implementation. Indeed, a 2025 policy directive issued by the Chinese government once again emphasized strengthening rural ecological and environmental governance and underscored the need to continuously enhance the development of township leadership teams and cadre ranks to improve the rural governance system.

Village cadres, who are officials responsible for administering China’s administrative villages and serve as the primary group executing governance functions at the grassroots level, form the foundation and guarantee for implementing the rural revitalization strategy5. Within the relatively close cadre-mass relationship, village cadres, as leaders of rural communities, exert a strong demonstrative and leading effect on villagers’ environmental governance practices6,7. They can effectively mobilize villagers to participate in environmental governance, thereby playing a pivotal role in determining the effectiveness of rural environmental governance8. Therefore, studying village cadres’ pro-environmental behavior (PEB), which is defined as actions that benefit the natural environment while avoiding those that cause harm9, and its influencing factors is of great practical significance for promoting rural ecological governance.

Current research on the factors influencing PEB primarily revolves around the following four domains: The first stream focuses on environmental regulation, examining how policies constrain, guide, and incentivize behavioral change10,11. A second domain concerns environmental cognition, probing the mechanisms through which environmental knowledge is internalized and influences individual behavioral decisions12,13. Third, studies on community interaction analyze the transmission of norms and peer influence within social networks14–16. Finally, a fourth area explores how digital technologies like the internet promote PEB by reshaping the information environment17,18. As a representative digital technology, the internet not only disseminates environmental knowledge to the public but also possesses powerful functions for value guidance and social mobilization19,20, having been shown to significantly enhance people’s environmental sustainability awareness21.

In recent years, with the continuous advancement of China’s Digital Village Construction initiative, the internet and its applications have penetrated rural areas more extensively. However, relevant studies predominantly focus on the PEB of ordinary farmers18,22, implicitly treating them as policy recipients and overlooking the unique nature of village cadres, who combine the roles of government agents, village representatives, and rational actors23. As key actors in grassroots governance, the PEB of village cadres is further shaped by institutional constraints, political incentives, public responsibility attribution, and community networks8,24. Consequently, how internet use specifically affects the PEB of village cadres, distinct from its impact on ordinary farmers, remains an underexplored question. Secondly, regarding underlying mechanisms, existing research indicates that ecological policies and cognitive levels significantly influence farmers’ participation in eco-compensation programs25. Furthermore, social networks can broaden farmers’ information acquisition channels, which in turn is positively associated with their cognitive levels and facilitates the adoption of green technologies26. Additionally, individuals with heightened perception of environmental degradation exhibit greater sensitivity to ecological threats27, which correspondingly increases their willingness to undertake threat-mitigating actions28. Nevertheless, current scholarship has not established a clear theoretical framework explaining how internet use, in conjunction with ecological policy awareness, affects village cadres’ PEB, or the specific roles played by social networks and perceived environmental degradation within this relationship.

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), proposed by Bandura29, centers on “triadic reciprocal determinism,” emphasizing the dynamic, interactive determinism among personal factors, environmental influences, and behavior. From this theoretical perspective, the internet can be viewed as a “symbolic environment,” serving as a key channel for individuals to acquire resources for observational learning30. Ecological policy awareness belongs to the cognitive dimension within “personal factors,” influencing an individual’s expectation judgments regarding behavioral outcomes26. Social networks, as a crucial component of the “social environment,” constitute an important pathway for information dissemination4. Perceived environmental degradation represents an individual’s “cognitive assessment” of the natural environment, which likewise affects behavioral decisions28. Grounded in this theoretical lens, this study focuses on the dominant causal pathway of “how environmental and personal factors influence behavior.” Specifically, it aims to systematically examine the mechanism linking the internet (an environmental factor), ecological policy awareness (a personal factor), and PEB (the behavior). Furthermore, it tests the potential moderating effects of social networks (an environmental factor) and perceived environmental degradation (a personal factor) within this mechanism, thereby seeking to reveal the underlying processes and their intensity. It is important to clarify that the term “environmental factors” in SCT refers to the external context, including physical space, social relations, and symbolic information environments, whereas the term “environment” in “pro‑environmental behavior” refers to the natural ecological environment. These two uses of the term are conceptually distinct.

In summary, utilizing micro-level survey data from village cadres in Hainan Province, China, this study aims to investigate the impact of internet use and ecological policy awareness on their PEB, and to further analyze the mechanistic roles played by social networks and perceived environmental degradation. The marginal contributions of this research are primarily reflected in two aspects: First, it addresses the gap in existing literature regarding the insufficient attention paid to village cadres as key governance actors, thereby providing a novel perspective for understanding rural environmental governance. Second, by applying the triadic reciprocal determinism of SCT, it constructs and empirically tests the potential pathways through which internet use influences PEB, thereby extending the analytical framework for understanding the behavioral decisions of village cadres in the context of digital technology enablement. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of the environmental governance behaviors of grassroots cadres in the digital age and provides a scientific basis for empowering village cadres and promoting sustainable agricultural and rural environmental development, while also offering implications for other rural regions facing similar digital and environmental transitions.

Theoretical background and research hypothesis

Impact of internet use on village cadres’ PEB

Within SCT, the environment encompasses not only physical space but also the symbolic environment. Constructed through semiotic systems such as language, images, and texts, the symbolic environment transmits information, knowledge, values, and behavioral models29,30. As an emerging medium, the internet constitutes a significant extension of this symbolic environment, which transcends the spatiotemporal barriers inherent in traditional knowledge dissemination30,31. Through visualized and localized information delivery, it lowers the cognitive threshold for village cadres to understand environmental issues, thereby enhancing their scientific comprehension and prioritization of these challenges22. Furthermore, as the primary agents responsible for village governance, village cadres possess stronger policy responsiveness and knowledge internalization capabilities compared to ordinary farmers32. This enables them to translate environmental knowledge acquired online into PEB more effectively13. Additionally, SCT posits that individuals can learn by observing and imitating the behaviors of others29. The internet facilitates this process by enabling village cadres to efficiently access successful cases of environmental practices and their visualized benefits from other villages or individuals through real-time, algorithmically curated multimedia platforms. When village cadres witness, through internet use, that peer villages obtain positive feedback such as environmental improvements and economic benefits from specific conservation practices, this observational learning triggers emulation motivation under dual pressures of institutional constraints and socio-communal expectations. Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. Internet use positively influences village cadres’ PEB.

Mediating role of ecological policy awareness

SCT emphasizes that the environment does not directly determine behavior but rather exerts its influence indirectly, primarily by affecting core cognitive processes within the self-regulatory system33. Ecological policy awareness constitutes a key personal cognitive factor linking the internet environment to PEB. It reflects village cadres’ comprehension and internalization of policy objectives, requirements, and potential values34, essentially representing a positive outcome expectation for the behavior. Specifically, proficiency in internet use is positively associated with village cadres’ capacity to access, interpret, and apply digital policy resources. For instance, when cadres engage with policy interpretations or expert lectures disseminated via government mini-programs or short-video platforms, they gain a more accurate grasp of policy goals and core requirements. This process also mitigates cognitive biases arising from the complexity of policy texts35, thereby facilitating the formation of policy-aligned cognition and behavioral intentions. Furthermore, internet platforms can demonstrate policy implementation cases and simulate outcomes through visual representations, enabling a deeper recognition of the ecological and social value of PEB among village cadres. Such value internalization translates into motivational drivers for concrete environmental practices36. Consequently, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. Internet use positively influences village cadres’ PEB by enhancing their ecological policy awareness.

Moderating role of social networks

Bandura30 posits that the social environment is not an abstract concept but rather a constellation of specific structures, including “social networks, community settings, and media symbol systems.” Among these, social networks represent the core structural form through which the social environment exerts its influence. They manifest as the connectivity, relational patterns, and interaction structures among individuals or groups, with their core function lying in facilitating the acquisition, flow, and transformation of informational resources4. Firstly, village cadres with more extensive social networks can identify and access high-quality policy information through exchanges with peers and experts. This information filtering and guidance mechanism is positively associated with the precision of internet use, mitigates cognitive burdens from information overload37, and thereby amplifies the positive effect of internet use on ecological policy awareness. Secondly, in tightly knit village-level networks, standardized policy information obtained online is transformed into localized knowledge through frequent face-to-face interactions and informal deliberative practices. Such localized interpretation mechanisms enable village cadres to accurately contextualize abstract policy provisions within specific local conditions, clarifying implementation boundaries. This not only magnifies the influence of internet use on policy cognition but also elevates its practical guidance value for governance actions38. Consequently, we hypothesize:

H3. Social networks exert a positive moderating effect on the relationship between internet use and village cadres’ ecological policy awareness.

Moderating role of perceived environmental degradation

Perceived environmental degradation constitutes an individual’s subjective assessment of the deterioration of their local ecological environment39. According to SCT, this assessment, serving as environmental feedback, is compared with an individual’s internal values, thereby activating self-regulatory processes and strengthening their behavioral inclination to take corrective actions33. For village cadres, their PEB decisions are influenced not only by their assessment of environmental conditions but also by the imperatives of fulfilling governance responsibilities and meeting policy performance evaluations40. Specifically, heightened perceived environmental degradation generates a concrete awareness of local ecological challenges among village cadres. This cognitive state systematically orients them towards prioritizing environmental information during internet use. Concurrently, their governance obligations compel the integration of such information with practical work needs, thereby amplifying the behavioral impact of internet use. Conversely, when village cadres have a weaker perception of environmental degradation, the lack of contextual relevance means that, even with comparable internet access or exposure to similar information, their motivation to adopt such information is susceptible to suppression by information overload37. This weakened perception diminishes the connection between online information and practical governance needs, ultimately attenuating the positive effect of internet use on PEB. Consequently, we hypothesize:

H4. Perceived environmental degradation positively moderates the effect of internet use on village cadres’ PEB.

Data sources, method, and variables

Data sources

To test the research hypotheses, this study selected Hainan Province in China as the research area for the following reasons: First, the rapid proliferation of the internet in rural China provides a suitable context for observing the behavioral impacts of digital enablement. Second, Hainan Province, designated as a National Ecological Civilization Pilot Zone, bears the significant mission of providing demonstrative models for China’s ecological progress41. This unique strategic positioning makes it an ideal region for conducting this investigation.

As shown in Fig. 2, the data for this study were drawn from two field questionnaire surveys on ecological conservation conducted by the research team in Hainan Province in July 2021 and July 2023, respectively. The surveys utilized largely overlapping instruments capturing all essential variables, with this methodological approach enhancing sample size and representativeness compared to standard cross-sectional designs. Such pooled data yield more precise estimators and statistically powerful tests42. Both surveys employed a combination of stratified and random sampling, with optimal allocation based on population size, regional heterogeneity, and research weights of each county/city. Specifically, 2–4 townships were randomly selected from each county or city, then 2–4 administrative villages were randomly selected from each township, and finally a certain number of village cadres were randomly selected from each administrative village. Data were collected through face-to-face questionnaire interviews. Before the formal survey, the research team conducted a pilot study to optimize the wording and response options, and all interviewers received standardized training to ensure consistency in interviewing methods, question scope, and recording procedures. The final sample encompassed 51 villages across 20 townships in seven jurisdictions. A total of 311 questionnaires were collected from village cadres. After excluding cases with mismatched key information, severe missing values, or outliers, 298 valid responses were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 95.82%. The questionnaire mainly captured cadres‘ demographic and household characteristics, pro‑environmental behavior, ecological policy awareness, social networks, and perceived environmental degradation.

The basic characteristics of the sample are as follows. Among the 298 village cadres, 80.5% were male and 19.5% were female. The age structure was dominated by middle‑aged adults: 39.3% were aged 41–50 years, followed by 22.5% aged 31–40 and 24.5% aged 51–60. In terms of education, 48.3% had completed junior high school, 27.5% had senior high school or technical secondary school education, and 17.1% held a college degree or above, reflecting a higher education level than that of ordinary farmers. Communist Party members accounted for 63.8% of the sample, significantly higher than the proportion among ordinary farmers, highlighting the leading role of the Party in grassroots governance. Ethnically, 58.1% were Han and 41.9% belonged to ethnic minorities, consistent with Hainan’s multi‑ethnic composition. Regarding household production and livelihood, 49.0% of cadres operated small farms (≤ 10 mu), while 23.2% managed large farms (≥ 30 mu), indicating a dual role as both ordinary farmers and production leaders. The majority (77.5%) did not engage in off‑farm work; only 22.5% had supplementary income from off‑farm activities. These characteristics align well with the typical profile of village cadres in rural Hainan, suggesting that the sample is broadly representative of the target population.

Variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable of this study is village cadres‘ pro-environmental behavior. Drawing on established measurement approaches in prior research10,14 and aligned with policy frameworks such as Action Plan for Rural Living Environment Improvement (2021–2025) and the National Agricultural Green Development Plan (14th Five-Year Period), this study measured PEB using six behavioral indicators across two dimensions (lifestyle-oriented and production-oriented), as presented in Table 1. Lifestyle-oriented PEB reflects cadres’ daily environmental habits and their practice of environmental concepts at the household and community levels, while production-oriented PEB reflects the green transition of agricultural production methods and is closely related to the control of rural non-point source pollution. Based on the cadres‘ responses, the total number of implemented PEBs was used as the primary measure of PEB intensity, ranging from 0 to 6; the sub‑indices for lifestyle-oriented and production-oriented PEB each range from 0 to 3.

Independent variable

The core independent variable of this study is village cadres’ internet use. Communication engagement captures the capacity for online information exchange and discussion, which helps deepen environmental knowledge through interaction and enhances behavioral intentions via social interaction. In addition, content creation represents a higher level of internet use, more comprehensively reflecting an individual’s active participation and expression online.

Moderating variables

The moderating variables of this study include social networks and perceived environmental degradation.

Social networks were quantified across three dimensions (network size, interaction frequency, and resource investment), following previous studies26,45.These dimensions were measured using three items: “number of contacts in the mobile phone”, “frequency of contact with relatives and friends”, and “annual ceremonial expenditure”. The entropy method was used to compute weights and generate a composite index ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating more extensive social networks. Network size reflects the breadth of information channels. According to Granovetter’s weak ties theory, a larger network size implies more weak ties connecting different circles, which increases access to heterogeneous information and the likelihood of filtering high-quality information46. Interaction frequency captures the density of interactions. Frequent interaction creates opportunities for discussion and refinement, enabling macro-level policies to be repeatedly adapted to the local context and eventually transformed into locally actionable knowledge38. Resource investment reflects relationship strength and embeddedness. Ceremonial expenditure is an important means by which households actively build social networks; higher expenditure indicates deeper embeddedness, which enhances the trust and persuasiveness of information transmitted through the network26.

Perceived environmental degradation was measured following the framework of Alfonso et al.39 across three dimensions: forest degradation, biodiversity loss, and water pollution, given the rich forest resources and diverse flora and fauna in Hainan Province. Specific survey items are presented in Table 3. The entropy method was applied to assign weights and generate a composite index ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a higher perception of environmental degradation. Forest degradation reflects direct changes in ecosystem structure and is the most intuitive environmental signal. Biodiversity loss indicates functional changes in the ecosystem, as a decline in species often signals environmental deterioration. Water pollution reflects the direct impact of environmental degradation on residents‘ daily lives and is one of the most easily perceived environmental risks.

Time fixed effects are incorporated to capture potential temporal influences. Specific variable descriptions are provided in Table 4.

Regarding control variables, the results reveal distinct domain-specific influences on PEB. Overall, education attainment, health status, household labor supply, cultivated land area, and agricultural insurance purchase significantly affect environmental practices. However, their impacts diverge markedly across behavioral domains when analyzed separately. Notably, education emerges as the sole factor exerting statistically significant positive effects on both lifestyle- and production-oriented behaviors. Enhanced educational attainment correlates with improved environmental knowledge and policy comprehension, enabling cadres to better recognize the multifaceted benefits of diverse conservation actions. In contrast, health status and labor availability predominantly influence lifestyle behaviors. Healthier cadres demonstrate stronger preferences for healthy living practices, while abundant household labor frees up temporal resources, facilitating daily PEB49. Within the production domain, economic considerations prove particularly influential: household income directly determines capacity for adopting green agricultural technologies; larger farm operators exhibit heightened caution toward eco-technologies due to amplified exposure to yield volatility and economic risks50; while agricultural insurance mitigates production uncertainties by alleviating concerns about conservation technology returns, thereby actively promoting sustainable production behaviors50. These interconnected factors collectively shape the economic calculus underlying agricultural environmental decisions.

Robustness and endogeneity checks

Robustness checks

The robustness of our findings was verified by employing two distinct approaches: replacing the core explanatory variable and altering the estimation model, with the corresponding results detailed in Table 6. First, we replaced the multidimensional “internet use” index with the unidimensional construct “digital information acquisition”, measured by the binary item “Do you obtain information by mobile devices or computers?” This substitution evaluated the sensitivity of findings to alternative variable operationalizations. Results demonstrated directional consistency and unchanged statistical significance for PEB. Second, Poisson regression was employed as an alternative estimator to the baseline ordered probit model, testing specification robustness under different distributional assumptions. The Poisson estimates retained identical significance levels and coefficient signs across all behavioral dimensions, collectively indicating robust core findings.

Endogeneity checks

To mitigate the potential endogeneity problem of sample selection bias caused by unobserved factors, this study employs a Heckman two-stage selection model51. In the first stage, a probit model estimates the probability that an individual belongs to the high internet use group (mastering at least two skills), and the inverse Mills ratio (IMR) is calculated. In the second stage, the IMR is included as a control variable in the outcome equation to correct for sample selection bias.

The Heckman model requires a valid exclusion restriction variable. This study selects “per capita household telephone and internet expenditure” as the exclusion restriction variable for the following reasons. First, this variable reflects household investment in communication, directly affecting internet access and usage intention, thus satisfying the relevance requirement. Second, this variable pertains to household consumption habits, which belong to a different behavioral domain from village cadres’ environmental awareness and pro-environmental behavior decisions, thus satisfying the exogeneity requirement.

As shown in Table 7, the first-stage estimation results show that the exclusion restriction variable is significantly positive at the 1% level, confirming its validity. In the second stage, after controlling for sample selection bias, the effect of internet use on village cadres’ pro-environmental behavior remains significantly positive at the 5% level, and the IMR is not significant. This indicates that there is no serious sample selection bias in this study, that the baseline regression results are reliable, and that the core conclusions remain robust after correcting for endogeneity.

Mediating effect

To elucidate the mediating mechanism of ecological policy awareness in the relationship between internet use and PEB, we conducted formal mediation tests with bootstrap validation (Tables 8 and 9). Model 1 confirms that internet use is significantly positively associated with ecological policy awareness at the 1% level. When both internet use and policy awareness are incorporated in Model 2, internet use maintains a significant direct effect on composite PEB, while policy awareness functions as a partial mediator. Critically, the indirect mediation pathway accounts for 19.51% of the total effect, supporting H2. For behavioral subtypes, policy awareness mediates 14.29% of the effect on lifestyle-oriented behaviors (Model 3) and 30.10% on production-oriented behaviors (Model 4). Bootstrap sampling (2,000 replicates) validates the statistical significance of all indirect effects, with 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals excluding zero (Table 9). These findings are consistent with the interpretation that ecological policy awareness serves as a mediator, although the mediation is full for production-oriented PEB and partial for the other types.

Heterogeneity analysis

To examine whether the association between internet use and PEB varies across village cadres with different socioeconomic characteristics, we conducted split‑sample analyses based on three dimensions: livelihood diversification, farm size, and education level. In terms of livelihood structure, village cadres who take agricultural production as their main source of income and do not engage in additional off-farm work or business are categorized as the agriculture-dominant group. By contrast, those who gain extra income from off-farm employment or business are classified as the diversified livelihood group. For farm size, using the sample mean of cultivated land (25 mu) as the cutoff, cadres were divided into small‑scale (< 25 mu) and large‑scale (≥ 25 mu) groups. For education level, based on China’s nine‑year compulsory education policy, cadres with less than nine years of schooling were categorized as low‑education, and those with nine or more years as high‑education.

The estimation results are presented in Table 12. For livelihood diversification, internet use was significantly positively associated with PEB among agriculture-dominant cadres, but no significant association was found for diversified livelihood cadres. For farm size, a significant positive association was observed for small‑scale farmers, whereas the coefficient for large‑scale farmers was not significant. For education level, internet use was significantly positively associated with PEB among high‑education cadres, while no significant association was found for low‑education cadres.

Discussion

Drawing on SCT, the findings of this study reveal the complex mechanisms through which internet use influences the PEB of village cadres. Firstly, as a crucial environmental factor, internet use provides village cadres with rich channels for observational learning, and is positively associated with their PEB. This finding, viewed through the lens of SCT, builds upon previous research on digital technology enablement19,22,55. Furthermore, this study reveals that village cadres, as grassroots policy implementers, possess stronger motivational drivers and policy responsiveness56. This enables them to more effectively translate environmental knowledge acquired via the internet into environmental governance practices. This finding contrasts with the conclusion by Na & Kang55, who found that mere internet access did not necessarily promote farmers’ PEB, thereby highlighting the importance of studying specific governance actors.

Secondly, the mechanism tests indicate that internet use is positively correlated with village cadres’ PEB by enhancing their ecological policy awareness. This pathway aligns with the “cognitive mediation” mechanism of SCT, suggesting that the internet is consistent with behavioral change by altering individual cognition. Specifically, by providing visualized and easily understandable ecological policy information, the internet helps village cadres construct positive outcome expectations regarding the results of PEB. These positive outcome expectations form the core motivation for their PEB, a conclusion consistent with the research of Zhu et al.25. Additionally, while the internet offers vast amounts of information, it can also induce cognitive load37. The “social environment” constituted by strong social networks amplifies the utility of the internet as a “symbolic environment” through information filtering, localized interpretation, and normative support. This validates the perspective emphasized by Pratiwi and Juerges35,57 regarding the necessity of contextualizing policy implementation within local settings.

Third, this study finds that perceived environmental degradation positively moderates the effect of internet use on lifestyle-oriented PEB. This result is consistent with the conclusion of Soares et al.13 that environmental perception influences daily actions such as plastic pollution reduction. The characteristics of lifestyle-oriented PEB, namely operational simplicity and low cost, make its positive outcome expectations more readily activated and reinforced under a sense of environmental threat. Conversely, production-oriented PEB involves complex agricultural decision-making alongside high transition costs and risks. Consequently, its behavioral decisions remain predominantly governed by economic rationality, where high discount rates incentivize prioritizing short-term economic gains over long-term ecological benefits54. The synthesis by Kollmuss and Agyeman40 also elucidates this disparity, noting the existence of complex mediating barriers, including economic costs and social norms, between environmental perception and concrete action, providing a theoretical lens for understanding the differential results observed for lifestyle-oriented versus production-oriented PEB in this study.

Fourth, the observed heterogeneity patterns may be tentatively explained by differences in attention allocation, transition costs, and information processing capacity. Agriculture-dominant cadres, whose primary livelihood depends on farming and village governance, likely focus more on agricultural environmental issues, which may facilitate the translation of online information into action. In contrast, diversified livelihood cadres allocate attention across multiple economic activities, potentially limiting their cognitive resources for environmental engagement. Similarly, small‑scale farmers face lower transition costs and production risks when adopting green practices, making them more responsive to internet‑based environmental knowledge, whereas large‑scale operators may prioritize short‑term yield stability due to higher discount rates and risk aversion54. Furthermore, higher education enhances information processing skills, enabling cadres to better filter and internalize online policy information, while lower‑educated cadres may struggle with information overload37.

This research has several limitations. First, although causal pathways were proposed based on the SCT framework, the cross-sectional data cannot completely circumvent endogeneity concerns. The findings of this study should be more accurately interpreted as indicating “associations” rather than definitive “causal relationships.” Future research could employ panel data or quasi-experimental designs to further validate causal inferences. Second, as the sample is drawn from Hainan Province, which holds a unique ecological strategic position, future studies should utilize cross-regional assessments to test the generalizability of the conclusions. Finally, how institutional factors like government credibility interact with the digital environment to influence the cognitive processes of village cadres represents a valuable direction for future in-depth investigation.

Conclusions

Grounded in SCT and utilizing micro-level survey data from village cadres in Hainan Province, this study examined the association between internet use and PEB and its underlying mechanisms. The main conclusions are as follows: First, internet use is significantly positively associated with village cadres’ PEB. Second, internet use is positively associated with PEB primarily by elevating ecological policy awareness, and social networks are associated with a stronger positive effect of internet use on this awareness. Third, perceived environmental degradation significantly strengthens the positive relationship between internet use and lifestyle-oriented PEB, but its moderating effect on production-oriented PEB is not significant. Fourth, heterogeneity analysis shows that internet use is significantly positively associated with PEB among agriculture-dominant, small‑scale, and higher‑educated cadres, but not among diversified livelihood, large‑scale, or low‑educated cadres.

Based on the empirical findings, this study proposes the following actionable policy recommendations: First, implement targeted digital skills training programs for village cadres, focusing on enhancing their capabilities in information retrieval, policy interpretation, and case-based learning. Develop dedicated digital policy dissemination platforms for agriculture, rural areas, and farmers, presenting ecological policies and successful practices through visualized and localized methods to provide cadres with more efficient and intuitive learning materials. Special attention should be paid to less‑educated cadres, for whom more concise and intuitive online information delivery should be designed to effectively reduce the digital usage gap caused by differences in education level.

Second, optimize policy communication and value conveyance mechanisms to strengthen the mediating role of ecological policy awareness. The study confirms that ecological policy awareness is a key mediating variable through which internet use influences pro‑environmental behavior, and this effect is more prominent for production‑oriented behavior. In ecological policy campaigns, it is recommended not only to convey the content of policy texts but also to emphasize the comprehensive benefits of policy implementation, including ecological, economic, and social benefits, thereby helping cadres form clear positive outcome expectations. Visual methods such as short videos, case studies, and policy infographics can be used to transform abstract policies into easily understandable and acceptable value consensus, facilitating the transition from policy “awareness” to “internalization”. For large‑scale farmers, who are more sensitive to economic returns, highlighting the long‑term profitability and risk reduction associated with green production may be particularly effective in shaping positive outcome expectations.

Third, leverage the supportive effects of social networks and build an integrated online‑offline learning community. Moderation analysis shows that social networks positively moderate the effect of internet use on cadres’ ecological policy awareness, effectively enhancing the information transformation efficiency of internet use. It is recommended to encourage the establishment of online communities and offline discussion groups focused on environmental governance at the village level, facilitating experience exchange and knowledge sharing among cadres. By constructing a dual support system of “internet + social networks”, the breadth of internet information dissemination can be combined with the localized filtering and in‑depth interpretation enabled by social networks, helping cadres deepen policy understanding and form behavioral consensus through interaction. For diversified livelihood cadres, who may have limited time and attention for environmental issues, well‑structured social networks can serve as an efficient information filter, delivering concise and actionable policy insights and reducing the cognitive burden of self‑directed online searching.

Fourth, strengthen the guiding role of perceived environmental degradation and adopt tailored strategies to improve the efficiency of behavior transformation. This study finds that perceived environmental degradation does not significantly moderate production‑oriented pro‑environmental behavior. Accordingly, in areas with prominent ecological and environmental problems, internet platforms should be used to push localized environmental quality data, visualized simulations of pollution consequences, and successful ecological restoration cases, thereby enhancing cadres’ sense of urgency and responsibility regarding environmental issues.
