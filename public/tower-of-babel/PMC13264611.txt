Health promotion and options for digital health interventions on board merchant vessels
Authors: Lorenz Scheit, Dorothee Dengler, Lukas Belz, Chiara Reck, Volker Harth, Marcus Oldenburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth promotion measures on board seagoing vessels are subject to special requirements due to limited access to health services on board. This study examines the needs and digital possibilities for modern health promotion and digital intervention on board merchant ships considering the different occupational groups. The data was collected using questionnaires completed by 903 seafarers (participation rate 99.4%) from 23 countries on 68 seagoing vessels belonging to two German shipping companies. A comparison of the responses was conducted to identify significant differences between ratings and officers, because this could be relevant for future specific interventions. Adjustments were made for cultural background and age using odds ratio. Ratings assessed the relevance of topics for well-being higher than officers did, especially regarding healthy food (p = 0.83), exercise (p = 0.05), and learning relaxation exercises (p = 0.04). The study shows that ratings are more than twice willing to do more sports (p = 0.04), learn relaxation exercises (p = 0.01), and practice measures against fatigue (p = 0.05). Health apps and sports competitions were highly valued in the communication of health information. Ratings and officers used smartphones to a high degree (98.3%) and would use a health app (87.5% ratings vs. 83.1% officers; OR 1.62 (1.07–2.43)) and both professional groups would like to participate in a bonus system based on a reward principle. In the multivariate analysis, interest in health promotion measures was essentially independent of age and cultural background. Key aspects for intervention measures on board include sports, learning relaxation techniques, offering healthy food, and raising awareness about exemplary health behavior of superiors. Modern digital intervention measures involving e-learning platforms, applications and wearables are of interest to seafarers and should be considered when planning health promotion measures at sea.Keywords: Seafarer, Digital health, Digital health promotion, Health promotion, Health prevention, Health managementSubject terms: Health care, Health humanities

Introduction

Health promotion, like positive influence factors for well-being, willingness to change health behavior, is defined as the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health1. Improving health promotion for seafarers on board merchant ships is a high priority in maritime medicine, especially since the maritime working environment, with its wide range of potential health risks, places high demands on health prevention and care2. Another factor in improving health promotion on board is to make the profession more attractive, as analyses show that the existing shortage of skilled workers will continue to increase in the future. Working on board far away from land-based medical facilities makes it difficult to access medical and health-promoting counseling services A metanalysis by Shi et al. (2024) showed a positive effect of eHealth-based interventions to manage lifestyle risk behaviors3. Arslan et al. (2024) investigated the potential of digital health prevention using apps and concluded that they are an effective approach to raise awareness on health-related issues on board, especially since the seafarers in their study demonstrated a high level of mobile and IT competence4,5.

Health promotion in the context of maritime medicine is conceptualized and defined by measures such as setting up sports and recreation areas—often equipped with TVs and video game consoles—posting informational flyers on health topics or distributing them through supervisors, and organizing sports events on board.

A large number of studies point to the various challenges facing medical care and health promotion on the high seas2,6,7. Other factors that can hinder health promotion on board besides limited internet access, include differences in culture/socio-economic, educational status and professional qualification, as well as varying levels of motivation.

Today, it is common to encounter multicultural crews with a high proportion of Asian seafarers on board. According to the BIMCO Seafarers Report 2021, ratings from Indonesia, China, India, Russia, and the Philippines account for 49.3% of the world merchant fleet8. In a systematic review by Al Shamsi et al. in 2020, language barriers in particular are described as a negative factor impairing healthcare9. Regarding educational differences it can be assumed that seafarers from different cultures also have different prior knowledge and needs when it comes to health promotion services. Regarding the relevance of culturally sensitive health promotion measures for seafarers, Zyriax et al. (2018) described significant differences in the dietary habits of Kiribati seafarers compared to European seafarers10. Another survey from 2020 indicates that the proportion of health app users in China and India was over 60%, but no higher than 35% in Germany and Switzerland11. The increasing number of users of medical services via internet platforms in Asia was already evident in China in 2020, when the user rate rose by a factor of 20 to over 300 million users as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic12.

In addition, it is widely acknowledged that age-related differences can also influence the efficacy of health promotion measures. Studies by Paradis et al. (2022) indicate that 80% of people under the age of 24 used mobile health (mHealth) apps, whereas only 20% of people over the age of 60 used an mHealth app13. On the other hand, Jokisch et al. (2022) describe a high acceptance of digital health service among older people14. Further target group differences for health promotion measures could exist among the various occupational groups of ratings and officers. Sociocultural background, education and qualifications, as well as areas of responsibility and scope of duties, are some of the aspects in which the occupational groups differ. Several studies indicate that a higher level of education is often associated with better health status15,16.

The above examples show that the factors influencing the acceptance of health promotion measures and interventions are generally diverse. Therefore, when developing contemporary health promotion programs for seafarers, it is necessary to take into consideration differences in culture, age, and rank.

In order to implement targeted and needs-oriented health promotion in merchant shipping in a contemporary manner, it is necessary to conduct a precise analysis of relevant health issues among seafarers and to understand how best to apply this knowledge on board. Jespen et al. (2015) showed that fatigue on board can be a relevant safety risk and recommended the introduction of preventive measures, including tools for predicting fatigue and the establishment of individual management systems to reduce fatigue5. Similarly, Oldenburg et al. (2019) concluded that stress and strain among seafarers could be reduced by developing tailored health promotion programs on board, which could lead to professionally adapted sports programs17. The integration of modern and established digital media for health-promoting interventions can make a valuable contribution in this context. Particularly, digital health promotion offerings or competitive programs could potentially lead to greater acceptance among users.

The aim of this study is therefore to identify seafarers’ needs and possibilities for health promotion, as well as the digital intervention on board merchant ships, considering relevant influencing factors such as the occupational group.

Methods

Study design and protocol

This study is a single-center, cross-sectional quantitative and qualitative study. The applied questionnaire survey was part of the EU-funded e-healthy ship study18,19.

Ethical considerations

This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hamburg Medical Association (registration number PV7174). The study adheres to the ethical guidelines according to the Declaration of Helsinki20.

Participants

In the survey, conducted from May to June 2019, 908 seafarers from two Hamburg shipping companies were interviewed, of whom a total of 903 participated (participation rate 99.4%). The participants were recruited from 68 ships. Shipping company 1, with 875 study participants (group 1), had a fleet of 307 ships, and shipping company 2, with 33 study participants (group 2), had a fleet of 5 ships. In total, women accounted for 0.8% of this study´s participants; this reflects the typical male-dominated sex distribution among seafarers and must be considered when interpreting results of maritime studies21. The study group was divided into 371 officers (nautical and technical officers/electricians) and 532 ratings (engine and deck ratings, galley personnel, and cadets). Officers are defined as personnel in leadership positions who hold a government-recognized license and have command responsibilities on board a ship, while ratings are defined as support personnel who do not hold an officer’s rank. This division was made because officers and ratings differ in terms of their areas of responsibility, training, and sociocultural background. These distinctions could contribute to different approaches to the content of potential shipboard interventions. Socioeconomic status was not directly assessed in this study. However, occupational rank (officers vs. ratings) was considered a proxy for socioeconomic differences, as it reflects variations in education, income, and job responsibilities within the seafaring population. This approach has been used in previous maritime health research22.

In addition to the comparison between rank groups, an exploratory comparison between cultural groups (Europeans vs. non-Europeans) was performed. Voluntary participation in the survey was carried out using a data protection-compliant online evaluation tool.

The survey was conducted anonymously, and all respondents were informed in advance about the content and objectives of the study via flyers.

Procedure

The questionnaire was developed on an interdisciplinary approach by six experts from the fields of maritime psychology, maritime medicine, occupational medicine and nautical science who also had professional expertise in the field of health promotion. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The survey was conducted in English and the processing time was approximately 25 min.

The data was collected anonymously and entered into an Excel spreadsheet by researcher of the Institute for Occupational and Maritime Medicine.

Survey instrument

The questionnaire on “Health Promotion for Seafarers and Health Management on Board” was a survey instrument consisting of 40 qualitative and quantitative questions spread over 4 pages of an electronic document, which was emailed to the head of study after being completed.

The following items are rated using a four-point Likert scale 1–4 (strongly disagree – strongly agree): eat healthy food, do sports, learning relaxation techniques, it is likely to follow superiors. The question regarding “interest in fitness tracker” was rated on a 6-point Likert scale (from 1 (not at all interesting) to 6 (very interesting)), as explained in the footnotes. The question “suggestion to improve health on board” was analyzed using qualitative data collection methods involving open-ended questions, followed by content analysis (categorization/clustering).

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using SPSS version 27 with descriptive statistics, differentiating between the occupational groups of ratings and officers. To quantify the magnitude of the differences between ratings and officers, we used Odds Ratios (OR) as the primary effect estimator, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals to indicate precision. For the multivariate analysis, adjusted odds ratios were calculated to estimate the independent effect of the occupational rank while controlling for age and cultural background. In addition, comparative analyses were performed using Pearson Chi-squared tests and the calculation of odds ratios (OR) using binomial logistic regression and confidence interval measurements (CI 95%) to examine associations between occupational group and health-related behaviors while adjusting for confounders. Confounders were identified based on theoretical considerations and prior literature on occupational health in seafarers23.

Age and cultural background were considered confounders, as both are plausibly associated with occupational group and independently influence health behaviors. These variables may introduce backdoor paths if not controlled for and were therefore included as covariates in the analyses. Variables that were assumed to influence both exposure and outcome, but not lie on the causal pathway, were included as confounders. Accordingly, calculations of odds ratios were adjusted for age and cultural background.

Results

Demographics

99.2% of the study participants were male. The median age was 38 years (min. 19 years – max. 66 years). There were no significant age differences between officers and ratings or between Europeans and non-Europeans. The median number of years spent at sea was 12.

The ratings consisted of 95.3% non-European seafarers, while the officer occupational group included 66.2% seafarers of European nationality. Of the 882 seafarers who provided information on their current ship type, 71.7% were employed on container ships, 4.2% on tankers, and 24.1% on bulk carriers, with no relative difference between the occupational groups. Health promotion content

In terms of health behavior, healthy food, sport exercise, learning relaxation techniques, and setting a good example are very important for both, ratings and officers to feel good (at least a score of 3.46 on a scale of 1 to 4). Healthy food played the most important role, followed by sporting activities, learning relaxation techniques, and the influence of exemplary health behavior. All factors influencing well-being were more important for ratings than for officers.

After adjusting for age and cultural group, it was found that healthy food was significantly more important for Europeans than for non-Europeans. Recommendations for improving healthcare on board

The question of whether seafarers were aware of health apps, movies, e-books, or tutorials that are helpful for healthcare was answered in the affirmative by 156 (37.1%) of the 420 seafarers who responded. Forty-nine (11.7%) of the seafarers most frequently mentioned fitness/workout apps (e.g., “Samsung Health App” or “Shaun T Insanity Workout”), followed by medical advice websites (e.g., “e-doctor”) or internet media for fitness advice (e.g., “YouTube”).

The qualitative responses to the open questions regarding improvements to health on board were grouped into five categories and one category “others” if the response couldn´t be categorized (no recommendation was an additional separate count). The analysis was conducted by categorizing the content by topic and evaluated according to frequency (Fig. 1). The most frequently mentioned issues were more sports activities/better fitness equipment (30.3%), followed by healthier nutrition, organizational optimization, and improvements to internet access and sleep quality. Organizational optimization included, for example, improvements in staffing, better working time models, training and qualification of cooks, better pay, more free time, better contract terms, and shorter periods of service at sea. Multiple answers were possible.

Suggestions to improve health on board.

The question regarding interest in a bonus system as an external reward incentive for learning about health issues was answered by 836 seafarers, of whom 761 (83.8%) were interested in a bonus system.

Discussion

Health promotion content

The evaluation of seafarers’ needs for promoting well-being and health revealed that healthy food, physical activity, learning relaxation techniques, and countermeasures against fatigue are highly valued. This confirms the results of preliminary studies, according to which seafarers expressed a high level of interest in the aforementioned health topics in the context of health promotion6.

Interestingly, after cultural adjustment, ratings rate healthy food as significantly more important than officers. With 64% of the total sample being of Asian origin, this could indicate a cultural influence. Research from the e-healthy ship study has already shown that fat consumption among European seafarers is higher than among crews of Asian descent24. Furthermore, Schwingshackl et al. (2024) showed that Asian dietary patterns with a high proportion of fish, vegetables, soy products, and little red meat are associated with a 16–22% lower cardiovascular risk rate, which may indicate a healthier diet among Asian individuals25.

The greater affinity for sports among ratings is surprising, as their physical job requirements are generally much more demanding than those of officers, and one would therefore expect them to have less interest in sporting activities. A survey of 323 seafarers on 23 container ships showed that the greatest physical strain was experienced by the ratings, with an energy consumption of 801 kcal per day for deck ratings and 777 kcal for engine room personnel, compared to 568 kcal per day for nautical officers17. Another contrary aspect has been described in literature concerning the relationship between socioeconomic status and participation in physical activities and sport. Here, individuals with higher socioeconomic status tend to show a greater willingness to participate in sporting activities26,27. Possible reasons for the increased interest in sports among ratings in this study could be that, for cultural reasons, the communal experience of sports creates interpersonal connections and is highly valued in socio-centered groups of people with lower socioeconomic status. This shows that typical onboard team sports (such as soccer or basketball) are more frequently practiced by people with lower socioeconomic status26.

The greater interest in learning relaxation techniques among ratings may be related to the higher proportion of people of Asian cultural descent in this occupational group. Traditionally, relaxation techniques and philosophical approaches (such as Tai Chi and Qigong in China, Yoga in India, and Ikigai in Japan) are also widely used for enhancing relaxation and reducing stress. Studies show that relaxation techniques such as Yoga practices, including pranayama and meditation, are practiced by 16.9% of adults in India28, and a study by the Pew Research Center India indicates that 22% of the Indian population practices Yoga once a month; a relevant proportion of this is likely to be the female population29. A study by Cagas et al. found that women practiced yoga significantly more often than men. The results showed that women were more likely to be motivated to practice yoga by positive emotions, fitness, and stress management, while men were more likely to be motivated by additional activities and competition. In comparison, a study of US study participants showed that only 19% practice meditation30. According to a non-scientifically validated survey, only 15% of the European population practices Yoga31.

Factors influencing health behavior

In addition to these health issues, the role model function of superiors in health behavior seems to play a significant role, especially for ratings. In 2004, Bandura reported on the positive effect of role models on health-promoting behavior, a finding that is supported by the social cognitive theory32. Another study shows that health-promoting behavior by physiotherapists is perceived as exemplary by their patients33.

The professional group of ratings is disproportionately recruited from seafarers of Asian origin. Obedient and compliant behavior has a special significance in Asian collectivist cultures as an expression of respect for elders, adherence to norms, and obedience to authority34,35. This connection promotes orientation toward role models among superiors. Conversely, the theory of the “privilege of power” by Keltner et al. (2003) shows that superiors (officers) more often do not follow rules and guidelines due to their position of power and self-perceived immunity, so that the effect of role modeling seems to be less significant here36.

These factors influencing role modeling should not be ignored in health promotion and should therefore be included in information campaigns or health interventions. In addition, these findings should also be integrated into the training of prospective nautical officers in intercultural leadership37.

Concerning the educational background, the influence of education on the survey was not explicit asked; however, the authors assume a positive correlation between rank and education, since, given the academic training pathway for officers, rank can likely be interpreted as a surrogate parameter for a higher level of education. In addition to behavioral measures to improve health promotion, structural preventive measures are also highly relevant. This study focusses primarily on behavioral measures.

Willingness to change health behavior

Motivation to change and personal commitment are key drivers for adopting health-conscious behavior38. Without individual willingness to change, improvement concepts in health promotion cannot be successfully implemented. In this survey, there was a high overall willingness to adopt health-promoting behaviors, which was significantly more frequently reported among ratings than among officers in the areas of sports, learning relaxation exercises, and countermeasures against fatigue. The willingness to change health behavior had been assessed without linking this change to an incentive. Previously unpublished data from the authors’ own research confirms an above-average interest in the mentioned health-promoting measures among ratings and officers. The results of Arslan et al. 2024 demonstrate the frequent use of health apps as mHealth applications among seafarers. Prioritized topics were activity tracking (74.8%), weight loss (41.8%), and sleep tracking (41.0%). This underscores the interest in physical activity and sleep improvement on board5.

The fact that both professional groups show a high level of interest in changing health behaviors provides a strong foundation for potential interventions. A meta-analysis by Ntoumanis et al. (2021) showed that an increase in intrinsic motivation is often associated with a positive change in health behaviors. Furthermore, a New Zealand study reported that up to 60% of the adult rural population was interested in health-promoting “lifestyle changes” related to fitness, weight loss, and stress reduction39,40.

The greater willingness of ratings to change could be due to the fact that, although occupational groups with higher socioeconomic status often have greater health literacy, ratings are more frequently affected by health problems such as back pain41,42. A lower level of knowledge about health promotion could lead to a higher interest and a greater willingness to change behavior in ratings for this very reason. According to Prochaska’s transtheoretical model (1997), the apparent willingness to change behavior initially shows a stage of intention with high motivation. However, structural support is necessary to achieve a sustainable change in health behavior43, which is primarily the responsibility by each person, but would be helpful if supported by the officers on board. It can therefore be assumed, that the willingness of the crews to change their health behavior largely depends on the leadership culture of their superiors.

Access to health information

Electronic media are contemporary and widely used in today’s society for communicating health information. An analysis of the preferred media (tablet, app, DVD, screen, etc.) for communicating information facilitates needs-based selection for subsequent intervention implementation on board. Overall, it was found that both ratings and officers showed a high level of interest in electronic and non-electronic media for conveying information on health topics.

The above-average interest in health information (score 3–4 out of 4) via analog and electronic media among the study participants in this survey indicates a high level of health awareness among seafarers. The greatest interest in accessing health information in the study group was the free download of a health app. Arslan et al. (2023) already showed that, with 52.5% of health app downloaders, there is a high level of interest in the use of health apps among seafarers. Overall, the use of apps seems to be contemporary, practical, and attractive.

Ratings significantly preferred (1.6 times more often) “TV screens in mess rooms” for information transfer than officers. It is possible that officers are already saturated with this medium due to their frequent work on monitors and therefore show less interest. However, officers also have greater access to their own internet connection in their cabins, so there is probably no increased need for information flow during mealtimes. Future health interventions should use apps and digital media in addition to conventional methods.

E-health equipment and reasons for use

98.3% of the seafarers surveyed used a smartphone, it’s the only connection to family and friends. Compared to the frequency of smartphone use among people over the age of 16 in Germany (approx. 82% according to a survey by the Association of German Engineers), this is above average. This results in an overall high digital affinity for modern information technology among seafarers, as already described in the study by Arslan et al. (2023), where 98.4% of seafarer used a smartphone5.

Both professional groups also showed a high level of interest in fitness trackers and health apps. 37% of seafarers were familiar with health apps and rated them positively. Compared to users on land, this seems rather low, as a 2023 survey by the German digital association Bitkom showed that among more than 1,000 mobile phone users over the age of 16, 69% use a digital health app related to fitness, nutrition, or mental health44. However, based on the survey period in 2019, it can be assumed that the proportion of seafarers who use a health app is now significantly higher due to trends, partly because digital user behavior has probably changed following the global COVID-19 pandemic. The question of how much high internet costs for data transmission on board lead to reduced use of online services was not investigated here, but it can be assumed. Overall, therefore, health apps and e-learning programs should be taken into account when implementing interventions.

Recommendations for improving healthcare on board

In the free-text responses regarding recommendations for improving healthcare on board, the most frequently mentioned improvements were related to sports and fitness facilities on board, healthy nutrition, improving organizational processes, use of digital media (internet), and measures to improve sleep quality. Just under a third (31.6%) of respondents had no suggestions for improvement. Of those surveyed who did not provide a recommendation, 72 (67.9%) were officers and 34 (32.1%) were ratings. The reasons for no recommendations were not asked, but maybe officers rather than ratings were presumably satisfied with the conditions on board or don’t have enough time to think about optimization measures. The responses reflect the needs of the seafarers surveyed and should be considered in future interventions. In this context, it also became apparent that bonus systems for improving health were very popular (83.8%) among seafarers. The “HealthPlus Health Quotient Program” in 2000 already showed an improvement in the health status of more than 2,500 US employees as part of a financial bonus program45.

Limitations

In addition to the strengths resulting from a large seafarer sample and the early to scientifically identifying the need for digital health promotion among seafarers, this study also has limitations. The study reflects the working standards of two shipping companies operating German seagoing vessels. Worldwide, there are many different shipping companies flying the flags of other nations, which in the majority have similar but also different working conditions. Documenting all these varying standards would go beyond the scope of this study; therefore, the findings of this investigation must also be evaluated within the context of the examined or similar shipping companies. The proportion of women in this study was very low at 0.8%, but this is due to the overall low rate in this occupational setting, meaning that no reliable influencing factors for the female gender could be investigated. The focus on quantitative analysis limits the range of possible answers, but this was expanded somewhat by a qualitative question with freely formulated answers. Although the restriction of the health topics covered in the questionnaire is a limitation in principle, it is based on findings from preliminary studies, which had already shown that nutrition, exercise, relaxation techniques for stress reduction, and sleep hygiene are relevant topics for health promotion among seafarers4,46. Possible limiting factors could include language barriers or responses influenced by social desirability; however, the last factor is likely to have a relatively minor impact in the context of the present anonymous online survey.

As this is a cross-sectional study, there is no assessment of changes over time, meaning that changes caused by the COVID-19 pandemic in particular could not be recorded. Influences due to culturally related differences in previous experiences with health interventions, as well as social desirability bias in the responses, may have contributed to a distortion of the results. Finally, a potential limitation could be that this study focuses on behavioral measures and did not examine structural measures; however, doing so would exceed the scope of this study and should be the subject of further research.

Conclusions

Important areas of focus for needs-based health promotion for seafarers on board include promoting opportunities for physical activity, providing healthy food, teaching relaxation techniques, promoting healthy sleep, and raising awareness of exemplary health behavior among superiors. The use of modern digital media such as apps for smartphones or tablets should be considered for communicating health promotion measures, and bonus programs should not be overlooked. Promoting positive behavior towards health-conscious behavior, among other interventions through health education and information, could be promising approaches to improve health promotion on board. Possible approaches to age-dependent health promotion are conceivable based on scientific findings, but since both comparison groups in our study were age-adjusted, no relevant differences emerged here. Despite differences in culture and rank, the above measures and digital options represent promising opportunities for communicating health promotion interventions in merchant shipping. Additional analysis focusing on cultural differences could be beneficial for developing targeted prevention strategies. The relationship between socioeconomic status, interest in health promotion and physical activity should be analyzed in subsequent longitudinal intervention studies on board. The results presented here were used in the follow-up AI-healthy-ship study on the use of digital interventions—including a mobile app—to improve health promotion on board. No conclusion can be drawn about women in this context.
