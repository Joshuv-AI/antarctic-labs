Chronic Absence as a Public Health Priority: A Framework for Coordinated Action
Authors: CATHERINE FALCONER, MICHELLE SHANKAR, BETH D. MARSHALL, SARA B. JOHNSON, MEGAN M. TSCHUDY, ELLIOTT ATTISHA, JOSH SHARFSTEIN
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Policy Points Chronic absence should be recognized as a public health indicator and early warning sign that systems are failing to meet the developmental, social, and health needs of students.Improving student attendance requires cross‐sector policy action across education, health, and public health to address the structural and social determinants of chronic absence.A prevention‐oriented public health approach is essential, focusing on root causes that schools cannot address alone such as poor health, housing instability, and unreliable transportation. ContextChronic absence, defined as missing more than 10% of time in school, has risen sharply in the United States following the COVID‐19 pandemic and now affects more than one in four students. It reflects unmet health and social needs and is patterned by deep structural inequalities. Both short‐ and long‐term consequences include adverse impacts on educational attainment, health, and social outcomes. Despite this, chronic absence remains largely framed and addressed as an education‐sector problem, limiting the scope and effectiveness of current responses.MethodsThis perspective synthesizes interdisciplinary evidence from education, public health, and child development literature, drawing on ecological and life course frameworks to reconceptualize chronic absence as a public health issue. We develop a conceptual model integrating multilevel determinants of attendance across individual, family, school, community, and structural domains, and identify implications for policy and cross‐sector action.FindingsViewing chronic absence through a public health lens reframes it from a purely educational outcome to a signal of unmet need and a multidimensional indicator of system performance. Attendance patterns reflect the interaction of health, social, and structural factors that lie largely outside of the control of schools. Current approaches often emphasize individual responsibility, while overlooking the broader conditions that shape attendance. Reframing chronic absence in this way underscores the need for coordinated cross‐sector interventions that address underlying determinants.ConclusionsPositioning chronic absence as a public health priority enables a more coherent response. We propose three principles to guide action: (1) use school attendance data as a vital sign of student and system well‐being; (2) develop strategic partnerships to align goals and drive progress; and (3) develop strengths‐based policies and programs to prevent chronic absence. Without this shift, efforts to reduce chronic absence are likely to remain fragmented and insufficient to achieve equitable improvements in child health and educational outcomes.

In the 2023‐24 school year, over one in four children in the united States missed more than 10% of school days for any reason, defined as chronic absence. 1 , 2 With evidence linking missed school to academic difficulties, it is no coincidence that reading and math test scores have fallen to their lowest point in decades. 3 The White House Council of Economic Advisers estimated $2 trillion in lost earnings from absenteeism and called for an “all hands on deck” response. 4

Chronic absence is not a new problem, but the sharp post‐pandemic rise 5 and persistent disparities demand urgent, coordinated, system‐level action. Research shows that chronic absence is a marker of current unmet health and social needs, and a predictor of adverse health, education, and economic outcomes across the life course. 6 , 7

In this perspective, we argue that chronic absence can be understood and addressed as a public health priority. We propose a conceptual model grounded in ecological theory and life course concepts, positioning chronic absence as a vital sign of student well‐being and the functioning of the wider education, health, and social systems that shape the lives of children and families.

This perspective offers a structured framework, applicable across contexts, to describe the challenge of chronic absence, assess system‐level contributors, and implement coordinated, upstream action.

Chronic Absence Is a Public Health Priority

Chronic absence is typically defined as missing at least 10% of school days, approximately 18 days in a standard 180‐day school calendar. This rate‐based measure allows for consistent measurement across schools and years and captures cumulative lost instructional time. In addition, because chronic absence is calculated as a proportion of overall attendance, it is a dynamic metric that can be assessed at any point in the school year to identify emerging patterns and trigger early intervention.

In contrast to truancy, which includes only unexcused absences, chronic absence counts both excused and unexcused absences. As a result, truancy may underestimate a student's overall exposure to missed school time, making chronic absence a more comprehensive indicator of student and system well‐being. It is particularly relevant for understanding how exposure to missed school time and learning opportunities shapes educational and health outcomes over time.

School attendance reflects how children are functioning across health, academic, and social domains and indicates how well the needs of children and families are being met. The effects of chronic absence are significant and cumulative. In early childhood, chronic absence is linked to lower literacy development and a reduced likelihood of achieving grade‐level reading proficiency by third grade. 8 , 9 As students progress through middle and high school, chronic absence is associated with increased risk of academic disengagement and higher dropout rates. 10 School absences also limit access to school‐based supports, including mental health services and free or subsidized meals. 11 Prior absence is among the strongest predictors of future absences, 12 underscoring the importance of early and sustained intervention. Beyond the individual, high levels of absence disrupt classroom instruction and affect the learning environment for all students. 10 , 12 Across the life course, chronic absence disrupts educational attainment and workforce participation, with negative effects on health and social outcomes. 10 Chronic absence is also associated with health risk behaviors in adolescence, chronic disease, and involvement in the criminal justice system. 13

Chronic absence is widespread but shows stark disparities among communities in the United States. In the 2022‐23 school year, absence rates were 37.0% among Black students, 46.1% among American Indian students, and 45.1% among Pacific Islander students, compared with 21.9% among White students. Students with disabilities were also disproportionately affected, experiencing chronic absenteeism at rates 36% higher than their peers without disabilities. 14 Economically disadvantaged students have experienced the steepest increases in chronic absence since the pandemic, alongside the most severe learning losses. 3 , 5 , 15 These disparities reflect long‐standing structural inequalities that result in differential access to health care, stable housing, and food security, as well as exposure to adverse childhood experiences and punitive disciplinary policies that disproportionately affect marginalized students, which may impact school attendance. Many disadvantaged students also face acute and chronic health needs, family stress, and trauma that impact school attendance.

Despite this broad mix of factors that contribute to chronic absence, many schools focus on addressing individual student and family behaviors to improve attendance rather than broader structural and contextual factors. 16 This approach may include fines, attendance contracts, and legal enforcement. A public health approach offers a more comprehensive response by targeting these underlying drivers and promoting environments that support regular attendance, health, and well‐being.

A public health approach also naturally engages with health systems in efforts to address chronic absence. Prominent institutions, including the National Academy of Medicine, have advocated for the inclusion of education‐related measures, such as school readiness, 17 chronic absence, 13 and high school graduation 18 as “vital signs” of child health and development. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Whole School, Whole Community, Whole Child framework exemplifies a holistic approach that stresses collaboration and coordination across health, education, and community. 19 Despite the existence of these frameworks, health systems have yet to operationalize school attendance as a potential health metric. 20 It is also underused in population health strategies and largely absent from most incentive frameworks such as value‐based payment models.

Like other public health challenges historically viewed as the purview of other sectors (e.g., gun violence, historically viewed as purely a criminal justice issue 21 ), chronic absence affects large populations, particularly in marginalized communities; is rooted in health‐related barriers and unmet need; contributes to long‐term negative health outcomes; and can be addressed through preventive and collaborative solutions that align education, health, and community systems. 13 , 16 Reconceptualizing chronic absence in this way invites health systems to see school attendance not as an educational priority but as a population health indicator and target for upstream intervention.

Conceptual Model: A Public Health Approach to Chronic Absence

We propose a conceptual model that frames chronic absence through a public health lens (Figure 1). The model is grounded in ecological theory and life course perspectives and draws on established frameworks that integrate education and health in child well‐being. These include Brofenbenner's socioecological model of human development, 22 which places attendance within interacting individual, family, school, and community systems; the Kids and Teens at School (KiTeS) framework, 23 which identifies the multilevel biological, psychological, and social mechanisms contributing to attendance problems; and the Whole Child framework, 21 , 24 which emphasizes health, safety, engagement, and supportive relationships as prerequisites for learning. Together, these frameworks offer complementary perspectives on child well‐being and school attendance.

Determinants of School Attendance

School attendance is shaped by interdependent factors across individual, family, school, community, and structural and policy levels. Recognizing and addressing these determinants are essential for creating the conditions that support school attendance and child well‐being.

Individual Child Needs and Strengths

At the center of the model are the needs and strengths of the individual child, described as the factors essential for school attendance, as well as the protective elements that support more positive outcomes. 6 , 10 , The model focuses on three core domains: physical and mental health; academic environment and school climate; and basic needs and social support. Identifying needs and strengths across these domains can help systems develop the right supports so children and families have what they need to succeed. Of note, while these needs and strengths are present at the individual level, many individual needs reflect system‐level failures rather than deficits with the child.

Physical and mental health needs, including access to adequate nutrition, socioemotional support for managing trauma and emotions, physical activity, and health care, are consistently associated with school attendance, with chronic conditions such as asthma, neurodevelopmental disorders, and emotional and behavioral difficulties linked to increased school absence in both the United States and the United Kingdom. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Academic and school climate factors, including a child's sense of belonging, safety, and engagement with teachers and peers, shape attendance, whereas bullying and exclusionary discipline contribute to disengagement. 32 Basic needs and social support, such as stable housing and food security, further influence school attendance patterns.

Though individual child‐level factors are the most extensively researched, high rates of chronic absence reflect broader, population‐level mechanisms that require attention to family, school, community, and structural contexts.

Family Level

Family determinants are factors related to the caregiver or family unit. Socioeconomic status, caregiver mental and physical health, caregiver education, and family structure have been shown to influence school attendance. 24 , 31 , 33 Studies from the United Kingdom and the United States consistently identify poverty and lower parental education as key drivers of absence. 26 , 34 , 35 More recently, research highlights key family dynamics such as conflict, detachment, isolation, and unhealthy functioning as contributors to attendance problems by disrupting routines, creating unstable caregiving environments, and limiting the capacity of the family to support consistent school attendance. 36

School Level

School determinants refer to factors within the school environment that influence students’ health, learning, and development. Protective factors for regular attendance at the school level include a positive school climate, where students feel safe, valued, and connected to their teachers and peers, and well‐structured attendance policies, which set clear expectations while providing timely support. 37 Safety concerns are highly relevant; 44% of parents polled in August 2024 expressed fear for their eldest child's safety at school, affecting attendance. 38 School connectedness, fostered through strong teacher and peer relationships, is also strongly linked to attendance and academic engagement. 6 , 39 , 40 , 41

Unmet mental health and social needs are key risk factors for chronic absence, making schools a vital component of the child and adolescent mental health system. For many children and adolescents, schools are the primary or only place where mental health needs are identified and addressed, positioning them as a critical site for prevention, early intervention, and ongoing support.

Community Level

Community determinants encompass place‐based factors such as neighborhood safety, transportation, community cohesion, crime rates, and availability of resources, such as health care. 6 , 42 Housing instability, neighborhood safety, lack of affordable childcare, and unreliable public transportation create daily barriers to attendance. 11 , 42 Exposure to community violence, whether directly experienced or perceived, is consistently linked to increased absence. 33

Structural Level

Structural determinants encompass broader systemic social, political, and economic factors that shape the conditions within which communities exist. Policies related to housing supply, transportation investment, exclusionary discipline, and immigration enforcement all contribute to disparities in school attendance. 6 , 43 For example, recent immigration raids in five school districts in California were associated with marked increases in school absences, especially among younger children. 44 Similarly, sustained underinvestment in low‐income neighborhoods can amplify barriers such as unsafe housing, unreliable transportation, and scarce local services, directly undermining children's ability to attend school regularly.

Compounding Interactions Between Determinants Exacerbate School Absence

Determinants of chronic absence interact over time, shaping both immediate and cumulative risk for chronic absence.

Poor school infrastructure increases absence among children with asthma, 43 while school‐based health centers reduce absence, particularly for children with a mental health diagnosis. 45 Housing instability and frequent moves disrupt routines, increase transportation barriers, and weaken engagement in school; sustained absence then reduces attainment and attachment, raising the likelihood of further mobility. Over time, these interacting processes reinforce disadvantage and widen inequities in attendance, health, and educational opportunity.

Understanding these determinants collectively and dynamically, rather than in isolation, is essential for designing effective, strengths‐based multilevel interventions that address chronic absence as a public health priority. The Value of Chronic Absence as a Critical Metric for Student and System Well‐Being

Like other clinical measures, chronic absence functions as an early indicator of distress and unmet needs at both individual and population levels. Patterns of absence can signal emerging health, educational, or social issues, such as infectious disease outbreaks or rising mental health challenges, or deeper structural issues such as negative school climate, inequalities in access to health care, or resource gaps in specific communities. 7 , 13

Chronic absence also acts as both a symptom and a mediator of health. As a symptom, it reflects upstream barriers to regular attendance; as a mediator, it connects those upstream determinants to later life outcomes such as educational attainment, employment, and adult health and well‐being. Data on chronic absence can provide actionable, real‐time information to guide proactive, multisector responses and enable early, targeted support for students and families.

Currently, chronic absence data are underutilized outside the education sector and collected primarily for administrative purposes rather than leveraged as actionable indicators within health and social systems. In most states, attendance data are collected at the school and district level, reported to state education departments, and, in some cases, incorporated into state accountability systems. However, the data are rarely available in forms that allow integration with health, social service, or community‐level data systems. In addition, there is limited systematic collection of data on the reasons for student absences, which restricts the ability to interrogate patterns, identify underlying drivers, and target interventions effectively.

There are wide variations in data accessibility, standardization, and interoperability across states, limiting the ability to use chronic absence as a population‐level indicator across sectors.

Embedding chronic absence as a shared accountability metric across education, health, and public health sectors can drive a shift toward preventive approaches that address the underlying causes of absence. However, any shift should also consider safeguards to ensure that the data are not used punitively against children, families, or schools, such as by withdrawing funding for schools with high absence rates, and that personal data are protected. Chronic absence data should be used to identify need, target resources, and monitor system performance.

Reframing chronic absence as a vital sign for child health and well‐being expands it beyond an education‐only issue and encourages cross‐sector collaboration. Doing this will require attention to the way the data are collected, shared, and used to ensure the data support interventions that promote equity, rather than reinforce disadvantage.

Opportunities for Cross‐Sector Prevention and Intervention

Currently, the burden of addressing chronic absence falls primarily on schools. If chronic absence is treated solely as an education issue, there is a risk that a reactive response focuses on addressing individual student or family behaviors, such as by using attendance contracts or legal approaches, rather than addressing the underlying causes of absence. Such reactive approaches often emphasize punitive or disciplinary actions, which research shows can further harm already marginalized students and undermine long‐term attendance. 32

By focusing upstream, schools, communities, and health systems can work together to identify and address barriers to school attendance early and holistically. Aligned with established frameworks such as Multitiered Systems of Support (MTSS) and the Health Impact Pyramid, 16 , 46 , 47 interventions can span universal supports that promote regular attendance for all students; targeted interventions for those at risk of becoming chronically absent; and intensive, individualized support for students already experiencing significant absences.

MTSS is a tiered framework that helps schools deliver academic, behavioral, and socioemotional support by matching resources to student needs, whereas the Health Impact Pyramid illustrates how population‐level strategies at the base (e.g., improving social conditions) complement more intensive, individualized interventions at the top. Together, these frameworks highlight the importance of combining universal prevention with targeted and intensive supports to achieve equitable improvements in attendance.

For cross‐sector prevention and intervention to be effective at scale, systems must also be equipped with the right enabling conditions. Policy levers, such as school accountability measures, public health funding mechanisms, or transportation subsidies, can create the supportive infrastructure for these interventions to succeed.

Public Health Strategies to Address Chronic Absence

The conceptual model outlined earlier provides a shared framework for understanding chronic absence through a public health lens. It identifies the multiple, interacting factors that shape attendance; underscores the importance of strengths‐based responses; and frames chronic absence as both a signal of need and an opportunity for early intervention.

To translate this model into practice, we propose three core principles that together form an integrated, actionable strategy for preventing and responding to chronic absence.

Use School Attendance Data as a Vital Sign of Student and System Well‐Being

Chronic absence provides an actionable entry point for collaboration across health, education, and social systems. It reflects the cumulative impact of barriers to regular school attendance in both home and school environments, such as health needs, unmet basic services, and weak engagement, and should be used to guide early, coordinated responses.

Despite widespread data collection, fragmentation and inconsistent definitions hinder efforts to understand and address chronic absence holistically. Prioritizing interoperability and data integration across systems is critical to uncover root causes and target support.

Embedding chronic absence as a shared accountability metric, at both individual and population levels, can shift systems toward proactive, equitable, and coordinated intervention. For example, the Oregon Child Integrated Dataset brings together data from five different state agencies to provide an objective data resource for answering policy questions. Analysis of the class of 2020 identified chronic absence as one of the top three barriers to on‐time high school graduation and resulted in a deeper exploration of the topic. 48 Another example of this approach is the Collaborative for Attendance Resources in Education and Health (CARE‐H), a partnership between Children's National Hospital, the District of Columbia Public Schools, and regional data partners. CARE‐H enables secure, sharing of student attendance data with health care providers that is compliant with the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act. This allows primary care teams to proactively identify students who are chronically absent or at risk and to engage families to assess barriers and connect them with appropriate supports. 49 This model illustrates how health systems can partner with schools to address chronic absence through coordinated, preventive care.

Develop Strategic Partnerships to Align Goals and Drive Progress on Chronic Absence

Improving attendance is a collective responsibility. Strategic, cross‐sector partnerships across education, health, social services, local government, community organizations, and families are essential to address the underlying drivers of absence.

Formal agreements such as memoranda of understandings can clarify roles, align funding, and coordinate action. Although chronic absence is widely recognized as a problem, recent polling suggests many caregivers underestimate its relevance to their own child. 50 Meanwhile, student disconnection from school is rising, despite school connectedness being a known protective factor for mental health and academic success.

Cross‐sector collaboration can help build a culture that values attendance as both a personal and a shared responsibility. For example, in Rhode Island, under the leadership of the governor, a statewide coalition of mayors, business leaders, hospitals, and other stakeholders beyond the state's education ecosystem has been formed to work together to address chronic absence. 51

Develop Strengths‐Based Policies and Programs to Prevent Chronic Absence and Promote School Attendance

A public health approach to school attendance requires multitiered interventions and aligned policy levers that operate across individual, family, school, community, and societal levels. 40 , 51 Local tailoring is essential. Cross‐sector collaboration should be supported by ongoing research and evaluation. 52

To be effective, policies and programs must be designed as part of a coherent strategy, backed by a cross‐sector coalition, that reflects the multidimensional nature of school attendance and invests in prevention at every level.

For example, community schools have shown promise in tackling chronic absence by ensuring a focus on comprehensive coordinated services and strategies to improve family engagement and increase student connectedness. 53 As part of a multisector strategy, community schools can act as hubs to connect students and families to essential resources.

Conclusion

Chronic absence is a public health challenge with far‐reaching implications for the physical, mental, and social well‐being of children and families. It reflects structural inequities, reduces access to school‐based support, and disrupts pathways to long‐term health and educational success. Like other public health priorities, it is widespread, driven by underlying social and health‐related barriers, and preventable through coordinated cross‐sector action.

This article proposes an integrated public health approach based on three core principles: Use school attendance data as a vital sign of student and system well‐being to drive action;Develop strategic partnerships to align goals and drive progress on chronic absence; andDevelop strengths‐based policies and programs to prevent chronic absence and promote school attendance.
