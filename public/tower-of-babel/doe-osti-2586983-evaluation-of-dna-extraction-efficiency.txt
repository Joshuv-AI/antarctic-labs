 Evaluation of DNA Extraction Efficiency in Diverse Algae Strains Using
 Commercial Kits and Lysis Approaches
 Matthew Jensen
Abstract
Efficient DNA extraction is essential for accurately monitoring microalgae communities
in large-scale cultivation systems such as raceway ponds and wastewater ponds.
Traditional phenol chloroform extracts are a staple in microbiology but are obsolete for
routine sampling due to its high toxicity reagents and time intensive setups. Commercial
DNA extraction kits are more favorable for the microbes found in these ponds, but lack
specific kits made for these communities. Little is known about which kits perform the
best, leading researchers to use a variety of different kits with inconsistent results. This
project compared one precipitation based commercial kit (Lucigen Masterpure) and five
wash based kits (Monarch, Zymo Quick-DNA, and three Qiagen DNeasy kits) using four
brackish algae strains to determine which methods yield the greatest quantity and
quality of genomic DNA. Extractions were evaluated using the manufacturers protocol,
and additional pretreatment options were administered before a single kit to compare its
potential in being added routinely before extractions. Pretreatment options included both
cryogenic freeze-thawing and heat incubation using enzymes. DNA was quantified
using Qubit fluorometry and NanoDrop purity ratios. Overall, the Qiagen PowerWater kit
provided the highest DNA yield and purity, but at a significantly higher cost then the
precipitation-based kit (MasterPure). It was also noted that while the precipitation-based
kit was significantly cheaper, provided similar results, it took significantly more time to
complete a single run. Cryogenic pretreatment (6x cycles) increased average DNA
yields by up to 80%, whereas enzymatic pretreatment most improved purity ratios
without substantially improving quantity. The results suggest that it may be more cost
and time efficient to use Qiagen kits with the addition of lysis pretreatments to procure
better results. Future works includes developing a better system to efficiently collect
multi variable data, and to upscale to artificial polycultures using similar methodologies
alongside sequencing to confirm kit results.

1. Introduction
Algae cultivation has become an important focus in both commercial and scientific
research due to its potential as a valuable resource for sustainable bioenergy, biofuels
and wastewater treatment. As global demand for sustainable biotechnologies increases,
algae has emerged as a versatile solution for increasing energy independence. Beyond
conventional biofuels, algae are also being investigated as a sustainable source of
aviation fuels, accentuating their potential as a next generation bioresource (Bwapwa et
al., 2017).
Large-scale cultivation systems, such as raceway ponds (RWPs) and waste
stabilization ponds (WSPs), are utilized for both biomass production and wastewater
treatment (Christenson & Sims, 2011). These open systems facilitate the assimilation of
nitrogen and the degradation of organic carbon. Raceway ponds are typically
engineered for large-scale algae cultivation, and are often designed for biofuel
production. Waste stabilization ponds are employed worldwide as a cost-effective
solution for wastewater treatment on a global scale.
These open systems contain a mix of both bacteria and algae, which complicates
downstream DNA analysis. The need to characterize the microorganisms in these
systems is important for understanding how controllable variables affect the growth of
different community members. Traditionally, species identification has relied on both
microscopy and Phenol-chloroform like extractions to aid the field in understanding the
microbiome of different algae growth environments.
The process of identifying these species through microscopy work are typically passed
down through both long-standing practice and scattered scientific literature making it
unreliable for future researchers. Phenol-chloroform based extraction methods work
extraordinarily well due to their high quality and quantity DNA yields (Roager et al.
2023).However, the toxic reagents being used, alongside the long extraction times
make it an undesirable method for large amounts of sample volumes (Baptista et al.,
2021). The need for a reliable modern method without these problems has made
commercial DNA kits more popular throughout the field.).
While commercial DNA extraction kits have become widely adopted in algae research,
many of the kits being used are not designed specifically for algae (Eland et al., 2016).
They are designed for use in specific microbial systems and or species, which may not
perform well in diverse open and closed environments. There is also very little research
on which kits are the best to use on these scalable systems. Recent work has shown
that microalgal diversity plays a critical role in maintaining stable biomass productivity in
wastewater treatment systems, highlighting the importance of extraction protocols that
can accommodate for diverse taxa (Cho et al., 2017). There is an urgent need to
evaluate and specify extraction tools to match the diversity found in these complex
systems.

This project addresses that need by comparing six commercial DNA extraction kits
across four varying brackish algae strains commonly found throughout these systems.
The goal is to optimize a protocol that consistently produces abundant, high-quality
DNA from ecologically complex systems to support both research reproducibility and
scalability for environmental and industrial applications. This is done through both
following manufacturer extraction protocols and altering protocol steps. An additional
objective in the project included adding pretreatments to the algae to improve cell lysis.
The project concluded that both a washing based, and precipitation kit provided the best
results alongside cryogenic freeze-thawing being a worthwhile addition to these kits. It
also laid the groundwork for a future project, involving a scaled-up version of what was
done with the work I was using.

2. Progress
2.1 Technical Approach
I conducted a comparative analysis of six commercial DNA extraction kits to determine
the most effective method for isolating genomic DNA from multiple algal strains: (the
Monarch Genomic DNA Purification, the Zymo Quick-DNA Plant/Seed Miniprep kit,
MasterPure Complete DNA and RNA Purification kit, and three varieties of Qiagen
DNeasy kits (PowerWater, PowerSoil Pro, Plant Mini).
These kits were evaluated on four different stationary-phase algae cultures:
Monoraphidium minutum 26B-AM, Tetradesmus obliquus UTEX393, Picochlorum celeri
TG2, and Tribonema sp. UTEX639 (hear after referred to by strain names). The strains
26B-AM, UTEX 393, and TG2 are unicellular while Tribonema is a filamentous strain,
aiming to give different results with kits across strains.

Fig.1. Representative images of unicellular (T. obliquus UTEX393) and filamentous
(Tribonema) algae before and after mechanical, cryogenic, and enzymatic lysis
treatments. Samples were imaged under brightfield and using a chlorophyll
autofluorescence filter, where chlorophyll emits a magenta signal corresponding to cell
morphology. Disruption of the normal chlorophyll signal pattern indicates loss of cell
structure and effective lysis.
The images above represent a rough expectation of how differing lysis treatments will
affect both unicellular and multicellular samples using a chlorophyll filter. Chlorophyll
auto fluoresces at this specific wavelength and shines a bright magenta signal matching
the cellular morphology of the algae being viewed. Chlorophyll appears as discrete dots
in the unicellular species (UTEX393) and elongated striations in the filamentous species
(Tribonema). The chlorophyll splattered throughout the samples after lysis represents a
loss in heterocyst’s, containing the DNA needed for extraction. The defined, saturated
chlorophyll outlines represent intact heterocyst’s, indicating lysis step more likely to
present better extraction outcomes. The extraction kits deployed, varied in these lysis
mechanisms; mechanical (Zymo, PowerWater), biochemical (PowerSoil, Plant Mini),
and enzymatic (Monarch, MasterPure). Biochemical and enzymatic approaches were
expected to improve purity but have minimal effects on yields. Mechanical methods
were predicted to yield more DNA but at the cost of strand quality. Strand quality is
important because excessive lysis not only disrupts chlorophyll patterns but also shears
genomic DNA, resulting in shorter fragments. For sequencing, longer intact gDNA is
preferred because it improves read quality and downstream bioinformatic analysis
making species identification and community composition more accurate.

Fig.2. General workflow of DNA extraction showing lysis, binding, washing, and elution
steps leading to purified DNA.
Each extraction was initially performed using the manufacturer’s standard protocol.
Modifications were then introduced based on observed limitations and the predicted
limitations of each kit. Pretreatment to the samples before extraction included the
addition of enzymes, specifically Proteinase K and RNase A, and the application of flash
freezing using liquid nitrogen. A protocol step from the monarch Genomic DNA
Purification kit involving enzymatic treatment was adapted for use in other kits that rely
on biochemical lysis. Flash freezing was applied to both TG2, UTEX393 and Tribonema
samples to enhance cell disruption. Although flash freezing was implemented in only
one kit, its effect was assessed by varying the number of freeze-thaw cycles (3 vs. 6),
emphasizing the impact of repetition on lysis efficiency.
DNA extraction efficacy was measured through cell counting, concentration testing, and
purity analysis. Cell counting was done through the utilization of a countess FL3
Automated Cell counter, in which sample cell concentrations were analyzed to compare
average algae cells between differing strains. The Qubit 2.0 was used to approximate
dsDNA yield, while the Nano Drop spectrophotometer was used to assess DNA purity
through absorbance at 260, 280, and 230 nm wavelengths. The wavelengths
represented RNA and proteins (280nm), DNA (260nm) and other contaminates
(230nm). The ratio of the 230nm contaminates was of upmost importance due to its
detrimental effects on sequencing and the difficulty later on in removing them from the
samples.

It was concluded Qiagen PowerWater provided the best quality and quantity of DNA
yield, averaging 567.33 ng of dsDNA per kit run with a purity ratio of 1.865 at $14.32 a
kit run. MasterPure produced similar results, yielding 518 ng dsDNA and a slightly lower
purity ratio of 1.76 due to protein/RNA contamination, but remained a cost-effective
alternative at about $3.11 per run. Monarch provided the cleanest DNA with the lowest
contamination ratios (1.1125 for protein/RNA and 1.125 for general contaminates)
thought at reduced yield. Pretreatment strategies such as cryogenic flash-freezing (6x)
and enzymatic digestion, improved outcomes further boosting purity by up to 80% and
increasing dsDNA yield by 87% compared to manufacturer protocols.
3. Future Works
Future work will involve applying the most successful extraction protocols to mixed
polyculture microalgae communities. This is done by using the same strains now,
among other microbial members found throughout these ponds to create an artificial
mock culture that will set a baseline for what is to be expected when extracting DNA.
The goal of this project would be to test the extraction efficiency, alongside the
sequencing results to confirm which kit provides the best gDNA and most accurate
community compositions. It would also include the pretreatments already being
examined, with the addition of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to provide an
efficient way to analytically look at the results with less time invested in runs. Response
Surface Methodology will allow multiple factors and their interactions to be tested in one

combined run, rather than separately. This reduces the number of trials needed while
still providing statistical measurements such as means, deviations, and significance
values of individual variables.
4. Impact on Laboratory or National Missions
The work provided Sandia with a clear evidence-based comparison of commercial DNA
extraction kits, identifying two different kits as the most effective options for use in their
microalgae cultures. By quantifying the impacts of cryogenic and enzymatic
pretreatments, the project established practical methods to increase genomic DNA
extraction yields. Most importantly, it also laid the groundwork for a follow-up project
aimed at evaluating extraction efficiency in polyculture communities alongside
developing efficient data-driven methodologies.
Accurately identifying algal species through improved DNA extraction supports Sandia’s
broader research efforts in renewable energy-based systems. It enables researchers to
better understand the biological characteristics and nutrient preferences of cultivatable
algae species. This directly impacts cultivation efficiency, growth rate optimization, and
further analysis. These improvements enhance the overall quality and reproducibility of
lab experiments; while the mock culture system and Response Surface Methodology
make it easily accessible for future interns or collaborators to continue sequencing and
evaluate extraction efficiencies.
5. Conclusions
The project concluded that Lucigen MasterPure and Qiagen PowerWater would be the
most ideal kits to use for researchers extracting genomic DNA from mixed freshwater
microalgae communities. Lucigen MasterPure compared to the PowerWater provides
slightly worse yields at a fifth of the price while also taking almost three times amount of
the time making each kit’s specific use dependent upon the researcher. Cryogenic
flash-freezing at 6 cycles increases the yields of the PlantMini kit remarkably, while
enzymatic incubation increases the purity similarly while also having little effect on the
yields. Both have promising potentials, but a larger sample size would have to be tested
to confirm results.
