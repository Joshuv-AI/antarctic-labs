Electrophysiologic Assessment of the Successful Left Bundle Branch Pacing Using the Standardized Criteria

Introduction

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has emerged as a physiological pacing technique designed to overcome the limitations of conventional right ventricular pacing (RVP), particularly in preventing ventricular dyssynchrony. Since its introduction in 2017 [1], LBBAP has gained widespread acceptance due to its feasibility, safety, and promising outcomes in patients with bradyarrhythmia and heart failure [2, 3, 4]. Previous studies have assessed LBBAP without distinguishing successful left bundle branch pacing (LBBP) from other septal capture patterns. Most research also incorporated left ventricular septal pacing (LVSP) as an outcome measure for LBBAP despite growing evidence indicating that LVSP does not accurately reflect similar outcomes to LBBP. The lack of standardized criteria limits the comparability and interpretation of outcomes across studies.

To standardize definitions and improve clinical application, the European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) [5] published consensus criteria to classify LBBAP outcomes into three categories: LBBP, LVSP, and deep septal pacing (DSP). Among these, LBBP has demonstrated superior electrical synchrony, more favorable hemodynamics, and clinical outcomes, including reductions in heart failure hospitalizations and overall mortality compared to LVSP and biventricular pacing (BIVP) [2, 3, 6, 7].

Before the publication of the consensus, various studies used inconsistent definitions of successful LBBP, contributing to variability in reported success rates. Moreover, recent investigations have proposed subclassifications of LBBP into proximal left bundle pacing (LBP) and left fascicular pacing (LFP), including left anterior fascicular pacing (LAFP), posterior fascicular pacing (LPFP), and septal fascicle pacing (LSFP) [8]. Despite anatomical differences, these subtypes share key electrophysiological criteria, such as a short V6 left ventricular activation time (V6‐LVAT), supporting their inclusion under successful left bundle branch (LBB) capture, which is associated with effective electrical synchronization [9]. Safety remains a critical concern, given the proximity of the septal pacing site to vital cardiac structures. Although studies have reported low complication rates and stable long‐term lead parameters, few have compared long‐term performance and complication rates between successful and unsuccessful LBBP cases.

This study aims to describe the 5‐year experience of LBBP implantation at a leading tertiary cardiac center in southern Thailand, focusing on (1) procedural success rate of LBBP according to the EHRA consensus guideline, (2) electrophysiologic parameters and lead performance over time, and (3) incidence of acute post‐procedural and long‐term complications. Additionally, we explore patient and procedural factors associated with successful LBBP to inform optimal patient selection, equipment choice, and safety monitoring in clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Songklanagarind Hospital, a leading tertiary cardiac center for cardiovascular care in southern Thailand. All adult patients aged 18 years or older who underwent LBBAP implantation for indications of symptomatic bradycardia, including atrioventricular heart block, sick sinus syndrome (SSS), and heart failure, between January 1, 2019, and April 30, 2025, were included, and those who failed LBBAP implantation were excluded.

Outcomes

The main outcome was the success rate of LBBP, as defined by the EHRA clinical consensus statement on conduction system pacing implantation, which represents achieving LBB capture or left fascicular (LF) capture. Successful LBBP was determined by the presence of at least one of the following five criteria: (1) a transition in QRS morphology during threshold testing; (2) a stimulus to V6‐LVAT less than 75 ms in native narrow QRS or isolated right bundle branch block (RBBB) and less than 80 ms in left bundle branch block (LBBB), interventricular conduction delay (IVCD), RBBB with fascicular block, wide escape rhythm and asystole; (3) a V6–V1 interpeak interval greater than 44 ms; (4) an interval from the recorded LBB or LF potential to V6‐LVAT that equals to the a stimulus to V6‐LVAT within ±10 ms; or (5) a similar QRS morphology transition observed during programmed stimulation. Successful LBBP was categorized into LBP or LFP based on electrocardiographic (ECG) characteristics and the duration from the LBB potential to QRS onset, according to the MELOS study [8]. Only patients fulfilling all predefined criteria were classified as successful LBBP. Borderline or ambiguous cases were categorized as unsuccessful.

The electrophysiologic parameters assessed included QRS duration, stimulus to V6‐LVAT by unipolar activation, the presence of a discrete left bundle or fascicular potential, the interpeak interval between V6 and V1, and the potential‐to‐QRS onset interval.

Safety outcomes were assessed by measuring sensing, pacing threshold, and lead impedance immediately after implantation, and at 1, 3, and 12 months, and annually thereafter. Acute and delayed complications, including lead perforation, dislodgement, pneumothorax, pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, stroke, and death, were systematically recorded. Heart failure hospitalization and mortality after implantation until the end of the study period were also recorded.

Description of Implantation Procedure for Eligibility

LBBAP was performed under fluoroscopic guidance, with additional assessment of the suitable location based on the pre‐screw paced QRS morphology. These included a characteristic “W” pattern in lead V1 and a taller R wave in lead II compared to lead III, along with a discordant pattern in limb leads, specifically a positive R wave in lead aVL and a negative R wave in lead aVR. After confirming the optimal site, the lead was screwed into the interventricular septum until the helix reached the area indicated for capture of the LBB. Also, the current of injury (COI) was observed using unipolar EGM to evaluate lead perforation.

Data Collection

Clinical characteristics, procedural details, and follow‐up data were reviewed from the hospital information system of Songklanagarind Hospital. Electrophysiological data were collected using the EP WorkMate system (Abbott Laboratories, USA). Missing data were handled by complete‐case analysis, which may introduce bias if data were not missing completely at random (MCAR). No imputation was performed. The proportion of missing data was minimal and unlikely to affect the overall results.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The sample size was calculated to estimate the success rate of LBBP implantation using a precision formula. Based on a previously reported success rate with 95% confidence interval of 78.5% [8] and an acceptable error of 0.04, the required sample size was 406 patients.

The success rate of LBBP implantation was calculated as a percentage of total attempted cases. The electrophysiological characteristics and procedural complications were analyzed descriptively. Pacing parameters during follow‐up were observed at immediate, 1 month, 3 months, 12 months, and annually after implantation. The factors associated with successful LBBP were analyzed by univariate analysis and backward stepwise logistic regression models. A two‐tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 411 LBBAP procedures were performed during the study period. Four procedures (1.0%) were classified as failed attempts and were excluded from the analysis. As a result, we finally included 407 successful LBBAP procedures in the analysis. Of these, 348 cases (85.5%) were categorized as successful LBBP, including 19.2% with LBB capture and 66.3% with LF capture. In the LFP group, LSFP accounted for 35.6%, LPFP for 27.5%, and LAFP for 3.2%. The remaining 59 cases (14.5%) were classified as unsuccessful, consisting of 10.3% LVSP and 4.2% DSP (Figure 2). The median follow‐up duration was 1.0 year, with a range of 0.4−5.9 years.

Successful LBBP

Since the first LBBAP implantation was performed in 2019, the number of procedures has steadily increased each year, with 1, 6, 39, 73, and 128 cases from 2019 to 2023, respectively. However, a slight decline was observed in 2024, with 110 cases. By 2025, during the first 4 months, 50 procedures were recorded. The annual successful LBBP rates from 2019 to 2025 were 100%, 50%, 82.0%, 83.5%, 82.0%, 93.6%, and 86.0%, respectively. Statistical analysis demonstrated a significant year‐to‐year variation in procedural success (p = 0.022). However, this interpretation is limited by the small sample size in the earlier years, particularly 2019 and 2020, which included only one and six patients, respectively (Figure 3A). Compared to unsuccessful LBBP, successful LBBP required fewer lead deployment attempts (1.7 ± 1.1 vs. 2.6 ± 2.3, p = 0.0047) and shorter fluoroscopy time (16.8 ± 11.5 vs. 28.2 ± 25.8 min, p = 0.0035) (Figure 3B,C).

(A) Annual outcome of LBBP from 2019 to 2025 (p = 0.02). (B) Distribution of lead deployment attempts stratified by LBBP outcome (p = 0.005). Bubble size corresponds to the number of cases per attempt. (C) Fluoroscopic time stratified by LBBP outcome (p = 0.004). Each dot represents one case and its corresponding fluoroscopic time.

In both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, no clinical or echocardiographic factors were found to be significantly associated with successful LBBP. Age, sex, baseline LBBB, Q wave, LVEF, LVDd, RAD, RV dimension, QRS duration, presence of valvular abnormalities, sheath, and lead type were not significantly related to procedural success.

Electrophysiological Finding

Among the 348 procedures meeting the EHRA consensus criteria for successful LBBP, the most common diagnostic indicator was QRS transition during threshold testing in 304 patients (87.4%). The criterion of stim‐to‐V6 LVAT was present in 258 cases (74.1%). Other supporting criteria included V6–V1 interpeak duration > 44 ms in 129 patients (39.9%), and equality between potential‐to‐V6 LVAT and stim‐to‐V6 LVAT in 139 patients (37.1%). QRS transition during programmed electrical stimulation (PES) was rarely observed (n = 1, 0.3%), due to the early fulfillment of other diagnostic criteria (Figure 4).

In addition, the most frequently observed criteria combination was QRS transition during threshold testing together with stimulus‐to‐V6 LVAT (55%). Combinations involving additional criteria were progressively less frequent (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

In addition, details of electrophysiologic parameters were compared between successful and unsuccessful LBBP. The stim‐to‐V6 LVAT (70 [65, 78] vs. 88 [85, 96.5] ms, p < 0.001) and QRS duration (106 [98, 117] vs. 115 [108, 122] ms, p < 0.001) in successful LBBP were significantly shorter than unsuccessful LBBP (Figure 5).

Stim‐to‐V6 LVAT (A) and QRS duration (B).

A discrete left bundle or fascicular potential was observed more frequently in the successful group compared to the unsuccessful group (62.8% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.09). The mean potential‐to‐QRS onset interval did not differ significantly between the successful and unsuccessful groups (23.4 ± 7.3 vs. 25.3 ± 8.1 ms, p = 0.21). Similarly, there was no significant difference in the potential‐to‐LVAT interval between groups. While the V1–V6 interpeak interval was significantly longer in the successful group (39 ms [IQR 30–46]) compared to the unsuccessful group (24 ms [IQR 19–32], p < 0.001).

Safety Outcome

Lead parameters were evaluated at multiple follow‐up intervals. Data were available for 407 patients at the immediate post‐implantation period, with the number of follow‐up cases gradually decreasing over time. The pacing threshold was 0.6 ± 0.2 V at 0.4 ms at the time of implantation, 0.8 ± 0.3 V at 0.4 ms at 1 month, 0.9 ± 0.3 V at 0.4 ms at 3 months, and 1.0 ± 0.3 V at 0.4 ms at 12 months and remained stable subsequently. The sensed R‐wave amplitude was 17.9 ± 3.4 mV at implantation, decreased at month, and remained stable in the following period. Pacing impedance decreased rapidly during the first month post‐implantation and remained stable during follow‐up (Table 3).

Acute complications occurred in 15 patients (3.7%), with the most common being pocket infection (n = 4), followed by pocket hematoma or skin bleeding (n = 3), pneumothorax (n = 2), and acute lead dislodgement (n = 3). Of the dislodgement cases, one patient developed loss of capture on the first day after implantation, interrogation, and chest radiography revealed lead perforation into the left ventricle. Two others developed high RVP thresholds at 38 days post‐implantation. Less frequent events included cardiac tamponade due to right atrial lead perforation (n = 1), pericardial effusion without tamponade (n = 1), and resuscitated cardiac arrest from asystole (n = 1). Delayed complications were rare, observed in three patients (0.7%), comprising pocket infection (n = 1) and delayed lead dislodgement at 37 days post‐implantation (n = 1); the latter case developed high RVP thresholds at 80 days post‐implantation, but no intervention was required. No stroke or procedure‐related mortality was reported in this cohort. No statistically significant differences in complication rates were observed between the successful and unsuccessful LBBP groups. Despite a higher number of failed attempts in the unsuccessful group, overall procedural complication rates remained comparable (Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

During the follow‐up periods (median, 1.0 year; range, 0.4–4.9 years), the incidence of heart failure hospitalization was five patients (1.2%). Overall mortality rate was found in 27 patients (6.6%), of which the majority was attributed to non‐cardiac causes, followed by deaths of unknown origin, whereas cardiovascular mortality accounted for the smallest proportion (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Discussion

A favorable success rate of achieving LBBP, together with stable long‐term lead performance and low complication rates, was observed in our real‐world practice setting. Although our study strictly applied the EHRA consensus diagnostic criteria [5] for successful LBBP, the overall success rate of 85.5% reflects the true feasibility of achieving LBB and LF capture in routine clinical practice, where more stringent definitions may yield lower apparent success rates. Comparing our findings to the previous studies conducted in China and several European countries [4, 8], our success rate of achieving LBBP appeared relatively lower, which may be attributable to differences in operator experience and the criteria used to define success.

The first study applied strict criteria for defining successful LBBP, including a paced morphology with RBBB pattern, identification of the LBB potential, equality between the potential‐to‐LVAT and stimulus‐to‐LVAT intervals, assessment of the pacing stimulus to LV activation time, and determination of selective versus non‐selective LBBP. Even though the study used the rigorous definition, the success rate in that study was still higher than ours, possibly because cases from the learning phase were excluded to minimize the influence of the operator's learning curve [4]. The latter study included LVSP in the definition of achieving LBBP [8], which was opposite to our study. One of 10 patients in our setting was LVSP, primarily based on the electrophysiologists' preference, given the limited evidence favoring LVSP over LBBP in terms of electrical or clinical outcomes. Over the last decade, publications have demonstrated the superiority of LBBP over LVSP in terms of electrical synchrony, mechanical coordination, and clinical outcomes, particularly in heart failure populations [6, 7, 10, 11]. Our findings support previous reports suggesting that LBBP provides more effective electrical synchronization than LVSP [9]. Both LVAT and QRS duration were significantly shorter in the successful group compared to the unsuccessful group.

The high electrophysiological criteria used to define successful LBBP in our study were supported by previous studies, which applied QRS morphology transition and a shortened V6‐LVAT (< 75 or < 80 ms) as diagnostic markers [12, 13]. This finding differs from large registry data, in which the shortest V6‐LVAT was most frequently observed, whereas in our cohort it was the second most common feature, followed by the V6–V1 interpeak interval criterion [14]. Moreover, the V6‐LVAT criterion in our study was more stringent than in some previous reports [8], yet the overall procedural success rate of achieving LBBP remained comparable. PES to assess LBB capture was performed in only one patient, in whom the other criteria were inconclusive, and it confirmed successful LBB capture [15]. The potential was found to be present in half of the unsuccessful achieving group, indicating the presence of an LBB potential does not guarantee successful capture, as such potentials can be recorded over a relatively broad area and may represent far‐field signals [16]. In cases where an LBB potential is observed but the stimulus‐to‐LVAT remains prolonged, further advancement of the pacing lead deeper into the septum may be required to achieve LBB capture. Therefore, identification of an LBB potential alone is not included in the EHRA criteria for successful capture.

Combinations of criteria were less frequently observed, suggesting that more stringent definitions reduce the proportion of patients meeting the criteria. Future studies evaluating combined criteria may further clarify their association with improved clinical outcomes following successful LBBP.

Lead performance parameters remained favorable and stable during long‐term follow‐up, supporting the safety and reliability of LBBP. Some early complications were probably under‐detected in our study which may be due to the retrospective design and lack of routine immediate post‐procedural echocardiography. Importantly, no life‐threatening events, including acute ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), as previously reported in other studies [8], were observed in our cohort. This may reflect the relative procedural safety in our real‐world experience, though prospective data with routine imaging would be valuable for comprehensive complication surveillance. Although previous reports have suggested that septal hypertrophy may hinder lead penetration and contribute to LBBP failure [17, 18, 19, 20], we did not observe a similar trend because most patients in our cohort had normal cardiac structure. Right atrial enlargement, heart failure indication, baseline LBBB, and dilated LV were not significantly associated with success, likely due to the small number of heart failure patients resulting in limited statistical power.

We observed a relatively low incidence of adverse clinical outcomes, including death and heart failure hospitalization. The low rate of heart failure events may be partly explained by the preserved baseline cardiac function of our study population. In addition, the retrospective design of the study may have limited the systematic detection of clinical events. Furthermore, routine follow‐up echocardiography was not consistently performed, which may have reduced our ability to detect subclinical changes in cardiac function.

A key strength of this descriptive study is the use of standardized criteria to define successful LBBP, in contrast to earlier reports relying on center‐specific definitions. This approach reflects real‐world practice and supports the broader applicability of LBBAP as a physiological pacing strategy. However, some limitations were presented as follows. First, the retrospective study design led to missing data in echocardiographic parameters and complication records. As a tertiary referral center, some patients returned to local hospitals for follow‐up due to transportation limitations, potentially leading to an underestimation of complication rates. Second, we cannot explain the cases of unsuccessful LBBP despite having apparently similar baseline characteristics to those with successful LBBP. Although advanced cardiac imaging might have provided deeper mechanistic insights, it is not routinely performed in patients without significant structural heart disease. Lastly, the study population was characterized by a preserved mean LVEF and a relatively narrow baseline QRS duration, and the proportion of patients with LV dysfunction was relatively low. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to patients with heart failure or prolonged QRS duration, who often represent a key target population for LBBP. Consequently, the applicability of our results to this important subgroup is limited.

Conclusion

The use of standardized EHRA criteria further enhances diagnostic precision and comparability, providing valuable real‐world data. A study on long‐term follow‐up of clinical outcomes after LBBP, particularly the incidence of pacing‐induced heart failure and echocardiographic parameters, is crucial to further assess.
