Post-traumatic painful thigh mass in a 5-year-old child revealing rhabdomyosarcoma with vascular encasement and biopsy-tract seeding: A diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Hind Qajia, Zaineb Ehrichiou, Saleck Choumad, Nidal Mrani Alaoui, Tarik Madhi, Lina Belkouchi, Nazik Allali, Siham El Haddad, Latifa Chat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common soft-tissue sarcoma in children. Its clinical presentation may be misleading, particularly when it develops after minor trauma, mimicking benign post-traumatic conditions such as hematoma. Diagnostic delay may occur if radiological warning signs are overlooked. We report the case of a 5-year-old child presenting with a rapidly enlarging painful thigh mass following minor trauma. Initial clinical suspicion favored a post-traumatic hematoma. Multimodal imaging, including ultrasound, plain radiography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed findings inconsistent with a benign lesion. MRI demonstrated a large intramuscular mass with diffusion restriction, heterogeneous enhancement, intralesional necrosis, femoral vascular encasement with distal revascularization, and tumor seeding along the biopsy tract. Histopathological examination confirmed rhabdomyosarcoma. This case highlights a common yet critical diagnostic pitfall in pediatric musculoskeletal imaging. Rapid growth, solid components, diffusion restriction, vascular encasement, and biopsy-tract seeding should raise suspicion for malignancy, even in the setting of recent trauma.

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) accounts for approximately 50% of pediatric soft-tissue sarcomas and represents the most common malignant mesenchymal tumor in children. The disease most commonly involves the head and neck region, genitourinary tract, and extremities. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific and may include pain or swelling. A history of recent trauma can be misleading, as it may incorrectly suggest benign conditions such as hematoma or muscle contusion, resulting in delayed diagnosis.

Imaging, particularly MRI, plays a crucial role in distinguishing benign post-traumatic lesions from malignant soft-tissue tumors. We report a case of thigh rhabdomyosarcoma in a 5-year-old child initially presumed to be post-traumatic, illustrating important radiological warning signs, including vascular encasement and biopsy-tract tumor seeding.

Rhabdomyosarcoma is histologically classified into embryonal, alveolar, spindle cell/sclerosing, and pleomorphic subtypes, each characterized by distinct epidemiological distribution, imaging appearance, and prognostic behavior.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 5-year-old child was referred for evaluation of a painful swelling involving the medial aspect of the thigh, evolving over approximately 3 weeks. Symptoms developed shortly after minor blunt trauma sustained during play, without immediate functional impairment.

Over the following days, the parents reported rapid enlargement of the mass, persistent localized pain disproportionate to the initial trauma, and absence of fever or systemic symptoms.

Physical examination revealed a firm, deep-seated, poorly mobile mass within the medial thigh, tender on palpation, without overlying skin changes. Distal neurovascular examination was normal. Laboratory investigations showed no evidence of inflammatory syndrome.

Ultrasound revealed a large heterogeneous intramuscular mass with mixed echogenicity and internal vascularity on color Doppler, findings atypical for a simple hematoma.

MRI demonstrated a well-defined, predominantly oval intramuscular mass centered within the medial compartment of the thigh, measuring 105 × 66 × 70 mm. The lesion appeared hypointense on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, with areas of intralesional necrosis. Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated marked diffusion restriction, suggesting high cellularity. Following gadolinium administration, the mass showed heterogeneous enhancement.

The lesion encased the femoral arterial and venous axis, with absence of proximal vascular opacification and distal revascularization, consistent with tumoral vascular involvement. The adjacent femoral cortex was preserved, without evidence of bone invasion.

Additionally, a subcutaneous soft-tissue implant along the medial thigh corresponding to the biopsy tract was identified, measuring 110 × 50 × 21 mm, consistent with tumor seeding.

MRI protocol included axial, coronal, and sagittal T1-weighted, T2-weighted, fat-suppressed sequences, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI with ADC maps), and postcontrast fat-suppressed T1-weighted sequences.

An image-guided core needle biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of rhabdomyosarcoma. The patient was subsequently referred to a multidisciplinary pediatric oncology team for further management.

Discussion

Post-traumatic diagnostic pitfall minor trauma is frequently reported in children and may be coincidental rather than causative in cases of soft-tissue sarcoma [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Trauma can draw attention to a previously unnoticed mass or lead to misinterpretation as a hematoma. Any rapidly enlarging soft-tissue mass persisting beyond 2-3 weeks should prompt further investigation [[2], [3], [5]].

Imaging red flags MRI is the modality of choice for evaluating pediatric soft-tissue masses [6]. Features strongly suggestive of malignancy include: Large size (>5 cm), solid intramuscular mass, heterogeneous enhancement, diffusion restriction, intralesional necrosis and vascular encasement.

These findings were all present in our case and are incompatible with an organizing post-traumatic hematoma [7].

Vascular encasement of major vessels is an indicator of locally aggressive disease and has important implications for surgical planning and prognosis [8]. Distal revascularization reflects collateral circulation secondary to vascular compression or invasion.

Biopsy-tract tumor seeding Tumor seeding along the biopsy tract is a rare but recognized complication in soft-tissue sarcomas [9].

This complication highlights the importance of meticulous biopsy planning.

The biopsy tract should be carefully positioned within the planned surgical resection field to avoid tumor dissemination beyond resectable margins.

This emphasizes the necessity of careful biopsy planning, ensuring that the tract lies within the future surgical resection field.

Differential diagnosis differential considerations include: Organizing hematoma, muscle abscess, myositis ossificans (early stage) and other pediatric soft-tissue sarcomas.

Organizing hematoma typically demonstrates evolving MRI signal intensity related to blood degradation products without solid enhancing nodular components.

Muscle abscess classically shows peripheral rim enhancement with central diffusion restriction and surrounding inflammatory changes.

Early myositis ossificans may initially appear as a poorly defined intramuscular lesion before developing peripheral calcification on follow-up imaging.

Bowing of long bones may be observed in pediatric soft-tissue sarcomas due to chronic mass effect and should be considered an additional imaging warning sign, even when absent in the present case.

However, the combination of imaging features and aggressive behavior strongly favored rhabdomyosarcoma.

Conclusion

Rhabdomyosarcoma may present as a post-traumatic painful thigh mass, leading to diagnostic delay. Radiologists should remain vigilant when encountering rapidly enlarging soft-tissue masses in children, particularly when imaging reveals solid components, diffusion restriction, heterogeneous enhancement, vascular encasement, or biopsy-tract seeding. Early recognition of these warning signs is essential for prompt diagnosis and optimal management.
