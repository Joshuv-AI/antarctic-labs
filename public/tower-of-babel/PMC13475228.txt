Hair cortisol and other analytes continue to decline and show reduced stability after two years of storage: Follow-up data from a systematic within-subject investigation
Authors: Katharina Huthsteiner, Johannes B Finke, Tim Klucken, Tobias Stalder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe recently highlighted pre-analytical storage duration over a one-year period as an important methodological influence on hair endocrine data. Here, we extend these findings using a 24-month follow-up of our systematic intra-individual investigation, examining mean-level changes and relative stability of hair analytes across storage durations.MethodsHair samples were collected from 81 healthy participants from the posterior vertex. Samples were subdivided into five aliquots and analyzed either immediately or stored under standardized conditions (dark, dry, room temperature) for 3, 6, 12 or 24 months. Analyses focus on changes in the 24-month follow-up data. Concentrations of cortisol, cortisone, progesterone, dehydroepiandrosterone and endocannabinoids (AEA, 1/2-AG, OEA, SEA, PEA) were quantified using LC-MS/MS.ResultsConcentrations of hair cortisol and cortisone declined linearly across the two-year storage period. Between 12 and 24 months of storage, additional numerical decreases were observed (hair cortisol: −5.6%, hair cortisone: −8.9%), which however were not significant for hair cortisol. For hair cortisol, relative stability decreased with prolonged storage, reflected in weaker correlations between immediately analyzed and stored samples. Across other analytes, storage-related effects varied but generally suggested poorer data quality with increasing storage duration.ConclusionsWe provide further support for the importance of storage duration as a methodological influence on endocrine hair analysis. For hair cortisol, longer storage was associated not only with declining concentrations but also with weaker correspondence with immediately analyzed samples. Our findings reinforce the recommendation to conduct hair analyses as early as possible, ideally within three-to-six months of sampling.Keywords: Hair, Cortisol, Storage, Steroid, Endocannabinoid

Introduction

The analysis of cortisol and other endocrine analytes in hair is a common, non-invasive approach in psychoneuroendocrine research to assess long-term cumulative hormone exposure [1]. While it was initially assumed that hair samples can be stored for extended periods under controlled conditions (dark, dry, room temperature) without compromising data quality (e.g., Ref. [2,3]), some indirect evidence suggested that prolonged storage may negatively affect hair cortisol concentrations [4,5]. However, previous data was only based on circumstantial between-subject analyses, not well-suited for providing firm conclusions concerning the influence of storage duration on hair-derived endocrine measures. To address this issue, we recently conducted a first systematic, within-subject investigation across a one-year period, where we demonstrated significant declines in concentrations of hair cortisol, cortisone and other steroid and endocannabinoid analytes, with some declines already emerging after six months. We further observed indications of reduced stability in some analytes [6]. Here, we extend these findings by presenting follow-up data after 24 months of storage to examine the persistence of storage-related changes in mean analyte concentrations and the stability of data across storage time, thereby providing a longer-term perspective on storage effects beyond the first year.

Methods

The current data comprise a follow-up assessment of our previously published work. A detailed description of the study sample, design and procedure can be found in the original publication [6]. The study was preregistered prior to data collection on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/4qpjx) and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Siegen and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In short, 81 healthy participants (66 female, 15 male; mean age: 24.78 (±6.44) years) provided hair samples from the posterior vertex region (as described in Ref. [7]). For each participant, the first proximal 3 cm of hair was segmented, thoroughly mixed and divided into five aliquots, which were analyzed either immediately or after 3, 6, 12 and 24 months of storage. Samples were wrapped in aluminum foil and stored under dark and dry conditions at room temperature. Hair samples were sent to Dresden LABservice GmbH (Clemens Kirschbaum, PhD, Dresden, Germany). Endocrine hair analysis followed previously published liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) protocols using 7.5 mg of hair per sample, which demonstrated good analytical precision, with reported intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation ranging from 3.7 to 9.1% for steroids and 4.5–13.3% for endocannabinoids [8,9]. Analyses quantified concentrations of steroid hormones (cortisol, cortisone, progesterone, dehydroepiandrosterone [DHEA]) and endocannabinoids (N-arachidonoylethanolamine [AEA], arachidonoylglycerol [1/2-AG], oleoylethanolamide [OEA], stearoylethanolamide [SEA], palmitoylethanolamide [PEA]).

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) on log-transformed (natural logarithm) hormone concentrations. Data preprocessing for the 24-month data followed the procedures described in the original publication [6], resulting in the exclusion of two extreme values for PEA (>16344.00 pg/mg). Sample sizes varied slightly due to non-detectable values, with a particularly high rate for hair progesterone and DHEA (see Supplementary Table S1). To explore whether the frequency of non-detectable values varied as a function of storage duration, we conducted chi-square tests comparing non-detectable-rates between immediately analyzed vs. 24-month-stored samples. Associations between storage duration and analyte concentrations across all storage durations (0, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months) were examined using linear mixed-effects models (using the lme4 package in R; [10]), with random intercepts and slopes being specified at the participant level. A random intercept for laboratory batch was included to account for potential variability across analytical cycles. Non-linear effects of storage duration were explored by entering a centered quadratic term. Degrees of freedom and p-values were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation. Pairwise comparisons between concentrations after 12 and 24 months of storage were analyzed using Bonferroni-corrected paired-sample t-tests. Effect sizes are reported as marginal R2 values for the mixed-effects models and as Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the stability of analyte concentrations across storage durations. Correlations between directly analyzed and 3-month stored samples were compared with those between directly analyzed and 24-month stored samples using Steiger's z tests (using the cocor package in R; [11]). To enhance reproducibility, the R code used for statistical analyses and the data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Effect of storage duration

Changes in analyte concentrations across storage durations are visualized in Fig. 1 and descriptive data for all analytes are provided in the supplementary material (Table S1). Extending our previous findings, the negative association between storage duration and both hair cortisol (b = −.10, SE = .02, p < .001, β = −.24, R2m = .06) and cortisone (b = −.08, SE = .02, p < .001, β = −.26, R2m = .07) remained significant across the whole two-year storage period, including the samples stored for 24 months. Mean cortisol levels showed a numerical, non-significant decrease by 5.6% from 12 months (M = 2.68, SD = 2.34) to 24 months of storage (M = 2.53, SD = 2.31; t[79] = 1.29, p = .401, d = .14). In contrast, hair cortisone concentrations declined significantly by 8.9% from 12 months (M = 10.00, SD = 5.88) to 24 months (M = 9.11, SD = 4.56; t[76] = 5.13, p < .001, d = .58).

Further, progesterone levels showed a significant negative association with storage duration across the two-year period (b = −.13, SE = .04, p = .001, β = −.21, R2m = .04). Mean progesterone concentrations showed a small numerical decrease from 12 months (M = 1.17, SD = 1.69) to 24 months (M = 1.03, SD = 2.27), but this effect was not statistically significant (t[50] = .91, p = .731, d = .13). Consistent with our previous findings, DHEA concentrations were not significantly associated with storage duration across the two-year period (b = .02, SE = .05, p = .721). A heterogeneous pattern of results was observed for hair endocannabinoids. While storage duration remained a significant negative predictor for 1/2-AG across the two-year storage period (b = −.15, SE = .04, p < .001, β = −.24, R2m = .06), concentrations numerically increased between 12 months (M = 21.30, SD = 52.40) to 24 months of storage (M = 27.90, SD = 35.00). In contrast to our previous findings, OEA concentrations were no longer significantly associated with storage duration (b = −.05, SE = .04, p = .266). Similarly, there were no storage effects for AEA (b = .05, SE = .05, p = .378), SEA (b = −.01, SE = .05, p = .814), or PEA (b = .01, SE = .04, p = .720) across the two-year period. Although descriptive data for OEA, AEA and SEA suggest notable increases in concentrations at different storage times (see Fig. 2B), these associations were no longer statistically significant after including analysis batch as random effect. Adding a quadratic term did not significantly improve model fit for any of the examined analytes (all ps ≥ .128).

Pearson correlations between concentrations after direct analysis and after 3 months or 24 months of storage for (A) hair glucocorticoids and (B) further hair steroids and endocannabinoids. Data are shown as pg/mg, ln-transformed values.

Besides changes in quantifiable hair analyte concentrations, we further observed an increase in the frequency of non-detectable values after 24 months relative to direct analysis for both DHEA (χ2[1] = 25.29, p < .001) and progesterone (χ2[1] = 7.04, p = .008). For other analytes, no corresponding changes in the frequency of non-detectable values were observed (all ps ≥ .371).

Relative stability (correspondence between immediately analyzed and stored samples)

Pearson correlations between directly analyzed samples and stored samples are presented in Table 1. Direct–3 months and direct–24 months correlations are visualized in Fig. 2. For several analytes, correlation strength declined with increasing storage duration. Hair cortisol correlations decreased significantly from r = .81 (direct–3 months) to r = .49 (direct–24 months; z = 4.29, p < .001). Similar patterns were observed for progesterone, DHEA, AEA, OEA and PEA (all ps < .012), indicating reduced stability over prolonged storage. In contrast, correlations remained relatively stable for hair cortisone (z = 1.23, p = .219). For 1/2-AG and SEA, correlations were either generally low across all storage durations or showed a fluctuating pattern over time (see Table 1).

Discussion

The present data extend our previous findings by demonstrating that storage-related alterations in several endocrine hair analytes persist beyond the first year of storage. Most notably, concentrations of both hair glucocorticoids showed a linear decline across the two-year storage period. Between 12 and 24 months of storage, additional numerical decreases were observed (hair cortisol: −5.6%, hair cortisone: −8.9%), but these lacked statistical significance for hair cortisol. The magnitude of these second-year changes was smaller than respective hair glucocorticoid changes during the first year of storage (−23.7–28.0%; [6]) and comparable to storage-related hair cortisol decreases previously observed between 9 months and 5 years of storage (−8.4%; [12]). Indeed, the presently observed magnitude of hair glucocorticoid changes was in a similar range as previously reported intra- and inter-assay variability (3.7–8.8%; [8]). However, unlike laboratory-induced variability that can be assumed to be random, the systematic nature of storage-related changes may be associated with a greater risk of confounding. Further, in addition to mean level differences, hair cortisol also exhibited reduced relative stability with prolonged storage, as indicated by progressively weaker correlations between immediately analyzed and stored samples. This suggests that the ability to accurately capture individual differences in long-term cortisol levels is compromised for samples stored over extended periods, likely reflecting reduced data quality and thus less reliable results.

For other hair-derived endocrine analytes, patterns of storage susceptibility were analyte-specific. For progesterone and DHEA, mean-level changes between 12 and 24 months of storage were not significant, although progesterone concentrations descriptively declined. However, both analytes showed marked reductions in relative stability and a significantly increased rate of non-detectable values in samples stored for 24 months, again indicating reduced data quality with prolonged storage. For hair endocannabinoids, a more heterogeneous pattern was observed. Although descriptive increases in mean concentrations were evident at different durations (SEA and PEA after 12 months; OEA, AEA and 1/2-AG after 24 months), storage-related associations were not statistically significant after accounting for laboratory batch. This tentatively suggests that these apparent endocannabinoid changes over time also partly resulted from variability across analytical runs. The notion that analytical issues may have adversely affected endocannabinoid results is also reflected in our finding of low stability estimates for several hair endocannabinoids, which for AEA and 1/2-AG already emerged after 3 to 6 months of storage. Potential reasons underlying storage-related endocannabinoid instability remain unclear. It is conceivable that storage-related changes in hair matrix may have induced analyte- and sample-specific variability in extraction efficiency, thus inducing fluctuating and less reliable results. In addition, potential influences of ex vivo enzymatic conversions also cannot be fully excluded, although we would consider this less likely given the keratinized, biologically inactive hair matrix. Overall, our data raise concerns about the analytic stability of several hair endocannabinoids across even shorter storage times. An exception to this was PEA with relatively stable and reliable results across storage durations.

The present research has limitations that should be addressed in future research. While we implemented several measures to reduce the likelihood of laboratory batch effects biasing our findings, including a distributed sampling scheme and additional statistical adjustment, this possibility still cannot be completely discounted. Future research could further minimize this risk by staggering initial hair sampling so that analyses are distributed across an even larger number of independent batches of approximately equal size. In addition, a critical follow-on goal for future research is to determine whether adverse storage effects can be prevented by modifying storage conditions, for example through frozen or vacuum-sealed storage. This question is of high practical relevance because data collection in psychobiological research often spans several months or years, requiring researchers to decide how frequently to run laboratory cycles. This involves a trade-off between two potentially adverse influences: storage effects, as suggested by our findings, and laboratory batch effects. Whereas our data support repeated analysis during data collection (e.g., every three months), concerns about batch effects favor joint analysis of all samples at the end of data collection. Given this tension, identifying storage conditions that prevent storage-related alterations and preserve data quality would be highly advantageous.

Combined, the present follow-up data from a systematic, intra-individual investigation further support the notion that storage duration is an important methodological factor to consider in hair endocrine research. Overall, prolonged storage was associated with indications of reduced data quality across most analytes, although the primary effects (mean-level changes, relative stability, or non-detectable values) varied between analytes. Our results thus further strengthen the recommendation that if permitted by the research design, hair endocrine analyses should be conducted as early as possible (ideally within three-to-six months of hair sampling).
