Abstract
. We introduce C ∗ C^{*} -algebras associated with directed graphs, along with two generalizations of this concept, namely Exel-Pardo C ∗ C^{*} -algebras associated with a self-similar action of a group on a directed graph, and the C ∗ C^{*} -algebras associated with separated graphs. These constructions have in common that they have a dynamical behavior, being the groupoid C ∗ C^{*} -algebras associated to certain topological groupoids, which are built from the combinatorial structure. An important invariant one may associate to these dynamical systems is the so-called type semigroup. We will find a formula to compute the type semigroup for a general self-similar action of a group on a row-finite graph E E without sources, following a recent paper by Kwaśniewski, Meyer and Prasad, and for any finite bipartite separated graph, following a paper by Exel and the author. In addition, we will review various results concerning the structure of the type semigroup for different dynamical systems.

1.
Introduction

These notes are an expanded version of the lecture notes for the course “Introduction to separated graphs” that I delivered at the Summer School Luis Santaló “Structure and approximation of
C^{*}
-algebras”, held at the UIMP, Santander, in July 2025.

Graph
C^{*}
-algebras are a central object in modern operator algebra theory, connecting functional analysis, topology, dynamics, and even number theory. Their importance comes from how they translate combinatorial data (graphs) into rich analytic structures (
C^{*}
-algebras), creating a powerful bridge between discrete and continuous mathematics. Graph
C^{*}
-algebras associated to directed graphs generalize the important class of Cuntz-Krieger algebras
[
29
]
, and provide important models for the classification of
C^{*}
-algebras. In turn, they have been generalized in several directions, some of which will be considered here.

In these notes, we introduce
C^{*}
-algebras associated with directed graphs, along with two generalizations of this concept, namely Exel-Pardo
C^{*}
-algebras associated with a self-similar action of a group on a directed graph, and the
C^{*}
-algebras associated with separated graphs. These constructions have in common that they have a dynamical behavior, being the groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras associated to certain topological groupoids, which are built from the combinatorial structure. As such, an important invariant one may associate to the dynamical systems is the so-called
type semigroup
. This has its origins in the work of Tarski
[
68
,
70
]
on paradoxical decompositions, where the type semigroup serves to express paradoxical decompositions (or their absence) in an algebraic language.
Hence the type semigroup is a purely dynamical device that one may try to directly compute from the combinatorial object. We show formulas for the computation of the type semigroup for a general
self-similar action of a group on a row-finite graph
E
without sources, following the recent paper
[
46
]
, and for any finite bipartite separated graph, following
[
11
]
.

There is a natural map from the type semigroup to the Murray-von Neumann monoid of projections of the associated
C^{*}
-algebras, which in some cases, turns out to be a monoid isomorphism.
We advocate here that this map is a non-stable version of the well-known map
H_{0}(\mathcal{G})\to K_{0}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}))
that shows up in some formulations of Matui’s Conjectures
[
53
,
20
]
, see Remark
3.9
.

We will survey two important results of Wehrung
[
73
]
concerning the type semigroup. The first asserts that every countable conical refinement monoid can be represented as the type monoid of an
action of a countable group on a locally compact Hausdorff zero-dimensional metrizable space (Theorem
4.26
). The second asserts that the type semigroup associated to the action of any supramenble group on a locally compact Hausdorff zero-dimensional metrizable space is always strongly separative (Theorem
5.3
), which is a powerful cancellation condition.

In these notes, I intend to follow a lively, direct expository style, trying to avoid technical conditions that may obscure the essence of the arguments.
I have included several concrete examples through the notes in order to illustrate the concepts and results.

2.
Graph
C^{*}
-algebras

2.1.
Graphs

We start by fixing notation for graphs, following Raeburn’s book
[
63
]
. We warn the reader that different notations are used in other sources.

Definitions 2.1
.

A
directed graph

E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
consists of two countable sets
E^{0}
(the vertices) and
E^{1}
(the edges) and functions
r,s\colon E^{1}\to E^{0}
giving the range and the source of an edge
e\in E^{1}
.

A
path
in
E
is given by a finite string of edges
\gamma=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
such that
s(e_{i})=r(e_{i+1})
for all
1\leq i<n
. The integer
n
is called the
length
of the path, denoted by

|\gamma|
. Vertices are considered as paths of length
0
, and are called
trivial paths
.
We set
s(\gamma)=s(e_{n})
and
r(\gamma)=r(e_{1})
. We also will consider
infinite paths

\gamma=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots
. In this case, we set
r(\gamma)=r(e_{1})
.

We will deal here with
row-finite graphs
, which are the graphs
E
such that the adjacency matrix
A_{E}
,

A_{E}(v,w)=\#\{e\in E^{1}:r(e)=v,s(e)=w\}

has a finite number of nonzero entries at each row. This means that the vertices in
E
receive only finitely many arrows from the graph.
Of course, this includes all
finite graphs
, which by definition are those graphs
E
such that
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
are both finite sets.

A
source
in a directed graph
E
is a vertex
v\in E^{0}
such that
v
does not receive any edge, that is, such that
r^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
. Similarly, a
sink
is a vertex
v\in E^{0}
such that
s^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
. We denote the set of all sources of
E
by
\mathrm{Sour}(E)
, and the set of all sinks by
\mathrm{Sink}(E)
.

2.2.
Cuntz-Krieger
E
-families

Recall that a
projection
in a
*
-ring (or in a
*
-semigroup) is a self-adjoint idempotent element
p
, that is, an element
p
such that
p=p^{*}=p^{2}
. A
partial isometry
is an element
w
such that

w=ww^{*}w
. Note that
w
is a partial isometry if and only if
w^{*}
is a partial isometry, and that, for a partial isometry
w
, the elements
ww^{*}
and
w^{*}w
are both projections.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph. A
Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family

\{S,P\}
in a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is a set
\{P_{v}:v\in E^{0}\}
of mutually orthogonal projections and a set
\{S_{e}:e\in E^{1}\}
of partial isometries such that

(CK1)

S_{e}^{*}S_{e}=P_{s(e)}
for all
e\in E^{1}
,

(CK2)

P_{v}=\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}S_{e}S_{e}^{*}
whenever
v
is not a source.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph. Then the
graph
C^{*}
-algebra

C^{*}(E)
of
E
is the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a universal Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family
\{s,p\}
.

Specifically, the universal property described in the above definition says that for any Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family
\{S,P\}
in any
C^{*}
-algebra
A
, there exists a unique
*
-homomorphism
\phi\colon C^{*}(E)\to A
such that
\phi(p_{v})=P_{v}
for all
v\in E^{0}
and
\phi(s_{e})=S_{e}
for all
e\in E^{1}
.

There is another way to define a graph
C^{*}
-algebra, using a
*
-algebra called the
Leavitt path algebra

of
E
. Although this algebra can be indeed defined over an arbitrary commutative unital ring, we will use here only algebras over the complex numbers. We consider complex
*
-algebras, which are algebras over
\Mb{C}
with an involution
*
such that
(\lambda a)^{*}=\overline{\lambda}a^{*}
for all
\lambda\in\Mb{C}
and
a\in A
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph. Then the
Leavitt path algebra

L(E)
is the complex
*
-algebra with generators
\{v,e:v\in E^{0},e\in E^{1}\}
, subject to the
following relations:

(V)
vw=\delta_{v,w}v
and
v=v^{*}
for all
v,w\in E^{0}
,

(E)
r(e)e=es(e)=e
for all
e\in E^{1}
,

(CK1)
e^{*}f=\delta_{e,f}s(e)
for all
e,f\in E^{1}
, and

(CK2)
v=\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}ee^{*}
for every
v\in E^{0}\setminus\mathrm{Sour}(E)
.

The graph
C^{*}
-algebra is the
enveloping
C^{*}
-algebra
of
L(E)
, that is, it is a
C^{*}
-algebra with a
*
-homomorphism
L(E)\to C^{*}(E)
such that every
*
-homomorphism
\tau\colon L(E)\to A
to a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
factors through
C^{*}(E)
.

It turns out that
L(E)
is naturally a
*
-subalgebra of
C^{*}(E)
and
C^{*}(E)
is a completion of
L(E)
with respect to a suitable norm. See
[
2
]
for a comprehensive introduction to the theory of Leavitt path algebras.

R_{n}
has a single vertex and
n
edges
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
. For
n\geq 2
, the graph
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(R_{n})
is the famous
Cuntz algebra

\mathcal{O}_{n}

[
27
]
. Explicitly,
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
n
isometries
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with orthogonal ranges and whose final projections add up to
1
:

x_{i}^{*}x_{j}=\delta_{ij}1,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}=1.

These algebras were introduced by Cuntz
[
27
,
28
]
, and have played and still continue to play a very important role in the theory of
C^{*}
-algebras. Similar algebras, known nowadays as
Leavitt algebras
, were built by Leavitt
[
49
,
50
]
in order to study the (failure of the) IBN property for rings. Indeed, for each
1\leq m<n
and for any field
K
, Leavitt constructed an algebra
L_{K}(m,n)
with the property that
L_{K}(m,n)^{m}\cong L_{K}(m,n)^{n}
as right
L_{K}(m,n)
-modules, but such isomorphism does not hold for smaller integers. Moreover the algebras
L_{K}(m,n)
are universal satisfying the stated isomorphism. It is worth to mention that the work of Cuntz was done independently of the work of Leavitt. Observe that Cuntz algebras only deal with the case
m=1
, that is, the Leavitt path algebra
L(R_{n})
coincides with the algebra
L_{\Mb{C}}(1,n)
. Some years later, McClanahan
[
54
,
55
]
constructed
C^{*}
-algebras
U_{m,n}^{\text{nc}}
which are related to Leavitt algebras
L{C}(m,n)
. Namely
U_{m,n}^{\text{nc}}
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the entries of a unitary
m\times n
matrix
U=(u_{ij})
.
The work of McClanahan (see also
[
23
]
) was also done independently of the work by Leavitt.

2.4.
When is a graph
C^{*}
-algebra simple?

We determine here when a graph
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
is simple. We will deduce in particular that
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is simple for all
n\geq 2
.

For a path
\mu=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{n}
in
E
, set
s_{\mu}=s_{e_{1}}s_{e_{2}}\cdots s_{e_{n}}
. Thanks to the relation (CK1),
C^{*}(E)
is the closed linear span of the family of elements
s_{\mu}s_{\nu}^{*}
, where
\mu,\nu
range on all pairs of paths such that
s(\mu)=s(\nu)
.

A
cycle
in
E
is a non-trivial closed path
\mu=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
(i.e.
r(\gamma)=s(\gamma)
) such that
s(e_{i})\neq s(e_{j})
for all
i\neq j
. We say that a cycle
\mu
as above
has an entry
if there is an edge
f
and
1\leq i\leq n
such that
r(f)=r(e_{i})
and
f\neq e_{i}
.

We first state two important uniqueness results for graph
C^{*}
-algebras. For the proof see e.g.
[
63
]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

(The Cuntz-Krieger uniqueness theorem)
Suppose
E
is a row-finite directed graph in which every cycle has an entry.
If
A
is a
C^{*}
-algebra and
\varphi\colon C^{*}(E)\to A
is a
*
-homomorphism such that
\varphi(p_{v})\neq 0
for all
v\in E^{0}
, then
\varphi
is injective.

For the second theorem, we need the notion of the gauge action of
\mathbb{T}
on
C^{*}(E)
. For
z\in\mathbb{T}
, this action is defined on generators by
\gamma_{z}(p_{v})=p_{v}
for all
v\in E^{0}
,
\gamma_{z}(s_{e})=zs_{e}
and

\gamma_{z}(s_{e}^{*})=\overline{z}s_{e}^{*}
for all
e\in E^{1}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

(The gauge-invariant uniqueness theorem)

Let
E
be a row-finite directed graph, and suppose that
B
is a
C^{*}
-algebra with a continuous action of
\mathbb{T}
. Then every equivariant
*
-homomorphism
\varphi\colon C^{*}(E)\to B
such that
\varphi(p_{v})\neq 0
for all
v\in E^{0}
is injective.

We can now discuss the simplicity of graph
C^{*}
-algebras. It turns out that certain subsets of vertices of a graph
E
give rise to ideals of
C^{*}(E)
. Hence to get a simple
C^{*}
-algebra we need to get rid of these special subsets. Let
E^{\infty}
be the set of infinite paths
\mu=e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}\cdots
on
E
, and let
E^{\leq\infty}
be the set of infinite paths on
E
together with the set of all finite paths starting at a source.
We say that
E
is
cofinal
if for each
\mu\in E^{\leq\infty}
and for each
w\in E^{0}
there exists a (finite) path
\gamma
such that
s(\gamma)
is a vertex in the path
\mu
and
r(\gamma)=w
.

For vertices
v,w
in
E^{0}
write
w\leq v
if there exists a path
\mu
such that
s(\mu)=v
and
r(\mu)=w
. This defines a preorder relation on
E^{0}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
E
be a row-finite directed graph. Then
C^{*}(E)
is simple if and only if
E
is cofinal and each cycle of
E
has an entry.

Sketch of the proof.
Suppose that
E
is cofinal and each cycle has an entry. We show that if
\{S,P\}
is a nonzero Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family in a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
, then
P_{w}\neq 0
for all
w\in E^{0}
. By assumption there is a vertex
v
in
E^{0}
such that
P_{v}\neq 0
.

Let
w\in E^{0}
. If
v
is not a source, the relation (CK2) implies that there is an edge
e
such that
r(e)=v
and
S_{e}S_{e}^{*}\neq 0
. Then
P_{s(e)}=S_{e}^{*}S_{e}\neq 0
. If
s(e)
is not a source, the same argument can be repeated at
s(e)
. In this way we build either an infinite path
\mu
or a finite path
\mu
starting at a source such that
P_{u}\neq 0
for all vertex
u
in
\mu
. By cofinality there is a finite path
\alpha
such that
s(\alpha)=u
and
r(\alpha)=w
, where
u
is a vertex in
\mu
. Since
S_{\alpha}^{*}S_{\alpha}=P_{u}\neq 0
and

P_{w}S_{\alpha}S_{\alpha}^{*}=S_{\alpha}S_{\alpha}^{*}\neq 0
, it follows that
P_{w}\neq 0
.

This implies that
P_{w}\neq 0
for all
w\in E^{0}
. Hence any nonzero representation
C^{*}(E)\to A
is injective by Theorem
2.5
, and it follows that
C^{*}(E)
is simple.

For the converse, we only show that
E
must be cofinal if
C^{*}(E)
is simple. Indeed if
\mu\in E^{\leq\infty}
, and the set of vertices
w
such that there is no finite path from a vertex of
\mu
to
w
is non-empty, then the set
H
of these vertices would generate a nontrivial closed two-sided ideal
I
of
C^{*}(E)
, contradicting the simplicity of
C^{*}(E)
. Indeed it is trivial that

H\neq\emptyset
and that
H\neq E^{0}
, hence we are done if we can show that

H=\{v\in E^{0}:p_{v}\in I\}

because then necessarily
I\neq 0
and
I\neq C^{*}(E)
.
To show the above set equality, one needs to verify that
H
satisfies two important properties in the theory. First,
H
is a
hereditary subset
of
E^{0}
, meaning that whenever
v\leq w
in
E^{0}
and
v\in H
then also
w\in H
. Second,
H
is a
saturated subset
of
E^{0}
, meaning that whenever
v\in E^{0}
is not a source in
E
and
s(e)\in H
for all
e\in r^{-1}(v)
, then
v\in H
. It is easy to check that
H
satisfies both conditions.

We refer the reader to
[
63
, Theorem 4.14]
for the detailed proof.
∎

We obtain that the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{n}=C^{*}(R_{n})
(with
n\geq 2
) is simple. However
C^{*}(R_{1})=C(\mathbb{T})
is not simple since the only cycle in
R_{1}
does not have an entry.

2.5.
The monoid of projections

The Murray-von Neumann monoid of projections
\mathrm{V}(A)
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is the monoid of Murray-von Neumann equivalence classes of projections
p
in
M_{\infty}(A)
, endowed with the operation of orthogonal sum.

For a ring
R
, the monoid
\mathrm{V}(R)
is defined as the monoid of equivalence classes of idempotents in
M_{\infty}(R)
, by the relation
e\sim f
if and only if there are
x,y\in M_{\infty}(R)
such that
e=xy
and
f=yx
. The monoid
\mathrm{V}(R)
can be identified with the monoid of isomorphism classes of finitely generated projective right
R
-modules. In the non-unital case, this must be interpreted as the monoid of isomorphism classes of finitely generated projective
S
-modules
P
such that
P=PR
, where
S
is a (fixed) unital ring containing
R
as a two-sided ideal, see e.g.
[
38
, Section 5]
. For a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
the two definitions of
\mathrm{V}(A)
agree by
[
19
, Section 4.6]
.

For a row-finite graph
E
, one may define a (commutative) monoid
M(E)
associated to
E
. Concretely,
M(E)
is the commutative monoid with a family of generators
a_{v}
, for
v\in E^{0}
, and the defining relations

a_{v}=\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}a_{s(e)}\qquad(v\in E^{0}\setminus\mathrm{Sour}(E))

Observe that we always have a well-defined monoid homomorphism

\eta_{E}\colon M(E)\to\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(E))

defined by
\eta_{E}(a_{v})=[p_{v}]\in\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(E))
.

Theorem 2.8
.

[
18
]

Let
E
be a row-finite graph. Then the map
\eta_{E}
is a monoid isomorphism.

The proof goes through the
Leavitt path algebra

L(E)
of
E
. Namely, it is first shown in
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
that the natural map
M(E)\to\mathrm{V}(L(E))
is an isomorphism, and then it is shown in
[
18
, Theorem 7.1]
that the natural map
L(E)\to C^{*}(E)
induces an isomorphims of
V
-monoids

V(L(E))\cong\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(E))
. Obviously the composition of these two monoid isomorphisms is the canonical map

\eta_{E}
, which is therefore an isomorphism.

As a consequence one can get a formula for
K_{0}(C^{*}(E))
, although this can also be obtained in a different way
[
63
, Chapter 7]
. We denote by
A^{\prime}_{E}
the matrix obtained from the adjacency matrix
A_{E}
of
E
by suppressing all the zero rows (the ones corresponding to the sources of
E
). This will be called the
reduced
adjacency matrix of
E
.
We consider
(A_{E}^{\prime})^{T}
as a map from
\mathbb{Z}^{(E^{0}\setminus\mathrm{Sour}(E))}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{(E^{0})}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

[
63
, Chapter 7]

Let
E
be a row-finite graph, and let
A^{\prime}_{E}
be the reduced adjacency matrix of
E
. Then we have

(a)

K_{0}(C^{*}(E))\cong{\rm Coker}\,(I-(A_{E}^{\prime})^{T})
.

(b)

K_{1}(C^{*}(E))\cong{\rm Ker}\,(I-(A_{E}^{\prime})^{T})
.

For instance for the
n
-rose quiver, we have
K_{0}(\mathcal{O}_{n})\cong\mathbb{Z}/(n-1)\mathbb{Z}

and
K_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{n})=0
. Observe that
K_{1}(C^{*}(E))
is always a subgroup of a free abelian group, so it is itself a free abelian group. Szymański
[
67
]
proved that every pair
(G_{0},G_{1})
consisting of a countable abelian group
G_{0}
and a free countable abelian group
G_{1}
can be realized as the pair
(K_{0}(C^{*}(E)),K_{1}(C^{*}(E)))
for some row-finite graph
E
such that
C^{*}(E)
is a simple and purely infinite
C^{*}
-algebra. To obtain all possible pairs
(G_{0},G_{1})
of countable abelian groups, one can use Katsura algebras, which have also a graphical model, found by Exel and Pardo, see Section
3
.

2.6.
The groupoid model

We now introduce the groupoid picture for graph
C^{*}
-algebras. These groupoids have been introduced in a certain
ad hoc
way in several instances, and this is indeed the case in the initial paper
[
45
]
. However the construction of the groupoid can be done from an algebraic structure called an
inverse semigroup
, see
[
33
]
and
[
34
]
for this approach. This is the most satisfying way to build this structure, but we will restrict to the ad hoc method in these notes.

We refer the reader to
[
65
]
for a quick introduction to the theory of étale groupoids.

A groupoid is a small category with inverses. Although this is the most economical definition, we want to phrase it in more concrete terms. We will deal thus with a set
\mathcal{G}
, endowed with two maps

s,r\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{0}
, where
\mathcal{G}^{0}
is the set of objects of
\mathcal{G}
. These maps are called the source and the range of
\mathcal{G}
. We have a partially defined multiplication

\mu\colon\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\to\mathcal{G},\qquad\mu(g,h)=gh

where
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
is the set of pairs
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
such that
s(g)=r(h)
, and
s(gh)=s(h)
,
r(gh)=r(g)
. Moreover the product is associative and each “arrow”
g\in\mathcal{G}

is invertible, with
s(g^{-1})=r(g)
,
r(g^{-1})=s(g)
,
gg^{-1}=r(g)
,
g^{-1}g=s(g)
.

We consider only étale topological groupois, which are groupoids
\mathcal{G}
with a locally compact topology
such that the group operations are continuous and the source and range maps
s,r\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{0}
are local homeomorphisms. The étale condition implies that
\mathcal{G}^{0}
is an open subset of
\mathcal{G}

[
65
, Lemma 8.4.2]
.

It is always assumed that
\mathcal{G}^{0}
is a (locally compact) Hausdorff space, but it is not assumed that the groupoid itself is Hausdorff. Indeed in some interesting situations, the groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is not Hausdorff.

An étale groupoid is said to be
ample
if it has a basis of compact open sets. All groupoids appearing in these notes will be ample groupoids.

Given a Hausdorff ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, one can define a convolution product and an involution on the algebra
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
of compactly supported continuous complex functions:

(f\ast g)(x)=\sum_{yz=x}f(y)g(z),\qquad(f^{*})(x)=\overline{f(x^{-1})}.

The universal
C^{*}
-algebra associated to
C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is called the groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra of
\mathcal{G}
, and it is denoted by
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
. A reduced
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
can also be defined, see
[
65
]
.

Example 2.10
.

Let
G
be a discrete group acting by homeomorphisms on a compact set
X
. Then the
transformation groupoid
associated to the action is the groupoid

\mathcal{G}_{G,X}
whose underlying set is
G\times X
, with unit space
\{e\}\times X\cong X
, and with
s(g,x)=x
,
r(g,x)=gx
, and

(h,gx)(g,x)=(hg,x),\qquad(g,x)^{-1}=(g^{-1},gx).

The groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra associated to the transformation groupoid of an action of
G
on
X
as above is isomorphic with an important structure in the theory of
C^{*}
-algebras, called the
crossed product
.
First we observe that if
\alpha
is an action of
G
on
X
as above, then
\alpha
induces an action of
G
on
C(X)
by
*
-automorphisms by
\alpha_{\gamma}(f)(x)=f(\alpha_{\gamma}^{-1}(x))
for
\gamma\in G
,
f\in C(X)
and
x\in X
.
Now there is an
algebraic
crossed product algebra
C(X)\rtimes^{\mathrm{alg}}_{\alpha}G
, which is the linear span of elements of the form
f\delta_{\gamma}
, where
f\in C(X)
and
\gamma\in G
, with the sum defined componentwise and the product and involution defined by

(1)

(f\delta_{\gamma})(g\delta_{\mu})=(f\alpha_{\gamma}(g))(\delta_{\gamma\mu}),\qquad(f\delta_{\gamma})^{*}=\alpha_{\gamma^{-1}}(f^{*})\delta_{\gamma^{-1}}.

The crossed product
C(X)\rtimes_{\alpha}G
is then the enveloping
C^{*}
-algebra of the algebraic crossed product
C(X)\rtimes^{\mathrm{alg}}_{\alpha}G
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
G
be a discrete group acting by homeomorphisms on a compact set
X
. Then

C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{G,X})\cong C(X)\rtimes_{\alpha}G.

Thinking of the algebraic crossed product
C(X)\rtimes^{\mathrm{alg}}G
as the algebra
C_{c}(G,C(X))
, the isomorphism in Theorem
2.11
is induced by an isomorphism
\omega\colon C_{c}(\mathcal{G}_{G,X})\to C_{c}(G,C(X))
given by

\omega(f)(\gamma)(x)=f(\gamma,x),\qquad(f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}),\gamma\in G,x\in X)\,,

see
[
65
, Example 9.1.7]
.

We can now give the definition of the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{E}
associated to a graph
E
. We will assume that
E
has no sources. Let
E^{\infty}
be the set of all the infinite paths
e_{1}e_{2}\cdots
on
E
. Given
x=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots\in E^{\infty}
define

\sigma(x)=\sigma(e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}\cdots)=e_{2}e_{3}\cdots\in E^{\infty}.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph without sources. The graph groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{E}
is given by

\mathcal{G}_{E}=\{(x,k-l,y):x,y\in E^{\infty},k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\sigma^{k}(x)=\sigma^{l}(y)\}.

The triples
(x,n,y)
and
(y^{\prime},m,z)
are composable if and only if
y=y^{\prime}
and in this case

(x,n,y)(y,m,z)=(x,n+m,z).

The inverse of
(x,n,y)
is
(x,n,y)^{-1}=(y,-n,x)
. The space of units is
E^{\infty}
identified with the set of triples
(x,0,x)
.

A basis of compact open subsets of
E^{\infty}
is given by all the
cylinder sets

\mathcal{Z}(\mu)
, where
\mu
is a finite path and

\mathcal{Z}(\mu)=\{x\in E^{\infty}:x=\mu y\text{ for some }y\in E^{\infty}\}

is the set of all infinite paths ending in
\mu
.

The topology of
\mathcal{G}_{E}
is generated by the cylinder sets
\mathcal{Z}(\mu,\nu)
given by

\mathcal{Z}(\mu,\nu)=\{(x,|\mu|-|\nu|,y)\in\mathcal{G}_{E}:x\in\mathcal{Z}(\mu),y\in\mathcal{Z}(\nu)\}.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph without sources. Then there is an isomorphism

C^{*}(E)\to C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{E})

sending
p_{v}
to
1_{\mathcal{Z}(v)}
and
s_{e}
to
1_{\mathcal{Z}(e,s(e))}
.
The graph groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{E}
is always a Hausdorff groupoid.

Example 2.14
.

The
Cuntz groupoid

\mathcal{G}_{n}
is the groupoid associated to the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{n}
.
Set
E=R_{n}
. Then
E^{\infty}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}^{\Mb{N}}
, with the product topology, and the generators
x_{i}
of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
are identified with the characteristic functions of the cylinder sets

\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\{(x_{i}\gamma,1,\gamma):\gamma\in E^{\infty}\}.

Observe that

1_{\mathcal{Z}_{i}}^{*}1_{\mathcal{Z}_{j}}=1_{\mathcal{Z}(v,-1,x_{i})}1_{\mathcal{Z}(x_{j},1,v)}=\delta_{i,j}1_{\mathcal{Z}(v)}=1

and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{\mathcal{Z}_{i}}1_{\mathcal{Z}_{i}}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{\mathcal{Z}(x_{i})}=1.

Recall that a
bisection
of a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is a subset
B
of
\mathcal{G}
such that the restrictions of both
s
and
r
to
B
are injective.

One can introduce a
type semigroup
associated to an ample Hausdorff groupoid
\mathcal{G}
. This is the commutative semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})
generated by classes
[U]
of open compact subsets
U
of
\mathcal{G}^{0}
subject to the relations:

(1)

[\emptyset]=0
,

(2)

[U\cup V]=[U]+[V]
if
U\cap V=\emptyset
,

(3)

[r(U)]=[s(U)]
if
U
is an open compact bisection of
\mathcal{G}
.

There is a well-defined monoid homomorphism

\mathfrak{t}_{\mathcal{G}}\colon\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}))

defined by
\mathfrak{t}_{\mathcal{G}}([U])=[1_{U}]
for each open compact subset
U
of
\mathcal{G}^{0}
.
To see that it is well-defined one has to check the preservation of (1)-(3) in the definition of the type semigroup. (1) and (2) are clearly preserved. For (3), suppose that
U
is an open compact bisection of
\mathcal{G}
. Since
1_{A}1_{B}=1_{AB}
for every pair of open compact bisections
A,B
of
\mathcal{G}
, we have

1_{U}1_{U}^{*}=1_{UU^{-1}}=1_{r(U)},\qquad 1_{U}^{*}1_{U}=1_{U^{-1}U}=1_{s(U)}.

Hence
1_{r(U)}\sim 1_{s(U)}
and thus (3) is preserved as well.

It is shown in
[
46
]
that
\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G}_{E})\cong M(E)
for all row-finite graphs
E
, with
[\mathcal{Z}(v)]
corresponding to
a_{v}
for each
v\in E^{0}
. See also (
9
) below. Combining this fact with Theorem
2.8
one obtains that the natural map

\mathfrak{t}_{\mathcal{G}_{E}}\colon\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G}_{E})\to\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(E))

is an isomorphism for each row-finite graph without sources
E
.

3.
Exel-Pardo
C^{*}
-algebras

We will follow
[
35
]
to introduce Exel-Pardo
C^{*}
-algebras. These algebras generalize the
C^{*}
-algebras associated to self-similar groups
[
59
]
, and also the
C^{*}
-algebras introduced by Katsura in
[
43
]
.

We refer the reader to
[
60
]
for a recent overview on self-similar graphs and their associated algebras.

Let
G
be a countable discrete group and let
E
be a finite graph without sources.

Let
\sigma\colon G\to\mathrm{Aut}(E)
be an action of
G
on
E
, that is,
g\mapsto\sigma_{g}
is a group homomorphism from
G
into the group of graph automorphisms of
E
. Hence
\sigma_{g}(s(e))=s(\sigma_{g}(e))
and
\sigma_{g}(r(e))=r(\sigma_{g}(e))
for all
g\in G
and
e\in E^{1}
.

Let
\varphi\colon G\times E^{1}\to G
be a one-cocycle for
\sigma
, that is,

(2)

\varphi(gh,e)=\varphi(g,\sigma_{h}(e))\varphi(h,e)

for all
g,h\in G
and all
e\in E^{1}
, which additionally satisfies

(3)

\sigma_{\varphi(g,e)}(s(e))=\sigma_{g}(s(e))\qquad\forall g\in G,\quad\forall e\in E^{1}.

Remark 3.1
.

By setting
g=h=1
in (
2
) one gets

(4)

\varphi(1,e)=1\qquad\forall e\in E^{1}.

The pair
(\sigma,\varphi)
may be extended to a corresponding pair
(\sigma^{*},\varphi^{*})
on the set
E^{*}
of finite paths on
E
. This extension satisfies the following key relations (see
[
35
, Proposition 2.4]
):

(5)

\sigma_{g}^{*}(\alpha\beta)=\sigma_{g}^{*}(\alpha)\sigma^{*}_{\varphi^{*}(g,\alpha)}(\beta)

(6)

\varphi^{*}(g,\alpha\beta)=\varphi^{*}(\varphi^{*}(g,\alpha),\beta).

From now on, we denote by
g\alpha
the action
\sigma^{*}_{g}(\alpha)
for a path
\alpha
. We also write
g|_{\alpha}
instead of
\varphi^{*}(g,\alpha)
. Relations (
5
) and (
6
) become:

(7)

g(\alpha\beta)=(g\alpha)(g|_{\alpha}\beta)

(8)

g|_{\alpha\beta}=(g|_{\alpha})|_{\beta}.

The action of
G
on
E
can also be extended to the space
E^{\infty}
of infinite paths on
E
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
associated to the
self-similar graph
(G,E,\varphi)
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a set

\{p_{v}:v\in E^{0}\}\cup\{s_{e}:e\in E^{1}\}\cup\{u_{g}:g\in G\}

such that

(1)

\{p_{v}:v\in E^{0}\}\cup\{s_{e}:e\in E^{1}\}
is a Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family,

(2)

u\colon G\to\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
,
g\mapsto u_{g}
, is a unitary representation of
G
,

(3)

u_{g}s_{e}=s_{ge}u_{g|_{e}}
for all
g\in G
and all
e\in E^{1}
,

(4)

u_{g}p_{v}=p_{gv}u_{g}
for all
g\in G
and
v\in E^{0}
.

The
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
is the closed linear span of the set
\mathcal{S}
of elements of the form

s_{\alpha}u_{g}s_{\beta}^{*}

such that
\alpha,\beta\in E^{*}
,
g\in G
and
s(\alpha)=gs(\beta)
, see
[
35
, Proposition 3.9]
.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that
(G,E,\varphi)
is
pseudo-free
if whenever
g\in G
,
e\in E^{1}
,
ge=e
and
\varphi(g,e)=1
we have
g=1
.

The natural map
C^{*}(E)\to\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
is always injective, and the natural map
C^{*}(G)\to\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
is injective whenever
(G,E)
is pseudo-free
[
35
, Propositions 11.1 and 11.8]
.

Conditions for simplicity of
\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
are given in
[
35
, Theorem 16.1]
. Very roughly, these conditions are
G
-equivariant versions of the conditions for simplicity of
C^{*}(E)
, given in Theorem
2.7
.

An interesting fact is that one can describe an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
so that

\mathcal{O}_{G,E}\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{G,E}).

The groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
resembles the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{E}
associated to a directed graph, but of course incorporating the group
G
in the picture. We refer the reader to
[
35
]
for full details of the construction.

Let us give here a description of the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
in the case where
(G,E)
is pseudo-free, following
[
35
, Proposition 8.6]
. First define a set
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
by

\mathcal{S}_{G,E}=\{(\alpha,g,\beta)\in E^{*}\times G\times E^{*}:s(\alpha)=g\,s(\beta)\}.

The elements of
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
are meant to model the elements
s_{\alpha}u_{g}s_{\beta}^{*}
of the algebra
\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
. As such, a product and an involution can be defined on
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
that make it an
inverse semigroup
, see
[
35
]
.

Now define an equivalence relation
\sim
on the set of elements
(\alpha,g,\beta;\xi)
, where
(\alpha,g,\beta)\in\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
and
\xi\in E^{\infty}
is such that
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}(\beta)
as follows. Let
(\alpha_{i},g_{i},\beta_{i};\eta_{i})
,
i=1,2
, two quadruples as before, and assume that
|\beta_{1}|\leq|\beta_{2}|
. Then
(\alpha_{1},g_{1},\beta_{1};\eta_{1})\sim(\alpha_{2},g_{2},\beta_{2};\eta_{2})
if and only if there exists a finite path
\gamma
and an infinite path
\xi
such that

(1)

\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}g_{1}\gamma
,

(2)

g_{2}=g_{1}|_{\gamma}
,

(3)

\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}\gamma
,

(4)

\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=\beta_{1}\gamma\xi
.

The set of equivalence classes, denoted by
[\alpha,g,\beta;\xi]
, can be endowed with a structure of ample groupoid,
which is denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}

[
35
]
. We have
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}^{0}=E^{\infty}
, and

s([\alpha,g,\beta;\beta\gamma])=\beta\gamma,\qquad r([\alpha,g,\beta;\beta\gamma])=\alpha g\gamma.

Since we are assuming that
(G,E)
is pseudo-free, the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
is Hausdorff
[
35
, Proposition 12.1]
. However there are important cases where the associated groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
, constructed in
[
35
]
, is not Hausdorff (and thus the self-similar action is not pseudo-free).
This is the case for the famous Grigorchuk self-similar group, which was the first finitely generated group of intermediate growth. In
[
24
]
, the authors make an extensive study of the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}_{G,E}
associated to the Grigorchuk self-similar group
(G,E)
.

The following are key examples of the theory.

Example 3.4
.

Let
G
be a group and
X
a finite set. A faithful (level preserving) action of
G
on the set
X^{*}
of finite words on
X
is a
self-similar action
if for each
g\in G
and
x\in X
there is

g|_{x}\in G
such that
g(xw)=g(x)g|_{x}(w)
for all
w\in X^{*}
. This can be interpreted as a self-similar action of
G
on the
n
-rose graph
R_{n}
. The algebras associated to self-similar actions have been studied by Nekrashevych
[
59
]
, amongst others.

Example 3.5
.

Fix a positive integer
N
. Let
A\in M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
be a non-negative square matrix, and let
B\in M_{N\times N}(\mathbb{Z})
be a square matrix. We will assume that
A
has no zero rows and that
A_{ij}=0\implies B_{ij}=0
. Let
E
be the directed graph with
E^{0}=\{1,\dots,N\}
and with
A_{ij}
edges from vertex
j
to vertex
i
. We fix the notation

\{e_{ijn}:1\leq i,j\leq N,0\leq n<A_{ij}\}

for the set of edges from vertex
j
to vertex
i
.

We define an action
\sigma
of
\mathbb{Z}
on
E
as follows. The action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
E^{0}
is trivial. Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
e_{ijn}
be an edge of
E
. Performing the euclidean division of
n+mB_{ij}
by
A_{ij}
we get unique integers
\hat{k},\hat{n}
such that

n+mB_{ij}=\hat{k}A_{ij}+\hat{n},\qquad 0\leq\hat{n}<A_{ij}.

Then we set
\sigma_{m}(e_{ijn})=e_{ij\hat{n}}
and
\varphi(m,e_{ijn})=\hat{k}
. The corresponding
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Z},E}
coincides with Katsura algebra
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
built in
[
43
]
.

Katsura algebras (allowing
N
to be
\omega
), realize all Kirchberg algebras (separable, nuclear, simple, purely infinite and with UCT) by a result of Katsura
[
43
]
. Indeed, Katsura obtained in
[
43
]
the following formulas for
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
:

K_{0}(\mathcal{O}_{A,B})\cong{\rm Coker}\,(I-A^{T})\oplus{\rm Ker}\,(I-B^{T}),

K_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{A,B})\cong{\rm Coker}\,(I-B^{T})\oplus{\rm Ker}\,(I-A^{T}).

See also
[
58
]
.

Using Katsura algebras, we will see an example where the canonical map
\mathfrak{t}_{\mathcal{G}}\colon\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}))
defined in Section
2
is not surjective.
First we notice a recent result of Kwaśniewski, Meyer and Prasad
[
46
]
, concerning the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G}_{G,E})
associated to a self-similar graph
(G,E)
. In
[
46
, Corollary 7.7]
the authors show that the type semigroup of the groupoid

\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
is precisely the monoid of coinvariants
M(E)_{G}
of the graph monoid
M(E)
of
E
.
First observe that
G
acts on
M(E)
by
g\cdot a_{v}=a_{gv}
. To see that the action is well-defined, we need to check that this rule defines a monoid homomorphism of
M(E)
into itself. For this purpose, take a vertex
v\in E^{0}
and
g\in G
. Then we need to check that the relation

a_{gv}=\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}a_{gs(e)}

holds in
M(E)
. However since
g
acts by automorphisms on
E
, we have that

r^{-1}(gv)=\{ge:e\in r^{-1}(v)\}
and thus

\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}a_{gs(e)}=\sum_{f\in r^{-1}(gv)}a_{s(f)}=a_{gv},

as desired. We can now consider the monoid of coinvariants
M(E)_{G}
for this action, which is the quotient of
M(E)
by the congruence generated by all the pairs
(z,g\cdot z)
for all
z\in M(E)
.

In the following lemma, we find a convenient presentation of the monoid
M(E)_{G}
, which is indeed a graph monoid. The graph
E_{G}
appearing in Lemma
3.6
has been first constructed by Larki in
[
47
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a complete set of representatives for the
G
-action on
E^{0}
.
Consider the graph
E_{G}
such that
E_{G}^{0}=E^{0}/G
and
E_{G}^{1}=\bigsqcup_{v\in\Omega}r^{-1}(v)
, with
r_{G}(e)=[r(e)]
and
s_{G}(e)=[s(e)]
for all
e\in E_{G}^{1}
. Then
M(E)_{G}\cong M(E_{G})
and therefore
M(E)_{G}
is a graph monoid.

Proof.

Define
\phi\colon M(E_{G})\to M(E)_{G}
by
\phi(a_{[v]})=[a_{v}]\in M(E)_{G}
. The relations of
M(E_{G})
are clearly preserved. Indeed, if
v\in E^{0}
and
\overline{v}\in\Omega
is the unique element of
\Omega
such that
[v]=[\overline{v}]
in
E^{0}/G
, then we have

\phi(a_{[v]})=[a_{v}]=[a_{\overline{v}}]=[\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(\overline{v})}a_{s(e)}]=\sum_{e\in r_{G}^{-1}([v])}[a_{s(e)}]=\sum_{e\in r_{G}^{-1}([v])}\phi(s_{G}(e)).

Hence
\phi
defines a monoid homomorphism. Conversely, define
\psi\colon M(E)\to M(E_{G})

by
\psi(a_{v})=a_{[v]}
. The relations of
M(E)
are preserved because every
g\in G
induces a bijection between
r^{-1}(v)
and
r^{-1}(gv)
, for all
v\in E^{0}
. To see that
\psi
factors through
M(E)_{G}
, it suffices to check that
\psi(a_{v})=\psi(g\cdot a_{v})
. But this is obvious, because
g\cdot a_{v}=a_{gv}
.
Hence we obtain a homomorphism
\overline{\psi}\colon M(E)_{G}\to M(E_{G})
.
The maps
\phi
and
\overline{\psi}
are clearly mutually inverse.
∎

Hence, we can state, by
[
46
, Proposition 7.7]

(9)

\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G}_{G,E})\cong M(E)_{G}\cong M(E_{G}).

In particular, taking as
G
the trivial group, we obtain that
\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G}_{E})\cong M(E)
, providing a proof of the fact mentioned in Section
2
.

Let us just show that there is a natural well-defined homomorphism
\lambda\colon M(E)_{G}\to\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})
, where
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
. Denoting by
[v]
the equivalence classes of
v\in E^{0}
in
E^{0}/G
, we define

\lambda(a_{[v]})=[\mathcal{Z}(v)].

Now notice that there is a basis of open compact subsets of
\mathcal{G}
of the form
\mathcal{Z}(\alpha,g,\beta)
, for
(\alpha,g,\beta)\in\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
.
Taking
U=\mathcal{Z}(gv,g,v)
, for
g\in G
, we see that
UU^{*}=\mathcal{Z}(gv)
and
U^{*}U=\mathcal{Z}(v)
.
This shows that
[\mathcal{Z}(v)]
does not depend on the representative of
[v]
.
Now one easily shows that, for
v\in E^{0}
,

[\mathcal{Z}(v)]=\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}[\mathcal{Z}(s(e))],

using the open compact bisections
V_{e}=\mathcal{Z}(e,1,s(e))
for
e\in r^{-1}(v)
. The homomorphism
\lambda
is shown to be an isomorphism in
[
46
, Proposition 7.7]
.

We are now ready to present an example where the canonical map
\mathfrak{t}_{\mathcal{G}}\colon\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathrm{V}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}))
is not surjective. Observe that if in Example

, since

fixes the vertices of

Definition 4.1
.

[
14
]

A

where

graph. This necessarily holds if

row-finite (that is, if

The set

is a
trivial separation
of

for each

The following definition gives the Leavitt path algebra

The

-algebra

with

(E)

(SCK1)

, and

(SCK2)

for every finite set

,

The Leavitt path algebra

-algebra

Definition 4.4
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. We then define

\mathcal{O}(E,C)=C^{*}(E,C)/J

where

is the closed two-sided ideal of
C^{*}(E,C)
generated by all the commutators
[uu^{*},vv^{*}]
, where

range on all elements of the multiplicative subsemigroup
U
of

generated by

.
Similarly we define

where

is the two-sided ideal of

generated by all the commutators
[uu^{*},vv^{*}]
as above.

The above construction corrects a “defect” which the algebras

and

might have. Namely it is not true in general that the product of two partial isometries of a
C^{*}
-algebra is again a partial isometry. The exact condition needed for the product
uv
of two partial isometries

of a
C^{*}
-algebra to be a partial isometry is that the projections
vv^{*}
and
u^{*}u
commute.

Indeed if

and

commute then

(uv)(uv)^{*}(uv)=u(vv^{*})(u^{*}u)v=(uu^{*}u)(vv^{*}v)=uv,

so

is a partial isometry. This holds in any
*
-ring, and in fact in any
*
-semigroup.
The reverse implication uses the fact that

is a
C^{*}
-algebra, see
[
34

Returning to our graph

-algebras

4.2.
The groupoid model

It has been shown in complete generality in
[
7
]
that the tame
C^{*}
-algebras
\mathcal{O}(E,C)
admit a groupoid model. The corresponding groupoid is always Hausdorff. The structure of this groupoid is a special one, and comes from a structure of
\mathcal{O}(E,C)
as a partial crossed product. In these notes, we will sketch the construction of the partial action in a particular case, namely in the case of a finite bipartite separated graph. We will always assume that a finite bipartite separated graph
(E,C)
satisfies that
s(E^{1})=E^{0,1}
and
r(E^{1})=E^{0,0}
. The groupoid has better properties whenever the finite bipartite separated graph
(E,C)
satisfies an additional rule, the “three-twos” rule:

(TT1)

|C_{v}|\geq 2
for all
v\in E^{0,0}
;

(TT2)

|X|\geq 2
for all
X\in C
;

(TT3)

|s^{-1}(w)|\geq 2
for all
w\in E^{0,1}
.

For instance, the separated graphs
E(m,n)
for
2\leq m\leq n
satisfy these conditions.

We first recall the definition of a partial action of a group, following
[
34
]
. The definition can be given in various categories, the most basic of them is the category of sets. But we are also interested in partial actions on topological spaces, on
*
-algebras, and on
C^{*}
-algebras. We will give first the definition for sets, and then we will indicate the necessary upgradings for the remaining structures.

Definition 4.9
.

[
34
, Definition 2.1]

A partial action of a group
G
on a set
X
is a pair
\theta=(\{D_{g}\}_{g\in G},\{\theta_{g}\}_{g\in G})
, where
D_{g}
are subsets of
X
and

\theta_{g}\colon D_{g^{-1}}\to D_{g}

are maps such that

(i)

D_{1}=X
and
\theta_{1}
is the identity,

(ii)

\theta_{g}\circ\theta_{h}\subseteq\theta_{gh}
for all
g,h\in G
.

Property (ii) says that the map
\theta_{gh}
extends the composition
\theta_{g}\circ\theta_{h}
of the partially defined maps
\theta_{g}
,
\theta_{h}
. Recall that for two partially defined maps
f,g
on
X
, the domain of
f\circ g
is defined as
g^{-1}(\text{dom}(f))=\{x\in\text{dom}(g):g(x)\in\text{dom}(f)\}
, and the range of
f\circ g
is defined as
\text{range}(f\circ g)=f(\text{range}(g)\cap\text{dom}(f))
. It follows that each map
\theta_{g}
is bijective, and
\theta_{g}^{-1}=\theta_{g^{-1}}

[
34
, Proposition 2.4]
.

If
X
is a topological space, then a topological partial action of
G
on
X
is a partial action
\theta
such that all sets
D_{g}
are open subsets of
X
and all maps
\theta_{g}
are homeomorphisms.
If
A
is a
*
-algebra, then we require that all the sets
D_{g}
are
*
-ideals of
A
, and that all maps
\theta_{g}
are
*
-algebra isomorphisms. Finally, if
A
is a
C^{*}
-algebra, we require all the subsets
D_{g}
to be closed two-sided ideals of
A
, and all maps
\theta_{g}
to be
*
-isomorphisms.

4.3.
The type semigroup of a separated graph I

We are now interested in the properties of the type semigroup associated to the groupoid model of a separated graph. This semigroup can be computed in the case of a finite bipartite separated graph, using a construction from
[
11
]
, see Section
5
for details. When applied to the
(m,n)
-dynamical system (Example
4.6
) this produces examples of
(m,n)
-paradoxes.

Before we give the promised description, we need to introduce the subject of
(m,n)
-paradoxes.

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
X
. In the setting of the classical Banach-Tarski paradox, one is interested in the study of equidecomposability for subsets of
X
. Recall that two subsets
U
and
V
of
X
are
G
-equidecomposable
if there are decompositions

U=U_{1}\sqcup U_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup U_{n},\qquad V=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{n}

and elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in G
such that
g_{i}U_{i}=V_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

A subset
E
of
X
is called
G
-paradoxical if there are two disjoint subsets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
of
E
such that each
E_{i}
is
G
-equidecomposable to
E
. Tarski’s Theorem states that a subset
E
of
X
is
not

G
-paradoxical if and only if there is a finitely additive invariant measure
\mu\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\to[0,\infty]
such that
\mu(E)=1
. Tarski’s Theorem can be
expressed as a result on the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)
associated to the action of
G
on
X
, see

[
70
, Theorem 11.1 and Corollary 11.2]
. This type semigroup coincides with the type semigroup associated to the transformation groupoid of the action which, in this case, is a discrete groupoid.

As Tarski shows in

[
69
, Theorem 16.12]
, his result holds also if we replace
global actions with partial actions.

In the topological setting, that is, when
G
is a discrete group acting by (partial) homeomorphisms on a locally compact Hausdorff topological space
X
, we may restrict the sets considered to the family
\mathbb{K}
of open compact subsets of
X
. In this way, a version of the type monoid may be constructed, see for instance
[
11
, Section 7]
. This type monoid will be denoted here by

\mathrm{Typ}(X,G,\mathbb{K})
in order to distinguish it from the set-theoretical type monoid
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)
. Note that
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G,\mathbb{K})\cong\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G}_{G,X})
, where
\mathcal{G}_{G,X}
is the transformation groupoid of the (partial) action.

The proof of Tarski’s Theorem is based on
two special properties of the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)
, and a general semigroup-theoretic result.

If
S
is a commutative monoid, we consider the so-called
algebraic pre-order
on
S
, defined by
x\leq y
if and only if there is
z\in S
such that
y=x+z
. In the present notes, we only consider the algebraic pre-order, but of course there are in general other pre-orders on semigroups, which may be of interest in specific contexts.

The crucial observation linking paradoxicality of subsets of
X
with the type monoid
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)
is that
E\subseteq X
is
G
-paradoxical if and only if
2[E]\leq[E]
in the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)
.
Observe also that finitely additive invariant measures
\mu
correspond exactly with semigroup homomorphisms
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)\to[0,\infty]
.

With these preliminaries, we can state Tarski’s dichotomy as follows:

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
X
. For a subset
E
of
X
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

[E]
is not paradoxical, that is,
2[E]\nleq[E]
in
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)
.

(b)

There exists a monoid homomorphism
\mu\colon\mathrm{Typ}(X,G)\to[0,\infty]
such that
\mu([E])=1
.

On the other hand, Tarski’s semigroup theoretic result reads as follows:

Theorem 4.17
.

[
70
, Theorem 11.1]

Let
S
be a commutative monoid and let

.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

For any

,
(n+1)e\nleq ne
.

(b)

There is a monoid homomorphism
\mu\colon S\to[0,\infty]

This theorem has been generalized to arbitrary commutative pre-ordered monoids, see for instance

injectivity of the monoid

in the category of positively ordered commutative monoids, see
[

for a detailed account of this sort of dichotomies for preordered commutative monoids.

Remark 4.20
.

Suppose that
M
is a simple conical commutative monoid. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

M
has plain paradoxes.

(b)

M
is either stably finite (i.e.
a+b=a\implies b=0
for
a,b\in M
) or purely infinite (i.e.
2a\leq a
for every
a\in M
).

Indeed, suppose that (a) holds and that
M
is not stably finite. Then there exists
a,b\in M
such that
a+b=a
and
b\neq 0
.
Take any nonzero
c
in
M
. Then, by the simplicity of
M
, there exists
n,m\geq 1
such that
a\leq nc
and
c\leq mb
. Take
x\in M
such that
nc=x+a

We have

(n+1)c=nc+c\leq nc+mb=x+a+mb=x+a=nc.

Since
M
has plain paradoxes, we get

. Hence
M
is purely infinite.

Conversely, suppose that (b) holds, and that
(n+1)a\leq na
for some
n\geq 1
and a nonzero element

in
M
. Then
M
is not stably finite, hence by (b) it must be purely infinite.
Hence,
2a\leq a
, and thus

has plain paradoxes.

In favorable circumstances the dichotomy stably-finite/purely-infinite can be improved to a dichotomy cancellative/purely-infinite in the simple setting,
see Subsection
5.1
.

The proof of Tarski’s Theorem is based on the following properties
of

(see
[

(a)

and

then

.

(b)

and

then

.

Any semigroup satisfying these two properties has plain paradoxes, and hence the

[

5.
Special properties of type semigroups

In this section we will state some properties of type semigroups in certain favorable circumstances. In particular, we review an interesting result of Wehrung
[
73
, Theorem 5.3.8]
. This result goes far beyond the framework of minimal actions. In the final part of the section, we will describe how to compute the type monoid associated to any finite bipartite separated graph, and we will consider the specific example of the type monoid of the full shift, which illustrates very well this computation, and establishes a link to symbolic dynamics.

5.1.
Different levels of cancellation in commutative monoids

We will follow
[
10
]
and the recent paper
[
16
]
concerning notation and terminology.

Let
M
be a commutative monoid. We say that
M
is
cancellative

if
x+z=y+z
implies
x=y
for any
x,y,z\in M
. We say that

M
is
separative
(or has separative cancellation) if
2x=x+y=2y
implies
x=y
for
every
x,y\in M
. We say that
M
is
strongly separative
if
2x=x+y
implies
x=y
for any

x,y\in M
.

Observe that a cancellative monoid is necessarily stably finite.

For a commutative monoid
M
, separativity is equivalent to cancellation of “two-sided small” elements:
M
is separative if and only if whenever we have

x+z=y+z
and
z\leq nx
,
z\leq my
for some
n,m\geq 1
, then we have
x=y
, see
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Similarly, strong separativity is equivalent to cancellation of “one-sided small” elements:
M
is strongly separative if and only if whenever we have

x+z=y+z
and
z\leq nx
for some
n\geq 1
, then we have
x=y
, see
[
10
, page 126]
.

Separativity leads to a strong form of dichotomy in the simple case, that is, a simple conical monoid
M
is separative if and only if it is either cancellative or purely infinite.
If a simple conical monoid is strongly separative then it is necessarily cancellative.

We now state the definition of a Boolean inverse monoid, see for instance
[
73
]
.

Recall that two elements
s,t
of an inverse semigroup are said to be
orthogonal
if
s^{*}t=0=st^{*}
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
Boolean inverse semigroup
is an inverse semigroup
S
such that the set of idempotents is a (generalized) Boolean algebra and such that every pair of orthogonal elements
x,y\in S
has a supremum, denoted by
x\oplus y
.

We denote the semilattice of idempotents of an inverse semigroup by
\mathcal{E}(S)
.

Given a Boolean inverse semigroup
S
, one can define the
type monoid

\text{Typ}(S)
as the commutative monoid with generators
\text{typ}(x)
, where
x\in\mathcal{E}(S)
, and relations:

(1)

\text{typ}(0)=0
,

(2)

\mathrm{typ}(x)=\mathrm{typ}(y)
whenever
x,y\in\mathcal{E}(S)
and there is some
s\in S
such that
x=s^{*}s
and
y=ss^{*}
.

(3)

\mathrm{typ}(x\oplus y)=\mathrm{typ}(x)+\mathrm{typ}(y)
whenever
x,y\in S
are orthogonal.

The type monoid is always a conical refinement monoid, see
[
73
, Corollary 4-1.4]
.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample Hausdorff groupoid. Then the type monoid
\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
as defined in these notes agrees with the type monoid
\mathrm{Typ}(B(\mathcal{G}))
, where
B(\mathcal{G})
is the Boolean inverse semigroup of open compact bisections of
\mathcal{G}
.

As mentioned above, this type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(\mathcal{G})
also agrees with other versions of the type semigroup, as defined in
[
21
]
,
[
61
]
,
[
62
]
.

In the case where
G
is a discrete group acting on a zero-dimensional, metrizable locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, the Boolean inverse monoid associated to the transformation groupoid of the action
is isomorphic to the Boolean inverse monoid
\mathrm{\bf pHomeo}(X,G)
, defined as the set of partial homeomorphisms of
X
defined on open compact subsets of
X
and such that are locally in
G
. More precisely, we consider finite families
(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
,
(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})
, and
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
such that
U_{i},V_{i}
are open compact subsets of
X
and
g_{i}\in G
, for
i=1,\dots,n
, with the property that the
U_{i}
’s are mutually disjoint, the
V_{i}
’s are mutually disjoint, and
g_{i}(U_{i})=V_{i}
for all
i
. We say that
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
is the support of the corresponding partial homeomorphism

g:=g_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup g_{n}\colon\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\to\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}.

Following Wehrung
[
73
, Definition 5.3.1]
, we define a
fork
in a Boolean inverse semigroup
S
as a triple
(c,g_{1},g_{2})
of elements of
S
such that
c\in\mathcal{E}(S)
,
g_{1},g_{2}\in S
,
c\leq d(g_{i}):=g_{i}^{*}g_{i}
for
i=1,2
and
g_{1}\langle c\rangle g_{2}\langle c\rangle=0
. Here
g\langle c\rangle=gcg^{-1}
for
c\in\mathcal{E}(S)
and
g\in S
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
the expression
\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle^{-n}(c)
denotes the product of all the elements
g^{-1}\langle c\rangle
, where
g=g_{i_{1}}g_{i_{2}}\cdots g_{i_{r}}
with
r\leq n
and
i_{j}\in\{1,2\}
for all
j
. Note that, setting
c_{n}=\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle^{-n}(c)
, we have

c_{n+1}=g_{1}^{-1}\langle c_{n}\rangle g_{2}^{-1}\langle c_{n}\rangle c_{n}

for all
n
.

A fork
(c,g_{1},g_{2})
is
nilpotent
if there is a non-negative integer
n
such that

c_{n}=\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle^{-n}(c)=0
.

The following result, due to Wehrung, is the main technical tool needed to show Theorem

Theorem 5.2
.

[
73
, Theorem 5.3.4]

Let
S
be a Boolean inverse monoid. If every fork in
S
is nilpotent, then the type monoid
\mathrm{Typ}(S)
is strongly separative.

Recall that a group
G
is supramenable if no nonempty subset of
G
is paradoxical with respect to the natural left action of
G
on itself.

The following theorem was proven in an equivalent way by Wehrung. We present a proof (essentially his proof)
here for convenience of the reader.

Theorem 5.3
.

[
73
, Theorem 5.3.8]

Let
G
be a supramenable group acting by homeomorphisms on a zero-dimensional, locally compact Hausdorff space
X
. Then the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G,\mathbb{K})
is strongly separative.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2
, it suffices to show that all forks in
\mathrm{\bf pHomeo}(X,G)
are nilpotent. Set
S:=\mathrm{\bf pHomeo}(X,G)
.

The set of idempotents of
S
is the set of identities
1_{K}
of open compact subsets
K
of
X
.
Note that for
g\in S
we have
g1_{K}g^{-1}=1_{g(K\cap\text{dom}(g))}
. Hence we may identify
\mathcal{E}(S)
with the algebra of open compact subsets of
X
and
g\langle 1_{K}\rangle
with

g(K\cap\text{dom}(g))
.

Let
(c,g_{1},g_{2})
be a fork of
S
. Then
c
may be identified with an open compact subset, say
C
, of
X
, such that
C
is contained in the domain of
g_{i}
, for
i=1,2
, and

g_{1}(C)\cap g_{2}(C)=\emptyset.

Now consider the sets
C_{n}
defined inductively by

C_{0}=C,\qquad C_{n+1}=g_{1}^{-1}(C_{n}\cap\text{range}(g_{1}))\cap g_{2}^{-1}(C_{n}\cap\text{range}(g_{2}))\cap C_{n}.

Suppose by way of contradiction that
(c,g_{1},g_{2})
is not nilpotent. Then we have that
C_{n}\neq\emptyset
. Observe that then the sequence
\{C_{n}\}
is a decreasing sequence of non-empty compact subsets of
X
and hence
K:=\cap_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\neq\emptyset
. Observe that
K
is a compact subset of
X
with
K\subset\text{dom}(g_{1})\cap\text{dom}(g_{2})
and

g_{1}(K)\cap g_{2}(K)\subseteq g_{1}(C)\cap g_{2}(C)=\emptyset.

Moreover
g_{i}(K)\subseteq K
for
i=1,2
.
We now want to “translate” these relations to
G
, in order to contradict the hypothesis that
G
is supramenable. For this, we fix a point
t_{0}\in K
and consider the map

\varphi\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(G)
(where
\mathcal{P}(A)
denotes the powerset of a set
A
) defined by

\varphi(Y)=\{g\in G:g(t_{0})\in Y\}\qquad(Y\subseteq X).

It is straightforward to check that
\varphi
preserves arbitrary unions and intersections and sends the emptyset to itself. Observe also that
\varphi(K)\neq\emptyset
and
\varphi(gY)=g\varphi(Y)
for each
Y\in\mathcal{P}(X)
.

But now, setting
Z=\varphi(K)
and
Z_{i}=\varphi(g_{i}K)=g_{i}\varphi(K)=g_{i}Z
for

, we have
Z\neq\emptyset
,
Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}\subseteq Z
,
Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}=\emptyset
and
Z\sim_{G}Z_{i}
for

. Hence
Z
is
G
-paradoxical which contradicts the hypothesis that
G
is supramenable.
∎

The following corollary is immediate:

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
G
be a supramenable group acting minimally on a compact zero-dimensional space

. Then the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G,\mathbb{K})
is cancellative.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.3
, the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G,\mathbb{K})
is strongly separative.
Since the action is minimal, it is cancellative.

It seems to be an open problem whether the type semigroup
\mathrm{Typ}(X,G,\mathbb{K})
of a minimal action of an amenable group on a Cantor set is cancellative, see
[

, Proposition 2.20]

and the comments after it.

There are other properties of interest concerning type semigroups. A conical monoid
M
is said to be
unperforated
in case, for each positive integer
n
, we have that
na\leq nb
in
M
implies that
a\leq b
in
M
. By a result of Chen
[
25
, Theorem 1]
, an unperforated refinement monoid is necessarily separative.

Any cancellative and unperforated conical refinement monoid is a direct limit of finitely generated free commutative monoids (see
[
15
, Theorem 3.14]
). These are precisely the
positive cones of dimension groups.
Generalizing the above, we may consider
tame refinement monoids

[
15
]
, which, by definition, are those refinement monoids that may be written as a direct limit of
a directed system of finitely generated refinement monoids. Tame refinement monoids are always unperforated and separative
[
15
, Theorem 3.14]
. If a simple conical refinement monoid is
tame, then it is either cancellative (and thus the positive cone of a dimension group) or purely infinite. Hence a strong form of the dichotomy principle holds in this case.

A refinement monoid is said to be
wild
if it is not tame. See
[
15
]
and also Theorem
5.10
below for some examples of wild refinement monoids related to
specific examples of separated graphs.

We can now state an important structural result for the type monoid associated to a self-similar action.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{G,E}
be the groupoid associated to a self-similar graph

Theorem 5.10
.

For each
n\geq 1
, set

be the set of words of length

. Let

Let

\varphi_{n}(b)=0b+1b=b0+b1

Proof.

Note that for any clopen set

Remark 5.11
.

Although we know that the monoids

from Theorem
5.5
are strongly separative, its fine structure remains elusive. We indeed know that these monoids are strongly separative by an indirect argument, as indicated in the proof of Theorem

In work in progress, Claramunt, Nazemian and the author tackle the problem of establishing a

for the elements of

are unperforated or not.
