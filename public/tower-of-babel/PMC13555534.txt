Antibody format matters: A comparative analysis of VHH and scFv domains reveals superior in vivo CAR T cell function with VHH domains
Authors: Alexander Kinna, Preeta Datta, Reyisa Bughda, Farhaan Parekh, Mathew Robson, Ram Jha, Phillip Wu, Ian Scott, Elizabeth Hogben, Oliver Amin, Gordon Weng-Kit Cheung, Warren Hazelton, Zulaikha Akbar, Katarina Lamb, Emily Macken, Chris Allen, Sofi Panovska, Lisa Hogan, Mohamed El-Kholy, Reevesh Shrestha, Jonathan Popplewell, Evangelia Kokalaki, James Sillibourne, Simon Thomas, Shimobi Onuoha, Mathieu Ferrari, Martin Pule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells require an extracellular targeting domain for antigen specificity, typically an scFv. VHH antibodies are emerging as alternative CAR binding regions due to their reduced size, improved stability, and CDR3 loop architecture. Herein, we generated and compared VHH and scFv antibodies targeting CD123, via immunized phage display libraries, selecting ten antibody pairs based on comparable domain targeting and kinetic profiles (KD range M 10−9–10−11). The VHH antibody fragments showed improved stability (Tm50 Δ7.85°C), reduced aggregation, and a favorable surface charge. CARs incorporating a VHH demonstrated higher IL2 and IFNγ secretion than scFv-derived CARs during in vitro analysis and substantially enhanced survival and decreased tumor burden in a xenograft murine model of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). This comprehensive comparison of the two most adopted antibody classes provides a rationale for the selection of VHH as preferred CAR binding domains.Keywords: CAR, VHH, sVHH, scFv, T cell, CD123, IL3RA, antibody

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-modified T cells have become established as treatment for refractory lymphoid malignancies and are being explored in other therapeutic areas such as solid tumors and autoimmunity. Of the currently licensed CAR T cell therapies, six use a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) as the antigen recognition domain, while one—ciltacabtagene autoleucel—uses a llama-derived VHH (variable domain of a heavy-chain-only antibody, also called sdAb, HcAbs, Nbs).1 Other binding domains such as natural ligands,2,3 peptides, or other variable domains4 have also been investigated.

VHHs are the smallest naturally occurring antibody fragment, consisting solely of the variable region of a heavy-chain-only antibody. These antibodies retain functional target binding with no light chain pairing required. VHHs have several distinctions from standard antibody scaffolds, offering potential advantages such as an extended CDR3 enabling recognition of cryptic epitopes, high expression in pro/eukaryotes, high stability, and a compact size of 12–15 kDa (∼400 bp).5 Single-domain antibodies can be isolated from camelids,6 certain cartilaginous sharks (VNAR),7,8 or synthetic/immunized libraries by display technologies without loss of function caused by variable heavy (VH)/ variable light (VL) mispairing.

In addition to cilta-cel,9 several VHH CARs have been described. These include VHH CARs recognizing BCMA, mesothelin, CD19, CD20, and CDH17 currently in clinical testing, alongside a range of pre-clinical studies including successful use of multiple VHHs to target the same antigen.10,11,12 The properties of VHHs may be advantageous over scFv when used in CARs—for example, in terms of overall avidity, epitope-paratope interaction, stability, and surface aggregation.13 While several studies have compared scFv with VHH CARs, these have typically involved only a small number of binders, limiting the ability to draw general conclusions.

To address these limitations, this study sets out to address the biophysical and functional differences between scFv- and VHH-derived CARs through a more comprehensive comparison. To this end, we generated multiple distinct anti-CD123 (interleukin 3 receptor alpha, IL3RA) antibodies through immunized phage display library panning from camelid and rodent sources. These antibodies were characterized for biophysical properties, and a set of affinity matched binders was selected before reformatting into CARs.

Results

Generation and biophysical profile of scFv and VHH candidates

Llamas and rats were immunized against the human CD123 extracellular domain to generate phage display single-domain and scFv libraries, respectively. The libraries were panned against recombinant human CD123 and antibodies selected through phagemid screening or NGS (next generation sequencing) approaches. These were subsequently cloned as scFv or VHH murine IgG2a-Fc chimeras and screened via ELISA for CD123 binding (Figure S1A).14 A total of 33 scFv and 46 VHH CD123-binding antibody clones were identified from their respective libraries. Both libraries generated binders with wide CDR3 sequence diversity, with the VHH cohort expectedly demonstrating a wider range of CDR3 sequence lengths (Figures S1B and C).

SupT1 cells transduced to express truncated forms of CD123 were used in domain mapping experiments via flow cytometry. Most antibodies across both the scFv and VHH pools bound to the D2 domain of CD123 (88% and 78%, respectively). The VHH binders also targeted the D3 and NTD domains, while the scFv library generated specificity only toward D2 and D3 (Figure 1A).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) indicated that the affinity (KD) of the majority of antibodies screened fell within the nM to pM range (Figures 1B and 1C; Figure S2). Antibodies binding to each domain varied in their affinity, and neither the VHH nor scFv pool showed a distinct advantage in generating higher affinities. Comparing binding kinetics showed that ka (on rate) values for most constructs clustered between 4.87 × 105 and 1.2 × 106 M−1s−1 (median κa values for VHH and scFv binders, respectively), while the kd (off rate) had greater variation with a median value of 4.36 × 10−4 s−1 and 1.58 × 10−4 s−1 for VHH and scFv binders, respectively (Figure 1C).

A selection of 20 high affinity clones among the D2 binders was made from the scFv and VHH cohort. Kinetic profiles showed a range of KD from 6.59 × 10−9 to 1.85 × 10−11, with ka 8.72 × 104 to 5.31 × 107 and kd 3.35 × 10−5 to 1.23 × 10−2 (Table 1; Figure S2). Epitope binning via SPR grouped all but two clones into a single interacting group (bin 1), while sc24 and VHH B12 were in distinct bins (bins 2 and 3) (Figure S1D), and sc14 was separately epitope binned (bin 1) (Table 1). Flow cytometry titration was performed on the MOLM-14 AML cell line deriving functional binding affinity for each clone, which was used to identify scFv and VHH pairs (Figures 1D–S1E and S1G–S1P). Despite showing promising affinities by SPR, scFv clone 86 failed to show a significant binding profile at relevant concentrations and was excluded from subsequent CAR development.

Antibody thermal stability was compared via differential scanning fluorimetry. The VHH pool had a relatively consistent mean temperature for onset of unfolding (T onset) of 61.1°C (range 56.1°C–67.5°C) and a 50% unfolded state (Tm50, inflection 1) of 67.1°C (range 59.8°C–71.3°C). The scFv group displayed instead an average T onset of 55.1°C (range 37.1°C –72.8°C), with a mean Tm50 of 59.2°C (range 42.7°C–65.6°C) (Figure 1E, F; Figure S1E). For two scFv clones (sc14 and sc86), a discrete unfolding event could not be identified.

Expectedly, all VHH sequences displayed close homology to the human VH3 gene family, with an average human germline content of 70%, while the scFv cohort matched with human VH3 and VH7 (average germline content 75%) and with human Vk1, Vk2, and Vk6 (average germline content 65%) (Figure S3A). However, in contrast to the scFv sequences, all VHHs were classified as human based on the Hu-mAb humanness criteria,15 although no significant difference was determined for the peptide-based analysis given by the Observed Antibody Space Identity Score (OASis)16 (Figure S3B and S3C).

Biophysical properties and therapeutic developability

Several biophysical factors affecting the binding moiety impact the therapeutic developability of CAR T cells. An in silico homology modeling technique was used to predict the 3D structure of the VHH and scFv antibodies and determine the aggregation propensity, solvent accessible positive patch area, and overall surface charge. For context, VHH and scFv pools were compared with the anti-GD2 14G2a scFv, known to elicit a tonically active CAR T cell,17 and to the clinically validated anti-CD19 antibody FMC63. The VHH pool had a lower mean aggregation score of 52.8 compared with 142.2 for the scFvs (122.1 and 212.3 for 14G2a and FMC63, respectively), and a positively charged surface patch area of 3183 Å vs. 5454 Å (5543 Å and 5980 Å for 14G2a and FMC63, respectively) (Figure 2A; Figure S4). Most clones had an overall positive surface charge, in line with both 14G2a and FMC63 (Figure 2A). Dynamic light scattering determined proteins were at the expected particle size with even monomeric protein distribution, except for 1 clone in each pool (Figure 2B).

The 10 VHH and 9 scFv antibody sequences were reformatted into a cassette consisting of the RQR8 transduction marker18 followed by a T2A self-cleaving peptide and then a second-generation CAR comprising the antibody fragment, a CD8 spacer and transmembrane domain (TM), the 4-1BB intracellular costimulatory domain, and the CD3ζ signaling domain (Figure 2C). There was no difference in the retroviral supernatant titers generated for the two CAR cohorts (Figure S5A). Flow cytometry staining on transduced healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) showed higher levels of RQR8 expression for the VHH CAR cohort but equivalent levels of surface CAR expression, as determined by soluble CD123 staining (Figure 2D). VHH CARs also demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of transduced cells (CD123+/RQR8+) compared with scFv CARs.

Basal tonic activation propensity was measured on a reporter cell line. Jurkat cells expressing mClover under the control of Nur77 were transduced with the VHH and scFv CAR cohorts, alongside FMC63 and 14G2a CARs. Although no overall significant difference was determined between both cohorts, the scFv CARs showed 4/9 clones with high tonic activation, while 3/10 were identified in the VHH cohort (Figure 2E). None of the biophysical antibody properties described above, nor the surface CAR density predicted this enhanced tonic activity. The avidity effect of CAR T cell engagement with antigen-bearing target cells was analyzed via application of centrifugal force (Lumicks Avidigo, Amsterdam) using SupT1 cells transduced to express low and high surface levels of CD123 (CD123low and CD123high, respectively) and MOLM-14 target cell lines. Surface levels of CD123 were measured at 10,178, 166,023, and 6,601 copies/cell, respectively (Figure S5B). Target-effector cell interaction strength was consistent with antigen expression levels. scFv CARs had a higher avidity to the SupT1high cell line (Figure 2F), and scFv binding affinity correlated with higher avidity to the SupT1low/high cells (p = 0.029, 0.0283), while VHH binding affinity did not (Figure 2G).

Comparing in vitro performance of matched affinity VHH or scFv CARs

In vitro cytotoxicity and cytokine release was determined for both CAR T groups against SupT1 non-transduced (NT), SupT1low, SupT1high, and MOLM-14 showing efficient cytotoxicity toward antigen expressing cell lines (Figure 3A). Both cohorts were comparable (no significant differences) with no evidence of correlation between binding affinity and cytotoxicity (Figure 3A; Figures S5C and S5D).

VHH CARs also showed a significantly higher IL2 secretion compared with scFv CARs when co-cultured against SupT1 CD123high and MOLM-14 for 24 h (Figures 3B; S5E). 7/9 paired VHH CARs showed higher IL2 levels compared with their respective scFv CAR counterparts against SupT1 CD123+ cell lines and 8/9 on MOLM-14 co-cultures (Figures 3B; S5E). Similarly, IFNγ levels were also higher among the VHH CAR cohort on CD123high cells and the MOLM-14 co-cultures in particular (Figures 3B; S5F). The VHH CARs showed reduced donor variation compared with scFv CARs (p = 0.03) (Figure S5G). Assessment of CD69 and CD25 upregulation on CAR T cells at 24 and 72 h, respectively showed no difference in tonic signaling between groups vs. CD123-negative cell line and SupT1low targets, while the VHHs showed greater early CD69 against higher antigen densities in SupT1high cells (Figure 3C).

Expansion and exhaustion of CAR T cells in response to antigenic stimulation was assayed after 7 days co-culture with target cells. The exhaustion profile, as measured by population of cells expressing 0, 1, 2, or 3 exhaustion markers (LAG-3, PD-1, and TIM-3), showed that exhaustion increased in line with target antigen density, except for NT PBMCs. There was a higher proportion of exhausted VHH CARs compared with the scFv CARs after co-incubation with target cells (Figure 3D). Proliferation was negligible (<5-fold) after co-incubation with SupT1 NT target cells for all CARs. Median expansion in VHH and scFv CAR cohorts was 43- and 38-fold, respectively; in response to CD123high cells, the scFv CAR sc25 did not proliferate despite cytotoxicity (Figures 3E; S5H). Phenotype analysis of expanded CARs indicated a shift toward CD8+ expansion in scFv CARs and CD4+ expansion in the VHH CARs (Figures 3F; S5I).

Comparison of CAR efficacy in an MOLM-14 xenograft NSG in vivo model

A CD123+ xenograft tumor model was established to compare in vivo efficacy of CAR groups. Human AML-derived MOLM-14 cells were engineered to express firefly luciferase as a reporter of tumor burden (MOLM-14_FLuc). Preliminary tumor engraftment and CAR dose escalation studies identified a sub-optimal CAR dose as an efficacy challenge model (Figure S6).

NSG mice were engrafted with 1 × 105 MOLM-14_Fluc cells per mouse 7 days prior to receiving intravenous injections of 2 × 106 CAR T cell/mouse via the tail vein (n = 5, unless stated otherwise). NT PBMCs were included as negative control. Survival and tumor burden (bioluminescence imaging, BLI) were monitored throughout the experiment, while tissues and blood samples were analyzed via flow cytometry after termination (based on humane endpoints) (Figure 4A).

Mice receiving NT PBMCs reached limiting tumor burden (according to humane endpoints) by day 8, while those dosed with VHH CARs showed improved control of tumor and subsequent survival benefit over both NT and scFv CAR groups (median survival: NT = 8 days, scFv CAR = 16 days, VHH CAR = 27 days) (Figure 4B, C; Figure S7). While no correlation was identified between binding affinity and median survival (Pearson’s r = −0.1145), each VHH CAR showed improved median survival compared with the respective scFv CAR counterpart, except B1-sc23, which was identical (Figures 4C and S8).

Due to the challenging nature of the MOLM-14 NSG mouse model adopted, most animals reached the humane endpoint for tumor burden before the prespecified study endpoint of 40 days. Following sacrifice, MOLM-14 cells were identified in the bone marrow across all CAR treatment groups, with the VHH CAR cohort showing a greater proportion of mice with low MOLM-14 levels (Figures 4D; S9A and S9B). Fewer MOLM-14 events were also detected in the blood of mice post sacrifice. All MOLM-14 cells detected in the bone marrow were CD123+ with levels comparable to the PBMC NT group, ruling out tumor growth by antigen escape (Figure 4E). Human T cell presence in the bone marrow at sacrifice was identified in both CAR cohorts, with both CAR T groups showing significantly higher levels compared with NT treated cohort (Figures 4F; S9C and S9D), and an average of 63% (±14%) and 60% (±21%) of CAR+ T cells for scFv and VHH cohorts (Figures 4G; S9E). Similarly to what was identified during in vitro CAR characterization, the VHH CAR cohort exhibited significantly higher levels of CD4+ T cells compared with the scFv counterparts (Figure 4F).

Analysis of significant correlative factors (p < 0.05) (Figure S10A) suggested that across all binders both in vitro cytokine secretion (IL2 and IFNγ) (r > 0.7) and Tm50 (r = 0.25) correlated positively with median survival (Figure S10), while surface positive patch area (r < −0.7) and predicted aggregation score correlated negatively as a function of antibody size (Figures S10B–S10D). Other biophysical factors, including affinity within the range studied, showed little or moderate correlation or significance with survival (R2 = 0.00131) or IL2 secretion (R2 = 0.00481). CAR-MOLM-14 cell avidity correlated with median survival in the scFv-derived CAR group (R2 = 0. 2834 vs. MOLM-14 cells), while there was substantially less variation between the avidity of VHH CARs (Figures S10I–S10K).

Discussion

CAR T cell therapy has had a significant impact on the field of immunotherapy owing to its success in treating hematological malignancies. The scFv antibody format has historically been the binding moiety of choice within CAR constructs, with 6 out of the 7 approved CAR T cell products adopting it. VHH CARs were first described by Sharifzadeh et al. in 2013.19 The recent FDA approval of the VHH-based CAR T therapeutic, Ciltacabtagene autoleucel, for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma in 2022, has further validated this alternative format.9 In the wider therapeutic landscape, only three additional VHH-based therapeutics have been approved for clinical use: Caplacizumab (USA), Envafolimab (China), and Ozoralizumab (Japan).20 A further 17 VHH-based therapeutics are in active clinical evaluation, including three CAR T products, Ciltacabtagene autoleucel (NCT04133636), and a CD19/20 bispecific VHH-derived CAR T cell (NCT06880913, NCT03881761), alongside a 2021 anti-CD7 CAR trial for AML (NCT04004637) (source: www.clinicaltrials.gov).

Adoption of VHHs over scFv fragments poses several potential advantages in cell therapy. VHHs, which are naturally devoid of a light chain, enable simple library design, engineering and reformatting into single- and multi-antigen-targeting CARs with limited risk of instability, aggregation, or light chain mispairing events in CAR or library format. In contrast, CAR self-aggregation caused by scFv framework interactions can cause tonic signaling events in the absence of stimulation, unlike VHHs, which are monomeric by nature.21 The high degree of sequence similarity to the human VH3 gene family, which is heavily used during human immune responses,22 further reduces engineering liabilities. Indeed, multiple pre-clinical and clinical studies have shown no increase in anti-drug antibodies against VHHs over conventional humanized antibodies.23,24

While comparisons of monoclonal VHH/scFv antibodies have been widely performed and recently reviewed elsewhere,13 comparative studies between scFv- and VHH-derived CARs have been limited to individual clones in an unpaired manner. Arguably the most characterized CAR binder is FMC63, the anti-CD19 scFv used in axicabtagene ciloleucel (Yescarta), tisagencleucel (Kymriah), brexucabtagene autoleucel (Tecartus), and lisocabtagene maraleucel (Breyanzi), which was recently compared against two VHH-derived CARs. The authors found a comparable behavior across all measured indicators including cytotoxicity, proliferation, IL2 and IFNγ secretion with no loss of efficacy in vitro.25 The anti-PSMA scFv J591 and anti-mesothelin scFv derived from amatuximab were also compared against fully human VHH counterparts showing that in vitro performance was again similar. While in vivo the PSMA CARs performed equally well, the mesothelin VHH CAR induced significantly greater tumor clearance and survival compared with the scFv, despite a similar CAR expansion.26 VHH-derived CARs targeting a range of cancer-associated antigens have shown promise, including but not limited to CEACAM5,27 VEGFR2,28 GPC2,29 PD-L1,30 CD38,31 B7-H3,32 CD33,33 CD20,33 CD7,34 PSMA, and mesothelin,26 with pre-clinical data generally showing strong activation, cytotoxicity, and cytokine release.

To better compare and characterize VHH- and scFv-derived binding domains in the context of a CD123- targeting CAR T therapeutic modality, we performed an antibody discovery campaign from immunized libraries derived from llamas and rats (for VHH and scFv, respectively). Initial screening of the antibody pools showed a wider spread of domains targeted in the VHH pool compared with the scFvs, likely due to the CDR3 structure of VHHs enabling convex shapes with varied protein-protein interfaces or contact points, such as binding to cryptic epitopes, driving a more diverse epitope profile as previously shown.35,36,37 From these campaigns a curated set of domain- and affinity-matched binders were selected for further evaluation. A limitation of this approach is that the broadest antibody selections were available against the D2 domain of CD123 rather than a membrane proximal epitope and that despite attempting to create a uniform pool and minimize functional differences due to CAR T synapse distance, functional variations may still arise from the epitope targeted and binding angle.38

The curated binders were cloned into a standard 4-1BB-CD3ζ CAR format39 and compared through in vitro and in vivo study. The strength of effector-target interaction has previously been implicated in CAR T cell efficacy.40,41 Here, there was no difference between each CAR class as measured by avidity, but there was less variation in the VHH CARs, suggesting a more uniform interaction. Both groups were equally effective in killing high- and low-density targets after 72 h, yet the VHH CARs maintained a significantly higher IL2 and IFNγ secretion against all antigen positive cells in vitro. Interestingly, proliferative response showed a higher proportion of CD4+ T cells for the VHH CAR group, which may be linked to the higher IL2 secretion detected in cytotoxicity assays.42,43,44 Finally, in vivo the VHH CAR cohort demonstrated a better efficacy with improved survival and decreased overall tumor burden in a challenging MOLM-14 AML model in NSG mice. A limitation of this study was the use of a single donor from previous in vitro experiments. Importantly, although binder affinity constant did not correlate with therapeutic efficacy, the VHH CAR candidates showed a clear survival advantage, with the IL2 and IFNγ cytokine release levels detected in vitro strongly correlating with in vivo performance.

Our data showed improved function of VHH- versus scFv-derived CARs in vivo. The biophysical characteristics of the scFv and VHH antibody pools—including thermal stability and binding affinity—fell within expected ranges for their respective formats45 and were broadly comparable, excluding a trivial explanation for the differences observed. VHHs replace the hydrophobic amino acids typically required for VH-VL interface packing with hydrophilic residues. Combined with the CDR3 loop capping the VL surface, this substitution reduces solvent-exposed hydrophobic patches, resulting in a low-aggregating, highly soluble molecule. Additionally, the antigen-binding paratopes of VHHs are often elongated, enabling greater conformational flexibility and broader epitope accessibility compared with scFvs.13 We postulate that these fundamental differences in molecular architecture yield a smaller and more stable CAR binding domain, resulting in a tighter immune synapse that translates to improved CAR function and CAR T cell efficacy.

In conclusion, this study presents the first in-depth, parallel comparison of matched VHH- and scFv-based CAR T cells targeting CD123, integrating biophysical profiling, in vitro cytotoxicity, and in vivo efficacy. Collectively, these findings support the consideration of VHH domains as a promising alternative to scFvs for future CAR T cell engineering, given their favorable molecular and functional characteristics, and suggest that the design principles identified here may be broadly applicable to CARs directed against other target antigens.

Materials and methods

Animal models and cell lines

4- to 5-week-old NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NOD SCID gamma; NSG) mice were purchased from the Charles River Laboratories, UK, and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions (Hammersmith Hospital, Imperial college London). All experiments were approved by The Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body and the United Kingdom Home Office (Autolus PPL No. PP7328369). All procedures were performed in accordance with the United Kingdom Home Office Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and adhered to Imperial College London or Autolus SOPs.

Human PBMCs were derived either from buffy cones (NHS-BT) or leukopak (BioIVT). SupT1 cells (ATCC, UK), Jurkat E6-1 cells (ATCC, UK), MOLM-14 cells (DSMZ, Germany), and PBMCs were cultured in R-10 medium, consisting of RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (HI-FBS, Hyclone, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 2 mM GlutaMax (Sigma), unless specified otherwise. HEK293T cells (ATCC, UK) were cultured in I-10 medium, consisting of Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% HI-FBS and 2 mM GlutaMax. ExpiCHO-S cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were cultured under serum-free conditions in ExpiCHO Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All commercially available cell lines were cultured as per manufacturers’ instructions. All cells were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 unless otherwise stated.

SupT1-CD123 cell lines were generated by retroviral transduction with either full-length human CD123 (UniProt ref P26951) or truncated CD123 as a chimeric extracellular protein with CD8 stalk and transmembrane domain. SupT1-CD123 cells were diluted to monoclonality before CD123 copy number was assessed and CD123low or CD123high cell line selection. MOLM-14 cells were retrovirally transduced with firefly luciferase (FLuc) followed by the T2A cleavage site, an HA tag, and CD8 transmembrane domain carrying the Zeocin resistance gene for selection purposes.

DNA construct design

VHH and scFv sequences were derived from library sources as described below using either Sanger sequencing or NGS approaches. Antibody-coding DNA sequences were cloned into both murine IgG2a-Fc chimeric antibody format in the Abvec (Addgene) plasmid construct for soluble protein expression and into the SFG (MoMLV-based retroviral) construct for CAR expression. Second-generation CAR constructs consisted of an RQR8 sort/suicide marker,18 self-cleaving T2A peptide, antibody sequence with a huCD8 stalk and transmembrane domain, and a 41BB/CD3ζ signaling endodomain.

Immunization and library construction

Anti-CD123 VHH antibodies were generated through immune phage display library panning using a combination of genetic and recombinant protein-based immunizations in 2 llamas (Llama glama). Briefly, the extracellular and transmembrane portion of the open reading frame of human CD123 (GenBank: P26951) was cloned into the pVAC2 immunization vector (InvivoGen, Fr) before amplification as endotoxin-free plasmid. Recombinant CD123 protein was procured for boost immunizations (Cat ILA-H52H6 AcroBiosystems, China). Immunization was performed at Wageningen University, the Netherlands, with a DNA prime and recombinant protein boost strategy before collection of blood samples for seroconversion analysis. Antibody display libraries representing separate animals were generated from peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) isolated on days 49 and 70. PBL RNA was purified (RNAeasy Maxi Kit, Qiagen), and synthesis of whole cDNA was performed via oligo-dT priming with Superscript III reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) activity. RNA was digested with RNaseH, and RT-PCR reactions were performed using primer sets specific for VHH that included the PST1 and NOT1 restriction sites. An ∼400-bp heavy-chain-only antibody-coding DNA fragment was fractionated through gel electrophoresis (Zymoclean PCR clean up kit, Zymo research, USA). Finally, the cleaned VHH coding fragments were ligated into the pRL144 phage display vector5 as a fusion protein with phagemid P3 before electroporation into TG1 E. coli yielding a library size of 5–9 × 107.

Antibody isolation through phage display

VHH antibodies were isolated via phage display panning using immobilized human CD123-His tag with deselection against an irrelevant His-tagged protein at 10-fold molar excess. Antigen was isolated on Immunotubes at a concentration of 1 μg/mL with decreasing concentrations in subsequent rounds of selection. VHH-bearing phages were added at a 100- to 1000-fold representation of library diversity and incubated at room temperature (RT) with agitation for 1 h prior to washing 10× with PBS.Tween (0.01%). Washing stringency was increased with consecutive panning rounds. Finally, both phage titrations and a polyclonal antigen-specific ELISA were used to assess target-specific enrichment before single-colony picking and sequencing via the Sanger method.

scFv isolation was performed separately; see Nannini et al.14

ELISA

Antibody binding ELISA

His-tagged CD123 was coated at 1 μg/mL in 50 μL/well on a 96-well ELISA plate (Nunc Maxisorp) overnight at 4°C. Plates were washed three times in PBS 0.05% Tween 20 between each step. Blocking was in 200 μL PBS 2% BSA for 1 h at RT on a plate shaker. Serial dilutions of antibodies were prepared in PBS 0.5% BSA and added and incubated for 1 h at RT on a plate shaker in 50 μL/well in duplicate. Secondary anti-mouse antibody HRP conjugate (Jackson ImmunoResearch) (1:5,000 dilution in PBS 0.5% BSA) was added for 1 h at RT (50 μL/well). Assay development occurred with 45 μL/well of 1-Step Ultra TMB reagent (Thermo Fisher scientific) for 5 min at RT. The reaction was blocked with 45 μL/well of 1 M H2SO4 and absorbance read at OD450nm on a Varioskan Lux plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cytokine ELISA

Interleukin-2 (IL2) (Biolegend) and Interferon gamma (IFNy) (Biolegend) concentrations in supernatant samples were measured using ELISA Max Deluxe set according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Expression and purification of antibodies

Antibodies were expressed as VHH-Fc carrying a murine IgG2a Fc domain by transient transfection in ExpiCHO cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Supernatant from transfected ExpiCHO cells was clarified using 0.22-μm filers and purified using HiTrap MabSelect PrismA (GE Healthcare) affinity chromatography. A HiTrap MabSelect Prism 1-mL column was equilibrated with 5 column volumes of PBS (pH 7.4). Supernatant was applied to the column using Akta Pure system at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The column was washed with 20 column volumes of PBS and sample eluted with 3 mL of IgG elution buffer (Pierce) at 0.5 mL/min, which was directly loaded onto 2 HiTrap 5-mL desalting columns previously equilibrated in PBS and collected on a 96-well plate using a fraction collector unit. Fractions containing the protein were pooled and quantified using OD280nm absorbance on a Nanodrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific) instrument, using specific molecular weight (MW) and extinction coefficients.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

The Prometheus NT.48 NanoDSF instrument was used to characterize the thermal and chemical unfolding of antibodies under native conditions. A dye-free protocol was used whereby the intrinsic fluorescence of tryptophan and tyrosine was measured by scanning samples at 330 and 350 nm to determine protein unfolding over a thermal increase ramp of run at 1°C/min temperature increments from 20°C to 95°C. Tmonset, Tm50, and secondary events were calculated via first derivative of 350/330 nm ratio. Protein samples were normalized to 0.2–1 mg/mL.

Multi-angle dynamic light scattering

Aggregation propensity and average particle size of the test proteins were determined using a Zetasizer Ultra device and ZS Xplorer software (Malvern Panalytical) by MADLS. Samples were loaded into a low-volume quartz cuvette (Malvern Panalytical, ZEN2112) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Triplicate measurements were taken for each sample.

Domain mapping flow cytometry

SupT1 cells engineered to express truncated CD123 domains (membrane distal - D1, D2 and NTD-membrane proximal) followed by an IRES-eBFP were used for domain mapping of antibodies. Binding to each domain was analyzed by VHH or scFv-Murine IgG2a-Fc chimeric protein staining at 1 μg/mL against 2 × 105 cells of the appropriate SupT1 cell line. Secondary detection was performed using anti-Mouse APC conjugate (Jackson Laboratory, USA) and before analysis on the MACSQuant X (Miltenyi Biotech, Germany). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (BD).

Cell binding affinity

Cell binding analysis was determined by antibody titration using a 4-fold serial dilution from 10 μg/mL. The human cell lines SupT1-NT, SupT1-CD123, and MOLM-14 were used (1 × 105 cells per well). For MOLM-14 cells, Fc receptors were blocked using Human TruStain FcX Fc Receptor Blocking Solution (BioLegend, 422301) for 10 min at RT, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following incubation with primary antibodies, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG2a (Invitrogen) for 30 min at RT in the dark. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS and stained with SYTOX Blue Dead Cell Stain (Invitrogen) to exclude non-viable cells. Data were acquired using a MACSQuant X. Dose-response curves were generated by plotting fluorescence intensity against the logarithm of antibody concentration. Cell binding affinity calculation was performed using the one-site specific binding function on GraphPad Prism v10.5.

Surface plasmon resonance

Antibodies in mouse IgG2a-Fc format were analyzed using Biacore 8 K instruments (Cytiva). Antibodies were captured on flow cell 2 on a Protein A Series S CM5 chip (Cytiva). HBS-P+ buffer was used as running buffer in all experimental conditions. Recombinant His-tagged human CD123 (Acro Biosciences and Sino biologicals separately) was used as the “analyte” and injected over the respective flow cells with 150-s contact time and 1,000-s dissociation at 30 μL/min of flow rate with a constant temperature of 25°C (250 nM with 2-fold serial dilutions, sensograms colored by concentration in order from high to low green, orange, blue, red, yellow, purple, brown, pink, light green, and light orange). In each experiment, flow cell 1 was used for reference subtraction. A “0 concentration” sensorgram of buffer alone was used as a double reference subtraction to factor for drift. Data were fit to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model. Since a capture system was used, a local maximal analytical response (Rmax) parameter was used for the data fitting in each case.

Epitope binning methods

Epitope binning experiments were performed in a classical sandwich assay format using the Carterra LSAXT (Carterra, Utah, USA). A HC30M chip was conditioned with four 60-s injections of 50 mM NaOH, before activation (7 min) with a freshly prepared 1:1:1 mixture of HBSTE, 100 mM sulpho-N-hydroxysuccinimide, and 400 mM 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride. VHHs and scFvs prepared at 50 μg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5) were coupled to the surface: the chip surface was first activated with a freshly prepared 1:1:1 mix of HBSTE, 100 mM sulpho-N-hydroxysuccinimide, and 400 mM 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (7 min injection), followed by the VHHs and scFvs, with 10-min injection time. The chip surface was then quenched with 0.5 M ethanolamine (pH 8.5, 7-min injection), followed by a 30-s injection with 10 mM glycine (pH 2.5). Following regeneration scouting to establish that 3 × 30 s of pH 1.8 glycine (10 mM) was the most effective regeneration agent, sequential injections of 300 nM CD123 protein (Acro) (4 min) were followed immediately by 800 nM of the first antibody as an analyte (4 min). At the end of each CD213 and antibody fragment cycle the surfaces were regenerated with 10 mM glycine (pH 1.8) using three 30-s regeneration cycles, and the cycle repeated with CD123 and the next antibody fragment. Data were analyzed using the Carterra Epitope software.

Alternatively, a capture-based sandwich assay on a Serries S CM5 chip (Cytiva) functionalized with a mouse capture kit (Cytiva) was performed on a Biacore 8 k. Antibodies were captured on the chip surface, and residual anti-mouse binding sites were saturated with murine IgG2a Fc domain prior to analyte injection. Recombinant His-tagged human CD123 (Sinobiological) was used as analyte 1 and injected over the captured antibodies at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 150 s at 25°C, followed by injection of a second antibody as analyte 2 for 150-s association and 150-s dissociation at the same flow rate. The surface was regenerated by two successive injections of 10 mM glycine (pH 1.7) (Cytiva). Reference subtraction was performed using flow cell 1, which lacked primary antibody capture but subjected to the same Fc saturation, analyte 1, and analyte 2 injections to correct for non-specific binding and bulk refractive index effects. Competition was assessed relative to cycles lacking second antibody injection (analyte 2 = 0). Data were analyzed using the Biacore Insight Evaluation Software. All SPR experiments were performed at 25°C.

In silico modeling

Modeling of aggregation, solvent-accessible charged patch area, overall charge, and 3D structures was performed using Maestro Bioluminate software (Schrödinger). Calculation of hydrogen atom positions, bond network optimization, and energy minimization was performed using the Protein Preparation Wizard tool. Hu-mAb and OASis modeling was performed as per Marks et al.15

γ-Retrovirus production

HEK293T cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 106 cells/mL in 10-cm dishes (Corning) in 10 mL of I-10 medium for 18–24 h prior to transfection. Cells were transiently transfected using GeneJuice transfection reagent (Millipore) containing a DNA mixture of Moloney murine leukemia virus Gag-Pol expression plasmid (pEQ-Pam3-E, a gift from E. Vanin, Baylor College of Medicine), an RD114 envelope-expressing plasmid (RD114, RDF, a gift from M. Collins, University College London), and a plasmid expressing the CAR T construct on an SFG retroviral backbone. The mixture was prepared in plain IMDM medium at a ratio of 1.5:1:1.5, respectively, with each plate receiving 12.5 μg of total DNA. Cells were cultured 37°C, 5% CO2, and medium was exchanged to a smaller volume 15 h post transfection. γ-Retroviral supernatants were harvested 48 h post transfection and frozen at −80°C prior to use.

γ-Retrovirus titration and MOI calculation

Frozen γ-retroviral supernatants were thawed, 4-fold serially diluted, and used to transduce 25,000 HEK293T cells in the presence of 8 μg/mL Polybrene (TR-1003, Sigma-Aldrich) in 48-well plates (Corning). Plates were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 25 min, and cells cultured for 72 h as described above. Levels of RQR8 transgene expression (an indication of CAR expression) were measured by flow cytometry and equated to transduction efficiency. Viral titers and MOI were calculated from cells <20% transduced.

CAR T cell generation

Cryopreserved human PBMCs were thawed and activated for 48 h in R-10 medium at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL using TransAct (Miltenyi Biotec), as per manufacturers guidelines. In addition, cells were supplemented with cytokines (Miltenyi Biotec). Activated T cells were harvested, washed, and plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in Retronectin (TakaraBio)-coated 6-well plates (Corning) in R-10 medium supplemented with cytokines, at a final concentration of 10 ng/mL, each. Transduction was performed by layering titered viral supernatant at MOI = 1 or 2.5 (as indicated), followed by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 40 min. Cells were cultured for 72 h, as described previously. Cells were then harvested, washed, and an aliquot assayed for transduction efficiency by flow cytometry. The remainder were re-cultured at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in R-10 medium supplemented with cytokines, each, for a further 24 or 72 h. Upon harvest, cells were either processed for cytotoxicity assays or cryopreserved in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich) with HI-FBS.

Flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assays

NT or CAR T cells were prepared as described above, were co-cultured at a 1:4 E:T ratio with target cells in a 96-well flat-bottomed plate (Corning) with R-10 medium. The co-cultured cells were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 24 or 72 h. At the point of analysis, co-cultured cells were washed, stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (see below), washed again, and resuspended in 10% Invitrogen CountBright Absolute Counting beads (Invitrogen) in FACS buffer consisting of 2% HI-FBS in PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Absolute cell numbers were determined by flow cytometry (see below).

Flow cytometry

Harvested cells were processed for flow cytometry in 96-well round-bottomed plates at a density of 2 × 106 or 5 × 106 cells/mL, where possible. Briefly, cells were washed in FACS buffer and pelleted by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Cells were stained in 100-μL volumes in fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human antibody cocktails at 1:50 dilution, unless otherwise stated, for 30 min on ice in the dark. Cells were washed twice in FACS buffer and resuspended in FACS buffer containing the appropriate viability dye at 1:1,000 dilution. Single-stained compensation controls were prepared using the appropriate fluorochrome-conjugated antibody against UltraComp Compensation beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. FMO controls (fluorescence minus one) were stained with all antibodies except one to set gating thresholds during analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using MACSQuant 10 or MACSQuant X analyzers (Miltenyi Biotech) and data processed and analyzed using the FlowJo analysis software (BD).

Viral titration and transduction efficiency

Harvested HEK293T cells or primary T cells were stained with R-Phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated soluble CD123 (ACRO BioSystems, USA) and/or Allophycocyanine (APC)-conjugated anti-CD34 QBEnd10 (R&D systems, FAB7227A, recognizing RQR8), followed by SYTOX Blue Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as per manufacturer’s instructions.

FACS-based cytotoxicity analysis

CAR T cells were evaluated by staining with PE/Cyanine 7 (PE-Cy7)-conjugated anti-human CD3 (BioLegend, SK7) and PE-conjugated anti-human CD2 (BioLegend, RPA-2.10) and RQR8 antibody. Live cells were selected using SYTOX Blue, and target cells were defined by exclusion of the CAR T cell population. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-human CD69 (BioLegend, FN50) and Alexa Fluor 488 anti-human CD25 (BioLegend, BC96) were used to evaluate activation of CAR T cells at 24 and 72 h post co-culture respectively. Exhaustion phenotype was evaluated using a cocktail of VioGreen-conjugated REAfinity anti-human CD3 (Miltenyi Biotec, SK7), VioBlue-conjugated REAfinity anti-human CD8 (Miltenyi Biotec, HIT8a), RQR8, VioBright 515-conjugated REAfinity LAG-3 anti-human CD223 (Miltenyi Biotec, 17B4), PE-conjugated REAfinity PD-1 anti-human CD279 (Miltenyi Biotec, PD-1.3.1.3), and PE-Vio 770-conjugated REAfinity TIM-3 anti-human CD366 (Miltenyi Biotec, F38-2E2), followed by SYTOX AADvanced Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as per manufacturer’s instructions.

Target cell antigen density analysis

Antigen density was determined using PE-conjugated soluble CD123 and BD Quantibrite Beads, as per manufacturer’s instructions.

Mouse Tissue processing

Bone marrow and cardiac blood (terminal bleed) were harvested from animals at sacrifice and processed separately. Bone marrow was extracted by centrifugation of the femurs, tibia, and fibula, as described previously (Ferrari et al.46). Terminal bleeds were obtained by cardiac puncture into heparinized capillary tubes (BD Microcontainer 365966, BD). Single-cell suspensions were washed by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Red blood cells were lysed using 20× volume of ACK Lysis Buffer (Sigma) at 37°C for 15 min. Cells were washed as above and resuspended in the appropriate volume of FACS buffer. Bone marrow cells were passed through 70-μm cell strainers (Corning) in the presence of FACS buffer (PBS + 2% HI-FBS). Cell concentration for each tissue was measured prior to further processing.

Mouse tissue analysis

Tissues were processed into single-cell suspensions as described above. Cells were washed in PBS and stained with 100 μL Invitrogen LIVE/DEAD Fixable Violet Dead Cell Stain Kit, for 405-nm excitation (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as per manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were washed twice in FACS buffer. Murine and human Fc receptors were blocked in an Fc-blocking cocktail consisting Human TruStain FcX and TruStain FcX (anti-mouse CD16/32, clone 93) (both Biolegend) at 1:20 dilution, each, in a 50-μL volume at RT for 10 min. Cells were stained without washing in 50 μL 2× concentrate antibody mastermixes to detect either MOLM-14_FLuc cells or T cells. Both mastermixes contained eFluor 450-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 (30-F11) and eFluor 450-conjugated Ter119 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In addition, the MOLM-14-detecting mastermix consisted of PE-conjugated anti-human CD123 (6H6) and APC-conjugated anti-CD33 (P67.6) (Biolegend), while the T cell-detecting mastermix consisted of PE-conjugated RQR8 (FAB7227A), APC-conjugated CD3 (OKT3), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-CD8 (HIT8a) (Biolegend), and PE-Vio 770-conjugated recombinant anti-CD4 (REA623-SK3, Miltenyi Biotech). Cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer prior to flow cytometry.

Basal T cell activity in Jurkat-Nur77 mClover

The Jurkat cell line was engineered to express mClover under the control of the NUR77 (NR4A1) early activation gene (Jurkat-Nur77) as a reporter cell line for CAR activation. Briefly, Jurkat cells were nucleofected with sgRNA, one targeting the C terminus of NR4A1 gene and one targeting HPRT1 Exon2 and two repair templates encoding mClover and a puromycin resistance cassette. This allowed the puromycin selection of edited cells. Selected Jurkat-NUR77 cells were transduced to express CARs as described previously. 50,000 cells were plated on flat-bottom 96-well TC multi-well plates (n = 3). After 72 h, cells were harvested and stained for RQR8 transduction marker and viability (7AAD) for gating purposes and intrinsic mClover expression analyzed. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Lumicks CAR T cell avidity analysis

Concanavalin A (5 mg/mL in PBS) was coated onto Ibidi chips (Ibidi cat. 80606) for 30 min at RT (30 μL/well) before aspirating and drying overnight at 37°C. Target cells in suspension were added to respective channels with the chip inverted and cultured at 37°C until a monolayer had formed. Medium exchange was performed midway through monolayer adherence. Effector cells in R10 medium were stained with 100 μL CellTrace Far Red dye (C34564, Invitrogen) diluted 1:2000 for 15 min (37°C) prior to washing in PBS and resuspension in R10 medium. Avidity measurements were performed by adding 40 μL of effector cells (8.75 × 105 cells/mL)/channel to the chip, inverting until monolayer target cell engagement (approx. 2.5 min) and cell-cell binding analyzed by image capture. R10 (60 μL/channel) was added prior to centrifugation (1,000 × g for 2 min) to remove low interaction cells before re-imaging the chip. Cell avidity was determined as ratio of cells retaining binding post centrifugation and expressed as percentage with untransduced (UNT) PBMC interaction subtracted.

CD123+ tumor xenograft in vivo model

4- to 5-week-old female NSG mice were intravenously (tail vein) injected with the indicated doses of MOLM-14_FLuc tumor cells (passage 3). Tumor engraftment was monitored by bi-weekly measurements of bioluminescence signals (BLI) using the IVIS imaging system (Xenogen Corporation). Briefly, mice were anesthetized by isofluorane inhalation and intraperitoneally injected with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin (Promega). Images of the ventral surface of the mice were captured 10–15 min post luciferin administration. Frozen NT or CAR T cells that were transduced with the CAR construct at an MOI of 2.5, and with a minimum transduction efficiency of 75% (as measured by RQR8 transgene expression), were thawed and rested for 24 h at a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL in R-10 medium supplemented with cytokines. 7 days post tumor engraftment (day 0 in the assays), cells were washed twice and resuspended in PBS. Mice were intravenously injected at the indicated doses in 100 μL volumes. Tumor progression was monitored as described above. Animals were sacrificed by overdose of isofluorane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation if BLI ≤1 × 1010 or if weight loss exceeded 20% over the course of the experiment.

Statistical analysis and data presentation

Statistical analysis and graphical representations were performed using the GraphPad Prism 10 software package for Windows (Graphpad Software, La Jolla, CA; RRID: SCR_000306). Specific statistical tests used for each figure were described in the corresponding figure legend. Survival determined from the time of tumor cell injection was analyzed by the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences in survival between groups were compared by the log rank test.
