Stability of localized solutions to lattice dynamical systems
Bocheng Ruan, Jack M. Hughes, Jason J. Bramburger

Abstract
Localized patterns are spatially confined structures that arise in lattice dynamical systems and play an important role in physics, biology, and materials science. While their existence and bifurcation structure are well-understood, the stability of these solutions remains largely unexplored, particularly in discrete and high-dimensional settings. In this work, we develop a general theoretical framework to analyze the spectral stability of localized steady states in one-dimensional and multi-dimensional rectangular lattices. Our approach leverages the properties of front and back solutions, combined with a discrete Evans function formulation, to characterize the spectrum of localized solutions. We prove that, for well-separated regions of localization, the Evans function asymptotically factorizes into contributions from the underlying fronts and backs, allowing explicit counting of unstable eigenvalues. This framework applies to solutions with single or multiple plateaus, including oscillatory and multi-pulse configurations. We illustrate the results on a real-valued cubic–quintic Ginzburg–Landau lattice, a prototypical Nagumo-type system, and provide numerical demonstrations of bifurcation structures and eigenvalue spectra.

Introduction

Localized patterns play a central role in a wide range of spatially extended systems arising in applications across physics, biology, and materials science. Their formation and structure have been extensively studied over the past several decades, resulting in a well-developed theory describing the existence and bifurcations of such states in both continuous and discrete settings. In contrast, their stability properties remain comparatively less understood. We refer the reader to the recent review
[
5
]
, as well as earlier surveys
[

,
16
]
, for comprehensive overviews of localized patterns, their bifurcation structure, and applications.

In this paper, we study localized steady-state solutions in lattice dynamical systems, focusing on prototypical Nagumo-type lattices
[

\dot{U}_{n}=\theta(U_{n+1}+U_{n-1}-2U_{n})+f(U_{n},\mu),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(1.1)

\begin{split}u^{(1)}_{n+1}&=u^{(2)}_{n},\\

One of the most striking features of lattice systems is propagation failure

The central question of this work is the stability of these localized solutions. Linearizing about a steady state
\{U_{n}^{*}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with a perturbation
U_{n}=U_{n}^{*}+V_{n}e^{\lambda t}
leads to the eigenvalue problem

\lambda V_{n}=\theta(V_{n+1}+V_{n-1}-2V_{n})+f^{\prime}(U_{n}^{*})V_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(1.3)

\begin{bmatrix}v^{(1)}_{n+1}\\
v^{(2)}_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&2-\frac{(f^{\prime}(U_{n}^{*})-\lambda)}{\theta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v^{(1)}_{n}\\

(1.4)

For

Our method exploits the structure of front and back solutions to infer the stability of the localized states they generate. The key analytical tool is the discrete Evans function

For large and well-separated regions of localization, the Evans function factorizes asymptotically into a product of Evans functions for the underlying fronts and backs, allowing a clear and general characterization of stability across families of solutions.

This framework extends previous PDE-based approaches
[
17
]
in several ways. First, it addresses lattice dynamical systems, requiring a discrete Evans function formulation. Second, in the small-coupling regime, the structural hypotheses on fronts, backs, and localized solutions can be verified analytically, rather than relying on numerical checks. Third, it handles localized states with multiple disconnected regions of localization (Figure
1

[
6
]
or computer-assisted proofs
[
8
]

2
Stability of localized solutions

In this section, we present our main theoretical contributions. Section
2.1
introduces exponential dichotomies and their role in characterizing the spectrum of lattice systems linearized about steady-states. In Section
2.2
, we outline a sequence of hypotheses and their implications, culminating in our main stability result, Theorem
2.4
, for localized structures with a single connected region of localization. Section
2.3
extends this result to steady states with two disconnected regions of localization and briefly discusses the extension to multiple regions, which follows by routine, though increasingly tedious, arguments of the same type.

2.1
Exponential dichotomies and the spectrum

Throughout this subsection we consider a linear non-autonomous difference equation

v_{n+1}=A_{n}v_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.1)

where each
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is nonsingular. The solution operator is denoted

\Phi(n,m)=\begin{cases}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{m}&n>m\\
I&n=m\\
A_{n}^{-1}\cdots A_{m-1}^{-1}&n<m.\end{cases}

(2.2)

Let us denote
\mathbb{Z}_{-}
as the nonpositive integers and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
as the nonnegative integers. We present the following definition from
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

The difference equation (
2.1
) has an
exponential dichotomy
on
J\in\{\mathbb{Z}_{-},\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{Z}\}
if there exists projectors
P(n)
,
n\in J
, in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constants
K,\alpha>0
such that

P(n)\Phi(n,m)=\Phi(n,m)P(m),\quad\forall n,m\in J,

and

\begin{split}\|\Phi(n,m)P(m)\|&\leq K\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(n-m)},\\
\|\Phi(m,n)(I-P(n))\|&\leq K\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(n-m)},\end{split}

for all
n\geq m
in
J
.

With the definition of an exponential dichotomy now presented, we turn to parameter dependent linear difference equations. Indeed, inspired by (
1.4
), consider the difference equation

v_{n+1}=A_{n}(\lambda)v_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.3)

for which the nonsingular
A_{n}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
depend continuously on a parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Considering the Hilbert space

\ell^{2}=\{\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|v_{n}|^{2}<\infty\},

we also define the linear mapping
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
acting on
v=\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\ell^{2}
by

[\mathcal{T}(\lambda)v]_{n}=v_{n+1}-A_{n}(\lambda)v_{n},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2.4)

We say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is in the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
if
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is not invertible on
\ell^{2}
for the given value of
\lambda
.

The spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
is further decomposed into two distinct components: the
point spectrum
and the
essential spectrum
. The point spectrum is all such
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is not invertible with Fredholm index zero, while the essential spectrum is the remaining elements of the spectrum. The connection between
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
and (
2.3
) is that
\lambda
does not belong to the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
if and only if (
2.3
) has an exponential dichotomy on both
\mathbb{Z}_{-}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and the associated projectors
P_{-}(n)
and
P_{+}(n)
, respectively, satisfy
\mathrm{rank}(P_{-}(n))=\mathrm{rank}(P_{+}(n))
. The values
i_{\pm}(\lambda)=\mathrm{rank}(P_{\pm}(n))
are called Morse indices, while the Fredholm index of
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is given by
\mathrm{rank}(P_{+}(n))-\mathrm{rank}(P_{-}(n))
.

Assuming that
A_{n}(\lambda)\to A_{\pm}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
as
n\to\pm\infty
, the essential spectrum is characterized by the asymptotic matrices
A_{\pm}(\lambda)
. The two possibilities are as follows:

1.

At least one of
A_{+}(\lambda)
or
A_{-}(\lambda)
has eigenvalues in the unit circle at
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is not invertible at this value of
\lambda
, and so
\lambda
belongs to the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
.

2.

If both
A_{\pm}(\lambda)
have no eigenvalues on the unit circle at
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, then
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is Fredholm. If the Fredholm index is non-zero, then this
\lambda
belongs to the essential spectrum.

Thus, we see that the essential spectrum can be characterized by simply examining the asymptotics of the sequence of parametrized matrices
\{A_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. The point spectrum is more difficult to characterize as it corresponds to having an eigenvector
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)v=0
. Such an eigenvector takes the form of a bounded solution of (
2.3
) belonging to
\ell^{2}
.

Application to lattice systems.

Recall that our interest in this work is to characterize the stability of solutions to lattice dynamical systems. Returning to the prototypical system (
1.1
), we saw that the stability of a bounded solution
\{U_{n}^{*}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
comes from analyzing the eigenvalue equation (
1.3
). Introducing the transformation
(v^{(1)},v^{(2)})=(V_{n-1},V_{n})
results in the
\lambda
-dependent linear maps (
1.3
) having

A_{n}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&2-\frac{(f^{\prime}(U_{n}^{*})-\lambda)}{\theta}\end{bmatrix},

(2.5)

while the operator
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is equivalent to (
1.4
). If
U_{n}^{*}\to U^{*}_{\pm}
as
n\to\pm\infty
then the asymptotic matrices become

A_{\pm}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&2-\frac{(f^{\prime}(U^{*}_{\pm})-\lambda)}{\theta}\end{bmatrix},

(2.6)

which can be used to characterize the essential spectrum as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
\{U_{n}^{*}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a bi-infinite sequence satisfying
U_{n}^{*}\to U^{*}_{\pm}
as
n\to\pm\infty
. Then, the essential spectrum of (
1.3
) is given by

\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}|\ \mathrm{Re}(\lambda)\in[f^{\prime}(U^{*}_{-})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{-}^{*})]\cup[f^{\prime}(U^{*}_{+})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{+}^{*})]\ \&\ \mathrm{Im}(\lambda)=0\}.

Proof.

First, we note that the right hand side of (
1.3
) is self-adjoint as an operator on
\ell^{2}
. Thus, its spectrum is purely real-valued, meaning we can restrict the following analysis to
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
to characterize its spectrum.

Now, consider the constant asymptotic matrix
A_{+}(\lambda)
in (
2.6
) with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The eigenvalues of
A_{+}(\lambda)
are

\mu_{1,2}=\frac{2\theta-(f^{\prime}(U_{+}^{*})-\lambda)\pm\sqrt{(2\theta-(f^{\prime}(U_{+}^{*})-\lambda))^{2}-4\theta^{2}}}{2\theta}.

(2.7)

If
(2\theta-(f^{\prime}(U_{+}^{*})-\lambda))^{2}-4\theta^{2}>0
then the eigenvalues are real with
|\mu_{2}|<1<|\mu_{1}|
, giving that (
1.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with Morse index
\mathrm{rank}(P_{+}(n))=1
. Alternatively, if

(2\theta-(f^{\prime}(U_{+}^{*})-\lambda))^{2}-4\theta^{2}\leq 0\implies\lambda\in[f^{\prime}(U_{+})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{+})]

then
A_{+}(\lambda)
has spectrum on the unit circle and so the interval
[f^{\prime}(U_{+})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{+})]
belongs to the essential spectrum of (
1.3
).

An identical argument shows that
A_{-}(\lambda)
has eigenvalues on the unit circle if and only if
\lambda\in[f^{\prime}(U_{-})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{-})]
, which means that this real interval also belongs to the essential spectrum of (
1.3
). Moreover, when
\lambda\notin[f^{\prime}(U_{-})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{-})]
the Morse index is again
\mathrm{rank}(P_{-}(n))=1
, giving that the Fredholm index is always zero when
\lambda\notin[f^{\prime}(U_{-})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{-})]\cup[f^{\prime}(U_{+})-4\theta,f^{\prime}(U_{+})]
. Thus we have completely characterized the essential spectrum, completing the proof.
∎

It is important to note that even with an explicit linear equation such as (
1.2
) to study, there is no indication of what the point spectrum might be. This leads to the work in the next subsection where we leverage information about the point spectrum of front and back solutions to obtain the point spectrum of localized structures in lattice systems.

2.2
Main stability result

To provide our main stability result, we begin by considering a diffeomorphism
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the iterative scheme

u_{n+1}=F(u_{n}).

(2.8)

Furthermore, for a bounded solution
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of (
2.8
) and a
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we consider the linear mapping about this solution

v_{n+1}=[DF(u_{n})+B(u_{n},\lambda)]v_{n},\quad v_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

(2.9)

where
DF
denotes the Jacobian of
F
and
B:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
depends analytically on
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. We assume that both
DF
and
B
smoothly depend on their arguments and that the matrices
DF(u)+B(u,\lambda)
are invertible for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The inclusion of
B
in (
2.9
) is inspired by the stability problem (
1.2
) where it is a constant matrix, while we note that the invertibility assumption is satisfied. The next assumption provides the existence of possible asymptotic states for bounded solutions
\{u_{n}^{*}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of (
2.8
). In what follows we say a matrix is hyperbolic if it has no eigenvalues on the unit circle in the complex plane.

Hypothesis 1
.

There exists a nontrivial
u^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
u=0,u^{*}
are fixed points of (
2.8
).

Evaluating the linear map (
2.9
) about the constant sequences
\{0\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{u^{*}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
leads to the autonomous difference equations

\begin{split}v_{n+1}&=A^{0}(\lambda)v_{n},\quad A^{0}(\lambda):=[DF(0)+B(0,\lambda)],\\
v_{n+1}&=A^{*}(\lambda)v_{n},\quad A^{*}(\lambda):=[DF(u^{*})+B(u^{*},\lambda)].\end{split}

(2.10)

The
n
-independence of the above difference equations coupled with the assumed invertibility of
A^{0,*}(\lambda)
provides that neither has any point spectra. Moreover, the essential spectra of (
2.10
) are simply given by the sets

\begin{split}\Sigma^{0}&=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}|\ A^{0}(\lambda)\mathrm{\ is\ not\ hyperbolic}\},\\
\Sigma^{*}&=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}|\ A^{*}(\lambda)\mathrm{\ is\ not\ hyperbolic}\}.\end{split}

(2.11)

Let us set

\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\Sigma^{0}\cup\Sigma^{*}\},

(2.12)

which is open since each of
\Sigma^{0}
and
\Sigma^{*}
are closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. Importantly, both difference equations in (
2.10
) have exponential dichotomies and Fredholm index zero for all
\lambda\in\Omega
. We now make the assumption that there exists heteroclinic connections between
0
and
u^{*}
in (
2.8
).

Hypothesis 2
.

There exist bounded trajectories
\{u^{f}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{u^{b}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of (
2.8
) along with constants
C,\alpha>0
so that

\begin{split}|u_{n}^{f}-u^{*}|&\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha n},\\
|u_{n}^{b}|\leq C&\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha n},\end{split}

(2.13)

for all
n\geq 0
and

\begin{split}|u_{n}^{f}|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\alpha n},,\\
|u_{n}^{b}-u^{*}|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\alpha n},\end{split}

(2.14)

for all
n\leq 0
.

The above hypothesis posits the existence of heteroclinic connections from
0
to
u^{*}
and vice-versa under the dynamics of
F
. Due to their connection to lattice systems, we term
\{u^{f}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
a
front
solution as it represents a step from
0
to
u^{*}
over the lattice
\mathbb{Z}
, while
\{u^{b}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a
back
representing a step from
u^{*}
to
0
; see Figure
1
panels (a) and (b), respectively. The following result follows from the roughness theorem for exponential dichotomies
[
3
, Proposition 2.5]
(see also
[
20
,
19
]
for similar results in the continuous setting).

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
\lambda\in\Omega
the systems

v_{n+1}=[DF(u_{n}^{j})+B(u_{n}^{j},\lambda)]v_{n},\quad j=f,b,

(2.15)

have exponential dichotomies on
\mathbb{Z}_{-}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with projections
P^{-,s}_{j}(n;\lambda)
and
P^{+,s}_{j}(n;\lambda)
. We denote the associated unstable projections by
P_{j}^{-,u}(n;\lambda)=I-P^{-,s}_{j}(n;\lambda)
and
P_{j}^{+,u}(n;\lambda)=I-P^{+,s}_{j}(n;\lambda)
.

Although
\Sigma^{0}\cup\Sigma^{*}
is part of the essential spectrum of linear systems (
2.15
), the following hypothesis gives that only the point spectrum can be found in
\Omega
.

Hypothesis 3
.

For all
\lambda\in\Omega
the Morse indices are such that

\mathrm{rank}(P^{-,s}_{j}(n;\lambda))=\mathrm{rank}(P^{+,s}_{j}(n;\lambda))=i_{\infty},

(2.16)

which in turn gives that

\mathrm{rank}(P^{-,u}_{j}(n;\lambda))=\mathrm{rank}(P^{+,u}_{j}(n;\lambda))=d-i_{\infty},

(2.17)

as well.

Hypothesis
3
indicates that Lemma
2.3
completely characterizes the essential spectrum of the front and back solutions. In practice, it may be the case that Hypothesis
3
only holds for a subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subsetneq\Omega
, in which case we can replace
\Omega
with
\Omega^{\prime}
in what follows. However, for reversible systems with an even dimension
d\geq 2
, such as our motivating system (
1.2
), Hypothesis
3
is always satisfied with
\mathrm{rank}(P^{-,s}_{j}(n;\lambda))=\mathrm{rank}(P^{+,s}_{j}(n;\lambda))=d/2
.

Since Hypothesis
3
gives that only the point spectrum of the fronts and backs can occur in
\Omega
, we define the following functions for all
\lambda\in\Omega
:

\begin{split}D_{f}(\lambda)&:=\mathrm{det}\bigg[\mathrm{Rg}(P^{-,u}_{f}(0;\lambda))\ \ \mathrm{Rg}(P^{+,s}_{f}(0;\lambda))\bigg],\\
D_{b}(\lambda)&:=\mathrm{det}\bigg[\mathrm{Rg}(P^{-,u}_{b}(0;\lambda))\ \ \mathrm{Rg}(P^{+,s}_{b}(0;\lambda))\bigg],\end{split}

(2.18)

where
\mathrm{Rg}
denotes the range of the operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The functions
D_{f}
and
D_{b}
identify in overlap in the range of the unstable projection on
\mathbb{Z}_{-}
and the stable projection on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. If
D_{j}(\lambda)=0
for some
\lambda
, then there exists an initial condition that converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
in (
2.15
), thus giving an eigenfunction associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda
. Specifically,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
m
for the front (resp. back) solution if and only if
\lambda
is a root of multiplicity
m
of
D_{f}
(resp.
D_{b}
).

Hypothesis 4
.

There exists a family of bounded trajectories
\{u_{n}^{\ell}(N)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
to (
2.8
), parametrized by
N\geq N_{*}
for some
N_{*}\geq 1
, satisfying

u^{\ell}_{n}(N)=\begin{cases}u_{n+N}^{f}+w^{f,-}_{n+N}(N)&n\leq-N\\
u_{n+N}^{f}+w^{f,+}_{n+N}(N)&-N<n\leq 0\\
u_{n-N}^{b}+w^{b,-}_{n-N}(N)&0<n\leq N\\
u_{n-N}^{b}+w^{b,+}_{n-N}(N)&N<n\end{cases}

(2.19)

and moreover there exist
C,\alpha>0
so that

\begin{split}&|w^{f,-}_{n}(N)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(N-n)},\qquad n\leq 0\\
&|w^{f,+}_{n}(N)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha N},\qquad 0<n\leq N\\
&|w^{b,-}_{n}(N)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha N},\qquad-N<n\leq 0\\
&|w^{b,+}_{n}(N)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(N+n)},\qquad n>0.\end{split}

(2.20)

The solutions
\{u_{n}^{\ell}(N)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
in Hypothesis
4
represent
localized solutions
since they decay to zero as
n\to\pm\infty
. The parameterization by
N
is meant to denote the width of the localization region where the elements
u_{n}^{\ell}(N)
are close to the fixed point
u^{*}
, as shown in the localized lattice structures in Figure
1
(c,d,e). We have the following important points related to the spectrum of (
2.9
) about a localized solution:

1.

The set
\Sigma^{0}
belongs to the essential spectrum since
u_{n}^{\ell}(N)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
;

2.

Based on the work in the continuous setting
[
20
]
, one expects that
\Sigma^{*}
breaks up into
\mathcal{O}(N)
eigenvalues;

3.

For any
\lambda\in\Omega
, (
2.9
) about a localized solution has Fredholm index zero, meaning that only point spectra can lie in
\Omega
.

The following theorem characterizes the point spectrum of localized solutions based on our understanding of the point spectra of the front and back solutions.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume Hypotheses
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
. Fix
\lambda_{*}\in\Omega
and suppose that for
c_{f},c_{b}\neq 0
,
m_{f},m_{b}\geq 0
, and for some
\delta>0
, we have

\begin{split}D_{f}(\lambda)&=c_{f}(\lambda-\lambda_{*})^{m_{f}}+\mathcal{O}(|\lambda-\lambda_{*}|^{m_{f}+1})\\
D_{b}(\lambda)&=c_{b}(\lambda-\lambda_{*})^{m_{b}}+\mathcal{O}(|\lambda-\lambda_{*}|^{m_{b}+1})\end{split}

(2.21)

for
\lambda\in B_{\delta}(\lambda_{*})
, the ball in
\mathbb{C}
of radius
\delta
centered at
\lambda_{*}
. Then, we can define an analytic function
D_{\ell,N}(\lambda)
such that there exists a
\delta_{*}>0
sufficiently small, with
0<\delta_{*}<\delta
, and an
N_{*}\geq 1
sufficiently large, such that the following holds uniformly in
N\geq N_{*}
:

1.

D_{\ell,N}
has precisely
m_{f}+m_{b}
roots, counted with multiplicity, in
B_{\delta_{*}}(\lambda_{*})
. These values of
\lambda
are
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha N})
close to
\lambda_{*}
, with
\alpha>0
.

2.

The system
v_{n+1}=[DF(u_{n}^{\ell}(N))+B(u_{n}^{\ell}(N),\lambda)]v_{n}
has a nontrivial bounded solution if and only if
D_{\ell,N}(\lambda)=0
.

3.

We have
D_{\ell,N}(\lambda)=D_{f}(\lambda)D_{b}(\lambda)+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha N})
with
\alpha>0
.

2.3
Extension to multi-pulses

The previous subsection focused on the stability of localized solutions with a single localization region, so-called
single-pulse solutions
, parametrized by the value
N
in Hypothesis

Theorem 2.5

4.1
Basic properties of the model

We begin by reviewing key properties of the model (
4.1
) and recasting it to align with the framework of our theoretical results. Lattice systems of the form (
4.1
) have been studied previously in
[
7
,
21
]

where appropriate.

For
\mu\in[0,1]
, the model (
4.1
) admits five spatially homogeneous steady states given by
U_{n,m}=0
and
U_{n,m}=\pm U_{\pm}(\mu)
, where

U_{\pm}(\mu):=\sqrt{1\pm\sqrt{1-\mu}}

(4.2)

The nontrivial branches
\pm U_{-}(\mu)
emerge via a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation from the trivial state at
\mu=0
. At
\mu=1
, two saddle-node bifurcations occur, where
\pm U_{+}(\mu)
and
\pm U_{-}(\mu)
coalesce. In the absence of spatial coupling (
\theta=0
), the trivial state and
\pm U_{+}(\mu)
are stable, while
\pm U_{-}(\mu)
are unstable, yielding the bistable structure underlying localized pattern formation.

The system (
4.1
) admits a gradient flow structure on
\ell^{2}
, which can be written as
\dot{U}_{n,m}=-\partial\mathcal{E}/\partial U_{n,m}
, where the energy functional
\mathcal{E}:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\mathcal{E}(\{U_{n,m}\})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}(U_{n+1,m}-U_{n,m})^{2}+\frac{\theta}{2}(U_{n,m+1}-U_{n,m})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu U_{n,m}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}U_{n,m}^{4}+\frac{1}{6}U_{n,m}^{6}\right)

(4.3)

In particular,
\dot{\mathcal{E}}\leq 0
, so solutions with initial data in
\ell^{2}
evolve toward equilibrium as
t\to\infty
. For spatially homogeneous steady states
U_{n,m}=U^{*}
, the energy per lattice site is

\mathcal{E}(U^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\mu(U^{*})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(U^{*})^{4}+\frac{1}{6}(U^{*})^{6}.

(4.4)

The trivial state
U^{*}=0
has zero energy, while the energy of the upper state
U^{*}=U_{+}(\mu)
depends on
\mu

4.4
Extension to rectangular lattices

We now turn to the case
M>1
and consider extensions to rectangular lattices. Much of the analysis for
M=1

Moreover, our general framework for stability is sufficiently flexible to apply to localized solutions constructed from these fronts and backs, including multi-dimensional patterns with flat or oscillatory plateaus. In two dimensions, one can anticipate localized structures such as stripes, rectangles, or multi-pulse arrays. Here, we focus on a numerical demonstration of these phenomena, illustrating the existence and stability of such solutions, while refraining from a full—or even partial—analytical treatment.

5
Discussion

In this work, we provide theoretical contributions that extend previous PDE-based studies by establishing a general framework for analyzing the stability of localized solutions in lattice dynamical systems via the properties of front and back solutions. Using a discrete Evans-function approach, we connect the spectral stability of a localized pattern to the spectra of its constituent fronts and backs, enabling a systematic characterization of eigenvalues for both single- and multi-pulse localized states. Our framework is sufficiently general to accommodate one- and higher-dimensional lattices, arbitrary numbers of disconnected regions of localization, and fronts asymptotic to
k
-cycles. This approach not only rigorously confirms the stability properties of many previously known localized solutions but also provides a practical tool for investigating new classes of patterns whose stability was previously inaccessible.

We illustrated the application of our results using the real-valued cubic–quintic Ginzburg–Landau equation on one- and two-dimensional lattices. In the one-dimensional case, we analyzed the stability of localized solutions with flat and oscillatory plateaus, confirming the predicted eigenvalue structure for single- and multi-pulse patterns. These findings naturally fall within the broader context of Nagumo-type lattice dynamical systems, which have long served as a prototypical setting for studying propagation failure and front-back interactions. Extending the framework to rectangular lattices (
M>1
), we demonstrated numerically that fronts along a single spatial direction generate multi-dimensional localized patterns, with reversibility providing the corresponding back solutions, and that the Evans-function-based spectral characterization remains valid.

Looking ahead, an important direction for future work is the extension of our stability analysis to localized time-periodic solutions. Such structures have been observed numerically to exist and lie on isolas
[
18
]
, and their existence has recently been rigorously established in spatially discrete complex-valued Ginzburg–Landau systems
[
2
]
. While the methods developed here for steady-state solutions provide a natural starting point, analyzing the stability of time-periodic patterns would require Floquet theory, introducing additional technical challenges. Another limitation of the current work is that it does not directly handle fully infinite two-dimensional lattices; the multi-dimensional lattices we consider require boundedness in all but one spatial dimension. Extending the discrete Evans-function framework to address time-periodic solutions, as well as truly infinite lattices or more general lattice geometries, represents a promising avenue for future research.
