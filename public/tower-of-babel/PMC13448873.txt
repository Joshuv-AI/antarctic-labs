Dual Glucagon and GLP‐1 Receptor Agonist Survodutide Improves Biomarkers of Beta‐Cell Function and Insulin Sensitivity in People With Type 2 Diabetes or Living With Overweight/Obesity
Authors: Elif I. Ekinci, Sandra González Maldonado, Anna Unseld, Jorge Arrubla Martinez, Anita M. Hennige, Corinna Schoelch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis post hoc analysis evaluated the effect of survodutide on beta‐cell function, insulin sensitivity, and glucose biomarkers in two phase 2 trial populations.Materials and MethodsTrial 1404‐0002 randomised 413 participants with type 2 diabetes on metformin to receive survodutide, placebo, or semaglutide over 16 weeks. Trial 1404‐0036 randomised 387 participants with overweight/obesity and normoglycaemia to receive survodutide or placebo for 46 weeks. Descriptive statistics were derived from both trials. Biomarker responses were analysed using mixed models for repeated measures and regression analysis to assess the impact of bodyweight changes.ResultsIn trial 1404‐0002, survodutide was associated with significant and rapid increases in HOMA‐β versus placebo, with significant decreases in HOMA‐IR, glucagon, and fasting plasma glucose (FPG). No significant changes occurred in high‐molecular‐weight (HMW) adiponectin, C‐peptide, or fasting insulin levels. In trial 1404‐0036, there was no significant change in HOMA‐β with survodutide versus placebo, likely owing to normoglycaemia in participants. Survodutide was associated with significant decreases in HOMA‐IR, glucagon, C‐peptide, fasting insulin, and FPG versus placebo, and significant increases in HMW adiponectin. Combining all survodutide dose groups for each trial, absolute change in bodyweight from baseline explained the largest proportion of total variability in changes from baseline in fasting insulin and HOMA‐IR. However, it did not explain other changes observed.ConclusionsSurvodutide was associated with significantly improved beta‐cell function and insulin sensitivity in adults with type 2 diabetes and with overweight or obesity. The durability of beta‐cell improvements after treatment cessation remains unknown. These effects were largely independent of bodyweight changes, suggesting additional metabolic benefit beyond weight loss. Trial Registration: Trial 1404‐0002, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04153929; EudraCT number: 2019‐002390‐60. Trial 1404‐0036, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04667377; EudraCT number: 2020‐002479‐37.

Introduction

Obesity is characterised by excess adiposity associated with complex neuroendocrine adaptations and metabolic changes related to energy status. Pancreatic beta‐cells are central to these processes by integrating metabolic signals to maintain glucose homeostasis. The pathophysiology of obesity, insulin resistance, and beta‐cell dysfunction is complex—adiposity‐induced beta‐cell dysfunction and multi‐organ insulin resistance can lead to type 2 diabetes—although the exact pathogenic processes are yet to be delineated [1, 2]. Ectopic fat deposition around the pancreas is associated with insulin resistance, fatty infiltration of pancreatic tissue and can lead to impaired insulin secretion [3]. Exposure to high levels of plasma non‐esterified fatty acids can lead to beta‐cell dysfunction, interfering with the pathways of glucose‐induced insulin secretion and insulin production [4].

Bodyweight loss, particularly intra‐abdominal and pancreatic fat loss, is associated with improvements in glucose metabolism in people with type 2 diabetes [5], and substantial weight loss (≥ 10%–15%) can lead to diabetes remission, especially if weight loss can be maintained [6, 7]; although longer‐term studies are needed. This improvement is often linked to a reduction in pancreatic fat and increased insulin sensitivity [8]. Several drugs have regulatory approval for chronic weight management in people living with overweight or obesity [9, 10, 11], and additional agents are in clinical development for this indication [12, 13]. These therapies—mainly consisting of entero‐pancreatic hormone‐based mono, dual, or triple receptor agonists—also show therapeutic potential in the management of type 2 diabetes [14], and several are already approved for its treatment [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. These agents improve surrogate markers of beta‐cell function, including homeostatic model of assessment (HOMA) for pancreatic beta‐cell function (HOMA‐β) and other indices of insulin secretory capacity. Dual agonists such as tirzepatide (glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide [GIP]/glucagon‐like peptide‐1 [GLP‐1] receptor agonist) produce greater increases in HOMA2‐B and reductions in proinsulin ratios than selective GLP‐1 receptor agonists, indicating more robust effects on beta‐cell secretory efficiency and reduced beta‐cell stress in type 2 diabetes [20, 21, 22, 23]. However, clinical studies suggest that these improvements are largely treatment‐dependent and probably decrease after therapy withdrawal, indicating predominantly reversible enhancement rather than durable restoration of beta‐cell function [24, 25, 26, 27].

Survodutide is a glucagon receptor/GLP‐1 receptor dual‐agonist in clinical development for the treatment of obesity [28, 29], and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with fibrosis [30]. In a phase 2 clinical trial in adults with a BMI 25─50 kg/m2 and type 2 diabetes (trial 1404‐0002; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04153929, EudraCT 2019–002390‐60), survodutide elicited dose‐dependent mean reductions in HbA1c of up to −18.72 mmol/mol (−1.71%) after 16 weeks, versus −1.62 mmol/mol (−0.15%) for placebo, and mean reductions in bodyweight of up to −8.7% for survodutide, versus −1.01% for placebo [31]. In a further phase 2 clinical trial in adults with a BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2 without diabetes (trial 1404‐0036; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04667377, EudraCT 2020‐002479‐37), survodutide led to significant and dose‐dependent reductions in bodyweight versus placebo after 46 weeks (survodutide: 0.6 mg, p = 0.026; ≥ 2.4 mg, p < 0.001) [29]. Mean changes in bodyweight reached −14.9% and −18.7% at week 46 for planned treatment (all data, censored for COVID‐19–related treatment discontinuations) and actual treatment (on‐treatment data), respectively, with survodutide 4.8 mg, versus −2.8% and −2.3% with placebo [29]. Phase 3 trials of survodutide are ongoing (SYNCHRONIZE‐1, NCT06066515; SYNCHRONIZE‐2, NCT06066528; SYNCHRONIZE‐CVOT, NCT06077864; and SYNCHRONIZE‐MASLD, NCT06309992 [32, 33, 34]; LIVERAGE, NCT06632444; and LIVERAGE‐Cirrhosis, NCT06632457 [35, 36]).

Here, we report an exploratory post hoc analysis using data from two phase 2 clinical trials of survodutide to evaluate changes in beta‐cell function, insulin sensitivity, and glucose biomarkers in people with type 2 diabetes and/or living with overweight or obesity.

Methods

Trials Overview

This was a post hoc analysis of data derived from two phase 2 clinical trials; full details of the protocols and primary outcomes were published (Table S1) [29, 31]. Briefly, these clinical trials were: trial 1404–0002, a phase 2, randomised, double‐blind, dose‐finding trial of survodutide versus placebo or open‐label semaglutide, with a 16‐week treatment period in people with type 2 diabetes (receiving concomitant stable doses of metformin of ≥ 1000 mg/day immediate or extended release for ≥ 3 months before screening), and with a BMI of 25─50 kg/m2 [31]; and trial 1404‐0036, a phase 2, randomised, double‐blind, dose‐finding trial of survodutide versus placebo, with a 46‐week treatment period (20 weeks dose escalation; 26 weeks dose maintenance) in people with a BMI of ≥ 27 kg/m2, stable bodyweight of ≥ 70 kg (females) or ≥ 80 kg (males), HbA1c < 48 mmol/mol (< 6.5%; i.e., without diabetes) at screening, and who had undergone at least one previous unsuccessful non‐surgical weight‐loss attempt [29]. These clinical trials were conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants in each clinical trial provided signed informed consent and trial protocols were approved by local ethics review boards.

Sample Collection and Analysis

Blood samples were collected from trial participants in a fasted state for the analysis of glucose biomarkers, including homeostatic model of assessment (HOMA) for pancreatic beta‐cell function (HOMA‐β), HOMA‐insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR), glucagon, high‐molecular‐weight (HMW) adiponectin, C‐peptide levels, fasting insulin, and fasting plasma glucose (FPG). Biomarker levels were measured at baseline (before first drug administration), weeks 5, 8, 12, 17 (end‐of‐treatment; 1 week after the last dose), and 21 (follow‐up) in trial 1404‐0002, and at baseline (before first drug administration), and weeks 10, 20, and 47 (1 week after the last dose) in trial 1404–0036. Blood samples were collected in sodium fluoride, K2‐EDTA, serum or P800 tubes, and stored at −20°C. Biomarker measurements for C‐peptide levels, fasting insulin, and FPG were performed by the central laboratory using validated assays according to the manufacturer's protocols (LabCorp Central Laboratory Services, Burlington, NC, USA). Glucagon was measured at a dedicated CRO (MLM Medical Labs GmbH, Monchengladbach, Germany) using a validated, commercially available radioimmunoassay. HMW adiponectin was measured at a CRO (menal GmbH, Emmendingen, Germany) in trial 1404‐0036.

Statistical Analysis (Post Hoc)

In trial 1404‐0002, data were analysed for the treated set (TS), defined as all randomised patients who received at least one dose of study drug according to doses assigned at randomisation (planned treatment); only results for survodutide and placebo groups are presented here. In trial 1404‐0036, data were analysed for the full analysis set (FAS), defined as all randomised participants who received at least one dose of study drug and had analysable data for at least one efficacy endpoint according to planned treatment, as well as by doses received during the maintenance period (actual treatment) using on‐treatment data. Descriptive statistics were summarised as mean and standard deviation (SD) or geometric mean (gMean) and geometric SD (gSD) for continuous variables, depending on the skewness of their distributions. Responses to treatment on biomarker levels over time were estimated using mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) with main effects for treatment, baseline, and visit, and interaction terms for treatment‐by‐visit and baseline biomarker value by‐visit, with an unstructured covariance matrix structure for repeated measurements within participants. Furthermore, the Spearman correlation coefficient of change from baseline to end‐of‐treatment was calculated for all biomarker pairs. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. For biomarkers with a skewed distribution, MMRM were fitted to the change from baseline in log‐transformed values. Estimates are shown after back‐transformation to the original scale. To investigate the impact of changes in bodyweight on changes in biomarkers, linear regression analyses were conducted for HOMA‐β, HOMA‐IR, fasting insulin, FPG, HMW adiponectin, glucagon, and C‐peptide. The basis model included change from baseline in log‐transformed biomarker after 16 weeks on treatment in trial 1404‐0002 and after 46 weeks on treatment in trial 1404‐0036 as the response, and absolute change from baseline in bodyweight, together with baseline biomarker values, treatment group, and an interaction term between change in bodyweight and treatment as independent variables. Model selection was performed for each biomarker based on the Akaike information criterion and a stepwise approach, considering age, sex, baseline bodyweight, and baseline HbA1c values as candidate covariates and using data from all treatment groups. The selected models were applied to data from each treatment group separately. The contribution of change in bodyweight to the total variability in change from baseline in biomarker values was estimated based on type III sum of squares and the total sum of squares and reported as semi‐partial eta‐squared and semi‐partial omega‐squared.

Results

Across both trials, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between treatment groups, as previously described [29, 31]. In trial 1404‐0002, 413 people were randomised, of whom 411 were treated (TS) [31]. In trial 1404‐0036, 387 people were randomised (one participant withdrew); the FAS consisted of 384 participants, as two treated participants did not provide post‐baseline efficacy data [29].

Trial 1404‐0002

Survodutide was associated with significant and rapid increases in HOMA‐β (Figure 1A), significant decreases in HOMA‐IR (Figure 1B), and significant reductions in the levels of glucagon (Figure 1C) from baseline to week 17, when compared with placebo. The mean (SD) absolute change in HOMA‐β from baseline at week 17 for survodutide 1.8 mg given twice weekly (BIW) versus placebo was 47.00 (42.50) versus 5.44 (35.32), respectively; and the adjusted‐fold change (AFC) versus placebo was 1.80 ([95% confidence interval (CI) 1.42, 2.27]; p < 0.0001). For the same dose and period, the absolute change in HOMA‐IR was −0.91 (5.25) versus 0.93 (3.29) for placebo; AFC versus placebo was 0.68 ([0.51, 0.91]; p = 0.0086); and the absolute change in glucagon was −15.07 (15.94) pmol/L versus −3.79 (8.92) pmol/L for placebo; AFC versus placebo was 0.83 ([0.76, 0.90]; p < 0.0001). There were no significant changes in the levels of HMW adiponectin, C‐peptide, or fasting insulin for survodutide versus placebo to week 17; however, significant reductions in FPG were observed (Table 1 and Figure 1D). Mean (SD) absolute change in FPG from baseline at week 17 for survodutide 1.8 mg BIW versus placebo was −2.32 (2.46) mmol/L versus 0.25 (2.30) mmol/L; AFC versus placebo was 0.71 ([0.65, 0.78]; p < 0.0001).

Discussion

This exploratory post hoc analysis demonstrated that survodutide improved markers of beta‐cell function and insulin sensitivity when compared to placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes, and in adults living with overweight or obesity who did not have type 2 diabetes. Improvements in insulin sensitivity may effectively reduce the metabolic demand on beta‐cells and, therefore, facilitate reversal of beta‐cell dysfunction.

Our findings are consistent with glucose biomarker data from studies of other entero‐pancreatic hormone‐based agents in people with type 2 diabetes, and those living with overweight or obesity without diabetes. Tirzepatide was associated with significant improvements in markers of beta‐cell function and insulin sensitivity in people with type 2 diabetes, as demonstrated by greater increases in HOMA2‐β and reductions in HOMA2‐IR, fasting C‐peptide, glucagon, and insulin levels compared with semaglutide and placebo [20, 21]. Semaglutide similarly increased HOMA‐β [37], decreased HOMA‐IR [37, 38], and increased rates of insulin secretion compared with placebo [39], with comparable effects observed in adults with overweight or obesity without diabetes [40]. Liraglutide also improved insulin sensitivity, as shown by changes in HOMA‐IR, HOMA2, and the Matsuda index, and decreased fasting and postprandial glucose, insulin, C‐peptide, and glucagon levels compared with sitagliptin and placebo [41].

To place these biomarker findings in context, it is important to consider the integrated physiological effects of concurrent incretin and glucagon receptor signalling. GLP‐1 receptor agonism enhances glucose‐dependent insulin secretion and suppresses glucagon release, thereby contributing to its antihyperglycaemic efficacy [42]. Although glucagon receptor agonism has traditionally been viewed as counteracting glucose‐lowering effects due to increased hepatic glucose production and its association with fasting hyperglucagonaemia in people with type 2 diabetes, in the context of dual agonism it also facilitates anti‐obesogenic metabolic effects despite this hyperglycaemic potential [43]. The glucagon receptor‐agonistic component—although inherently capable of raising hepatic glucose output—simultaneously increases energy expenditure, stimulates lipid mobilisation, and enhances amino‐acid turnover, effects that collectively improve whole‐body metabolic flexibility and reduce adiposity [44, 45]. When combined in a single molecule, these opposing yet synergistic actions allow GLP‐1‐mediated glucagon suppression to mitigate hyperglycaemic risk, while glucagon‐driven catabolic signalling promotes negative energy balance. The net result is an integrated metabolic profile in which improved insulin sensitivity, reduced ectopic fat, and increased energy expenditure counterbalance glucagon's glycaemic effects, supporting overall glucose control despite partial glucagon receptor activation [28, 44, 45]. The physiological actions of glucagon in normoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia remain incompletely understood, partly because earlier research focused primarily on its role in hypoglycaemia [46]. Glucagon is a highly conserved hormone essential for metabolic homeostasis and survival [47]. Its capacity to increase energy expenditure is well recognised, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood [48]. Glucagon acts via the glucagon receptor, a G protein‐coupled receptor expressed predominantly in the liver, with additional expression in extrahepatic tissues including the brain, heart, kidney, gastrointestinal tract, and adipose tissue [48]. Glucagon receptor activation, particularly in combination with GLP‐1 receptor agonism, improves metabolic outcomes by coordinating hepatic, central, and islet‐cell‐level mechanisms that promote weight loss and enhance beta‐cell function [29]. In the liver, glucagon receptor signalling activates cAMP‐PKA pathways, increases hepatic lipid oxidation, and raises resting energy expenditure, contributing to weight reduction in preclinical and clinical models [48, 49]. Hepatic glucagon receptor signalling induces other endocrine mediators such as fibroblast growth factor 21, which enhances systemic fat oxidation and energy expenditure and contributes to weight‐lowering [49]. Centrally, glucagon exerts anorectic effects via the hypothalamus and brainstem nuclei, including the area postrema, resulting in reduced food intake [48]. Although glucagon increases brown adipose tissue thermogenesis in animal models [50, 51], evidence in humans remains limited [52], and data on its effects on energy intake are inconclusive, with reported increases observed acutely [53], but not with chronic administration [54]. Despite acutely raising hepatic glucose production, intra‐islet glucagon signalling can paradoxically enhance beta‐cell function by increasing intracellular cAMP and potentiating glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion in a glucose‐dependent manner [55]. When hyperglycaemia is mitigated via concurrent GLP‐1 receptor agonism, glucagon receptor activation can reduce adiposity and improve insulin sensitivity, thereby indirectly supporting beta‐cell functional recovery while maintaining thermogenic and anorectic benefits [29, 56].

In this post hoc analysis, survodutide treatment was associated with reductions in FPG in people living with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, together with improvements in HOMA‐β in people with type 2 diabetes, supporting the therapeutic potential of GLP‐1 and glucagon receptor dual‐agonism in the treatment of type 2 diabetes and related metabolic disorders. Whether GLP‐1 receptor agonism counterbalances potential glucagon‐mediated effects on hepatic glucose production needs further evaluation; although, co‐administration of glucagon and GLP‐1 has been shown to eliminate the net glucagon‐associated hyperglycaemic excursions in both mice and humans [57]. Further advantages of glucagon receptor agonism include its role in regulating substrate utilisation in the liver and its effect on energy expenditure [28]. In addition to survodutide, several glucagon/GLP‐1 receptor dual agonists are in clinical development for the treatment of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and MASH. These include cotadutide, mazdutide, pemvidutide, NN 9277 (NNC 9204‐1777), and bamadutide, as well as efinopegdutide for MASH only. Variations in their reported efficacy and safety may be related to differences in glucagon receptor and GLP‐1 receptor activation [43]. Mazdutide (IBI362/LY3305677) has shown greater improvements in FPG, HOMA‐IR, and HOMA‐β versus placebo in people with type 2 diabetes [58], with similar metabolic benefits reported in people living with overweight or obesity [59]. Bamadutide (SAR‐425899), although discontinued after phase 2 clinical trials, showed significantly greater improvement in beta‐cell function and postprandial glucose control than liraglutide after 28 days of treatment in people with type 2 diabetes [60, 61, 62]. Conversely, pemvidutide led to substantial bodyweight loss in people living with obesity but had no effect on fasting glucose and HbA1c levels in people with type 2 diabetes [63]. In addition, retatrutide, a triple‐agonist targeting glucagon, GLP‐1, and GIP receptors, increased insulin sensitivity (reductions in fasting insulin, fasting C‐peptide, and HOMA2‐IR), increased adiponectin, and decreased fasting serum glucose and glucagon versus placebo in people with type 2 diabetes [64]. It also improved metabolic parameters (HbA1c, FPG, and fasting insulin) versus placebo in people living with obesity [65].

Our results also demonstrated that survodutide was associated with significant reductions in fasting glucagon, which is consistent with glucagon receptor target engagement and was shown in preclinical studies [28]. This behaviour likely indicates compensatory adjustments in endogenous glucagon secretion under chronic glucagon receptor agonism. Whether decreases in circulating glucagon in the context of a sustained glucagon receptor activation might contribute to reductions in HbA1c needs further evaluation.

In addition, survodutide was associated with significant increases in HMW adiponectin in people with overweight and obesity, whereas no significant change was observed in people with type 2 diabetes—most likely due to the shorter treatment duration in trial 1404‐0002. Reduced levels of adiponectin correlate with increased adiposity [66], and are associated with an increased risk of developing obesity and type 2 diabetes [67, 68]. Adiponectin enhances fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and may have anti‐inflammatory and anti‐atherogenic properties [69, 70], as well as reduce oxidative stress to improve insulin resistance [71]. A meta‐analysis has shown that GLP‐1 receptor agonism increased adiponectin substantially; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear [72]. These findings underscore the potential of survodutide as a therapeutic option to improve metabolic health and reduce cardiovascular risk, extending its benefits beyond glucose regulation. Observed alterations in the selected markers were only partially explained by changes in bodyweight, suggesting that additional mechanisms may be involved that are mainly independent of weight loss. This was most evident in the significant reductions in fasting glucagon and FPG, and the improvement in HOMA‐β.

The efficacy of survodutide in enhancing beta‐cell function and mitigating insulin resistance suggests that survodutide has the potential to regulate glucose homeostasis and lessen the severity of metabolic disease, thereby contributing to improved metabolic health. Survodutide offers a valuable treatment option for people with type 2 diabetes and those living with overweight or obesity. Moreover, due to variable intra‐individual responses to incretin therapies, having an additional drug that targets the glucagon and GLP‐1 pathways is important in clinical practice. Current data indicate that the ratio of receptor activities offered by survodutide may achieve an optimal balance between safety and glucose‐lowering efficacy.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as beta‐cell function was evaluated only during active therapy, the persistence of any improvements after treatment withdrawal is unclear (however, such treatment dependence would be an expected biological phenomenon, based on existing clinical trials data). The potential range of insulin‐sensitising effects of survodutide may have been underestimated in this analysis, as the participants of trial 1404‐0002 received concomitant metformin therapy. Participants in these two trials were predominantly White; therefore, the results may not be generalisable to other populations. Lastly, an inherent limitation on our post hoc design is that sample sizes were based on power calculations for primary study endpoints rather than biomarker evaluations.

Beta‐cell function and insulin sensitivity will be investigated further in ongoing phase 3 trials of survodutide in people with obesity with or without type 2 diabetes (SYNCHRONIZE‐1, NCT06066515; and SYNCHRONIZE‐2, NCT06066528) [32]. Our data suggest that survodutide could influence specific risk factors beyond those associated with bodyweight loss, although this remains to be further explored.

Conclusion

In conclusion, survodutide significantly improved markers of pancreatic beta‐cell function and insulin sensitivity, versus placebo, in people with type 2 diabetes receiving concomitant metformin, and in people living with obesity who were otherwise healthy. However, whether these beta‐cell improvements persist after treatment withdrawal remains unknown. Furthermore, our data demonstrate that glucagon receptor agonism does not adversely affect glucose homeostasis. Changes in the selected markers were only partially explained by concurrent changes in bodyweight, suggesting that additional mechanisms may be involved. These data support the efficacy of survodutide in modifying metabolic parameters, as previously reported [29, 30, 73, 74]. By enhancing the function of beta‐cells and mitigating insulin resistance via the dual activation of glucagon and GLP‐1 receptors, survodutide has the potential not only to regulate glucose levels and promote bodyweight loss, but also to lessen the severity of metabolic disease, thereby contributing to improved metabolic health.
