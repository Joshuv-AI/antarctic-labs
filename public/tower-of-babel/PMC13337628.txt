Creating The Canadian Emergency Department Research Network (CEDRN): a new era for emergency medicine research
Authors: Corinne M Hohl, Rhonda J Rosychuk, Patrick T Fok, Patrick Archambault, Ivy Cheng, Kavish Chandra, Steven C Brooks, Hana Wiemer, Kayla Furlong, Gabrielle Legault, Chenoa Cassidy-Mathews, Amber Cragg, Jeffrey P Hau, Laurie J Morrison, Andrew D McRae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEmergency physicians care for diverse patients with undifferentiated and evolving conditions and often make clinical decisions with limited scientific evidence. Administrative barriers to effective pan-Canadian data collection from Emergency Departments (EDs) limit our ability to generate evidence in a timely manner, particularly in times of crisis. Informed by our prior COVID-19 collaboration (https://ccedrrn.ca), we created the Canadian ED Research Network (CEDRN, https://cedrn.ca) to address these barriers. Our objective is to describe the Canadian ED Research Network’s approach to enabling pan-Canadian, harmonized clinical ED data collection using established infrastructure and processes to accelerate research, quality improvement, and surveillance.Preliminary workThe Canadian ED Research Network has executed data sharing and membership agreements, privacy approvals, and multi-stakeholder governance that can support the timely launch of national multi-center observational data collection for patients presenting to participating EDs, including rural and remote sites. The Canadian ED Research Network’s standardized privacy-approved data collection platform, data flow, and data management processes facilitate the creation of nationally harmonized datasets including data cleaning and verification, permit linkages with administrative datasets, and secure access to data for research ethics board approved analyses within Canada. Outcomes and analytic methods for studies are determined by study-specific steering committees. By allowing the use of data for quality improvement and surveillance, Canadian ED Research Network data can be transferred to provincial health improvement networks to create metrics to monitor public health threats and track implementation of evidence-based interventions.Future directionsThe Canadian ED Research Network is a pan-Canadian research network that has the potential to facilitate and accelerate emergency medicine research and improve care. Its broad geographic and clinical scope will allow Canadian ED Research Network studies to expand to any research relevant to emergency medicine and enhance Canada’s health emergencies and pandemic research preparedness. Introduction

The rapid unexpected spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) Coronavirus (CoV)−2 in 2020 had devastating consequences. The virus caused over 55,000 excess deaths in Canada and exacerbated health inequities [1–3]. Lack of preparedness for pandemic research and healthcare led to delays in generating evidence, wasted resources, unnecessary loss of life, and emphasized the need for timely scientific research to support evidence-based decision-making [4]. This occurred despite previous calls to improve pan-Canadian data collection, harmonization of research processes and data linkages after the first SARS outbreak in 2003 [5].

Canadian Emergency Departments (EDs) remained universal access points for essential health care during the SARS and COVID-19 pandemics. However, lack of funding for sustained national emergency research networks, dedicated infrastructure, and pre-approved data collection protocols and data linkage contracts limited our ability to accrue timely high-quality clinical data needed to evaluate emergency care, diagnostics, screening protocols, therapeutics, vaccines, equity, and enable risk prediction. While many EDs contributed administrative data to the National Care Ambulatory Reporting System (NACRS), the data captured are mainly operational [6]. While some hospitals report presenting complaints or discharge diagnoses, only a few report interventions. Clinical data needed to evaluate care quality and effectiveness are lacking in administrative data sources. This includes presentation characteristics, vital signs, clinical course, nursing and physician interventions, laboratory testing, diagnostic imaging, consultations, fluids and medications, transfusions, oxygen delivery, and ED procedures. Not surprisingly, countries with pre-established funded clinical research network infrastructure and protocols, such as the United Kingdom, had greater levels of research preparedness and produced more timely and impactful research that saved the lives of millions [7]. To achieve similar impact, Canadian emergency medicine research requires efficient, timely, national clinical data collection and linkages to administrative data sources [8].

Canadian emergency physicians have an opportunity to build on an existing network to implement a broad, ready pan-Canadian emergency research network [9]. In March 2020, the Canadian COVID-19 ED Rapid Response Network (CCEDRRN) brought together 51 EDs to generate a nationally harmonized clinical dataset on COVID-19 tested patients [9, 10]. The network garnered almost $9 million in peer-reviewed funding, produced 26 publications, and contributed to six government reports. We evaluated diagnostic testing [11, 12], screening [13], treatments and prognosis [10, 14, 15], interventions [14], vaccine effectiveness [16], health equity [17, 18], and derived[19, 20] and externally validated[21] clinical decision instruments. We followed patients for complications and patient-reported outcomes [22, 23]. In 2021, the Climate Institute Canada leveraged our infrastructure to evaluate heatstroke presentations during British Columbia’s heat event, illustrating the relevance of our network for climate emergencies [24]. In 2022, site principal investigators proposed pivoting our infrastructure to accelerate research in emergency medicine and enable use of data for research, quality improvement, and surveillance across the clinical spectrum of emergency medicine. The objective in this manuscript is to describe the infrastructure, governance, and methodological considerations of the Canadian Emergency Department Research Network (CEDRN, https://cedrn.ca), whose aim is to facilitate collaborative research, streamline study launch and data collection, and enhance access to curated data for research, quality improvement, and surveillance.

Preliminary work

This report describes the Canadian ED Research Network’s governance, infrastructure, and work thus far to facilitate launching retrospective or prospective observational studies in Emergency Medicine.

Canadian ED research network sites & study populations

To date, of 70 interested sites in 10 provinces, 60 sites in seven provinces have executed the Canadian ED Research Network’s data sharing and membership agreement (see Fig. 1 or Table 2). Participating sites can propose single or multi-site registries and studies (subsequently called studies) and can voluntarily choose to participate in studies led by other Canadian ED Research Network members. Each Canadian ED Research Network study specifies its inclusion and exclusion criteria, and may include patients who access emergency care for any medical, surgical, mental health and/or social emergency.

Data sharing and membership agreement

Following the Expert Panel Report of the Review of the Federal Approach to the Pandemic Science Advice and Research Coordination, we developed an ever-ready collaborative research network with pre-approved infrastructure and processes to initiate national data collection and linkages on evolving health threats [4]. Critical to this was an overarching agreement allowing sites to harmonize and share data about any medical, mental health, surgical, or social condition seen in EDs, removing lengthy study-by-study contract negotiations that commonly take place between collaborating institutions. The agreement specifies a multi-stakeholder governance model, terms and conditions for participation, data and sample ownership, data access and use, privacy, and a financial model based on cost recovery (network cost calculator). Once a study has been funded, a financial contract template is executed between the grant-holding institution and the study sites.

Governance

Each participating site designates an investigator to sign the Canadian ED Research Network’s data sharing and membership agreement. The Executive Committee can approve additional site investigators.

The Executive Committee is composed of the Canadian ED Research Network’s Chair and Vice Chair (elected 2-year positions), the Past Chair, provincial leads, and Chairs of all standing committees (Fig. 2). The Executive Committee oversees daily operations, governance, study and grant approvals, study-related data builds, financial sustainability, and updates to the network cost calculator and policies, as needed. Four standing committees are advisory to the Executive and include the following: the Finance Committee, which monitors the financial viability of the network and the coordinating center, study budgets, and proposes updates to the network cost calculator as needed. The Protocol Review Committee oversees study-level data governance, protocol feasibility, privacy and ethics compliance, and data quality and consistency. The Patient Engagement Committee integrates patient and public perspectives and supports study-level engagement. The Indigenous Advisory Committee guides the collection and use of Indigenous identifiers and provides recommendations on the analysis and interpretation of study results. It provides guidance and oversight to ensure that Indigenous governance, data sovereignty, and ethical engagement principles are upheld in all Canadian ED Research Network research activities.

Each study has a Study Steering Committee led by the study’s nominated principal investigator or a designate, and includes co-investigators, site leads, methodologists, and patient partners to oversee study operations. Study-specific Scientific Advisory or Patient Engagement Committees are focused on the content or methodological area of the specific study. Study costs are covered by the nominated principal investigator’s institution.

Ethical considerations

All studies must be approved by the Research Ethics Boards of participating institutions. To date, approved Canadian ED Research Network studies have requested a waiver of informed consent for study enrollment due to the minimal risk nature of the observational data being collected. Some studies may request permission to phone or text patients to consent them for follow-up (i.e., “consent to contact”) or specimen collection (e.g., viral respiratory pathogen swabs). During the pandemic, most Research Ethics Boards approved consent to telephone contact using standardized scripts drafted by patient partners [26].

Data collection, management, and access

De-identified clinical data are collected at participating sites by medical record review or prospectively from patients. Retrospective data may be collected manually from sites with paper-based records or extracted automatically from electronic medical records. Development of code (i.e., macros) to automate extraction of data from the same or similar electronic medical records for common data elements can be re-used for multiple Canadian ED Research Network studies (e.g., vital signs).

When studies are capturing data, data flow from sites to the University of British Columbia’s instance of REDCap with a Canadian ED Research Network-assigned Study ID (Fig. 3). Locally held site trackers link Study IDs to patient identifiers for data cleaning, verification, and linkages. The coordinating center then uploads the cleaned and verified data from the University of British Columbia’s instance of REDCap into a secure research environment for analysis and linkage with administrative data.

Canadian ED Research Network data can be accessed by security-trained and approved analysts from within Canada using standardized security and remote access provisions. All analyses are approved by the Research Ethics Board and the Canadian ED Research Network Executive. No Canadian ED Research Network data can be downloaded from secure research environments. These procedures minimize privacy risks and enable distributed analyses by Canadian ED Research Network investigators.

Outcomes, analyses, and sample size calculations

Canadian ED Research Network studies will define their outcomes, analyses, and sample sizes appropriate to their research questions. Data linkages will provide the opportunity to leverage administrative data for additional patient-level (e.g., adherence to discharge prescriptions) or system-level outcomes (e.g., mortality). We anticipate that a wide range of analytic methods will be applied to our data holdings, including regression modelling, causal inference techniques, time-to-event analyses, diagnostic test evaluations, clinical decision rule derivations, validation and implementation methods, interrupted time series, and economic analyses. Studies with sufficiently large sample sizes may be appropriate for machine learning approaches. Random selection can be used to capture patients to enable comparative designs [22, 23]. Federated approaches to analysis may be required for provincial data holders unable to release data into a national secure research environment [27]. Data from Canadian ED Research Network studies can be used for secondary use analyses with the approval of the study steering committee and the Research Ethics Board.

Discussion

Our objective was to develop an emergency research network to enhance Canada’s preparedness for future health emergencies, while accelerating observational data collection on existing health threats. At the start of the pandemic, we lacked a data and membership agreement, which delayed the start of ED-based data collection and scientific output, despite ED's critical role in health service delivery during the pandemic. Since then, we have launched the Canadian ED Research Network, recruited sites, developed a governance model, and standardized data management, flow and linkage procedures, and executed data sharing and membership agreements. This will allow member institutions to collaborate without repetitive and lengthy study-by-study contract negotiations that commonly delay studies. We believe this work has helped to secure Canadian Institutes of Health Research funding to address the toxic drug crisis and enhance pandemic and health emergencies preparedness.

Comparison to previous network

In comparison with our prior network and other disease-oriented networks, the Canadian ED Research Network’s overarching agreement allows sites to harmonize, curate, analyze and link data on any medical, mental health, surgical, or social condition seen in EDs [9]. This allows us to study a broad range of clinical conditions and pivot to new areas with fewer administrative delays, allowing us to maintain readiness for new health threats to address calls that emerged after the SARS and COVID-19 pandemics [5]. We integrated the ability of sites and provinces to use curated data for surveillance and quality improvement, enabling monitoring of disease trends and tracking of research implementation using a learning health systems approach [25]. This addressed our inability to ensure implementation of our findings during the COVID-19 pandemic. These features distinguish the Canadian ED Research Network from other Canadian research networks that do not use a common data platform, such as Pediatric Emergency Research Canada (PERC) and the Network of Canadian Emergency Researchers (NCER), whose focus is on researcher engagement, knowledge translation, and grant writing support [28]. We believe the Canadian ED Research Network’s key characteristics will facilitate future work, open doors to new collaborations, and increase our relevance to public health agencies and governments.

Strengths and limitations

With 60 sites, the Canadian ED Research Network is Canada’s largest active emergency medicine research network, with access to large numbers of study participants and investigators [4, 28]. Participating sites include rural and remote sites, increasing the generalizability of our findings. Another strength is our explicit multi-stakeholder governance, with geographic representation across committees to maintain collaborative relationships. Patient partners and citizens are included and consulted when developing studies, and have been the instigators of patient-oriented research [23]. Grant holders join Canadian ED Research Network committees to help shape operations and policies while balancing their financial interests with network sustainability. The Canadian ED Research Network incorporates meaningful inclusion of Indigenous identifiers to ensure that the unique experiences of Indigenous Peoples with healthcare institutions are considered—a model that may inform others undertaking similar research.

As more jurisdictions adopt one common electronic medical record, automated data extraction provides ready access to data from rural and remote sites using the same build, increasing efficiencies and generalizability. Extracting data from clinical notes has traditionally been performed by research assistants. Recent advances in artificial intelligence will allow extraction from free text and are likely to transform observational data collection. The Canadian ED Research Network will need to adapt data collection, flow, and management to integrate these advances and may achieve this through partnership with other networks (e.g., General Medicine Inpatient Initiative (GEMINI), https://geminimedicine.ca). Disaggregated and fragmented health care jurisdictions often necessitate province-by-province data linkage contract negotiations [4, 30]. During the pandemic, many provincial data custodians did not approve linkage with their vaccination data, even though vaccination registries were publicly funded, highlighting the need for ongoing advocacy for provincial data governance that advances public health [30]. Ongoing funding to sustain the network will be critical. Thus far, we have secured funding for observational studies on chest pain [31], opioid poisoning, and pandemic preparedness. While grant funding has launched our work, funding from varied sources will be needed to sustain ongoing operations [28]. The Canadian ED Research Network’s data and membership agreement enables members to conduct observational studies. Should members wish to engage in interventional studies, additional agreements need to address the unique legal, privacy, and insurance considerations of clinical trials. Recently, the Accelerating Clinical Trials consortium (https://act-aec.ca/sub-site-template/) has developed a template agreement for clinical trials with over 50 research sites, which promises to accelerate this process [32]. Finally, the Canadian ED Research Network is only as strong as the willingness of Canadian emergency researchers to participate in network activities. While policies can help govern our operations, a shared culture of curiosity, collaboration, partnership, inclusion, and representation will need to endure. Ongoing feedback from the emergency medicine community will be helpful in addressing concerns or barriers to use of the infrastructure.

Clinical implications

The ability to use Canadian ED Research Network data for quality improvement enhances the relevance of our data holdings to clinicians. Health delivery organizations and quality improvement networks may use Canadian ED Research Network data for analyses to understand practice variation and can develop metrics and dashboards to track implementation progress. This may support the quintuple aims of improving patient and system-level outcomes, equity, affordability, and workforce sustainability and the transition to a learning health system [25]. Nationally, data holdings can be leveraged to understand interprovincial differences in care and for surveillance purposes, for example, to garner more rapid information about future pandemics or emerging injury or drug toxicity trends that may vary across Canada.

Research implications

Creating large accessible datasets provides opportunities for emergency medicine researchers and clinicians, including medical trainees and graduate students, to lead investigations with support through the coordinating center or their academic institution. While funding is needed to launch new datasets (e.g., e-scooter injuries, heat-related illness), the Canadian ED Research Network’s data access provisions and analytic supports may support practicing clinicians and researchers to collaborate more effectively and accelerate learning through research. This offers senior researchers the opportunity to mentor the next generation of researchers. The Canadian ED Research Network also offers the opportunity to link data with other networks using federated analytic approaches, leveraging our ecosystem to support cross-disciplinary research and collaborations, for example, in global pandemic preparedness. Those interested in accessing existing Canadian ED Research Network data or launching or participating in a Canadian ED Research Network study should contact their institution’s Canadian ED Research Network Member or the coordinating center (admin.cedrn@ubc.ca).

Conclusions

In summary, the Canadian ED Research Network is a new pan-Canadian research network that builds on lessons learned during the pandemic to chart a path toward a mature, pan-Canadian emergency medicine research platform with broad utility. It has a strong potential to facilitate and accelerate emergency medicine research. The Canadian ED Research Network is designed to detect and evaluate emerging pathologies, enable health system and operations research, and bolster Canada’s health emergencies and pandemic preparedness.
