Association between a citywide ban on syringe distribution and provision of harm reduction services
Authors: Pranav Padmanabhan, Paul Christine, Jarratt Pytell, Nikki Wagner, Jason Gibbons, Christine Charron, Katherine LeMasters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSyringe services programs (SSPs) decrease HIV and overdose incidence among people who use drugs (PWUD). In May 2024, Pueblo, Colorado, banned syringe distribution, citing public complaints about syringe litter. The ban was overturned in June 2024. The two SSPs in Pueblo halted syringe distribution during the three-week ban but continued other services. The effect of short-term syringe bans on harm reduction services is unknown.MethodsWe analyzed program data from one SSP in Pueblo, Colorado, and syringe litter pickup data from the Pueblo Department of Parks and Recreation from January to September 2024. We used Interrupted Time Series analysis to evaluate the association between the syringe distribution ban and four weekly outcomes: SSP visits, naloxone kits distributed, syringes distributed, and syringe litter collected.ResultsThe ban was associated, on average, with 31 fewer visits (95% CI: −48, −15), 23 fewer naloxone kits distributed (95% CI: −34, −12), and 2406 fewer syringes distributed (95% CI: −3308, −1504) every week. There was an immediate decrease of 120 syringe litter units collected (95% CI: −285, 46) weekly. After the ban was overturned, there was an immediate increase and increasing trend in visits, naloxone kits, and syringes distributed, and no changes in syringe litter. Naloxone distribution did not return to expected levels in the 16 weeks following the ban being overturned.ConclusionBanning syringe distribution in Pueblo, Colorado was associated with sustained decreases in SSP usage and naloxone distribution. Eliminating funding for certain SSP activities could inadvertently decrease access to naloxone.Keywords: Harm reduction, Naloxone, Syringe service program, Syringe litter, Interrupted time series

Background

Harm reduction programs, including syringe services programs (SSPs), are part of the spectrum of community health services for people who use drugs (PWUD). SSPs began operating in the United States in the late 1980s with support from HIV/AIDS activists and state and local governments (Des Jarlais, 2017). SSPs provide comprehensive and essential healthcare services, including distribution of sterile syringes and naloxone, safe smoking supplies, overdose prevention education, HIV, hepatitis C, and sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing, wound care, integrated behavioral health care, and linkage to treatment for substance use disorders (Des Jarlais, 2017). SSPs are associated with significant reductions in HIV and hepatitis C transmission and overdose morbidity and mortality (Broz et al., 2021). Further, people who access SSPs are five times more likely to enter substance use treatment than those who do not access SSPs (Adams, 2020). About half of PWUD do not have a primary care provider and many report experiencing less stigma at SSPs compared to traditional medical settings, making SSPs essential community resources (Behrends et al., 2025).

SSPs have experienced challenges since their operations began in the United States, largely due to political pressure and public stigma, threatening to limit the scope and scale of harm reduction operations in high-need areas (Diaz et al., 2024). Opposition to SSPs has resulted in reduced funding, closures, and restrictions on services in California (Reyes, 2024) and West Virginia (Bergdorf-Smith, 2024), among others. In addition, the U.S. President issued an executive order in July 2025 to ban federal funding for “so-called ‘harm reduction’ efforts,” threatening to further jeopardize SSP access. In a subsequent letter from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), the federal government clarified that federal funding would be accessible to SSPs, including to purchase naloxone, but restricted from being used to fund the acquisition and distribution of syringes and other supplies imagined to “promote and facilitate drug use” (Kleinschmidt, 2025). Over half of SSPs receive the majority of their funding from public sources (Behrends et al., 2025).

The effects of recent SSP restrictions on harm reduction access and use are unknown. Historically, after the closure of an SSP in Connecticut in 1997, there were observed increases in sharing and reusing of syringes among PWUD, and no reductions in syringe litter (Broadhead, 1999). Similar behavior changes were observed following the closure of a fixed-site SSP and accompanying shift to mobile-only services in a Canadian city in 2008, where there was a decrease in clients reached and syringes distributed and collected (Macneil and Pauly, 2010). Reducing funding support or eliminating commonly used services at SSPs, specifically syringe distribution and needle exchange activities, may result in lower use of those services and spillover effects on other services offered by the SSP by changing the likelihood and frequency at which PWUD return to the SSP. Quantifying these effects is crucial to understand the potential ramifications of new harm reduction restrictions being implemented in the United States.

On May 13, 2024, Pueblo, Colorado banned syringe distribution, citing concerns about syringe litter (Bartolo, 2024; 2020). On June 6, 2024, the Pueblo County District Court overturned the ban following legal challenges (Bartolo, 2024). During the three-week ban, Pueblo’s SSPs halted syringe distribution and collection, but continued other services, including naloxone distribution. In this exploratory study, we evaluated SSP visits, syringe distribution, and public syringe litter collection before, during, and after Pueblo, Colorado’s ban. To assess spillover effects on other evidence-based practices, we also evaluated changes to naloxone distribution during and after the ban. The goal of the study was to measure the association between Pueblo’s syringe distribution ban and local outcomes as more localities consider similar SSP regulation, as well as to highlight the potential consequences of recent policy changes that limit funding for SSPs.

Methods

Our primary dataset was client contact log data from one of two SSPs in Pueblo, which included supplies received during each visit from January 1 to September 26, 2024. Due to the rapidly changing policy environment, a convenience sample was taken from a single SSP with established data infrastructure to provide timely assessment. At all intakes and subsequent visits, SSP client visits are recorded in a contact log, which includes a unique client identifier, date of visit, client date of birth, and services received (syringes returned and received, naloxone, and additional services). The outcome of naloxone distribution represents the sum of all types of naloxone kits distributed (intranasal 4 mg, intranasal 8 mg, and injectable naloxone). Each kit includes two doses of intranasal naloxone or one dose of double-strength intramuscular naloxone. Demographic and behavioral information, such as race, gender, and injection frequency, are collected infrequently. These data are provided only in Table 1 for descriptive purposes and were not used in further analysis. Contact log data are collected in compliance with the state of Colorado’s reporting requirements for SSPs.

We also obtained Pueblo Department of Parks and Recreation data recording syringe litter collection between January 1 and November 2, 2024, using public records requests. Data included the number of all littered syringes collected by the Department of Parks and Recreation from public spaces (including parks, trails, spaces surrounding department stores, etc.) on each pickup date. Data were aggregated to the weekly level for longitudinal analysis.

Statistical analysis

We conducted interrupted time series (ITS) analyses to study the associated change in weekly SSP visits, naloxone distribution, syringe distribution, and syringe litter from before the ban to when the ban was in effect, and then to after the ban was overturned. ITS fits time series data to model trends in the outcome before and after an “interruption”, such as a new policy, that might be expected to change the outcome (Lopez Bernal et al., 2016). By modeling trends in the outcome prior to the interruption, ITS models project what the expected outcome trend would have been without the interruption. Differences between the observed and expected outcomes after the interruption, assuming no confounding, are attributed to the interruption. ITS enables us to quantify both immediate changes associated with each interruption point (“level changes”) and changes in trends across time after each interruption point (“slope changes”). We leveraged segmented regression, which allowed us to separately estimate the associated change in the level and slope of service use across multiple timepoints. To study the initial ban and its subsequent removal, we used two interruption points: the date of the ban’s passing and the date of the ban’s overturn. We retained level change and slope change parameters for each interruption using stepwise backward elimination with a p < 0.2 threshold, removing non-significant model parameters consecutively until all were under this threshold. We also conducted three sensitivity analyses. In the first analysis we used the syringe ban enactment date (five days following the vote) as the first interruption rather than the syringe ban vote to measure potential chilling effects (LeMasters et al., 2025). Second, we excluded the week of September 3, 2024, which coincided with a large homeless encampment clearance, and may have artificially increased syringe litter pickup. Finally, we excluded the final week as an outlier due to potentially incomplete data from administrative delays in reporting.

This study was a secondary analysis of administrative data and was approved for exemption by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (24–1063).

Results

During the study period, the SSP served 2268 unique clients across 8604 visits. The mean number of visits per client was 3.8 (SD=6.6). There were 4800 naloxone kits distributed and 355,543 syringes distributed. There were 5760 littered syringes collected between January 1-September 3, 2024. Demographics were available for 260 clients (11.5%) (Table 1). Parameter estimates from the regression models are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

SSP visits

In January 2024, there were, on average, 208 total SSP visits per week. Before the ban, visits were increasing, on average, by 2 per week (95% CI: −1, 5). During the ban, there were no immediate level changes in visits, and an associated average rate decreaseof 31 visits per week (95% CI: −48, −15). After the ban was overturned, there was an associated level increase of 54 visits on average (95% CI: −11, 119) and average rate increase of 5 visits per week (95% CI: 2, 9). Visits per week did not reach expected levels based upon pre-ban trends in 16 weeks following the ban.

Naloxone distribution

In January 2024, there were 114 total naloxone kits distributed per week. Before the ban, the total number of naloxone kits distributed was increasing, on average, by 2 per week (95% CI: −0.3, 4). During the ban, there was no level change in naloxone kits distributed, but there was an associated average rate decrease of 23 kits per week (95% CI: −34, −12). After the ban was overturned, there was an associated average level increase of 48 kits (95% CI: 5, 91) and a subsequent average rate increase of 3 kits per week (95% CI: 0.3, 5). Naloxone distribution did not reach expected levels based upon pre-ban trends in 16 weeks following the ban.

Syringe distribution

In January 2024, there were 9023 syringes distributed per week, which remained stable before the ban. During the ban, there was no level change in syringes distributed, and an associated average rate decrease of 2406 syringes distributed per week (95% CI: −3308, −1504). After the ban was overturned, there was an associated average level increase of 6287 syringes (95% CI: 2166, 10,408) and an average rate increase of 595 syringes per week (95% CI: 347, 842).

Syringe litter

In January 2024, there were 217 littered syringes collected per week, which remained stable before the ban. Syringe litter collection decreased immediately after the ban, with 120 fewer discarded syringes collected (95% CI: −285, 46) the following week. The trend in syringe litter collection did not change during the ban. After the ban was overturned, there were no immediate or sustained changes in syringe litter collected.

Sensitivity analyses

In a sensitivity analysis using the vote enactment date rather than the ban date, SSP visits followed similar trends, but exceeded pre-ban levels nine weeks after the ban was overturned. Naloxone and syringe distribution followed similar trends as the primary analysis. However, there was no decrease in the syringe litter level or rate associated with the ban (Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Figure 1). In a sensitivity analysis of syringe litter excluding the week of a large homeless encampment sweep, there remained a level change associated with the ban, and there was an increasing rate in both pre-ban and post-ban periods (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Figure 2). When excluding the final week of the study period, SSP visits, naloxone distribution, and syringe distribution followed similar trends as the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Figure 3).

Discussion

After Pueblo, Colorado banned syringe distribution for three weeks, there were associated reductions in the use of harm reduction services. SSP visits and distribution of naloxone kits decreased and did not return to expected levels based upon pre-ban trends in the 16 weeks following the ban being overturned. Decreases in SSP visits may translate into decreased uptake of services such as HIV testing and treatment for substance use disorders, though these outcomes were not systematically available in our data (Macneil and Pauly, 2010). Removing access to clean syringes from SSPs may increase needle sharing, which could lead to increased injection-related infections (Marotta et al., 2021). Syringe distribution decreased after the ban but surpassed expected levels based upon pre-ban trends six weeks after the ban was overturned, potentially reflecting pent-up demand or stockpiling behaviors. Analogously, following a 2-week period of increased police enforcement of public drug use around homeless encampments in Boston, MA in 2019, visits to an opioid use disorder treatment clinic that also provides harm reduction services surpassed previous levels after falling during the action (Weisenthal et al., 2022).

The impetus for the vote was public concern about syringe litter (Bartolo, 2024). While the number of discarded syringes collected from public land decreased immediately following the ban in the primary analysis, no association was found when using the ban enactment date as the interruption point, suggesting inconclusive evidence for an association between the ban and changes in syringe litter. In all scenarios, syringe litter did not increase beyond pre-ban levels after the ban was overturned, even as syringe distribution resumed and surpassed pre-ban trends during the same period. This may be due to efforts by SSPs to promote syringe return or increased awareness of proper syringe disposal practices after the ban period, as the threat of further bans may have deterred littering. Notably, previous research has linked opening new SSPs with decreased syringe litter (Levine et al., 2019). While the ultimate relationship between syringe distribution bans and syringe litter would likely benefit from further research with systematic data collection on syringe litter, any findings should be contextualized with respect to the well-documented harms of withholding sterile syringe distribution on injection-related infections.

Pueblo’s syringe ban may have disrupted naloxone distribution efforts which reduce overdose deaths, as the ban was associated with approximately 900 fewer naloxone kits being distributed over a 20-week time span encompassing the during- and post-ban periods compared to expected values had pre-ban trends continued (Zang et al., 2024). Previous work has shown that one overdose death is prevented for approximately every 230 naloxone kits distributed (Coffin and Sullivan, 2013).

There is currently an unmet need for additional naloxone distribution across the United States, particularly at community-based sites, as PWUD are key first responders to overdose (Irvine et al., 2022). Beyond Pueblo, threats to achieving community saturation of naloxone have proliferated in the form of funding cuts and community and political pushback. SSPs distributed over 700,000 doses of naloxone in the United States in 2019; extrapolating the disruption in naloxone distribution associated with Pueblo’s temporary ban to the United States would yield over 110,000 fewer naloxone kits (Association, 2023). Our results suggest that the current administration's efforts to reduce funding for specific services at SSPs may further increase unmet naloxone need and may lead to preventable overdose deaths across the United States.

Limitations of this study include our data coming from only one of two SSPs in Pueblo, Colorado and lacking data on potential control counties with similar demographic information to Pueblo. However, this study is a critical first step in understanding harm reduction bans, particularly how bans intended for one service spill over onto others. We also relied on limited public records of syringe litter, and lacked data on other evidence-based services that may have been impacted by the ban including HIV testing and referrals for treatment of substance use disorder (Macneil and Pauly, 2010). While counts of public syringe litter from the parks department could be inaccurate, we expect that the inaccuracies are likely consistent over time, enabling valid comparisons and minimizing bias in our effect estimates. Future studies are planned to evaluate the effect of restrictions on harm reduction programs on health outcomes including HIV testing, injection-related infections, emergency department visits, and fatal and non-fatal overdoses.

This study adds to the evidence base around SSP closures, suggesting that even temporary service bans may have sustained negative impacts on overdose prevention efforts.
