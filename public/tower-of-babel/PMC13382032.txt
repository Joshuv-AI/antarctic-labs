A Scoping Review of Direct-to-Consumer Nutrigenetic Testing: Mapping Genes and Associated Nutrition Recommendations
Authors: Caitlin McCartney, Kaitlin Day, Melissa Adamski, Judy Bauer, Aimee L Dordevic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConsumer demand for autonomy in their own health care is fueling the rise of personal genetic testing. Nutrigenetics tests promise tailored dietary recommendations to help consumers achieve their health and well-being goals. To support informed decision-making by both practitioners and consumers, there is a need for a comprehensive evaluation of the current market landscape and the specific nutrition claims being made. A scoping review was completed via an internet search. Companies that provided direct-to-consumer tests with nutritional recommendations were included. Nonhuman, or pediatric tests, and tests requiring a health practitioner to order were excluded. Genes and nutrition recommendations used in the testing panels were mapped, and company characteristics were summarized. The review was prospectively registered with Open Science Framework (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/A4K2R). There were 104 companies providing 204 nutrition-related testing panels that were included. The mean cost was US$234, with North America the most common continent of company registration. Only 56 (54%) companies publicly disclosed the genes used to make nutritional recommendations, with 3309 unique genes identified across testing panels. Micronutrients (n = 1593 genes), cardiovascular health (n = 1446 genes), and weight loss (n = 1383 genes) were the most commonly reported nutrition categories. Posttest support was provided by 55 (53%) companies, but this was often at additional cost (n = 33), and the qualifications of those providing support varied greatly. The expanding nutrigenetics market continues to be unregulated, with high variability in offerings. With thousands of unique genes linked with nutrition recommendations, it is challenging for healthcare practitioners and consumers to keep pace with this dynamic market. Evidence analysis and resources are required to support healthcare practitioners to provide consumers with evidence-based guidance and ensure their best interests are protected.Keywords: direct-to-consumer genetic testing, nutrigenetics, nutrition, precision medicine, precision nutrition, diet

Statement of Significance

This scoping review extensively maps the diverse landscape of direct-to-consumer nutrigenetic services, identifying >3000 genes being used to make nutrition recommendations to consumers. Key consumer risk areas highlighted include gaps in appropriate posttest support and implementation, which have implications for consumer safety, health literacy, and equitable translation of genetic and nutrition science into practice.

Introduction

Elements of healthcare are shifting from provider-directed, treatment-focused care to a consumer-driven, preventative approach [1,2]. In the era of big data and real-time health metrics, consumer demand for personalized health information is increasing [3,4]. One of the more recent advancements in nutrition science is nutrigenetics; the interpretation of an individual’s genetic profile (DNA sequence) to develop a therapeutic, individualized nutrition prescription with the goal of optimizing health outcomes [5,6]. Two models exist for the provision of personal genetic testing, both involving the collection of a buccal swab or other biological sample for laboratory-based genetic analysis: 1) direct-to-consumer (DTC), where the sample collection and submission are managed by the consumer at home, and 2) practitioner-facilitated, where testing is coordinated through a healthcare professional [7]. The DTC market has grown exponentially, and is forecast to reach USD10.5 billion by 2032 [8].

The substantial variability in individual responses to dietary interventions has long been a source of frustration for consumers. For example, in the context of weight loss, one individual may experience significant success on a high-protein diet, whereas another may see little to no improvement from the same program, yet respond positively to a high-fat approach [9]. Using the well-founded premise that an individual’s genotype can predict (with varying degrees) the phenotypic response to an intervention, nutrigenetics is appealing to consumers and health practitioners, potentially saving time and resulting in more efficient distribution of health resources. This level of predictive precision has been possible for some time in the context of uncommon but well-understood monogenic diseases. The classic example is the recommendation of a phenylalanine-free diet for individuals with a phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) gene mutation causing phenylketonuria [10]. In contrast, the influence of genetics on polygenic diseases is considerably more complex; yet, given their prevalence, well-substantiated nutrigenetics coupled with evidence-based recommendations holds considerable potential as a transformative tool for preventative health [3,10].

Although genome-wide association studies have identified associations between genetic variants and polygenic health outcomes, these are far from causal relationships, and do not infer how diet can influence outcomes in people with specific polymorphisms [11]. Appropriate, randomized controlled interventions must be conducted to support nutrition recommendations based on genotype. Industry innovation, driven by rapidly advancing technologies and consumer demand, has outpaced acceptance of nutrigenetics in traditional models of healthcare delivery, prompting concerns about misinformation, weak regulation, and potential consumer exploitation [12,13]. In 2020, Floris et al. [3] identified 45 companies that offered nutrigenetic testing, but the landscape of this market has rapidly evolved, and many more companies and products are now available. These concerns extend to privacy, where in the era of data breaches, the potential indefinite storage and ownership of genetic data by private companies, and transfer of genetic information across country borders, raise ethical and security issues [14]. These risks must be justified by clear benefits; however, current professional consensus within the healthcare community suggests that there remains a lack of robust scientific evidence and established clinical guidelines to support widespread implementation [4,15].

To provide guidance for practitioners and consumers, it is essential to have a comprehensive understanding of the market and recommendations that are being made, including the specific genes being used within nutrition advice categories. This scoping review aims to provide a comprehensive snapshot of companies offering DTC nutrigenetic testing and provide further insight into the genes commonly included in testing panels.

Methods

A scoping review and content analysis were conducted to examine online nutrigenetic tests. The review was prospectively registered with Open Science Framework (doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/A4K2R).

Search strategy

Google Advanced and DuckDuckGo search engines were used to identify companies that provide DTC nutrigenetic tests. These search engines were selected for their popularity with consumers and capacity to perform anonymized searches, thereby minimizing algorithmic bias from cookies or Internet Protocol (IP) tracking. The following query strings were used in Google Advanced: 1) DNA test nutrition, 2) nutrition genetic test, 3) personalized diet test, and 4) genetic test diet; DuckDuckGo: 1) DNA nutrition test, 2) DNA test wellness. DuckDuckGo yielded limited results (2 and 49 unique hits for the query strings, respectively); therefore, Google Advanced was deemed sufficient to achieve data saturation, and no additional queries were executed via DuckDuckGo.

Companies were included if they made nutrition recommendations based on genetic results and offered their service directly to consumers from their website. Exclusion criteria were; companies that used nonhuman genetic testing (e.g., pets), had a target audience aged <18 y, provided nutrition recommendations that were not based on genetic results (e.g., based on blood markers or microbiome results), or only provided testing via clinics or health practitioners. The search was considered complete when <20% of results per page met the inclusion criteria. News articles (n = 46) were excluded from analysis; however, they were manually reviewed to identify potential companies for inclusion.

To mitigate the potential influence of search engine personalization, one query string was replicated by a second reviewer on a separate device to confirm completeness of the search strategy. The search strategy was considered comprehensive if >90% results overlapped between reviewers.

Recognizing the evolving nature of the nutrigenetic market and the fluidity of online content, the findings represent a temporal snapshot of available services at the time of the search and data extraction. To account for delays between the initial search and publication, the Google Advanced search was updated in June 2025, limited to the first 2 pages of results for each query. Consumers do not commonly view results past the first few pages [16]; thus, this was deemed sufficient to ensure that the companies that are being accessed by consumers were included in the review.

Eligibility, screening, and data extraction

Website screening was conducted from June 2023 to March 2024. Non-English sites were included through the use of Google Translate. Each website’s landing page was reviewed to determine eligibility, and if it was unclear whether a DTC nutrigenetic service was offered, the “services” or similar page was examined.

Final eligibility was determined simultaneously with data extraction, summarized in Supplementary Figure 1. Data extraction was shared among 3 authors between November 2024 and June 2025. Screenshots were taken of all relevant webpages, with the date of collection recorded for each. For validation, 10% of the included websites were randomly selected and independently extracted by a second author. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion.

Data extracted included company demographics; date of extraction; testing laboratory information; availability of a sample report and the genes tested stratified by nutrition category; cost; availability and nature of posttest consultation; scientific experts/advisory panel qualifications; and availability and nature of legal documents prepurchase (terms and conditions and privacy policy).

Genetic panels that did not have an obvious nutritional component, such as those providing fitness analysis, skin analysis, or disease risk profiles, were excluded during data extraction.

Data analysis and summary synthesis

Nutrition advice categories and their corresponding genes were extracted and collated. The majority of companies reported genes using National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) gene symbols. Where possible, gene aliases were converted to the same NCBI gene name. For companies that listed only Reference Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) ID (rsID) numbers, these were converted to NCBI gene symbols using the NIH database [17] or GeneCards [18] to enable data synthesis. Polymorphism positions or gene subtypes, such as alpha and beta variants, were not included, as this level of detail was not consistently reported on company websites. Each characteristic was standardized (as subcategories) and grouped into one of 18 nutrition advice themes: micronutrients, cardiovascular health, weight management, food intolerance, detoxification, metabolic health, disease risk, inflammation recommendations, macronutrients, bone health, neurotransmitters, multisystem health, DNA damage and repair, hormones, diet patterns, diet and nutrition, digestive health, and other. Allocation to themes was based on the intent of advice; for example, fat metabolism advice could be aimed at cardiovascular protection or weight loss. Where intent was unclear, broader categories were applied (e.g., fat metabolism classified under “macronutrients”).

Themes such as detoxification, hormones, neurotransmitters, DNA damage and repair, inflammation, and “other” (including topics such as sleep, stress, and mental health) were included when embedded within nutrition-focused panels. However, panels where these topics were presented separately or as stand-alone tests were excluded from analysis.

Qualifications of practitioners providing further posttest support offered by the company were categorized into A) genetic counselors or geneticists; B) medical practitioners , dietitians, registered nutritionists; C) other (e.g., naturopath, health coach); and D) artificial intelligence (AI).

For each of the 104 companies, pricing for nutrigenetic tests was collated and recorded in local currency. During analysis, all prices were converted into USD as of 6 June, 2025, using online currency conversion [19] for comparison. Dollar figures were rounded to the nearest USD1.00.

The word cloud was generated using an online webpage, Wordart.com, by inputting all recorded genes and their frequencies. The network diagram was generated using the visNetwork package in R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024), including genes that were reported ≥20 times across nutrigenetic panels and their associated nutrition recommendations.

Results

Nutrigenetic company and product characteristics

A total of 204 individual nutrigenetic testing panels from 104 companies were included. Company locations are summarized in Table 1, with most companies registered in North America, followed by Europe and the United Kingdom. Sixty companies (58%) disclosed the location of their testing laboratory, with six using a laboratory located in a different country from their registration. Eight companies reported the use of multiple laboratories globally and were not clear whether genetic data crossed country borders. Laboratory accreditation details were provided by 62 companies.

Company characteristics are summarized in Table 3, including the nutrition categories of recommendations, whether disease risk was included in the nutrigenetic panels (n = 44), posttest support offered, the costs and inclusions of these posttest support sessions, qualifications of those providing support, and whether further electronic support was provided beyond the standard written report. All companies provided a report of analysis and action-based recommendations upon purchase, but only 45% (n = 47) of companies made a sample report publicly available on their website before purchase. In addition to written reports, 49 companies offered access to an online platform or mobile application for consumers to explore their results. Furthermore, 55 companies provided interactive support, most commonly through one-on-one consultations with a practitioner. The qualifications of individuals providing this support were disclosed by 41 companies, 75% of those offering support. Eleven companies provided support through formally trained genetics experts (geneticists or genetic counselors), and 28 by formally trained medical or nutrition experts (doctor of medicine (MD)), dietitians, and registered nutritionists), with two companies having an individual with both genetics and nutrition qualifications. Some companies list credentials that may not be globally recognized or locally standardized. Four companies used AI to provide support to consumers. For 14 companies, these individual consultations were included in the cost of the nutrigenetic test, whereas 33 companies offered this support at an additional cost, and eight companies did not disclose whether consultation was included. In cases where companies offered a range of DTC genetic tests, consultations with genetic counselors or geneticists were generally reserved for services that included disease risk profiling, and were not available for nutrigenetic results. In addition, 15 companies referred customers to external practitioners for posttest support; however, all of these practitioners were self-certified through company-designed training modules to interpret results.

Terms of service and privacy policies were freely available online for 86 and 92 companies, respectively, but varied greatly in the level of detail and readability. Twenty companies reported storage of genetic data (physical sample or results not specified) for an undisclosed or indefinite time period, with many citing that this was to continue to provide retesting opportunities as the field of knowledge evolves.

Genes tested and nutrition advice

Fifty-six companies disclosed the genes included in their testing panels, with 3309 unique genes identified across these companies that were described as being used to generate diet and nutrition advice. Micronutrient recommendations represented the largest category of nutrigenetic testing, with 1593 genes reported, and calcium requirements comprising the largest subcategory (n = 344 genes). Cardiovascular health was the second largest category, involving 1446 genes, with fat metabolism the largest subcategory (n = 637 genes). Weight management was the third largest category, with 1383 genes tested across companies, and obesity was the largest subcategory (n = 712). A summary of all nutrigenetic testing categories and the corresponding number of genes is presented in Table 4. The full list of subcategories is included in Supplementary File 1.

Discussion

In this review, we have summarized 104 DTC companies offering nutrigenetic services, a 43% increase compared with a scoping review conducted in 2019 [3]. Growth is evident not only in the number of companies providing nutrigenetic services but also in the number of genes being used to generate nutrition advice. The number of genes disclosed by companies before purchase has increased by 63%, and the true number is likely considerably higher. This is the most extensive snapshot of the commercial nutrigenetic market to date, providing a comprehensive map of genes and linking them to categories of nutritional advice. Although nutrigenetic products and services are in abundance globally, companies vary substantially in the composition of their genetic panels, the nutrition categories they address, the availability of posttest support, and the transparency of their data-privacy practices. These findings are consistent with other reviews on this topic [3,5,16,20].

Testing panels are highly variable between companies

There is high variability between the companies’ testing panels, with limited to no resources available for practitioners to assess which tests have clinical utility [6,15,21]. Common monogenetic conditions, such as lactose intolerance or alcohol sensitivity, have less genetic testing variation, with four and five genes associated, respectively, across all tests reviewed. Emerging literature [3,11] considers that monogenetic nutrient-based recommendations are more reliable compared with complex, polygenic diseases such as obesity. Further compounding the complexity is the heterogeneity between companies regarding the gene polymorphisms used to provide nutritional advice, with genes reported across multiple categories and companies interpreting these findings differently, adding to confusion. The network diagram visually indicates the interconnected web between genes and nutrition recommendations, and the difficulty both consumers and healthcare providers face in navigating interpretations. It should be noted that this diagram only demonstrates the more commonly reported genes (n = 55), accounting for 16% of the total genes mapped in this review.

An example of this variability can be seen in sodium sensitivity related to cardiovascular health and blood pressure. Forty-eight genes were reportedly associated with sodium-sensitive hypertension across companies, but only five genes were reported by ≥4 genetic panels— angiotensinogen gene (AGT), angiotensin I converting enzyme (ACE), solute carrier family 4 member 5 (SLC4A5), angiotensin II receptor type 1 (AGTR1), and adducin 1 (ADD1). Notably, only one of these genes appears in the network diagram, further highlighting the substantial variation and the overwhelming number of genes being referenced across nutrition recommendation categories.

The top 5 most reported genes linked with weight management, food intolerance, and micronutrient requirements

In today’s obesogenic developed world, it is unsurprising that three of the five most reported genes, FTO, PPARG, and TCF7L2, were associated with weight management recommendations. Studies suggest that a Mediterranean dietary pattern may be particularly beneficial for individuals with specific FTO and PPARG genotypes [11,22]; however, these findings remain modest, with no statistically significant effects demonstrated [23]. Although emerging research supports a potential interaction between weight regulation and polymorphisms of FTO and PPARG, the clinical relevance and specific dietary recommendations needed to observe meaningful effects are still unclear. Adding to further complexity, although there may be some evidence of nutrient–gene interactions, genes do not act in isolation [24]. The interactions between multiple genetic variants are not well-understood, and unique testing panels designed by nutrigenetic companies are unlikely to have been tested in rigorous randomized controlled trials, or results have not been peer reviewed and published. This significantly limits the current ability to provide personalized, gene-based nutritional guidance on complex, polygenic diseases such as obesity.

In contrast, HLA-DQ genotyping used for gluten-free diet recommendations in coeliac disease is supported by a more substantial body of research. Certain HLA heterodimers account for ∼40% of the coeliac disease’s heritability, yet their presence does not guarantee disease development [25]; for example, ∼30% of Caucasians carry the alleles, but only ∼3% actually develop the condition [25]. Indeed, although genetic testing is included in clinical diagnostic pathways for coeliac disease, it is not used in isolation [26]. A negative result rules out coeliac disease, avoiding unnecessary invasive tests, whereas a positive result typically leads to further invasive investigation before potential diagnosis [26]. However, when offered DTC genetic testing, there is a risk of oversimplification where individuals may adopt a gluten-free diet based solely on genetic results, without medical guidance or a confirmed diagnosis. Although a well-planned gluten-free diet can be nutritionally adequate, those who make this change with little to no support risk excluding important food groups and nutrients, potentially reducing the overall quality of their diet without due cause [27].

The MTHFR gene was the third most frequently reported gene and is well-established in its associations with folate metabolism and increased cardiovascular disease risk [28]. Shi et al. [29] reported that individuals with the TT genotype demonstrated a 66% reduction in first stroke incidence after folate supplementation. MTHFR and folate are arguably one of the more extensively studied gene–diet interactions, with research dating back to the 1990s [30]. However, the lack of consistent associations demonstrated in meta-analyses highlights the ongoing challenge in translating mechanistic insights into clinically actionable interventions [30,31]. While some companies have used this information to provide food-based recommendations to support adequate intake of B vitamins, others offered minimal dietary guidance and instead promote their own proprietary “personalized” supplement lines as the primary solution, prompting concerns around conflicts of interest. As many healthcare practitioners continue to dismiss these tests as pseudoscience or too complex to interpret [4,7,16], consumers are left to interpret the findings on their own, leading to unnecessary and potentially negative dietary changes.

Concerningly, 44 nutrigenetic testing panels include disease risk assessments (Table 3). We found 385 genes reportedly linked to conditions such as diabetes mellitus (type 1 and type 2), hypothyroidism, nonalcoholic hepatic steatosis, hemochromatosis, and dementia. In a test that is marketed at improving health outcomes through dietary advice, including disease risk commentary to consumers who are not explicitly seeking it, and doing so without the involvement of a qualified healthcare practitioner, raises significant ethical concerns [2,5]. For example, one company claimed to assess genetic risk for anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa, while simultaneously offering weight loss advice in its sample report. However, there was no indication of appropriate models of care provided for eating disorders, such as access to psychological support [32]. Another company tested for the well-known breast cancer (BRCA) gene variants and provided nutrition advice aimed at reducing breast cancer risk, which seems inappropriate considering the severity of BRCA-related outcomes and the complex multidisciplinary care typically required [33]. Moreover, providing potentially distressing or confusing disease risk information, often based on data with limited evidence [5], can create an unnecessary burden on consumers and the healthcare systems when seeking posttest support [2]. Concerns extend beyond disease risk to the quality of dietary advice itself. One company recommended a phosphate-rich diet that included carbonated beverages, without excluding sugar-sweetened options, which are widely recognized as nutritionally harmful [34].

Companies varied in the quality of posttest support

Our review shows that nutrigenetics companies may not be providing practitioners who are sufficiently qualified in either nutrition or genetics to provide appropriate follow-up support for the results of these tests. However, this raises the question of what profession should be providing guidance around nutrigenetic result interpretation. As a general rule, genetic counselors have the genetic knowledge but not necessarily the dietary knowledge, whereas dietitians have the inverse expertise [15]. Although dual qualifications across the specialties are possible, and such a professional was available from 2 companies, this would be considered relatively uncommon.

Only 53% of companies offered one-on-one posttest support, and fewer than a third of these made genetic professionals available. The cost of this support varied widely, and consumers are unlikely to factor these additional expenses into their initial decision to pursue nutrigenetic testing. As a result, the unforeseen financial burden of obtaining credible follow-up guidance raises concerns that consumers may be unable to obtain appropriate support if confusion or distress arises as a direct result of nutrigenetic test results. Some companies provided lists of external practitioners of varying qualifications and price points, but all were required to be “accredited” by the company through company-provided product training, leading to the same potential for bias as company-employed practitioners.

Data use and privacy

In an era of corporate data breaches, the lack of transparency and the indefinite storage and ownership of genetic data by private companies are concerning [14]. Privacy statements are often vague, with the fate of biological samples and electronic records of genetic codes posttest often not clearly stated [16,20]. This concern is not unique to nutrigenetics, but is also present for any service where biological samples are provided, such as genetics for ancestry tracking, or blood for biomarker analysis. A notable example is the legal proceedings surrounding the potential acquisition of 23andMe Inc. by a pharmaceutical company, and the uncertain future of the hundreds of thousands of genetic profiles submitted by its users [35]. As genetic data freely crosses international borders, national laws are redundant, making meaningful regulation of the industry a challenge [5,36]. These risks must be justified by clear benefits; however, current professional consensus within the healthcare community suggests that there remains a lack of robust scientific evidence and established clinical guidelines to support widespread implementation [4,15].

Limitations and future directions

The limitations of this review primarily stem from the inherent challenges associated with using grey literature sources exclusively [37]. The unregulated and unstandardized nature of the information analyzed introduces several constraints when attempting to map this field, particularly the difficulty in capturing a static snapshot of webpages at the time of data extraction and detecting all global companies providing commercial nutrigenetic offerings. Furthermore, commercial websites are subject to frequent updates, domain changes, or content removal, often without record, making reproducibility and historical comparison difficult. In this analysis, 23 companies were excluded due to changes in their webpages between initial screening and data extraction. Consequently, any review of commercial offerings represents only a snapshot in time and is not exhaustive. A focus for our review, gene-level data were available for only 54% of companies without the purchase of a product. Several providers explicitly stated that the gene lists presented were not comprehensive but rather an example only. Where no disclaimer was provided, it was assumed that all genes tested were disclosed; however, it is recognized that the list of genes extracted in this analysis is likely incomplete. The depth of analysis was also constrained by the level of detail provided for each nutrition-related category used within testing panels. Broad or vague terms such as “detoxification” were often used without clarification, and may include traits such as “alcohol sensitivity” that other companies reported on separately.

Reviewing the evidence that links genes with nutrition advice was outside the scope of this review, but this is a necessary step required to determine the utility of nutrigenetics. It was noted during data extraction that some companies conduct research and development activities, using the samples provided by consumers. Although these research activities were presumably to improve their products and recommendations, the outcomes of these studies were not openly disclosed. Many companies marketed their products as evidence-based and up-to-date with the latest advancements in the field, but did not disclose what evidence was used to determine the nutrigenetic panel inclusions. To the knowledge of the authors, there are limited systematic literature reviews and meta-analyses examining the outcome of specific genotypes and dietary adjustment, raising the question whether the “evidence-based panels” are peer reviewed and scientifically rigorous.

Several critical gaps in the current landscape of commercial DTC nutrigenetic products have been identified in this review, which are summarized in Table 5 [15,21,36,[38], [39], [40], [41]] with our recommended strategic solutions. A key proposed strategy is international collaboration between nutrigenetic scientists and nutrition practitioners (e.g., dietitians) to form expert working groups to generate guidelines to standardize practice. These would need to be living guidelines due to the rapidly progressing nature of nutrigenetics [42]. These approaches have been successfully used by pharmacogenetics, a similar scientific discipline, to standardize the use and interpretation of pharmacogenetic testing[40] []. The living guidelines could include up-to-date and summarized information on nutrition-related genes with links to published evidence, as well as examples of how to use the evidence for giving nutrition recommendations. They could also include a checklist for consumers and practitioners to assess a companies’ compliance with voluntary standards of ethical practice. Making these guidelines openly accessible to industry may support product improvement and promote closer alignment with evidence-based recommendations and ethical standards, although progress is likely to be incremental and gradual [43,44]. For example, guidelines could recommend that nutrigenetic panels exclude disease-screening claims. We found that 42% of panels in our review currently combine nutrition advice with disease screening. Outcomes of disease-screening tests may cause distress for consumers, especially when they are not explicitly seeking this information [7]. Uncoupling the two may, therefore, represent a more ethical practice to minimize potential consumer harm. While adoption of the proposed recommendations may need to be optional for companies, as mandatory regulation is complex [5,20,43,45] and outside the scope of this review, they would provide guidance for companies seeking to provide a quality, clinically useful tool to support health outcomes. As a very minimum, consumers and practitioners could use these guidelines to make informed decisions about whether these health tools are valid and useful for them. The proposed recommendations included in Table 5 are not exhaustive, as international governance issues are challenging [5,45], especially when dealing with sensitive personal data, and these issues warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, the lack of clinical guidelines and the large variation in commercial nutrigenetic products create uncertainty for both practitioners and consumers. The extensive network of interconnected genes is complex, with no way to determine which panels are supported by the best evidence. Consumers seek out unbiased support, but also disengage with traditional practitioners in favor of those who will be supportive of alternative endeavors, but who may not be appropriately qualified [7]. Healthcare practitioners need to be prepared to discuss both the benefits and limitations of nutrigenetics with consumers seeking advice. The first step to untangling this interconnected web of information is to collate and critique the available evidence linking genetics, nutrition recommendations, and health outcomes. Nutrigenetics continues to promise a powerful healthcare tool. Future research needs to decipher the benefits and limitations for consumers and practitioners, and coordinated efforts are required to ensure the responsible development of the market and to support its wider-spread acceptance and use.
