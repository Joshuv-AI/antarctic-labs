Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The overall goal of the project is to explore and identify suitable candidate materials for high-
temperature molten sulfur thermal energy storage (TES) operating up to a maximum temperature
of 400°C–420°C for industrial process heat applications. A detailed corrosion and strength
analysis of coupons subjected to long-term thermal testing in molten sulfur will be conducted to
characterize the fundamental corrosion mechanism, corrosion rates, and mechanical durability.

The results from the project contribute to the engineering design of sulfur TES systems with
desired longevity and lower construction and maintenance costs. The research outcome benefits
the TES research and development community by providing detailed corrosion and degradation
information for more effective engineering design of the molten sulfur TES containment. It
improves the diversity and reliability of the renewable technologies available for the U.S.
industries and U.S. power grid.

Summary of Research Results:

Introduction and Motivation
Molten sulfur (S) has a decent heat capacity of 1022–1263 J/kg∙K between 200°C and 400°C [1],
which is comparable to the state-of-the-art solar salt’s heat capacity of 1500–1540 J/kg·K [2]. It
is also abundant, affordable ($60–100/ton compared to $1000–1200/ton for solar salt), thermally
stable as an element, and has a low vapor pressure up to a temperature of 440oC [3]. The unique
properties, abundant availability, and low cost of molten sulfur make it an attractive choice as a
TES medium for medium-temperature industrial process heat applications. Element 16 has
developed a series of relevant technologies to commercialize the potential of molten-sulfur-based
TES. A key material selection question is the construction material for the storage tanks, as
molten sulfur tends to react with various metallic elements at the target operating temperature of
around 300–400°C. Hence, this project aims to investigate the corrosion resistance and
mechanical properties of several steel materials after exposure to molten sulfur at 400°C for up
to about 1000 hours.

Element 16 Task 1: Design of Experiment Matrix
The suspected main corrosion mechanism is the formation of various sulfide species between the
molten sulfur and the major elements in the steels tested, i.e., Fe, Cr, Ni, Mo, Nb, etc. Table 1
provides the compositions of the steel materials selected for corrosion. Given by the Fe-S phase
diagram [4] and depending on the overall composition, sulfur and iron form multiple sulfide
compounds such as FeS, Fe1-xS, and FeS2 and solid solutions between these compounds at
200°C–400°C. Similarly, sulfur and the other alloying elements also form multiple sulfide
species such as CrS, Cr2S3, MnS, MoS2, NiS2, etc. [5] These thermodynamics complicate the
material selection of the construction materials for molten salt storage.

Element 16 Task 3: Identification of Feasible Materials and TES Costs
Analysis
Task Objective
The final objective of this task is to evaluate the cost of a 500-MWh molten sulfur TES unit
compliant with American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) standards, using corrosion
rate data and mechanical testing results obtained in this project. The molten sulfur TES system
specifications include:

 • Discharge duration: 10 hours
 • Peak thermal charge rate: 2.5 times the constant thermal discharge rate for integration
 with a parabolic trough solar field with a solar multiple of 3.5
 • System Lifetime (𝑁𝑁𝑦𝑦 ): 25 years
 • Thermal cycling range: 210°C–380°C. The discharge temperature of 210°C allows for a
 10°C difference between the process heat requirement of 200°C and bulk sulfur
 temperature at the end of discharge, while the charge temperature of 380°C accounts for a
 10°C difference from the solar field outlet temperature of 390°C.

Among the tested materials, SS 304L and SS 347L exhibited the lowest corrosion rates, at ~100
microns per year after 1,000 hours of exposure to 400°C molten sulfur (discussed in NREL Task
2.1: Corrosion Characterization). Due to its lower cost, SS-304L was selected for the cost
analysis presented here.

Molten Sulfur TES Design

Element 16’s molten sulfur TES design configuration shown in Figure 2 has two major
components: (1) an internal heat exchanger (HX) pipe assembly, referred to as “internal TES-
HX,” located within molten sulfur, and (2) a vessel housing molten sulfur that is hermetically
sealed and blanketed with inert gas such as nitrogen. During charging, heat in the form of hot
heat transfer fluid (HTF) from the solar field will be circulated through the internal TES-HX to
charge the molten sulfur TES. During discharge, cold HTF is circulated through the internal
TES-HX to retrieve stored heat from molten sulfur. The hot HTF from sulfur thermal storage
will be used in commercially available shell and tube HTF heat exchangers to generate process
steam for industrial facilities.

Results and Discussion
Figure 3 below illustrates the required wall thickness for the internal TES-HX pipe calculated
based on Equation 7 for various nominal pipe sizes. The pipe design pressure is set to 50 psi
above the vapor pressure of Syltherm 800 at a maximum temperature of 390°C (Pvap =185 psi).
Syltherm-800, a silicone-based HTF, offers good thermal stability at temperatures up to 400°C,
with a maximum recommended film temperature of 427°C. The 50-psi margin is to
accommodate the frictional head loss for HTF flow along the TES loop, which can be achieved
through careful planning of the HTF pipe layout and flow distribution. The figure also includes
the wall thicknesses for Schedule 10 and Schedule 40 pipes. From the figure, it can be concluded
that for molten sulfur TES operating up to 400°C, SS 304L pipe of schedule 40 or above should
be used for nominal pipe size between 1 inch and 2.5 inches.

In the DOE project (award number DE-EE0009329), Element 16 determined that an optimal
internal heat exchanger specific surface area of 𝐴𝐴′ = 2.8 m2/m3 would support 10 hours of
discharge duration with a peak thermal charge rate 2.5 times that of a constant thermal discharge
rate for integration with a parabolic trough solar field of solar multiple = 3.5. This specific
surface area dictates the spacing between pipes inside sulfur TES. The cost analysis for a 500-
MWh TES with this configuration, using SS 304L for a sulfur temperature cycle of 380–210°C,
is shown in Figure 4 for various nominal pipe sizes of the internal TES-HX. The lowest cost,
$29.5/kWh, was obtained for 1” nominal pipe size. The design specifications for the 500-MWh
sulfur TES with molten sulfur thermally cycling between 210–380°C are a tank height of 12 m, a
tank diameter of 29 m, a shell wall thickness of 4” housing 9,550 metric tons of sulfur, and an
internal TES-HX surface area of 16,500 m2.

Compared to a TES cost of $36/kWh obtained for molten sulfur cycling between 210°C and
300°C (discussed in DOE project DE-EE0009329), operating at a higher temperature of 400°C
reduces the sulfur TES cost by approximately $6.5/kWh. This cost reduction is achieved because
of the increased energy density, despite the higher unit cost of thermal oil HTF ($7.6/kg for
multitherm-ig4 mineral oil versus $20.5/kg for Syltherm-800 silicone oil) and a higher corrosion
allowance requirement. SS 304L in 300°C molten sulfur demonstrated a corrosion rate of 20
microns per year (as determined in DE-EE0009329), compared to 100 microns per year at 400°C
(as determined in this project).

NREL Task 1: Corrosion Coupon Identification and Preparation
Three types of stainless steel (304L, 316L, and 347L) and one type of carbon steel (A516-Gr70)
were identified by Element 16 for the molten sulfur exposure tests based on their previous
experience. The A516-Gr70 carbon steel is unlikely to have superior corrosion resistance on its
own; therefore, a Si-based Silcolloy 2000 corrosion resistance coating was applied by SilcoTek
Corporation via a proprietary process. As shown by SilcoTek’s Raman spectroscopy study [12],
the Silcolloy 2000 is an amorphous Si coating at 580–2400 nm thickness. It remains amorphous
and non-oxidized after a 6-hr exposure in air at 600°C and reduces the corrosion rates of stainless
steels significantly when exposed to a 51 wt.% H2SO4 solution at room temperature. This
reported performance forms the basis of selecting this commercially available amorphous Si
coating for the carbon steel. Similar to Fe and Cr, Si also forms a SiS2 compound at about 400°C
[13]. The investigation hence aims to understand whether the amorphous Si indeed forms
sulfides and whether the formed sulfides slow down further sulfide formation (i.e., with the
underneath Fe) in the A516-Gr70 carbon steel.

Table 1 shows the compositions for the steel materials selected for corrosion testing. The
primary difference among stainless steel 304L, 316L, and 347L is the alloying elements of Nb or
Mo. The presence of these two alloying elements may play a role in the overall corrosion
performance of the stainless steels. For example, it was reported that Mo has a sulfur scavenging
effect if added to SS 304L, which can convert certain metal sulfides to stable MoS2 [14]. This
warrants investigation of the effect of Mo on SS 316L when in direct contact with molten sulfur.
Similarly, the presence of Nb in SS 347L, commonly for improved sensitization prevention and
resistance to intergranular corrosion, could also complicate the corrosion behavior of SS 347L in
molten sulfur.

the coupons. Equation 8 defines the commonly used corrosion rate 𝑅𝑅1 (i.e., loss of metal

Calculation of 𝑅𝑅1 assumes that (1) the corrosion is uniform on the metal surface, which allows

calculated using the specimen weight after an extensive ultrasonic cleaning process in toluene 1 at
an elevated temperature of about 50°C for a few hours to remove most of the residual sulfur and
sulfides. We believe this better represents the true weight of the base metal after exposure to the

Corrosion of Carbon Steel A516-Gr70 with Silcolloy 2000 Si-based Coating
Figure 9 shows the typical appearances of the post-corrosion A516-Gr70 carbon steel sample and
the SS 304L samples before and after chemical cleaning with toluene. The appearances in Figure
9 are consistent across all corrosion durations, and there is no noticeable difference between
different types of stainless-steel samples. The key observation is that for the carbon steel
samples, corrosion products formed on the surface in the form of “discrete islands,” but they
generally stayed on the base metal even after chemical cleaning. On the other hand, very
noticeable scaling occurred on every stainless-steel sample in a uniform and continuous fashion,
most of which were cleaned off by toluene. The implication is that the carbon steel may be
subject to continued corrosion if there is uncorroded surface on the base metal, whereas the
stainless steels may form a continuous scale, which could be either protective or non-protective
against further corrosion. This latter may explain why there is a difference in behavior between
SS 304L/347L and SS 316L.

Figure 10 shows the surface SEM image and EDS analysis for an area not covered by residual
sulfur on an A516-Gr70 sample with Silcolloy 2000 coating after 300 hours of molten sulfur
corrosion at 400°C. By comparing the EDS scans and the estimated compositions before and
after corrosion, this area not covered by residual sulfur, did not seem to experience substantial
interaction with molten sulfur despite some minor amounts of residual sulfur. Higher-
magnification SEM imaging and EDS analysis (Figure A1 in Appendix) do reveal some residual
sulfur but the overall composition of the base metal, including the Si coating, has not been
significantly changed. The rest of the sample is mostly covered by a layer of residual sulfur, as
shown by either the yellow color or dark color (confirmed by a separate EDS analysis).

Figure 11 shows the surface SEM and EDS analysis on the corrosion products after chemical
cleaning with toluene on an A516-Gr70 sample with Silcolloy 2000 coating after 300 hours of
molten sulfur corrosion at 400°C. The EDS analysis was conducted on the entire imaged area.
Figure 12 shows the higher magnification SEM and EDS analysis for these corrosion products.
Scans #2 and #3 are on the corrosion products, whereas Scans #4–#6 are on the exposure areas
between the corrosion products. At 20 keV accelerating voltage of the electrons, EDS
penetration depth should be on the order of a few µm. The size and thickness of the corrosion
products seem to be at least on the order of tens of µm. Therefore, the signals for Scans #2 and
#3 should be mostly from the corrosion products without signal interference from the underneath
base metal. The results indicate that the corrosion products are mostly rich in Fe and S. And the
exposed areas (Scan #4–#6) seem to be similar in composition to the pre-corrosion reference
composition. In conclusion, the results suggest that for 300 hours of corrosion in molten sulfur at
400°C, the A516-Gr70 carbon steel with the Si-based Sicolloy 2000 coating corroded by forming
island-like corrosion products on the surface. The coating seemed to limit the corrosion products
in certain areas. However, in other areas, the coating was not effective at reducing corrosion.
This is concerning as the corrosion could continue, and the coating would eventually be

consumed, which agrees with the observed corrosion rate increase in Figure 8. In addition, the
formation of a Fe- and S-rich corrosion product that could not be cleaned off the surface by the
chemical cleaning procedure indicates that the mass-loss-based corrosion rate is underestimated.

For longer corrosion time of A516-Gr70 with the Si coating, the surface SEM and EDS results

Cross-sectional SEM and EDS analysis on the carbon steel samples revealed the nature of the
corrosion mechanism. As shown by Figure 13–Figure 15, the corrosion seems to proceed by
forming a sulfide-rich pit on the base carbon steel metal. The surrounding base metal is barely
changed in composition (as shown by Scans #2–#4 in Figure 13). The corrosion then proceeds by
growth of the pit with an outward diffusion of Fe (as shown by the concentration difference of Fe
in Scans #5/#6 vs. Scans #7/#8 in Figure 14). The outermost layer of the sulfide pit (Scans
#9/#10 in Figure 14) is slightly less rich in Fe, but its composition is the closest to FeS2 (which
has a theoretical Fe-to-S weight ratio of 0.87:1), which may be the equilibrium phase when
diffused Fe reacts with an abundance of molten sulfur at the forefront of corrosion at 400°C
(based on the Fe-S phase diagram [4]). It is also interesting to see from Figure 15 that some EDS
signals from Si are visible, which again indicates that the presence of the Si coating does not
reduce corrosion. Additional SEM images can be found in Figure A2 in the appendix which
show the extensive formation of the corrosion products on the A516-Gr70 sample with Silcolloy
2000 coating after molten sulfur corrosion at 400°C for up to 1072 hours.

In summary, A516-Gr70 sample with Silcolloy 2000 coating is not considered a good
construction material for molten sulfur storage applications due to its poor long-term corrosion
resistance.

Corrosion Stainless-steel 304L/316L/347L
Figure 16 shows the surface SEM image of an SS 304L sample after 300 hours of molten sulfur
corrosion at 400°C before chemical cleaning with toluene. The appearance is consistent for all
three stainless-steel materials tested at all corrosion durations, where a surface scale is formed
and readily peels off the base metal area. It must be noted that the SEM and EDS results for all
three stainless-steel materials are highly similar. It does not necessarily mean the corrosion
mechanisms are the same. Instead, it means the effects from the Mo and Nb in SS 316L and SS
347L cannot be easily detected by the techniques because (1) the low concentrations of Mo and
Nb in the range of 1–2 wt.% cannot be easily detected by EDS, and (2) the overlap of EDS
signals from Mo and S makes distinguishing the SS 316L effect nearly impossible in a study that
involves molten sulfur. Hence, only the SEM and EDS results for 304L are presented.

It was not easy to perform cross-section analysis of the sulfide scale as the scale was very fragile

sulfide phase in the middle, and (3) a Cr-rich sulfide phase closest to the SS 304L base metal.
Although a comprehensive understanding of the formation mechanisms of these distinct sulfide
phases is outside of the scope of this work, it is possible that the different diffusivity and
thermodynamic reactivity of the three major metallic elements with molten sulfur led to the
formation of the layered structure. This assessment is supported by the SEM and EDS analysis
on the SS 304L sample after chemical cleaning. Figure 21 shows the cross-section SEM and

suggests that the chemical cleaning removed the Ni-rich sulfide and most of the Fe-rich sulfide.
Note that the removal of these two sulfide phases may not be solely attributed to the chemical
cleaning, as the peeling of the scale already happened before chemical cleaning.

The cross-section analysis of SS 304L in Figure 21 shows that there is only a thin layer of
residual corrosion product (< 5 µm) after chemical cleaning and before weight measurement,

It is necessary to discuss the scaling behavior as a major corrosion mechanism. If the rate of
scale formation is consistent, then the thickness of the scale and mass loss should be relatively
proportional to the total corrosion time. This is clearly not the case for the stainless steel samples,
as shown by Figure 7, as well as by the final thickness measurement of the samples, which,
within a reasonable measurement error caused by the surface profile variation, does not show
any measurable trends as a function of corrosion time. The decreasing trend of the SS 304L and
SS 347L corrosion rates as a function of corrosion time is also intriguing. It could be
advantageous if further sulfide formation is slowed down by the initial sulfide formation (i.e., a
somehow protective sulfide layer) if the initial sulfide layer remains chemically and physically
stable. Table 3 and Table 4 show the coefficient of thermal expansion for the selected steel

Therefore, for the stainless steels that have a nearly uniform coverage of surface sulfide scale due

interface. Experimentally, researchers at Element 16 indeed heard a loud cracking noise when
the test coupons were cooled to 120°C–150°C. If there were no cooling and hence no
temperature swing, there could be a chance that the sulfide layers stay protective of the base
metal against further corrosion, which may explain why SS 304L and SS 347L corrosion trended
down over time. It would be worthwhile to conduct a set of future corrosion experiments where
the total corrosion time is the same while the individual corrosion groups experience a different
number of cooling cycles to 120°C–150°C or even room temperature.

The different corrosion behavior of SS 316L cannot be easily explained given the limitations of
the SEM/EDS technique when distinguishing Mo and S. We can only postulate an explanation
given the known behavior of Mo as a key alloying element in SS 316L when it is exposed to
molten sulfur. As pointed out by reference [14] and the following Table 5, which shows the
calculated Gibbs free energy of formation for various metal sulfides by FactSage, Mo does have
a relatively high affinity for sulfur, as indicated by its more negative Gibbs free energy of
formation at 400°C, out of the six key metallic elements in carbon steel and stainless steels.
Hence, Mo may either readily form MoS2 when it is exposed to sulfur during the exposure to
molten sulfur or react with other metal sulfides formed (e.g., all forms of Fe-S and Ni-S, and
most forms of Cr-S) throughout the corrosion process, which would be protective against further
reaction with molten sulfur.

NREL Task 2.2: Mechanical Durability Characterization
A series of tensile tests were performed on the post-corrosion metal samples. It should be noted
that the size and geometry of the corrosion coupons did not adhere to those for a standardized
tensile test. As shown by the schematic in Figure 1, the corrosion coupons do not have a typical
dog-bone shape and have a hole for hanging the coupons in the molten sulfur bath. Therefore, the
measured strength should not be compared directly with the reported values for the metals, even
for the pre-corrosion coupons. However, the tensile tests are self-consistent, and hence it is more
appropriate to compare the observed trend of the strength as a function of the corrosion time to
understand the effect of corrosion time.

Figure 22 shows the ultimate strength 3 of the tested steel materials as a function of corrosion
time. Most data points were obtained with three samples, with only two exceptions where two
samples were used (1072-hr A516-Gr70 with Si coating and 300-hr SS 347L). It should be noted
that only A516-Gr70 with Si coating and SS 347L had one reference test sample without any
corrosion (i.e., 0 hours of corrosion). SS 316L and SS 347L did not because either all available
samples were used for corrosion, or the reference sample had a noticeable welding defect that
should not be used for tensile testing. In addition, because the tensile test samples did not adhere
to a dog-bone shape, the failure location was very close to the grip and outside the two legs of
the extensometer used for strain measurement, as shown by Figure 23. This means strain could

into the bulk base metal. This agrees with Element 16’s previous corrosion studies at 300oC
molten sulfur temperature.

Conclusion
The corrosion study and mechanical property characterization on the A516-Gr70 carbon steel
with the amorphous Si coating and the SS 304L, SS 316L, and SS 347L suggest that SS 304L
and SS 347L exhibit the best corrosion resistance of about 100 µm/year in molten sulfur at
400°C after 1072 hours of exposure. Given the material cost between SS 304L and SS 347L, SS
304L is considered the more cost-effective material.
From the mechanical property perspective, up to 1072 hours of exposure to molten sulfur at
400°C did not substantially affect the ultimate strength of any of the four steel materials, likely
due to the corrosion effect being limited only to the first tens of microns at the material’s surface.
As the extensive surface scaling observed for all three stainless steels may have been affected by
the final cooling of the corrosion tests, it is recommended to perform a series of thermal cycling
tests in the future for the stainless-steel materials where the total corrosion time is the same, but
the individual corrosion groups experience a different number of cooling cycles to 120°C–150°C
or even room temperature.
The cost analysis revealed that the total cost for a 500-MWh molten sulfur TES constructed with
SS 304L with a 1” nominal pipe size that operates at 400°C is about $29.5/kWh. Because of the
increased energy density, the higher operation temperature reduces the TES cost by about
$6.5/kWh when compared to the previous TES design that cycles molten sulfur between 210°C
and 300°C despite a higher unit cost of the HTF and a higher corrosion allowance requirement.
