Inhibition of red blood cell-derived small extracellular vesicles release by calpeptin attenuates neutrophil inflammation
Authors: Cheng Chen, Qi Zhang, Fei Wang, Zhicheng Wang, Jun Zhang, Shujun Shao, Rong Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transfusion of aged red blood cells (RBCs) is associated with adverse outcomes in critically ill patients. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), as potential risk factors for transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM), play a critical role in the development of sepsis. However, little is known about the effects and mechanisms of red blood cell-derived small extracellular vesicles (RBC-sEVs) that aggravate inflammation and cause poor prognosis in septic patients after RBC transfusion. This study characterized RBC-sEVs from human and mouse leukoreduced packed RBC units and their role in promoting neutrophilic inflammation in vitro and in vivo. We found that RBC-sEVs induce host inflammatory responses in a dose-dependent manner. These vesicles significantly upregulate the expression of TLR4/NF-κB, resulting in increased inflammatory cytokines, and cause dysfunction of umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC). Furthermore, RBC-sEVs significantly aggravated CLP-induced septic mice inflammation by promoting the production of inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating lung injury, and reducing survival rate of CLP mice, all these effects were alleviated by treatment with the EV inhibitor calpeptin. Our findings indicate that RBC-sEVs induce host inflammatory responses, which may contribute to adverse outcomes in septic patients receiving RBC transfusions, whereas calpeptin may alleviate these pro-inflammatory effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56508-3.

Introduction

RBC transfusion is a life-saving treatment that rapidly enhances the oxygen-carrying capacity in anemic patients1. However, the process of packed RBC (pRBC) unit storage is accompanied by a series of biochemical and morphological changes, which eventually lead to a decline in the quality and function of the RBCs. This phenomenon is referred to as red blood cell storage lesion2. Previous clinical and animal studies have demonstrated that RBC transfusion may aggravate inflammation in recipients3–5, and transfusion of aged pRBC units may increase the risk of adverse events6–8.

The potential role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) as biological response regulators has raised concerns about their post-transfusion effects9. Previous studies have shown concrete evidence of their involvement in immune regulation, inflammatory response, and coagulation activity following interaction with several receptor cells10. RBC-derived EVs are generated in circulating RBCs during normal aging by complement-mediated calcium influx leading to plasma membrane detachment followed by vesicle detachment11–13. Previous studies have shown that more than 95% of EVs isolated from pRBC units are derived from RBCs14, and that RBC-derived EVs are important triggering factors for thrombosis, hemostasis, infectious diseases, cancer, and inflammation15. Moreover, large extracellular vesicles (lEVs) and small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) isolated from pRBC unit can carry different cargoes and induce different cellular effects16, and sEVs (< 200 nm) usually account for the majority and may play a major role17. Nevertheless, previous studies have rarely made this distinction. Therefore, in the present study, we focused on the RBC-sEV fraction.

Sepsis is defined as a life-threatening syndrome of organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection18. Neutrophils (also known as polymorphonuclear leukocytes, PMN), endothelial cells, and cytokines play a major role in sepsis-associated excessive inflammation19. The mammalian bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) receptor Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) plays a beneficial role in controlling bacterial infections but is also a major driver of abnormal inflammation in fatal sepsis20. Activated TLR4 stimulates downstream signaling molecules, mainly including the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB). The active NF-κB is released from the cytoplasm into the nucleus and promotes the expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines such as IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-821. Sepsis leads to a decrease in blood volume and abnormal coagulation due to various factors. Blood transfusion is a common treatment for patients22. However, the causes and mechanisms of poor prognosis of septic patients after blood transfusion remain unclear.

Calpain comprises a family of calcium-dependent neutral, cytosolic cysteine proteases that are widely expressed in cells. These proteins promote EV release through cytoskeleton remodeling, and calpain inhibitors have been shown to block this process. Calpeptin is the most studied inhibitor of calpain. Due to its inhibitory activity on calpain, calpeptin has been widely used as a micro-vesiculation inhibitor. The use of calpeptin has been reported to reduce the amount of EVs released from cells23.

In the present study, we investigated the dose‑dependent pro‑inflammatory effects of RBC-sEVs. We aimed to evaluate whether RBC-sEVs promote neutrophilic inflammation through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway and mediate HUVEC dysfunction. Furthermore, the potential effect of calpeptin on inhibiting RBC-sEVs release was investigated using a mouse model of cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis.

Results

RBC-sEVs accumulated during human pRBC units storage

Characterization of the morphology, size, and concentration of the RBC-sEVs was performed using TEM and NTA. As shown in Fig. 1A-D, the morphology and the mean size of RBC-sEVs were 130.2 nm (D5) and 133.7 nm (D35), respectively. The concentration of the RBC-sEVs (D35) (1.7 ± 0.3 × 1012 particles/mL) was higher than RBC-sEVs (D5) (2.2 ± 0.2 × 1011 particles/mL) (Fig. 1E). EV marker was identified using Western blot (Fig. 1F-G) and results showed that the level of CD81 was increased in the RBC-sEVs (D35) compared to RBC-sEVs (D5).

Human RBC-sEVs promoted neutrophil inflammation

To evaluate the effects of RBC-sEVs on neutrophils, we labeled the RBC-sEVs with PKH26 and cocultured with neutrophils for 6 h. To exclude potential pro‑inflammatory effects induced by PKH26 labeling itself, we further detected IL‑6 secretion in neutrophils using an ELISA assay (Supplementary Data 2). The immunofluorescence assay showed that neutrophils uptake the RBC-sEVs (Fig. 2A-C), indicating an interaction between the RBC-sEVs and neutrophils. TLR4 is a key receptor involved in the recognition and signaling of LPS. It can bind to and activate downstream NF-κB via receptor dimerization. Activated components of the NF-kB signaling pathway regulate inflammation by promoting the expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). To investigate the role of RBC-sEVs in triggering neutrophil inflammation, neutrophils were treated with LPS and/or TAK-242, followed by coculturing with RBC-sEVs. Figure 2D-F demonstrate that culture with RBC-sEVs (D35) resulted in an upregulation of TLR4, NF-κB P65 upon LPS stimulation. However, treatment with TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 indicated the degradation of TLR4, NF-κB p65. A significant increase was observed in IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8 production when neutrophils were treated with RBC-sEVs (D35) even in the absence of LPS stimulation. Furthermore, similar results were observed with LPS stimulation in terms of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-8 production. However, treatment with TAK-242 resulted in decreased expression of IL-1β production (Fig. 2G-J). These results suggest that treatment with RBC-sEVs can promote the activation of the TLR4/ NF-κB pathway and the production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. There is a significant synergistic effect observed between RBC-sEVs and LPS. Previously studies indicated that mature neutrophils undergo apoptosis during sepsis. In the present study, RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35) were unable to induce neutrophil apoptosis without LPS stimulation. A significant increase was observed in RBC-sEVs (D35), which promoted neutrophil apoptosis by LPS stimulation. However, treatment with TAK-242 did not significantly decrease neutrophil apoptosis (Fig. 2K-L). Taken together, these findings indicate that RBC-sEVs were taken up by neutrophils and promote inflammatory reaction in neutrophils.

Human RBC-sEVs mediated HUVECs damage

To observe whether RBC-sEVs have proinflammatory properties on HUVECs in the absence of LPS and neutrophils, the proliferation ability of HUVECs was detected. Figure 3A showed no difference in the proliferation ability of HUVECs at 0, 24, and 48 h. To evaluate the effects of RBC-sEVs on inflammation, adhesion, and apoptosis in HUVECs, the cells were treated with LPS and then incubated with neutrophils and RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35). As shown in Fig. 3B-E, RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35) were unable to promote the secretion of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-8 without LPS stimulation. RBC-sEVs (D35), however, promoted the secretion of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-8 through LPS stimulation. Western blot results showed that RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35) were unable to promote ICAM-1, VCAM-1 expression without LPS stimulation. RBC-sEVs (D35) were found to promote the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 when stimulated with LPS (Fig. 3F-H). As shown in Fig. 3I-J, RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35) were unable to induce apoptosis in HUVECs without LPS stimulation. However, a significant increase in HUVECs apoptosis was observed when RBC-sEVs (D35) with stimulation of LPS. Similar results were also obtained with the expression of caspase-3 (Fig. 3K, L). Transendothelial electrical resistance measurements showed that RBC-sEVs (D5&D35) did not alter the permeability of HUVECs in the absence of LPS stimulation. However, under LPS stimulation, RBC-sEVs (D35) increased HUVEC permeability (Fig. 3M).

RBC-sEVs accumulated during mouse pRBC units storage

Characterization of the morphology, size, and concentration of mice RBC-sEVs was performed using TEM and NTA. As shown in Fig. 4A-E, the morphology and the mean size of RBC-sEVs were 102.6 nm (D0), 103.2 nm (D15), and 104.4 nm (D15 + calpeptin), respectively. The concentration of RBC-sEVs (D15) (1.8 ± 0.1 × 1012 particles/mL) was significantly higher than RBC-sEVs (D0) (1.4 ± 0.1 × 1011 particles/mL). However, the concentration of RBC-sEVs in the presence of 30µM calpeptin (8.8 ± 0.1 × 1011 particles/mL) was significantly lower than RBC-sEVs (D15) (Fig. 4F). EV marker was identified using Western blot. As shown in Fig. 4G-H, the expression of CD81 in the RBC-sEVs (D15) was significantly higher than RBC-sEVs (D0). Upon treatment with calpeptin at concentrations of 20µM, 30µM, and 40µM, the expression of CD81 was inhibited.

Effect of EV inhibitor calpeptin on mouse pRBC units

Researchers have suggested that calpeptin can inhibit EV release, but the toxicity to the cells should also be considered. To investigate the effects of calpeptin on mouse pRBC units, hematologic parameters and free hemoglobin levels were analyzed after 15 days of storage. As shown in Fig. 5A-H, the administration of 40µM calpeptin resulted in a significant decrease in RBC, Hb, and Hct levels, as well as a significant increase in MCH, MCHC, RDW, and free hemoglobin level. However, there were no significant differences observed in the 20µM and 30µM calpeptin groups compared to the D15 group. There was no significant difference between the D15 group and each of the inhibitor groups in terms of MCV and RDW.

Calpeptin attenuated inflammatory response and improved survival in CLP mice

To evaluate the potential role of RBC-sEVs in vivo, we established a CLP-induced septic mouse model. A pilot study was conducted to confirm the reliability of our model. As shown in Supplementary Data 3, white blood cell counts measured 12 h after CLP surgery were significantly lower in CLP mice than in sham mice, consistent with the leukopenia typically observed in the early phase of sepsis. Based on the results of routine blood tests, NTA, and Western blot, 30µM calpeptin was used in this part of the experiment. As shown in Fig. 6A, the mortality of mice is significantly increased in CLP mice compared to sham-operated mice. Notably, the survival curves exhibit a consistent pattern of black straight lines in Fig. 6a causing all mice in the four sham-operated groups survived within 48 h. Furthermore, a significant survival rate was also observed in the CLP + RBC-sEVs (D0) group and CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) group (Fig. 6B), the CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) group and CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) + calpeptin group (Fig. 6C). However, there were no significant differences between the CLP + RBC-sEVs (D0) group and the CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) + calpeptin group (Fig. 6D). The critical role of calpeptin in reducing RBC-sEVs release in vitro led us to speculate whether it could also reduce inflammation in vivo. As expected in the ELISA test, although there was a limited effect of RBC-sEVs in the sham-operated mice groups, the expression of IL-6 and CXCL1 in serum and BALF significantly decreased in the CLP + RBC-sEVs (D0) group and CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) + calpeptin group compared to the CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) group (Fig. 6F-I). Tissue damage and inflammation in the lung was assessed using H&E staining (Fig. 6E) and IHC (Fig. 6J-K). CD11b is a highly expressed marker on infiltrating neutrophils. The IHC results suggested that the CLP mice groups exhibited an increased positive signal, indicating higher CD11b expression compared to the sham-operated groups. In addition, injection of RBC-sEVs from calpeptin-treated pRBC units reduced this effect in the lung. In line with the pathology analysis, the lung wet-to-dry weight ratio showed a similar trend (Fig. 6L).

Discussion

In the present study, we confirmed that RBC‑sEVs, a subpopulation of RBC‑derived vesicles, induce host inflammatory responses, consistent with previous reports. RBC-sEVs promote inflammation in human neutrophils through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway and mediate HUVEC dysfunction. Treatment of mouse pRBC units with calpeptin decreased RBC-sEV release and reduced inflammation in CLP-induced septic mice. Together, these findings suggest that RBC-sEVs aggravate neutrophilic inflammation and that calpeptin may help reduce adverse effects associated with transfusion of aged pRBC units.

We isolated RBC-sEVs using differential ultracentrifugation and characterized them via morphological analysis, particle size measurement, and surface marker detection24. Although previous studies have implicated RBC-derived EVs as a potential risk factor for transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM), most literature does not distinguish between EV subgroups or fully characterize them5,14,25. Our TEM and NTA results demonstrated that the morphology and size of human and mouse RBC-sEVs were consistent with typical sEV characteristics26. Additionally, the concentration and EV marker levels of human (D5 vs. D35) and mouse (D0 vs. D15) RBC-sEVs were significantly higher than those at the beginning of the storage period. In our study, TEM, NTA, and Western blot were used to detect the morphology, particle size, concentration, and EV marker level of RBC-sEVs. The results confirm that RBC-sEV concentration increases with pRBC unit storage time. This multi-faceted approach provides reliable validation of RBC-sEV identity and concentration.

Upon release, EVs may reach their target cells either nearby or through a variety of biological fluids (including peripheral blood, lymph, cerebrospinal fluid, urine and ascites)27. EVs are taken up by target cells to regulate a variety of cellular processes through multiple mechanisms, including endocytosis, membrane fusion, micro phagocytosis or phagocytosis28. Our immunofluorescence assays demonstrated RBC-sEVs uptake by neutrophils, suggesting a role in inflammatory regulation. As an important component of the innate immune system, neutrophils regulate the inflammatory process mainly through phagocytosis and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps, cytokines, and chemokines. Under steady-state conditions, the lifespan of neutrophils in circulation is 6–8 h. However, during sepsis, neutrophils show increased lifespan and impaired migration, leading to extensive tissue damage. We verified the pro-inflammatory effects of RBC-sEVs by cell co-culture and showed that RBC-sEVs promoted the expression of TLR4/NF-κB, leading to increased levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α, as well as neutrophil apoptosis. Based on these results, our data suggest that human RBC-sEVs promote neutrophil inflammation via the TLR4/NF-κB pathway.

Endothelial cells act as unconventional immune cells that shift toward a proinflammatory, proadhesive, and proapoptotic phenotype during sepsis, resulting in structural and functional impairment29. In this study, we investigated the effect of RBC-sEVs on HUVECs in vitro. Our data show that RBC-sEVs mediate HUVEC dysfunction in inflammation, adhesion, and apoptosis. Furthermore, RBC-sEVs had no significant effect on the proliferation of HUVECs in the CCK-8 assay. We also examined the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in co-culture supernatants from HUVECs and RBC-sEVs in the absence of LPS and neutrophils, but their expression levels were below the detection limit of the ELISA kits (data not shown). These results suggest that the pro-inflammatory role of RBC-sEVs can be amplified by LPS.

RBC-sEV levels increases and drives disease progression in most reported diseased states, such as transfusion-related acute lung injuries (TRALI), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and cardiovascular-related disease15,30. This implies that inhibiting RBC-sEVs release or blocking their function may assist in the treatment of these diseases. Calpeptin is the most studied inhibitor of calpain. Due to its inhibitory activity on calpain, calpeptin has been widely used as a micro-vesiculation inhibitor. The use of calpeptin has been reported to reduce the amount of EVs released from cells. Studies have shown that calpeptin is typically used at concentrations ranging from 0 to 300 µM in vitro, depending on the cell type and experimental purpose23,31. Researchers have suggested that while calpeptin can inhibit EV release, the potential cytotoxicity of the concentrations used in experiments should also be considered (including other EV inhibitors)23. In our study, three different concentrations of calpeptin were treated on mouse pRBC units. Before starting the experiments, we ran a pilot test to assess hemolysis. Significant hemolysis was observed at concentrations above 40 µM (Supplementary Data 4). Hematologic parameters and free hemoglobin level showed significant hemolysis in mouse pRBC units upon treatment with 40 µM calpeptin, whereas 20 µM and 30 µM calpeptin did not cause hemolysis. Although NTA and Western blot results showed that treatment with 40 µM calpeptin significantly inhibit the RBC-sEVs release from mouse pRBC units, this concentration could not be used in subsequent experiments because it caused hemolysis. We also observed that mouse RBC-sEVs aggravated the inflammatory response in CLP-induced septic mice in a dose-dependent manner. However, the proinflammatory property of mouse RBC-sEVs was barely observed in normal mice, suggesting that the role of RBC-sEVs can be amplified by inflammation, which is consistent with the findings of in vivo experiments. Treatment with calpeptin significantly reduced IL-6 and CXCL1 levels in serum and BALF, as well as CD11b expression in the lungs of CLP mice. Additionally, the survival rate was significantly improved.

Undeniably, there are some limitations in the current study. First, common methods used for EV isolation, such as ultracentrifugation and immunoprecipitation, usually produce mixtures of different types of EVs, resulting in limited purity of the samples we studied. Due to current technological limitations, we cannot obtain a single type of EV. Second, it is not sufficient to assess whether calpeptin is toxic to cells only through routine blood tests and free hemoglobin levels. Given the concerns about calpeptin dosage, further in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to evaluate its safety and efficacy, including assessments of RBC viability and function, histopathology and serum biochemistry in multiple organs, and pharmacokinetic studies. Third, previous studies have shown that the incidence of post-liver transplant sepsis exceeds 50%, and RBC transfusion is one of the risk factors32. Therefore, it is particularly important to reduce the damage of pro-inflammatory mediators in pRBC units to the recipients. However, clinical studies on RBC‑derived EVs are still limited, and most current evidence, including ours, comes from in vitro and in vivo experiments. More clinical studies are needed to further support this conclusion.

Our study provides further evidence that RBC‑sEVs induce host inflammatory responses in a dose-dependent manner, extending previous reports on RBC‑derived vesicles. Furthermore, we show that calpeptin reduces the release of RBC-sEVs from pRBC units. These findings add to our understanding of transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM) and suggest that inhibiting RBC-sEV release by calpeptin may represent a potential strategy to mitigate inflammation in septic patients receiving RBC transfusions.

Methods

Collection and storage of blood

Human packed leukoreduced RBC units were collected from 6 healthy volunteers and stored in additive solutions containing CPDA at 4℃ according to standard blood bank procedures. Briefly, Whole blood (50 mL) was leukocyte-reduced by filtration, then centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15 min to separate plasma. After plasma removal, additive solution was added at a 2:1 ratio and mixed thoroughly to obtain packed leuko-reduced RBC unit. When storing the pRBC units until the 5th (D5) and 35th days (D35), sterile consumables were used to obtain aliquots. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (No. 2025 − 1344). Informed consent was obtained from all healthy donors. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

For mice, wild-type male C57BL/6 mice (six to eight weeks old, 22–25 g, SPF) were purchased from the SiPeiFu (Suzhou) Biotechnology Ltd. The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, and all experiments were performed in accordance with the standard guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals in Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (No. 2025 − 1344). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Mice blood was collected and stored as described previously33. Briefly, after being anesthetized with 3% isoflurane delivered via an anesthesia vaporizer, all 50 mice underwent retro-orbital bleeding for blood collection and were then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Blood was pooled in a 50 mL tube with approximately 14% CPDA-1 concentration and a leukocyte reduction filter was used to remove leukocytes. The leuko-reduced blood was centrifuged at 400 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Remove a portion of the supernatant to obtain a hematocrit level of around 75%. Mouse pRBC units were stored in 500 µL aliquots at 4 °C, and calpeptin (20 µM, 30 µM, 40 µM) was added to them for 15 days. Changes in morphology and pathophysiology of mouse pRBC unit stored for 15 days are equivalent to those of human pRBC unit at the end of the storage period34. After the expiration of storage, the supernatants were obtained by centrifugation at 300 g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at -80 °C for future use.

RBC-sEVs Isolation

Human and mice RBC-sEVs were isolated and purified using differential centrifugation as described previously35,36. Briefly, the pRBC unit aliquots were centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min at 4℃ to obtain the supernatant. 2 mL aliquot of the supernatant was taken and centrifuged again at 2000 g for 10 min at 4℃ to remove any remaining cells. Then, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged again at 10,000 g at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was subsequently filtered (0.22 μm) and then ultracentrifuged at 110,000 g at 4℃ for 70 min. After discarding the supernatant, the RBC-sEVs were resuspended in cold PBS and subjected to ultracentrifugation again. RBC-sEVs were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS and stored at − 80 °C. All experiments were performed using a fixed volume of 100 µL RBC-sEVs suspension unless otherwise indicated. The mean protein concentrations of RBC-sEVs, as determined by BCA assay, were as follows: for human samples, 1.1 ± 0.3 µg/mL (D5) and 8.4 ± 0.7 µg/mL (D35); for mouse samples, 0.9 ± 0.2 µg/mL (D0), 7.5 ± 0.7 µg/mL (D15), and 4.2 ± 0.4 µg/mL (D15 + calpeptin).

RBC-sEVs Identification

Human and mice RBC-sEVs were identified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blot.

The morphology of RBC-sEVs was observed using TEM. A 5 µL sample of RBC-sEVs was placed on a glow-discharged Formvar carbon-coated grid and negatively stained with a 2% uranyl acetate solution. Images were obtained using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL 1230, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

Particle size and concentration of RBC-sEVs were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) with the ZetaView PMX 110 instrument (Particle Metrix, Germany) and the accompanying ZetaView software (v8.04.02). Isolated RBC-sEVs samples were appropriately diluted using PBS to measure the particle size and concentration. NTA measurements were recorded and analyzed at eleven positions. The ZetaView system was calibrated using 110 nm polystyrene particles. The temperature was maintained between 23 °C and 30 °C. Particles ranging from 30 to 200 nm were designated as small extracellular vesicles.

EV marker was tested using Western blotting.

Human Neutrophiles Isolation and Coculture

Whole blood was collected from healthy donors. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (No. 2025 − 1344). Informed consent was obtained from all healthy donors. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Neutrophils were purified from peripheral blood using the MACSxpress Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Neutrophils (1 × 107/mL) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma, USA) in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After being treated or not treated with LPS (2 µg/mL, Sigma, USA) and the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 (2µM, Beyotime, China) for 4 h, 100 µL RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35) or PBS were introduced into a human neutrophil culture and incubated until 24 h, respectively.

Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cell Treatment and Coculture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from ScienCell, and cultured in an endothelial cell medium (ScienCell, USA). HUVECs were grown in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. HUVECs (1 × 105/mL) were seeded into a 12-well plate and treated or not treated with 2 µg/mL LPS for 4 h to establish the endothelial cell injury model37,38. Neutrophils (1 × 106/mL) were added and settled for 30 min. After settling, 100 µL RBC-sEVs (D5& D35) or PBS were introduced into cell culture and incubated until 48 h, respectively.

RBC-sEVs uptake

RBC-sEVs were labeled with PKH26 (Umibio, China) at a concentration of 100 nM for 30 min in the dark. Labeled RBC-sEVs were washed with PBS and then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 110,000 g at 4℃ for 70 min to remove the unbound dye. Neutrophils (1 × 107/mL) were cocultured with labeled RBC-sEVs (4 µg/mL) for 30 min and were then centrifuged at 250 g for 5 min. Cells were then fixed with 2 mL of 4% formaldehyde for 20 min. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 250 g for 5 min and then resuspended in 0.5 mL of PBS. Pipette 200 µL of cell suspension and drop it dispense it into a small oval (3 cm x 2 cm) drawn with a liquid blocker pen (Servicebio, China). Samples were blocked with 3% BSA (Servicebio, China) for 30 min. Slides were then incubated with TLR4 (1:200, ABclonal, China) and NF-κB p65 primary antibodies (1:200, Cell Signaling, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Slides were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:500, Servicebio, China) for one hour. Slides were then washed as described previously. DAPI solution was added and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The uptake of RBC-sEVs by neutrophils was visualized using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cell counting Kit-8 assay

The proliferation ability of HUVECs was determined using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, HUVECs (1 × 103/mL) were co-cultured with RBC-sEVs (D5& D35, 5µL) for 0, 24, and 48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well of 96-well plates and incubated for another 1 h at 37 C. The optical density (OD) values in each well were monitored at 450 nm by an ELISA plate reader (Thermo, USA).

Transendothelial electrical resistance

The permeability of HUVECs was evaluated by measuring the transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) using a MERSSTX01 electrode (EMD Millipore, USA). Briefly, HUVECs (1 × 10⁵/mL) were seeded in the apical compartment of 24-well plate culture inserts with 0.4 μm pores, with one blank insert included. After reaching confluence, cells were treated with or without 2 µg/mL LPS for 4 h, followed by the addition of neutrophils (1 × 10⁶/mL) and either 100 µL RBC-sEVs (D5 & D35) or PBS, and then incubated until 48 h. TEER values were expressed in Ω·cm² after subtracting the blank insert value and multiplying by the membrane area.

Mouse pRBC units routine blood tests

After the expiration of storage, the mouse pRBC units were tested using an automated hematology analyzer. Hematologic parameters such as RBC, Hb, MCV, MCH, MCHC, and RDW were analyzed.

Cecal ligation and puncture mice model

Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery was performed on mice to induce a sepsis model, as described previously39. Briefly, the anaesthetized mouse was fixed in a supine position, and a 2 cm incision was made in the abdominal wall. The cecum was ligated 1/3 of the way with a thread through the incision into the abdomen. A hole was made at the ligated end, and a small amount of fecal material was squeezed out to confirm that the puncture was unobstructed. The cecum was then returned to the abdomen, and the peritoneum and skin were sutured. Sham-operated mice underwent the same abdominal incision without cecum ligation and puncture. After surgery, mice were monitored every 6 h for changes in appearance, activity, and posture. Mice were randomly divided into eight groups: (1) Sham group, (2) Sham + RBC-sEVs (D0) group, (3) Sham + RBC-sEVs (D15) group, (4) Sham + RBC-sEVs (D15) + calpeptin group, (5) CLP group, (6) CLP + RBC-sEVs (D0) group, (7) CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) group, and (8) CLP + RBC-sEVs (D15) + calpeptin group. After surgery, different groups of RBC-sEVs resuspended in 100 µL PBS were injected via the tail vein, and the sham group was injected with PBS. To evaluate survival rates, mice were monitored every 6 h for 2 days. All mice were sacrificed after 48 h. After euthanasia, whole blood was collected from the eyeballs. The lower two-thirds of the right lung were ligated, and 1 mL of ice-cold PBS was instilled into the remaining portion twice (total 2 mL) to collect the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Ligated 2/3 of the right lobe of the lung was dissected and 1/3 was used for analysis of the lung wet-to-dry weight ratio, and the other 1/3 was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for tissue histology.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Human IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels in the culture supernatants were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Novus, USA). Mouse IL-6 and CXCL1 levels in the serum and BALF were measured using ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Novus, USA). Mouse free hemoglobin level in the plasma was measured using ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Novus, USA).

Western blotting

The lysates were obtained using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) pre-mixed with PMSF (Beyotime, China). RBC-sEVs and cells were lysed for 30 min and then centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 g at 4 °C. Next, the supernatant containing protein was resolved on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel, and the proteins were transferred onto a 0.45-mm PVDF membrane (Millipore, Germany). After blocking with blocking buffer (Beyotime, China) for 15 min, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and then incubated with horseradish-peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-CD81 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), anti-TLR4 (1:2000, ABclonal, China), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), anti-ICAM-1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), anti-VCAM-1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), anti-Caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), and anti-β-actin (1:1200, Beyotime, China).

Flow cytometry

The apoptosis ratio of human neutrophils and human umbilical vein endothelial cells was measured with Annexin V-FITC apoptosis assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Absin, China). Cells were measured using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.4).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

After 24 h fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, tissues were embedded in paraffin and 4-mm sections were cut. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining were performed on sections using anti-CD11b antibody (1:500, Servicebio, China) according to standard techniques. CD11b-positive cells were quantified in five random fields and imaged using a slide scanner microscope (Nikon, Japan). The occupied area was analyzed by selecting brown areas using Image J software (NIH, USA).

Lung wet-to-dry weight ratio

The wet weight of the lungs was measured using an electronic scale. The tissue was then dried in an oven at 70 °C for 24 h and the dry weight was reweighed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. All data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Significances of two-group comparisons were determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons of significant differences of more than two groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Results were considered statistically significant at p-value < 0.05.
