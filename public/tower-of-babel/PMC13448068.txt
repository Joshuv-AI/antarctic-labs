Site-controlled quantum dot arrays edge-coupled to integrated silicon nitride waveguides and devices
Authors: John O’Hara, Nicola Maraviglia, Mack Johnson, Jesper Håkansson, Salvador A. Medina-Rangel, Gediminas Juska, Luca Colavecchi, Frank H. Peters, Brian Corbett, Emanuele Pelucchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The scalability of quantum photonic integrated circuits opens the path towards large-scale quantum computing and communication. To date, this scalability has been limited by the stochastic nature of the quantum light sources. Moreover, hybrid integration of different platforms will likely be necessary to combine state-of-the-art devices into a functioning architecture. Here, we demonstrate the active alignment and edge-coupling of arrays of ten site-controlled gallium arsenide quantum dots to an array of ten silicon nitride single-mode waveguides, at cryogenic temperatures. The coupling is facilitated by the fabrication of nanopillars, deterministically self-aligned around each quantum dot, leading to a high-yield and regular array of single-photon sources. An on-chip beamsplitter verifies the triggered emission of single photons into the silicon nitride chip. The low inhomogeneous broadening of the ensemble enables us to observe the spectral overlap of adjacent site-controlled emitters, and we show that it is possible to tune these into sub-picometre alignment using additional laser. Across the array of waveguides, the signal collected from each coupled quantum dot is consistently and reproducibly 0.17 relative to the free-space collection from the very same single-photon source. Comparing measurement with waveguide simulations, we infer that absolute coupling efficiencies of ≈ 5% are currently obtained between our quantum dots and the waveguides. Paths to improve this coupling value are discussed.

Introduction

The promise of light as the medium in which to realise large-scale quantum information processing (QIP) rests on the unique properties of the photon1,2 and the theoretical scalability of quantum photonics3–5. In particular, the latter arises from the photon’s robustness against environmental decoherence and the ability to manufacture dense photonic integrated circuits (PICs). However, the highest quality single-photon sources (SPSs) remain probabilistic, either in growth position—as with randomly nucleated quantum dots (QDs)6,7 and colour centres8, or in photon number—as with spontaneous parametric down-conversion and four-wave mixing9. Thus, the ability to create dense, ordered arrays of deterministic quantum emitters and combine them with integrated circuits has not yet been demonstrated.

Most schemes for photonic quantum information processing involve a path through the distinct steps of deterministic light generation, manipulation, and detection10. Currently, the best-performing options for the various quantum PIC components are to be found across a range of platforms3,4, and all of these face their own challenges to become a high-yield technology. For these reasons, a modular hybrid approach11,12, wherein the fundamental operations remain confined to their respective functionally separate chips, and are combined by leveraging advances in photonic coupling and packaging, has become a common approach to scalability13.

In early work on QD integration, devices were fabricated using high-density QD samples. This increased the chance of having a QD well-positioned within the device and relied on the large inhomogeneous broadening of the ensemble to filter out extraneous emitters. This approach is still the groundwork for proof-of-principle demonstrations14,15, which indeed often exploit the random variations in the dot-field characteristics. Nevertheless, deterministic integration of such QDs with photonic circuits is then only possible by pre-selecting and registering QDs, and tailoring the circuit design to the source positions16–19, or by one-by-one transfer of sources to the input waveguides20. Neither of these approaches is particularly scalable to many emitters and/or complex circuits. Ultimately, scalable and deterministic integration will require site-control of the emitters, with circuits designed around known and reproducible emitter positions21, as well as a method to tune the emitters into mutual indistinguishability, if necessary.

Site-control of SPSs has been achieved by a variety of methods, including patterning of growth substrates with nanoholes or pyramidal recesses22,23, pre-strained substrates24, growth on nanostructure arrays25–27, as well as laser-guided growth28. So far, integration of multiple site-controlled QDs with a PIC has not been demonstrated. Here we show that it is possible to actively align and couple an array of site-controlled quantum dots in self-aligned nanopillars29 to an array of single-mode waveguides of matching pitch, and to other integrated photonic devices. We demonstrate the low inhomogeneous broadening and high single-photon purity of the QDs. Importantly, we show that each source can be tuned to match the neighbouring source's emission with subpicometre accuracy, an essential step towards arrays of identical sources. Lastly, we discuss the advantages, current limitations, and future directions of this approach, and compare it with other integration strategies.

Results

Devices

Our light sources consisted of arrays of single site-controlled GaAs QDs, with neutral excitonic emission at around 778 nm. All samples were grown via metal-organic vapour-phase epitaxy in a hexagonal array of lithographically defined pyramidal recesses30, in this case with a 10 μm pitch. The optical lithography, together with the wet etch process, resulted in a standard deviation of each recess position of less than 50 nm with respect to the ideal local lattice node, as confirmed by SEM measurement. During growth, the QDs (which have a lateral dimension of ≈ 15 nm) naturally align with nanometre precision in the centre of each recess31,32. The QDs were grown between GaAs/AlAs superlattices33 and Fe-doped AlGaAs barriers.

In order to improve the far-field emission profile and increase the coupling to waveguides, photonic nanopillars were shaped around the QDs through chip-wide post-growth processing29. This procedure automatically aligns the centre of the triangular cross-section pillars with the QD. For the PIC, we utilised SiN waveguides and devices in SiO2 cladding on a 700 μm thick Si substrate. The single transverse-electric (TE) and transverse-magnetic (TM) mode waveguides, in order to operate at 780 nm, were defined with a 400 nm width in a 300 nm thick layer of SiN, with 2 μm and 3 μm of SiO2 above and below, respectively. These waveguides tapered to a width of 200 nm at the facet. The designed effective mode area for the 780 nm TE-mode was 0.21 μm2 in the main waveguide, and expanded to 0.33 μm2 at the facet. The chips were fabricated at a commercial foundry (Cornerstone), where the features were defined by optical stepper lithography, giving a centre-centre uncertainty in waveguide position of < 25 nm. Subsequently, the facets were commercially polished.

The PICs consisted of a range of nanophotonic devices and gates. The structures relevant to this paper include the simple straight waveguides, the array of ten waveguides, and the 2 × 2 multi-mode interferometer (MMI) that functioned as a beamsplitter. On one side of the PIC, the waveguide facets for each multi-port device were arranged with 10 μm separation in order to match the QD array. Since it is necessary to retrieve the light from the PIC to complete the measurements, all devices had output facets on the opposite edge of the chip. In this case, there was no strict requirement on the spacing, but the facets were placed further apart to avoid cross-talk and background light. Thus, the waveguide arrays fanned out to have 20 μm separation at the outputs, while the MMI outputs were 50 μm apart.

Alignment procedure

In our approach, the PIC chip was fixed to a copper mount such that the bottom facets were suspended, while the QD chip was mounted orthogonally below this on a separate copper mount on an xyz piezoelectric stage, and thus could be moved into proximity/contact with the photonic chip with sub-μm precision. These were both actively cooled within a closed-cycle helium cryostat (and probe station), shown in Fig. 1b, and optically accessible through a top window. To prevent heating of the QD sample when the PIC was in close proximity, we ensured an efficient thermal contact between the PIC and the inner radiation shield of the cryostat (nominally 12–13 K), see SI for details.

Before xyz alignment of the chips, it was necessary to align the chips so that the facet plane of the photonic chip was parallel to the surface of the QD chip, and that the edge of the PIC was aligned with one basis vector of the hexagonal QD array. Both of these alignments were performed manually at room temperature, as detailed in the SI. Assuming one waveguide was optimally aligned, the uncertainty of the angular alignment procedure ( <1 mrad) corresponded to a misalignment of the adjacent waveguide of <20 nm, less than the QD or waveguide positional variation expected from the lithography. See below and the SI for further discussion on alignment tolerances.

Due to the fact that the QD chip and its xyz stage may cool at different rates than the PIC and its mount, the cooling was initiated with the chips apart. It was observed that there was a small systematic rotation between the room temperature position of the PIC (with respect to the QD chip) and its final position at low temperature, and so two of the PIC angles were intentionally slightly offset when prepared at room temperature so that the PIC was optimally aligned with the QD sample when at ≈ 10 K.

Once the chips were cold, we could begin the active alignment using the QD signal. The quantum dots were excited with a continuous-wave above-band laser (636 nm), coupled through one of the top facets of the PIC, and the QD signal was collected from the same facet, as shown in Fig. 1a, b. It was possible to observe the signal from the QDs while still at a relatively large distance from contact (chip-chip separation Δz≥100 μm), and so the xyz alignment could be performed with the QD signal alone—firstly via a multimode waveguide (also of 300 nm nominal thickness, but ≥10 μm in width), and subsequently with the single-mode waveguides. Figure 2a also shows that the spectrum is sometimes redshifted abruptly with respect to the initial condition. This suggests that the pillars had contacted the PIC and been compressed along their long axis, and that this caused the QD emission to redshift. The optimal coupling was generally observed under a small amount of redshift; however, the improvement from the not-in-contact condition was minimal (see Fig. 2b). At higher levels of strain (more forceful contact), the spectrum redshifted further before broadening (several such events can be seen in Fig. 2a). In a small minority of cases, an unusually large shift and/or a change in the average charge state was observed. The effect of this strain was mostly reversed upon disconnection.

We verified that the approach to coupling was mechanically and thermally stable. After aligning the QD array to the waveguide chip, the signal was recorded at various intervals over a period of 41 hours (see SI). Without any chip-chip realignment, the signal was stable. This indicates both that the chip-chip alignment was stable and that the QDs were not negatively affected by the (near-)contact.

Single-mode waveguide array coupling

Next, we performed the coupling of ten QDs to a ten-waveguide array. After aligning a single QD to a single waveguide of the array, we scanned the laser spot across the array facets, which at the top fanned out to 20 μm separation. As noted above, and as can also be seen in Fig. 5c, the spectrum observed through the waveguides could be slightly redshifted with respect to that observed directly—between 50 and 300 pm depending on the QD-pillar. For the data reported in Fig. 5, the direct-to-objective measurements were obtained after the waveguide array measurement, so in fact, we see here the reversal of the contact-induced redshift.

Comparing the light emission measured through the waveguide array, to that from the same pillars measured directly with the objective lens (numerical aperture 0.42), we saw that the detected signal variations arose only from the QD-nanostructures themselves, with the level of pillar-waveguide coupling approximately equal from waveguide 1 to waveguide 10, as shown in Fig. 5c, d. Indeed, all ten waveguide spectra were taken at the same excitation power. Thus, after aligning one QD, the entire QD array was successfully aligned to the waveguide array, with the waveguide-coupled signal 0.17 ± 0.02 of that measured directly. As expected from the design of the waveguides, which may collect equally into TE and TM modes, no deviations due to potentially varying dipole orientations were observed.

Absolute QD-waveguide coupling efficiency

In order to understand the absolute coupling efficiencies between the QDs and the PIC waveguide modes, far-field radiation simulations were performed of the waveguide-objective system and compared with the quasi-resonant excitation experiment. The calculations accounted for the expected propagation losses of the 6 mm waveguide (assumed to be 1 dB/cm), and the transmission and emission profile from the waveguide facet towards the objective. The preparation efficiency of the 80 MHz quasi-resonant excitation was calculated based on a comparison with the above-band excitation, in terms of count rates, spectral profiles, and measured multi-photon contributions. The tabletop efficiency was measured at the QD wavelength 780 nm. Combining these with the observed quasi-resonant waveguide-coupled count rates for the QD-pillar in Fig. 3, we obtained an estimated QD-waveguide coupling efficiency of 0.05 ± 0.02. This value combines the efficiency of the QD dipole to nanopillar coupling (in the direction of the PIC), and the efficiency of the pillar-waveguide coupling. Here, the uncertainty is mostly arising from the uncertainty of the preparation efficiency – see SI for more details. This level of coupling gives 0.5 to 2 MHz single-photons in each of the SiN waveguides, for quasi-resonant and above-band pulsed excitation, respectively.

Across the array, the pillar-waveguide coupling was approximately constant, but the absolute count rates varied due to intensity or spectral variations in the QD-pillar sources. Although pulsed-excitation single-photon measurements were not performed systematically across the array, the waveguide-coupled count rates for the QD-pillar of Fig. 3 were very close to the mean value observed during the array measurements, under equivalent conditions. Thus, the above estimation of the absolute QD-waveguide coupling is also representative of the mean value obtained across the array in Fig. 5.

Coupling simulations and tolerance

To better understand the tolerances of our scheme and to identify potential paths to improve coupling efficiency, we next performed finite-difference time-domain simulations of QD-to-waveguide and QD-to-objective coupling mediated by the nanopillar.

Because the pillars presented an irregular side etch, as shown in Fig. 1c, we adopted a simplified geometry whereby the lateral etch is described by three symmetrical cylindrical grooves. Initially, it was assumed that this etch was approximately at the same vertical level as the QD. This geometry produced a collimated far-field and a four-to-five-fold increase in the collection efficiency when coupling directly to the objective lens, when compared with straight-sided pillars (see SI for the simulation results). Indeed, the fabricated pillars were several times brighter than most previously measured straight-sided pillars with similar lateral dimensions.

Based on the experimentally observed count rate of the positive trion line under above-band 80 MHz excitation, the measured coupling from QD to the objective was calculated to be 0.07 ± 0.01. The corresponding value obtained from the simulated geometry was 0.13. For optimal alignment in xyz, the same geometry gave a value for the QD-waveguide coupling of 0.11, where 0.05 ± 0.02 was observed experimentally. The discrepancy between experiment and modelling is similar in both configurations. Therefore, it may arise from additional unknown losses (e.g. non-radiative states) in the real system, or from imperfect photonic simulation of the pillar.

A comparison of the real QD position, estimated from the growth recipe, with the lateral etch height, obtained from SEM imaging, indicated that the etch was in fact about 500 nm higher than the QD, as shown schematically in Fig. 1d. We proceeded by performing additional parameter sweeps (across four geometric degrees of freedom: pillar lateral size, QD vertical placement, cylindrical etch vertical placement, and radius of the etched cylinders) to identify an effective geometry that reproduced the experimentally observed emission properties and better matched the SEM images. Of these sets of parameters, none were found to produce a coupling into the objective ≥0.044, leading to the conclusion that either our simplified geometric model was not fully representative of the fabricated pillar, or else the true QD position was indeed closer to the lateral etch than that inferred from the growth recipe and SEM imaging.

In any case, the pillar geometries (both free and SEM-restricted) that optimised the coupling into the objective showed a saturation trend of the QD-to-waveguide coupling at a chip-chip separation Δz ≲ 0.5 μm, consistent with experimental observations. Furthermore, the simulation model for the excitation of the nanopillar (which takes into account the pump beam coupled into and emitted from the PIC towards the nanopillar) confirmed that the QD excitation efficiency continues to increase for smaller chip-chip separations (down to Δz ≲ 0.1 μm, SI), in agreement with what was observed in the experiments (Fig. 2a). This, along with the observed wavelength shifting of the emission, suggests that a small Δz consistent with close to maximal coupling was obtained. The simulations also showed that the system's tolerance to xy misalignment was high relative to the minimum piezoelectric stage step size, and experimentally, there was no indication of cumulative misalignment across the array. The simulations and the experimental observations, therefore, indicate that the coupling was performed with near-optimal xyz-alignment.

Paths to improve the coupling efficiency

The measured coupling efficiency of 5% approaches the simulated coupling of these QD-pillars to these waveguides of 11%. Improvements to the structure will therefore be necessary to increase the coupling to the high values needed for QIP protocols.

Simulations and other experimental results on other samples suggested that narrower pillars are, in general, brighter and that tapering the pillars may allow for better mode-matching to the waveguides. Additionally, an important variable was the distance from the QD to the top of the pillar35. We found that a geometry based on a SiO2-coated pillar terminating with a short taper, as shown in Fig. 6a, achieved more than 30% simulated coupling at 780 nm into an alternative waveguide design (a spot size converter with an input facet of 850 by 750 nm, and made of a transparent polymer with refractive index n = 1.57), under conditions comparable to that achieved experimentally (50 nm chip-chip separation). While we have performed the etching of tapers, the coating of pillars with SiO2 (and other materials)29, and the addition of reflective elements, these have not yet been combined into a single structure. Further exploration of parameters is likely to yield higher ideal coupling values. One of the advantages of the presented approach is that the particular nanostructure geometry is not strongly restricted, and any structures emitting normal to the QD sample surface could be used. Fundamentally, the coupling limit is achievable only through edge coupling to waveguides. Very high values (>95%) have been experimentally demonstrated in other contexts with single-mode fibre coupling to single-mode SiN/SiO2 waveguides36. Thus the coupling between the QD and the PIC, mediated by the nanopillar, can be expected to be improved by methodically optimising several waveguide and pillar parameters.

Emitter spectral uniformity, tuning, and linewidth

The dominant positive trion line was on average centred at 779 nm (1.59 eV), with a standard deviation of 1.0 nm (n = 39 QD-pillars), over a region hundreds of microns in dimension. Because of this low inhomogeneous broadening of the system, we observed in several places across the sample that adjacent quantum dots were significantly overlapping in their spectral features. Multiple instances were found of adjacent QD overlapping in the dominant spectral line, being only 10-20 pm detuned with respect to one another. Figure 7a shows an example of two adjacent QDs that overlapped in the dominant trion line, that were simultaneously coupled to and observed through adjacent waveguides of the array. More examples are provided in the SI. We emphasise that in all these cases there were no additional emitters, just a single QD at each array site. This represents a first step towards demonstrating quantum interference with scalable site-controlled quantum emitters. Indeed, we further demonstrated that it was possible to perform independent tuning of multiple pairs of adjacent (10 μm apart) site-controlled QDs through the use of additional laser fluence (brief high-power red laser pulses). The laser most likely modified the charge environment near the QD to induce a Stark shift, with a Stark shift of 400 pm possible without any change in the average charging or brightness of the QD, as shown in Fig. 7b.

A large majority of the tested QD-pillars responded to this tuning protocol. Thus, with this method, multiple pairs of adjacent site-controlled QD lines could be tuned into overlap with sub-pm precision, as shown in Fig. 7c (with additional examples in SI). In the example shown here, initially the lines already had a strong degree of overlap, with line centres λ0 separated by only 20 pm. After independently tuning both QDs, they overlapped to within the fitting uncertainty of 0.3 pm. This level of tuning will be necessary when the linewidths are improved towards the transform limit ( ≈ 0.7 pm, or 1.4 μeV, for the 450 ps lifetime). The present sample, when excited above-band under conditions of maximum trion brightness, had trion linewidths of around 30 pm, reducing to 11–12 pm (deconvolved) at low power or under the quasi-resonant excitation (resonant two-photon excitation of the biexciton, as shown in the SI, was similarly broadened). Other site-controlled samples (with an alternative nanostructure) have shown system resolution-limited lines33, indicating QD linewidths ≪ 10 pm, and thus approaching the transform-limit. Thus, improvements to the growth and processing recipes, such as the use of surface passivation, may be helpful in reducing this broadening. The complementary approaches of adding a diode structure (to sweep away charges) and a cavity (to increase emission rate and natural linewidth) can also be explored, as these are usually used to help produce transform-limited QD lines.

While this tuning shift was temporary, with the spectra recovering on timescales of tens of minutes, subsequent experiments on similar samples (without a pillar) showed that this tuning could also be performed with low-power continuous lasers of higher energy (blue). Thus, this may be a method for tuning and stabilising the sources over longer time scales. In the longer term, it indicates the feasibility of using a small amount of tuning to bring a near-homogeneous array of site-controlled quantum-light sources into mutual indistinguishability.

Discussion

In the recent past, it was believed that useful and scalable quantum computing would be possible through demultiplexing from single high-quality sources. However, it has become clear that the success of demultiplexing at the few-photon level is not endlessly scalable due to the reduced clock speeds that would result. Therefore, efforts are now moving towards multiple source architectures37,38, and everything which has been demonstrated probabilistically in high-quality photon sources will have to be performed deterministically and at the chip-scale.

In general, recent high-quality SPSs involve integrating a simple quantum system, such as a quantum dot or colour centre, into a photonic nanostructure, such as a photonic crystal cavity, bullseye cavity, or micropillar. The engineered photonic environment of the nanostructure enhances the emission quality, rate, and spatial control. Thus, the scalable coupling of SPSs to waveguides will, in general, involve first the deterministic alignment of the emitters to nanostructure modes and then the deterministic coupling of these to the waveguide modes. Here we have demonstrated that the steps can be performed and combined deterministically and at scale – our site-controlled (deterministically positioned) QDs were, through chip-wide post-processing, all deterministically embedded in self-aligned photonic nanostructures, and these were then simultaneously and actively (deterministically) aligned to a single-mode waveguide array, marking a significant step for the scalability of quantum photonics. We also emphasise that in this work the site-controlled emitters enabled us to design the PIC in advance, with reference to the known source positions. The alternative, which obviously provides a significant barrier to scalability, is to design the PIC around the random source positions39.

The inhomogeneous broadening of the QD distribution remained low, allowing adjacent emitters to exhibit significant spectral overlap without any post-tuning. Furthermore, we showed for the first time the active tuning of adjacent site-controlled QDs. A large tuning range was possible without significantly altering the spectrum. At the same time, fine-tuning allowed the matching of adjacent site-controlled QDs with sub-pm precision. While it is still to be shown that this procedure can actively tune multiple adjacent site-controlled QDs into quantum protocol-relevant degrees of quantum interference, it remains a significant step in that direction.

As well as the scalability, another advantage of the presented approach is its chip-to-chip flexibility. In other words, the integration is stable but reversible, and the chips can be separated and realigned, or changed. This enables easy source changes within a photonic circuit or the movement of sources between different circuits. Here, we sequentially couple different rows of site-controlled emitters to the same PIC. Further, the same QD chips can be coupled to PICs composed of entirely different material stacks, and different source material architectures can be coupled to the same photonic chip. These properties may especially be of value in the situation of low device yields, ageing, or damage. The requirements are instead geometrical: in our case, the pillar fabrication process results in a planar surface as defined by the top of the pillars, and thus the entire array can be aligned to the plane of the PIC edge facets.

One potential issue is the observed strain. However, even in the case of chip-chip contact, the stress is distributed among the array of nanostructures, and no (permanent) changes or damage were observed to occur in the majority of cases. This contact can be avoided without a significant reduction in the coupling efficiency. With other wider pillar structures29 (lateral dimension > 2 μm), the effect of strain was much less, and optimal coupling was achieved with (near-)contact but without any redshift. Other approaches to QD coupling may also have to deal with such strain effects either during the integration process or during cooling of the hybrid system (e.g. a slight blueshift was observed in ref. 39. upon cooling).

Because most other approaches to integration have used randomly positioned QDs, it is difficult to make a straightforward comparison based on the number of QDs coupled, as, in general, there is a large difference between SPS density and waveguide density. However, we can look at the number of intended SPS-devices coupled, even if in general they contain multiple emitters: see Table 1. Currently, the maximum number of such sources that have been integrated with an array of waveguides in a single step is ≈ 10, in ref. 40. In that work, a high density of random quantum dots was combined with site-controlled diodes, and several quantum dots were coupled to each waveguide via edge coupling. In previous works coupling or integrating multiple single-photon sources, only simulated or relative coupling efficiencies have been able to be reported39–43. For example, in ref. 39, through photonic wire-bonding, an average waveguide-coupled signal of 0.29 relative to that seen with the objective was obtained, similar to the ratio we have reported above. In ref. 42, eight micro-chiplets, each consisting of an adiabatic taper and a high density of QDs, were transferred one-by-one in a single fabrication run to a silicon-on-insulator PIC. In that case, the theoretical efficiency for an optimally placed QD in an optimally placed chiplet to couple to the Si waveguide was 0.41, although the maximum and average experimental values were not provided. This would have been highly variable due to the random positions and orientation of the QDs, with the coupling reducing with both the misorientation of the dipole and the variation in position ( ≈ 100 nm). Similarly, in ref. 43, 20 GaAs waveguide micro-chiplets were transferred to a lithium niobate PIC. Due to the inclusion of a reflector, the simulated QD-to-waveguide efficiency doubled to 0.83. However, after accounting for the experimental waveguide misalignment range ( ≈ 100 nm lateral and 7 mrad angular deviation), the maximum coupling (for a perfectly positioned and oriented QD) could reduce to 0.33. The waveguides contained randomly nucleated QDs at a density of 1–10 μm−2, and the experimental coupling efficiencies across the 20 waveguides were not reported. In all these cases, site control would be necessary to achieve consistent coupling.

An important parameter for the potential scalability of any coupling/integration strategy is its alignment accuracy relative to its misalignment tolerance. Even with site control of QDs, the approach will have to be tolerant of centre-centre variations in both QD and waveguide positions that will arise from lithographic limitations. For example, even if electron-beam rather than optical lithography is used to pattern the substrate, uncertainties of 10–50 nm will remain44. Here, the nano-positioner resolution is nominally 100 nm over millimetres, and sub-nm over 500 nm at 4 K, and thus the overall alignment accuracy of this approach (for a single QD-waveguide) is sub-nm, an order-of-magnitude better than that currently achievable via other methods44. For coupling multiple QD-waveguides simultaneously, the biggest factor is therefore the centre-centre variations. As mentioned above, the QD positions vary by less than 50 nm from the ideal lattice node, and the waveguides by less than 25 nm. Our nanopillars theoretically achieved up to 90% of their maximum coupling at displacements up to 100 nm. Thus, the presented approach was not limited by lithographic or alignment constraints, but rather the edge-coupling efficiency of the fabricated photonic nanostructures.

The performance of the fabricated nanopillars was a limiting factor, and these operated close to their simulated capability. Improvements to the pillar design (and waveguide facet cross-section dimensions), as were discussed, will increase the ideal and hence experimental coupling values.

In our approach, the coupling of all sources and waveguides is performed simultaneously, and thus the coupling of, e.g., 1000 QDs will not entail significantly more resources than two, in contrast with one-by-one processes (e.g. one-at-a-time photonic wire-bonding or micro-chiplet transfer). We have verified the simultaneous coupling of ≈ 10 QDs, but in principle, hundreds are possible with the described setup and procedure. Any potential chip-scale integration could be affected by bowing or warping of chips or carrier films, leading to cumulative misalignments that would require corrective steps. In our case, we observed (from in-situ optical imagery) that the distance between the chips remained the same along the chip length. Further, while only the waveguide array was intentionally matched to the QD array, when this was aligned, the MMI, which was 600 μm away, was still partially coupled (see SI). Only a small adjustment along the array direction was required, indicating that the chip-chip separation had not changed significantly, and that it should be possible to couple devices at least this far apart by matching all of the PIC devices to the QD grid. However, in this case, if GaAs and SiN chips are again used, the differential contraction upon cooling would have to be accounted for. Over the 100 μm wide array described above, the calculated difference is approximately 50 nm, and was not observed to affect the coupling (in agreement with simulations). Over 1 cm however, this difference would become 5 μm, and thus a correction factor would need to be applied to the waveguide positions during the design of the PIC.

Denser integration could also be achieved by stacking waveguides or coupling to laser-written 3D circuits45, an approach not obviously compatible with current transfer printing or pick-and-place schemes. This will increase the challenge of simultaneously exciting multiple QDs. For this purpose, ring resonators can be used to couple to multiple QDs, and to demultiplex the laser from the QD signals. If the QDs are grown in a diode structure, electrical excitation also becomes an option.

In terms of the alignment procedure and experimental arrangement, rotation of the piezoelectric stages would aid in ensuring that the coupling across the chip is uniform. Fibre-coupling could also be used at the detector side of the PIC5,40 to improve the collection efficiency and the number of PIC device outputs simultaneously accessible. Eventually, on-chip detectors may be included on the PIC46. With modifications to the QD stack, room temperature alignment and bonding could also become feasible.

In conclusion, significant steps towards a scalable quantum photonic computing architecture were demonstrated through the active alignment of arrays of site-controlled deterministic quantum light sources to arrays of nanophotonic waveguides. The alignment was flexible and reconfigurable at cryogenic temperatures, while maintaining stability. We verified the high single-photon purity and low inhomogeneous broadening of the site-controlled emitter array, in which multiple adjacent emitters could be tuned into spectral overlap. Future improvements in SPS-nanostructure quality and coupling efficiency can enable scalable demonstrations of fundamental quantum information protocols.

Methods

Spectra acquisition and filtering

In all cases, the signal from the QD or waveguide was sent to a monochromator with a 950 lines/mm grating. In the case of above-band excitation (636 nm), a 700 nm longpass filter was used to filter the laser before the monochromator; and in the case of the quasi-resonant excitation, between one and three notch filters were placed in the collection path(s) in order to suppress the laser. For the spectra, the signal dispersed by the grating was collected by a silicon CCD. For the single-photon measurements, the signal was sent to the exit slit of the monochromator, which was fixed at 0.3 mm, corresponding to a 0.2 nm filtering window around the line of interest.

Correlation measurements

For the G(2)(τ) measurements, the two signals were sent through two separate monochromators and registered by avalanche photo-diodes. These were correlated with a single-photon counter module. 50 ps bins were used to obtain the raw data, and these were converted to 2.5 ns bins for display and analysis. Array ratio calculation

The ratio of the signals observed through the waveguide and directly with the objective were determined by performing Voigt peak fits of the dominant lines in the spectra. Different fitting procedures did not differ from this value (0.17) by more than 0.01, and since the linewidths were all similar during and after coupling, comparison of the peak CCD counts alone also gave a similar mean value (0.16). The uncertainty was obtained via a min-max approach.

Tabletop efficiency

The power of a 780 nm laser was measured before entering the back of the objective, as well as after the multi-mode fibre, which was connected to the single-photon detector. Correcting for losses due to overfilling and passing through the objective, an extra reflection and an extra pass through the cryostat window and using the detector specification at 780 nm, we obtained an overall efficiency of 0.064 ± 0.005 at 780 nm. This was reduced to 0.056 when using a notch filter, and 0.041 when using three notch filters and a beam-splitter. More details are provided in the SI.

Temperature monitoring

The temperature of the QD sample was monitored using a sensor on top of the xyz stage, while the temperature of the SiN chip was monitored using the temperature-dependent PL of a triple GaAs quantum well in close proximity on its copper mount.
