On the Benefits of Free Exploration for Regret Minimization in Multi-Armed Bandits
Yunlong Hou yhou@u.nus.edu, Zixin Zhong zixinzhong@hkust-gz.edu.cn, Vincent Y. F. Tan vtan@nus.edu.sg

Abstract
We study a stochastic multi-armed bandit problem where an agent is granted a free exploration budget before regret accumulates, a setting not captured by the classic regret minimization or pure exploration paradigms. The goal is to design an adaptive policy that strategically explores the bandit instance in the initial free exploration phase and minimizes the cumulative regret in the subsequent phase. We formalize this regret minimization with free exploration problem and identify an interesting regime where the free exploration budget scales logarithmically with the time horizon. To quantify the amount of regret saved with high probability as a result of the availability of the free exploration phase, we introduce a novel set of policies known as ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) -probably saving policies. We propose a two-phase, probably saving algorithm, UFE-KLUCB-H , which consists of a principled free exploration policy, UFE , and a history-aware regret minimization policy KLUCB-H . Instance-dependent upper bounds on UFE-KLUCB-H are derived, showing that UFE-KLUCB-H accumulates strictly less regret than policies that do not have access to a free exploration phase. Complementarily, we derive instance-dependent lower bounds based on novel multi-instance perturbation arguments tailored to the free-exploration setting, demonstrating the near-optimality of UFE-KLUCB-H for two-valued bandits. Our upper and lower bounds reveal sharp phase transitions in the accumulated regret depending on the amount of available free exploration. Simulations are conducted to demonstrate that forced exploration and adaptivity in the algorithm lead to greater regret savings.

Introduction

In this paper, we study a non-standard
rm
(

fe

Such staged exploration-and-deployment paradigms arise naturally in many modern learning systems. In practice, learning systems are rarely deployed directly into fully regret-bearing environments. Instead, they are typically developed through multiple stages, such as offline experimentation, simulation, sandbox testing, or beta deployment before large-scale deployment. During these preliminary stages, the learner can often collect informative samples at negligible operational cost because mistakes do not directly affect end users, revenue, or safety-critical performance. Consequently, many practical systems naturally possess a limited exploration budget prior to deployment, during which aggressive information acquisition is possible before regret begins to accumulate. This phenomenon is common across robotics, online experimentation, and broader machine learning pipelines.

Motivated by these practical considerations, our work advances the theoretical understanding of the exploration–exploitation trade-off in a direction that has received scant prior attention. We describe two representative motivating examples in the next subsection.

1.1
Motivating Examples

Robotics:

Consider a mobile robot that is eventually deployed to perform a repetitive task, such as warehouse navigation, infrastructure inspection, or package delivery along a fixed route. Engineers must choose among several candidate control strategies that differ in their speed, robustness, and safety characteristics. At the outset, however, it is unclear which controller performs best in the target environment, since performance depends on factors such as sensor noise, wheel slippage, and unmodeled dynamics.

Before deployment, the robot typically undergoes a
calibration phase
(corresponding to the
fe
phase in our formulation) in a laboratory or simulated environment. During this stage, engineers are free to test all candidate controllers, including poorly performing ones, in order to understand their behavior and estimate their reliability. The objective of this phase is purely informational rather than operational: inefficient or unstable behavior during calibration does not affect customers, production throughput, or safety-critical operations, and is therefore not counted as a loss. Consequently, the robot can explore aggressively and gather informative samples across all controllers without incurring regret.

Once the robot is deployed in the real world, however, controller choices have immediate operational consequences. Selecting a suboptimal controller may lead to slower task completion, increased energy consumption, or greater mechanical wear. At this
deployment stage
—the
ra
phase in our formulation—the robot must continue selecting among the same set of controllers over time, but now the objective shifts to minimizing cumulative regret relative to the best controller in hindsight. The robot therefore exploits the information gathered during calibration to favor the most promising controllers, while still performing limited additional exploration to account for environmental changes or inaccuracies in the calibration process.

A/B Testing:

Another example can be the widely deployed A/B testing pipelines. In modern large-scale systems, candidate variants (e.g., ranking algorithms or UI designs or advertisements) are first evaluated on a small scale (Beta versions) before being exposed to users of larger populations
(

8

The balance between exploration and exploitation has been studied extensively in stochastic bandits with a series of seminal works on

In traditional

(

Beyond classic

In the

;

;

While most work treats

\frac{1}{2}
.
Naturally, when

is large, we expect that the confidence level

can approach

. Thus, we consider the setting in which
\delta=\delta_{T}
vanishes with

. Inspired by the fixed-confidence

(

in which

given the

budget. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3

(\alpha,\beta)
-Probably Saving Policies)

Given
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
and free exploration budget
T_{\mathrm{FE}}
,
a policy
\pi
is an
(\alpha,\beta)
-probably-saving policy if
for any instance
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i\in[L]}
in the instance class

, as

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})\geq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})\big)\geq 1-O(T^{-\beta}).

(9)

Remark 4
.

An
(\alpha,\beta)
-probably saving policy (for any
\beta\in(0,1)
) is an

-saving policy because, by applying Markov’s inequality to (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})\big]\geq\big(1-O(T^{-\beta})\big)\cdot\alpha\cdot\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}}),

(10)

which clearly implies (

).

4.1
Free Exploration Policy:
UFE

Given the
fe
budget
T_{\mathrm{FE}}
and the error parameter
\beta
, we aim to design an
fe
policy that can save as much regret as possible, i.e., maximize
\alpha
.
Our policy,
UFE
, presented in Algorithm
1
, acts in phases.
It first initializes the number of pulls for each phase
t(k,\delta)=2\sigma^{2}d^{2k}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}
and the active item set
\mathcal{A}(0)
(Line
1
).
In phase
k
, it samples each item
i\in\mathcal{A}(k-1)
in a round-robin manner for a total of
T_{i,t}=t(k,\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{(k+1)^{2}})=:T(k)
times
(Line
3
), and updates the empirical means after
k
phases
\hat{\mu}_{i}(k)=\hat{\mu}_{i,t}
(Line
4
), where

\displaystyle T_{i,t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{s}=i\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\hat{\mu}_{i,t}=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{i,s}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{s}=i\}}{T_{i,t}}.

(12)

Next,
UFE
identifies the empirically best item
i^{*}(k):=\argmax_{i}\hat{\mu}_{i}(k)
, the bad item set
\mathcal{B}(k)
and maintains the
active set

\mathcal{A}(k)
(Line
5
) defined respectively as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(k)

\displaystyle=\big\{i\in\mathcal{A}(k-1):\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}(k)}(k)-\hat{\mu}_{i}(k)\geq c\cdot d^{-k}\big\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{A}(k)=\mathcal{A}(k-1)\setminus\mathcal{B}(k).

(13)

For each bad item
i\in\mathcal{B}(k)
, forced exploration (by round-robin) is applied to guarantee that the bad items are sampled sufficiently (Line
6
).
The algorithm terminates whenever the budget is used up,
even when a phase has not been completed (in Lines
3
or
6
).
We assume it ends after a complete phase for ease of understanding.

Intuitively, as the definition of the empirical saved regret in (
7
) suggests, maximizing savings resembles
rm
and a policy that chooses the item with the smallest lowest confidence bound should be employed. While this is plausible, we highlight that the
\min
operator in (
7
) hints that a good policy should stop sampling the bad items in a timely manner after sufficient exploration, as excess pulls are not counted. To stop promptly, a good estimate of
D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})
(or
\mu_{1}
) is required. Therefore, both the good and worst items need to be sampled to provide suboptimality information and to save regret, respectively.

UFE
achieves this by allocating
O(\beta\ln T)
pulls to the good items, which is necessary to satisfy Definition
3
, as suggested by the lower bound (see Section
5
). This is followed by forced explorations of the bad items to approach
\frac{\ln T}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})}
.
The
\frac{c^{2}}{(c-2)^{2}}
factor in Line
6
takes the uncertainty of the empirical means into account.
UFE
samples conservatively to guarantee arm
i\in\mathcal{B}(k)
is sampled sufficiently many times.

Remark 5
.

While
UFE
, Sequential Halving (SH)
(
23
)
, Successive Elimination (SE) and the Naïve algorithm
(
15
)
all sample items—except for those already identified as suboptimal—for the same number of times. By contrast,
UFE

enforces additional exploration
of suboptimal items for more times (Line 6 of Algorithm
1
). This unique design is essential for an efficient
fe
policy because the SH, SE and Naïve algorithms will stop pulling an item once it is identified as suboptimal with certain confidence level. However, unique to our
rm
with
fe
problem, such an item needs to be pulled more times so that it is not likely to be subsequently pulled and generate (large) regret during the
ra
phase.

To quantify the fluctuations of the KL divergence under perturbations, for fixed
c>0
, we define

\displaystyle\rho_{i,\min}(\varepsilon):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\mu}_{i}\in[\mu_{i}-\varepsilon,\;\mu_{i}+\varepsilon]\\
\hat{\mu}_{1}\in[\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\;\mu_{1}+\varepsilon]\\
\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{i}\geq c\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{D_{\nu}(\mu_{i},\mu_{1})}{D_{\nu}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\mu}_{1})}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\rho_{i,\max}(\varepsilon):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\mu}_{i}\in[\mu_{i}-\varepsilon,\;\mu_{i}+\varepsilon]\\
\hat{\mu}_{1}\in[\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\;\mu_{1}+\varepsilon]\\
\hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i}\geq c\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{D_{\nu}(\mu_{i},\mu_{1})}{D_{\nu}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\mu}_{1})}.

(14)

In addition, we define the
critical phases

k_{i}^{\pm}
and corresponding
critical pull counts

T_{i}^{\pm}
for each item
i
as

\displaystyle k_{i}^{+}:=\Big\lceil\log_{d}\frac{c+2}{\Delta_{i}}\Big\rceil,\qquad T_{i}^{+}:=\frac{c^{2}}{(c-2)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\rho_{i,\max}(\frac{\Delta_{i}}{c-2})\cdot\ln T}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})},

(15)

\displaystyle k_{i}^{-}:=\Big\lfloor\log_{d}\frac{c-2}{\Delta_{i}}\Big\rfloor,\qquad T_{i}^{-}:=\frac{c^{2}}{(c-2)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\rho_{i,\min}(\frac{\Delta_{i}}{c-2})\cdot\ln T}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})}.

(16)

We show in Appendix
C.1
that item
i
will be in the bad item set
\mathcal{B}(k)
for some
k\in\{k_{i}^{-},k_{i}^{-}+1,\ldots,k_{i}^{+}\}
, and will be sampled for a total of
T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\in\{T_{i}^{-},T_{i}^{-}+1,\ldots,T_{i}^{+}\}
times with high probability.

Therefore, let
\kappa
denote the termination phase of
UFE
.
If
\kappa>k_{i}^{+}
, then item
i
has been sufficiently explored;
if
\kappa<k_{i}^{-}
, then item
i\notin\mathcal{B}(k)
for any
k\leq\kappa
;
if
\kappa\in\{k_{i}^{-},k_{i}^{-}+1,\ldots,k_{i}^{+}\}
, item
i
can be forced explored or is not in
\mathcal{B}(k)
yet.
Given these insights, we let
\hat{R}(\kappa;\nu,T,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
be the minimum value to the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\{T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\}_{i\in[L]}}\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\Delta_{i}\cdot\min\Big\{T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}},\frac{\ln T}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})}\Big\}

(17)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \quad\mbox{s.t.}\qquad T_{i}^{-}&\leq T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\leq T_{i}^{+},\quad\;\;\kappa>k_{i}^{+}\\
T(\kappa-1)&\leq T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\leq T_{i}^{+},\quad\;\;k_{i}^{-}\leq\kappa\leq k_{i}^{+}\\
T(\kappa-1)&\leq T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\leq T(\kappa),\quad\kappa<k_{i}^{-}\\
\sum_{i\in[L]}T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}&=T_{\mathrm{FE}}.\end{aligned}

(18)

By convention, we set the minimum to be
\infty
when the optimization problem (
18
) is infeasible.

Theorem 6
.

Given an instance
\nu
, error parameter
\beta
, free exploration budget
T_{\mathrm{FE}}
and time horizon

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})\geq\min_{\kappa\in[T]}\hat{R}(\kappa;\nu,T,T_{\mathrm{FE}})

The proof of Theorem

As the saved regret depends on the specific instance, the value of

5
Lower Bound and Asymptotic Optimality

5.1
Information-Theoretic Lower Bound

We provide an instance-dependent lower bound on the regret in this section.
Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}}):=\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\Delta_{i}\min\bigg\{\frac{\ln T}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})},\mathbb{E}[T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}]\bigg\},

(34)

a quantity that is similar to the empirical saved regret in (
7
). We first show that the number of pulls for each item in the
whole horizon
can be lower bounded similar to the standard
rm
setting (i.e., without
fe
).

Lemma 11
.

For any consistent algorithm
\pi
, given instance
\nu
and free exploration budget
T_{\mathrm{FE}}=o(T)
, the expected number of pulls of item
i
satisfies

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{i,T}]}{\ln T}\geq\frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})}.

(35)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\underline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi,\nu)\geq\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\!\!\frac{\Delta_{i}}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})}-\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})}{\ln T}.

(36)

The proof is presented in Appendix
D.1
.
This lemma shows the total number of pulls for each item is still lower bounded by
\frac{\ln T}{D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})}
asymptotically as
T\to\infty
. The performance of the algorithm depends on how well
\pi_{\textrm{FE}}
behaves.
In the following, we upper bound the second term involving
\widetilde{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
for the class of
(\alpha,\beta)
-probably saving algorithms.

Compared to standard
rm
or
bai
, the main difficulty in our problem lies in constructing meaningful alternative instances. In
rm
, one can simply replace a suboptimal item
i
by an item with mean
\mu_{1}+\varepsilon
(with the rest of the items unchanged), where
\varepsilon
can be arbitrarily small
(
28
;
9
)
. This ubiquitous technique unfortunately fails here: changing the mean of a single item may have a negligible effect on the empirical saved regret
\widehat{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
. For example, even if we exclude the regret incurred by pulling the worst item
L
, the saved regret can be larger than
\alpha\cdot\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
. This phenomenon becomes more pronounced when there are more bad items. Thus, the construction of appropriate alternative instances, in general, requires
replacing more than one item
and thus is highly instance-dependent.

Below we state a lower bound for the two-valued case. This simpler setting suffices to convey the key insights and to highlight the subtleties of the construction.
Other instances are discussed in Appendix
B
. Consider the two-valued instance
\nu
with
L_{\mathrm{g}}
good items and

7
Conclusions and Future Works

Conclusions

We formulate the
rm
with
fe
problem, motivated by practical scenarios (Section
1.1
), and introduce a novel class of policies which we call
(\alpha,\beta)
-probably-saving policies
(Section
3
). We present
UFE-KLUCB-H
(Section
4
), which achieves substantial regret savings. We derive matching instance-dependent upper and lower bounds for specific instances which can be computed explicitly in terms of the parameters of the instance (Section
5
). As such, we can demonstrate that
UFE-KLUCB-H
is nearly asymptotically optimal for the class of two-valued instances (Section
5.2
). This problem offers new insights into the exploration–exploitation trade-off in the bandit literature: with an additional exploration budget, an agent can devote more effort to exploitation during the
ra
phase.

Future Works

In this work,
UFE-KLUCB-H
adopts uniform sampling among active items, which proves to be simple and effective. It contributes
O(\beta\ln T)
pulls which is small, as
\beta
is instantiated as a small parameter. Nevertheless, it is still of interest whether we can improve the factor by designing better exploration policies when only rare information about the reward distributions is available. Promising algorithms include variants of LUCB
(
22
)
.

Furthermore,
as Thompson Sampling
(
2
)
performs well in practice and is also asymptotically optimal for certain instances (e.g., Gaussian), it would be interesting to see whether the history-aware version of Thompson Sampling can be derived and how the regret is reduced with the assistance of the historical data.

Moreover,
we assume the distribution family of the reward is given in this work. While this is common in the literature
(
24
;
16
;
17
)
, the non-parametric bandit problem may be more practical when such information is unavailable
(
33
)
. The extension to the non-parametric bandits is a promising direction for future research.

We study
K
-armed bandits in this work. It would be interesting to extend the
fe
formulation to structured bandits, e.g., linear bandits
(
1
)
, Lipschitz bandits
(
30
)
, unimodal bandits
(
38
;
13
)
, etc.
Under such setups, instead of sampling individual items, we expect that the agent can use the
fe
budget to explore the
structure
of the instance, which can help to eliminate suboptimal items more efficiently. Additionally, the extension to the constrained setup is also of interest, where the agent needs to take the constraint into consideration, beyond the exploration-and-exploitation trade-off
(
3
;
19
)
. The balance among these factors requires a more delicate algorithm design.

Finally, in this work, the reward distributions of the arms do not shift from the
fe
phase to the
ra
phase. In practice, there can be potential distribution shifts between these two phases. Because the
fe
&
rm
setting has not been studied systematically, we first address the simpler no-shift case to build insights before tackling distribution shifts.
At a high level, the observations gathered during the
fe
phase are most useful when there is no distribution shift between phases; our algorithm is most effective in that setting. As the shift increases, the value of
fe
data diminishes.
12
shows that FE data is unhelpful when the shift is unbounded or exceeds
2c\Delta_{i}
for arm
i
(for some
c>0
). Intuitively, if the relationship between arms in the
fe
and
ra
phases can be revealed or learned, then
fe
information can be leveraged to reduce regret in the
ra
phase. For example: (1) when the distribution shift is small, or (2) in switching bandits where arms evolve via a learnable transition matrix
P
at certain change points, our formulation could be extended to incorporate that structure.

Appendix A
More Discussions

A.1
Discussions on
\alpha
-Saving Algorithms

As mentioned, since the
fe
budget
T_{\mathrm{FE}}=O(\ln T)
, a naïve algorithm such as uniform sampling can save
\alpha\cdot\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
with constant probability.
However, when
T
and
T_{\mathrm{FE}}
grow, we expect the regret can be saved more reliably. Therefore, while this naïve approach can achieve (
8
), it can still be improved.
This is illustrated in the following example.

Example 14
.

Consider a two arm Gaussian instance
\nu=\{\nu_{i}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},1)\}_{i=1,2}
. We apply the non-asymptotic law of iterated logarithms (LIL)
(
20
, Lemma 3)
to the sequence
(X_{1,t}-X_{2,t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\sigma,\varepsilon
fixed and
\delta
sufficient small. Let
b_{t}=\Theta\big(\sqrt{t^{-1}\ln\ln t}\big)
and
\hat{\Delta}_{t}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}(X_{1,s}-X_{2,s})

be the empirical gap.
Then
\mathbb{P}\big(\forall\,t\in\mathbb{N}:|\hat{\Delta}_{t}-\Delta_{1,2}|\geq b_{t}\big)\leq 0.99.

We uniformly sample the two arms until
b_{t}\leq c|\hat{\Delta}_{t}|
with
c=\frac{1-\sqrt{\alpha}}{1+\sqrt{\alpha}}
. Then, we sample the empirically better arm
\frac{2}{(1+c)^{2}\hat{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}\ln T
times or until the
fe
budget is exhausted. With probability at least
0.99
, it holds that

\alpha\cdot\frac{2}{\Delta_{1,2}^{2}}\ln T\leq\frac{2}{(1+c)^{2}\hat{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}\ln T\leq\frac{2}{\Delta_{1,2}^{2}}\ln T.

The expected saved regret is thus lower bounded by
0.99\alpha\cdot\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
(We can set
\delta
or
c
smaller to guarantee exactly
\alpha\cdot\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
is saved).
Furthermore, it can be proved that
b_{t}\leq c|\hat{\Delta}_{t}|
occurs at most
\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{1-\sqrt{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1}{\Delta}\ln\frac{1}{\delta})
times.
We highlight that this stopping time is
independent
of
T
.
Therefore, by using uniform sampling with a
finite
number of pulls, with an additional forced exploration, this simple algorithm guarantees that (
8
) holds. But the guarantees on the empirical saved regret hold with constant probability, which does not vanish as
T\to\infty
.

\overline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi,\nu)-\underline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi^{\prime},\nu)=\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{R_{T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1:T}(\pi)}{\ln T}-\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{R_{T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1:T}(\pi^{\prime})}{\ln T}.

•

When
\frac{L}{L_{\mathrm{b}}}(c+2)^{2}\beta+O(\varepsilon)\leq\gamma\leq 1+\frac{L_{\mathrm{g}}}{L_{\mathrm{b}}}(c+2)^{2}\beta+O(\varepsilon)
,
this gap is upper bounded as

\overline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi,\nu)-\underline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi^{\prime},\nu)\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\Delta_{2}}{\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})}\bigg(\frac{(L_{\mathrm{g}}(c+2)^{2}-\frac{1}{4})\beta}{L_{\mathrm{b}}}+O(\varepsilon)\bigg).

•

Furthermore, when
\frac{3L_{\mathrm{g}}(c+2)^{2}\beta}{L_{\mathrm{b}}}+O(\varepsilon)\leq\gamma\leq\min\big\{1,\frac{L_{\mathrm{g}}-2}{4L_{\mathrm{b}}}\big\}
,
the bound on the gap can be further decreased to

\overline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi,\nu)-\underline{\mathfrak{R}}(\pi^{\prime},\nu)\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{b}}\cdot\Delta_{2}}{\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})}\bigg(\frac{((c+2)^{2}-\zeta)L_{\mathrm{g}}\beta}{L_{\mathrm{b}}}+O(\varepsilon)\bigg)

where
\zeta=\frac{1}{\max\{4\gamma L_{\mathrm{b}}(1+O(\varepsilon)),1\}}=\min\{\frac{1+O(\varepsilon)}{4\gamma L_{\mathrm{b}}},1\}
.
As we consider the case where
\beta
is small, this gap is considered as small.

•

When
\gamma>1+\frac{L_{\mathrm{g}}}{L_{\mathrm{b}}}(c+2)^{2}\beta+O(\varepsilon)
,
UFE-KLUCB-H
can save almost all regret up to
\frac{\Delta_{L}\ln T}{\mathrm{d}(\mu_{L},\mu_{1})}\cdot O(\varepsilon)
.

This completes the proof of Corollary
13
.

Lemma 22
.

Given any instance
\nu
and any consistent algorithm
\pi
with a deterministic
fe
policy
\pi_{\textrm{FE}}
, with
fe
budget
T_{\mathrm{FE}}
, the regret saved by the algorithm
\pi
is upper bounded by
\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
as in (
4
).

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{KL}_{i}:=D_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu_{i},\nu_{1})
and
R_{T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1:T}=R_{T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1:T}(\pi;\nu)
. The regret is

\displaystyle R_{T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1:T}=\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\mathbb{E}[(T_{i,T}-T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}})_{+}]\Delta_{i}\geq\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\bigg(\frac{\ln T}{\mathrm{KL}_{i}}-T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\bigg)_{+}\Delta_{i},

(256)

where Jensen’s inequality is applied to the convex function
(\cdot)_{+}
, and
\mathbb{E}[T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}]=T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}
since we use a deterministic
fe
policy. Thus, for any asymptotically optimal algorithm,

R_{1:T}-R_{T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1:T}\leq\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\min\bigg\{T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}},\frac{\ln T}{\mathrm{KL}_{i}}\bigg\}\Delta_{i}=:\widehat{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}}).

The maximum value
\mathrm{Save}^{*}(\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}})
is the solution to

\max_{T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}},i\in[K]}\widehat{\mathrm{Save}}(\pi;\nu,T_{\mathrm{FE}}),\quad\text{s.t. }\quad T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}\geq 0,\\
\sum_{i}T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}=T_{\mathrm{FE}}.

where the
fe
budget is allocated to larger-gap arms first, which gives (
4
).
∎

Lemma 23
.

For any arm
i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{t}=i,\mu_{1}\leq u_{1,t}\}\bigg]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{s=T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{s\cdot\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i}^{s},\mu_{1})\leq\log T+3\log\log T\}\bigg]

(257)

Proof.

By the design of
i_{t}
,
i_{t}=i
and
\mu_{1}\leq u_{1,t}
indicates
u_{i,t}\geq u_{1,t}\geq\mu_{1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i,t},\mu_{1})\leq\mathrm{d}(\hat{\mu}_{i,t},\mu_{1,t})\leq\frac{\log t+3\log\log t}{T_{i,t}}.

(258)

We can bound the summation as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{t}=i,\mu_{1}\leq u_{1,t}\}

(259)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{t}=i,T_{i,t}\cdot\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i,t},\mu_{1})\leq\log t+3\log\log t\}

(260)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1}^{T}\sum_{s=T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}+1}^{t}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{t}=i,T_{i,t}=s,s\cdot\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i}^{s},\mu_{1})\leq\log T+3\log\log T\}

(261)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{s=T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}+1}^{T}\sum_{t=\max\{s,T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1\}}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{t}=i,T_{i,t}=s,s\cdot\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i}^{s},\mu_{1})\leq\log T+3\log\log T\}

(262)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{s=T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}+1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{s\cdot\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i}^{s},\mu_{1})\leq\log T+3\log\log T\}\cdot\!\!\sum_{t=\max\{s,T_{\mathrm{FE}}+1\}}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{i_{t}=i,T_{i,t}=s\}

(263)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{s=T_{i,T_{\mathrm{FE}}}+1}^{T}\!\!\mathbbm{1}\{s\cdot\mathrm{d}^{+}(\hat{\mu}_{i}^{s},\mu_{1})\leq\log T+3\log\log T\}.

(264)

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Given two distributions
p_{\theta_{i}},i=1,2
in the one-dimensional canonical exponential family, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{\theta_{i}}(x)=\exp\big(x\theta-b(\theta)+c(x)\big)

(265)

where
\theta
is a real parameter,
c
is a real function and the log-partition function
b
is assumed to be twice differentiable,
then there exist
\hat{\theta},\tilde{\theta}\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
such that

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{kl}}(p_{\theta_{1}},p_{\theta_{2}})=\frac{b^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})}{2}\frac{(\mu(\theta_{1})-\mu(\theta_{2}))^{2}}{(b^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\theta}))^{2}}.

(266)

Furthermore, if the variance
\mathbb{V}_{p_{\theta}}[X]\in[\sigma_{\min}^{2},\sigma_{\max}^{2}]
for any
\theta\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
, then

\displaystyle\frac{(\mu(\theta_{1})-\mu(\theta_{2}))^{2}}{2}\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{\sigma_{\max}^{4}}\leq D_{\mathrm{kl}}(p_{\theta_{1}},p_{\theta_{2}})\leq\frac{(\mu(\theta_{1})-\mu(\theta_{2}))^{2}}{2}\frac{\sigma_{\max}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}^{4}}.

(267)

Proof.

Denote the mean
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}}[X]
and the variance
\sigma^{2}(\theta)=\mathbb{V}_{p_{\theta}}[X]
.
According to
16
,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{kl}}(p_{\theta_{1}},p_{\theta_{2}})=\mu(\theta_{1})(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})-b(\theta_{1})+b(\theta_{2}).

(268)

By the property of the one-dimensional canonical exponential family, we also have
\mu(\theta)=b^{\prime}(\theta)
and
\sigma^{2}(\theta)=b^{\prime\prime}(\theta)
. Because
b
is twice differentiable, by the mean value theorem

\displaystyle b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{2})=b^{\prime}(\theta_{1})+\frac{b^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})}{2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})^{2},

(269)

\displaystyle b^{\prime}(\theta_{1})-b^{\prime}(\theta_{2})=b^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\theta})(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})

(270)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{kl}}(p_{\theta_{1}},p_{\theta_{2}})=\frac{b^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})}{2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})^{2}=\frac{b^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})}{2}\frac{(\mu(\theta_{1})-\mu(\theta_{2}))^{2}}{(b^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\theta}))^{2}}.

(271)

Additionally, if
\mathbb{V}_{p_{\theta}}[X]\in[\sigma_{\min}^{2},\sigma_{\max}^{2}]
, then

\displaystyle\frac{(\mu(\theta_{1})-\mu(\theta_{2}))^{2}}{2}\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{\sigma_{\max}^{4}}\leq D_{\mathrm{kl}}(p_{\theta_{1}},p_{\theta_{2}})\leq\frac{(\mu(\theta_{1})-\mu(\theta_{2}))^{2}}{2}\frac{\sigma_{\max}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}^{4}}.

(272)

This lemma is a version of the well-known connection between the relative entropy and the Fisher information
(
4
)
adapted to our needs in which the distributions are not necessarily close.
∎
