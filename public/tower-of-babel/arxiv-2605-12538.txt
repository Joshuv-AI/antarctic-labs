Quantum chaos with graphs: a silicon photonics plateform Preprint: APS/123-QED
H. Girin,, X. Checoury, B. Odouard, S. Bittner,, J.-R. Coudevylle, B. Dietz,, C. Lafargue, M. Lebental

Abstract
We provide a versatile plateform to investigate wave-particle duality. This photonic waveguide network implements quantum (wave) graphs as proposed in the seminal paper by Kottos & Smilansky [PRL 85 968 (2000)]. We experimentally demonstrated that the spectral statistics of a mixing (i.e. strongly chaotic) graph follows the predictions of random matrix theory, contrary to an ergodic (i.e. less chaotic) graph, in agreement with the Bohigas-Giannoni-Schmit conjecture [PRL 52 1 (1984)]. This plateform also gives access to the wavefunction patterns, which are expected to verify the quantum ergodicity theorem.



Wave graphs (also known as quantum graphs), introduced as theoretical models in the late 20
{}^{\textrm{th}}
century

provide a simple yet powerful framework for studying quantum chaos, wave dynamics, and spectral statistics.
They consist of one-dimensional bonds (edges) connected at vertices, where wave propagation is governed by the Schrödinger equation
with boundary conditions ensuring continuity and current conservation

39
.
The classical analogue is a particle hopping from bond to bond, where the dynamics can be ergodic or even mixing.
The latter corresponds to the strongest form of classical chaos, and it plays a central role in determining the spectral properties of the quantum system.

According to the Bohigas-Giannoni-Schmit (BGS) conjecture
7
, the spectral statistics of
quantum systems with classically chaotic counterparts follow the universal predictions of random matrix theory (RMT)

;
20
.
This universality has been experimentally verified in microwave, acoustic, and mesoscopic systems
37
;
18
;
38
;
11
;
28
.
For wave graphs, the conjecture can be made rigorous: if the bond lengths are incommensurate and the corresponding classical dynamics is mixing and time reversal invariant,
then all spectral correlation functions coincide with those of the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) in the limit of large graphs without cycle
31
;
32
.
This makes wave graphs not only a tractable theoretical model, but also a rare instance where universality in quantum chaos can be mathematically proven.

Several experimental platforms have been developed to realize wave graphs, most prominently microwave cable networks
22
. These so-called Neumann graphs are constructed from coaxial cables connected by T-joints as vertices, where they fulfill the Neumann boundary conditions. One big advantage of these graphs is that all three Wigner–Dyson universality classes from Dyson’s threefold way can be realized
14
;
44
;
6
;
34
;
29
;
26
;
10
, even for the symmetry classes of the tenfold way
1
;
35
. While these approaches provide excellent controllability, they suffer from important limitations: backscattering at junctions leads to non universal localized modes
13
. Furthermore, the wavefunction can be probed only at the vertices, not along the bonds. These were measured and studied experimentally in Ref.
42
in waveguide graphs with a geometry which was previously used in superconductive microwave waveguide
12
to investigate the spectral properties of quantum graphs.

In this work, we introduce an experimental platform:
Integrated Silicon Optical Waveguide Networks
.
These systems are fabricated on a silicon photonics platform and implement wave graphs using
2:2 bidirectional couplers
,
which consist of two parallel waveguides coupled by evanescent tunneling.
Unlike T-joints in microwave networks, such couplers eliminate backscattering and ensure proper redistribution of optical amplitudes.
Our devices operate at the telecommunication wavelength
\lambda_{0}=1.55~\mu
m and offer several additional advantages:
they function at room temperature, are scalable and compact due to the short optical wavelength, are compatible with existing telecom technology,
and thus fully integrated on chip.
Most importantly, they allow
direct imaging of the wavefunction
inside the graph via third-harmonic generation (THG) in silicon
41
,
providing access to spatial information with unprecedented accuracy.

Integrated photonic networks have already been explored for diverse applications, including high-finesse spectroscopy
43
,
random laser networks
36
, and quantum information processing via quantum random walks
15
;
33
;
3
.
Numerical simulations have also shown that photonic crystal graphs exhibit GOE-type spectral fluctuations
27
,
and the theoretical framework of wave graphs has been applied to describe resonances in lithium niobate random networks
40
.
However, these works did not investigate the signatures of quantum chaos, nor did they allow direct wavefunction imaging.

The central goal of this work is to test whether these silicon-integrated wave graphs reproduce the universal predictions of quantum chaos.
To this end, we focus on two representative five-vertex networks: a
Flower graph (FG)
, which is ergodic but non-mixing, and a
Bow-Tie graph (BTG)
, which exhibits mixing dynamics.
We find that the spectral statistics of the BTG approach those of the GOE,
yet still display small deviations attributable to the finite size effects and cycles.
By contrast, the non-mixing FG shows clear deviations from universality.
These results establish silicon photonic waveguide networks as a powerful new platform for probing how graph topology and system size govern the emergence of quantum chaos.

The paper is organized as follows.
First, we present the silicon photonic waveguide networks forming our experimental platform and detail the measurement setup used for their optical characterization.
Then the theoretical framework of wave graphs is introduced and compared with the experimental spectral statistics of the FG and BTG. In particular, we compare the nearest-neighbor spacing distributions with theoretical predictions and examine the length spectra, whose dominant peaks are shown to correspond to periodic orbits of the graphs.
Finally we demonstrate direct imaging of optical wavefunctions using THG.

Experiments.

We first present the design and fabrication of the silicon photonic waveguide network, followed by the experimental setup used for optical characterization.

The network consists of rib waveguides interconnected at bidirectional couplers to form closed resonant structures, as illustrated in Fig.
1

incommensurate

While the internal network forms a closed graph, the inclusion of bus waveguide for optical injection and collection renders the system open.

The devices were fabricated on a 220 nm-thick silicon layer on a 2 µm-thick silica layer.
The patterns were defined using hydrogen silsesquioxane (HSQ) resist and electron-beam lithography (EBPG5200), followed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP-RIE) etching using an SPTS system. The resulting rib waveguides have a total height of

The coupler parameters were optimized following Ref.

The optical characterization setup is shown in Fig.

The transmitted light is collected by another lensed fiber at the output and directed to a photodetector (Thorlabs DET01CFC), whose signal is recorded using a digital oscilloscope (PicoScope 4227, 100 MHz bandwidth).

Wave Model.
We now introduce the theoretical framework used to model our waveguide networks.
The formalism originates from the theory of wave graphs, which provides a general description
for wave propagation on networks. Although initially developed in the context of quantum mechanics, this approach applies equally well to classical wave systems, such as the optical waveguide networks studied in this work.

Wavefunction Imaging.
A central advantage of our silicon platform is the possibility to directly image the optical wavefunction inside the graph via third-harmonic generation (THG): continuous-wave excitation around 1550 nm produces visible emission near 515 nm, which is not guided by the waveguides and radiates out of plane.
The emitted green light is collected from above with a high-numerical-aperture microscope objective and recorded on a visible (EM)CCD camera (Andor iXon Ultra 897).
An erbium-doped fiber amplifier provides an injected power of 17 dBm to generate a detectable THG signal.

Because the THG wavelength is shorter than the infrared excitation wavelength in the material (
\lambda_{\mathrm{IR}}/n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 640
nm), this approach enables sub-wavelength spatial resolution while remaining minimally invasive.
Efficient collection of the radiated THG requires a numerical aperture larger than
n_{\mathrm{eff}}/3\simeq 0.8
, which constrains the minimal magnification.
Due to the resulting limited field of view, the full device image is reconstructed by merging six partially overlapping frames.

Figure
5
shows three experimental resonant modes of the BTG at nearby wavelengths.
The spatial intensity distributions differ markedly from one resonance to another, directly revealing the mode-to-mode fluctuations expected in complex wave systems.
These measurements provide access to spatial observables that are typically inaccessible in other experimental realizations of wave graphs.

Beyond visualization, the images enable quantitative analysis of wavefunction localization.
From the measured intensity distributions, we extract a normalized entropy quantifier (see App.
E
), which is equal to 0 for complete localization on a single bond and 1 for perfectly uniform delocalization.
Averaged over all measured modes, we obtain
0.93\pm 0.02
experimentally, in good agreement with
0.90\pm 0.04
, which is inferred from the eigenfunctions of the closed-graph model.
These results are consistent with the tendency towards quantum ergodicity in mixing graphs
2
.
A systematic statistical study of localization will be presented elsewhere.

The magnified view in Fig.
5
(e) resolves the spatial modulation of the standing wave along a single bond. The effective index can be extracted from the measured fringe spacing. At
\lambda=1530.5~\mathrm{nm}
, we obtain
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=2.410\pm 0.014
, determined from the spatial Fourier transform of the standing-wave pattern in excellent agreement with simulations (see App.
F
).

These results establish nonlinear imaging as a powerful and quantitative probe of wave dynamics in integrated wave graphs, providing direct access not only to spectral statistics, but also to the spatial structure of individual eigenmodes.

Conclusion.
We have realized integrated silicon photonic waveguide networks as an experimental platform for wave graphs and quantum chaos.
By comparing two five-vertex graphs with distinct classical dynamics, we establish a direct link between mixing properties and spectral universality: the Bow-Tie graph roughly follows GOE statistics, whereas the non-mixing Flower graph exhibits clear deviations.
The excellent agreement with the quantum-map description shows that a minimal unitary graph model captures the essential physics of these photonic networks.

A central feature of our platform is the direct imaging of optical wavefunctions via third-harmonic generation, which provides spatial information beyond spectral observables.
This capability enables systematic studies of wavefunction localization in complex photonic graphs and, from a technological perspective, broadband measurements of the effective index, dispersion, and propagation anisotropies directly on chip.

Operating at room temperature, fully compatible with CMOS fabrication, and scalable to large networks at telecom wavelengths, silicon photonic wave graphs offer a versatile platform to explore larger graph topologies and investigate universality, symmetry breaking, and nonlinear wave dynamics in complex systems.

Appendix C
Model of Open Graphs

For measurements, the closed network must be coupled to an external (bus) waveguide.
This is achieved by attaching semi-infinite leads (ports) to a selected vertex of the graph, thereby turning the system into an open scattering network. Using the port numbering of the inset of Fig.
9
, the network is connected to ports 2 and 4 of the input-output coupler, while the external leads are connected to ports 1 and 3.

As in the closed case, the field on each internal bond is written as a superposition of counter-propagating waves, and vertex scattering is described by local scattering matrices
\sigma
.
The only modification is that the input-output coupler now connects internal bonds
b
to external leads
\beta
(
\beta=1
or 2).
Following the standard formalism of open wave graphs
4
, the transmission matrix
\mathcal{T}
is a 2
\times
2 matrix and relates incoming to outgoing amplitudes on the external leads,

\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\bm{\beta}}=\mathcal{T}\,\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\bm{\beta}},

(11)

Similar to the closed-graph operator, the internal operator of the open graph
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{B}
reads

\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{B}(k)=P(k)\,\tilde{\Sigma}

(12)

where
\tilde{\Sigma}
takes into account the input-output coupler.
Now, the matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{B}(k)
is
subunitary
, since part of the energy escapes through the external leads.
Consequently, no quantization condition exists on the real axis.
Resonances are instead defined as the complex poles of the scattering matrix, or equivalently as the eigenvalues
k_{p}
such that an invariant distribution exists on the internal bonds, or even

\det\!\big(\mathbb{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{B}(k_{p})\big)=0,\qquad\Imag(k_{p})<0.

(13)

Each resonance can be written as

k_{p}=\textrm{Re}(k_{p})-i\,\frac{\Gamma_{p}}{2},

(14)

where
\Gamma_{p}
quantifies the decay rate induced by leakage into the leads.
The imaginary part therefore determines the finite lifetime of the corresponding quasi-bound state.

We use this open-graph formalism to compute the complex resonances of the BTG.
The resulting poles are compared to the real eigenvalues of the closed graph in Fig.
10
.

Figure 10:

Comparison between the closed- and open-graph spectra of the BTG in the wavelength window 1.498–1.502
\mu
m.
Black dots denote the real eigennumbers obtained from the closed-graph model, while red open circles represent the complex poles of the open-graph scattering matrix.
For each resonance, the vertical segment connects the closed eigenvalue to its corresponding open-system pole.

As evidenced in the figure, opening the graph produces only a small imaginary shift while leaving the real part of the spectrum essentially unchanged.
In the parameter regime investigated here, the coupling to the bus waveguide acts as a weak perturbation of the internal dynamics.
The resonance positions are therefore governed primarily by the closed quantum map
\mathcal{U}_{B}(k)
, while the open formalism mainly introduces finite lifetimes. This explains why the closed-graph model is sufficient to capture the spectral statistics of the BTG reported in Fig.
3
.
The bus waveguide modifies the linewidths but does not significantly alter the underlying level fluctuations.

D.1
Weyl Law and spectral unfolding

The statistical analysis of spectral fluctuations requires separating the
smooth part of the density of states from its universal fluctuations. For
wave graphs, the average number of resonances below a wave number
k

is given by the Weyl law (Sec. 5.1 of
17
):

N_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(k)=\frac{n_{g}L_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\pi}\,k,

(15)

where
L_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{b}L_{b}
is the total geometric length of the graph
and
n_{g}
is the group index of the waveguides (potentially dispersive). Actually in our photonic platform,
the propagation constant along each bond is
n_{\mathrm{eff}}k
while
the spectral density is governed by the group index
n_{g}
.

To compare spectral fluctuations with universal predictions of
random-matrix theory (RMT), the measured resonance sequence
\{k_{p}\}
is

unfolded
by removing the smooth density of states using the Weyl
law,

\tilde{k}_{p}=N_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(k_{p}).

(16)

This mapping produces a dimensionless spectrum with unit mean spacing,
allowing direct comparison with RMT statistics, in particular the NNSD presented in Fig.
3
.

Appendix E
Quantifier of wave localization

To characterize the spatial localization of the measured optical modes,
we introduce two complementary quantifiers: a normalized Shannon entropy
and the inverse participation ratio (IPR).

E.1
Normalized Shannon entropy

On each bond
b
of the graph, the optical field is modeled as a
superposition of counter-propagating plane waves, as described in Eq. (
1
). The modal intensity along a given bond is therefore uniform on average, so that each bond can be characterized by a single intensity value.

Experimentally, the bond intensities are extracted from the near-field
images of the resonant modes, see Fig.
5
.
Since the measured third-harmonic generation (THG) signal scales as

I_{\mathrm{THG}}\propto I^{3}
, the fundamental intensity in each bond
is obtained by taking the cubic root of the recorded signal before
spatial integration along the bond.

Denoting by
I_{b}
the resulting intensity associated with bond
b
,
we define a discrete probability distribution over the
B
bonds as

p_{b}=\frac{I_{b}}{\sum_{b^{\prime}=1}^{B}I_{b^{\prime}}},\qquad\sum_{b=1}^{B}p_{b}=1.

(17)

The Shannon entropy of the mode is then

S=-\sum_{b=1}^{B}p_{b}\ln p_{b},

(18)

and the normalized entropy is defined as

S_{\mathrm{norm}}=\frac{S}{\ln B},

(19)

such that
0\leq S_{\mathrm{norm}}\leq 1
.

A value
S_{\mathrm{norm}}\approx 1
corresponds to a mode evenly
distributed over all bonds, while
S_{\mathrm{norm}}\ll 1
indicates
strong localization on a small subset of bonds.

E.2
Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR)

As an alternative and widely used localization measure, we compute the
inverse participation ratio (IPR), defined as
21

\mathrm{IPR}=\sum_{b=1}^{N}p_{b}^{2}.

(20)

For a perfectly delocalized state (
p_{b}=1/N
), the inverse participation ratio is
\mathrm{IPR}=1/N
,
whereas for a fully localized state concentrated on a single bond
b_{0}
(
p_{b_{0}}=1
, and 0 elsewhere),
\mathrm{IPR}=1
. Thus, the IPR increases with increasing localization.
