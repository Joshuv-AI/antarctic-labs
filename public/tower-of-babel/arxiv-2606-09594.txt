Constraint residuals, graph posteriors, and determinant-corrected full-space targets in Bayesian inverse problems
Jonathon Cottom, Emilia Olsson

Abstract
Bayesian inverse problems constrained by state equations are often sampled in a full parameter-state space by penalising the residual, rather than in a reduced space where the state is eliminated. We show that these formulations are not automatically equivalent as posterior measures. For finite-dimensional discretisations of equality-constrained inverse problems, assume the state equation c ⁡ ( θ , u ) = 0 c(\theta,u)=0 has a unique solution u = G ⁡ ( θ ) u=G(\theta) and nonsingular state Jacobian D u ​ c \mathrm{D}_{u}c . The reduced posterior, its graph lift, and the zero-noise residual posterior are then distinct. A local change of variables shows that an uncorrected Gaussian residual penalty converges, after marginalisation over u u , to the reduced density multiplied by | det D u ​ c ​ ( θ , G ⁡ ( θ ) ) | − 1 \left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1} . Thus algebraically equivalent residuals can define the same feasible set but different limiting posteriors. We derive determinant corrections for unweighted, weighted, and rescaled residual penalties that have the graph-lifted reduced posterior as their hard-constraint limit. The result separates feasibility from posterior calibration: driving the residual to zero is not sufficient for exact sampling of the graph-lifted reduced posterior unless the sampling or correction step targets the corresponding corrected density.



Keywords:
Bayesian inverse problems; constrained posterior; augmented Lagrangian; ADMM; coarea formula; manifold MCMC; sequential Monte Carlo; Stein variational gradient descent.

Introduction

Many inverse problems in the physical sciences are constrained by a model equation. After discretisation, a typical problem has an unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, a state variable
u\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
, observations
y
, and a state equation

c(\theta,u)=0.

(1)

Examples include elliptic and parabolic coefficient inverse problems, electromagnetic and acoustic inverse scattering, full waveform inversion, inverse transport, and kinetic or rate-equation models
[
60
,
61
,
65
]
. In the standard reduced Bayesian formulation, one solves (
1
) for
u=G(\theta)
and samples

\pi_{\rm red}(\theta\,|\,y)\propto\pi_{0}(\theta)L(y\,|\,\theta,G(\theta)),

(2)

where
\pi_{0}
is the prior density and
L
is the likelihood. This reduced formulation is conceptually clean and aligns with the classical Bayesian inverse-problem framework
[
41
,
59
,
13
,
21
]
, but it can be difficult to sample when each likelihood evaluation requires a full PDE solve or when the inverse problem is highly ill conditioned
[
9
,
28
,
47
,
55
,
20
,
19
]
.

Full-space and dual-space formulations introduce
u
as an inference variable and enforce (
1
) by a penalty, an augmented Lagrangian, a splitting scheme, or a constrained dynamics. These methods are natural from the point of view of deterministic all-at-once and PDE-constrained optimisation
[
32
,
37
,
7
,
3
,
63
]
, and they can improve conditioning by relaxing the state equation while the algorithm is far from a feasible solution. They are also increasingly used as building blocks for uncertainty quantification: for example, function-space, Riemannian, and manifold MCMC methods exploit PDE-induced geometry
[
10
,
18
,
6
,
34
,
43
,
11
,
30
,
4
,
22
,

,
69
,
45
,
31
]
, ADMM-type splitting has been developed for MCMC
[
24
,
8
,
54
,
66
]
, and particle variational methods such as Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD) have been coupled with constrained or augmented-Lagrangian updates
[
46
,
39
,
58
,
1
]
.

This paper concerns a target-measure issue that is independent of any single algorithm. Driving the residual
c(\theta,u)
to zero is necessary for a hard-constraint limit, but it is not by itself enough to determine a Bayesian posterior measure. The feasible set

\Gamma=\{(\theta,u):c(\theta,u)=0\}

is unchanged if one replaces
c
by
A(\theta)c
, where
A(\theta)
is any invertible matrix. Such residual transformations are optimisation-equivalent: they define the same feasible points. They are not automatically posterior-equivalent, because a residual penalty also specifies a reference volume in the residual coordinates. A full-space Bayesian formulation must therefore answer a modelling question before it answers an algorithmic one: should the target be the reduced posterior lifted to the graph
u=G(\theta)
, the zero-noise posterior for an observed residual, or another constrained measure?

The paper distinguishes three objects that are often conflated. The first is the reduced posterior on
\theta
, obtained by solving the state equation and evaluating the likelihood at
u=G(\theta)
. The second is the graph-lifted reduced posterior on
\Gamma
, which is the push-forward of the reduced posterior under
\theta\mapsto(\theta,G(\theta))
. The third is the zero-noise residual posterior obtained by placing a small-noise likelihood on the residual coordinates
c(\theta,u)
in the ambient full space. These measures live on the same feasible set in the hard-constraint limit, but they need not induce the same distribution over
\theta
.

The determinant factor derived below is not presented as a new coarea formula. The coarea formula, disintegration, and finite-dimensional change-of-variables identities are classical. The contribution is to show that this classical volume factor changes the limiting posterior targeted by common full-space residual-penalty formulations in Bayesian inverse problems, and to turn that observation into corrected targets, weighted-residual formulas, and reproducible diagnostics.

We do not argue against augmented-Lagrangian, splitting, ADMM, SMC, MCMC, or particle variational methods. On the contrary, these methods are useful once the target measure is specified. They can provide proposal geometry, preconditioning, tempering paths, initialisation strategies, and variational approximations. Posterior correctness, however, comes from an invariant transition, a valid sequential correction, or a clearly declared variational approximation to a specified target. Residual convergence and posterior calibration must therefore be monitored separately.

The main result is simple. Suppose that
c(\theta,u)=0
has a unique solution
u=G(\theta)
and that
\mathrm{D}_{u}c
is nonsingular. Let

r(\theta,u)=\pi_{0}(\theta)L(y\,|\,\theta,u).

(3)

Then the
\theta
-marginal of the naive penalty posterior

\pi_{\rho}(\theta,u)\propto r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]

(4)

converges, as
\rho\to\infty
, to a density proportional to

r(\theta,G(\theta))\,\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1}.

(5)

This is generally not the reduced posterior. To recover the graph-lifted reduced posterior from a full-space penalty, one should instead use

\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\theta,u)\propto r(\theta,u)\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(6)

The determinant correction in (
6
) is the finite-dimensional Jacobian needed to make the full-space residual penalty converge to the same parameter posterior as the reduced formulation.

The constructive correction is accompanied by a sampler-agnostic software package,
detcorr
, which evaluates the determinant term, assembles corrected finite-penalty log densities, and emits diagnostics that distinguish constraint feasibility from posterior calibration. The package is not needed for the mathematical statements below, but it makes the residual-scaling tests and the benchmark in Section
6
directly reproducible.

Contributions

The paper makes four contributions.

1.

We identify a target-measure ambiguity in full-space Bayesian inverse-problem penalties by distinguishing the reduced posterior, its graph lift to the feasible manifold, and the zero-noise residual posterior induced by a penalty on
c
.

2.

We prove a finite-dimensional penalty-limit theorem, under explicit residual-coordinate, tail, domination, and normalisation assumptions, showing how the naive penalty posterior differs from the graph-lifted reduced posterior by a state-Jacobian volume factor.

3.

We derive corrected full-space targets for the graph-lifted reduced posterior, including the weighted-residual correction, and distinguish hard-constraint invariance from exact finite-
\rho
invariance under consistently transformed residual weights.

4.

We give sampler-agnostic SMC, MCMC, manifold, and particle-method templates in which augmented-Lagrangian and ADMM geometry is used as proposal, preconditioning, tempering, initialisation, or variational machinery, and we validate the residual-scaling invariance and elliptic benchmark through code-generated diagnostics.

The results are stated for finite-dimensional discretisations. This is the natural level at which many computational algorithms are implemented. Infinite-dimensional versions require an additional choice of reference measure and a careful treatment of determinants, discretisation limits, and possible singularity of the state map; these issues are discussed in Section
7
.

2
Reduced, graph-lifted, and residual posteriors

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
be open sets. We assume that
\theta
is the inverse parameter and
u
is the state. The data likelihood is represented by a nonnegative density
L(y\,|\,\theta,u)
, and the prior density of
\theta
is
\pi_{0}(\theta)
. Define

r(\theta,u)=\pi_{0}(\theta)L(y\,|\,\theta,u).

Throughout this finite-dimensional full-space construction, the ambient reference measure is
\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\,\mathrm{d}u
. Thus
r
is the base density with respect to
\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\,\mathrm{d}u
, with no additional state reference density unless one is explicitly included in
r
. If a non-flat state reference density is intended, it should be absorbed into
r(\theta,u)
; the determinant statements below then apply to that enlarged base density.

Assumption 1

(Well-posed state equation)
.

For every
\theta\in\Theta
, the equation
c(\theta,u)=0
has a unique solution
u=G(\theta)
. The map
c:\Theta\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
is continuously differentiable,
\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))
is nonsingular for all
\theta
, and
G
is continuously differentiable.

This is the regular single-branch setting supplied by the implicit function theorem and the submanifold calculus used below
[
44
]
. The single-branch assumption is used only to avoid an additional modelling choice over branches. If finitely many nonsingular branches are present, the same local calculation gives a branch sum; this case is recorded in Appendix
C
.

Under Assumption
1
, the reduced posterior is

\pi_{\rm red}(\theta\,|\,y)=\frac{r(\theta,G(\theta))}{Z_{\rm red}},\qquad Z_{\rm red}=\int_{\Theta}r(\theta,G(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(7)

The corresponding graph-lifted posterior is the push-forward of
\pi_{\rm red}(\theta\,|\,y)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
under the map

\Phi(\theta)=(\theta,G(\theta)).

(8)

Equivalently, for any bounded test function
\varphi
,

\int_{\Gamma}\varphi(\theta,u)\,\pi_{\Gamma}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\,\mathrm{d}u)=\int_{\Theta}\varphi(\theta,G(\theta))\,\pi_{\rm red}(\theta\,|\,y)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(9)

This is the full-space posterior that exactly represents the reduced Bayesian inverse problem.

The same measure can be written, using standard surface-measure and coarea notation
[
26
,
25
]
, as a density with respect to surface measure on

\Gamma=\{(\theta,u)\in\Theta\times U:c(\theta,u)=0\}.

(10)

Let
\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}
denote the Euclidean surface measure on
\Gamma
, and let

J_{c}(\theta,u)=\mathrm{D}_{(\theta,u)}c(\theta,u)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}c(\theta,u)&\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

On
\Gamma
, differentiating
c(\theta,G(\theta))=0
gives

\mathrm{D}_{\theta}c(\theta,G(\theta))+\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\mathrm{D}G(\theta)=0.

(12)

The surface element associated with the graph parametrisation is

\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}=\sqrt{\det\left(I_{p}+\mathrm{D}G(\theta)^{T}\mathrm{D}G(\theta)\right)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(13)

Using (
12
), one obtains

\sqrt{\det\left(J_{c}J_{c}^{T}\right)}=\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c\right\rvert\,\sqrt{\det\left(I_{p}+\mathrm{D}G^{T}\mathrm{D}G\right)}\qquad\text{on }\Gamma.

(14)

Hence the graph-lifted reduced posterior has surface density

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{\Gamma}}{\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}}(\theta,u)=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm red}}r(\theta,u)\frac{\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert}{\sqrt{\det\left(J_{c}(\theta,u)J_{c}(\theta,u)^{T}\right)}},\qquad(\theta,u)\in\Gamma.

(15)

Equation (
15
) should be compared with the measure obtained by formally conditioning an ambient density
r(\theta,u)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}u
on the event
c(\theta,u)=0
, a distinction closely related to conditioning by disintegration
[
15
]
. Using the coarea formula
[
26
,
25
]
, the distribution with density proportional to

r(\theta,u)\delta(c(\theta,u))

(16)

has surface density

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{\rm res}}{\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}}(\theta,u)\propto r(\theta,u)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\left(J_{c}(\theta,u)J_{c}(\theta,u)^{T}\right)}}.

(17)

This is the zero-noise residual posterior. It is a legitimate Bayesian target if the residual itself is modelled as an observed noisy quantity. It is not, in general, the graph-lifted reduced posterior. The two surface densities differ by the factor
\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c\right\rvert
. The factor
\sqrt{\det\left(J_{c}(\theta,u)J_{c}(\theta,u)^{T}\right)}^{-1}
is the classical coarea/Fixman surface-density factor for the residual-noise constrained target
[
27
,
69
,
68
]
. The graph-lifted reduced posterior differs from this residual-noise target by the additional state-Jacobian factor
\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c\right\rvert
.

Remark 1

(A modeling distinction, not a contradiction)
.

The graph posterior and the residual posterior answer different questions. The graph posterior represents the usual reduced inverse problem in which
u
is a deterministic state determined by
\theta
. The residual posterior represents a model in which the residual
c(\theta,u)
is itself observed with small noise in the ambient
(\theta,u)
-space. Neither is intrinsically wrong. The point is that they should not be interchanged without accounting for the induced reference measure.

For the theorem, write

J_{u}(\theta,u)=\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert.

The following assumption is a finite-dimensional sufficient condition for the Laplace/coarea limit used below. It replaces informal phrases such as “exponentially negligible” and “uniformly regular local coordinates” by conditions that can be checked or deliberately strengthened in examples.

Assumption 2

(Uniform residual coordinates and dominated tails)
.

Let
r:\Theta\times U\to[0,\infty)
be measurable. There exist
\delta>0
and a measurable family of neighbourhoods
N_{\theta}\subset U
, with
G(\theta)\in N_{\theta}
, such that the following conditions hold.

1.

Uniform local residual coordinates.

For every
\theta\in\Theta
, the map

u\mapsto c(\theta,u)

is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism from
N_{\theta}
onto
B_{\delta}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
Its inverse is denoted by

\psi_{\theta}:B_{\delta}(0)\to N_{\theta},\qquad\psi_{\theta}(0)=G(\theta).

The tube

\mathcal{N}=\{(\theta,u):u\in N_{\theta}\}

is measurable.

2.

Regularity of the inverse coordinates.

The map
(\theta,v)\mapsto\psi_{\theta}(v)
is measurable on

\Theta\times B_{\delta}(0)
. For almost every
\theta
,

r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))\to r(\theta,G(\theta))\quad\text{and}\quad J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))\to J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))\quad\text{as }v\to 0.

Moreover
J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))>0
for all
v\in B_{\delta}(0)
.

3.

Negligible mass outside the tube.

As
\rho\to\infty
,

\rho^{q/2}\int_{\Theta}\int_{U\setminus N_{\theta}}r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta\longrightarrow 0,

(18)

and

\rho^{q/2}\int_{\Theta}\int_{U\setminus N_{\theta}}r(\theta,u)J_{u}(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta\longrightarrow 0.

(19)

4.

Dominated convergence after residual scaling.

There exist
\rho_{0}>0
and integrable functions

H_{\rm res},H_{\rm graph}\in L^{1}(\Theta\times\mathbb{R}^{q})
such that, for all

\rho\geq\rho_{0}
,

\displaystyle r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(z/\sqrt{\rho}))J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(z/\sqrt{\rho}))^{-1}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{\left\lVert z\right\rVert<\delta\sqrt{\rho}\}}\leq H_{\rm res}(\theta,z),

(20)

\displaystyle r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(z/\sqrt{\rho}))\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{\left\lVert z\right\rVert<\delta\sqrt{\rho}\}}\leq H_{\rm graph}(\theta,z).

(21)

5.

Finite nonzero limiting normalising constants.

The limiting constants

Z_{\rm res}=\int_{\Theta}r(\theta,G(\theta))J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(22)

and

Z_{\rm red}=\int_{\Theta}r(\theta,G(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(23)

satisfy

0<Z_{\rm res}<\infty,\qquad 0<Z_{\rm red}<\infty.

Remark 2

(Compact sufficient conditions)
.

Assumption
2
is stated in dominated-tail form to avoid imposing compactness. A simpler sufficient condition is obtained when
\Theta
is compact,
r
and
J_{u}
are continuous and bounded on a uniform residual tube, the local diffeomorphisms are uniform in
\theta
, and the residual is bounded away from zero outside the tube, up to integrable tails in
u
. The theorem below uses the more explicit dominated-tail formulation because many discretised inverse problems are naturally posed on noncompact parameter or state spaces.

3
Penalty limits and the determinant correction

We now derive the limiting parameter posterior induced by a Gaussian residual penalty. For
\rho>0
, define the naive full-space penalty density

\pi_{\rho}(\theta,u)=\frac{1}{Z_{\rho}}r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(24)

The parameter marginal is

\pi^{\theta}_{\rho}(\theta)\propto\int_{U}r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u.

(25)

Theorem 1

(Penalty limit)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and suppose that the finite-
\rho
normalising constants for the densities below are finite and positive for all sufficiently large
\rho
. Let
\mu_{\rho}^{\theta}
be the
\theta
-marginal of the naive full-space penalty density

\pi_{\rho}(\theta,u)=\frac{1}{Z_{\rho}}r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(26)

Then
\mu_{\rho}^{\theta}
converges weakly, as
\rho\to\infty
, to the probability measure on
\Theta
with density

\pi^{\theta}_{\rm res}(\theta)=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm res}}r(\theta,G(\theta))J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))^{-1}=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm res}}r(\theta,G(\theta))\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1}.

(27)

Let
\widetilde{\mu}_{\rho}^{\theta}
be the
\theta
-marginal of the determinant-corrected finite-penalty density

\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\theta,u)=\frac{1}{\widetilde{Z}_{\rho}}r(\theta,u)J_{u}(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(28)

Then
\widetilde{\mu}_{\rho}^{\theta}
converges weakly to the reduced posterior

\pi_{\rm red}(\theta\,|\,y)=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm red}}r(\theta,G(\theta)).

(29)

All measures and determinants in this theorem are finite-dimensional discretisation-level objects; no infinite-dimensional determinant or mesh-refinement limit is asserted.

Proof.

It suffices to test the
\theta
-marginals against an arbitrary bounded
continuous function
h:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
. Define the unnormalised naive marginal
integral

I_{\rho}(h)=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{U}r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(30)

Split
I_{\rho}(h)=I_{\rho,{\rm tube}}(h)+I_{\rho,{\rm out}}(h)
, where the tube contribution integrates over
N_{\theta}
and the outside contribution integrates over
U\setminus N_{\theta}
. By
(
18
),

\rho^{q/2}\left\lvert I_{\rho,{\rm out}}(h)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\infty}\rho^{q/2}\int_{\Theta}\int_{U\setminus N_{\theta}}r(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta\longrightarrow 0.

On the tube, use the residual coordinate
v=c(\theta,u)
. By Assumption
2
,
u=\psi_{\theta}(v)
and

\,\mathrm{d}u=J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}v.

Therefore

\displaystyle I_{\rho,{\rm tube}}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))^{-1}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}v\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(31)

Substituting
z=\sqrt{\rho}\,v
gives the exact identity

\displaystyle\rho^{q/2}I_{\rho,{\rm tube}}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{\left\lVert z\right\rVert<\delta\sqrt{\rho}}r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(z/\sqrt{\rho}))J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(z/\sqrt{\rho}))^{-1}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}z\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(32)

For almost every
(\theta,z)
, the integrand in
(
32
) converges to

h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))^{-1}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right].

The domination condition (
20
) and boundedness of
h
justify dominated convergence. Hence

\displaystyle\rho^{q/2}I_{\rho}(h)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{q/2}\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(33)

Taking
h\equiv 1
gives

\rho^{q/2}I_{\rho}(1)\longrightarrow(2\pi)^{q/2}Z_{\rm res},

with
0<Z_{\rm res}<\infty
by Assumption
2
. Therefore

\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\,\mu_{\rho}^{\theta}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)=\frac{I_{\rho}(h)}{I_{\rho}(1)}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{Z_{\rm res}}\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))J_{u}(\theta,G(\theta))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

which proves weak convergence of the naive penalty marginal to
(
27
).

For the corrected density, define

\widetilde{I}_{\rho}(h)=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{U}r(\theta,u)J_{u}(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(34)

Again split

\widetilde{I}_{\rho}(h)=\widetilde{I}_{\rho,{\rm tube}}(h)+\widetilde{I}_{\rho,{\rm out}}(h)
. The outside contribution satisfies

\rho^{q/2}\left\lvert\widetilde{I}_{\rho,{\rm out}}(h)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\infty}\rho^{q/2}\int_{\Theta}\int_{U\setminus N_{\theta}}r(\theta,u)J_{u}(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta\longrightarrow 0

by (
19
).

On the tube, the same change of variables gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{\rho,{\rm tube}}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))J_{u}(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))^{-1}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}v\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(v))\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}v\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(35)

Thus the state-Jacobian determinant in the corrected density cancels the Jacobian of the residual-coordinate transformation. With
z=\sqrt{\rho}\,v
,

\displaystyle\rho^{q/2}\widetilde{I}_{\rho,{\rm tube}}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\int_{\left\lVert z\right\rVert<\delta\sqrt{\rho}}r(\theta,\psi_{\theta}(z/\sqrt{\rho}))\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}z\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(36)

The integrand converges pointwise almost everywhere to

h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right],

and (
21
) gives the required domination.
Consequently

\displaystyle\rho^{q/2}\widetilde{I}_{\rho}(h)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(2\pi)^{q/2}\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(37)

Taking
h\equiv 1
gives

\rho^{q/2}\widetilde{I}_{\rho}(1)\longrightarrow(2\pi)^{q/2}Z_{\rm red},

with
0<Z_{\rm red}<\infty
. Therefore

\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\,\widetilde{\mu}_{\rho}^{\theta}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)=\frac{\widetilde{I}_{\rho}(h)}{\widetilde{I}_{\rho}(1)}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{Z_{\rm red}}\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)r(\theta,G(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

which proves weak convergence to the reduced posterior
(
29
).

Corollary 1

(Equivalent residuals can give different naive limits)
.

Let
a:\Theta\to(0,\infty)
be smooth and define the scalar-rescaled residual

c_{a}(\theta,u)=a(\theta)c(\theta,u).

(38)

The feasible set
\{c_{a}=0\}
equals
\{c=0\}
. However, the naive penalty limit associated with
c_{a}
has
\theta
-density proportional to

r(\theta,G(\theta))a(\theta)^{-q}\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1}.

(39)

The determinant-corrected hard-constraint limit is invariant under this rescaling. Indeed,

J_{u,a}(\theta,u)=\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c_{a}(\theta,u)\right\rvert=a(\theta)^{q}J_{u}(\theta,u),

so applying Theorem
1
to
c_{a}
and the corrected density gives limiting
\theta
-density proportional to
r(\theta,G(\theta))
. At finite
\rho
, however, the unweighted corrected density is generally not identical to (
28
), since it is proportional to

r(\theta,u)\,a(\theta)^{q}J_{u}(\theta,u)\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}a(\theta)^{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

Exact finite-
\rho
invariance under algebraic residual transformations is obtained by transforming the residual weight together with the residual, as in Remark
4
.

Corollary 2

(Weighted residual penalties)
.

Let
R:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
be smooth, symmetric positive definite, with
\det R(\theta)>0
for all
\theta
. Consider the weighted residual penalty

\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}c(\theta,u)^{T}R(\theta)c(\theta,u)\right].

(40)

Equivalently, define the whitened residual

w(\theta,u)=R(\theta)^{1/2}c(\theta,u).

(41)

Assume that the hypotheses of Theorem
1
hold with

w
in place of
c
. On the graph
c(\theta,G(\theta))=0
,

\mathrm{D}_{u}w(\theta,G(\theta))=R(\theta)^{1/2}\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta)),

(42)

and hence

\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}w(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert=\det(R(\theta))^{1/2}\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert.

(43)

Therefore the naive weighted residual penalty converges to a
\theta
-density
proportional to

r(\theta,G(\theta))\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1}\det(R(\theta))^{-1/2}.

(44)

The graph-corrected weighted finite-penalty target has log-density addend

\log\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert+\frac{1}{2}\log\det R(\theta)-\frac{\rho}{2}c(\theta,u)^{T}R(\theta)c(\theta,u),

(45)

in addition to the user’s prior and likelihood log density.
Singular or indefinite weights are outside this corollary.

Remark 3

(Unnormalised penalties versus normalised residual likelihoods)
.

Corollary
2
treats

\exp\!\left[-\frac{\rho}{2}c(\theta,u)^{T}R(\theta)c(\theta,u)\right]

as an unnormalised penalty kernel. If the residual term is instead specified as a normalised Gaussian likelihood with precision
\rho R(\theta)
, then the likelihood normalisation already contains the factor
\det R(\theta)^{1/2}
, up to constants independent of
\theta
. In that case the
\frac{1}{2}\log\det R(\theta)
term should not be added a second time.

Proof.

Since
R(\theta)
does not depend on
u
, differentiating
w(\theta,u)=R(\theta)^{1/2}c(\theta,u)
with respect to
u
gives (
42
). Taking absolute determinants gives (
43
). Applying Theorem
1
to the residual coordinate
w
gives the naive limit

r(\theta,G(\theta))\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}w(\theta,G(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1},

which is exactly (
44
). The corrected target for the whitened residual multiplies the weighted penalty by
\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}w\right\rvert
. Using (
43
) yields the log addend (
45
).
∎

Remark 4

(Residual-rescaling invariance)
.

Let
A(\theta)
be a smooth invertible
q\times q
matrix and define

c_{A}(\theta,u)=A(\theta)c(\theta,u),\qquad R_{A}(\theta)=A(\theta)^{-T}A(\theta)^{-1}.

Then

c_{A}(\theta,u)^{T}R_{A}(\theta)c_{A}(\theta,u)=c(\theta,u)^{T}c(\theta,u),

(46)

and

\mathrm{D}_{u}c_{A}(\theta,u)=A(\theta)\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u).

Moreover

\det R_{A}(\theta)=\left\lvert\det A(\theta)\right\rvert^{-2},

so the combined graph correction satisfies

\displaystyle\log\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c_{A}(\theta,u)\right\rvert+\frac{1}{2}\log\det R_{A}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\log\left\lvert\det A(\theta)\right\rvert+\log\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert-\log\left\lvert\det A(\theta)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\log\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert.

(47)

Thus the graph-corrected weighted target is invariant under this algebraic residual transformation. This is the mathematical statement implemented by the weighted-residual helper described in Appendix
B
.

Remark 5

(State-equation determinants)
.

For a linear state equation
c(\theta,u)=A(\theta)u-f(\theta)
, the determinant in Theorem
1
is
\left\lvert\det A(\theta)\right\rvert
. Thus the naive penalty limit includes
\left\lvert\det A(\theta)\right\rvert^{-1}
in the parameter posterior. In large discretised PDEs this term may be expensive and mesh-dependent, but its possible computational cost does not make it mathematically absent. It either belongs to the model, or it must be cancelled by choosing a graph-corrected target or by sampling directly in the reduced parameter space.

4
Sampling templates after target specification

The previous sections specify the finite-dimensional target measures. We now describe how augmented-Lagrangian and splitting ideas can be used without turning residual reduction into an implicit posterior definition. The optimisation primitives are classical in multiplier, augmented-Lagrangian, Douglas–Rachford/ADMM, proximal, and primal-dual methods
[
36
,
56
,
24
,
8
,
54
,
14
]
. Here they are used only as proposal geometry, preconditioning, tempering, initialisation, or variational transport. Posterior sampling validity is supplied by an invariant Markov transition, a valid SMC correction, a Metropolis correction on a manifold, or another mechanism that preserves the specified target
[
52
,
16
,
51
,
5
]
. The templates below are therefore not claims that augmented-Lagrangian residual updates alone sample the posterior.

4.1
A corrected augmented-Lagrangian path

For fixed penalty
\rho>0
and multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, define an
unnormalised determinant-corrected augmented-Lagrangian density

\gamma_{\rho,\lambda}(\theta,u)=r(\theta,u)\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert\exp\!\left[-\lambda^{T}c(\theta,u)-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(48)

For fixed
(\rho,\lambda)
, this is an ordinary density on
\Theta\times U
. If
\lambda
is fixed as
\rho\to\infty
, the term
-\lambda^{T}c
shifts the centre of the finite-
\rho
residual tube but does not change the leading graph-limit marginal in
\theta
. In residual coordinates
v=c(\theta,u)
,

-\lambda^{T}v-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}=-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert v+\lambda/\rho\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert^{2}}{2\rho},

so the shift is
O(\rho^{-1})
and the remaining Gaussian factor is
independent of
\theta
. The same conclusion holds for multiplier schedules

\lambda=\lambda_{\rho}
with
\left\lVert\lambda_{\rho}\right\rVert/\rho\to 0
, provided the
completed-square factor remains independent of
\theta
. If multipliers grow
too rapidly with
\rho
, or if they are made
\theta
-dependent, the
graph-limit marginal may change and must be analysed as part of the declared
target. Thus (
48
) can be used as an intermediate density
in a tempering or SMC path, or as the invariant density of a Markov kernel at a
given stage. If
(\rho,\lambda)
is adapted during a computation, the
adaptation must be accounted for by the surrounding SMC or MCMC scheme.

A generic invariant implementation is as follows.

(c)

invariant.
Augmented-Lagrangian, ADMM, Gauss–Newton, stochastic-gradient, or particle

This distinction is essential: an augmented-Lagrangian or ADMM step may make an excellent proposal, preconditioner, mutation, or warm start, but posterior correctness is a property of the invariant transition and its declared target density.

4.2
Direct manifold sampling

An alternative, related to constrained geometric integration and manifold Monte
Carlo

[

,
69
,

,

,

,

]
,
is to sample directly on the constraint manifold

\Gamma=\{(\theta,u):c(\theta,u)=0\}.

(50)

The graph-lifted reduced posterior has surface density
(
15
). A constrained HMC or RATTLE-type method
introduces momentum
p
in the tangent space

T_{(\theta,u)}\Gamma=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p+q}:J_{c}(\theta,u)v=0\},

(51)

simulates Hamiltonian dynamics constrained to
\Gamma

Augmented-Lagrangian or ADMM curvature can still be used to choose the mass matrix or local preconditioner. The projection and acceptance steps are what preserve the desired measure; reducing the residual alone is not a substitute for this correction.

4.3
Where particle variational methods fit

Particle variational methods, including SVGD and recent dual augmented-Lagrangian variants for constrained inverse problems
[
46
,
58
,
1
]
, can be useful in this framework. They can approximate a finite-penalty density, construct a transport proposal, initialise an SMC population, or identify a low-dimensional likelihood-informed subspace. The distinction is terminological and mathematical. A deterministic KL-descent particle flow is a variational approximation to a target density. It becomes an exact or asymptotically exact posterior sampling component only when embedded in a procedure with the appropriate invariant measure or sequential correction, such as a Metropolis correction, a valid SMC sampler, or another exact transition. The determinant correction is orthogonal to the choice of particle or Markov method: it specifies which finite-dimensional target density the method should approximate or preserve.

5
Analytic example: same feasible set, different posterior

The determinant effect is already visible in a scalar nonlinear inverse problem. Let

\theta\sim N(0,1),\qquad y\,|\,u\sim N(u,\sigma_{y}^{2}),\qquad u=\theta^{2}.

(52)

The reduced posterior is

\pi_{\rm red}(\theta\,|\,y)\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y-\theta^{2})^{2}}{2\sigma_{y}^{2}}\right].

(53)

Now represent the same feasible set by the residual

c_{a}(\theta,u)=a(\theta)(u-\theta^{2}),\qquad a(\theta)=\exp(\theta).

(54)

The condition
c_{a}=0
is exactly equivalent to
u=\theta^{2}
. However, Theorem
1
gives the naive penalty limit

\pi_{\rm naive}(\theta\,|\,y)\propto\exp\!\left[-\theta\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y-\theta^{2})^{2}}{2\sigma_{y}^{2}}\right].

(55)

Thus residual scaling has changed the posterior, despite leaving the physical constraint unchanged. The determinant-corrected penalty multiplies by
\left\lvert\mathrm{D}_{u}c_{a}\right\rvert=\exp(\theta)
and recovers (
53
).

7
Discussion

The theorem has several implications for Bayesian inverse problems.

First, it separates physical feasibility from posterior correctness. A sequence of samples or particles may satisfy
\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert\approx 0
while still approximating the wrong constrained measure. This is not a pathology of a particular algorithm; it is a consequence of changing variables from state coordinates to residual coordinates.

Second, it explains why residual scaling matters. In deterministic optimisation, replacing
c
by
A(\theta)c
often changes conditioning but not the set of feasible minimisers. In Bayesian computation, the same replacement changes the volume of the residual tube around
\Gamma
. Unless the target is corrected, the limiting posterior depends on this arbitrary choice.

Third, it gives a constructive role to augmented-Lagrangian and splitting methods. These methods can improve proposals, define tempering paths, and produce excellent variational ensembles. A rigorous sampling algorithm should additionally specify the invariant density at each step and include the determinant correction if the intended target is the graph-lifted reduced posterior.

Fourth, it clarifies the finite-dimensional nature of the statement. In a mesh-refined PDE inverse problem, determinants such as
\det A(m)
may diverge, vanish, or require renormalisation. Function-space Bayesian inverse problems are usually formulated with respect to carefully chosen Gaussian or Besov reference measures rather than Lebesgue measure
[
59
,
18
,
6
,
34
,
43
,
11
]
. Therefore, Theorem
1
should not be read as a complete infinite-dimensional result. Instead, it is a discretisation-level warning: the posterior induced by a residual penalty depends on the residual reference measure, and this dependence should be controlled before interpreting full-space particles as samples from a reduced posterior.

Finally, there are situations in which the residual posterior (
17
) is the desired model. For example, if
c(\theta,u)
represents a physical discrepancy or model-error observation with a specified noise law, as in calibration, ABC/model-error interpretations, or residual-noise physics-constrained generative modelling, then the determinant factor in (
27
) is part of the intended Bayesian model
[
42
,
67
,
68
]
. The correction (
28
) is needed only when the goal is to reproduce the reduced posterior (
7
) in a full-space formulation.

8
Conclusion

Full-space and augmented-Lagrangian formulations are powerful tools for constrained Bayesian inverse problems, but a hard constraint does not uniquely define a posterior measure. For finite-dimensional discretised state equations
c(\theta,u)=0
, a naive Gaussian penalty on the residual converges to a zero-noise residual posterior whose parameter marginal is reweighted by
\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c\right\rvert^{-1}
. This residual posterior is a legitimate target when the residual itself is modelled as a noisy observed quantity. It is not, in general, the reduced Bayesian posterior lifted to the constraint graph.

The graph-lifted reduced posterior is recovered as the hard-constraint limit of a full-space finite-penalty formulation by multiplying by the state-Jacobian determinant
\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c\right\rvert
, with the additional
\frac{1}{2}\log\det R
term when an unnormalised weighted residual penalty
c^{T}Rc
is used. If the residual term is instead included as a normalised Gaussian likelihood, this weight determinant belongs to that likelihood normalisation and should not be counted twice. This correction explains why algebraically equivalent constraints can be optimisation-equivalent but not automatically posterior-equivalent, and why residual-scaling diagnostics are a useful audit of the intended target measure.

The resulting hierarchy is: residual convergence gives feasibility; determinant correction specifies the graph-posterior target; invariant MCMC, valid SMC, or Metropolis-corrected manifold dynamics supply posterior sampling validity; augmented-Lagrangian, ADMM, SVGD, and other particle or splitting methods improve exploration, conditioning, initialisation, or variational approximation but do not by themselves determine the posterior measure. This separation is the main practical message. Full-space and dual-space machinery can be highly effective, provided the target measure is declared first and the sampler is then constructed to preserve or explicitly approximate that target.

Appendix A
Surface-density calculation

This appendix verifies (
15
). Let
\Phi(\theta)=(\theta,G(\theta))
. The Jacobian of
\Phi
is

\mathrm{D}\Phi(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}\\
\mathrm{D}G(\theta)\end{bmatrix},

(63)

so

\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}=\sqrt{\det\left(\mathrm{D}\Phi(\theta)^{T}\mathrm{D}\Phi(\theta)\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\sqrt{\det\left(I_{p}+\mathrm{D}G(\theta)^{T}\mathrm{D}G(\theta)\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(64)

The graph-lifted posterior is

\pi_{\Gamma}(\Phi(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm red}}r(\theta,G(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(65)

Therefore

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{\Gamma}}{\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Gamma}}(\theta,G(\theta))=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm red}}\frac{r(\theta,G(\theta))}{\sqrt{\det\left(I_{p}+\mathrm{D}G(\theta)^{T}\mathrm{D}G(\theta)\right)}}.

(66)

Since
\mathrm{D}_{\theta}c+\mathrm{D}_{u}c\mathrm{D}G=0
on
\Gamma
,

J_{c}J_{c}^{T}=\mathrm{D}_{\theta}c\mathrm{D}_{\theta}c^{T}+\mathrm{D}_{u}c\mathrm{D}_{u}c^{T}=\mathrm{D}_{u}c\left(I_{q}+\mathrm{D}G\mathrm{D}G^{T}\right)\mathrm{D}_{u}c^{T}.

(67)

Thus

\sqrt{\det\left(J_{c}J_{c}^{T}\right)}=\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c\right\rvert\,\sqrt{\det\left(I_{q}+\mathrm{D}G\mathrm{D}G^{T}\right)}.

(68)

The nonzero eigenvalues of
\mathrm{D}G\mathrm{D}G^{T}
and
\mathrm{D}G^{T}\mathrm{D}G
coincide, so

\det(I_{q}+\mathrm{D}G\mathrm{D}G^{T})=\det(I_{p}+\mathrm{D}G^{T}\mathrm{D}G).

(69)

Substituting this identity into (
66
) gives (
15
).

Appendix B
Software interface for corrected finite-penalty targets

This appendix records the minimal
detcorr
interface used by the validation benchmarks. The package does not choose a posterior measure and does not implement an exact sampler by itself. Instead, it exposes the state-Jacobian correction and packages the resulting log-density terms so that an external MCMC, SMC, manifold, or particle method can use them in its own proposal, weighting, acceptance, or approximation machinery. All sampler-facing helpers require an explicit declaration of the intended target measure.

For a residual function
c(\theta,u)
, the graph-target finite-penalty log-density contribution is

\log\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert-\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert c(\theta,u)\right\rVert^{2},

(70)

plus the user’s prior and likelihood log density. The direct helper evaluates both the residual and the determinant correction:

import detcorr

def c(theta, u):
return residual_vector(theta, u)

def base(theta, u):
return log_prior(theta) + log_likelihood(theta, u)

evaluation = detcorr.evaluate_corrected_finite_penalty_target(
c,
theta,
u,
rho=100.0,
target_measure="graph",
base_logdensity=base(theta, u),
log_correction_kwargs={"backend": "scipy", "singular_policy": "raise"},
)
logpi = evaluation.logdensity
diagnostics = evaluation.sampler_diagnostics()

When the state Jacobian is already available from a PDE code or automatic differentiation, it can be supplied explicitly:

J_u = Du_c(theta, u)
logdet = detcorr.log_correction(jacobian=J_u, method="sparse_lu")

evaluation = detcorr.corrected_finite_penalty_target(
c(theta, u),
rho=100.0,
logdet=logdet,
target_measure="graph",
base_logdensity=base(theta, u),
)

The determinant route can therefore be exact dense linear algebra, sparse LU, Cholesky for symmetric positive definite operators, a structured diagonal or triangular product, an autodiff Jacobian, or a matrix-free estimator with recorded estimator diagnostics, using stochastic trace and stochastic Lanczos ideas where exact factorization is unavailable
[
38
,
62
,
49
]
.

For a weighted residual penalty
\frac{\rho}{2}c(\theta,u)^{T}R(\theta)c(\theta,u)
, where
R(\theta)
is symmetric positive definite, the software helper implements Corollary
2
. The corresponding graph-target log-density contribution is

\log\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,u)\right\rvert+\frac{1}{2}\log\det R(\theta)-\frac{\rho}{2}c(\theta,u)^{T}R(\theta)c(\theta,u).

(71)

Equation (
71
) is therefore the software form of the main-text weighted-residual correction, not a separate derivation. The weight can be passed as
R=R(theta)
, as a Cholesky factor, as a preconditioner, or as a precomputed weight log-determinant. Singular, indefinite, or negative-determinant weights are outside Corollary
2
and should be rejected or handled by an explicitly different model.

Declaring
target_measure="graph"
includes the state-Jacobian correction, and, when a weighted residual is supplied, the
\frac{1}{2}\log\det R
volume term. Declaring
"residual_noise"
intentionally omits the graph correction and rejects an accidental state-Jacobian log determinant. This explicit declaration is meant to prevent the software from silently switching between the graph posterior and the residual-noise posterior.

The benchmark scripts use only this interface. The scalar residual-scaling test passes
c
,
A(\theta)c
, and the compensating weight
R_{A}(\theta)=A(\theta)^{-T}A(\theta)^{-1}
, verifying the invariance in Remark
4
. The elliptic benchmark passes the affine state Jacobian
A(m)
and evaluates
\log\left\lvert\det A(m)\right\rvert
by an exact SPD route. Feasibility diagnostics report residual norms and projection errors; calibration diagnostics report effective sample size, split-
\hat{R}
, and simulation-based calibration hooks. The two diagnostic types are intentionally kept separate because residual convergence is necessary for a hard-constraint limit but not sufficient for posterior calibration.

Appendix C
Multiple state solutions

If the state equation has finitely many nonsingular solutions
u=G_{j}(\theta)
,
j=1,\ldots,J(\theta)
, the same change-of-variables argument gives the naive penalty limit

\pi^{\theta}_{\rm res}(\theta)\propto\sum_{j=1}^{J(\theta)}r(\theta,G_{j}(\theta))\left\lvert\det\mathrm{D}_{u}c(\theta,G_{j}(\theta))\right\rvert^{-1}.

(72)

The determinant-corrected penalty gives

\widetilde{\pi}^{\theta}(\theta)\propto\sum_{j=1}^{J(\theta)}r(\theta,G_{j}(\theta)).

(73)

Which of these is appropriate depends on the intended model over branches. The single-solution case in the main text is the common setting for well-posed discretised state equations.
