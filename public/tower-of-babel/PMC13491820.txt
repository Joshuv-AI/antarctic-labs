An ERP-based study of the effects of music tempo on inhibitory control among male drug addicts with different levels of psychological resilience
Authors: Hui Jiang, Gaoshen Zhang, Yuting Li, Ying Wu, Xing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaired inhibitory control is a key factor in drug relapse. Music therapy shows potential for cognitive intervention, but individual differences, particularly in psychological resilience, and the mechanisms underlying tempo effects remain unclear. This study investigated the interactive effects of music tempo and psychological resilience on inhibitory control in male drug addicts, employing a Go/No-go task with drug-related cues.MethodsForty-four male drug addicts were stratified into high and low resilience groups based on the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. They performed a Go/No-go task where No-go stimuli were drug-related pictures, under four conditions: fast (160 bpm), medium (105 bpm), and slow (50 bpm) tempo music, and a no-music control. Behavioral data (response time, accuracy) and event-related potentials (ERPs; N2, P3) were recorded.ResultsBehaviorally, the high-resilience group exhibited significantly shorter response times than the low-resilience group, an effect modulated by tempo. ERP analyses revealed that the low-resilience group generated larger N2 amplitudes, suggesting greater conflict monitoring demand. Conversely, the high-resilience group exhibited larger P3 amplitudes, indicating more efficient resource allocation for later inhibitory evaluation. A critical interaction was found in the N2 difference wave (N2d): under slow-tempo music, the high-resilience group demonstrated a significantly larger N2d than the low-resilience group, implying enhanced conflict monitoring in a stabilized state. Notably, no significant group differences in N2d were observed at fast tempo, despite persistent behavioral advantages for the high-resilience group.ConclusionsPsychological resilience moderates the impact of music tempo on inhibitory control. High-resilience individuals may optimally mobilize neural resources for conflict monitoring under slow-tempo music, which potentially reduces emotional interference. These findings underscore the importance of considering individual psychological traits in developing personalized, music-based rehabilitation strategies.Keywords: Music tempo, Psychological resilience, Drug rehabilitation, Inhibitory control, Event-related potential

Introduction

Drug addiction is detrimental to both public health and social stability, representing a problem of global significance [1]. In recent years, the number of drug users worldwide has reached a historical high. According to the World Drug Report 2025, a record 316 million people used illicit drugs in 2023, accounting for 6% of the global population aged 15–64, a significant increase from 5.2% in 2013. Ineffective responses to drug addiction have incurred staggering costs: in 2021, drug use directly caused nearly 500,000 deaths globally, with an additional 28 million healthy years of life lost due to disability or premature mortality attributable to drug use. A particularly critical concern is that in 2023, only approximately one in twelve people with drug use disorders had received any form of treatment for drug dependence [2]. Therefore, the urgency of drug rehabilitation is self-evident. Long-term drug abuse adversely affects both an individual’s physical health and cognitive functions, particularly inhibitory control [3–6]. As a core component of executive functioning [7], inhibitory control is critical for suppressing relapse impulses, resisting drug-related cues, and maintaining abstinence [8]. Numerous studies have shown that relapse behavior is closely related to impaired inhibitory control and that effective enhancement of drug addicts’ inhibitory control can significantly reduce their risk of relapse [9–13].

Traditional approaches to drug treatment have predominantly emphasized physiological detoxification and behavioral modification, often overlooking the rehabilitation of cognitive functions [14]. In recent years, music therapy has emerged as a promising non-invasive, low-cost, and easily implementable cognitive intervention within the field of drug rehabilitation [15, 16]. Music influences neural activity via the auditory system and modulates neurotransmitter release, thereby contributing to cognitive improvement [17]. Moreover, music of varying tempos can activate different brain regions and elicit complex neurophysiological responses, which in turn differentially affect an individual’s mood, attention, and cognitive processing [18–21]. For instance, fast-tempo music tends to enhance brain arousal and mental processing speed [22], whereas slow-tempo music facilitates physical and mental relaxation, promotes emotional regulation, and establishes a stable psychological state conducive to cognitive activities [23]. While such effects are well-documented in healthy individuals and clinical populations such as Alzheimer’s patients, their generalizability to people with substance use disorders remains unverified. More critically, existing studies often presume uniform intervention effects across addicted populations, neglecting the impact of individual differences, particularly in psychological traits such as resilience.

Psychological resilience, defined as the capacity to maintain or restore psychological stability in adverse situations [24], offers a valuable lens through which to examine individual variability in responses to music-based interventions. Research indicates that individuals with high resilience demonstrate greater cognitive flexibility and more efficient neural resource allocation under stress or high cognitive demand [25, 26]. Neuroimaging studies further reveal stronger functional connectivity between the default mode network and the executive control network among highly resilient individuals, enabling more effective mobilization of neural resources during cognitive tasks [27, 28]. Of direct relevance to inhibitory control, individuals with higher resilience show enhanced prefrontal cortex engagement and more efficient conflict monitoring [25, 29], processes that are central to successful response inhibition. Within addiction recovery, resilience has been shown to support emotion and behavior regulation, as well as treatment adherence [30–32]. However, whether resilience interacts with external modulators such as music tempo to shape cognitive outcomes, particularly inhibitory control, remains unexplored.

A neurocognitive resource allocation framework may help theoretically integrate these constructs. Inhibitory control, especially under demanding conditions, is a resource-intensive process reliant on fronto-cortical networks [33]. EEG source localization evidence suggests that early cognitive resource allocation, which is indexed by P2 amplitude, modulates the interplay between automaticity and cognitive control during response inhibition [34]. Individuals with higher resilience consistently exhibit superior cognitive flexibility and more efficient neural resource deployment under cognitive demand [25, 26], supported by stronger network connectivity that facilitates agile resource mobilization [27, 28]. Music tempo, as an external modulator, affects arousal and emotional state: fast-tempo music may heighten arousal and potentially overwhelm limited cognitive resources, thereby impairing conflict monitoring, which is a key component of inhibitory control [35]. Slow-tempo music, by contrast, may aid emotion regulation and free up cognitive resources by fostering a stable internal state [23]. Therefore, we hypothesize that psychological resilience moderates the effect of music tempo on inhibitory control. Specifically, high-resilience individuals, with their inherent advantage in efficient resource allocation, may be better equipped to counteract the disruptive effects of fast-tempo music or capitalize on the stabilizing effects of slow-tempo music. Conversely, low-resilience individuals, with less efficient resource management, may be more susceptible to the detrimental impact of fast music and less able to benefit from slow music.

Against this background, the present study combined behavioral and event-related potential (ERP) measures to examine the interactive effects of music tempo and psychological resilience on inhibitory control in male drug users. Participants were stratified into high- and low-resilience groups and performed an inhibitory control task under four conditions: fast (160 bpm), medium (105 bpm), and slow (50 bpm) tempo music, as well as a no-music control. It is crucial to note that the No-go stimuli in our Go/No-go task were drug-related pictures. This design intentionally simulates a core challenge in rehabilitation, exerting inhibitory control in the presence of cues that are emotionally salient and triggers of craving. This allows us to examine inhibitory function in an ecologically relevant context, but it also implies that the recorded neural activity likely reflects a confluence of emotional responses to the cues (craving) and the inhibitory process itself. The study aims to elucidate how these factors interact at both behavioral and neural levels, thereby informing the development of personalized music intervention strategies in drug rehabilitation based on individual psychological traits.

Methods

Participants

A total of 100 male drug addicts were recruited in a drug rehabilitation center. Inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 to 65 years; (2) meeting the diagnostic criteria of DSM-V drug addiction; (3) having been admitted to the center for at least two weeks; and (4) elementary school education or above. Exclusion criteria: (1) having serious head injury and neurological disease; and (2) use of any medications known to affect the nervous system within the preceding two weeks. Participants were further screened using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC). The top and bottom 23% of scorers were assigned to the high- and low-resilience groups, respectively. During subsequent data processing, two participants were excluded due to excessive artifacts (> 25% of trials) in their ERP data. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 22 participants in the low psychological resilience group and 22 in the high psychological resilience group.

The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Shanxi Medical University (2025GLL021) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Psychological resilience scale

The psychological resilience scale (CD-RISC) was developed by Connor et al. [36], and consists of 25 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘never’) to 4 (‘almost always’). The total score is calculated by summing all items, yielding a range from 0 to 100. Higher total scores indicate greater levels of psychological resilience.

Musical and picture material

The musical stimuli were derived from three piano pieces by Richard Clayderman: “Mariage d’amour,” “Murmures,” and “Greensleeves.” Their original tempos were 166 bpm, 95 bpm, and 77 bpm, respectively. Following the tempo classification criteria established by Fernández-Sotos et al. [37] (where fast tempo is ≥ 120 bpm, medium tempo is 76–120 bpm, and slow tempo is ≤ 76 bpm), we selected target tempos of 160 bpm (fast), 105 bpm (medium), and 50 bpm (slow) for this study, creating a consistent tempo difference of 55 bpm between conditions. Using Adobe Audition 2022, the tempo of each piece was modified to match these target values. Consequently, “Mariage d’amour” was adjusted to 160 bpm, “Murmures” to 105 bpm, and “Greensleeves” to 50 bpm. All musical excerpts were then edited to the same duration. Additionally, silent control clips of identical length were generated to serve as the no-music condition for assessing baseline inhibitory control. The presentation loudness was standardized at an initial 70 dB SPL [35]. However, prior to the formal experiment, participants were allowed to adjust the volume to a comfortable level once, which was then maintained throughout all experimental trials.

The picture stimuli consisted of 4 drug-related images and 16 neutral images. Drug-related cues illustrated scenarios of drug use (e.g., syringe injection). In contrast, neutral cues depicted emotionally neutral everyday objects (e.g., timber, stones). All images were edited and matched using Adobe Photoshop 2020 for size, color and resolution to control for potential confounding effects.

Experimental procedures

This study used the Go/No-go paradigm as a three-factor mixed design of 2 (psychological resilience: high vs. low) * 2 (stimulus type: Go vs. No-go) * 4 (music tempo: 160 bpm, 105 bpm, 50 bpm, no music). Psychological resilience was a between-subjects variable, while stimulus type and music tempo were within-subjects variables. The dependent variables were behavioral data (response time and accuracy) and ERP data (the mean amplitudes of N2, P3, N2d and P3d; N2d and P3d are difference waves derived by subtracting Go-trial from No-go-trial waveforms for the N2 and P3 time windows, respectively).

The experimental program was prepared using E-Prime 2.0 and was divided into a practice phase and a formal experimental phase. The formal experimental phase comprised four blocks corresponding to the four tempo conditions: 160 bpm, 105 bpm, 50 bpm, and no music. The presentation order of these blocks was counterbalanced across participants. Each block contained 150 trials with a Go/No-go trial ratio of 4:1 (120 Go trials and 30 No-go trials). During Go trials, a neutral picture was presented, and participants were required to press a key with their right index finger as quickly as possible. During No-go trials, a drug-related picture was presented, and participants were instructed to withhold their response. The specific experimental flow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Experimental flow diagram of Go/No-go task

To control for individual differences in music perception, participants rated the music stimulus for each block after its completion. Ratings were made on a 9-point scale for the dimensions of pleasantness, arousal, and familiarity.

EEG recording and preprocessing

Electroencephalogram (EEG) signals were recorded using an iRecorder-W system with a 32-channel electrode cap arranged according to the international 10–20 system. The signals were acquired at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Throughout the recording, electrode impedances were maintained below 10 kΩ to ensure signal quality and stability.

The EEG data were preprocessed using EEGLAB 2024.0. The signals were referenced to the bilateral mastoids and band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 30 Hz. The clean_rawdata plugin was employed for bad channel detection, with the following criteria: flatline criterion (10), channel correlation criterion (0.6), and linear noise criterion (6). Identified bad channels were reconstructed using spherical interpolation. The continuous data were segmented into epochs spanning from 200 ms before stimulus onset to 1000 ms after onset, and baseline correction was applied relative to the − 200 ms to 0 ms pre-stimulus interval. Artifact detection was performed on specific electrode sites (Fz, F3, F4, Pz, P3, P4), and trials with amplitudes exceeding ± 150 µV were excluded. Independent component analysis (ICA) was conducted using the extended runica algorithm. Component classification was carried out with ICLabel, and components identified as ocular or muscle artifacts with a probability greater than 0.8 were removed.

ERP analysis

ERP analyses focused on data from the Go/No-go task for the two resilience groups across the four music tempo conditions (fast, medium, slow, no music). Electrode sites and time windows were determined a priori based on established findings in the inhibitory control literature [38] and visual inspection of the grand-average waveforms. The N2 component, which is maximal over frontal-central sites, was analyzed at Fz, F3, and F4 within the 250–350 ms post-stimulus window, consistent with its typical time course. The P3 component, maximal over parietal sites, was analyzed at Pz, P3, and P4 within the 300–500 ms window, consistent with its characteristic time course for inhibitory processing. For statistical analysis, amplitudes were averaged across the specified electrode clusters (Fz/F3/F4 for N2; Pz/P3/P4 for P3). For each participant and condition, difference waveforms were computed by subtracting the Go-trial ERP from the No-go-trial ERP. The N2d and P3d components were then quantified as the mean amplitude of the difference wave within the 250–350 ms and 300–500 ms windows, respectively. ERP waveforms at Fz and Pz were plotted to visualize the Go/No-go contrast. Topographical maps were also generated to illustrate the scalp distribution of the N2d (250–350 ms) and P3d (300–500 ms).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. For demographic and drug-related variables, categorical data were analyzed with chi-square tests and are presented as frequencies (percentages). Continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t-tests and are reported as means (standard deviations). A three-way mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on accuracy, response time, and the mean amplitudes of the N2 and P3 components. The model examined the main effects of music tempo, psychological resilience group, stimulus type, and their interactions. In addition, a two-way mixed-design ANOVA was employed to examine the main effects of music tempo and psychological resilience group on the difference wave components (N2d and P3d), as well as their interaction. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. For any significant interaction effects, post hoc tests were performed using the Bonferroni correction.

ERP results

For the N2 component, the mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of psychological resilience (F(1, 42) = 4.475, p = 0.040, ηp2 = 0.096), with the low-resilience group exhibiting larger (more negative) N2 amplitudes than the high-resilience group (Fig. 2). A significant interaction was found between music tempo and resilience (F(3, 126) = 5.694, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.119), as well as a significant three-way interaction among tempo, resilience, and stimulus type (F(3, 126) = 4.519, p = 0.013, ηp2 = 0.097). To unpack this three-way interaction, we focused on the N2 difference wave (N2d, derived by subtracting Go-trial from No-go-trial waveforms; see topographical maps in Fig. 3), which confirmed a significant Tempo × Resilience interaction (F(3, 126) = 4.519, p = 0.013, ηp2 = 0.097). Simple effects analysis confirmed that the high-resilience group generated significantly larger N2d amplitudes than the low-resilience group specifically under the slow-tempo condition (2.394 µV vs. -1.410 µV), with no significant group differences at other tempos (Fig. 4; Table 5).

Discussion

The present study employed a Go/No-go paradigm incorporating drug-related cues and ERP measures to investigate the interactive effects of music tempo and psychological resilience on inhibitory control in male drug addicts. Our key behavioral finding is that individuals with high psychological resilience demonstrated faster response times in Go trials compared to their low-resilience counterparts, an effect that was modulated by music tempo. At the neural level, the low-resilience group exhibited significantly larger (more negative) N2 amplitudes overall, whereas the high-resilience group generated larger P3 amplitudes. Crucially, a significant interaction was observed in the N2 difference wave (N2d), with the high-resilience group showing a larger N2d amplitude specifically during the slow-tempo condition.

Interpreting these ERP findings requires consideration of the established functional significance of the N2 and P3 components within the context of our cue-elicited Go/No-go task. The N2 component is widely implicated in conflict monitoring and the initial engagement of inhibitory control [39]. A larger N2 amplitude typically signifies greater cognitive conflict and a higher demand for inhibitory resources. Conversely, the P3 component is associated with the later stage of inhibitory evaluation, context updating, and the allocation of attentional resources [40]. A larger P3 amplitude in No-go trials is often linked to successful inhibition, but this interpretation is most robust when accompanied by higher behavioral accuracy. It is critical to acknowledge that our No-go stimuli were drug-related cues, which carry inherent emotional salience and are potent elicitors of craving. Therefore, the observed N2 and P3 modulations likely reflect a confluence of processes, including cue-elicited arousal, emotional conflict, and inhibitory control, rather than pure inhibition in a neutral context.

In our study, the overall larger N2 amplitude in the low-resilience group suggests that these individuals experienced greater conflict and required more cognitive effort to monitor and initiate control over their responses, indicating less efficient neural processing at this early stage. This interpretation is further supported by the observation that No-go accuracy was consistently lower than Go accuracy across all participants, a pattern which confirms the basic task demand. Despite this, there were no significant group differences in accuracy. This pattern suggests that the low-resilience group’s neural inefficiency in conflict monitoring did not translate into overt performance deficits in this task. It is possible that both groups ultimately achieved relatively high and comparable levels of behavioral control. Conversely, the overall larger P3 amplitude in the high-resilience group points to their advantage in mobilizing attentional resources for the evaluative phase of inhibition. Critically, since behavioral accuracy did not differ between groups, this enhanced P3 amplitude in high-resilience individuals likely reflects more efficient or more confidently allocated neural resources for implementing successful inhibition. It does not appear to represent a compensatory mechanism for poor performance. The larger N2 in the low-resilience group may thus index heightened conflict generated by the salience of the drug cues, with which they struggle to cope effectively [39].

The core of our investigation was the interaction between psychological resilience and music tempo. This was most clearly demonstrated by the N2d findings under slow-tempo music. The significantly larger N2d in the high-resilience group during the slow-tempo condition suggests that this specific environment allowed them to recruit more neural resources for conflict monitoring. We theorize that the calming nature of slow-tempo music may have mitigated emotional interference and fostered a stable internal state, thereby freeing up cognitive resources that highly resilient individuals could deftly deploy towards enhanced conflict monitoring [23]. This aligns with the neurocognitive resource allocation framework, which posits that high-resilience individuals possess a superior capacity for efficient neural resource deployment [25, 26]. In contrast, the low-resilience group, with their inherently less efficient resource management, were unable to capitalize on the stabilizing potential of slow music, as reflected in their smaller N2d.

The pattern for fast-tempo music is also instructive. While both groups showed comparable N2d amplitudes, the high-resilience group maintained significantly faster behavioral response times. This dissociation suggests that high-resilience individuals may possess compensatory mechanisms that allow them to maintain behavioral efficiency even under the potentially arousing conditions of fast-tempo music, which might otherwise be expected to impair performance [22]. Their neural efficiency might be manifested in other, unmeasured networks, allowing them to behaviorally counteract the potential disruptive effects of high arousal without showing a marked change in early conflict monitoring (N2d).

Notably, there were no significant differences in the subjective ratings of musical pleasantness, arousal, and familiarity between the two groups across different tempos. This key finding suggests that the observed behavioral and neural differences are unlikely to be driven by conscious, subjective appraisals of the music itself. Instead, it strengthens the proposition that the interplay between psychological resilience and music tempo operates at a more fundamental, pre-conscious level of neurocognitive processing.

Beyond the specific effects of tempo, the overall pattern of our ERP results reveals a stage-specific processing dynamic. We interpret the non-significant P3d findings as evidence for the stage-specific nature of the interaction. Our results demonstrate a clear dissociation: the synergistic benefits of high psychological resilience and specific music conditions manifest primarily during the early, evaluative stage of conflict detection (N2) rather than the later stage of motor response inhibition (P3). We propose two complementary explanations. First, following a stage-specific processing account, the efficient detection and signaling of response conflict during the N2 time window may represent the primary locus for modulation by psychological traits and external stimuli like music. Once a conflict is effectively registered, the successful implementation of behavioral inhibition may depend more on fundamental neural circuits that are consistently impaired in addiction and thus less susceptible to such modulation [41]. Second, from a resource allocation perspective, the low-resilience group’s larger N2 amplitudes suggest they expended considerably more neural resources during the demanding early conflict monitoring stage. This initial resource expenditure could lead to a depletion that attenuates subsequent group differences in the P3 stage. This precise localization of the interactive effect to the conflict monitoring stage enhances the theoretical precision of our findings.

This stage-specific account is further corroborated by the null findings for medium-tempo music, which can be interpreted through the framework of the Yerkes-Dodson law [42]. The extreme tempos of fast and slow music likely shifted arousal levels toward more optimal zones for the high-resilience group, thereby revealing their cognitive advantages. In contrast, medium-tempo music may create an intermediate state that fails to sufficiently modulate arousal levels in a manner that would engage or differentially affect the neurocognitive systems underlying resilience. The absence of group differences in subjective ratings for medium-tempo music effectively rules out simple preference as an explanation and lends support to this neurocognitive account.

Limitations and practical implications

Our study has several limitations that warrant mention. First, the exclusive focus on male participants combined with the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings, although this design helped control for gender-related confounding variables. Future research should include larger sample sizes and explicitly recruit female participants to explore potential gender differences. Second, the reliance on a single music genre (piano pieces by a single composer) introduces the possibility that musical features beyond tempo, such as emotional valence and stylistic familiarity, may have influenced the results. Individual music preferences, which were not assessed in this study, represent another critical factor that future research should take into account and incorporate to further personalize interventions. Third, the spatial resolution of ERP is limited. Future work could combine ERP with fMRI or fNIRS to delineate the underlying neural circuits more comprehensively. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about long-term effects. Longitudinal studies tracking the impact of tempo-tailored music interventions on relapse rates are a vital next step.

Despite these limitations, our findings carry practical implications for drug rehabilitation. The demonstrated interaction between psychological resilience and music tempo suggests that a “one-size-fits-all” approach to music therapy may be suboptimal. For individuals with high psychological resilience, slow-tempo music could be strategically used to enhance cognitive control during therapy sessions, potentially by deepening their engagement in conflict monitoring. For those with lower resilience, interventions might first focus on building resilience or combining music with other cognitive training to strengthen their foundational capacity for resource allocation. Implementing such personalized strategies in rehabilitation centers may face obstacles, including the need for initial resilience assessments, staff training, and access to appropriate music technology. However, the non-invasive, low-cost, and scalable nature of music-based interventions presents a compelling case for overcoming these hurdles.

Conclusions

In conclusion, psychological resilience and music tempo interact to shape inhibitory control in male drug addicts at both behavioral and neural levels, with these effects being most salient during the early stage of conflict monitoring. Our study underscores the necessity of considering individual differences in psychological traits when designing non-pharmacological interventions for addiction. Future research should adopt a multidisciplinary approach, integrating measures of music preference, employing diverse musical stimuli, and utilizing longitudinal designs to fully unlock the potential of personalized music therapy in the journey toward sustained recovery.
