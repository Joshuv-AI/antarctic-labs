Reservoir computing-based cryptanalysis of structured phase-masked chaos encryption
Authors: Eren Tosyali, Yesim Oniz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the robustness of a chaos-based audio encryption scheme against learning-assisted cryptanalysis using Echo State Networks (ESNs). The encryption pipeline is driven by a chaotic key stream generated from the Rössler attractor. The plaintext audio is segmented into fixed-length blocks, permuted in time, scrambled in the frequency domain via a conjugate-symmetric permutation, and further obscured by a structured frequency-domain phase mask, yielding a noise-like ciphertext. A partial-leakage threat model is considered in which an eavesdropper (Eve) knows the algorithmic structure and block order, observes only a fraction of the frequency-permutation mappings, and receives noisy phase samples on a randomly selected subset of blocks (random missing leakage). Here, “random missing leakage” refers to a setting in which phase observations are available only for a randomly selected subset of blocks, while the remaining blocks contain no phase information. To exploit the structured phase model, Eve trains an ESN only on leakage-observed blocks and imputes latent phase-basis coefficients for non-observed blocks, enabling approximate decryption with the estimated permutation and reconstructed phase mask. Reconstruction quality is evaluated using intelligibility- and error-oriented metrics (STOI, SNR, CORR, and NMSE), reported both overall and separately on leakage-observed and leakage-missing segments. The results reveal a non-linear transition in adversarial recoverability as permutation leakage increases, and highlight the coupled roles of frequency-bin alignment and phase-model learnability in determining the attack effectiveness.Keywords: Cryptanalysis, Chaos encryption, Reservoir computingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The growing reliance on networked communication platforms has increased the volume and diversity of sensitive information transmitted over open channels, making confidentiality and integrity essential requirements of modern communication systems. Beyond text, multimedia streams—particularly speech and audio—are widely exchanged in instant messaging, teleconferencing, telemedicine, and other professional or mission-critical settings, where unintended disclosure can have immediate privacy and safety implications1. While conventional cryptography offers strong security foundations, multimedia payloads introduce practical constraints related to data volume, real-time latency, and strong temporal correlation, which motivate domain-aware designs emphasizing rapid confusion and diffusion at feasible computational cost2,3.

Chaos-based multimedia security remains an active research direction because nonlinear chaotic systems can generate deterministic yet irregular sequences that are frequently exploited for keystream generation, scrambling indices, and masking parameters4,5. Early and representative chaos-driven audio encryption methods commonly follow a permutation–substitution paradigm: Kordov (2019) proposed an audio cipher based on permutation and substitution operations and evaluated it through waveform/spectrogram and statistical tests6. Chaotic masking has also been combined with domain-specific mechanisms, such as DNA-based coding operations7,8, self-adaptive bit scrambling and dynamic DNA computations9, and hyperchaotic constructions of varying dimension10. More recent variants include memristor-chaos driven audio encryption11 and broader multimedia encryption/decryption frameworks that couple permutation with masking stages12. Complementary to design-oriented studies, security analysis papers have investigated encrypted-audio robustness under hybrid chaotic constructions and communication settings13.

In parallel, the literature has increasingly addressed practical constraints and deployment targets. Roy et al. (2024) combined chaos with DNA encoding for audio encryption and validated performance with statistical and perceptual indicators14. Hardware realizations have been emphasized in FPGA-oriented implementations, including a 2D chaotic-map audio encryption system with dynamic S-box design15 and a HW/SW co-design targeting real-time speech transmission in the transform domain16. At the low-cost edge, Charalampidis et al. (2024) presented a microcontroller-friendly speech encryption scheme combining hash-based shuffling and XOR diffusion driven by a 1D chaotic map17. Recent algorithmic proposals also continue to appear, such as a discrete hyperchaotic family applied to audio encryption with additional grouping/fractal operations18 and hybrid chaotic-steganographic frameworks for IoT-oriented audio transmission19. Feature-driven designs remain relevant as well; for example, Demirtas (2024) proposed sound encryption based on time-domain feature extraction and a modified Chebyshev chaotic map20. Finally, learning is increasingly intertwined with chaos-based cryptosystems themselves (e.g., ANN-assisted chaotic frameworks)21.

Although the above studies differ in transform domain, key generation, and implementation platform, many works primarily (i) propose new encryption designs and (ii) validate security through global statistical indicators (entropy, histogram uniformity, adjacent-sample correlation) and/or implementation metrics (throughput, power), often under idealized attacker assumptions6,9,12,15–17. In practice, however, adversaries may exploit partial and noisy side information (e.g., metadata exposure, incomplete parameter leakage, or side-channel-informed measurements) rather than requiring full key disclosure22,23. Moreover, chaos-based ciphers remain deterministic under a fixed key, and determinism can introduce exploitable structure when sufficient input–output relationships or partial leakage are available; cryptanalytic studies of chaotic encryption have repeatedly highlighted that apparent randomness alone does not guarantee security24,25.

Learning-assisted cryptanalysis further expands the attacker toolbox. Deep neural networks have been investigated as data-driven cryptanalytic models for learning inverse or restoration-like mappings under known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, ciphertext-only, or protected-representation settings. In optical and phase-based cryptanalysis, Hai et al. demonstrated that random-phase-encoding-based optical cryptosystems can be vulnerable to chosen-plaintext attacks using deep learning, where a trained DNN acts as an effective decryption system26. Liao et al. further proposed a deep-learning-based ciphertext-only attack on optical double random phase encryption, showing that plaintext images can be recovered from ciphertext-only observations without explicitly retrieving the random phase keys27. Wang and Wei also investigated a U-Net-based cryptanalysis framework for compressive interference-based optical encryption, demonstrating that neural restoration models can exploit structural information in encrypted optical data28. More recently, Jiang et al. introduced DDCM, a denoising diffusion cryptanalytic model for cracking anonymized facial images through a coarse-to-fine generative reconstruction framework29. These studies show that DNN-, U-Net-, and diffusion-based models can exploit residual statistical, phase-domain, or semantic structure in protected multimedia data. Reservoir computing has also been explored for cracking chaos-based cryptography in related settings30.

Unlike prior learning-based cryptanalysis approaches that typically learn a direct mapping from ciphertext to plaintext under known-plaintext or chosen-plaintext attack settings30, the present work adopts a fundamentally different perspective. In contrast to restoration-oriented DNN, U-Net, or diffusion-based attacks, the proposed ESN-assisted method is not trained to directly reconstruct the protected waveform. Instead of approximating the full decryption function, we focus on inferring latent structural parameters of the cipher—specifically, blockwise phase-basis coefficients—under a partial-leakage regime. In this setting, the attacker has access only to incomplete and noisy side information, without direct plaintext–ciphertext pairs. The ESN is therefore used not as a universal inverse mapper, but as a mechanism for imputing missing latent parameters, which are subsequently used in a model-based approximate decryption pipeline. This distinction highlights the novelty of the proposed approach as a form of structure-aware, leakage-driven cryptanalysis rather than conventional end-to-end learning-based attacks. A particular form of partial leakage considered in this work is “random missing leakage”, where phase observations are available only for a randomly selected subset of blocks, while the remaining blocks contain no phase information. This setting reflects practical scenarios in which leakage is incomplete and irregular across time, requiring the adversary to infer missing information from partially observed data.

In this work, a multi-stage, chaos-driven block–FFT audio encryption pipeline has been proposed that includes (i) block-level time permutation, (ii) intra-block frequency permutation, and (iii) frequency-domain phase masking, all driven by a Rössler-based keystream31. The emphasis is not on proposing a new chaotic map or a new permutation–substitution architecture, but on cryptanalytic recoverability under a structured partial-leakage model. Specifically, it is assumed that (a) partial leakage of the frequency-permutation mapping and (b) noisy phase leakage available only on a subset of blocks, while the remaining blocks exhibit random missing leakage. To analyze adversarial recovery under these constraints, an Echo State Network (ESN)32 has been employed to impute latent phase-mask coefficients on leakage-missing blocks using only the leakage-available subset; in a hybrid reconstruction strategy, leakage-derived coefficients are retained on observed blocks while ESN-predicted coefficients are used on non-observed blocks. ESN-assisted recovery has been compared to a simple non-learning baseline (carry-forward imputation), in terms of intelligibility- and error-oriented metrics (STOI, SNR, CORR, NMSE), including subset-wise reporting that separates leakage-observed and leakage-missing segments to isolate recovery precisely where direct leakage is absent. The main focus was adversarial signal recovery under partial leakage rather than full key recovery.

The main contributions of this paper are: (i) a structured partial-leakage model jointly capturing partial frequency-permutation exposure and noisy, block-sparse phase leakage with random missing leakage; (ii) an ESN-based imputation mechanism for predicting missing latent phase parameters from partially observed blocks; and (iii) a systematic experimental evaluation with comparisons to a non-learning baseline and explicit subset-wise reporting. Limitations and broader security implications are discussed in the Discussion section.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces the Rössler system and the generation of the chaotic key stream; Section 3 describes the proposed block–FFT cipher, including frequency and block permutations and structured phase masking; Section 4 presents the ESN-based adversarial inference strategy used in the cryptanalysis; Section 5 defines the evaluation metrics; Section 6 reports the experimental results; and Section 7 discusses the security implications, limitations, and future directions.

Echo state networks

Echo State Networks (ESNs) represent a prominent class of recurrent neural networks within the reservoir computing paradigm, distinguished by their ability to effectively process complex temporal and nonlinear data. Unlike traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) that rely on backpropagation through time, the ESN utilizes a large, fixed, and sparsely connected hidden layer termed the reservoir. The reservoir maps the input signal into a high-dimensional nonlinear state space. Since only the readout layer is trained, the optimization reduces to a linear regression problem, avoiding the vanishing-gradient issues commonly encountered in fully trained recurrent networks while enabling efficient modeling of temporal dynamics.

ESNs represent a paradigm of Reservoir Computing (RC), where the learning process is restricted solely to the output weights, making the training computationally efficient and mathematically stable. This property is particularly useful in the present cryptanalytic setting, where Eve must infer missing latent parameters from incomplete leakage rather than train a fully recurrent end-to-end inverse model. The cryptanalysis framework proposed in this study utilizes an ESN to infer latent phase-related parameters from partial leakage, enabling approximate inversion of the encryption process under incomplete information.

The general structure of an ESN.

In this study, the ESN does not learn a direct ciphertext-to-plaintext mapping. The plaintext waveform is obtained subsequently by applying approximate decryption using the estimated frequency permutation and the reconstructed phase mask.

The choice of ESN was motivated by the structure of the proposed cryptanalytic problem. In the considered partial-leakage setting, training labels are available only for leakage-observed blocks, and the learning task is not end-to-end waveform reconstruction but block-level latent phase-coefficient inference. Compared with fully trained recurrent models such as vanilla RNNs, LSTMs, or GRUs, an ESN keeps the recurrent reservoir fixed and trains only the linear readout. This reduces the number of trainable parameters, avoids backpropagation through time, and enables rapid recalibration when only a limited number of leakage-observed blocks are available. Therefore, the ESN provides a computationally efficient and stable baseline for testing whether partial phase leakage can be generalized to leakage-missing blocks.

Cryptanalysis

In accordance with Kerckhoffs’s principle, the adversary is assumed to know the complete signal processing pipeline (blockwise FFT, frequency permutation, phase masking, and block permutation) but not the secret instantiations of nor the blockwise phase mask ϕ (:,b). In the proposed attack setting, the Echo State Network is used by the adversary to estimate the latent phase-basis coefficients (and thus ϕ) from partial leakage, thereby enabling approximate decryption under the same noise-corrupted observation model . Under a Kerckhoffs-compliant assumption, the eavesdropper (Eve) is assumed to have full knowledge of the algorithmic structure as well as public parameters such as the block length L and the evaluation protocol, while the secret instantiation of the keys remains unknown. In particular, the true intra-block frequency permutation and the phase mask ϕ are not available to Eve; conversely, the block permutation is treated as known in order to model a strong partial-leakage setting and to isolate the security contribution of and ϕ. After encryption, an additive channel-noise component is injected into the ciphertext c[n] according to a target channel SNR, emulating realistic transmission conditions; both the legitimate receiver and the attacker operate on noisy observations. In contrast, Eve relies on partial leakage: (i) a fraction of the positive-frequency output-to-input mappings of the intra-block permutation is leaked (i.e., is revealed for a subset of ), while the remaining mappings are unknown and completed to a valid conjugate-symmetric permutation; and (ii) sparse, noisy phase samples are available only on a subset of blocks (Scenario B). Specifically, each block is leakage-observed with probability , and within observed blocks the phase leakage is provided on a fixed subset of positive-frequency bins (constant across blocks), corrupted by additive phase noise with standard deviation .

The central role of the ESN in this threat model is to exploit these incomplete observations to infer the missing phase information on non-observed blocks. Concretely, the ESN is trained using the observed blocks to predict latent phase-basis coefficients; in the proposed hybrid reconstruction, leakage-derived coefficients are used for observed blocks, whereas ESN-predicted coefficients are used for non-observed blocks. These coefficients are then mapped back into a full-band phase estimate for every block, yielding a block-complete phase hypothesis. Finally, Eve performs decryption using the estimated and , producing an approximate reconstruction whose quality is quantified in the Results section. This threat model intentionally grants Eve strong capabilities (knowledge of and full algorithmic transparency), so that any residual degradation can be attributed primarily to the difficulty of recovering and, crucially, generalizing phase information across blocks without direct leakage—precisely the component learned by the ESN. This random missing leakage model is consistently used throughout the evaluation.

It should be noted that the ESN-based inference stage is key-instance dependent. If the encryption key changes, the induced frequency permutation , block permutation , and blockwise phase-mask coefficients also change. Consequently, the leakage-to-coefficient relationship learned by the ESN is not expected to transfer directly to a different key realization. In such a case, Eve would need new leakage-observed blocks and would have to recalibrate the ESN readout, although the reservoir architecture and hyperparameters may remain unchanged. This key-specific retraining requirement limits the generality of a trained ESN attacker and should be considered when interpreting the adversarial results.

Results

The speech audio used in the experiments was synthesized with the VoiceMaker online text-to-speech platform (https://voicemaker.in/) by converting the text “Reservoir Computing-Based Cryptanalysis of Structured Phase-Masked Chaos Encryption” into an audio waveform. This waveform was used as the plaintext input to the proposed block-based chaos encryption pipeline. The generated plaintext is segmented into non-overlapping blocks of length L=1024. Each block is transformed to the frequency domain, permuted by a conjugate-symmetric frequency-bin permutation (to preserve real-valued inverse transforms), and multiplied by a structured blockwise phase mask generated from a low-dimensional basis model with K=12 coefficients. Finally, blocks are reordered by a block permutation . The transmitted ciphertext is perturbed by additive uniform noise calibrated to a channel SNR of dB with respect to ciphertext power.

Discussion

This study evaluates a multi-stage chaos-driven block–FFT masking scheme under a practically motivated partial-leakage threat model and quantifies when learning-assisted recovery becomes feasible. The qualitative evidence in Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 clarifies how the encryption suppresses intelligible time–frequency structure and how much of that structure can be recovered under different knowledge assumptions, whereas the sweep in Fig. 8 provides a systematic characterization of adversarial performance as the leaked permutation fraction increases. Beyond reporting performance values, the results identify which components act as enabling bottlenecks for recovery and which design choices increase learnability under leakage. The ablation analysis in Table 2 further supports this interpretation by separating the contributions of frequency-permutation estimation, phase compensation, non-learning imputation, and ESN-based coefficient imputation.

Compared with much of the chaos-based audio encryption literature, which often emphasizes proposing new chaotic maps or permutation–substitution variants and validating security primarily through global statistical indicators (e.g., entropy, histogram uniformity, correlation reduction) and/or implementation metrics (e.g., throughput and power), the main contribution here is methodological and threat-model driven. A structured partial-leakage setting, in which only a fraction of the intra-block frequency-permutation mappings is exposed, has been formalized and tested, while phase leakage is both block-sparse (each block is observed with probability ) and within-block sparse (a fraction of bins leaks), corrupted by measurement noise with standard deviation . This model narrows the gap between idealized adversaries and realistic leakage conditions, where observations are typically incomplete and noisy. In addition, the proposed ESN-assisted hybrid recovery mechanism explicitly leverages the cipher’s structured phase generator: the ESN is trained only on leakage-observed blocks and imputes latent phase-basis coefficients on missing blocks, while leakage-derived coefficients are retained on observed blocks. Finally, the subset-based reporting (all/obs/mis) isolates the most security-relevant regime (mis), where recovery depends entirely on imputation rather than direct phase measurements, providing clearer diagnostic insight into what the attacker can generalize.

A fundamental requirement is that authorized decryption remains accurate even when the ciphertext suppresses intelligible structure. Figure 6 shows that the ciphertext becomes visually noise-like in the time domain and exhibits dispersed energy in the spectrogram, qualitatively indicating that direct speech cues are concealed. At the same time, Fig. 7 demonstrates that Bob restores the dominant temporal envelope and speech-related spectral patterns, confirming reversibility under correct keys. This is reinforced by the near-perfect numerical performance for Bob in Table 1. Together, these observations help attribute the remaining degradation under attack primarily to adversarial limitations rather than intrinsic distortion of the encryption–decryption pipeline.

Eve is granted strong capabilities, including Kerckhoffs-compliant algorithm knowledge and known block order via metadata leakage, yet still lacks the true intra-block permutation and the full phase mask. The attack is dominated by two coupled obstacles: incorrect frequency-bin alignment due to imperfect recovery of , and sparse-label inference caused by block-sparse phase leakage. The ESN is introduced specifically to address the second obstacle by predicting low-dimensional phase coefficients on blocks where leakage is absent. However, the first obstacle acts as an enabling bottleneck: even accurate coefficient prediction cannot yield faithful inversion if the inverse permutation is wrong, because misaligned spectral bins introduce irreversible distortions after inverse transformation. This explains why increasing is not merely additive information, but rather determines whether the remaining inference problem becomes sufficiently well-posed for learning-based imputation to translate into waveform recovery.

The sweep in Fig. 8 reveals a distinctly non-linear dependence of adversarial performance on . As leakage increases, NMSE decreases while CORR, SNR, and STOI increase, but improvements are concentrated within a transition region around –0.60 in the present setting, after which recovery improves markedly, including on the missing subset (mis). Because the sweep was repeated over five independent random seeds and reported with mean±standard deviation, the observed transition behavior should be interpreted as a systematic trend rather than as the outcome of a single favorable leakage realization. The remaining localized non-monotonic fluctuations around the transition are consistent with the stochastic elements of the experiment, including random selection of leaked permutation mappings, random block-level leakage masks, and noisy phase observations. Therefore, the onset of meaningful recovery is best interpreted as a transition region rather than a sharp deterministic threshold.

Separating evaluation into all, obs, and mis subsets is crucial for correct interpretation. The obs subset reflects how effectively Eve can exploit direct, though partial and noisy, phase leakage, whereas the mis subset quantifies generalization to blocks with no direct phase observations—the security-critical regime. The lagged improvement of the mis curves in Fig. 8 supports the interpretation that imputation becomes effective only after frequency-bin alignment is sufficiently reliable. Likewise, the metrics in Table 1 show a clear gap between observed-only and missing-only performance, indicating that missing blocks remain the limiting factor even when learning-based imputation is applied.

The ablation analysis further refines this interpretation. The comparison among no phase compensation, leakage-only zero-missing coefficients, carry-forward imputation, and the proposed ESN-hybrid strategy shows that partial frequency-permutation leakage is the dominant contributor to adversarial recoverability under the representative setting. The ESN-hybrid strategy improves over the carry-forward baseline on leakage-missing blocks, indicating that learned coefficient-domain imputation is preferable to simple temporal propagation of the last observed leakage. However, its gain over no-phase or zero-missing variants is modest and remains within the observed run-to-run variability. Therefore, the ESN component should not be interpreted as a standalone universal decoder. Rather, it acts as a structure-aware coefficient-imputation module whose usefulness depends on the reliability of frequency-bin alignment and the amount of available phase leakage.

The results also highlight a key trade-off introduced by the structured phase model. A low-dimensional basis, such as the K=12 configuration used in the reported experiments, simplifies legitimate generation and makes the phase process smoother and more predictable; however, it also reduces the number of latent degrees of freedom the attacker must infer per block, increasing learnability under sparse leakage. From a defensive standpoint, several countermeasures are suggested by the observed failure modes: increasing latent complexity, introducing block-wise nonstationarity, randomizing or re-keying the basis across blocks, or coupling phase generation more tightly to secret states that are not partially observable. In parallel, limiting any leakage of the intra-block permutation is critical because accurate frequency alignment appears to be a prerequisite for meaningful inversion, regardless of the strength of coefficient inference.

The use of ESN is consistent with the objective of this study, which is not end-to-end waveform restoration but block-level latent phase-coefficient inference under limited leakage observations. Since pseudo-labels are available only on leakage-observed blocks, the fixed-reservoir and trainable-readout structure of ESNs provides a computationally efficient framework for evaluating whether partial phase information can be generalized to leakage-missing blocks. Future work may extend this analysis by benchmarking alternative recurrent or temporal models, such as LSTM, GRU, or TCN, under the same partial-leakage protocol.

This study has some limitations that point to concrete future research directions. First, the leakage model is simulated through partial frequency-permutation exposure and noisy phase observations rather than measured from a specific physical or protocol-level implementation. Future work should validate the same threat model using measured side-channel traces or protocol-level leakage logs and quantify how , , , and map to realistic leakage sources. Second, although the sweep experiments report mean±standard deviation over five independent random seeds, larger seed ensembles, additional audio samples, and confidence-interval-based uncertainty estimates would further refine the characterization of the transition region. Third, the current evaluation focuses on a controlled speech input, including synthesized speech for reproducibility. Real-world audio signals may exhibit additional variability, including multi-speaker characteristics, background noise, channel distortions, and non-speech content. These factors can alter both the leakage characteristics and the learnability of the latent phase model; therefore, evaluation on diverse real-world audio datasets is needed to assess robustness and generalizability. Fourth, the present threat model assumes that the block order is known through metadata leakage. If is unknown, Eve would need to solve an additional block-alignment problem before phase-coefficient imputation can be meaningfully applied, which would increase attack complexity and is expected to reduce adversarial recovery quality. Fifth, the ESN readout is key-instance dependent. When the encryption key changes, the induced frequency permutation and blockwise phase-mask coefficients also change, so the leakage-to-coefficient mapping learned for one key realization is not expected to transfer directly to another. In a new-key scenario, Eve would require new leakage-observed blocks and would need to recalibrate the ESN readout, although the reservoir architecture and hyperparameters could remain unchanged. Finally, the reported results demonstrate adversarial signal recovery under partial leakage rather than full key recovery. Extending the analysis toward stronger CPA capabilities, additional side information, explicit key-recovery objectives, and comparisons with alternative learning-based attackers would provide a more complete security assessment.

Overall, the results show that the scheme maintains high usability for authorized users, whereas adversarial reconstruction improves once permutation leakage enters the transition region. This observation indicates that accurate frequency-bin alignment is a key enabler of learning-assisted recovery. At the same time, the ablation results caution against overstating the role of the ESN alone: the learned imputation stage contributes mainly when the frequency-permutation estimate is sufficiently reliable and when direct phase leakage is unavailable. Therefore, limiting permutation-related leakage and reducing the learnability of the phase-generation process are important considerations for improving robustness in scenarios where partial information exposure may occur.
