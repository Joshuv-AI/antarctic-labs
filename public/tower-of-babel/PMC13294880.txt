Enhancing the Antibacterial Activity of Zinc-Based Layered Double Hydroxide Nanoparticles in the Presence of UVA Light
Authors: Jazia Awassa, Fabienne Quilès, Damien Cornu, Christian Ruby, Sofiane El-Kirat-Chatel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pristine zinc-based layered double hydroxides (LDHs) possess remarkable antibacterial properties. The antibacterial activity of zinc-based LDHs could be explained by three possible mechanisms: (1) direct contact interactions between the LDH and bacterial surfaces, (2) release of antibacterial Zn2+ aq ions, and (3) generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by LDHs in response to irradiation by a suitable light source. In this work, the potential contribution of ROS generation in the antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH nanoparticles (NPs) was determined by comparing their effect against Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus bacteria in the presence and absence of UVA irradiation (λ = 365 nm, irradiation period = 4 h). The bactericidal efficacy of ZnAl LDH NPs was significantly enhanced in the presence of UVA light. The bactericidal activity was hindered in the presence of histidine acting as a ROS scavenger and giving indirect evidence of the role of ROS. Moreover, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy revealed denaturation and suppression of several structural components in S. aureus cells exposed to ZnAl LDH NPs under dark conditions and in the presence of UVA radiation, with more pronounced alterations for the latter. The impacts of such alterations on the topographical properties of S. aureus cells were determined by atomic force microscopy imaging. Treated cells with ZnAl LDH NPs were completely deformed and exhibited increased roughness, especially upon treatment in UVA conditions. All these results suggest the presence of a dual antibacterial effect between ZnAl LDH NPs and their generated ROS to provide amplified antibacterial activity.

Introduction

Bacterial contamination and subsequent pathogenic infections have been recognized for several years as a critical threat affecting the quality of human healthcare worldwide. − With the rapid spread of antimicrobial resistance phenomena caused by the excessive use of antibiotics, a growing interest was drawn toward the fabrication of new materials preventing biofouling or pathogenic bacteria proliferation. − Recent advances in the field of material science have opened up new gates for designing new antimicrobials based on synthetic inorganic materials. ,,

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) are one class of inorganic lamellar solids having a structure based on the stacking of brucite-like sheets comprising divalent and trivalent metal cations. These sheets hold a net positive charge, which is compensated by negatively charged intercalating anions and water molecules present in the interlayer regions. − These materials possess several interesting properties, including flexible composition, high anion exchange capacity, and biocompatibility. In this context, the use of inorganic pristine LDHs as efficient antibacterial agents could provide one possible means to fight bacterial infections and to overcome the problem of antibiotic resistance. − Indeed, the use of inorganic pristine LDHs, in particular zinc-based LDHs, as efficient antibacterial agents was previously demonstrated in 2016 by Moaty et al. against MRSA. Lobo-Sánchez et al. also showed that ZnAl LDHs could provide promising antibacterial properties against multiresistant bacteria. Moreover, many practical applications of these Zn-based LDHs were also described in the literature, including bone implant applications, antibacterial patches, as well as wound healing applications.

To optimize the antibacterial effect of pristine LDHs, it is of the utmost importance to determine their followed antibacterial mechanisms. In fact, we recently reviewed the proposed antibacterial mechanisms explaining the antibacterial action of pristine LDHs. The three suggested antibacterial mechanisms are as follows: (1) direct contact interactions between LDHs and bacterial surfaces, which could further lead to their damage; ,,,, (2) release of antibacterial constituent divalent metal ions, which disturbs the permeability of bacterial membranes by misbalancing their active normal transport system, thus leading to their damage; ,,,,,− and (3) generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by LDHs. ,, Despite the fact that these hypotheses were suggested to explain the antibacterial action of LDHs, very few experimental setups were designed to validate their existence. Indeed, we previously performed a global study on a large number of LDH samples to determine their antibacterial activity. We found that the antibacterial effect of pristine LDHs was mainly related to the nature of their constituent divalent metal, by which zinc-based LDHs performed best in the antibacterial activity tests against the Gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus aureus. The antibacterial effect of the prepared zinc-based LDH samples was also found to be dependent on the amount of Zn2+ aq ions released in broth media, thus validating the suggested antibacterial mechanism of LDHs by divalent metal release (mechanism 2). Moreover, the direct contact mechanism (mechanism 1) was also established by our group as well, by which we measured adhesion forces between the surface of ZnAl LDH nanoparticles (NPs) and the cell wall of S. aureus bacteria using atomic force microscopy-based force spectroscopy (AFM-based FS). Our results suggested that the direct contact interactions occurring between the NP surface and bacterial surfaces lead to their damage, which was evidenced by serious cell wall damages observed using AFM imaging for ZnAl LDH NP-treated cells. On the other hand, no experimental evidence was previously reported for the ROS generation mechanism (mechanism 3), where this mechanism is still uncertain with respect to the other two suggested ones.

In terms of pathogen clearance mechanism, ROS are known to be “the double-edged swords of life”, where bacteria can exploit endogenous ROS to ensure their growth and adaptation, and on the other hand, exogenous ROS generated by an external environmental factor, e.g., LDH in this case, are well-known to impose an efficient antibacterial effect by direct damage of bacterial membranes, DNA, lipids, and proteins through oxidative stress. − When a suitable light source irradiates LDH nanoparticles (NPs), an electron (e –) and a hole (h +) are created, which could subsequently generate ROS as hydroxyl (OH·), superoxide (O2 · –), hydrogen superoxide (HOO·) radicals, as well as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). , These generated ROS are thus expected to enhance the antibacterial activity of LDHs. Previous reports proved that the antibacterial activity of zinc oxide nanoparticles could be enhanced by the generation of ROS in the presence of a UV or visible light source with a suitable wavelength. − However, no proof of evidence was reported concerning the antibacterial activity of Zn­(II)-based LDHs by ROS-generated oxidative stress. In general, antibacterial activity tests using LDHs or any antibacterial agent take place inside an incubator in dark conditions (no ROS could be generated by LDHs in this case). However, it is of great interest as well to determine whether the antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs could further be enhanced through the generation of ROS in the presence of light.

The work presented here provides, for the first time, an experimental framework to confirm the existence of a ROS-mediated antibacterial mechanism induced by ZnAl LDH nanoparticles against the pathogen S. aureus. To this end, we evaluated the enhancement of antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs used in our previous study, in the presence of UVA light (λ = 365 nm). The antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs against S. aureus in UVA conditions was compared to that found in the dark. Such a comparison was achieved through evaluating the bactericidal efficiency by colony-forming unit (CFU) count, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination, and agar disk diffusion tests in both dark and UVA conditions. As primary evidence of ROS generation, the antibacterial activity of ZnAl NPs was also determined in the presence of histidine, a well-known ROS scavenger. − Finally, changes in surface topography and structural composition of S. aureus treated with ZnAl LDH NPs in dark and UVA conditions were also determined using atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging and Fourier-Transform InfraRed in Attenuated Total Reflection mode (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Antibacterial Activity of ZnAl LDH NPs in Dark and UVA Conditions Using CFU Counts

Before testing the bactericidal efficiency of LDH NPs, ZnAl LDH NPs were prepared by a fast coprecipitation method reported in our previous work. ZnAl NPs presented a hexagonal lamellar structure as revealed by their SEM and AFM images (Figure S1). The average particle size of ZnAl LDH NPs extracted from their SEM and AFM height images was 167 ± 40 nm and 167 ± 33 nm, respectively. The NPs were well crystallized presenting neither crystalline nor amorphous impurities, as suggested by our previously performed XRD, XPS, and IR analyses.

The bactericidal activity of ZnAl LDH NPs in dark and UVA light conditions (exposure period: 0–4 h) was first examined against S. aureus using the CFU count method. The concentration of viable bacterial cells was determined by spreading a suspension of S. aureus cells of fixed concentration exposed or not to ZnAl LDH NPs (93.8 μg/mL) on Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) agar Petri dishes at defined time points. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to determine bacterial viability by counting the number of colonies formed. The average concentration of viable cells, the concentration of viable cells (CFU/mL), the bactericidal efficiency after 24 h, and the bactericidal rate at each defined time point were further determined.

Upon exposure of the bacterial suspension cultured in dark conditions to ZnAl NPs, the concentration of S. aureus significantly decreased (Figure a) reaching an average bactericidal efficiency of 88.9% after 24 h (Table ). The bactericidal efficiency of ZnAl LDH NPs was further enhanced in UVA conditions, where the decrease in the bacterial concentration was more pronounced (Figure a), reaching a bactericidal efficiency of 100% after 24 h (Table ). Similar findings were previously observed by Nocchetti et al., who reported a remarkable enhancement in the bactericidal efficiency of AgCl NP-loaded ZnAl LDHs by the generation of ROS upon being irradiated with a source of light (λ > 350 nm) for 3 h. Therefore, ZnAl LDH NPs are able to kill a great portion of bacteria without the generation of ROS. However, the generation of ROS, which is a possible explanation of the enhanced antibacterial activity, could further aid in the observed bactericidal effect, leading to the death of all the present S. aureus cells.

Another important factor highlighting the remarkable antibacterial properties of ZnAl LDH NPs in the presence of UVA light is the bactericidal rate. Figure b shows the rate of bactericidal efficiency of ZnAl LDH NPs at different time points in both dark and UVA conditions. The bactericidal rates in UVA conditions during the first 4 h of the experiment were remarkably higher than those obtained in dark conditions, suggesting that the possible generated ROS enhance the antibacterial activity but also increase the rate of bacterial killing. On the other hand, the bactericidal rate in dark conditions after 4 h without any exposure to UVA light was slightly greater than that obtained after 4 h of UVA exposure. The lower bactericidal rate observed under UVA conditions after 4 h of exposure is due to the lower number of remaining bacteria alive accessible for the LDH NPs to kill.

The observed bactericidal rate enhancement during the first 4 h can be explained by the fact that ROS were generated only during exposure to UVA light. After turning off the UVA light and returning to dark conditions, the possible generated ROS are recombined/decayed, and the LDH NPs continue to impose the antibacterial activity alone.

However, according to the UV–visible spectrum of ZnAl LDH NPs, it is not clear whether LDH NPs absorb at 365 nm to be able to generate ROS (Figure S2). The observed lower transmission can be attributed to beam scattering. Therefore, to ensure that ROS could be produced by ZnAl NPs, an additional experiment was performed using a scavenger for ROS (histidine).

Modification of the Antibacterial Activity of ZnAl LDH NPs in UVA Conditions Using Scavengers

As a primary evidence of ROS generation by our ZnAl LDH NPs, we aimed to determine their bactericidal efficiency under UVA conditions in the presence of different amounts of histidine (20 mg, 40 mg, 80 mg, 160 mg), which is a well-known ROS scavenger with remarkable antioxidative properties. − As a first step, we ensured that this ROS scavenger, e.g., histidine, did not impose by itself any antibacterial effect against S. aureus in both UVA (Figure a) and dark conditions (Table ).

CFU count experiments in UVA conditions, in the presence and absence of different amounts of histidine. (a) Concentration of S. aureus (CFU/mL). (b) Bactericidal rate (%/h) of LDH NPs (formula for calculation is given in the Material and Methods section). Colored backgrounds correspond to the irradiation periods (first 4 h of experiment).

Upon adding increasing amounts of histidine from 20 to 80 mg to the suspension of S. aureus and LDH NPs irradiated by UVA, the final achieved bactericidal efficiency after 24 h was lower in the presence of increasing amounts of histidine up to 80 mg (Table ). Moreover, bacterial concentration in the presence of increasing amounts of histidine under UVA conditions was greater than that obtained in the absence of histidine for each time point (Figure a).

Such results fall in good agreement with those of Dutta et al., who noted a decrease in bactericidal efficiency of ZnO NPs in the presence of increasing amounts of histidine. Indeed, the authors performed fluorescence dye measurements and ensured that histidine was able to scavenge generated ROS up to a certain limit. Histidine is an efficient ROS scavenger; however, its scavenging action is limited to hydroxyl and singlet oxygen radicals, while hydrogen peroxide, which is also a possible generated ROS by LDHs, might not be scavenged by histidine. In this context, the inability of histidine to completely suppress the action of possibly produced ROS could be explained by its incapability to scavenge all types of generated ROS. This can also be a reasonable explanation for the limit of the amount of histidine to provide a suppressing effect above 80 mg. In other words, after the addition of 80 mg of histidine, a plateau of its scavenging action is obtained.

As suggested by Figure b, the rate of bactericidal efficiency was remarkably lower in the presence of an increasing amount of histidine than that found in UVA in the absence of histidine during the first 4 h (irradiation period). This provided primary evidence of the suppression of generated ROS produced only during the UVA irradiation period. While these findings support the involvement of ROS in the antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs, complementary approaches aimed at identifying and quantifying specific ROS species could provide additional mechanistic insight.

After 4 h, the rate in the presence of increasing amounts of histidine was greater than that found under UVA conditions (without histidine), which could be explained by the higher number of bacteria still present after 4 h in the case of histidine addition.

On the contrary, the bactericidal efficiency in the presence of increasing amounts of histidine under UVA conditions was still higher than that in dark conditions during the whole 24 h of the experiment. One may hypothesize that histidine did not scavenge all the possible generated ROS, and therefore the action of ROS on increasing the bactericidal rate was not completely suppressed during the first 4 h. After turning off the UVA light at t = 4 h, generated ROS were recombined/decayed due to their very short lifespan, resulting in an antibacterial activity imposed by the NPs themselves by the ion release and direct contact mechanisms.

Antibacterial Activity of ZnAl LDH NPs in Dark and UVA Conditions Using Macro-Dilution and Agar Tests

The next step was to compare the MIC of ZnAl LDH NPs in UVA conditions with the one in dark conditions. Control experiments with UVA illumination in the absence of LDH NPs were also carried out, and no decrease in turbidity or optical density was noticed compared to dark conditions over 24 h. The MIC value of ZnAl LDH NPs against S. aureus was twice divided in UVA conditions compared to dark conditions (Figure a). Considering that UVA light does not contribute to any inhibitory effect by itself, the antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs was significantly enhanced in the presence of UVA light, with a MIC value decreasing from 93 to 45 μg/mL. Taking into consideration the suggested antibacterial mechanism of LDHs by the generation of ROS under a suitable light source irradiation, the enhanced antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs may be explained by ROS generation in response to UVA radiation.

Agar disk diffusion test was further used to evaluate the antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs in agar gel environments. On the contrary to CFU count performed after exposure to LDH NPs in liquid and broth macro-dilution turbidimetric tests, no remarkable change was detected in the antibacterial effect of LDH NPs in agar disk diffusion tests where the inhibitory zone diameter of 21 mm generated by ZnAl LDH NPs did not change upon irradiation with light (Figure b). This suggests that UVA light does not improve the antibacterial activity of these LDH NPs in agar gel environments. In the agar context, ROS are generated under UVA exposure, but their lifetime is probably too short as compared to their diffusion rate in the gel, and therefore, they cannot provide any improvement to the antibacterial activity in the agar disk diffusion tests. In fact, the average lifetime of free radicals is 10–9–10–6 seconds, thus limiting their diffusion into the agar gel. On the other hand, LDH NPs and generated ROS are in direct contact in liquid with the bacteria during MIC and CFU count experiments, which makes them more accessible to S. aureus cells in liquid environments.

ZnAl LDH NPs Impose More Pronounced Cell Wall Damages to S. aureus Cells in UVA Conditions

AFM imaging was used to visualize the influence of ZnAl LDH NPs (93.8 μg/mL, corresponding to the MIC value obtained in dark conditions) in dark and UVA conditions on the surface topography of S. aureus cells. For this purpose, untreated and ZnAl LDH NP-treated cells were trapped in porous membranes and imaged in TSB buffer. The roughness parameter Rq (root-mean-square of the heights, calculated as an average on 15 cells) was also obtained from AFM images performed on the top of bacteria at a high magnification of 400 nm × 400 nm scanning areas.

As shown in Figure a,b, untreated cells in dark and UVA conditions presented regular spheroid morphologies and smooth surfaces with a similar Rq roughness parameter (Table ). Such an observation suggests that UVA light alone does not impose any damage to the cell wall of S. aureus cells.

As we previously reported, cells treated with ZnAl LDH NPs in dark conditions were seriously damaged as they appeared distorted with highly corrugated surfaces showing several hollows and crests (Figure c). The increase of roughness of the cells treated with ZnAl LDH NPs in dark conditions (∼3 times rougher surfaces) was similar to that noted in our previous work. The cell wall damages imposed by ZnAl LDH NPs were even more pronounced in UVA conditions, where most treated cells in UVA conditions lost their spheroid morphology and showed deeper grooves (Figure d) as well as an increase of ∼20% in surface roughness (Table ) compared to treated cells in dark conditions.

Cell imaging in the air was also performed in order to examine the morphological impacts of ZnAl LDH NP treatment in dark and UVA conditions on a greater fraction of cells immobilized on mica substrates. In fact, cell imaging in the air can also help to better differentiate topographical differences between treated cells in dark and UVA conditions. Untreated S. aureus cells in both dark and UVA conditions showed homogeneous cellular shapes with a spheroid geometry and regular surfaces, typical for this type of bacteria (Figure ). , However, cells treated with ZnAl LDH NPs under dark conditions were completely distorted, showing deep grooves, crests, hollows, and even some split surfaces, leaving behind some cell debris. On the other hand, only lysed cells and cell debris were observed for those treated under UVA conditions. Such an observation comes in line with our CFU count experiments, by which we reported a 100% bactericidal efficiency of ZnAl NPs under UVA conditions, ensuring that all cells were killed.

The more pronounced cell damage of S. aureus treated with ZnAl LDH NPs (observed in both liquid and air imaging environments) in UVA conditions could be due to the dual antibacterial effect of ZnAl LDH NPs, through the mechanisms of Zn2+ release (mechanism 2) and direct contact interactions with the bacteria (mechanism 1), and the possible generated ROS under UVA irradiation (mechanism 3).

ZnAl LDH NPs Impose Serious Biochemical Alterations to S. aureus Cells in Both Dark and UVA Conditions

As the bacterial cell wall properties are defined by their biochemical composition, we aimed to determine which compositional changes of S. aureus cells were associated with the antibacterial effect of ZnAl LDH NPs, which was previously evidenced by a high bactericidal efficiency and serious cell wall damages in both dark and UVA conditions. Attenuated total reflection Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) was used to establish the biochemical fingerprints of S. aureus cells treated or not with ZnAl LDH NPs (93.8 μg/mL, corresponding to the MIC value obtained in dark conditions) in both dark and UVA conditions.

Before investigating the influence of ZnAl LDH NPs on the biochemical structure of S. aureus cells, it is important to define the general biochemical components of this bacterium and to determine the effect of the culture media, i.e., TSB, on the IR spectrum of ZnAl NPs. Figure S3a shows the obtained FTIR spectra of S. aureus cells recorded during different time intervals. The spectrum recorded after 15 min showed the most intense peaks due to the time needed by the bacterial suspension to accumulate on the ATR crystal. Therefore, all other FTIR spectra provided will be the ones recorded after a time interval of 15 min relative to the initial time of suspension deposition on the crystal. The spectral features of S. aureus cells were typical to those reported previously in the literature, and the band assignments are given in Table S1. Two main regions in the fingerprint spectrum (1800–800 cm–1) are prominent. The spectral region between 1800 and 1500 cm–1 arises mainly from the vibration of amide I and amide II bonds (proteins), whereas the region between 1170 and 800 cm–1 was dominated by vibrations from nucleic acids, polysaccharides, and phospholipid functional groups (Figure S3a).

The FTIR spectrum of ZnAl LDH NPs was strongly altered by the TSB culture media (Figure S3b). Two strong vibration bands were noticed at 1095 cm–1 and 985 cm–1 which were absent in the spectrum recorded in pure water. These bands are assigned to vibration bands of adsorbed or intercalated phosphate groups, − e.g., H2PO4 – and HPO4 2– present in a pH range of 7.2–12. These adsorbed/intercalated phosphate groups arise from the TSB culture medium. The XRD powder diffractogram of LDH NPs exposed to TSB medium for 24 h did not show any significant change either in the relative intensity or in the position of the (001) basal diffraction peaks (Figure S4), which are affected by ionic exchange, as they are linked to the size of the intercalated anion. The possibility of a bulk exchange of anions to intercalate phosphate groups has to be excluded. However, the IR spectra of LDH NPs in TSB media (Figure S3b) suggest the exchange (or the adsorption) of a small portion of nitrate anions with phosphate ones. This could be derived from the slight change in chemical environment and decrease in the intensity of intercalated nitrate anions (∼1360 cm–1) in TSB media compared to that found in water, which suggests the partial exchange of a portion of nitrate anions with phosphate ones.

Several remarkable changes in the infrared fingerprint were noted after treating S. aureus cells with ZnAl LDH NPs in dark conditions and UVA conditions (Figure ). In the region between 1800 and 1500 cm–1, the intensities of the amide I, amide II and protein bands located at around 1638 and 1546 cm–1, respectively, were reduced for ZnAl NP-treated S. aureus cells in dark conditions. This decrease in intensity was even more pronounced for cells treated with ZnAl NPs in UVA conditions (Figure b). This observation may be attributed to the decrease in biomass production leading to bacterial growth inhibition. Such inhibition may be due to the deletion of the transpeptidation process, the reaction that leads to the cross-linkage of two glycan-linked peptide chains. As a result, a newly synthesized bacterial cell wall will no longer be cross-linked and cannot maintain its strength, − leading to the death of S. aureus cells as previously observed in the CFU count results. Furthermore, the broadening of the amide II band for treated cells in UVA conditions indicates that the proteins underwent a significant structural modification. Most probably, these proteins undergo denaturation associated with the destruction of the cell wall of S. aureus as suggested by the AFM imaging of treated cells in UVA conditions (Figure d and Figure d).

In the region of 1500 to 1170 cm–1, the most remarkable change was observed for the phospholipids and nucleic acids bands located at 1241 and 1220 cm–1 corresponding to the antisymmetric stretching of OPO groups. In both treated samples in dark and UVA conditions, the band disappeared, suggesting that bacterial membrane phospholipids were destroyed by LDH treatment and that no nucleic acids were present (Figure a and b). A new band also appeared for both treated conditions at around 1206 cm–1 assigned to organophosphorus compounds, which may be assigned to the synthesis of new phosphorus compounds by the bacteria in response to the antibacterial effect induced by ZnAl LDH NPs in dark and UVA conditions. Previous works reported that alterations in membrane phosphorus compounds take place in response to environmental stress. Cell lysis and the formation of cell debris may be another explanation for the appearance of the new phosphorus compounds, where cell lysis results in the release of phosphorus-containing cell components and active enzymes that could persist after the lysis process. Additionally, a significant decrease was observed in the intensities of amide I and II bands from proteins in the range of 1700 and 1500 cm–1 for both treated samples (dark and UVA conditions), with a more pronounced effect in the case of ZnAl NP-treated cells under UVA conditions. This highlights again the efficiency of ZnAl LDH NPs in UVA conditions to suppress the production of these essential cell compounds. Finally, the band located at ∼1358 cm–1 is assigned to nitrate intercalated anions previously observed in the ZnAl LDH control spectra in TSB medium.

In the region between 1170 and 800 cm–1, the spectral fingerprints of S. aureus were unfortunately masked by the adsorbed/intercalated groups on the LDH in the case of ZnAl LDH NP-treated samples in dark and UVA conditions (Figure a and b). However, the shape and the intensity of the bands in this region were not exactly similar to those of pure LDH NPs in TSB. Yet, it is still difficult to detect changes induced by the NPs in this region due to the overlap with bands coming from the LDHs in contact with the TSB medium.

ZnAl LDH NPs impose serious alterations on the biochemical structure of S. aureus. This impact was more definite in UVA conditions, providing evidence of the generation of ROS by the LDHs in response to the illumination. In this context, when UVA light irradiates ZnAl LDH NPs, their antibacterial activity is enhanced due to the dual effect of the LDH NPs themselves (by Zn2+ ion release and direct contact) and the possible generated ROS, thus inducing a more efficient bactericidal effect against S. aureus cells by causing serious damage to their cell wall composition and structure.

Conclusion

The bactericidal efficiency of ZnAl LDH NPs against S. aureus was enhanced from a partial bactericidal effect in dark conditions (88.9%) to a complete killing effect (100%) under UVA light, implying the contribution of the ROS generation mechanism to the antibacterial properties of LDH NPs in response to the irradiation. This was also associated with an increase in the rate of killing bacteria during the irradiation period. The bactericidal activity of the suggested produced ROS was less pronounced in the presence of histidine acting as an ROS scavenger. Such a finding provided primary evidence concerning the generation of ROS by ZnAl LDH NPs in the presence of UVA light. Moreover, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy revealed remarkable changes in the structural components of the cells imposed by ZnAl LDH NP treatment under both dark and UVA conditions, with more pronounced alterations in the latter. The impacts of such alterations were highlighted in the morphology and textural features of S. aureus cells exposed to LDH NP treatment, by which they seemed completely distorted, possessing rough surfaces, especially those treated under UVA conditions. All these findings suggest a dual antibacterial mechanism associated with ZnAl LDH nanoparticles, involving both direct contact effects and Zn2+ ion release, along with the generation of ROS under UVA irradiation, leading to an amplified antibacterial activity. While the present results support the contribution of ROS to this effect, further investigations aimed at identifying and quantifying the specific ROS generated could provide additional insight into the underlying mechanisms of ZnAl LDH activity. Overall, this work demonstrates that ZnAl LDH nanoparticles are promising antibacterial agents, possessing different antibacterial mechanisms and offering a valuable strategy for the development of advanced antimicrobial materials.

Materials and Methods

LDH NPs Synthesis

Nitrate-intercalated ZnAl LDH NPs were prepared as previously reported. Briefly, a mixture of 0.2 M Zn­(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.1 M Al­(NO3)3·9H2O (Sigma-Aldrich) was added quickly (within 5 s) to a 0.15 M NaOH solution (VWR Chemicals) under a nitrogen atmosphere and vigorous stirring for 15 min. The LDH slurry was obtained by centrifugation, washed twice, and dispersed again in decarbonated water. The resultant suspension was then transferred to a stainless-steel autoclave with a Teflon lining and heated at 80 °C for 24 h. ZnAl LDH NPs were then collected by centrifugation and air-dried at 80 °C.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Imaging

The morphology of ZnAl LDH NPs was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JEOL JSM-IT500HR with a field emission gun (FEG) and a voltage of 2 kV. The analysis was performed under high vacuum, and a 60 μm diaphragm aperture was set. A secondary electron detector (SED) was used for imaging.

Antimicrobial Activity Tests

The antimicrobial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs was evaluated using CFU count tests, broth macro-dilution turbidity methods, and agar disk diffusion against Gram-positive S. aureus bacterial cells under dark and UVA conditions (irradiation time = 4 h, using a UVA lamp, 4 W, λ = 365 nm, Labomoderne).

The antibacterial activity of ZnAl LDH NPs in dark and UVA light conditions was first evaluated against S. aureus cells using the CFU count method. S. aureus cells were precultured in 5 mL sterile tryptic soy broth (TSB, Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 37 °C, then washed twice and resuspended in sterile tris-buffered saline (TBS, Sigma). This bacterial suspension was further diluted to obtain an optical density OD600 = 0.05. One mL of diluted bacterial suspension was deposited on 12-well plates and gently agitated at 37 °C with and without ZnAl LDH NPs (93.8 μg/mL) in dark and UVA conditions. At defined time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h), 100 μL was pipetted, serially diluted (1/10000, 1/20000, 1/50000) using tryptic soy broth, and spread on TSB agar Petri dishes using sterilized beads. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C, and the results of CFU count experiments were reported in CFU/mL (colony-forming unit/mL): 1 CFUmL=Ndv

where N is the number of colonies formed on the plate, d is the dilution factor, and v is the volume of the initial bacterial inoculum at OD600 = 0.05.

Since CFU count is only accounted for viable bacterial cells, the bactericidal efficiency of ZnAl LDH NPs was determined using the following formula: 2 %bactericidal efficiency=NC−NANC×100

where NC is the number of bacterial colonies in a control group in the absence of the antibacterial agent, and NA is the number of bacterial colonies found in the presence of the tested antibacterial agent.

The bactericidal rate at each defined data point was also calculated as follows: 3 Bactericidal rate=N0−NtN0t×100

where N 0 is the number of colonies formed under a certain experimental condition at t = 0, Nt is the number of colonies formed at a defined time point, and t is the defined time point.

Similar experimental setup was used to determine the cell viability of S. aureus in the presence of ZnAl LDH NPs and different amounts of l-Histidine (20, 40, 80, and 160 mg, ≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich) acting as a ROS scavenger in UVA conditions. Control experiments were carried out on untreated cells in the presence of histidine in both dark and UVA conditions.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of LDH NPs was evaluated using the broth macro-dilution turbidimetric method. S. aureus cells were precultured in 5 mL TSB overnight at 37 °C. An inoculum of S. aureus was then prepared and diluted to obtain an optical density OD600 = 0.1. ZnAl LDH NPs were dispersed in TSB broth, and 2-fold dilution series were then prepared in different Eppendorf tubes. Each tube included 250 μL of the diluted ZnAl LDH NPs and 1750 μL of bacterial inoculum. Uninoculated tubes containing the different used LDH dispersion concentrations were used as a negative standard growth control. As a positive control, Eppendorf tubes were prepared with 1750 μL of bacterial inoculum and 250 μL of TSB. The tubes were then incubated at 37 °C under agitation for 24 h. The MIC value was recognized as the lowest LDH concentration tube that had no visible turbidity due to S. aureus growth. A similar experimental setup was used to evaluate the MIC of ZnAl LDH NPs in the presence of 160 mg of histidine acting as a ROS scavenger in UVA conditions. Control experiments were carried out on untreated cells in the presence of histidine in both dark and UVA conditions.

Inhibition zone diameters generated by ZnAl LDH NPs were determined by the agar disk diffusion test. S. aureus cells were precultured in 5 mL TSB overnight at 37 °C and then diluted to 1/1500 in TBS. Then, 250 μL of diluted bacterial suspension was spread on sterilized TSB agar Petri dishes using sterile cotton-tipped swabs (COPAN) to prepare a homogeneous bacterial layer. Filter disks (diameter = 6 mm, Dominique Dutscher) were placed on the dishes, and 20 μL of LDH dispersions (10 mg/mL) were loaded on the respective disks. Petri dishes were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Finally, inhibition zones were observed around the disks. The diameters of the circular zones were measured using a centimeter ruler, and mean values of three repeated trials in both dark and UVA were recorded and represented in millimeters.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) Imaging and Force Spectroscopy

All AFM experiments were performed at 21 °C in an air-conditioned room using a Bioscope Resolve AFM (Bruker Corporation, Santa Barbara, CA), and the spring constants of the used cantilevers were determined using the thermal noise method.

ZnAl LDH NPs immobilized on silicon substrates were imaged in peak-force tapping mode using SNL-C tips (Bruker Corporation) in air. ZnAl LDH NPs were dispersed in an ethanol solution, sonicated for 30 min, deposited on clean silicon substrates, and dried at 30 °C overnight. The average particle size of ZnAl LDH nanoparticles was obtained from their respective AFM height images and estimated as an average particle size of 100 NPs.

For topography images of S. aureus cells, cells were cultivated alone (untreated) or with (treated) MIC of ZnAl NPs during 24 h in TSB at 37 °C under dark and UVA conditions.

For imaging in TBS buffer solution, S. aureus cells were centrifuged, washed twice in MOPS, and filtered into porous polycarbonate membranes (pore size = 0.8 μm, Millipore). After filtration and rough rinsing, a 1 × 1 cm2 piece of membrane was cut and attached with double-sided adhesive tape at the bottom of a Petri dish and immersed in TBS. The roughness parameter Rq (root-mean-square of the heights) of the cells was calculated using the commercial Nanoscope analysis (Bruker) from AFM images performed on the top of bacteria at a high magnification of 400 nm × 400 nm scanning areas. The measured roughness parameter for all treated/untreated cells was performed on a similar area to that of the high-magnification AFM images, e.g., 400 nm × 400 nm (512 samples/line; 262,144 data points).

For imaging in air, cells were centrifuged, washed twice, and resuspended in ultrapure distilled water, then deposited on mica substrates. After 2 h, substrates were gently rinsed in ultrapure distilled water and dried overnight at 30 °C. For both air and liquid conditions, images were acquired in peak-force tapping mode using SNL-C tips.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

ATR-FTIR spectra of S. aureus bacterial suspensions with and without ZnAl LDH NPs in dark and UVA light conditions (exposure for 4 h) were recorded between 4000 and 800 cm–1 on a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer equipped with a KBr beam splitter and a DTGS detector and driven by the OPUS 7.5 software. The resolution of the single beam spectra was 4 cm–1. A nine-reflection diamond ATR accessory (DurasamplIR, SensIR Technologies, incidence angle: 45°) was used for acquiring the spectra. The number of bidirectional double-sided interferogram scans was 200, which corresponds to a 2 min accumulation. All interferograms were Fourier processed using the Mertz phase correction mode and a Blackman–Harris three-term apodization function. No ATR correction was performed. Measurements were performed at 22 °C in an air-conditioned room. A drop of 50 μL of the bacterial suspension in its culture medium was put on the ATR crystal. The used spectral background was the supernatant free of bacteria, which was obtained by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. Four measurements were carried out on each sample, separated by a time interval of 5 min to allow the bacterial/LDH suspensions to accumulate on the crystal, thus enhancing the intensity. Water vapor subtraction was performed when necessary. Baseline correction was performed at 1800 and 800 cm–1.
