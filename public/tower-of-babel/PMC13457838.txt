A cross sectional study of perceived couple communication patterns and influencing factors among colorectal cancer patients
Authors: Xiaobei Liu, Xuehong Cheng, Fei Yang, Haiyan Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the current status of couple communication among patients with colorectal cancer and to identify the key factors influencing communication patterns, thereby providing a reference for clinically targeted communication interventions. Based on Walsh’s family resilience theory and the Interpersonal Process Model of Intimacy (IPMI), a theoretical framework of “resource-motivation-activation” for couple communication was developed. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling and included 306 hospitalized colorectal cancer patients from a Grade A tertiary hospital in Anhui Province between July 2023 and September 2024. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Communication Pattern Questionnaire, the Chinese version of the Family Resilience Questionnaire, the Quality of Relationship Index, and the Distress Disclosure Index. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing couple communication patterns. The level of constructive communication among couples affected by colorectal cancer was relatively low, while the demand–withdraw pattern was the most prevalent communication style. Among demographic and disease-related variables, age, primary caregiver, ostomy status, and pathological stage were found to be significantly associated with couple communication patterns. Family resilience, intimacy, and self-disclosure were identified as variables positively associated with constructive communication. Intimacy was negatively associated with both demand-withdraw and mutual avoidance communication patterns, whereas self-disclosure was negatively associated with mutual avoidance communication. These results provide preliminary support for the “resource-motivation-activation” theoretical framework. Couple communication patterns among patients with colorectal cancer are influenced by multiple factors. Family resilience, intimacy, and self-disclosure may play important roles in shaping communication behaviors. Clinical intervention strategies may consider patients’ demographic and disease characteristics, with particular emphasis on strengthening family resilience, enhancing intimacy, and encouraging patient self-disclosure, which may help improve couple communication among colorectal cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59613-5.

Introduction

The World Health Organization’s Global Cancer Report 20201 prioritizes improving psychosocial outcomes for cancer survivors and their families. China’s Healthy China Action Plan (2019–2030)2 promotes patient- and family-centered care through comprehensive support services, psychological care, and palliative care, emphasizing the role of spouses or partners as primary sources of support. Collectively, these policies reflect a paradigm shift in cancer control objectives, from a sole focus on improving survival rates to the simultaneous promotion of adaptive growth among patients and their spousal and family systems.

According to global cancer statistics for 2022, colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide3. Chinese national reports also indicate that colorectal cancer ranks as the second most prevalent cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in China4. As a major traumatic stressor, colorectal cancer not only compromises patients’ physical health but also leads to significant psychological changes, substantially reducing quality of life5. In many cases, spouses serve as primary caregivers, providing essential physical and emotional support. Couple communication is an important determinant of dyadic coping effectiveness6 and can be defined as the interactive exchange of emotions, information, support, and problem-solving strategies between partners7. Empirical evidence has shown that effective communication enhances emotional support, strengthens marital intimacy, facilitates the resolution of disease-related concerns, and alleviates psychological distress8. However, research specifically examining couple communication patterns among patients with colorectal cancer remains limited. Within the broader context of global and national policies promoting psychosocial support in cancer care, it is essential to clarify the characteristics of couple communication patterns and their underlying mechanisms among patients with colorectal cancer. Such efforts would both respond to policy initiatives and address existing gaps in the literature.

Heavey et al.9 classified couple communication into three distinct patterns: (1) constructive communication, in which both partners actively and collaboratively engage in identifying and resolving problems; (2) demand–withdraw communication, characterized by one partner actively initiating discussion and seeking solutions while the other withdraws through silence, avoidance, or refusal to engage. This pattern includes two subtypes: patient-demand/spouse-withdraw and spouse-demand/patient-withdraw; and (3) mutual avoidance communication, in which both partners consistently avoid addressing problems. This classification of couple communication patterns was adopted in the present study because it encompasses both functional communication (constructive communication) and dysfunctional communication (demand–withdraw and mutual avoidance communication), thereby capturing a broad range of interaction styles between partners. The Chinese version of the questionnaire10 has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in Chinese populations. This classification framework has been widely applied in psycho-oncology research. Previous studies involving couples coping with breast cancer11 and cervical cancer12 have shown that constructive communication is associated with lower levels of depression, anxiety, and fear of cancer recurrence, as well as better treatment adherence. In contrast, avoidant communication patterns are linked to increased emotional distress and poorer relationship satisfaction. These findings highlight the value of this framework for examining communication dynamics in couples facing cancer and support its extension to the study of couples coping with colorectal cancer.

Based on the aforementioned classification framework of communication patterns, this study aims to further identify the key determinants influencing communication patterns among couples coping with colorectal cancer. The selection of these determinants was guided by two theoretical considerations. First, these factors may explain specific changes in couple communication patterns when partners are confronted with major stressors such as cancer. Second, they contribute to the formation of an integrated mechanistic pathway that accounts for communication behaviors within couples. Drawing on Walsh’s family resilience theory13 and the Interpersonal Process Model of Intimacy (IPMI)14, three core determinants were proposed: family resilience, intimacy satisfaction, and self-disclosure.

Grounded in Walsh’s family resilience theory, this study posits that families actively mobilize internal resources to foster resilience and growth when confronting crises15. Walsh conceptualizes family resilience through three key dimensions: belief systems, organizational patterns, and communication processes13.Belief systems refer to shared values, meaning-making, and hope that help family members contextualize the illness experience. Organizational patterns involve family roles, structural flexibility, and social support networks that provide practical resources for coping. Communication processes emphasize open, empathetic, and collaborative interactions that facilitate the integration of resources and the expression of shared beliefs. Within this framework, family resilience serves as the resource base for couple communication in the context of cancer. Specifically, belief systems provide emotional and cognitive resources that may help mitigate illness-related stigma16.Organizational resources offer practical support for communication and improve illness management17.Communication processes supply emotional resources that facilitate more effective communication patterns between partners18. Accordingly, family resilience is conceptualized as the “resource” component within the proposed framework.

According to the IPMI14, intimacy is established through a dynamic interpersonal process, the core of which is a reciprocal cycle of self-disclosure and partner responsiveness. In this process, one partner discloses personal thoughts, feelings, and needs, and the other responds with understanding, validation, and care. Such interactions foster emotional bonding and deepen relational intimacy. Within the context of cancer, intimacy may not only provides emotional security for couples but also may motivates partners to confront the illness together. On the one hand, it may encourage patients to openly discuss disease-related concerns with their spouses; on the other hand, it enhances spouses’ willingness and motivation to provide support. Previous studies have shown that strong intimacy can function as a “family buffer,”19helping to reduce patients’ psychological distress and anxiety20.Furthermore, it may mitigate tendencies toward conflict or avoidance within the couple relationship and promote constructive communication regarding treatment decisions and recovery planning. Accordingly, intimacy is conceptualized as the “motivation component” within the proposed framework.

Self- disclosure21 refers to the extent and depth to which patients are willing to share their distress, needs, fears, and illness-related experiences with their spouses. It represents a key process variable in the development of intimacy and is incorporated in this study as an “activation mechanism.” According to the IPMI14, self-disclosure functions as a “starting valve,” initiating partner responsiveness and facilitating the establishment of emotional connection. In this way, self-disclosure activates the communication process by transforming the resources provided by family resilience and the motivations generated by intimacy into concrete communication behaviors. Higher levels of self-disclosure22 may encourage patients to openly express their pain, needs, and fears, thereby eliciting emotional support and informational feedback from their spouses and promoting constructive communication. In contrast, lower levels of self-disclosure23 may keep these resources and motivations in a latent state, which may lead couples to adopt avoidant communication patterns in response to intense emotions. Accordingly, self-disclosure is conceptualized as the “activation component” within the proposed framework.

In summary, the proposed framework posits a “resource–motivation–activation” pathway. Within this framework, family resilience (the resource component) provides the foundational belief systems, organizational support, and emotional resources necessary for effective communication. Intimacy (the motivation component) transforms these resources into psychological motivation for constructive communication between partners. Self-disclosure (the activation component) further converts these resources and motivations into concrete interaction strategies during couple communication. Together, these three factors may exert a combined influence on communication patterns. Based on this framework, it is hypothesized that higher levels of family resilience, intimacy, and self-disclosure would positively predict constructive communication and negatively predict demand–withdraw and mutual avoidance communication patterns (Fig. 1).

Methods

Research participants

Participant selection

Between July 2023 and September 2024, colorectal cancer patients receiving inpatient treatment at a tertiary hospital in Anhui Province were recruited using a convenience sampling method.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age ≥ 18 years with a confirmed pathological diagnosis of colorectal cancer. (2) Sufficient language and communication abilities to independently complete the questionnaire. (3) Voluntary provision of informed consent to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Presence of other severe physical illnesses, diagnosed mental disorders, or a history of psychiatric illness that could interfere with the participant’s ability to complete the survey. (2) Being divorced or widowed. (3) Current participation in other research studies.

No specific inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied regarding time since diagnosis. All participants were hospitalized colorectal cancer patients receiving standardized treatment (surgery and/or chemotherapy/radiotherapy) and were required to be alert and able to complete the questionnaire independently.

Sample size calculation

Based on the criterion that the minimum sample size should be 5–10 times the number of independent variables, and with 25 predictors included in this study, the required sample size was estimated. Accounting for an anticipated 20% rate of attrition and invalid responses, the minimum required sample size was calculated as follows: 25 × (5–10) ÷ (1–0.2) = 156–313. The final sample consisted of 306 participants, which met the theoretical requirement.

Research tools

General information questionnaire (GIQ)

The GIQ was developed by the research team. Demographic information included 11 items: age, gender, length of marriage, ethnicity, place of residence, religious affiliation, educational level, occupation, monthly household income, primary caregiver, and method of medical payment. Disease-related information comprised eight items: site of diagnosis, presence or absence of a stoma, treatment modality, pathological stage, time since surgery, presence or absence of metastasis, number of chronic conditions, and history of previous surgeries. Time since surgery, rather than time since diagnosis, was recorded as a measure because it is highly correlated with time since diagnosis; most patients undergo surgery within 1–2 weeks of pathological confirmation. Therefore, it serves as an indirect indicator of the post-diagnosis period.

Communication pattern questionnaire (CPQ)

The Chinese version of the Couple CPQ, originally developed by Heavey et al.24and translated and psychometrically validated by Dai et al.10, was used to assess communication patterns in couples in which one partner had colorectal cancer. This 16-item instrument comprises three subscales: Constructive Communication, Demand/Withdraw Communication, and Mutual Avoidance Communication. The Constructive Communication subscale includes three items measuring mutual constructive communication and four items measuring mutual destructive communication; the subscale score is calculated by subtracting the sum of destructive items from the sum of constructive items. The Demand/Withdraw subscale contains six items in total: three assessing patient-demand/spouse-withdraw patterns and three assessing spouse-demand/patient-withdraw patterns, with scores summed across all six items. The Mutual Avoidance subscale score is the sum of its three items. All responses are recorded on a 9-point Likert scale (1 = “least likely,” 9 = “most likely”), with higher scores indicating stronger tendencies toward the corresponding communication pattern. In this study, the CPQ demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.835. Subscale reliability coefficients were 0.789 for Constructive Communication, 0.812 for Demand/Withdraw Communication, and 0.753 for Mutual Avoidance Communication.

Family resilience questionnaire (FaRE)

The Chinese version of the FaRE Scale, originally developed by Faccio et al.25and translated and psychometrically validated by Li26., was used to assess family resilience among colorectal cancer patients. The questionnaire comprises 24 items across four dimensions: communication and cohesion, perception of social support, perception of family coping, and religious belief. Responses are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater levels of family resilience. In the present study, the FaRE demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.918. Subscale reliability coefficients were 0.876 for communication and cohesion, 0.823 for perception of social support, 0.845 for perception of family coping, and 0.732 for religious belief.

The quality of relationship index (QRI)

The Chinese adaptation of the Questionnaire for Marital Intimacy (QRI), developed and revised by Patrick et al.27and translated and psychometrically validated by Shan28. was used to assess marital intimacy satisfaction among colorectal cancer patients, as reflected in relationship strength, stability, and emotional closeness. The QRI comprises six items and employs a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with marital intimacy. In the present study, the QRI demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.893.

The distress disclosure index (DDI)

The Chinese version of the Distress Disclosure Inventory (DDI), originally developed by Kahn et al.29and translated and psychometrically validated by Li30., was used to assess colorectal cancer patients’ willingness to disclose distress, pain, and personal needs to their spouses. The questionnaire comprises 12 items and employs a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with items 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, and 10 reverse-scored. Higher total scores indicate a greater propensity for self-disclosure. In the present study, the DDI demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.927.

Data collection and quality control

Participants were recruited through a collaborative effort between researchers from Anqing Medical College and the medical and nursing teams of the Gastrointestinal Surgery Department at Anqing Municipal Hospital. During routine clinical care, attending surgeons and primary nurses conducted a preliminary screening of hospitalized colorectal cancer patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and provided a brief introduction to the study. Patients who expressed initial interest were subsequently visited by the research team, which offered detailed explanations using standardized instructions, covering the study’s purpose, procedures, questionnaire completion methods, confidentiality measures, and the voluntary nature of participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they had fully understood the study, and questionnaires were completed independently on-site. No flyers or word-of-mouth recruitment strategies were used. For participants who experienced difficulties, the investigator read each item aloud verbatim, allowing patients to provide informed and accurate responses, which were recorded by the investigator.

Questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site, with immediate verification by the research team. Each questionnaire was checked for missing items, and participants were asked to complete any omissions immediately. Consequently, all valid questionnaires included in the analysis contained no missing data. Of the 320 questionnaires distributed, 14 were excluded due to incomplete responses or patterned answers, resulting in 306 valid responses and an effective response rate of 95.63%. All study procedures, data collection methods, and measures to protect participant rights strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant regulations. The research protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Anqing Municipal Hospital (Approval No.: 2023 − 151; Date: June 18, 2023). Prior to data collection, participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and privacy protections, and all provided voluntary written informed consent. Participants’ rights to access information, voluntarily participate, withdraw at any time, and maintain the confidentiality of personal data were fully safeguarded throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Quantitative data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas qualitative data were summarized using frequencies and percentages (%). Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed to examine demographic differences in scores for constructive communication, demand–withdraw communication, and mutual avoidance communication. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore associations between family resilience, intimacy, self-disclosure, and couple communication patterns. To identify factors influencing couple communication, three separate stepwise multiple linear regression analyses were performed, with constructive communication, demand–withdraw communication, and mutual avoidance communication serving as dependent variables in distinct models. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Between group comparisons were quantified using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and standardized regression coefficients (β) with their 95% CIs were reported for multiple regression analyses. Prior to conducting the multiple linear regression analyses, variables that were statistically significant (P < 0.05) in univariate analyses were identified.

Only these variables, along with the core study variables, were entered into the regression models to minimize the influence of extraneous factors. Stepwise regression was employed to further control for multicollinearity. All statistical analyses met the key assumptions for linear regression: (1) Normality: Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that all quantitative measures were normally distributed (P > 0.05). (2) Homogeneity of variance: Levene’s tests confirmed equal variances across groups (P > 0.05). (3) Independence: All participants were independent hospitalized patients, with no paired or clustered correlations. (4) Absence of multicollinearity: Variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all independent variables were < 3.

Univariate analysis on general information of colorectal cancer patients and scores of couple communication patterns

Univariate analyses revealed several significant differences in couple communication patterns among colorectal cancer patients. Male patients scored significantly higher on demand–withdraw and mutual avoidance communication, but lower on constructive communication compared to female patients (P < 0.05, Cohen’s d=−0.336 ~ 0.475). Constructive communication scores were positively associated with advancing age, whereas demand–withdraw and mutual avoidance scores were negatively associated with age (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.071 ~ 0.194); patients aged 60 years and above exhibited the highest levels of constructive communication. Regarding education level and monthly household income per capita, patients with higher education and income reported greater constructive communication scores and lower demand–withdraw communication scores (P < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.024 ~ 0.051). Patients whose primary caregiver was their spouse demonstrated significantly higher constructive communication scores than those cared for by others (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d=−0.695 ~ 0.787). Concerning medical payment methods, patients covered by rural cooperative medical schemes or basic medical insurance reported the highest constructive communication scores, while self-paying patients had higher demand–withdraw communication scores (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.065 ~ 0.155). Patients with either permanent or temporary ostomies exhibited higher demand–withdraw communication scores (P < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.037 ~ 0.065). For pathological stage, patients with stage II or III cancer scored higher on constructive communication than those with stage I or IV cancer, whereas stage IV patients showed the highest levels of mutual avoidance communication (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.060 ~ 0.068).

Time since surgery was not significantly associated with scores for constructive communication, demand–withdraw communication, or mutual avoidance communication (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that the post-diagnosis period, as reflected by time since surgery, was not significantly associated with couple communication patterns among colorectal cancer patients; therefore, this variable was excluded from subsequent multiple linear regression analyses. Discussion

Current status, influencing factors, and mechanisms of constructive communication

The results indicated that constructive communication scores among colorectal cancer patients were relatively low, suggesting that their capacity for constructive communication requires improvement. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted among stroke and breast cancer patients in China31,32. As a major traumatic stressor, a colorectal cancer diagnosis not only compromises physical functioning but also frequently triggers negative emotions such as anxiety and fear33. To avoid imposing additional caregiving or psychological burdens on their spouses, patients often deliberately avoid discussing disease-related issues34. Moreover, changes in body image resulting from an ostomy may lead spouses to limit active communication for fear of addressing the patient’s psychological vulnerabilities35. Both of these factors may impede the development of constructive communication patterns necessary for collaborative problem solving.

Multiple linear regression analyses identified age and primary caregiver as demographic factors influencing constructive communication among colorectal cancer patients. Older patients demonstrated higher constructive communication scores, which may be attributable to more mature emotional regulation and greater marital adjustment, predisposing them to approach problems in a calm and collaborative manner36. Patients whose primary caregiver was their spouse scored significantly higher than those in other caregiver groups. This association may be related to the dual role of spousal caregiving, which combines physical support with emotional connection; through verbal reassurance and encouragement, spouses may help create a safe environment that facilitates open and constructive communication37.

Family resilience, intimacy, and self-disclosure emerged as positive predictors of constructive communication among colorectal cancer patients, providing preliminary support for the hypothesized “resource–motivation–activation” pathway. Family resilience, conceptualized as the “resource component,” may provide a supportive foundation for couple communication. When confronted with surgical trauma, ostomy adjustment, chemotherapy side effects, and long-term rehabilitation challenges, families with high resilience may be better able to integrate belief systems, organizational resources, and emotional support38. A stable family belief system may create conditions conducive to constructive communication, as spouses proactively acquire knowledge about ostomy care and chemotherapy management, making interactions more targeted and collaborative39. In this way, family resilience may provide essential resource security for constructive communication. Intimacy serves as the “motivation component,” providing the intrinsic drive for effective couple communication. Anxiety, stigma, and psychological distress are frequently provoked by the diagnosis and treatment of colorectal cancer. High-quality intimacy may function as a “family buffer,” reducing patients’ tendency to withhold communication40. Elevated levels of intimacy may enhance couples’ motivation to engage in open and honest discussions about diagnosis, treatment, symptom management, and psychological needs, thereby supplying motivational support for constructive communication41. Self-disclosure constitutes the “activation component,” translating resources and motivation into concrete communication behaviors. Colorectal cancer patients may limit disease-related expression due to concerns about changes in physical functioning and privacy. However, patients with higher levels of self-disclosure are more willing to share their experiences with their spouses. Clear and timely self-disclosure may facilitate constructive communication, allowing couples to understand each other’s condition more accurately and collaborate in addressing practical challenges arising during treatment and rehabilitation42.

Current status, influencing factors, and mechanisms of demand/withdraw communication

Demand–withdraw communication scores were the highest among the three communication patterns. Notably, scores for spouse-demand/patient-withdraw were slightly higher than those for patient-demand/spouse-withdraw, suggesting that this pattern represents the predominant form of couple communication. This finding aligns with clinical observations that family members, motivated by concern, actively seek communication, while patients, burdened by psychological distress, tend to withdraw35. Fundamentally, this pattern may reflect an imbalance in couple communication under disease-related stress, in which one partner’s demand and the other’s withdrawal may create a maladaptive cycle that could further exacerbate communication barriers.

Multiple linear regression analyses identified gender, age, ostomy status, and pathological stage as demographic and clinical factors associated with demand–withdraw communication. Male patients scored significantly higher on this pattern than females, which may be explained by traditional masculine norms emphasizing “toughness and endurance”43, discouraging the expression of vulnerability and potentially predisposing men to silence or withdrawal when faced with communication demands. Younger patients (aged 18–44 years) scored higher than middle-aged and older patients, potentially because younger individuals struggle to confront premature mortality and, together with their spouses, may lack experience in coping with serious illness. Discussions about prognosis and end-of-life issues may therefore elicit withdrawal in response to uncertainty36. Patients with permanent ostomies scored significantly higher than those without ostomies or with temporary ostomies. Dependency on ostomy care may generate feelings of shame, leading patients to avoid discussions of ostomy-related issues through withdrawal44. Additionally, higher pathological stage was associated with elevated demand–withdraw communication scores; patients with advanced disease, who experience more severe symptoms and greater risks of recurrence or metastasis, may hold more negative illness perceptions that could hinder their ability to respond to their spouse’s communication demands45.

Intimacy emerged as a core factor that significantly and negatively predicted demand–withdraw communication, providing preliminary support for the regulatory role of the “motivation component” within the resource–motivation–activation framework. Couples with high intimacy may share strong emotional bonds and may demonstrate better mutual understanding of each other’s psychological states. When facing sensitive colorectal cancer-related topics, such as ostomy care, treatment-related discomfort, or prognostic concerns, they may be more likely to communicate with empathy and acceptance, which might reduce the occurrence of demand–withdraw interactions46. Conversely, couples with low intimacy often lack sufficient emotional support and mutual understanding. One partner’s anxious questioning or emotional expression may readily elicit withdrawal or silence from the other, potentially perpetuating a maladaptive demand–withdraw cycle47. Notably, family resilience and self-disclosure did not enter the regression model, suggesting that intimacy may have a more prominent role than family resilience or self-disclosure in relation to this communication pattern within the current model. Demand–withdraw communication therefore may appear to reflect an affective coping imbalance under disease-related stress.

Current status, influencing factors, and mechanisms of mutual avoidance communication

Although mutual avoidance communication scores were lower than demand–withdraw scores among colorectal cancer patients, this pattern warrants considerable clinical attention. In mutual avoidance communication, couples fail to engage in effective dialogue, resulting in a lack of emotional support and exacerbating patient feelings of loneliness and helplessness. This pattern may reflect the shared psychological stress experienced by both partners: patients, burdened by the physical and emotional toll of colorectal cancer, may tend to avoid discussing disease-related topics, while spouses, concerned about triggering negative emotions in patients and feeling inadequately prepared to provide effective support, may also engage in avoidant communication. The outcome is a state of “mutual silence and avoidance”35.

Multiple linear regression analyses identified age and primary caregiver as demographic factors influencing mutual avoidance communication. Older patients exhibited lower mutual avoidance scores, which may reflect a more rational perspective toward illness and greater congruence in marital communication, and might increase the likelihood of breaking periods of silence36. Patients whose primary caregiver was not their spouse scored significantly higher. Caregivers such as adult children or paid attendants may have weaker emotional bonds with the patient, lack motivation for active communication, and fail to recognize the patient’s psychological needs, potentially resulting in a pattern of “caregiving without communication”37.

Among the core study variables, both intimacy and self-disclosure significantly and negatively predicted mutual avoidance communication, highlighting their potential importance in mitigating this maladaptive pattern. The predictive effect of intimacy was particularly pronounced. As the core “motivation component,” intimacy may provide couples with emotional security, reduce anxiety among colorectal cancer patients48, and encourage active engagement with the illness, which might decrease mutual avoidance at the motivational level. The negative predictive effect of self-disclosure emphasizes the role of the “activation component.” Patients with high levels of self-disclosure may be more willing to express concerns about privacy, treatment-related discomfort, and emotional distress, which may reduce anticipatory grief and emotional suppression49. Such disclosure may elicit supportive responses from spouses, and might gradually transform the pattern of mutual avoidance communication. Conversely, low intimacy may limit motivation for communication, while low self-disclosure may fail to activate effective communication behaviors; this may lead to patient resistance to treatment plans and increased risk of complications50. Collectively, these two factors may reinforce the emergence of mutual avoidance communication. Notably, family resilience did not enter the regression model, suggesting that intimacy and self-disclosure may have more direct associations with mutual avoidance communication than family resilience within the current model.

Validation of the “resource-motivation-activation” theoretical framework

The hypothesized “resource–motivation–activation” framework, grounded in Walsh’s family resilience theory13 and the IPMI14, appears to be supported in the context of couple communication patterns among colorectal cancer patients. Within this framework, family resilience functions as the “resource component,” integrating internal and external family resources to provide a foundation for family communication. It may support the development of constructive communication while indirectly mitigating demand–withdraw communication patterns through resource provision. Intimacy serves as the “motivation component,” bridging resources and communication behaviors. By providing emotional security, intimacy may enhance the value of family resilience resources, may facilitate patient self-disclosure, and may reduce tendencies toward mutual avoidance communication. Self-disclosure, as the “activation component,” translates the resources afforded by family resilience and the motivation derived from intimacy into concrete interactive strategies, potentially promoting constructive communication and disrupting the silence characteristic of mutual avoidance communication.

The resource–motivation–activation pathway may provide a useful explanation for the heterogeneity observed in couple communication patterns among colorectal cancer patients. The combination of high family resilience, strong intimacy, and active self-disclosure may promote the development of constructive communication. Conversely, deficiencies in any of these components may contribute to avoidant communication patterns, including demand–withdraw and mutual avoidance. For instance, insufficient family resilience may limit the resources available to support communication; low intimacy may undermine intrinsic motivation to engage; and inadequate self-disclosure may fail to translate available resources and motivation into concrete communication behaviors. Deficits in these domains may predispose couples to maladaptive communication patterns. This framework may offer a systematic theoretical foundation for designing interventions aimed at improving couple communication in colorectal cancer contexts.

Clinical implications based on communication patterns

Considering the current status and influencing factors of the three communication patterns, clinical interventions may follow the principle of “targeted intervention based on pattern classification.” Interventions may address demographic characteristics such as gender and age, for example, challenging traditional masculine norms among male patients to counter the mindset of “unwilling to show weakness,” or providing illness-related education and emotional guidance for younger patients to promote a more rational understanding of the disease, while simultaneously drawing on the resource–motivation–activation framework. Interventions may focus on the three core dimensions: enhancing family resilience, improving intimacy, and encouraging self-disclosure, with the goal of optimizing couple communication patterns among colorectal cancer patients. For patients with insufficient constructive communication, interventions may aim to strengthen family resilience by promoting resource integration, guiding family members to participate jointly in coping with the illness, enhancing couple intimacy, and providing psychological support that may help patients release negative emotions and increase their willingness to communicate actively. For patients predominantly exhibiting demand–withdraw communication, couple communication skills training51 may be effective in improving intimacy, guiding patients or spouses to express communication needs more gently, and potentially reducing avoidance behaviors. For patients demonstrating mutual avoidance communication, interventions may focus on enhancing intimacy through emotional support52 to help create a secure communication environment, while encouraging patients to engage in self-disclosure via methods such as expressive writing, group counseling53, or emotional support group activities54. These approaches may facilitate active disclosure of inner feelings to the spouse and simultaneously guide spouses to perceive patient needs and respond with empathic communication.

Limitations

Although this study systematically examined the current status and influencing factors of couple communication patterns among colorectal cancer patients and provided preliminary support for the resource–motivation–activation theoretical framework, several important limitations are acknowledged.

First, the cross-sectional design with single-time-point data collection only allows for concurrent correlations. Temporal precedence cannot be established, reverse causality (e.g., poor communication may result from reduced family resilience) cannot be ruled out, and confounding by unmeasured variables such as personality traits cannot be excluded. This design does not allow for causal inferences. Future longitudinal research could employ repeated measurements at 1, 6, and 12 months post-surgery and use cross-lagged panel models to test causal directions.

Second, due to practical and ethical constraints, no demographic or clinical data were collected from patients who declined participation. This prevents comparisons between responders and non-responders and may introduce selection bias. Future studies could seek ethical approval to record basic characteristics of these patients, which could enable non-response bias analysis.

Third, all data were derived from patient self-reports, with no corresponding spouse data. Couple communication is fundamentally a dyadic process, and single-party reports are susceptible to individual cognitive biases and cannot capture perceptual congruence between partners. Future dyadic designs using the Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) could allow for in-depth bidirectional analyses.

Fourth, 60.8% of participants were married for ≥ 20 years, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to shorter marriage cohorts. Future studies could use stratified sampling or conduct subgroup analyses to address this limitation.

Fifth, this was a single-center convenience sample of inpatients from a Grade-A tertiary hospital in Anhui Province, China. These results may not be generalizable to other geographic regions or outpatient colorectal cancer patient populations. Future multi-center, cross-regional surveys including diverse healthcare settings could be valuable to enhance the external validity of this study.

Future directions

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study provides novel insights and a theoretical foundation for psychosocial interventions targeting colorectal cancer patients. Future research may develop targeted couple communication interventions based on the resource–motivation–activation framework and evaluate their effectiveness through randomized controlled trials. Further investigation of the mediating and moderating mechanisms through which family resilience, intimacy, and self-disclosure influence couple communication patterns could advance theoretical understanding. Additionally, subgroup analyses focusing on specific populations, such as patients with permanent ostomies, advanced-stage colorectal cancer, or younger patients, could elucidate unique communication characteristics and intervention priorities, thereby informing precision clinical interventions.

Future intervention studies may consider integrating couple communication interventions into comprehensive nursing care for colorectal cancer patients. With core objectives of enhancing family resilience, improving intimacy, and promoting self-disclosure, multidisciplinary collaboration involving nursing and psychological services may provide tailored communication guidance and emotional support for both patients and their spouses. Improved couple communication patterns resulting from such interventions may ultimately improve patients’ psychological well-being and overall quality of life.
