Multi-infusion setups in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) a mass transport evaluation and graphical user interface development
Authors: Gloria Palazzo, Maria Grazia Romitti, Carmen Rodriguez-Perez, Elena Pezzotti, Cristina Serena Marzari, Eleonora Salurso, Gianfranco Beniamino Fiore, Alberto Luigi Cesare Redaelli, Clarissa Ferrari, Francesco Maria Risso, Giuseppe Paterlini

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritically ill neonates in Intensive Care Units require complex pharmacological and nutritional therapies often administered through small-calibre, single-lumen vascular accesses. This practice, known as multi-infusion, involves the simultaneous administration of multiple solutions, presenting significant challenges in terms of fluid dynamic management and clinical safety. This study analyses the performance of common infusion line configurations, focusing on transit times and the concentration profiles of solutions delivered to the patient. A software tool was implemented to simulate and predict solute transport under various infusion settings, providing an instrument for assessing real-life clinical scenarios. Furthermore, a practical graphical user interface was developed to translate these complex numerical methods into an accessible educational tool. As a preliminary study, it allows clinicians to input patient-specific details and infusion parameters, and the application calculates and visualizes the underlying fluid dynamics in real-time. This approach enables the pre-emptive simulation of various infusion settings, making it possible to predict concentration profiles and transit times. The interface improves clinical awareness by enabling healthcare providers to simulate and compare real-world scenarios. Introduction

Critically ill neonates, often premature, admitted to Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICU) require complex support, including nutrition, vasoactive medications, sedatives, and antibiotics. These fragile patients have immature and underdeveloped organs and systems that are highly vulnerable and poorly adapted to the external challenges they face outside the uterine environment. Their gastrointestinal system is unable to tolerate the volumes of enteral feeding required to meet their high caloric and nutritional needs; therefore, the preferred route of nutritional support is via the venous circulation. However, neonatal veins are extremely small in calibre, leaving limited options - primarily the umbilical vein in the first days of life - for vascular access. The low blood volume and limited drug metabolism capacity in these patients1 require exceptionally small fluid volumes and precise flow regulation, as even minor deviations can result in severe clinical consequences, such as fluctuations in cerebral blood flow or renal dysfunction2. Consequently, most treatments must be administered through a single venous access. The most commonly used vascular accesses for this patient population are the Umbilical Venous Catheter (UVC) at birth, the Epicutaneo-Caval Catheter (ECC), Centrally Inserted Central Catheters (CICC), or Femorally Inserted Central Catheters (FICC) placed under ultrasound guidance3. The CICC and FICC are placed in NICUs where staff are specifically trained and qualified to perform the procedure, which requires effective sedation and appropriate equipment; however, the ECC remains the most widely used device, despite being the smallest and least efficient in terms of flow performance. Clinicians often face the challenge of multi-infusion therapy, where 4 to 10 or more different solutions converge into a single lumen.

Multi-infusion therapy in the NICU introduces significant fluid-dynamic complexities that can compromise dosing accuracy. According to the literature4,5, these challenges stem from the interaction of three primary physical factors:

Dead volume: The internal space where different solutions converge acts as a reservoir. At the characteristically low neonatal flow rates, this volume significantly delays drug arrival and prolongs the time required to reach steady-state concentrations6,7.

From a clinical perspective, these phenomena translate into a high risk of dosing errors, particularly for medications with a narrow therapeutic window, like inotropes. The system’s balance is further destabilized by the frequent need to modify the setup, such as transitioning between 24-hour continuous nutrition and intermittent medication administration. Meticulous planning of the “spatial organization” of the infusion set (i.e., “where to put what”) is crucial to prevent adverse drug interactions and ensure that medications reach the patient at the most accurate prescribed concentration.

Despite these findings, the absence of standardized protocols for multi-infusion setups in NICUs remains a significant gap. Currently, NICUs rely on local practices and practical expertise, which are often limited by the lack of patient-specific quantitative tools. Effective planning of the infusion set based on the number and type of medications is essential, yet clinicians lack the means to visualize the real-time impact of their configuration choices.

The objective of this research is to overcome these limitations by providing a software framework suitable for use by experienced clinicians to better understand the fluid dynamics of multi-infusion, or by apprentices as a virtual simulator for training. By combining mass transport evaluations with a dedicated testing interface, this study aims to: (1) investigate the fluid dynamics of multi-infusion systems, focusing on transit times and concentration profiles; (2) develop a user-friendly interface as an educational and awareness tool for clinicians to visualize and compare different infusion configurations to select the most adequate one for the specific patient. To this end, a mathematical model was developed to simulate fluid dynamics and drug concentration delivery, based on the experimental characterization of real-world infusion components (e.g., infusion lines, connectors, and catheters). Additionally, a dedicated Graphical User Interface (GUI) was developed to provide an intuitive prototype tool for clinical staff to launch simulations and visualize the results of different infusion setups.

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first study to integrate patient-specific infusion transport simulations with a dedicated, clinician-oriented planning GUI specifically designed for NICU multi-infusion systems. The clinical urgency is underscored by recent systematic evidence highlighting that neonates are at the highest risk for medication errors due to the complexity of micro-infusion setups. While standardizing concentrations can reduce error risks by up to 95%11, existing solutions lack a proactive planning component to visualize the physical transit delays. Our work fills this gap by translating fluid dynamic modeling into an accessible bedside tool, addressing a critical and currently unmet need in neonatal safety.

Results

Preliminary mass transport evaluations were performed to characterize the transport mechanisms within the multi-infusion system. The flow regime was identified as purely laminar, consistent with the micro-flow rates typical of neonatal therapy. Mass transport is dominated by convection, as evidenced by high Péclet numbers indicating that molecular diffusion has a negligible impact on the overall concentration profiles compared to advective transport.

Discussion

The clinical challenges of multi-infusion therapy in neonatal care are well-documented, yet they remain largely “invisible” at the bedside. While previous studies have defined flow variability as potentially hazardous2,5,7,12, a significant gap persists between theoretical awareness and practical tools for real-time risk mitigation. Our study addresses this gap by providing a quantitative framework that translates complex mass transport phenomena into a patient-specific clinical tool. Since 2010, European e-learning and best-practice programs have addressed this issue to increase awareness13,14. Van der Eijk et al.12 defined the observed flow variability as “potentially hazardous,” particularly for preterm neonates with immature organs, leading to dangerous dosing errors for drugs with a narrow therapeutic range, such as inotropes2,5,7,15.

While Maiguy-Foinard et al.16 analysed how infusion set architecture impacts delivery, clear clinical recommendations remain scarce. Recently developed methods focused on real-time monitoring17,18, but the novelty of our work lies in the upstream planning and patient-specific evaluation conducted before administration. Using the case studies, the GUI highlights that during the transit phase, actual delivery is significantly lower due to dead volume. Visualizing this delay is crucial to prevent clinicians from prematurely increasing flow rates when an immediate effect is not observed, thereby avoiding subsequent overdose once the drug reaches the patient.

Our results align with the experimental trends observed by Krysiak et al.7 regarding transit time and washout dynamics. In the Continuous Infusion scenario, we observed a nearly 3-fold difference in time depending on the vascular access: 31 minutes were required to reach 95% of the target steady-state for UVC, compared to only 11 min 20 s for ECC. This discrepancy is purely mechanical, driven by the relationship between internal geometry and parabolic velocity profile. The ECC has a significantly reduced internal diameter, and that forces the fluid to reach higher fluid velocities compared to the UVC at identical flow rates. Consequently, the drug front displaces the priming solution in the dead volume more rapidly, shortening the clinical latency. From a clinical perspective, however, the UVC remains the first choice device at birth for infusions, blood withdrawal, transfusions and central pressure monitoring. Therefore, these findings do not necessarily suggest a clinical preference for one catheter over the other, but rather highlight a critical need for augmented clinical awareness. The 31-minute lag of the UVC represents a substantial ‘Latency-Subtherapeutic Gap’ that is currently invisible to bedside staff. Without tools like the proposed GUI to visualize this delay, clinicians may misinterpret the lag as a lack of drug efficacy, potentially leading to unnecessary dose escalations or ‘rescue boluses’ that result in unintended overdoses once steady-state is finally achieved. Thus, the GUI serves as a vital decision-support layer for the safe management of indispensable, yet slow-reacting, vascular accesses.

Furthermore, the Intermittent Infusion simulation reveals a “Double-Nadir” phenomenon: background therapies suffer prolonged sub-therapeutic windows not only during the new drug infusion but also during the saline flush, confirming that a simple flush destabilizes all other medications in the circuit. In the current prototype, these procedures are simulated using fixed time-steps—specifically a 40-second pause for syringe change and a flush duration mirroring the drug infusion—to represent a common clinical scenario.In this context, the GUI serves as a tool to visualize the risks associated with current protocols and it provides clinicians with a quantitative basis to understand the ‘invisible’ duration and depth of these sub-therapeutic gaps. This visualization is crucial for clinical optimization: it allows the medical team to anticipate hemodynamic fluctuations and provides a scientific rationale for potentially revising institutional flush protocols for high-risk drugs. By making these transitions visible, the GUI transforms a routine procedure into a conscious, data-driven clinical action.

The New Continuous Infusion scenario introduces the concept of Decoupled Latency. When a third pump is added, the volumetric flow rate increases instantly, speeding up the delivery of pre-existing infusions before the new drug arrives. This creates a dangerous window where the neonate receives a transient surge in mass flow rate. Additionally, we observed a failure to reach target concentrations for short Intermittent Infusions (Drug 3), which reached only 40.7% of its nominal value. This “Truncated Delivery” proves that transit dynamics can prevent the patient from ever receiving the programmed dose, suggesting that monitoring these fluctuations is essential for maintaining system stability.

The spatial architecture of the infusion set further compounds these delays, as demonstrated by our comparison of proximal and distal connection points in Setup C. The simulation revealed that introducing a new medication through a distal line instead of a proximal one results in an additional transit delay of approximately 1 min and 22 s (82 s). Although this difference may appear minor in routine infusion contexts, it may be clinically relevant in the NICU, particularly during acute stabilization or when administering vasoactive agents with rapid pharmacodynamic effects, such as adrenaline or dopamine. This delay is not directly observable at the bedside and may therefore lead to misinterpretation of the patient’s response. Clinicians may prematurely escalate doses under the assumption of treatment failure, when in fact the initial dose has not yet reached the systemic circulation. Such unintended dose may delay the achievement of the desired hemodynamic effect and increase the risk of overshoot once drug delivery converges.These findings suggest that the physical organization of the multi-infusion setup is a critical, yet often overlooked, variable in ensuring timely pharmacological delivery. Optimizing line organization may therefore contribute to more predictable pharmacological responses and safer administration of time-critical therapies in critically ill neonates.

Limitations and future development

While the proposed framework provides a robust analysis, certain limitations exist. First, the component library is currently restricted to the specific infusion sets and geometries utilized by the clinical partners involved in this study; future iterations will aim to expand the hardware library. Additionally, the mathematical framework is optimized and validated for the low-flow range typical of neonatal micro-infusions. Applications outside this boundary, where different fluid dynamic scales or transient behaviors may emerge, fall outside the scope of this initial prototype.

Geometrical assumptions for Needle Free Connectors (NFC) and anti-reflux valves were approximated as cylindrical; however, complex internal geometries may induce minor deviations in latency times. Furthermore, the fluid was modelled as Newtonian and incompressible. While valid for most medications, highly viscous solutions like Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) may require non-Newtonian modelling. However, given the extremely low Reynolds numbers () typical of neonatal infusions, the system operates within a laminar flow regime, where transport is primarily governed by volumetric flow rate, internal geometry, and dead volume displacement. While a non-Newtonian velocity profile could theoretically deviate from the parabolic distribution and cause alterations in local transit-time dynamics, the system’s mass transport remains predominantly convection-driven at very high Péclet numbers (). Consequently, the Newtonian approximation provides a reliable estimation of clinical latency. Future developments will incorporate non-Newtonian constitutive models to further refine transport predictions for highly concentrated nutritional formulations.

Regarding intermittent practices, the current software implementation utilizes fixed time-steps for clinical manoeuvres—specifically a 40-second pause for syringe changes and a saline flush duration mirroring the drug infusion time. While these values represent a representative clinical case study based on observed NICU practices, they do not yet account for the high variability in manual dexterity or institutional protocols. Future GUI updates will focus on allowing user-defined intervals for these auxiliary procedures to enable a more personalized optimization of the ‘Double-Nadir’ window. In addition, the current numerical implementation relies on a first-order explicit Euler scheme for the advection equation. While effective for this proof-of-concept, this approach is computationally intensive and requires strictly small time steps to maintain numerical stability. Future code optimization will focus on implementing more stable and efficient semi-implicit or higher-order schemes to reduce execution time and enhance accuracy. Finally, integrating these solver improvements with Fluid Structure Interaction (FSI) to account for catheter compliance, which may induce micro-fluctuations under peristaltic pump pulsations, represents a significant future advancement.

Conclusion

This study aims to bridge the gap between engineering theory and bedside practice, providing a foundational step toward safer pharmacological treatments in the NICU. The implementation of a clinician-oriented GUI enables healthcare professionals to visualize the impact of dead volume and flow rate variations on dosing accuracy. The practical utility of the GUI lies in its ability to transform invisible fluid dynamic risks into predictive insights for clinical planning. Rather than a real-time decision-making tool, the current prototype serves as an educational instrument. For instance, by visualizing ‘Clinical Latency,’ staff can perform a pre-analysis of specific setups to anticipate the time required for a newly started vasoactive drug to reach the bloodstream. This awareness could help prevent premature and potentially dangerous dose adjustments that might occur when a clinician is unaware that the medication is still traversing the infusion line. Additionally, the tool allows for the comparative study of different configurations, such as the strategic selection of infusion ports or the optimization of carrier flow rates to minimize transit delays in extremely low-birth-weight infants. These examples demonstrate how the GUI serves as a knowledge-based system to align prescribed dosage and the physical reality of drug delivery, supporting a deeper understanding of multi-infusion risks during the therapy planning phase. Currently, an in vitro experimental validation campaign is underway to allow for a refined calibration of parameters. The long-term objective is the evolution of this tool into a comprehensive clinical decision support system that can prospectively compensate for infusion delays and tailor delivery to the specific neonate’s requirements. Ultimately, addressing multi-infusion challenges through such quantitative technologies is essential for improving NICU outcomes.

Fluid properties

To approximate physical behaviours, under a controlled Newtonian framework, standardized glucose-water (selected as a viscous model that could represent the TPN) and saline-water solutions (representing other medications) at T = 25 °C were adopted. These model fluids allow for controlled evaluations of laminar flow conditions with Reynolds numbers Re<13 and negligible diffusion effects due to high Peclet numbers (). At each nodal junction a perfect mixing assumption was implemented to determine the resultant concentration of the converging streams. This simplification is supported also by the calculated hydrodynamic entrance length (), which remained below 0.109 mm for the simulated flow regimes—significantly shorter than the geometric length of the connection hubs. Consequently, treating the junctions as ideal nodes with instantaneous homogenization provides a reliable approximation for predicting the overall concentration profiles at the patient side. Numerical model validation

To ensure the reliability of the numerical simulations, a formal validation was conducted by comparing the computational results against the analytical solution for the mass transport equation in Setup A. An analysis was performed by testing eight different discretization configurations (Supplementary F), varying the temporal () and spatial () steps to evaluate grid independence and minimize approximation errors.

The performance of each test was quantified through two complementary error metrics: the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which assesses the overall adherence of the numerical concentration profile to the analytical sigmoidal curve, and the Percentage Relative Error (PRE), which measures the accuracy in predicting the initial transit time ().

Among the evaluated configurations, Test 4 was identified as the optimal discretization for this study. Although Test 7 showed a lower PRE for the onset of delivery, Test 4 achieved the lowest global MAE (3.55 mOsm/L), providing the most accurate representation of the dynamic concentration gradient and the approach to steady-state. This choice ensures a more robust representation of the full concentration-time profile, which is critical for accurately simulating complex phenomena such as drug-to-drug dilutions and the steady-state transitions. The agreement between the numerical (red) and analytical (blue) profiles in Test 4 (Fig. 8) confirms that the solver correctly captures the underlying physics of the system. This validation establishes a foundation for extending the numerical method to more complex configurations (Setup B and C) and varying infusion protocols, where analytical solutions are not available due to the intricate boundary conditions and mixing zones.

The tool quantifies how much the concentration of a primary drug is diluted when a new medication is added to the common dead volume, providing a numerical value for the final steady-state concentration (). The tool is designed to act as an educational resource to improve clinical awareness regarding infusion risks. By simulating different “what-if” scenarios (e.g., “What happens to the drug delivery if I change the carrier flow rate?“), the GUI provides a quantitative basis for optimizing infusion protocols, such as calculating the duration of a saline flush needed to clear drug remnants from the common dead space. The interface was refined based on informal feedback from NICU staff to ensure clinical relevance. GUI workflow is reported in Supplementary G.

GUI input parameters

To demonstrate the GUI’s capabilities and quantify fluid dynamics under realistic conditions, four distinct simulation scenarios were defined (Table 2). Setup A was employed to investigate standard continuous infusion (comparing UVC and ECC geometries), new drug additions, and intermittent administration. Setup C was specifically designed to simulate a high-complexity multi-infusion environment (5 concurrent medications) to quantify the impact of proximal vs. distal injection line on transit delays. No human participants were involved in this study. All simulation results were obtained using standard clinical parameters representative of a 1.5 kg neonate. Therefore, no patient data were collected or analyzed, and no ethical approval was required. It is also important to clarify that body weight functions primarily as a referential clinical parameter within the interface. The GUI does not currently incorporate an automated weight-based dose calculator; it relies entirely on the clinician to input weight-adjusted, pre-calculated flow rates and concentrations.
