Abstract
Teams frequently compete on multiple fronts: political parties contest districts for majority control, contractors field specialized units to win procurement contracts, and squads play match by match for titles. Although the prize accrues collectively to the winning team, individual incentives depend on how it is divided internally. We study a majoritarian team contest in which two rival managers simultaneously divide their teams’ prizes among heterogeneous members. The contest admits a unique pure-strategy equilibrium: both managers choose identical relative allocations—regardless of heterogeneity in winning values or player costs—with each battle’s share proportional to its discriminatory power, symmetry, and pivotality. JEL Classification Numbers: C61, C72, D72, D74. Keywords: Multi-battle Contest, Team Contest, Prize Allocation, Nash equilibrium.



1
Introduction

Many large-scale competitions are waged by rival teams whose members meet head-to-head on multiple fronts. In U.S. legislative elections, Democratic and Republican candidates contest seats district by district, and the party securing a majority gains control of the chamber, along with the agenda-setting power that entails. In defense procurement, prime contractors such as Boeing, Lockheed Martin, and Northrop Grumman assemble specialized engineering groups for subsystems like avionics, propulsion, and stealth; the agency then evaluates competing prototypes component by component before awarding the contract. In team sports, the Ryder Cup, Davis Cup, and Sudirman Cup pit national squads against each other through pairwise matches, with the title going to the side that wins a majority. These are all instances of
multi-battle team contests
: two teams field members across multiple battles, each participant enters one battle and bears his own effort cost, and team victory is determined by aggregate performance.

A defining feature of these competitions is that the winning team earns a collective prize whose internal division is a strategic choice. A victorious party must apportion the spoils of majority control—committee chairs, earmarks, legislative priorities—among candidates of differing electoral strength facing districts of varying competitiveness. A winning contractor must distribute net benefits across engineering groups that differ in expertise and task difficulty. A championship team’s manager must reward players of varying ability who faced opponents of different calibers. These allocations are inherently strategic: each manager’s optimal scheme depends on the rival’s, since both reward structures jointly shape effort incentives and the stakes in every battle.

Despite its practical importance, within-team prize allocation remains largely unexplored, raising a first-order strategic question:
How should a manager divide the team prize among her players to maximize the team’s winning probability, given that her rival faces the same problem?
Should rewards go to the strongest players, who are most likely to win? To the weakest, who need the sharpest incentives?

Model and challenges.
We endogenize within-team prize allocation by modeling two team managers who simultaneously promise to partition the prize among heterogeneous teammates conditional on team victory, each seeking to maximize the probability that her team wins a majority of pairwise battles. Consistent with all leading examples, simultaneity is natural because each manager’s internal reward scheme is proprietary and never disclosed to the rival, and players are exogenously matched one-to-one across teams. We assume players are risk-neutral and each battle is resolved through a generalized Tullock contest with discriminatory power at most one. The framework accommodates rich heterogeneity: players differ in marginal costs, contest technology may vary across battles, and the two teams’ value of the team prize (i.e.,
budget
) need not be equal.

The strategic problem has two layers. In the first stage, managers design internal incentive schemes, anticipating how these choices shape effort provision. In the second stage, matched players from opposing teams compete in pairwise battles, with the team winning a majority claiming the prize. This structure generates two compounding difficulties for the managers’ problem. First, each player responds strategically not only to his direct opponent but also to players in other battles, whose strategies determine how much his own battle matters for team victory. Second, the manager’s objective is well-behaved at the battle level—each winning probability is concave in own prize—but majority aggregation is itself nonlinear and not quasiconcave in the vector of battle-winning probabilities, while the budget constraint couples all battles. The favorable battle-level structure therefore does not by itself deliver concavity of the manager’s allocation problem.

Main results.
We characterize the unique pure-strategy subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium in closed form. Both managers choose identical budget shares across battles, so that the ratio of the two teams’ prizes in any given battle equals the ratio of their total budgets. This
proportional-prize property
holds regardless of how players’ abilities or battle technologies differ across fronts, and it strengthens to exact equality of prizes when the two teams value team victory equally. The common share assigned to each battle is proportional to its
salience
, defined as the product of three terms: (i) the
discriminatory power
of the battle’s Tullock winner-selection mechanism; (ii) the
symmetry
of the battle, measured by the product of the two teams’ winning probabilities; and (iii) the
pivotality
of the battle, the probability that the remaining battles split evenly so that this one is decisive. Consequently, little is spent on near-predetermined battles, whose outcomes barely respond to incentives, or on battles unlikely to affect majority control however closely fought; the largest rewards flow to intermediate fronts that are simultaneously contestable and consequential.

This salience principle resonates with a long-standing theme in political economy.

We instead introduce strategic candidates into each battlefield, making contestability endogenous to equilibrium effort—a formulation better suited to legislative elections. This isolates the discriminatory power of the winner-selection technology as a determinant of allocation, alongside pivotality and symmetry. Despite the added complexity, rival managers converge on identical relative shares.

The equilibrium also delivers sharp comparative statics: strengthening one’s team, either by lowering members’ costs or by raising the team prize, always benefits that team even as allocations adjust across all battles; and in well-matched battles, the allocation depends solely on the contest technology, with more discriminating battles commanding larger shares.

A notable robustness property is that the equilibrium is invariant to the temporal structure of the contest: whether battles are played simultaneously, sequentially, or in any partial order, the equilibrium allocation is unchanged. The intuition is simple. In any nontrivial battle, each player’s incentive equals the assigned prize times the increase in team-winning probability from his own victory, and since one team’s win is the other team’s loss, this latter term is identical for both competing players. Relative incentives are therefore shaped entirely by relative prizes and relative costs, neither of which depends on the order of play. In trivial battles—where one team has already secured a majority—resetting the battle outcome leaves overall winning probabilities untouched, so each battle can be treated as an independent lottery.

Technical contribution.
Establishing equilibrium existence is the central technical challenge. The manager’s objective is neither concave nor quasiconcave in the vector of battle-winning probabilities, so standard composition arguments through battle-level concavity do not apply. We instead show that, given the rival’s strategy, each manager’s team-winning probability is
log-concave
in her own allocation. The proof reduces log-concavity to the positive semidefiniteness of a structured matrix built from the conditional covariance of battle outcomes given team victory, which we then verify by decomposing the matrix coefficientwise in a polynomial expansion and certifying each coefficient block as positive semidefinite. Log-concavity elevates the closed-form solution of the first-order conditions to a global best response, yielding existence and uniqueness for any odd number of battles.

The underlying intuition is simple. Suppose one team assigns zero prize to a battle while its opponent assigns a positive amount. Conversely, if neither team allocates prize to a battle, either manager can deviate by committing an infinitesimal amount, thereby securing a nontrivial gain in the team’s overall winning probability at negligible cost.

3
Equilibrium Characterizations

We establish the existence and uniqueness of a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium in this section. By
Lemma 3
, we work on
\textnormal{int}(\Delta_{A})\times\textnormal{int}(\Delta_{B})
throughout. The argument proceeds in three steps. First, the pivotality decomposition together with the homogeneity of degree zero of the contest success function forces the two teams to allocate their budgets in identical proportions across battles—a
proportional-prize property
. Second, we exhibit a candidate closed-form profile
(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{A},\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{B})
and verify that it is the unique solution to the first-order conditions (
Theorem 1
). Third, we prove that each team’s contest-winning probability is log-concave in its own strategy (
Theorem 2
). The key log-concavity step is established for arbitrary odd numbers of battles by a coefficientwise positive-semidefinite certificate for the covariance-domination matrix; log-concavity then ensures that any interior critical point is a global maximum.

Proportional-Prize Property

Proposition 1
(Proportional-Prize Property)

At any pure-strategy equilibrium,
v_{Bt}^{*}/v_{At}^{*}=W_{B}/W_{A}
for every

W_{A}=W_{B}

Proposition 1
provides a key structural property: in equilibrium, both managers allocate prizes to every battle in

, differing only by the scalar
W_{B}/W_{A}
. This proportionality is surprising because the two teams may face entirely different strategic considerations. Consider a 3-battle toy example in which team
A
’s players in battles 1 and 2 are slightly stronger than their opponents, while team
A
’s player in battle 3 is severely weaker. One might expect team
A
to concentrate resources on battles 1 and 2—securing two wins and conceding battle 3.
Proposition 1
shows that no such asymmetric tilting occurs.

The result emerges from the interplay of two forces. First, homogeneity of degree zero (HD-0) of the contest success function (CSF) implies that only the
ratio

v_{Bt}/v_{At}
matters for battle outcomes, not the levels. This means that if one manager scales up a battle’s prize, the rival can neutralize the effect by scaling up proportionally. Second, the pivotality of each battle is
team-symmetric
: the event “the other
2N
battles split” is the same regardless of which team’s perspective we adopt. Together, these two properties force the first-order conditions of both managers into a common structure that admits only the proportional solution in equilibrium. Returning to the toy example, if team
A
tried to concentrate resources on battles 1 and 2, team
B
would counteract by reducing resources spent on battle 3 and increasing resources spent on the first two battles. This strategic offset is what ultimately leads to proportional prizes.

When
W_{A}=W_{B}
, proportionality becomes

v_{At}^{*}=v_{Bt}^{*}
for every battle
t

A

t
is vastly more talented than team
B
’s player, both managers assign the same prize to that battle. This is because the asymmetry in ability is already “priced in” through the CSF: with equal prizes, the stronger player wins more often.

3.2
Main Results

The proportional-prize property immediately pins down equilibrium winning probabilities. Substituting
v_{Bt}^{*}=k\,v_{At}^{*}
with
k\triangleq W_{B}/W_{A}
into the Tullock CSF (
1

p_{At}^{*}=\frac{c_{t}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}},\qquad p_{Bt}^{*}=\frac{k^{r_{t}}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}},

(5)

where

\rho_{t}\triangleq c_{Bt}/c_{At}

(c_{At},c_{Bt},r_{t})
and the aggregate budget ratio

pivotality
can be pinned down:

\theta^{*}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{-t}\\
|w_{i}|=N\end{subarray}}\;\prod_{t\in w_{i}}p_{it}\;\prod_{q\in\mathcal{N}_{-t}\setminus w_{i}}p_{jq},\qquad j\in\{A,B\}\setminus\{i\}.

(6)

To understand how managers split their budgets, consider team
A

1

v_{Bt}=k\,v_{At}

\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}\bigg|_{v_{Bt}=k\,v_{At}}=\frac{r_{t}\,p^{*}_{At}\,p^{*}_{Bt}}{v^{*}_{At}}.

(7)

Since the denominator
v^{*}_{At}

r_{t}\,p^{*}_{At}\,p^{*}_{Bt}

Definition 2
(Responsiveness)

The
responsiveness
of battle
t
is defined as

R_{t}\triangleq r_{t}\cdot\underbrace{p_{At}^{*}\,p_{Bt}^{*}}_{\text{symmetry level}},\qquad\forall t,

(8)

where
p_{At}^{*}
and
p_{Bt}^{*}
are equilibrium winning probabilities given by (
5
).

The quantity
R_{t}
measures how effectively prize investment translates into a higher winning probability in battle
t
. It is the product of two components: the discriminatory power
r_{t}
, which governs how sharply the contest technology distinguishes between unequal efforts, and the symmetry level
p_{At}^{*}p_{Bt}^{*}
, which captures how evenly matched the two sides are. Intuitively, responsiveness should increase with both discriminatory power and the
symmetry level
, which is maximized at
1/4
when
c_{t}=k^{r_{t}}
(i.e., one player’s ability advantage exactly offsets the other’s budget advantage). Conversely,
R_{t}
vanishes as either side’s effective strength dominates, because a lopsided battle whose outcome is nearly predetermined cannot be swayed by marginal changes in prize allocation. Two invariance properties are also worth noting:
R_{t}
is unaffected by a common rescaling of both players’ costs in that battle, and it is likewise unaffected by a common rescaling of the two teams’ budgets, since in both cases the ratio of effective strengths—and hence the symmetry level—remains unchanged.

Under majority rule, however, the marginal return to winning a battle depends not only on responsiveness but also on whether that victory is likely to change the contest outcome. We therefore introduce a composite measure that aggregates both dimensions.

Definition 3
(Salience)

The
salience
of battle
t
is defined as
S_{t}\triangleq\theta^{*}(t)\cdot R_{t}
.

Note that salience naturally combines two distinct forces: how
responsive
a battle is to additional investment and how
pivotal
it is for determining the contest winner. Since
p^{*}_{At},p^{*}_{Bt}\in(0,1)
and
r_{t}>0
, salience is strictly positive for every battle. With this measure in hand, we can state the main equilibrium characterization.

Theorem 1
(Equilibrium Characterization and Uniqueness)

For any
r_{t}\in(0,1]
,
t\in\mathcal{N}
, and any budgets
W_{A},W_{B}>0
, the team contest has a
unique
pure-strategy Nash equilibrium
(\mathbf{v}_{A}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{B}^{*})
, given by

v_{At}^{*}=W_{A}\cdot\frac{S_{t}}{\sum_{\tau}S_{\tau}},\qquad v_{Bt}^{*}=W_{B}\cdot\frac{S_{t}}{\sum_{\tau}S_{\tau}},\qquad\forall t.

Each manager allocates budget across battles in proportion to their salience, directing larger shares to battles that are simultaneously more responsive and more pivotal. Neither component alone governs the allocation: a highly pivotal battle may receive a small share if it is too lopsided for additional incentives to make a difference, while a perfectly balanced battle may receive little if it is unlikely to be decisive—for instance, in a three-battle contest, a balanced battle carries low pivotality when one team dominates the other two.

Remark 1

The battle salience
S_{t}
is a measure of the “weight” of a battle.
7
propose an alternative notion of battle weight that is
increasing
in asymmetry—in sharp contrast with ours, where more balanced battles receive greater weight. The difference stems from distinct institutional settings. In their framework, a centralized contest designer chooses a potentially biased prize allocation rule. Their optimal design is a
majority-score rule with a headstart
: all battles are assigned team-invariant scores, the weaker team receives an initial headstart score, and the team accumulating the higher total score from its battle victories wins the entire prize. Facing an asymmetric battle, the designer can increase its score weight while simultaneously increasing the headstart score to offset the stronger team’s advantage, heating up the competition by “leveling the playing field.” In our setting, managers take the contest environments as given and can only allocate prizes; a highly asymmetric battle is unresponsive to prize incentives, regardless of how much budget is directed toward it, so equilibrium optimally tilts resources toward more balanced fronts.

Remark 2
(Temporal Structure Independence)

By
Lemma 2
, the equilibrium
(\mathbf{v}_{A}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{B}^{*})
and the resulting team-winning probabilities are invariant to the temporal structure. In a fully sequential setting where each outcome is publicly revealed before the next battle begins, one might expect managers to exploit the temporal structure—loading prizes onto early battles to build momentum, or reserving resources for a clinching scenario. No such dynamic consideration arises because of an exact offsetting effect. In a three-battle contest, shifting the budget toward the final battle raises the winning probability there but lowers it in the earlier battles. This creates two offsetting harms: the team is more likely to have already lost both earlier battles (making the final battle irrelevant) and less likely to have already clinched victory (also making it irrelevant).

3.4
Proof of Log-concavity Condition

This section is devoted to proving the following result.

Theorem 2

The
Log-concavity Condition
holds for every
N\geq 1
.

Recall that the model and the equilibrium characterization in
Theorem 1
are stated for an arbitrary odd number
2N+1
of battles, and that the only nonstandard ingredient in that proof is the
Log-concavity Condition
. Establishing this condition for every
N\geq 1
is therefore the central technical task of the paper. Our proof proceeds in three stages: we first derive the Hessian of
\log\textnormal{Prob}_{i}
in closed form; we then reduce its negative semidefiniteness to a covariance-domination inequality for a certain truncated product measure; and we finally verify that inequality through an explicit coefficientwise decomposition.

We now turn to the proof of
Theorem 2
. For each
t\in\mathcal{N}\triangleq\{1,\dots,2N+1\}
, let
X_{t}\sim\textnormal{Bernoulli}(p_{t})
be independent indicators of team
A
winning battle
t
, where
p_{t}=p_{At}(v_{At},v_{Bt})
is the equilibrium battle-winning probability. Let

A\triangleq\Bigl\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{2N+1}:\sum_{t}x_{t}\geq N+1\Bigr\}

denote the majority event, and write
f(\mathbf{v}_{A})\triangleq\textnormal{Prob}_{A}(\mathbf{v}_{A},\mathbf{v}_{B})=\Pr(A)
for team
A
’s probability of winning the overall contest. Our goal is to show that
\log f
is concave in
\mathbf{v}_{A}
.

From log-concavity to a matrix condition.

Log-concavity of
f
in
\mathbf{v}_{A}
is, by definition, the negative semidefiniteness of the Hessian
H
of
\log f
. Define

G_{t}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid A]-p_{t},

which measures how much conditioning on team
A
’s overall victory raises the marginal probability of winning battle
t
. Let
D=\mathrm{diag}(p_{t}(1-p_{t}))
collect the unconditional Bernoulli variances, let
\Sigma_{A}=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}\mid A)
denote the conditional covariance matrix, and set

M\triangleq D+\mathrm{diag}(G_{t}/r_{t})-\Sigma_{A}.

Lemma 5
(Negative semidefiniteness of
H
)

H\preceq 0
if and only if
M\succeq 0
.

The proof rests on a convenient reparametrization. Working in the log-odds
\eta_{t}=\log\bigl(p_{t}/(1-p_{t})\bigr)
, the joint distribution of
\mathbf{X}
becomes a canonical exponential family, and standard exponential-family identities yield

\frac{\partial\log f}{\partial\eta_{t}}=G_{t},\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}\log f}{\partial\eta_{t}\,\partial\eta_{s}}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t},X_{s}\mid A)-\delta_{ts}\,p_{t}(1-p_{t}).

The first identity has a clean interpretation: raising the log-odds of winning battle
t
improves team
A
’s overall win probability at a rate proportional to
G_{t}
, the very quantity that captures how decisive battle
t
is for securing a majority. The second identity says that the curvature of
\log f
along
\bm{\eta}
is governed by how battle outcomes co-move conditional on victory, net of each Bernoulli’s intrinsic variance
p_{t}(1-p_{t})
; the latter contributes only on the diagonal, as recorded by the Kronecker delta
\delta_{ts}
.

To translate these derivatives back into the original prize variables, we exploit the Tullock structure
\eta_{t}=r_{t}\log v_{At}+\text{const}
, which gives
\partial\eta_{t}/\partial v_{At}=r_{t}/v_{At}
and
\partial^{2}\eta_{t}/\partial v_{At}^{2}=-r_{t}/v_{At}^{2}
. Setting
V=\mathrm{diag}(v_{At})
and
R=\mathrm{diag}(r_{t})
, the chain rule produces

H=(RV^{-1})(\Sigma_{A}-D)(RV^{-1})-RV^{-2}\,\mathrm{diag}(G_{t}).

The invertible change of variables
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=RV^{-1}\mathbf{u}
then converts the quadratic form
\mathbf{u}^{\top}H\mathbf{u}
into
-\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}M\,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
, so that
H\preceq 0
if and only if
M\succeq 0
.

An
r
-free relaxation.

The matrix
M
is still inconvenient to analyze directly because its diagonal entries
G_{t}/r_{t}
depend on the discriminatory powers
r_{t}
, which vary across battles and parameter regimes. To eliminate this dependence we relax
M
to a sharper,
r
-free matrix. The key observation is that
G_{t}\geq 0
: conditioning on team
A
’s overall victory can only raise the marginal probability of winning any individual battle. Intuitively, the event
A
is a “good news” event for every
X_{t}
, since it requires that at least
N+1
of the indicators turn up favorable.
3
3

3

Formally, by Bayes’ rule and the independence of
X_{t}
from
\mathbf{X}_{-t}

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid A]=\beta p_{t}/[\beta p_{t}+\gamma(1-p_{t})]
, where
\beta=\Pr(\sum_{s\neq t}X_{s}\geq N)
and
\gamma=\Pr(\sum_{s\neq t}X_{s}\geq N+1)\leq\beta
. Replacing
\gamma

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid A]\geq p_{t}
.
Combined with the assumption

G_{t}/r_{t}\geq G_{t}
, so the diagonal of

M^{0}\triangleq D+\mathrm{diag}(G_{t})-\Sigma_{A},

and consequently

Lemma 6
(Positive semidefiniteness of
M^{0}
)

M^{0}\succeq 0
.

We sketch the proof strategy here. The first step is a change of variables to the loss odds
\phi_{t}=(1-p_{t})/p_{t}\in(0,+\infty)
, under which the conditional distribution given the majority event becomes clean to manipulate: every entry of

admits a closed form as a ratio of polynomials in
\bm{\phi}

\widetilde{M}(\bm{\phi})
is positive semidefinite (PSD) for all positive

in monomials and establish the stronger statement that
each coefficient matrix is individually PSD

Finally, we verify that these five-number patterns are PSD by reducing to a handful of scalar inequalities, and prove it algebraically.

Together,

4.1
Comparative Statics

We examine how the equilibrium of
Theorem 1
responds to changes in primitives, organizing the analysis around two objects: the team-winning probability and the battle salience.

Team-winning probability.

We now show that a team’s equilibrium winning probability rises with any advantage in budget or marginal cost.

Proposition 2

Let
\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}
denote team
A
’s equilibrium contest-winning probability.

(i)

For every

,
\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}
is strictly increasing in
c_{t}\triangleq(c_{Bt}/c_{At})^{r_{t}}
, and therefore strictly decreasing in
c_{At}
.

(ii)

\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}
is strictly decreasing in

W_{A}
.

Part (i) embodies a
monotone strengthening property
: strengthening any single player always raises the team’s winning probability, even though equilibrium prize allocations adjust across
all

a priori
, since improving player

could in principle absorb prize budget from other battles and thereby undermine the team’s performance elsewhere. The proportional-prize property (
Proposition 1
) closes this channel: because
v_{Bt}^{*}/v_{At}^{*}=k
is independent of cost ratios, each

depends only on own-battle fundamentals, so a unilateral improvement in
c_{At}
feeds through to
\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}
without disturbing the winning odds in any other battle. Part (ii) records the parallel and unambiguous benefit of a larger team-prize valuation.

When
W_{A}=W_{B}
and
c_{t}=1
for all
t
, we have
\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}=1/2
; and if

for every
t

for all

, strict somewhere), then
\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}>1/2
. A natural conjecture is therefore that the product

of battle-level cost advantages summarizes which team is stronger overall. This conjecture, however, is false. For instance, with

r_{t}=1
for all

, and
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})=(99,\,99,\,1/9801)

Battle Salience.

Recall that the salience of battle

denote the budget ratio of the two teams.

Proposition 3
(Comparative Statics of Battle Salience)

For every

:

(i)

Holding

,

, and the primitives of all other battles fixed,

is strictly single-peaked in

, attaining its unique maximum at

. Moreover,

as

or
\rho_{t}\to\infty

(ii)

Holding

is strictly increasing in

.

(iii)

Holding

Part (i) formalizes the recurring intuition that “optimal incentives favor intermediate battles”: salience is maximized at the cost ratio that equalizes equilibrium winning probabilities, not at the cost ratio that equalizes marginal costs. The peak location

Part (ii) isolates the cleanest case in which discriminatory power affects salience: when matched players are equalized at the prevailing budget ratio (
\rho_{t}=k

, and raising

amplifies effort incentives through the prefactor alone. Away from this benchmark, raising

also widens any asymmetry between

and

Proof
.
In battle

e^{*}_{At}=\frac{r_{t}\,p^{*}_{At}\,p^{*}_{Bt}\,V_{At}}{c_{At}},\qquad e^{*}_{Bt}=\frac{r_{t}\,p^{*}_{At}\,p^{*}_{Bt}\,V_{Bt}}{c_{Bt}},

(i)

Holding

(ii)

4.3
Contest Technologies: The Roles of Concavity and Homogeneity

Our baseline accommodates substantial heterogeneity: the two teams may differ in their valuation of the team prize; within each battle, the matched players may face different marginal costs; the discriminatory power may vary across battles; and the temporal structure may be simultaneous, sequential, or any hybrid. Within this environment, two distinct properties of the contest technology drive our results, playing
separate
roles: one governs equilibrium

The first is concavity, delivered by the bound

but not necessary for existence. This bound ensures that (i) each battle admits a unique pure-strategy equilibrium given any profile

The second is homogeneity of degree zero (HD-0) of the

Definition 4

To formalize this idea, suppose that, for every battle

4.4
Implications and Caveats

The equilibrium formula yields an allocation principle with direct organizational content: a battle commands a large prize share when its discriminatory power, symmetry, and pivotality are simultaneously high. A manager should not reward a battle merely because it is close—closeness matters only if the battle can swing the team outcome—nor merely because it is pivotal, since pivotality matters only if additional incentives can change the battle outcome.

A second implication follows from the proportional-prize property. Rival organizations facing the same battles and the same majority objective allocate internal rewards in identical relative proportions, even when their absolute budgets differ and their team members have different costs: primitive asymmetries are absorbed by the equilibrium winning probabilities, leaving a common relative allocation rule.

Our analysis further delivers two empirical predictions. First, strengthening any single player raises that team’s overall winning probability, despite the induced reallocation of rewards across battles. Second, the equilibrium allocation and winning probabilities are invariant to timing—whether battles are simultaneous, sequential, or partially sequential.

Legislative elections.

Legislative elections provide a natural interpretation of the theory. A party wins the collective prize of majority control only by winning enough district- or state-level races. Party leaders and campaign committees then face an internal allocation problem: where should campaign funds, strategic support, leadership attention, and post-victory rewards be directed? The salience formula implies that the largest rewards should flow to races that are both competitive and consequential for control of the chamber. Safe seats have low salience because their outcomes are largely insensitive to additional incentives; the same is true of hopeless races. Competitive races that do not affect majority control also have limited salience. The highest-salience races are the battlegrounds that can plausibly decide the majority.

This interpretation also highlights the proportional-prize property. In close election cycles, both parties typically target similar sets of battleground races, despite differing in incumbency advantages, fundraising bases, and candidate quality. The model attributes this convergence to a common pivotality structure: the parties need not have equal resources, but their relative attention across races should align after scaling by total budgets. Campaign spending, independent expenditures, leadership visits, coordinated support, and committee assignments are plausible empirical proxies. Preelection campaign receipts are especially attractive: as
31
emphasizes, fundraising is the most widely used preelection indicator of candidate viability and is built into prominent forecasting models such as FiveThirtyEight and the Cook Political Report.

The legislative application should be read with two caveats. First, post-election rewards such as committee assignments, legislative priorities, and leadership access are not literal binding contracts. Their credibility arises from repeated interaction, party discipline, and the value of future cooperation. Second, some rewards are investments in future electoral performance rather than pure payments for past effort. The model is best viewed as isolating one force inside a richer political organization: when majority control is valuable, party leaders have a reason to direct scarce rewards toward races that are both contestable and pivotal.

Procurement and consortium competition.

The same logic applies to procurement contests in which prime contractors assemble teams of specialized subcontractors. A prime contractor competes for a single award, but the probability of winning depends on performance across multiple dimensions—for example, propulsion, avionics, software, integration, reliability, and cost. The prime’s workshare promises or revenue-sharing arrangements play the role of within-team prizes. The model suggests that workshare tends to be concentrated in subsystems where performance is measurable, the rival teams are closely matched, and the subsystem is likely to determine the award. Subsystems that are either nearly predetermined or unlikely to affect the final evaluation receive smaller incentive shares in equilibrium.

This application is necessarily approximate because procurement awards usually involve weighted scoring, technical thresholds, and holistic “best value” determinations rather than strict majority rule. Subsystems may also interact, especially when systems integration is important. Nevertheless, procurement illustrates the broader organizational insight: when a collective prize must be generated through decentralized specialized effort, the internal division of the prize tracks the salience of each task.

5
Concluding Remarks

This paper studies how organizations divide the spoils of team competition. In a majoritarian contest with pairwise battles, two rival managers choose internal prize allocations before their players exert effort. Although the strategic problem is high-dimensional and nonlinear, the unique equilibrium has a simple form: both managers allocate prizes according to the same salience index. A battle is salient when it is discriminatory in selecting the winner, closely contested, and likely to be pivotal for majority control. The resulting proportional-prize property implies that rival organizations choose identical relative reward schemes across battles, even when their budgets, player costs, and battle technologies differ. The central technical challenge is equilibrium existence: we show that each manager’s team-winning probability is log-concave in her own allocation given her opponent’s strategy. We establish this log-concavity for every odd number of battles via a coefficientwise positive-semidefinite decomposition, which completes the global equilibrium characterization.

Several questions remain open.
First
, the model takes the player-to-battle matching as exogenous; an interesting extension would let managers assign players to battles
before
allocating prizes, leading to a joint problem in which matching may substitute for or complement reward design.
Second
, history-dependent prize promises—in which managers observe interim outcomes and adjust subsequent splits before later battles are played—may overturn the proportional-prize feature; the trade-off between commitment and contingent incentives is a natural follow-up.
Third
, the managers’ objective in our model is contest-winning probability. Under welfare, participation, or aggregate-effort objectives, the optimal allocation may diverge from the salience rule.
Fourth
, richer budget-design questions arise if an organizer or sponsor can choose the teams’ total budgets before managers allocate them internally. We leave these directions for future work.

\Pr(Y_{t}=1\text{ exactly for }t\in S\mid A)=\frac{\prod_{t\in S}\phi_{t}}{\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})}.

Closed-form entries of
M^{0}
.

Let
a_{t}\triangleq\Pr(Y_{t}=1\mid A)
,
b_{t}\triangleq\phi_{t}/(1+\phi_{t})
,
q_{t}\triangleq 1-a_{t}
, and
p_{t}\triangleq 1-b_{t}
. Since
M^{0}=D+\operatorname{diag}(G_{t})-\Sigma_{A}
with
G_{t}=q_{t}-p_{t}
and
\operatorname{Var}(X_{t}\mid A)=q_{t}(1-q_{t})
, the diagonal simplifies to

M^{0}_{tt}=p_{t}(1-p_{t})+(q_{t}-p_{t})-q_{t}(1-q_{t})=q_{t}^{2}-p_{t}^{2},

while
M^{0}_{ts}=-\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{t},Y_{s}\mid A)
for
t\neq s
. Summing over winning configurations containing
t
,

a_{t}=\sum_{S\ni t,\,|S|\leq N}\frac{\prod_{s\in S}\phi_{s}}{\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})}=\frac{\phi_{t}\,\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})}{\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})}.

Conditioning on whether battle
t
is lost yields the splitting identity

\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})=\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})+\phi_{t}\,\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t}),

(15)

hence
q_{t}=\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})/\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})
. Using (
15
),

b_{t}-a_{t}=\frac{\phi_{t}}{1+\phi_{t}}-\frac{\phi_{t}\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})}{\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})}=\frac{\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})}{(1+\phi_{t})\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})},

where the second equality follows from
\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})-(1+\phi_{t})\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})=\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})-\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})=e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})
. A parallel calculation gives

q_{t}+p_{t}=\frac{2\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})+\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})}{(1+\phi_{t})\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})},

and multiplying via
M^{0}_{tt}=(q_{t}-p_{t})(q_{t}+p_{t})
,

M^{0}_{tt}=\frac{\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\bigl(2\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})+\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\bigr)}{(1+\phi_{t})^{2}\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})^{2}}.

(16)

For
t\neq s
,
M^{0}_{ts}=a_{t}a_{s}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}Y_{s}\mid A]
, with

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}Y_{s}\mid A]=\frac{\phi_{t}\phi_{s}\,\Phi_{N-2}(\bm{\phi}_{-\{t,s\}})}{\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})}.

Combining this with
a_{t}a_{s}=\phi_{t}\phi_{s}\,\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-s})/\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})^{2}
and applying (
15
) to expand
\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})
and
\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})
relative to index
s
, the
\phi_{t}
- and
\phi_{s}
-cross-terms cancel and the bracket reduces to the
Turán defect

T_{N}(\bm{\psi})\triangleq\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\psi})^{2}-\Phi_{N-2}(\bm{\psi})\Phi_{N}(\bm{\psi}),

evaluated at
\bm{\psi}=\bm{\phi}_{-\{t,s\}}
. Hence

M^{0}_{ts}=\frac{\phi_{t}\phi_{s}\,T_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-\{t,s\}})}{\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})^{2}}\qquad(t\neq s).

(17)

Reduction to a polynomial matrix
\widetilde{M}
.

Let
D_{\phi}\triangleq\operatorname{diag}(1+\phi_{t})
and
\widetilde{M}\triangleq\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})^{2}\,D_{\phi}M^{0}D_{\phi}
. Since
\Phi_{N}>0
and
D_{\phi}\succ 0
on the positive orthant, this congruence preserves inertia, so
M^{0}\succeq 0\iff\widetilde{M}\succeq 0
. Clearing denominators in (
16
)–(
17
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{tt}

\displaystyle=\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\bigl(2\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi})+\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\bigr),

(18)

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{ts}

\displaystyle=\phi_{t}\phi_{s}(1+\phi_{t})(1+\phi_{s})\,T_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-\{t,s\}}),\quad t\neq s.

(19)

Every entry of
\widetilde{M}
is a polynomial in
\bm{\phi}
, which opens the door to a coefficientwise PSD argument.

Step 2: Block structure and closed forms for the coefficients of
\widetilde{M}

Expanding
\widetilde{M}
in the monomial basis,

\widetilde{M}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}}C_{\alpha}\,\bm{\phi}^{\alpha},\qquad\bm{\phi}^{\alpha}\triangleq\prod_{t}\phi_{t}^{\alpha_{t}},

it suffices to show
C_{\alpha}\succeq 0
for every
\alpha
, since then
v^{\top}\widetilde{M}v=\sum_{\alpha}\bm{\phi}^{\alpha}(v^{\top}C_{\alpha}v)\geq 0
on the positive orthant. We adopt the conventions
\binom{m}{r}=0
for
r\notin\{0,\dots,m\}
and
[x]_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
.

Support and block structure of
C_{\alpha}
.

By (
18
)–(
19
), each variable
\phi_{t}
appears in
\widetilde{M}
to power at most
2
; hence only
\alpha\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
contribute. Partition the support of such
\alpha
as

S_{1}=\{t:\alpha_{t}=1\},\quad S_{2}=\{t:\alpha_{t}=2\},\quad b=|S_{1}|,\quad a=|S_{2}|,\quad L=N-a.

If
\alpha_{t}=0
, every monomial in row
t
of
\widetilde{M}
contains
\phi_{t}
to at least the first power (via the leading
\phi_{t}
factor), so it cannot contribute to
\bm{\phi}^{\alpha}
; the same holds column-wise. Therefore
C_{\alpha}
is supported on
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
.

The symmetry of
\Phi_{N},e_{N},T_{N}
in their arguments, together with the fact that
\bm{\phi}^{\alpha}
depends only on
S_{1},S_{2}
as sets, forces
C_{\alpha}
to be invariant under permutations preserving
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
setwise. Hence its entries take only five values, classified by index-pair type:

\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&t=s&t\neq s\\
\hline\cr t,s\in S_{1}&d_{1}&c_{11}\\
t,s\in S_{2}&d_{2}&c_{22}\\
t\in S_{1},\,s\in S_{2}&-&c_{12}\end{array}

Ordering
S_{1}
before
S_{2}
,

C_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}I_{b}+c_{11}(J_{b}-I_{b})&c_{12}\,\mathbf{1}_{b}\mathbf{1}_{a}^{\top}\\
c_{12}\,\mathbf{1}_{a}\mathbf{1}_{b}^{\top}&d_{2}I_{a}+c_{22}(J_{a}-I_{a})\end{pmatrix},

(20)

where
J_{m}=\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}
.

Closed forms for
d_{1},d_{2},c_{11},c_{12},c_{22}
.

We claim

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=2\binom{b-1}{L}\mathbf{1}_{\{b-1\leq 2L\}},

(21)

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=2\binom{b}{L+1}\mathbf{1}_{\{b\leq 2L+1\}}+\binom{b}{L+1}\mathbf{1}_{\{b=2L+2\}},

(22)

\displaystyle c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\binom{b-2+(i+j-2)}{L-1+(i+j-2)}-\binom{b-2+(i+j-2)}{L+(i+j-2)}\Bigr]_{+},\quad(i,j)\in\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,2)\};

(23)

that is,
c_{11}=[\binom{b-2}{L-1}-\binom{b-2}{L}]_{+}
,
c_{12}=[\binom{b-1}{L}-\binom{b-1}{L+1}]_{+}
, and
c_{22}=[\binom{b}{L+1}-\binom{b}{L+2}]_{+}
. The derivations rest on one combinatorial fact: in
e_{N}(\bm{\psi})\cdot\Phi_{K}(\bm{\psi})
, the coefficient of a squarefree monomial of degree
r^{*}
on a support of size
r^{*}
counts size-
N
subsets
U_{1}
of the support such that
|U^{*}\setminus U_{1}|\leq K
, giving
\binom{r^{*}}{N}\mathbf{1}_{\{r^{*}-N\leq K\}}
.

(i) Off-diagonal coefficients.
For
t\neq s
, expanding
\phi_{t}\phi_{s}(1+\phi_{t})(1+\phi_{s})=\phi_{t}\phi_{s}+\phi_{t}^{2}\phi_{s}+\phi_{t}\phi_{s}^{2}+\phi_{t}^{2}\phi_{s}^{2}
, each exponent pair
(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{s})\in\{1,2\}^{2}
is selected from this prefactor with multiplicity
1
, so the coefficient of
\bm{\phi}^{\alpha}
in
\widetilde{M}_{ts}
equals the coefficient of
\bm{\phi}^{\alpha_{-\{t,s\}}}
in
T_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-\{t,s\}})
.

Fix a monomial in
\bm{\phi}_{-\{t,s\}}
with
a^{\prime}
squared and
r
first-power variables; set
L^{\prime}=N-a^{\prime}
. We claim its coefficient in
T_{N}
equals

\bigl[\binom{r}{L^{\prime}-1}-\binom{r}{L^{\prime}}\bigr]_{+}.

(24)

Indeed, expand
T_{N}=\Phi_{N-1}^{2}-\Phi_{N-2}\Phi_{N}
. In each
\Phi
-factor, the
a^{\prime}
squared variables must occupy slots in the index set (a single
\Phi
is squarefree), leaving residual budgets
L^{\prime}-1,L^{\prime}-1,L^{\prime}-2,L^{\prime}
. Writing
h
for the number of first-power variables drawn from the first
\Phi
-factor in each product (the rest in the second), the coefficient of the target monomial is

\underbrace{\sum_{h:h\leq L^{\prime}-1,\,r-h\leq L^{\prime}-1}\binom{r}{h}}_{\Phi_{N-1}^{2}}-\underbrace{\sum_{h:h\leq L^{\prime}-2,\,r-h\leq L^{\prime}}\binom{r}{h}}_{\Phi_{N-2}\Phi_{N}}.

The two index ranges are
[A,B]
and
[A-1,B-1]
with
A=\max\{0,r-L^{\prime}+1\}
and
B=\min\{r,L^{\prime}-1\}
; they overlap on
[A,B-1]
, where the binomials cancel, leaving
\binom{r}{B}-\binom{r}{A-1}
. In the regime
L^{\prime}\leq r
this simplifies to
\binom{r}{L^{\prime}-1}-\binom{r}{L^{\prime}}
(using
\binom{r}{r-L^{\prime}}=\binom{r}{L^{\prime}}
); when
L^{\prime}>r
, both sums collapse to
2^{r}
and the difference vanishes. The bracket
[\cdot]_{+}
encodes both regimes, since
\binom{r}{L^{\prime}-1}\geq\binom{r}{L^{\prime}}
iff
r\leq 2L^{\prime}-1
. This proves (
24
).

Applying (
24
) with
(a^{\prime},r)
equal to
(a,b-2)
,
(a-1,b-1)
,
(a-2,b)
— corresponding to
(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{s})=(1,1),(1,2),(2,2)
— yields
c_{11},c_{12},c_{22}
respectively.

(ii) Diagonal coefficients.
Substituting (
15
) into (
18
),

\widetilde{M}_{tt}=\underbrace{2\phi_{t}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\Phi_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})}_{\text{degree 1 in }\phi_{t}}+\underbrace{2\phi_{t}^{2}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})\Phi_{N-1}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})+\phi_{t}^{2}\,e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})^{2}}_{\text{degree 2 in }\phi_{t}}.

Case
\alpha_{t}=1
.
The complementary monomial in
\bm{\phi}_{-t}
has
b-1
first-power and
a
squared variables on a support of size
(b-1)+a
. By the combinatorial fact with
K=N
, the coefficient in
e_{N}\Phi_{N}
counts size-
N
subsets
U_{1}
containing all
a
squared variables (each squared variable must lie in both factors), with the remaining
N-a=L
slots chosen from the
b-1
first-power variables: this gives
\binom{b-1}{L}
choices subject to
|U^{*}\setminus U_{1}|=(b-1)-L\leq N
, equivalently
b-1\leq 2L
(using
L=N-a
). Multiplying by
2
yields (
21
).

Case
\alpha_{t}=2
.
The complementary monomial has
b
first-power and
a-1
squared variables. The first summand
2e_{N}\Phi_{N-1}
contributes, by the same reasoning with
K=N-1
, the count
\binom{b}{L+1}\mathbf{1}_{\{b\leq 2L+1\}}
, doubled to
2\binom{b}{L+1}\mathbf{1}_{\{b\leq 2L+1\}}
. The second summand
e_{N}^{2}
requires writing the target as a product of two size-
N
monomials of
e_{N}
. Each squared variable (in
S_{2}\setminus\{t\}
) must lie in
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
; each first-power variable must lie in exactly one of
U_{1}\setminus U_{2}
or
U_{2}\setminus U_{1}
. Setting
k=|U_{1}\setminus U_{2}|
, the constraint
|U_{1}|=N
gives
k=N-(a-1)=L+1
, so by symmetry
|U_{2}\setminus U_{1}|=L+1
and
b=2(L+1)=2L+2
. When
b=2L+2
, the number of valid pairs is
\binom{b}{L+1}
; otherwise this contribution vanishes. Adding the two summands yields (
22
).

Step 3: Verifying
C_{\alpha}\succeq 0
across all regimes

Degenerate cases
L<0
.

Each entry of
\widetilde{M}
has total degree at most
2N+2
, attained by
\phi_{t}^{2}e_{N}(\bm{\phi}_{-t})^{2}
, so
C_{\alpha}=0
whenever
b+2a>2N+2
. If
L\leq-2
, then
b+2a\geq 2N+4
, so
C_{\alpha}=0
. If
L=-1
and
b\geq 1
, then
b+2a\geq 2N+3
, so again
C_{\alpha}=0
. The only nontrivial case is
L=-1,b=0
: then
S_{2}
has
N+1
elements, and (
22
)–(
23
) give
d_{2}=1,c_{22}=1
, so
C_{\alpha}=I_{N+1}+(J_{N+1}-I_{N+1})=J_{N+1}\succeq 0
. Henceforth assume
L\geq 0
.

Reduction to three scalar inequalities.

A block of the form
dI_{m}+c(J_{m}-I_{m})
has eigenvalues
d+(m-1)c
on
\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{1}_{m})
and
d-c
on its orthogonal complement. Since the off-diagonal coupling
c_{12}\mathbf{1}_{b}\mathbf{1}_{a}^{\top}
in (
20
) acts only on
\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{1}_{b})\oplus\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{1}_{a})
, the restriction of
C_{\alpha}
to the orthogonal complement decouples, yielding the PSD conditions
d_{1}-c_{11}\geq 0
and
d_{2}-c_{22}\geq 0
there. On the two-dimensional coupling subspace,
C_{\alpha}
acts as

\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}+(b-1)c_{11}&\sqrt{ab}\,c_{12}\\
\sqrt{ab}\,c_{12}&d_{2}+(a-1)c_{22}\end{pmatrix},

whose PSD reduces (given nonnegative diagonal entries) to nonnegativity of its determinant. Hence
C_{\alpha}\succeq 0
iff

d_{1}-c_{11}\geq 0,\quad d_{2}-c_{22}\geq 0,\quad(d_{1}+(b-1)c_{11})(d_{2}+(a-1)c_{22})\geq ab\,c_{12}^{2},

(25)

with the corresponding inequality dropped when a block is absent (
a=0
or
b=0
).

First two inequalities of (
25
).

If
c_{11}>0
, then
b\leq 2L+1
, so
d_{1}=2\binom{b-1}{L}
. Pascal’s identity
\binom{b-1}{L}=\binom{b-2}{L-1}+\binom{b-2}{L}
gives

d_{1}-c_{11}=2\binom{b-1}{L}-\binom{b-2}{L-1}+\binom{b-2}{L}=\binom{b-2}{L-1}+3\binom{b-2}{L}\geq 0.

If
c_{11}=0
, the inequality is trivial. Similarly, if
c_{22}>0
and
b\leq 2L+1
, then
d_{2}=2\binom{b}{L+1}
and

d_{2}-c_{22}=2\binom{b}{L+1}-\binom{b}{L+1}+\binom{b}{L+2}=\binom{b}{L+1}+\binom{b}{L+2}\geq 0.

The boundary subcase
b=2L+2
is handled below.

Third inequality of (
25
) for
b\leq 2L+1
.

The cases
a=0
(no second block) and
b=0
(RHS vanishes) are trivial. If
b=1
, then
c_{11}=0
, and
c_{12}=0
unless
L=0
; when
L=0
, direct substitution gives
d_{1}=d_{2}=2
,
c_{12}=c_{22}=1
, and
d_{1}(d_{2}+(a-1)c_{22})=2(a+1)\geq a=ab\,c_{12}^{2}
.

Henceforth assume
a\geq 1
,
b\geq 2
,
b\leq 2L+1
. Introduce
\rho\triangleq 2L+2-b\geq 1
and
x\triangleq\binom{b-1}{L}
. Pascal’s identities yield

d_{1}=2x,\quad c_{11}=\tfrac{\rho-1}{b-1}x,\quad c_{12}=\tfrac{\rho}{L+1}x,\quad c_{22}=\tfrac{b(\rho+1)}{(L+1)(L+2)}x,\quad d_{2}=\tfrac{2b}{L+1}x,

so that
d_{1}+(b-1)c_{11}=(\rho+1)x
and
d_{2}+(a-1)c_{22}=\tfrac{b}{L+1}x\bigl[2+\tfrac{(a-1)(\rho+1)}{L+2}\bigr]
. The third inequality of (
25
) becomes

\tfrac{bx^{2}}{L+1}\Bigl[(\rho+1)\bigl(2+\tfrac{(a-1)(\rho+1)}{L+2}\bigr)-\tfrac{a\rho^{2}}{L+1}\Bigr]\geq 0.

(26)

The bracket is linear in
a
with slope
\tfrac{(\rho+1)^{2}}{L+2}-\tfrac{\rho^{2}}{L+1}=\tfrac{-\rho^{2}+2(L+1)\rho+(L+1)}{(L+1)(L+2)}
, whose numerator (a downward quadratic in
\rho
) has positive root
(L+1)+\sqrt{(L+1)(L+2)}>2L+1\geq\rho
, so the slope is nonnegative. At
a=1
, the bracket equals
2(\rho+1)-\tfrac{\rho^{2}}{L+1}\geq 0
iff
\rho^{2}-2(L+1)\rho-2(L+1)\leq 0
, whose positive root
(L+1)+\sqrt{(L+1)(L+3)}
likewise exceeds
2L+1
. Hence the bracket is nonnegative at
a=1
and weakly increasing thereafter, establishing (
26
).

Boundary case
b=2L+2
.

At
b=2L+2
, the identity
\binom{2L+1}{L}=\binom{2L+1}{L+1}
forces
c_{12}=0
, decoupling the two blocks. The first block vanishes (
d_{1}=c_{11}=0
since
b-1>2L
). The second block has
d_{2}=\binom{2L+2}{L+1}
and
c_{22}=\binom{2L+2}{L+1}-\binom{2L+2}{L+2}
, so
d_{2}-c_{22}=\binom{2L+2}{L+2}\geq 0
. All conditions in (
25
) hold. For
b>2L+2
, all coefficients vanish by the indicator constraints, so
C_{\alpha}=0
.

Conclusion.

The four cases above establish
C_{\alpha}\succeq 0
for every
\alpha
, hence
\widetilde{M}(\bm{\phi})\succeq 0
on the positive orthant. By the congruence of Step 1,
M^{0}\succeq 0
, completing the proof.

Proofs for

This part collects the proof of
Proposition 2
,
Proposition 3
,
Proposition 5
, and
Proposition 6
.

Proof of
Proposition 2

By
Proposition 1
,
v_{Bt}^{*}/v_{At}^{*}=k
for every
t\in\mathcal{N}
, so the equilibrium battle-winning probabilities (
5
) are

p_{At}^{*}=\frac{c_{t}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}},\qquad p_{Bt}^{*}=\frac{k^{r_{t}}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}},

where
c_{t}=(c_{Bt}/c_{At})^{r_{t}}
and
k=W_{B}/W_{A}
. Since
p_{At}^{*}
depends only on
(c_{t},r_{t},k)
, team
A
’s majority-winning probability factorizes as

\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\subseteq\mathcal{N}\\
|w|\geq N+1\end{subarray}}\prod_{t\in w}p_{At}^{*}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{N}\setminus w}p_{Bq}^{*},

yielding
\partial\,\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}/\partial p_{At}^{*}=\theta^{*}(t)>0
, where
\theta^{*}(t)
is the pivotality of battle
t
.

For (i),
\partial p_{At}^{*}/\partial c_{t}=k^{r_{t}}/(c_{t}+k^{r_{t}})^{2}>0
, and the chain rule gives
\partial\,\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}/\partial c_{t}>0
. Since
c_{t}
is strictly decreasing in
c_{At}
and strictly increasing in
c_{Bt}
, the claim follows. For (ii),
\partial p_{At}^{*}/\partial k=-r_{t}c_{t}k^{r_{t}-1}/(c_{t}+k^{r_{t}})^{2}<0
for every
t
, so
\partial\,\textnormal{Prob}_{A}^{*}/\partial k<0
.

Proof of
Proposition 3

The equilibrium battle-winning probabilities are

p_{At}^{*}\;=\;\frac{c_{t}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}},\qquad p_{Bt}^{*}\;=\;\frac{k^{r_{t}}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}},

with
c_{t}=\rho_{t}^{r_{t}}
. Since
\theta^{*}(t)
depends only on
\{p_{As}^{*},p_{Bs}^{*}\}_{s\neq t}
, it is invariant to perturbations of own-battle primitives that leave the remaining battles unaffected.

Part (i).
Fix
r_{t}
,
k
, and all other primitives. The map
\rho_{t}\mapsto c_{t}=\rho_{t}^{r_{t}}
is a strictly increasing bijection on
(0,\infty)
, so it suffices to establish strict single-peakedness in
c_{t}
with the maximum at
c_{t}=k^{r_{t}}
. Let
x\triangleq c_{t}
and
a\triangleq k^{r_{t}}>0
. Because
r_{t}
and
\theta^{*}(t)
are independent of
x
, the dependence of
S_{t}
on
x
is fully captured by

\mathrm{Sym}_{t}(x)\;=\;\frac{a\,x}{(x+a)^{2}}.

Differentiating,

\frac{d\mathrm{Sym}_{t}}{dx}\;=\;\frac{a\,(x+a)^{2}-2a\,x\,(x+a)}{(x+a)^{4}}\;=\;\frac{a\,(a-x)}{(x+a)^{3}},

which is strictly positive for
x<a
, zero at
x=a
, and strictly negative for
x>a
. Hence
\mathrm{Sym}_{t}
, and therefore
S_{t}
, is strictly single-peaked in
x
with unique maximum at
x=a
, i.e.,
c_{t}=k^{r_{t}}
. Pulling back through
c_{t}=\rho_{t}^{r_{t}}
yields the unique maximum at
\rho_{t}=k
. Substituting
c_{t}=k^{r_{t}}
into the expression for
p_{At}^{*}
gives
p_{At}^{*}=1/2
, so the peak coincides with equal equilibrium winning probabilities. Finally,
\mathrm{Sym}_{t}(x)=a/(x+2a+a^{2}/x)\to 0
as
x\to 0
or
x\to\infty
, so
S_{t}\to 0
as
\rho_{t}\to 0
or
\rho_{t}\to\infty
.

Part (ii).
Fix
\rho_{t}=k
and all other primitives. With
c_{t}=\rho_{t}^{r_{t}}=k^{r_{t}}
,

\mathrm{Sym}_{t}\;=\;\frac{k^{r_{t}}\cdot k^{r_{t}}}{(k^{r_{t}}+k^{r_{t}})^{2}}\;=\;\frac{1}{4},

which is constant in
r_{t}
. Moreover,
\theta^{*}(t)
depends only on
\{p_{As}^{*},p_{Bs}^{*}\}_{s\neq t}
, which are unaffected by perturbations of
r_{t}
. Hence
S_{t}\;=\;r_{t}\cdot\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\theta^{*}(t)
is strictly increasing in
r_{t}
.

Part (iii).
Fix
\{c_{s},r_{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{N}}
. From
\mathrm{Sym}_{t}=c_{t}k^{r_{t}}/(c_{t}+k^{r_{t}})^{2}
,

\log\mathrm{Sym}_{t}\;=\;\log c_{t}+r_{t}\log k-2\log\!\left(c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}\right).

Differentiating with respect to
\log k
, and using
\partial k^{r_{t}}/\partial\log k=r_{t}k^{r_{t}}
,

\frac{\partial\log\mathrm{Sym}_{t}}{\partial\log k}\;=\;r_{t}-2\cdot\frac{r_{t}k^{r_{t}}}{c_{t}+k^{r_{t}}}\;=\;r_{t}\left(1-2p_{Bt}^{*}\right)\;=\;r_{t}\,(p_{At}^{*}-p_{Bt}^{*}),

where the last equality uses
p_{At}^{*}+p_{Bt}^{*}=1
. The sign claim is immediate:
\partial\log\mathrm{Sym}_{t}/\partial\log k>0
if and only if
p_{At}^{*}>p_{Bt}^{*}
.

Proof of
Proposition 5

Proof of (i).
The budget level
W_{A}+W_{B}
enters
E^{*}
only as a multiplicative prefactor. The equilibrium winning probabilities
p^{*}_{At}=c_{t}/(c_{t}+k^{r_{t}})
depend on the budget
ratio

k
but not on the budget level, so all saliences and the HHI are invariant to proportional scaling of both budgets. The result follows immediately.

Proof of (ii).
When
\rho_{t}=1
and
r_{t}=r
for all
t\in\mathcal{N}
, we have
p_{At}^{*}=1/(1+k^{r})
and
R_{t}=rk^{r}/(1+k^{r})^{2}
for every
t
. All battles are symmetric, so all saliences are equal,
S_{t}=S
for every
t
, and
\mathrm{HHI}(\mathbf{S})=1/(2N+1)
. Total effort cost therefore reduces to

E^{*}=(W_{A}+W_{B})\cdot(2N+1)\,S\cdot\frac{1}{2N+1}=(W_{A}+W_{B})\cdot S.

Now
S=\theta^{*}(t)\cdot R_{t}
, and setting
x=k^{r}
we may write

R_{t}=r\cdot\frac{x}{(1+x)^{2}},\qquad\theta^{*}(t)=\binom{2N}{N}\!\left(\frac{x}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)^{\!N}.

The function
f(x)=x/(1+x)^{2}
satisfies
f^{\prime}(x)=(1-x)/(1+x)^{3}
, so
f
attains its global maximum on
(0,\infty)
uniquely at
x=1
. Hence both
R_{t}
and
\theta^{*}(t)
are maximized at
x=1
, i.e., at
k=1
. So does their product
S
.

Proof of (iii).
Since
\rho_{t}=1
in battle
t
, we have
c_{t}=\rho_{t}^{r_{t}}=1
for every value of
r_{t}
, so
p^{*}_{At}=1/2
independently of
r_{t}
. Consequently, all pivotalities
\theta^{*}(t)
for
t\in\mathcal{N}
are unaffected by
r_{t}
. The responsiveness
R_{t}=r_{t}/4
is linear in
r_{t}
, so
S_{t}=\theta^{*}(t)\cdot r_{t}/4
increases linearly in
r_{t}
while
S_{s}
for
s\neq t
remains unchanged. Using the representation
E^{*}=(W_{A}+W_{B})\cdot\sum_{q}S_{q}^{2}/\sum_{q}S_{q}
, we compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{t}}\left(\frac{\sum_{q}S_{q}^{2}}{\sum_{q}S_{q}}\right)=\frac{2S_{t}(\sum_{q}S_{q})-\sum_{q}S_{q}^{2}}{(\sum_{q}S_{q})^{2}}=\frac{2S_{t}-g}{\sum_{q}S_{q}},

where
g\triangleq\sum_{q}S_{q}^{2}/\sum_{q}S_{q}
. This derivative is strictly positive iff
2S_{t}\sum_{q}S_{q}>\sum_{q}S_{q}^{2}
, equivalently,

S_{t}^{2}+2S_{t}\sum_{s\neq t}S_{s}>\sum_{s\neq t}S_{s}^{2}.

A sufficient condition is
S_{t}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\max_{s\neq t}S_{s}
. Indeed, under this condition, for every
s\neq t
,

2S_{t}S_{s}\geq S_{s}\cdot\max_{s^{\prime}\neq t}S_{s^{\prime}}\geq S_{s}^{2},

so
2S_{t}\sum_{s\neq t}S_{s}\geq\sum_{s\neq t}S_{s}^{2}
, and adding the nonnegative term
S_{t}^{2}
to the left side preserves the strict inequality. The chain rule then gives

\frac{\partial E^{*}}{\partial r_{t}}=\frac{\partial E^{*}}{\partial S_{t}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{*}(t)}{4}>0.

Proof of
Proposition 6

Proof of (i).

Within battle
t
, taking the pivotality
\theta(t)=\pi_{A}^{(t)}=\pi_{B}^{(t)}
as given by the team-level play in other battles, the matched players’ payoffs are

U_{A(t)}=\pi_{A}^{(t)}\,\tilde{p}_{A(t)}(e_{A(t)},e_{B(t)})\,v_{At}-c_{At}e_{A(t)},\qquad U_{B(t)}=\pi_{B}^{(t)}\,\tilde{p}_{B(t)}(e_{A(t)},e_{B(t)})\,v_{Bt}-c_{Bt}e_{B(t)}.

The interior within-battle first-order conditions read

\pi_{A}^{(t)}\,\frac{\partial\tilde{p}_{A(t)}}{\partial e_{A(t)}}(e_{A(t)}^{*},e_{B(t)}^{*})\,v_{At}=c_{At},\qquad\pi_{B}^{(t)}\,\frac{\partial\tilde{p}_{B(t)}}{\partial e_{B(t)}}(e_{A(t)}^{*},e_{B(t)}^{*})\,v_{Bt}=c_{Bt}.

(27)

Differentiating the effort-HD-0 identity
\tilde{p}_{i(t)}(\sigma e_{A},\sigma e_{B})=\tilde{p}_{i(t)}(e_{A},e_{B})
gives

\frac{\partial\tilde{p}_{i(t)}}{\partial e_{i(t)}}(\sigma e_{A(t)},\sigma e_{B(t)})=\sigma^{-1}\frac{\partial\tilde{p}_{i(t)}}{\partial e_{i(t)}}(e_{A(t)},e_{B(t)}).

Now scale the prizes by
\mu>0
, holding pivotality fixed (pivotality is a team-level object determined by play in other battles and does not respond to a within-battle rescaling of
(v_{At},v_{Bt})
). Substituting
(e_{A(t)},e_{B(t)})=(\mu e_{A(t)}^{*},\mu e_{B(t)}^{*})
into (
27
) at prizes
(\mu v_{At},\mu v_{Bt})
yields

\pi_{A}^{(t)}\cdot\mu^{-1}\frac{\partial\tilde{p}_{A(t)}}{\partial e_{A(t)}}(e_{A(t)}^{*},e_{B(t)}^{*})\cdot\mu v_{At}=\pi_{A}^{(t)}\,\frac{\partial\tilde{p}_{A(t)}}{\partial e_{A(t)}}(e_{A(t)}^{*},e_{B(t)}^{*})\,v_{At}=c_{At},

and analogously for
B(t)
. By uniqueness of the interior within-battle equilibrium,

e_{i(t)}^{*}(\mu v_{At},\mu v_{Bt})=\mu\,e_{i(t)}^{*}(v_{At},v_{Bt}),\qquad i\in\{A,B\}.

Applying effort-HD-0 of
\tilde{p}_{A(t)}
,

p_{At}(\mu v_{At},\mu v_{Bt})=\tilde{p}_{A(t)}\!\big(\mu e_{A(t)}^{*},\mu e_{B(t)}^{*}\big)=\tilde{p}_{A(t)}\!\big(e_{A(t)}^{*},e_{B(t)}^{*}\big)=p_{At}(v_{At},v_{Bt}).

Hence
p_{At}
is HD-0 in
(v_{At},v_{Bt})
.

Proof of (ii).

Because
v_{At}
is the prize
conditional on team A winning the overall contest
, the marginal effect on
\mathrm{Prob}_{A}
of an extra unit of
v_{At}
at an interior team-level equilibrium is
\theta^{*}(t)\cdot\partial p_{At}/\partial v_{At}
. The manager’s Lagrangian first-order conditions are therefore

\theta^{*}(t)\cdot\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}=\lambda_{A},\qquad\theta^{*}(t)\cdot\frac{\partial p_{Bt}}{\partial v_{Bt}}=\lambda_{B},\qquad t\in\mathcal{N},

(28)

where
\theta^{*}(t)
is the equilibrium pivotality (common to both teams by the zero-sum identity
\mathrm{Prob}_{A}+\mathrm{Prob}_{B}=1
holding battle by battle), and
\lambda_{A},\lambda_{B}
are the budget multipliers.

By part (i),
p_{At}
is HD-0 in
(v_{At},v_{Bt})
. Euler’s identity gives

v_{At}\,\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}+v_{Bt}\,\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{Bt}}=0,

and
p_{At}+p_{Bt}=1
implies
\partial p_{Bt}/\partial v_{Bt}=-\partial p_{At}/\partial v_{Bt}
, so

v_{At}\,\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}=v_{Bt}\,\frac{\partial p_{Bt}}{\partial v_{Bt}}.

(29)

Dividing team A’s first-order condition by team B’s at the same battle
t
cancels
\theta^{*}(t)
, and applying (
29
) yields
v_{Bt}/v_{At}=\lambda_{A}/\lambda_{B}
, independent of
t
. Combined with
\sum_{t}v_{At}^{*}=W_{A}
and
\sum_{t}v_{Bt}^{*}=W_{B}
,

\frac{v_{Bt}^{*}}{v_{At}^{*}}=\frac{W_{B}}{W_{A}}\triangleq k\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathcal{N}.

Proof of (iii).

By part (i) and part (ii),

p_{At}^{*}=p_{At}(v_{At}^{*},v_{Bt}^{*})=p_{At}(1,\,v_{Bt}^{*}/v_{At}^{*})=p_{At}(1,k),

which depends only on the primitives of battle
t
and on
k
.

Proof of (iv).

Substituting
v_{Bt}^{*}=kv_{At}^{*}
into team A’s first-order condition in (
28
),

\lambda_{A}=\theta^{*}(t)\cdot\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}\bigg|_{(v_{At}^{*},\,kv_{At}^{*})}.

Since
p_{At}
is HD-0 by part (i),
\partial p_{At}/\partial v_{At}
is homogeneous of degree
-1
, so for any reference scale

v_{At}^{*}\cdot\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}\bigg|_{(v_{At}^{*},\,kv_{At}^{*})}=x\cdot\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}\bigg|_{(x,\,kx)}.

Multiplying the first-order condition by

,

v_{At}^{*}\,\lambda_{A}=\theta^{*}(t)\cdot x\cdot\frac{\partial p_{At}}{\partial v_{At}}\bigg|_{(x,\,kx)}=S_{t}^{\mathrm{HD0}}.

Summing over

v_{At}^{*}=W_{A}\cdot\frac{S_{t}^{\mathrm{HD0}}}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{N}}S_{s}^{\mathrm{HD0}}},\qquad v_{Bt}^{*}=k\,v_{At}^{*}=W_{B}\cdot\frac{S_{t}^{\mathrm{HD0}}}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{N}}S_{s}^{\mathrm{HD0}}}.
