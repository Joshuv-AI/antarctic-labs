Parkinsonism induced by carbon monoxide intoxication: A “hidden gem” in the neurological literature
Authors: Omer Abdullah, Peter A. LeWitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinsonism can be acquired by various toxic exposures, among them carbon monoxide. Since the publication of a 1972 report of Parkinsonism associated with repeated episodes of carbon monoxide exposure, the medical literature has recorded multiple cases. A distinctive bilateral change in a specific brain region, the globi pallidi, provides strong evidence for carbon monoxide-induced Parkinsonism. In addition to acute intoxication with carbon monoxide, more chronic environmental exposures may also increase the risk for Parkinsonism. Paradoxically, recent research suggests that exposure to low-dose carbon monoxide might actually have a protective effect against Parkinson disease - but this needs to be carefully and safely tested.Plain language summary titleParkinsonism associated with carbon monoxide exposure

Exposure to carbon monoxide (CO) has long been recognized for causing widespread and severe CNS damage. 1 Other outcomes can be associated with more selective neurological syndromes such as Parkinsonism. As reported in a “hidden gem” from 1972, neurologists Steven Ringel and Harold Klawans described a 48-year-old man who, over several years, experienced several CO exposures associated with repeated episodes of unconsciousness. 2 Eventually, levodopa-responsive Parkinsonism developed. This report proposed that the patient's prior CO exposures best explained the emergence of Parkinsonian features, based on his relatively young age and enlarged lateral cerebral ventricles seen on pneumoencephalography. Whether or not the authors’ diagnostic assertion was correct (especially in the absence of post-mortem confirmation), this publication directed medical attention at the possibility that a relatively common environmental exposure could lead to Parkinsonism. The authors reviewed other cases also suggesting a cause-and-effect relationship between CO exposure and acquired Parkinsonian features. A subsequent publication by Klawans and colleagues provided another case of Parkinsonism following CO exposure. 3 In this report, the patient provided more compelling evidence in the form of a typical lesion arising from CO intoxication: a brain CT scan revealing bilateral pallidal lucencies. This radiological picture conforms to the neuropathological hallmark of CO-induced damage, localized bilateral necrosis of globi pallidim.4,5 While the vulnerability of the globi pallidim from acute CO exposure is not well understood, the distinctive pathological findings in this brain region include hemorrhagic and edematous changes6,7 that are sometimes associated with MRI signal changes found in substantia nigra. 8 Another neuroimaging hallmark of CO intoxication was described using mapping of brain metabolism using 3-Tesla T1-weighted MR imaging and 18-F-fluoro-deoxyglucose positron emission tomography. 9 In these studies, the structural and functional changes resulting from CO intoxication were associated with a distinctive brain metabolic covariant network changes that differed substantially from normal brain and findings typical of Parkinson disease. 9

The medical literature now includes other instances of subacute or delayed Parkinsonism developing after CO exposure. For example, in a review of 242 CO-poisoned patients who underwent neurological examination 2–4 weeks later, 13.2% of them developed a movement disorder (of these, 72% were Parkinsonism). 10 Of 23 acute CO exposures leading to Parkinsonism, the onset of motor impairments (gait disturbance, slowed movement, rigidity, masked face, and cognitive impairment) followed an asymptomatic interval that lasted between 2–26 weeks. 11 Resting tremor was not observed. These patients tended not to be responsive to levodopa or to anticholinergic medication. In another study, a population-based cohort investigation, one or more prior CO exposures that resulted in medical attention were subsequently associated with a 9.08-fold increased adjusted Cox hazard ratio (95%-confidence interval: 6.21–13.3) for the documented development of Parkinsonism. 12 Another study also concluded that the chronic environmental influence from exposure to CO-containing air pollution was associated with increased incidence of Parkinsonism. 13

The patient's neurological history was noncontributory except for his occupational experience. For more than 3 decades, his work involved daily exposure to automobile exhaust fumes in a poorly-ventilated workshop. He had never experienced episodes of intoxication. His 3-Tesla head MRI (Figure 1) exhibits generalized cerebral atrophy and bilateral globi pallidi lesions characteristic of CO-induced focal necrosis. The axial T2-weighted and axial FLAIR images demonstrate rounded areas of hyperintensity within the globi pallidi (arrows). An axial T2 gradient echo image demonstrates central foci of blooming artifact (which are suggestive of either chronic hemosiderin deposition or calcification). Based on the patient's history and the characteristic radiological findings, his chronic CO exposure from inhalation of engine exhaust fumes provides a likely explanation for the Parkinsonian motor impairment (and, possibly, his cognitive decline).

While any amount of exposure to CO might seem undesirable for brain health, there have been paradoxical observations that low doses actually initiate responses with the potential for counteracting progression of PD. 14 Such findings may help to explain the findings of multiple epidemiological studies describing reduced risk for PD conferred as a dose-related effect from habitual tobacco use.15,16 The CO content of cigarette smoke results in chronic measurable levels of hemoglobin-bound CO (generally less than 10% of concentration thresholds associated with neurotoxicity). 14 Nonetheless, this amount of CO exposure may exert sufficient pharmacological impetus for promoting cascades of CNS cytoprotective responses (through anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative, and lysosomal activation). These responses appear to be mediated by heme oxygenase-1, as has been shown experimentally in several PD-relevant animal toxin and genetic models. 14

Another consequence of chronic CO exposure is a dose-dependent reduction in tissue transport of oxygen. Recent research found that, by itself, hypoxia can be neuroprotective for dopaminergic neurons. 17 In experiments that used a mouse model of PD (created by injection of alpha-synuclein preformed fibrils into substantia nigra), a hypoxic state was produced by reducing the ambient atmosphere to 11% oxygen. This change from the normal 21% concentration of air prevented preformed fibril-induced outcomes that are otherwise observed in the experimentally-treated mice, including lipid peroxidation, alpha-synuclein toxicity to substantia nigra neurons, and neuronal dropout. 18 The presumed protective mechanism is thought to be the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-dependent genes and their downstream actions.
