Foliar application of chitosan-silicon nanoparticles and salicylic acid enhances salinity tolerance and yield of common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) under field conditions
Authors: El-Sayed M Desoky, Mohammed S Alotaibi, Mohamed T El-Saadony, Mohammad AA Al-Najjar, Fardous I Alhashmi, Betty T Mathew, Khaled A El-Tarabily, Synan F AbuQamar, Mostafa M Rady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalinity stress restricts the growth and productivity of common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), a salt-sensitive legume. This study evaluated the foliar application of nanoparticle (NP) formulations to alleviate salt stress under moderate soil salinity (EC 7.71–7.83 dS m⁻¹).A two-season field experiment tested foliar sprays with salicylic acid (SA), silicon nanoparticles (Si-NPs), chitosan nanoparticles (Ch-NPs), chitosan-silicon nanoparticles [Ch-Si (NPs)], and Ch-Si (NPs) + SA. Growth parameters, photosynthetic pigments, gas exchange, oxidative stress markers, antioxidant enzyme activities, osmoprotectants, nutrient content, and pod yield were measured.ResultsSalinity stress reduced plant growth, photosynthetic efficiency, and nutrient balance while increasing oxidative damage. All treatments mitigated these effects, with Ch-Si (NPs) + SA showing the greatest improvement. Compared with the saline control, this treatment increased shoot dry weight and green pod yield by approximately twofold and enhanced net photosynthetic rate by about 40%. Leaf K⁺/Na⁺ ratios increased by 113.9%–126.3%, while Na⁺ content decreased by 37.2%–39.9%, electrolyte leakage by 44.7%–46.7%, and malondialdehyde by 55.8%–59.0% across both seasons. Ch-Si (NPs) + SA also increased activities of enzymatic antioxidants (catalase, peroxidase, ascorbate peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, glutathione reductase) and levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants (proline, ascorbate, glutathione, α-tocopherol) compared with the control.ConclusionsFoliar application of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA was associated with improved physiological performance, oxidative balance, and yield of common bean grown under saline field conditions. This combination may offer a promising biostimulant strategy for legume production in salt-affected soils, though further research is needed to establish broader applicability and long-term effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09248-5.Keywords: Antioxidant defense, Biostimulants, Nanotechnology, Oxidative stress, Physiological performance, Salinity tolerance, Soil salinization

Introduction

Legume crops play a central role in global food and nutritional security as the world population continues to rise [1]. They are a rich source of plant-based protein and dietary minerals and contribute to sustainable agriculture by fixing atmospheric nitrogen, thereby reducing the need for synthetic fertilizers [2, 3]. Within this group, the common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is among the most widely cultivated and consumed legumes, serving as a key protein source for humans and livestock worldwide [4].

Despite its nutritional and agronomic importance, the common bean is highly sensitive to salt stress, which limits productivity, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions [5]. In the Middle East, including Egypt, 20–30% of bean cultivation occurs on salt-affected soils, and industrial activities further exacerbate salinity contamination [6, 7]. This widespread soil degradation threatens the sustainability of legume production and regional food security [8].

Soil salinization is one of the most pervasive environmental constraints to crop production, affecting more than 20% of irrigated land globally [9]. High salinity disrupts plant water relations and ion balance, resulting in osmotic stress, ionic toxicity, nutrient deficiencies, and oxidative damage [10, 11]. Sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions accumulate in plant tissues, disturbing potassium (K⁺) uptake and enzymatic function, while osmotic stress restricts water absorption and cell expansion [12]. The resulting metabolic impairment manifests in reduced leaf area, root growth, and biomass accumulation [13–15]. Beyond these growth restrictions, salinity also severely compromises photosynthetic performance by reducing chlorophyll content, inducing stomatal closure, and inhibiting carbon dioxide assimilation [16–18]. These constraints are further exacerbated by the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and hydroxyl radicals (OH−), which can damage membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids [19, 20].

To cope with oxidative stress, plants deploy an antioxidant defense system comprising enzymatic components such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and glutathione reductase (GR), as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants such as proline, ascorbate, and α-tocopherol (α-TOC) [21, 22]. Nevertheless, the efficiency of these endogenous defenses is often insufficient under prolonged or severe salinity stress, necessitating the use of external ameliorative agents [23]. Among such agents, salicylic acid (SA), silicon (Si), and chitosan (Ch) have emerged as particularly promising.

SA acts as a key signaling molecule by modulating antioxidant metabolism, osmolyte synthesis, and the activity of ion transporters such as salt overly sensitive 1 (SOS1) and sodium/hydrogen exchanger 1 (NHX1) [24, 25]. Si enhances stress tolerance by strengthening cell walls, improving water status, and regulating ion transport, thereby reducing Na⁺ accumulation and increasing K⁺/Na⁺ ratios [26–28]. Ch, a biodegradable biopolymer, enhances plant resilience by activating defense signaling cascades, promoting stomatal regulation, and improving osmotic adjustment [29–32].

Recent advances in nanotechnology offer new opportunities to deliver these agents more effectively, as nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit unique properties, such as high surface area and enhanced bioavailability, that enable efficient delivery at lower concentrations [33, 34]. Si-NPs are particularly effective in reducing oxidative damage and improving photosynthesis under salinity [35], while Ch-NPs have shown promise in activating antioxidant systems [31, 36].

Despite these promising individual effects, field-based assessments of combined nanoscale formulations containing SA, Si-NPs, and Ch-NPs in legumes remain scarce. Most published studies have evaluated single compounds or dual combinations under controlled greenhouse conditions, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of how multifunctional nanocomposite sprays perform under realistic field salinity. Furthermore, the potential synergistic effects of combining Ch-Si (NPs) with SA have not been tested in common bean under moderate soil salinity in open-field conditions across multiple growing seasons.

Given the distinct benefits of Si and Ch in a nanoscale formulation together with SA, this approach may offer synergistic protection against salinity stress by integrating structural reinforcement, hormonal signaling, and antioxidant activation. Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that foliar application of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA would enhance the growth, physiology, and yield of common bean under salinity stress by improving photosynthesis, ion balance, and antioxidant defense, compared with individual or dual treatments.

Accordingly, this study aimed to: (i) assess the effects of SA, Si-NPs, Ch-NPs, Ch-Si (NPs), and Ch-Si (NPs) + SA on bean growth and yield grown under saline field conditions; (ii) characterize physiological and biochemical responses, including photosynthetic pigments, gas exchange, water status, and oxidative stress; and (iii) elucidate stress tolerance mechanisms through antioxidant activity, osmoprotectant accumulation, and nutrient homeostasis. To our knowledge, this is the first field-based study evaluating the combined foliar application of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA in common bean under moderate salinity across two consecutive growing seasons, providing new insight into how multifunctional nanocomposite sprays can enhance legume resilience and productivity on salt-affected soils.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of Ch-NPs and Si-NPs

An isolate of Penicillium oxalicum was utilized to synthesize green, eco-friendly Ch-NPs. Ch (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Taufkirchen, Germany) was mixed with the fungal solution under optimal conditions. UV-visible spectroscopy was utilized to confirm NP formation. The particle size distribution and morphology were determined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The surface charge (zeta potential) and hydrodynamic diameter were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) [37, 38].

The resulting Ch-NPs exhibited a zeta potential of + 33.1 mV and a size of 62.1 nm. They also absorbed UV radiation at 266 nm and exhibited a spherical, polydisperse morphology, consistent with the findings reported earlier [38].

On the other hand, the eco-friendly Si-NPs were synthesized by reacting potassium hexafluorosilicate (K₂SiF₆) (Sigma-Aldrich) with the Si-tolerant fungal strain Aspergillus tubingensis AM11 under optimal conditions [37]. The synthesized Si-NPs exhibited UV absorbance at 350 nm by UV spectroscopy and were confirmed to be spherical by TEM.

Si-NPs exhibited a negative charge (zeta potential: -23.3 mV) and possessed a diminutive size of 31.55 nm, as measured by a zeta sizer, similar to the findings reported previously [37].

Experimental layout

Two field experiments were conducted during the 2024 and 2025 growing seasons at a private farm in Al-Husayniyah, Sharqia Governorate, Egypt. The soil at the site (Table S1) was moderately saline, with paste-extract electrical conductivities (EC) of 7.71 dS m⁻¹ and 7.83 dS m⁻¹ in 2024 and 2025, respectively, following Black [39] and Jackson [40]. Soil was classified according to Dahnke and Whitney [41].

Common bean (cv. Bronco), a salt-sensitive cultivar widely cultivated in Egypt, was obtained from the Vegetative Research Section, Horticulture Research Institute, Agricultural Research Center, Giza, Egypt. Seeds were surface sterilized in 0.1% HgCl2, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, and air-dried at 20°C.

Sowing was performed on February 24, 2024, and February 25, 2025, at a rate of 95 kg ha⁻¹. Plots measured 3.0 m × 3.5 m (10.5 m²) and consisted of five rows spaced 60 cm apart, with plants spaced 15–20 cm within the row. Four seeds hill− 1 were sown and later thinned to two plants before the first irrigation. Plots were irrigated with a drip irrigation system throughout the growing seasons, in accordance with local agronomic recommendations. Before planting, the soil was amended with 280 kg ha⁻¹ of ammonium sulfate ((NH4)2SO4, 20% N), 350 kg ha⁻¹ of calcium superphosphate (15.5% P2O5), and 35 kg ha⁻¹ of potassium sulfate (50% K). The experiment was arranged in a randomized complete block (RCB) structure with three replicates.

Foliar application

Six foliar treatments were applied on salinity-stressed plants sprayed with (i) tap water only (saline control), (ii) SA (Sigma-Aldrich; 70 mg L− 1), (iii) Si-NPs (100 mg L⁻¹), (iv) Ch-NPs (100 mg L⁻¹), (v) Ch-Si (NPs) (100 mg L⁻¹ each), and (vi) Ch-Si (NPs) (100 mg L⁻¹ each), and SA (70 mg L⁻¹). The concentrations used in this study were based on previous research on legume plants [42, 43].

All plants, including those in the saline control group, were grown under the same moderate soil salinity conditions (EC 7.71–7.83 dS m⁻¹). The saline control plants received only tap water with no protective foliar treatment, whereas treated plants received the specified foliar sprays. All treatment solutions were prepared fresh on the day of application using tap water as the carrier. Tween-20 (0.1%, v/v, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to all solutions as a surfactant to ensure uniform droplet distribution on leaf surfaces.

Foliar sprays were applied three times during each growing season at the following growth stages: first application at 20 days after sowing (DAS, vegetative growth stage), second application at 30 DAS (flowering initiation), and third application at 40 DAS (pod formation stage). Applications were performed early in the morning (between 6:00 and 8:00 a.m.) to maximize absorption and minimize evaporative loss.

A hand-held pressure sprayer (Matabi, 16 L capacity, Goizper, Spain) fitted with a cone nozzle was used to spray plants until runoff, ensuring complete coverage of both upper and lower leaf surfaces. The spray volume was calibrated to deliver approximately 500 L ha⁻¹ per application, equivalent to 525 mL plot− 1 (10.5 m²). Care was taken to avoid spray drift between adjacent plots by using portable plastic shields during application.

Morphological trials and yield components

Fifty-five DAS, ten plants were randomly sampled from each plot to measure shoot length, number of leaves plant− 1, leaf area, and shoot dry weight. Dry weight was determined after oven-drying at 70°C to constant mass.

At the green pod stage, the number of pods plant⁻¹ was determined from 50 randomly selected plants plot− 1. Green pod yield was calculated based on the total fresh pod yield harvested from the entire plot area (10.5 m²) and then converted to Mg ha⁻¹.

Photosynthetic pigments, gas exchange, and nutrient content

Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids were quantified in 80% acetone extracts following Fadeel [44] using a Shimadzu spectrophotometer (UV-160 A, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), and transpiration rate (Tr) were measured on fully expanded leaves using a portable photosynthesis system (LF6400XTR, LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Leaf N, P, K, calcium (Ca2⁺), and Na⁺ were determined after sulfuric acid–H₂O₂ digestion [45]. K⁺, Na⁺, and Ca2⁺ were measured using flame photometry [46], P colorimetrically using the ascorbic-acid (AsA) method [47], and the total N using the Kjeldahl method [48].

Leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized with 5 mL 0.07% NaH2PO4·2H2O and 1.6% Na2HPO4·12H2O (50 mM) and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 25 min at 4°C. The results of MDA were expressed as µM g− 1 FW [52].

Bean leaves (100 mg) were cut into 1 mm × 1 mm fragments and immersed for 1 h at room temperature in 10 mM K-phosphate buffer, pH 7.8, 0.05% nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), and 10 mM Sodium azide (NaN3). Two mL of the immersed solutions were heated at 85°C for 15 min, then cooled rapidly. Optical density was measured colorimetrically at 580 nm, and the O2•− content was expressed as µM g− 1 FW [53].

H₂O₂ was determined using the method described previously [54]. Plant tissue was extracted in acetone. Titanium reagent and ammonium were added to the extract and dissolved in sulphuric acid (1 M). Absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 415 nm. The results of H2O2 concentration were expressed as µM g− 1 FW.

Osmoprotectants, antioxidants, and phenolic compounds

The method described by Bates et al. [55] was used to determine proline accumulation in bean leaves. Second fresh leaf material, 0.1 g, was ground with 10 mL of 3% (w/v) aqueous sulphosalicylic acid, and the homogenate was filtered through Whatman 2 filter paper (Whatman, Maidstone, UK), and 1 mL of filtrate was reacted with 1 mL acid ninhydrin reagent and 1 mL glacial acetic acid in test tube for one h at 100°C, and the reaction terminated in an ice bath. Two mL of toluene were added to the mixture, and the upper toluene layer was measured for absorbance at 520 nm using a UV spectrophotometer.

The method described by Kampfenkel et al. [56] was used to assess the content (µM g‒1 FW) of AsA by adding the extract to a constituent of 30 mM potassium phosphate buffer pH 7.4 + 2.5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) + 8.4% H2PO4 + 0.8% bipyridyl + 0.3% ferric chloride (FeCl3). At 40°C, the reaction was performed for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 525 nm.

Contents (µM g‒1 FW) of reduced and total glutathione (GSH) were assessed as previously described [57]. GSH content was assessed using a reaction mixture [leaf extract + 130 mM sodium phosphate buffer; pH 7.4 + 7 mM sodium phosphate buffer; pH 6.8 with 6 mM 5,5-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB)]. At 30°C, the reaction mixture was left for 10 min, and the absorbance was then recorded at 412 nm.

Total soluble sugar (TSS) content was assessed as follows: 0.2 g of leaves was washed with 5 mL 70% ethanol and homogenized with 5 mL 96% ethanol. The extract was centrifuged at 3500 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and stored at 4◦C [58]. Freshly prepared anthrone (3 mL) was added to 0.1 mL supernatant. This mixture was incubated in hot water bath for 10 min. The absorbance was recorded at 625 nm using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan).

α-TOC was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using methanol/water (94:6, v/v) as the mobile phase, with detection at 292 nm [59]. Total phenolics were analyzed using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent [60], while flavonoids and flavonols were determined as described by Bahorun et al. [61] and Kumaran and Karunakaran [62, 63]. The antioxidant potential was assessed using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical-scavenging activity [64] and reducing power [65].

Antioxidant enzyme assays

Enzyme extraction was performed as previously described [66]. Fresh leaf samples were collected in an icebox, then washed with distilled water and wiped to remove surface moisture. Leaf samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in ice-cold 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) containing 0.5 mM EDTA with a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was then transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min in a Beckman refrigerated centrifuge at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to 30 mL tubes and designated the enzyme extract.

CAT was assayed spectro-photometrically according to Maehly and Chance [67]. The enzyme extract (100 µL) was added to 100 µL of 100 mM H2O2, and the mixture was brought to a final volume of 1 mL with 250 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). The decrease in optical density at 240 nm relative to the blank was recorded every minute.

The method described by Thomas et al. [68] was used to estimate peroxidase (POX) activity in bean leaves. The enzyme was assayed using guaiacol as the substrate. The reaction mixture consisted of 3 mL of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.0), 30 mL of H2O2 (20 mM), 50 mL of enzyme extract, and 50 mL of guaiacol (20 mM). The reaction mixture was incubated in a cuvette at room temperature for 10 min. The optical density was measured at 436 nm, and the enzyme activity was expressed as absorbance units g− 1 of fresh leaf weight.

APX was assayed spectrophotometrically as described by Nakano and Asada [69]. The assay was carried out at 25°C in a 1.0 cm light path cuvette, and the reaction mixture consisted of 1500 µL pH 7.0 phosphate buffer, 20 µL EDTA, 1000 µL sodium ascorbate, and enzyme extract (20 µL). After mixing, the reaction was initiated by adding the 480 µL H2O2, and the decrease in optical density at 290 nm against the blank (without extract) was continuously recorded every min (for two min).

SOD activity was determined by measuring the decrease in absorbance of the superoxide-NBT complex catalyzed by the enzyme [70]. About 3 mL of reaction mixture containing 0.2 mL of 200 mM methionine, 0.1 mL of 1.5 M sodium carbonate, 0.1 mL of 3 mM EDTA, 0.1 mL of 2.25 mM NBT, 1.5 mL of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer pH 7.0, 1 mL of distilled water, and 0.05 mL of enzyme were taken in test tubes in duplicate from each enzyme sample.

Two tubes without enzyme extract were used as controls. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 mL of riboflavin (60 µM) and placing the tubes under a light source consisting of two 15 W fluorescent lamps for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by switching off the light and covering the tubes with a black cloth. Tubes without the enzyme developed maximal color.

A non-irradiated complete reaction mixture, which did not develop color, served as a blank. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm, and one unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to reduce the absorbance of the samples to 50% of that of tubes lacking the enzyme.

GR activity was measured by monitoring the oxidation of NADPH over three absorbance readings at 340 nm and expressed as A564 min− 1 g− 1 protein [71].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed separately for the growing seasons of 2024 and 2025 to account for year-to-year environmental variations, including differences in soil salinity, temperature, and rainfall. Data was analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for an RCB design with three replicates, implemented in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Within each season, treatment means were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) test at P ≤ 0.05%. Comparisons between seasons were not performed, as evaluating seasonal effects was beyond the scope of this study and would require a different experimental framework (e.g., multi-year blocking). A principal component analysis (PCA) was also performed using XLSTAT version 2019.3.2 (Addinsoft, Long Island, New York, USA). Prior to PCA, the data were mean-centered and scaled to unit variance to place all variables on a comparable scale.

Results

Effect of foliar applications on growth and yield of P. vulgaris

To evaluate whether foliar treatments could ameliorate salinity-induced growth suppression, we first measured key growth and yield parameters. Foliar application of SA, Si-NPs, Ch-NPs, Ch-Si (NPs) or Ch-Si (NPs) + SA significantly affected growth and yield attributes of bean plants grown under saline field conditions (Fig. 1). All treatments improved shoot length (Fig. 1A), leaf number (Fig. 1B), leaf area (Fig. 1C), plant dry weight (Fig. 1D), number of pods plant − 1 (Fig. 1E), and green pod yield (Fig. 1F) compared with the saline control. The greatest responses were observed with the Ch-Si (NPs) + SA treatment.

Relative to the control, Ch-Si (NPs) + SA increased shoot length by 125.4% and 125.6%, leaf number by 55.6% and 61.0%, and leaf area by 50.3% and 43.0% in the 2024 and 2025 seasons, respectively. Plant dry weight increased by 112.4% in 2024 and 113.1% in 2025. Yield components showed similar trends, with pod number increasing by 72.0% and 53.7%, and green-pod yield by 127.3% and 111.7% in the 2024 and 2025 seasons, respectively (Fig. 1). The Ch-Si (NPs) treatment showed the second-largest improvement, while individual SA, Si-NPs, and Ch-NPs treatments produced moderate but significant improvements relative to the control (Fig. 1).

Effect of foliar applications on leaf photosynthetic pigments and photosynthetic efficiency of P. vulgaris

We also checked if these benefits were associated with enhanced photosynthetic capacity. Foliar treatments significantly influenced leaf pigment composition and photosynthetic activity in common bean under salinity. Salinity control plants sprayed with water showed reduced chlorophyll a (Fig. 2A), chlorophyll b (Fig. 2B), carotenoids (Fig. 2C), Pn (Fig. 2D), Tr (Fig. 2E), and Gs (Fig. 2F).

All foliar treatments were associated with higher values of these parameters than the control, with the greatest differences observed in plants treated with Ch-Si (NPs) + SA. Compared with the salinity control, this treatment increased chlorophyll a by 31.1% and 31.1%, chlorophyll b by 45.5% and 43.0%, and carotenoids by 55.0% and 54.5% during the 2024 and 2025 seasons, respectively. Photosynthetic performance also showed treatment-associated differences. Pn was higher by 40.0% and 42.2%, Tr by 85.7% and 85.0%, and Gs by 85.2% and 80.0% compared to the control across the two seasons (Fig. 2). Ch–Si (NPs) showed the second-largest improvements, followed by individual SA, Si-NPs, and Ch-NPs treatments (Fig. 2).

Effect of foliar applications on leaf integrity and oxidative stress markers in P. vulgaris

To determine whether the observed improvements in photosynthesis were linked to reduced oxidative damage, we assessed membrane integrity and oxidative stress markers. Salinity stress was associated with increased oxidative injury and membrane damage in saline-stressed bean plants, as evidenced by higher levels of H2O2, O2•‒, MDA, and EL, and lower RWC and MSI than in treated plants (Table 1).

Effect of foliar applications on osmoprotectants and antioxidant metabolites of P. vulgaris

Because reduced oxidative stress can result from enhanced osmotic adjustment and non-enzymatic antioxidant accumulation, we measured osmoprotectants and key metabolites. Foliar application of SA, Si-NPs, Ch-NPs, Ch–Si (NPs), or Ch-Si (NPs) + SA was associated with significant differences in the accumulation of osmoprotectants and non-enzymatic antioxidants in common bean leaves exposed to salinity stress (Fig. 3). Discussion

Soil salinity is a major abiotic constraint that reduces plant growth and yield by disrupting water relations, nutrient uptake, and metabolism through osmotic and ionic stress [72]. In the present study, moderate salinity (EC ~ 7.8 dS m⁻¹) was associated with substantial decreases in shoot growth, leaf area, photosynthetic rate, and yield of common bean, consistent with previous reports in beans and other glycophytes [73, 74].

These effects are primarily attributed to osmotic inhibition of root water absorption, reduced cell turgor, and decreased rates of cell division and expansion [15]. Moreover, salinity promotes the generation of ROS such as H2O2, O2•−, and OH−, which cause oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and damage to chloroplasts, membranes, and nucleic acids [75, 76]. The accumulation of Na⁺ in leaf tissues (Table 3) further exacerbates this damage by displacing K⁺ from enzyme-binding sites and disrupting protein conformation [12]. Against this background of multifactorial stress, the foliar treatments tested in this study, particularly the Ch-Si (NPs) + SA formulation, appear to have mitigated these deleterious effects through complementary mechanisms at the physiological, biochemical, and ionic levels.

The foliar application of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA was associated with superior growth, photosynthesis, and yield compared with saline controls or single-component treatments. This synergistic effect can be explained by the distinct but complementary functions of each component. Si strengthens cell walls by depositing amorphous silica in leaf epidermal cells, thereby improving mechanical resistance and reducing water loss under osmotic stress [77, 78]. Si also enhances the activity of H⁺-ATPase in root plasma membranes, facilitating K⁺ uptake and limiting Na⁺ influx [79].

As a phytohormone, SA activates systemic acquired resistance and modulates the expression of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes (CAT, APX, and SOD) and ion transporters (SOS1 and NHX1) [24, 25]. Ch acts as a biotic elicitor, triggering defense signaling cascades involving nitric oxide and H₂O₂ as secondary messengers, which primes the plant for faster and stronger activation of stress responses [31, 80]. When delivered as a composite nanoscale formulation, these three agents likely act in a coordinated manner, in which Si-NPs provide structural reinforcement and regulate ions, Ch-NPs enhance cellular uptake and elicit defense priming, and SA fine-tunes hormonal signaling and antioxidant gene expression.

The observed increases in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids in Ch-Si (NPs) + SA-treated plants (Fig. 2) are consistent with recent findings that Si-NPs protect chloroplast ultrastructure by stabilizing thylakoid membranes and preventing chlorophyll degradation under salinity [35, 81]. Similarly, SA has been shown to upregulate genes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis (HEMA1, CHLH) and downregulate chlorophyllase activity [82].

The substantial improvements in Pn, Tr, and Gs indicate that Ch-Si (NPs) + SA treatment alleviated both stomatal and non-stomatal limitations to photosynthesis. The higher Gs in treated plants suggest that the treatment helped maintain stomatal aperture, possibly through SA-mediated regulation of abscisic acid signaling [83], while the concurrent increase in Tr indicates improved water transport, consistent with the higher RWC and MSI observed in these plants (Table 1). Non-stomatal improvements likely include enhanced Rubisco activity and chloroplast electron transport, as recently demonstrated in barley under salt stress treated with Si-NPs [77].

A key finding of this study is that the Ch-Si (NPs) + SA treatment was associated with substantially lower oxidative stress markers (MDA, H₂O₂, and O₂•⁻) and lower EL compared with saline controls (Table 1). This reduction in oxidative damage can be attributed to the coordinated upregulation of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems. Among enzymatic antioxidants, Ch-Si (NPs) + SA increased CAT activity by 29%, POX by 66%, APX by 20%, SOD by 45%, and GR by 32% relative to controls (Table 4). The simultaneous activation of SOD (which dismutates O₂•⁻ to H₂O₂) and CAT/APX (which scavenge H₂O₂) indicates that the treatment enhanced the complete ROS scavenging pathways, preventing H₂O₂ accumulation and subsequent hydroxyl radical formation via the Fenton reaction [21, 22]. Recent studies have shown that Ch-NPs can directly activate the expression of SOD, CAT, and APX genes through the NPR1-dependent defense signaling pathway [31, 36].

SA, in turn, binds to NPR1 and promotes its nuclear translocation, leading to the transcriptional activation of pathogenesis-related genes and antioxidant enzymes [25]. The combination of Ch-NPs and SA may therefore have a synergistic effect on antioxidant gene expression, though direct molecular evidence (e.g., qPCR or transcriptomic data) would be needed to confirm this mechanism in common bean.

Non-enzymatic antioxidants and osmoprotectants also showed marked increases in treated plants, including proline, TSS, AsA, GSH, and α-Toc, which were all higher compared with controls (Fig. 3). Proline and soluble sugars contribute to osmotic adjustment by lowering cellular water potential, thereby facilitating water uptake under saline conditions [22]. AsA and GSH function as direct ROS scavengers and also participate in the ascorbate-glutathione cycle, which regenerates oxidized antioxidants using NADPH [21].

The higher GSH levels in treated plants (Fig. 3D) are particularly noteworthy because GSH is a substrate for glutathione peroxidase and helps maintain cellular redox balance. α-Toc, a lipophilic antioxidant localized in chloroplast membranes, protects photosystem II from photo-oxidative damage by quenching singlet oxygen and terminating lipid peroxidation chain reactions [83]. Secondary metabolites, including total phenols, flavonoids, and flavonols, were also higher in treated plants (Table 2). These compounds not only scavenge ROS directly but also chelate metal ions and inhibit lipid peroxidation [84].

The DPPH radical-scavenging activity (+ 20%) and reducing power (+ 26%) in treated plants confirm that the enhanced phenolic content translated into greater functional antioxidant capacity. These findings align with recent reports that chitosan application stimulates the phenylpropanoid pathway by upregulating PAL and C4H genes [29, 85].

In glycophytes such as common beans, maintaining a low cytosolic Na⁺ concentration and a high K⁺/Na⁺ ratio is essential for salt tolerance [86]. In the current study, Ch-Si (NPs) + SA-treated plants showed 38–40% lower leaf Na⁺ accumulation and 33–35% higher K⁺ content compared with saline controls, resulting in a 114%–126% increase in the K⁺/Na⁺ ratio (Table 3). This improvement in ion homeostasis is likely reflected in several mechanisms. First, Si is known to reduce Na⁺ influx by downregulating the expression of non-selective cation channels and upregulating H⁺-ATPase activity, which generates a proton gradient that drives Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters (SOS1 and NHX1) [28, 79].

Second, SA has been shown to increase the transcript levels of SOS1 and NHX1 in Brassica species under salt stress [24]. Third, Ch oligosaccharides can induce the expression of HAK (high-affinity K⁺ transporter) genes, improving K⁺ uptake and retention [31]. The combination of these three agents in a nanoscale formulation may therefore act on multiple-ion transport pathways simultaneously, leading to the superior K⁺/Na⁺ ratios observed. In addition, treated plants showed higher leaf concentrations of N, P, and Ca+2 (Table 3). Improved N content may result from enhanced root N uptake and assimilation, as Si has been reported to increase nitrate reductase activity [27].

Higher P levels could reflect improved root architecture and mycorrhizal colonization, although these were not directly assessed. The dramatic increase in Ca+2 is particularly interesting because Ca+2 serves as a secondary messenger in stress signaling and helps stabilize cell walls and membranes [13]. Elevated Ca+2 may also contribute to Na⁺ exclusion by activating the SOS pathway, as SOS3 is a Ca-binding protein [24].

PCA (Fig. 5) provided an integrated view of treatment effects, with PC1 explaining 97.97% of total variance. The clear separation of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA from all other treatments along PC1 indicates that this formulation induced a distinct physiological and biochemical state characterized by higher values for growth, photosynthesis, antioxidant enzymes, osmoprotectants, and nutrient content, and lower values for stress markers (MDA, H₂O₂, and EL). The high contribution of PC1 suggests that the measured traits were strongly correlated and largely aligned along a single latent axis representing overall stress tolerance and physiological resilience.

This multivariate separation provides strong statistical support for the conclusion that Ch-Si (NPs) + SA outperformed single or dual treatments in mitigating salinity stress. This field-based study demonstrated the synergistic effects of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA on salinity tolerance in common bean across two consecutive growing seasons. While previous studies have evaluated individual components in controlled environments [31, 35, 81], our work provides real-world field evidence under moderate soil salinity, which is highly representative of salt-affected agricultural lands in arid and semi-arid regions. The multi-season design strengthens the reliability of our findings, as consistent results were obtained despite year-to-year environmental variations in temperature, rainfall, and baseline soil salinity.

Several mechanistic considerations and study limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. The mechanistic interpretations presented above are inferred from physiological and biochemical measurements rather than from direct molecular evidence. For example, while we observed increased activities of CAT, SOD, and APX, we did not measure the transcript levels of the corresponding genes or protein abundances. Future studies should employ qPCR, Western blotting, or proteomic approaches to confirm whether the enhanced enzyme activities result from transcriptional upregulation, post-translational activation, or both. Similarly, the improved ion homeostasis we observed is consistent with enhanced SOS1/NHX1 activity, but direct measurements of ion fluxes or gene expression analysis would be needed to confirm this mechanism. Our study used a single concentration for each treatment (100 mg L⁻¹ for nanoparticles; 70 mg L⁻¹ for SA) based on previous legume studies [42, 43].

A dose–response experiment would be necessary to determine the optimal concentration for each component and their combinations. The field experiments were conducted at a single location over two growing seasons. Multi-location trials across different soil types, salinity levels, and climatic zones would be required to establish broader applicability. We also did not assess potential long-term effects of repeated NP applications, such as NP accumulation in edible pods (food safety), effects on soil microbial communities, or off-target movement to non-crop plants or groundwater [87]. Economic feasibility was not evaluated. A cost–benefit analysis comparing the additional yield gained with the costs of NP synthesis, formulation, and application would determine if this approach is economically viable for smallholder farmers in salt-affected regions.

Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA action using transcriptomics and proteomics, optimizing NP dosage and application frequency, conducting multi-location field trials, assessing long-term soil health and food safety, and evaluating economic viability. In addition, integrating Ch-Si (NPs) + SA with other sustainable practices, such as biochar application, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi inoculation, or precision irrigation, could potentially enhance resilience further through additive or synergistic effects.

In summary, this field study demonstrates that Ch-Si (NPs) + SA was associated with improved growth, photosynthetic efficiency, osmotic regulation, and membrane integrity in common bean under saline conditions.

Conclusions

This field study demonstrates that foliar application of Ch-Si (NPs) + SA improves salinity tolerance in common bean (P. vulgaris L.) under moderate soil salinity (EC ~ 7.8 dS m⁻¹). Mechanistically, Ch-Si (NPs) + SA treatment enhanced (i) the activities of enzymatic antioxidants (SOD, CAT, APX, GR, POX) and levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants (proline, AsA, GSH, and α-Toc), reducing oxidative damage (lower MDA, H₂O₂, O₂•⁻, and EL); (ii) K⁺/Na⁺ homeostasis by reducing Na⁺ accumulation while increasing K⁺ content; and (iii) photosynthetic performance (higher chlorophyll pigments, Pn, Tr, and Gs) and osmoprotectant accumulation (proline and TSS). Collectively, these mechanisms resulted in superior growth and pod yield compared with individual SA, Si-NPs, or Ch-NPs treatments.

The stated hypothesis is supported by improved growth and yield, preserved photosynthetic capacity, reduced oxidative stress via enhanced antioxidant systems, improved ion homeostasis and K⁺/Na⁺ ratios, and showed distinct clustering in multivariate analyses in plants treated with Ch-Si (NPs) + SA.

From a practical perspective, Ch-Si (NPs) + SA foliar spray offers a promising, environmentally friendly biostimulant strategy to support legume production on salt-affected soils in arid and semi-arid regions. From a scientific perspective, this study provides field-based evidence across two growing seasons that a triple-component nanocomposite formulation (Si-NPs, Ch-NPs, and SA) can synergistically activate antioxidant defenses, maintain ion balance, and protect photosynthetic function under moderate salinity. However, further research is needed to validate these findings across diverse environments, optimize application parameters, elucidate molecular mechanisms, and assess long-term environmental and economic impacts before large-scale recommendations can be made.
