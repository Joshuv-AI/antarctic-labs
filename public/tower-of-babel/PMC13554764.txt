Clinical Impacts and Procedural Risks of Rotational Atherectomy for Heavily Calcified Femoropopliteal Lesions
Authors: Takahiro Tokuda, Naoki Yoshioka, Akiko Tanaka, Shunsuke Kojima, Kohei Yamaguchi, Takashi Yanagiuchi, Kenji Ogata, Tatsuro Takei, Tatsuya Nakama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether atherectomy-assisted drug-coated balloon (DCB) therapy provides durable benefit in severely calcified femoropopliteal (FP) lesions remains uncertain.ObjectivesIn this study, we wanted to compare clinical outcomes of rotational atherectomy plus DCB vs DCB alone in severely calcified FP lesions.MethodsIn this multicenter retrospective study, 582 de novo FP lesions with Peripheral Arterial Calcium Scoring System grades 3 to 4 were evaluated. After propensity score matching, 153 lesion pairs treated with DCB alone or rotational atherectomy plus DCB were analyzed. The primary endpoint was 2-year primary patency. Hierarchical Cox models adjusted for baseline, procedural, and acute angiographic factors.ResultsThe median follow-up duration was 439 days (IQR: 232-760). Rotational atherectomy use resulted in significantly lower postprocedural residual stenosis (16.7% [95% CI: 14.9-18.4] vs 20.3% [95% CI: 17.8-22.9]; P = 0.019) and fewer lesions with residual stenosis >30% (9/153 lesions, 5.9% [95% CI: 3.1-10.8] vs 22/153 lesions, 14.4% [95% CI: 9.7-20.8]; P = 0.012). One-year primary patency was higher in the rotational atherectomy group (86.2% [95% CI: 80.0-92.3] vs 75.1% [95% CI: 67.8-82.4]; P = 0.01), but this difference attenuated at 2 years (64.4% [95% CI: 52.4-76.4] vs 57.1% [95% CI: 47.1-65.9]; P = 0.07). In hierarchical Cox models, rotational atherectomy use remained independently associated with improved patency after adjusting for acute angiographic results (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.31-1.00; P = 0.05). Procedural complications were markedly higher with rotational atherectomy, driven by distal embolization.ConclusionsAlthough rotational atherectomy improves acute luminal results, the early patency benefit is not durable and is offset by increased procedural risk, supporting selective rather than routine use.

Severe arterial calcification remains one of the most formidable barriers to durable femoropopliteal (FP) endovascular therapy (EVT).1,2 Extensive arterial calcium reduces vessel compliance, impairs optimal balloon expansion, and may limit effective drug transfer, thereby attenuating the efficacy of drug-coated balloons (DCBs). In particular, lesions classified as Peripheral Arterial Calcium Scoring System (PACSS) grade 4 have been associated with higher rates of restenosis and repeat revascularization despite contemporary DCB therapy.3

Importantly, clinical outcomes may not be determined solely by the longitudinal extent of calcification. Emerging data suggest that focal but nodular or protrusive calcified lesions can also disrupt drug delivery and mechanical expansion, potentially leading to unfavorable patency even in relatively short segments.4,5 These findings highlight the complex interplay between calcium burden, lesion morphology, and vascular response after EVT.

To address these challenges, plaque-modification strategies aimed at improving lesion preparation have gained increasing attention. Atherectomy devices are designed to debulk calcified tissue, increase acute luminal gain, and potentially facilitate more homogeneous drug uptake when used in conjunction with DCB therapy.6,7 Among these, the rotational atherectomy system, which combines rotational cutting with active aspiration, has been increasingly used in the treatment of severely calcified FP lesions.8,9

However, the incremental clinical benefit of atherectomy-assisted DCB therapy remains uncertain. Small sample sizes, heterogeneous definitions of calcification severity, and variability in lesion complexity limit available data.10 Furthermore, safety concerns persist, particularly regarding the risk of distal embolization associated with plaque debulking.11 Notably, contemporary guidelines do not provide specific recommendations supporting the routine use of atherectomy in FP interventions, reflecting the absence of robust high-level evidence.12

Beyond conventional time-to-event endpoints such as primary patency or clinically driven target lesion revascularization (CD-TLR), restenosis morphology itself may carry important mechanistic and therapeutic implications. Patterns of restenosis—focal, diffuse, or occlusive—may reflect distinct biological responses to mechanical plaque modification and drug delivery, yet these morphologic outcomes have not been systematically examined in comparative studies.13,14 Accordingly, we conducted a multicenter study to compare procedural safety, long-term clinical outcomes, and restenosis morphology between rotational atherectomy plus DCB and DCB alone in patients with severely calcified FP lesions classified as PACSS grade 3 or 4.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a multicenter, retrospective, observational study conducted at 8 cardiovascular centers in Japan. Consecutive patients undergoing EVT for de novo FP lesions between April 2018 and December 2024 were screened. Lesions were eligible if they were classified as PACSS grade 3 or 4 based on angiographic assessment. Patients with in-stent restenosis, nonatherosclerotic disease, or acute limb ischemia (ALI) at presentation were excluded. Given the extremely small number of bailout stent implantations, these lesions were excluded from the primary analysis to preserve the integrity and statistical robustness of comparisons between the predefined endovascular strategies. Because prior studies have reported inferior patency outcomes with the Lutonix (BD) DCB platform and its use was limited in our cohort, lesions treated with Lutonix DCBs were excluded from the primary analysis to minimize potential confounding related to device-specific performance.1 Therefore, 582 lesions met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. Treatment strategy was determined at the operator's discretion and consisted of either DCB angioplasty alone or rotational atherectomy followed by DCB therapy.

This multicenter retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of the coordinating center (Ethics Committee, Nagoya Heart Center; approval no. NHC2024-1007-02). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Endovascular procedure

All procedures were performed according to contemporary standard practice. Lesion crossing was achieved using guidewires and support catheters at the discretion of the operator. In the rotational atherectomy group, the rotational atherectomy system was used before DCB angioplasty. Adjunctive lesion preparation, including high-pressure, cutting, or scoring balloons, was allowed in both treatment arms according to operator judgment. In cases where intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) was used, balloon sizing was based on the external elastic membrane diameter. Balloon inflation time was left to the discretion of the operator and generally ranged between 1 and 3 minutes.15

DCBs were selected according to lesion length and reference vessel diameter, with overlapping inflation when necessary. The use of embolic protection devices was left to operator discretion.

Endpoints and definitions

The primary effectiveness endpoint was 2-year primary patency, defined as freedom from binary restenosis (>50% diameter stenosis on angiography or peak systolic velocity ratio ≥2.4 on duplex ultrasound) and freedom from CD-TLR. Prespecified secondary endpoints were 1-year primary patency, CD-TLR, ALI, major amputation, and procedural outcomes. Procedural outcomes were defined as dissection greater than or equal to type D, residual stenosis, distal embolization, vessel perforation, access site complications requiring treatment, or other adverse events occurring during or immediately after the procedure.

Restenosis morphology was assessed angiographically among lesions that developed restenosis and categorized as focal, diffuse, or occlusive patterns. Morphologic analyses were considered exploratory.

Follow-up

Clinical follow-up was performed at regular intervals according to institutional protocols. Clinical follow-up was performed according to institutional practice. Vascular imaging evaluation, including duplex ultrasound, computed tomography angiography, or catheter-based angiography, was typically conducted at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years after the index procedure, as clinically indicated. The choice of imaging modality was determined by the treating physician at each participating center.

Follow-up duration was calculated from the date of the index EVT procedure to the date of the first occurrence of the relevant clinical endpoint or the last available clinical or imaging follow-up. For time-to-event analyses, lesions without the specific endpoint were censored at the date of the last documented follow-up confirming the absence of that endpoint. For primary patency, lesions were censored at the last imaging assessment if binary restenosis or CD-TLR had not occurred. Death without prior loss of patency was treated as censoring at the date of death.

Statistical analysis

To adjust for baseline differences between treatment strategies, propensity score matching was performed using a logistic regression model incorporating clinically relevant patient and lesion characteristics. The propensity score for receiving rotational atherectomy plus DCB therapy was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model that included patient characteristics, medication status, preprocedural index, and lesion characteristics listed in Tables 1 and 2. Specifically, the following variables were included: age, sex, body mass index, ambulatory status, chronic limb threatening ischemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hemodialysis, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, current smoking, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, direct oral anticoagulant use, cilostazol use, statin use, preprocedural ankle brachial index, chronic total occlusion, popliteal artery involvement, lesion length, diameter stenosis, reference vessel diameter, and the number of runoff vessels. Covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences, with an absolute standardized mean difference < 0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance between groups. Standardized mean differences were calculated for all variables included in the propensity score model.16

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard models were constructed to evaluate the association between treatment strategy and time-to-event outcomes. HRs with 95% CIs were reported.

In the matched cohort, Cox proportional hazard models were fitted using robust sandwich variance estimators clustered by matched pairs to account for within-pair correlation. The primary Cox model included treatment strategy as the only covariate and was considered the main treatment-effect model. Additional exploratory models were sequentially constructed to examine the robustness of the association and to explore potential procedural mechanisms. Model 2 further adjusted for procedural and device-related variables, including balloon diameter, adjunctive balloon strategy (high-pressure, cutting, or scoring balloon use), balloon inflation time, IVUS use, and DCB type, to account for differences in treatment technique. Model 3 additionally adjusted for acute angiographic results, including postprocedural residual stenosis >30% and severe dissection great than or equal to type D. Because postprocedural residual stenosis and dissection may lie on the causal pathway between atherectomy and subsequent restenosis, these sequentially adjusted models were interpreted as exploratory mechanistic analyses rather than as primary causal estimates. These sequential models were prespecified as sensitivity analyses to examine whether the observed treatment effect was mediated by procedural characteristics or acute luminal optimization.

Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to evaluate whether the association between treatment strategy and primary patency differed across clinically relevant patient and lesion subgroups. The following subgroups were examined: sex, clinical presentation, diabetes mellitus, hemodialysis, chronic total occlusion, lesion length ≥ 200 mm, popliteal artery involvement, and reference vessel diameter ≥ 6 mm. These subgroups were selected before outcome analysis based on prior evidence and biological plausibility suggesting that patient risk profile, lesion complexity, and vessel size may influence the effectiveness and safety of atherectomy-assisted DCB therapy in calcified FP lesions. Because this was a retrospective observational study, these subgroup analyses were not registered in a public repository. However, the subgroup variables were defined before outcome analysis in the study analysis plan. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons, subgroup findings were interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Student's t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 19 (SAS Institute).

Results

Study population

Between April 2018 and December 2024, 582 de novo FP lesions with severe calcification (PACSS grade 3 or 4) were included in the analysis. Of these, 319 lesions were treated with DCB angioplasty alone, and 263 lesions were treated with rotational atherectomy followed by DCB therapy.

After propensity score matching, 153 well-balanced lesion pairs were identified (Figure 1). Baseline patient and lesion characteristics were comparable between the 2 groups after matching, with all standardized mean differences < 0.10. The median follow-up duration in the matched cohort was 439 days (IQR, 232-760). Mean patient age was 73.4 ± 10.5 years in the DCB group and 73.6 ± 9.2 years in the rotational atherectomy plus DCB group. Of 153 lesions, 113 (73.9%) occurred in men in the DCB group and 112 (73.2%) occurred in men in the rotational atherectomy plus DCB group. Chronic limb threatening ischemia was present in 56 of 153 (36.6%) and 49 of 153 (32.0%), respectively, and hemodialysis was common (83/153 [54.3%] and 86/153 [56.2%]) (Table 1).

Procedural characteristics and outcomes

Lesion characteristics were well balanced after matching, including chronic total occlusion (37/153 [24.2%] vs 40/153 [26.1%]), popliteal artery involvement (66/153 [43.1%] in both groups), lesion length (150 mm [IQR: 80-240] vs 150 mm [IQR: 80-250], baseline diameter stenosis (92.0% ± 6.6% vs 91.9% ± 8.2%), and reference vessel diameter (5.76 ± 0.60 mm vs 5.78 ± 0.71 mm) (Table 2).

Procedural findings demonstrated significantly lower residual stenosis in the rotational atherectomy plus DCB group compared with the DCB-alone group (16.7% [95% CI: 14.9-18.4] vs 20.3% [95% CI: 17.8-22.9]; P = 0.019). Primary patency

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly higher primary patency in the rotational atherectomy plus DCB group at 1 year compared with the DCB-alone group (86.2% [95% CI: 80.0-92.3] vs 75.1% [95% CI: 67.8-82.4]; log-rank P = 0.01). However, this difference attenuated over time and was no longer statistically significant at 2 years (64.4% [95% CI: 52.4-76.4] vs 57.1% [95% CI: 47.1-65.9]; log-rank P = 0.07) (Figures 2A and 2B). Discussion

Principal findings

In this multicenter, propensity score–matched analysis restricted to severely calcified FP lesions (PACSS grade 3 or 4), several mechanistically important observations emerged. First, rotational atherectomy–assisted DCB therapy achieved significantly lower postprocedural residual stenosis and fewer lesions with substantial residual narrowing (>30%) without increasing severe dissection, demonstrating improved acute luminal optimization without exacerbating major intimal injury. Second, this mechanical advantage translated into a reduction in early restenosis hazard, as reflected by improved 1-year primary patency and significant associations in adjusted Cox models. Notably, this association persisted even after adjustment for residual stenosis and dissection severity, indicating that the observed early benefit was not fully explained by angiographic luminal gain alone. Third, this early hazard reduction attenuated over time and did not reduce CD-TLR, whereas procedural complications such as predominantly distal embolization were substantially increased. Collectively, these findings delineate a paradox: Atherectomy improves acute mechanics but does not overcome the long-term biological limits imposed by severe arterial calcification (Central illustration).

Acute luminal optimization vs biological ceiling

The significant reduction in residual stenosis without an increase in severe dissection confirms the procedural efficacy of plaque debulking in rigid calcified segments. In heavily calcified arteries, elastic recoil and incomplete balloon expansion often compromise drug delivery and luminal geometry. By improving vessel compliance, atherectomy may facilitate more homogeneous paclitaxel uptake and reduce early hazard.17

However, the persistence of the treatment effect after statistical adjustment for residual stenosis suggests that acute luminal diameter does not fully capture the mechanistic impact of plaque modification. Potential mechanisms include improved surface drug contact, microfracturing of calcified plates, and altered local hemodynamics. Yet, despite these early advantages, the attenuation of benefit at 2 years strongly suggests the presence of a biological “ceiling effect.” Medial calcification, chronic inflammatory signaling, and progressive negative remodeling may ultimately dominate long-term vessel fate, limiting the durability of mechanically driven strategies.18 Thus, in the setting of severe calcification, mechanical optimization appears to be necessary but insufficient for sustained disease modification.

Lack of reduction in CD-TLR

Despite improved early patency, rotational atherectomy use did not result in a significant reduction in CD-TLR at either 1 or 2 years. This apparent discordance highlights an important distinction between angiographic durability and clinically driven reintervention. In patients with severe calcification, restenosis may remain asymptomatic or hemodynamically tolerated, particularly in those with limited functional demand. Conversely, when restenosis does occur after atherectomy, its morphology might be more complex, potentially offsetting any early patency benefit in terms of clinical outcomes.

Procedural safety considerations

Procedural complications were markedly more frequent in the rotational atherectomy group, primarily driven by distal embolization. This finding is consistent with prior atherectomy studies and reflects the inherent risk of plaque fragmentation in heavily calcified lesions.11 Although most embolic events were manageable, their high incidence raises important safety concerns and highlights the need for meticulous procedural planning. Selective use of embolic protection devices, careful patient selection, and operator experience may be essential to mitigate this risk.

Restenosis morphology as a novel insight

Another novel observation was the numerical shift toward an occlusive pattern of restenosis in the rotational atherectomy group (43% vs 23% in DCB alone). Although not statistically significant, this trend warrants caution. Extensive debulking in severely calcified segments may lead to deeper vascular wall injury, extending into the media or adventitia. Such mechanical trauma is known to provoke a more aggressive fibroproliferative response and negative remodeling compared with the diffuse neointimal hyperplasia typically observed after conventional balloon angioplasty.19 From a clinical standpoint, converting a diffuse restenosis into a total occlusion could complicate future reinterventions and limit the long-term durability of the limb-salvage strategy. Further studies are warranted to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these observations.

Subgroup and interaction analysis

Interaction analysis demonstrated no significant treatment effect modification across a wide range of clinical and lesion characteristics, including diabetes, hemodialysis, lesion length, chronic total occlusion, and vessel diameter. These findings suggest that the modest benefit of atherectomy-assisted DCB therapy is broadly consistent and that no clearly defined subgroup derives a disproportionate advantage. This reinforces the notion that atherectomy should not be applied indiscriminately but rather considered selectively based on individualized procedural goals.

Clinical implications

Our results do not support the routine, indiscriminate use of rotational atherectomy for all calcified FP lesions. Instead, a "selective atherectomy" strategy appears to be more rational, reserved for cases where adequate balloon expansion is unachievable or for specific nodular morphologies where debulking is procedurally essential. Furthermore, the high rate of embolic events observed here mandates the standardized use of embolic protection devices and meticulous aspiration techniques whenever rotational atherectomy is used in vessels with a high calcium burden.

Study limitations

First, its retrospective and nonrandomized design introduces the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding despite the use of propensity score matching. Although matching improved covariate balance, it reduced the effective sample size and may have limited statistical power, particularly for less frequent outcomes. In addition, treatment strategies were determined at the discretion of the operators, and certain lesion-specific characteristics, such as calcium morphology, were not systematically captured. Therefore, unmeasured confounding cannot be completely ruled out, and the findings may not generalize to unmatched patients with extreme baseline characteristics.

Procedural techniques, device selection, and the use of embolic protection were not standardized across participating centers, reflecting real-world practice. Especially, the use of embolic protection devices was left to the operator's discretion and not standardized. Given the high rate of distal embolization in the rotational atherectomy group, the lack of mandatory use of embolic protection devices use may have negatively influenced the clinical outcomes and safety profile of the atherectomy cohort. In addition, various types of DCBs were used during the study period. Although we adjusted for DCB type in our Cox models, potential differences in drug-transfer efficiency among different platforms in the presence of severe calcium cannot be entirely ruled out. Exclusion of bailout stent cases may have introduced selection bias; however, the extremely low frequency makes a significant impact on overall results unlikely.

Lesion severity was assessed using angiographic PACSS grading without systematic use of intravascular imaging, such as IVUS or optical coherence tomography. Although PACSS is a widely used angiographic classification, the lack of detailed intravascular imaging limits mechanistic interpretation, particularly regarding calcium distribution and plaque morphology.

Residual stenosis and dissection severity were assessed at each site and were not adjudicated by an independent core laboratory, which may have introduced measurement variability and potential misclassification. Furthermore, although postprocedural variables were included in selected multivariable models, these factors may act as mediators rather than confounders, and thus overadjustment cannot be fully excluded.

Restenosis and its morphology was determined using angiography, computed tomography angiography, or duplex ultrasound according to local clinical practice. Although a standardized classification scheme was applied, the use of multiple imaging modalities may have introduced classification bias. Finally, the high prevalence of hemodialysis patients in this cohort may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. However, this reflects a high-risk real-world population, and the present findings may be particularly relevant to clinical decision-making in this challenging subgroup.

Conclusions

Rotational atherectomy–assisted DCB therapy significantly reduces postprocedural residual stenosis without increasing severe dissection and improves early primary patency in severely calcified FP lesions. However, this acute mechanical advantage does not translate into durable clinical benefit and is offset by substantially increased procedural risk. These findings support a selective, mechanism-driven use of atherectomy rather than routine application in heavily calcified lesions.
