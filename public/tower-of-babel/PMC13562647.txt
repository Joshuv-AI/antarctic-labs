High-gamma frequency-tagged magnetoencephalography reveals time-resolved task-related functional coupling during spatial cognition
Authors: Kenji Yoshiki, Masashi Kinoshita, Ruochu Xiong, Yoshihiro Misaka, Kiwamu Kudo, Eiichi Okumura, Hirofumi Morise, Riho Nakajima, Mitsutoshi Nakada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFast periodic visual stimulation (FPVS) combined with magnetoencephalography (MEG) provides a powerful approach for extracting frequency-specific brain responses from perceptual processing areas. However, its application in cognitive paradigms, particularly those addressing visuospatial attention, remains underexplored. This study developed an FPVS-based line bisection paradigm to probe visuospatial cognition during visual exploration of the line center using eye movements without manual responses. Line stimuli were spatially modulated at high-gamma frequencies (70–90 Hz), centered at 80 Hz. Source analysis in healthy volunteers revealed a sequential pattern of cortical activation, beginning in the bilateral occipital cortices around 100 ms and subsequently involving right temporoparietal, frontal, and superior parietal regions between approximately 130 and 220 ms. Formal frequency-domain analysis confirmed that 80 Hz Morlet time-frequency power was significantly higher during stimulation than during the pre-stimulus baseline period. Functional connectivity analysis revealed task-related coupling between parietal regions and occipital, frontal, and temporal cortices. These connectivity patterns were anatomically plausible in the context of visuospatial processing. Overall, FPVS-MEG enabled temporally precise mapping of task-related cortical activity and functional coupling during visuospatial processing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58801-7.Keywords: Fast periodic visual stimulation, Frequency tagging, High gamma band, Line bisection task, Magnetoencephalography, Visuospatial cognitionSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Visuospatial ability is a core aspect of visual perception that enables the processing of spatial orientation and the location of objects in the environment1. The right hemisphere predominantly governs visuospatial processing in most individuals, and lesions in this region frequently result in visuospatial deficits, most prominently manifested as unilateral spatial neglect2. This condition is characterized by a failure to detect or respond to stimuli on the side contralateral to a brain lesion, despite preserved visual fields, sensory input, and motor functions3. The right parietal lobe—particularly the inferior parietal lobule (IPL) and supramarginal gyrus—has been consistently implicated as a key region underlying such impairments4,5.

Various clinical tools, including cancellation and drawing tasks, are widely used to evaluate visuospatial disorders. Among these, the line bisection task is simple, intuitive, and frequently used at the bedside6. Although factors such as hemianopia and handedness limit the task’s diagnostic sensitivity, it remains valuable as a research tool for probing spatial position judgment, providing insights into a distinct aspect of visuospatial processing beyond visual search7. Neuroimaging studies have also applied the line bisection paradigm to magnetoencephalography (MEG), reporting robust activity in the right inferior parietal cortex, consistent with lesion-based evidence8,9.

To further explore visuospatial cognition and expand the utility of the line bisection task within the context of MEG, we focused on fast periodic visual stimulation (FPVS)—a technique that elicits steady-state responses at specific stimulation frequencies10. High-frequency FPVS confers several advantages, such as generating robust brain responses to visual periodicity11; minimizing interference with endogenous oscillations12; and allowing the use of visual stimuli above 50 Hz, which are nearly imperceptible and do not disrupt the perception of images or text12,13. Although FPVS has been widely used in MEG studies to investigate sensory processing10, its application in cognitive paradigms—particularly those addressing visuospatial attention—remains underexplored.

Gamma-band activity has been implicated in local cortical information processing14, and high-frequency visual tagging has been used to probe visual cortical responses while reducing interference with lower-frequency endogenous rhythms11,12,15. Thus, examining tagged responses in the high-gamma range is physiologically plausible in the context of visuospatial processing. At the same time, the selection of the 70–90 Hz range in the present study was driven primarily by methodological considerations. Lower-frequency flicker is more perceptible and may be more likely to cause discomfort or photosensitive responses in susceptible individuals16, whereas rapid invisible frequency tagging above approximately 50–60 Hz remains measurable with MEG11,12,15. We therefore selected the 70–90 Hz range as a practical compromise between physiological plausibility, reduced perceptibility of flicker, and signal detectability.

Therefore, the present study employed the line bisection task as a simple and intuitive paradigm tailored for FPVS in MEG, rather than as a clinical screening tool. By embedding high-frequency tags at different spatial locations along the line, we aimed to detect visuospatial attention-related neural activity directly through frequency-specific MEG signals. Here, we introduce a novel FPVS-based MEG paradigm that incorporates the line bisection task, enabling frequency-tagged decoding of visuospatial attention and offering a promising approach for mapping higher-order cognitive processes in the human brain.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-five healthy volunteers (13 males and 12 females), with a mean age of 41.9 years (standard deviation = 12.3), participated in the study. The inclusion criteria were no history of psychiatric or neurological disorders and normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity. Participants completed the line bisection test on paper before MEG recording. All showed a deviation from the true midline within 12.5 mm (within the normal range), with a mean deviation of 4.25 ± 0.34 mm.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study’s protocol was approved by the Kanazawa University Medical Ethics Review Committee (approval no. 3341) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with the journal’s anonymisation policy, no personally identifiable information appears in the manuscript, figures, tables, or supplements; coloured bars/shapes or blurring were not used. Where individual anatomical MRIs are shown, scans were defaced prior to export. The manuscript includes an illustrative photograph of the MEG setup (Fig. 1C); written informed consent to publish this image in an online open-access journal was obtained from the individual depicted. No other identifiable participant information or images are included. Handedness information was not available for all participants and therefore was not included as a covariate in the present analysis.

Protocol of the line bisection task with fast periodic visual stimulation (FPVS). (a) Line bisection task with FPVS. In the conventional tabletop line bisection test, commonly used in clinical practice to assess left unilateral spatial neglect, three-line segments are presented at 37.56° (204 mm) in the upper left, center, and lower right positions. In the present task, a line segment at 37.56° (204 mm) was displayed on a screen (300 × 210 mm) with a line thickness of 1.84° (10 mm). The normal range was defined as 2.41° (12.6 mm) inward from the midpoint on both sides, and modulation was set at 80 Hz. Modulation frequency was altered by ± 2 Hz in 3.15-mm increments beyond the normal range, with the rightmost and leftmost sides set at 70 and 90 Hz, respectively, at ≥ 25.1 mm from the center. The image contrast parameters (8-bit RGB, 0 = black and 255 = white) were as follows: background, [R, G,B]=[63,63,63]; line segment, [128,128,128]-[255,255,255]; and upper/lower portions of the line segment, [0,0,0]-[128,128,128]. (b) Stimulus protocol. The paradigm consisted of 120 trials, each alternating between the 3-s random presentation of line segments and a 3-s fixation block with a central crosshair. (c) Magnetoencephalography (MEG) data acquisition. Participants performed the FPVS-based line bisection task while lying supine in the MEG scanner. Stimuli were projected onto a screen positioned 300 mm in front of the eyes inside a magnetically shielded room. The screen width was 300 mm, and line segments were scaled to 37.56° (204 mm) to match the standard paper-based bisection task. Participants were instructed to remain still, use only eye movements to explore the line center, and minimize blinking during the task. Thus, the upper-left/center/lower-right arrangement refers to the conventional paper-based clinical test, whereas the present MEG paradigm used single-line presentations at three horizontally shifted positions.

Task design for magnetoencephalography (MEG)

A line bisection task specially designed for MEG was implemented. Stimuli were programmed using MATLAB 2020a (MathWorks Inc., Natick, USA) with Psychophysics Toolbox v3.0.1717,18 and presented via a PROPixx system (VPixx Technologies, Canada). This system receives a single 1920 × 1080, 120 Hz RGB video signal and divides it into four 960 × 540 quadrant images, achieving a 12-fold increase in refresh rate (4 quadrants × 3 color channels × 120 Hz = 1,440 Hz)13. By modulating RGB codes’ amplitude at specific frequencies, line segments and other display elements could be uniquely distinguished by their frequency.

Details of the visual stimuli used in the line bisection task are described below. In the conventional tabletop line bisection test, three lines are typically arranged in upper-left, center, and lower-right positions on the paper sheet. In contrast, the MEG task used in the present study presented a single line stimulus at one of three horizontal positions on the screen: centered, shifted 32 mm to the left, or shifted 32 mm to the right. The central portion of the line segment, defined as the “normal” range (12.6 mm inward from the midpoint on both sides, consistent with clinical criteria for line bisection), was tagged at 80 Hz. Frequencies were increased or decreased by 2 Hz for each 3.15 mm step beyond the normal range, with the rightmost and leftmost portions tagged at 70 and 90 Hz, respectively (≥ 25.1 mm from the center; Fig. 1a). Each line segment measured 10 mm in vertical width. Three types of line bisection stimuli with different spatial positions were prepared: one centered and two shifted 32 mm either to the left or right. These stimuli were randomly presented across trials. These three horizontal positions were used to reduce position-related bias across trials and were not intended as separate experimental conditions for testing position-specific neural effects. Therefore, trials were pooled across these spatial positions in the present analyses. Each trial lasted 6 s, comprising a 3-s fixation stimulus (a central crosshair) followed by a 3-s line bisection stimulus. One set included 120 trials (Fig. 1b and Supplementary figure S1 and Supplementary video 1). Participants performed the 12-min line bisection task (120 trials × 6 s) while lying in a supine position (Fig. 1c). Participants were instructed to keep their head and limbs still and to use eye movements only to visually explore and judge the center of the line stimulus. No manual pointing or button response was required during MEG recording. The task was completed in two separate sessions (12 min each), resulting in MEG signals from a total of 240 trials per participant.

A distinctive feature of this study is the use of frequency tagging at high-gamma frequencies (70, 80, and 90 Hz) during the line bisection task to probe higher cognitive functions, enabled by a projector with a 1,440-Hz refresh rate. The 70–90 Hz range was selected as a practical compromise between physiological plausibility, reduced perceptibility of flicker, avoidance of low-frequency flicker-related discomfort or photosensitive responses, and measurable MEG signal detectability. This range was not intended to isolate a uniquely meaningful endogenous 80 Hz oscillation. Specifically, high-gamma tagging frequencies were selected to (i) avoid consciously perceived visual flicker and minimize disruption of visual perception; (ii) reduce the risk of low-frequency flicker-related discomfort or photosensitive responses, which are more commonly associated with flicker below approximately 30 Hz and are rare above 65 Hz16; (iii) maintain spectral separation between spatial tags at 2-Hz steps while allowing potential intermodulation components to be evaluated separately; and (iv) keep all tagged frequencies within the high-gamma range, thereby reducing ambiguity in interpreting frequency-tagged responses. High-frequency visual tagging above approximately 50–60 Hz is less perceptible while remaining measurable with MEG11,12,15.

Magnetic resonance imaging and MEG data acquisition

Three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetic resonance images were acquired for each participant using a SIGNA Explorer 1.5 T system (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). MEG recordings were obtained using a MEG system (Ricoh Company, Ltd., Kanazawa, Japan) installed at our research center. Signals were sampled at 2,000 Hz with an online band-pass filter (0.1–500 Hz). The procedures for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and MEG acquisition were adapted from a study conducted at the same research center19. Spherical lipid markers were placed at five MEG fiducial points to facilitate accurate co-registration of the MEG and MRI coordinate systems.

MEG data analysis

Preprocessing

MEG data were analyzed using Brainstorm20, a MATLAB-based software package. Noise recordings were conducted under stimulus-on condition without participants present (i.e., the PROPixx stimulus device was operating while the MEG room was empty), revealing abnormal electromagnetic noise at 80 Hz in seven sensors (ZF01, ZF02, LF12, RF12, LP21, RC43, RT16). These sensors were identified before the main analyses and excluded from all subsequent preprocessing, time-frequency analysis, source estimation, and connectivity visualization. The identified sensors and the sensor-exclusion procedure are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Preprocessing steps were as follows: (i) a notch filter was applied to remove 60 Hz harmonics; (ii) a 1–110 Hz band-pass filter was applied; (iii) independent component analysis was performed to remove cardiac and blink artifacts; and (iv) segments with large-amplitude noise (exceeding ± 3.5 pT) were excluded. For each trial, an epoch (analysis unit) was defined as a 6-s segment time-locked to the onset of the line bisection stimulus, spanning from 3 s before to 3 s after stimulus onset. Epochs containing muscle artifacts were identified and removed by one analyst (K.Y.). On average, 119.2 epochs per session remained after preprocessing. Each participant completed two sessions (240 trials in total), and the preprocessed epochs from both sessions were averaged and used for source estimation.

Sensor-level analysis of evoked response

Additively averaged waveforms at the sensor level were decomposed into frequency components using a fast Fourier transform from − 3,000 to 3,000 ms. All averaged epochs were further subjected to Morlet wavelet transforms at frequencies between 68 and 92 Hz for time-frequency analysis.

Source-level analysis

Source estimation was performed for each individual using the following procedure. T1-weighted anatomical MRI data were initially processed with FreeSurfer v7.1.121, and individualized three-layer boundary element models were constructed for each participant. Source activity was estimated using an overlapping-sphere head model with 15,000 cortical vertices. Distributed source modeling was performed using the minimum norm estimate (MNE), specifically the current density map implementation in Brainstorm20. Dipole orientations were constrained to be normal to the cortical surface. Depth weighting was enabled with an order of 0.5 and a maximal amount of 10. The noise covariance regularization parameter was set to 0.1, and the signal-to-noise ratio was set to 3.0. The MNE algorithm22 provides distributed estimates of cortical current density without statistical normalization (e.g., dSPM or sLORETA). No statistical normalization such as dSPM or sLORETA was applied. Individual source maps were subsequently projected onto a common anatomical template for group-level comparisons.

Group-level analysis of cortical activation

Functional analyses were conducted across 68 regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas, which were relabeled into conventional anatomical terms based on prior literature23. Time-frequency decomposition was performed using Morlet wavelet transformation within the 68–92-Hz gamma band. Signals were baseline-corrected to a pre-stimulus window of −3.000 to −0.0005 s.

Frequency-tagged response extraction (80 Hz)

A narrow band-pass filter centered at 80 Hz with a half-bandwidth of 0.25 (79.75–80.25) Hz was applied to extract the 80 Hz-centered tagged response used for frequency-specific visualization and ROI time-course summarization. The spectral resolution of our data guided the choice of this passband. Passbands narrower than Δf do not increase effective selectivity. Participant-wise power spectral density analyses confirmed that individual 80 Hz peaks consistently fell within 79.75–80.25 Hz (≈ 3·Δf). This narrow-band step was used for frequency-specific visualization and ROI time-course summarization alone.

Connectivity analysis

Imaginary coherence (iCoh)—a phase-based connectivity metric intended to reduce spurious correlations arising from volume conduction—was employed to evaluate functional connectivity between cortical regions during the FPVS-based line bisection task24. Since iCoh considers only the imaginary component of the cross-spectral density, it is more sensitive to phase-lagged interactions and less influenced by instantaneous common-source artifacts24. Although iCoh does not eliminate all possible spurious correlations (e.g., those due to common drivers or propagation effects), it provides a more conservative and interpretable estimate of functional coupling than conventional coherence metrics. This approach provides a robust framework for characterizing frequency-specific cortical network dynamics underlying visuospatial attention, minimizing artifact contamination while enhancing the interpretability of MEG-based connectivity patterns. Connectivity analysis was conducted using Brainstorm, following established methodological standards, and the results were visualized as connectograms20. iCoh values were computed over a broader gamma band (e.g., 68–92 Hz), which preserves phase variability and avoids the loss of phase-lag information that can occur with excessively narrow bands. No ultra-narrow bands (e.g., 80 ± 0.25 Hz) were used for iCoh, and the analysis window was set between − 3.0 and 3.0 s.

Source time-series data used for iCoh computation were extracted from 68 cortical regions defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas.

Seed-based analyses

In connectivity analyses, ROIs are defined based on neuroscientific priors and the purpose of a given study25. For seed-based analyses (1 × N), the right superior parietal lobule (SPL) and IPL were selected a priori based on their established roles in visuospatial attention and line-bisection performance4,5,8,9,26–28.

Whole-brain functional connectivity analysis

To examine the time-resolved dynamics of visuospatial network reorganization, whole-brain functional connectivity at 80 Hz was computed using iCoh between all cortical region pairs defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas (N × N). Connectivity maps were generated at three time points as follows: pre-stimulus (−500 ms), early post-stimulus (180 ms), and late post-stimulus (600 ms). The − 500 ms time point was selected as a representative pre-stimulus reference within the baseline period. The 180 ms time point was selected to capture the early post-stimulus frontoparietal phase observed in the present source-activity analysis. The 600 ms time point was selected as a representative late phase of network reconfiguration, guided by previous task-based MEG evidence showing attention-related parietal functional connectivity in the 575–700 ms range during attentional reorienting29. The connectivity maps were visualized as Virtual Fiber Tracks using Brainstorm30,31.

Results

Evoked visual responses during the line bisection task

Figure 2a shows representative sensor-level waveforms recorded during the line bisection task. Magnification of a representative single-sensor response showed periodic 80 Hz waveforms during visual stimulation (Fig. 2b). To formally confirm that the 80 Hz response exceeded background activity, we compared 80 Hz Morlet time-frequency power between the pre-stimulus baseline and stimulation periods across participants. The 80 Hz response was significantly higher during stimulation than during baseline (paired t-test, t (24) = 17.74, p = 2.663 × 10^−15; Fig. 2c), supporting the presence of stimulus-locked 80 Hz activity above background activity.

Figure 3 presents the time–frequency maps averaged across all participants for the left and right pericalcarine cortices. The bilateral pericalcarine cortex was selected for analysis because it encompasses the primary visual cortex (V1) in both hemispheres, where early visual responses to periodic flickering stimuli are most prominent32. Group-averaged source-space time-frequency maps in the bilateral pericalcarine cortices showed a prominent response centered in the high-gamma tagged range during visual stimulation. These findings support the ability of the high-gamma FPVS paradigm to elicit measurable stimulus-locked visual responses in healthy individuals.

Additional control analyses were performed to evaluate neighboring tagged frequencies and potential low-frequency intermodulation components. First, broad-range source-space time-frequency maps in the bilateral pericalcarine cortices showed a marked stimulus-related increase around the 80 Hz tagged frequency during visual stimulation, whereas the main low-frequency difference components at 10 and 20 Hz did not show increases comparable to the 80 Hz response (Supplementary Figure S3). Although weak increases were observed at some lower-frequency ranges, these were not comparable to the 80 Hz response and were not located at the main difference-frequency components of 10–20 Hz. This finding provided supplementary evidence that the observed 80 Hz response was not primarily explained by low-frequency difference-frequency components. In addition, within-subject sensor-space spatial correlation analyses were performed to compare the 80 Hz map with maps at 10, 20, 70, and 90 Hz (Supplementary Figure S4). Spatial correlations were computed based on baseline-corrected relative-power topographies to isolate stimulus-related activity. The 10 and 20 Hz components corresponded to the main low-frequency difference components generated by simultaneous 70, 80, and 90 Hz stimulation. The 80 Hz map showed low spatial similarity to the 10 and 20 Hz maps, whereas weak positive correlations were observed with the neighboring tagged frequencies at 70 and 90 Hz. Because stimulus-related power changes at 10 and 20 Hz were small, the low-frequency spatial correlations should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, these findings support the interpretation that the 80 Hz response was not primarily driven by low-frequency difference-frequency components, while adjacent high-frequency tagged responses shared partially overlapping spatial structure.

Frequency-specific cortical activation in healthy participants

Analyses were initially performed across all frequencies. Group-level one-sample t-tests against the pre-stimulus baseline in 25 participants revealed substantial activity at distinct time windows: around 100 ms in the bilateral lateral occipital cortex (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figure S5a), 130 ms in the right IPL and right banks of superior temporal sulcus (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Figure S5b), 180 ms in the posterior portion of the right middle frontal gyrus (putative frontal eye field) (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Figure S5c), and 220 ms in the right SPL and supramarginal gyrus along the intraparietal sulcus (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figure S5d). In these all-frequency source-activation maps (Fig. 4), no additional focal activation cluster was observed after 300 ms at the applied visualization threshold.

The results of the statistical analysis across all cortical ROIs, defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas, are summarized in Fig. 5, highlighting brain activity phase-locked to the 80-Hz visual stimulation while suppressing other frequency components.

A widespread pattern of frequency-specific cortical activation was observed at a liberal threshold (p < 1.0 × 10− 4) (Fig. 5a). Substantial responses were identified in a bilateral network including the banks of the superior temporal sulcus, cuneus, fusiform gyrus, IPL, inferior temporal gyrus, left insula, isthmus of the cingulate gyrus, lateral occipital cortex, lingual gyrus, middle temporal gyrus (MTG), paracentral lobule, parahippocampal gyrus, right pars opercularis, pericalcarine cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, SPL, supramarginal gyrus, and transverse temporal gyrus.

Applying a more stringent threshold (p < 1.0 × 10-15) revealed a focused subset of regions showing the most robust stimulus-locked 80 Hz responses (Fig. 5b). Early responses appeared in the bilateral precuneus, cuneus, pericalcarine cortex, and lateral occipital cortex shortly after stimulus onset. This was followed by activation in the bilateral SPL and bilateral IPL, which are regions well known for their role in visuospatial attention. At subsequent time points, sustained activation was detected in the lingual gyrus, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, isthmus of the cingulate gyrus, and left supramarginal gyrus. To aid readability of the dense ROI-time map, the main regions highlighted in Fig. 5b are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, in which onset latency reflects the first sustained threshold crossing of the 80 Hz-centered tagged response rather than the time of maximal activation.

Because the main analysis focused on the 80 Hz-centered tagged response, neighboring tagged frequencies were also evaluated using the same source-level ROI-time mapping framework and the same stringent threshold (p < 1.0 × 10^−15). Robust and spatially broad responses were observed at 70 Hz, whereas the 90 Hz map showed little or no clearly detectable source-level response under the same stringent threshold (Supplementary Figure S6). The 80 Hz map represented the main tagged response analyzed in the present study. These findings indicate that responses at neighboring tagged frequencies were not uniform and support the interpretation of the main result as an 80 Hz-centered tagged response rather than a fully isolated 80 Hz-specific component.

The distribution of substantial activation was largely confined to visual and visuospatially relevant regions, supporting the spatial plausibility of the narrow-band FPVS approach. These findings indicate that frequency-tagged responses at 80 Hz delineate the spatial distribution of recruited regions and capture their temporal dynamics, revealing the sequential recruitment of cortical areas underlying visuospatial processing.

Intercortical connectivity during visuospatial processing

The right IPL (Figs. 6a and 7a) exhibited functional coupling with left temporal regions and ipsilateral frontal/parietal regions, including the left superior temporal gyrus (STG), MTG, and anterior temporal lobe (ATL). These patterns were anatomically plausible and involved cortical regions commonly implicated in visuospatial and visual-association networks. The right SPL (Figs. 6b and 7a) showed functional coupling with the left medial occipital cortex, left ATL, and right IPL. However, because no diffusion MRI or tractography was performed, these findings should be interpreted as anatomically plausible functional coupling among cortical regions rather than direct evidence of signal propagation through white-matter pathways.

Regarding the results of whole-brain functional connectivity (N × N) time-resolved connectivity maps, at −500 ms (Fig. 8a), before stimulus onset, the network showed no prominent frontoparietal integration. At 180 ms, right-hemispheric functional coupling involving the IPL and FEF became apparent (Fig. 8b). At 600 ms (Fig. 8c), late-phase connectivity patterns appeared more bilaterally distributed, with contralateral and interhemispheric connections involving occipital, temporal, and parietal regions. Overall, these findings illustrate a clear temporal progression of cortical connectivity, revealing dynamic reorganization of the visuospatial attention network following periodic visual stimulation. Overall, these descriptive connectivity maps illustrate representative phases of cortical network reconfiguration during the task.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the localization of visuospatial cognition in humans using high-gamma frequency tagging with FPVS during a manual-response-free visual exploration task. This paradigm enabled objective measurement of stimulus-locked high-frequency brain responses while participants visually explored the line center using eye movements without manual responses. It also substantially enhanced brain activity in the high-gamma band, which is generally considered weak33,34. Collectively, these findings indicate that FPVS is a novel and non-invasive technique with potential for assessing higher cognitive functions in the human brain, with prospective applications in patients with neurological disorders.

Neural localization of visuospatial cognitive functions

Electroencephalography (EEG)/MEG signals at tagged frequencies reflect underlying sensory processing12. The right parietal lobe is most closely associated with visuospatial deficits, particularly in the IPL, supramarginal gyrus8,9, angular gyrus35, and temporoparietal junction—regions most commonly implicated in unilateral spatial neglect35,36. Our results align with this evidence, as substantial activity was detected in the right intraparietal sulcus compared with the left parietal lobe. We observed early occipital activation around 100 ms, followed by activity in right temporoparietal regions around 130 ms, the posterior right middle frontal gyrus around 180 ms, and the right superior parietal lobule and supramarginal gyrus around 220 ms. In the present study, the temporal profile of cortical activation was broadly consistent with prior MEG findings. A previous MEG study without frequency tagging also reported the largest number of active sources in the right IPL, primarily 200–300 ms after stimulus onset8. These findings align with prior reports and suggest a stimulus-driven response corresponding to visuospatial cognition.

The SPL showed increased activity during the line bisection task. Some studies have shown that damage to the ventral IPL and temporoparietal junction contributes to unilateral spatial neglect5,35. Functional neuroimaging evidence further suggests that the SPL is engaged in visuospatial attention during line bisection and related tasks26. The region interacts with the dorsal frontal cortex, including the FEF, and the intraparietal sulcus to form a dorsal attention network, which mediates voluntary control of visual attention and response selection37. However, the temporoparietal junction and ventral frontal cortex form the ventral attention network, specialized for detecting unexpected stimuli37. In this study, participants were presented with up to 120 stimuli per session, a design that may have directed their focus more strongly toward the on-screen task and, consequently, produced prominent activity in the dorsal attention network.

High-frequency MEG reveals dynamic interhemispheric networks for spatial cognition

We demonstrate a distinct interhemispheric network underlying spatial cognition using high-frequency MEG combined with FPVS. The observed functional connectivity patterns were anatomically plausible and involved cortical regions typically linked by major white-matter pathways, including the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) and vertical occipital fasciculus (VOF). However, because MEG measures neuronal current activity in cortical gray matter and no diffusion MRI, tractography, or structure–function correlation was performed, the present findings cannot be interpreted as direct evidence of signal propagation through white-matter pathways. Instead, they should be interpreted as anatomically plausible functional coupling among cortical regions engaged during visuospatial processing.

A key methodological feature of this study is the implementation of frequency-tagged analysis centered at 80 Hz to characterize task-related cortical responses and functional coupling during visuospatial processing. Connectivity was visualized using Brainstorm software and showed spatially plausible patterns involving cortical regions typically linked by known anatomical pathways. Notably, the IPL and SPL emerged as key parietal hubs engaged in visuospatial cognition, attention, and higher-order processing28. Functional and structural neuroimaging studies have shown that the parietal cortex maintains strong reciprocal connections with both frontal and occipital regions, underscoring its integrative role in visuospatial attention and cognitive control37. The IPL demonstrates strong connectivity with the prefrontal cortex and FEF, supporting top-down attentional control, saccadic eye movements, and visuospatial working memory27. Its spatial relationship to cortical regions associated with the VOF may be relevant to linking dorsal and ventral visual processing streams and integrating spatial location with object recognition and semantic processing38. These findings are consistent with the view that the IPL contributes to spatial perception, attention modulation, and visual search strategies.

Our study further demonstrates that substantial functional connectivity emerged between the right SPL/IPL and left temporal lobe during the line bisection task. The right IPL is widely recognized as a core region for visuospatial attention, critical for allocating attentional resources to specific spatial locations37. In contrast, the left MTG and STG play an essential role in integrating perceptual information into cognitive frameworks39. Clinical evidence shows that left temporal lobectomy is associated with marked impairment in spatial binding, with patients exhibiting a considerably higher rate of swap errors when recalling the color of spatially defined targets40. These findings suggest that the left temporal lobe is crucial for linking spatial and feature information, and that damage to this region can disrupt interhemispheric communication, potentially increasing reliance on the right parietal cortex40. Consistent with this, we observed functional coupling between SPL/IPL and the left temporal lobe during visuospatial tasks: the right IPL may contribute to spatial attention, whereas the left temporal lobe may support the integration of spatial and feature information27. Previous studies have also highlighted the role of the left medial temporal lobe (MTL) in processing spatial and temporal associations. Patients with left MTL damage usually exhibit compensatory network recruitment in the right hemisphere, reinforcing the notion that the right SPL-left ATL contributes to integrating spatial information into cognitive representations.

Our approach with FPVS provided temporally resolved insights into the reorganization of visuospatial attention networks at 80 Hz. Cognitive tasks are known to induce dynamic reconfigurations of cortical connectivity. Functional MRI studies have shown that connectivity between the central executive and default mode networks undergoes flexible modulation depending on cognitive demands41. Similarly, MEG evidence indicates that distinct connectivity patterns are engaged across different phases of task processing—such as perception, maintenance, and response—highlighting the temporal evolution of brain networks in relation to behavioral context42. Collectively, these findings suggest that high-frequency, frequency-tagged MEG may provide a useful time-resolved approach for visualizing task-related cortical functional coupling, offering a new avenue for time-resolved mapping of cognition-related cortical networks.

Frequency tagging with FPVS in the high-gamma band

Recent biologically realistic models and in vitro/in vivo evidence suggest that gamma-band oscillations (30–70 Hz) are essential for local information processing14. However, gamma-band potentials are relatively small because EEG power density and cortical local field potentials are inversely proportional to frequency43. High-gamma activity in EEG recordings is particularly susceptible to contamination by muscle artifacts, which usually exhibit relatively large amplitudes33,34. Therefore, subtle gamma modulations can be obscured by low signal-to-noise ratios. Rapid visual stimulation has been reported as a useful approach for high-frequency tagging to track neural responses, with reduced interference from lower-frequency endogenous rhythms12,15. The present study demonstrates that frequency tagging in the high-gamma range can elicit measurable stimulus-locked cortical responses in visuospatial networks, consistent with previous work suggesting reduced interference from lower-frequency endogenous rhythms12,15, supporting its utility as a non-invasive tool for mapping spatial cognition.

Limitations

This study has some limitations, particularly regarding its clinical applicability. First, the optimal presentation time of visual stimuli in MEG has not been clearly established clinically. Previous studies examining spatial exploration in patients with unilateral spatial neglect have reported delayed responses compared with healthy participants, although these patients were still able to respond within 1 s when presented with glowing dots44. In our design, the line segment was presented for 3 s. However, this duration may be insufficient to obtain reliable data for patients with brain disorders. In addition, eye movements were not recorded during MEG acquisition. Therefore, although participants were instructed to explore the line center using eye movements only while keeping the head and limbs still, trial-by-trial gaze position could not be verified. Future studies incorporating simultaneous eye tracking will be needed to dissociate visuospatial exploration, fixation behavior, and frequency-tagged neural responses more precisely.

Second, the term visuospatial cognitive impairment encompasses diverse clinical manifestations that reflect dysfunctions across multiple components of spatial cognition, and symptom profiles may vary depending on the task used5,45. Therefore, future work should examine brain dynamics during a broader range of visuospatial tasks using FPVS.

Third, although depth weighting was applied to the MNE solution, the present source analysis was intended to evaluate task-related cortical surface activity rather than deep-source quantification. MNE provides intuitive current density estimates but may still show sensitivity biases related to source depth and model assumptions22. Alternative noise-normalized inverse methods such as dSPM46 or sLORETA47 may provide different spatial properties and should be considered in future confirmatory analyses.

Fourth, individual variability in frequency-tagged responses must be considered. Prior studies have shown that FPVS signal strength and spatial specificity can vary considerably across individuals and are modulated by both attention and stimulus properties11. Although frequency tagging provides high temporal resolution and a strong signal-to-noise ratio, cortical source pattern interpretation may be influenced by inter-subject variability and the spatial spread of signals into adjacent regions—factors not specifically controlled in the present study. Nevertheless, our group-level analysis of 25 healthy participants demonstrated robust and considerable frequency-tagged neural responses in regions implicated in visuospatial cognition, suggesting that FPVS can reliably capture task-related brain activity at the group level despite inter-individual differences.

Fifth, a limitation of the present paradigm is that three tagged frequencies—70, 80, and 90 Hz—were presented simultaneously within a single line stimulus. In frequency-tagging paradigms, simultaneous multi-frequency stimulation can generate nonlinear interactions and intermodulation components, and neural responses to each tagged component may not be completely separable. In the present study, broad-range time-frequency maps showed that the main low-frequency difference components at 10 and 20 Hz did not exhibit stimulus-related increases comparable to the 80 Hz response. In addition, spatial correlation analysis showed low similarity between the 80 Hz map and the 10/20 Hz maps, although these low-frequency correlations should be interpreted cautiously because stimulus-related power changes at 10 and 20 Hz were small. Weak positive correlations with neighboring tagged frequencies at 70 and 90 Hz suggested partial overlap among adjacent high-frequency tagged responses rather than complete spatial identity. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting an 80 Hz-centered tagged response rather than a fully isolated 80 Hz-specific neural component. In addition, three horizontal stimulus positions were used to reduce position-related bias across trials, but the present study was not designed to compare position-specific neural responses. Accordingly, trials were pooled across positions, and potential effects of horizontal stimulus position on brain activity were not separately evaluated. Future studies should explicitly model position effects to determine how line position modulates frequency-tagged visuospatial responses. In addition, sum-frequency intermodulation components above the analyzed frequency range, such as 150–170 Hz, were not directly evaluated.

Sixth, although all participants showed normal performance on the paper-based line bisection test and had no clinical evidence of visuospatial dysfunction, handedness information was not available for all participants and therefore could not be included as a covariate. Because handedness may influence line bisection performance and hemispheric asymmetry, this should be addressed in future studies.

Seventh, device-related contamination is another important methodological consideration because 80 Hz was the principal tagging frequency. Although seven sensors showing abnormal 80 Hz electromagnetic noise during stimulus-on empty-room recordings were excluded from all subsequent analyses, residual device-related effects cannot be completely excluded. The main 80 Hz findings were obtained after sensor exclusion and were supported by source-space localization and baseline-versus-stimulation statistics, but future studies should include systematic empty-room validation and independent replication.

Finally, while the present study focused on steady-state visual responses to frequency-tagged stimuli, whether these responses can predict visuospatial behavior at the individual level remains unclear. Future studies should test whether FPVS-derived measures, such as spectral power or connectivity metrics (e.g., iCoh), correlate with task performance or line bisection bias. No diffusion MRI, tractography reconstruction, or quantitative structure–function correlation was performed. Therefore, the connectivity findings should be interpreted as anatomically plausible functional coupling among cortical regions rather than direct evidence of signal propagation through white-matter pathways. Moreover, applying time–frequency analysis to source-level signals may reveal induced oscillatory dynamics beyond stimulus-driven responses, providing further insights into the temporal organization of visuospatial attention.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that high-gamma FPVS-MEG can capture stimulus-locked cortical activity and task-related functional coupling during a line bisection paradigm. The observed connectivity patterns involved cortical regions that are anatomically plausible in the context of visuospatial processing. These findings support the potential of high-frequency FPVS-MEG as a non-invasive, time-resolved approach for mapping cortical activity and functional coupling during higher-order cognitive processing.
