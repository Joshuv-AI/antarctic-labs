Optimization of Litsea cubeba ethanol extract and its antibacterial mechanism against Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Mengyue Wei, Zihan Yu, Yunbin Zhang, Qianyu Wang, Yuan Wang, Xiaofei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study optimized ultrasonic-assisted ethanol extraction of dried Litsea cubeba fruits using response surface methodology, identified chemical components by GC‑MS, and explored the antibacterial mechanism against Staphylococcus aureus. The optimal conditions were 60 °C, 60 min, and solid‑liquid ratio 1:25 g/mL, with an antibacterial rate of 83.42%. GC‑MS detected 20 compounds, dominated by lauric acid, oleic acid, and palmitic acid. The extract showed concentration‑dependent bactericidal activity with MIC 0.16 mg/mL and MBC 0.31 mg/mL. It disrupted bacterial cell wall and membrane integrity, causing leakage of AKP, nucleic acids, and proteins. This work supports L. cubeba as a natural antibacterial agent for food and pharmaceutical applications.Keywords: Litsea cubeba, Response surface methodology, GC‑MS, Staphylococcus aureus, Antibacterial mechanism, Cell membrane integritySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Microbiology

Introduction

Litsea cubeba (Lour.) Pers., a valuable aromatic plant belonging to the Lauraceae family, is widely distributed in southern and southwestern China, such as Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, and Hunan provinces. It has a long history of application as a traditional spice and medicinal herb, with the effects of dispelling cold, relieving pain, promoting digestion, and resisting bacteria1,2. In recent years, with the improvement of people’s health awareness and the increasing concern about the side effects of chemical synthetic preservatives, natural plant‑derived antibacterial agents have become a research hotspot in the fields of food, medicine, and cosmetics due to their advantages of high safety, good biocompatibility, and environmental friendliness3,4.

The fruits of L. cubeba are rich in a variety of bioactive components, including volatile essential oils and non‑volatile substances such as fatty acids, polyphenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids5. Compared with volatile essential oils that are easy to volatilize and unstable, non‑volatile active ingredients have better thermal stability and processing adaptability, which can maintain stable biological activity during food processing, storage, and transportation, making them ideal candidates for developing new natural antibacterial agents and functional food additives4. At present, most studies focus on fresh L. cubeba fruits6. However, fresh fruits have high water content, respiratory intensity, and are easy to rot and deteriorate after harvesting, resulting in poor stability of active components and limiting their large‑scale industrial application7. In contrast, dried L. cubeba fruits have stable physicochemical properties, long storage time, and are convenient for processing and utilization, which is more suitable for industrial production.

Drying treatment can induce the transformation and redistribution of bioactive components in plant fruits, enhancing the specific biological activities of dried products8. Nevertheless, systematic studies on the optimization of extraction process, chemical composition identification, and antibacterial mechanism of dried L. cubeba ethanol extract against S. aureus are still insufficient. S. aureus is a typical Gram‑positive foodborne pathogen that causes food spoilage, poisoning, and clinical infections worldwide. It can form biofilms and produce heat‑stable enterotoxins, posing a serious threat to food safety and public health9,10. Exploring natural antibacterial substances against S. aureus has become an important research direction in food safety and natural product fields11,12.

Ultrasonic‑assisted extraction is a green, efficient, and energy‑saving extraction technology widely used in the extraction of plant active ingredients13,14. It uses the cavitation effect, mechanical effect, and thermal effect of ultrasound to break plant cell walls, accelerate the dissolution of intracellular active substances, and improve extraction efficiency and yield15. Response surface methodology is a scientific and effective optimization method for experimental design, which can evaluate the interactive effects of multiple factors on response values, establish a regression model between factors and response values, and obtain the optimal process parameters with high prediction accuracy and good reliability16–18. GC‑MS is a powerful analytical technique for qualitative and quantitative analysis of complex components in plant extracts, which can accurately identify the chemical structure and content of each component19,20.

Therefore, this study took dried L. cubeba fruits from Sichuan as raw materials, adopted ultrasonic‑assisted ethanol extraction method, and used Box‑Behnken response surface methodology to optimize the extraction process with antibacterial rate as the response value. On this basis, GC‑MS technology was used to analyze the chemical composition of the optimized extract. The in vitro antibacterial activity against S. aureus was evaluated by determining MIC, MBC, growth curves, and AUC values. Furthermore, the damage to bacterial cell wall and membrane integrity was explored by detecting AKP activity and extracellular leakage of nucleic acids and proteins (OD260, OD280), and the correlation between antibacterial activity and cell damage was analyzed by Pearson correlation analysis. This study aims to clarify the optimal extraction process, chemical basis, and antibacterial mechanism of L. cubeba ethanol extract, provide technical support and theoretical basis for the high‑value utilization of L. cubeba resources, and offer a new natural antibacterial option for food preservation and food safety control.

Results and discussion

Single‑factor experimental results

Effect of extraction time on antibacterial rate

The effect of extraction time on the antibacterial rate of L. cubeba ethanol extract is shown in Fig. 1A. With the extension of extraction time, the antibacterial rate showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. When the extraction time was 60–80 min, the antibacterial rate reached the best, which were 84.06% and 84.43% respectively, and there was a significant difference compared with other time groups (P < 0.05).

Effect of extraction time, extraction temperature, and liquid-to-solid ratio on the inhibition rate against S. aureus.

The reason may be that appropriate ultrasonic time can produce a cavitation effect, effectively destroy the cell wall structure of L. cubeba fruit, promote the full dissolution of intracellular antibacterial active ingredients, and thus improve the antibacterial activity of the extract15. However, when the extraction time is too long, the ultrasonic system continuously absorbs high‑frequency energy, which may lead to the degradation and inactivation of some heat‑sensitive and unstable active substances, reducing the comprehensive antibacterial effect of the extract21. Therefore, 40–80 min was selected as the time range for subsequent response surface optimization.

Effect of extraction temperature on antibacterial rate

The effect of extraction temperature on the antibacterial rate is shown in Fig. 1B. In the range of 40–80 °C, the antibacterial rate increased first and then decreased, and reached the highest value of 69.42% at 60 °C. Significance analysis showed that there was no significant difference in antibacterial rate among 50, 60, and 70 °C groups (P > 0.05), but they were significantly higher than those in 40℃ and 80℃ groups (P < 0.05).

Appropriate heating can increase the molecular movement rate, reduce the viscosity of the extraction solvent, promote the dissolution of alcohol‑soluble antibacterial components in L. cubeba, and improve the extraction efficiency22. However, when the temperature is too high (more than 80 °C), it will not only accelerate the volatilization of ethanol, reduce the actual solid‑liquid ratio of the system, but also lead to the degradation of active ingredients and the dissolution of a large number of invalid impurities, which will reduce the antibacterial activity of the extract23. Therefore, 50–70℃ was selected as the temperature range for response surface optimization. Ethanol was selected as the exclusive extraction solvent due to its biosafety, suitable polarity, and ability to preserve bioactivity. As a Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) solvent, ethanol meets the safety requirements for food-related applications. Its moderate polarity allows efficient extraction of fatty acids and terpenoids, the main antibacterial constituents in L. cubeba. In addition, ethanol helps stabilize heat-sensitive compounds during ultrasonic extraction, ensuring the antibacterial activity of the final extract.

Effect of solid‑liquid ratio on antibacterial rate

The effect of solid‑liquid ratio on the antibacterial rate is shown in Fig. 1C. In the range of 1:10–1:30 g/mL, the antibacterial rate increased significantly with the increase of solvent ratio and then tended to be stable. The antibacterial rate reached the highest (87.90%) at 1:25 g/mL, and there was no significant difference between 1:25 and 1:30 groups (P > 0.05), but it was significantly higher than other low‑ratio groups (P < 0.05).

The increase of solvent volume can expand the concentration gradient between solid and liquid phases, promote the mass transfer process of active ingredients from raw materials to solvent, and accelerate the dissolution of antibacterial substances24. When the solid‑liquid ratio exceeds 1:25 g/mL, the dissolution of active ingredients tends to dynamic equilibrium, and continuing to increase the solvent volume has no significant effect on improving the antibacterial rate, but will increase the consumption of reagents and the energy consumption of subsequent concentration processes25. Therefore, 1:20–1:30 g/mL was selected as the solid‑liquid ratio range for response surface optimization.

Optimal process prediction and experimental verification

The regression model was optimized by Design‑Expert software, combined with the feasibility of actual operation, the optimal extraction conditions of L. cubeba ethanol extract were determined as follows: extraction temperature 60℃, extraction time 60 min, solid‑liquid ratio 1:25 g/mL. Under these conditions, the model predicted that the antibacterial rate was 83.60%. Three repeated verification experiments were carried out under the optimal process conditions, and the measured antibacterial rates were 83.25%, 83.41%, and 83.60% respectively, with an average of 83.42%. The relative error between the actual experimental value and the model predicted value was only about 0.2%, indicating that the regression model could truly and reliably reflect the influence of each process parameter on the antibacterial rate, and the optimized process conditions had high feasibility and reliability.

GC‑MS analysis of chemical composition

GC-MS identified 20 compounds in the optimized L. cubeba ethanol extract, and the total ion chromatogram is shown in Fig. 3. The identified components are mainly fatty acids, terpenoids, esters, and glycerides, among which fatty acids account for the highest proportion (Table 3). The cumulative relative content of free fatty acids reaches 77.18%, derived from the summation of individual percentages: 31.07% lauric acid, 29.15% oleic acid and its isomers, 10.78% palmitic acid, 1.80% palmitoleic acid, 1.37% myristic acid, and 3.01% capric acid. Meanwhile, glycerides account for 15.59% (comprising 1.58% tridecanoin, 4.82% 1,2-dilaurin, and 9.19% trilaurin). This high-abundance fatty acid profile, dominated by lauric acid and oleic acid, aligns closely with the findings of Liu et al., who established that lauric acid serves as the primary non-volatile antimicrobial constituent in L. cubeba fruit5. Medium- and long-chain fatty acids are the main antibacterial components in the extract; they possess good lipophilicity and can interact with bacterial cell membranes to disrupt membrane structure and exert antibacterial effects26,27. Plant-derived fatty acids have been confirmed to exert prominent antibacterial and anti-biofilm effects against pathogenic bacteria, alongside inherent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties28. Furthermore, medium-chain fatty acids can serve as promising alternatives to antibiotics, effectively improving intestinal immunity and regulating gut microbial composition29. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that fatty acids are multifunctional bioactive substances with broad-spectrum antibacterial capacity. They inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria and interfere with biofilm formation, which fundamentally accounts for the antibacterial performance of L. cubeba extract observed in this study. Moreover, a potential synergistic effect exists between these dominant fatty acids and the trace terpenoids identified in the extract. Although the total terpenoid content is low—comprising 0.55% caryophyllene oxide (an oxygenated sesquiterpene) and 0.21% β-caryophyllene (a hydrocarbon sesquiterpene), totaling 0.76%—these highly lipophilic molecules may potentially disrupt the bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan network and lipid bilayer12. This preliminary permeabilization may act as a facilitating factor, potentially enabling the subsequent penetration of high-concentration fatty acids (such as lauric acid and oleic acid) into the cells to attack intracellular targets, thereby yielding a multi-target antibacterial outcome.

Antibacterial activity analysis

MIC and MBC determination

In vitro antibacterial experiments showed that the MIC and MBC of L. cubeba ethanol extract against S. aureus were 0.16 mg/mL and 0.31 mg/mL respectively, and the MBC/MIC ratio was 1.94. The results showed that the optimized L. cubeba ethanol extract had a direct bactericidal effect on S. aureus, not just inhibiting growth30. Lauric acid, the most abundant component in the extract, has been confirmed to have significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus, which is consistent with previous studies5.

Growth curves and AUC analysis

The growth curve of S. aureus treated with different concentrations of extract is shown in Fig. 4A. The control group showed a typical bacterial growth cycle, with 4–12 h as the logarithmic growth phase and then entering the stationary phase. After treatment with different concentrations (1/2 MIC–8 MIC) of extract, the growth of bacteria was inhibited to varying degrees, showing a significant concentration‑dependent relationship.

Effect of the ethanol extract on the growth curves of S. aureus.

Compared with the control group, the logarithmic phase of 1/2 MIC and 1 MIC treatment groups was significantly delayed, and the absorbance value after entering the stationary phase was significantly reduced; when the treatment concentration reached 4 MIC and 8 MIC, the absorbance of the bacterial solution hardly increased within 24 h, and failed to enter the logarithmic growth phase, indicating that the high‑concentration extract had a direct killing effect on S. aureus.

The AUC values of each group were further calculated and compared (Fig. 4B). The results showed that the AUC values of all treatment groups were significantly lower than those of the control group (P < 0.05), and decreased continuously with the increase of treatment concentration. Under the sub‑inhibitory concentration (1/2 MIC), the accumulation of total biomass had been significantly inhibited; the AUC value of the 8 MIC treatment group was the lowest. The quantitative analysis of AUC was consistent with the dynamic growth curve results, confirming that L. cubeba ethanol extract not only reduced the growth rate of bacteria but also reduced the total biomass during the culture cycle, reflecting its bactericidal efficacy.

Cell wall and membrane damage analysis

AKP activity change

AKP is located in the periplasmic space between the bacterial cell wall and cell membrane. As shown in Fig. 5A, during the 0–12 h culture cycle, the extracellular AKP activity of the control group remained stably at a low level. After treatment with the extract, the AKP activity in the bacterial solution began to increase at 2 h, and reached the release peak at 8 h (the highest in the 8 MIC group, about 3.7 U/mL). After 8 h of culture, the AKP activity of each group tended to be stable, presumably because the free AKP in the cell wall space was rapidly released and stopped increasing after emptying. This result indicates that L. cubeba ethanol extract can destroy the integrity of S. aureus cell wall, leading to the leakage of periplasmic enzyme AKP.

Effect of the ethanol extract on the cell wall and cell membrane permeability of S. aureus.

OD260 and OD280 changes

OD260 and OD280 are important indicators to characterize the leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins. As shown in Fig. 5B and C, the OD260 and OD280 values of the control group remained low during the culture process. After treatment with the extract, the OD260 and OD280 values of each treatment group increased significantly at 2 h and showed a continuous upward trend. At 12 h, the absorbance values increased with the increase of extract concentration, and the 8 MIC group reached the maximum. This indicates that the extract increases the permeability of S. aureus cell membrane, leading to a large leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins, and the damage degree is positively correlated with the extract concentration.This concentration-dependent leakage of intracellular macromolecules strongly verifies the physical disruption and permeabilization effects on the bacterial cell membrane barrier exerted by the amphiphilic free fatty acids (such as lauric acid and oleic acid) that dominate the extract, as identified in the preceding GC-MS analysis.

Pearson correlation analysis

To clarify the correlation between the antibacterial activity of the ethanol extract from L. cubeba and the damage degree of bacterial cells, Pearson correlation analysis was performed on AUC, AKP activity, and intracellular substance leakage (OD260, OD280) in different concentration groups (Table 4). The results showed that the treatment concentration had extremely significant positive correlations with AKP activity, OD260, and OD280, with correlation coefficients of 0.958, 0.985, and 0.988, respectively (P < 0.01), indicating that the damage of the ethanol extract to the cell wall and cell membrane exhibited a strict dose‑effect relationship. In contrast, AUC, representing bacterial growth activity, showed significant or extremely significant negative correlations with all the above damage indicators, and the correlation coefficient with AKP activity was − 0.892 (P < 0.05). These results indicated that the total biomass of bacteria decreased significantly with the aggravation of cell wall and membrane damage.

Conclusion

In this study, the ultrasonic‑assisted ethanol extraction process of dried L. cubeba fruits was optimized by Box‑Behnken response surface methodology, and the antibacterial activity and mechanism of the optimized extract against S. aureus were systematically studied. The optimal extraction conditions were determined as follows: extraction temperature 60 °C, extraction time 60 min, solid‑liquid ratio 1:25 g/mL, and the antibacterial rate under this condition was 83.42%. GC‑MS analysis revealed that the extract was rich in fatty acids, with lauric acid (31.07%), oleic acid (29.15%), and palmitic acid (10.78%) as the main components.

The optimized ethanol extract had a concentration‑dependent bactericidal activity against S. aureus, with MIC and MBC of 0.16 mg/mL and 0.31 mg/mL respectively, and MBC/MIC of 1.94, showing a direct bactericidal effect. The antibacterial mechanism was clarified: L. cubeba ethanol extract acts on the cell wall and cell membrane of S. aureus, destroys the structural integrity of the cell envelope, increases cell permeability, leads to the leakage of intracellular substances (nucleic acids, proteins) and AKP, blocks the normal physiological metabolism of bacteria, and finally causes bacterial death. Pearson correlation analysis further confirmed that the antibacterial activity was significantly negatively correlated with the degree of cell wall and membrane damage.

This study systematically clarified the optimal extraction process, chemical basis, and antibacterial mechanism of dried L. cubeba ethanol extract, enriched the research system of natural plant antibacterial agents, provided a theoretical basis and technical support for the deep development and utilization of L. cubeba resources, and had important practical significance for the application of L. cubeba as a natural antibacterial agent in food preservation, functional food, and pharmaceutical fields.

Building upon these findings, future research will focus on transitioning these in vitro mechanistic insights into real food matrices, while systematically decoding the precise molecular interactions between the identified fatty acids and the bacterial cell membrane to fully generalize their practical applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Dried L. cubeba fruits were purchased from Chengdu, Sichuan Province. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 was provided by the Microbiology Laboratory, School of Food and Pharmacy, Shanghai Zhongqiao Vocational and Technical University, China, preserved on nutrient agar (NA) slants at 4℃, and activated twice before use. LB liquid medium and NA medium were purchased from Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China. Anhydrous ethanol and 2,3,5‑triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) were obtained from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd., China. All reagents were of analytical grade. AKP assay kit was purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China.

Preparation of ethanol extract

Dried L. cubeba fruits were placed in a blast drying oven at 40℃ to constant weight, then crushed with a high‑speed pulverizer, and sieved through a 40‑mesh standard sieve to obtain uniform fruit powder, which was stored in a dry and sealed container for later use. Exactly 10 g of L. cubeba powder was accurately weighed and placed in a 250 mL round‑bottom flask, mixed with anhydrous ethanol according to a certain solid‑liquid ratio, and soaked at room temperature for 12 h to fully infiltrate the raw materials. Then, ultrasonic‑assisted thermal reflux extraction was carried out under the conditions of ultrasonic frequency 40 kHz and power 240 W, with controlled extraction temperature and time31. The ultrasonic parameters were determined based on preliminary experiments. A frequency of 40 kHz provides stable and moderate cavitation to effectively disrupt plant cell walls and promote the dissolution of active ingredients, while avoiding excessive shear force that may degrade bioactive compounds. A power of 240 W ensures sufficient energy to improve extraction efficiency without causing excessive local heating, which could damage heat-sensitive antibacterial components and reduce the activity of the final extract.

After extraction, the mixed extract was taken out and cooled to room temperature, then centrifuged at 6500 r/min for 15 min at 25℃ to remove insoluble residues. The supernatant was collected and vacuum‑filtered three times with a circulating water vacuum pump to obtain a clear filtrate. The filtrate was concentrated to a viscous extract at 65℃ using a rotary evaporator, and the residual ethanol was removed. The obtained L. cubeba ethanol extract was sealed and stored in a refrigerator at 4℃ for subsequent antibacterial activity determination and mechanism analysis.

Determination of antibacterial rate

The antibacterial rate of L. cubeba ethanol extract against S. aureus was determined using the microdilution method referring to previous studies with slight modifications15. The prepared ethanol extract was dissolved in LB liquid medium to prepare a stock solution with a concentration of 1.25 mg/mL. Then, 100 µL of the extract solution and 100 µL of the activated bacterial suspension (OD600 ≈ 0.6) were added to each well of a 96‑well cell culture plate, so that the final concentration of the extract in the reaction system was 0.625 mg/mL. The control group was set by replacing the extract solution with the same volume of sterile water, and the blank group was set with only LB liquid medium.

All groups were incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the absorbance value (OD600) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 600 nm using a microplate reader. Single‑factor experiments

Single‑factor experiments were designed to investigate the effects of three key extraction parameters on the antibacterial rate of L. cubeba ethanol extract, so as to determine the appropriate level range for response surface optimization. The specific settings were as follows:

Extraction time: fixed extraction temperature 60 °C, solid‑liquid ratio 1:20 g/mL, set time gradients of 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 min;

Extraction temperature: fixed extraction time 60 min, solid‑liquid ratio 1:20 g/mL, set temperature gradients of 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 °C;

Solid‑liquid ratio: fixed extraction time 60 min, extraction temperature 60 °C, set solid‑liquid ratio gradients of 1:10, 1:15, 1:20, 1:25, 1:30 g/mL.

Each group of experiments was repeated 3 times, and the antibacterial rate was used as the evaluation index to analyze the change rule of each factor, so as to determine the optimal level range of each factor for response surface optimization.

Response surface methodology optimization

On the basis of single‑factor experimental results, a three‑factor, three‑level Box‑Behnken experimental design was carried out. Extraction time (A), extraction temperature (B), and solid‑liquid ratio (C) were taken as independent variables, and antibacterial rate (Y) was taken as the response value. A total of 17 experimental groups including 12 factorial points and 5 central points were designed (Table 5). Design‑Expert 13.0 software was used for data processing, quadratic polynomial regression fitting, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to verify the significance of the model and each factor, and analyze the interactive effects between factors. Finally, the optimal extraction process parameters were predicted and verified by experiments.

GC‑MS analysis of chemical composition

The optimized L. cubeba ethanol extract was analyzed using an HP‑5MS elastic quartz capillary column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm). The column temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature 50 °C, raised to 100 °C at 10 °C/min, then to 300℃ at 30℃/min, and the total running time was 20.667 min. The vaporization chamber temperature was 300 °C, carrier gas was high‑purity helium (99.999%), column head pressure was 7.6522 psi, carrier gas flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. Injection volume was 1 µL, split ratio was 50:1. The mass spectrometry adopted electron impact (EI) ion source, ion source temperature 230 °C, quadrupole temperature 150 °C, electron energy 70 eV, emission current 34.593 µA, multiplier voltage 1286 V, interface temperature 300 °C, and mass collection range 29–500 amu. Compounds were identified by comparing with NIST mass spectrometry database and combined with retention time. The relative content of each compound was calculated by peak area normalization method.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were determined by the microdilution method combined with plate coating method32. The optimized L. cubeba ethanol extract was serially diluted with LB liquid medium to prepare a series of solutions with final concentrations of 10.00, 5.00, 2.50, 1.25, 0.63, 0.31, 0.16, 0.08, 0.04 mg/mL. Then, 100 µL of each concentration extract and 100 µL of bacterial suspension were added to a 96‑well plate, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

After incubation, 50 µL of 0.2% TTC chromogenic agent was added to each well, and placed in the dark for 4 h. The color change of the solution in the wells was observed: red or pink indicated the growth of viable bacteria, and no color change indicated that bacteria were inhibited. The lowest concentration of the extract without color change was recorded as MIC. For the wells with no color change (MIC concentration and above), 100 µL of bacterial solution was absorbed and coated on NA solid medium, incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the number of colonies on the plate was counted. The lowest extract concentration corresponding to the number of colonies less than 5 was defined as MBC. Each concentration was set with 3 parallel replicates to ensure the accuracy of the results.

Growth curve and AUC analysis

The optimized extract was diluted with LB liquid medium to final concentrations of 8 MIC, 4 MIC, 2 MIC, 1 MIC, and 1/2 MIC33. The control group was treated with the same volume of sterile water instead of the extract, and the blank group was set with only LB medium. All groups were cultured in a constant temperature oscillation incubator at 37℃ and 180 r/min. Within 24 h, the absorbance value (OD600) of each group was measured every 2 h at a wavelength of 600 nm, and the growth curve of S. aureus was drawn with culture time as the abscissa and OD600 value as the ordinate.

To quantitatively evaluate the cumulative antibacterial effect of the extract during the whole culture cycle, the area under the growth curve (AUC) was calculated according to the OD600 value at each time point. AUC can reflect the cumulative biomass of bacteria, and the smaller the AUC value, the stronger the cumulative antibacterial effect of the extract34. All experiments were carried out in 3 parallel replicates.

Cell wall and membrane permeability assays

The concentration setting of the extract, control group setting, and culture conditions were consistent with the growth curve experiment. Samples were taken at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h of culture respectively, and the culture solution was centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 10 min to collect the supernatant for standby.

The activity of alkaline phosphatase (AKP) in the supernatant was determined with an AKP assay kit at a wavelength of 405 nm. AKP exists in the periplasmic space between the bacterial cell wall and cell membrane. When the cell wall is damaged, AKP will leak into the extracellular supernatant, so the AKP activity can reflect the integrity of the cell wall35. The absorbance values of the supernatant at 260 nm and 280 nm (OD260, OD280) were measured by ultraviolet spectrophotometer to evaluate the leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins, which can reflect the permeability of the cell membrane. All indicators were measured in 3 parallel replicates.

Data analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Design‑Expert 13.0 software was used for regression analysis and ANOVA of response surface experimental data. Origin Pro 2025 software was used for data processing, mapping, and one‑way ANOVA (One‑Way ANOVA). SPSS 27.0 software was used for Pearson correlation analysis to explore the correlation between antibacterial activity indicators (AUC) and cell damage indicators (AKP activity, OD₂₆₀, OD₂₈₀). P < 0.05 indicated a significant difference, and P < 0.01 indicated an extremely significant difference.
