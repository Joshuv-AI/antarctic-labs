Report number: NU-QG-15 Ringdown waves from hairy black holes
Ariadna Uxue Palomino Ylla, Kosuke Makino, Akane Tanaka, Akihiro Ishibashi, Chul-Moon Yoo

Abstract
We derive general formulas for quasi-normal mode (QNM) frequencies of hairy black holes by exploiting the QNM–geodesic correspondence. The black hole hair is treated as an anisotropic fluid perturbatively added to the vacuum black holes (Schwarzschild and Kerr black holes). Under this setting, independent of energy conditions, our formulas offer a systematic method to compute quasi-normal mode frequencies for a broad class of hairy black holes.



1
Introduction

Recent cosmological and astrophysical observations have accumulated compelling evidence for the existence of black holes, making them prime candidates for testing general relativity. On the theoretical side, a variety of non-vacuum or hairy black hole models have been proposed. These models may be interpreted as effective descriptions for black holes surrounded by dark sector fields or effects of modified gravity theories near a black hole. It is highly relevant to investigate the basic characteristics of hairy black holes and to develop techniques for identifying them, for instance through gravitational wave measurements. When a black hole is perturbed, the remnant emits gravitational radiation known as the ringdown phase. This signal is primarily characterized by a collection of damped oscillations whose complex frequencies correspond to the quasinormal modes (QNMs). These modes encode essential information about the black hole’s physical properties. If QNMs from a black hole endowed with “dark hair” could be detected, they would allow us to determine not only the mass and angular momentum of the source but also the state parameters of the surrounding dark-sector fields or the coupling constants that define the underlying modified gravity theory.

However, apart from a few particular cases (e.g., analysis of QNMs from Bardeen black hole by Konoplya et. al.
[
19
]
), so far, a systematic study of QNMs applicable to a broad class of hairy black holes still remains unexplored. This is in part because the metrics describing hairy black hole models are generally complicated, making it challenging to perform QNM calculations. There is a strong need to develop general analytic formulas that can be uniformly used to analyze QNMs of various types of hairy black holes, independently of the specific details of each hairy black hole model (e.g., independent of the dark sector equation of state).

To address this problem, in this paper, we consider hairy black hole models constructed by perturbatively adding anisotropic fluids (mimicking dark sector fields) to vacuum black hole metrics (i.e., the Schwarzschild and Kerr metrics). To systematically understand how QNMs of these constructed hairy black holes are modified by their non-trivial hair, compared to their corresponding vacuum counterparts, we exploit the relationship between the unstable circular orbit of a photon (UCOP) and second-order WKB results for QNMs. This relationship is well-established in the eikonal limit, where the perturbation’s angular momentum is large, but remains valid even at lower angular momentum
quantum number
\ell\gtrsim 4

Along with

, the QNMs are indexed by the quantum numbers

\mathbf{m}
, the azimuthal shape and
n
, the overtone index. In the non-rotating vacuum case, the sign difference of
\mathbf{m}
is degenerate.
This correspondence has been justified at least for static, spherically symmetric, asymptotically flat spacetimes
[

For further justification using the Penrose limit, see Refs.

According to the analysis done by Cardoso et al.

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm QNM}=\Omega\ell-i(n+1/2)\lambda\,.

In this context, it is worth noting the no-short hair theorem, according to which, if a static black hole supports hair in the form of an anisotropic fluid satisfying certain energy conditions, that hair must extend beyond the radius of the UCOP, namely the photon sphere

2.1
Static hairy black holes and null geodesics

We start with the following general static spherically symmetric metric

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+h(r)dr^{2}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2})\,.

(2.1)

To consider geodesic curves on this background, let
{\cal L}
be the Lagrangian,

\displaystyle{\cal L}:=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(-f\dot{t}^{2}+h\dot{r}^{2}+r^{2}\dot{\varphi}^{2}\right)=\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\,,

(2.2)

where we have already set
\theta=\pi/2
and
\epsilon=-1
for timelike geodesics and
\epsilon=0
for null geodesics.
From the isometries along
t
and
\varphi
we immediately find the two conserved quantities

\displaystyle E:=-\dfrac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\dot{t}}=f\dot{t}\,,\quad L:=\dfrac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\dot{\varphi}}=r^{2}\dot{\varphi}\,.

(2.3)

Plugging these two into the Lagrangian (
3.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+V(r)=0\,,\quad V(r):=\dfrac{1}{2h}\left(-\epsilon+\dfrac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}-\dfrac{E^{2}}{f}\right)\,.

(2.4)

The conditions for geodesic curves to be spatially closed are given by

\displaystyle V=0\,,\quad V^{\prime}=0\,,

(2.5)

on the orbit, where the prime denotes the derivative by
r
.
From the above two conditions, we obtain, respectively,

\displaystyle\dfrac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}=\dfrac{E^{2}}{f}+\epsilon\,,\quad\dfrac{2L^{2}}{r^{3}}=\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{f^{2}}E^{2}\,.

(2.6)

Combining these two, we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon\circeq\dfrac{E^{2}}{2f^{2}}(rf^{\prime}-2f)\,,

(2.7)

where here and hereafter “
\circeq
” implies the equality holds on the circular orbit under consideration.
Using
V=0=V^{\prime}
, we can write
V^{\prime\prime}
as

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}\circeq\dfrac{1}{2h}\left[6\dfrac{L^{2}}{r^{4}}+\left(\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{f^{2}}\right)^{\prime}E^{2}\right]\,.

(2.8)

Let us focus on null geodesic curves describing UCOP. Combining Eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) with
\epsilon=0
, we have

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}\circeq\dfrac{L^{2}}{2r^{2}h}\left(\dfrac{f^{\prime\prime}}{r}-\dfrac{2}{r^{2}}\right)\,.

(2.9)

Then, following the reference
[
4
]
, we obtain the Lyapuonv exponent
\lambda
and the orbital frequency
\Omega
as

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}:=-\dfrac{V^{\prime\prime}}{\dot{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle\circeq

\displaystyle\dfrac{f}{2h}\left(\dfrac{2}{r^{2}}-\dfrac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}\right)\,,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Omega:=\dfrac{\dot{\varphi}}{\dot{t}}

\displaystyle\circeq

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{f}}{r}\,.

(2.11)

For the Schwarzschild metric case, i.e.,
f=h^{-1}=1-2M/r
, we obtain the well-known result

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=\dfrac{1}{3\sqrt{3}M}\,,\quad\Omega_{0}=\dfrac{1}{3\sqrt{3}M}\,.

(2.12)

2.2
Anisotropic fluid surrounding black holes and QNM frequency

Let us consider the case in which our metric solves the Einstein equations.
We consider fluids with anisotropic pressure so that the stress-energy tensor
T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}
is given
by the components,

\displaystyle T^{t}{}_{t}=-\rho\,,\quad T^{r}{}_{r}=P_{r}\,,\quad T^{\theta}{}_{\theta}=T^{\varphi}{}_{\varphi}=P_{\theta}\,,

(2.13)

and the rest of the components are vanishing. Combining the components of the Einstein tensor,
G_{00}=8\pi\rho
,
G^{r}{}_{r}=8\pi P_{r}
, and
G^{\theta}{}_{\theta}=8\pi P_{\theta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\circeq\dfrac{f}{r^{2}}-\dfrac{f}{r^{2}}\left[2\pi r^{2}(\rho-3P_{r}+4P_{\theta})+2\pi r^{2}h(\rho+P_{r})+16\pi^{2}r^{4}hP_{r}(\rho+P_{r})\right]\,.

(2.14)

Now, let us suppose that the equations of state for our fluid are given by two parameters

\displaystyle P_{r}=w_{r}\rho\,,\quad P_{\theta}=w_{\theta}\rho\,,

(2.15)

and furthermore that the anisotropic fluid can be treated as linear perturbations from the vacuum Schwarzschild background, so that

\displaystyle|\rho|\ll 1\,.

(2.16)

Then, denoting the UCOP radius of the hairy black hole by
r_{\star}
, and approximating
h^{-1}(r)\approx f_{0}(r)=1-2M/r
, justified by the fact that it appears multiplied by the perturbative fluid density
\rho
, we obtain the leading-order expression for
\lambda
(
2.14
) at the UCOP location as follows

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\Omega_{\star}-\Omega_{\star}\pi r_{\star}^{2}\rho\left[1-3\,w_{r}+4\,w_{\theta}+\dfrac{(1+w_{r})}{f_{0}}\right]\bigg|_{r=r_{\star}}\,,

(2.17)

where
\Omega_{\star}=\dfrac{\sqrt{f(r_{\star})}}{r_{\star}}
represents the orbital frequency at the UCOP.

To obtain fully linearized expressions for the QNM coefficients around the Schwarzschild background, we further need to expand

\displaystyle r_{\star}\simeq r_{0}+\delta r=3M+\delta r\,,

(2.18)

\displaystyle f(r)\simeq f_{0}(r)+\delta f(r)\,,

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}\simeq\lambda_{0}+\delta\lambda=(3\sqrt{3}M)^{-1}+\delta\lambda\,,

(2.20)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\star}\simeq\Omega_{0}+\delta\Omega=(3\sqrt{3}M)^{-1}+\delta\Omega\,.

(2.21)

where
r_{0}=3M
and
f_{0}(r_{0})=1/3
denote the Schwarzschild UCOP radius and the corresponding metric function value, respectively. The terms
\delta r
and
\delta f
represent small linear deviations from these Schwarzschild quantities.

We start by substituting values in the UCOP expression
g(r)=rf^{\prime}(r)-2f(r)
, derived from (
2.7
), which vanishes for the circular photon orbit. By evaluating it in the UCOP radius for the hairy black hole, we obtain

\displaystyle g(r_{\star})\simeq((r_{0}+\delta r)(f_{0}^{\prime}(r)+\delta f^{\prime}(r))-2(f_{0}(r_{0}+\delta r)+\delta f(r_{0}+\delta r)))|_{r=r_{0}+\delta r}\,.

(2.22)

By using the result for the Schwarzschild photon sphere
g(r_{0})=r_{0}f_{0}^{\prime}(r_{0})-2f_{0}(r_{0})=0
, and by keeping perturbation terms up to the first order, we can solve this expression for
\delta r
as

\displaystyle\delta r\simeq\frac{r_{0}\,\delta f^{\prime}(r_{0})-2\,\delta f(r_{0})}{f_{0}^{\prime}(r_{0})-r_{0}f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(r_{0})}\,=\,\frac{3M}{2}(r_{0}\,\delta f^{\prime}(r_{0})-2\,\delta f(r_{0}))\,.

(2.23)

In a similar way, we proceed to linearize the orbital frequency (
2.11
) evaluated at the UCOP

\displaystyle\Omega_{\star}^{2}\simeq\frac{1}{3r_{0}^{2}}(1+3\,\delta f(r_{0}))\,,

(2.24)

which leads to

\displaystyle\Omega_{\star}\simeq\Omega_{0}(1+\frac{3}{2}\delta f(r_{0}))\,.

(2.25)

Finally, by substituting
\Omega_{\star}
into the Lyapunov exponent (
2.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}\,r_{0}}(1+\frac{3}{2}\delta f)\,\bigg(1-\pi r_{\star}^{2}\rho(1-3w_{r}+4w_{\theta}+\frac{(1+w_{r})}{f_{0}})\bigg)\bigg|_{r=r_{0}}\,,

(2.26)

and by expanding up to the first order for the perturbations, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}\simeq\lambda_{0}(1-4\pi r_{0}^{2}\rho(1+w_{\theta})+\frac{3}{2}\delta f)|_{r=r_{0}}\,.

(2.27)

2.3
Modifications of QNMs and Energy Conditions

In this section, we quantify the deviation of the QNMs for hairy black holes from their Schwarzschild counterparts, and relate them to the fluid state parameters
w_{r}
and
w_{\theta}
. We can observe how it affects the QNM frequency as

\frac{\delta\Omega}{\Omega_{0}}=\frac{3}{2}\,\delta f(r_{0}),\qquad\frac{\delta\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}=\frac{3}{2}\,\delta f(r_{0})-4\pi r_{0}^{2}\rho\big[1+w_{\theta}\big]|_{r=r_{0}}.

(2.28)

Both shifts are controlled by the metric correction
\delta f(r_{0})
in the same way, but the Lyapunov exponent also presents an explicit contribution from the tangential pressure
P_{\theta}
.

Although
\delta f
appears geometric, it is not independent of the matter. To illustrate this, we re-parametrize our general metric as

f(r)=1-\frac{2m(r)}{r},\quad h(r)=\left(1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right)^{-1},

(2.29)

where
m(r)\simeq M+\delta m(r)
. At first order expansion we obtain an explicit expression for the geometric term

\delta f(r)=-\frac{2\,\delta m(r)}{r}.

(2.30)

By using the Einstein equations, we can relate the metric function
m(r)
to the matter field as

m^{\prime}(r)=\delta m^{\prime}(r)=4\pi r^{2}\rho(r)

(2.31)

so that
\delta f
is ultimately determined by
\rho
.
Assuming
m(\infty)=M
, we can rewrite the QNM shifts directly in terms of the fluid parameters as follows

\frac{\delta\Omega}{\Omega_{0}}\simeq\frac{12\pi}{r}\int_{r}^{\infty}\rho(s)s^{2}\,ds\,|_{r=r_{0}},

(2.32)

\frac{\delta\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}\simeq\frac{12\pi}{r}\int_{r}^{\infty}\rho(s)s^{2}\,ds-4\pi r_{0}^{2}\rho(1+w_{\theta})|_{r=r_{0}}\,.

(2.33)

As shown in Appendix
C
, the conservation of
an anisotropic fluid, expressed by the relation
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}_{\nu}=0
, leads to a generalized Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equation. This relation connects the radial and tangential pressures as

P_{\theta}(r)=P_{r}(r)+\frac{r}{2}P^{\prime}_{r}(r)+\frac{(P_{r}+\rho)}{2}\left(\frac{4\pi r^{3}P_{r}(r)+m(r)}{r-2m(r)}\right)\,,

(2.34)

which shows that
P_{\theta}

is constrained once
\rho
and
w_{r}
are specified.

Energy conditions require normal and healthy properties of the matter field from a viewpoint of certain causality or stability.
It is worthwhile to interpret the relationship between the properties of matter fields surrounding a black hole and QNMs in terms of energy conditions.
The most used conditions in general relativity are the Null, Weak, Strong, and Dominant energy conditions, which are locally defined and often abbreviated as: NEC, WEC, SEC, and DEC, respectively. WEC and SEC imply NEC, while DEC implies WEC. For their definitions and usages, see, e.g, a review
[
14
]
and references therein.
Satisfying these conditions is necessary for interpreting the source as regular matter.
For an anisotropic effective fluid with energy density
\rho
and principal pressures
(P_{r},P_{\theta},P_{\theta})
, the energy conditions then read

NEC:

\displaystyle\quad\rho+P_{r}\geq 0,\ \ \rho+P_{\theta}\geq 0\ ,

(2.35)

WEC:

\displaystyle\quad\rho+P_{r}\geq 0,\ \ \rho+P_{\theta}\geq 0\ \&\ \ \rho\geq 0\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \rho\geq 0,\ w_{r}\geq-1,\ w_{\theta}\geq-1,

(2.36)

SEC:

\displaystyle\quad\rho+P_{r}\geq 0,\ \ \rho+P_{\theta}\geq 0\ \ \&\ \ \rho+P_{r}+2P_{\theta}\geq 0\ ,

(2.37)

DEC:

\displaystyle\quad|P_{r}|\leq\rho,\ \,|P_{\theta}|\leq\rho\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\rho\geq 0\ ,\ \ |w_{r}|\leq 1,\ |w_{\theta}|\leq 1\,.

(2.38)

In our barotropic model of an anisotropic fluid, with
i
as an indicator of radial or tangential, the parameter
w_{i}=0
correspond to dust,
w_{i}=1/3
to radiation,

w_{i}<-1/3

to dark-energy
and
w_{i}<-1
to phantom energy.
A particularly important case is
w_{r}=-1
(cosmological-constant-like). Not only does it satisfy most energy conditions, but it is also the only scenario that ensures the continuity of the energy density across the black hole event horizon
[
6
]
with non-zero energy density. In this scenario
\rho+P_{r}=0
(at least on the horizon), and equation (
2.34
) reduces to

w_{\theta}=-1-\frac{r}{2}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(r)}{\rho(r)}=-\frac{r}{2}\frac{m^{\prime\prime}(r)}{m^{\prime}(r)}.

(2.39)

By considering the expression (
2.39
), we can derive an expression for the radial pressure in terms of the second derivative of the mass function
P_{r}(r)=-\frac{m^{\prime\prime}}{8\pi r}
.
When considering
\rho(r)>0
, for regular matter, we find
\frac{\delta\Omega}{\Omega_{0}}>0
from Eq. (
2.32
), which means the QNM of the hairy black hole oscillates faster than in the vacuum case. In particular, it is also interesting to analyze

\frac{\delta\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}-\frac{\delta\Omega}{\Omega_{0}}\simeq-4\pi r_{0}^{2}\rho(1+w_{\theta})|_{r=r_{0}}\,.

(2.40)

Since
\rho\,(1+w_{\theta})\geq 0
is required by NEC (and therefore by WEC, SEC, and DEC), the difference (
2.40
) is negative when treating regular matter (
\rho>0
) satisfying the energy conditions.
This result indicates that, if we observe a positive value of
\delta\lambda/\lambda_{0}-\delta\Omega/\Omega_{0}
, the frequency shift cannot be explained by the distribution of a regular matter field surrounding the black hole, given that our setting is relevant.

It is also interesting to examine the displacement of the UCOP radius position

\delta r\simeq-12\,\pi\,M\left(r_{0}^{2}\rho(r)+3\int_{r}^{\infty}\rho(s)s^{2}\,ds\right)\,,

(2.41)

which, for regular matter with positive energy density, is always negative. The UCOP radius position comes closer to the center when considering the presence of regular matter hair.

3
Examples of Static Spherical Hairly Black Holes

In this section, we apply the formulas derived in the previous section to three specific black hole models: Bardeen, Hayward, and Kiselev black holes. These are asymptotically flat, static, spherically symmetric black holes that resemble the Schwarzschild spacetime at large distances, but have no singularity at the center. They are non-vacuum solutions, and hence can be interpreted as “hairy” black hole deviating from the Schwarzschild vacuum black hole.

In this section, we apply the formulas derived in the previous section to three specific static, spherically symmetric black hole models: the Bardeen, Hayward, and Kiselev spacetimes. The Bardeen and Hayward solutions are regular black holes that approach the Schwarzschild geometry at large distances. The Kiselev solution, in contrast, represents a non-vacuum black hole surrounded by an anisotropic fluid. Unlike the Bardeen and Hayward models, it typically features a central curvature singularity, and its asymptotic behaviour is determined by the chosen value of the equation-of-state parameter.

4.1
Rotating Hairy Black Hole and null geodesics

We focus on the following form of the metric

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-fdt^{2}-2a\sin^{2}\theta(1-f)dtd\varphi+\left\{\Sigma+(2-f)a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right\}\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{\Sigma}{\Sigma f+a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}dr^{2}+\Sigma d\theta^{2}\,,

\displaystyle f:=1-\dfrac{2m(r)r}{\Sigma}\,,\quad\Sigma:=r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\,,

where
m(r)
is an arbitrary differentiable function of
r
, and
a
denotes the angular momentum parameter. For
m(r)=M
, this metric reduces to the Kerr solution.
The above metric can be obtained from the static metric (
A.1
) by setting
f(r)=1/h(r)
and performing Janis-Newman transformation
[
21
,
8
]
.
This class of metric encompasses a fairly large class of stationary rotating hairy black holes, including the rotating Bardeen, the rotating Hayward, the rotating Kiselev, the quantum improved Kerr
[
7
]
, among others. This metric can be defined by replacing the mass function
m(r)
by its corresponding value in the static case, defined as in expression (
2.29
).

Let us consider circular geodesic orbits. We restrict our attention to the equatorial plane
\theta=\pi/2
.
The Lagrangian is given as

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(-f\dot{t}^{2}+\dfrac{r^{2}\dot{r}^{2}}{a^{2}+r^{2}f}-2a(1-f)\dot{t}\dot{\varphi}+[r^{2}+(2-f)a^{2}]\dot{\varphi}^{2}\right)=\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\,,

(4.3)

where
\epsilon=1
for the timelike case or
\epsilon=0
for the null case.
As for the static case, we define the two conserved quantities
E
and
L
as

\displaystyle E:=-\dfrac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\dot{t}}=f\dot{t}+a(1-f)\dot{\varphi}\,,\quad L:=\dfrac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\dot{\varphi}}=-a(1-f)\dot{t}+[r^{2}+(2-f)a^{2}]\dot{\varphi}\,.

(4.4)

Then, by solving for
\dot{t}
and
\dot{\varphi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{t}=\dfrac{1}{\triangle}\left\{\left(r^{2}+a^{2}+\dfrac{2m(r)a^{2}}{r}\right)E-\dfrac{2m(r)a}{r}L\right\}\,,\quad\dot{\varphi}=\dfrac{1}{\triangle}\left\{\dfrac{2m(r)a}{r}E+\left(1-\dfrac{2m(r)}{r}\right)L\right\}\,,

(4.5)

where
\triangle:=a^{2}+r^{2}f=r^{2}+a^{2}-2m(r)r
.
Then, from the expression of the Lagrangian, we find

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+V(r)=0\,,\quad V(r):=\dfrac{1}{2r^{2}}\left[L^{2}-(r^{2}+a^{2})E^{2}-\dfrac{2m(r)}{r}(aE-L)^{2}-\triangle\epsilon\right]\,.

(4.6)

The conditions for circular orbits with radius
r_{\star}
are
V(r_{\star})=0
and
V^{\prime}(r_{\star})=0
. From these conditions, we have

\displaystyle(a^{2}+r^{2})E^{2}-L^{2}+\dfrac{2m}{r}(aE-L)^{2}+\triangle\epsilon=0\,,\quad 2E^{2}r+\left(\dfrac{2m^{\prime}}{r}-\dfrac{2m}{r^{2}}\right)(aE-L)^{2}+\triangle^{\prime}\epsilon=0\,.

(4.7)

For the null geodesic
case (
\epsilon=0
), it is convenient to introduce the impact parameter
D:=L/E
. Then, the two circular-orbit conditions become

a^{2}-D^{2}+r^{2}+\dfrac{2m}{r}(a-D)^{2}\circeq 0\,,\quad 2r^{2}+\left(2m^{\prime}-\dfrac{2m}{r}\right)(a-D)^{2}\circeq 0\,,

(4.8)

and we find that the impact parameter and the UCOP radii should satisfy

D_{\varepsilon}\circeq a-\varepsilon\sqrt{\dfrac{r^{3}}{m-rm^{\prime}}}\,,

(4.9)

r\circeq(3m(r)-rm^{\prime}(r))\dfrac{D_{\varepsilon}-a}{D_{\varepsilon}+a}.

(4.10)

The second derivative of the potential is given by

V^{\prime\prime}\circeq-\dfrac{E^{2}(D_{\varepsilon}-a)^{2}}{r^{5}}\left[3(m-rm^{\prime})+r^{2}m^{\prime\prime}\right]\,.

(4.11)

From the standard definitions of the orbital frequency and the Lyapunov exponent, we derive

\Omega_{\varepsilon}\circeq\dfrac{1}{D_{\varepsilon}}\,,\quad\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\circeq\left(\dfrac{D_{\varepsilon}+a}{D_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\dfrac{\triangle^{2}}{r^{3}}\dfrac{3(m-rm^{\prime})+r^{2}m^{\prime\prime}}{[(D_{\varepsilon}-2a)m-D_{\varepsilon}\\
\ r\\
\ m^{\prime}]^{2}}.

(4.12)

Note that for the non-rotating vacuum case, i.e.,
a=0
,
m=M
, the above formula reduces to the Schwarzschild result
r_{\star,\varepsilon}=r_{0}=3M
.

Now by using the Einstein equations, we find (in Appendix
B
) that

m^{\prime}=-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2}G^{r}{}_{r}=-4\pi r^{2}P_{r}\,,\quad m^{\prime\prime}=-rG^{\theta}{}_{\theta}=-8\pi rP_{\theta}\,.

(4.13)

Therefore, at the UCOP radius position
r_{\star,\varepsilon}
, we find

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=D_{\varepsilon}^{-1}=\left(a-\varepsilon\sqrt{\dfrac{{r_{\star,\varepsilon}}^{3}}{m+4\pi{r_{\star,\varepsilon}}^{3}P_{r}}}\right)^{-1}\,,\quad

(4.14)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\dfrac{D_{\varepsilon}+a}{D_{\varepsilon}}\dfrac{\triangle}{{r_{\star,\varepsilon}}^{3/2}}\dfrac{(3m-4\pi{r_{\star,\varepsilon}}^{3}(2P_{\theta}-3P_{r}))^{1/2}}{(D_{\varepsilon}-2a)m+4\pi D_{\varepsilon}\,{r_{\star,\varepsilon}}^{3}P_{r}}\bigg|\,,

(4.15)

where all quantities are evaluated at
r=r_{\star,\varepsilon}
.

In order to model these general expressions as a small hair perturbation introduced to a vacuum Kerr black hole, we expand the expressions up to the first order

\displaystyle r_{\star,\varepsilon}\simeq r_{K,\varepsilon}+\delta r_{\varepsilon}\,,\quad

(4.16)

\displaystyle m(r)\simeq M+\delta m(r)\,,\quad

(4.17)

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}\simeq D_{K,\varepsilon}+\delta D_{\varepsilon}\,,\quad

(4.18)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}\simeq\Omega_{K,\varepsilon}+\delta\Omega_{\varepsilon}\,,\quad

(4.19)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}\simeq\lambda_{K,\varepsilon}+\delta\lambda_{\varepsilon}\,,\quad

(4.20)

where
r_{K,\varepsilon}
,
D_{K,\varepsilon}
,
\Omega_{K,\varepsilon}
,

are the values of the parameters in the Kerr case for the branch
\varepsilon
.

\displaystyle r_{K,\varepsilon}=2M\left(1+\cos{\left(\frac{2}{3}\arccos{\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\,a}{M}\right)}\right)}\right)\,,\quad

(4.21)

\displaystyle D_{K,\varepsilon}=a-\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{r_{K,\varepsilon}^{3}}{M}}\,,\quad

(4.22)

\displaystyle\Omega_{K,\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{D_{K,\varepsilon}}\,,\quad

(4.23)

\displaystyle\lambda_{K,\varepsilon}=\bigg|\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\triangle(D_{K,\varepsilon}+a)}{\sqrt{M}r_{K,\varepsilon}^{3/2}\,D_{K,\varepsilon}\,(D_{K,\varepsilon}-2a)}\bigg|=\frac{\sqrt{3}(r_{K,\varepsilon}-M)}{r_{K,\varepsilon}(r_{K,\varepsilon}+3M)},

(4.24)

where the allowed radii are constrained by the extremal Kerr limit
|a|=M

\displaystyle\delta D_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}^{3/2}}{2\,M^{1/2}}\Big(\frac{\delta m-{r_{K,\varepsilon}}\delta m^{\prime}}{M}-\frac{3\delta r_{\varepsilon}}{r_{K,\varepsilon}}\Big),

(4.25)

\displaystyle\delta\Omega_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\,\Omega_{K,\varepsilon}^{2}\,\delta D_{\varepsilon}.

(4.26)

D
from the null circular-orbit conditions (
4.9
) and (
4.10
). Then we obtain the following

(4.27)

which reduces to
r(3M-r)+2a\,\varepsilon\sqrt{Mr}\circeq 0

F_{\varepsilon}(r_{K,\varepsilon}+\delta r_{\varepsilon},M+\delta m,\delta m^{\prime};a)=0

\delta r_{\varepsilon}\circeq-\frac{(\partial F_{\varepsilon}/\partial m)_{K}\,\delta m+(\partial F_{\varepsilon}/\partial m^{\prime})_{K}\,\delta m^{\prime}}{(\partial F_{\varepsilon}/\partial r)_{K}}\Bigg|_{r=r_{\mathrm{K,\varepsilon}}},

where the subscript

indicates that the corresponding quantities are evaluated in the Kerr spacetime, i.e., at
r=r_{K,\varepsilon}
with
m(r)=M
,

\left(\frac{\partial F_{\varepsilon}}{\partial r}\right)_{K}=\frac{3}{2}\,r_{K,\varepsilon}(M-r_{K,\varepsilon}),\quad\left(\frac{\partial F_{\varepsilon}}{\partial m}\right)_{K}=\frac{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}^{2}(r_{K,\varepsilon}+3\,M)}{2M},\quad\left(\frac{\partial F_{\varepsilon}}{\partial m^{\prime}}\right)_{K}=-\,\frac{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}^{3}(r_{K,\varepsilon}-M)}{2M}.

(4.29)

Then the perturbation on the photon orbit positions is given by

\delta r_{\varepsilon}\simeq-\frac{1}{3M}\left({r_{K,\varepsilon}}^{2}\,\delta m^{\prime}-\frac{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}({r_{K,\varepsilon}}+3\,M)}{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}-M}\,\delta m\right)\Bigg|_{r=r_{K,\varepsilon}}.

(4.30)

Substituting the expression for
\delta r_{\varepsilon}
(
4.30

\delta D_{\varepsilon}
(
4.26
), we obtain

\delta D_{\varepsilon}\simeq-\frac{2\,\varepsilon}{r_{K,\varepsilon}-M}\sqrt{\frac{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}^{3}}{M}}\,\delta m\Bigg|_{r=r_{K,\varepsilon}},

(4.31)

which gives the perturbation of the impact parameter for the UCOPs. The corresponding orbital frequency is then expressed as

\delta\Omega_{\varepsilon}\simeq\varepsilon\,D_{K,\varepsilon}^{-2}\left(\frac{2}{r_{K,\varepsilon}-M}\sqrt{\frac{{r_{K,\varepsilon}}^{3}}{M}}\right)\delta m\Bigg|_{r=r_{K,\varepsilon}}\quad.

(4.32)

\delta(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2})\simeq\frac{1}{M\,r_{K,\varepsilon}^{2}(3M+r_{K,\varepsilon})^{3}}\left(-2(r_{K,\varepsilon}^{3}+13M\,r_{K,\varepsilon}^{2}-21M^{2}\,r_{K,\varepsilon}-9M^{3})\delta m\right.\\
\left.+2\,r_{K,\varepsilon}(r_{K,\varepsilon}-M)(r_{K,\varepsilon}+3M)(r_{K,\varepsilon}-5M)\delta m^{\prime}+r_{K,\varepsilon}^{2}(r_{K,\varepsilon}-M)^{2}(r_{K,\varepsilon}+3M)\delta m^{\prime\prime}\right)\Bigg|_{r=r_{K,\varepsilon}}.

+2(r_{K,\varepsilon}-5M)\delta m^{\prime}+(r_{K,\varepsilon}-M)r_{K,\varepsilon}\,\delta m^{\prime\prime}\Bigg)\Bigg|_{r=r_{K,\varepsilon}}.

(4.34)

a=0

In a similar way, the shift of the Lyapunov exponent for

We continue the analysis from the static case. Although an analytically simple expression for

6
Summary and discussions

In this work, we developed a unified perturbative framework to study QNM frequencies of stationary hairy black holes by exploiting the correspondence between the unstable circular null geodesics and QNMs.
We modelled deviations from the corresponding vacuum solutions of the Einstein equations as perturbations in the form of an anisotropic fluid.
Using the relation between the eikonal limit of QNMs and unstable null circular orbits, we derived explicit formulas for the QNM frequencies in terms of the state parameters of the hair field.
Our formulas provide a systematic approach to the analysis of QNMs for hairy black holes without assuming any specific underlying theory or model. We also examined the relationship between the state parameters and the QNM frequencies, including possible violations of the energy conditions.

In the static case, we showed that the QNM shifts can be expressed directly in terms of the matter distribution and its equation of state, with the Lyapunov exponent receiving an additional explicit contribution from the tangential pressure.
In particular, for the Bardeen and Hayward black hole models, the small-deviation approximation from the vacuum solution is incompatible with the dominant energy condition (DEC).
This implies that, if the corresponding deviation is confirmed through the QNM observations, we need to consider a matter field violating the DEC or any other modification in the gravitational wave emission mechanisms.
Since the violation of the DEC implies a superluminal flux, it might be more physically reasonable to consider a modified gravity theory than to rely on exotic matter fields. By contrast, for the Kiselev case, the compatibility with the energy conditions depends on the parameters
k
and
w_{q}
, and all of the standard energy conditions can be satisfied within suitable parameter ranges even in the small-
|k|
regime.
WEC and SEC are satisfied for all our examples, solutions still maintain a positive energy density, and gravity is still locally attractive in both timelike and null geodesics.

In the rotating case, the QNM components are separated into co-rotating and counter-rotating branches according to the equatorial UCOP structure. This shows that rotation does not merely shift the static result, but introduces an asymmetry in how the hair modifies the oscillation frequency and damping rate, with both branches reducing smoothly to the static case in the non-rotating limit. We considered the Bardeen, Hayward, and Kiselev metrics obtained by applying the Janis–Newman method to their static counterparts.
We then restricted our attention to UCOPs on the equatorial plane and derived the corresponding angular frequencies and Lyapunov exponents, from which the QNM information can be inferred. Moreover, because the effective energy density and principal pressures in the co-rotating frame are the same as in the corresponding static case, the energy-condition analysis performed for the static models remains applicable to the rotating case as well.
Although the correspondence between QNMs and UCOPs is not fully established in the rotating case, equatorial UCOPs are expected to be associated with the
\ell=|m|
mode of QNMs
[
4
]
.

In the construction of our hairy black hole models, we considered the effects of matter fields as perturbative deviations from the Schwarzschild and the Kerr solutions. In particular, we assumed the simplest form of the equations of state (
2.15
) and focused only on the first-order perturbative effects of the matter field. It should be noted that our formulas given in Sec.
2.1
(also the formula (
2.14
)) for the static case, and those in Sec.
4.1
(see e.g., (
4.14
), (
4.15
)) for the rotating case can apply for the general cases. It would therefore be interesting to extend our present analyses to higher-order perturbative analyses, just like higher-order WKB analyses, and to include more general equations of state, such as the polytrope type. Possible modifications of QNMs induced by matter fields or deviations from general relativity were also discussed by Cardoso et al.
[
5
]
and McManus et al.
[
20
]
, who considered perturbative deformations of the effective potential in the wave equations. It would be interesting to compare their results with those obtained in our analysis.

Overall, our results suggest that black hole ringdown signals can probe not only the spacetime geometry, but also the physical nature of the matter environment and possible deviations from vacuum general relativity in the vicinity of black holes.
