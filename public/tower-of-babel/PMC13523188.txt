Social enhancement of associative learning in neurotypical, speaking autistic, and minimally speaking autistic preschoolers
Authors: Eleonora Paolizzi, Loredana Orsili, Meagan Talbott, Gioia Cavalli, Aubyn C Stahmer, Nancy Raitano Lee, Diana L Robins, Giacomo Vivanti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssociative learning, defined as the ability to form connections between events based on temporal or spatial contiguity, is typically enhanced by social stimuli. Whether social stimuli similarly enhance associative learning in autism, particularly among minimally speaking children, is unknown. This study examined social enhancement of associative learning in neurotypical (NT), autistic preschoolers who use spoken language (ASD-S), and minimally speaking autistic (ASD-MS) preschoolers.MethodsSeventy-two children aged 24–56 months (19 NT, 30 ASD-S, 23 ASD-MS) completed a novel eye-tracking task. During 4 learning trials, neutral cues were associated with the appearance of a social or a non-social stimulus in different areas of the screen. In subsequent test trials, only the neutral cue was shown, allowing assessment of (a) associative learning (i.e., preferential looking toward the previously paired area of the screen) and (b) enhancement of associative learning in the social condition. General linear models evaluated group differences across conditions.ResultsWhen the neutral cue was paired with the social stimulus, during the test trial NT and ASD-S preschoolers looked longer toward the area where the social stimuli had previously appeared, compared to the area associated with the non-social stimulus (NT: p < .001; ASD-S: p = .04). In contrast, ASD-MS preschoolers showed no significant difference in their attention to the areas previously associated with social or non-social stimuli. Analyses focused on differential scores (attention toward the social-paired location minus the non-social-paired location) significantly favored the social condition in NT (p = .005) and ASD-S children (p = .007), but not in ASD-MS children. Between-group comparisons showed greater social enhancement in NT compared with ASD-MS preschoolers (p = .03).LimitationsThe social stimuli used in the experiment may not fully capture the complexity of everyday social interactions.ConclusionsSocial stimuli enhanced associative learning in neurotypical and speaking autistic preschoolers, but not in their minimally speaking autistic peers. This pattern suggests meaningful heterogeneity in the mechanisms supporting social learning across the autism spectrum and underscores the need to consider spoken language abilities when characterizing learning profiles in autism.Keywords: Associative learning, Social enhancement, Minimally speaking, Eye tracker

Background

Associative learning refers to the process through which two events or stimuli become associated by virtue of their temporal [1] or spatial contiguity [2] (such as children playing with a jack-in-the-box toy learning that pressing a button is followed by the appearance of a puppet). Recent literature has documented the phenomenon of social enhancement of associative learning; i.e., associative learning is enhanced in response to social stimuli and experiences [3–5]. For example, the association between pressing a button and the appearance of a puppet in the scenario above becomes more robust when a play partner provides social cues indicating anticipation (e.g., widening eyes and smiling) before the button is pressed – this amplifies engagement and strengthen associative learning. In typical development, associative learning and its social enhancement play an important role in spoken language acquisition. For example, phonetic learning of a foreign language in young children has been shown to be enhanced in response to social interactions with a person speaking that language compared to exposure to pre-recorded audio [6].

The interplay between associative learning and its social enhancement is critically relevant to our understanding of autism and its treatment, as many interventions aim to leverage social valence of stimuli to facilitate learning. For example, in Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions social interactions characterized by positive affect (for example, a therapist widening eyes and smiling before delivering an instruction) are used to facilitate child’s engagement and learning [7]. Additionally, this area of research has the potential to provide insight into communication differences in the autistic population, given the role of social enhancement of associative learning in spoken language acquisition. For example, Houston-Price and colleagues found that 15-month-old infants demonstrated more effective novel word learning when both social engagement and perceptual salience of the referent were present, compared to when only one cue was available, reflecting the interplay of social and associative processes [8]. In autism, the degree to which associative learning is enhanced by social stimuli remains unclear, with mixed findings and important gaps. Associative learning per se appears to be preserved in this population, with studies reporting intact associative learning for visual and auditory stimuli among autistic individuals across age groups [9, 10], and spoken language levels [11]. However, a recent study found that the social context failed to facilitate associative learning in autistic participants, whereas it enhanced learning in the comparison groups [12]. Further, an event-related potentials study showed that neural responses during an associative learning task were enhanced in typical but not autistic individuals when the to-be-associated stimuli where social in nature [13]. Although this research suggests that associative learning in autism might be intact but not subject to social enhancement, studies suggest substantial heterogeneity across tasks and across populations within the autism spectrum [14]. For example, Jones and colleagues reported reduced associative learning in autistic children compared to typically developing peers at the group level in a visual statistical learning task– however, a subset of autistic children demonstrated normative performance, suggesting that variability in associative learning skills in the autistic population may be masked by group-level comparisons and influenced by task demands (i.e., passive viewing versus following verbal instructions) [15]. Additionally, social enhancement of associative learning differed depending on autism features [16].

As associative learning and its social enhancement contribute to spoken language development [8] it is plausible that differences in these processes might contribute to variability in communication within the autistic population. However, a critical limitation of the current literature in this area is its predominant focus on autistic individuals with spoken language and cognitive skills in the typical range [11]. The exclusion of individuals with more pronounced impairments in spoken language and cognitive skills might reflect challenges with test-taking skills (e.g., following verbal instructions) required in most tests used to assess associative learning and its social enhancement [17]. However, understanding how learning is enhanced by social stimuli is particularly important for understanding and addressing the challenges experienced by children who are minimally speaking, a subgroup representing approximately 30% of the autistic population [18, 19]. Although spoken language differences in autism are manifested as a continuum, children who do not develop spoken language may represent a distinct, clinically meaningful subgroup [20, 21], characterized by complex support needs that encompass both verbal and non-verbal cognition and adaptive behavior [22, 23]. Understanding associative learning and its social enhancement in this population is critical given the lack of research on mechanisms underlying spoken language differences in this population.

Against this background, the current study was designed to examine whether social stimuli enhance associative learning in neurotypical and autistic preschoolers, including minimally speaking children and children using spoken language (see Methods section for classification criteria). This was achieved through a novel eye-tracking paradigm that does not rely on prerequisite test-taking skills, designed to test whether participants (a) learned the association between a neutral cue and a social or non-social stimulus, and (b) whether this was enhanced in response to social compared to non-social stimuli.

We hypothesized that:

Neurotypical preschoolers would learn associations between stimuli, with enhanced performance when associations involve social stimuli.

Neurotypical preschoolers would learn associations between stimuli, with enhanced performance when associations involve social stimuli.

Autistic preschoolers who use spoken language would learn associations between stimuli, but their performance would not be enhanced when associations involve social stimuli.

Autistic preschoolers who use spoken language would learn associations between stimuli, but their performance would not be enhanced when associations involve social stimuli.

Minimally speaking, autistic preschoolers would show reduced associative learning and social enhancement compared to both neurotypical and speaking autistic peers.

Minimally speaking, autistic preschoolers would show reduced associative learning and social enhancement compared to both neurotypical and speaking autistic peers.

Methods

Parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB protocols: 1512004088, 1607004653, 2103008436) of Drexel University and conformed to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Seventy-four preschoolers enrolled in the study. After removing two participants two to invalid data (see Procedure section for validity criteria), a final sample of 72 participants (age range = 24.00–56.00 months, M = 33.89, SD = 8.02) was included in the analyses. This included 19 neurotypical (NT) children, 30 speaking autistic (ASD-S), and 23 minimally speaking autistic (ASD-MS) preschoolers.

Following previous literature [24], minimally speaking status among autistic participants was defined as a current Expressive Communication age equivalent ≤ 14 months, as assessed with the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales-Third Edition (VABS-3) [25]. When VABS-3 data were unavailable (n = 2), the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL) [26] Expressive Language age equivalent was used. Based on this criterion, 23 of the 53 autistic participants were classified as minimally speaking.

Only participants who showed age-appropriate development across domains and parents reported no developmental, neurological, or psychiatric concerns, were included in NT group. Autistic children’s diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder as based on DSM-5 criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). For most autistic participants (ASD-S n = 19 and ASD-MS = 15 preschoolers), autism diagnosis was confirmed through the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule – Second Edition (ADOS-2) [27] by research reliable clinicians. For the remaining participants, the ADOS-2 had been completed by other service providers within six months. Exclusion criteria for the current study included [1] chronological age below 24 months [2], co-occurrence of severe motor or sensory impairment, and [2] uncorrected sensory impairment. Participant characteristics are reported in Table 1. Groups did not significantly differ in chronological age; however, they differed in developmental level and adaptive functioning, with minimally speaking children presenting lower scores compared to the other groups in all domains except for motor functioning.

Procedure

This study is part of a larger multi-site project. Participants were recruited through local early intervention services, community outreach events, IRB-approved recruitment materials, or pediatric primary care practices. Assessments were conducted at the A.J. Drexel Autism Institute, UC Davis MIND Institute and University of Connecticut, following written informed consent, written at an eighth grade reading level, using procedures approved by the institutional review board.

Eye tracking apparatus and experimental paradigm

To ensure cross-site consistency, administration of the eye-tracking task and data preprocessing were standardized across sites using shared standard operating procedures. Identical eye-tracking hardware and software versions were used at each site. Gaze data were preprocessed using the standard preprocessing procedures implemented in Tobii Pro Lab, and data from all sites were subsequently merged for analysis.

Testing took place in a quiet room. Participants were seated comfortably in an age-appropriate chair positioned approximately 60 cm from a Tobii Pro Spectrum eye-tracking system monitor. Participants were encouraged to watch a series of video stimuli interspersed with child-friendly “filler” clips designed to maintain attention. Prior to each stimulus presentation, a 5-point calibration procedure was conducted to ensure accurate and reliable eye-tracking data. The collected eye-tracking data were processed and analyzed using Tobii Pro Lab software on a connected laptop. For each stimulus, Areas of Interest (AOIs), i.e., spatial regions within the visual display, were defined in advance, along with corresponding Times of Interest (TOIs), which specified the temporal segments during which gaze data were extracted for each AOI. Fixation criteria were aligned with Tobii Studio defaults: 30-pixel dispersion threshold for 100ms. The primary gaze metric extracted was total attention duration, i.e., gaze duration, within each AOI.

Stimuli presented via the eye-tracker were designed to test whether participants (a) learned the association between a neutral cue and a subsequent event (which was either social or non-social), and (b) whether associative learning was enhanced in response to social compared to non-social stimuli. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the paradigm included four learning trials followed by two test trials, each presented in two conditions (social vs. non-social). Each learning trial began with a neutral cue - a ball falling down a staircase and landing in either a green or a blue bucket (Fig. 1a-b). This bucket color was reliably associated with the subsequent appearance of a stimulus in one of two possible areas. When the ball landed in the green bucket, a non-social stimulus (bubbles popping) appeared on the top-left side of the screen (Fig. 1a). When the ball landed in the blue bucket, a social stimulus (an unfamiliar adult clapping and smiling) appeared on the top-right side of the screen (Fig. 1b). After the learning trials, children viewed two test trials, one non-social (Fig. 1c) and one social (Fig. 1d). In both trials, the same neutral cue shown in the learning trials was presented, but no follow-up stimulus appeared in the previously associated areas. This allowed us to test whether participants spontaneously looked toward the area that had been associated with the cue during the learning trials. Specifically, during the test trials, children were expected to look toward the toward the social area (top-right, previously paired with the ball landing in the blue bucket) or the non-social area (top-left, previously paired with the ball landing in the green bucket), depending on the bucket color shown in that trial. Accordingly, our attentional index of associative learning was the duration of attention directed to (a) the social area, where the social stimulus had appeared during the learning trials, and (b) the non-social area, where the non-social stimulus had appeared. Social enhancement of associative learning was defined as a greater differentiation in attention allocation between the social and non-social areas in the social test trial compared to the non-social test trial.

Social enhancement of associative learning experiment design

Presentations of social and non-social stimuli were alternating, and the sequence was counterbalanced across participants. Accordingly, for half of the sample, the task began with a learning trial featuring the social video, followed by a non-social learning trial in alternating sequence, and concluded with the social test trial presented before the non-social test trial. For the other half of the sample, the order was reversed. Data were considered valid if participants completed at least two social and two non-social learning trials. Based on this criterion two ASD-S preschoolers were excluded. Additionally, missing data due to procedural error specific to the non-social test trial led to the exclusion of two ASD-S and one ASD-MS participants in the analyses related to the non-social condition.

Analytical plan

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30.0.0.0 (172). Descriptive statistics and one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were used to examine sample characteristics and group differences among NT, ASD-S, and ASD-MS preschoolers. Statistical significance was set at p < .05, and t-tests were two-tailed. We fitted repeated-measures general linear models (GLMs) to examine group differences (NT vs. ASD-S vs. ASD-MS) in attention duration towards the social and non-social areas in the test trials (Fig. 1c-d) across conditions (social vs. non-social). When significant main effects or interactions were observed, Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were performed to control for multiple testing and reduce the risk of Type I error. Partial eta-squared (η²ₚ) values are reported to indicate the magnitude of observed effects.

Although significant group differences were observed in verbal and non-verbal cognitive skills (Table 1), cognitive level was not included as a covariate term in the analyses. The rationale for this approach is that low cognitive functioning was conceptualized as a defining and clinically meaningful characteristic of the ASD-MS group, rather than an extraneous confounding variable. Covarying for cognitive or language functioning would therefore remove variance that is intrinsic to the ASD-MS phenotype, rather than removing a source of bias. Additionally, we conceptualized associative learning processes as mechanistically related (although not overlapping) to cognitive ability that contribute to measured cognitive functioning. Therefore, we reasoned that adjusting for IQ would risk over-controlling for variance that lies on the causal pathway between diagnostic group and associative learning performance, potentially obscuring real-world, clinically meaningful differences between groups. This is in line with other studies suggesting that covarying for IQ would produce overcorrected results when studying neurodevelopmental conditions [28].

Results

Social test trial

A repeated-measures general linear model (GLM) was conducted to examine differences in total attention duration toward the social and non-social areas during the social test trial (Fig. 1d) across diagnostic groups (NT, ASD-S, ASD-MS). The within-subjects factor was area (social vs. non-social), and the between-subjects factor was group. Descriptive statistics are reported in Table 2. Results revealed a significant main effect of area, F(1, 69) = 13.00, p < .001, η²ₚ = 0.16, indicating that, overall, participants looked longer toward the social area than toward the non-social area, with a large effect size. There was no main effect of group, F(2, 69) = 2.46, p = .09, η²ₚ = 0.07, suggesting that the attention duration was cumulatively similar across group. The interaction between area and group was significant, F(2, 69) = 5.49, p = .006, η²ₚ = 0.14, indicating differences in the attention allocation toward the different areas across groups, with a large effect size. Examination of the within-group effects showed that for the NT group, there was a robust preference for the social area, F(1, 69) = 17.91, p < .001, η²ₚ = 0.21, with longer attention duration toward the social compared to the non-social area. For the ASD-S group, a smaller but significant effect was observed, F(1, 69) = 4.40, p = .04, η²ₚ = 0.06, similarly reflecting greater attention to the social compared to the non-social area. Conversely, for the ASD-MS group, no significant difference emerged between attention towards the social and non-social areas, F(1, 69) = 0.07, p = .79, η²ₚ = 0.001.

Non-social test trial

A repeated-measures GLM was conducted with area (social vs. non-social area) as a within-subjects factor and group (NT, ASD-S, ASD-MS) as a between-subjects factor, to examine differences in the duration of attention toward the social and non-social areas during the non-social test trial (Fig. 1c). See Table 2 for descriptive statistics. As illustrated in Fig. 3, no significant main effect of area was observed, F(1, 66) = 0.03, p = .86, η²ₚ < 0.001, indicating that, overall, participants looked equally at the social and non-social areas during the non-social test trial. The between-subjects effect of group on overall attention was not significant, F(2, 66) = 1.93, p = .15, η²ₚ = 0.06. The interaction between area and group was also not significant, F(2, 66) = 1.46, p = .24, η²ₚ = 0.04, suggesting that the distribution of attention across the two areas did not differ significantly between diagnostic groups.

Attention duration across test trials

To further examine social enhancement of associative learning and distinguish it from a mere social preference, which would predict an increased attention to social areas regardless of test trial condition, we compared attention duration to the same social or non-social AOIs across the social and non-social test trials.

A repeated-measures GLM examined attention to the social area, with test trial (social vs. non-social), as the within-subjects factor and group as the between-subjects factor. There was a significant main effect of test trial, F(1, 66) = 5.73, p = .02, η²ₚ = 0.08, indicating greater attention to the social area during the social test trial compared to the non-social test trial. This pattern is inconsistent with a mere social preference, as attention to the social area varied depending on whether the neutral cue predicted a social or non-social event. A significant main effect of group emerged, F(2, 66) = 4.05, p = .02, η²ₚ = 0.11. However, the interaction between trial and group was not significant, F(2, 66) = 1.57, p = .22, η²ₚ = 0.05. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons indicated that NT preschoolers looked significantly longer toward the social area compared to ASD-MS preschoolers, p = .02, whereas ASD-S preschoolers did not significantly differ from either group (ps > 0.14).

A second repeated-measures GLM examined attention to the non-social area across test-trials. There was no main effect of test trial, F(1, 66) = 2.65, p = .11, η²ₚ = 0.04, indicating no overall difference in attention to the non-social area between social and non-social test trials. No significant main effect of group was observed for the non-social test trial. However, a significant trial × group interaction was observed, F(2, 66) = 3.77, p = .03, η²ₚ = 0.10, showing that patterns of attention across trials differed between groups. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons revealed that ASD-MS preschoolers looked significantly longer toward the non-social area during the social test trial compared to ASD-S (p = .03) and NT preschoolers (p = .03). Within-group analyses showed that ASD-S preschoolers exhibited a significant difference between test trials (p = .003), looking longer toward the non-social area in the non-social test trial, consistent with associative learning, whereas the other groups did not show such within-group differences (all ps > 0.33).

Differential attentin duration to social and non-social areas across test trials

To examine how attention to the social and non-social areas differed across test trials, we first computed a differential score for each trial by subtracting attention to the non-social area from attention to the social area (i.e., social minus non-social). Positive values indicate greater attention to the social area, whereas negative values indicate greater attention to the non-social area. A repeated-measures GLM was then conducted with test trial (social vs. non-social) as the within-subjects factor and group (NT, ASD-S, ASD-MS) as the between-subjects factor to assess whether children showed stronger associative learning in one test condition than the other—specifically, whether the preference for the area associated with the neutral cue was greater in the social test trial than in the non-social test trial. Whereas the preceding analyses examined attention to each area independently, this integrated analysis tested whether overall attention allocation was modulated by the associative context, providing a direct index of social enhancement of associative learning. Descriptive statistics are presented in Table 3, and Fig. 4 displays the differential attention scores across groups and test trials. There was a significant main effect of test trial, F(1, 66) = 9.43, p = .003, η²ₚ = 0.13, indicating that participants showed a larger positive differential during the social than the non-social test trial, and a main effect of group, F(2, 66) = 3.88, p = .03, η²ₚ = 0.11. A significant test trial X group interaction was also found, F(2, 66) = 3.30, p = .04, η²ₚ = 0.09, suggesting that the magnitude and direction of this attentional difference varied by group.

Discussion

The present study investigated social enhancement of associative learning in early childhood among neurotypical preschoolers as well as speaking and minimally speaking autistic preschoolers. Consistent with our hypothesis, neurotypical preschoolers demonstrated social enhancement of associative learning. Specifically, they showed greater attention to the social area during the social test trial—when the neutral cue predicted a social outcome—indicating successful learning of the association. Neurotypical children also showed a consistent bias toward the area previously paired with a social stimulus even during the non-social test trial (i.e., when the neutral cue predicted a non-social outcome). Importantly, however, their performance in the task cannot be fully attributed by a uniform social bias, as attention to the social area was highest in the social test trial and decreased in the non-social test trial, suggesting that participants learned the alternative, non-social association and adjusted their anticipatory looking, despite the persistence of a social bias.

The performance of speaking autistic children also indicated evidence of associative learning across conditions and a pattern consistent with social modulation of learning (i.e., increased associative learning performance in the social relative to the non-social condition). However, social modulation was less pronounced than in neurotypical peers, as reflected in the smaller effect size for attention duration toward the social area during the social test trial. However, speaking autistic children exhibited lower overall attention to social stimuli across trials compared to neurotypical children. These findings suggest that social stimuli may modulate associative learning in speaking autistic children, despite lower overall attention to social stimuli. Importantly, however, speaking autistic children also demonstrated evidence of learning in the non-social condition, indicating that associative learning was not dependent on social information.

By contrast, minimally speaking autistic preschoolers showed no evidence of associative learning as their attention was evenly distributed to the areas previously associated with social and non-social stimuli, and no evidence of social enhancement, as no differences emerged across test trials, and attention to the non-social area was overall larger than the other groups during the social test trial. These results seem to diverge from prior findings suggesting preserved statistical learning in minimally speaking preschoolers [11]. One potential explanation for this discrepancy is the greater task complexity in the current experimental paradigm. In the Paolizzi et al. [11] paradigm, associative learning outcomes (learning where a ball was going to appear) was consistently associated with a single event (i.e., the ball reliably descended along the same one of three possible streams in the learning trials). In contrast, the current task required participants to infer outcomes (social versus non-social locations) across multiple locations (left or right) based on one of two the initial events (green bucket or blue bucket being hit by the ball). This increased cognitive demand may have reduced learning efficiency in this group.

The distinct performance of autistic minimally speaking preschoolers challenges the notion that reduced social enhancement of associative learning characterizes the autistic population as a whole. Instead, these findings underscore the importance of examining heterogeneity within the autism spectrum, particularly with respect to spoken language abilities. Prior studies have largely examined heterogeneous autistic samples without stratifying by language profiles, which may have obscured meaningful subgroup differences [13, 15]. Our findings show that minimally speaking autistic children not only performed differently from their autistic peers but also demonstrate a unique attentional profile that may reflect distinct learning strategies or priorities. If social cues do not reliably support learning in minimally speaking autistic preschoolers, opportunities to learn from everyday social stimuli (including inputs important for the acquisition of spoken language) may be reduced or qualitatively different, potentially contributing to delays in language acquisition. Indeed, as previously discussed, word learning has been shown to be supported by social engagement in neurotypical infants [8], whereas evidence for this mechanism in autism remains less consistent [29].

Taken together, these findings suggest that for neurotypical and speaking autistic preschoolers, social stimuli act as learning enhancers, consistent with models emphasizing the interplay between social salience and learning. In contrast, the absence of such enhancement in minimally speaking autistic preschoolers points to subgroup-specific area of difficulty and highlights the need to identify strategies that more effectively support engagement and learning in this group. Although preliminary, these findings provide insight into heterogeneity in associative learning within the autism spectrum and highlight the need to identify learning supports that are responsive to individual attentional and learning profiles. Familiarity-based or interest-based supports may represent promising avenues for future investigation. Whether enhancing the salience of social stimuli facilitates learning in minimally speaking autistic individuals remains an empirical question that should be directly examined in future studies. More broadly, these findings reinforce the need for theoretical models that do not assume homogeneity in socio-communicative and learning abilities and processes across the autism spectrum.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the relatively small and imbalanced sample, which may have reduced statistical power for detecting group differences. However, the consistency of patterns across conditions and the medium-to-large effect sizes observed across key analyses suggest that the findings are unlikely to be driven solely by sample size constraints. Further, although sex differences are an important consideration, the small number of females in the autistic minimally speaking group precluded the inclusion of sex as a covariate, as doing so would yield unstable and potentially misleading estimates. As a result, we cannot exclude the possibility that some of the observed group differences may be partially influenced by sex-related effects. Future studies with more balanced sex distributions will be necessary to explicitly examine sex-related effects.

A limitation of the present design is that different phenomena, including associative learning, social modulation and social bias might have contributed to eye-gaze patterns in response to social and non-social stimuli within the same task. Although our paradigm was designed to infer the relative contribution of these processes, future studies should employ complementary designs that allow for a more fine-grained orthogonalization of these processes.

Another limitation concerns the generalizability of our findings to different types of stimuli and populations. The role of stimulus familiarity warrants further investigation, as familiarity with social agents may influence both engagement and learning outcomes [30]. Additionally, in young children “minimally speaking” status should be interpreted as a current profile subject to developmental change, rather than a stable phenotype. Further, a limitation of the present study is that learning was assessed based on a single test trial for each condition with relatively brief exposure, a design choice driven by feasibility constraints when including very young and minimally speaking autistic children, but one that limits the strength of the inferences and warrants replication.

Additionally, while anticipatory gaze provides a sensitive index of implicit learning, it does not capture the subjective value of stimuli. Future research should adopt a multimethod approach, integrating physiological or neural measures (e.g., ERP or fMRI) to better characterize the stimuli processing in minimally speaking autistic preschoolers. Further, a fine-grained characterization of heterogeneity in associative learning within different autistic populations, would be beneficial for future work. Finally, our design does not permit direct examination of the mechanisms underlying the observed group differences (e.g., attentional versus reward-related processes), thus precluding causal or mechanistic interpretations of why minimally speaking participants performed differently compared to the other groups.

These limitations notwithstanding, the inclusion of autistic minimally speaking preschoolers, a population rarely represented in research, represents a significant strength. It should be noted that cognitive functioning, while being significantly lower in minimally verbal autistic children compared to the other groups, was not included as a covariate in the analyses. This reflected our conceptualization of low cognitive functioning as a clinically meaningful feature of the ASD-MS group, rather than as an extraneous confound, whereby adjusting for cognitive or language ability would partial out variance that is intrinsic to the ASD-MS phenotype. Moreover, we conceptualized associative learning as mechanistically related to—though distinct from—general cognitive ability, contributing to observed cognitive performance. From this perspective, covarying for IQ would risk over-adjustment by controlling for variance that plausibly contributes to the causal pathway between diagnostic group and associative learning outcomes, thereby potentially obscuring clinically meaningful, real-world group differences [28].

While previous literature had investigated associative learning and its social enhancement, this is the first study in the area, to our knowledge, that focused on the minimally speaking autistic population. By employing a fully nonverbal eye-tracking paradigm, we were able to avoid the verbal and motor demands that often limit participation of minimally speaking autistic children in experimental research, enabling us to capture learning-related attentional patterns in this under-researched group.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence that social stimuli enhance associative learning in neurotypical and speaking autistic preschoolers, but not in their minimally speaking autistic peers.

These findings support the need for theoretical frameworks and intervention programs that account for subgroup differences in learning processes within the autism spectrum. For minimally speaking autistic preschoolers, alternative forms of scaffolding, such as familiarity-based or approaches that enhance the value and salience of social stimuli, may represent promising strategies for supporting learning.
