A Regularized Shallow Water System
Evgueni Dinvay and Henrik Kalisch

Abstract
The shallow-water system is a standard model for long waves in shallow water. The system is hyperbolic and, for a large class of initial data, solutions develop steep gradients leading to shock formation in finite time. Since such singularities violate the long-wave assumptions underlying the model, their appearance limits the regime of validity of the equations. In this work, we introduce a regularized shallow-water system in which the nonlinear terms are modified by a bounded operator. This regularization removes the standard derivative-steepening mechanism responsible for shock formation in the classical system while remaining consistent with the long-wave regime. We establish local well-posedness and small-data global well-posedness in Sobolev spaces that exclude singularity formation. In addition, numerical simulations indicate that the system admits solitary-wave solutions.



1
Introduction

The shallow-water system is one of the most widely known hyperbolic systems of
partial differential equations
[
13
]
,
and it is also one of the most popular systems
for the modeling of long waves in shallow water
[
33
]
.
Numerical approaches to
discretizing the shallow-water system form the basis of many popular coastal models used in coastal engineering practice and river flow modeling as well as academic research.

As explained for example in
[
30
]
, the shallow-water equations are
usually written in terms of the total flow depth
h(x,t)
and the average
horizontal velocity
u(x,t)
as the unknowns, and it is assumed that
the pressure is hydrostatic since the flow conditions are changing very slowly.
The most fundamental version of the shallow-water system concerns
the case of long-crested waves propagating over a flat bed.
Using the assumption of hydrostatic pressure,
the equations of mass and momentum conservation take the form

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}h_{t}+(hu)_{x}&=&0,\\
(hu)_{t}+(hu^{2}+gh^{2}/2)_{x}&=&0,\end{array}\right\}

(1.1)

where
g
denotes the gravitational acceleration.
For smooth solutions and with undisturbed depth
h_{0}
and free-surface deflection
from rest denoted by
\eta(x,t)
, the system can be rewritten in the form

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\eta_{t}+h_{0}u_{x}+(\eta u)_{x}&=&0,\\
u_{t}+(u^{2}/2+g\eta)_{x}&=&0.\end{array}\right\}

(1.2)

As mentioned above, this system applies to long waves in a river or
in shallow coastal areas, and it can be generalized to a two-dimensional
version for waves in the coastal region with realistic bathymetry.
Such equations and even more general equations incorporating dispersion
and friction are widely used for numerical simulations of various aspects of
nearshore waves
[
3
,
22
,
29
]
, and the discretizations
used for these equations are usually based on general numerical
methods for hyperbolic systems such as detailed in

[
26
]
, for example.

One of the problems with the system shown above is that
even though the derivation explicitly makes use of the assumption that
the waves to be described are long, solutions of this equation
often develop singularities in the form of infinite derivatives.
Indeed, it is shown formally in
[
33
]
that waves that
carry a decrease in elevation form derivative singularities. From a modeling point of view, these shocks can be interpreted as bores (see
[
2
,
20
,
33
]
), but from a mathematical point of view they can be a problem.

A rigorous mathematical proof can be given that solutions corresponding to compressive initial configurations develop unbounded gradients,
leading to shock formation (see
[
19
]
, ch. 11.3).
On the other hand,
it is also known that global smooth solutions may persist
for certain non-compressive or monotone classes of initial data

[
4
,
24
]
,
so shock formation is not universal.

For times up to singularity formation, it can be shown mathematically
that the shallow-water equations arise as a limit point in the convergence
of the more fundamental free-surface water-wave problem based on the
full inviscid Euler equations to the shallow-water system
(given some appropriate assumptions on the regularity of the solutions)

[
23
]
.
The problem is that solutions of (
1.2
) only exist for a short
time for a large class of initial data.
Indeed it can be shown with fairly elementary analysis methods
that solutions always blow up given some mild assumptions (see
[
19
]
).
Unfortunately, once infinite derivatives and shocks appear, the
very assumption upon which the shallow-water system is based, is invalidated.
This problem is known as the
Long wave paradox
in some circles

[
25
,
32
]
.

In the present note, it is our purpose to introduce a regularized
system which avoids the problem of singularity formation.
The system we put forward is

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\eta_{t}+h_{0}u_{x}+K(\eta u)_{x}&=&0,\\
u_{t}+g\eta_{x}+K(u^{2}/2)_{x}&=&0,\end{array}\right\}

(1.3)

where a regularizing operator can be chosen in
such a way that
K\partial_{x}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
At the same time one can make sure that
K\approx 1

in the shallow water regime.
Indeed, as an example of such an operator,
the Fourier multiplier

K=\frac{\tanh h_{0}D}{h_{0}D}

(1.4)

can be taken.
Here
D=-i\partial_{x}
,
and so at low frequencies the corresponding symbol
K(\xi)\approx 1
.
With this choice of
K
we have

K\partial_{x}=i\tanh h_{0}D/h_{0}
that is obviously
a bounded Fourier multiplier operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, this regularization naturally arises
in the Hamiltonian long wave approximation
of the full water wave problem
[
14
]
, and
is similar to a Whitham-type system
[
1
,
27
]
although
the regularization is now in the nonlinear terms as opposed to the linear terms.
The total energy of System (
1.3
) has the form

\mathcal{H}(\eta,u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(g\eta K^{-1}\eta+h_{0}uK^{-1}u+\eta u^{2}\right)dx

(1.5)

where the domain of functional
\mathcal{H}

is the Sobolev space
H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Note that System (
1.3
) has the following Hamiltonian structure

\eta_{t}=-K\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta u}\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{t}=-K\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\eta}

(1.6)

with the variational derivatives

\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\eta}=gK^{-1}\eta+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta u}=h_{0}K^{-1}u+\eta u.

(1.7)

In particular,
we have conservation of
\mathcal{H}
,
following from the skew-adjointness of
K\partial_{x}
,
since
K
is self-adjoint and commutes with
\partial_{x}
.

We study the Cauchy problem for the semilinear regularization (
1.3
)
of the shallow-water system.
The regularization modifies the nonlinear fluxes in such a way
that the derivative-steepening mechanism is suppressed.
As a consequence, we obtain local well-posedness
and global well-posedness for sufficiently small initial data in Sobolev spaces.
The large-data global dynamics is not addressed in this work.

Regularizations of shallow-water type systems that preserve conservative structure
have been proposed in several works.
In particular, a non-dispersive, non-dissipative regularization was introduced in

[
12
]
,
and its shock structure was further analyzed in
[
28
]
.
Extensions to uneven bottom topographies were considered in
[
9
]
.
These works address similar modeling questions from a different perspective
and provide useful context for the present approach.

2
Local well-posedness

In this section we work in the non-dimensional
settings
h_{0}=1
and
g=1
.
Introduce the space
X^{s}=H^{s}\times H^{s}

equipped with the standard norm.
Denote by
X^{s}_{T}
the space of continuous functions
defined on
[0,T]
with values in
X^{s}
,
equipped with the supremum-norm.
Regard the unitary group

\mathcal{S}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-itD}&0\\
0&e^{itD}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}

that is a composition of isometric operators in
X^{s}
.
Note that for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
,

v\in X^{s}
holds

\lVert\mathcal{S}(t)v\rVert_{X^{s}}=\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}},

and consequently

\lVert\mathcal{S}(t)v\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}=\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}

for any
T>0
,
due to the fact that symbols of
eigenvalues of
\mathcal{S}(t)
have absolute value
equal to one.
For any fixed
v_{0}=(\eta_{0},u_{0})^{T}\in X^{s}

function
\mathcal{S}(t)v_{0}
solves the linear
initial-value problem associated with
(
1.3
).
Regard a mapping
\mathcal{A}:X^{s}_{T}\to X^{s}_{T}

defined by

\mathcal{A}(\eta,u)=\mathcal{A}(\eta,u;v_{0})(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)v_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-t^{\prime})K\partial_{x}\begin{pmatrix}\eta u\\
u^{2}/2\end{pmatrix}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(2.1)

Then the Cauchy problem for System (
1.3
)
with the initial data
v_{0}
may be rewritten
equivalently
as an equation in
X^{s}_{T}
of the form

v=\mathcal{A}(v;v_{0})

(2.2)

where
v=(\eta,u)^{T}\in X^{s}_{T}
.
Below the latter integral equation is solved locally in time
by making use of Picard iterations.

Lemma 2.1

(local well-posedness)
.

Let
s>1/2
,
v_{0}=(\eta_{0},u_{0})^{T}\in X^{s}

and
T=(7C_{s}\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}})^{-1}

with some constant
C_{s}>0
depending only on
s
.
Then there exists a unique solution

v=(\eta,u)^{T}\in X^{s}_{T}
of Problem (
2.2
).

Moreover, for any
R>0
there exists
T=T(R)>0

such that the flow map associated with Equation (
2.2
)
is a real analytic mapping of the open ball

B_{R}(0)\subset X^{s}
to
X^{s}_{T}
.

Proof.

The proof idea is to show that the restriction of
\mathcal{A}

on some closed ball
B_{M}
centered at
\mathcal{S}(t)v_{0}

is a contraction mapping.
The Sobolev space
H^{s}
is an algebra for
s>1/2
, and so
there is a positive constant
C_{s}>0

such that

\lVert(\eta u,u^{2}/2)\rVert_{X^{s}}\leqslant C_{s}\lVert(\eta,u)\rVert_{X^{s}}^{2}

and

\lVert(\eta_{1}u_{1}-\eta_{2}u_{2},u_{1}^{2}/2-u_{2}^{2}/2)\rVert_{X^{s}}\leqslant C_{s}\lVert(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2},u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{X^{s}}(\lVert(\eta_{1},u_{1})\rVert_{X^{s}}+\lVert(\eta_{2},u_{2})\rVert_{X^{s}}).

Thus for any
T,M>0
and
v,v_{1},v_{2}\in B_{M}\subset X^{s}_{T}
hold

\lVert\mathcal{A}(v)-\mathcal{S}(t)v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}\leqslant\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(\eta u,u^{2}/2)\rVert_{X^{s}}\leqslant C_{s}T\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}^{2},

\lVert\mathcal{A}(v_{1})-\mathcal{A}(v_{2})\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}\leqslant C_{s}T\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}(\lVert v_{1}\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}+\lVert v_{2}\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}),

and so taking
M=2\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}}

and
T
as in the formulation of the lemma
we conclude that
\mathcal{A}
is a contraction in
the closed ball
B_{M}
.
The first statement of the lemma follows from the
contraction mapping principle.

We turn our attention to the smoothness of the flow map.
Let
R>0
,
T=(7C_{s}R)^{-1}
and

B=B_{R}(0)
be an open ball in
X^{s}
.
Define
\Lambda:B\times X^{s}_{T}\to X^{s}_{T}
as

\Lambda(v_{0},v)=v-\mathcal{A}(v;v_{0})

that is obviously a smooth map.
Its Fréchet derivative with respect to the second variable
is defined by

d_{v}\Lambda(v_{0},v)h=h+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-t^{\prime})K\partial_{x}\begin{pmatrix}u&\eta\\
0&u\end{pmatrix}h(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

where
v=(\eta,u)^{T}
and
h\in X^{s}_{T}
.
If
v_{1}\in X^{s}_{T}
is the solution of Problem (
2.2
)
corresponding to the initial data
v_{0}\in B
then

\Lambda(v_{0},v_{1})=0
.
Moreover, it satisfies the following estimate

\lVert v_{1}(t)\rVert_{X^{s}}\leqslant\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}}+C_{s}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert v_{1}(t^{\prime})\rVert_{X^{s}}^{2}dt^{\prime}

and so

\int_{0}^{t}\lVert v_{1}(t^{\prime})\rVert_{X^{s}}^{2}dt^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{t\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}}^{2}}{1-C_{s}t\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}}}

for any
t
.
The latter is used to estimate operator

I-d_{v}\Lambda(v_{0},v_{1})
as follows

\lVert h-d_{v}\Lambda(v_{0},v_{1})h\rVert\leqslant C_{s}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert v_{1}(t^{\prime})\rVert_{X^{s}}\lVert h(t^{\prime})\rVert_{X^{s}}dt^{\prime}\\
\leqslant C_{s}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(t\int_{0}^{t}\lVert v_{1}(t^{\prime})\rVert_{X^{s}}^{2}dt^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}\lVert h\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}\leqslant\frac{C_{s}T\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}}}{\sqrt{1-C_{s}T\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{X^{s}}}}\lVert h\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{42}}\lVert h\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}

which is true for any
h\in X^{s}_{T}
.
As a result operator
d_{v}\Lambda(v_{0},v_{1})
is invertible
and so the second assertion of the lemma follows from
the Implicit Function Theorem.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

The solution
v(t)
described in Lemma
2.1

is classical,
that is,

v\in C^{1}\left((0,T);H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R})\right)

and it satisfies (
1.3
).

Proof.

We denote by

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\partial_{x}\\
-\partial_{x}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad f(v)=f(\eta,u)=-K\partial_{x}\begin{pmatrix}\eta u\\
u^{2}/2\end{pmatrix}.

(2.3)

It is enough to demonstrate that

\left\|\frac{v(t+h)-v(t)}{h}-Av(t)-f(v(t))\right\|_{X^{s-1}}\to 0

as
h\to 0
for any
t\in(0,T)
.
Taking into account that
v
satisfies the integral equation (
2.2
),
one can regroup the functions staying under the norm as

\frac{v(t+h)-v(t)}{h}-Av(t)-f(v(t))=\underbrace{\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}(h)-I}{h}-A\right)v(t)}_{=\mathfrak{g}_{1}(h)}+\underbrace{(\mathcal{S}(h)-I)f(v(t))}_{=\mathfrak{g}_{2}(h)}\\
+\mathcal{S}(h)\left(\underbrace{\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\mathcal{S}(t-r)(f(v(r))-f(v(t)))dr}_{=\mathfrak{g}_{3}(h)}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\mathcal{(}S(t-r)-I)f(v(t))dr}_{=\mathfrak{g}_{4}(h)}\right).

The first term
\mathfrak{g}_{1}(h)
has the squared norm

\left\|\mathfrak{g}_{1}(h)\right\|_{X^{s-1}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=\pm 1}\int\left|\widehat{\eta}(\xi)+m\widehat{u}(\xi)\right|^{2}\frac{\left|e^{-mih\xi}-1+mih\xi\right|^{2}}{|h|^{2}}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s-1}d\xi

which tends to zero by the dominated convergence theorem,
due to

\frac{\left|e^{\pm ih\xi}-1\mp ih\xi\right|}{|h|}\to 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\left|e^{\pm ih\xi}-1\mp ih\xi\right|^{2}}{|h|^{2}}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s-1}\leqslant 4\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s},

since

\eta,u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}).

The second term
\mathfrak{g}_{2}(h)
tends to zero
by the continuity of the semigroup
\mathcal{S}
in
X^{s-1}
,
which also follows from the dominated convergence theorem by the similar argument.
Next,

\left\|\mathfrak{g}_{3}(h)\right\|_{X^{s-1}}\leqslant 2C_{s}\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}_{T}}\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\lVert v(r)-v(t)\rVert_{X^{s}}dr\right|\to 0

and

\left\|\mathfrak{g}_{4}(h)\right\|_{X^{s-1}}\leqslant\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\lVert\mathcal{(}S(t-r)-I)f(v(t))\rVert_{X^{s-1}}dr\right|\to 0

by continuity of the integrands.
∎

We turn our attention to the conservation of
\mathcal{H}(v(t))

with the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(v)
given in (
1.5
).
First, we notice that is it well defined for
v\in X^{1/2}
.
Indeed, on the one hand we have an equivalence of the energy norm

\left\|v\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\|K^{-1/2}v\right\|_{L^{2}}

(2.4)

and
X^{1/2}
-norm,
with

\left\|v\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

constituting the quadratic integral in (
1.5
).
On the other hand the cubic integral
in (
1.5
) is bounded as

\left|\int\eta u^{2}\right|\leqslant\left\|\eta\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\leqslant C\left\|\eta\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1/4}}^{2}\leqslant C\left\|v\right\|_{X^{1/2}}^{3}

by the Sobolev embedding
H^{1/4}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\mathbb{R})
.
As a matter of fact,

\mathcal{H}
is continuously differentiable on
X^{1/2}
.
Thus taking into account the Hamiltonian structure (
1.6
)
and the result of Lemma
2.2
,
one can easily verify the energy conservation by the chain rule for
s\geqslant 3/2
.
An extension of the energy conservation to
s\in(1/2,3/2)
is achieved
by the regularization argument,
the most technical part of the proof of the next lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

The energy functional
\mathcal{H}
is continuously differentiable on
X^{1/2}
.
Moreover,

\mathcal{H}(v(t))=\mathcal{H}(v_{0}),\quad t\in[0,T],

where
v,v_{0}
and
T
are described in Lemma
2.1
.

Proof.

Linearisation of
\mathcal{H}
around
v\in X^{1/2}

gives

d\mathcal{H}(v)\delta v=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\eta K^{-1}\delta\eta+uK^{-1}\delta u+\frac{1}{2}\delta\eta u^{2}+\eta u\delta u\right)dx,\quad\delta v=(\delta\eta,\delta u)^{T}\in X^{1/2},

which defines a bounded linear operator
d\mathcal{H}(v)
,
due to

\left|d\mathcal{H}(v)\delta v\right|\leqslant\left\|K^{-1/2}\eta\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|K^{-1/2}\delta\eta\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|K^{-1/2}u\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|K^{-1/2}\delta u\right\|_{L^{2}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\delta\eta\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{4}}^{2}+\left\|\eta\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|\delta u\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left(1+\left\|v\right\|_{X^{1/2}}^{2}\right)\left\|\delta v\right\|_{X^{1/2}},

and so
\mathcal{H}
is differentiable.
Its gradient with respect to the energy inner product, inducing the norm (
2.4
),
has the form

\nabla\mathcal{H}(v)=2K\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta v}=\left(2K\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\eta},2K\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta u}\right)^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}2\eta+K\left(u^{2}\right)\\
2u+2K\left(\eta u\right)\end{pmatrix},

where the variational derivatives are defined in (
1.7
).
Continuity of the derivative
d\mathcal{H}
is equivalent to
the continuity of the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{H}
.
The latter follows from the estimate

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{H}(v_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{H}(v_{2})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant 2\left\|v_{1}-v_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}\\
2\eta_{1}u_{1}-2\eta_{2}u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{L^{2}}\\
\lesssim\left(1+\left\|v_{1}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\left\|v_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\right)\left\|v_{1}-v_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}},

where we have used the boundedness of
K^{1/2}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
Hölder’s inequality

\left\|fg\right\|_{L^{2}}\leqslant\left\|f\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{4}}

and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1/4}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\mathbb{R})
.
This completes the proof of smoothness of
\mathcal{H}
.

In order to prove the energy conservation,
we introduce a regularized version of System (
1.3
) as follows.
For
\mu\geqslant 1
let
P_{\mu}

be the Fourier multiplier with an even smooth symbol
\theta_{\mu}(\xi)=\theta(\xi/\mu)
,
that is, for
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
,

\widehat{P_{\mu}f}(\xi)=\theta\left(\frac{\xi}{\mu}\right)\widehat{f}(\xi).

We assume that
\supp\theta\subset[-2,2]
,
hence
\widehat{P_{\mu}f}
is supported in
[-2\mu,2\mu]
, so
P_{\mu}f(x)
is smooth.
We also assume that
|\theta|\leqslant 1
.
Then
P_{\mu}
is bounded in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
for any
s
, with operator norm
\leqslant 1
.
Finally, we assume that
\theta=1
on
[-1,1]
,
so that
P_{\mu}
converges strongly to the identity operator
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
as
\mu\to\infty
.

Now consider the following frequency-truncated version

\partial_{t}v=AP_{\mu}v+f(v),\quad v(0)=v_{0}

(2.5)

of the Cauchy problem for System (
1.3
)
with the initial data
v_{0}
,
where
A
and
f
are defined in (
2.3
).
As above we can introduce the unitary group

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t)=e^{AP_{\mu}t}

and notice that a solution of (
2.5
)
satisfies the integral equation

v(t)=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-t_{0})v(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-r)f(v(r))dr,\quad 0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant t.

(2.6)

On
X^{1/2}
we define

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v)=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\eta,u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\eta K^{-1}P_{\mu}\eta+uK^{-1}P_{\mu}u+\eta u^{2}\right)dx

that is obviously smooth.
It is straightforward to repeat the above arguments and conclude
that there exists a unique solution

v=v_{\mu}\in C\left([0,T];X^{s}\right)\cap C^{1}\left((0,T);X^{s}\right)

of (
2.5
) for every given
\mu
.
Importantly,
here
s
and
T
are exactly as in the formulation of Lemma
2.1
.
In particular,
T
is independent of
\mu
.
Clearly,

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))

is smooth with respect to
t\in(0,T)
and the chain rule

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))=d\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))\partial_{t}v(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\eta K^{-1}P_{\mu}\partial_{t}\eta+uK^{-1}P_{\mu}\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\partial_{t}\eta+\eta u\partial_{t}u\right)dx

applies.
It is easy to see that (
2.5
) has
the Hamiltonian structure (
1.6
)
with the new functional
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
in place of
\mathcal{H}

and the new variational derivatives

\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta\eta}=K^{-1}P_{\mu}\eta+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta u}=K^{-1}P_{\mu}u+\eta u.

Therefore,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta\eta}\partial_{t}\eta+\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta u}\partial_{t}u\right)dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta\eta}K\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta u}+\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta u}K\partial_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta\eta}\right)dx\\
=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta\eta}K\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}{\delta u}\right)dx=0,

and so
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))
is constant.
By continuity,

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{0})

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
where

v=v_{\mu}
is the solution of the regularized Cauchy problem (
2.5
).

Now let
v
be the solution of the original Cauchy problem described
by Lemma
2.1

and
v_{\mu}
be the solution of (
2.5
).
We will show that
v_{\mu}\to v
in
L^{2}(0,T;X^{s})
as
\mu\to\infty
.
The idea is to split the whole interval
[0,T]
into small sub-intervals
of size
h>0
and to prove this convergence repeatedly on every sub-interval
moving from left to right.
Both
v
and
v_{\mu}
satisfy the integral equation (
2.6
)
with
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
in place of the semigroup, respectively.
Hence

v_{\mu}(t)-v(t)=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-t_{0})v_{\mu}(t_{0})-\mathcal{S}(t-t_{0})v(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-r)f(v_{\mu}(r))-\mathcal{S}(t-r)f(v(r))\right)dr

for
0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}+h\leqslant T

with
h
to be chosen below independently of
\mu
.
We estimate this difference as

\left\|v_{\mu}(t)-v(t)\right\|_{X^{s}}\leqslant\left\|v_{\mu}(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}+\left\|\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-t_{0})-\mathcal{S}(t-t_{0})\right)v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left\|f(v_{\mu}(r))-f(v(r))\right\|_{X^{s}}dr+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left\|\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-r)-\mathcal{S}(t-r)\right)f(v(r))\right\|_{X^{s}}dr.

Squaring this inequality and then integrating the result from
t_{0}
to
t_{0}+h
, one obtains

\frac{1}{16}\left\|v_{\mu}-v\right\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},t_{0}+h;X^{s})}^{2}\leqslant h\left\|v_{\mu}(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\left\|\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-t_{0})-\mathcal{S}(t-t_{0})\right)v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}dt+h^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\left\|f(v_{\mu}(r))-f(v(r))\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}dr\\
+h\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\left\|\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-r)-\mathcal{S}(t-r)\right)f(v(r))\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}drdt.

From the proof of Lemma
2.1
one can easily deduce

\left\|v_{\mu}\right\|_{X_{T}^{s}},\left\|v\right\|_{X_{T}^{s}}\leqslant 3\left\|v_{0}\right\|_{X^{s}},

and so

h^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\left\|f(v_{\mu}(r))-f(v(r))\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}dr\leqslant Ch^{2}\left\|v_{\mu}-v\right\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},t_{0}+h;X^{s})}^{2},

where
C
is independent of
\mu
.
This implies

\frac{1}{20}\left\|v_{\mu}-v\right\|_{L^{2}(t_{0},t_{0}+h;X^{s})}^{2}\leqslant h\left\|v_{\mu}(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathcal{F}\left[\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-t_{0})-\mathcal{S}(t-t_{0})\right)v(t_{0})\right](\xi)\right|^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s}d\xi dt\\
+h\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathcal{F}\left[\left(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-r)-\mathcal{S}(t-r)\right)f(v(r))\right](\xi)\right|^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s}d\xi drdt.

These two integrals tend to zero as
\mu\to\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem,
since

e^{\pm i(t-r)\xi\theta_{\mu}(\xi)}-e^{\pm i(t-r)\xi}\to 0\text{ as }\mu\to\infty\text{ for every }t,r,\xi

and the integrands are bounded by

2\left|\mathcal{F}\left[v(t_{0})\right](\xi)\right|^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s}\quad\text{ and }\quad 2\left|\mathcal{F}\left[f(v(r))\right](\xi)\right|^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)^{s},

respectively.
The first term

\left\|v_{\mu}(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}\to 0

by choosing
t_{0}
properly at least for a subsequence
\mu=\mu_{k}\to\infty
.
Indeed, for the first subinterval
t_{0}=0
and this term is zero.
Therefore,

v_{\mu}\to v

in

L^{2}(0,h;X^{s})

and so a subsequence

\left\|v_{\mu_{k}}-v\right\|_{X^{s}}\to 0

almost everywhere on
[0,h]
.
Next candidate
t_{0}
for left boundary of subinterval is taken in
(h/2,h]
,
so that

\left\|v_{\mu_{k}}(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right\|_{X^{s}}\to 0.

Thus by induction we obtain

\left\|v_{\mu_{k}}-v\right\|_{X^{s}}\to 0

in
L^{2}(0,T)

and almost everywhere on
[0,T]
.

Finally, we can prove the energy conservation
by analyzing the difference

\mathcal{H}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}(v_{0})=\mathcal{H}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{\mu}(t))+\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{0})-\mathcal{H}(v_{0})

(2.7)

for
t\in[0,T]
and with
\mu=\mu_{k}
described above.
Clearly,

\left|\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{0})-\mathcal{H}(v_{0})\right|\leqslant\left\|v_{0}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\left\|(P_{\mu}-I)v_{0}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\to 0.

The first difference in (
2.7
)
is bounded by

\left|\mathcal{H}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{\mu}(t))\right|\leqslant\left|\mathcal{H}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))\right|+\left|\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{\mu}(t))\right|,

where the first term is estimated in the same way

\left|\mathcal{H}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))\right|\lesssim\left\|(P_{\mu}-I)v(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\to 0,

while the last term is estimated by the mean-value theorem as follows.
The modified energy
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
is a continuously differentiable functional,
which one can check by repeating the arguments given
for analysis of
\mathcal{H}
at the beginning of the proof.
Moreover,

\left\|d\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(w)\right\|\leqslant C\left(1+\left\|w\right\|_{X^{1/2}}^{2}\right)\leqslant C\left(1+\left\|w\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}\right)

with the constant
C
independent of
\mu
.
Substituting
w=\lambda v(t)+(1-\lambda)v_{\mu}(t)

with
0<\lambda<1

and recalling that both
v(t)
and
v_{\mu}(t)
are bounded independently of
\mu
,
one obtains

\left|\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(v_{\mu}(t))\right|\leqslant C\left(1+9\left\|v_{0}\right\|_{X^{s}}^{2}\right)\left\|v(t)-v_{\mu}(t)\right\|_{X^{s}}.

The latter converges for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.
Thus, passing to the limit
\mu\to\infty
in (
2.7
),
we obtain

\mathcal{H}(v(t))-\mathcal{H}(v_{0})=0

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.
The proof is concluded
by continuity of

\mathcal{H}(v(t)).

∎

The next step is to extend the local result of these lemmas.

3
Global well-posedness

In this section we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Global existence)
.

Let
s>1/2
.
There exists
\delta>0
such that if

\eta_{0},u_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\eta_{0}\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\delta

then the Cauchy problem for (
1.3
)
with this initial data
has a global-in-time solution

(\eta,u)\in C\left(\mathbb{R};H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\right).

Moreover,
the solution is unique and
depends real analytically on the initial data
(\eta_{0},u_{0})

on any finite time interval
[0,T]
.

Firstly, note that
the continuity argument provides us with boundedness
of
X^{1/2}
-norm of a solution
v=(\eta,u)
.
It is more convenient to work with the energy norm (
2.4
) instead.
Indeed,
there exists
C>0
such that

\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(1-C\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{H}})\leqslant\mathcal{H}(v)\leqslant\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(1+C\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}).

Take
\delta=(4C)^{-1}

and regard a solution with
v_{0}=v(0)

having
\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant\delta
.
By continuity

\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant 2\delta

on some time interval
[0,T_{\delta}]
,
where
T_{\delta}
is the greatest lower bound of admissible
t

with
\lVert v(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}>2\delta
,
and so

\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant\sqrt{2\mathcal{H}(v)}=\sqrt{2\mathcal{H}(v_{0})}\leqslant\sqrt{2(1+C\delta)}\delta<2\delta

which means that
the continuous function

\lVert v(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
cannot reach the level

2\delta
as time evolves.

Lemma 3.2

(Persistence of regularity)
.

Suppose
s>1/2
and a pair

\eta(t)\in H^{s}
,
u(t)\in H^{s}

solves System (
1.3
).
Then the estimate

\frac{d}{dt}\lVert(\eta,u)\rVert_{X^{s}}\lesssim\left(\lVert\eta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)\lVert(\eta,u)\rVert_{X^{s}}

holds true.

Proof.

Calculate the derivative of the elevation norm

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}=-\int\left(J^{s}\eta\right)J^{s}\partial_{x}u-i\int\left(J^{s}\eta\right)J^{s}\tanh D(\eta u)

and the derivative of the velocity norm

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}=-\int\left(J^{s}u\right)J^{s}\partial_{x}\eta-\frac{i}{2}\int\left(J^{s}u\right)J^{s}\tanh Du^{2}.

After summation of these two lines,
the first parts on the right hand side will cancel each other.
To estimate the second parts notice

\lVert J^{s}\tanh D(\eta u)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{s}}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert\eta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}}

and

\left\lVert J^{s}\tanh Du^{2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

We conclude the proof by applying Hölder’s inequality.
∎

We will also need the following
Gronwall type inequality.
For the proof, see
[
16
]
, for example.

Lemma 3.3

(Gronwall inequality)
.

Let
y
be an absolutely continuous non-negative
function defined on some interval
[0,T]
.
Suppose that almost everywhere

y^{\prime}\leqslant y\log y.

Then there exists
C>0
independent of
T

such that

y(t)\leqslant\exp\left(Ce^{t}\right).

Our variables
\eta
,
u
are bounded in

H^{1/2}
-norm at least for small initial data and
so we are able to use
the following limiting case of the Sobolev embedding theorem.

Lemma 3.4

(Brezis-Gallouet-Wainger inequality)
.

Suppose
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
s>n/2
.
Then

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant C_{s,n}\left(1+\lVert f\rVert_{H^{n/2}}\sqrt{\log(2+\lVert f\rVert_{H^{s}})}\right).

(3.1)

Inequality (
3.1
) was first put forward
and proved for a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2

in the work by Brezis, Gallouet

[
5
]
.
It was extended to the other Sobolev spaces in

[
6
]
.
These two inequalities allow us to close
the regularity persistence argument.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Suppose
s>1/2
and

v(t)=(\eta(t),u(t))\in X^{s}
solves
System (
1.3
) on some time interval.
Let its initial data
v(0)
be small
with respect to
X^{1/2}
-norm.
Then
v(t)
stays bounded in
X^{1/2}

by a constant independent of the time interval.
Hence from the Brezis-Gallouet-Wainger limiting embedding
(
3.1
) one deduces

\lVert\eta(t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim 1+\log\left(2+\lVert v(t)\rVert_{X^{s}}\right).

Here we used the elementary estimate
\sqrt{\log z}\leqslant 1+\log z

valid for
z\geqslant 1
.
Now applying Lemma
3.2
we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}}\lesssim\left(1+\log\left(2+\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}}\right)\right)\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}}.

Finally, applying the Gronwall inequality we come to the estimate

\lVert v\rVert_{X^{s}}\leqslant\exp\left(Ce^{t}\right),

where constant
C
depends only on
s
,

\lVert v(0)\rVert_{X^{1/2}}
and

\lVert v(0)\rVert_{X^{s}}
.
This estimate allows one to reapply the
local result and extend the solution on any time interval.
∎

A fundamental question for nonlinear dispersive wave models
is whether they admit solitary-wave solutions.
In the case of the regularized system (
1.3
),
we address this question at a numerical level
and find clear evidence for the existence of solitary waves.
Establishing their existence rigorously would require a dedicated analytical framework
and is not pursued here.
We look for solutions
\eta(x,t)=\eta(x-ct)

and
u(x,t)=u(x-ct)
,
where we keep the same notation

\eta
,
u
for solitary wave profiles.
In our non-dimensional framework
speed
c
coincides with the Froude number.
The regularized shallow water system (
1.3
)
reduces to

\displaystyle c\eta

\displaystyle=u+K(\eta u),

(4.2)

\displaystyle cu

\displaystyle=\eta+Ku^{2}/2.

(4.3)

It can be rewritten as
\mathcal{L}(\eta,u)=\mathcal{N}(\eta,u)
,
where
the linear
\mathcal{L}
and the non-linear
\mathcal{N}

are as follows

\mathcal{L}(\eta,u)=\begin{pmatrix}c&-1\\
-1&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
u\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{N}(\eta,u)=\begin{pmatrix}K(\eta u)\\
Ku^{2}/2\end{pmatrix}.

In this form, the system can be solved numerically
applying the Petviashvili iteration method

[
10
]
.
The iterative scheme is defined via

(\eta_{n+1},u_{n+1})^{T}=S_{n}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\mathcal{N}(\eta_{n},u_{n}))

where
S_{n}
is a stabilisation factor computed by

S_{n}=\frac{\int(\eta_{n},u_{n})\mathcal{L}(\eta_{n},u_{n})dx}{\int(\eta_{n},u_{n})\mathcal{N}(\eta_{n},u_{n})dx}.

Note that matrix
\mathcal{L}

is invertible if and only if

c^{2}>1
.
The computational domain is set to
[-140,140]

with 8192 Fourier modes.
The iterative procedure
is terminated when the difference
between

(\eta_{n+1},u_{n+1})

and

(\eta_{n},u_{n})

in

-norm is less than
1.8\cdot 10^{-13}
.

]

5
Conclusions

We introduced a shallow water model (
1.3
) that has the potential
to substitute the standard system (
1.2
) in practice.
The analytical sections provide rigorous local and small-data global well-posedness results.
This is an improvement on the shallow-water system which can feature shock formation for
data of any size which prevents global well-posedness of smooth solutions
[
19
]
. Even popular regularizations
of the shallow-water system may feature large-time, but not necessarily global existence
[
21
]
.
Our numerical simulations support
the claim that the proposed model can propagate steep profiles without the same visible shock
formation displayed by the standard shallow water system in the reported experiment.
However,
a broader claim of complete shock suppression for all data
can only be formulated as a hypothesis at the current stage,
since it can only be based on an arbitrary-data global dynamics analysis.
We computed numerically solitary waves for (
1.3
).
A rigorous analytical proof of their existence will hopefully be presented elsewhere.
