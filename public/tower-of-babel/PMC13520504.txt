School health services in Saudi Arabia in the absence of school nurses: a national cross-sectional assessment
Authors: Khalid Abdullah Aljohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSaudi Arabia’s Healthy Schools Program operates without dedicated school nursing professionals, instead relying on teachers who serve as health advisors. This study addresses the following question: What is the level of readiness for implementing the healthy school program?MethodsA cross-sectional design was adopted, and 242 public schools were surveyed using the School Health Index adapted for the Saudi context. Data were collected from health advisors (i.e. teachers without healthcare certification) working at primary, secondary, and high schools. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, t-tests, and Spearman correlations.ResultsSignificant deficiencies were identified in physical education and staff health promotion. Specifically, low budget allocations for physical activity and minimal staff wellness initiatives were observed, which are systemic failures. Male students’ schools largely experienced financial disparities, whereas female students’ schools exhibited greater policy implementation and health education. The correlation analysis highlighted a reliance on external workshops rather than sustained clinical care.ConclusionThe absence of school nurses has created a leadership deficit, resulting in fragmented services, inequitable resource distribution, and inadequate emergency and chronic care management. Based on this study’s findings, it is recommended that the Ministry of Education adopt a nurse-led model that aligns with the Saudi Vision 2030 goals and international best practices for school programs.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: School nursing, Nursing leadership, School health services, Saudi Arabia

Background

Following the establishment of the Saudi Ministry of Education (MoE) in 1952, the Saudi Arabian government has mandated education for children starting at 6 years of age. Children spend 12 years navigating their educational journey through primary (6 years), secondary (3 years), and high school (3 years) before moving to higher education or vocational education, both of which are free for Saudi citizens. All educational institutions are segregated by gender [1], creating separate educational institutions for male and female students. Currently, over six million students attend Saudi Arabia’s network of 22,748 public schools, excluding those dedicated to early childhood education [2]. Historically, the MoE was the national governing body of school health and operated through the Saudi school health department. However, this role was transferred to the Ministry of Health (MoH) in 2012 as part of the health sector transformation program, and school health is now partially integrated with primary healthcare services through referrals. The scope and tasks of school health include health education, fitness screening, health promotion, environmental sanitation and occupational health for school staff, nutrition education, first aid provision within the school, and mental health and counselling [3].

The Royal Decree aligns with the World Health Organization (WHO) Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion (1986), which defines health promotion as enabling people to increase control over and improve their health through five key action areas: building healthy public policy, creating supportive environments, strengthening community action, developing personal skills, and reorienting health services [4].

The Saudi school health program includes obesity screenings, dental health checks, and physical examinations for grades 1, 4, 7, and 10, with medical referrals provided by the school healthcare team from the MoH, including nurses, dentists, and general practitioners [5]. Health advisors (teachers with minimal orientation regarding health issues who take on this role with a lower teaching load) work under the MoE as facilitators for healthcare interventions, while the MoH provides preventive interventions in public schools. From a prevalence standpoint, dental cavities, eye refractive errors, and overweight and obesity are the most common health challenges among students [6, 7]. Unhealthy lifestyles, dietary behaviours, smoking, and unsafe behaviours formed during early ages are reported to be significant factors that lead to mortality and morbidity [8]. Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are an increasing public health concern among Saudi youth, mainly because of major risk factors that begin early in life. Recent evidence shows that Saudi adolescents are exposed to obesity, physical inactivity, sedentary behaviour, poor dietary patterns, and early cardiometabolic abnormalities [9, 10]. Almuqbil et al. [9] reported a prevalence of 11.8% for selected NCDs among Saudi adolescents, with obesity being the most common condition. National data also indicate that approximately one-quarter of Saudi adolescents have dyslipidaemia, particularly among boys and those with higher BMI [11]. Furthermore, Bahathig et al. [10] reported that 92.7% of Saudi female adolescents do not meet recommended physical activity levels, highlighting the need for early school-based prevention efforts.

In 1995, WHO joined with partners to launch the Global School Health Initiative to use schools more systematically as a setting to improve children’s health worldwide. The initiative was built on earlier health promotion frameworks and on the growing recognition that the school-age years are critical for forming lifelong health behaviours [12].

Moreover, WHO confirms that successful education systems must promote student, staff, and community health [13]. School nurses play a crucial role in managing health-related challenges. However, school nursing is not well established in Saudi Arabia. The associated roles are scattered and performed by different healthcare providers within the MoH school health department or at healthcare centres in the school catchment area. Health advisors (teachers) perform simple initial healthcare interventions when needed at a school site. They have teaching backgrounds but no healthcare certification and receive basic training to perform their primary roles as health advisors and coordinators. For example, Health advisors do not conduct health workshops for students; instead, they coordinate with the MoH healthcare team to run them. Assigning teachers to additional roles outside their areas of qualification and preparation, with teaching responsibilities leaving insufficient time available for these roles, may increase children’s health risks. School nurses are present in only a few private international schools, and none work in the public school sector. Therefore, school health nursing is in its infancy in Saudi Arabia.

The school nurse role is irreplaceable and crucial for chronic disease management, emergency response, and risk mitigation. School nurses made invaluable efforts during the pandemic, including surveillance, reporting, education, communication with families, and collaboration with healthcare teams [14]. Food allergies pose a significant threat to students, and studies have revealed that nurses’ contributions through action plans, emergency medication administration, and psychological interventions help improve children’s quality of life and academic performance [15]. The leadership role of school nurses extends to that of a change agent, with their work informing public policy to accommodate young people’s health needs [16]. The complexity of this role highlights the need to improve schools’ health capabilities to support new generations of students and attain the 2030 Saudi Vision’s strategic objective of strengthening prevention against health threats [17]. Eliminating multiple healthcare team members, particularly school nurses and their contributions towards school health interventions, will worsen students’ healthcare and cause educational outcomes to decline. Moreover, delegating school nurses’ roles to nonmedical staff, as is the case in the Saudi Arabian public sector, will exacerbate health risks and affect immediate emergency medical interventions, potentially costing students their lives. From a nursing education standpoint, school nursing is part of the community health nursing course curriculum, with designated training requirements for student evaluation.

From a practical standpoint, the application of school nursing varies worldwide because of differences in school health needs across contexts, and the field has expanded over time [18, 19]. An Australian report [20] specified four primary school nursing models (p. 7): ‘School-located nurse-led models where the nurse is an integral part of the school team; school-located models that are led by a nurse external to the school (primary health care nurse); models led by practitioners other than nurses; and school-based health center models where the nurse is one of the center’s health care staff’.

International health literature, including reports from WHO and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), supports school nurses’ leadership roles and contributions towards students’ capacity-building, first aid, and support [18, 21, 22]. However, studies on the Saudi Healthy Schools Program have shown that limitations in support and legislation are significant weaknesses affecting its implementation [23, 24]. School nursing has not yet received adequate scientific attention at the national level, despite concerns raised regarding the current approach [6, 7, 24]. Saudi Arabia’s Healthy Schools Program attempts to implement a complex initiative without the foundational leadership of nursing professionals, making it an anomaly among school health initiatives worldwide. This creates a significant measurable deficiency in care quality and student safety.

This study is the first to quantitatively analyse the consequences of systemic deficiencies in implementing the Healthy Schools Program. Although the program was implemented to promote health, it currently operates without dedicated school nursing professionals. Accordingly, this study addressed the following question: What is the level of readiness for the implementation of healthy school programs? The operationalised definition of readiness in this context is the degree to which schools are prepared to adopt healthy school programs. The outcomes of this study can provide an evidence-based approach for nursing leadership to design and manage effective school health systems and provide a basis for future school health initiatives and policies.

Methods

Design and sample

This study adopted a cross-sectional design with the aim of engaging school health advisors (i.e. teachers without formal healthcare qualifications) in Saudi Arabian public schools. Previous research indicates that the components of the Healthy Schools Program have only been partially implemented across public schools. Furthermore, the reported level of adherence to comprehensive school health policies, coordinated health services, and supportive school environments remains limited, suggesting that only a minority of schools have implemented the program [24]. The sample size was determined based on the expected proportion, confidence level, and perception [25]. With this contextual background and empirical evidence, an implementation proportion of approximately p = 0.20 was considered a realistic expectation. Using this evidence-based assumption, the minimum required sample size at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error was approximately 242 schools. This study assessed public schools; therefore, all public schools were included in the invitations. Kindergarten and special needs education schools were excluded.

Measurements

Study data were collected using an Arabic survey-based questionnaire. The objective was to measure the implementation gap between current school health practices and the level of service provision recommended in internationally recognised school health frameworks that include professional nursing leadership. In this study, readiness was operationalised as the extent to which schools demonstrated the organisational structures, policies, health services, educational activities, and community engagement practices assessed through the eight domains of the adapted School Health Index. Lower scores indicate areas where implementation may be less aligned with recommended school health practices. The research instrument comprised two parts. The first part focused on demographic and general institutional data, including the number of students and staff, educational levels, and gender distribution within the school. The second part assessed school health practices based on eight subscales of the School Health Index (SHI), totalling 39 items [26]. The researcher recognises that the CDC designed the SHI primarily as a self-assessment and improvement tool rather than as a traditional psychometric instrument. Nevertheless, its standardised domains and alignment with the Whole School, Whole Community, Whole Child (WSCC) framework facilitate its use for systematic, population-level assessments. In this study, the SHI was used descriptively to explore the status of Saudi school health, which is consistent with prior peer-reviewed research [27]. Items from the MoH’s Healthy School Program guidelines were also included in the developed instrument to account for the Saudi Arabian context.

Face validity was established using a structured expert review performed by two associate professors in community health nursing with extensive experience in school health programs and public health research. The experts independently evaluated the instrument for item clarity, relevance to the study objectives, cultural appropriateness, and comprehensiveness. Their feedback was systematically incorporated into the instrument, resulting in minor refinements to item wording and phrasing to improve interpretability and contextual alignment with Saudi public school settings.

Pilot testing yielded acceptable psychometric scores. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.982, indicating excellent internal consistency reliability [28]. However, values exceeding 0.95 may also suggest potential item redundancy [29]. A redundancy check was performed during the face validity phase, as described above. The instrument’s subscale scores were as follows: 0.937 for school health policies (6 items); 0.940 for health education (4 items); 0.922 for physical education and other physical activity programs (5 items); 0.895 for nutrition services (4 items); 0.867 for school health services (4 items); 0.920 for school counselling, psychological, and social services (7 items); 0.924 for health promotion for staff (4 items); and 0.917 for family and community involvement (5 items). A 7-point Likert scale was used to minimise mean inflation and provide sufficient response options as follows: (1) strongly disagree, (2) disagree, (3) somewhat disagree, (4) neutral, (5) somewhat agree, (6) agree, and (7) strongly agree [30]. The subscale means were included in the analysis.

The questionnaire was written in Arabic to obtain responses from health advisors who may not speak English. The survey was hosted on the MonkeySurvey platform to facilitate online participation across Saudi Arabia. The MoE approved the study, and its research unit disseminated the invitation to public schools via official email, including an electronic link. Health advisors, which every school has, filled out the questionaire. The researcher had no information regarding the invited schools. Data were collected in September and October 2023.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Education Policy Research Center at the MoE (reference number: 4401274924). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation in the online survey was voluntary, as noted in the first section of the electronic invitation, and informed consent to participate was implied by completing and submitting the questionnaire. No personal or institutional identifiers were collected.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to identify fundamental trends and distributions in the analytical procedures. Prior to conducting parametric analyses, assumptions were assessed. Normality of the composite subscale scores was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values together with visual inspection of histograms. Homogeneity of variance between groups was examined using Levene’s test. The composite subscale means were considered sufficiently robust for parametric testing.

Comparative and relational analyses were conducted using independent t-tests to assess group differences based on the composite subscale means. In addition, Spearman’s rho was used to examine the relationships among variables. Spearman’s rho was selected because of the ordinal nature and/or non-normal distribution of some variables [31]. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0, which enabled data manipulation and interpretation.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate a need to invest in school health interventions and evaluate the current school health infrastructure in Saudi Arabia. Low levels of school health practices and variations among the participating schools were evident. The study findings suggest that the current Healthy Schools Program may not be operating at its full potential in the absence of dedicated school nurses. Involving nurses and expanding school health nursing can maximise the health support available to the next generation of students and ensure efficient health expenditure use.

Strengths in the Saudi public school curriculum

Health literacy is a priority when developing health and physical curricula [32]. Almost 90% of the participants in this study believed that the Life and Family Skills course can compensate for the limitations of the health education activities in the Healthy Schools Program. The course begins in primary school and continues through high school, with incremental coverage of health literacy topics, including personal hygiene, drugs and addiction, mental health, home pharmacy, and first aid. Female high school students receive a special curriculum focused on age-specific topics, including nutrition, cosmetics, breast examination, and maternity [33]. Despite the richness of the Life and Family Skills course, there is no evidence that designated teachers are qualified to teach these topics. Thus, further exploration is recommended.

Current practices

The study findings showed that health education, school health policies and environment, and school health services subscales had the highest scores. However, the presence of written policies and regulations may not translate into the optimal provision of related services. For example, a study on diabetic practices found that only 57% of teachers had sufficient knowledge of diabetes [34]. Similarly, a Jordanian study found that fewer than 25% of schools had asthma first-aid kits despite having asthma policies and regulations [35]. Relatively high policy development scores likely reflect administrative compliance rather than a deep understanding of clinical implications, which requires nursing expertise. This may indicate a need for further qualitative research. Therefore, it is imperative that school health nurses take the clinical lead in implementing school health program policies. This study revealed that staff physical education and health promotion are minimal at the schools investigated, despite this being a main task of school health, as mentioned in the Royal Decree [3]. This may indicate limited availability of health professionals dedicated to designing and implementing wellness programs within the participating schools, a key nursing management function aimed at reducing staff absenteeism and burnout [36]. A benchmark international program, such as the WSCC model, should be adopted in the Saudi context to enhance schools’ health processes and outcomes [37, 38].

Financial disparity

The lowest item score was for the lack of a budget to promote physical activity practices. Budget constraints negatively affect all school health activities that target student and staff health, including chronic disease management. Previous studies have raised similar concerns regarding the effects of budgeting on school health outcomes [23, 39]. However, financial support was higher for boys’ schools across the instrument’s subscales, indicating gender inequality.

Girls’ schools may require time to establish proper infrastructure to support the recent introduction of physical education [40]. This implies that policymakers should set short-term goals to develop the resources required to support physical activity in girls’ schools. Policymakers should build on the strengths of these schools, which have demonstrated higher scores on the subscales for school health policies, health education, nutrition services, and family and community involvement.

Leadership deficit

Teachers serving as health advisors may have limited formal preparation in evidence-based healthcare practice, health resource management, and interprofessional health leadership compared with trained health professionals. Thus, they depend on external health professionals to educate them, promote their own health, and support their health maintenance. The correlations observed in this study revealed a positive relationship between the number of health education workshops conducted by a school during the academic year and that school’s overall healthy program readiness. The observed association between workshop activity and readiness scores may suggest a greater emphasis on health awareness activities than on ongoing clinical health management. The findings support the international practice of moving towards school health nursing to fulfill the needs of students and staff [41].

Blueprint for nursing leadership

Current practice indicates that the Healthy Schools program is run by the MoE and MoH in Saudi Arabia. The roles and periodic assessment of the program’s implementation are not documented in official reports, reflecting a potential deficiency in the monitoring and evaluation of these programs. Furthermore, this study, as well as prior research, highlighted limitations in school health practices. School nurses are absent from official reports and scientific studies in Saudi Arabia, which does not align with evidence-based practices that consider school nurses integral to school health. Therefore, this researcher suggests introducing school health nursing in Saudi Arabia to bridge the gap between the MoH and MoE in their respective roles.

School health nursing should be established as a department at MoE headquarters in Saudi Arabia. The director of school nursing within the MoE would be responsible for health promotion, clinical oversight, and the establishment of professional standards. Models led by integrated school-based nurses could be adapted for operational use [20]. A shift to a centralised, nurse-led budgeting system is also recommended. Under this model, the school nursing director would facilitate budget allocations based on a transparent need-based approach, prioritise student health needs such as chronic diseases, and reward schools with high performance and efficiency, as was the case in girls’ schools in this study. Such a model would maximise resource utilisation, ensure strategic investment towards optimising health outcomes, and transform the budget into a powerful tool for quality improvement and health equity. This practical model is essential for aligning Saudi Arabia’s school health program with international best practices and, most importantly, for ensuring that every student has a fair and equitable opportunity for a healthy future.

Implications for practice

This study provides primary evidence to support the creation and regulation of a school nurse specialty situated under the health promotion domain in Saudi Arabia. This study revealed new insights that can help enhance the Saudi school health program. Nurse involvement in school health nursing is a priority for achieving the Saudi 2030 goals. This study further highlights the need for a new management structure wherein nurses report to nurse leaders. MoE and MoH leaders could use this study as a framework for interministerial collaboration to design and implement a national school nursing program with a primary focus on health and well-being and NCD prevention.

Policymakers and nurse leaders are in the best position to introduce school nursing as a distinctive nursing field that can help achieve healthy school program goals and the Saudi 2030 Vision. Considering the large number of Saudi public school students, investment in school nursing as an evidence-based intervention to support future generations is crucial. This study opens a new field of inquiry for nursing researchers in Saudi Arabia and provides direction for evaluating the impact of school nurses on student health outcomes, absenteeism, and academic performance. Most respondents supported the Life and Family Skills course in meeting needs. Additional research could explore these quantitative answers to yield qualitative data that could enrich the school health context. The call to improve school health is not restricted to nurses and should include healthcare professionals such as nutritionists and physiotherapists.

Strengths and limitations

The use of a convenience sample and an online self-reporting tool limits the generalisability of this study. A more important limitation is that invitations were centrally distributed by the MoE, and the researcher did not have access to the total denominator of invited schools. Consequently, the response rate could not be calculated, limiting the assessment of response bias and national representativeness. This should be considered when interpreting generalisability. Another limitation is the use of multiple independent t-tests across several domains and grouping variables. Although assumptions were assessed and effect sizes were reported, repeated testing may increase the probability of Type I errors. Therefore, statistically significant findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Despite these limitations, this study highlighted potential avenues for enhancing school health services and the vital role of school nurses in advancing school health initiatives in Saudi Arabia. The Arabic research instrument developed in this study exhibits acceptable psychometric properties and may be used in future research to track the progress of the Healthy Schools Program.

Conclusion

This study identified patterns that may indicate leadership and implementation challenges within Saudi Arabia’s Healthy Schools Program. The quantitative findings reveal systemic deficiencies, particularly in physical education and staff health promotion, compounded by inequitable budget allocation. The reliance on non-medical health advisors may lead to a greater emphasis on health awareness activities than on sustained clinical health management, potentially limiting the program’s ability to fully achieve its intended objectives. Strategic directions to address these failures may include establishing a dedicated nurse-led school health framework. Integrating a professional nursing department into the MoE would allow for clinical oversight, strategic management, and the evidence-based practices required to transform the program. Anchoring Saudi school health reforms provides a globally recognised framework for health-promoting schools and supports alignment with international standards and the broader preventive, equity-oriented public health agenda that underpins contemporary strategies such as the Sustainable Development Goals and Saudi Vision 2030’s emphasis on prevention and community engagement.
