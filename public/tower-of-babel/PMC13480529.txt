Sustainable Pd‐Catalyzed Aminations “on Dirty Water”
Authors: Erfan Oftadeh, Marco Ortiz, Kylee Dismuke Rodriguez, Jonathan Yang, Bruce H. Lipshutz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA new approach to Pd‐catalyzed C─N bond formation is disclosed based on observations from Nature, where its use of an “on water” phenomenon allows for variations in its pH and content. By employing highly basic conditions generated from addition of a certain amount of KOH (i.e., the “dirt”) to the water, aminations take place quickly. This “on dirty water” approach also provides the needed base, further simplifying reactions. The viscosity of the KOH/water (i.e., 30% KOH) presumably prevents dissolution of the organic coupling partners; hence, base‐sensitive groups (e.g., esters, nitriles, etc.) are readily accommodated, thereby broadening the scope of this process. Recycling of this highly basic medium is also illustrated, resulting in a very low complete E‐Factor. Several comparisons with recent literature routes are made using not only Pd catalysis, but also with aminations based on earth‐abundant metals such as Ni and Cu, which tend to be carried out in various organic solvents. Water‐based sequences are also shown, including chemoenzymatic catalysis. Prospects for extending this discovery to several other types of valuable bond formations in synthetic chemistry are also presented,

Introduction

As best that can be determined, Earth is ca. 4.55 billion years old [1]. Throughout geological time, the water that makes up roughly 75% of the surface has been anything but “pristine”, meaning that Nature has been developing chemistry in “impure” water. By modern standards that reflect a mere 250+ years or so, however, reactions have been developed mainly in organic solvents; in fact, most practitioners view water as the “enemy” to be avoided [2]. When used, however, in either an “on water” [3, 4, 5] or “in water” [6] sense, purified (usually “HPLC”, or pristine) water is involved, which reflects neither current nor evolutionary times. Given that Nature has accomplished so much over time using what today might be referred to as “dirty” water, it is reasonable to assume that there must be as yet unappreciated benefits to be gleaned for synthetic purposes. Perhaps, therefore, it's well past the time to ask: What's in the water? Hints abound, since under aqueous conditions, variables, such as, for example, pH, represent factors that may be important [7], but traditionally are irrelevant when doing synthetic chemistry as performed today under non‐aqueous conditions.

There exist many examples throughout Nature of “pockets” that offer both low pH exposure (such as several of the “hot springs” at Yellowstone National Park which contain acidic water based on H2S that is oxidized to various acids, such as limited amounts of H2SO4), or high pH (such as “Old Faithful” located at the same site) [8, 9]. In general, while there are many important advances in academia made over time in this area of “on water”, as summarized by Kobayashi [10], few would resort to this approach using water given the known inconsistencies [11] and challenges faced in terms of outcome, especially associated with chemistry at scale needed by the fine chemical industry [12]. Nonetheless, such an “on dirty water” situation, if standardized as well as made fully reproducible and applied to otherwise important processes, would present not only a synthetic advance (i.e., with potential for reductions in reaction times, lower costs, etc.), but also new opportunities in sustainability (including solventless or high concentration reactions, along with a recyclable aqueous medium). In this contribution, therefore, results are presented involving Pd‐catalyzed aminations (i.e., C─N bond formations) documenting how, following Nature's lead, an “on dirty water” approach can be used to synthetic advantage, citing the words of the late Roger Sheldon [13, 14] who insightfully stated: “The best solvent is no solvent, and if a solvent (diluent) is needed then water is preferred.”

Such a synthetic strategy might lead to efficient processes, shorter coupling reaction times, meaningful options for telescoping and/or opportunities for reaction sequences, to name a few potential advantages. But, from the environmental perspective, a new process along these lines would lead to an alternative pathway amounting to a net reduction in consumption of planetary resources, such as petroleum reserves (vide infra) that account for large amounts of organic solvent [15, 16], as well as levels of platinum group metal (PGM) usage as catalysts, including, most notably, Pd. The implications represent a “win‐win” situation, where resources are minimized, potentially lowering costs, as well as dramatically improving the impact of an important process (i.e., aminations) on the environment (Scheme 1).

Results and Discussion

The potential of an “on dirty water” phenomenon first focused on Pd‐catalyzed C─N bond formations which take place in the presence of base, such as aqueous KOH [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. In time, one of Nature's “secrets” was found where an aqueous medium containing specifically 30% KOH (vide infra), in fact, leads to a “Goldilocks” phenomenon. In all cases, a loading of only 0.5 mol% of a Pd catalyst was used along with the reaction partners (the aryl halide and aryl amine). Further screening of the amounts of catalyst indicated that catalyst loadings as low as 0.25 mol% are oftentimes sufficient. Importantly, and unexpectedly, replacement of KOH by NaOH was also found to be less effective, although these results, as with the amount of KOH, appeared to vary according to the substrate (vide infra). In most cases, only a 1:1 ratio of amine to aryl/heteroaryl halide is needed. The explanation behind the preference for potassium over sodium must reflect their relative sizes, especially when hydrated, and charge dispersity, since both have the same counterion. These noteworthy details, however, must await further experimentation as we learn about how to use and apply this “on dirty water” phenomenon.

O2 Solubility and High Conductivity, as Well as Density of 30% KOH in Water

Beyond its role as an inorganic base, concentrated aqueous KOH exhibits a set of unusual physicochemical properties that are potentially advantageous for Pd‐catalyzed C─N cross‐coupling reactions. At increased levels (≈25–30 wt%), the KOH present dramatically increases both the ionic strength and conductivity of water [26]. Additionally, the solution is denser [27], boiling above the boiling point of water (≈110–112°C) [28], and contains a substantially reduced concentration of dissolved oxygen [29, 30, 31] (Figure 1). In addition, these high concentrations of KOH also increase the surface tension of water [32, 33], and together with its high ionic strength give rise to a salting‐out effect which promotes hydrophobic aggregation at the organic‐aqueous interface [34].

Some of the physiochemical properties associated with 30 wt% aqueous KOH solution.

The effects of the KOH concentration in water on the extent of conversion were then studied. For this purpose, the reaction between 2‐(diethylamino)ethyl 4‐aminobenzoate and two different aryl bromides, including 1‐bromo‐4‐isopropylbenzene and 2‐bromo‐6‐methoxynaphthalene, were chosen as model substrates (Table 1). The choice of palladium allyl dimer as catalyst precursor and t‐BuXPhos as ligand was adopted from our previously published work on aminations [35, 36]. Initial results showed that although a range of 20 and 40 wt% KOH (4.2–9.3 M) gave excellent yields of products 1 and 2 (entries 2–4 and 7–9), 30 wt% KOH, in particular, consistently afforded the product in quantitative yields (entries 3 and 8). For both substrates, using 50 wt% KOH led to a decrease in yields, likely due to distinct phase separation between the aqueous and organic layers (entries 5 and 10). A potential reason for the observed yield enhancements using 30 wt% KOH may arise from the combined absence of oxygen, as well as hydrophobic aggregation driven by the salting‐out effect of the concentrated KOH solution (vide infra) [7, 37, 38, 39]. Screening of other bases showed that 30% KOH was the most efficient base/reaction medium, providing the highest yields with minimal‐to‐no competing hydrolysis (Scheme S3, entry 5, and Scheme S4, entry 1). Attempts to mimic the highly saturated and strong ionic strength of 30 wt% aqueous KOH by replacing it with other salts gave inferior results (Scheme S4, entries 2–4). While varying amounts of KOH (e.g., 20%) can occasionally afford similar results as those realized using 30 wt% KOH, this latter amount (30 wt%) consistently provided higher yields, as well as lower Pd loadings (Table 1, Scheme S4, entries 5–10, and Scheme S5). Screening reaction times also showed that these reactions can reach completion in as little as 30 min, or less (Scheme S6).

Results indicated that functional groups such as nitro groups (3‐5, 10), carbamate (6), and amide (7) can be well tolerated and gave the corresponding products in excellent yields. Secondary and sterically hindered primary amines that are generally known to be weak nucleophiles afforded products in good to excellent yields (8‐9 and 11). Heteroaromatics, like quinoline (12), benzofuran (14), pyridine (15), and pyrimidine (16), as well as potentially labile α‐protons in acetophenone derivative (13) are compatible participants with the reaction medium. Incorporating other types of nucleophiles, such as (diphenylmethylene)hydrazine and diphenylmethanimine as surrogates for hydrazine and ammonia, respectively, lead to the corresponding products in excellent yields (17‐18). Switching the aryl halide to chloro‐ and iodo‐ groups resulted in no decline in product yield (19‐20). Moreover, and especially important are the observations that exposing base‐sensitive functional groups, such as an aldehyde (20), nitrile (21), ester (22), and a common silyl protecting group (23) to a 30% KOH solution not only led to no major loss of material, the targeted products were obtained in high yields indicative of the rapidity of coupling and the overall robustness of the process.

Attempts to use a diarylamine (e.g., diphenylamine) to arrive at a tertiary system under identical conditions associated with the examples shown in Table 2 produced little product, suggesting that this side reaction is not to be expected (Scheme S7). However, by increasing the reaction temperature to 90°C and running the reaction for 11 h, some of the expected triarylamine began to form, indicative of opportunities that might exist along these lines, especially if the ratio of amine to aryl halide is increased beyond the 1:1.25 ratio being used.

With 30 wt% KOH established as an effective “on dirty water” medium, other parameters such as temperature, reaction time, and catalyst loading were adjusted as needed for individual substrates. Reactions were typically conducted at temperatures between 45–80° C using 0.5 mol% of commercially available [Pd(allyl)Cl]2/t‐BuXPhos under an argon atmosphere, with both taking place in an hour or less. Cases involving late‐stage aminations (Table 3, 24–33) document the functional group tolerance to be expected, although with some limitations (vide infra).

Considering the high levels of KOH present, with 30% being equated to ca. 15 equivalents relative to either coupling partner (since the ratio is usually 1:1; vide supra), base‐sensitive functional groups present in either or both reaction partners represent an important potential limitation, perhaps even a seemingly insurmountable issue. What need be recalled, however, is that at 30% KOH/H2O, the solubility of reaction partners as well as catalyst in the basic aqueous medium is practically zero and hence, any functionality present is exposed to base localized only at the interface. Thus, although counterintuitive at first, base‐sensitive groups, including esters (even a methyl ester; Scheme 2, product 34), and other moieties (e.g., esters 35 and 36, as well as nitrile 21 in Table 2) are amenable to these conditions. Once the amination is complete, usually within minutes, the product is then extracted using a minimum amount of a single recyclable organic solvent (e.g., EtOAc, MTBE, etc.), while the “dirty water” remains in the reaction vessel and can be recycled several times (Scheme 3).

Recycling of the Aqueous Medium

Recyclability of the 30 wt% KOH medium was evaluated using two situations in terms of coupling partners (Scheme 3). In Series 1 the same educts were used for each cycle; The product 37 was removed by filtration (Figure S2) or extraction using minimal amounts of a volatile organic solvent (CPME in this case) leaving the aqueous KOH solution behind. The flask was then re‐charged with fresh catalyst, reaction partners, and solid KOH (7 equiv) to restore the dirty water to its 30 wt% ionic strength. Results indicated that the reaction medium can be reused for at least three cycles without a significant drop in product yield. As for Series 2, the 30 wt% KOH was also reused for three cycles, albeit with different aryl bromide/amine pairs in each run. This test also led to excellent yields on average for all three cycles giving products 37–39.

Residual Pd Content

Importantly, the levels of residual palladium observed in products derived from these aminations, as typically seen with reactions run in aqueous micellar media [40, 41, 42, 43], are below the FDA‐allowed 10 ppm/dose/day [44]. As illustrated in Figure 2, three representatives yet highly nitrogen‐rich amines were tested for Pd content, and the results are in line with expectations. Crucial was the observation that final processing of each included a simple filtration through Celite prior to analysis. This minor additional handling led to levels of Pd, as determined by ICP‐MS, which were quite low. Without filtration through Celite the amount of residual Pd was higher and while levels, such as these are still reasonable, they are outside the acceptable window for drug substances [45].

E‐Factor Calculations

In terms of metrics associated with this process, a complete E‐Factor (cEF) was determined for the amination shown in Scheme 4 [14]. Initially, with all components (including co‐solvent and “dirty water”), the cEF was calculated to be 12.7 (Table S2). When the aqueous medium (and hence, the base as well) was recycled only once, the new cEF drops dramatically to only 1.4. This comes as no surprise, given that these aminations use very little, if any (recyclable) organic solvent, and take place on recyclable (dirty) water. Moreover, reaction workup (on a small, academic scale) involves extraction with a minimum amount of a single organic solvent (e.g., EtOAc), which is also recyclable. When performed at scale, however, solid products are simply filtered and the aqueous medium is ready for reuse (Figure S2).

Comparison Studies

Several direct comparisons were conducted with known processes for aminations that illustrate the advances, from both the practical as well as environmental perspectives. Examples were taken from the literature, with most being quite recent, involving metal catalysts containing Pd, Ni, and Cu (Table 4, 37–43). The results indicate that, not only is this technology favored from the standpoint of environmental considerations (e.g., choice of reaction medium and amounts of metal required for catalysis), but also documents the synthetic benefits (e.g., yields and reaction times) of using an “on dirty water” approach that can be challenging to match.

Reactions in Seawater

The option to even use ocean water, which has undergone a simple filtration, and as tested previously in other processes [7, 46], rather than purified (usually HPLC‐grade) water to which is added KOH is yet another positive feature of this technology. As shown in Scheme 5 (compounds 15 and 37), virtually identical results are obtained, indicative of the finding that (a) the process can be carried out with impurities even in seawater, and that (b) the presence of additional salts do not impact aminations run “on dirty water.”

Multi‐Step Sequences

Chemoenzymatic Catalysis

Opportunities also exist to use an “on dirty water” approach for sequencing reactions, even though the highly basic medium may at first seem limiting in this regard. This is apparent especially when carrying out chemoenzymatic catalysis, where the selected enzyme may be pH sensitive. Nonetheless, use of “on dirty water” allows for facile replacement of this water without changing the reaction vessel. As seen in Scheme 6, following amination in 30% KOH/H2O, addition of small amounts of EtOAc allowed for an in‐flask product extraction (i.e., via gentle stirring), and then draining of the lower layer containing the KOH/water (in this case, via syringe). Subsequent removal (and potential recovery) of the EtOAc in vacuo, followed by addition of an aqueous micellar medium (such as 2 wt% TPGS‐750‐M [45]) afforded conditions amenable to introduction, for example, of an enereductase, which led to the corresponding nonracemic ketone. Again, without isolation, addition of a ketoreductase gave the final nonracemic product (44) as a single diastereomer, which after purification afforded an overall yield of 91% for this 1‐pot chemoenzymatic sequence.

1‐Pot, 3‐step chemoenzymatic sequence.

Chemocatalyses

Application to a multi‐step sequence, in this case using solely chemocatalysis was also carried out en route to product 47 (Scheme 7). An initial Pd‐catalyzed C─N coupling (Step 1) between 1‐bromo‐3‐nitrobenzene A1 and 2‐(diethylamino)ethyl 4‐aminobenzoate A2 in 30 wt% KOH furnished the corresponding intermediate 45 in quantitative yield. After extraction, 45 was subjected to catalytic hydrogenation (Step 2) using the residual Pd from the previous reaction along with added 5 wt% charcoal (relative to the starting nitroarene) to reduce the nitroarene, providing the corresponding diarylamine intermediate 46 in quantitative yield. In the final step (i.e., Step 4), amide bond formation between 46 and acid chloride A4, as its HCl salt, prepared separately from the benzoic acid A3 (using oxalyl chloride and catalytic DMF) afforded 47 in 87% isolated yield over four steps.

Multistep chemocatalytic sequence to product 47.

Extension to Aliphatic Amines: Preliminary Studies

The potential to extend aminations to include aliphatic amines was then briefly investigated (Scheme 8). The corresponding coupling products 48 and 49 were obtained in good‐to‐excellent yields when 30 wt% KOH was used (entries 1 and 4). By contrast, when the medium and base were replaced with either potassium t‐butoxide or a stoichiometric amount of KOH, each in degassed water, a significant drop in yields was observed (entries 2–3 and 5–6). These results are consistent with the likelihood that aliphatic amines, which usually have higher solubility in water than anilines, benefited from the strong ionic strength and salting‐out characteristic of 30 wt% aqueous KOH. Accordingly, the effective concentration of the amine at the organic/aqueous interface is likely maintained at a level that supports efficient C–N coupling.

Conclusions

In summary, an unprecedented technology for Pd‐catalyzed aminations has been developed, following, in an evolutionary sense, Nature's secret based on “dirty” water containing a specific amount (30 wt%) of a specific base (KOH). It is now possible to make valued C─N bonds “on dirty water” which (a) typically can be constructed in the absence of an organic solvent, or at most, where small amounts of the solvent serves to assist with highly crystalline substrates; (b) Can be made quickly, usually in minutes, rather than the traditionally required hours when using organic solvents [20, 22, 23, 47, 48]; (c) Utilize low levels of a palladium catalyst; hence, a significant potential reduction in costs while extending access to this precious metal well into the future; (d) Can be employed in a variety of sequences, including those involving both chemo‐ and bio‐catalysis; and (e) Use a recyclable aqueous medium leading to highly competitive metrics as a measure of minimal environmental impact, thereby making this new chemistry especially attractive from the standpoint of sustainability. Future reports using transition metal catalysis (e.g., an extensive survey of Suzuki‐Miyaura couplings, primary amine synthesis, etc.) as well other types of valued reactions (e.g., SNAr reactions) amenable to use “on dirty water” will be reported in due course. Perhaps yet another of Nature's secrets has been uncovered, which may provide not only a general approach to organic synthesis, but one which at the same time is sustainable.
