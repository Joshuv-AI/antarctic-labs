Incidence of Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction after syphilis treatment and the role of awareness effect
Authors: Yoshihiro Ono, Yoshiyuki Miyazawa, Seiji Arai, Yoshitaka Sekine
Published: 2026

DEAR EDITOR,

We read with great interest the article by Tsai et al, “Increased serum APOC3 in patients with syphilis can predict the absence of Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction after benzathine penicillin treatment.”1 The mechanisms underlying the Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction (JHR) after syphilis therapy remain unclear, and it is not yet known why some patients develop JHR while others do not. Although usually transient and self-limiting, JHR can be of particular concern in pregnant women because of possible adverse fetal effects. Further research on this phenomenon remains important.

The reported incidence of JHR ranges from 8% to 56%, with higher rates in women.2 In Japan, syphilis cases have steadily increased since 2010, and cases among pregnant women are also rising.3 In our clinic, direct microscopic detection of Treponema pallidum allows rapid diagnosis and immediate treatment.4 We have encountered patients who misinterpreted JHR during oral penicillin therapy as a drug allergy and discontinued treatment. After re-explanation, they were able to resume and complete therapy.

Although we had previously provided only brief information about JHR, since April 2025 we have reinforced this by offering structured pretreatment explanations that included: (i) potential symptoms (fever, chills, malaise, myalgia, arthralgia, headache, rash, lesion exacerbation), (ii) onset within 3 to 24 hours, (iii) resolution within 12 to 24 hours, (iv) usefulness of antipyretic/analgesic medication, (v) periodic temperature monitoring, (vi) documentation of symptoms, and (vii) seeking emergency care if anaphylaxis is suspected.

In the year before this reinforced explanation, JHR occurred in 19.2% of early syphilis patients receiving oral penicillin (5/26 male patients). After implementation, the incidence increased to 85.7% (6/7 patients; six men and one woman). All six cases developed fever ≥37.5°C, with malaise in one and headache in another. Because febrile reactions were consistent, we believe this marked increase was unlikely due solely to a nocebo effect.5 Instead, detailed explanations appear to enhance recognition and reporting of symptoms, representing an “awareness effect” that may have led to underestimation of JHR in previous studies.

The sharp difference before and after our intervention makes a randomized study ethically difficult. Nonetheless, further accumulation of real-world data across institutions may refine the understanding of the true incidence of JHR in clinical practice.
