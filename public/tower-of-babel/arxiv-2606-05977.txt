Direct reconstruction for acoustic inverse Born scattering
Nuutti Hyvönen, Lisa Schätzle

Abstract
We consider the inverse medium scattering problem for the Helmholtz equation in two dimensions, i.e., the task to recover a compactly supported penetrable two-dimensional scatterer from full knowledge of the associated far field data or, equivalently, the far field operator. Although this problem is uniquely solvable, it is severely ill-posed and nonlinear. In the regime of weak scattering, the Born approximation yields a linearized relation between the contrast and the far field data, thus overcoming the second difficulty. This linear setting allows to build on recent work on linearized electrical impedance tomography, which relies on triangular Zernike decompositions, to derive an explicit reconstruction formula that expresses the expansion coefficients of the contrast in terms of those of the far field data. By choosing the expansion functions appropriately, the resulting system matrix decouples into separate (infinite) triangular systems for the spatial angular frequencies in the contrast. Consequently, each of these systems can be solved independently by performing forward substitutions. Our numerical experiments indicate that this approach, combined with an adequate regularization method, remains effective even when applied to full nonlinear far field data beyond the Born regime.

Introduction

In inverse scattering, one seeks for an unknown material parameter, described by a contrast function, from associated scattered near or far field data, with applications in, e.g., seismology, medical imaging, radar technology, geophysical exploration and nondestructive testing.
Comprehensive surveys of this active research area are provided in
[
6
,
9
]
.
In this article, we focus on the inverse medium scattering problem for time-harmonic acoustic waves, a setting governed by the Helmholtz equation. We assume to observe far field data for plane wave incident fields along all possible illumination directions at a fixed frequency and present in two dimensions a direct, fast reconstruction algorithm in the framework of the Born approximation, following the ideas in
[
2
,
13
]
for electrical impedance tomography (EIT).

It is well-known that full knowledge of the far field data uniquely determines the contrast.
However, inverse scattering is ill-posed as small perturbations in the observed data may result in huge errors in the reconstructed contrast.
Moreover, the data depend nonlinearly on the contrast due to multiple scattering effects.

There are two classes of reconstruction algorithms for this problem, either aiming to reconstruct (1) the entire contrast function or (2) the shape or boundary of its compact support.
Methods focusing on solving problem (2) are referred to as qualitative methods.
Prominent examples include the Linear Sampling Method, the Factorization Method, and monotonicity-based techniques (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
7
,
16
,
23
,
22
]
).
Quantitative methods addressing problem (1) typically either solve a computationally expensive regularized optimization problem or seek to invert a linearized model in the weak scattering regime, with the hope that the resulting inversion procedure remains effective even when the measured data contain nonlinear
effects (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
21
,
30
]
).
We particularly emphasize the recent low-rank method
[
30
]
in the latter category as it is conceptually close to the approach proposed in this paper.

We assume that the contrast is supported in an
a priori
known
{\bm{c}}
-centered ball
B_{R}({\bm{c}})
of radius
R
.
By interpreting the available far field data in the standard way as the kernel of the far field operator on
{L^{2}}({S^{1}})
,
the linearized forward map can be presented as a linear operator from

{L^{2}}(B_{R}({\bm{c}}))
to the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators
\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))
, which allows its parametrization as an infinite matrix after introducing bases for these separable Hilbert spaces.
In particular, expanding the angular dependence of the unknown contrast and the observed Born far field data in Fourier bases reveals a decoupling of the system in the angular direction: a certain angular frequency in the contrast only affects a corresponding diagonal in the Born far field operator expanded with respect to a (modulated) Fourier basis.
This observation motivates the application of concepts in numerical linear algebra to solve each of the decoupled infinite linear systems that connect the radial behavior of the contrast for a certain angular frequency to a diagonal in the data matrix. Our proposed algorithm essentially applies a QR factorization in an offline stage to the angular subsystems, allowing one to only solve (small) triangular systems when the data become available.

Another key component is the intrinsic low-rank structure of the measured far field data caused by the super-exponential decay of Bessel functions with respect to their order, providing a natural justification for truncating the infinite-dimensional systems while essentially maintaining the retrievable information. This is particularly important for our algorithm since the QR factorization step applies a Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization in an appropriately weighted
L^{2}
topology on a finite interval to certain products of Bessel functions, a process that is guaranteed to become unstable if run too long due to the aforementioned super-exponential decay.
The sparsity of far field data has been previously investigated for related inverse problems in
[
17
,
18
]
, and foundational results on this research direction can be found in
[
15
,
19
,
20
]
.

The low-rank structure of the inverse medium scattering problem has also been analyzed from a different perspective in
[
26
,
30
]
.
In these works, the far field data are expanded in terms of eigenfunctions of a restricted Fourier integral operator, known as the generalized prolate spheroidal wave functions.

The remainder of this article structures as follows.
In Section
2
,
we provide theoretical background on acoustic inhomogeneous medium scattering as well as on its linearization given by the Born approximation.
Section
3
then derives a representation for the linearized system, decoupling it into triangular systems along the radial direction over the angular frequencies in the contrast.
Finally, in Section
4
, the essential support of the expansion coefficients of the far field data is utilized to justify a truncation-based regularization for inverting the infinite-dimensional triangular systems.
At this stage, we also discuss numerical instability in the construction of the radial basis functions.
Numerical results are presented in Section
5
, where we test our method using (noisy) linearized Born and nonlinear full far field data and benchmark it against the low-rank method
[
30
]
and the MATLAB’s built-in nonuniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT). We close the article with some conclusions.

2
Inhomogeneous medium scattering

We consider scattering of time-harmonic acoustic waves by a compactly supported penetrable object lying in a homogeneous background medium.
Let
\kappa>0
denote the wave number and assume the incident field to be a plane wave

u^{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{d}})\,:=\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\kappa{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{d}}}\,,\qquad{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,,

(2.1a)

that propagates along an illumination direction
{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}:=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,:\,|{\bm{x}}|=1\}
.
In the following, all dependencies on
{\bm{d}}
are marked by a second argument.
The incident field
u^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
hits and interacts with a compactly supported penetrable scatterer
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, modeled by the exterior support of a real-valued contrast function
q\in{L^{\infty}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
satisfying
q>-1
a.e. on
\Omega
and
q=0
a.e. on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
.
This interaction generates a total field
u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\in H^{1}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
that solves

\Delta u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})+\kappa^{2}(1+q)u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\,=\,0\qquad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,,

(2.1b)

such that the scattered field
u^{s}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}}):=u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})-u^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition

\lim_{r\to\infty}\sqrt{r}\Bigl(\frac{\partial u^{s}}{\partial r}({\bm{x}},{\bm{d}})-\mathrm{i}\kappa u^{s}({\bm{x}},{\bm{d}})\Bigr)\,=\,0\,,\qquad r=|{\bm{x}}|\rightarrow\infty\,,

(2.1c)

uniformly with respect to the direction
\widehat{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{x}}/|{\bm{x}}|\in{S^{1}}
.

It is known that the unique weak solution
u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\in H^{1}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2}})
of (
2.1
) (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Thm. 7.13]
) satisfies the Lippmann–Schwinger integral equation

u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\,=\,u^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})+\kappa^{2}\int_{\Omega}q({\bm{y}})u({\bm{y}},{\bm{d}})\Phi(\,\cdot\,-{\bm{y}})\,\dif{\bm{y}}\,=:\,u^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})+L_{q}u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\qquad\text{in }\Omega\,,

(2.2)

with
L_{q}:{L^{2}}(\Omega)\rightarrow{L^{2}}(\Omega)
and
\Phi({\bm{x}}):=\mathrm{i}/4\,H^{(1)}_{0}(\kappa|{\bm{x}}|)
,
{\bm{x}}\not={\bm{0}}
, denoting
the fundamental solution to the Helmholtz equation in free space at wave number
\kappa
(see, e.g.,
[
25
, Thm. 7.12]
).
The function
H^{(1)}_{0}
is the Hankel function of the first kind and order zero. Due to the asymptotic behavior of
H^{(1)}_{0}
for a large argument, the scattered field
u^{s}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
fulfills the asymptotic far field expansion

u^{s}({\bm{x}},{\bm{d}})\,=\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\kappa r}}{\sqrt{\kappa r}}u^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})+O\bigl(r^{-\frac{3}{2}}\bigr)\,,\qquad r=|{\bm{x}}|\to\infty\,,

(2.3)

uniformly with respect to the observation direction
\widehat{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{x}}/|{\bm{x}}|\in{S^{1}}
. Here, the far field pattern
{u^{\infty}\in{L^{2}}({S^{1}}\times{S^{1}})}
is given by

u^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})\,=\,\kappa^{2}\int_{\Omega}q({\bm{y}})u({\bm{y}},{\bm{d}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa\widehat{{\bm{x}}}\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\dif{\bm{y}}\,,\qquad\widehat{{\bm{x}}}\in{S^{1}}\,,

(2.4)

(see, e.g.,
[
25
, Thm. 7.15]
), and it determines
the associated far field operator

F:{L^{2}}({S^{1}})\rightarrow{L^{2}}({S^{1}})\,,\quad\bigl(Fg\bigr)(\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\,:=\,\int_{{S^{1}}}u^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})g({\bm{d}})\,\dif s({\bm{d}})\,,

(2.5)

which maps superpositions of plane wave incident fields to the far field patterns of the associated scattered fields.
This operator is well-known to be compact, normal and of trace class (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
). In particular, it is Hilbert–Schmidt on
{L^{2}}({S^{1}})
, i.e.,
F\in\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))
, and it can thus be viewed as an infinite-dimensional matrix after fixing an orthonormal system for
{L^{2}}({S^{1}})
.

In this work, we are interested in the inverse medium scattering problem of recovering the contrast
q
from the knowledge of the associated far field operator
F
, or equivalently, from the knowledge of the far field data
u^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})
for all
\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}
.
This broadly studied problem is known to be uniquely solvable (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Thm. 7.28]
) but severely ill-posed and nonlinear as multiple scattering effects have to be taken into account.

2.1
Linearization by considering the Born approximation

If
L_{q}
from (
2.2
) satisfies
\|L_{q}\|_{\mathscr{L}({L^{2}}(\Omega))}\ll 1

the total field
u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
can be accurately approximated by its Born approximation
u_{B}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}}):=(I+L_{q})u^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
for all
{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}
.
This follows by only accounting for the first two terms in the Neumann series of
(I-L_{q})^{-1}
when viewing the Lippmann–Schwinger equation (
2.2
) as a fixed-point equation for
u(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
.
The field
u_{B}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
solves a source problem for the Helmholtz equation,

\Delta u_{B}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})+\kappa^{2}u_{B}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\,=\,-\kappa^{2}qu^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})\qquad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,,

with the associated scattered field
u^{s}_{B}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}}):=u_{B}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})-u^{i}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
fulfilling the Sommerfeld radiation condition (
2.1c
).
The Born far field pattern
u_{B}^{\infty}(\,\cdot\,,{\bm{d}})
is defined by replacing the scattered field
u^{s}
by its Born approximation
u^{s}_{B}
in the asymptotic expansion (
2.3
), which leads to the representation

u_{B}^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})\,:=\,\kappa^{2}\int_{\Omega}q({\bm{y}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\dif{\bm{y}}\,,\qquad\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}\,.

(2.6)

The associated Born far field operator

F_{B}:L^{2}({S^{1}})\rightarrow L^{2}({S^{1}})\,,\qquad(F_{B}g)(\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\,:=\,\int_{{S^{1}}}u_{B}^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})g({\bm{d}})\,\dif s({\bm{d}})\,

(2.7)

is also a Hilbert–Schmidt operator. We refer to inverse Born scattering as the task to recover the contrast
q
from the knowledge of the Born far field operator
F_{B}
.

Formula (
2.6
) indicates that knowing
u_{B}^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})
for all
\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}
is equivalent to knowing the two-dimensional Fourier transform of
q
on the origin-centered disk of radius
2\kappa

B_{2\kappa}({\bm{0}})\,=\,\{\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\;:\;\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

However, sampling
\widehat{{\bm{x}}}
and
{\bm{d}}
uniformly on
{S^{1}}
demonstrates that the natural sampling pattern for the Fourier transform of
q
over
B_{2\kappa}({\bm{0}})
is non-uniform, as visualized in Figure
2.1
. Since
q
has compact support, its Fourier transform is an analytic function by virtue of the Paley–Wiener theorem, and the unique continuation principle thus reveals that the contrast
q
is uniquely determined by the knowledge of the Born far field operator
F_{B}
. However, it follows from
[
24
]
that the eigenvalues of
F_{B}
decay at the same rate as those of
F
, implying that inverse Born scattering is ill-posed to the same extend as the original inverse medium scattering problem.

Figure 2.1
:
The natural non-uniform sampling pattern (blue crosses) for the Fourier data of the contrast
q
in the definition (
2.6
) of the Born far field pattern
u_{B}^{\infty}
for
20
equiangular illumination directions
{\bm{d}}
(red crosses) and observation directions
\widehat{{\bm{x}}}
.

Inspired by the treatment of the linearized continuum model of EIT in
[
2
,
14
]
, we adopt formulas (
2.6
)–(
2.7
) as the starting point for deriving a direct reconstruction formula for inverse Born scattering. Since we plan to treat the contrast
q
as an element of
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
F_{B}
as a Hilbert–Schmidt operator on
L^{2}({S^{1}})
, i.e., as an element of
\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))
, we complete this section by noting that the forward map of Born scattering is indeed bounded between these spaces.

Proposition 2.1
.

The linear forward map

T_{B}:q\,\mapsto\,F_{B}

defined by (
2.6
)–(
2.7
) is bounded from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))
with

\|T_{B}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\Omega),\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))}\,\leq\,2\pi\kappa^{2}\sqrt{|\Omega|}\,.

Proof.

Since

(F_{B}g)(\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\,=\,\kappa^{2}\int_{{S^{1}}}\int_{\Omega}q({\bm{y}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\dif{\bm{y}}\,g({\bm{d}})\,\dif s({\bm{d}})\,,\qquad\widehat{{\bm{x}}}\in{S^{1}}\,,

the basic theory on Hilbert–Schmidt integral operators yields (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
)

\|T_{B}q\|_{\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))}^{2}\leq\kappa^{4}\int_{{S^{1}}}\int_{{S^{1}}}\bigg|\int_{\Omega}q({\bm{y}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\dif{\bm{y}}\bigg|^{2}\,\dif s({\bm{d}})\,\dif s(\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\,.

(2.8)

As

\bigg|\int_{\Omega}q({\bm{y}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\dif{\bm{y}}\bigg|^{2}\leq|\Omega|\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,

by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the assertion follows by integrating twice over
{S^{1}}
in (
2.8
) and taking the square root.
∎

3
Angularly decoupled triangular systems for Born scattering

We start by formulating Born scattering in a matrix form between orthonormal bases of
L^{2}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
and
\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))
, then consider a specific choice for the radial parts of the basis for
L^{2}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
, and finally show that our choices lead to angularly decoupled triangular systems for determining the expansion coefficients of the contrast. In the following, we assume prior knowledge of a discoidal region of interest (ROI)
B_{R}({\bm{c}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
containing
\Omega
.

3.1
Matrix representation

We begin by deriving the expansion coefficients for the observed far field data with respect to a modulated Fourier basis following
[
17
]
. By linear substitution, we rewrite the Born far field pattern (
2.6
) as

u_{B}^{\infty}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})\,=\,\kappa^{2}\int_{B_{R}({\bm{0}})}q({\bm{y}}+{\bm{c}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa{\bm{c}}\cdot(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\dif{\bm{y}}\,,\qquad\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}\,.

Due to the Jacobi–Anger expansion (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Eq. (3.112)]
), the plane wave term in this formula can be expanded as

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,=\,2\pi\sum_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathrm{i}^{n-m}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n-m)\arg{\bm{y}}}J_{m}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|)J_{n}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|){\bm{e}}_{m}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\overline{{\bm{e}}_{n}({\bm{d}})}\,,

where
({\bm{e}}_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}:=(\exp(\mathrm{i}m\arg(\,\cdot\,))/\sqrt{2\pi})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is the standard Fourier basis of
{L^{2}({S^{1}})}
and
J_{m}
denotes the Bessel function of the first kind and order
m
.
By introducing the modulated Fourier system
({\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}:=(\exp(-\mathrm{i}\kappa{\bm{c}}\cdot(\,\cdot\,)){\bm{e}}_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, which also forms an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}}({S^{1}})
, we conclude that

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa{\bm{c}}\cdot(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\kappa(\widehat{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{d}})\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,=\,2\pi\sum_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathrm{i}^{n-m}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n-m)\arg{\bm{y}}}J_{m}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|)J_{n}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|){\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{m}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\overline{{\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{n}({\bm{d}})}\,.

This immediately yields a representation for the Born far field operator corresponding to
q
in the orthonormal basis
(\langle\,\cdot\,,{\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{n}\rangle_{{L^{2}}({S^{1}})}{\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{m})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of
\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))
with expansion coefficients

\displaystyle a_{m,n}

\displaystyle\,:=\,\langle F_{B}{\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{n},{\bm{e}}^{\bm{c}}_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}({S^{1}})}

\displaystyle\,=\,2\pi\kappa^{2}\mathrm{i}^{n-m}\int_{B_{R}({\bm{0}})}q({\bm{y}}+{\bm{c}})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n-m)\arg{\bm{y}}}J_{m}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|)J_{n}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|)\,\dif{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle\,=\,(2\pi)^{3/2}\kappa^{2}\mathrm{i}^{n-m}\int_{B_{R}({\bm{0}})}q({\bm{y}}+{\bm{c}})\overline{{\bm{e}}_{m-n}(\widehat{{\bm{y}}})}J_{m}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|)J_{n}(\kappa|{\bm{y}}|)\,\dif{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle\,=\,(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}\mathrm{i}^{n-m}\int_{B_{1}({\bm{0}})}q(R{\bm{y}}+{\bm{c}})\overline{{\bm{e}}_{m-n}(\widehat{{\bm{y}}})}J_{m}(\kappa R|{\bm{y}}|)J_{n}(\kappa R|{\bm{y}}|)\,\dif{\bm{y}}\,,

(3.1)

where
\widehat{{\bm{y}}}={\bm{y}}/|{\bm{y}}|\in{S^{1}}
.

We continue by introducing an orthonormal basis
(\Psi_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
of
L^{2}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
for expanding the shifted and rescaled contrast
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
to enable representing the forward map (cf. Proposition
2.1
)

L^{2}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))\ni q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})\mapsto F_{B}\in\mathrm{HS}({L^{2}}({S^{1}}))

(3.2)

between orthonormal bases. Observing that the kernel of the integral transform (
3.1
), mapping
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
to
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, separates into a radial
|{\bm{y}}|
-dependent part and an angular
\widehat{{\bm{y}}}
-dependent factor that is given by the standard Fourier basis, it is natural to search for the basis of
L^{2}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
in the form

\Psi_{j,k}({\bm{y}}):={\bm{e}}_{j}(\widehat{{\bm{y}}})R_{k}^{|j|}(|{\bm{y}}|),\qquad{\bm{y}}\in B_{1}({\bm{0}})\,.

(3.3)

Here, the radial functions
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
are chosen such that
(\Psi_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
forms an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
; due to the orthonormality of the standard Fourier basis on
{L^{2}}({S^{1}})
, it straightforwardly follows that the necessary and sufficient condition is that
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is an orthonormal basis for the weighted
L^{2}
-space

L^{2}_{r}(0,1)=\bigg\{f:(0,1)\to{\mathbb{C}}\text{ measurable}\;:\;\int_{0}^{1}|f(r)|^{2}r\,\dif r<\infty\bigg\}

(3.4)

for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We may thus expand

q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})\,=\,\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{j}\,=\,\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{j,k}\Psi_{j,k}\qquad\text{for }c_{j,k}\,:=\,\big\langle q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}}),\Psi_{j,k}\big\rangle_{{L^{2}}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))}\,,

(3.5)

where we call

the

-th angular frequency in

.
Note that

,
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
(c_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
all depend on
{\bm{c}}
and

, but we suppress this dependence to improve readability. The specific choice of the radial basis functions
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
will be considered in Subsection

We address the inverse medium scattering problem by recovering the coefficients
(c_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
of the unknown contrast from the knowledge of the coefficients
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

Inserting (
3.5
) into (

) and exploiting the orthonormality of the Fourier basis reveals the underlying infinite-dimensional system matrix
(b_{m,n}^{j,k})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}},j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, that characterizes the Born forward map (

) with respect to the chosen orthonormal basis:

a_{m,n}\,=\,\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{m,n}^{j,k}c_{j,k}\,,\qquad m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

(3.6)

for

b^{j,k}_{m,n}\,:=\,\begin{cases}(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\big\langle R^{|j|}_{k},J_{m}(\kappa R\,\cdot\,)J_{m-j}(\kappa R\,\cdot\,)\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}&\text{if }n=m-j\,,\\[2.84526pt]
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(3.7)

Having a closer look at (
3.6
)–(

), we make the following central structural observations.

Remark 3.1
.

(i)

As in
[
2
,
14
]
for the case of EIT, (
3.7
) uncovers a decoupling of angular frequencies, which enables considering the diagonals
(a_{m,m-j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of the data matrix separately for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Indeed, all available information on the
j
-th angular frequency
q_{j}
in
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
is included in the
(-j)
-th diagonal of the data matrix, which allows to solve for the associated expansion coefficients
(c_{j,k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
from

a_{m,m-j}\,=\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{m,m-j}^{j,k}c_{j,k}\,,\qquad m\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

(3.8)

for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(ii)

For each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, a half of the equations in (
3.8
) are redundant due to a symmetry in
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which means that only a half of the equations needs to be considered and the remaining data can be used for noise filtering.
Indeed, the reciprocity relation in the Born far field data

u^{\infty}_{B}(\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}})\,=\,u^{\infty}_{B}(-{\bm{d}},-\widehat{{\bm{x}}})\qquad\text{for all }\widehat{{\bm{x}}},{\bm{d}}\in{S^{1}}\,,

yields
[
17
, Eq. (2.28)]

a_{-(m-j),-m}\,=\,(-1)^{j}\,a_{m,m-j}\qquad\text{for all }m,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

(3.9)

The same can alternatively be deduced from (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Eq. (10.4.1)]
)

J_{-m}J_{-(m-j)}\,=\,(-1)^{j}J_{m}J_{m-j}\qquad\text{for all }m,j\in{\mathbb{Z}},

which also gives

b^{j,k}_{-(m-j),-m}\,=\,(-1)^{j}\,b^{j,k}_{m,m-j}\qquad\text{for all }m,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

by virtue of (
3.7
).
Consequently, the
(-j)
-th diagonal of the data matrix
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is symmetric up to the factor
(-1)^{j}
with respect to the (possibly virtual) element
a_{j/2,-j/2}
, and the analogous conclusion also holds for the system matrix
(b_{m,n}^{j,k})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
with fixed
j
and
k
.
In consequence, we do not discard any unique equations in (
3.8
) if we only consider

a_{m,m-j}\,=\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{m,m-j}^{j,k}c_{j,k}\,,\qquad m\geq\tfrac{j}{2}\,,

(3.10)

where
a_{m,m-j}
could be replaced by the averaged data

\widetilde{a}_{m,m-j}:=(a_{m,m-j}+(-1)^{j}a_{-m+j,-m})/2\,,

(3.11)

without altering the equations, to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

\lozenge

It remains to construct orthonormal bases
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, for
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
so that inverting (
3.6
), or equivalently (
3.10
), becomes straightforward.
Following
[
2
,
14
]
, we aim for a choice that makes the system matrix in (
3.10
) lower triangular for every
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The radial Zernike bases employed in
[
2
,
14
]
are unsuitable for this purpose, but it turns out they can be replaced by bases generated through a Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization process of the products of Bessel functions appearing in (
3.7
).

3.2
Choice of the radial bases

For
j,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define

P_{m}^{j}(r)\,:=\,J_{m}(\kappa Rr)J_{m-j}(\kappa Rr)\,,\qquad r\in(0,1)\,,

(3.12)

which appear in (
3.7
) and will act as our initial, i.e., non-orthonormalized, radial basis functions. To ease the notation, we set

{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq c}:=\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\;:\;k\geq c\}

for
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the functions
(P_{m}^{j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}}
are linearly independent and their linear span is dense in
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
.

Proof.

We start by proving that
(P_{m}^{j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}}
are linearly independent. For
m\geq j/2
and any
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the lowest order term in the converging origin-centered power series representation of
P_{m}^{j}(r)
behaves as
r^{2m-j}
(e.g.,
[
28
, Sec. 5.41, Eq. (1)]
). Hence, each function in
(P_{m}^{j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}}
has its own distinct polynomial behavior close to the origin, which proves that they are linearly independent.

Let
\rho\in L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
. We prove the assertion on the density by showing that
\rho
can be orthogonal in
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
to all functions in the set
(P_{m}^{j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}}
only if it vanishes. Define a shifted and scaled contrast as
q(R{\bm{y}}+{\bm{c}})={\bm{e}}_{j}(\widehat{{\bm{y}}})\rho(|{\bm{y}}|)
,
{\bm{y}}\in B_{1}({\bm{0}})
, for a fixed but arbitrary
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which according to Remark
3.1
means that the data matrix
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
only has nonzero elements on its
(-j)
-th diagonal. By inserting our choice of
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
into (
3.1
) and integrating over
{S^{1}}
, we get the representation (cf. (
3.7
))

a_{m,m-j}=(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\langle\rho,P_{m}^{j}\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)},\qquad m\in{\mathbb{Z}},

for the elements on the
(-j)
-th diagonal. If
\rho
is orthogonal to all functions in
(P_{m}^{j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}}
, the
(-j)
-the diagonal of
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is thus empty due to the symmetry (
3.9
), meaning that the data matrix altogether vanishes. This means that our
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
is in the nullspace of the forward operator (
3.2
), and thus
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
is zero almost everywhere by the unique solvability of the considered inverse Born scattering problem. This completes the proof.
∎

For any fixed
m,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

P_{m-j}^{-j}\,=\,J_{m-j}J_{(m-j)-(-j)}\,=\,J_{m}J_{m-j}\,=\,P_{m}^{j}\,,

(3.13)

from which it follows that for any
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

(P_{m}^{j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}}=(P_{m}^{-j})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq-j/2}}=(P_{m}^{|j|})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq|j|/2}},

with the functions in these sets given in the same ordering with respect to increasing
m
. Hence, we only need to consider the radial bases
(P_{m}^{|j|})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq|j|/2}}
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, in what follows.

Now we are ready to properly introduce our basis
(\Psi_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
for
L^{2}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
by defining the orthonormal basis
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
of
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
in (
3.3
) for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Definition 3.3
.

The radial basis functions
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, in (
3.3
) are defined by applying the Gram–Schmidt ortogonalization process with respect to the inner product of
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
to the functions
(P_{m}^{|j|})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq|j|/2}}
. That is,

R_{k}^{|j|}\,:=\,\frac{\widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|}}{\big\|\widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|}\big\|_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}\qquad\text{with}\ \widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|}\,:=\,P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}-\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\big\langle P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|},R_{m}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}R_{m}^{|j|}

(3.14)

for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

\lozenge

Since by Proposition
3.2
the functions
(P_{m}^{|j|})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq|j|/2}}
are linearly independent and their linear span is dense in
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
, the set
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a well-defined orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is precisely the requirement for the construction leading to (
3.10
) to be valid. On the negative side, the procedure in (
3.14
) is numerically unstable, which we will address in Section
4
below.

3.3
Angularly decoupled triangular systems

Let us then examine how our specific choice for the radial bases simplifies the system (
3.10
). For
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, formula (
3.7
) gives

b_{m,m-j}^{j,k}=(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\big\langle R^{|j|}_{k},P_{m}^{j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}=(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\big\langle R^{|j|}_{k},P_{m}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}.

(3.15)

The right side of (
3.15
) vanishes if
k>m-\lceil|j|/2\rceil=m-\lceil j/2\rceil
since
R^{|j|}_{k}
is orthogonal to the subspace of
L^{2}_{r}(0,1)
spanned by the first
k
functions in
(P_{m}^{|j|})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq|j|/2}}
due to the Gram–Schmidt process (
3.14
). On the other hand, for
-j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}
, it follows from (
3.13
) that

\displaystyle b_{m,m-j}^{j,k}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\big\langle R^{|j|}_{k},P_{m}^{j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}

\displaystyle=(-\mathrm{i})^{2j}(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{-j}\big\langle R^{|j|}_{k},P_{m-j}^{-j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}=(-1)^{j}b_{m-j,m}^{-j,k}.

(3.16)

Since in this case
P_{m-j}^{-j}=P_{m+|j|}^{|j|}
, the orthogonalization process (
3.14
) dictates that
b_{m,m-j}^{j,k}=(-1)^{j}b_{m-j,m}^{-j,k}
vanishes when

k>m+|j|-\lceil|j|/2\rceil=m+\lfloor|j|/2\rfloor=m-\lceil j/2\rceil.

Together with (
3.10
), these conclusions yield angularly decoupled triangular systems for determining the expansion coefficients
(c_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
from the knowledge of the data matrix
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
:

a_{m,m-j}\,=\,\sum_{k=0}^{m-\lceil j/2\rceil}b_{m,m-j}^{j,k}c_{j,k},\qquad m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq j/2}\,,

(3.17)

for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

In order to recast (
3.17
) for a fixed
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
in a matrix-vector form, we introduce the infinite-dimensional vectors

{\bm{c}}^{j}\,:=\,\big[c_{j,k-1}\big]_{k=1}^{\infty}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{a}}^{j}\,:=\,\big[a_{(m-1)+\lceil j/2\rceil,(m-1)-\lfloor j/2\rfloor}\big]_{m=1}^{\infty},

(3.18)

corresponding to the
j
-th angular frequency in
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
and a half of the
(-j)
-th diagonal in the data matrix, respectively, as well as the lower triangular infinite-dimensional system matrix
{\bm{F}}^{j}:=[F^{j}_{m,k}]_{m,k=1}^{\infty}
given componentwise as

F^{j}_{m,k}\,=\,\begin{cases}(2\pi)^{3/2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}(\kappa R)^{2}\big\langle R_{k-1}^{|j|},P_{(m-1)+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}&\text{if }k\leq m\,,\\[2.84526pt]
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(3.19)

For each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the resulting system

{\bm{F}}^{j}{\bm{c}}^{j}\,=\,{\bm{a}}^{j}

(3.20)

corresponds to (
3.17
) when
m
runs from
\lceil j/2\rceil
to infinity and can be solved through a forward substitution (not accounting for instability).
Note that according to (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), the latter term in the inner product in (
3.19
) should, in fact, be
P_{(m-1)+\lceil j/2\rceil}^{j}
, but employing (
3.13
) for
j<0
, one deduces

P_{(m-1)+\lceil j/2\rceil}^{j}=P_{(m-1)+\lceil j/2\rceil-j}^{-j}=P_{(m-1)+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|},

which allows the presented form.

These considerations lead to the following reconstruction formula that is our main result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Denote by
(\Psi_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
the orthonormal system for
{L^{2}}(B_{1}({\bm{0}}))
defined by (
3.3
) and (
3.14
), and let
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the given expansion coefficients of the Born far field operator as in (
3.1
).
Then, the expansion coefficients of the shifted and scaled contrast
q(R(\,\cdot\,)+{\bm{c}})
with respect to
(\Psi_{j,k})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, as given in (
3.5
), can be computed separately for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
via a recursion with respect to
k
:

c_{j,k}\,=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\big\|\widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|}\big\|_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}a_{k+\lceil j/2\rceil,k-\lfloor j/2\rfloor}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\big\langle R_{i}^{|j|},P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}{\big\|\widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|}\big\|_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}\,c_{j,i}

(3.21)

for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Here,
(\widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
are the unnormalized orthogonal basis functions from (
3.14
) and the sum in (
3.21
) is viewed to be empty for
k=0
.

Proof.

Fix
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Solving the lower triangular system (
3.20
) via forward substitution directly gives

c_{j,k}\,=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}(\kappa R)^{2}(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\big\langle R_{k}^{|j|},P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}a_{k+\lceil j/2\rceil,k-\lfloor j/2\rfloor}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\big\langle R_{i}^{|j|},P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}{\big\langle R_{k}^{|j|},P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{r}(0,1)}}c_{j,i}\,

for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Representing
P_{k+\lceil|j|/2\rceil}^{|j|}
as in (
3.14
) and utilizing the orthogonality of
(R_{k}^{|j|})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
allows to replace the inner product in the denominator by the norm of
\widetilde{R}_{k}^{|j|}
, which is nonzero by construction. This completes the proof.
∎

Observe that all inner products and norms appearing in (
3.21
) have already been computed (and stored) when running the Gram–Schmidt process in (
3.14
). Hence, applying the reconstruction formula (
3.21
) is essentially for free, assuming the Gram–Schmidt process has been run offline prior to having the data in hand. It is also worth noting that our approach to solving the system (
3.10
) is essentially an infinite-dimensional QR factorization, with (
3.21
) corresponding to solving the triangular system defined by the “R” matrix.

Example 3.5
.

As an example we consider
q=\chi_{B_{r}({\bm{0}})}
for some

\chi_{(\,\cdot\,)}

Thus, for this specific choice of

Regularization of the infinite-dimensional systems

As elaborated in
[
17

, almost all expansion coefficients
(a_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of the given far field data are close to zero and thus negligible. More precisely, it is reasonable to estimate that

a_{m,n}\,=\,0\qquad\text{for }|m|,|n|>N

(4.1)

with
N
chosen slightly larger than
\kappa R
.
The aforementioned work also provides explicit bounds for the approximation error introduced at this stage.
The nonzero structure of the expansion coefficients for the observed far field data under such a truncation is visualized in Figure
4.1
(left), with only the elements needed for (
3.21
) included (i.e., without employing the noise filtering step in (
3.11
)).

:

(a_{m,n})_{|m|,|n|\leq N}

)) after introducing the truncation index

(blue), with a representative diagonal
(a_{m,m-j})_{|m|,|m-j|\leq N}

To make the effect on the individual diagonals explicit, we rewrite this assumption as

a_{m+\lceil j/2\rceil,m-\lfloor j/2\rfloor}\,=\,0\qquad\begin{cases}\text{for }|j|>2N\,,\\
\text{for }-2N\leq j<0\,,\,m>N+\lfloor j/2\rfloor=N-\lceil|j|/2\rceil\,,\\
\text{for }0\leq j\leq 2N\,,\,m>N-\lceil j/2\rceil=N-\lceil|j|/2\rceil\,\end{cases}

for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
Consequently, after introducing a truncation index
N
, only
4N+1
systems for
j\in\{-2N,\ldots,2N\}
are to be solved instead of the infinite number of systems in (
3.20
), and for a particular angular index
j
, the corresponding system becomes
(N-\lceil|j|/2\rceil+1)
-dimensional, cf. Example
3.6
.

For
j\in\{-2N,\ldots,2N\}
, we define the truncated vectors (cf. (
3.18
))

{\bm{c}}^{j,N}\,:=\,\big[c_{j,k-1}\big]_{k=1}^{N+1-\lceil|j|/2\rceil}\,,\;{\bm{a}}^{j,N}\,:=\,\big[a_{(m-1)+\lceil j/2\rceil,(m-1)-\lfloor j/2\rfloor}\big]_{m=1}^{N+1-\lceil|j|/2\rceil}\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{N+1-\lceil|j|/2\rceil}

and the lower triangular finite-dimensional system matrix

{\bm{F}}^{j,N}\,=\,\big[F^{j}_{m,k}\big]_{m,k=1}^{N+1-\lceil|j|/2\rceil}\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{(N+1-\lceil|j|/2\rceil)\times(N+1-\lceil|j|/2\rceil)}\,.

Solving the resulting finite-dimensional systems

{\bm{F}}^{j,N}{\bm{c}}^{j,N}\,=\,{\bm{a}}^{j,N},\qquad j\in\{-2N,\ldots,2N\},

(4.2)

in place of the infinitely many infinite-dimensional systems of type (
3.20
) constitutes the first step of our regularization scheme. If no further regularization is introduced, the forward substitution formula (
3.21
) still applies to solving the individual systems in (
4.2
) for
k\in\{0,\dots,N-\lceil|j|/2\rceil\}
.

Remark 4.1
.

The choice of the truncation index
N
involves a trade-off between two competing criteria.
It should be large enough to retain the relevant information in the observed far field data, yet not so large that the Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization becomes numerically unstable.
Indeed, due to the super-exponential decay of the Bessel function
J_{n}(t)
for fixed
t>0
as
{n\rightarrow\infty}
, choosing
N
too large leads to divisions by values that are numerically indistinguishable from zero during the normalization step.
To illustrate this phenomenon, we construct for fixed
\kappa R>0
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
the corresponding
(N+1)^{2}
Gram–Schmidt basis functions
{(R_{k}^{j})_{j=0,\ldots,2N,k=0,\ldots,N-\lceil j/2\rceil}}
as defined by (
3.14
).
For the numerical integration, we use
N_{r}=100
Gauss–Legendre quadrature nodes and weights
(r_{i},\omega_{i})_{i=0,\ldots,N_{r}-1}
.
By arranging the involved function evaluations for each
j
as elements of a matrix, we obtain

{\bm{Q}}^{j,N}\,:=\,\begin{pmatrix}R_{0}^{j}(r_{0})&R_{1}^{j}(r_{0})&\cdots&R_{N-\lceil j/2\rceil}^{j}(r_{0})\\
R_{0}^{j}(r_{1})&R_{1}^{j}(r_{1})&\cdots&R_{N-\lceil j/2\rceil}^{j}(r_{1})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
R_{0}^{j}(r_{N_{r}-1})&R_{1}^{j}(r_{N_{r}-1})&\ldots&R_{N-\lceil j/2\rceil}^{j}(r_{N_{r}-1})\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{r}\times(N+1-\lceil j/2\rceil)}\,,

whose columns are ideally orthonormal with respect to the inner product defined by the diagonal weight matrix
{\bm{W}}=\diag(\omega_{0}r_{0},\ldots,\omega_{N_{r}-1}r_{N_{r}-1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{r}\times N_{r}}
.
Consequently, we may quantify how much the constructed basis functions deviate on average from forming orthonormal systems via evaluating the mean error

\varepsilon_{\text{GSO}}\,:=\,\frac{1}{N+1}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{2N}\big\|({\bm{Q}}^{j,N})^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{Q}}^{j,N}-{\bm{I}}_{N+1-\lceil j/2\rceil}\big\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}}

(4.3)

where
{\bm{I}}_{N+1-\lceil j/2\rceil}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(N+1-\lceil j/2\rceil)\times(N+1-\lceil j/2\rceil)}
is the identity matrix and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm.
This error is shown in Figure
4.1
(right) for
N\in\{1,\ldots,40\}
and
\kappa R\in\{5,10,15,20\}
on a semi-logarithmic scale.
All error curves exhibit a regime of very low error for small
N
, followed by a sharp increase near
N\approx\kappa R
, after which they plateau.
This behavior suggests that choosing
N
significantly larger than
\kappa R
is not reasonable.
The rule
N:=\lceil\mathrm{e}\kappa R/2\rceil
used in
[
17
]
turns out to be too large for our purposes, but
N:=\lceil\kappa R\rceil
proves to be a reliable choice in our numerical experiments.

The truncated systems (
4.2
) can be rearranged into a single block-diagonal system

{\bm{F}}^{N}{\bm{c}}^{N}\,=\,{\bm{a}}^{N}

(4.4)

by introducing

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{N}\,

\displaystyle:=\,\diag\!\big({\bm{F}}^{-2N,N},\ldots,{\bm{F}}^{2N,N}\big)\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{M\times M}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}^{N}\,

\displaystyle:=\,\big[{\bm{a}}^{-2N,N};\ldots;{\bm{a}}^{2N,N}\big]\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{c}}^{N}\,

\displaystyle:=\,\big[{\bm{c}}^{-2N,N};\ldots;{\bm{c}}^{2N,N}\big]\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\,,

where semicolon denotes vertical concatenation and

M\,:=\,\sum_{j=-2N}^{2N}\left(N+1-\left\lceil\tfrac{|j|}{2}\right\rceil\right)\,=\,(N+1)+4\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\,=\,(N+1)(2N+1)\,.

In our numerical experiments documented in Section
5
below, we further employ a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) for the system (
4.4
) to handle full nonlinear far field data and/or additive noise.
The block-diagonal structure of the system matrix
{\bm{F}}^{N}
allows its SVD to be completely characterized by SVDs of the individual small blocks
{\bm{F}}^{j,N}
,
j=\{-2N,\ldots,2N\}
, thereby avoiding the need to compute a high dimensional SVD. See
[
2
]
for more detailed analysis on applying a truncated SVD to a similar block-diagonal system in the framework of EIT.

Conclusions

Following the ideas in
[
2
,
14
]
for EIT, we introduced a direct reconstruction method for inverse medium Born scattering for the Helmholtz equation in two spatial dimensions. Choosing appropriate basis for representing the far field operator and the contrast function, the proposed method reduces the inverse problem to solving decoupled triangular systems that correspond to different angular frequencies in the contrast. The bases for representing the far field operator and the angular behavior of the contrast function are of Fourier type and can be given explicitly, but introducing the needed radial bases for the contrast requires numerically orthogonalizing certain products of Bessel functions, which adds an additional unstable step to the algorithm. On the positive side, this orthogonalization can be performed offline before the data is available and can also be stabilized by truncating the to-be-inverted system based on ideas in
[
17
]
. Due to the achieved angular decoupling and the triangular structure of the subsystems, the proposed method allows an efficient numerical implementation, as well as an explicit recursive reconstruction formula (Theorem
3.4
) if instability issues are not considered.

The presented numerical experiments demonstrate that our method produces reconstruction of good quality from (noisy) Born far field data, and it can also be applied to full nonlinear far field data to obtain reconstructions ranging in quality from good to reasonable depending on the extent of multiple scattering effects. According to our numerical experiments, the proposed method compares favorably to other algorithms designed for solving the inverse medium Born scattering problem, in particular, producing reconstructions comparable to those by the low-rank method introduced recently
in
[
30
]
.

A natural direction for future work is to derive explicit representations for the radial basis functions; this would also be a first step toward extending the stability results of
[
14
]
from EIT to inverse medium scattering. Another promising avenue is to generalize our reconstruction method to three dimensions, building on the ideas in
[
13
]
.
