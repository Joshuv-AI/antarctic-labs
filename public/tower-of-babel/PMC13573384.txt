Integrating lipometabolic and adiposity indices to enhance risk stratification for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in type 2 diabetes: clinical utility and interplay between triglyceride-glucose body mass index and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol
Authors: Yanxiu Yang, Xiaoxiao Qu, Mengran Shi, Jialu Huang, Guanghong Li, Xingyu Lu, Enxin You, Jingyun Qian, Jing Xu, Minghua Jiang, Guosong Jiang, Qipeng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) significantly exacerbates the prognosis of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). We aimed to compare metabolic and adiposity-related surrogates for MASLD using data-driven feature selection and to validate a parsimonious risk model across distinct populations.MethodsWe included 449 T2DM patients from the WMU cohort (discovery) and 306 from the Japanese NAGALA cohort (external validation). A two-stage data-driven feature-selection framework (Boruta and LASSO) was implemented to identify a parsimonious two-variable signature (TyG-BMI and SGLT2i). Based on these results, TyG-BMI was prioritized for systematic evaluation. Association and dose–response relationships were assessed via multivariate logistic regression and restricted cubic splines. Multiplicative and additive interactions between TyG-BMI and LDL-C were further explored. Clinical utility was evaluated via AUC, NRI, IDI, and decision curve analysis.ResultsThe Boruta algorithm ranked TyG-BMI as the feature with the highest importance score for MASLD classification. Subsequently, LASSO regression (utilizing the 1-standard-error criterion λ1se) identified a parsimonious two-variable signature comprising TyG-BMI and SGLT2i. In the WMU cohort, TyG-BMI exhibited a potent association with MASLD (T3 vs. T1: OR = 7.36, 95% CI 3.89–13.94) and a significant linear dose–response relationship (P for overall < 0.001). Incorporation of TyG-BMI into the baseline model improved discriminative performance (AUC increased from 0.7288 to 0.7920) and was associated with improved continuous reclassification (NRI: 0.6088, P < 0.001). DCA and calibration plots confirmed high clinical net benefit and accuracy. Furthermore, a significant synergistic interaction was observed between TyG-BMI and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).ConclusionsTyG-BMI, selected through data-driven feature selection, may serve as a practical candidate predictor of MASLD in patients with T2DM. The observed interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C suggests that their joint assessment may further refine MASLD risk stratification. The derived parsimonious model offers a high-performing, non-invasive tool for early MASLD risk stratification across Asian populations.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03272-3.Keywords: Triglyceride-glucose body mass index, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Decision curve analysis, Subgroup analysis

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has reached epidemic proportions globally, establishing itself not only as a leading cause of chronic liver disease but also as a systemic condition deeply intertwined with cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1–3]. This clinical burden is disproportionately magnified within the type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) population, where the coexistence of MASLD aggressively accelerates the progression of hepatic fibrosis and cardiometabolic multimorbidity [4–6]. Despite the severe prognostic implications for this high-risk demographic, large-scale clinical screening remains severely constrained by the cost, invasiveness, and limited accessibility of gold-standard diagnostic modalities such as liver biopsy or magnetic resonance imaging. Consequently, many epidemiological studies have resorted to indirect surrogate markers, such as the Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP) [7, 8]. However, these conventional indices frequently lack the diagnostic precision and biological specificity necessary to navigate the complex metabolic heterogeneity inherent in the diabetic milieu. This underscores an urgent clinical imperative for more accurate, non-invasive risk stratification tools based on routine parameters.

The pathophysiological core driving the MASLD-T2DM phenotype is insulin resistance (IR) [9, 10]. While systemic IR initiates aberrant lipid metabolism and hepatic fat accumulation, clinical risk assessment often relies on isolated biochemical proxies [11, 12]. From a clinical research perspective, integrating functional biochemical indicators of IR with morphological anthropometric parameters—which capture the physical burden and distribution of systemic adiposity [13]—theoretically offers a more holistic and robust estimation of MASLD risk.

Previous epidemiological studies have largely relied on predefined empirical markers, with limited data-driven comparison of multidimensional metabolic and adiposity-related indicators. In this insulin resistance-related metabolic context, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) may further reflect an adverse lipid profile associated with hepatic steatosis and cardiometabolic risk [9, 10, 14]. Whether LDL-C modifies the association between an insulin resistance-adiposity composite profile and MASLD remains underexplored and may have implications for risk stratification in patients with T2DM.

To bridge these methodological and clinical gaps, we designed a dual-cohort study to establish a non-invasive identification framework for MASLD in patients with T2DM. Rather than presupposing the predictive superiority of any single established index, we applied a data-driven feature-selection approach—using the Boruta algorithm and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression in parallel [15, 16]—to systematically screen and compare candidate predictors from a comprehensive multidimensional clinical dataset. After identifying TyG-BMI as a consistently selected composite indicator, we investigated its multiplicative and additive interactions with LDL-C to assess their joint association with MASLD. Finally, the incremental predictive value and clinical net benefit of the final risk model were evaluated and externally validated across two distinct populations, including the Wenzhou Medical University (WMU) cohort and the Japanese NAGALA (NAFLD in the Gifu Area Longitudinal Analysis) cohort [17, 18].

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study was a retrospective cross-sectional analysis based on two independent cohorts and used a discovery-validation design. The discovery cohort comprised hospitalized patients with T2DM from the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children's Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (WMU cohort). The external validation cohort comprised patients with T2DM from the publicly available Japanese NAGALA cohort. The study was designed to evaluate the association between TyG-BMI and prevalent MASLD in patients with T2DM and to assess the incremental value of TyG-BMI for MASLD risk identification [17].

The WMU study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children's Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (approval No. 2025-K-270–01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Owing to the retrospective design and use of de-identified clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The NAGALA cohort was publicly released after de-identification by the original investigators, and the present secondary analysis did not involve additional participant contact [18].

Study population

The WMU cohort was derived from the electronic medical record system and included patients aged 18 years or older who were hospitalized in the Department of Endocrinology between September 1, 2024 and September 30, 2025. A total of 854 patients were initially screened. Exclusion criteria were as follows: non-T2DM status, including type 1 diabetes or no diabetes (n = 152); viral hepatitis, including hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, or hepatitis E virus infection (n = 67); acute infection during hospitalization that could affect interpretation of liver-related indicators (n = 27); heavy alcohol consumption, defined as an average alcohol intake > 25 g/day in men or > 15 g/day in women (n = 58); pregnancy (n = 1); first in-hospital alanine aminotransferase (ALT) > 10 times the upper limit of the assay reference range (ALT > 400 U/L) and clinically judged as acute liver injury unrelated to MASLD, such as drug-induced or ischemic liver injury (n = 1); missing information required for MASLD outcome ascertainment (n = 13); and missing variables required to calculate TyG-BMI, including triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), or body mass index (BMI) (n = 86). Finally, 449 patients with T2DM were included in the WMU discovery analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The NAGALA cohort was established from a general health management center in Gifu, Japan, between 2004 and 2015. The original study included 20,944 adults aged 18 years or older and collected data for evaluating associations of metabolic abnormalities with fatty liver and diabetes risk. Detailed information on data collection, biochemical testing, and abdominal ultrasonography procedures has been described in the original NAGALA report [18]. In the present study, participants were selected from the public dataset if they were aged 18 years or older, had ascertainable T2DM status, and had complete data for TyG-BMI calculation and hepatic steatosis assessment. A total of 306 patients with T2DM were included in the external validation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Covariates

In the WMU cohort, baseline demographic characteristics, clinical information, medication use, and laboratory test results were extracted from the electronic medical record system. Demographic and lifestyle variables included age, sex, duration of diabetes, smoking status, and drinking status. Comorbidities and medication variables included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, glucose-lowering medications recorded at admission, lipid-lowering medications, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker use. Laboratory variables included white blood cell count, platelet count, ALT, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, total cholesterol, TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LDL-C, FPG, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c).

Anthropometric measurements were obtained by trained nurses during the early stage of hospitalization. Height and weight were measured with participants barefoot and wearing light clothing, and BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower costal margin and the iliac crest at the end of normal expiration. Smoking status and drinking status were treated as binary variables. Information on tobacco use and alcohol intake was obtained from participant questionnaires and coded as binary variables. Ever-smokers included current and former smokers, whereas never-smokers had no history of smoking. Drinkers were defined as participants reporting any alcohol intake during the preceding 12 months, and non-drinkers as those reporting no lifetime alcohol use. In accordance with the exclusion criteria, participants with heavy alcohol consumption, defined as an average alcohol intake of > 25 g/day for men or > 15 g/day for women according to the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2016), were excluded [19]. This exclusion was applied to reduce the likelihood of including alcohol-related steatotic liver disease under the current MASLD nomenclature framework [1]. Thus, all drinkers included in the analysis had alcohol intake below these sex-specific thresholds. Hypertension was defined as a previous clinical diagnosis, repeated resting systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg during hospitalization, or current antihypertensive treatment. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a previous clinical diagnosis, total cholesterol ≥ 6.2 mmol/L or LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L at admission, or current lipid-lowering treatment such as statins or ezetimibe.

Because the publicly available NAGALA cohort contained fewer variables than the WMU cohort, the validation models included variables that were available and defined as consistently as possible across cohorts, including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, ALT, and HbA1c. Comorbidities and medication history that were unavailable in the NAGALA cohort were not included in the validation models.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was prevalent MASLD. MASLD was defined according to the 2023 multisociety nomenclature framework as hepatic steatosis in the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, after exclusion of heavy alcohol consumption and other specific causes of steatotic liver disease. Because all participants in the present analysis had T2DM, they fulfilled the glycemic cardiometabolic risk criterion by design [1].

T2DM in the WMU cohort was defined according to the diagnostic criteria of the American Diabetes Association and electronic medical record documentation. Participants were classified as having T2DM if any of the following criteria were met: a previous diagnosis of T2DM recorded by an endocrinologist, current glucose-lowering treatment, FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-h plasma glucose during an oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with typical hyperglycemic symptoms. In the NAGALA cohort, T2DM status was determined according to the diabetes diagnosis and glucose- and HbA1c-related variables available in the original dataset [20].

In the WMU cohort, hepatic steatosis was primarily ascertained from abdominal ultrasonography reports obtained during hospitalization. Steatosis was judged using standardized semiquantitative ultrasonographic features, including diffuse hyperechogenicity or a bright liver pattern, attenuation of the ultrasound beam, and blurred visualization of intrahepatic vessels or the diaphragm. For reports with unclear or borderline descriptions, investigators reviewed the imaging report and medical record information to determine the final steatosis status. Although ultrasonography is not the gold standard for quantifying liver fat, it is non-invasive, clinically accessible, and widely used for steatosis screening in epidemiological and real-world studies.

In the NAGALA cohort, hepatic steatosis was also based on abdominal ultrasonography. The present analysis used the binary fatty liver label generated through the standardized health-checkup procedures in the original dataset and combined it with T2DM status to define MASLD. Differences between the two cohorts in data source, clinical setting, and ultrasonography workflow were considered part of the external validation context.

To ensure consistency across cohorts, TG and FPG values reported in mmol/L in the WMU cohort were converted to mg/dL before calculation using conversion factors of 88.57 for TG and 18.02 for FPG. In the WMU cohort, venous blood samples were obtained after at least 8 h of fasting and were analyzed in the clinical laboratory of the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children's Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Serum TG was measured using the glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase–peroxidase method, and FPG was measured using the hexokinase method on a Beckman AU5800 automatic biochemical analyzer. Laboratory testing followed internal quality control procedures and routinely participated in national and provincial external quality assessment programs. The NAGALA cohort used laboratory values reported by the clinical laboratory of the health management center in Gifu, Japan.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for approximately normally distributed data or median with interquartile range for skewed data. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test, as appropriate.Missing data are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. In the WMU cohort, HbA1c had the highest missing proportion (15.59%); all other variables had missing proportions < 3%. Missing covariates were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption, generating five imputed datasets pooled with Rubin’s rules [21, 22]; complete-case analysis served as a sensitivity check. The NAGALA validation analyses contained no missing data after participant selection. Feature selection was performed using a parallel Boruta and LASSO logistic regression approach [15, 16]. The same set of clinical candidate predictors for MASLD was evaluated independently using both methods. The Boruta algorithm was used to evaluate the relative importance of candidate predictors, whereas LASSO logistic regression was applied with tenfold cross-validation to determine the regularization parameter. Variables retained by both procedures were considered candidate predictors with consistent support in this dataset. TyG-BMI was selected as the core exposure based on its Boruta ranking, retention in the LASSO model, and clinical interpretability.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between TyG-BMI and MASLD. TyG-BMI was modeled both as a continuous variable per 1-unit increment and as a categorical variable. For categorical analyses, TyG-BMI was divided into cohort-specific tertiles (T1–T3), with the lowest tertile used as the reference group. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for each model. In the WMU cohort, covariates were adjusted using a stepwise modeling strategy. Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking status, drinking status, ALT, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, and HbA1c. Model 3 further adjusted for hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Model 4 additionally adjusted for statin use, ezetimibe use, biguanide use, and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor use. In the NAGALA cohort, a reduced but conceptually parallel adjustment strategy was used because of limited public variables: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking and drinking status; Model 3 further adjusted for ALT; and Model 4 additionally adjusted for HbA1c.Model adequacy was assessed using diagnostic plots and statistics, including calibration-related plots, binned residual plots, influential point assessment based on Cook's distance, and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. All covariates had VIF values < 5, indicating no substantial multicollinearity (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to evaluate potential non-linear dose–response relationships between TyG-BMI and MASLD risk [23, 24]. The spline models were fitted using the Model 4 covariate set, and graphical displays were restricted to the main distributional range of TyG-BMI to reduce the visual influence of extreme values. To examine whether the association between TyG-BMI and MASLD was consistent across clinically relevant subgroups, stratified analyses were performed by age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), sex, BMI (< 24 or ≥ 24 kg/m2), LDL-C (< 3.4 or ≥ 3.4 mmol/L), and smoking status [25, 26]. Subgroup-specific ORs and 95% CIs were presented in forest plots. Interaction was tested by adding a product term between TyG-BMI and the stratification variable to the main model. For variables with evidence of effect modification, additional interaction analyses were performed to assess their role in the TyG-BMI-MASLD association. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, clinical net benefit, and reclassification. A reference model was constructed using the Model 4 covariate set. TyG, BMI, and TyG-BMI were then added separately to the reference model, and the resulting models were compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). AUCs were compared using DeLong's method where applicable [27–29]. External validation analyses in the NAGALA cohort repeated the main association analyses, dose–response analyses, subgroup analyses, and model performance evaluations. TyG-BMI categories in the NAGALA cohort were defined according to the distribution within that cohort to avoid distributional bias from applying WMU cut points directly. Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. In the WMU cohort, TyG-BMI was regrouped into quartiles and quintiles to examine the stability of dose–response patterns; BMI was additionally adjusted in Models 2, 3, and 4 to evaluate whether the TyG-BMI association was independent of BMI itself; the main regression analyses were repeated using complete-case data before multiple imputation; and analyses were repeated after excluding extreme TyG-BMI values. In the NAGALA cohort, quartile and quintile grouping, exclusion of extreme values, and incremental model performance analyses were performed to assess reproducibility in the independent validation dataset.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the Free Statistics platform (version 2.4.0; Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Feature selection via Boruta and LASSO algorithms

The Boruta algorithm identified 10 confirmed important features (green boxes) with importance scores exceeding the shadow variables (shadowMax). Among these, TyG-BMI exhibited the highest importance score, followed by BMI, TyG-WHtR, TyG-WC, TyG-CVAI, and TyG-BRI (Fig. 1A). Other confirmed factors included Age, SGLT2i use, TyG index, and HbA1c. TyG-WWI, TyG-ABSI, and BUN were classified as tentative features (yellow boxes), whereas the remaining candidate variables were rejected (red boxes). In parallel, LASSO logistic regression analysis was performed using the same set of candidate variables. Based on the 1-standard-error criterion (λ1se), the optimal tuning parameter (λ) was identified (log λ ≈ − 2.9), favoring a more parsimonious model (Fig. 1B). At this λ value, the LASSO model simplified the candidate variables and ultimately selected 2 variables—TyG-BMI and SGLT2i—for the final model (Fig. 1C). The consistent selection of TyG-BMI across both the Boruta and LASSO frameworks suggests its consistent predictive support for MASLD classification in this cohort.

Baseline characteristics of the study population

This study included 449 participants with an average age of 59.9 ± 13.6 years. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants across the TyG-BMI tertiles. Higher TyG-BMI levels were typically associated with younger age, higher BMI, and increased waist circumference (WC). Additionally, higher TyG-BMI was associated with hyperlipidemia, as well as higher levels of ALT, TG, LDL-C, and FPG. Regarding pharmacological interventions, higher TyG-BMI was associated with the use of biguanides and SGLT2i, whereas lower TyG-BMI showed an association with ezetimibe use. No significant differences were observed across the TyG-BMI tertiles regarding sex, smoking status, drinking status, HbA1c, serum creatinine, or the use of statins. Notably, the prevalence of MASLD increased significantly across the TyG-BMI tertiles, rising from 40.7% in the lowest tertile to 88.0% in the highest tertile.

Restricted cubic spline and subgroup analysis

RCS analysis was performed to characterize the dose–response relationship between TyG-BMI and the probability of MASLD. In the overall population, a significant linear association was observed (P for overall association < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 5), while the test for non-linearity was non-significant (P for non-linearity = 0.552), indicating that the risk of MASLD increased steadily across the spectrum of TyG-BMI levels. Subgroup analysis further assessed potential differences in this association across various clinical strata. While no significant interactions were found for age, sex, BMI, or smoking status (Supplementary Fig. 6), a significant interaction was observed between TyG-BMI and LDL-C levels (P for interaction = 0.018). Specifically, the association between TyG-BMI and MASLD risk was more pronounced in participants with LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L.

Interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C

Visual analysis confirmed a synergistic effect between TyG-BMI and LDL-C on the risk of MASLD. As illustrated in the column chart, the combined presence of elevated TyG-BMI and LDL-C resulted in a substantially higher risk of MASLD compared to the risk associated with either factor alone (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the predictive plot demonstrated that the association between TyG-BMI and MASLD probability was notably steeper in individuals with LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L compared to those with lower levels (Fig. 2B). Quantitative analysis of these interactions is summarized in Table 3. A significant multiplicative interaction was observed (OR = 3.13, 95% CI 1.09–9.03, P = 0.03), suggesting that LDL-C significantly modifies the association between TyG-BMI and MASLD risk. Regarding the additive interaction, the attributable proportion (AP) was 0.51 (95% CI 0.05–0.97), indicating that 51% of the MASLD risk in individuals with both high TyG-BMI and high LDL-C could be attributed to the interaction between these two factors.

Incremental discriminative value of TyG-BMI compared to BMI and TyG

The discriminative performance of TyG-BMI, BMI, and TyG for prevalent MASLD was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, along with NRI and IDI metrics. Compared to the Baseline Risk Model, which yielded an AUC of 0.7288, the addition of TyG increased the AUC to 0.7597, while the addition of BMI increased it to 0.7741. The incorporation of TyG-BMI into the baseline model resulted in the highest discriminative performance, yielding an AUC of 0.7920 (Fig. 3A). Further quantitative assessment of model improvement is summarized in Table 4. The addition of TyG-BMI to the baseline model resulted in a significant continuous NRI of 0.6088 (95% CI 0.4210–0.7966, P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.0935 (95% CI 0.0642–0.1229, P < 0.001). For comparison, the addition of BMI yielded a lower continuous NRI of 0.5144 and an IDI of 0.0631, while the addition of TyG resulted in an NRI of 0.3733 and an IDI of 0.0512. The calibration of the models was assessed through calibration plots. The calibration curve for the TyG-BMI-enhanced model (Fig. 3B) demonstrated a high degree of agreement between the predicted probability of MASLD and the actual observed prevalence. The curve remained closely aligned with the 45-degree ideal diagonal line across the entire range of predicted probabilities, indicating minimal systematic bias in the model’s predictions. Finally, DCA was employed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the models. As illustrated in Fig. 3C, the model incorporating TyG-BMI (Reference model + TyG-BMI, blue line) provided a higher net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (approximately 0.25–0.75) compared to the baseline risk model and models supplemented with TyG or BMI individually.

Sensitivity analysis in the WMU cohort

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of our findings. First, sensitivity analysis with additional adjustment for BMI in the multivariable model confirmed the independent predictive value of TyG-BMI for MASLD (Model 4: OR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, P = 0.002; Supplementary Table 2). Second, the association remained consistent when participants were categorized according to TyG-BMI quartiles (Supplementary Table 3) and quintiles (Supplementary Table 4), with a significant trend observed in both models (P for trend < 0.001). Third, a sensitivity analysis using complete-case data prior to multiple imputation yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Model 4: OR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03, P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 5). Finally, after excluding extreme values of TyG-BMI, the findings remained robust, indicating that the observed association was not driven by outliers (T3: Model 4: OR = 6.93, 95% CI 3.68–13.04, P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 6). In all sensitivity models, the P for trend remained significantly below 0.01, further confirming the stability of the observed associations.

External validation and robustness of TyG-BMI

To assess the generalizability and clinical utility of TyG-BMI, a comprehensive validation framework was implemented using the NAGALA cohort (N = 306). The baseline characteristics of this cohort, stratified by TyG-BMI tertiles, are detailed in Supplementary Table 7, showing that participants in the highest tertile (T3) had significantly higher BMI, ALT, and TG levels (P < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that TyG-BMI was independently and strongly associated with MASLD risk (Supplementary Table 8). Notably, participants in the highest tertile (T3) exhibited a 12.53-fold higher risk of MASLD compared to those in T1 (OR = 12.53, 95% CI 5.41–29.01; P < 0.001) after adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle factors, ALT, and HbA1c. Second, RCS analysis in the NAGALA cohort confirmed a significant linear dose–response relationship between TyG-BMI and the probability of MASLD (P for overall < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 7). Third, the incremental discriminative value of TyG-BMI was evaluated using three nested assessment models (Supplementary Fig. 8). In the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, adding TyG-BMI to the reference model achieved a significantly higher AUC compared to both the BMI-augmented and TyG-augmented models (Supplementary Fig. 8A). The calibration of the model was assessed through calibration plots, which demonstrated excellent agreement between the predicted probability of MASLD and the actual observed prevalence (Supplementary Fig. 8B). Furthermore, DCA illustrated that the inclusion of TyG-BMI provided the highest clinical net benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 8C). These findings were further supported by significant improvements in NRI (0.8855) and IDI (0.1719) analyses (both P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 9).

Finally, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of these findings. The strong association between TyG-BMI and MASLD remained consistent after the exclusion of extreme values in the NAGALA cohort (Supplementary Table 10), reinforcing the stability of TyG-BMI as a reliable predictor of MASLD across different population contexts. Consistent and potent associations were observed when TyG-BMI was analyzed by quartiles (Supplementary Table 11) and quintiles (Supplementary Table 12).

Discussion

In this dual-cohort study, we integrated data-driven feature selection with interaction analyses to investigate non-invasive indicators associated with MASLD in patients with T2DM. Utilizing an algorithmic screening approach across a multidimensional clinical dataset, TyG-BMI—a composite index reflecting both insulin resistance-related metabolic status and morphological adiposity—was consistently selected as a candidate indicator associated with MASLD. Furthermore, our analysis identified a significant multiplicative interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C. This statistical interaction may be interpreted in the context of the classical “two-hit” hypothesis and the more recent multiple-hit framework of fatty liver disease, in which insulin resistance-related metabolic dysfunction, adiposity, and adverse lipid profiles may jointly characterize a higher-risk phenotype [30, 31]. However, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating and should not be viewed as direct evidence of a biological mechanism. The stability of these associations was subsequently verified in an independent external cohort (NAGALA). The inclusion of TyG-BMI into the conventional reference model provided significant incremental predictive value, as evidenced by improvements in NRI and IDI, along with enhanced net clinical benefit. Overall, these findings highlight the utility of incorporating multidimensional metabolic parameters into risk stratification frameworks for MASLD.

Although recent epidemiological efforts have explored various composite indices for MASLD assessment [12, 32–34], the reliance on predefined empirical marker selection often introduces analytical bias and obscures the relative determinative weight of multidimensional clinical variables. Specifically, TyG-related indices have been associated with mortality among individuals with NAFLD or MASLD [35], with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease among NAFLD patients [36], and with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in adults with MASLD [37]. Similar TyG-related prognostic signals have also been reported in metabolic syndrome [38]. Furthermore, a substantial conceptual gap persists in existing literature regarding the complex interplay among these metabolic parameters. Previous risk models predominantly evaluate variables in isolation, thereby neglecting the synergistic pathogenic cross-talk between systemic insulin resistance and specific atherogenic lipid fractions. To address these methodological and conceptual limitations, the current analysis used a prespecified feature-selection framework to evaluate TyG-BMI and related clinical indicators within a broad clinical spectrum. More critically, by delineating the interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C, this study extends beyond conventional single-marker assessments and supports the value of joint metabolic-lipid profiling for MASLD risk stratification. Rather than indicating a proven pathogenic synergy, this finding highlights a subgroup in whom concurrent elevations in TyG-BMI and LDL-C may signal greater MASLD burden and warrant closer clinical attention.

The observed association between TyG-BMI and MASLD risk in patients with T2DM may relate to its ability to integrate insulin resistance-related metabolic status with morphological adiposity. While the TyG index serves as a validated proxy for systemic IR [11, 12], it does not capture the adiposity burden reflected by BMI. Recent evidence that TyG combined with obesity indicators is associated with cardiovascular disease supports the clinical relevance of integrating metabolic and anthropometric domains [39]. Comparative work on different insulin-resistance surrogates [40] and the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) [41] also suggests that composite IR markers carry prognostic information across cardiometabolic populations. By incorporating BMI, TyG-BMI may therefore capture both metabolic dysfunction and adiposity-related risk more directly than either component alone. This interpretation is consistent with established evidence linking insulin resistance and excess adiposity to increased free fatty acid delivery, enhanced de novo lipogenesis, and ectopic hepatic lipid accumulation [9, 10, 13]. Thus, TyG-BMI may serve as a practical composite marker of the metabolic and adiposity burden relevant to hepatic steatosis in the diabetic milieu.

Furthermore, the significant multiplicative interaction observed between TyG-BMI and LDL-C suggests that LDL-C may help distinguish higher-risk individuals among those with elevated TyG-BMI. This finding can be considered alongside the classical “two-hit” hypothesis and the more recent multiple-hit framework of fatty liver disease, in which insulin resistance, adiposity, and adverse lipid profiles may jointly contribute to hepatic steatosis and related liver injury [30, 31]. Prior mechanistic studies have linked LDL-C and oxidized LDL to hepatic lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and inflammatory signaling [9, 10, 14], providing biological context for the observed epidemiological interaction. However, in the present cross-sectional analysis, LDL-C should be interpreted as part of an adverse lipid profile associated with higher MASLD likelihood, rather than as evidence of a proven causal lipotoxic pathway. The importance of lipid-related risk is also supported by recent MASLD work showing that the atherogenic index of plasma is associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [42]. Together, these findings support the clinical value of evaluating TyG-BMI and LDL-C jointly, rather than relying solely on isolated metabolic or lipid markers.

Several methodological and analytical strengths reinforce the robustness of these findings. First, the selection of TyG-BMI for further evaluation was grounded in a data-driven feature-selection framework. By applying the Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression to the same candidate predictor set, this study reduced reliance on empirical variable selection and allowed clinical parameters to be compared within a multidimensional dataset. However, because several TyG-related indices share overlapping mathematical components, these results should be interpreted as showing consistent predictive support within the present datasets, rather than definitive superiority of TyG-BMI over all related indices. Second, a significant contribution of this analysis is the rigorous quantification of the synergistic interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C. Moving beyond isolated association studies, the assessment of additive interaction through metrics such as relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and AP provides a more granular understanding of how insulin resistance and lipid load disproportionately escalate MASLD risk. More recent evidence on the C-reactive protein-TyG index further indicates that inflammatory-metabolic composites may be associated with MASLD and long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [43]. Third, the reliability and generalizability of the results were appraised through external validation in a large-scale, independent population (the Japanese NAGALA cohort). The consistency of the association across distinct ethnic and clinical settings underscores the potential trans-ethnic utility of the TyG-BMI-based framework. Finally, the clinical relevance of the proposed model was thoroughly evaluated using comprehensive metrics of incremental value, including continuous NRI, IDI, and DCA [27–29]. These analyses suggest that the integration of TyG-BMI may improve risk reclassification and net clinical benefit beyond statistical significance. Because AUC and NRI capture different aspects of model performance, the relatively large continuous NRI should be interpreted alongside changes in AUC, calibration, IDI, and DCA. In addition, category-free NRI may be sensitive to small changes in predicted probabilities, particularly when clinically established MASLD risk categories are unavailable.

Strengths and limitations

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because the primary analysis was based on observational and cross-sectional data, causal relationships among TyG-BMI, LDL-C, and MASLD cannot be inferred. To assess reproducibility, we validated the main findings in an independent population-based cohort, where the results were generally consistent. However, external validation does not establish temporality or exclude residual confounding. Accordingly, the observed interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C should be interpreted as a statistical interaction and a hypothesis-generating finding, rather than as proof of a biological “double-hit” mechanism.

Second, MASLD was diagnosed by abdominal ultrasonography rather than liver biopsy or magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF). Although ultrasonography is widely used in clinical and epidemiological studies, prior biopsy-referenced studies summarized in previous reviews indicate that B-mode ultrasonography has limited sensitivity but relatively high specificity for mild steatosis [44, 45]. Thus, ultrasound-based diagnosis is less likely to be dominated by false-positive MASLD classification, but mild steatosis may have been missed. Importantly, such misclassification may not be entirely random. If participants with higher TyG-BMI had more severe steatosis, MASLD may have been more readily detected in this group, whereas mild steatosis among participants with lower TyG-BMI may have gone undetected. This detection-related misclassification could have biased the observed association away from the null and may have overestimated the true association. Because both cohorts relied on ultrasonography for MASLD assessment, this limitation could not be fully addressed by external validation. Confirmatory studies using MRI-PDFF, transient elastography, or biopsy-based assessment are warranted [46].

Finally, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. In addition, alcohol intake was assessed via self-reported questionnaires and modeled as a binary covariate; however, heavy alcohol consumption was excluded according to predefined sex-specific thresholds. Although we adjusted for a broad range of clinical covariates and used prespecified feature-selection procedures to compare candidate predictors, insufficiently measured factors, such as dietary patterns, physical activity, and genetic susceptibility, may still have influenced the results. Future prospective studies with more detailed lifestyle, genetic, and imaging data are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, TyG-BMI serves as an accessible non-invasive indicator for MASLD risk stratification in the T2DM population. The observed interaction between TyG-BMI and LDL-C suggests that concurrent metabolic-adiposity burden and adverse lipid status may identify individuals with higher MASLD risk. Incorporating this multidimensional framework into routine clinical practice may facilitate early identification and support targeted metabolic interventions for this high-risk population.
