Optimal management of giant cell tumor in the tibial plateau: A case report on curettage and cement reinforcement
Authors: Joseph Mouawad, Mohamad Omar Honeine, Christopher Sarkis, Youssef Jamaleddine, Sarah Hajjar, Chahine Assi, Wael Bayoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is a benign yet locally aggressive neoplasm that commonly arises in the epiphyseal metaphyseal region of long bones, particularly around the knee, where management is challenged by the need to achieve complete tumor excision while preserving joint function in young, active individuals. We describe a 26-year-old female who presented with acute left knee pain and mechanical locking after squatting during exercise. Imaging demonstrated a well-defined 1.7 × 1.7 × 1.7 cm eccentric lesion in the posterior medial tibial plateau with minimal cortical breakthrough and surrounding marrow edema, and CT guided biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of GCTB. She underwent surgery through a posterior Löbbenhofer approach, including meticulous curettage, high speed burring, and phenol application. To shield the articular cartilage from the thermal effects of PMMA cement and avoid long term subchondral stiffness, a protective layer of autologous morselized bone graft was interposed between the cement and the joint surface. Recovery was uneventful, and this single-stage technique provided definitive management with no need for further surgery, ensuring structural support while minimizing recurrence risk. This case highlights the value of combining extended curettage with a graft cement interface to optimize joint preservation in periarticular GCTB and underscores the need for future studies evaluating long term radiological and functional outcomes of such hybrid reconstructions.

Introduction

Bone giant cell tumors (GCTs) are benign tumors that are known to be locally aggressive; they typically afflict young individuals. Approximately 70% of cases are identified in individuals aged 20-40, with its incidence peaking in the third decade of life [1]. GCTB is more commonly seen in females, as indicated by most datasets. Although it can affect any bone, 50%-65% of cases are localized to the knee, specifically in the metaphyseal-epiphyseal regions of the distal femur and proximal tibia. Nonspecific symptoms such as pain, edema, and restricted movement in the afflicted joint are common in GCTB. Osteolytic lesions in the epiphyseal-metaphyseal regions of the bone are a crucial characteristic of radiographs and MRIs, which are utilized for diagnosis. Histologically, mononuclear cells are what define the tumor and affect its activity [2]. However, a range of therapeutic strategies is suggested due to the tumor's erratic behavior, which doesn't always correspond to its histology or radiographic aspect [3] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Graphic abstract.Fig 1 – dummy alt text

The best course of treatment for GCTB is hotly debated, particularly considering the disease's tendency to return locally following surgery [4]. The rates of local recurrence after surgery for GCTB vary considerably, with estimates ranging from 3% to 51% [5]. Furthermore, malignant transformation and lung metastases happen in 2.4% and 1%-9% of GCTB patients, respectively [6].

The goal of various treatment approaches is to remove the tumor while maintaining joint function [7]. Surgery is the only effective treatment for GCTB; however, it is associated with a relatively high rate of secondary osteoarthritis due to the frequent involvement of the epiphysis and subchondral bone [8]. Regarding the recurrence rate, it drops to about 10%-20% with high-speed burr and adjuvant therapy, including bone cement and phenol, which offer thermal effects and complete soft tissue removal, compared to 50% to 60% with traditional intra-lesional curettage [1].

Some surgeons advise bone grafting between the cement and the cartilage or subchondral bone layer in order to restore the subchondral architecture since the heat effects of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) might harm articular surfaces [9]. The main objective of GCTB therapy is to eradicate the tumor while maintaining joint function [10]. However, due to the young age group typically affected and the lack of clear treatment guidelines, there is ongoing debate about the most effective management strategy for GCTB.

In this case report, we used a novel definitive, single-stage surgical approach to reduce the risk of recurrence while also protecting the joint surface from further surgical consequences, avoiding the need for a staged bone grafting procedure.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old female presented to our clinic with an acute onset of left knee pain that began suddenly while squatting during a gym session. The pain was associated with a locking sensation and was aggravated by deep knee flexion and weight-bearing activities. There was no history of direct trauma. The patient's medical and surgical history was unremarkable.

On physical examination, neurovascular findings in the left lower limb were normal. Mild medial joint line tenderness was noted, with no visible swelling or effusion. A McMurray test was performed to rule out meniscal injury, given the mechanism of pain onset, and was negative.

The posterior capsule was incised to access the lesion adjacent to the articular surface. This case highlights the importance of a thorough diagnostic workup and a multi-modal, joint-sparing surgical approach in the management of giant cell tumors, emphasizing the need for vigilant postoperative surveillance.

Discussion

The management of giant cell tumors (GCT) of the knee requires a carefully planned, multidisciplinary approach that balances complete tumor eradication with the preservation of joint function. This involves the integration of diagnostic imaging, histopathological confirmation, surgical excision, adjuvant therapy, and reconstruction techniques aimed at minimizing recurrence and long-term complications [3].

Imaging plays a central role in both diagnosis and surgical planning. While MRI is essential for assessing the subchondral distance and bone marrow edema, CT scans are important for identifying specific cortical breaches, which help determine the most appropriate surgical approach. CT-guided biopsies not only confirm the diagnosis but also guide intraoperative navigation, particularly when cortical defects are present. Accessing the lesion through these existing cortical windows allows for direct visualization and optimal removal of the tumor, thereby reducing the risk of incomplete excision and recurrence [3,11]. In our case report, a CT-guided biopsy was performed, which confirmed the diagnosis of GCT and identified a minimal posterior cortical breakthrough. This breach guided the choice of a posterior surgical approach for optimal tumor access.

The Campanacci grading system is commonly used to assess tumor aggressiveness and guide treatment decisions. Grade I lesions exhibit well-defined margins without cortical destruction or soft tissue involvement; Grade II lesions have expanded but defined margins with thinning or focal cortical breach; and Grade III lesions present with cortical destruction, indistinct borders, and soft tissue extension [11].

Based on imaging, the tumor in this case was classified as Campanacci Grade II, as it demonstrated definable margins with a minimal 2 mm cortical breakthrough and extensive bone marrow edema, without extraosseous soft tissue extension. The radiologic differential diagnosis for a small epiphyseal-subchondral tibial plateau lesion in a young adult includes chondroblastoma (which typically features a sclerotic rim), clear cell chondrosarcoma, and intraosseous ganglion. The presence of significant bone marrow edema, the lack of a sclerotic rim, and the specific subchondral location ultimately favored GCTB over these differentials.

Surgical strategies are largely influenced by tumor grade and location relative to the joint surface. While wide en bloc resection is the preferred treatment for Grade III lesions due to its low recurrence rate (∼2%) [10], this approach compromises functional outcomes and requires reconstruction with prostheses, which carry risks such as loosening, infection, and periprosthetic fracture [5]. In the presented case, en bloc resection was avoided due to the tumor’s proximity to the articular cartilage and the goal of preserving the subchondral bone in a young, active patient. Joint reconstruction with a mega-prosthesis was not considered appropriate.

Intralesional curettage remains the preferred treatment for Campanacci Grade I and II lesions and select Grade III lesions, especially when combined with adjuvants. Curettage alone is associated with recurrence rates up to 33%, which can be reduced to 22% when combined with adjuvants like bone cement [2]. Incorporating a high-speed burr further decreases recurrence to 12%–25% by enhancing tumor bed clearance [8]. Additionally, the use of curved curettes and dental mirrors facilitates visualization during deep cavity curettage [4]. It is important to note that complete mechanical removal of the tumor is still the most critical factor for success [4]; adjuvants are supplementary. In this case, meticulous curettage was performed using straight and curved curettes along with high-speed burring, while preserving the cartilage layer overlying the lesion. A power burr grinds an extra 1–2 mm of bone from the cavity surface, helping eliminate tumor remnants in small pockets [12]. A 2010 meta-analysis confirmed that curettage with high-speed burring significantly lowers recurrence versus curettage without a burr. A theoretical concern is that burring could push tumor cells into the circulation or surrounding tissue, but evidence suggests the benefit (in terms of local control) outweighs this risk [13].

Adjuvant therapies such as phenol, hydrogen peroxide, liquid nitrogen, and PMMA bone cement are employed to reduce microscopic residual disease. Phenolization causes protein coagulation and tumor cell necrosis upon contact. Typically, phenol is applied by soaking gauze in phenol and swabbing or pouring it onto all surfaces for 3-6 minutes, avoiding contact with surrounding soft tissue [13]. Dürr et al. [14] documented that adding phenol reduced their curettage recurrence from 42.9% to 9.1%. On the other hand, some reports found that phenol did not significantly change outcomes if curettage was adequate. Trieb et al observed similar recurrence rates with or without phenol [15]. PMMA provides immediate structural support and exerts a thermal cytotoxic effect, contributing to lower recurrence rates. In a large multicenter analysis by Becker et al., intralesional treatment without any adjuvant had a 49% recurrence, whereas using PMMA dropped recurrence to ∼22% [15]. Another series by Gaston et al. found curettage + cement had a 14.3% recurrence rate, significantly better than ∼29.7% with curettage without cement [16]. However, its exothermic reaction can damage adjacent cartilage, especially in lesions close to the subchondral area [1]. To address this concern, phenol was carefully injected into the cavity using a syringe to avoid damaging surrounding soft tissues. Furthermore, a protective layer of morselized autologous bone graft was interposed between the cement and the articular cartilage, minimizing the thermal effect of PMMA on joint cartilage. The distance between the cement and the articular surface is a crucial determinant of long-term joint health. Tumors with less than 10 mm of subchondral bone remaining have an increased risk of postoperative osteoarthritis. When less than 5 mm is preserved, a multi-layer technique using bone graft beneath the cartilage followed by cement filling is strongly advised [4,6,9]. This strategy was implemented in our case by inserting cancellous bone graft harvested from the proximal tibia beneath the articular surface, before filling the defect with PMMA. This aimed to preserve cartilage viability while providing mechanical stability.

Although bone grafts (autografts or allografts) support subchondral bone reconstruction, they are associated with higher recurrence rates (26%-65%) and hinder early detection of recurrence on imaging [9]. Conversely, PMMA enhances radiologic surveillance due to the distinct cement-bone interface. The development of a 2 mm sclerotic rim at this interface is an important radiological marker; its absence may signal recurrence [4]. In this case, the lesion was located in a non-weight-bearing area of the tibial plateau, and the use of cement enabled early mobilization without the need for additional fixation. Postoperative radiographs confirmed preservation of the articular surface and complete filling of the defect. Should recurrence occur, repeated curettage combined with PMMA and adjuvants remains an effective salvage strategy with acceptable outcomes [2].

This case highlights the successful application of evidence-based practices in the management of a Campanacci Grade II GCT of the proximal tibia. The use of targeted imaging, safe curettage technique, selective adjuvant use, and single-stage cement-bone graft layering offered optimal tumor control while preserving joint function. We acknowledge that the 8-month follow-up is a limitation of this report; while early functional outcomes are excellent, longer-term surveillance is necessary to definitively evaluate recurrence control.

Conclusion

Giant cell tumor of the bone (GCTB) involving the proximal tibial plateau demands a surgical strategy that ensures oncologic control while preserving joint function. Literature strongly supports the use of extended intralesional curettage with high-speed burring and adjuvant therapies—such as PMMA and phenol—as effective measures to reduce recurrence. The interposition of autologous morselized bone graft between the cement and the articular surface, as adopted in this case, provides thermal protection to the cartilage and structural support to the subchondral bone. Importantly, this technique serves as a definitive treatment, avoiding the need for a second-stage procedure to remove the cement and replace it with bone graft and internal fixation. By enabling early mobilization, maintaining joint integrity, and ensuring oncologic safety, this single-stage approach is particularly advantageous for young patients with lesions in non-weight-bearing zones. This case underscores the value of tailored surgical strategies guided by imaging and intraoperative findings. It also opens new avenues for future research to investigate long-term joint outcomes following cement-graft interface techniques and to explore novel biomaterials that enhance cartilage preservation while maintaining low recurrence rates in GCTB.

Patient perspective

The patient expressed high satisfaction with the treatment and recovery process, describing the postoperative period as smooth and nearly painless. She reported regaining full knee mobility earlier than expected, without any functional limitations in daily activities.
