Akkermansia muciniphila attenuates apatinib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma through NF-κB-mediated reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages
Authors: Xiaoxiao He, Hongying Gan, Fan Wu, Yijie Wang, Heng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains among the most lethal malignancies worldwide, and acquired resistance to targeted therapy limits durable clinical benefit. Apatinib, a selective vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 inhibitor, is used in advanced HCC, but the contribution of the gut microbiota to Apatinib resistance remains incompletely defined. This study aimed to determine whether gut microbiota dysbiosis, particularly loss of Akkermansia muciniphila (Akk), contributes to an Apatinib-resistant HCC phenotype and to evaluate whether Akk-derived factors modulate macrophage NF-κB signaling.MethodsA Hepa1-6 syngeneic subcutaneous HCC model was established in C57BL/6 mice. The apatinib-resistant phenotype was operationally defined by continued tumor growth under apatinib pressure together with an ex vivo right shift in the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Fecal microbiota were analyzed by 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene sequencing, Akk abundance was validated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and inferred microbial functional potential was explored using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2. Macrophage polarization, tumor-level NF-κB signaling, live Akk oral supplementation, and bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) responses to Akk supernatant or pasteurized Akk were assessed.ResultsApatinib-resistant mice showed gut dysbiosis characterized by reduced microbial diversity, distinct beta-diversity clustering, and marked depletion of Akk. Resistant tumors exhibited reduced M1-like and increased M2-like macrophage fractions, together with lower phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα in whole-tumor lysates. Oral live Akk administration restored intestinal Akk abundance, reduced tumor volume and weight, re-established M1-like macrophage polarization, and reactivated tumor-level NF-κB signaling. In BMDMs exposed to apatinib-resistant Hepa1-6-conditioned medium, Akk supernatant and pasteurized Akk partially restored NF-κB phosphorylation and attenuated M2-like transcriptional skewing.ConclusionsAkk depletion accompanies an apatinib-resistant HCC phenotype and Akk-centered microbial modulation may help counteract resistance by reshaping macrophage-associated antitumor immunity. These findings support further validation of Akk-derived signals in community-level microbiome models and clinically relevant HCC settings.

Introduction

Primary liver cancer is diagnosed in more than one million individuals each year, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents its predominant histological subtype, ranking second among all cancers for global mortality (1,2). Viral hepatitis, chronic alcohol intake, aflatoxin contamination, and metabolic-associated liver disease are firmly established as drivers of hepatocarcinogenesis (3,4). Because early-stage HCC is typically clinically silent, a substantial proportion of patients are identified only at advanced stages, when curative locoregional options—resection, transplantation, ablation, arterially directed therapies, and radiotherapy—are no longer feasible (5,6). For this population, systemic therapy is the mainstay of disease control.

Deregulated angiogenesis is a hallmark of HCC. The disease is characterized by elevated expression of pro-angiogenic mediators, notably vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), angiopoietin-2, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), which together drive aberrant neovascularization (7). Contemporary molecular therapy therefore centers on VEGF-axis blockade, immune checkpoint inhibition, and rational combinations of anti-VEGF and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Apatinib, a small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) with high selectivity for VEGFR-2, suppresses tumor vascularization and has been shown to prolong overall survival as a second-line or later option in advanced HCC (8). Real-world analyses by Zheng et al. corroborate its efficacy and tolerability (9), and head-to-head data indicate that apatinib delivers comparable progression-free and overall survival to sorafenib while achieving a superior objective response rate (10). Mechanistically, apatinib restrains HCC cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis by disrupting VEGF and PI3K/AKT signaling (11). Despite these advances, the emergence of resistance substantially limits the long-term utility of antiangiogenic therapy (12). In this study, the apatinib-resistant phenotype was defined as a state in which tumors continue to grow under sustained apatinib exposure and tumor-derived cells exhibit a right-shifted Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)-based half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), reflecting reduced cellular drug sensitivity rather than simple drug suppression in vivo. This phenotype was selected because clinical resistance to VEGFR-2 inhibition is not purely cell-autonomous but is shaped by the tumor microenvironment, host immunity, and systemic factors. Apatinib resistance has been documented across gastric, esophageal, non-small-cell lung, and anaplastic thyroid cancers (13-17), and in HCC has been linked to aberrant RB1 signaling (18). Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of how apatinib resistance arises in HCC is still lacking.

Parallel to these developments in targeted therapy, the intestinal microbiota has emerged as a decisive environmental determinant of host physiology. A growing body of work links gut dysbiosis to the onset and progression of HCC, primarily by sustaining pro-tumorigenic hepatic inflammation and compromising antitumor immunity (19,20). The microbiota also appears to mediate the hepatocarcinogenic effects of dietary insults, as demonstrated by Zhang and colleagues for cholesterol-driven fatty liver cancer (21). Within this community, Akkermansia muciniphila has attracted particular attention as a next-generation probiotic with documented benefits across immune and metabolic disorders (22-24). Akk synergizes with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade in fatty-liver-associated HCC (25), and constrains the transition from nonalcoholic steatohepatitis to HCC by expanding natural killer T cells and curtailing macrophage infiltration (26). Mechanistically, Fan et al. showed that Akk engages TLR2/NF-κB signaling to drive M1-like macrophage polarization and thereby restrain colorectal tumorigenesis (27). Whether Akk or the broader gut community influences HCC responsiveness to apatinib has, however, not been examined.

Motivated by these observations, we set out to determine whether the gut microbiota contributes to HCC resistance to apatinib, to define the resistant phenotype with paired in vivo and ex vivo criteria, to test whether Akk restoration can overcome resistance, and to evaluate whether macrophage-intrinsic NF-κB signaling responds to Akk-derived factors in an ex vivo bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) system. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0975/rc).

Methods

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Committee of Animal Ethics and Experimental Safety of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, in compliance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, institutional and national regulations for the care and use of animals. All animal experiments were conducted at the animal facility of The Central Hospital of Wuhan, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China. The ethical tumor-burden ceiling was set at 2,000 mm3; measurements confirmed that this threshold was not exceeded. Mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia with intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), with death confirmed by apnea, absent heartbeat, and loss of reflex responses. Animal-level n values refer to individual mice, whereas Western blotting, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and ex vivo macrophage assays used independent biological replicates as specified below. Tumor-bearing mice were assigned by simple randomization using a random-number table after tumors became palpable. Investigators performing caliper measurements, flow-cytometric gating, Western blot quantification, and qPCR analysis were blinded to group allocation until the primary data matrix was locked.

Establishment of the apatinib-resistant model

Male C57BL/6 SPF mice aged 8 weeks received subcutaneous injection of 4×106 Hepa1-6 cells into the right upper flank to generate a syngeneic HCC model. Hepa1-6 is a murine hepatoma cell line derived from C57L/J mice that grows reproducibly in immunocompetent C57BL/6 hosts, thereby preserving host-microbiota and host-immune interactions that would be obscured in immunodeficient xenograft systems. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to an apatinib-sensitive (Sen) group, which received no apatinib prior to endpoint analysis, or an apatinib-resistant (Re) group, which was treated by oral gavage with low-dose intermittent apatinib (50–100 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks to model acquisition of resistance (n=6 per group). The first day of apatinib-pressure modelling was defined as day 0, when tumor volumes reached approximately 80–100 mm3. Resistance was operationally defined in vivo as continued tumor growth under ongoing apatinib exposure, evidenced by a positive tumor-growth slope after day 0 rather than complete suppression, and ex vivo as a right-shifted apatinib IC50 in tumor-derived cells. Tumor dimensions were measured with calipers every 2–3 days, and body weight and general condition were monitored to flag treatment-related toxicity. At the experimental endpoint, tumors and fecal samples were harvested for downstream analyses.

Akk oral gavage

For the rescue study, syngeneic HCC tumors were generated as described above and tumor-bearing mice were allocated to three groups (n=6 each): an apatinib-sensitive control group (Sen), a vehicle-treated apatinib-resistant group [phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) + Re], and an apatinib-resistant group receiving live Akk (Akk + Re). Akk (ATCC BAA-835) was propagated in brain-heart infusion broth supplemented with vitamin K/hemin and 0.4% mucin at 37 °C under strict anaerobic conditions, and strain identity was verified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The administered dose of 2×109 colony-forming unit (CFU) was quantified immediately before gavage by anaerobic serial dilution plating on mucin-containing brain-heart infusion agar, cross-checked against OD600-based growth curves generated from the same culture system, and diluted into 0.2 mL anaerobic PBS. Akk + Re animals received daily oral gavage for 14 consecutive days, while PBS + Re animals received an equivalent volume of anaerobic PBS. Tumor growth was tracked throughout the intervention, and tumors and feces were collected at the end of the study for immunophenotyping and molecular analyses.

CCK-8 assay and IC50 determination

Tumors from Sen and Re mice were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and seeded into 96-well plates, then exposed to apatinib at graded concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 µM) in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. After 48 h of incubation, the medium was replaced and CCK-8 reagent (Servicebio Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) was added at 10% v/v. Following a 1-h incubation protected from light, absorbance was read at 492 nm. Dose-response curves were fit in GraphPad Prism 9.0 using nonlinear regression [log(inhibitor) vs. normalized response, variable slope], and the resistance index (RI) was calculated as IC50 (Re)/IC50 (Sen).

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissue was minced and digested with collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) in a 37 °C shaking incubator for 30 min. After filtration through a 70-µm strainer and erythrocyte lysis, single-cell suspensions were stained with a fixable live/dead dye and fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CD45, anti-CD11b, anti-F4/80-APC (clone BM8, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), anti-CD86-PE (clone GL1, Invitrogen), and anti-CD206-FITC (MR6F3, Invitrogen). The gating sequence was defined a priori as time-stable events, singlets by FSC-A/FSC-H, viable CD45+ leukocytes, CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages, and then CD86+ or CD206+ fractions within the macrophage gate. Macrophage subsets were reported as M1-like (F4/80+CD11b+CD86+) and M2-like (F4/80+CD11b+CD206+) states rather than definitive terminal phenotypes, with isotype and fluorescence-minus-one controls included for gating. At least 10,000 viable CD45+ events per sample were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II and analyzed with FlowJo v10.0 by an investigator blinded to group allocation. Western blotting

Tumor tissues or BMDM pellets were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, clarified by centrifugation, and quantified by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred to 0.45-µm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Following a 1-h block in 5% non-fat milk at room temperature, membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-p65 (#3033, 1:1,000, CST, Danvers, USA), p65 (#8242, 1:1,000, CST), phospho-IκBα (Ser32) (#2859, 1:1,000, CST), IκBα (#4814, 1:1,000, CST), and GAPDH (ab9485, 1:5,000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) washes, blots were incubated with species-matched horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000), developed with Clarity Western ECL, and quantified in ImageJ v1.53 (NIH). p-p65/p65 and p-IκBα/IκBα ratios were computed for semi-quantitative comparison, and three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each ex vivo BMDM condition.

Ex vivo BMDM assay with Akk-derived factors

BMDMs were generated from femurs and tibias of male C57BL/6 mice and differentiated for 7 days in macrophage colony-stimulating factor-containing medium. To model macrophage exposure to the resistant tumor secretome, BMDMs were incubated with apatinib-resistant Hepa1-6-conditioned medium (AR-Hepa1-6-CM). Four conditions were analyzed: control medium, AR-Hepa1-6-CM alone, AR-Hepa1-6-CM plus sterile-filtered Akk culture supernatant, and AR-Hepa1-6-CM plus pasteurized Akk. Pasteurization was performed at 70 °C for 30 min, and loss of viability was confirmed by anaerobic culture. Cells were harvested at 2 h for NF-κB Western blotting and at 24 h for polarization-associated qPCR analysis. Three independent BMDM preparations were used for each condition.

Quantitative real-time PCR for BMDM polarization markers

Total RNA was extracted from BMDMs with TRIpure Total RNA Extraction Reagent (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), reverse-transcribed using EntiLink 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ELK Biotechnology), and analyzed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex real-time PCR system using EnTurbo SYBR Green PCR SuperMix (ELK Biotechnology). Relative mRNA levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as the internal reference. M1-like transcripts included inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)/Nos2, Tnf, and Il1b, and M2-like transcripts included Arg1, Mrc1/CD206, and Il10. Primer specificity was verified by single-peak melting curves, and each biological replicate was assayed with technical triplicates.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and bioinformatics

Fecal microbial DNA was extracted with the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The V3-V4 hypervariable region was amplified using the 341F/806R primer pair and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed in QIIME2 (v2021.4), and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were classified against the SILVA database (v138). Alpha-diversity (Shannon and Chao1 indices) and beta-diversity [principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities] were calculated in QIIME2. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) threshold of 2.0 and P<0.05, and differential abundance of Akkermansia was corroborated by Mann-Whitney U tests. Functional potential was inferred with PICRUSt2 from 16S rRNA profiles; these outputs were treated as predicted metagenomic potential rather than measured microbial gene content or pathway activity. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Ortholog counts were mapped to pathways, and pathway-level comparisons, including NF-κB signaling and immune-related pathways, were performed in STAMP with Welch’s t-test and false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Confirmation of Akk colonization

To verify engraftment of orally administered Akk, fecal samples collected before and after the 2-week gavage regimen were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR using Akk-specific primers (forward, 5'-CAGCACGTGAAGGTGGGGAC-3'; reverse, 5'-CCTTGCGGTTGGCTTCAGAT-3'), with universal 16S rRNA primers serving as the internal reference.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For animal experiments, n denotes individual mice and was set at n=6 per group to provide adequate precision for detecting large treatment-associated differences in tumor burden while limiting animal use under ARRIVE principles. Flow cytometry was performed on individual tumors with n=3 representative biological samples per group for phenotyping figures, Western blotting used n=3 independent biological replicates, and BMDM qPCR used n=3 independent BMDM preparations with technical triplicates. Two-group comparisons used two-sided Student’s t-test when data distribution and variance were compatible with parametric testing, and multi-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Tumor growth over time was analyzed descriptively from day 0 to endpoint by comparing growth slopes and endpoint tumor burden. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9.0 and SPSS 26.0.

Results

Apatinib resistance is accompanied by gut microbial dysbiosis and depletion of Akkermansia muciniphila

Given the growing appreciation that commensal bacteria shape responses to oncological therapy, we next asked whether the intestinal community is remodeled during the acquisition of apatinib resistance. Figure 1 was organized to first present the microbiome signature and then the tumor-level validation of resistance, allowing the microbial and pharmacological phenotypes to be interpreted together. Species-level quantification showed a substantial loss of Akk in Re samples (P<0.01), with individual Re mice displaying Akk abundances generally an order of magnitude below those of Sen counterparts (Figure 1A). LEfSe analysis with an LDA threshold of 2.0 identified Akkermansia as the most discriminative biomarker of the Sen state (LDA =4.23), together with Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Faecalibacterium, whereas Enterobacteriaceae, Bacteroides, Clostridium, and Escherichia were enriched in Re animals (Figure 1B). 16S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal samples revealed an alpha-diversity contraction in Re mice, with the Shannon index falling from 4.87±0.38 to 3.24±0.45 and the Chao1 index from 623.78±67.89 to 412.56±52.34 (both P<0.01) (Figure 1C). Beta-diversity analyses echoed this restructuring: PCoA on Bray-Curtis distances resolved Sen and Re samples into separated clusters [permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) R2=0.456, P<0.01] (Figure 1D). These data support the conclusion that apatinib resistance is accompanied by reproducible gut microbial dysbiosis, with Akk depletion representing the most prominent taxonomic feature.

Establishment and validation of an apatinib-resistant HCC model

To quantify the cellular loss of sensitivity, we dissociated tumors from both groups and performed CCK-8-based dose-response assays across a 0–80 µM range of apatinib. Nonlinear regression yielded an IC50 of 8.47±1.23 µM for Sen cells and 45.82±6.54 µM for Re cells (P<0.001), corresponding to a resistance index of 5.41 (Figure 1E). Whereas Sen-derived cells exhibited a steep drop in viability with increasing drug concentration, Re-derived cells maintained higher viability across the pharmacologically relevant range, formally establishing reduced apatinib sensitivity at the cellular level.

To interrogate the biology underlying apatinib resistance, we generated paired Sen and Re cohorts by implanting Hepa1-6 cells into the right upper flank of C57BL/6 mice and subjecting Re animals to 2 weeks of low-dose intermittent apatinib beginning at day 0. The in vivo resistant state was not defined by larger endpoint tumors relative to untreated Sen animals; instead, it was defined by continued growth under ongoing apatinib pressure combined with the ex vivo IC50 shift described above. Endpoint quantification showed a lower tumor volume in the Re group (186.72±34.28 mm3) than in the Sen group (449.53±68.66 mm3) (Figure 1F), with a parallel reduction in tumor weight (Figure 1G), consistent with residual pharmacological suppression during resistance modelling. Representative macroscopic images of excised tumors and tumor-bearing mice showed the corresponding gross dimensions (Figure 1H,1I). Thus, the Re group reflects a drug-exposed, reduced-sensitivity state rather than simple uncontrolled tumor expansion in the absence of therapy.

A microbiome-to-tumor NF-κB axis: inferred pathway suppression converges with tumor-level NF-κB inactivation and macrophage repolarization

Because NF-κB participates in the balance between M1-like and M2-like macrophage states (28) and is a key transcriptional regulator of macrophage inflammatory programming (29,30), we examined the intratumoral macrophage compartment by flow cytometry. Re tumors displayed a clear repolarization: the M1-like (F4/80+CD11b+CD86+) fraction contracted from 28.67%±3.42% in Sen tumors to 12.34%±2.15% in Re tumors (P<0.01), while the M2-like (F4/80+CD11b+CD206+) fraction expanded from 15.78%±2.87% to 31.56%±4.23% (P<0.01) (Figure 2A). These markers were interpreted as polarization-associated states rather than fixed macrophage lineages, in recognition of tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) heterogeneity.

We therefore probed NF-κB activation in whole-tumor lysates as a tissue-level correlate of the immune phenotype. Western blotting revealed reduced phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα in Re tumors relative to Sen tumors (Figure 2B). Semi-quantification confirmed a lower p-p65/p65 ratio in Re tumors (0.32±0.08 vs. 0.78±0.12 in Sen, P<0.01) and a corresponding reduction in the p-IκBα/IκBα ratio (0.28±0.06 vs. 0.71±0.09, P<0.01). Because whole-tumor lysates include tumor cells, endothelial cells, stromal cells, and immune cells, these blots were interpreted as tumor-level pathway readouts and were integrated with flow cytometry and the ex vivo BMDM assay rather than used alone as proof of TAM-specific NF-κB suppression.

To connect the dysbiotic community to events within the tumor, we inferred the functional potential of the resistant microbiome and then asked whether the predicted changes were echoed at the tumor level. PICRUSt2-based metagenome inference indicated reduced predicted abundance of immune-relevant KEGG pathways in the Re microbiome, including NF-κB signaling pathway (ko04064), antigen processing and presentation (ko04612), Toll-like receptor signaling (ko04620), and chemokine signaling (ko04062) (all P<0.05) (Figure 2C). These outputs represent inferred metagenomic potential from 16S rRNA profiles and do not directly measure microbial genes, metabolites, or host pathway activity. Nevertheless, the convergence of predicted microbial functional potential, tumor-level NF-κB phosphorylation, and macrophage polarization prompted direct testing of macrophage-intrinsic pathway modulation in BMDMs.

Oral supplementation with Akk restores apatinib sensitivity and re-activates the NF-κB-M1-like axis

We next tested whether replenishing the deficient commensal could reverse the resistant phenotype. Tumor-bearing Re mice received daily oral gavage of live Akk (2×109 CFU) or anaerobic PBS for 14 days, with Sen animals included as a reference. Fecal qPCR confirmed robust engraftment: Akk abundance in the Akk + Re group rose approximately 8.5-fold relative to the PBS + Re group (P<0.001).

We then asked whether this restoration traced back to the immune and signaling axes identified in earlier sections. Western blotting revealed that the suppressed NF-κB activity of PBS + Re tumors (p-p65/p65 0.35±0.07; p-IκBα/IκBα 0.31±0.06) was largely reversed in Akk + Re tumors (p-p65/p65 0.72±0.11; p-IκBα/IκBα 0.68±0.09; both P<0.05) (Figure 3A). Flow cytometry showed a near-doubling of the intratumoral M1-like fraction in Akk + Re animals compared with PBS + Re controls (25.43%±3.67% vs. 13.12%±2.34%, P<0.01), with a concomitant contraction of the M2-like fraction (Figure 3B), recapitulating the polarization profile of Sen tumors.

Quantification confirmed a reduction in both tumor volume (Akk + Re vs. PBS + Re: 89.34±15.23 vs. 178.45±28.67 mm3, P<0.01) (Figure 3C) and tumor weight (Akk + Re vs. PBS + Re: 0.21±0.05 vs. 0.42±0.08 g, P<0.01) (Figure 3D), indicating that Akk supplementation restored meaningful responsiveness to apatinib. Gross inspection revealed that PBS + Re tumors retained the bulk characteristic of resistant disease, whereas Akk + Re tumors were visibly smaller (Figure 3E). Taken together, these experiments indicate that oral Akk supplementation attenuates HCC resistance to apatinib in association with re-engagement of tumor-level NF-κB signaling and restoration of M1-like macrophage polarization.

Akk-derived factors modulate NF-κB activation and polarization-associated transcripts in BMDMs

To address whether the NF-κB-polarization axis could be modulated within macrophages, we performed an ex vivo BMDM assay using AR-Hepa1-6-CM with or without Akk-derived factors. AR-Hepa1-6-CM markedly suppressed NF-κB phosphorylation in BMDMs, reducing p-p65/p65 from 1.00±0.10 in control cells to 0.10±0.04 (one-way ANOVA F=49.121, P<0.001; control vs. stimulus P=0.002) and p-IκBα/IκBα from 1.00±0.25 to 0.16±0.07 (F=13.363, P=0.002; control vs. stimulus P=0.022). Akk supernatant partially restored both readouts (p-p65/p65 0.58±0.15, P=0.024 vs. stimulus; p-IκBα/IκBα 0.68±0.15, P=0.015 vs. stimulus), while pasteurized Akk showed a weaker but directionally similar effect (Figure 4A-4C). Consistently, AR-Hepa1-6-CM decreased M1-like transcripts iNOS, Tnf, and IL-1β and increased M2-like transcripts Arg1, Mrc1, and IL-10. Akk supernatant increased iNOS (0.78±0.02 vs. 0.40±0.03), Tnf (0.77±0.05 vs. 0.24±0.03), and IL-1β (0.67±0.03 vs. 0.35±0.04), while reducing Arg1 (1.76±0.12 vs. 3.42±0.09), Mrc1 (2.13±0.16 vs. 3.66±0.19), and IL-10 (1.74±0.14 vs. 3.41±0.13) relative to stimulus alone (all P<0.001 by ANOVA; Figure 4D,4E). These ex vivo data support macrophage-intrinsic responsiveness to Akk-derived soluble factors and show that pasteurized Akk retains partial activity, consistent with a mechanism that is not solely dependent on live bacterial colonization. Discussion

The central finding of this study is that an acquired shift in the intestinal microbiota, most distinctively the depletion of Akk, accompanies apatinib-resistant HCC, and that restoring this commensal in vivo is associated with reinstated drug responsiveness. The evidence chain is intentionally framed with appropriate specificity: flow cytometry reveals M2-like macrophage repolarization within resistant tumors, Western blotting identifies reduced NF-κB phosphorylation at the whole-tumor level, PICRUSt2 provides hypothesis-generating inference of reduced microbial functional potential related to immune pathways, and the BMDM assay demonstrates macrophage-intrinsic modulation by Akk-derived factors. This cross-scale convergence supports a gut-tumor immunological axis without overstating PICRUSt2 predictions or whole-tumor blots as direct TAM-specific proof.

Generating a tractable, reproducible in vivo model is a prerequisite for the mechanistic study of acquired resistance. The intermittent, low-dose apatinib regimen employed here builds on established approaches in the field (31), and the resulting model satisfied both operational criteria for resistance: Re tumors continued to grow under apatinib exposure after day 0, and Re-derived cells exhibited a more than five-fold shift in IC50 (RI =5.41). The smaller endpoint size of Re tumors relative to untreated Sen tumors therefore reflects residual on-treatment drug suppression and does not by itself define resistance. The resistant phenotype was instead assigned by the combination of positive in vivo growth under drug pressure and reduced ex vivo cellular sensitivity. This clarification is important because resistance to antiangiogenic therapy is usually expressed as incomplete suppression, regrowth, or diminished cellular drug response rather than absolute tumor size superiority over untreated controls.

TAMs are crucial orchestrators of the tumor microenvironment, but their polarization states are plastic and context-dependent. Accordingly, we used the terms M1-like and M2-like to describe CD86- and CD206-associated macrophage states rather than definitive macrophage lineages. Classically activated M1-like macrophages can exert cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory pressure through IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, whereas M2-like states frequently support tumor progression through arginine metabolism, tissue remodeling, and immunoregulatory cytokines (32,33). The M2-like-dominant landscape observed in Re tumors is therefore consistent with a microenvironment that permits reduced therapy responsiveness. NF-κB lies upstream of inflammatory macrophage programming: pharmacological or genetic inhibition of NF-κB curtails M1-like polarization and biases macrophages toward immunoregulatory states (34,35). Our whole-tumor Western blot data align with this paradigm but are not cell-type specific; the added BMDM assay strengthens the mechanistic interpretation by showing that Akk supernatant directly restores NF-κB phosphorylation and shifts macrophage transcriptional profiles in an ex vivo system.

The emergence of Akk as the single most discriminative taxon between Sen and Re communities is particularly noteworthy. Akk has previously been shown to potentiate PD-1 blockade in HCC by reshaping bile acid metabolism (36) and to engage TLR2/NF-κB signaling to bias macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype in colorectal cancer (27). Our rescue experiment integrates with this mechanism: oral gavage of live Akk restored intratumoral NF-κB phosphorylation, expanded the M1-like fraction, contracted the M2-like fraction, and reduced tumor burden in resistant animals. The ex vivo comparison of Akk supernatant and pasteurized Akk adds an additional layer of causality by showing that soluble or heat-stable Akk-derived factors can influence macrophage signaling even without live colonization. These observations suggest that Akk may act through more than one route, including colonization-dependent microbiome remodeling, epithelial barrier effects, and direct microbial products such as outer-membrane proteins or metabolites.

Several design choices warrant explicit discussion. We employed oral gavage of live Akk in specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice rather than germ-free animals. While germ-free models remain the gold standard for formally assigning causality to a single commensal, SPF-based supplementation more faithfully recapitulates the clinical context in which microbial modulation would be deployed, namely patients who already harbor a complex microbiota and frequently have cirrhosis-associated dysbiosis. This framework has supported causal claims about Akk in several liver disease models: Schneider et al. showed that Akk gavage in conventional mice improved barrier integrity and dampened hepatic inflammation (19); Li et al. demonstrated suppression of NASH-associated HCC through Akk-mediated modulation of NKT cells and macrophage infiltration (26); and Wu et al. reported that Akk potentiated PD-1 therapy across multiple metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD)-HCC models (25). Our findings extend this precedent to the setting of antiangiogenic drug resistance, while acknowledging that community-level causality will require future antibiotic-pretreated, fecal microbiota transfer, or gnotobiotic designs.

The translational context of apatinib also requires nuance. Current systemic therapy for advanced HCC increasingly prioritizes immune checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab or durvalumab plus tremelimumab in eligible Child-Pugh A patients, while TKIs including sorafenib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib, regorafenib, and apatinib retain roles across contraindicated, later-line, regional, and country-specific treatment settings (37,38). Akk-centered modulation is therefore unlikely to replace established systemic therapy. A more plausible clinical role would be as an adjunctive strategy designed to improve the immune-metabolic context in which anti-VEGF/TKI or immune checkpoint inhibitor-containing regimens act. Translation will require careful attention to cirrhosis, portal hypertension, antibiotic exposure, diet, baseline microbiome composition, probiotic manufacturing consistency, and safety in patients with impaired gut-barrier function.

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. First, our immune profiling centered on TAMs; other populations, including CD8+ T cells, natural killer cells, and regulatory T cells, likely contribute to the observed phenotype and deserve systematic characterization. Second, although the convergence of tumor-level Western blots, microbiome-level PICRUSt2 predictions, live Akk rescue, and the BMDM ex vivo assay forms a coherent chain of evidence implicating NF-κB, we did not include pharmacological NF-κB inhibition, such as BAY 11-7082 or pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium (PDTC), to formally establish pathway necessity. Third, PICRUSt2 predicts metagenomic potential from 16S rRNA profiles and does not measure actual microbial gene content, transcription, metabolites, or host pathway activity; these outputs should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Fourth, while qPCR confirmed engraftment of orally delivered Akk and the pasteurized-Akk control supports activity beyond live colonization alone, we did not perform metabolomic profiling to identify the specific Akk-derived mediators, candidate molecules for which include outer-membrane proteins such as Amuc_1100, short-chain fatty acids, and bile acid derivatives. Future work will address these questions and evaluate whether Akk supplementation synergizes with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade or other TKIs, given the mechanistic overlap with prior reports of microbiome-therapy interactions.

Conclusions

In HCC resistant to apatinib, TAMs are skewed away from an M1-like phenotype in parallel with suppressed tumor-level NF-κB signaling and an inferred dysbiotic gut-microbiome functional landscape characterized by loss of Akk. Restoring Akk by oral gavage reactivates tumor-level NF-κB signaling, rebalances macrophage polarization toward an M1-like state, and restores apatinib responsiveness. Ex vivo BMDM experiments further support macrophage-intrinsic responsiveness to Akk-derived factors. These findings nominate Akk-centered microbial modulation as a rational adjunctive strategy to delay or overcome apatinib resistance in HCC, while requiring further validation in community-level microbiome models and clinically relevant combination regimens.
