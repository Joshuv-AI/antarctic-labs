Iodine-Impregnated Adhesive Film Enhances Dressing Integrity and Patient Satisfaction After Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Krit Boontanapibul, Chanon Thassanaleelaporn, Sirada Phongpetra, Piya Pinsornsak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional film dressings (CFDs) are routinely applied for approximately 2 weeks after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) to maintain a moist wound environment and prevent contamination. Premature dressing detachment, particularly after showering, may increase dressing changes, elevate wound care costs, and reduce patient satisfaction. This study evaluated the clinical efficacy of an iodine-impregnated adhesive film (IAF)–covered CFD compared with CFD alone after primary TKA.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized controlled trial, patients undergoing primary unilateral TKA were allocated to receive either IAF-covered CFD (IAF group) or CFD alone (control group). The primary outcome was the number of dressing changes required after hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes included dressing integrity (peel-off), patient satisfaction, time to resume showering, wound care–related costs, wound complications, and postoperative knee flexion at 2 and 6 weeks.ResultsEighty-six patients were included (IAF: 45; control: 41). No postdischarge dressing changes were required in the IAF group (0 ± 0) compared with 0.8 ± 0.8 in the control group (P < .001). Dressing integrity was fully maintained in the IAF group (100% without peel-off), whereas the control group showed significantly higher detachment rates (P < .001). Patient satisfaction was higher in the IAF group (9.9 ± 0.3 vs 6.4 ± 2.2; P < .001), and showering was resumed earlier (3.1 ± 0.4 vs 4.6 ± 2.7 days; P = .047). Postoperative knee flexion and wound complication rates were comparable between groups.ConclusionsIAF-covered CFD significantly reduces dressing detachment and postoperative dressing changes, improves patient satisfaction, facilitates earlier showering, and lowers wound care–related costs without compromising clinical outcomes after TKA.Level of EvidenceTherapeutic level I.Keywords: Iodine-impregnated adhesive film, Waterproof dressing, Dressing integrity, Wound care, Total knee arthroplasty, Patient satisfaction

Introduction

The global prevalence of degenerative joint disorders, particularly knee osteoarthritis, has been steadily increasing in recent decades. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a highly effective intervention for advanced disease, and its utilization continues to rise across healthcare systems worldwide. With increasing procedural volume, optimizing postoperative wound management has become a critical aspect of perioperative care, as effective wound care not only minimizes complications—especially prosthetic joint infection—but also facilitates functional recovery, enhances patient satisfaction, and promotes efficient use of healthcare resources [1,2].

Postoperative dressings are broadly categorized as passive or active. Passive dressings, including sterile gauze, absorbent pads, and adhesive tapes, primarily serve as barriers to external contamination. However, they lack the ability to maintain a moist wound environment conducive to optimal healing and often require frequent changes, which may increase infection risk, material costs, and patient burden [3,4]. Active dressings, such as transparent adhesive films, hydrocolloid, and hydrofiber-based materials, are specifically designed to maintain moisture balance while providing effective barrier protection [5]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Sharma et al. demonstrated that active dressings reduce wound complications, decrease the frequency of dressing changes, improve patient comfort, and increase satisfaction compared with conventional gauze-based dressings, with no significant differences observed among transparent adhesive film, hydrocolloid, and hydrofiber dressings in terms of wound complication rates or mean number of dressing changes [3]. These findings support the growing consensus that transparent film dressings with absorbent pads (eg, Tegaderm + Pad, Opsite Post-Op Visible) provide an optimal balance of protection, flexibility, and wound visibility, facilitating early detection of exudate accumulation or infection in TKA wounds with low to moderate exudate, while maintaining a low-to-moderate unit cost [6].

In routine clinical practice, transparent film-with-pad dressings or conventional film dressings (CFDs) are typically applied for approximately 2 weeks postoperatively to support wound healing and prevent contamination [2]. However, premature detachment of these dressings remains a common issue, particularly after showering and range of motion (ROM) exercise. Such incidents frequently necessitate unscheduled dressing changes, thereby increasing wound care costs, resource utilization, and the risk of wound contamination through moisture intrusion [7]. To mitigate these complications, some patients resort to improvised protective measures, such as wrapping the knee with towels or plastic film prior to showering. These methods are often cumbersome, inconsistently applied, and inconvenient. Others may avoid showering entirely, negatively impacting hygiene, comfort, and quality of life during the recovery period.

Ioban is a transparent, adhesive, iodophor-impregnated incise drape commonly used intraoperatively to provide a sterile barrier at the surgical field. In addition to its physical barrier function, Ioban exerts a sustained antimicrobial effect through the continuous release of free iodine from its iodophor matrix [8,9]. However, evidence regarding the effectiveness of Ioban as a postoperative barrier dressing when used for up to 2 weeks following TKA remains limited.

To address these challenges, we conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial to evaluate whether the application of an iodine-impregnated adhesive film (IAF)–covered CFD, as compared to CFD alone, could reduce the incidence of premature dressing detachment and enhance postoperative wound management following TKA. The primary outcome was the number of dressing changes required after hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes included dressing integrity (measured by the degree of peel-off), patient satisfaction, the ability to shower without compromising dressing adherence, wound care–related costs, postoperative ROM, and wound complications postoperatively. We hypothesized that the addition of an IAF overlay would improve dressing adherence, reduce the incidence of unplanned dressing changes, facilitate safe showering during the recovery period, enhance patient-reported satisfaction, and decrease overall wound care–associated expenditures before routine postoperative follow-up.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted involving a consecutive series of patients undergoing unilateral primary TKA at a single institution between January 1, 2025, and May 31, 2025. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the Human Research Ethics Committee (MTU-EC-OT-0-261/66) and was registered with the clinical trial registry (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06943053). All patients were reenrolled in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (Fig. 1).

CONSORT diagram. CONSORT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials.

Eligible participants were adults aged 55 to 80 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification I to III, diagnosed with symptomatic primary knee osteoarthritis, and scheduled for unilateral primary TKA. Exclusion criteria comprised the inability to undergo spinal anesthesia, known hypersensitivity or contraindications to any study-related medications (including celecoxib, paracetamol, pregabalin, omeprazole, or ondansetron), secondary osteoarthritis due to autoimmune diseases or trauma, prior surgery on the target knee, or the presence of active dermatologic conditions such as contact dermatitis or psoriasis. Patients with a history of wound infection involving the affected knee were also excluded. Additional exclusions applied to patients with any condition or comorbidity that could compromise wound healing, including peripheral vascular disease, varicose veins, chronic smoking, poor nutritional status (serum albumin <3.5 g/dL), renal or hepatic dysfunction, a body mass index (BMI) greater than 35 kg/m2, chronic corticosteroid use, or known allergy to IAF. Patients with cognitive impairment or those unable to comprehend or comply with the study protocol were likewise excluded. Postoperative exclusion criteria included the development of serious complications, such as venous thromboembolism, ischemic heart disease, hemodynamic instability, or loss to follow-up during the study period.

Study interventions

Participants were randomly allocated into 2 postoperative wound care groups, with 47 patients assigned to each group. Group I (control group) received a CFD, specifically Opsite Post-Op Visible (Smith & Nephew Advanced Wound Management, Hull, UK; 25 × 10 cm), which was applied directly over the surgical incision. Group II (intervention group) received the same CFD, supplemented with an IAF (Ioban, 3M Health Care, St. Paul, MN, USA; 29 × 14 cm) applied over the waterproof layer, ensuring a 2 cm peripheral overlap to reinforce dressing adherence and integrity.

Patients were randomly allocated using a computer-generated block randomization sequence, with group assignments concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes. On postoperative day 3, an independent study nurse opened the envelopes to reveal group assignment. Wound assessment, cleansing, and dressing reapplication were subsequently performed by the attending physician under sterile conditions according to the assigned protocol. All participants received the allocated dressing prior to discharge and were instructed to maintain the dressing in place until the scheduled follow-up visit, unless earlier removal was required due to dressing failure or wound-related complications.

Perioperative management

All patients received a standardized preemptive medication regimen, consisting of oral celecoxib (400 mg), paracetamol (1000 mg), pregabalin (75 mg), omeprazole (20 mg), and ondansetron (8 mg), administered 1 hour prior to surgery. Regional anesthesia was achieved using spinal anesthesia combined with an ultrasound-guided adductor canal block, both performed by experienced anesthesiologists. Intravenous dexamethasone (8 mg) and cefazolin (2 g) were administered prophylactically prior to the surgical incision.

Surgical site preparation was performed under sterile conditions. To minimize the risk of skin contamination, large iodine-IAFs (60 × 60 cm) were applied to the operative field. Additionally, for all patients, a smaller IAF (29 × 14 cm)—separate from one of the primary surgical drapes (Fig. 2)—was prepared and stored under sterile conditions for later use in the inpatient clinic. This reserved IAF was designated for application over the waterproof dressing on postoperative day 3, immediately prior to discharge, but only in patients assigned to the intervention group.

A tourniquet was applied and inflated to 100 mmHg above the patient's systolic blood pressure. All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon (K.B.) using a minimal invasive medial parapatellar approach with the measured resection technique for bone cutting. A cemented, posterior-stabilized Triathlon TKA prosthesis with patellar resurfacing (Stryker) was used in all patients. A peri-articular cocktail injection (0.5% bupivacaine 100 mg, 0.1% adrenaline 0.6 mg, ketorolac 30 mg, tranexamic acid 1 g, morphine 5 mg diluted with normal saline to 100 mL) was administered around the capsule for perioperative pain control. Subcutaneous closure was performed using 2-0 Vicryl (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) and 4-0 Monosyn (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany), followed by the application of 3M Steri-Strip (3M Health Care, St. Paul, MN, USA) for reinforcement of wound edge approximation. No suction drains were used. Following wound closure, all patients initially received the same conventional film dressing (CFD; Opsite Post-Op Visible, 25 × 10 cm) applied with the knee positioned at 90° of flexion without tension [10].

Postoperatively, all patients received a standardized multimodal pain management protocol. This included intravenous parecoxib (40 mg) every 12 hours for the first 48 hours, followed by oral celecoxib (400 mg) once daily. Intravenous morphine (3 mg) was administered every 4 hours as needed for breakthrough pain if the visual analog scale score was ≥4/10 during hospitalization. Ondansetron (4 mg) was given intravenously every 6 hours on an as-needed basis for nausea and vomiting. Oral medications included paracetamol (1000 mg) every 6 hours for the first 2 days, followed by paracetamol (500 mg) one tablet every 6 hours, pregabalin (75 mg) at bedtime, and aspirin (81 mg) once daily for 14 days. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered for 24 hours postoperatively.

Wound care protocol

The initial postoperative dressing was maintained until postoperative day 3 in all patients. On postoperative day 3, prior to discharge, wound dressings were removed, the surgical wound was cleansed with chlorhexidine solution, and dressings were reapplied under sterile conditions by the attending physician according to the assigned randomization protocol.

In Group I (control group), the wound was covered with a CFD (Opsite Post-Op Visible, 25 × 10 cm; pad size: 20 × 5.6 cm), applied with the knee flexed to 90° and without excessive tension. In Group II (IAF group), the same CFD (Opsite Post-Op Visible, 25 × 10 cm; pad size: 20 × 5.6 cm) was applied in an identical manner, then reinforced with an iodine-IAF (29 × 14 cm). The IAF was placed directly over the waterproof dressing with at least a 2-cm overlap beyond all wound edges to improve adherence and maintain dressing integrity (Fig. 3). This procedure was also performed with the knee flexed to 90° and without tension [10]. All patients were permitted to shower following discharge and received standardized wound care instructions (Table 1). Showering was allowed daily, with patients advised to face away from the showerhead to avoid direct water exposure to the incision and to gently pat the area dry without rubbing.

All patients were also provided with the same cold pack (3M Nexcare). The cold pack was recommended to be used in a cloth cover for 20 minutes, 5 times per day, during the first 2 weeks post-surgery. Additionally, patients were instructed to recognize the grading of dressing peel-off into 3 grades (Table 2). Wound dressings were not to be changed until postoperative day 14, except under specific conditions: [1] Grade II or III dressing peel-off, [2] bleeding through more than 50% of the dressing, or [3] suspected surgical site infection. All patients were photographed prior to discharge and followed up at day 14 before dressing removal (Fig. 4).

To facilitate postoperative care, all patients were invited to join a chat group for real-time consultation regarding wound care. A rapid rehabilitation protocol was implemented in all cases, including early knee ROM exercises, quadriceps strengthening, ankle pumping, and ambulation as tolerated. Additionally, each patient received a wound care manual specific to TKA.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the number of wound dressing changes required after hospital discharge. This was recorded from the day of discharge until postoperative day 14, when the wound dressing was officially removed.

Secondary outcomes were evaluated to comprehensively assess multiple domains related to the effectiveness, patient experience, and safety of the postoperative dressing protocol, including: [1] dressing integrity, assessed by the degree of peel-off of the initial dressing using a 3-level grading system (Table 2) on postoperative day 14, or earlier in cases of dressing failure; [2] patient satisfaction and functional use, measured using a self-reported satisfaction score (1–10; 0 = dislike, 10 = most liked) reflecting satisfaction with showering while wearing the dressing, along with the postoperative day on which showering was first resumed (time to first shower). Pain associated with dressing removal was recorded using a self-reported pain score on a 0–10 visual analog scale (0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain) at 2 weeks postoperatively, serving as an indicator of self-care ease and overall comfort with wound management; [3] wound-related complications, including skin erythema, blistering, maceration, surgical site infection, need for reoperation, or other unanticipated wound events, assessed during clinical follow-up visits at 2 and 6 weeks postoperatively; [4] postoperative knee flexion, measured using a long-arm goniometer at 2 and 6 weeks postoperatively, with anatomical landmarks defined as the femoral axis (greater trochanter to lateral epicondyle) and leg axis (fibular head to lateral malleolus). Flexion angles were recorded to evaluate the impact of dressing technique on early mobilization and rehabilitation. Wound-related complications and flexion angles were assessed by an independent outcome assessor who was not involved in the treatment protocol; and [5] wound care–related costs, calculated as the total cost of dressing materials used from discharge until postoperative day 14, including the number of dressing changes, any additional materials, nursing time, and clinic visits required for unplanned care.

Data analyses

A priori sample size calculation was performed based on the primary outcome, defined as the number of wound dressing changes after hospital discharge. A 2-sample means test with Satterthwaite’s method (assuming unequal variances) was used. Based on a previous study [2], the expected mean difference between groups (δ) was −0.64, with a standard deviation of 0.78 in both groups. With a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 95%, the required sample size was calculated to be 78 knees in total (approximately 39 per group). To account for a potential dropout rate of 25%, the sample size was increased to 48 knees per group, yielding a total of 96 participants. This sample size was considered sufficient to detect a statistically significant difference in the primary outcome. Statistical analyses were conducted by independent investigators who were blinded to group allocation. The primary analysis was conducted using a modified intention-to-treat approach, excluding patients who did not adhere to the postoperative showering protocol, as the study was designed to evaluate dressing performance under conditions involving water exposure [11].

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics. Continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences between the intervention and control groups were assessed using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Results

Of the 96 patients assessed for eligibility, 2 were excluded (inflammatory arthritis requiring chronic corticosteroid therapy, n = 1; peripheral vascular disease, n = 1). The remaining 94 patients were randomized into 2 groups: 47 in Group I (control/CFD) and 47 in Group II (intervention/IAF-cover waterproof dressing). In Group I, 41 patients were included in the final analysis, with 6 excluded due to protocol violations; these patients were reluctant to shower for 14 days because of concern that the wound dressing might detach and therefore chose to perform sponge bathing only. In Group II, 45 patients were included in the final analysis, with 2 excluded due to loss to follow-up (Fig. 1).

The cost of postoperative wound care was significantly higher in the control group (USD 49.7 ± 11.5) compared with the iodine-IAF group (USD 0 ± 0; P < .001). Discussion

With the rising prevalence of osteoarthritis, demand for TKA has steadily increased. While surgical advances are pivotal, effective postoperative wound management is essential to minimize periprosthetic joint infection, promote healing, enhance patient satisfaction, and optimize healthcare resource use [1,12]. Ioban drapes are routinely used intraoperatively to establish a sterile barrier that limits bacterial contamination, with the iodophor component providing continuous antimicrobial activity to prevent surgical site infections. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the postoperative application of an IAF and assess its effectiveness in wound care over a 2-week period. In this randomized trial, IAF dressings significantly reduced postoperative dressing changes, maintained dressing integrity, improved patient confidence with showering, and allowed earlier showering without increasing complications.

Selecting an appropriate wound dressing is critical. An ideal dressing maintains a moist, warm, and clean environment; is waterproof, transparent, absorbent, and stable; and accommodates joint motion without frictional compromise [[13], [14], [15]]. Accordingly, in our study, the procedure was performed with the knee flexed to 90° and without tension in both groups [10]. However, the considerable ROM of the knee often prevents CFDs from fully meeting these requirements. In regions with high ambient temperature and humidity, CFDs are particularly susceptible to premature detachment, resulting in inadequate wound protection and more frequent dressing changes, which may compromise postoperative care. Hydrocolloid or hydrofiber dressings were not used in this study because a previous systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated no significant differences among transparent adhesive films, hydrocolloid, and hydrofiber dressings in terms of complication rates or mean dressing changes, while the cost of transparent adhesive films is lower. Hydrocolloid and hydrofiber dressings may be reserved for moderate- to high-exudate wounds or patients at higher risk of delayed healing or infection [16,17].

In our study, the IAF group demonstrated superior fluid-handling capacity and a lower incidence of dressing peel-off (P < .001), reflecting improved early postoperative dressing stability. Patients in this group were more confident in resuming showering, whereas 6 patients in the control group avoided showering for 14 days due to concerns about dressing detachment. Reduced dressing disruption may further minimize environmental pathogen exposure, thereby supporting safer wound healing [18,19]. Moreover, early showering prior to suture removal has been shown to enhance patient satisfaction and facilitate postoperative rehabilitation [20].

Enhanced durability of the IAF dressing translated into significantly lower postoperative wound care costs. In the present study, the control group incurred substantially higher costs due to more frequent unplanned dressing changes, which required additional materials, nursing time, and clinic resource utilization, as well as occasional patient transportation. In our study, the smaller IAF (29 × 14 cm) used postoperatively was prepared from the larger iodine-impregnated adhesive drapes routinely used during surgery, thereby minimizing additional material waste. The mean cost per unplanned dressing change was approximately USD 50 per patient episode. In routine clinical practice outside the study setting, a commercially available small IAF (35 × 35 cm) used at our institution costs approximately USD 10 per sheet. Therefore, the reduction in unplanned dressing changes and associated healthcare resource utilization still resulted in an overall cost reduction. These findings suggest that IAF-covered dressings not only improve clinical outcomes, including patient satisfaction and ease of self-care, but may also represent a cost-effective strategy for postoperative wound management following primary TKA.

Wound complications after TKA can lead to severe consequences; therefore, it is essential for surgeons to be well-versed in best practices for their prevention and management [12]. Previous studies have investigated the incidence of various wound complications, including blistering, persistent exudate, and erythema. Both Cosker et al. and Dobbelaere et al. demonstrated that film dressings were less likely to develop wound complications compared with passive dressings [6,13]. Our findings support these results. In our study, wound dressings were applied with the knee flexed to 90° and without tension in both groups [10]. Wound complications were rare in both groups, with no cases of surgical site infection, blister formation, or reoperation. One patient in the IAF group developed mild peripheral erythema, which resolved spontaneously within 2 weeks. Despite these favorable outcomes, it is important to acknowledge that the current study’s sample size may have been too limited to fully assess the risk of surgical site infection. Future research with larger cohorts is warranted to explore this issue more comprehensively. Additionally, the incorporation of objective wound assessment tools, such as the Southampton Wound Assessment Scale, could provide more rigorous evaluation of wound appearance and complications in subsequent investigations.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, Opsite Post-Op Visible was the only type of film dressing used as a comparator, which may not represent all commercially available film dressings. Second, the sample size was relatively small, potentially limiting the statistical power to detect uncommon complications such as surgical site infection. Third, patients with conditions that could impair wound healing—such as peripheral vascular disease, varicose veins, chronic smoking, poor nutritional status, renal or hepatic dysfunction, obesity (BMI >35 kg/m2), or chronic corticosteroid use—were excluded. Therefore, the applicability of IAF dressings in these high-risk populations remains uncertain and warrants further investigation. In addition, patients who did not adhere to the postoperative showering protocol were excluded from the primary analysis; therefore, the results should be interpreted within the context of a modified intention-to-treat approach, which may introduce a risk of selection bias. Fourth, postoperative functional outcomes, including ROM, were assessed only up to 6 weeks, which may not fully reflect long-term recovery. Moreover, ROM alone may not comprehensively capture functional improvement; validated scoring systems such as the Knee Society Score or Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index could enhance reliability in future studies. In addition, the study protocol—specifically dressing reapplication on postoperative day 3 prior to discharge—reflects our institutional practice and healthcare setting. Many patients reside in rural or remote areas with limited access to hospital follow-up, and home healthcare services are not routinely available. Therefore, the findings may not be fully generalizable to settings with early discharge protocols (same day or postoperative day 1) and established home-based postoperative care. Lastly, the amber coloration of the Ioban film occasionally obscured direct wound visualization without removing the dressing, potentially influencing clinical assessment.

Conclusions

The addition of IAF over CFD after TKA substantially reduces postoperative dressing changes and peel-off rates, enhances patient satisfaction, and lowers wound care–related costs without compromising clinical outcomes. This intervention constitutes a safe, effective, and patient-centered strategy for postoperative wound management following TKA. These findings support the integration of advanced wound management strategies into standard postoperative protocols for TKA, promoting a better patient experience and more efficient use of healthcare resources.
