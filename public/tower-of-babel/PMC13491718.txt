Genome-wide analysis of MYB and F3’5’H gene families in Vaccinium bracteatum provides insights into anthocyanin biosynthesis
Authors: Lu Yang, Anqi Huang, Jinming Liu, Xiaojie Wang, Zhe Deng, Jianye Huang, Lixing Xiao, Rui Liu, Minghui Li, Dongsheng Du, Xuan Gao, Jiaxin Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe fruit of the oriental blueberry (Vaccinium bracteatum) is rich in anthocyanins, with the MYB transcription factor family and F3’5’H gene family playing key roles in anthocyanin biosynthesis. In this study, the MYB and F3’5’H gene families were investigated using bioinformatics analyses, including phylogenetic, motif, and gene structure analyses, while further functional characterization of VbF3’5’H7 was conducted.ResultsIn this study, a total of 156 VbMYB and nine VbF3’5’H members have been identified. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed the evolutionary relationships, and motif analysis revealed that both families contained conserved motifs, with greater diversity in VbMYBs. Moreover, purifying selection dominated VbMYBs and VbF3’5’Hs evolution. For gene location, VbMYBs were distributed randomly across all 12 chromosomes (Chr), whereas VbF3’5’Hs were localized exclusively to Chr4/6/10. The cis-acting elements prediction results showed that the promoters of most VbF3’5’Hs harbored MYB binding sites and ripening-related cis-elements such as ABRE and CGTCA. In addition, transcriptome data indicated that VbMYB and VbF3’5’H families may be involved in Vaccinium bracteatum fruit ripening. Transgenic tomato overexpressing VbF3’5’H7 showed elevated anthocyanins and photosynthetic pigments, with enhanced anti-inflammatory effects on RAW 264.7 cells.ConclusionsIn summary, this work provided insights into anthocyanin biosynthesis by genome-wide analysis of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families, combined with the heterologous functional characterization of VbF3’5’H7 in transgenic tomato.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09193-3.

Background

Oriental blueberry (Vaccinium bracteatum Thunb., referred to as VBT), also known as “Wu Fan Shu”, is an evergreen shrub belonging to the bilberry genus of the rhododendron family and congeneric with cultivated blueberries. VBT is widely distributed in eastern and southern China, and this species was first documented as a herbal medicine in the Kai Bao Ben Cao, a pharmacological text from the Song Dynasty [1]. The ripe VBT fruits (VBTF) are purple black berries rich in flavonoids and nutrients. Compared to common blueberries, the diameter of VBTF is slightly smaller, and its phenological period differs [2]. VBTF are utilized in the food industry for producing fruit juices, jams, and vinegar, while pigments extracted from its bract leaves (VBTL) can serve as natural dyeing materials [3]. In China, the juice extracted from VBT leaves was often used for dyeing and cooking rice (known as “Wumifan”) by residents [4]. Previous studies have shown that VBT holds potential for developing edible and medicinal products [2]. The total amino acid content of VBTF was higher than that of Sharpblue blueberry [5]. Moreover, the VBTL exhibited significant antibacterial [6], antiviral [7], anti-inflammatory activities [8], and hypoglycemic effect [9].

Anthocyanins are stable flavonoids synthesized by the combination of anthocyanidins and various monosaccharides, which contribute to the diverse colors observed in leaves, flowers, and fruits. Due to the significant health and pharmacological effects of anthocyanins, including radiation protection, cardiovascular disease prevention, and a reduced risk of cancer, the study of anthocyanins is a highly active area of research in biology [10]. Anthocyanin synthesis is catalyzed by various types of enzymes under the regulation of various transcription factors (TFs), among which MYB is an important transcription factor widely reported to regulate anthocyanin synthesis in various species. For instance, VvMYB5a and VvMYB5b in grape could activate the expression of the ANS gene during berry ripening, thereby regulating anthocyanin accumulation [11]. MdMYB1 in apple could activate the expression of DFR and UFGT genes [12]. MdMYBA could specifically bind to the ANS promoter region to regulate anthocyanin synthesis [13]. MdMYB9 and MdMYB11 could form an MBW complex with MdbHLH3 and MdTTG1, and regulate anthocyanin and PA accumulation by binding to the promoters of structural genes. In parallel, MdbHLH3 was also recruited to the promoter regions of MdMYB9 and MdMYB11 genes to activate their expression [14]. It has been proven that VbMYBA was a strong anthocyanin activator and was capable of activating the promoters of DFR and UFGT from blueberry [15]. MYB belongs to the multigene family and has been identified in many species. For example, a total of 125 members of the Arabidopsis genome were identified [16]. Then, 127 and 158 MYB transcription factors were identified in tomato and potato, respectively [17, 18]. Furthermore, 244 MYB members were identified in soybean [19]. Also, 229 and 256 MYB members were identified in apple and peach genomes, respectively [20, 21]. A recent study has shown that 221 MYB members were identified in tea plants [22].

The biosynthesis of anthocyanins mainly includes three stages: the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, key reactions of flavonoid metabolism, and synthesis and modification of anthocyanins [23–25]. In the anthocyanin synthesis pathway, flavonoid 3’5’-hydroxylase (F3’5’H) belongs to the monooxygenase of the cytochrome P450 family, catalyzing the flavanones into hydroxy-containing derivatives, which serve as direct precursors to the synthesis of purple anthocyanins, so F3’5’H is often referred to as the “blue gene” [26]. In the field of genetic engineering, flower color has been altered in many flower varieties upon introduction of the F3’5’H gene. For example, expression of petunia F3’5’H and DFR genes in a DFR mutant resulted in the first worldwide-sold GM floricultural crop: FLORIGENE® Moondust™ [27]; a Torenia gene encoding anthocyanin 5-aromatic acyltransferase was introduced with pansy F3′5′H, resulting in the world’s first blue rose, Suntory blue rose Applause [28]; Compared to the wild type, PhF3’5’H transgenic petunia plants have deeper flower color and higher anthocyanin content [29]. In addition, in fruits, the expression of the F3’5’H gene in grapes and blueberries was enhanced in ripe berries with anthocyanin accumulation [30, 31]; The expression of the F3’5’H gene of tomato was significantly increased in purple Del/Ros1 fruits, which were rich in anthocyanins, but the F3’5’H gene was hardly expressed in Lc/C1 fruits without anthocyanins [32, 33]. However, the gene function of F3’5’H has not been reported in VBT.

In 2022, the genomic information of Vaccinium bracteatum Thunb. was deciphered and published, indicating that V. bracteatum exhibited a diploid genome (2n = 2x = 24), and accounting for contaminants and extranuclear DNA content, the genome size was estimated at 579.42 Mb using K-mer analysis, facilitating the identification and functional analysis of key gene families involved in anthocyanin synthesis [34]. This study focused on VBT in regions south of the Yangtze River, emphasizing the identification and characterization of MYB (transcription factors) and F3’5’H (enzyme) gene families that played a critical role in anthocyanin synthesis. In detail, comprehensive bioinformatics analyses were performed on the members of these two gene families, including phylogenetic analysis, motif analysis, gene structure, chromosomal location, subcellular localization, protein function prediction, cis-acting element prediction, and MCScanX collinearity analysis. Also, heat maps were generated based on transcriptome data to visualize the expression patterns of these two gene families. Additionally, the potential associations of F3’5’H and MYB were preliminarily explored through bioinformatics methods, and one member in the F3’5’H gene family, VbF3’5’H7, with high expression during fruit ripening, was selected for further functional study.

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is cultivated worldwide and serves as an important economic crop. It is also a model plant for studying the development and ripening of fleshy fruits. Although ripened tomato fruits rich in lycopene are beneficial to health, their anthocyanin content can be ignored, and the content of flavonoids in the peel is extremely low (about 5–10 mg/kg fresh weight), mainly naringin chalcone and rutin [35]. Considering the background of low anthocyanin content in tomato fruits, here, one of the VbF3’5’H gene family members, VbF3’5’H7, which showed a higher expressed level in ripening VBTFs, was heterologously expressed into tomato to obtain transgenic fruit. The observation of fruit phenotype, detection of genes related to anthocyanin synthesis, and physiological indicators related to fruit quality verified the role of VbF3’5’H7 in anthocyanin synthesis. Additionally, the anti-inflammatory activity of the fruit crude extract in transgenic tomato was explored through pharmacological experiments.

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties of VbMYBs and VbF3’5’Hs

The VBT used in this study was a wild material, collected from Nanling County, Wuhu City, Anhui Province, China (30° 51′ 6.93″ N, 118° 23′ 46.66″ E, altitude 93 m). The branches, leaves, and harvested fruits of VBT were photographed (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1A, the leaves of VBT were elliptical or oval in shape, with sharp tips and sparse serrations on the edges. Its petiole is short and inconspicuous. Fruits of VBT in different ripening stages can be divided into green, pink, and blue fruits according to their colors (Fig. 1B). A total of 156 MYBs and nine F3’5’Hs (CYP75A) in VBT were identified and named based on their chromosomal positions (VbMYB1-VbMYB153, VbF3’5’H1-VbF3’5’H9); three of VbMYB, which couldn’t be mapped on chromosomes, were also named (VbMYB154-VbMYB156) (Additional File 1: Table S1, Table S2). The average number of amino acids in the VbMYB proteins was 341.24 aa, of which VbMYB137 contained the highest number (1655 aa), and VbMYB49/VbMYB155 contained the lowest number (70 aa). Consistently, the average protein molecular weight was 38.41 kDa, ranging from 8.21 kDa (VbMYB49) to 180.16 kDa (VbMYB137). Moreover, the theoretical pI ranged from 4.27 (VbMYB13) to 9.9 (VbMYB113); the instability index ranged from 27.64 (VbMYB131) to 88.25 (VbMYB74); the aliphatic index ranged from 47.31 (VbMYB131) to 92.25 (VbMYB46); overall average hydropathicity ranged from − 1.218 (VbMYB74) to -0.363 (VbMYB46, VbMYB109). The results indicated hydrophilicity within this gene family. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that 148 VbMYB proteins were localized in the nucleus, confirming their essential function as transcription factors (Additional File 1: Table S1). Among the nine VbF3’5’H proteins, VbF3’5’H6 had the highest molecular weight (78.57 kDa, 700 aa), while VbF3’5’H7 had the smallest molecular weight (48.14 kDa, 431 aa). Moreover, the theoretical pI ranged from 6.84 (VbF3’5’H9) to 9.3 (VbF3’5’H3), the instability index ranged from 31.67 (VbF3’5’H6) to 41.34 (VbF3’5’H2), with an average instability index of 36.99. Except for VbF3’5’H2, all other members exhibited an instability index below 40, indicating the stability of the VbF3’5’H family proteins. Aliphatic index ranged from 83.44 (VbF3’5’H5) to 96.33 (VbF3’5’H2), reflecting the high aliphatic amino acid content of the VbF3’5’H enzyme family. Overall average hydropathicity ranged from − 0.203 (VbF3’5’H6) to 0.06 (VbF3’5’H7), with an average value of -0.056, indicating hydrophilicity. All nine members lacked signal peptides, suggesting that they may not be secreted proteins. Subcellular localization prediction results showed VbF3’5’H predominantly expressed in chloroplasts (Additional File 1: Table S2).

Phylogenetic analysis of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

To further explore the phylogenetic relationships among these members of the MYB family in VBT, a phylogenetic tree was constructed and compared with AtMYBs (Fig. 2). A total of 289 MYB proteins were classified into nine groups (I-IX). Among them, Group I was the largest subfamily with 72 proteins, while Group VI was the smallest, with only three proteins. Each subfamily included both AtMYB and VbMYB family members, suggesting that VbMYB proteins were more closely related to AtMYB proteins in the same branch than other MYB-family proteins in the other branch. Among all 156 VbMYB members, 19 VbMYBs contained only one MYB domain, 121 VbMYBs contained two MYB domains, 12 VbMYBs contained three MYB domains, two VbMYBs (VbMYB93 and VbMYB140) contained five MYB domains, and the other two VbMYBs (VbMYB135 and VbMYB154) contained six MYB domains (Additional File 1: Table S1). The phylogenetic tree of the VbF3’5’H family showed that VbF3’5’H7 and VbF3’5’H8 were relatively distant from the rest of the VbF3’5’H family, while the other seven members formed a more closely related subfamily (Additional File 2: Fig. S1).

Motif analysis and gene structure of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

To better understand the features of the VbMYB proteins, the Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) analysis was performed on the VbMYB proteins identified above; the detailed motif sequence information is presented in Additional File 2: Fig. S2. Motif organization in VbMYB proteins correlates with their phylogenetic relationships, illustrated in Fig. 3A. Each VbMYB protein contained a varying variety of conserved motifs, ranging from one to six, with most motifs distributed at the N-terminus of the sequence. In addition to widely distributed motifs (1, 2, 3, and 4), VbMYB members located on adjacent branches of the phylogenetic tree typically shared similar motif compositions, indicating conserved motif distribution within subfamilies (Fig. 3B). These VbMYB family members contained 1–13 CDS, with most genes containing three CDS, indicating diverse gene structures. VbMYB in closely clustered subfamilies exhibited similar gene structures, but differences were observed between distinct sub-branches (Fig. 3C). MEME analysis was also performed on VbF3’5’H proteins identified above; the detailed motif sequence information is presented in Additional File 2: Fig. S3. VbF3’5’Hs structure correlates with their phylogenetic relationships, illustrated in Fig. 4. The motif composition and arrangement of the VbF3’5’H enzyme family were highly conserved. The ubiquitous motifs, 1, 2, and 3, were likely to be the core sequences that were essential for the enzyme function of flavonoid 3’, 5’-hydroxylase (Fig. 4B). Gene structure analysis showed that VbF3’5’H had 2–9 CDS of varying lengths, scattered at both ends of the sequence, suggesting that biologically significant regions were concentrated at these positions (Fig. 4C).

Collinearity analysis of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

There were 5,061 pairs of segmental duplications in the VBT genome, of which 33 pairs belonged to the VbMYB family (Fig. 5). Meanwhile, there were 2,256 pairs of tandem genes, with the VbMYB family occupying seven pairs. These results suggested that some VbMYB genes might be generated by gene duplication, and segmental replication events were important drivers of VbMYBs evolution. All large fragments and tandem repeats of VbMYB gene pairs, as well as most orthologous MYB gene pairs with the Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1, indicated that the MYB gene family in VBT may have undergone strong purifying selection pressure during evolution (Additional File 1: Table S3). There were no members of the VbF3’5’H family among the 5,061 pairs of large fragment duplications in the genome of VBT. Among the 2,256 pairs of tandem duplicated genes in the genome of VBT, the VbF3’5’H family comprised four pairs (VbF3’5’H2 and VbF3’5’H3, VbF3’5’H1 and VbF3’5’H2, VbF3’5’H3 and VbF3’5’H4, VbF3’5’H4 and VbF3’5’H5). These four pairs of VbF3’5’H tandem repeat genes exhibited a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1, indicating that the VbF3’5’H gene family in VBT was also under strong purifying selection pressure (Additional File 1: Table S4).

Multiple sequence alignment and chromosome localization of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

The MYB domain of VbMYB protein was highly conserved, which contained conserved amino acid Trp (W) residues at multiple sites and was consistent with the sequence characteristics of MYB family proteins in previous studies (Additional File 2: Fig. S4). The chromosome mapping results showed that the VbMYB gene family members were randomly distributed on 12 chromosomes. Chr8 contained the greatest number of VbMYB genes, with a total of 23, while Chr9 contained the least number of VbMYB genes, with only seven. Three or more genes were found to be arranged in gene clusters on Chr1, Chr8, and Chr12 (Fig. 6). Sequence alignment was also performed on VbF3’5’H, and the amino acid sequences of nine VbF3’5’Hs revealed a high degree of evolutionary conservation (Additional File 2: Fig. S5). The chromosome mapping of the VBT genome showed that VbF3’5’Hs only existed in Chr4, Chr6, and Chr10. Moreover, on Chr4, VbF3’5’H1, VbF3’5’H2, VbF3’5’H3, VbF3’5’H4, and VbF3’5’H5 existed continuously in the form of gene clusters, suggesting that gene family expansion and duplication events might have occurred at this location (Fig. 6).

Analysis of cis-acting elements of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

MYB is the core transcription factor for anthocyanin synthesis [13] and F3’5’H is the key gene determining blue color [26], with both playing a crucial role in anthocyanin synthesis. A potential associations may exist between the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families. The PlantCARE prediction identified 4,111 cis-acting elements in the 2000 bp region upstream of the TSS (Transcription Start Site) of VbMYBs (Additional File 1: Table S5), revealing that this region contains 1001 cis-acting regulatory elements associated with stress and adversity, including TC-rich repeats, ARE, LTR, and GC-motif; 224 cis-acting elements related to growth metabolism, including HD-Zip 1, O2-site, CAT-box, and GCN4_motif; 1,671 photoreactive elements, including GA-motif, ACE, AE-box, and G-box; 777 cis-acting elements related to plant hormones, including ABRE, WUN-motif, TGA-element, and TCA-element (Additional File 2: Fig. S6). All 274 cis-acting elements of VbF3’5’Hs (Additional File 1: Table S6), located 2000 bp upstream of the start codon, contained 71 cis-acting elements related to stress and adversity, including ARE, LTR, GC-motif, and TC-rich repeats; 13 cis-acting elements related to growth metabolism, including MSA-like, O2-site, CAT-box, and GCN4_motif. 103 photoreactive elements, such as G-box, AE-box, Sp1, ACE; 59 cis-acting elements related to plant hormones, such as ABRE, TCA-element, TGA-element, GARE-motif. In addition, 15 predicted MYB binding sites and 3 MYBHv1 binding sites indicated that a potential upstream-downstream relationship might exist between the VbMYB and VbF3′5′H gene families (Fig. 7).

Interaction networks of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H with functional proteins

In pursuit of a deeper understanding of the biological functions and potential associations of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H, a homology-based approach was utilized to predict protein-protein interaction (PPI). Many PPI existed within the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H families (Fig. 8A), with VbMYB33, VbMYB125, and VbF3’5’H9 being identified as the key nodes within these two gene families. These proteins were predicted to possess the ability to interact with the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H proteins (Fig. 8B). Furthermore, the predicted protein-protein interactions among VbMYB and VbF3’5’H, as well as other functional proteins, were analyzed. The result showed VbMYB interacted with NAC proteins, bHLH proteins, and TTG proteins family, all of which played vital roles in plant anthocyanin synthesis (Fig. 8B).

Expression patterns of VbMYBs and VbF3’5’Hs in VBT fruits at different ripening stages

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on VBT fruit samples of different ripening stages (green, pink, and blue). Using dual thresholds of |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 1.5 and standard deviation > 0.5 for filtering, a total of 78 differentially expressed VbMYB genes were identified (Fig. 9A). Additionally, six differentially expressed genes were screened from nine VbF3’5’H genes, and gene expression heatmaps were plotted (Fig. 9B). It was found that six VbF3’5’H genes was highly expressed during fruit ripening. To validate these findings, RT-qPCR analysis was conducted on five VbF3’5’H genes (VbF3’5’H2, VbF3’5’H9, VbF3’5’H5, VbF3’5’H4, VbF3’5’H7) (Fig. 9C). All five genes were up-regulated during fruit ripening; notably, VbF3’5’H7 showed higher expression levels than the other four genes at the pink and blue stages. Simultaneously, 12 VbMYB genes were screened randomly and analyzed by RT-qPCR analysis to investigate their expression patterns during different ripening stages (Fig. 9D). It was verified that the tendency of relative expression of the 12 VbMYB genes was basically consistent with the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) values. For example, VbMYB4, VbMYB13, VbMYB55 and VbMYB97, exhibited higher expression during pink to blue stages, which were consistent with the transcriptome data, suggesting the possibility of the VbMYB transcription factor family being involved in the fruit ripening of VBT (Fig. 9D). In addition, the protein sequences of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H—the same genes used for qPCR analysis and tobacco transformation in this study—were compared with those of Vaccinium darrowii and Vaccinium corymbosum. It was found that there is homology among these three species, with sequence identity mostly above 90%, and some even reaching 100%, indicating that the F3’5’H and MYB gene families are highly conserved (Additional File 1: Table S7).

Promoter activity analysis of the VbMYB4 and VbMYB97 genes

According to the transcriptome data, two genes, VbMYB4 and VbMYB97, which were highly up-regulated expressed during fruit ripening, were used for promoter activity research. In detail, the 2000 bp promoter sequence upstream of TSS of the two genes was respectively cloned into the pBI101 vector to generate the GUS fusion expression vectors named pVbMYB4::GUS and pVbMYB97::GUS, which were then transformed into tobacco leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated transient assay. The constructed recombinant vector p35S::GUS was used as a positive control group (Fig. 10A). The results of histological GUS staining showed that tobacco leaves injected with p35S::GUS showed a clear blue color, while CK tobacco leaves used as negative control (blank control group) became colorless after decolorization. Compared with CK, tobacco leaves injected with pVbMYB4::GUS and pVbMYB97::GUS showed blue color after staining and decolorization, and were weaker than the GUS expression group driven by the 35 S promoter, indicating that the VbMYB4 and VbMYB97 promoters could drive the expression of GUS normally and had promoter activity. Compared with the group injected with pVbMYB97::GUS, the group injected with pVbMYB4::GUS had a deeper blue color, indicating that the VbMYB4 promoter had stronger activity than the VbMYB97 promoter (Fig. 10B).

Phenotypic Analysis of VbF3’5’H7 Over-expressed tomato

Whether in the leaves (T0 generation) or the fruits (T2 generation) of VbF3’5’H7-overexpressed transgenic tomato plants (14# and 22# lines), the expression of VbF3’5’H7 was significantly up-regulated when compared to wild type (WT), confirming the success of heterologous transgenes. In the present study, the expression of other genes (PAL2, PAL3, F3H, F3’H, ANS, DFR) related to anthocyanin synthesis was measured in fruits at the breaker stage (BR) (Fig. 11A). The results showed that, compared with WT, these genes were up-regulated in the 14# line. Strangely, these genes were not significantly up-regulated and even down-regulated in the 22# line, which had a prominently higher expression than 14# in the T0 generation, but a significantly lower expression in the T2 generation. Meanwhile, the relevant physiological indicators, including anthocyanin, titratable acid, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, chlorophyll a + b, and carotenoid content, were measured in WT and transgenic tomatoes (Fig. 11B, C and D). The results showed that the content of anthocyanin in fruits at different ripening stages (Br, Br + 4, Br + 7) of transgenic lines (14# and 22#) was significantly higher than that of the WT, and the content of anthocyanin in the 14# line was higher than that in the 22# line. In addition, the content of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll (a + b), as well as carotenoid in the transgenic lines (14# and 22#) was also higher than those in the WT.

Gain-of-function of VbF3’5’H7 enhanced the anti-inflammatory effect of the tomato fruit

To investigate whether the acquisition of VbF3’5’H7 function was beneficial for the anti-inflammatory effect of tomato fruit, the crude extracts of WT and transgenic tomato fruits were dried in a vacuum freeze dryer for two days and dissolved in DMSO at a ratio of 0.01 g to 0.1 mL. The mRNA expression levels of three inflammatory cytokines (Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)) in RAW 264.7 cells of mice were evaluated (Fig. 12). At the concentration of 100 µg/mL, all of the fruit extracts could obviously inhibit the mRNA expression of three cytokines in comparison with the LPS groups (1 µg/mL). On the whole, the extracts from tomato fruit at the Br + 7 stage showed a stronger anti-inflammatory effect than those from tomato fruit at the breaker stage, which was consistent with the anthocyanin level assays. Interestingly, tomato fruit extract derived from the Br + 7 stage of strain 14 (14 #) showed the best anti-inflammatory effect in this experiment.

Discussion

Anthocyanins are a class of flavonoids and are widely present in plants as water-soluble natural pigments. It not only endows flowers, fruits, and vegetables with brilliant colors, but also has strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [36]. Its synthetic pathway is a hot topic in the field of agricultural food chemistry research. MYB transcription factors, one of the largest TF families in plants, played essential roles in the regulation of plant secondary metabolism [37]. MYB transcription factors hold a dominant position within the regulatory network of anthocyanin biosynthesis and accumulation [38]. Cytochrome P450 enzymes play a crucial role in the flavonoids biosynthesis pathway. Flavonoid 3’-hydroxylase (F3’H, CYP75B) and flavonoid 3’, 5’- hydroxylase (F3’5’H, CYP75A) can regulate the color of anthocyanins by determining the hydroxylation mode of the anthocyanin B ring. Among them, flavonoid 3’, 5’- hydroxylase mediates the hydroxylation reaction at the 3’- and 5’- positions of the B ring, which is necessary for anthocyanin synthesis derived from delphinidin (color range from purple to blue) [39]. Recently, Ma et al. reported that in MYB117-overexpressing poplar trees, the F3’5’H1 was strongly up-regulated, exhibiting a 150-fold increase in expression, while the F3’5’H2 gene showed a remarkable 1,391-fold up-regulation [40]. MYB overexpression has a statistically significant effect on the regulation of structural genes in the later stages of anthocyanin biosynthesis, with the greatest impact on the F3’5’H gene [41]. Karppinen et al. [42] reported that the overexpression of VmMYBPA1.1, VmMYBPA2, VmMYBPA3, and VmMYB7 from Vaccinium myrtillus led to the up-regulation of NbF3’5’H expression in Nicotiana benthamiana. However, the specific functions of VbMYBs and VbF3’5’Hs in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, as well as the potential associations among these gene families in Oriental blueberry, remain largely unknown.

As mentioned before, the genome sequencing of VBT was completed and published in 2022, enabling a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the VbMYBs and VbF3’5’Hs [34]. Typically, each MYB family member contains a conserved MYB domain composed of 1–4 incomplete repeats (R) of 50 to 54 amino acids [43]. In VBT, 121 proteins with two MYB-conserved domains were identified, representing the highest number, while no proteins with four MYB domains were detected. Interestingly, among the 156 MYB proteins in oriental blueberry, the domain range was one to six, two proteins with five MYB domains (VbMYB93 and VbMYB140) and two proteins with six MYB domains (VbMYB135 and VbMYB154) were discovered (Fig. 2, Additional File 1: Table S1). In both pairs of protein sequences, a specific MYB domain was present in each pair, exhibiting high sequence identity but distinct specificity compared to other VbMYB domains. (Additional File 1: Table S1).

Most VbMYB proteins contained motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. The composition and distribution order of motifs were highly conserved within branches or subfamilies of the phylogenetic tree, while significant variations existed among different groups. The combination and sequential order of motifs vary among different subfamilies, suggesting potential functional diversification among various groups. Furthermore, the majority of MYB genes possessed two introns and three exons (Fig. 3), a feature also observed in other plant species [44]. The motif composition and arrangement of VbF3’5’Hs were highly conserved, with CDS regions primarily located at both termini of the sequence (Fig. 4). This suggested that biologically significant domains were concentrated in these conserved positions.

In this study, a total of 156 VbMYB members were identified in the VBT genome. The notably large size of the VbMYB family may be associated with whole-genome duplication events. Within the VbMYB family, seven tandem duplication events and 33 segmental duplication events were detected (Fig. 5, Additional File 1: Table S3). Meanwhile, four tandem duplication events and no segmental duplication events were detected in nine VbF3’5’H members (Additional File 1: Table S4). Gene duplication mechanisms were found to contribute not only to genomic expansion and family size amplification but also significantly shape chromosomal distribution patterns. Chromosomal localization analysis showed that the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H genes were unevenly distributed throughout the genome (Fig. 6). Gene duplication is a major evolutionary mechanism driving the emergence of new genes and influencing gene function and identity, thereby enhancing the adaptability of related species to diverse environmental changes [45]. It was also reported that the divergence in subfamily or gene number within the same gene family of different species was associated with their genome size or evolutionary differences [46]. The ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions was an effective predictor of selection pressure during gene duplication. A Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1 indicated that the evolution of gene was influenced by purifying selection rather than environmental factors [47]. Ka/Ks analysis revealed that both VbMYB genes and VbF3’5’H genes have undergone strong purifying selection rather than environmental selection pressure (Additional File 1: Table S3, Table S4).

Promoters serve as gene switches located upstream of coding regions and contain specific cis-acting elements that play a critical role in regulating gene transcription and expression [48]. The presence of MYB-binding sites on the VbF3’5’H7 promoter further suggested a potential association between VbMYB transcription factors and the VbF3’5’H gene family (Fig. 7). Through predicted protein-protein interaction, the associations between the VbF3’5’H and VbMYB gene families have been further revealed (Fig. 8A). Expression analysis via RT-qPCR revealed significant up-regulation of VbMYB4 and VbMYB97 during VBTF ripening (Fig. 9D). All VbF3’5’H members (except VbF3’5’H9) exhibited significant up-regulation during VBTF ripening (Fig. 9B). This may be mediated by the transcriptional activation of VbMYB transcription factors that were up-regulated simultaneously during this developmental stage. Then, the expression pattern of VbF3’5’Hs was verified by RT-qPCR in fruit samples collected at different ripening stages (Fig. 9C). Validation experiments encompassing 12 candidate genes were conducted via RT-qPCR; statistical analyses revealed a strong concordance between RT-qPCR quantification and RNA-seq expression profiles (Fig. 9D).

Agrobacterium-mediated transient assay demonstrated that both pVbMYB4::GUS and pVbMYB97::GUS constructs could activate GUS expression in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Comparative promoter activity analysis showed these MYB promoters exhibited reduced transcriptional potency compared to the positive control group, with the VbMYB4 promoter displaying marginally stronger activity than the VbMYB97 promoter (Fig. 10B).

The percent identity value between VbMYBA [15] and VbMYB97 was 98.25%, as confirmed by an BLASTn analysis. Also, VbMYBA was a strong anthocyanin activator and was capable of activating the promoters of DFR and UFGT from blueberry. This implied that the activator effect of VbMYB97 was not caused by an independent action of its promoter. The diminished activity in tobacco may indicate dependence on blueberry-specific transcriptional co-regulators (e.g., bHLH partners or WD40 proteins) absent in the heterologous system. This suggests that MYB transcriptional efficacy often requires species-adapted protein interaction networks [49]. Our results reinforced that transient expression systems may inadequately replicate native chromatin environments, particularly for plant-derived promoters requiring precise epigenetic landscapes [50]. Gene function can be reflected, to some extent, in the expression patterns of the genes [51]. The expression level of VbF3’5’H7 was significantly higher in blue fruit compared to green fruit and pink fruit. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of VbF3’5’H7 peaked in the blue fruit, suggesting it may participate in regulating fruit ripening (Fig. 9C).

The overexpression effect of the transgene frequently leads to stochastic and fluctuating expression patterns, primarily attributed to co-suppression [52–54]. This phenomenon was observed in the current study, where RT-qPCR analysis revealed a progressive and substantial reduction in VbF3’5’H7 transcript accumulation across successive generations (T0 to T2) in transgenic tomato lines engineered for constitutive overexpression of this key anthocyanin biosynthetic gene (Fig. 11A). Transcriptional profiling of anthocyanin biosynthetic genes was conducted in T2 generation fruits of VbF3’5’H7-overexpressing transgenic tomato lines (Solanum lycopersicum). The VbF3’5’H7 was successfully up-regulated in both line 14# and 22#, but the endogenous gene response levels were different, indicating that the position effect of the transgene significantly affected the regulatory efficiency [55]. The up-regulation of gene expression in line 14# proved that exogenous VbF3’5’H7 can drive the endogenous pathway in tomatoes. In line 22#, only the upstream PAL gene responded weakly, while the downstream genes (F3H/F3’H/DFR/ANS) were silent or even inhibited, suggesting that there might be an expression threshold in the downstream pathway. When the expression level of VbF3’5’H was insufficient, it could not be activated. In line 22#, ANS was down-regulated significantly, which might reflect the negative feedback triggered by the accumulation of metabolites (Fig. 11A). In the VbF3’5’H7-overexpressed transgenic tomato, the up-regulation of VbF3’5’H7 exhibited a positive correlation with anthocyanin accumulation (Fig. 11B). The content of titratable acid in VbF3’5’H7-overexpressed transgenic tomato fruits showed a slight increase compared with the WT (Fig. 11C). Crucially, photosynthetic pigment similarity indicated the genetic modification specifically enhanced anthocyanin biosynthesis without disrupting core photosynthesis [33].

Proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α modulate neuroendocrine signaling pathways, drive central nervous system neurotransmitter dysregulation, and precipitate the symptom complex clinically recognized as sickness behavior syndrome [56]. RT-qPCR results revealed that fruit crude extracts from tomato at the Br + 7 stage exhibited maximal anti-inflammatory potency in LPS-induced RAW264.7 macrophages, demonstrating significant positive correlation with anthocyanin accumulation. Similarly, the anthocyanins stemmed from red clover (Trifolium pratense) also possessed anti-inflammatory properties [57].

In summary, this study conducted a comprehensive investigation of the genome-wide analysis and functional characteristics of VbMYBs and VbF3’5’Hs in VBT, including physicochemical properties, phylogenetic analysis, gene structure and motifs, collinearity analysis, sequence comparison, chromosomal localization, cis-acting elements, and gene expression pattern. Comprehensive analysis of transcription factor binding sites (TFBS), predicted protein-protein interaction networks, and VbMYB expression patterns suggested a potential association between the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families (Figs. 7, 8 and 9A and B). Additionally, research showed that VbMYBA, as an anthocyanin regulatory factor, can activate the DFR and UFGT gene promoters of Vaccinium species [15]. In this study, the expression levels of seven anthocyanin biosynthesis-related genes (VbF3’5’H, PAL2, PAL3, F3H, F3’H, ANS, and DFR) were upregulated in transgenic tomato overexpressing VbF3’5’H7 (Fig. 11A), suggesting that VbF3’5’H may be associated with the VbMYB family. Further experimental validation (Y1/2H, Co-IP, and EMSA) was required to define the regulatory relationships between the two gene families. In addition, both anthocyanin content and anti-inflammatory activity were enhanced in VbF3’5’H7-overexpressing transgenic tomato (Figs. 11B and 12). These findings contributed to a better comprehension of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families, and provided insights into anthocyanin biosynthesis in Vaccinium bracteatum.

Methods

Identification of the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families

DNA, annotation file (GFF3), RNA, CDS and protein sequences used in this study are available in the China National Center for Bioinformation [58] (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gwh/Assembly/29523/show) under the BioProject PRJCA012184. Additionally, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) files for MYB and P450 domains (PF00249, PF00067) were obtained from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org) [59].

The hmmsearch function of HMMER [60] was used to identify MYB structural domains (e-value < 1.0 × 10− 5) in VBT. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MUSCLE [61], and a specific HMM for the MYB proteins of VBT was constructed using the hmmbuild function. A subsequent search was conducted to screen all proteins with e-value < 1.2 × 10− 22. These proteins were then submitted to the CDD [62], Pfam, and SMART [63] databases to confirm the presence of conserved structural domains. Protein sequences identified as containing MYB structural domains were recognized as VbMYB members and renamed according to their order of arrangement on the chromosome.

The F3’5’H protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana were obtained from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) [64] to construct a customized search library, which was aligned against the whole-genome sequence of VBT [65]. Candidate genes were retained based on the thresholds of e-value < 1.2 × 10− 20 and amino acid sequence identity higher than 50%. HMMER software was then used to detect the protein sequences of CYP75 candidates (e-value < 1.2 × 10− 22). CYP75A (F3’5’H) and CYP75B (F3’H) were distinguished based on amino acid sequence homology, with homology above 40% indicating the same family and homology above 55% indicating the same subfamily [66]. The genes were renamed according to their chromosomal arrangement order.

Analysis of physicochemical properties of the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families

The amino acid sequences of the identified members of the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene families were analyzed for their physicochemical properties using the ExPASy [67] online tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). This analysis included determining the number of amino acids, molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability coefficient, aliphatic amino acid index, average hydrophobicity, and signal peptide presence. Subcellular localization was predicted using the WoLF PSORT [68] online tool (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Phylogenetic Analysis of the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

The amino acid sequences of all 133 MYB of Arabidopsis thaliana (AtMYB) were downloaded from the TAIR database [69] (https://www.arabidopsis.org). Phylogenetic analysis was then performed with the identified MYB family members of VBT. Full-length MYB protein sequences from both groups were aligned using MUSCLE. Non-conserved region was trimmed using TrimAL [70], and an unrooted Neighbor-Joining tree (1,000 bootstrap replicates) was constructed using MEGA11 [71]. The resulting tree files were then visualized using the online tool EvolView [72] (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview-v2/). Similarly, the phylogenetic tree of the VbF3’5’H family was constructed using the same methods.

The protein sequences of Vaccinium darrowii and Vaccinium corymbosum were downloaded from GENOME DATABASE FOR VACCINIUM (https://www.vaccinium.org/). Using the protein sequences of the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H gene, which were used for qPCR analysis and tobacco transformation in this study, as queries, BlastP analysis was performed on the protein sequences of Vaccinium darrowii and Vaccinium corymbosum, respectively (with an E-value threshold < 1 × e⁻⁵). For each target sequence, we extracted the highest-scoring sequence as the most likely orthologous sequence and calculated its amino acid sequence identity (Identity %) (Additional File 1: Table S7).

Gene structure and motif analysis of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

The amino acid sequence files for the members of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H families were submitted to the Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) [73] program to predict conserved motifs. The search parameters were set to identify up to 10 motifs with optimal lengths ranging from 6 to 50 amino acids, with other settings left at their default values. The intron, CDS (Coding Sequences), and UTR (Untranslated Region) positions of the genes on the chromosome were obtained from the annotation file (GFF) of VBT and were submitted to Tbtools [74] to generate gene structure maps.

Collinearity analysis of the VbMYB

To further explore the expansion of the MYB gene family and elucidate the evolutionary relationships and events among different family members in VBT, an intraspecific BLAST [75] comparison of the protein sequences for all the genes within VBT was performed. The maximum number of target sequences (max_target_seqs) was set to five with an e-value < 1 × 10− 5. Gene location information was obtained from the annotation file (GFF3) of the VBT genome. MCScanX [76] was used to identify intraspecies gene collinearity, and the resulting data were visualized using Circos [77]. To gain further insight into the evolutionary dynamics of the MYB transcription factor family in VBT, Ka/Ks ratios for large fragment duplication gene pairs and tandem duplication gene pairs within the VbMYB family were also calculated by ParaAT [78] and KaKs_Calculator 3.0 [47].

Multiple sequence comparison and chromosomal localization of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

Multiple sequence alignments of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H family members in VBT were performed using ESPript, and the results were visualized using ESPript [79], with numbering enabled and 59 amino acid residues per line, while other settings remained at their defaults to export the alignment plot. Chromosome location information for the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H family members and chromosome length data were obtained from the VBT annotation file (GFF3). Gene localization on chromosomes was mapped using the MG2C [80] v2.1 online tool (https://qiaoyundeng.github.io/).

Analysis of cis-acting elements of the VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

The promoter sequences of all gene family members were submitted to PlantCARE [81] for analysis of cis-acting elements in their promoter regions. A total of 2000 bp sequence upstream of each gene TSS was used to identify cis-acting elements. The predicted cis-acting elements were divided into functional groups, including photoresponsivity, growth and metabolism, stress response, and hormone regulation. The MYB binding site and MYBHv1 binding site were also listed separately. The results were compiled and visualized using GSDS [82].

Transcriptomic analysis and differential gene expression profiling of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H

Sequencing and transcriptomic data used in this study are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database [34] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/794927) under the BioProject PRJNA794927. The following detailed information can be found for BioSample: SAMN24663519; Sample name: Vaccinium bracteatum leaf; DNA sequence Nanopore; SRA: SRS11518104. The screened differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subsequently filtered using dual thresholds of |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 1.5 and standard deviation > 0.5 across biological replicates. For visualization, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed based on Euclidean distance metrics [83], implemented in R Studio using the pheatmap package [84] (v1.0.12) with complete linkage clustering. The heatmap color scheme was optimized using the viridis palette to enhance the interpretability of expression patterns [85].

VbMYB and VbF3’5’H protein-protein interaction network prediction

Based on Arabidopsis homologs, the interaction predictions of VbMYB and VbF3’5’H proteins with other proteins were obtained using the online program STRING [86] (v12.0), with confidence levels > 0.400, and were used to construct a predicted protein-protein interactions network. Interaction networks were visualized in Cytoscape [87] (v3.10.2). A predicted protein-protein interaction network was constructed by submitting the sequences of 156 VbMYBs and nine VbF3’5’Hs to the STRING database. Then, Arabidopsis thaliana was used as a reference to map a protein network based on predicted interactions of orthologs.

Construction of the GUS fusion expression vector pVbMYB4::GUS and pVbMYB97::GUS

The VBT DNA was extracted from the leaf tissue of VBT using the FlaPure Plant DNA Extraction Kit (Genesand Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Approximately 2000 bp of the VbMYB4 and VbMYB97 upstream promoter sequences were isolated by PCR using specific primers (Additional File 1: Table S8). To obtain GUS fusion expression vector pVbMYB4::GUS and pVbMYB97::GUS, two promoter fragments were respectively cloned into the pBI101 vector between the SalI/BamHI restriction sites using the Hieff Clone® Universal II One Step Cloning Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) and fused with the GUS reporter gene. Then, the vectors were transformed into DH5α Chemically Competent Cell (Tolo Biotech Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) for transformation and positive clone screening. The completed recombinant vectors were submitted to the sequencing company General Biol Co., Ltd. (Chuzhou, China) for verification through sequencing.

Promoter activity analysis of VbMYB4 and VbMYB97 genes in tobacco

The two GUS fusion expression vectors pVbMYB4::GUS and pVbMYB97::GUS were transformed into GV3101 competent cells. The pBI101 vector fused with 35 S (p35S::GUS) was used as a positive control. Subsequently, the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method was employed, where an injection solution containing 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, and 200 µM AS was infiltrated into the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana (approximately 40-day-old, seeds preserved in our laboratory) for transformation [88]. The leaves were incubated overnight in the dark and then exposed to normal light for two days. The tissues were incubated in the staining solution (0.1% X-gluc, 50 mM NaH2PO4, 50 mM Na2HPO4, and 0.1% Triton X-100) and maintained in the dark at 37 °C for 24 h. After staining, the tissues were decolorized in 70% ethanol at 60 °C, and the staining results were observed once the leaves were completely decolorized [89].

Obtaining for VbF3’5’H7 over-expressed transgenic tomato

The length of 1,293 bp VbF3’5’H7 coding region was amplified (F: 5’-ACGGGGGACTCTTGACCATGGCTGTAGACACA-3’, R: 5’-CCTCAGATCTACCATAGTAGCATAGGCATTTTGA-3’) and then inserted into the pCAMBIA1301 plant expression vector with NcoI and BgIII endonucleases. The successfully constructed recombinant plasmid was named VbF3’5’H7-pCAMBIA1301. After that, the recombinant plasmid was transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101, and the leaf disc method was employed in WT tomato plants (seeds preserved in our laboratory) to obtain transgenic individuals [90].

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL (Yfxbio Biotech (Nanjing) Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). RNA concentration was measured with Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland), and 2 µL of RNA samples with OD260/280 and OD260/230 values between 1.8 and 2.0 were selected for the experiment. Then, the reverse transcription kit (with dsDNase) (Biosharp Biotechnology, China) was used to reverse transcribe qualified RNA into cDNA.

The SYBR Prime qPCR Set (BioGround Biotechnology Institution (Chongqing) Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China) and CFX96 real-time PCR system (Hercules, CA, USA) were used for RT-qPCR to determine the relative expression levels of genes, with three independent biological replicates for each treatment. Slactin was used as an internal reference gene; the relative expression level of the target genes was calculated using the cycle threshold (Ct) 2−ΔΔCt method [91]. The primers of PAL2, PAL3, F3H, DFR, ANS, and F3’H were sourced from Tang et al. [92], the primer of IL-1β was sourced from Yang et al. [93], the primer of IL-6 was sourced from Kumpunya et al. [94], and the primer of TNF-α was sourced from Zeng et al. [95] All the primers used in this study are listed in Additional File 1: Table S9.

Determination of anthocyanins contents

Total anthocyanin content (TAC) in the tomato fruit sample was determined using the pH differential method [96]. Firstly, about 2 g of the fruit sample was ground sufficiently with liquid nitrogen. Then, 95% acidic ethanol was added with a ratio of 1:6 (g/mL) and transferred into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The centrifuge tube containing the sample was placed in a 40℃ ultrasonic cleaning machine (with the bottle cap tightened) for 20 min and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3 min. After that, the extract was filtered and transferred into another 50 mL centrifuge tube. And the residue was extracted twice again according to the above procedure (the extract was collected three times in total). For the determination of content, two 1 mL volumes of extraction solution were added to buffer A (0.2 mol/L KCl: 0.2 mol/L HCl = 25:67, pH = 1.0) and buffer B (1 mol/L NaOAc: 1 mol/L HCl: ddH2O = 100:60:90, pH = 4.5). After standing for one and a half hours, the light absorption value at 520 nm was measured and calculated according to the formula: C (mg/g) = ((A0-A1) *V*N*M)/(e*m), where A0 and A1 represent the light absorption values of anthocyanins at pH 1.0 and pH 4.5, respectively.

Measurement of titratable acidity

Determination of titratable acid concentration by continuous titration with an acidimeter [97]. 1 g (m) of homogenized tomato juice was added to beaker and diluted with 25 mL distilled water. Subsequently, 0.1 mol/L NaOH was added, while the pH of the solution was monitored in real time using pH meter until the pH reached 8.2. The volume of 0.1 mol/L NaOH consumed (V₁) was recorded. A control group was prepared by replacing the sample solution with distilled water, and the corresponding volume of 0.1 mol/L NaOH consumed (V0) was also recorded. The titratable acidity content (%) = (V1-V0)*C*K*100/m. C represents the concentration of NaOH. K represents the coefficient of acid, and the citric acid coefficient of 0.064 is used in this article.

Measurement of chlorophyll and carotenoid contents

Determination contents of chlorophyll and carotenoid using spectrophotometer method [98]. 0.1 g tomato sample was ground into powder using liquid nitrogen, then transferred to a test tube containing 5 mL 95% ethanol. The mixture was homogenized and allowed to stand for 24 h in the dark. The extract was filtered into a brown volumetric flask. Using 95% ethanol as the blank control, the absorbance of the sample extract was measured at wavelengths of 665 nm, 649 nm, and 470 nm, respectively. Preparation of tomato fruit crude extract

The fruits of WT and heterologous VbF3’5’H7-OE transgenic tomatoes (lines 14# and 22#) at the breaker and seven days post breaker (Br + 7) stages were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground into powder, and then completely freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze dryer (LGJ-10, Songyuan, Beijing, China). For extraction, 0.2 g of dried samples was mixed with 60 mL of methanol, and shaken in the dark for 24 h at room temperature. After filtration, the supernatant was collected and concentrated using a rotary evaporator (R-1001VN, Great Wall Scientific Industrial and Trade Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China) to remove methanol completely. Subsequently, the crude extracts were resuspended in deionized water and sonicated (KQ-300DE, Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd., Kunshan, China). The extracts from WT and transgenic tomato fruits were then completely dried again using a vacuum freeze dryer. Finally, the samples were dissolved in DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) to a concentration of 100 mg/mL and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter before cell treatment [99].

Gain-of-function of VbF3’5’H7 enhanced the anti-inflammatory effect of the tomato fruits

The murine RAW 264.7 macrophage cells were obtained from ATCC (American Type Culture Collection) and cultivated at 37℃ with 5% CO2. The high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium added with 1% antibiotics (streptomycin and penicillin) and 10% fetal bovine serum was used for macrophage cultivation. The cells were treated with the tomato extracts at 100 µg/mL in the presence of LPS (1.0 µg/mL). The control group was treated with an equal volume of DMSO. After 16 h of treatment, the cytokine expressions in macrophages, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, were measured by RT-qPCR [100].
