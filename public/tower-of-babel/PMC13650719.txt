Image-guided moderately hypofractionated proton beam therapy for localized prostate cancer: 5-year outcomes of a phase II trial
Authors: Takashi Iizumi, Hitoshi Ishikawa, Yuta Sekino, Motohiro Murakami, Keiichi Tanaka, Daichi Takizawa, Hirokazu Makishima, Yutaro Mori, Satoshi Kamizawa, Masashi Mizumoto, Toshiyuki Okumura, Hiroyoshi Suzuki, Hiroyuki Nishiyama, Hideyuki Sakurai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of the present study is to confirm the efficacy and feasibility of moderately hypofractionated proton beam therapy (PBT) at a total dose of 63 Gy relative biological effectiveness (RBE) in 21 fractions for prostate cancer. This phase II clinical trial (PPS-002: UMIN 000017679) enrolled 125 men with histologically confirmed prostate cancer (cT1c-3aN0M0). The primary endpoint was grade ≥2 late gastrointestinal (GI) toxicities. Secondary endpoints included biochemical recurrence rates, clinical recurrence rates, overall survival (OS) and acute toxicities and late genitourinary (GU) toxicities. With a median follow-up period of 71.0 months (IQR: 60–84), the 5-year cumulative incidences of late grade 2 or higher GI and GU adverse events (AEs) were 3.3% and 8.1%, respectively. Biochemical recurrences were observed in three patients; no clinical recurrences occurred. The 5-year biochemical recurrence-free, clinical recurrence-free and OS rates were 97.6% (95% CI: 94.9–100.0), 100% and 97.5% (95% CI: 94.8–100.0), respectively. The long-term follow-up results of moderately hypofractionated PBT of 63 Gy (RBE) in 21 fractions confirmed low incidences of late AEs, successfully meeting the primary endpoint. Furthermore, the low incidence of tumor recurrence supports the overall safety and feasibility of this fractionation schedule.

INTRODUCTION

Prostate cancer is a global health burden, with nearly 1.5 million new cases in 2022 [1]. In Japan, a total of 102 094 newly diagnosed prostate cancer cases were observed [2] and prostate cancer is projected to become the leading cancer burden in terms of incidence [3]. Regarding disease control, external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) seems comparable to prostatectomy in the treatment for localized prostate cancer [4] and thus is thought as one of the curative treatment options.

Technological advancements in treatment delivery and image guidance have enabled the highly conformal delivery of higher biologic doses of radiation and have significantly improved oncologic outcomes with reduced treatment-associated adverse events (AEs). Proton beam therapy (PBT) has the inherent physical property of particles by which most of their energy is deposited over the narrow target area, with a sharp falloff in dose beyond the target [5]. The resulting dose distribution allows PBT to deposit the lower dose and volume in the rectum and bladder compared with EBRT and can potentially reduce gastrointestinal (GI) and genitourinary (GU) AEs in RT for prostate cancer [6]. Hypofractionation, delivering higher radiation doses per treatment session, is enticing due to its potential to provide similar treatment outcomes for localized prostate cancer with the shorter total number of treatment sessions. The radiobiological basis for this strategy lies in the low α/β ratio of prostate cancer cells, estimated to be ~1.5–3.0 Gy. This low value indicates that prostate cancer cells are more sensitive to the increased dose per fraction provided by hypofractionation compared to surrounding normal tissues, which have higher α/β ratios. This differential sensitivity offers a therapeutic advantage, allowing for improved tumor control while maintaining or reducing toxicity. Previous randomized clinical trials have shown that moderate hypofractionation has been established as a standard-care option [7–11].

PPS is a trial evaluating hypofractionated PBT for the treatment of localized prostate cancer at our institution. PPS-001 was a single institutional phase II trial (UMIN000010510) of a moderately hypofractionated PBT course of 70 Gy relative biological effectiveness (RBE) with a fractional dose of 2.5 Gy (RBE) and has shown the safety and efficacy of this hypofractionation regimen [12, 13]. However, there have been relatively few reports on hypofractionated PBT for prostate cancer, especially on long-term safety and efficacy of 3.0 Gy (RBE) single dose PBT. Building on findings in PPS-001, the current phase II study investigates the safety and efficacy of an increased dose of 3.0 Gy (RBE) per fraction, aiming to enhance the radiobiological benefits of this approach. The synergy of PBT’s precise dose deposition and the radiobiological benefits of hypofractionation makes moderately hypofractionated PBT a promising modality for localized prostate cancer.

Here, we report the primary analysis of a single institutional phase II trial (PPS-002), which assessed the 5-year clinical outcomes of 63 Gy (RBE) with a fractional dose of 3.0 Gy (RBE).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

From June 2015 to November 2018, 127 men were enrolled in a prospective clinical trial of moderately hypofractionated PBT for prostate cancer. This trial has been approved by the institutional review board and local ethics committee of the University of Tsukuba Hospital (document number H27–020). Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Eligible patients must have histologically confirmed primary prostate cancer of stage cT1c-3aN0M0 and age of 20–80 years. Patients had staging work-up with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pelvis, computed tomography (CT) scan of the pelvis and bone scan. All the patients were categorized according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network risk criteria after discussion with urologists in our department of urology. Patients with pelvic or distant lymph node metastasis, with serious complications (severe infection, heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, respiratory failure, liver failure, renal failure, acute cerebrovascular disorder, or poorly controlled diabetes), with active multiple cancers, with a history of transurethral resection within 6 months of obtaining informed consent, with a history of pelvic irradiation, with severe dementia, or receiving antithrombotic therapy that could not be temporarily discontinued during insertion of the fiducial markers were excluded.

Protocol treatment

Simulation, CTV and treatment planning

For each patient, two fiducial markers (Gold Anchor; Naslund Medical AB, Huddinge, Sweden, or VISICOIL; RadioMed, Barlett, TN, USA) were implanted in the prostate before CT simulation for the use of image-guided PBT. No patient received perirectal hydrogel spacer placement. A patient was in supine position with a custom vacuum-locked back during a planning CT and each session of PBT. The clinical target volume (CTV) was contoured on CT images, including the entire prostate and proximal seminal vesicles. MRI was used to assist in delineating the CTV. The planning target volume was constructed by a 3D 10-mm expansion around the CTV, except 5-mm expansion in the posterior direction. To account for range uncertainties and daily anatomical shifts resulting from fiducial-based alignment, the CTV was expanded to the PTV with appropriate target margins and the opposed lateral beams were designed with range-compensator smearing and appropriate distal margins during treatment planning. The spread out Bragg peak (SOBP) width was selected to sufficiently encompass the PTV after applying the distal margin, thereby providing sufficient margin on the proximal side. For the dose reduction to the rectum, the posterior end of the irradiation field was set in front of the anterior wall of the rectum using multi-leaf collimators after twelve fractions. Dose-volume histogram (DVH) parameters of rectum and bladder were collected from treatment plans.

Daily proton therapy

The prescribed PBT dose was a total of 63 Gy (RBE) in 21 fractions with a fractional dose of 3.0 Gy (RBE). Daily treatment was performed using a passive scattering method with daily matching of the intraprostatic fiducial markers using kilovoltage orthogonal X-ray images, while confirming the bladder volume by daily ultrasound just before each treatment. The PBT planning details have been reported previously [14].

Hormone therapy

Hormone therapy was administered according to the patient risk classification. Intermediate-risk patients received neoadjuvant hormone therapy for 6–12 months before irradiation, which was terminated immediately after the completion of PBT. High-risk patients received long-term hormone therapy for a total duration of 3 years, continuing for ~2.5 years after PBT. The standard regimen consisted of a luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LH-RH) agonist alone or orchiectomy, although combined androgen blockade with an antiandrogen was permitted. Antiandrogen monotherapy was not allowed.

Follow-up

Toxicities were evaluated using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 4.0. During treatment, physicians scored AEs weekly during PBT. Subsequently, follow-up assessments were conducted every 3 months until 3 years after treatment initiation and every 6 months from 3 to 5 years after treatment initiation. Late AEs were events persisting or developing >3 months from the start of PBT. The prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level was obtained before and at the end of treatment and then at the follow-up visits. Biochemical recurrence was defined as a rise in PSA level by 2 ng/ml or more above the nadir PSA according to the Phoenix definition [15] and clinical recurrence was defined as local recurrence, nodal recurrence distant metastases, or death from prostate cancer.

Study design

This single-institutional, single-arm, open-label, phase II trial (PPS-002: UMIN 000017679) was designed to assess the efficacy and feasibility of moderately hypofractionated PBT. The primary endpoint of the study was grade ≥2 late GI toxicities. The secondary endpoints were biochemical recurrence, clinical recurrence, overall survival (OS) and prostate cancer-specific survival and late AEs other than GI toxicities. Data from patients without an event were censored on the date of the last PSA assessment.

Statistical analysis

A sample size of this study was calculated with a hypothesis that ≤3% of patients would have grade ≥2 late GI AEs with threshold of 10% based on the previous studies [14, 16, 17]. One-hundred fourteen patients were required for enrollment to achieve at upper limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the event rate below the threshold rate of 10%. We finally decided on a sample size of 120, assuming a rate of patient ineligibility of 5%. The planned accrual and follow-up periods after registration were set to 3 and 5 years, respectively.

The results are presented as median (interquartile range (IQR)) for continuous variables and as numbers (percentage) of patients for categorical variables. The survival probabilities of the primary and secondary endpoints were estimated using Kaplan–Meier method. The analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://r-project.org/).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 127 patients were enrolled in the study between June 2015 and November 2018 and all completed the planned PBT. Of these, two patients were excluded: one withdrew consent due to a preference for conventional fractionation and one did not meet the eligibility criteria after registration. As a result, 125 patients were finally evaluated. Table 1 depicts the characteristics of these patients. Median age was 68 years (IQR: 65–73). 60 high-risk patients (48.0%) were included in this study, whereas 13 (10.4%) and 52 (41.6%) patients were stratified into low-risk and intermediate-risk, respectively. 19 patients (15.2%) had diabetes mellitus (DM) and 15 patients (12.0%) were on anticoagulant therapy.

Toxicity

Within 5 years, grade 2 or higher GI AEs were observed in four of 125 patients (3.2%) (grade 2 bleeding in three and grade 3 rectal ulcer in one). The cumulative incidence of late grade 2 or higher GI AEs up to 5 years was 3.3% (95% CI: 0.1–6.4) (Fig. 1a).

Incidence of grade 2 or higher GI and GU toxicity.

By 5 years, grade 2 or higher GU AEs were observed in 5 of 125 patients (4.0%). The cumulative incidence of late grade 2 or higher GU AEs up to 5 years was 8.1% (95% CI: 4.4–14.5) (Fig. 1b). No grade 3 or higher late GU AEs were observed.

Survival

At a median follow-up of 71.0 months (IQR: 60–84), biochemical recurrence occurred in three patients (all of whom were in the intermediate-risk group, occurring at 3, 16 and 29 months, respectively), while no clinical recurrence was observed. The 5-year biochemical recurrence-free (BCRF) rate or clinical recurrence-free (CRF) rate were 97.6% (95% CI: 94.9–100.0) and 100% (Fig. 3a and b).

Cumulative survival outcomes.

Death associated with prostate cancer was not reported. However, three patients died of gastric cancer at 56 months, pneumonia at 46 months, or Coronavirus disease 2019 at 48 months, respectively, after PBT without any signs of recurrence from prostate cancer. The 5-year OS rate was 97.5% (95% CI: 94.8–100.0) (Fig. 3c).

DISCUSSION

Based on established evidence from X-ray EBRT, the present evidence-based guidelines recommend the use of moderate hypofractionation for patients with localized prostate cancer who prefer RT as their treatment modality [18], but long-term data regarding moderately hypofractionated PBT remain limited. The present phase II trial, designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of a moderately hypofractionated PBT regimen of 63 Gy (RBE) in 21 fractions, successfully demonstrated low rates of late grade 2 or higher GI AEs. Specifically, the 5-year cumulative incidence of late grade 2 or higher GI AEs was limited to 3.3% (95% CI: 0.1–6.4), which successfully met the primary endpoint of this study. Notably, this regimen also achieved excellent disease control, yielding a 5-year BCRF rate of 97.6%, CRF rate of 100% and OS rates of 97.5%, respectively. When compared to the 5-year cumulative incidences of 1.0–4.3% and 4.0–5.8% for grade 2 or higher GI and GU AEs observed in our previous reports of conventional fractionation (74–78 Gy(RBE) in 37–39 fractions) or moderate hypofractionation (70 Gy(RBE) in 28 fractions) for localized prostate cancer [12, 14], the current protocol (63 Gy(RBE) in 21 fractions) demonstrated equivalent GI toxicity and slightly higher GU toxicity (8.1%). However, this incidence of late GU toxicity is comparable to the rates reported in other major Japanese studies of intensity-modulated radiotherapy, carbon-ion radiotherapy and PBT, which typically range from 8 to 12% [16, 19, 20]. Therefore, we consider this toxicity profile to be clinically acceptable, and the current protocol remains a feasible option under our treatment policy.

The use of moderately hypofractionated PBT has been previously explored, with two Japanese groups on acute AEs and patient reported outcome measures utilizing a total dose of 60–63 Gy (RBE) delivered in 20–21 fractions (corresponding to 3.0 Gy (RBE) per fraction) [13, 18]. When this trial was designed in 2015, prior to the establishment of the 60 Gy in 20 fractions regimen as the universal standard in current guidelines, a dose of 63 Gy (RBE) was selected. This regimen was calculated using radiobiological modeling to provide a high biological effective dose (BED) equivalent to high-dose conventional fractionation, aiming to maximize local tumor control while leveraging the physical properties of PBT to spare normal tissues. Regarding late AEs, Slater et al. reported 5.1% of grade 2 or higher GI AEs at 3 years [21]. Crucially, the methodology of this earlier trial noted that fiducial markers were not used for daily patient positioning. In contrast, the present study utilized an almost identical dose-fraction regimen but observed a notably lower incidence of corresponding Grade 2 or higher GI AEs, which was limited to 3.3% at 5 years. While this difference in toxicity might be suggestive, making direct comparisons of toxicity rates across studies remains challenging due to inherent patient heterogeneity, including variability in baseline characteristics such as DM status and the use of anticoagulants. However, the lower toxicity observed in the current cohort is hypothesized to result from the use of image-guided RT (IGRT) with fiducial markers using a shrunken field technique, significantly contributed to this more favorable toxicity rate.

Despite adherence to generally accepted rectal dose constraints, a 70-year-old patient developed Grade 3 rectal ulcer in the present study. The patient presented with a complex medical history, including DM and concomitant anticoagulant therapy. This grade 3 rectal ulcer was observed subsequent to a new diagnosis of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and prophylactic argon plasma coagulation (APC), which had been performed for radiation-associated vascular ectasias (RAVE) when no notable active bleeding was present. We postulate that the severity of rectal ulcer in this case was potentially amplified by the presence of pre-existing complications such as DM and MDS, concomitant anticoagulant therapy and prior prophylactic APC. Especially, APC is an effective ablative therapy for RAVE but carries a known risk of nonhealing ulceration with bleeding [22]. Notably, the healing process in the irradiated rectum is impaired in a dose-dependent manner [23]. To mitigate the risk of severe rectal bleeding and rectal ulcer in similar high-risk patients, a proactive preventive strategy such as use of hydrogel spacer is warranted.

This study has several inherent limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the results. First, the current study is a single-arm, single-institution design. This limits the generalizability of the findings and precludes a direct comparison with other treatment modalities, such as conventionally fractionated PBT or X-ray intensity-modulated radiation therapy. Meanwhile, two multi-center prospective Japanese trials are currently addressing these comparative questions. PC-001 (UMIN000025453) is a multi-institutional study evaluating the safety of 63 Gy (RBE) in 21 fractions using PBT for intermediate-risk patients, and JROSG-IMRT-PC01 (UMIN000033172) is ongoing to compare different treatment modalities. Second, the trial design for this study excluded patients with serious comorbidities or those requiring continuous anticoagulant therapy that could not be temporarily discontinued. Consequently, the observed low toxicity profile may not be directly translatable to the broader, more heterogeneous patient population in the real-world clinical settings. Caution is therefore necessary when applying this hypofractionated PBT regimen to patients with significant baseline comorbidities. Third, the primary endpoint of this study was toxicity and the limited case numbers within each defined risk group prevent definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of this specific dose fraction across all risk categories. Nonetheless, it is notable that despite the presence of high-risk patients (comprising almost half of the cohort), the observed recurrence rate was extremely low in the present study.

CONCLUSION

This study provides crucial long-term results for prostate cancer patients underwent moderately hypofractionated PBT of 63 Gy (RBE) in 21 fractions. We demonstrated low incidence of late adverse GU and GI events alongside excellent disease control, validating the safety of this fractionation schedule.
