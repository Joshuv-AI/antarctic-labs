Factors associated with tuberculosis symptom reporting among persons deprived of liberty in Brazil during active case finding: a cross-sectional study
Authors: José Victor Bortolotto Bampi, Argita D Salindri, Isabella Beatriz Gonçalves, Jaqueline Silveira Coene, Mariana Garcia Croda, Alessandra Moura da Silva, Esther Jung, Caroline Busatto, Roberto Dias de Oliveira, Eunice Atsuko Totumi Cunha, Jason R Andrews, Julio Croda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionActive case finding (ACF) can improve tuberculosis (TB) diagnosis in high-burden populations. Many programmes rely on symptom evaluation as the first screening step; however, symptom reporting may vary according to the context of evaluation.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional analysis of persons deprived of liberty (PDL) diagnosed with TB in an ACF programme conducted in two male Brazilian prisons from February 2023 to February 2025. Every 4 months, participants underwent sputum testing by GeneXpert Ultra (Xpert) and culture. Study nurses administered symptom questionnaires during the initial evaluation. Individuals with positive results were interviewed by a physician. We compared characteristics of individuals with microbiologically confirmed TB according to their symptom reporting at initial screening and medical consultation, which was used to define four groups (e.g., combinations of symptomatic/asymptomatic at each of the two symptom assessments).ResultsAmong 6919 PDL screened, 448 (6.4%) had microbiologically confirmed TB. We included 292 (65.1%) individuals in the analysis, after excluding those with missing clinical information and consults >15 days after screening. 113 (38.7%) reported symptoms at initial screening, while 192 (65.8%) reported symptoms at medical consultation (p<0.001). Compared with individuals asymptomatic at both evaluations, those who reported symptoms at the two instances were more likely to have medium or high semiquantitative Xpert results (45.0% vs 11.4%, p<0.001), report symptomatic cellmates (61.0% vs 25.2%, p<0.001), have higher LunitTB scores (95.1 vs 91.9; p=0.037) and lower body mass index (19.4 vs 21.4, p<0.001). There was a trend of more cavitary lesions (60.9% vs 50.0%, p=0.073) and bilateral disease (56.7% vs 45.0%, p=0.056) in chest X-ray of participants with symptomatic than asymptomatic TB at medical consultation.ConclusionsMore than half of individuals who reported no TB symptoms at initial screening endorsed symptoms at first medical consultation. Clinical severity and contextual factors can influence TB symptom reporting during ACF.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Mass Screening, Population Surveillance

Introduction

Despite recent advances in prevention and diagnosis strategies, tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the biggest global health problems, affecting ~10.8 million people worldwide in 2024.1 With growing focus on early diagnosis, asymptomatic TB is increasingly recognised as an important driver of the global TB epidemic.2 3 To date, many active case finding (ACF) programmes rely on symptom screening.4 5 However, due to the inconsistencies in how symptoms are defined and ascertained, there is a considerable variability in asymptomatic TB burden reported in studies and prevalence surveys.6,8 Furthermore, symptom reporting, like other self-reported measures, is prone to response biases including those induced by differential recall, social pressures, mistrust of healthcare providers, or disinterest.9 10 Assessing the validity of and understanding sources of variation of symptom screening ascertainment methods is critical to accurate estimates of asymptomatic TB burden in different settings.

Additionally, whether the clinical manifestations and lung abnormalities differ between individuals with symptomatic and asymptomatic TB remains unclear. Currently, ACF strategies using X-ray are recommended by the WHO11 and can provide important data about how radiographic manifestations of TB relate to symptoms. Understanding the extent of disease manifestation among individuals with asymptomatic TB will be important for designing effective screening strategies and understanding how early detection can reduce morbidity.

WHO screening guidelines identify people deprived of liberty (PDL) as a key population for systematic screening. Carceral settings serve as a reservoir for TB disease and transmission, which may contribute to the increase in TB incidence among the general population.11,13 In Latin America, the TB incidence rate among PDL was estimated to be nearly 27-fold higher than that of the general population. Emerging findings suggest that, even in high-prevalence settings such as prisons, asymptomatic TB still contributes substantially to Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission.314,18

To assess variations in symptom reporting and clinical characteristics associated with symptomatic status, we evaluated symptom reporting by participants with microbiologically confirmed TB screened during an ACF study in high TB-burden prisons, comparing responses obtained during their initial TB screening and subsequent clinic-based assessment. Further, we compared demographic and clinical characteristics of individuals diagnosed with TB according to their symptom reporting in these two evaluations.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted an analysis of individuals with microbiologically confirmed TB diagnosed during a prospective cohort study of ACF conducted in two male prisons located in the state of Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil, from February 2023 to February 2025. The prisons had an occupancy of approximately 4000 individuals incarcerated at any given time during the period of data collection.

Eligible participants included adult PDL (≥18 years old) who provided consent. The study included baseline TB screening and follow-up screening every 4 months for up to 36 for those who had (a) two negative GeneXpert Ultra (Xpert) test results (ie, pool and individual Xpert tests) at baseline screening and (b) at least 12 months remaining sentence time. For our primary analyses, we only included PDL with microbiologically confirmed TB disease who had a medical consultation within 15 days of their initial screening. We excluded individuals with missing chest radiographs or incomplete clinical data and those who were diagnosed outside of the systematic screening procedures. Prison staff were not involved in data collection and were not informed about individual decisions to participate in the study and there were no reprimands or penalties for individuals who did not want to participate.

Screening procedures

Our study was performed in the context of systematic screening offered to all PDL in the study prisons. We approached individuals through trained study team members announcing study procedures at the entrance of cell blocks during yard time to offer participation and determine eligibility. We did not collect information on individuals who refused to participate; however, in the past, participation rates were above 95% of the total population at any given time.

Study nurses obtained consent discussion for eligible individuals, followed by the administration of standardised study questionnaires to collect data on sociodemographic characteristics, medical and incarceration history, use of illicit drugs (marijuana, crack, cocaine, base paste, glue/solvents and intravenous drugs) and tobacco in the last year, and exposure to cellmates presenting with TB symptoms. Subsequently, PDL were asked about their TB symptoms (cough, fever, weight loss, night sweats, shortness of breath and chest pain), regardless of duration. Each symptom was individually queried in sequence (i.e., ‘Do you have cough?’, ‘Do you have fever?).

Following the interview, individuals who provided consent, regardless of their symptoms, were requested to provide a spot sputum sample for GeneXpert MTB/Rif Ultra (Xpert) and culture test using solid media (i.e., Lowestein-Jensen and Ogawa-Kudoh media for those with a positive Xpert result, Ogawa-Kudoh media for those with a negative Xpert result). Two sputum samples were collected at the initial screening: the first evaluated through pooling eight samples and testing by Xpert19 and the second tested individually by Xpert. After sputum sample collection, a posterior–anterior chest X-ray (CXR) image was obtained by using either a conventional Sawae unit (Prison A) or the FDR Xair portable X-ray system (Prison B). All CXR images were scored using LunitTB (V.3.1.5.1), a deep learning algorithm developed by Lunit (Seoul, South Korea). In our analyses, we included the numerical scores of each pulmonary abnormality (i.e., TB, consolidation, nodule, fibrosis, atelectasis and calcification) and a composite score called ‘abnormality score’ with the highest result among those reported. The scores provided by Lunit vary between 0 and 100, where higher scores indicate a higher likelihood of TB (for the LunitTB score) or the abnormality described.

Definitions and standard of care

We defined microbiologically confirmed TB disease by a positive Xpert or culture result. Individuals with Xpert trace results and negative culture test results were considered as having invalid Xpert results and, therefore, excluded from primary analyses. Individuals with positive Xpert or culture results were invited to visit the prison health clinic for a medical evaluation; study participants were not informed in advance about their test results prior to consult but the responsible physicians were aware of the results. Two infectious diseases physicians were responsible for patient care during the study period, performing the initial medical consultation and informing patients about the test results. The consult was performed according to Brazilian guidelines of TB care and symptom data was collected through individual report during consult, all symptoms reported were evaluated in respect to those inquired in the screening questionnaire, regardless of duration. Physicians also reviewed CXR results for abnormalities consistent with active TB, including the presence of cavitary lesions of any size, noting whether abnormal findings involved one (unilateral) or both lungs (bilateral). Height and weight (for body mass index (BMI) calculation) were also collected during the first medical consultation. Treatment was initiated at this first medical consult to individuals with microbiologically confirmed TB.

Study measures and definition

For our main analysis, we referred to symptom status responses collected during the medical consultation, where ‘asymptomatic TB’ was defined as TB disease in PDL who did not report any TB symptoms, irrespective of duration; otherwise PDL were categorised as ‘symptomatic TB’. To determine symptom concordance, we compared responses to TB symptom questionnaires administered during the initial screening (i.e., the same day as sputum sample collection for most participants) versus the first medical consultation. We defined concordance when PDL gave consistent answers with respect to reporting of any TB symptoms during the initial screening and the first medical consultation. Thus, discordance was defined when PDL gave different answers for the initial TB screening and the first medical consultation (i.e., reported at least one TB symptom during the initial screening but none during the first medical consultation or vice versa).

Data management and statistical analysis

Data collected during the initial TB screening and the first medical consultations were stored in an online RedCap (Vanderbilt University, Tennessee, USA) database hosted by Fiocruz, Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil.20 We used χ² (categorical predictors) and Wilcoxon rank sum (continuous predictors) tests to determine the crude association between sociodemographic and clinical predictors with symptomatic TB and discordance of symptom reporting.

We aimed to evaluate factors related to symptomatic disease in a predictive model that was performed under the assumption that symptom reporting at medical consult would provide a better clinical description of the symptoms. Thus, we used multivariable logistic regression models to determine factors predictive of symptomatic TB, referenced as responses given in medical consultation. In the model adjustment, we included the variables that had statistically significant association to the symptom outcome in the bivariate analysis and possible confounders reported in previous studies. Xpert semiquantitative results were grouped into High/Medium and Low/Very Low/Trace for the group comparison. For the primary comparison of clinical characteristics among individuals with TB who reported symptoms at the medical visit versus those who did not, the minimum detectable OR detectable with 80% power would range from 2.00 for a covariate present in 50% of the reference group to 2.45 for a covariate present in 10% of the reference group.

In addition to evaluating any reported TB symptom, we performed secondary analyses in which we defined symptomatic TB as reporting any of the symptoms included in the WHO four symptom screening (W4SS) tool.11 All statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.3.2).21

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in this study.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

During the 2-year study period, 6919 PDL provided consent to participate and were included in the study. A total of 448 (6.4%) individuals with TB disease were identified, and TB treatment was initiated. Of these, 156 were excluded from our primary analyses (23 individuals had missing CXR data or clinical data, 41 individuals were diagnosed outside of our ACF procedures, and 92 individuals had >15 days gap between their TB screening and the first medical consultation dates) (figure 1). Of the 292 individuals (65.1%) with microbiologically confirmed TB included in this analysis, the median age was 31 years (IQR 27–36), and the majority were mixed-race (80.1%) and completed 4–12 years of schooling (84.6%) (online supplemental table 1). Previous incarceration was common (87.0%). The prevalence of current tobacco smoking was 75.0%, and 79.5% of study participants reported use of illicit drugs in the past year. 24.0% of individuals reported previous TB and the prevalence of HIV co-infection was 2.4%. Nearly half (43.2%) of study participants reported having one or more cellmates with any TB symptoms.

Overall, the median time between TB screening and the first medical consultation was 8 days (IQR 6–9), and the majority (65%) of individuals had ≤10 day interval. Most individuals had a low, very low, or trace Xpert result (72.9%). The median BMI at diagnosis was 20.5 kg/m2 (IQR 18.6–22.8), and 21.9% of our study participants were underweight (BMI<18.5 kg/m2) at the first medical consultation (online supplemental table 2).

Burden of symptomatic TB and discordance in symptom reporting

Overall, only 38.7% (113/292) of individuals with microbiologically confirmed TB disease in our study reported TB symptoms during the initial screening. However, at the first medical consultation, this number significantly increased to 65.8% (192/292, p<0.001) (table 1). Among those who reported at least one TB symptom during the initial screening, the majority (67/113; 59.3%) reported two or more TB symptoms. The top three most reported symptoms during the initial screening included cough (34.9%), chest pain (16.0%), and difficulty breathing (14.0%). Among those who reported at least one TB symptom during the first medical consultation, more than a third reported two TB symptoms (71/192, 36.9%) and the top three most frequently reported symptoms included cough (52.3%), weight loss (27.3%) and chest pain (20.8%). We observed a similar trend when we defined symptomatic TB using the W4SS definition (table 1).

Individuals who reported no symptoms at initial screening but did report symptoms at medical consultation had distributions of several clinical and exposure characteristics that fell between those of individuals who were either asymptomatic or symptomatic at both evaluations. These included reporting having a cellmate with symptoms (41.3%, compared with 25.2% in those asymptomatic on both evaluations and 61% among those with symptoms on both evaluations; p<0.001 for both comparisons); underweight (21.7% vs 11.4% and 33%; p=0.027 and p=0.009); Xpert semiquantitative load medium/high (22.9% vs 11.4% and 45%; p=0.050 and p=0.001) and Lunit abnormality score (median 84.2 vs 80.9 and 87.5; p=0.032 and p=0.558) (table 2).

Clinical manifestations of symptomatic TB at medical consult

We considered symptom status at medical consult for the primary analysis. Individuals with symptomatic TB were similar to those with asymptomatic TB in regard to age distribution, race, education attainment level, previous incarceration, use of illicit drugs in the past year, previous TB history, HIV co-infection status and culture positivity (p>0.05). Compared with individuals with asymptomatic TB, individuals with symptomatic TB were more likely to report having ≥1 cellmates with any TB symptoms (51.5% vs 27.0%, p<0.001) or current smoking (78.6% vs 68.0%, p=0.013). The median BMI among individuals with symptomatic TB was lower compared with those with asymptomatic TB (19.9 vs 21.4, p=0.002) (online supplemental table 1).

Individuals with high/medium bacterial load on semi-quantitative Xpert were more likely to have symptomatic TB (34.3% vs 13.0%, p<0.001). The Lunit TB, abnormality and consolidation scores were all significantly higher in individuals with symptomatic TB compared with those with asymptomatic TB. We observed a trend of more advanced radiographic manifestations, such as cavitary lesions (60.9% vs 50.0%, p=0.073) and bilateral disease (56.7% vs 45.0%, p=0.056) in participants with symptomatic TB (online supplemental table 1).

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, the odds of symptomatic TB were higher among individuals who reported having ≥1 cellmates with any TB symptoms (adjusted OR (aOR) 2.60, 95% CI 1.51 to 4.57), current smokers (aOR 2.37, 95% CI 1.01 to 5.70), those with High/Medium Xpert semi-quantitative load (aOR 2.88, 95% CI 1.44 to 6.09) and those who had BMI <18.5 kg/m2 during the first medical consultation (aOR 2.88, 95% CI 1.44 to 6.09) (table 3).

Discussion

In this systematic screening study conducted in two high TB burden prisons, we found that half of individuals with microbiologically confirmed TB who reported no symptoms at initial screening endorsed symptoms at a first clinic visit with a physician. Compared with participants without symptoms, those who were symptomatic at medical consult had higher sputum bacillary load, lower BMI and more extensive radiographic abnormalities. Symptomatic patients were also substantially more likely to report other individuals in their cells with TB symptoms. Individuals who reported no symptoms initially but endorsed symptoms at the clinic visits generally had clinical characteristics that fell between those who were asymptomatic or symptomatic at both visits, suggesting that symptom reporting might be more variable among individuals with less advanced disease.

Overall, one in three individuals with TB disease detected through our ACF screening study did not report any TB symptoms during the first medical consultation despite all individuals included in the analysis being referred to treatment. This finding is consistent with several studies, including those conducted in prison settings, which reported a range of asymptomatic TB prevalence between 34.4% and 70.5%, despite differences in definitions of asymptomatic TB.16 17 22 23 Our study design likely allowed us to detect individuals with TB early on, when disease manifestations and pathology are mild.

Over one-third of our study participants provided different responses to symptom screening questionnaires between the initial screening and the first medical consultation. Although it is plausible that the observed differences in symptom reporting were due to natural disease progression,24 25 it may also reflect the challenge of capturing TB symptoms accurately in ACF settings. This finding is similar to those reported from a community ACF study in Vietnam, where one third of the study population was symptomatic before the diagnosis and when questioned again after TB diagnosis, 47% reported symptoms.26

Also, studies conducted in carceral settings may be limited by space and security constraints. All our study procedures were performed outside of the prison cell blocks during PDLs’ yard time; it is plausible that our study participants may feel rushed in answering symptom questionnaires because they wanted to resume their activities during this limited time. Since medical consults were only performed in individuals who had tested positive for TB, individuals who are called to the prison clinic might perceive that there must be a reason for the visit (i.e., a positive TB test) and may be more likely to report any symptoms compared with the initial screening.

Another factor that may contribute to the high rate of discordance on symptom reporting is stigma around TB, which could lead to exclusion among cellmates. PDL may deny the presence of any TB symptoms during screening, as responses may be overheard by others. Additionally, individuals frequently associate the two most discordant symptoms (cough and weight loss) with the incarceration process. During closed cell time, burning plastics and paper for light or heat is customary, and more than half of the total PDL population reports current tobacco use, rendering cough a common occurrence. Weight loss is also perceived as a habitual consequence of an insufficient or poor-quality diet inside Brazilian prisons.27 28

In our study, symptomatic TB was associated with higher bacterial burden and higher Lunit scores. This finding is consistent with other studies indicating that higher bacterial burden and more CXR abnormalities are correlated with symptomatic disease.8 29 30 We hypothesise that these findings could be indicators of disease severity31 and thus these patients would more likely be symptomatic.

We also found that individuals who reported having a cellmate with any TB symptoms were more likely to report TB symptoms during the initial screening, the first medical consultation or both. It is plausible that this subgroup represents those who had better awareness of TB symptoms because they had personal experiences with TB. Of note, our study population has been exposed to multiple active TB case finding efforts in the last decade; thus, it is not surprising that some PDL may gain TB knowledge from their prior and current participation in the TB ACF studies. This finding demonstrates that there are factors outside of disease progression and individual burden of disease that could define symptom status. Understanding better these factors is paramount, and future clinical trials for new TB interventions that plan to include asymptomatic TB endpoints32 need to consider the potential influence of these factors.

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, our study was performed in a single prison health centre, serving two prisons, with two physicians evaluating all individuals with TB disease. Thus, our findings may not be generalisable to other carceral settings with different TB burdens or the general population. Also, all the individuals included were male; previous studies have shown that there is variability in symptom reporting between male and female individuals with active TB33 that would not be detected in our study. Second, due to the nature of our systematic screening and prison activities, including transfers, security searches, and riots, the first medical consultation is often delayed. We decided to use the 15-day cut-off to define our inclusion, as TB symptoms typically progress slowly during the earlier phase of the disease, and more distinct/severe symptoms tend to emerge later.24 25 Third, our study may be affected by response bias introduced during the consent process for systematic screening. Although individuals were not informed about their TB diagnosis before the first medical consultation, they may have retained TB-related knowledge from the consent process and/or their past participation in other TB ACF efforts. Clinicians evaluating the individuals with positive TB results were also aware of the diagnosis at the moment of medical consultation. This knowledge may have influenced their perception of TB symptoms. Fourth, we did not compare Lunit scores with physicians’ interpretation of the CXR images, and we did not have a second reader to interpret CXR images to evaluate consistent and objective readings. Lastly, we did not perform a more comprehensive evaluation of mental health aspects, such as mental disorders diagnosis or patterns of substance abuse that could influence symptom reporting.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that over one-third of individuals with microbiologically confirmed pulmonary TB provided different answers to symptom inquiry performed at initial screening compared with the first medical consultation. More often, they presented as asymptomatic at first contact and then reported symptoms during consults. Symptom reporting at medical consult was associated with higher bacterial load, lower BMI, current smoking, and reporting cellmates with TB symptoms. Those who reported no symptoms at initial screening but reported symptoms at medical consultation had disease severity markers that fell between those who reported symptoms or no symptoms at both visits. Our findings highlight that CXR findings, Xpert semiquantitative load, tobacco use, weight and perceptions of other symptomatic cellmates are associated with symptom reporting among individuals identified with TB during ACF.
