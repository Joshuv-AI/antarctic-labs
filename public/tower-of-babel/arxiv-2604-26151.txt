Pricing with Passion: The Local Occupied Volatility (LOV) Model
Valentin Tissot-Daguette

Abstract
We introduce the Local Occupied Volatility (LOV) model that sits between Dupire’s local volatility and fully path-dependent dynamics. By design, the LOV model ensures automatic calibration to European vanilla options, while offering the flexibility to capture stylized facts of volatility or fit additional instruments. This is achieved by tuning the occupation sensitivity function that quantifies the effect of path-dependent shocks on volatility. We validate the model through the joint American-European calibration of options chain on non-dividend paying stocks.



Keywords:
Path-dependent volatility, occupation flows, American options, Markovian expansion, smile calibration.

1
Introduction

Path-dependent volatility models
[
15
,
12
,
13
,
11
]
have gained in popularity in recent years, notably for their flexibility to capture stylized facts of volatility, fit a variety of liquid contracts, while preserving market completeness. Herein, we illustrate the need for path dependence in volatility through the following practical, yet overlooked, example.

Consider the calibration of volatility to vanilla options on a U.S. non-dividend paying stock such as Amazon.com Inc. (AMZN). Assume constant, positive interest rate
r>0
throughout. Although U.S. single name options are American-style, due to the absence of dividends, the prices of call options coincide with their European counterparts
[
20
]
. If
c^{E}(K,T),c^{A}(K,T)
denote respectively the mid-price of the
(K,T)
European and American call option then

c^{A}(K,T)=c^{E}(K,T),\quad\forall K,T.

Classically, the local volatility (LV) model
[
6
]
provides perfect calibration to European vanilla options; if under
\Q_{\text{loc}}
, the underlying evolves according to

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=rdt+\sigma_{\text{loc}}(t,X_{t})dW_{t},

(1)

where
\sigma_{\text{loc}}
is obtained from Dupire’s formula,
then

c^{E}(K,T)=\E^{\Q_{\text{loc}}}[e^{-rT}(X_{T}-K)^{+}]
holds for all
K\geq 0
.
On the other hand, the put options do carry an early exercise premium, and there is no guarantee that the local volatility model generates exactly the observed put option quotes. That is, there typically exist
(K,T)-
put options such that

p^{A}(K,T)\ \neq\ \sup\hskip 0.56905pt\big\{\E^{\Q_{\text{loc}}}[e^{-r\tau}(K-X_{\tau})^{+}]\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \tau\leq T\;\text{ stopping time}\big\},

where the right-hand side is the price of the American put under
\Q_{\text{loc}}
.
Consequently, one needs to look beyond Markovian models to jointly fit single name call and put options. In other words, volatility
must be
path-dependent.

The aim of this work is to explore a specific type of path dependence to model volatility, generated by the
occupation times

\calO_{t}(A)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{A}(X_{s})e^{\kappa s}ds,\quad A\subseteq\R_{+},

for some exponential decay parameter
\kappa\geq 0
. The use of occupation times is motivated by recent studies on
occupied stochastic differential equations

[
24
,
23
,
22
]
, where
\calO_{t}
notably appear in the diffusion coefficient, i.e., volatility. The Markovian model (
1
) is thus extended to the path-dependent dynamics,

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=rdt+\sigma_{t}dW_{t},\quad\sigma_{t}=\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t}).

(2)

As argued in
Section

4
, careful choice of the occupied volatility
can solve the aforementioned joint American-European calibration problem.
First, the volatility process in (
2
) must satisfy the
smile calibration condition
,

\E^{\Q}[\sigma^{2}_{t}\ |\ X_{t}=x]=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,x),\quad\forall\ t,x,

(3)

where
\Q
is the risk neutral measure associated with (
2
). From
[
7
,
14
]
, identity (
3
) is equivalent to a perfect match of all European call option prices under
\Q
. The smile calibration condition is guaranteed when
applying a first order
Markovian expansion
(see
Section

3.1
) to the volatility functional in (
2
). We then postulate the local occupied volatility (LOV) model,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t}=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})+\gamma_{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\ell(t,X_{t},x)(\mathcal{O}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t})(dx),

(4)

with
\gamma_{t}=\calO_{t}(\R)^{-1}
and the projected measure
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t}=\E^{\Q}[\calO_{t}|X_{t}]
. Indeed, the smile calibration condition (
3
) holds, since the rightmost term in (
4
) has been centered.
Subsequently, the
sensitivity function

\ell(\cdot)
can be tuned to match the American put option quotes. The LOV model is still useful when all vanilla options are European, e.g. if
X
is the price of an index, in which case the sensitivity function is used to create more realistic spot-vol dynamics than in a plain LV model, see
Section

3.2
.

2
Occupied Volatility

Consider the calendar time occupation flow
\calO=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\delta_{X_{s}}ds

[
24
]
and assume that the asset price process
X
follows the dynamics,

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=(r-q)dt+\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})dW_{t},

(5)

with interest rate
r\in\R
and dividend yield
q\geq 0
, both assumed to be constant for simplicity.
The diffusion coefficient
\sigma
shall be termed
occupied volatility
.
A special case is Dupire’s local volatility model
[
6
]
. Indeed, if
\sigma_{\text{loc}}

denotes the local volatility function, then

\sigma(\mathfrak{o},x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sigma_{\text{loc}}(\mathfrak{o}(\R),x)\;\Longrightarrow\;\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})=\sigma_{\text{loc}}(t,X_{t}).

(6)

Hence (
5
) gives a sensible generalization of the local volatility model as long as
\sigma
satisfies the

smile calibration condition

[
3
,
7
,
14
]
,

\E^{\Q}[\sigma^{2}(\calO_{t},X_{t})\ |\ X_{t}]=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t}).

(7)

ensuring that vanilla call and put options are correctly priced.
On the other hand, occupied volatility is a particular example of path-dependent volatility
[
3
,
12
,
13
,
11
,
15
]
and thus enjoys similar benefits. Notably, as the occupation flow is endogenous, the model remains complete, i.e., any contingent claim can be perfectly replicated by dynamically trading the underlying.

From an econometric perspective,
calendar time may not be the most realistic clock to model volatility, due to the chronology invariance of
\calO
. As an illustration, fix a one-year horizon with current time
t=1
, and consider two scenarios
\omega^{1}
,
\omega^{2}
such that
X_{1}(\omega^{1})=X_{1}(\omega^{2})
and
\calO_{1}(\omega^{1})=\calO_{1}(\omega^{2})
. Then
\omega^{1}
,
\omega^{2}
generate the same occupied volatility level. However, suppose that in the first (respectively second) scenario, a financial crash occurred a week (resp. a year) ago, while the market has been bullish otherwise. One would then expect much higher volatility in the first scenario.

:

\calO_{t}
, left) compared with exponential time (

, right). As can be seen,
\calO
puts more emphasis on recent levels of the path.

\Q
.

(b)

.

The above example
calls for a clock that captures a memory decay in past information.
Among others, this is achieved through the
exponential time
occupation flow,

d\calO_{t}=\delta_{X_{t}}e^{\kappa t}dt,\quad\kappa\geq 0.

(8)

The calendar time occupation flow is recovered by setting
\kappa=0
. Note that calendar time is still relevant for the pricing/hedging of exotic options due to the unified framework it offers; see Section 4 in
[
24
]
. Hence in general,
calendar time is preferred under
\Q
(pricing measure), while exponential time is preferred under
\Pb
(historical measure).

The volatility now takes the exponential time occupation flow as input, leading to

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=(r-q)dt+\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})dW_{t}.

(9)

By increasing the
exponential decay parameter
\kappa
, the occupation flow puts more emphasis on the present. This adjustment makes volatility more reactive to recent shocks, and allow the model to replicate stylized facts such as volatility clustering.

Example 1
.

(Guyon’s toy volatility model)
Consider the occupied volatility

\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})=\Sigma(\Upsilon(\calO_{t},X_{t})),\quad\Sigma(y)=-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\gamma y^{-\beta},

(10)

with parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
, and functional

\Upsilon(\calO_{t},X_{t})=\frac{X_{t}}{\frac{1}{\calO_{t}(\R)}\int_{\R}x\calO_{t}(dx)}.

(11)

The ratio (
11
) characterizes the asset’s trend by noting that the barycenter of
\calO
coincides with the exponentially weighted moving average (EMA) of the asset,

\text{EMA}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\kappa t}}\int_{0}^{t}X_{u}\kappa e^{-\kappa(t-u)}du=\frac{1}{\calO_{t}(\mathbb{R})}\int x\calO_{t}(dx).

(12)

Then
dX_{t}=\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})X_{t}dW_{t}
is
precisely Guyon’s toy volatility model
[
13
]
, further analyzed by
2
.
Since
\Sigma
in (
10
) is a decreasing function, the volatility
\sigma
is inversely proportional to the asset’s trend.
This is in line with
the
leverage effect
, a stylized fact in equity markets that volatility increases when the asset price drops.

2.1
Simulation of Occupied SDEs

The numerical integration of OSDEs inevitably requires a discretization of the occupation flow.
Focusing on the occupied volatility model
(
9
),
a natural way consists of keeping track of finitely many occupation times
\calO_{t}(C_{1}),\ldots,\calO_{t}(C_{M})
, where
(C_{m})
forms a partition of
\R_{+}
.
Concretely, let
x_{1}<\ldots<x_{M}
,
M\in\N
, and introduce the corridors,

C_{m}=[x_{m}-\varepsilon_{m-1},x_{m}+\varepsilon_{m}),\quad\varepsilon_{m}=\frac{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}{2},\quad(\varepsilon_{0}=x_{1},\varepsilon_{M}=\infty).

The nodes
(x_{m})
are chosen so as to match the scale of
X_{0}
and OSDE coefficients.
We then replace
\calO_{t}
by
the discrete measure
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\calO_{t}(C_{m})\delta_{x_{m}}
,
and approximate OSDE (
9
) by the finite-dimensional system

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=(r-q)dt+\sigma^{M}(O_{t},X_{t})dW_{t},\qquad dO_{t,m}=\mathds{1}_{C_{m}}(X_{t})e^{\kappa t}dt,

(13)

with
O_{t}=(\calO_{t}(C_{1}),\ldots,\calO_{t}(C_{M}))\in\R^{M}
. In light of (
13
),
(O,X)
is an
M+1-
-dimensional Markov process. Moreover, the projected volatility

\sigma^{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R^{M+1}\to\R,\qquad\sigma^{M}(O_{t},X_{t})=\sigma\Big(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}O_{t,m}\delta_{x_{m}},X_{t}\Big),

converges to
\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})
when
\sigma(\mathfrak{o},x)
is weakly continuous in
\mathfrak{o}
.
We then generate sample paths from (
13
) as follows using standard discretization techniques
[
17
,
8
]
. Given the time grid
t_{n}=n\delta t
,
\delta t=\frac{T}{N}
,
N\in\N
,
X_{0}\in\R_{+}
, and
O^{\text{\tiny tmp}}=0\in\R^{M}
, the SDE (
13
) is updated according to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sigma_{t_{n}}&=\ \sigma^{M}(O^{\text{\tiny tmp}},X_{t_{n}}),\\[3.00003pt]
X_{t_{n+1}}&=\ X_{t_{n}}\exp\{\sigma_{t_{n}}\sqrt{\delta t}Z_{n}+(r-q-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t_{n}}^{2})\delta t\},\\[3.00003pt]
O^{\text{\tiny tmp}}_{m}&\leftarrow\ O^{\text{\tiny tmp}}_{m}+e^{\kappa t_{n}}\delta t,\quad X_{t_{n}}\ \in\ C_{m},\end{cases}

where
Z_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\calN(0,1)
,
n=0,\ldots,N-1
. Note that the occupation measure is represented by the temporary variable
O^{\text{\tiny tmp}}
updated at each iteration. Indeed,
it is not necessary (and costly) to store the occupation times
O_{t_{n}}
for each
n
.

Remark 1
.

More generally, one may employ
a partition of unity
\{f_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R_{+}\to[0,1]\}_{m\leq M}
,
\sum_{m}f_{m}\equiv 1
, leading to the
cylindrical projection

\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})\approx\sigma^{M}(f_{1}\cdot\calO_{t},\ldots,f_{M}\cdot\calO_{t},X_{t})
. The occupation time approximation is thus recovered by setting
f_{m}=\mathds{1}_{C_{m}}
, and similarly to
(C_{m})
being a partition, the property
\sum_{m}f_{m}\equiv 1
ensures that the total mass of
\calO
is preserved. A convergence analysis of cylindrical projections of OSDEs as
M\to\infty

is addressed in the companion paper
[
22
]
.

Remark 2
.

The simulation of fully path-dependent volatility models,

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=(r-q)dt+\sigma(t,\omega)dW_{t},

typically involves discretization of the form

\sigma(t_{n},\omega)\approx\sigma^{N}_{n}(X_{t_{1}}(\omega),\ldots,X_{t_{n}}(\omega))
for some function
\sigma^{N}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R^{n}\to\R
.
Note the input dimension of
\sigma^{N}_{n}
grows linearly with
n
, which becomes highly problematic when the total number of time steps
N
is large.
In contrast, occupied SDEs decouple the dimensionality of the space and time discretization, allowing for efficient simulations
as shown in
Example

1
and
Section

4.2
.

3
Local Occupied Volatility (LOV)

3.1
Markovian Expansion

Let us start with an autonomous path-dependent volatility model of the form

\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}=(r-q)dt+\sigma(F_{t},X_{t})dW_{t},

for some path-dependent feature
F_{t}(\omega)=\textnormal{f}(t,\omega)\in\frakF
,
\textnormal{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\times\Omega_{T}\to\frakF
. The
feature space

\frakF
may be finite-dimensional, e.g.
F_{t}\in\{\sup_{s\leq t}X_{s},\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}ds\}
in
3
, or infinite-dimensional in occupied volatility models where
\frakF=\calM
; see
Section

3.2
.
Recall the smile calibration condition

\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})=\E^{\Q}[\sigma^{2}(F_{t},X_{t})\ |\ X_{t}],

(14)

and introduce the
projected feature

\hat{F}_{t}=\E^{\Q}[F_{t}|X_{t}]=\hat{f}(t,X_{t})
for some map
\hat{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R_{+}^{2}\to\frakF.

If
\sigma^{2}(F,X)
were linear in
F
, then

\E^{\Q}[\sigma^{2}(F_{t},X_{t})|X_{t}]=\sigma^{2}(\E^{\Q}[F_{t}|X_{t}],X_{t})=\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t}),

so (
14
) would imply that
\sigma_{\text{loc}}(t,X_{t})=\sigma(\hat{f}(t,X_{t}),X_{t})
.
In general, one can linearize
F\mapsto\sigma^{2}(F,X)
via a first order
Markovian expansion
around
\hat{F}_{t}
, that is

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(F_{t},X_{t})\approx\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})+D\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})\cdot(F_{t}-\hat{F}_{t}),

(15)

with Fréchet derivative
D\sigma^{2}(\cdot,X_{t})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frakF\to\frakF^{*}
and pairing
\ell\cdot F=\ell(F)\in\R,

(\ell,F)\in\frakF^{*}\times\frakF
. Note that
D\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})
is the sensitivity of variance with respect to non-Markovian shocks.
In view of (
14
) and the fact that
\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})
,
D\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})
are functions of
(t,X_{t})
, we can
postulate
the following local path-dependent volatility (LPDV) model,

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}

\displaystyle=(r-q)dt+\sigma_{t}dW_{t},

(16)

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})+\ell(t,X_{t})\cdot(F_{t}-\hat{F}_{t}).

(17)

That is, we have replaced the term
\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})
in (
15
) with the local variance, and the derivative
D\sigma^{2}(\hat{F}_{t},X_{t})
with an arbitrary map
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R_{+}^{2}\to\frakF^{*}
. Due to
\E^{\Q}[F_{t}-\hat{F}_{t}|X_{t}]=0
, the above specification automatically satisfies the smile calibration condition (
14
) for any reasonable choice of
\ell
, e.g. such that
\sigma^{2}_{t}
remains positive. Since
\hat{F}_{t}
depends on
\Q
, so does
\sigma_{t}
in (
17
), leading to McKean type dynamics.

Remark 3
.

The LPDV model (
17
) is reminiscent of local stochastic volatility (LSV) models
[
10
,
9
]
where one multiplies
\sigma_{\text{loc}}
by an exogenous stochastic processes
\alpha_{t}
and normalized
such that (
14
) holds. The volatility process is thus of the form

\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{\text{loc}}(t,X_{t})\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{\E^{\Q}[\alpha_{t}^{2}|X_{t}]}},

(18)

where the conditional expectation is typically estimated using the particle method of
10
. It is a powerful family of models that is consistent with vanilla options and capable of producing more realistic spot-vol dynamics than the standard local volatility model.
On the other hand, local path-dependent volatility models also preserve market completeness by explaining future volatility from the price history directly. Moreover, considering an additive correction term is arguably preferred numerically over the fraction in (
18
) with distribution-dependent denominator. Indeed, any error introduced when computing
\hat{F}_{t}

is controlled in a linear way.

3.2
The LOV Model

Let us set
F_{t}=\calO_{t}
.
The feature space is
\frakF=\calM
, and the Fréchet derivative in (
15
) becomes the linear derivative
[
5
]
, which we denote by
\delta_{\mathfrak{o}}
.
Recalling the dual pairing
\phi\cdot\mathfrak{o}=\int_{\R}\phi(x)\mathfrak{o}(dx)
,
(\phi,\mathfrak{o})\in\calC_{b}(\R)\times\calM
, we can then rewrite (
15
) as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\calO_{t},X_{t})\approx\sigma^{2}(\hat{\calO}_{t},X_{t})+\int_{\R_{+}}\delta_{\mathfrak{o}}\sigma^{2}(\hat{\calO}_{t},X_{t})(x)\ (\calO_{t}-\hat{\calO}_{t})(dx).

(19)

The
occupation sensitivity

\delta_{\mathfrak{o}}\sigma^{2}(\hat{\calO}_{t},X_{t})(x)=\frac{d}{dh}\sigma^{2}(\hat{\calO}_{t}+h\delta_{x},X_{t})|_{h=0}
describes the change in variance when the asset spends more time at level
x
than implied by the expected occupation measure
\hat{\calO}_{t}
. We then replace
\sigma^{2}(\hat{\calO}_{t},X_{t})
with the local variance, and rewrite the occupation sensitivity as

\delta_{\mathfrak{o}}\sigma^{2}(\hat{\calO}_{t},X_{t})(x)=\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}\ell(t,X_{t},x),\quad\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}=\calO_{t}^{\kappa}(\R)^{-1}=\frac{\kappa}{e^{\kappa t}-1},\;\,\kappa>0,\quad\gamma_{t}^{0}=\frac{1}{t}.

Altogether, we have derived the Local Occupied Volatility (LOV) model,

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}

\displaystyle=(r-q)dt+\sigma_{t}dW_{t},

(20)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})+\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}\int_{\R_{+}}\ell(t,X_{t},x)(\mathcal{O}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t})(dx).

(21)

The factor
\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}
is introduced so as to maintain a constant magnitude of
\ell
over time, and allow to interpret the linear term as the average sensitivity of variance when the realized occupation measure
\mathcal{O}_{t}
deviates from the implied
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
. Similar to (
17
), the occupied variance depends on
\Q
through
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
, hence
\sigma_{t}=\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t},\Q)
.

Without careful specification of the sensitivity function (
\ell
),
the LOV model may produce negative variances because the path-dependent term in (
21
) is centered around zero. However, mild conditions on
\ell
can guarantee
\sigma^{2}

remains positive, as shown next.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the LOV model (
20
)–(
21
) with sensitivity function
\ell
such that
|\ell(t,x^{\prime},x)|<\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}(t,x^{\prime})
for all
t,x^{\prime},x
. Then
\sigma_{t}(\omega)>0
for all
t
,
\omega\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
c(t,x^{\prime})=\sup_{x}|\ell(t,x^{\prime},x)|
. By assumption,
c(t,x^{\prime})<\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}(t,x^{\prime})
, hence

\sigma^{2}_{t}\geq\sigma_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})-\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}\int_{\R}|\ell(t,X_{t},x)|(\mathcal{O}_{t}+\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t})(dx)\geq\sigma_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})-2c(t,X_{t})>0.

∎

Under the above assumption on
\ell
, the sensitivity function can nearly double the local variance or push it down closer to zero. The path-dependent term in (
21
) thus offers a wide
range to adjust the local variance while ensuring positivity.

Remark 4
.

Equivalently, the local occupied variance can be reformulated using a multiplicative correction, that is

\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})\Big(1+\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}\int_{\R_{+}}\tilde{\ell}(t,X_{t},x)(\mathcal{O}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t})(dx)\Big),

(22)

where
\tilde{\ell}
represents the path-dependent sensitivity of variance relative to the baseline local variance.
Analogous to
Proposition

1
, the condition
|\tilde{\ell}(t,X_{t},x)|<\frac{1}{2}
ensures the strict positivity of
\sigma^{2}
.

Example 2
.

(one-factor LOV)
Consider the multiplicative version (
22
), with
\tilde{\ell}(t,X_{t},x)=\beta\mathds{1}_{A}(x)
,
A\in\calB(\R_{+})
, and
|\beta|<1/2
. Then,

\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})\Big(1+\beta\gamma_{t}\big(\mathcal{O}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t}\big)(A)\Big).

In view of
Proposition

1
,
\sigma^{2}>0
. Recalling that
\gamma_{t}=\calO_{t}(\R_{+})^{-1}=\hat{\calO}_{t}(\R_{+})^{-1}
, the local variance is thus rescaled according to the fraction of time
\gamma_{t}\mathcal{O}_{t}(A)

spent by the asset in
A
compared to what is expected. Here, the occupied volatility depends only on
t,X_{t}
, and the occupation time
\calO_{t}(A)
. As
(\calO(A),X)
is a two-dimensional Markov process, trajectories from the LOV model can thus be effectively simulated under this simple specification. In
Algorithm

1
, this would correspond to choosing
M=1
corridor.

Examples for
A
include the upside corridor
A^{+}=[X_{0},\infty)
, where a positive parameter
\beta
would be consistent with the leverage effect. Alternatively, one can consider a narrow corridor around the money, e.g.
A=[0.9X_{0},1.1X_{0}]
, where high values for
\mathcal{O}_{t}(A)
indicate some mean reversion of the recent path toward the initial value. This mean reversion would, in turn, translate into lower volatilities than
\sigma_{\text{loc}}
by choosing
\beta<0
.

Example 3
.

(EMA LOV)
Suppose that
\ell(t,X_{t},x)=\beta\log x
for some parameter
\beta\in\R
.
Then, using (
12
), we can rewrite the local occupied volatility (
21
) as

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})+\beta(\text{EMA}_{t}-\widehat{\text{EMA}}_{t}),

\displaystyle\text{EMA}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\kappa t}}\int_{0}^{t}\log X_{u}\kappa e^{-\kappa(t-u)}du,\qquad\;\widehat{\text{EMA}}_{t}=\E^{\Q}[\hskip 0.56905pt\text{EMA}_{t}\ |\ X_{t}\hskip 0.56905pt].

The trend
\text{EMA}_{t}-\widehat{\text{EMA}}_{t}
is
similar to the first offset process of
15
, where the projected EMA is replaced by the log spot
\log X_{t}
. Note that choosing
\beta>0
makes the path-dependent correction term consistent with the leverage effect.

where

\psi(\delta)=\frac{15}{16}(1-(\delta/h)^{2})^{2}/h

following the guidelines in
[

. The complete simulation scheme is provided in

4
Joint American-European Calibration

In this section, we describe the calibration of the LOV model to market quotes for non-dividend-paying U.S. stocks.
We then assess the model’s empirical performance on the Amazon.com, Inc. (AMZN) option chain.
While the local volatility function guarantees perfect calibration to European vanilla options, the sensitivity function
\ell
is tuned to match the price of the American puts:

\sigma^{2}(\calO_{t},X_{t})=\underbrace{\sigma_{\text{loc}}^{2}(t,X_{t})}_{\text{European calls}}+\ \gamma_{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\ \underbrace{\ell(t,X_{t},x)}_{\text{American puts}}\ (\calO_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{t})(dx).

The presence of American puts in the calibration process necessitates an efficient method to price early-exercise products. This is the focus of the next section.

4.1
Least Square Monte Carlo

We extend the least square Monte Carlo (LSMC) algorithm of
[
19
]
to our setting. In short, LSMC estimates the continuation value using regressions, and then compare it to the intrinsic value leading to nearly-optimal stopping decisions. In our setting, we can therefore leverage the Markov property of
(\calO,X)
to express the continuation value as linear combination of adequate regressors depending on the current occupation measure and spot value. We shall use the following features:

1.

Black-Scholes price
p_{\text{\tiny BS}}(t,X_{t},\sigma_{t};K,T)
of the
(K,T)
put option,

2.

Laguerre polynomials of the normalized spot
X_{t}/x_{0}
, up to degree
3
,

3.

Total volatility
\sigma_{t}\sqrt{T-t}
as of
t\leq T
,

4.

Aggregate occupation times
\calO_{t}(A_{n})
, with
A_{n}=\bigcup_{m=m_{n-1}}^{m_{n}-1}C_{m}
,
m_{n}=1+[nM/5]
(
m_{0}=1
), and interaction terms
X_{t}\cdot\calO_{t}(A_{n})
,
n=1,\ldots,5
.

4.2
Neural Calibration

We parameterize the implied sensitivity function by a feedforward neural network

\ell(t,X_{t},x)\approx\ell^{\theta}(t,X_{t},x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Phi(t,X_{t},x;\theta),\quad\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\times\R_{+}^{2}\times\Theta\to\R_{+},

(24)

for some parameter set
\Theta\subseteq\R^{p}
,
p\gg 1
.
The parameters are then calibrated to market quotes of vanilla call and put options (see
Algorithm

2
) using stochastic gradient descent.
Note that a single neural network is used in (
24
) for all time points, which presents significant benefits in terms of computational cost and smoothness in the time variable; see
[
21
]
. The optimization is also done on the full horizon all at once, often called global-in-time approach
[
16
]
.

Consider a collection of traded put and call options written on a given non-dividend paying stocks. First, we extract the implied volatility skews from the call options (being de facto European), as well as the local volatility function
\sigma_{\text{loc}}
.
Suppose we are given
I\in\N
quoted put options with strike and maturity respectively denoted by
K_{i},T_{i}
.
We then train the parameters of the neural sensitivity (
24
) so that the model prices,

p^{\theta}_{i}=\sup_{\tau\leq T_{i}}\E^{\Q}[e^{-r\tau}(K_{i}-X_{\tau}^{\theta})^{+}],

lie between the bid and ask market prices
p^{\text{\tiny bid}}_{i},p^{\text{\tiny ask}}_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,I
. Training loop is summarized in
Algorithm

2
. In Step V., the calibration is deemed complete when the loss has stabilized and remains below the following bid/ask threshold (see also
[
1
]
)

\alpha=\Big(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}|w_{i}(p^{\text{\tiny bid/ask}}_{i}-p^{\text{\tiny mid}}_{i})|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}|w_{i}(p^{\text{\tiny ask}}_{i}-p^{\text{\tiny bid}}_{i})|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Remark 5
.

(Calibration weights)
Similar to
1
, we use the inverse Black-Scholes Vega, retrieved from the call options, in (
23
) to
translate prices into implied volatilities.
As the Vega becomes arbitrarily small for out-of-the-money options and short maturities, we also cap the weights by setting instead
(\vartheta^{\text{\tiny mid}}_{i}\vee\varepsilon)^{-1}
in (
23
) with, e.g.,
\varepsilon=10^{-2}
; see also
[
1
]
. Although one could directly use the implied volatility errors
|\sigma^{\theta}_{i}-\sigma^{\text{\tiny mid}}_{i}|
in the loss function, this would necessitate the computation of the model implied volatilities
(\sigma^{\theta}_{i})_{i=1}^{I}
at every training step, which is prohibitively costly for large
I
.
The Vega weight is then multiplied by the inverse bid-ask spread
\frac{1}{p^{\text{\tiny ask}}_{i}-p^{\text{\tiny bid}}_{i}}
to
give greater importance to liquid contracts.

5
Adding Dividends

When the underlying asset issues dividends, both call and put options carry an early exercise premium.
This implies that matching call option quotes is no longer equivalent to the smile calibration condition (
14
). Hence, there is no need to consider McKean dynamics in this case, so the instantaneous variance in the LOV model becomes

\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})^{2}=\sigma_{\text{loc}}(t,X_{t})+\gamma_{t}^{\kappa}\int_{\R_{+}}\ \ell^{\Q}(t,X_{t},x)\hskip 0.56905pt\calO_{t}(dx).

Note that the local volatility function is
not
obtained from Dupire’s formula. Instead,
\sigma_{\text{loc}}
shall be parameterized, e.g. using neural networks, along the sensitivity function
\ell
to quotes of American put and call options. A notable challenge in this context is the discrete nature of dividends, making the valuation of American options much more involved. We thus leave this direction for future work.

An alternative approach, called
de-Americanization
, consists of extracting the prices of synthetic European options from observed American options. This can be done using a binomial model on a fine grid, or using neural networks
[
18
]
. One can then calibrate a volatility model to the synthetic European prices or their corresponding implied volatility surface.
While the calibration to European-style options is significantly faster, the de-Americanization step is model-dependent and thus prone to error
[
4
]
. Crucially, there is no guarantee that a model calibrated to the synthetic European prices will accurately recover the original American option quotes.
