 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND

Conduit hydropower leverages existing water infrastructure to provide energy solutions, though
deployment has been slow. A 2022 US Department of Energy (DOE)–funded report led by Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) summarizes a reconnaissance-level study of hydropower potential at
national conduits (Kao et al., 2022). The report includes power and energy estimates across three major
sectors of conduit hydropower potential: municipal, agricultural, and industrial. Water resources include
public water supply systems (municipal and industrial), irrigation canal systems (agricultural),
thermoelectric cooling systems (industrial), and wastewater systems (municipal and industrial). In total,
1.41 GW of power potential was estimated nationwide. Subsequently, DOE has funded ORNL through
the Conduit Hydropower Engineering, Evaluation, and Technology Acceleration project (CHEETA) to
improve deployment potential for conduit hydropower. Work thus far has focused on “in-conduit”
hydropower (ICH)—or “in-pipe” hydropower—for public water supply systems, though the project is
planning to cover conduit hydropower more broadly. The timeline of ORNL’s conduit hydropower-
related research and development (R&D) is shown in Figure 1. The results of CHEETA should benefit the
hydropower industry by providing insights into costs, barriers, and other considerations that impact
project development. The hydropower industry and municipal/industrial conduit stakeholders will use the
output to inform development decisions and advance low-impact hydropower growth in the US.

1.2 SCOPE OF REPORT

This report focuses on ICH development for public water supply (PWS) systems. This report provides a
resource for better understanding the project development process (Section 2), challenges facing
stakeholders (Section 3), and development costs (Section 4), and it includes case studies (Section 5 and
Appendices) that exemplify the considerations, engineering, and feasibility used to assess project
development. Future work is also being planned to leverage increased stakeholder engagement to shape
R&D efforts and to equip stakeholders with additional resources for improved decision-making.

 2. DEVELOPING MUNICIPAL IN-CONDUIT HYDROPOWER

2.1 TECHNOLOGY

Multiple technologies are available to implement hydropower in an existing PWS system. The potential
for implementation is mainly governed by parameters such as hydraulic head (i.e., pressure) and flow
rate; however, spatial and financial constraints also play a vital role. An example water distribution
system is shown in Figure 2. As shown, PWS systems, in general, convey water from a water source (e.g.,
reservoirs, lakes, or rivers) to an end user (e.g., commercial, industrial, or municipal customer), with
intervening steps to store and treat the water. Pumping stations are also used, as needed, to provide the
necessary energy for reliable delivery. Pressure management within water networks is critical to effective
operation and distribution (del Teso et al., 2019). Within the system, pressure reducing valves (PRVs) are
often used at strategic locations to maintain safe pressure levels and avoid pipeline ruptures in the
distribution system (Lima et al., 2017; Pasha et al., 2020).

Figure 2. Conceptual sketch of a water distribution system, including PWS systems and potential hydropower
 development locations. Modified from Bekker et al. (2021).

For PWS systems, ICH can be added to existing water infrastructure to harness energy within certain
pressure zones, with PRV locations being an opportune target. Although a PRV can simply be replaced

 2

by a turbine to extract energy and thereby reduce pressure, bypass piping is more often added to convey
water around a PRV and through a turbine. Such bypass loops provide redundancy to allow for maximum
flow through the turbine and ease of operation during maintenance. Conceptually, excess pressure may be
available due to the natural variation in elevation from a water source to a treatment plant or at certain
points between a treatment plant and end users. For example, there are often significant differences in
elevation or pressure at the point where water enters or exits treatment plants (Majidi and Etezadi-Amoli,
2018). ICH provides several benefits: (1) predictable power generation as a result of continuous pressure
and flow monitoring, (2) extended PRV service life due to reduced workload, (3) reduced barriers to entry
thanks to licensing exemption for adding power generation capability to existing infrastructure with
limited to no environmental impact, and (4) low relative costs for up-front financing (Sari et al., 2018).
PWS systems can vary widely, so ICH potential is often site-specific, though the technological solutions
are well established.

To produce hydropower, in-line Kaplan or in-line Francis turbines are often used, though pumps-as-
turbines (PATs) are also prevalent. PATs make use of standard water pumps that operate in reverse to
generate electricity. They can be cost-effective because they use existing pump designs and are readily
available and adaptable for use in PWS systems (Lydon et al., 2017; Novara et al., 2019; Stefanizzi et al.,
2020). They are suitable for locations at which water pressure is reduced or flow is controlled.
Information on other novel and specialized technologies can be found in the DOE-funded Hydropower
Technologies Catalog1 when filtered for “In-Conduit” application. Development of new technologies
(e.g., Oladosu and Koya, 2018; Kumar and Sarkar, 2023) can often be expensive, which is why
established technologies such as PATs are often a favorable choice. Figure 3 shows the typical
operational ranges for technologies that might be used for ICH generation.

 Figure 3. Operating ranges of standard turbines and PATs. Source: Stefanizzi et al. (2020).

2.2 EXISTING, PROPOSED, AND POTENTIAL MARKET

The ORNL Existing Hydropower Assets (EHA) dataset provides annual updates on the US hydropower
fleet. Among the data included is the mode of operation. The 2024 dataset (Johnson et al., 2024) includes
337 hydropower facilities, totaling 836 MW, with a mode of operation as “canal/conduit.” To further
assess facilities that were likely to be associated with ICH for PWS systems, screening was conducted to
categorize each facility by sector: municipal, agricultural, or industrial. The results identified 151
agricultural, 25 industrial, and 159 municipal facilities, with two miscategorized. Agricultural facilities
account for 556 MW (average = 3.7 MW), industrial facilities account for 27.3 MW (average = 1.1 MW),

(i.e., municipal and industrial conduits) account for roughly 272 MW of the current operational
hydropower in the US. Figure 4 shows the distribution of these 184 facilities, and Figure 5 includes a map
view of the projects across the US. The state with the largest number of ICH facilities is California, with
69, and other states with a large amount of ICH include Colorado, Massachusetts, Oregon, and Utah (24,

Reviewing the data in more detail reveals other interesting features. For instance, of the 48 facilities for

different owner types documented, 62% are publicly owned utilities. Regarding the number of units, 72%

ORNL completed a study in 2022 to further assess the potential to develop hydropower across municipal,

study, documented in Kao et al. (2022), provides a high-level estimate of power and energy potential
across the US and estimates that 1,414 MW of conduit hydropower could be added in the US across these
three main sectors. Of that total, 374 MW is available from municipal conduit hydropower, 337 MW
(90%) of which is from municipal PWS systems and the remainder from municipal wastewater systems.
Industrial conduit hydropower accounts for 378 MW of the total, of which 224 MW is estimated for
thermoelectric cooling, 126 MW for industrial PWS, and 28 MW from industrial wastewater.

2.3 DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

A general summary of the conduit hydropower development process is shown in Figure 8, including
examples of activities within each phase. Although the figure displays a linear process, it is iterative in
nature. The process begins with stakeholder engagement and building the project team. Stakeholders, both
internal and external, come together to initiate the project and consider its impacts. Education of the
project team and stakeholders is a key aspect of this phase.

Once the organization has decided to pursue the opportunity, the technical and economic feasibility of
specific sites are evaluated. This can be performed with in-house expertise or through outside consultants.
Continued engagement with stakeholders is necessary throughout this phase to complete the evaluation.
Moving the project into design and construction depends on the feasibility evaluation and the interest or
willingness of the stakeholders. Throughout the life of the project, continued operation and maintenance
are required to achieve the expected performance.

The process of developing conduit hydropower is accelerated as compared to other types of higher-impact
hydropower development. This is mainly due to the Hydropower Regulatory Efficiency Act of 20132
(HREA) and the removal of such projects from Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC)
jurisdiction, referred to as “qualifying conduit facilities.” Removing the uncertainty, time, and cost
associated with a FERC license or exemption has allowed projects to be developed more quickly.
Additionally, the minimal construction due to the utilization of existing infrastructure and the limited
ground disturbance activities accelerate development. However, as explained in Section 3, challenges
remain throughout the process, specifically related to stakeholder engagement, other permitting
requirements, and power sales.

 3. STAKEHOLDER CHALLENGES

As with other forms of hydropower, ICH faces project development challenges. In April 2024, ORNL
organized and hosted a virtual workshop titled “Conduit Hydropower Development in the United States –
Opportunity and Challenges” that brought together municipal/industrial as well as agricultural conduit
stakeholders to discuss development challenges; a summary is documented in DeNeale et al. (2024).
Other such targeted stakeholder engagement has proven valuable in informing decision-makers and
producing products ready for use by stakeholders (e.g., CEC, 2020).

To identify challenges, ORNL used an interactive online platform to elicit input regarding the 7-phase
project development process (outlined in Figure 8). Participants, after being separated into sector-specific
breakout groups, were asked to rate how challenging each phase of project development is, which helped
identify the most and least challenging phases. Since each participant was linked to a particular
stakeholder type, the data could be analyzed and compared across different stakeholders. Each phase was
rated according to the perceived level of challenge, for which a level of 1 is “not at all challenging” and 5
is “very challenging.” The average ratings across the 36 municipal/industrial conduit stakeholders are
shown in Figure 9.

Based on the results, the top three most challenging phases were identified as financing and power sales
(mean: 3.95), internal and external stakeholder engagement (mean: 3.75), and permitting and licensing
(mean: 3.75). These three phases scored notably higher than the remaining four phases, which each had a
mean rating between 2.55 and 2.85. Following the challenge rating, a “challenges deep-dive” session was

 8

used to gather written (i.e., typed) and verbal feedback regarding experiences and perspectives across the
different conduit hydropower project development phases. Participants described specific challenges they
faced across the 7 phases, including explaining how those challenges affected outcomes, what lessons
were learned, and what would have been helpful in hindsight. For greater detail on the information
gathered, consult DeNeale et al. (2024).

Challenges associated with the different ICH project development phases include the following:

 • Phase 1. Internal and External Stakeholder Engagement (ranked 2nd most challenging):
 Internal and external stakeholder engagement stood out as a significant challenge that needs to be
 overcome. At a high level, general education of key stakeholders is needed to improve awareness
 of the opportunity to develop hydropower for in-conduit systems; many stakeholders, including
 utilities, are not aware of the potential for adding energy to their systems. Also, identifying the
 key project contributors early during project development was mentioned as a critical need. This
 can be challenging because there may be many entities involved, and identifying the appropriate
 contacts can be a daunting task. Furthermore, the lengthy timelines associated with bringing a
 project from conception to commissioning can be prohibitive, as knowledge and momentum may
 be lost. Project champions, who are responsible for executing a project, need to have expertise in
 generating energy; water utilities may not have this kind of in-house expertise. Successful
 development may require hiring such an expert for the length of the project, including to help
 navigate the project development process. Finally, investors and owners are averse to the
 perceived risk of a new technology or to the idea of ICH if it is not well understood. Sharing
 success stories would be one way to address this challenge.

 • Phase 2. Site Selection and Feasibility Assessment (ranked 4th most challenging): Several
 factors influence the feasibility of developing conduit hydropower, and these factors vary widely
 from site to site. The purpose of a feasibility study is to assess these factors (and others) and their
 impact on designing and building a project that meets expectations. Due to the site-specific nature
 of ICH, significant time must be spent to assess the technical feasibility of any project. Such
 initial investment requires time and resource commitment without the guarantee of success,
 which may be a hurdle for some utilities, especially at smaller scales.

 • Phase 3. Design and Technology Selection (ranked 6th most challenging): Design and
 technology selection decisions progress in conjunction with early-phase feasibility assessment as
 a part of an iterative process. Some factors that may present design challenges include the
 intermittency of distribution-side flows; large pressure variability in high-friction-loss systems,
 which may create issues for a PAT; inadequate space within the existing infrastructure; leaky
 valves and wet conditions in existing vaults; complex controls related to operating dual systems;
 allocating room for future flow capacity expansion, which could affect pressure and equipment
 performance; complex and possibly unknown underground lines within the project vicinity; and
 difficulty in predicting future flows, as historical records may not be reliable for forecasting
 future growth.

 • Phase 4. Permitting and Licensing (ranked 2nd most challenging): Although these types of
 projects are outside FERC licensing jurisdiction, other types of permitting also present a
 challenge. Allocating appropriate staffing and cultivating understanding during the permitting and
 licensing application process can be challenging. Also, though ICH can be sized to offset
 electrical loads of a treatment or distribution facility without significant infrastructure upgrades,
 application at some sites may be hindered by interconnection requirements. Since interconnection
 requirements are generally tailored to small inverter-based solar installations or larger, utility-
 scale electrical exports, ICH can be unfamiliar to utilities because it may fall in the range between

 9

 these small- and large-scale power providers. In some cases, jurisdictional issues may occur that
 can cause significant delays. Ultimately, if a project takes longer to receive approval and to
 develop, then cost overruns can occur.

• Phase 5. Financing and Power Sales (ranked 1st most challenging): Developers and owners
 must decide whether the overall cost of developing ICH will be profitable, and both financing and
 power sales are key aspects affecting feasibility. Obtaining better understanding of risk appetite
 can streamline decision-making. As with other forms of hydropower development, project
 economics are site-specific and depend on factors related to location and local utilities tariffs and
 policies. When a quick payback period is expected, power sales can often be too low to warrant
 development. Equipment must be sized properly to maximize the cost–benefit ratio to match the
 energy generation with demand when net metering is available, including the challenge
 associated with matching generation and demand on short (e.g., 15-minute) time intervals. If
 there is a lack of net metering, the power prices will often be too low to warrant development.
 Willingness to invest funds to perform a feasibility study can present a challenge, given
 uncertainties that are present early during the process. On a different note, remote locations offer
 their own set of challenges, including the cost of long transmission interconnections that may
 need to be constructed and thus increase project costs.

• Phase 6. Construction and Supply Chain (ranked 4th most challenging): Finding construction
 contractors willing to install hydro equipment can be difficult, and they may lack previous
 experience. Contractors who routinely work on municipal water supply systems do not generally
 have experience with electrical generation equipment. Although many of the tasks are closely
 related to their customary work (e.g., installing piping, pump stations, and controls), there is
 hesitancy to take on the responsibility of installing unfamiliar technology. Additionally, turbine
 procurement can be plagued by supply chain issues and long lead times due to its custom nature
 and limited use; lead times of 10 or more months are common. Also, the window of time in which
 construction can occur may be short due to flow seasonality and inclement weather conditions.
 Planning in advance to have equipment on site when construction can occur can be difficult.

• Phase 7. Operations and Maintenance (ranked 7th most challenging): Once the construction
 and commissioning are complete, ongoing operations and maintenance are needed. ICH does
 require ongoing operation and maintenance, though adding responsibility to existing staff can be
 challenging for municipalities with limited staffing availability. The amount of responsibility may
 warrant hiring additional staff, and contracting the work out can be expensive.

 4. COST ANALYSIS

This section analyzes the initial capital costs (ICC) of ICH. The Baseline Cost Model (BCM), originally
published by ORNL in 2015 through two versions (O’Connor et al., 2015a; 2015b). The BCM provides a
pre-feasibility assessment of costs using empirical models for estimating hydropower costs and was most
recently updated in 2023 (Oladosu and Sasthav, 2023). The underlying data are from existing and planned
projects throughout the US, collected from several sources. The model results are intended to provide a
lower bound on future project costs. Six categories of hydropower development are included in the
model, including “Canal/Conduit.” The modeled costs for Canal/Conduit are dependent upon three
variables: capacity, head, and stage (i.e., planning, engineering, or construction). Given its empirical
nature and limitations, the BCM is not intended to replace true engineering-based estimates.

Data for 14 recent ICH projects were collected to compare actual costs with the BCM. Data for 6 of these
projects were collected from Small Hydro Consulting’s ICH engineering experience, and the other 8 data
points were taken from the California Energy Commission’s 2020 report on ICH (CEC, 2020). The
collected costs were adjusted to 2020 dollars using the US Bureau of Reclamation’s Construction Cost
Trends Equipment index (USBR, 2024). The project data are shown in Table 1.

The results of the comparison are shown in Figure 10. The three projects that deviate the most from the
BCM-modeled ICC per kW are indicated by red ‘X’s and are considered outliers; the absolute value of
the residuals of the log transformed data are the greatest for these three projects. These three projects were
all in California and required additional equipment or infrastructure that may not be necessary for every
site. Two projects installed additional PRVs to reduce pressure fluctuations upstream of the turbine, and
one project required the addition of an equalization basin to reduce fluctuation in flow available to the
turbine. These measures increased the overall construction cost but also increased the capacity factor,
resulting in more favorable economics.

The BCM is shown to predict the actual costs of conduit hydropower well and generally provides a lower
bound of the expected cost. Site-specific conditions, such as the requirement of additional infrastructure
to reduce fluctuations in pressure and flow, may increase costs above those predicted with the BCM.
Further refinement of the BCM is not recommended based on this analysis. However, we recommend
continued updates to the BCM model with recent project costs.

 5. SUMMARY

This report examines the potential of ICH as a energy solution for PWS systems. By harnessing the
energy of flowing water within existing infrastructure, ICH offers a cost-effective alternative to
conventional energy generation methods. The concept of ICH relies on leveraging the existing water
infrastructure to generate electricity. This technology can significantly reduce the environmental impact
compared to traditional hydropower, which often requires the construction of large dams and associated
infrastructure. With its minimal footprint, ICH represents an underutilized resource with substantial
untapped potential for energy generation in public water systems.

Currently, there are 159 municipal ICH facilities operating across the United States, with a combined
output of 245 megawatts (MW). These systems primarily serve the dual purpose of providing water and
generating electricity. Despite the success of these existing systems, a significant opportunity remains to
expand ICH capacity, with an estimated additional 374 MW of potential within municipal PWS systems
that could be tapped into. Technologies used in these systems vary depending on the specific conditions at
each site, but proven technologies such as Kaplan turbines and PATs have been successful in optimizing
energy production. These systems are selected based on factors such as water flow rates, pressure
conditions, and other site-specific hydraulic factors that influence performance.

The development of ICH involves a series of iterative phases, each of which comes with its own set of
challenges. One of the first hurdles is engaging stakeholders, which can be particularly difficult due to
limited awareness and understanding of ICH technology. Utilities, which are often risk-averse due to the
high costs and regulatory complexities associated with energy projects, may hesitate to adopt this
technology. Building stakeholder consensus requires clear communication about the long-term benefits of
ICH, including reduced energy costs, a reduced carbon footprint, and a more resilient energy supply.
Moreover, stakeholder engagement efforts often need to address concerns about system reliability,
operational disruptions, and the overall impact on water delivery.

Financial feasibility is another key challenge in developing ICH. The economic viability of a project
depends heavily on several site-specific factors, such as the local tariff policies, the availability of net
metering, and the infrastructure already in place. Tariff policies determine how much utilities can earn
from the electricity generated by ICH systems, while net metering policies can provide incentives for
municipalities by allowing them to offset energy costs or earn credits for surplus electricity fed back into
the grid. However, these policies can vary greatly by location, leading to a disparity in the financial
attractiveness of ICH projects across regions.

Despite the financial and technical hurdles, there are regulatory frameworks in place that aim to
streamline the development of ICH systems. The Hydropower Regulatory Efficiency Act of 2013 has
played a significant role in simplifying the permitting process for qualifying ICH projects by exempting
them from the need for a full Federal Energy Regulatory Commission license. While this regulatory
change has facilitated project approvals, challenges related to interconnection requirements and
jurisdictional complexities persist. Coordination between local, state, and federal agencies, as well as
utility companies, is often required to navigate the approval processes for interconnection to the grid, and
these bureaucratic layers can delay project timelines and increase costs.

In terms of cost, ICH projects exhibit considerable variability depending on factors such as the size and
capacity of the system, the specific conditions of the installation site, and any additional infrastructure
needed. For instance, retrofitting existing infrastructure with energy-generating components or
establishing new power lines can significantly add to the capital cost of a project. The range of capital
costs for ICH systems can span from relatively modest amounts for small-scale projects to millions of
dollars for larger, more complex systems. The financial models currently available for predicting the costs

 14

of ICH systems have proven effective in providing general estimates; however, these models need to be
updated regularly as more data become available from ongoing and newly developed projects.

In conclusion, ICH is a promising avenue for reducing the environmental impact of water supply systems
while also providing a valuable source of energy. Although there are significant development challenges
related to financing, regulatory hurdles, and technical complexity, the untapped potential for energy
generation from existing water infrastructure is substantial. With continued innovation and supportive
policy frameworks, ICH could offer affordable and reliable energy solutions for urban water systems.
