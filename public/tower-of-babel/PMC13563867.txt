The impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being of nursing staff: the moderating role of coping styles
Authors: Sheng Li, Fei Wang, Wenjie Liu, Zhengyan Wei, Jinyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNursing staff consistently work in high-stress environments, and workplace violence has become a significant risk factor affecting their occupational well-being. However, the underlying mechanisms of this relationship remain to be fully elucidated.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted. From February to March 2025, a survey was administered to 9,466 nursing staff from over 50 hospitals in Lanzhou City using stratified convenience sampling. Spearman correlation analysis and hierarchical regression models were employed to examine the relationships among variables and to test the moderating effect of coping styles.ResultsWorkplace violence demonstrated a significant negative predictive relationship with occupational well-being (β = -0.238, p < 0.001). However, its zero-order correlation was relatively weak, suggesting the presence of a suppression effect. Positive coping significantly enhanced occupational well-being (β = 0.453, p < 0.001), while negative coping served as a negative predictor (β = -0.161, p < 0.001). Moderating analysis revealed that under high levels of workplace violence, the protective effect of positive coping was attenuated (β = -0.049, p < 0.001), whereas the adverse effect of negative coping was amplified (β = 0.075, p < 0.001).ConclusionWorkplace violence impacts occupational well-being through complex mechanisms. Interventions should prioritize enhanced organizational support and coping skills training for personnel highly exposed to violence. Implementation strategies should adopt a stratified management approach based on levels of violence exposure, with emphasis on strengthening organizational support and coping capacity building in high-risk contexts.Keywords: Workplace violence, Nursing staff, Occupational well-being, Coping styles, Moderating effect, Positive coping, Negative coping

Introduction

Nursing staff are the core force within the healthcare service system. They work long-term in high-load, high-risk environments. Their occupational health and well-being have become a key issue for the sustainable development of global healthcare systems. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines workplace violence (WPV) as “incidents where staff are abused, threatened, or assaulted in circumstances related to their work, including commuting to and from work, involving an explicit or implicit challenge to their safety, well-being, or health.” As the group with the most frequent contact with patients and their families, nursing staff face a significantly higher risk of violence exposure compared to other medical personnel [1]. Globally, about 63% of health workers report having experienced violence. Nurses are three times more likely to be exposed to violence than other health workers. In departments such as emergency and psychiatry, the exposure rate among nurses is as high as 89.9% [2, 3]. This occupational hazard not only directly damages the physical and mental health of nursing staff but also profoundly affects their occupational well-being and retention intention. This is achieved by reducing professional identity and increasing emotional exhaustion. Ultimately, it threatens nursing quality and patient safety [4, 5]. Therefore, clarifying the internal mechanism through which workplace violence affects occupational well-being has become a critical issue to be urgently addressed in the fields of nursing management and occupational health.

Workplace violence in the nursing field shows a global trend. However, its manifestations and severity vary significantly across regions and cultures. In developed countries, 2023 US data shows the past-year violence exposure rate among emergency department nurses reached 84.4%. Psychological violence accounted for 84.4%, and physical violence accounted for 61.3% [6]. A study conducted across five European countries found that about 54% of nurses had experienced non-physical violence, and 20% had experienced physical violence [7]. A multi-center study in mainland China showed the violence exposure rate am7ong nurses in tertiary hospitals reached 73%. Non-staff nurses and junior nurses faced even higher risks [8]. The forms of violence have expanded from traditional physical aggression to include psychological violence, whose traumatic effects tend to be more enduring [9].

The harm of workplace violence to nursing staff is multidimensional. At the physiological level, violent incidents can lead to physical symptoms such as sleep disorders, chronic fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain [5]. At the psychological level, it can trigger mental health issues like anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). A meta-analysis showed violence exposure increases nurses’ PTSD risk by 2.25 times [10]. At the occupational level, violent experiences are significantly correlated with nurses’ job burnout, decreased job satisfaction, and increased turnover intention. Research indicates that nurses who have experienced violence have a turnover risk about 1.7 times higher than those who have not [11, 12]. More critically, this negative impact is transmissible. Nurses’ emotional exhaustion can lower nursing quality and increase the risk of medical errors. This forms a vicious cycle of “violence exposure – job burnout – decline in nursing quality” [13].

Occupational well-being refers to the positive psychological experience individuals gain from their work activities. Its core dimensions include a sense of professional value, job satisfaction, physical and mental health, and social support [14]. For nursing staff, occupational well-being is not only a key indicator of their occupational health but also an important antecedent variable for predicting nursing quality and patient satisfaction [15]. Existing research shows that factors influencing the occupational well-being of nursing staff can be categorized into three levels: individual, organizational, and societal. The individual level includes personality traits, coping styles, and psychological resilience. The organizational level involves the work environment, salary, benefits, and management support. The societal level encompasses professional identity, public respect, and policy safeguards [16, 17]. Among various influencing factors, workplace violence has been widely recognized as a significant occupational stressor negatively associated with occupational well-being. However, existing research exhibits inconsistencies regarding the specific mechanisms underlying the “violence-coping-well-being” pathway.

Coping styles refer to the cognitive and behavioral strategies individuals employ when facing stressful events. On one hand, most studies confirm that positive coping strategies (e.g., seeking support) can buffer the negative impacts of violence [18]. Nevertheless, some research indicates that under high-frequency violence exposure, individuals’ coping resources may become depleted, leading to a diminished or even eliminated protective effect of positive coping, demonstrating a “threshold effect” [19]. On the other hand, while there is consensus that negative coping exacerbates harm, research remains insufficient regarding whether its effect increases linearly or exhibits a “cumulative effect” [11]. These inconsistencies suggest that the moderating role of coping styles may be more complex than simple main effects, and its boundary conditions urgently require clarification through large-sample empirical studies.

In recent years, some studies have begun to examine the moderating role of coping styles, yet certain limitations remain. First, most studies focus on the direct impact of violence. There is insufficient exploration of the chain mediation and moderation mechanisms of “violence – coping – well-being.” Particularly, there is a lack of analysis on the interaction between different coping strategies [20]. Second, sample representativeness is limited. Existing research mostly concentrates on tertiary hospitals or specific departments. There is insufficient attention to groups in primary care institutions and community nurses. These groups often face more complex violence risks and resource scarcity issues [21]. Third, there is a lack of a stratified intervention perspective based on different violence contexts [22, 23]. In the Chinese context, research on workplace violence against nursing staff also faces unique challenges due to healthcare system characteristics (such as patient-provider tensions and disparities in benefits between permanent and contract staff) and cultural factors (e.g., the tendency to endure adversity quietly within collectivist cultures). These limitations constrain the practical applicability of research findings in managerial practice.

Based on this, the present study takes nursing staff from different levels of hospitals in Lanzhou City as the research subjects and employs hierarchical regression analysis to systematically examine the impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being, with a specific focus on testing the moderating role of coping styles(see Fig. 1). The primary contribution of this study lies in its use of a large-sample basis to reveal the mechanism between workplace violence and occupational well-being, and to identify the differential moderating effects of coping styles under varying levels of violence, thereby providing empirical evidence for developing risk-stratified intervention strategies.

Based on the above theoretical and empirical foundation, this study proposes the following testable hypotheses:

H1

Workplace violence is significantly negatively correlated with the occupational well-being of nursing staff.

H2

Positive coping is positively correlated with occupational well-being, whereas negative coping is negatively correlated.

H3

Positive coping mitigates the negative impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being.

H4

Negative coping exacerbates the negative impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being.

Subjects and methods

Ethical review

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Lanzhou First People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: 2025 A-8). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before completing the questionnaire. As the questionnaire was distributed and collected online anonymously, handwritten informed consent forms were not collected from participants in this study. Submission of the completed questionnaire was considered evidence of the participant’s informed and voluntary consent to participate in the research.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated using the formula for estimating population proportions in cross-sectional studies: n=Z2 * P(1-P)/d2. Here, Z represents the critical value of the standard normal distribution (set at 1.96 for α = 0.05), P denotes the estimated prevalence, and d is the margin of error. Based on prior studies reporting the incidence of workplace violence among healthcare workers, P was set at 0.50 (to maximize the required sample size), with a margin of error d of 0.02. The calculated minimum sample size was 2,401 participants. To account for potential design effects from the multi-stage sampling strategy and invalid responses, the sample size was appropriately expanded. Ultimately, this study included 9,466 participants, ensuring sufficient statistical power for the analyses.

Research subjects

This study used a cross-sectional design and was conducted in Lanzhou City, China, from February to March 2025, involving clinical nursing staff as participants. This study employed a stratified convenience sampling strategy. First, based on the hospital tier classification in the Lanzhou Health Statistical Yearbook, the hospitals of the study subjects were divided into three tiers: tertiary hospitals, secondary hospitals, and primary hospitals. Subsequently, within each tier, the research team selected cooperating hospitals by considering the operational feasibility of the study implementation. “Operational feasibility” primarily included: (1) willingness of the hospital’s nursing management department to cooperate with the study; (2) ability to provide stable support for data collection; (3) possession of a complete nursing personnel management system; and (4) permission for the research team to complete questionnaire distribution and collection within a specified timeframe. Inclusion criteria: ① Work experience ≥ 1 year; ② Possession of a valid Nurse Practicing Certificate; ③ Participants must have signed the informed consent form and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria: ① Visiting/advanced training nurses; ② Standardized training nurses; ③ Intern nurses. To mitigate potential selection bias introduced by convenience sampling, this study aimed to include multiple hospitals within each tier as much as possible, covering medical institutions at provincial, municipal, and district/county levels to enhance sample heterogeneity and internal comparative value. Furthermore, hospitals of different tiers included institutions of varying scales and serving different population characteristics to increase the diversity of the sample structure. Despite these measures, this study remains a non-probability sampling design; therefore, the generalizability of the results to external contexts should be interpreted with caution.

Research methods

General information survey

A self-designed general information questionnaire was used. It included 13 items: gender, age, education level, professional title, monthly average income, administrative position, years of work experience, hospital affiliation, department, hospital level, employment nature (e.g., formal staff, contract staff), marital status, and number of children.

Survey on workplace violence against medical staff

The Questionnaire on Workplace Violence Experienced by Medical Staff, compiled by Chen Zuhui et al. from Southern Medical University [24], was used. This study adopted Part 1 of this questionnaire. This part was used to investigate the types and frequency of workplace violence experienced by medical staff within the hospital over the past year. It mainly includes three items: verbal violence, physical violence, and sexual harassment. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 points corresponding to “0 times,” “1 time,” “2–3 times,” and “>3 times,” respectively. The total score ranges from 0 to 9. A higher score indicates a higher frequency of experiencing workplace violence. Specifically, a total score of 0 indicates zero frequency, 1–3 indicates low frequency, 4–6 indicates medium frequency, and 7–9 indicates high frequency. This scale has been widely used and validated among nursing populations in China [25, 26]. To further confirm its applicability among nursing staff in the Lanzhou area, the research team invited 30 local nurses for a pilot test prior to the formal survey. The results indicated that all scale items were clear, comprehensible, and exhibited no significant cultural misunderstandings.

Survey on occupational well-being of medical workers

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from the Occupational Well-being Scale for Medical Workers compiled by Hu Dongmei [27]. The scale includes 5 dimensions: Physical and Mental Health (6 items), Value/Ability Realization (6 items), Social Support (5 items), Economic Income (3 items), and Work Environment (4 items), totaling 24 items. Each item is rated according to a Likert 5-point scale, where 5 point means “completely agree,” 4 points mean “basically agree,” 3 points mean “uncertain,” 2 points mean “basically disagree,” and 1 points mean “completely disagree.” A higher total scale score indicates a higher level of occupational well-being among medical workers. Notably, the 6 items in the Physical and Mental Health dimension are reverse-scored. The relevant scales have been extensively utilized among Chinese healthcare professionals, demonstrating good reliability and validity, making them suitable for the context of this study [28].

Simplified coping style questionnaire

This questionnaire was developed by Xie Yaning [29] based on foreign coping style questionnaires and adapted to the Chinese context. It is a self-rated scale containing two dimensions: Positive Coping Style and Negative Coping Style, with a total of 20 items. Items 1–12 belong to the Positive Coping dimension, and items 13–20 belong to the Negative Coping dimension. Each item uses a 4-point scale from 0 to 3 (0 = not used, 1 = occasionally used, 2 = sometimes used, 3 = often used). A higher average score on the Positive Coping dimension indicates that the individual tends to use more positive coping styles, and vice versa indicates a tendency towards more negative coping styles. These scales have undergone translation and cultural adaptation within the Chinese context, with their psychometric properties validated among medical personnel, thus deemed appropriate for the target population of this investigation [30].

Survey procedure

The electronic questionnaire was distributed online via the Wenjuanxing platform. After obtaining formal approval from the nursing department of the participants’ hospitals, research team members communicated with head nurses of relevant departments and conducted systematic training. The training clarified the research objectives, assessment content, academic value, and operational procedures. Special emphasis was placed on the principles of anonymity and confidentiality, as well as the specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Head nurses then distributed the questionnaire to nurses, ensuring completion was done anonymously based on fully obtained informed consent. It was stipulated that each participant could only complete the questionnaire once using the same device and IP address, and all questions had to be answered before submission. After collection, all questionnaires were reviewed one by one. Questionnaires showing patterned responses (e.g., straight-lining) or with a completion time of less than 2 min were excluded. Furthermore, as data were collected through self-reporting methods, social desirability bias and common method variance may exist. Although anonymous surveys were used to mitigate such biases, their influence cannot be entirely ruled out. Additionally, single-source data may introduce common method bias; future studies could consider employing multi-source data for further validation.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. According to the Shapiro-Wilk test, the core scale data in this study did not follow a normal distribution. Therefore, continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)], and categorical data are presented as frequency (percentage). Relationships between variables were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. To examine the moderating effect of coping styles in the relationship between workplace violence and occupational well-being, hierarchical regression analysis was employed. To reduce the impact of multicollinearity, all continuous variables were mean-centered before calculating interaction terms. The contribution of newly added variables was assessed by comparing the explained variance (R²) of each model and its change (ΔR²). The variance inflation factor (VIF) for all variables was less than 5, further supporting the absence of significant multicollinearity issues in the model. All statistical analyses considered a p-value of < 0.05 as indicative of statistical significance.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis of variables

The median, interquartile range (IQR), and Spearman correlation analysis results for the main study variables are presented in Table 3. The median score for total occupational well-being was 79 (IQR = 22). Positive coping showed a significant positive correlation with occupational well-being (r = 0.574, p < 0.01). Negative coping showed a positive correlation with occupational well-being in the correlation analysis (r = 0.292), but shifted to a negative predictor in the regression analysis (β = -0.161). This phenomenon suggests that, when other variables are not controlled for, negative coping might reflect an individual’s overall level of coping engagement. However, after controlling for positive coping, its independent negative effect becomes apparent. Additionally, this result may also be influenced by common method bias—self-reported data from a single source could create a spurious positive correlation between variables, whereas the true directional relationship is revealed in a multivariate model. The total score of workplace violence showed a weak direct negative correlation with occupational well-being (r = -0.025, p < 0.05). However, its effect became significantly stronger in the regression model (β = -0.238), indicating a typical suppression effect. Further analysis suggests that this suppression effect may stem from shared variance between coping style variables and workplace violence. Specifically, workplace violence was negatively correlated with positive coping (r = -0.070) and positively correlated with negative coping (r = 0.035), while coping styles were in turn strongly correlated with occupational well-being (positive coping r = 0.574). When these variables were not controlled for, part of the explanatory power was dispersed, leading to an underestimation of the true effect of workplace violence. After including coping styles in the model, this shared variance was partialled out, allowing the independent effect of workplace violence to emerge, resulting in a larger standardized regression coefficient.

From a statistical perspective, suppressor effects typically occur when the zero-order correlation between a predictor variable and an outcome variable is weak, but the true relationship emerges after controlling for a confounding variable that correlates with both. In this study, coping styles are correlated with both workplace violence and professional well-being. Therefore, when coping styles are not controlled for, part of the explanatory power of workplace violence on professional well-being is “diluted.” After including both active coping and passive coping in the model, this irrelevant variance is effectively removed, thereby enhancing the independent predictive effect of workplace violence. This further demonstrates the importance of controlling for key psychological and behavioral variables when studying the professional well-being of nursing staff.

The impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being: the moderating role of coping styles

To examine the impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being and the moderating role of coping styles, hierarchical regression analysis was conducted. The results are shown in Table 4. Model 1 included only demographic control variables, explaining 2.8% of the variance in occupational well-being (F = 53.677, p < 0.001). Among these, professional title (β = -0.044, p < 0.01), monthly income (β = 0.173, p < 0.001), and marital status (β = 0.030, p < 0.01) had significant predictive effects on well-being. Model 2 added workplace violence to the control variables, significantly increasing the explained variance to 8.4% (ΔR² = 0.056, p < 0.001). Workplace violence showed a significant direct negative predictive effect on occupational well-being (β = -0.238, p < 0.001). Model 3 further included both Positive and Negative Coping dimensions, substantially increasing the model’s explanatory power to 24.1% (ΔR² = 0.158, p < 0.001). The results show that positive coping had a strong positive predictive effect on occupational well-being (β = 0.453, p < 0.001), while negative coping had a significant negative predictive effect (β = -0.161, p < 0.001). After controlling for coping styles, the negative impact of workplace violence remained significant, although the effect size was somewhat attenuated (β = -0.205, p < 0.001). Based on Model 3, Model 4 added two interaction terms, “workplace violence × positive coping” and “workplace violence × negative coping,” to test for moderating effects. The final model explained 24.4% of the variance in occupational well-being (ΔR² = 0.003, p < 0.001). The moderating effect analysis indicated: The moderating effect of positive coping was significant (β = -0.049, p < 0.001). Simple slope analysis revealed that as the level of workplace violence increased, the positive protective effect of positive coping on occupational well-being gradually weakened. The moderating effect of negative coping was also significant (β = 0.075, p < 0.001). Simple slope analysis indicated that as the level of workplace violence increased, the negative damaging effect of negative coping on occupational well-being was significantly enhanced.

Discussion

Based on a large-sample dataset, this study systematically analyzed the impact of workplace violence on the occupational well-being of nursing staff, with a specific focus on examining the moderating role of coping styles. Compared with existing research, the core contributions of this study are threefold: first, it identified the “suppression effect” between workplace violence and occupational well-being; second, it revealed the context-dependent moderating characteristics of coping styles under different levels of violence; and third, it provided empirical evidence for risk-stratified intervention strategies. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the occupational psychological mechanisms among nursing staff.

The direct impact of workplace violence on occupational well-being

The findings of this study further support the negative impact mechanism of workplace violence on occupational well-being, suggesting that this effect is stable. For instance, Zhao et al. [31] confirmed that workplace violence is negatively correlated with nurses’ psychological well-being and influences their professional attitudes via the mediating role of burnout. The systematic review and meta-analysis by Wang et al. [10] also identified significant positive correlations between workplace violence and nurses’ work stress, job burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. It is noteworthy that the marked discrepancy between the simple correlation and regression analyses indicates that variable relationships may be moderated or obscured by other factors. This difference reflects a typical “suppression effect,” where certain variables (such as income, professional title, and coping styles) mask the true relationship between workplace violence and occupational well-being through shared variance when not controlled. When these variables are included in the model, the independent effect of workplace violence becomes apparent, demonstrating a stronger negative predictive power. Additionally, the correlation coefficients among the variables are at moderate levels, with no significant multicollinearity issues detected. Therefore, this phenomenon is more likely to reflect a statistically significant suppression effect rather than model instability. This finding suggests that relying solely on simple correlation analysis may underestimate the true impact of workplace violence on professional well-being. Therefore, incorporating key confounding variables in related studies holds important methodological significance.

From a clinical practice perspective, these results reveal the profound impact of workplace violence on nurses’ professional experience. Violent incidents not only destroy the sense of safety in the work environment but also directly erode nurses’ sense of professional meaning and achievement by diminishing their professional dignity and exacerbating emotional exhaustion. Workplace violence is prevalent among healthcare professionals and has been widely recognized as a significant occupational stressor [32]. Nursing staff face particularly high exposure risks, with a significantly higher likelihood of experiencing violence compared to other medical personnel [33]. A recent study of nurses in Taiwan showed that nearly 50% experienced at least one incident of workplace violence in the past 12 months [34]. This phenomenon can also be understood from the perspective of stress and coping theory, which posits that an individual’s cognitive appraisal of stressors and their coping strategies collectively influence psychological outcomes.

Main effect analysis of coping styles

This study further validates the differential roles of various coping styles in the formation of occupational well-being, supporting the fundamental assumptions of coping theory. Positive coping strategies (such as proactive problem-solving and seeking social support) can enhance an individual’s sense of control and self-efficacy, thereby improving occupational well-being; whereas negative coping styles (such as avoidance and self-blame) may exacerbate psychological burden and reduce well-being.

These findings are consistent in mechanism with conclusions from research in other fields. For instance, a meta-analysis by Ma et al. [35] targeting service industry employees found that positive coping strategies effectively predicted higher levels of job performance, further supporting the universality of positive coping as a key psychological process that plays a significant role between stress and positive work outcomes. Studies focusing on clinical nurses also indicate that nurses adopting positive psychological coping strategies exhibit lower stress levels and higher occupational well-being [36, 37]. Notably, the inconsistent direction of the relationship observed for negative coping across different analytical models suggests that its mechanism of action is relatively complex. This “direction reversal” phenomenon indicates potential interaction and confounding effects among variables. On one hand, when other variables are not controlled for, negative coping may reflect an individual’s overall level of coping engagement. On the other hand, after controlling for positive coping, its independent negative effect becomes apparent. Considering the correlation between the two types of coping styles, this suggests that in real-world scenarios they may not operate in strict opposition but rather jointly influence the individual’s psychological adaptation process.

Analysis of the moderating effect of coping styles

Further analysis reveals that coping styles play a context-dependent moderating role in the relationship between workplace violence and occupational well-being. Hierarchical regression results indicate that the model’s explanatory power improved after including interaction terms, suggesting that coping styles can alter the intensity of workplace violence’s impact. Specifically, as the level of workplace violence increases, the protective effect of positive coping on occupational well-being gradually diminishes. This implies that in high-intensity violence exposure scenarios, the effectiveness of individuals relying on their own coping strategies may be limited. This phenomenon can be explained by the Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory, which posits that sustained high-intensity stress depletes individuals’ psychological resources, thereby weakening the effectiveness of coping strategies.

On the other hand, the negative impact of negative coping becomes significantly stronger in high-violence contexts, potentially exacerbating the adverse effects of workplace violence by intensifying negative emotional responses and cognitive evaluations. This finding aligns with the study by Navarro-Abal et al. [38], which identified psychological harassment as a key factor affecting nurses’ work engagement, further supporting this study’s interpretation of the mechanisms underlying psychological violence and negative coping. These results demonstrate that coping styles not only have main effects but that their directional impact also depends on the intensity of the stress context.

Influence of demographic variables

The hierarchical regression analysis in this study reveals that multiple demographic variables have significant effects on occupational well-being. Specifically, monthly income shows a significant positive effect, suggesting that economic compensation remains an important factor influencing the occupational well-being of nursing staff. In this study’s sample, the majority of nursing staff had incomes concentrated in the range of 3,000–6,000 RMB, which aligns with the generally low income level of nursing professionals in China. Reports from the International Council of Nurses (ICN) also indicate that salary levels are closely related to job satisfaction and staff retention [39]. Relevant studies have shown that remuneration can indirectly reduce turnover intention by enhancing job satisfaction [40–42]. The negative predictive effect of professional title suggests that nursing staff with higher titles may face greater management pressures and responsibility burdens, though this interpretation requires further validation. The positive effect of marital status indicates the protective role of family support in occupational mental health, which is consistent with existing research conclusions [43–46]. The negative effect of age suggests that younger nurses may face greater challenges in occupational adaptation and stress coping; this group is also at higher risk of experiencing workplace violence [47–50]. Overall, the formation of occupational well-being is the result of the combined effects of multiple factors, including individual characteristics, career development, and social support.

Furthermore, the sample in this study has relatively concentrated demographic characteristics: females account for 96.57%, nursing staff under 40 years old account for 86.90%, and off-staff contract employees account for 82.77%. This structure is generally consistent with the current reality of the clinical nursing workforce in China, which is characterized by a younger age profile, a predominantly female composition, and a high proportion of contract-based employment. Previous studies have shown that young nurses, due to relatively limited clinical experience and incomplete career adaptation, are more likely to experience occupational stress when facing complex patient–nurse relationships and high work intensity, and constitute a high-risk group for workplace violence [51]. Meanwhile, contract-based nurses may face greater challenges in career advancement, salary and benefits, and perceived organizational support, thereby affecting their level of occupational well-being. Additionally, female nursing staff tend to invest more in interpersonal interaction and emotional labor, which may lead to higher sensitivity to workplace violence incidents. Therefore, the findings of this study, to a certain extent, reflect the occupational psychological characteristics of the current frontline young female nursing staff in China, and caution should be exercised when generalizing the results to male nurses, senior nurses, or permanently employed staff.

Practical implications

Based on the findings of this study, intervention strategies should be collaboratively advanced from two dimensions: “reducing violence exposure” and “enhancing coping capacity”. We propose the following practical recommendations:

(1) Build a Multi-level Violence Prevention System: Considering the characteristics of the moderating effects identified in this study, intervention measures should be designed in a stratified manner according to the level of violence exposure. Hospital administrators should integrate workplace violence prevention into the organizational safety management system. They should establish a comprehensive process management plan including violence risk assessment, early warning mechanisms, emergency response, and post-incident support. The American Association of Critical-Care Nurses (AACN), in its updated 2025 position statement, explicitly requires healthcare institutions to adopt a zero-tolerance policy and implement a comprehensive prevention plan encompassing staff education, risk assessment, enhanced security, and support systems [52]. This viewpoint is also supported by an editorial in the journal Nursing2025, whose editor-in-chief similarly advocates for a comprehensive strategy combining technology, training, and legislation, and establishing a zero-tolerance culture [53]. These authoritative positions collectively underscore the necessity of multi-level systematic interventions to reduce violence risk.

(2) Develop Targeted Coping Skills Training: Given that the effect of positive coping weakens as violence levels increase, training programs should differentiate between basic coping skills and coping strategies for high-intensity violence situations. For example, a national project in Portugal effectively reduced nurses’ violence exposure by improving early risk identification abilities and conflict de-escalation skills [54]. Simultaneously, the potential harm of negative coping should be guarded against. Cognitive-behavioral therapy and other means should be used to help nursing staff identify and change negative coping patterns.

(3) Improve the Professional Support System: Considering the limitations of individual coping resources in high-violence environments, support at the organizational level is crucial. Hospitals should establish a multi-dimensional support system including psychological counseling, post-trauma intervention, and legal consultation, such as the psychological counseling centers provided for healthcare workers who have experienced violence in Italy [55]. Furthermore, improving compensation, optimizing career development paths, and enhancing nurses’ economic security and professional identity are also important ways to enhance their overall occupational well-being.

(4) Pay Attention to the Needs of Special Groups: Differentiated intervention plans should be designed for high-risk groups such as young nurses and those with low professional titles. For example, specialized violence prevention training for novice nurses [56], and providing equal career development opportunities for non-staff contract nurses to reduce their professional alienation. Recent research indicates that enhancing job autonomy, establishing a safety culture, and ensuring effective violence management can significantly reduce nurses’ turnover intention [57].

Research limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference; future longitudinal or intervention studies are needed for verification. Second, the use of stratified convenience sampling with voluntary participants may introduce self-selection bias, as individuals more sensitive to workplace violence or occupational well-being might be more likely to participate, potentially affecting the representativeness and generalization of the results. Third, the reliance on self-reported data may be subject to social desirability bias and common method variance. For instance, respondents might under report negative experiences or over report positive behaviors, and collecting data from a single source at one time point could inflate correlations between variables. Future research could improve upon this by incorporating multi-source data or temporal separation designs. Furthermore, this study adopted the first part of Chen Zuhui’s Workplace Violence Questionnaire, which contains only three count-type items measuring verbal violence, physical violence, and sexual harassment. This tool is essentially a behavioral checklist of violence exposure rather than a scale measuring a single latent psychological construct. Since the occurrence of the three types of violence does not necessarily covary at the individual level, the interpretative value of calculating Cronbach’sα coefficient is limited. Therefore, the reliability index of this instrument was not reported in Table 1. This limitation is relatively common in violence-related research, and future studies may consider adopting multidimensional violence assessment tools to balance reliability estimation with content comprehensiveness. This study did not differentiate between the distinct impacts of different types of workplace violence; the mechanisms underlying the psychological consequences of various forms of violence warrant further exploration. Finally, cultural factors specific to China, such as conflict avoidance tendencies, may influence the reporting of violent incidents and coping style choices, potentially introducing cultural bias. Cross-cultural studies in the future could further validate the universality of these findings.

Conclusion

This study confirms that workplace violence has a significant negative impact on the occupational well-being of nursing staff, an effect that becomes more pronounced after controlling for confounding variables. Coping styles not only directly influence well-being but also exhibit differentiated moderating effects under varying levels of violence. The key contribution of this study lies in revealing this “context-dependent moderating mechanism,” providing an empirical basis for developing stratified intervention strategies.
