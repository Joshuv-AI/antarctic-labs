Psychological determinants of employee adaptation: a novel and robust learnability quotient-based fuzzy decision framework for organizational learning
Authors: Şenay Çaylan, Serhat Yüksel, Merve Acar, Serkan Eti, Hasan Dinçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn contemporary work environments, employees’ psychological capacity to adapt to continuous economic, technological, and cultural changes have become a central concern within organizational and applied psychology. While constructs such as adaptability, learning orientation, and cognitive flexibility have been widely discussed, there remains limited consensus on which psychological components of learnability most strongly influence employee adaptation and how adaptation-oriented strategies can be systematically evaluated under uncertainty. Moreover, existing research often lacks integrative analytical frameworks that link individual cognitive–behavioral characteristics with strategic organizational learning outcomes. To address this gap, the present study conceptualizes Learnability Quotient as a multidimensional psychological construct encompassing cognitive flexibility, questioning behavior, openness to experience, and reflective learning tendencies. Building on expert-based psychological evaluations, a novel fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making framework is proposed by integrating spherical fuzzy sets, a newly developed comparative weighting evaluation based on the best criteria (COWEB) weighting technique, and the multiple normalization rating analysis (MUNRA) ranking approach. This integrated model enables the simultaneous assessment of the relative importance of learnability-related psychological indicators and the prioritization of employee adaptation strategies. Empirical results demonstrate that questioning and inquiry as well as mental flexibility emerge as the most influential psychological dimensions of learnability, highlighting the critical role of critical thinking, cognitive openness, and adaptive mental processes in effective organizational learning. Furthermore, the stability of strategy rankings across multiple scenarios supports the psychological robustness and methodological reliability of the proposed framework. By bridging cognitive–behavioral psychology and decision science, this study offers both theoretical contributions to organizational learning psychology and practical insights for designing psychologically informed adaptation strategies in contemporary organizations.Keywords: Learnability quotient, Employee adaptability, Organizational learning culture, Fuzzy multi criteria decision making, Strategic managementSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

In today’s workplace, employees operate under increasing psychological pressure due to rapid technological advancements, economic volatility, and continuously evolving organizational structures. These conditions intensify stress levels, create cognitive overload, and challenge employees’ emotional resilience, ultimately affecting both individual performance and organizational effectiveness. As work environments become more complex and unpredictable, the ability to adapt quickly to new roles, expectations, and knowledge domains has become a fundamental psychological necessity. Adaptability, therefore, is not only a behavioral response but also a cognitive and emotional capability that enables individuals to manage uncertainty and sustain performance under pressure. In this context, identifying the underlying psychological mechanisms that support effective adaptation has become a critical research priority.

In response to this need, the concept of Learnability Quotient (LQ) has emerged as a comprehensive framework for understanding individuals’ capacity to continuously learn and adapt. It reflects not only how quickly new knowledge is acquired but also how effectively it is applied, reflected upon, and transformed into meaningful action. Central to LQ is the ability to adapt rapidly to novel situations, enabling individuals to respond constructively to unfamiliar tasks without being limited by established routines. This ability is closely linked to thinking from multiple perspectives and generating alternative solutions, as high learnability involves moving beyond single viewpoints when interpreting problems1. Openness to new knowledge and experiences further enhances LQ by encouraging curiosity and reducing resistance to change, while a consistent tendency to seek challenging tasks and pursue mastery signals a strong growth orientation. Learnability also requires purposeful planning, organization, and strategic regulation of learning, since effective learning depends on conscious goal setting and effort management. Monitoring and regulating one’s own thinking plays a key role in this process, as individuals assess their understanding, identify gaps, and adjust learning strategies through reflection2. Deeper learning is supported by justifying knowledge, questioning assumptions, and producing evidence-based solutions rather than relying on intuition alone. Motivation remains a core element, as intrinsic desire and sustained energy for learning drive engagement over time. Creativity, resilience in coping with setbacks, responsiveness to feedback, and the deliberate investment of time and effort together show that learnability is not only cognitive but also behavioral and motivational in nature.

These LQ components can be effectively used to strengthen employees’ ability to adapt to changing conditions, as they support continuous learning, mental flexibility, and goal-oriented behavior in dynamic work environments. By embedding these components into organizational practices, employees become more capable of responding to uncertainty, acquiring new skills, and adjusting their performance in line with evolving demands. Adaptation oriented flexible work systems such as hybrid work, task rotation, project-based assignments, and agile team structures expose employees to diverse experiences and support rapid adaptation and mental flexibility3. Artificial intelligence based personalized learning ecosystems enhance openness to learning by offering individualized learning paths, monitoring learning behaviors, and providing targeted feedback that aligns with personal development goals. Agile organizational transformation practices encourage experimentation, fast feedback, and cross functional collaboration, which strengthen both adaptation and learning orientation. Creativity and problem-solving initiatives contribute to analytical thinking and idea generation, while continuous feedback and reflection systems promote self-regulation and learning awareness4. Knowledge sharing platforms and a strong learning culture further accelerate learning across the organization, while personalized career and development planning increases motivation and clarifies long term learning goals. Overall, these strategies demonstrate that Learnability Quotient can be systematically developed through organizational design, leading to higher adaptability, resilience, and sustainable employee performance.

Determining the most critical strategies in the development of LQ is of central importance, as organizations operate in environments characterized by rapid change, uncertainty, and increasing skill demands. When strategic priorities are not clearly defined, learning initiatives may become fragmented, inefficient, or misaligned with organizational needs, leading to wasted resources, reduced employee engagement, and limited adaptive capacity5. The absence of clearly prioritized strategies can also create confusion among employees, weaken motivation, and slow down organizational responses to external changes. For this reason, identifying the most influential LQ components is a necessary step, since strategic interventions should be grounded in those dimensions that most strongly support adaptation, learning agility, and sustained performance. Without a clear understanding of which LQ components are most critical, organizations risk investing equally in all learning dimensions, even though some may have a greater impact on adaptability than others. This challenge is further reinforced by the fact that existing literature offers only a limited number of studies that systematically identify priority strategies and key LQ criteria, leaving practitioners with insufficient guidance for evidence-based decision making6. As a result, this research gap generates uncertainty regarding which interventions should be implemented first and how learning resources should be allocated. The lack of prioritization frameworks also limits the comparability of empirical findings and reduces the practical applicability of theoretical models. To address these limitations, a new priority analysis is required to determine the relative importance of LQ components and related strategies. Such an analysis would provide a structured basis for strategic decision making, reduce ambiguity, and contribute significantly to both academic knowledge and organizational practice.

This study aims to identify the priority strategies that can most effectively enhance employees’ ability to adapt to changing conditions, while also determining the most influential Learnability Quotient components that shape this adaptation process. The motivation of the study arises from the growing need for organizations to respond systematically to uncertainty and transformation, as well as from the limited guidance in existing literature regarding which learning oriented strategies and LQ dimensions should be prioritized. To achieve these objectives, a novel fuzzy multi criteria decision making model is proposed that integrates both methodological rigor and practical relevance. Within the scope of the study, a comprehensive literature review is conducted and seven alternative strategies related to employee adaptation and learning are identified. In addition, sixteen LQ based criteria that influence the selection of these strategies are determined. Expert judgments are collected from ten domain specialists to ensure the validity and applicability of the model. The proposed framework incorporates spherical fuzzy sets to better capture uncertainty and subjectivity in expert evaluations. Criterion weights are calculated using the newly developed comparative weighting evaluation based on the best criteria (COWEB) technique, while the multiple normalization rating analysis (MUNRA) approach is employed to rank the strategic alternatives. In this context, the study seeks to answer the following research questions: (1) Which strategies are most critical for enhancing employees’ adaptability under changing conditions? (2) Which Learnability Quotient components have the greatest influence on the prioritization of these strategies? By addressing these questions, the study aims to contribute to the literature by offering a structured priority analysis framework and by reducing ambiguity in strategic learning and adaptation decisions.

This study contributes to the literature by addressing a critical research gap through the systematic prioritization of employee adaptation strategies and key LQ components using a novel fuzzy multi criteria decision making framework. In addition, the integration of spherical fuzzy sets with the newly developed COWEB technique and the MUNRA approach offers a methodological contribution that enhances the analytical rigor and applicability of learnability-based decision models. The proposed model demonstrates several important advantages over previously developed decision-making frameworks, which strengthen both its theoretical contribution and practical applicability. (1) The development of the COWEB technique within this study represents a major methodological originality. In the existing literature, objective weighting methods such as LOPCOW and Entropy require the construction of alternatives to derive criterion weights, which may bias the weighting structure toward the selected alternatives. Methods such as SIWEC and CIMAS reduce the number of comparison questions by avoiding pairwise comparisons, yet this simplification may limit the depth of expert judgment. In SWARA, experts are required to rank criteria at the outset, meaning that priority structures are imposed before a detailed evaluation process takes place. In contrast, the COWEB technique takes the most important criterion as a reference point and derives the remaining weights accordingly, which helps overcome comparison related inconsistencies observed in other methods and allows clearer differentiation among criteria with close importance levels. (2) The criterion set is constructed directly based on Learnability Quotient components, which provides a strong conceptual foundation for the analysis. This approach ensures theoretical coherence, supports a holistic evaluation of learning and adaptation capabilities, and enables the model to capture cognitive, behavioral, and motivational dimensions simultaneously, resulting in a more comprehensive and realistic assessment. (3) The use of the MUNRA technique for ranking alternatives further enhances the model’s suitability, as MUNRA effectively balances benefit and cost type criteria and produces stable rankings under uncertainty. Compared to many traditional ranking techniques, MUNRA is particularly appropriate for complex strategic evaluations where learning driven adaptability and fuzzy information must be jointly considered.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. “Literature review” presents a comprehensive review of the relevant literature, focusing on LQ, employee adaptability, and existing decision-making approaches used in related studies. “Methodology” introduces the proposed methodology, including the structure of the model, the selected strategies and criteria, and the integrated fuzzy multi criteria decision making techniques. “Analysis” reports the analysis results and presents the prioritization outcomes obtained from the proposed model. “Discussion” discusses the findings in relation to the existing literature and highlights their theoretical and practical implications. Finally, “Conclusion” concludes the study by summarizing the main contributions, outlining limitations, and suggesting directions for future research.

Literature review

Learning agility is a key component of the LQ because it reflects an individual’s willingness and ability to quickly acquire knowledge from experience and apply those insights in new, often unfamiliar situations to sustain high performance. Research views learning agility as an experience-based learning process that combines cognitive flexibility, adaptive behavior, and openness to new situations7. One important indicator of learning agility is adaptation: the ability to modify strategies and methods in response to changing technologies and new circumstances8,9. Flexibility reflects a learner’s capacity to shift perspectives, test different rules, and change approaches when initial plans fail10,4. Openness to learning involves a willingness to engage with diverse viewpoints, new experiences, and feedback, which are essential for growth and for developing positive attitudes toward technology-enhanced and data-driven learning processes3. Huang and Kou11, showed that learning agility significantly predicts psychological well-being and academic resilience, making it a key factor in resilience in higher education students. In organizational and generational contexts, Nayak et al.12 found that learning agility plays a central role in developing employee skills among Gen Z by transforming a learning culture into adaptive capability. Similarly, studies on instructional design and teaching methods, such as those by Wang et al.13, demonstrate that integrating project-based blended learning and design thinking greatly enhances openness to experience and creative adaptability, emphasizing that learning agility is a skill that can be developed through pedagogical design rather than a fixed trait.

Lifelong learning, reflecting an individual’s willingness and proactive engagement to maintain high performance, is a primary constituent of the LQ. It involves learners’ capacity to self-direct, regulate, and adapt learning strategies to changing demands, positioning them as active constructors of knowledge who assume responsibility for monitoring and sustaining their learning over time14,2. Research conceptualizes lifelong learning as a dynamic process supported by goal orientation, self-regulated learning, and metacognitive learning, which together explain how individuals initiate, manage, and sustain learning over time. Goal orientation serves as the motivational foundation of lifelong learning by shaping how learners define success, pursue deep conceptual understanding, persist in challenging tasks, and engage with learning goals, particularly through mastery-oriented goals that prioritize competence development and continuous improvement15. Self-regulated learning represents the strategic and behavioral core of lifelong learning, enabling learners to intentionally plan, monitor, and regulate their cognition, motivation, behavior, and resource use in response to evolving educational and technological demands16. Metacognitive learning constitutes the reflective dimension of lifelong learning by enabling learners to monitor their understanding, evaluate progress, and adapt strategies based on feedback and task demands, thereby supporting knowledge transfer and continuous improvement5. Recent empirical studies demonstrate the application of these principles across diverse domains. For instance, Shams et al.17 found that metacognitive strategies significantly enhance communication and cognitive learning among secondary students, thereby fostering the independence and adaptability required to address real-life challenges. In higher education, Fabiyi et al.18 showed that fostering mastery-oriented goals and deep learning strategies among engineering students is critical for academic achievement and future readiness. Complementing these findings, Geng and Su19 showed that self-regulated and metacognitive learning processes significantly enhance learning performance in blended learning environments.

At the core of the LQ framework is critical thinking, a multidimensional twenty-first-century skill essential for deep cognitive development and meaningful learning. Modern educational research conceptualizes critical thinking as a dynamic capability that can be deliberately developed through inquiry and reasoning, enabling learners to engage with problems, question assumptions, and regulate their thinking while taking responsibility for monitoring, evaluating, and improving their own learning processes6,20. Critical thinking is intrinsically linked to reasoning, inquiry, and reflection, which together explain how individuals generate understanding, solve complex problems, and adapt their thinking over time. Inquiry initiates critical thinking by transforming passive learners into active learners through curiosity and critical engagement, while also enabling learners to evaluate evidence and generate hypotheses to examine both human- and AI-generated content critically21,22. This process is further reinforced by reasoning skills, which provide the logical framework for integrating information, drawing sound inferences, evaluating arguments, and making evidence-based decisions23,24. Complementing inquiry and reasoning, reflection constitutes the metacognitive core of critical thinking, enabling learners to monitor their understanding, evaluate the quality of their reasoning, and adjust strategies based on feedback and experience4. Building on this metacognitive function, problem-solving can be conceptualized as a goal-directed, action-oriented competence that involves identifying complex issues, selecting appropriate solutions that support informed decision-making, and effectively and ethically resolving issues25. In a broader synthesis of the literature, Zeng and Ravindran26, showed that instruction integrating inquiry, reasoning, and reflection, especially through peer feedback, is central to strengthening critical thinking across educational contexts. Similarly, Bosch et al.1 demonstrated that design thinking and maker-based learning environments position teachers and learners as reflective designers who iteratively evaluate and refine their thinking. Building on this inquiry-driven and reflective orientation, Garil and Dio27 conclude that problem-based learning translates inquiry and reflection into authentic problem engagement, supporting both constructive solution-seeking and the development of problem-solving and critical thinking.

Finally, in the LQ framework, growth represents the motivational mechanism for developing competencies through effort, and adaptive responses to challenges. Rather than viewing intelligence as fixed, a growth mindset frames learning as an ongoing developmental process that enables individuals to persist in the face of difficulty and remain engaged in continuous skill acquisition across educational and work-life contexts28,29. Contemporary research, therefore, conceptualizes a growth mindset as a dynamic construct supported by resilience, feedback orientation, effort, motivation, curiosity, and creativity, which together characterize the learning self. Resilience is the adaptive capacity to recover from adversity and maintain sustained engagement in learning and work despite challenging conditions30,13. Closely connected to resilience, feedback functions as a regulatory mechanism that reduces the gap between current performance and learning goals, while feedback orientation captures individuals’ openness to evaluative information and their capacity to use it constructively for improvement31,32. Building on these regulatory processes, effort forms the behavioral foundation of a growth mindset by sustaining engagement beyond initial interest and supporting persistence under cognitive demands, with evidence showing that effort and interest operate complementarily in tasks requiring sustained attention and strategic engagement33. In parallel, motivation shapes goal-directed learning by influencing individuals’ choices and commitment, with both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation supporting self-regulated learning and sustained engagement34,35. Extending this motivational structure, curiosity reflects the positive emotional and motivational dimension of a growth mindset, driving learners’ willingness to explore novelty, engage with emerging technologies, and approach challenging opportunities as learning experiences rather than threats36. Finally, creativity enables individuals to generate novel ideas and adapt knowledge flexibly across contexts, and research shows that inquiry- and creativity-oriented learning environments strengthen innovative thinking by enhancing self-efficacy and openness to experimentation37,38. Building on this behavioral mechanism, Hollaar et al.39 demonstrated that resilience functions as a key adaptive resource that enables individuals to cope effectively with academic and professional stressors by supporting emotional regulation, persistence, and recovery following setbacks. Extending these findings to occupational contexts, Fan et al. (2025) showed that a growth mindset of intelligence is positively associated with occupational well-being, with this relationship operating indirectly through perceived problem-solving ability and career adaptability.

Together, these findings provide a clear theoretical basis for positioning LQ as a strategic capability for adaptation and high performance in the age of AI, where sustainable success increasingly depends on a nurtured capacity for continuous learning. Accordingly, LQ is framed as an integrative, developable capability comprising learning agility, lifelong learning, critical thinking, and growth mindset. These components drive continuous cognitive renewal and sustained professional vitality across educational and professional landscapes. Extending from inquiry and reasoning to resilience and self-regulated learning, LQ functions as a dynamic, metacognitive capacity, cultivated through lifelong learning that enables individuals to remain agile as demands evolve. Although separate instruments assess related constructs, the literature still lacks a comprehensive, integrative scale that holistically captures learnability as a single, unified assessment framework. Beyond this fragmentation, the theoretical basis specifically addressing LQ remains insufficient and has not yet been consistently operationalized as a distinct construct in the empirical literature. This gap represents a critical deficiency in the ability to measure and develop human-centered capabilities; specifically, it obscures how learnability functions as a catalyst for professional adaptation, which is essential for sustaining employability alongside advancing technologies. To address the theoretical gap and measurement deficiency, this study develops a comprehensive LQ framework and assessment model. Through a rigorous scale-development process, the study produces a theoretically anchored instrument that moves beyond fragmented metrics to operationalize learnability as a measurable source of competitive advantage supporting dynamic reskilling, strengthening career mobility, and sustaining professional vitality amid the rapid, technology-driven disruption.

Methodology

Before presenting the technical structure of the proposed model, it is important to clarify the theoretical foundation of the selected indicators. The Learnability Quotient framework adopted in this study is grounded in the assumption that employee adaptability is not merely a reactive mechanism that postpones the negative effects of workplace stress, but rather a proactive capability that enables individuals to interpret, manage, and resolve challenges in real time. In this sense, adaptability is closely linked to sustainable well-being, as employees who can continuously learn, question, and restructure their cognitive processes are better equipped to transform stressful conditions into manageable and meaningful experiences. Therefore, the indicators included in this study, such as mental flexibility, questioning and inquiry, and self-regulated learning, are conceptualized not only as components of adaptation but also as mechanisms that support immediate problem solving and long-term psychological resilience. This perspective allows the model to capture both the short term and sustainable dimensions of employee well-being within dynamic organizational environments. This finding implies that the proposed model produces stable strategy rankings even when different assumptions, weight distributions, or scenario conditions are applied, which strengthens the reliability and credibility of the decision-making outcomes. In practical terms, this stability suggests that the identified priority strategies are not sensitive to minor changes in inputs or expert judgments and therefore can be considered structurally strong choices rather than context dependent or fragile options. From a managerial and policy perspective, such robustness provides decision makers with greater confidence when allocating resources, designing learning interventions, or implementing organizational change initiatives based on these rankings. It reduces the risk of strategic misalignment and supports long term planning by indicating that the selected strategies are likely to remain effective under varying organizational or environmental conditions. Furthermore, the demonstrated validity of the ranking analysis encourages the institutionalization of evidence-based decision frameworks in human resource and learning strategy development, enabling organizations to make more informed, transparent, and resilient strategic choices in the face of continuous change.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the most critical Learnability Quotient components influencing employee adaptability and to determine the most effective strategies for enhancing adaptation under changing conditions. The findings indicate that questioning and inquiry and mental flexibility are the most influential psychological dimensions, highlighting the importance of critical thinking and cognitive adaptability in organizational learning processes. In addition, continuous feedback and reflection programs emerged as the most effective strategy for supporting employee adaptation. These results suggest that adaptability is primarily driven by dynamic cognitive processes and structured learning mechanisms rather than static knowledge or routine practices.

Questioning and inquiry refer to the process of questioning and testing existing knowledge and generating critical questions to acquire new knowledge43. It emerges as the most effective criterion in increasing the effectiveness of Learnability Quotient components. The concept of questioning involves challenging the limits of knowledge with why and how questions, while inquiry encompasses the stages of data collection, evidence generation, and re-evaluation. These two concepts represent an active and deep learning approach rather than passive knowledge acquisition44. In his study, Massonnié45 expressed that one of the motivations in education and learning is continuity. He also mentioned that questioning and inquiry are among the motivations for learning. In another similar study, Wang et al.13 described developing a questionnaire to measure learning strategies. The study concluded that questioning and inquiry are self-regulated learning strategies. The study also stated that questioning and inquiry are negatively correlated with the avoidance of failure. In addition to all this, mental flexibility is one of the most important criteria for increasing the effectiveness of the learnability quotient concept. Without mental flexibility, the learning process often remains superficial46. Furthermore, individuals try to force new information into existing patterns. Indeed, Anderle et al.47 reported that cognitive flexibility is particularly effective in the development of cognitive abilities and the acquisition of learning skills in autistic children. Similarly, in another study, Gharalari et al.48 argued that cognitive flexibility is one of the fundamental factors that increase people’s learning abilities.

Feedback and reflection programs are effective investment strategies for increasing the effectiveness of learnability quotient components. They are defined as practices developed by individuals receiving regular feedback during their learning processes and consciously reflecting on this feedback. This alternative is one of the appropriate alternatives developed for learning speed, which is one of the basic components of LQ. Regular and qualified feedback prevents deviations in the learning process and enables individuals to directly identify their mistakes49. Similarly, Odem et al.50 stated in their article that pharmacy students need feedback programs for self-awareness and professionalism. In another related study, Daly et al.51 emphasized the importance of using feedback applications to improve students’ and teachers’ school skills. Finally, Lee et al.52 worked on self-regulated learning in students’ mathematics learning processes under the leadership of teachers. The study showed that learning conducted in this way had a positive effect on students’ understanding and interpretation of mathematics questions.

While continuous feedback and reflection programs are identified as the most effective strategy in this study, it is important to acknowledge that their impact is highly dependent on implementation quality. Although well-structured feedback systems can enhance learning, reduce uncertainty, and strengthen employee engagement, poorly designed or excessively frequent feedback may lead to feedback fatigue, reduced motivation, and increased psychological pressure. Unbalanced or overly critical feedback can undermine employees’ confidence and contribute to anxiety rather than alleviating it. Therefore, the effectiveness of feedback-based strategies should be understood as conditional rather than absolute. To ensure positive outcomes, organizations should adopt a balanced feedback approach that combines constructive guidance with psychological support, maintains an appropriate frequency, and promotes a safe environment for open communication. This perspective reconciles the positive findings of the study with the potential risks highlighted in the literature and emphasizes the importance of careful implementation in practice.

This study also provides important policy level implications for organizations aiming to strengthen employee adaptability through learnability-oriented strategies. First, organizations should systematically integrate inquiry driven learning mechanisms into their training systems by encouraging employees to question existing processes, critically evaluate information, and actively engage in knowledge construction. This can be operationalized through problem-based learning environments, scenario driven workshops, and cross functional project assignments that stimulate analytical thinking. Second, enhancing mental flexibility requires organizational structures that expose employees to diverse experiences, such as job rotation, interdisciplinary teamwork, and dynamic role assignments. These practices enable employees to move beyond rigid cognitive patterns and develop adaptive thinking capabilities. Third, the prominence of continuous feedback and reflection programs suggests that organizations should institutionalize structured feedback systems supported by digital platforms, real time performance analytics, and reflective learning sessions. Such systems not only improve learning efficiency but also foster self-regulated learning and long-term capability development. Finally, policymakers and organizational leaders should align human resource development strategies with learnability-based indicators by embedding these competencies into performance evaluation, promotion criteria, and leadership development programs. This alignment ensures that adaptability is not treated as an abstract concept but as a measurable and manageable strategic priority.

Conclusion

This study aimed to identify the most critical strategies for enhancing employees’ ability to adapt to changing conditions and to determine the most influential Learnability Quotient components shaping this process. To achieve this objective, a novel fuzzy multi criteria decision making framework was proposed by integrating spherical fuzzy sets with the newly developed COWEB weighting technique and the MUNRA ranking approach. Based on expert evaluations, the analysis results revealed that questioning and inquiry and mental flexibility are the most important learnability indicators, highlighting the central role of critical thinking and cognitive adaptability in employee learning. In addition, the stability of strategy rankings across all scenarios confirmed the robustness and validity of the proposed model. From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to the literature by addressing a significant research gap related to the prioritization of learnability-based strategies and indicators, while also offering an original methodological integration that enhances decision making under uncertainty.

This study offers a clear conceptual and methodological advancement by explicitly operationalizing Learnability Quotient as a measurable and multi-dimensional construct within a decision-making framework. Unlike existing studies that examine learning agility, adaptability, or related constructs in isolation, this research integrates cognitive, behavioral, and motivational dimensions into a unified structure and evaluates them simultaneously through a fuzzy multi criteria approach. In doing so, the study addresses a critical gap in the literature concerning the lack of systematic prioritization of learnability components and adaptation strategies under uncertainty. Furthermore, the introduction of the COWEB technique contributes to the methodological literature by providing a more consistent and cognitively grounded weighting mechanism compared to traditional approaches. Building on these contributions, future research can extend the proposed framework by incorporating longitudinal data to capture dynamic changes in learnability over time, applying the model across different industries and cultural contexts to enhance generalizability, and integrating artificial intelligence-based decision support systems to automate and scale the evaluation process. Additionally, hybrid models combining behavioral data and expert judgment could further improve the robustness and practical applicability of learnability-based decision making frameworks.

Despite its contributions, this study has several theoretical and methodological limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on expert judgments, which may introduce subjectivity despite the use of fuzzy modeling techniques. Second, the proposed model focuses on a predefined set of strategies and Learnability Quotient criteria, which may limit its generalizability to different organizational contexts or industries. Moreover, while the integration of the COWEB and MUNRA methods provides methodological advantages, the model’s complexity may restrict its practical implementation in organizations with limited analytical capabilities. Future studies may address these limitations by expanding the number and diversity of experts, incorporating empirical performance data, and comparing the proposed model with alternative decision-making frameworks. Additionally, future research could adapt the model to different sectors or extend it by integrating dynamic or longitudinal analyses to capture changes in learnability and adaptation over time.
