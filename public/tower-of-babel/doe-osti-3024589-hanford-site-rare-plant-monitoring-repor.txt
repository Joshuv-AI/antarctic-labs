 1.0 INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE

This report summarizes rare plant monitoring data collected in calendar year (CY) 2025 and
provides management recommendations accordingly. DOE/RL-2021-35, Central Hanford Rare
Plant Management Plan, guides the approach to rare plant monitoring and management at the
Hanford Site.
In CY 2025, rare plant monitoring efforts occurred on the portion of the Hanford Site managed
by the U.S. Department of Energy, Hanford Field Office (HFO; Figure 1-1), referred to herein as
Central Hanford. The goal of monitoring is to collect data to evaluate the conservation status of
rare plant species. Surveys conducted in CY 2025 built on previous monitoring efforts, tracking
the abundance and distribution of rare plants at Central Hanford. Results from previous
monitoring efforts are included for reference. Monitoring data are submitted to the Washington
Natural Heritage Program (WNHP), part of the Washington State Department of Natural
Resources (WA DNR), to evaluate statewide conservation statuses.

The term “rare plant” refers to any plant taxon with a state and/or federal conservation listing.
Data was not collected for federally listed plants protected under the Endangered Species Act
of 1973 in CY 2025, as these are monitored under other programs. State conservation status is
determined by the WNHP (Natural Heritage Report 2024-07, 2024 Washington Vascular Plant
Species). Factors considered in determining state conservation status include abundance,
occurrence patterns, vulnerability, threats, existing protection, and taxonomic distinctness. State
rankings include the following:
 • Endangered: In danger of becoming extinct or extirpated from Washington State
 • Threatened: Likely to become endangered in Washington State.
 • Sensitive: Vulnerable or declining and could become threatened or endangered in
 Washington State.
 • Review List 1: Species for which more information is needed before a conservation
 status can be assigned.
 • Review List 2: Species with unresolved taxonomic questions.

Throughout this report, the terms “native,” “non-native,” “invasive,” “exotic,” and “noxious
weed” are used to describe plant species:
 • Native species are generally considered to be those that evolved in a particular region
 without substantial influence from human activity.
 • Non-native species are those that were relatively recently introduced to a particular
 region outside of their native range, often directly or indirectly as a result of human
 activity. Non-native is a catch all term that includes invasive, exotic, and noxious species.
 • Invasive refers to non-native species with potential to grow aggressively, causing
 ecological or economic harm in their introduced range.
 • Exotic species are non-native species with little to no potential to cause harm in their
 introduced range.
 • Noxious weed is a legal designation given to some non-native species with requirements
 for control at the state level by the Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board
 (Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board; NWCB 2021).
The native, invasive, or exotic status is assigned by the WA Invasive Ranking System (WA DNR
2025) The taxonomy used in this report follows plant names found in Flora of the Pacific
Northwest: An Illustrated Manual (Hitchcock 2018), unless updated taxonomy is available from
another source.
The term “occurrence” is used throughout this report, referring to spatially distinct locations
where rare plants exist currently or historically. The term refers to rare plant sites that are
currently part of the WNHP rare plant database and to newly recorded rare plant locations that
were submitted for inclusion in the WNHP rare plant database.
The purpose of this report is to provide the CY 2025 ecological monitoring results at Central
Hanford to ensure compliance with environmental laws and regulations governing. Ecological
monitoring data provides baseline information about the plants, animals, and habitat under HFO
stewardship at the Hanford Site necessary for decision making under the National Environmental
Policy Act of 1969 (NEPA) and the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation,
and Liability Act of 1980. DOE/EIS-0222-F, Final Hanford Comprehensive Land-Use Plan
Environmental Impact Statement, helps ensure that HFO, its contractors, and other entities
conducting activities on the Hanford Site comply with the NEPA.
DOE/RL-96-32, Hanford Site Biological Resources Management Plan, is identified by
DOE/EIS-0222-F as the primary implementation control for managing and protecting natural
resources on the Hanford Site. According to DOE/EIS-0222-F, DOE/RL-96-32:
 …provides a mechanism for ensuring compliance with laws protecting
 biological resources; provides a framework for ensuring that appropriate
 biological resource goals, objectives, and tools are in place to make DOE an
 effective steward of the Hanford biological resources; and implements an
 ecosystem management approach for biological resources on the site. The
 BRMP provides a comprehensive direction that specifies DOE biological
 resource policies, goals, and objectives.

 1-4

HFO, under the guidance of DOE/RL-96-32, places priority on monitoring biological resources
(i.e., plant and animal species and habitats) with specific regulatory protections or requirements
that are rare and/or declining (e.g., federal- or state-listed endangered, threatened, or sensitive
species) or are of significant interest to federal, state, or Tribal governments or the public.
Rare plant monitoring on the Hanford Site is guided by DOE/RL-96-32, with the purpose of
providing a consistent approach to rare plant management. DOE/RL-96-32 ranks natural
resources (Levels 0 to 5), providing a graded approach to monitoring biological resources based
on the level of concern for each resource. Washington State endangered and threatened species
are categorized as Level 4 resources and are considered essential resources by DOE/RL-96-32,
requiring a high level of status monitoring. Washington State sensitive species are categorized as
Level 3 resources and are considered important resources by DOE/RL-96-32, requiring a
moderate level of status monitoring. Rare plant populations are dynamic and require consistent
monitoring to accurately track distributions and to inform ongoing Hanford Site activities.

 2.0 MONITORING METHODS

DOE/RL-2021-35 provides guidelines for consistent monitoring methods, resulting in reliable
long-term datasets. Consistent monitoring methods are necessary for evaluating the conservation
status and population dynamics of rare plant populations over time. This monitoring effort aligns
with guidance in DOE/RL-2021-35.
Surveys targeted known rare plant occurrences and potential habitat in the immediate
surrounding area and were carried out on foot by surveyors aiming to achieve full coverage of
the target area. New rare plant occurrences and expansions to existing occurrences were mapped
using a handheld Global Positioning System (GPS). Small populations were counted and mapped
with GPS points. Larger populations were counted at representative portions to achieve an
overall estimate and were mapped with GPS polygons. Habitat observations were recorded,
including photos, community species composition, and potential threats. No herbarium
specimens were collected during CY 2025 surveys. Multiple field visits coincided with the
timing of phenology of target species. Targeted surveys occurred periodically in April through
June 2025.
Other monitoring efforts recorded incidental encounters with rare plants. Surveyors collected
GPS coordinates for each new rare plant occurrence at the approximate center of each
occurrence. Surveyors recorded the species, estimates of the number of plants or number of
stems, and the approximate area covered.
Rare plant monitoring data was recorded in the Hanford Rare Plant Database. A Washington
Natural Heritage Program Rare Plant Sighting Form was completed for each targeted survey
and new occurrence and submitted to the WNHP.

 3.0 RESULTS

This section summarizes results from targeted surveys and incidental encounters from CY 2025.
Previous monitoring data are included for reference. Precise location data, including points and
polygons, are not provided due to the sensitive nature of these species. Requests can be made
with the WNHP to obtain specific location data. Historical population numbers for each
occurrence are provided when available. Population numbers are not provided for incidental
encounters since full surveys were not conducted.
An assessment of conservation status is provided for each occurrence based on:
 • Most recent observation
 • Total number of surveys on years with above-average precipitation
 • Population trends
 • Habitat degradation.
The presence of a rare plant at one or more occurrences during the season indicates germination
conditions were suitable for that species. Absences at other known occurrences for that species
may indicate an actual decline due to other factors, such as habitat loss. Population numbers may
vary widely between years and are correlated with seasonal weather patterns, so typically, only
large changes in population numbers for an occurrence are considered to represent a trend. The
site potential is also considered when evaluating trends in populations.
The presumed status at Central Hanford is evaluated on a case-by-case basis, but general
guidelines are used, such as the following:
 • Unknown if fewer than three surveys have been conducted during adequate precipitation
 years since the occurrence was initially recorded.
 • Unknown if the population numbers vary widely up and down between monitoring years.
 • Declining if the occurrence has been surveyed three or more times on adequate
 precipitation years across a 10-year span and large decreases in the population were
 recorded, and habitat degradation was recorded.
 • Extirpated if the occurrence has been surveyed five or more times since it was last
 detected on adequate precipitation years across a 20-year span and was not detected.

3.1.2   Status

Overall, the status of sand gilia at Central Hanford appears to be declining. Continued
monitoring is necessary to fully evaluate its status.
The primary threat to sand gilia is competition with cheatgrass (Bromus tectorum) and other
invasive species. Other disturbances, such as road maintenance and herbicide drift, may threaten
occurrences near roads. Altered wildfire regimes generally threaten native plant species;
however, the long-term impacts of fire on sand gilia are uncertain.
The taxonomy of this species is unresolved, and it is known to hybridize with Lott’s gily-flower
(Porter 1998), a species found across Central Hanford. Plants possessing characteristics
intermediate with Lott’s gily-flower have been observed at the Federal Avenue occurrence and
the black sands area. A number of plants were noted to have purple corolla lobes with a single
distinctly asymmetrical lobe, notched on one side as in sand gilia. These unusual plants co-occur
with typical Lott’s gily flower in these locations. For conservation purposes, potential hybrid
populations should be treated as rare; however, they are not counted here to avoid confusion with
morphologically distinct sand gilia populations.

 4.0 DISCUSSION

The status of rare plant species at Central Hanford varies from unknown, stable, declining, to
potentially extirpated. Monitoring in CY 2025 built on previous monitoring efforts to improve
baseline information on the distribution and population trends of rare plants to evaluate their
conservation statuses at Central Hanford. Incidental encounters with rare plants during other
monitoring efforts also provided valuable rare plant data in CY 2025. Monitoring results from
CY 2025 highlight trends that can be used to inform management decisions.
Rare plants can be categorized by differences in life history: annual vs. perennial and riparian vs.
upland. Categorizations such as these help interpret patterns recorded during monitoring. Upland
annuals represent a distinct category of rare plants vulnerable to a particular set of threats with
unique monitoring considerations; therefore, they are high-priority targets for status monitoring,
and a focus of discussion.

Climate and Weather

Upland annuals depend on specific weather patterns for germination, making it challenging to
detect and evaluate the status of these species. While differences exist in the specific germination
triggers for each of these species, above-average precipitation is necessary to trigger germination
for most of them. The influence of temperature and the precise weather patterns required to
trigger germination for these species are unknown, but historical monitoring data provides
insight into favorable weather conditions. Figure 4-1 shows a comparison of cumulative
precipitation throughout water years 1997, 2016, 2025, and the average from 1948 to 2025
(HMS 2025). The metric “cumulative water year” is a useful method to consider because
precipitation falling throughout the winter and spring has the greatest influence on early spring
upland annual germination. High precipitation conditions in CY 1997 triggered germination of
large flushes of rare upland annuals; in some cases, with population numbers that were orders of
magnitude above anything recorded since.
In CY 2025, conditions were favorable for rare upland annuals. Higher rates of germination were
observed relative to other recent years. Certain occurrences were recorded for the first time in
many years; for example, CY 2025 was the first time foxtail mousetail was detected at a vernal
pool on Central Hanford in 30 years. Results from CY 2025 support the idea that increasing
precipitation is correlated with increasing rare plant germination.

 Figure 4-1. Cumulative Water Year Precipitation Data.

Population Decline

Variability in annual precipitation does not fully explain the trends recorded for rare upland
annual populations. The precipitation conditions in CY 1997 have not been replicated since, so it
is not possible to make a direct comparison between the population levels recorded then to the
those recorded now. However, CY 2025 population declines and absent populations were
recorded at a higher number of occurrences compared to CY 2016 data. The precipitation in
CY 2016 was slightly above average, but lower than the precipitation in CY 2025. This indicates
that real declines have occurred and precipitation is not the only factor influencing population
trends. Additionally, the presence of a species at some occurrences and not others indicates that
conditions were favorable for germination during the CY 2025 season, and other factors may be
responsible for driving declines and absences. It is possible that if precipitation levels were to
reach that of CY 1997 on another year, it could trigger large flushes of rare upland annual
species across Central Hanford, potentially in occurrences that are presumed extirpated.

 4-2

However, degradation from cheatgrass and other invasive species may be reducing the available
habitat for rare annuals, causing populations to decline and potential extirpations at some
occurrences.

Invasive Species

In CY 2025, low to high levels of degradation from cheatgrass and other invasive species were
observed at a majority of rare upland annual occurrences, threatening rare upland annual species
(Figure 4-2). Cheatgrass was introduced to the region around 100 years ago and has dramatically
increased in abundance since then, fundamentally altering vegetation composition. As of CY 2017,
areas containing cheatgrass as a major component of the understory encompassed approximately
65 percent of the Hanford Site (HNF-61417, Upland Vegetation of the Central Hanford Site). As
of 2025, a study of vegetation composition across Central Hanford found cheatgrass cover above
17.5 percent at 97.0 percent of sites, and cheatgrass cover over 62.5 percent at 75.0 percent of
sites (HNF-72996).

 Figure 4-2. Photo-Point 1320 from Calendar Years 2017 (left) and 2025 (right).
The increase in wildfires across Central Hanford has resulted in increases in cover for cheatgrass
and other invasive species (“Trajectories of change in sagebrush steppe vegetation communities
in relation to multiple wildfires,” Davies et al. 2012; “Living with exotic annual grasses in the
sagebrush ecosystem,” Davies et al. 2021), as well as other vegetation composition changes across
the site. Some habitats associated with rare plants, such as vernal pools and swales, may be
especially vulnerable to invasion by cheatgrass and other invasive species due to additional
moisture (Cheatgrass Invasion: History, Causes, Consequences, and Solutions; Molvar et al.
2024). It is likely that high-invasive species cover adversely affects rare upland annuals through
habitat loss, competition for resources and canopy space, allelopathic interaction, and alteration of
soil conditions.
Categorizing rare plants by habitat type is another useful distinction for assessing threats to their
stability. Rare plants reliant on active sand dune habitat are vulnerable to dune stabilization
through invasive species. The rate of dune stabilization at Central Hanford is unknown, but it
was observed to be a widespread source of degradation during ecological integrity assessments
(HNF-72996), mirroring the statewide trend of dune stabilization (NHR 2015-04). Dune
stabilization occurs naturally; however, habitat is degraded and stabilization accelerated by
cheatgrass and other invasive species. Active dunes are not refreshed at the historically higher

 4-3

rate due to land use change in the surrounding area. The decline of the Active and Stabilized
Dune ecological system should be taken into consideration in conservation plans for these
species.

Altered Fire Regime and Climate Impacts

The threat of altered fire regimes to rare plant species is variable by habitat, soil type, and life
history. In general, some habitats and soil types associated with rare plants can be more resilient,
including sandy soils, lithosols, and basalt cliff habitats. Other habitats are more vulnerable, such
as vernal pools, swales, mature sagebrush steppe, and loamy soils. The specific interactions
between wildfire and rare plants at Central Hanford needs more research. In general, altered fire
regimes and habitat loss appear to be impacting native and rare plants. Continued monitoring is
necessary to understand the long-term effects of altered fire regimes on upland rare plant species.
Rare upland annuals may be uniquely impacted by wildfire. Monitoring results show that these
species may benefit from wildfire in the short term but be harmed in the long term. On two
occasions after wildfire germination flushes were recorded within the first two seasons post-fire.
Two years after the 2020 Gable Mountain fire, a flush of Suksdorf’s monkey flower was
recorded north of Gable Mountain. One year after the May 2024 Umtanum Ridge/McGee Ranch
fire, a flush of small-flowered mooncup was recorded in an area where it was not previously
known to occur (Figure 4-3). Both years coincided with above-average precipitation. Wildfire, in
the short term, greatly reduces cheatgrass cover, providing bare ground and canopy openings
needed by rare annuals. However, in the long-term wildfire is likely driving declines and
possibly local extinctions of rare upland annuals. After 1 to 3 years when cheatgrass cover is
reduced post-burn, cheatgrass generally achieves dominance, often reaching higher cover level
than were present pre-burn (HNF-72995, Hanford Site Post-Fire Vegetation Monitoring Report
for Calendar Year 2025). Monitoring should be conducted immediately post-burn and continued
routinely to better understand the impacts of wildfire on rare plants.

In general, anthropogenic climate change is expected to adversely affect many native plant
species (Climate Change Impacts and Adaptation in Washington State: Technical Summaries for
Decision Makers; Snover et al. 2013). The potential impacts of climate change on rare plant
species at Central Hanford are not well understood. The Pacific Northwest has experienced

0.7°C (1.3°F) of warming over the past century, which has resulted in changes to the water
cycling and seasonal temperatures. Future climate change is expected to cause increases in
average annual temperature, expansion of the frost-free season (growing season), increased
extreme precipitation events, and a reduction in snowpack, further altering water cycling
(Snover et al. 2013). Continued monitoring of rare plants at Central Hanford is essential for
determining conservation strategies addressing predicted climate outcomes.

 5.0 MANAGEMENT RECOMMENDATIONS

A consistent and thorough monitoring program is essential for documenting the status and
conservation of rare plants at Central Hanford. Species with unknown status and high
conservation ranking should be prioritized for monitoring. Occurrences that are presumed
extirpated should still be considered as high-value resource areas, and monitoring should
continue for these occurrences to determine if the presumed status at Central Hanford is accurate.
In addition to baseline rare plant monitoring, further study of habitat loss from cheatgrass and
other invasive species is recommended. Cheatgrass encroachment is a major threat to rare plant
habitat across Central Hanford. Tracking vegetation composition trends helps to inform
management strategies to address this threat. Occurrences in habitats particularly sensitive to
cheatgrass invasion should be prioritized for vegetation composition surveys, including vernal
pools and swales. Quantifying threats to rare plant species and occurrences is necessary to fully
evaluate their status.
Ongoing management of noxious weeds at and surrounding rare plant occurrences is
recommended. The specific interactions between noxious weeds and rare plants are not well
understood. Noxious weeds are known to be aggressive and harmful competitors, presumably
threatening rare plants. Noxious weed infestations encountered during rare plant monitoring are
reported to the Hanford Mission Integration Solutions, LLC Noxious Weed Program. Several
noxious weed infestations are identified as urgent threats to rare plants and sensitive habitat.
Infestation of perennial pepperweed (Lepidium latifolium, Class B) and yellow star-thistle
(Centaurea solstitialis, Class B) were recorded at the Vernita Grade and Gable Butte vernal pool
areas. These noxious weeds have high potential to spread to high-quality habitats and further
degrade the quality of native habitats and impacting native species.
Recent monitoring reports highlight the value of a robust rare plant monitoring program. Every
year since CY 2022, new rare plant occurrences have been documented at Central Hanford. This
indicates that there are still many undocumented rare plant occurrences at Central Hanford.
Without an accurate understanding of resource distributions, it is not possible to make informed
management decisions. Exploratory surveys in suitable habitat are needed to locate
undocumented rare plant occurrences. Additionally, rare plant conservation listings are updated
periodically, requiring changes to the value of these resources under the DOE/RL-92-32
framework and monitoring to map occurrences for newly listed species. Some species and
occurrences require more frequent monitoring, or monitoring to evaluate their status at Central
Hanford, and these should be prioritized for future monitoring.

Management Recommendations Summary

 • Treat noxious weeds at rare plant occurrences.
 • Survey rare upland annuals on years with above-average precipitation.
 • Prioritize surveys for the occurrences and species listed as status unknown in this report.
 • Prioritize surveys for newly listed species that may be impacted by Hanford project
 activities, such as Piper’s daisy (Erigeron piperianus) and Bailey’s buckwheat
 (Eriogonum baileyi)
