Recurrent and unusual infections unmasking a rare inborn error of immunity: a case report RAS-associated Autoimmune Lymphoproliferative Disease (RALD)
Authors: Mohamad Qazreen Ahmad Shawaludin, Mohd Anas Che Nik, Zarina Thasneem Zainudeen, Fahisham Taib, Intan Juliana Abd Hamid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRAS-associated Autoimmune Lymphoproliferative Disease (RALD) is a rare, non-malignant lymphoproliferative disorder caused by somatic mutations in RAS genes that impair lymphocyte apoptosis. Patients often present with features of lymphoproliferation, autoimmune manifestations, and an increased risk of malignant transformation.Case presentationWe report the case of a 3-year-old boy with splenic microabscesses and Burkholderia pseudomallei IgM serology positivity, who was treated as melioidosis. He had recurrent respiratory infections and chronic rhinorrhoea since the age of one year. Clinical examination revealed persistent lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory investigations demonstrated persistent absolute monocytosis and thrombocytopenia, while immunological evaluation showed elevated IgM levels and B-cell lymphocytosis. Whole exome sequencing identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in NRAS, NM_002524.5:c.35G > C, NP_002515.1:p.Gly12Ala, establishing the diagnosis of RALD. The patient was subsequently started on antibiotic prophylaxis and immunoglobulin replacement therapy, and his family was counseled regarding the potential role of immunosuppressive therapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in future management.ConclusionsThis case highlights the coexistence of an unusual infection with a rare inborn error of immunity, expanding the recognized infectious spectrum of RALD. It underscores the importance of considering RALD in patients presenting with recurrent or atypical infections and persistent lymphoproliferative features. Early recognition and molecular genetic testing are essential for confirming the diagnosis and guiding individualized management.

Background

RAS-associated Autoimmune Lymphoproliferative Disease (RALD) is a rare disorder of immune dysregulation that shares clinical features with Autoimmune Lymphoproliferative Syndrome (ALPS) but is considered a distinct entity and is often described as a phenocopy rather than a subtype of ALPS [1, 2]. It is a chronic, non-malignant lymphoproliferative condition characterized by defective lymphocyte apoptosis. The disorder results from somatic mutations in the NRAS and KRAS genes, which encode RAS proteins involved in regulating cell proliferation, leading to impairment of the intrinsic apoptosis pathway [3, 4]. Epidemiologically, RALD is extremely rare, with an estimated prevalence of fewer than 1 in 1,000,000 worldwide, typically presenting in childhood. To date, the European Society for Immunodeficiencies Registry (ESID-R) has reported 30 cases of phenocopies of inborn errors of immunity (IEI) associated with autoantibodies and/or somatic variants, comprising 20 with TNFRSF6, 6 with NRAS, and 4 with KRAS mutations [1, 5]. A review of 27 patients with RALD demonstrated a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1:1, with a median age of onset of 2 years. The earliest reported presentation occurred at 9 months of age in patients with NRAS mutations, whereas those with KRAS mutations typically presented at around 3 years of age [3]. The clinical presentation of RALD is variable and may include cytopenia, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, hypergammaglobulinemia, autoimmune disease, and a markedly increased lifetime risk of lymphoma [2–4]. Persistent absolute or relative monocytosis is considered a cardinal feature of the disease. In addition, peripheral blood and bone marrow findings may overlap with those seen in juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML) [6]. The management of RALD involves a combination of supportive care, targeted therapies, and genetic counselling. Treatment is primarily focused on controlling autoimmune symptoms and addressing potential complications, while hematopoietic cell transplantation remains the only curative option [3, 4].

This case highlights a unique combination of an unusual infectious presentation with a rare inborn error of immunity, underscoring the expansion of the infection spectrum in RALD. Furthermore, emphasis on early recognition of RALD is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and to guide appropriate therapeutic and follow-up strategies.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old Siamese boy was referred to the immunology clinic for evaluation of a suspected inborn error of immunity in view of persistent hepatosplenomegaly, recurrent infections, and hypergammaglobulinemia. He is the child of non-consanguineous parents and has one healthy sibling. He was born at term with no significant antenatal or postnatal events. His immunizations were up to date per the national immunization schedule, without complications. There is no significant family history of autoimmune disease, inborn errors of immunity, or malignancy. He remained well until the age of one year, after which he experienced recurrent hospital admissions for respiratory tract infections and chronic rhinorrhoea requiring antibiotics at the district hospital. Each episode was treated as pneumonia, and monthly clinic visits were held for upper respiratory tract infections. During these admissions, persistent hepatosplenomegaly and transaminitis were noted. Screening for infectious, hepatic, and metabolic causes was negative. Gamma-glutamyl transferase and total bilirubin were within normal limits. Abdominal ultrasound at that time revealed isolated splenomegaly with a normal liver span and no focal lesions. He was subsequently discharged from follow-up. At the age of two years, he presented to our center with respiratory distress following an upper respiratory tract infection. Laboratory evaluation showed leucocytosis, cytopenia, and elevated C-reactive protein. Nasopharyngeal aspirates for viruses were all negative. Viral and Mycoplasma studies were negative. Peripheral blood film demonstrated leucocytosis with a left shift. Persistent hepatosplenomegaly was again noted. He improved with intravenous antibiotics and was discharged. However, he was re-admitted with severe respiratory distress, rapid breathing, high-grade fever, and productive cough after one week. Examination revealed tachypnoea, moderate chest recession, and marked hepatosplenomegaly. He required high-flow oxygen therapy. He had persistent leucocytosis, cytopenia, and elevated C-reactive protein. Intravenous cefotaxime was commenced, but despite treatment, he continued to have persistent spiking fever and worsening hepatosplenomegaly. A repeat abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatomegaly and splenomegaly with multiple splenic microabscesses. Burkholderia pseudomallei (melioidosis) IgM serology was positive (1:320), while blood culture, fungal culture, and galactomannan assay were negative. The diagnosis was revised to melioidosis with splenic microabscesses, and the case was managed in consultation with the Paediatric Infectious Disease team. He completed a 15-week antibiotic regimen, consisting of 3 weeks of intravenous ceftazidime followed by 12 weeks of oral amoxicillin-clavulanate, with good clinical recovery and resolution of the microabscess (Table 1). Further evaluation demonstrated persistent monocytosis, elevated IgM levels, and elevated absolute B cells on lymphocyte subset enumeration (Table 2). Whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in NRAS, NM_002524.5:c.35G > C, NP_002515.1:p.Gly12Ala; establishing the diagnosis of RAS-associated autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (RALD). The patient was subsequently commenced on lifelong immunoglobulin replacement therapy (0.4 g/kg) via intravenous route (IVIG), administered every four weeks, together with oral bactrim prophylaxis. Following initiation of IVIG, his hospital admissions were markedly reduced, and he demonstrated good catch-up growth with overall clinical improvement, particularly in terms of infection control. The parents were counseled regarding the potential role of immunosuppressive therapy (sirolimus) and the option of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation as a definitive treatment. Genetic counseling was provided to the parents. Further genetic investigations in the parents were not performed due to financial constraints and their asymptomatic status. Targeted variant testing was performed on the patient’s sibling (the youngest sister) during early infancy and was negative.

Discussion

We reported a case of a three-year-old boy presenting with splenic microabscesses and positive Burkholderia pseudomallei serology, hepatosplenomegaly, persistent monocytosis, hypergammaglobulinemia, and B-cell lymphocytosis. The genetic testing confirmed a heterozygous NRAS mutation consistent with RALD. The combination of an unusual infectious presentation and a rare inborn error of immunity makes this case noteworthy and provides valuable insights into the expanding infectious spectrum of RALD.

Burkholderia pseudomallei is a Gram-negative, facultative intracellular bacterium that inhabits the rhizosphere of tropical soils. It is the causative agent of melioidosis, which is acquired through cutaneous inoculation, ingestion, or inhalation of contaminated soil or water [7, 8]. The disease is highly endemic in Southeast Asia and northern Australia, with incidence peaking during the wet season. Reported pediatric annual incidence rates in endemic areas range from 4.1 to 35 cases per 100,000 population. B. pseudomallei infection presents with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, including pneumonia, splenic abscesses, skin and soft tissue infections, osteoarticular disease, genitourinary involvement, and central nervous system infection. Annan et al. [9] reported that the lungs were the most commonly affected site (50%), followed by intra-abdominal organs such as the spleen (53%) and liver (7%) [7, 10]. To date, no case of B. pseudomallei infection has been reported in association with RALD. Most documented cases of melioidosis related to primary immunodeficiency have occurred in patients with Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD), underscoring the essential role of the NADPH oxidase system in host defense against intracellular pathogens [11, 12]. Less frequently, B. pseudomallei infection has been described in the context of IEIs (Table 3) that affect cellular immunity, particularly defects in the IFN-γ/IL-12 signaling pathway or mutations in STAT1 and STAT3, both of which impair immune responses to intracellular bacteria [13].

In contrast, RALD has not traditionally been associated with Burkholderia infection. The present case suggests that immune dysregulation driven by aberrant RAS/MAPK signaling, potentially affecting monocyte/macrophage function and intracellular bacterial clearance, may represent a previously unrecognized mechanism of susceptibility. We further hypothesize that neutropenia and monocyte/macrophage dysfunction related to aberrant NRAS signaling, along with impaired phagocyte function, including oxidative burst and intracellular killing, contribute to this vulnerability. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, these factors may predispose patients with RALD to intracellular-pathogen infections, such as those caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, thereby expanding the recognized infectious spectrum of this condition, particularly in endemic regions.

RALD is a rare, non-malignant lymphoproliferative disorder caused by somatic mutations in the RAS genes. It was initially classified as type 4 ALPS but was redefined in 2009 as a distinct entity [2, 3]. The RAS family, including HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS, plays a central role in intracellular signaling pathways regulating cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis. Mutations in these genes are implicated in up to 30% of malignancies, such as melanoma, lung, pancreatic, and bladder cancers, as well as acute myeloid leukemia [17]. Beyond tumorigenesis, germline and somatic mutations in RAS genes are also associated with RASopathies and altered immune signaling, predisposing affected individuals to autoimmunity. Evidence suggests that somatic mutations in hematopoietic lineage cells, particularly involving codons 12 and 13, are strongly associated with RALD [6]. In our patient, features of RASopathies such as dysmorphic facies, skeletal anomalies, or cardiac defects were absent, making a germline NRAS mutation unlikely. Instead, his presentation was dominated by lymphoproliferative and autoimmune manifestations, consistent with RALD.

RALD is exceedingly rare, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 1,000,000 and a near-equal sex distribution. A review of 27 cases has been reported, with a median age at onset of around 2 years. Genotype–phenotype correlations have been described, patients with NRAS mutations typically present earlier (median 9 months), whereas those with KRAS mutations present later (median 3 years) [3]. The clinical spectrum usually includes lymphoproliferation and autoimmunity, although recurrent infections have also been described, suggesting an underlying immunodeficiency. Its presentation often overlaps with disorders such as ALPS, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML) (Table 4). While RALD and JMML share RAS mutations and myeloproliferative features, JMML cells typically harbor additional oncogenic mutations that drive malignant progression. Importantly, some RALD patients may later evolve into JMML. Across published cohorts, cytopenia, splenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy are the most frequent initial findings [3, 6, 18, 19]. Neven et al. emphasized autoimmunity as a central feature, particularly autoimmune cytopenias such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), which are often associated with autoantibodies and a positive direct Coombs test [3].

Most reported cases of RALD exhibit monocytosis and hypergammaglobulinemia, with variable levels of double-negative T cells. While monocytosis is a recognized cardinal feature, lymphocyte subset abnormalities, including B-cell expansion, have also been described but are less consistently observed. Studies suggest that both B and T lymphocytes in RALD can undergo clonal expansion, with progressive restriction of antigen receptor diversity. Immunophenotypically, B-cell expansion often involves immature or transitional subsets, characterized by increased CD10++, CD19+, CD20+, and CD38 + B cells, and may manifest clinically as peripheral B-cell lymphocytosis [20, 21]. In our case, the marked B-cell lymphocytosis likely reflects immune dysregulation associated with aberrant NRAS signaling; however, the extent of expansion is not uniformly reported and may represent phenotypic variability. ANA and anti-dsDNA positivity, along with low complement levels, are also common and can lead to diagnostic confusion with SLE [3, 19]. Our patient demonstrated persistent monocytosis from initial presentation, accompanied by lymphoproliferative features and B-cell lymphocytosis, consistent with a highly suggestive profile of RALD.

Codon 12 mutations are well-recognized mutational hotspots, accounting for up to ~ 35% of NRAS mutations, and impair GTP hydrolysis, resulting in constitutive activation of the RAS protein in its GTP-bound state [22]. The identified NRAS variant NM_002524.5:c.35G > C, p.(Gly12Ala) alters a canonical GTP-binding hotspot (codon 12) and is predicted to abrogate intrinsic GTPase activity, thereby favoring a constitutively active, GTP-bound NRAS that chronically stimulates downstream MAPK/PI3K signalling cascades [23]. This specific substitution (G12A) has been catalogued in ClinVar with evaluations linking it to hematologic neoplasia and is classified as likely pathogenic in submitted records, supporting its functional and clinical relevance [24]. While codon 12 NRAS alterations are most frequently reported as somatic oncogenic drivers, germline and mosaic NRAS variants have been described in the spectrum of RASopathies and in immune-dysregulatory phenotypes, underscoring that NRAS activation can produce both developmental and immune-proliferative consequences depending on timing and cellular context [24]. Notably, NRAS-activating mutations have been implicated in RALD, a disorder characterized by lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, autoimmune cytopenias, and a predisposition to hematologic malignancy features that overlap with the clinical phenotype in our patient and lend pathogenic plausibility to the variant [24]. Taken together, the molecular location, established mechanism of action for codon-12 RAS mutants, ClinVar classification, and the recognized clinical syndromes associated with NRAS activation all support the conclusion that NM_002524.5:c.35G > C (p.Gly12Ala) is a clinically relevant, likely pathogenic variant in the context of immune dysregulation and hematologic diseases [18]. It is important to note that although the p.Gly12Ala variant typically indicates somatic mosaicism in RALD, a germline RASopathy cannot be definitively excluded without testing non-hematopoietic tissue.

The optimal management of RALD remains undefined. Corticosteroids are considered first-line therapy for autoimmune manifestations, while mTOR inhibitors and other immunosuppressants have shown variable success in controlling cytopenia and autoimmunity [3, 6]. Immunoglobulin replacement therapy is beneficial in patients with recurrent infections or impaired antibody responses. The only potential curative option is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). However, given the generally non-malignant course of RALD, many experts advise against HSCT unless there is progression or severe disease [6]. Lifelong monitoring is essential, particularly for malignant transformation or molecular evolution that could alter the disease trajectory.

There are several limitations to this case report that warrant consideration. First, while the p.Gly12Ala NRAS variant was identified via Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) of peripheral blood, its somatic nature was not definitively confirmed through the analysis of non-hematopoietic tissues, such as skin fibroblasts or hair follicles. Furthermore, we did not perform lineage-specific cell sorting; consequently, the variant allele frequency (VAF) across specific leukocyte subsets; including granulocytes, monocytes, and lymphocytes remains uncharacterized. While the clinical phenotype and the specific nature of the NRAS mutation are highly consistent with RALD, the lack of multi-tissue testing precludes the absolute exclusion of a germline RASopathy. Additionally, regarding the therapeutic interventions, the justification for IVIG administration was primarily clinical. As IgG subclass levels and specific antibody responses to vaccination were not measured, the presence of a functional antibody deficiency could not be formally established. Future cases would benefit from functional oxidative burst assays and deep sequencing across various cell lineages to more precisely map the immunopathological landscape of RALD-associated infections.

Conclusion

RALD is an exceptionally rare, non-malignant lymphoproliferative disorder that can closely mimic other hematological and autoimmune conditions. Our case highlights a unique combination of an unusual infectious agent, B. pseudomallei, and a rare inborn error of immunity, underscoring the expanding infectious spectrum of RALD. This emphasizes the importance of molecular genetic testing in establishing a definitive diagnosis and in differentiating RALD from conditions such as ALPS, SLE, and JMML. In the absence of standardized treatment guidelines, management should be tailored to the individual, focusing on infection prevention, immunomodulatory therapy for autoimmune manifestations, and vigilant long-term surveillance for potential malignant transformation. Early recognition of RALD is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and to guide appropriate therapeutic and follow-up strategies.
