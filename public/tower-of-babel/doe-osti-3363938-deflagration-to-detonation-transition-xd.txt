ABSTRACT
This report extends the recent SAND report titled “Deflagration to Detonation Transition Update:
XDDT Code Modularization” [1] with three developments—modernization to SI units, repair of
the adaptive mesh refinement, and adoption of a TIGER thermochemical equilibrium equation
of state—together with a validation campaign exercising all three against historical experimental
data.
The SI-units modernization replaced legacy CGS conventions in input decks and output files.
The conversion campaign surfaced four separate unit inconsistencies in the legacy CGS code;
subsequent validation against the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition case [2] (digitized as Fig. 3 of
Baer–Nunziato 1986 [3]) also revealed a post-DDT divergence between the SI and CGS builds,
traced to floating-point conditioning in the gas–solid energy-coupling expressions. The divergence
was closed by an architectural choice: XDDT internally uses CGS as the validated compute kernel,
with SI conventions applied only at the I/O boundary. The user-facing experience is SI throughout;
the compute engine is byte-identical to the legacy CGS reference, and a single canonical TIGER
table now serves both unit conventions.
The adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) was repaired and simplified to match the XCHEM (eXplosive
CHEMistry) prethermal ignition code [4]. The legacy parent–child coupling that prevented node
removal was replaced with a global level-array approach, restoring full add-and-remove capability
and enabling future coupling between the prethermal ignition model and the DDT solver. On the
McAfee configuration, a 200-node adaptive mesh produces a detonation velocity (𝐷 2 = 6.23 km/s)
closer to the McAfee experimental value (𝐷 2 = 6.36 km/s) than the 721-node fixed mesh (𝐷 2 =
6.12 km/s).
A TIGER thermochemical equilibrium equation of state (EOS) was implemented as a tabulated
alternative to the Jones–Wilkins–Lee (JWL) gas-phase EOS, eliminating JWL’s constant-𝑐 𝑣 as-
sumption and polynomial isentrope approximation. A two-part sensitivity study on the McAfee
configuration showed that varying the thermochemical library (JCZS, JCZS3, XDB) and gas-phase
EOS model (BKW, JCZ3, HBBS) at fixed reactant produces a narrow ∼3% spread in DDT observ-
ables, while varying only the reactant from which the table is generated produces a ∼70% spread
in induction timing and ∼10% in detonation velocity. The choice of reactant for the EOS table is
therefore the largest EOS-related uncertainty in DDT timing and will be a first-order consideration
for the planned XCHEM–XDDT coupling.
The primary validation target is the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition case [2], which most closely
mirrors the thermal-source scenarios that motivate the planned XCHEM–XDDT coupling; the
calibrated XDDT configuration reproduces the experimental burn-front and detonation-front tra-
jectories digitized from Fig. 3 of Baer–Nunziato 1986 [3]. A secondary validation against McAfee
Shot B-9036 (65% TMD granular HMX, 87 m/s piston-driven DDT) reproduces the ionization-pin
burn-front trajectory with excellent agreement after recalibration of the compaction-burn coefficient
𝐶𝛼 ; the computed detonation velocity of ∼7.0 km/s exceeds the experimental 𝐷 2 = 6.36 km/s, likely
due to lateral expansion losses in the finite-diameter experimental charge that a one-dimensional
model cannot capture.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENT

The XDDT (eXplosive Deflagration to Detonation Transition) code is a direct descendant of the
two-phase DDT framework developed by Melvin R. Baer and Jace W. Nunziato at Sandia National
Laboratories in the mid-1980s. The two-phase mixture theory and its original fixed-grid method-
of-lines discretization were published in Baer and Nunziato’s 1986 paper [3], together with the
validation against the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition experiments that this report revisits as its
primary acceptance benchmark. The adaptive parent–child refinement strategy was introduced in
the follow-on 1989 paper [5] and is the conceptual origin of the adaptive mesh scheme described
in this report. I am grateful to Mel Baer for providing the original FORTRAN-77 XDDT source
that served as the starting point for the modernization described in the first report [1] and continued
here, and for his mentorship at Sandia beginning in 1989.
Thanks also to Jackie K. Murton for supporting the refurbishment of the XDDT code, and to
Blaine Asay, William W. Erikson, Antonio R. “Tony” Garcia, and Alan Williams for reviewing this
document.

Use of AI-assisted tools. Portions of this report were drafted and edited with the assistance of
large language model tools. All technical content—including code modifications, simulation execu-
tion, calibration decisions, experimental data interpretation, and figure generation—was developed
and verified by the author. The AI tools were used for document preparation tasks including LATEX
editing, cross-reference consistency checking, and digitization of published experimental data for
figure overlays.

1. INTRODUCTION

The XDDT (eXplosive Deflagration to Detonation Transition) code is a direct descendant of
the two-phase DDT framework developed by Melvin R. Baer and Jace W. Nunziato at Sandia
National Laboratories in the mid-1980s. The two-phase mixture theory was introduced in Baer and
Nunziato’s 1986 paper [3], which also established the fixed-grid method-of-lines discretization and
validated it against the thermal-ignition experiments of Price and Bernecker [2]. The adaptive mesh
refinement strategy was introduced in Baer and Nunziato’s 1989 paper [5], which used eight levels
of parent–child refinement applied to the ball-propellant compaction experiments of Sandusky and
colleagues [6]. The adaptive scheme described in Chapter 3 of this report is a simplified descendant
of that 1989 formulation. The Baer–Nunziato model and alternative reduced-form DDT models
have been reviewed and compared by Bdzil and Son [7].
This report, building on the modernization and validation work presented in the first report [1],
documents three developments to the XDDT code:
 • Modernization to SI units, including four corrections to the legacy CGS code surfaced by the
 conversion campaign and an architectural choice (internal-CGS, presentation-SI) that closes
 the post-DDT divergence between the SI and CGS builds (Chapter 2);
 • Repair and simplification of the adaptive mesh refinement, restoring the node-removal capa-
 bility that was disabled in the first report (Chapter 3);
 • Adoption of a TIGER thermochemical equilibrium equation of state as a tabulated alternative
 to the legacy Jones–Wilkins–Lee (JWL) gas-phase EOS (Chapter 4).
The three developments are exercised against the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition case [2] as the
primary validation target (Chapter 6) and against the McAfee piston-driven DDT case [8] as a
secondary validation (Chapter 7). All figures and tables in the chapters that follow were generated
with the updated code.

1.1. Summary of Previous Work

The first report [1] documented the modernization of the legacy FORTRAN 77 XDDT code to
Fortran 90 with modular structure, namelist-driven input files, and adaptive mesh capability. Two
code defects were identified and corrected: an inconsistency in the nodal solid pressure evaluation
and a nonphysical burn-front tracking criterion. The code was validated against the piston-driven
DDT experiments of McAfee et al. [8] (Shot B-9036, 65% TMD granular HMX, 87 m/s piston).
With the compaction-driven burn acceleration coefficient 𝐶𝛼 (Eq. 2.10 of the first report [1])
calibrated to 75 on a 721-node fixed mesh, the code reproduced the DDT transition time to within
0.4% and the detonation velocity to within 3.8% of the experimental values. Additional validation

 19

against the Sandusky/Baer ball propellant experiment [5, 6] demonstrated the generality of the
code.

Several limitations were identified for future work, including: the adaptive mesh’s inability to
remove nodes (the combination logic was disabled), the reliance on an empirical Jones–Wilkins–
Lee (JWL) gas-phase equation of state with constant specific heat assumptions, and a single-
term compaction stress model that produced compaction wave velocities somewhat faster than
experiment.

1.2. From Thermal Ignition to Detonation

Predicting whether an accidental thermal event produces a mild pressure burst or a full detonation
requires modeling a sequence of physically distinct regimes, each governed by different physics
and spanning vastly different timescales. Figure 1-1 provides an overview of the complete event
sequence and identifies the computational codes responsible for each regime. The three panels of
the figure are discussed in turn below.

1.2.1. (a) Prethermal Ignition (UCM/MMP + XCHEM)

The top panel of Figure 1-1 shows the prethermal ignition regime, which unfolds over minutes
to hours and is modeled by the prethermal ignition code, XCHEM (eXplosive CHEMistry) [4],
which uses a Universal Cookoff Model (UCM) [9] coupled to a MicroMechanics Pressurization
(MMP) [10, 11] code. XCHEM solves the conductive energy equation with a volumetric source
term for reaction chemistry. UCM describes the reaction kinetics, and MMP couples the reaction
kinetics to the evolving pore structure, ultimately giving the pressure, solid and gas volume fractions,
and the specific surface-area-to-volume ratio.

Thermal threat. The sequence begins when an explosive is subjected to a sustained thermal
insult—fire, fragment impact, electrostatic discharge (ESD), or adjacent cookoff.

Thermal damage and decomposition. The thermal threat drives slow solid-phase decomposition
of the energetic material. The decomposition generates gaseous products that pressurize closed
porosity and increase the specific surface area of the granular bed. This regime is governed
by heat conduction, chemical kinetics, and micromechanical gas transport, and is modeled by
the UCM (Universal Cookoff Model) [9] coupled to the MMP (MicroMechanics Pressurization)
framework [10–13].

Thermal ignition (thermal runaway). As decomposition progresses, the volumetric energy
release rate eventually exceeds the rate at which heat can be conducted away from the reacting
material. At this point, the local temperature increases without bound on the chemical timescale—a
condition known as thermal runaway. The moment of thermal runaway is referred to as thermal
ignition (𝑡 = 𝑡 ign ) in this report and marks the transition from the slow cookoff regime to the fast
combustion regime.

1.2.2. (b) Solution Handoff (XCHEM to XDDT)

The center panel of Figure 1-1 illustrates the adaptive mesh at the moment of solution handoff. The
nodes are colored by temperature: red at the ignition point (where 𝑇 > 𝑇ign ), grading through orange
(warm), green (cool), blue (cold), and purple (ambient) with increasing distance from the thermal
front. The node spacing reflects the XCHEM-style adaptive refinement described in Chapter 3: the
finest resolution (level 6) is concentrated at the ignition point, with progressively coarser spacing
(down to level 1) in the undisturbed regions. The ellipses at both edges indicate that the grid extends
beyond the magnified view shown.
This mesh structure—and the spatially varying damage-state fields it carries—is the information
that would be transferred from XCHEM to XDDT at the onset of the DDT simulation. The mesh
compatibility required for this handoff is the primary motivation for the adaptive mesh improvements
described in Chapter 3 (see also Section 1.3).

1.2.3. (c) Post-Thermal Ignition (XDDT)

The bottom panel of Figure 1-1 shows the post-ignition DDT regime, which unfolds on a microsec-
ond timescale (with 𝑡 ign = 0 as the time origin for the DDT code) and is modeled by XDDT using
the Baer–Nunziato two-phase mixture theory. Four stages are identified:
Deflagration (burning). Following thermal ignition, a flame propagates through the pore space
between grains at subsonic velocities (tens to hundreds of meters per second). The burning
pressurizes the surrounding granular bed and drives mechanical compaction ahead of the flame.
Compaction. The pressure from the burning region (or from an external piston in the McAfee
experiments) drives a compaction wave into the undisturbed granular bed. This wave crushes the
material from its initial porosity (typically 50–80% TMD) to approximately 86–93% TMD. The
mechanical work of compaction heats grain surfaces and contact points, creating the conditions for
subsequent ignition of the compacted material.
DDT (transition). Stress waves generated by the accelerating burn coalesce to form a dense plug
of near-100% TMD material. When the burn pressure acts on this plug, a shock forms ahead of it
that strengthens rapidly.
Detonation. When the shock pressure exceeds the initiation threshold of the compacted explosive,
a self-sustaining supersonic detonation forms, propagating at 5–9 km/s depending on the material
and loading density.
Violence of the event. The outcome of this sequence ranges from a mild pressure burst (if conditions
are insufficient for DDT) to a full detonation, depending on confinement, bed density, and particle
size. The red gradient arrow beneath panel (c) of Figure 1-1 represents this outcome spectrum,
with the degree of violence plotted on a logarithmic scale. In the context of accidental explosive
events, violence denotes the rate of chemical energy release and its mechanical consequences—
peak overpressure, specific impulse, fragment velocity, and resulting structural damage. A low-
order fizzle releases its chemical energy over milliseconds to seconds with modest overpressures,
whereas a full detonation of the same material releases tens to hundreds of kilojoules per gram

 22

in microseconds, producing peak pressures on the order of tens of GPa and fragment velocities
of several km/s. The logarithmic scaling of the arrow reflects that quantities such as pressure rise
rate and energy deposition rate can differ by six or more orders of magnitude between a fizzle and
a detonation; a linear scale would compress all sub-detonative outcomes into an indistinguishable
band near the left edge. The XDDT code is designed to predict where along this spectrum a given
scenario falls and thereby to quantify the violence of the event.
The prethermal ignition codes (UCM/MMP implemented in XCHEM) model the regimes in
panel (a); the XDDT code models the regimes in panel (c). The handoff in panel (b) connects the
two and is the ultimate goal of the development effort.

1.3. Coupling Strategy: XCHEM to XDDT

The ultimate goal of the XDDT development is to couple it with XCHEM so that the complete
response of an explosive—from the initial thermal threat through thermal ignition and potentially
to detonation—can be predicted in a single analysis chain. This XCHEM–XDDT coupling is one
element of the broader XTIV (eXplosive Thermal Ignition to Violence) code-integration effort at
Sandia. At thermal ignition, the XCHEM solution fields (temperature, porosity, decomposition
products, and the evolving specific surface-area-to-volume ratio that characterizes the damage state)
would be transferred to the XDDT code as initial conditions for the DDT simulation.
This coupling requires that the two codes share a compatible mesh structure. The adaptive mesh
improvements described in this report (Chapter 3) were designed with this coupling in mind;
however, the actual XCHEM–XDDT coupling is beyond the scope of the present work and will be
the subject of a future SAND report.

1.4. Scope of This Report

This report addresses the following five items:
 1. Code corrections from CGS-to-SI conversion (Chapter 2). During the conversion of
 XDDT from CGS to SI units as part of the XTIV effort, four corrections were applied to
 bring the code into consistency with the original Baer–Nunziato derivations: dimensional
 reclassifications of two namelist variables (the compaction-viscosity coefficient and a pseudo-

1.5. Geometric Scope and Planned Follow-on Work

The XDDT code, as developed in this report and the preceding report [1], solves the Baer–Nunziato
two-phase equations in a one-dimensional planar coordinate system. This is appropriate for the
McAfee piston-driven DDT experiment used for validation throughout this work, as the McAfee
tube geometry is nominally planar (the piston drives a plane wave down the axis of a confined
granular bed). The planar assumption is also appropriate for the Sandusky–Baer ball propellant
experiment which was used to validate the DDT model in the first report.

The planar assumption is, however, not appropriate for the accidental thermal-threat scenarios that
motivate the coupled XCHEM–XDDT analysis introduced in Section 1.3. In particular, the hot-
fragment ignition scenario—in which a hot metal fragment is embedded in a granular explosive—
sets up a spherical thermal source (for an approximately spherical fragment) or a cylindrical thermal
source (for an approximately cylindrical fragment). In such geometries, the 1/𝑟 or 1/𝑟 2 geometric

 24

spreading of the thermal energy, the stress waves, and the burn front strongly affects whether
thermal ignition leads to a mild fizzle or a full detonation. A planar code cannot capture these
effects.
Extending XDDT to spherical and cylindrical coordinate systems is therefore a necessary next step
before coupled hot-fragment ignition analyses can be performed. The extension requires geometric
source terms in the Baer–Nunziato conservation equations, modification of the mesh construction
to handle the 𝑟 = 0 singularity in the spherical case, and suitable validation datasets. This work
is deferred to a planned follow-on SAND report, which will also document the XCHEM–XDDT
coupling that is the ultimate goal of the development effort.

2. CODE CORRECTIONS FROM CGS-TO-SI CONVERSION

During the preparation of this report, the XDDT code was converted from CGS (cm, g, s, dyn, erg)
to SI (m, kg, s, Pa, J) units as part of the XTIV code-integration effort. The conversion prompted a
careful review of the code’s implementation against the original Baer–Nunziato derivations [3, 5]
and led to four formulation updates that bring the code into full consistency with those derivations.
In each case the update restored a factor that had been numerically latent in the CGS implementation:
a dimensional factor that evaluates to O (1) in CGS units evaluates to a much larger or much smaller
number in SI, so a factor that is difficult to detect in CGS becomes immediate in SI. The updates
are documented in this chapter because they refine equations that enter the formulations used
in subsequent chapters, and because the pattern of discovery is itself a useful lesson for future
unit-system work.
The updates were developed on the modernized SI tree and back-ported to the legacy CGS tree
to maintain physics parity between the two code lines. All figures and tables in the chapters that
follow were generated with the updated code; results in the first report [1] predate these updates
and should be read against the updated values given here.

2.1. Motivation and Methodology

The Baer–Nunziato two-phase model [3, 5] was originally developed and published in CGS units,
and the XDDT code inherited that convention. For the planned coupling of XDDT to the prethermal-
ignition code XCHEM [4], which uses SI units, a systematic conversion was required. The
conversion proceeded parameter by parameter: each namelist variable, each internal constant,
and each formula factor was classified by its physical dimensions and scaled by the appropriate
SI-to-CGS factor.
Two complementary diagnostics were used to verify the conversion. The first was dimensional
analysis of every formula in the source code, checking that both sides of each assignment balance.
The second was a controlled-state regression in which a single node was initialized to a prescribed
physical state and every derived and local quantity in the right-hand-side evaluation was dumped
and compared between the CGS and SI builds at matched physical conditions. Any SI-to-CGS
ratio that differed from the expected unit factor indicated either a missing conversion or a hidden
dimensional inconsistency.
The four corrections documented in this chapter were found with a mix of the two diagnostics. Two
are dimensional reclassifications of namelist variables whose physical type had been misread during
the first-pass audit (Section 2.2). Two are missing or spurious density factors in continuum-level
formulas that had been silent in CGS because the HMX solid density is numerically close to unity

 27

in g/cm3 (Sections 2.3 and 2.4). Each correction was applied to both the modernized SI tree and
the legacy CGS tree, and each is covered by a regression test in the standing acceptance suite.

2.2. Dimensional Reclassification of Two Namelist Variables

Two namelist variables required dimensional reclassification rather than a change of formula. Both
errors arose from the first-pass audit inferring the physical type of a parameter from its descriptive
comment rather than from its usage in the governing equations; both are resolved by reading how
the parameter enters the right-hand side and assigning units accordingly.

2.2.1. Compaction viscosity coefficient rjotm

The namelist variable rjotm is commented in the input template as the “compaction viscosity
coefficient.” On the strength of that comment it was initially classified as a dynamic viscosity and
converted from poise to pascal-seconds by a factor of 10−1 . With this conversion, the SI build failed
to heat the gas at the piston during the early compaction transient: the gas temperature remained
pinned at the piston-side initial value while the CGS build developed the expected hot boundary
layer.
Inspection of the evolution equations shows that rjotm appears in the compaction-stress rate
expression in the combination RJOTM · 𝑝, where 𝑝 is pressure; the result is a reciprocal timescale
that governs how quickly solid volume fraction relaxes toward its equilibrium value. Dimensionally
rjotm must therefore carry units of 1/(pressure·time), not pressure–time. The correct SI conversion
is a multiplication by 10 (not 10−1 ), and the SI default value is rjotm = 1.0 × 10−3 (Pa s)−1 .
With the corrected conversion the gas-temperature history in SI matches the CGS result at every
node. No continuum equation in the report depends on the explicit form of rjotm; the correction
is purely a unit reclassification in the input deck.

2.2.2. Pseudo-density psdr

The namelist variable psdr is commented as “pseudo-density.” The first-pass audit, reading the
“pseudo-” prefix as indicating a normalized or dimensionless quantity, left psdr unchanged between
the CGS and SI builds.
The parameter is used in the gas-phase heating term through the intermediate rden_gp = gamg_gp/PSDR,
which is subsequently passed to an exponential of the form exp(1.439 rden_gp). For the exponent
to be dimensionless, rden_gp must be dimensionless; since gamg_gp is a gas density, PSDR must
have units of density. The correct SI conversion is therefore a multiplication by 103 (kg/m3 rather
than g/cm3 ), and the SI default value is psdr = 358.0 kg/m3 .
As with rjotm, no continuum equation in this report depends on the explicit form of psdr; the
correction affects only the input-deck unit convention.

2.5. Methodology Lessons

Two lessons emerged from the conversion and are worth recording for future work on this code
family.

 30

Dimensional analysis is necessary but not sufficient. All four corrections above (the two
namelist reclassifications in Section 2.2, the missing density factor in Section 2.3, and the spurious
factor in Section 2.4) involve dimensional inconsistencies that a careful term-by-term dimensional
audit can in principle detect. In practice, two of them (Sections 2.3 and 2.4) were found only after
the controlled-state diagnostic flagged a numerical disagreement between the SI and CGS builds
at a matched physical state: the missing or spurious factor was masked by a neighboring factor
that looked dimensionally motivated. The TERM3 case is the starkest example — a spurious 1/𝜌0
was hidden behind a /𝜌0 that appeared in an intermediate algebraic rearrangement of the inverse
map and read as natural to a reviewer. Unit-conversion work on a legacy code should include,
alongside the formula-by-formula dimensional audit, a suite of controlled-state regressions that
compare subsystem outputs between the two unit systems at matched physical conditions. Any
deviation from the expected unit factor is evidence of a hidden dimensional inconsistency that
dimensional analysis alone may miss, particularly when the offending factor is O (1) in the original
unit system.

O (1) density masking. The specific mechanism by which these oversights remained latent is
worth naming. The solid density of HMX is numerically close to unity in CGS (𝜌 ≈ 1.9 g/cm3 ),
and so is the Grüneisen coefficient (Γ ≈ 1). Products and ratios of such O (1) quantities produce
further O (1) numbers, and a missing or spurious factor of either leaves the computed answer within
a small fraction of the correct value — well inside the experimental uncertainty in DDT observables
such as transition time and detonation velocity. In a different unit system the same factors evaluate
to 103 or 10−3 and the error is immediate. The practical implication is that a CGS-native code that
has never been exercised in another unit system carries a latent risk of exactly this class of error,
and that unit-system conversion is a useful adjunct to any verification activity on such a code.

Adaptive-scheme simplification. The adaptive mesh scheme reimplemented for XDDT, de-
scribed in Chapter 3, uses a simplified global level-array construction that differs from the parent–
child refinement originally described by Baer and Nunziato [5]. The simplification was motivated
by compatibility with the XCHEM solution-transfer interface and by the need to support node re-
moval as well as node insertion. The simplified scheme satisfies the present validation requirements
at the recommended starting-mesh resolutions, and we record it here as the implementation choice
underlying the adaptive results in Chapter 3 and in the McAfee case study of Chapter 7.

2.5.1. Reflections on the SI Conversion Campaign

The four corrections of Sections 2.2 through 2.4 were the immediate yield of the CGS-to-SI
conversion. In the larger arc of the project, the conversion campaign also surfaced two further
classes of latent defect that do not fit the dimensional-inconsistency template, and ultimately led to
an architectural decision worth recording in its own right.

 31

Why convert in the first place. The motivation for SI conversion was institutional rather than
technical: input decks, journal manuscripts, and downstream coupling targets (XCHEM in partic-
ular) had been modernizing to SI, and the CGS-only XDDT had become a friction point at every
boundary. The conversion was undertaken with the expectation of a straightforward unit-relabeling
exercise: identify every dimensioned variable, multiply by the appropriate factor, and recompile.
The exercise turned out to be substantially more revealing. In addition to the four dimensional cor-
rections already described, the campaign exposed two further hidden-error classes that the original
single-tree code had carried for years without observable consequence.

A second class: silent default drift. During the Latin Hypercube Sampling sweep of Chapter 6,
the calibrated configuration in the CGS tree produced an asymptotic detonation velocity 𝐷 2 ≈
7.20 km/s, undershooting the experimental slope [3] by 6 %. The cause was traced to the HMX
strand-burn rate law: an early version of the input deck had been silently augmented at the source
by a two-branch high/low-pressure split with the low-pressure-regime defaults 𝑎 2,low = 11.39,
𝑏 𝑛,low = 0.8, 𝑃switch = 6.9 × 108 dyn/cm2 , which were applied whenever the user-provided deck did
not explicitly specify them. The two-branch behavior was not documented in the input-deck schema
and was invisible to a reader of either the deck or the rate law as written in the manuscript. The
defect had no observable consequence because the two branches happened to produce essentially
the same predictions for HMX at the calibrated operating regime; for other materials or other
regimes the same silent override would have been a serious modeling error. We made the defaults
match the single-branch published rate law in both trees and explicitly documented the two-branch
hook for users who need it. The lesson is that defaults that activate on missing input are a hidden
interface, and a controlled-state regression between code generations or between branches will
catch silent drift in those defaults that any deck-only audit will miss.

A third class: floating-point conditioning of the detonation feedback. The most subtle
defect was unrelated to algebra or to defaults. At the calibrated configuration, the SI build of
XDDT systematically overshot the CGS build’s 𝐷 2 by +14 % and 𝑃max by +28 %, with bit-
equivalent behavior through the DDT transition and a multiplicatively-growing divergence in the
post-DDT detonation regime. Section 6.7 documents the diagnostic chain in detail; the eventual
mechanism was that the gas-solid energy-coupling expressions in pde.f90 evaluate operands of
order 1010 in SI and order 103 in CGS, and that the resulting per-call rounding biases (small in
magnitude, ∼ 6×10−6 at the first integrator step) accumulated through the DDEBDF time integrator
and were amplified multiplicatively by the post-DDT detonation feedback to a macroscopic gap.
Neither the algebra, the algorithm, the tabulated thermodynamic data, nor the model physics was
at fault: the bug was that double-precision floating-point arithmetic does not commute with unit
choice when operand magnitudes shift by seven orders of magnitude. No unit-conversion factor
in the four-corrections audit could have caught this; the defect lived strictly in the floating-point
regime.

The architectural pivot. An obvious candidate fix was local nondimensionalization of the
energy-coupling expressions, which would have removed the operand-magnitude asymmetry by

 32

construction; this was estimated at ∼ 80–120 lines per tree of careful operand rescaling, with
reference-quantity choice as a new failure mode. We rejected this on cost-benefit grounds in favor
of a simpler architectural choice: XDDT internally uses CGS as the validated compute kernel;
SI conventions live only at the I/O boundary (input decks, output files, and the XCHEM-handoff
interface). Section 6.7 documents the implementation. The user-facing experience of XDDT is
unchanged (SI in, SI out); the compute engine, which has been validated for three decades in CGS,
is preserved bit-for-bit; and a single canonical TIGER table now serves both legacy and modernized
workflows. In retrospect this is the choice we should have made at the outset of the conversion
campaign. The four dimensional corrections in Sections 2.2 through 2.4 would still have been
required (those defects existed in the CGS code regardless of whether the SI tree was ever built),
but the post-DDT divergence would never have been introduced and the corresponding diagnostic
effort would have been avoided.

A plug for dimensionless numerics. The principled answer to all three classes of hidden error
described in this chapter is to write the compute kernel in dimensionless variables. Reference
quantities (𝜌0 , 𝑇0 , 𝑃0 , 𝐿 0 , 𝑡0 ) are chosen at problem setup; every state variable, parameter, and
intermediate is referred to its reference; the numerical scheme operates on O (1) operands by con-
struction. Dimensional analysis becomes mechanical (the units of every expression are immediate
from the reference set), default values cannot drift between unit systems (there is only one unit
system in the kernel), and floating-point conditioning is uniform regardless of the user-facing units.
We adopted internal-CGS rather than dimensionless because the cost of refactoring 30 years of
CGS code into dimensionless form is substantially higher than the cost of an SI-to-CGS conversion
layer at I/O, and because the validation surface of the CGS code is a real asset that is preserved
by the architectural choice. For a new code we would write dimensionless from the outset. For
a legacy code with a deep validation history, the internal-native-units / presentation-modern-units
pattern is a reasonable middle ground.

Why this story matters beyond this code. Many production scientific codes are sitting on
legacy unit-system choices that predate the modern preference for SI in publication and downstream
coupling. The conventional wisdom holds that converting them to SI is straightforward and entirely
beneficial. This experience suggests a more nuanced view. Unit-system migration is an excellent
forcing function for exposing hidden defects of multiple classes (dimensional, default-value, and
floating-point conditioning) that would otherwise remain latent indefinitely; it should be undertaken
deliberately and with controlled-state regression as a standing methodology. When the migration
target is the compute kernel, the cost of fighting floating-point conditioning expression by expression
can exceed the cost of a presentation-layer conversion that preserves the legacy validation surface.
The decision is worth making consciously rather than by default.

3. ADAPTIVE MESH REFINEMENT IMPROVEMENTS

This chapter documents the improvements made to the adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) capability
of the XDDT code. The legacy Baer code tracked evolving fronts by dynamically adding and
removing computational nodes based on local solution gradients—a strategy that concentrates
resolution where it is most needed (compaction and detonation fronts) while keeping the total node
count manageable [3]. During the modernization documented in the first report [1], the adaptive
mesh could add nodes but the combination (removal) logic was disabled, causing the node count
to grow monotonically and eventually degrade performance. The work described here restores the
full add-and-remove capability and aligns the initial grid construction with the XCHEM code [4],
enabling future coupling between the prethermal ignition model and the DDT solver.

3.1. Background: Adaptive Front Tracking

In a DDT simulation, the spatial resolution requirements vary dramatically across the domain.
The compaction wave front, the ignition zone, and the detonation wave each occupy only a few
millimeters at any given time, yet they must be resolved with node spacings an order of magnitude
smaller than the bed-averaged spacing. Away from these features, the solution varies smoothly and
coarse resolution suffices.

Baer’s original code [3] addressed this disparity through an ℎ-refinement strategy: nodes were
bisected (refined) where the local solution error exceeded a refinement tolerance 𝑟 ref , and neighbor-
ing nodes were combined (removed) where the error fell below a coarsening tolerance 𝑟 com . The
refinement and combination tolerances were related by 𝑟 com = 𝑐 𝑟 ref with 𝑐 ≈ 0.1, ensuring that
nodes were not immediately re-added after removal.

3.2. Phase 1: Combination Logic Fix

3.2.1. Problem Identification

During the modernization to Fortran 90, the combination tolerance rtolcom_in was set to a
negative value in the input file, effectively disabling all node removal. With combination disabled,
the adaptive mesh could only grow: every refinement event added nodes that were never reclaimed,
and the total node count increased monotonically until it reached the array ceiling or the simulation
ended.

 36

Restoring the combination logic by simply setting rtolcom_in to a positive value revealed a
deeper problem. The original code coupled each child node to its parent through a per-parent index
(iparent_loc) and a per-parent spacing array (dxpar). This parent–child coupling was designed
for a multi-block domain decomposition that is unnecessary for the single-layer DDT problem. The
coupling logic had accumulated several inconsistencies during the modernization—array indices
that referenced deallocated parent blocks, and spacing ratios that diverged after multiple refinement
and combination cycles.

3.2.2. Solution: XCHEM-Style Global Level Array

The parent–child coupling was replaced with a single global array dx_lev(0:ILEVMX) that stores
the element spacing at each refinement level, matching the approach used in the XCHEM (eXplosive
CHEMistry) prethermal ignition code. The level array is initialized from the base-mesh spacing Δ𝑥0

3.2.3. Verification

The simplified combination logic was tested with 𝑟 com = 0.1 𝑟 ref on the McAfee baseline con-

run starting from 30 nodes, the total node count fluctuated between 30 and 88 over the course of
the simulation, with no crashes or mesh-quality degradation. This fluctuation—nodes being both
added and removed as the fronts propagate—is the hallmark of a properly functioning adaptive
mesh and was absent in the modernized code prior to this fix.

3.3. Phase 2: XCHEM-Style Initial Grid

3.3.1. Motivation

The XDDT code will ultimately receive its initial conditions from the XCHEM prethermal ignition
code, which computes the thermal state of the explosive from the initial threat (fire, fragment
impact, etc.) up to the point of thermal ignition. For this coupling to work, the two codes must use

 37

compatible mesh structures. The XCHEM code constructs its initial mesh by inserting boundary-
refined nodes at each domain boundary, creating a smooth transition from the finest level at the
boundary to the coarsest level in the interior. The XDDT initial grid was modified to match this
construction.

3.3.2. Implementation

The new initial grid construction, implemented in node0.f90, proceeds as follows. Starting from
a user-specified number of interior nodes 𝑁, five additional nodes are inserted at each boundary
(𝑥 = 0 and 𝑥 = 𝐿) by successive halving of the local spacing. This produces a level transition of
the form

                         6, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, . . . , 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 6                   (3.2)

where the integers denote refinement levels. The 2:1 level constraint—no two adjacent nodes may
differ by more than one refinement level—is satisfied by construction.

The number of parent-mesh nodes (NPARENT) is derived from the total node count 𝑁 rather than
being prescribed independently. This removes the previous snap_npt_to_parent restriction that
forced the starting node count to be a specific multiple, and allows any starting count 𝑁 ≥ 3 to
produce a valid initial mesh.

4. TIGER THERMOCHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM EOS

This chapter describes the implementation of a thermochemical equilibrium equation of state (EOS)
for the gas-phase combustion products as an alternative to the Jones–Wilkins–Lee (JWL) form used
in the original Baer and Nunziato model [3]. The new EOS is based on the TIGER thermochemical
code [14] and is provided to the XDDT code as a tabulated EOS surface.

4.1. Motivation

The JWL equation of state represents the gas-phase combustion products as an analytic function of
density:
                       𝑝 = 𝐴 exp(−𝑅1 𝜌0 /𝜌) + 𝐵 exp(−𝑅2 𝜌0 /𝜌) + 𝜔𝜌𝑒,                       (4.1)
where 𝐴, 𝐵, 𝑅1 , 𝑅2 , and 𝜔 are empirical coefficients fitted to cylinder-expansion or plate-push
experiments. While computationally efficient, the JWL form has several well-known limitations:
 1. It is a polynomial approximation to the true product isentrope, valid only near the Chapman–
 Jouguet (CJ) state and along the expansion isentrope. Off-isentrope thermodynamic states—such
 as those encountered during the deflagration phase, where the gas is at low density and high
 temperature—may be poorly represented.
 2. The gas-phase temperature is not computed self-consistently from the EOS but must be
 inferred from an assumed constant specific heat 𝑐 𝑣 . This assumption breaks down at the
 extreme temperatures (3 000–10 000 K) and pressures (1–30 GPa) encountered during DDT,
 where molecular dissociation and ionization significantly alter the thermodynamic properties.
 3. The JWL coefficients are specific to a particular explosive formulation and loading density.
 Applying the same coefficients to a different initial density requires an assumption about how
 the product state scales with initial conditions—an assumption that is difficult to validate
 independently.
A thermochemical equilibrium EOS avoids these limitations by computing the product state from
first principles at each density–temperature point on the EOS surface. The TIGER code [14] solves
for the equilibrium composition and thermodynamic properties of the detonation products using a
Gibbs free energy minimization, accounting for molecular dissociation, ionization, and non-ideal
gas interactions through an intermolecular potential model.
The calculations in this report use the JCZ3 equation of state [15] with the JCZS3 thermodynamic
database [16]. The JCZ3 EOS is based on the exponential-6 potential (𝛼 = 13.5), which was
shown by Cowperthwaite and Zwisler [14] to be more accurate than the earlier Lennard-Jones
(JCZ1) and Mie 6–9 (JCZ2) potentials. The “S” in JCZS denotes the Sandia database of reference
thermodynamic properties and exponential-6 potential parameters; JCZS3 is the third revision of
this database. This combination is referred to throughout the report as JCZS3/JCZ3.

 45

4.2.1. The (𝑉, 𝑇) Table vs. the (𝜌, 𝑒) Code

The TIGER table is constructed on a (𝑉, 𝑇) grid: temperature is an independent variable of the
table, and every stored quantity—𝑝, 𝑒, 𝑐 𝑠 , 𝑐 𝑣 , Γ—is pre-computed at known 𝑇 values. At first
glance, this would seem to make temperature immediately available.

 47

However, the Baer–Nunziato conservation equations [1, 3] do not evolve temperature. The three
conserved quantities for the gas phase are mass (𝜙𝑔 𝜌𝑔 ), momentum (𝜙𝑔 𝜌𝑔 𝑢 𝑔 ), and total energy
(𝜙𝑔 𝜌𝑔 𝐸 𝑔 , where 𝐸 𝑔 = 𝑒 𝑔 + 21 𝑢 2𝑔 ). After each time step, the code updates these conserved fields and
can extract the gas density 𝜌𝑔 and the specific internal energy 𝑒 𝑔 . Temperature is a derived quantity
that must come from the equation of state—it is not carried independently by the solver.

The code therefore arrives at the EOS with known (𝜌𝑔 , 𝑒 𝑔 ) and asks for 𝑝 𝑔 , 𝑇𝑔 , and 𝑐 𝑠,𝑔 . Converting
to specific volume (𝑉 = 1/𝜌𝑔 ) gives one of the table’s two independent variables, but the other—
temperature—is precisely what the code does not know. The problem is an inverse lookup: the code
has one of the table’s outputs (energy) and needs to recover the corresponding input (temperature).

Why not tabulate on a (𝑉, 𝑒) grid instead? The alternative would be to rebuild the TIGER

standard approach used by most hydrocodes that couple to tabular EOS data (see, e.g., the CTH

4.3. Energy Reference Convention

As described in the preceding section, the table lookup requires a Newton iteration to invert
𝑒(𝑉, 𝑇) = 𝑒 target for temperature. This inversion can only succeed if the code’s 𝑒 𝑔 and the table’s 𝑒
are expressed in the same reference frame. They are not: the two systems assign different numerical
values to the same physical state, differing by a constant that reflects their respective definitions of
“zero energy.”

This mismatch is purely bookkeeping, not physics. The Baer–Nunziato conservation equations
depend only on energy differences—between one state and another, or between one time step and
the next—never on the absolute magnitude of 𝑒 𝑔 . Shifting every energy value in the table by a
constant therefore leaves all fluxes, source terms, and wave speeds unchanged; only the numerical
label attached to a given thermodynamic state is affected. Nevertheless, the Newton inversion
𝑒(𝑉, 𝑇) = 𝑒 target compares absolute values, not differences, so the code and the table must agree on
a common zero before the iteration can converge to the correct temperature.

4.3.1. The Two Energy Zeros

The DDT code’s energy reference was established by Baer and Nunziato [3] for the two-phase
mixture model. Unreacted solid is assigned 𝑒 𝑠 = 0 at ambient conditions (𝑇0 = 300 K, 𝜂 = 1). The
gaseous products, which carry 𝑄 det less chemical potential energy than the reactant, are assigned
                                 𝑒 𝑔,code = −𝑄 det   at 𝑇 = 𝑇0 , 𝑉 → ∞.                            (4.12)
This convention is baked into the initial conditions (𝐸 𝑔0 = −𝜌𝑔 𝑄 det ), the interphase mass-exchange
source terms, and the JWL temperature relation (Eq. (4.15)).

The heat of detonation 𝑄 det . The quantity 𝑄 det is the constant-volume heat of reaction: the
total chemical energy released per unit mass when the solid explosive is completely converted
to gaseous products at the same temperature and volume. The constant-volume qualification is
important because the JWL equation of state is formulated in terms of specific internal energy 𝑒,
not enthalpy ℎ = 𝑒 + 𝑝/𝜌. Standard heats of formation and Hess’s law yield the constant-pressure
heat of reaction Δ𝐻; the constant-volume quantity needed here is Δ𝑈 = Δ𝐻 − Δ𝑛 𝑅 𝑇, where Δ𝑛
is the change in moles of gaseous species per unit mass. Because an explosive converts from
a condensed solid to primarily gaseous products, Δ𝑛 is large and the Δ𝑛 𝑅 𝑇 correction is not
negligible.
𝑄 det is not the same as the CJ detonation energy 𝑒 CJ , which includes the kinetic energy of the
products at the CJ state and depends on the initial density and detonation pressure. Nor is it
path-dependent: whether the explosive burns slowly in a deflagration or detonates at 30 GPa, the
chemical energy difference between reactant and product states at the same 𝑇 and 𝑉 is the same,
which is why 𝑄 det is valid across the entire DDT process.
Thermochemical codes such as TIGER compute 𝑄 det directly as a constant-volume internal energy
difference. TIGER reports energies in a reactant-relative frame, 𝐸 (𝑅) = 𝑒 prod − 𝑒 reactant , so the heat
of detonation is obtained from a single equilibrium calculation at ambient conditions:
                                       𝑄 det = −𝐸 (𝑅) 𝑇=𝑇0 , 𝑃→0 .                                 (4.13)
Note the sign convention: for an exothermic explosive, the products lie below the reactants in
energy, so TIGER reports 𝐸 (𝑅) < 0 at ambient conditions. The DDT code defines 𝑄 det as a
positive quantity (energy released), hence the negation in Eq. (4.13).

4.3.2. Computing the Offset

The two energy zeros differ by a constant,

                                𝐸 offset = 𝑒 TIGER (𝑉ref , 𝑇0 ) − 𝑒 𝑔,code (𝑇0 ),                   (4.16)

evaluated at a common reference state. The conversion script tiger2ddt.py computes 𝐸 offset as
follows:
 1. The reference state is chosen at the largest specific volume in the table (𝑉ref = 10 000 cc/g,
 i.e., the ideal-gas limit) and 𝑇0 = 300 K.
 2. At this state, the TIGER table gives 𝑒 TIGER = −1495.0 cal/g = −6.255 × 1010 erg/g.
 3. The DDT code’s energy at the same physical state is 𝑒 𝑔,code = −𝑄 det = −7.955 × 1010 erg/g.
 4. The offset is

         𝐸 offset = (−6.255 × 1010 ) − (−7.955 × 1010 ) = +1.700 × 1010 erg/g = 406.2 cal/g. (4.17)

The offset is a property of the material (through 𝑄 det ) and the thermochemical database (through
the formation enthalpies), but not of the thermodynamic state: it is a single constant that applies at
every point on the EOS surface. To convert from the code’s energy frame to the TIGER frame for
the Newton iteration, the lookup routine (line 180 of tiger_eos_table.f90) simply computes

 𝑒 target = 𝑒 𝑔,code + 𝐸 offset , (4.18)

and then solves 𝑒(𝑉, 𝑇) = 𝑒 target for 𝑇. The offset is stored in the binary table header so that the
Fortran module reads it at initialization and applies it automatically; no changes are needed to the
DDT conservation equations, initial conditions, or source terms.

4.3.3. Verification

Because an incorrect 𝐸 offset shifts the entire 𝑝–𝑇 response without producing an obvious error
signal, the offset must be verified independently. Two checks are performed:

Ambient round-trip. The test driver (test_tiger_eos.f90, Test 3) calls the lookup routine
with the DDT code’s ambient gas state: 𝜌𝑔 = 𝜙𝑔 𝑃ref /(𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 𝑇0 ) and 𝑒 𝑔 = −𝑄 det . If 𝐸 offset is correct,
the Newton iteration converges to 𝑇𝑔 = 300 K and 𝑃𝑔 ≈ 1.013 × 106 dyn/cm2 (1 atm). An offset
error of even 1% (∼ 4 cal/g) shifts the recovered temperature by ∼15 K and the pressure by several
percent—easily detectable.

CJ consistency. The test driver (Test 4) evaluates the table at CJ-like conditions (𝑉 ≈ 0.66 cc/g,
𝑇 ≈ 3800 K) and compares the returned pressure and sound speed against the JWL values at
the same state. Because the JWL coefficients were originally fitted to the TIGER CJ isentrope,
agreement within a few percent at the CJ point confirms that the energy offset, unit conversions,
and table interpolation are all consistent.

4.4. Table Generation Workflow

This section describes the end-to-end procedure for generating a TIGER EOS table and integrating
it into the DDT code. The pipeline consists of three stages: (1) creating a TIGER input deck,
(2) running TIGER to produce the raw equation-of-state data, and (3) converting the output to
a binary table with the Python script tiger2ddt.py. The procedure is material-independent;
changing to a different explosive requires only a new TIGER composition line and a fresh run
through the same pipeline.

Where unit conversion happens. The TIGER code itself is taken unmodified as a third-party
legacy code that emits results in mixed units (atm, cal/g, m/s, cal/(g·K), and so on). All unit
conversion required by XDDT is performed downstream of TIGER, in the tiger2ddt.py script
of Stage 3. A single canonical version of the script is maintained, producing a CGS-format
.tbl file that is consumed by both the legacy CGS XDDT build and the modernized SI build. This
consolidation follows directly from the architectural choice documented in Section 6.7: XDDT uses
CGS as its internal compute kernel regardless of the user-facing unit convention, with SI conventions
applied only at the input-deck and output-file boundaries of the SI build. The provenance line that
the EOS-loader prints to xddt.log records the table source for cross-checking when a deck
specifies eos_table_file_in.

4.4.1. Stage 1: Creating the TIGER Input Deck

The TIGER code includes an interactive command, mki (“make input”), that generates an input
deck for computing EOS data on a structured (𝑉, 𝑇) grid. The command accepts the temperature
range and number of increments, the volume range and number of increments, and produces a deck
of point calculations that traverse the grid in a serpentine (boustrophedon, Greek for “as the ox
plows”) pattern:

                       ._______________________.
                 Tmax  | START--->--->--->---, | 1st isotherm
                       |                     v |
                       | .---<---<---<---<---’ | 2nd isotherm
                       | v                     |
                       | +--->--->--->--->---, | ...
                       |                     v |
                 Tmin | END-<---<---<---<---’ | last isotherm
                       |_______________________|
 Vmin Vmax

The serpentine ordering allows each point calculation to use the preceding solution as its initial
guess, which improves convergence of the chemical equilibrium solver. Volumes are spaced
logarithmically and temperatures are spaced uniformly.
For the 65% TMD HMX table used in this work, the command was:

 54

  mki, t, 250, 6000, 116, v, 0.3, 10000, 100

producing a 100 × 116 grid (𝑉: 0.35–10 000 cc/g, log-spaced; 𝑇: 250–6000 K, Δ𝑇 = 50 K). The
generated deck begins with the explosive composition and a plt line that requests the nine output
quantities:

  composition, hmx, 1
  point, p, 3000, t, 6000
  plt, T, V, P, E, S, sound, cv, cp, beta, adexp
  poi,v,    0.3500,t, 6000.0
  poi,v,    0.3882,t, 6000.0
  ...

The first point line seeds the equilibrium solver, and the remaining poi (point calculation) lines
step through the serpentine grid. Occasionally TIGER’s equilibrium solver fails at an interior grid
point (particularly at low temperatures and high compression where product speciation changes
rapidly). The mki command generates individual point calculations rather than isothermal sweeps
so that a failed point can be restarted with a new pressure or temperature guess without disrupting
the rest of the grid.

Why the 6000 K temperature ceiling is sufficient. The production temperature grid stops at
6000 K because that is well above any temperature the gas-phase EOS encounters in the steady
detonation regime that XDDT models. For HMX, the Chapman–Jouguet temperature lies near
3700 K, and post-CJ rarefaction temperatures are lower; the 6000 K ceiling provides comfortable
margin. Transient gas conditions at the von Neumann spike—visible in Figure 4-3, which was
generated with a specialized 250–20,000 K table for visualization—reach 20,000 K and 55 GPa,
but the spike occupies only a few computational cells and the gas mass fraction there is small:
the bulk of the matter at the spike is shock-compressed but unreacted solid, governed by the Mie–
Grüneisen solid EOS rather than the gas table. Table-edge clipping at the spike when the production
6000 K table is used therefore has no material effect on the burn-front trajectory. Extending the
production ceiling to 20,000 K would be appropriate for overdriven detonation, plasma-regime
shock loading, or aluminized formulations with extreme heat release—regimes outside the DDT
scope of XDDT—rather than for the present calculations.

4.4.2. Stage 2: Running TIGER

Executing TIGER with the input deck produces a plot file (tiger.plt) containing one row per
converged (𝑉, 𝑇) point. Each row lists the nine quantities requested by the plt line, in TIGER’s
native units (atm, cal/g, m/s, etc.). The file header identifies the column names, and a zone
line records the total number of converged points. If all grid points converge, the file contains
100 × 116 = 11,600 rows.

 55

4.4.3. Stage 3: Converting with tiger2ddt.py

The Python script tiger2ddt.py reads the TIGER output, applies all necessary transformations,
and writes the binary table used by the DDT code. It requires two input files and accepts an optional
output file name:

  python3 tiger2ddt.py tiger.plt tiger_ddt_sesame.inp [output.tbl]

The first argument is the TIGER plot file from Stage 2. The second is the original TIGER input
deck from Stage 1, which the script reads to recover the canonical 𝑉 grid—the exact volume values
specified in the deck. This step is necessary because TIGER’s output occasionally rounds the
volume to fewer significant figures than the input, and even small mismatches prevent the data
from being placed on a clean rectangular grid. The script snaps each output volume to its nearest
canonical value within a relative tolerance of 10−3 .

The conversion proceeds in seven steps:

 1. Read the tiger.plt file and parse the nine-column data array.
 2. Read the canonical 𝑉 grid from the input deck.
 3. Reshape the serpentine data onto a structured (𝑁𝑉 × 𝑁𝑇 ) rectangular grid, snapping volumes
 to the canonical values. Any grid cells that did not converge in TIGER are flagged and
 reported.
 4. Compute the energy offset 𝐸 offset at the reference state (𝑉 = 𝑉max , 𝑇 = 300 K), as described
 in Section 4.3. This step is automatic; the user does not need to compute the offset manually.
 5. Convert all quantities from TIGER’s native mixed units to CGS. TIGER itself is an unmod-
 ified legacy code that emits pressure in atm, energy and 𝑐 𝑣 in cal/g and cal/(g·K), sound
 speed in m/s, and other quantities in similarly mixed conventions; the conversion to a single
 coherent CGS unit system therefore happens here in tiger2ddt.py, not in TIGER. The
      script applies pressure (× 1.01325 × 106 to dyn/cm2 ), energy (× 4.184 × 107 to erg/g), sound
 speed (× 100 to cm/s), and 𝑐 𝑣 (× 4.184 × 107 to erg/(g·K)). The Grüneisen parameter is
 dimensionless and is computed as Γ = 1/𝛽 from the TIGER compressibility output. The
 resulting CGS table is consumed by both XDDT builds; the SI build’s input layer converts
 user-deck SI values to CGS at runtime (Section 6.7), so no SI-format EOS table is required.
 6. Validate the table: check for NaN entries, verify that 𝑒(𝑇) is monotonically increasing at
 every 𝑉 (required for the Newton inversion to converge), confirm positive sound speeds, and
 print a spot check at CJ-like conditions.
 7. Write the binary .tbl file and a companion text file (.txt) for human inspection. The
 binary format is described in Section 4.2.

and temperature the material experiences, but it exists only at the leading edge of the reaction
zone and persists for an extremely short time (on the order of nanoseconds for condensed-phase
explosives).

4.6. JWL versus TIGER DDT Simulation Results

4.6.1. Calibration and Validation Considerations

A persistent challenge in DDT modeling is the scarcity of experimental data with sufficient di-
agnostic detail for independent calibration and validation. In this work, the compaction-driven
burn acceleration coefficient 𝐶𝛼 was calibrated to the McAfee Shot B-9036 configuration [8],
and the same experiment serves as the primary validation case. This limits the independence of
the comparison: agreement between the code and the McAfee data partly reflects the calibration
itself.
Ideally, model parameters would be calibrated against one experimental configuration and validated
against a separate dataset. However, piston-driven DDT experiments with the comprehensive
diagnostics needed for model validation—simultaneous ionization-pin timing, radiographic density
measurements, and pressure histories—are rare and expensive.
With this caveat in mind, the comparison below focuses on directly measurable quantities—𝑡DDT ,
𝑥 DDT , and 𝐷 2 —rather than model-internal diagnostics. The JWL coefficients originate from the
Baer and Nunziato (1986) calibration to Sandusky/Bernecker compaction data [3, 6] and TIGER-
calculated product states at the McAfee loading density (𝜌0 = 1.2285 g/cm3 ). The TIGER table, by
contrast, is derived from first-principles thermochemical equilibrium and introduces no additional
fitted parameters beyond those already in the JCZ3 intermolecular potential model [15, 16].

4.8. Summary

The TIGER thermochemical equilibrium EOS has been implemented as a tabulated alternative
to the JWL gas-phase EOS. On both the 721-node fixed mesh and the 200-node adaptive mesh,
the TIGER EOS produces shorter DDT times (486 and 505 𝜇s vs. 522 and 536 𝜇s for JWL) and
shorter run-to-detonation distances (17.5 and 18.4 cm vs. 18.8 and 19.8 cm). Because the burn
acceleration coefficient 𝐶𝛼 = 75 was calibrated with the JWL EOS, the JWL case naturally matches
the McAfee experimental DDT time (∼520 𝜇s); the TIGER shift to earlier ignition therefore moves
𝑡DDT further from the calibrated match. Recalibrating 𝐶𝛼 with the TIGER EOS recovers agreement
with the experimental timing (Chapter 7). The shorter DDT times arise from the self-consistent gas-
temperature calculation, which yields higher temperatures during the deflagration phase than the
constant-𝑐 𝑣 JWL model, accelerating the interphase heat transfer and compaction-driven ignition.
The TIGER detonation velocity (𝐷 2 = 6.85–7.10 km/s) overshoots the experimental value (6.36 km/s
as reported by McAfee; ∼6.20 km/s from hand digitization [1]) more than JWL (6.12–6.23 km/s).
Both models overshoot experiment because the 1D formulation cannot account for lateral expan-
sion of the detonation products, which would reduce the effective driving pressure and lower the
propagation velocity.
The comparison is constrained by the fact that the compaction burn coefficient 𝐶𝛼 was calibrated to
the same McAfee experiment used for validation. The TIGER EOS itself introduces no additional
fitted parameters, which makes it a stronger basis for extending the model to configurations where
JWL coefficients may not be available or appropriate.

5. GAS-PHASE EOS SENSITIVITY

The TIGER EOS table used in the preceding chapter (JCZ3 EOS with the JCZS3 database)
represents one specific combination of reference thermodynamics and gas-phase EOS. To quantify
the sensitivity of the DDT predictions to these choices, five EOS tables were generated from three
thermochemical libraries and three gas-phase EOS models, as summarized in Table 5-1.
The three libraries differ in their reference thermodynamic data (heats of formation, heat capacities,
and entropy):
 • JCZS [15]: the original Sandia database.
 • JCZS3 [16]: the third revision, incorporating updated reference data.
 • XDB: a new library based on updated heats of formation from the Active Thermochemical
 Tables (ATcT) and the best available reference thermodynamics from the NASA, JANAF,
 Gurvich, and Burcat databases.
The three gas-phase EOS models span two theoretical foundations — covolume-based and intermolecular-
potential-based:
 • BKW: the Becker–Kistiakowsky–Wilson covolume-based semiempirical EOS, widely used
 in detonation product calculations since the 1960s. BKW represents each molecule as an
 effective hard sphere whose covolume is set geometrically from the van der Waals atomic radii
 under a spherical rotational-envelope approximation; these covolumes are fixed molecular
 constants. The non-ideal gas behavior is then represented by an exponential correction to the
 ideal-gas pressure with adjustable empirical parameters, rather than by a pair potential.
 • JCZ3: an exponential-6 intermolecular potential (𝛼 = 13.5) of Jacobs, Cowperthwaite, and
 Zwisler [15], with parameters fit to shock Hugoniot data.
 • HBBS: an exponential-6 EOS developed by Hallstadius [23] through Monte Carlo simulations
 of the exp-6 fluid and implemented at Sandia. The HBBS EOS is currently available only
 with the XDB library.

are closer to experiment than JWL, while 𝐷 2 overshoots the experimental value. As discussed
in Section 4.6, the 𝐷 2 overshoot is primarily a consequence of the 1D planar geometry, which
cannot account for lateral expansion losses present in the finite-diameter experimental charge. The
fact that the overshoot persists across all five library–EOS combinations with nearly the same
magnitude confirms this interpretation: the 𝐷 2 difference between the 1D code and the experiment
is a geometric effect, not a consequence of the product thermochemistry. Figure 5-1 illustrates this
graphically: the piston and compaction fronts are indistinguishable across all six cases, while only
the burn fronts — and hence the DDT coalescence point — shift with the gas-phase EOS.

5.2. Cross-Reactant EOS Table Sensitivity

The EOS sensitivity study of the preceding section held the reactant at HMX and varied the
thermochemical library and gas-phase EOS model used to generate the explosive-products table.
A complementary question is the following: how sensitive are the DDT observables to the reactant
itself ? The TIGER EOS tables used by the DDT code are explosive-products tables, each generated
from a specified starting reactant. If the EOS table were regenerated from a different reactant—
TNT, or TATB, or PETN—with everything else in the DDT model held fixed, how far would the
DDT predictions move?

To answer this, the McAfee Shot B-9036 configuration was re-run with the gas-phase TIGER table
replaced, in turn, by tables generated from ten different starting reactants spanning the common
energetic families: HMX, RDX, and CL-20 (nitramines); PETN and XTX-8003 (nitrate esters and
nitrate-ester-based PBX); TNT and Comp B (aromatics); PBX-9501 (HMX-based PBX); PBX-
9502 and TATB (insensitive, TATB-based). All other parameters were held at the values used
throughout Chapter 4: the HMX solid-phase EOS, the particle radius 𝑟 𝑝 = 0.0085 cm, the single-
term compaction law, the compaction-burn coefficient 𝐶𝛼 = 75, the hot-spot nucleation coefficient,
and the 87 m/s piston. The initial bed was 65% TMD HMX in all cases; only the &gas_eos
namelist entry—pointing to a different explosive-products table—changed between runs.

Interpretation. This is an EOS sensitivity study, not a DDT prediction for the ten explosives. The
solid phase, particle size, compaction stress law, compaction-burn coefficient, hot-spot kinetics,
and confinement all remain HMX-calibrated throughout. A legitimate DDT prediction for TNT
or PBX-9502 would additionally require material-specific values for each of these inputs. What
the present study isolates, and only isolates, is the contribution of the explosive-products EOS
table—and specifically its underlying reactant—to the DDT observables.

used as a proxy.

6. VALIDATION AGAINST PRICE–BERNECKER (BN 1986 FIG. 3)

The Baer–Nunziato two-phase model [3, 5] was originally validated against the Price–Bernecker
thermal-ignition experiments, with results presented as Figure 3 of the 1986 paper [3]. That figure
shows the burn-front trajectory through a 70 %-TMD HMX bed driven by a hot-gas burst boundary
condition (IBC=1), with monitoring stations at 3.81 and 7.62 mm from the ignition wall. This
case is adopted as the primary acceptance benchmark for the updated XDDT code because (i)
the thermal-ignition formulation directly mirrors how XCHEM [4] hands off to XDDT through
the planned XTIV coupling chain, namely as a temperature-driven boundary condition, and (ii)
the case is free of the moving-piston and adaptive-mesh interactions that complicate the McAfee
Shot B-9036 case (Chapter 7) and obscure the underlying physics fidelity.
The fixed-mesh JWL baseline establishes a starting point against which the TIGER thermochemical
EOS (Chapter 4) and the adaptive mesh refinement (Chapter 3) are evaluated. The chapter then
presents a Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) sensitivity analysis that quantifies which of fourteen
perturbed inputs drive the burn-front response, and identifies a calibrated parameter set within
the LHS sweep whose trajectory passes through the experimental data. The sensitivity workflow
follows the protocol the author has used in prior cookoff studies [11].

6.1. Configuration and Acceptance Criterion

The benchmark case is configured to reproduce the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition geometry as
modeled in Baer and Nunziato’s 1986 paper [3]. The propellant bed is HMX at 70 % theoretical
maximum density, with crystal density 𝛾𝑜 = 1.90 g/cm3 and a particle radius 𝑎 𝑝 = 50 𝜇m matching
the nominal experimental specification. Initial solid and gas temperatures are both 300 K and the
initial mixture pressure is 0.1 MPa (1 atm). Heat capacity, heat of pyrolysis, activation temperature,
and burn-rate parameters take the values established in the original paper.
The bed length is set to 30 cm, extending the simulation domain beyond the 10 cm shown in
Figure 3 of Reference [3] to cover the full experimental observation range. The Erikson digitization
of the original figure provides sixteen (𝑡, 𝑥) data points spanning 0.8 𝜇s – 48.5 𝜇s in time and 1 –
25.45 cm in space; at the standard 10 cm domain the model burn front exits the right boundary
before 𝑡 ∼ 35 𝜇s, eliminating the late-time data points from any direct comparison. The 30 cm
domain accommodates the full experimental record while preserving the spatial resolution of the
original benchmark.
The discretization uses 600 fixed nodes with Δ𝑥 = 0.05 cm, matching the spatial resolution of
the original BN 1986 200-node implementation on the 10 cm bed. The mesh-refinement flag is
disabled (imesh_in = 0) so that this benchmark case reflects fixed-grid method-of-lines integration
as described in the 1986 paper; the adaptive parent–child refinement of the 1989 follow-on paper [5]

 77

is exercised separately in Chapter 3. Two probe stations are placed at 𝑥 = 3.81 and 7.62 mm to
record the gas/solid pressures and temperatures at the original BN 1986 monitoring positions.
Boundary conditions follow the IBC = 1 thermal-ignition formulation of paper Section 4.1, equa-
tions [79]–[80]. The hot-gas burst BC imposes an instantaneous, spatially uniform hot-gas region
of length ℓ1,ign = 0.5 cm, gas pressure 𝑝 𝑔,ign = 10 MPa (108 dyn/cm2 ), and gas temperature
𝑇𝑔,ign = 3000 K at the left boundary at 𝑡 = 0, with the remainder of the bed at the initial condi-
tions. No piston motion is imposed; the right-boundary velocity is held at zero throughout. This
boundary-condition geometry directly reflects the form by which the planned XCHEM–XDDT
coupling will deliver an ignition-time temperature signal from the prethermal-ignition solver into
XDDT.
The strand-burn law is configured in single-branch mode: the coefficients 𝐴2 = 12.27 cm/(s·108 Pa)
and 𝑏 𝑛 = 1.0 are applied uniformly across the pressure range. The legacy FORTRAN-77 source
from which XDDT inherits contains an optional two-branch form for HMX with a switch at
𝑝 sw = 69 MPa; that form is preserved as a namelist option but defaults to single-branch behavior
because LHS sensitivity analysis (Section 6.4) showed it does not materially affect results.
Three configurations are evaluated against the data: the JWL analytic gas EOS (Section 6.2) as
the inherited baseline, the TIGER thermochemical-equilibrium gas EOS [14, 15] (Section 6.3) as
the report’s headline EOS update, and the TIGER EOS combined with adaptive mesh refinement
(Section 6.3) as the integrated end state. The acceptance criterion is that the calibrated configura-
tion’s burn-front trajectory match the experimental data within ∼ 1 cm RMS over the fifteen data
points retained for the metric (the first experimental point at 𝑡 = 0.8 𝜇s is excluded as a hot-gas
pressure-wave artifact; see Section 6.6 for justification).

The JWL gas EOS parameters used in this work are evaluated at the partial mass density 𝜌𝑔,0 =

Running the JWL configuration on the locked geometry of Section 6.1 produces a thermally-

This 𝐷 2 value falls 22 % below the experimental tail slope of approximately 7.66 km/s computed
from the Erikson digitization of the data points spanning 𝑡 = 32.32 and 48.5 𝜇s. The discrepancy
is also visible in the absolute trajectory: the JWL burn front reaches only 𝑥 ≈ 18 cm at 𝑡 = 48.5 𝜇s,

data point. Inspection of the local slope along the JWL trajectory shows that the velocity is in fact
decelerating with time, from approximately 7.16 km/s in the post-DDT interval 𝑡 ∈ [24, 34] 𝜇s,
to 6.96 km/s in [34, 44] 𝜇s, to 6.91 km/s in [44, 54] 𝜇s. A late-time fit to “the second half of the

The slow deceleration under JWL is attributed to two compounding effects. The JWL EOS’s
empirical form does not produce sufficiently hot product temperatures to drive vigorous reaction in
the unburnt zone ahead of the burn front, leaving the wave inadequately supported as it propagates
into the granular bed. Compounding this, the fixed 0.05 cm mesh under-resolves the detonation
reaction zone — a structure that physically extends over only a few mean free paths in fully-reacted
HMX product gas — so the integrator artificially smears the steepest gradients and damps the wave
further. Both deficiencies are addressed by the changes documented in Section 6.3.
The JWL fixed-mesh result therefore represents an unsatisfactory starting point: the model predicts a
propagation regime that is both qualitatively (decelerating rather than asymptotic) and quantitatively
(22 % deficit) inconsistent with the Price–Bernecker data. The motivation for the EOS update is
established by this gap.

6.3. TIGER and TIGER+AMR Improvements

The TIGER thermochemical-equilibrium gas EOS [14–16] is introduced in Chapter 4 as an alterna-
tive to the empirical JWL form. Switching to TIGER for the “fig3” benchmark1 is a single-parameter
namelist change (ieos_gas_in = 0 → 1 plus identification of the appropriate HMX product-gas
table), with all other geometry, boundary, and material parameters held at the values of Section 6.1.
The result is a substantial change in burn-front propagation behavior.

TIGER Fixed Mesh

Running the TIGER configuration on the same fixed 600-node mesh produces 𝑡ign = 3.80 𝜇s,
𝑡DDT = 12.00 𝜇s and 𝑥 DDT = 0.551 cm, and an asymptotic detonation velocity 𝐷 2 = 7.204 km/s
— a 6 % deficit relative to the experimental tail slope, down from the JWL deficit of 22 %. The
induction time is approximately 0.9 𝜇s longer than under JWL and the DDT transition occurs 2.1 𝜇s
later, but at nearly the same spatial position (0.55 cm versus 0.45 cm); the EOS update therefore
shifts the early-time response only slightly while substantially improving the post-DDT propagation
regime.
The mechanism is a physical one. The JCZ3 intermolecular potential underlying the TIGER table
predicts product gas temperatures behind the burn front in the range 4500–5500 K for fully reacted
HMX, compared with 2500–3500 K predicted by the JWL form at comparable conditions. The
hotter products drive more vigorous heat transfer into the unburnt grains ahead of the burn front,
which raises their surface temperature, accelerates the local Arrhenius reaction rate, and feeds

gas back into the reaction zone. The result is a self-sustaining propagation regime closer to the
Chapman–Jouguet ideal: the thermochemical equilibrium picture captures the energy partitioning
between product gas and reactive flow more accurately than the empirical JWL fit alone.

The trajectory under TIGER reaches 𝑥 ≈ 22.8 cm at the latest experimental data point (𝑡 = 48.5 𝜇s),
reducing the position deficit from JWL’s 7 cm to approximately 2.5 cm. The local-slope analysis

the wave, but it does not by itself sharpen the spatial discretization needed to reach the asymptotic
𝐷 2.

TIGER with Adaptive Mesh Refinement

The adaptive mesh refinement scheme of Chapter 3 is applied to the same TIGER configuration by
setting imesh_in = 1. The base node count of 600 is preserved to isolate the AMR effect from
any base-mesh resolution change; the adaptive scheme then refines internally where the gradient
indicators trigger, producing a maximum node count of 759 (min/max range 610–759 over the
simulation) and the finest locally-active spacing on the order of 10−4 cm. The result is a further
improvement to 𝐷 2 = 7.334 km/s, a 4 % deficit relative to experimental — a meaningful but smaller
refinement than the EOS update provided.

The character of the improvement is qualitatively distinct from the EOS contribution. Under
fixed mesh the burn-front velocity decelerates with time as the under-resolved reaction zone is
artificially smeared. Under AMR the velocity instead accelerates, with local slopes 6.74, 7.13,
7.27, and 7.32 km/s in successive intervals, approaching the experimental asymptote from below
as the wave has progressively more time to develop within the resolved reaction zone. This is the
qualitative signature of a properly-resolved detonation: the profile has time to converge to a self-
similar structure that propagates at the Chapman–Jouguet velocity, rather than artificially-smearing
as the wave advances.

Counterintuitive Findings

The Mie–Grüneisen Γ multiplier 𝑈𝑟,EOS correlates negatively with peak pressure (𝑟 = −0.40,
panel (e) of Figure 6-2). Larger Γ produces more thermal pressure for given (𝜌, 𝑇) and would be
expected to increase 𝑃max . The negative correlation suggests that the additional thermal pressure
couples into the solid’s own internal energy as a relief mechanism during compaction — energy
that would otherwise build up as mixture pressure goes instead into solid thermal expansion,
lowering the peak 𝑃mix attained at the burn-front location. This finding is consistent with the Mie–
Grüneisen correction documented in Section 2.3 and supports the focused EOS sweep presented in
the gas-phase EOS sensitivity study of Chapter 5.
The ignition-zone length ℓ1,ign correlates only weakly with the run-to-detonation distance 𝑥 DDT
(𝑟 = +0.37, panel (d) of Figure 6-2; rank 18 of the 18 significant pairs). The positive sign
indicates that a wider ignition zone modestly extends the spatial reach of the boundary condition’s
influence into the bed, pushing DDT slightly further from the wall, but the correlation magnitude
is small relative to the dominant burn-rate and porosity drivers. The ignition-zone gas temperature
𝑇𝑔,ign and the ignition pressure 𝑝 𝑔,ign do not appear in the significant-pair list at all under the new
defaults, indicating that within the ±10 % perturbation range sampled here, the BC-temperature and
BC-pressure parameters are not influential drivers of the run-up dynamics. The discrete output-
timestep banding visible in panel (d) of Figure 6-2 is a reporting-resolution artifact, not a physical
phenomenon (see in-figure footnote).

6.5. Calibrated Configuration from the LHS Sweep

The same LHS draw that supports the sensitivity analysis above can be used directly as a calibration
tool: among the thirty samples, the one whose burn-front trajectory most closely matches the
experimental data identifies a parameter set that reproduces the benchmark within physically
defensible uncertainty bounds. Because each sample lies in the interior of the Table 6-2 ranges,
an LHS-identified calibration carries the rigor of having been drawn from a designed-experiment
ensemble rather than tuned by hand.

For each LHS sample, the model burn-front position was interpolated linearly at each of the

acceptable parameter sets.

data points. The runner-up (LH sample 12, RMS 1.204 cm) and third-ranked (LH sample 19, RMS
1.205 cm) samples differ from LH sample 15 by less than 3 % in RMS, indicating that the calibration
is a defensible envelope rather than a pinpoint match. Table 6-4 lists the sampled parameter values
of LH sample 15 alongside the deck nominals, with the percent deviation shown in the rightmost

19 % reduction in particle radius), 𝑈𝑟,rjotm = 1.19 (a 19 % upward shift in the compaction-viscosity
multiplier), and 𝑈𝑟,burn = 1.13 (a 14 % upward shift in the burn-rate prefactor). The compaction
viscosity 𝑈𝑟,rjotm , the hot-spot nucleation multiplier 𝑈𝑟,dreact , and the heat-transfer factor ℎfact all
take values modestly above nominal, but the LHS analysis of Section 6.4 demonstrates that none
of these exerts measurable influence on the response, at least for this experiment.

6.6. Limitations and Boundary-Condition Discussion

The agreement of Section 6.5 is calibration to within an experimental envelope, not a perfect match.
Three systematic limitations of the present model emerge from the LHS analysis: an early-time
disagreement attributable to the boundary-condition formulation, a small set of LHS samples whose
parameter combinations failed to bootstrap ignition, and a late-time 𝐷 2 deficit that motivates the
adaptive mesh refinement work of Chapter 3. Each of these is discussed below, alongside an
integrator-hardening commit added during the LHS sweep that will improve the robustness of
future sensitivity studies.

The First Experimental Data Point and the IBC=1 Formulation

Figure 6-3 shows that the leftmost experimental data point at (𝑡 = 0.8 𝜇s, 𝑥 = 1 cm) sits above and
ahead of every model burn-front trajectory in the 30-sample LHS bundle. The disagreement is
robust across the parameter envelope: even at the most aggressive corner of the LHS draw (large
𝑈𝑟,burn , small 𝑎 𝑝 , high 𝜙 𝑠,0 , hot 𝑇𝑔,ign ), no parameter combination within the perturbed ranges
produces a model burn front reaching 𝑥 = 1 cm by 𝑡 = 0.8 𝜇s. As discussed in Section 6.5, the
implied flame velocity over the interval (0, 0.8 𝜇s) is 12.5 km/s, which exceeds the HMX detonation
velocity of approximately 9 km/s at full theoretical maximum density. No physical flame propagates
that fast in HMX.
The most plausible interpretation is that this earliest gauge response represents the ionization-pin
signature of the hot-gas pressure wave entering the granular bed at 𝑡 = 0, not the arrival of the
flame front itself. Price–Bernecker–style ionization pins trigger on the passage of ionized gas rather
than on solid consumption; the hot-gas burst from the IBC=1 boundary will produce a forward-
running pressure wave that ionizes the gas phase well ahead of any chemistry-driven flame. The
XDDT model’s “burn front” definition (the rightmost node where 𝜙 𝑠 < 0.98 𝜙 𝑠,0 ) tracks actual
solid consumption and is therefore physically distinct from the ionization-pin response. Excluding
this first data point from the calibration metric is methodologically appropriate, and the consistency
of the disagreement across the parameter envelope confirms that this is not a tuning failure but a
recognition that the model’s burn-front diagnostic does not measure the same observable as the
experimental gauge in the first millisecond.
The IBC=1 formulation itself contributes a second-order limitation. The boundary condition
imposes an instantaneous, spatially uniform hot-gas region of length ℓ1,ign at temperature 𝑇𝑔,ign . In
contrast, the actual hot-gas penetration in the Price–Bernecker apparatus is a transient pressure wave

 90

with finite rise time and a depth-graded temperature profile that falls off into the bed. The calibrated
value ℓ1,ign = 0.56 cm (Section 6.5, +12.5 % from nominal) is therefore not an arbitrary tuning
but the LHS sweep’s best attempt to reproduce the experimental ionization-pin response within
the available step-function BC. A more physically faithful BC formulation, with a time-dependent
hot-gas profile reflecting the experimental hot-gas penetration geometry, would relax the need for
this nominal-value adjustment; such a formulation is reserved for future work.

Late-Time End-of-Bed Stiffness

Of the 30 LHS samples, five (LH samples 9, 15, 16, 19, and 29) tripped integrator sentinels in
the late-time post-DDT regime (𝑡 > 47 𝜇s in all cases): LH sample 9 hit TIMESTEP_UNDERFLOW
at 𝑡 = 56.5 𝜇s, while LH samples 15, 16, 19, and 29 hit CONVERGENCE_FAILURE between 47 and
53 𝜇s. In every case the trip occurred well after DDT had completed and after all six response
variables (𝑡 ign , 𝑡DDT , 𝑥DDT , 𝐷 2 , 𝑡5 cm , 𝑃max ) had been recorded; the failure is therefore not a
calibration concern, and the LHS Pearson analysis of Section 6.4 uses all thirty cells.
The five tripping cells share a common operating regime — the detonation has propagated through
most of the 30 cm bed and is encountering the right-boundary reflective condition. At the very
finest timesteps the integrator’s adaptive controller cannot stably advance the steepest gradients in
the reaction zone adjacent to the boundary, and the sentinel halts protect against silent integration
of unreliable solutions. The trips do not indicate a problem with the underlying physics or with the
calibration; they indicate that the fixed-mesh discretization is at its useful limit at the trailing end
of the simulation window.
A more capable adaptive-mesh treatment of the right boundary (Chapter 3) is expected to extend
the integrable bed length without sentinel intervention; the late-time stiffness under fixed mesh is
documented as future work. The adjacent question of post-DDT tree-to-tree divergence is taken up
in Section 6.7. For the purposes of the present calibration, the late-time trips have no operational
impact: all 30 cells produce complete response data, the LHS Pearson analysis runs cleanly, and
the calibrated configuration of Section 6.5 (LH sample 15) is one of the trip cells but only in its
𝑡 > 47 𝜇s tail, where the trajectory has long since left the experimental data window.

Late-Time Detonation Velocity and Resolution

The fixed-mesh result of Section 6.3 produces a late-time detonation velocity 𝐷 2 ≈ 7.20 km/s,
compared with the experimental tail slope of approximately 7.66 km/s — a 6 % deficit. This deficit
persists across the LHS envelope: the calibrated configuration (LH sample 15) produces 𝐷 2 values
within ±4 % of the fixed-mesh value, and the LHS Pearson analysis identifies no single parameter
that closes the deficit. The nature of the residual is therefore not a parameter calibration issue but
a numerical resolution issue: the fixed 0.05 cm grid spacing is at the limit of resolving the steepest
gradients in the HMX detonation reaction zone, and the finite-resolution discretization yields a
slightly smeared detonation that propagates below the converged value.
This conclusion is supported by the adaptive-mesh result presented in Chapter 3, which on the
same boundary condition and material parameters as the fixed-mesh case produces 𝐷 2 ≈ 7.33 km/s,

 91

closing the deficit from 6 % to 4 %. The residual 4 % gap after AMR is consistent with the calibrated
parameter envelope of Table 6-4 and reflects the combined effect of small calibration uncertainties
(chiefly in the burn-rate prefactor 𝑈𝑟,burn ) rather than further resolution shortfalls. The progression
from JWL fixed (−22 %), through TIGER fixed (−6 %), to TIGER adaptive (−4 %) maps onto the
three-step revision narrative of this report: the EOS update is the dominant physical improvement,
with mesh refinement providing the final asymptotic refinement.

Integrator Hardening

An earlier exploratory sweep (predating the integrator-hardening commit) had several LHS cells
that progressed slowly through the post-DDT phase at sub-picosecond timesteps without producing
summary files; in those cases the simulation did not crash but neither did it gracefully report a
failure. The runtime simply continued attempting integration without indicating that the cell had
exceeded any meaningful resolution limit.

These cells motivated a subsequent integrator-hardening commit applied to both the CGS and
SI code trees. The hardening adds three explicit halt conditions: a retry-count limit (𝑁retry > 50
implies a convergence failure for the current step), a timestep floor (Δ𝑡 < 10−12 s implies underflow),
and a stuck-physics detector (no measurable burn-front advance over a sliding 100-step window
once the simulation has progressed past the typical induction window). When any of these trip,
the integrator now writes a sentinel-marked summary file with the response variables observed to
date, prints a structured “ERROR: halted at t=...Reason: ...” line in the run log, and
exits with a non-zero status code.

from an earlier sweep produced an essentially identical 14 % 𝐷 2 gap at the same single-branch,
TIGER, fixed-mesh setup), establishing the gap as a characteristic of the post-DDT regime in the
SI tree rather than a one-off artifact. The asymmetric placement of the early-regime agreement and
the post-DDT divergence narrowed the candidate explanations to physics modules that activate only
after DDT, motivating the diagnostic campaign documented in the remainder of this section.

Diagnostic Chain

The post-DDT divergence was investigated through a sequence of controlled-state diagnostic exper-
iments designed to localize the responsible code path. The diagnostic chain ultimately attributed
the divergence to a floating-point (FP) conditioning artifact: under double-precision arithmetic,

 93

the same algebraic expression evaluated on operands of very different magnitudes accumulates
rounding error differently in the two unit systems, even though the algebra and the algorithm are
identical. This is a particularly hard class of defect to find when converting a code from CGS to
SI because no dimensional audit, no algebraic review, and no algorithmic comparison will reveal
it: the physics, the math, and the code are all correct, and the discrepancy lives strictly in the
floating-point regime.

Comparing the source between the two trees. A direct comparison of the Mie–Grüneisen
and gas-EOS parameters between the SI and CGS source files confirmed that the algebraic forms,
operation order, and intermediate variable definitions are identical. Where the two trees execute
the same expression on the same inputs, they produce the same output.

TIGER table identity. A direct comparison of the TIGER-generated SESAME tables consumed
by each build (after the appropriate unit conversion) showed agreement to floating-point precision
(maximum relative difference 2.2 × 10−16 , the double-precision rounding floor). The tabulated
thermodynamic surfaces are not the source of the divergence.

Mie–Grüneisen parameter sensitivity. An 𝑛 + 1 Latin Hypercube sweep over the five Mie–
Grüneisen parameters (gov, Ur_solid_eos, akto, ans, gamo), run in both trees at matched phys-
ical states (DAKOTA seed-locked, unit-converted bounds), identified gamo (the reference solid
density 𝜌0 ) as the dominant driver of the SI/CGS divergence: the Pearson correlation coefficient
between gamo and 𝐷 2,SI /𝐷 2,CGS is 𝑟 ≈ +0.94; the corresponding correlations for 𝑃max,SI /𝑃max,CGS
and 𝑣 deflag,SI /𝑣 deflag,CGS are +0.87 and +0.93. Other Mie–Grüneisen parameters showed no com-
parable signal. The Mie–Grüneisen cold-curve algebra was thereby implicated, but ruled out as the
algorithmic source by the next step.

Bit-level instrumentation. Both trees were instrumented to dump every intermediate quantity
in the solid-phase equation-of-state evaluation—the chain of 35 algebraic terms by which the solid
pressure, temperature, and internal energy are computed from the local volume fraction, mass,
and energy state—at a matched post-DDT state (step 140, cell 33; 𝑡 = 14 𝜇s, 𝑥 = 1.6027 cm).
Where the inputs to this chain matched between the two trees, every intermediate matched as well;
the chain itself is faithful to its inputs. The first quantity to differ between the trees was DM_nod,
the local partial solid density carried as part of the integrator state, with a relative difference of
9.3 × 10−4 ; the local internal energy state EM_nod had drifted by ∼ 18 %. These are quantities
accumulated by the time integrator from many steps’ worth of differential updates, not values
produced by the equation-of-state chain itself. The conclusion is that the divergence lives in the
accumulated integrator state, not in the equation-of-state evaluation that consumes it.

RHS time-derivative dump. Having localized the divergence to the integrator state, the next step
asked whether the per-step time-derivative supplied by the right-hand side (RHS) routine pde.f90
was already biased between the two trees, or whether the bias arose only from accumulation. An

 94

instrumentation pass on pde.f90 dumped the full time-derivative vector immediately after each
call. The first slots to diverge were FT_BIGEG, FT_BIGEM, and FT_fh—the gas-solid energy-
coupling derivatives—with a per-call relative difference of ∼ 5.86 × 10−6 at the very first integrator
step (𝑡 = 0.1 𝜇s), about twelve times the input-precision baseline of ∼ 5 × 10−7 . The relevance is
that the bias is not an accumulation effect that grows over many steps; it is present from the first
step and originates in the per-call RHS evaluation of the gas-solid energy-coupling expressions.

Kahan-summation falsification. The natural hypothesis at this stage is that the bias is a sum-of-
many-terms problem: the gas-solid energy-coupling expressions add several quantities of different
magnitudes, and in double-precision arithmetic the small terms can be partially absorbed by larger
ones. Kahan-compensated summation [26] is a standard technique that addresses exactly this case
by carrying a small auxiliary correction variable that captures the rounding error discarded at each
addition and folds it back into the next addition. The hypothesis was tested directly: about 45
lines per tree of Kahan-style summation applied identically to both, in the specific energy-coupling
expressions identified by the previous step. The SI/CGS ratios were unchanged to three significant
figures. The falsification clarified the mechanism: the per-call asymmetry is not introduced by the
addition order, it is already present in the operands themselves before any summation occurs. SI
evaluates 𝜌 · 𝐸 · 𝑣-type quantities at ∼ 1010 , CGS at ∼ 103 , and the difference is structural to the
magnitude regime; Kahan summation cannot correct a bias that has already entered its inputs.

Pre-DDT bit-equivalence. An important positive finding emerged from the burn-front trajec-
tory comparison. Through the DDT transition (𝑡 ≤ 14 𝜇s), the two trees produce bit-identical
burn-front positions (𝑥BF agrees to four decimal places). Divergence onset is at 𝑡 ≈ 16–18 𝜇s, im-
mediately after DDT completes, and grows multiplicatively to ∼ 9 % by 𝑡 = 25 𝜇s. The early-time
physics—compaction, ignition, deflagration—is genuinely unit-system invariant; only the post-
DDT detonation feedback regime amplifies the operand-magnitude asymmetry to a macroscopic
gap.

Architectural Resolution

Three numerical-methods fixes were considered to close the post-DDT gap: (i) Kahan-compensated
summation in the energy update—falsified above; (ii) local nondimensionalization of the energy-
coupling expressions—principled but invasive (∼ 80–120 lines per tree of careful operand rescaling,
with reference-quantity choice as a new failure mode); and (iii) quad-precision intermediates—tied
to specific compilers, with a runtime cost penalty.
After evaluation we adopted a fourth, architectural option: XDDT internally uses CGS as the
validated compute kernel; SI conventions are used at the I/O boundary (input decks, output
files, and the XCHEM-handoff interface). The fix has been implemented and validated against the
calibrated configuration; the rationale:
 1. The 30-year CGS validation surface is preserved bit-for-bit; no historical regression coverage
 is invalidated.

 95

 2. The post-DDT operand magnitudes that trigger the FP-conditioning artifact only appear in
 the SI regime; using CGS internally side-steps the artifact at its source rather than fighting it
 expression-by-expression.
 3. The XCHEM/XDDT coupling, which is file-based and offline (XDDT reads an XCHEM out-
 put file as initial condition), is naturally unit-translated at the read boundary. XCHEM remains
 SI-internal in its own (conductive-energy) regime, where no analogous FP-conditioning issue
 arises; XDDT remains CGS-internal in its (full DDT) regime. Both codes are native in their
 own units; the unit conversion is a single layer at the I/O boundary.
 4. The user-facing experience is unchanged: input decks remain in SI, output files remain in SI,
 all reports and figures remain in SI. The only artifact of the architectural choice is the I/O
 conversion layer, which is straightforward and admits round-trip identity testing.

Local nondimensionalization—rescaling every operand to O (1) using reference quantities (𝜌0 , 𝑇0 ,
𝑃0 , 𝐿 0 , 𝑡0 ) chosen at problem setup, so the numerical scheme operates on uniformly-conditioned
operands regardless of unit system—remains the principled methodology for preventing this class
of unit-conversion defect in future codes. For XDDT as it stands, the internal-CGS / presentation-SI
architecture is the chosen resolution.

7. VALIDATION AGAINST MCAFEE SHOT B-9036 (TIGER EOS)

This chapter presents the validation of the XDDT code with the TIGER thermochemical equilibrium
EOS against the McAfee Shot B-9036 experimental data [8]. Unlike the fixed-mesh JWL validation
presented in Ref. [1] and the EOS comparison in Chapter 4, this validation uses the full adaptive
mesh with the TIGER EOS and includes recalibration of the model parameters to achieve the best
match to the experimental burn front trajectory.

7.1. Experimental Configuration

The experimental configuration is identical to that described in Section 6.1 of the first report [1]:
McAfee Shot B-9036 used 65% TMD Class A HMX (𝑑¯ ≈ 170 𝜇m) confined in a maraging steel
tube with a 12.7 mm inner diameter. A piston driven by low-density HMX propellant impacted
the granular bed at approximately 87 m/s. Ionization pins embedded along the tube recorded the
arrival times of the compaction wave (capped pins at 2–7 MPa threshold) and the detonation wave
(uncapped ionization pins).
For this simulation, the bed length was extended from 30 cm (used in the first report for computa-
tional efficiency) to 38 cm, which is the estimated physical bed length based on McAfee’s Figure 3.
The longer bed provides additional propagation distance for the detonation wave after it overtakes
the compaction front, allowing a more accurate determination of 𝐷 2 .

7.2. Crystal Density Consistency

The TIGER thermochemical equilibrium table was generated using an HMX crystal density of 𝛾𝑠0 =
1.905 g/cm3 , which is the commonly accepted value from modern crystallographic measurements.
McAfee et al. [8] used 𝛾𝑠0 = 1.89 g/cm3 in their analysis, a value that was standard in the 1980s
literature. The first SAND report [1] followed the McAfee convention.
To ensure thermodynamic consistency between the solid-phase properties and the gas-phase EOS
table, the crystal density in the XDDT input was changed from 1.89 to 1.905 g/cm3 for all TIGER
EOS simulations. This changes the initial solid partial density from

       𝜌 𝑠0 = 𝜙 𝑠0 𝛾𝑠0 = 0.65 × 1.89 = 1.2285 g/cm3    −→   0.65 × 1.905 = 1.2383 g/cm3 .       (7.1)

The 0.8% increase in 𝜌 𝑠0 has a correspondingly small effect on the compaction dynamics and does
not alter the qualitative DDT behavior. For the JWL simulations presented elsewhere in this report,
the original McAfee value 𝛾𝑠0 = 1.89 g/cm3 is retained for consistency with the first report.

7.3. Calibration

The compaction-burn coefficient was recalibrated to 𝐶𝛼 = 75.5 (from the JWL value of 𝐶𝛼 = 75)
to optimize the match between the computed burn front trajectory and the McAfee ionization pin
data. The compaction time constant was set to 𝜏1 = 1.2 × 108 , matching the value used in the first
report.

The calibration was performed on the 200-node adaptive mesh with the TIGER EOS and a 38 cm
bed length. The objective was to match the burn front arrival times at the ionization pin locations,
with particular emphasis on the steady-state detonation region where the experimental data are
most reliable.

The small increase in 𝐶𝛼 (from 75 to 75.5, a 0.7% change) compensates for the combined effects of
the EOS change (TIGER vs. JWL), the crystal density change (1.905 vs. 1.89 g/cm3 ), and the longer
bed (38 vs. 30 cm). That such a small recalibration suffices is an indication that the compaction-burn
coefficient is a robust parameter that absorbs the leading-order model uncertainties.

7.4.2. Compaction Wave Velocity

The XDDT code tracks two definitions of the compaction front. The stress precursor (denoted
𝑥 vel in the output) identifies the rightmost node where the solid velocity exceeds a threshold
(500 cm/s = 5 m/s); this detects the leading edge of the elastic disturbance propagating into the
undisturbed bed, and is directly comparable to McAfee’s capped-pin measurements, which trigger
at 2–7 MPa closing pressure. The density front (denoted 𝑥 𝜙 ) identifies the rightmost node where
the solid volume fraction exceeds 𝜙 𝑠0 by more than 0.01; this detects where the bed has physically
compacted.

 101

Between 𝑡 = 270 and 290 𝜇s—after the initial piston-startup transient has decayed—the stress
precursor propagates at approximately 320 m/s and the density front at approximately 300 m/s,
giving an average compaction wave velocity of ∼310 m/s. This is in close agreement with the
McAfee capped-pin measurement of 309 m/s. The overall simulation-averaged velocity (∼350 m/s)
is higher because it includes the initial transient, during which the piston accelerates from rest and
launches a stronger-than-steady disturbance into the bed.

7.4.3. Solid Volume Fraction Profiles

The solid volume fraction profiles presented in Figure 4-4 (Section 4.5) illustrate the compaction
and detonation wave structure at successive times. During the deflagration phase, the compaction
wave runs ahead of the burn front, compacting the bed from 𝜙 𝑠 = 0.65 to 𝜙 𝑠 ≈ 0.86. At DDT, the
burn front catches the compaction front. Once the detonation forms, it propagates at supersonic
velocity and immediately outruns the subsonic compaction wave, which cannot exceed the sound
speed in the unreacted bed. Beyond the DDT point, the detonation front propagates directly into
the undisturbed 𝜙 𝑠 = 0.65 (65% TMD) material; the compaction wave is effectively replaced by
the detonation shock.

7.5. Discussion: Detonation Velocity

The computed detonation velocity of 𝐷 2 ≈ 7.0 km/s exceeds the McAfee measurement of 𝐷 2 =
6.36 km/s by approximately 10%.

McAfee et al. [8] defined two distinct detonation velocities based on the material through which
the detonation propagates:

 • 𝐷 1 = 8.19 km/s: the detonation velocity in the compacted “plug” material at approximately
 93% TMD. This was measured from ionization pins at 𝑥 = 99, 125, and 150 mm, all within
 the compacted zone.
 • 𝐷 2 = 6.36 km/s: the detonation velocity in the undisturbed 65% TMD bed, measured from
 ionization pins beyond the point where the detonation overtakes the compaction wave.

 102

In the XDDT simulation, the detonation initiates behind the compaction front in material that has
been compacted to approximately 86% TMD. Once formed, the supersonic detonation immediately
outruns the subsonic compaction wave and propagates into undisturbed 65% TMD material for the
remainder of the simulation. The computed 𝐷 2 therefore corresponds to a detonation in the virgin
bed, the same regime as McAfee’s 𝐷 2 measurement.

The TIGER CJ detonation velocity at the virgin density 𝜌0 = 0.65 × 1.905 = 1.2383 g/cm3 is
𝐷 CJ = 6.676 km/s (Section 4.7). The computed 𝐷 2 ≈ 7.0 km/s slightly exceeds this ideal value,
likely because the detonation has not fully relaxed from its overdriven state at birth to the steady
CJ condition within the available propagation distance.

The experimental 𝐷 2 = 6.36 km/s, on the other hand, falls below the ideal CJ velocity. This is a
well-known consequence of lateral expansion: in the McAfee experiment, the detonation products
expand radially through the confining maraging steel tube, diverting energy away from the axial
propagation direction. This energy loss reduces the effective driving pressure behind the detonation
front and lowers the propagation velocity below the ideal 1D CJ value. The effect scales with the
ratio of the reaction-zone length to the tube diameter; for the 12.7 mm tube used by McAfee,
the velocity deficit is consistent with published diameter-effect data for granular HMX at similar
densities.

The 1D XDDT code cannot capture this lateral-expansion effect. In the planar geometry assumed
by the model, all detonation-product energy drives the wave forward, producing a velocity that
approaches the ideal CJ value from above. The ∼10% overshoot relative to experiment is therefore
a geometric limitation of the 1D formulation, not a deficiency in the TIGER EOS or the DDT model.
Incorporating lateral losses—either through a quasi-2D correction [27] or a full 2D simulation—
would be expected to reduce the computed 𝐷 2 toward the experimental value.

8. CONCLUSIONS

The adaptive mesh refinement capability of the XDDT code was restored to full functionality by
replacing the over-complicated parent–child coupling logic with a simplified global level-array
approach compatible with the XCHEM prethermal ignition code. The initial grid construction
was redesigned to match the XCHEM interface-refinement strategy, enabling future initializa-
tion of DDT simulations from prethermal ignition solutions. An adaptive mesh convergence
study demonstrated that the induction time converges to the fixed-mesh reference at 200 start-
ing nodes (agreement within 0.7%), and that the adaptive mesh produces a detonation velocity
(𝐷 2 = 6.23 km/s) closer to the McAfee experimental value (6.36 km/s) than the 721-node fixed
mesh (𝐷 2 = 6.12 km/s), because the adaptive mesh concentrates resolution at the detonation
front.

A TIGER thermochemical equilibrium equation of state was implemented as a tabulated alternative
to the JWL gas-phase EOS. The TIGER EOS computes the gas-phase thermodynamic properties
from first-principles equilibrium chemistry at each density–temperature point, eliminating the
constant-𝑐 𝑣 assumption, the polynomial isentrope approximation, and the fixed-density calibration
inherent in the JWL form. On a fixed 721-node mesh, the TIGER EOS produces shorter induction
times (469 vs. 503 𝜇s) due to more physical gas temperatures in the compacted region, and higher
detonation velocities (6.85 vs. 6.12 km/s) due to differences in the thermodynamic description of
the detonation products at the initial 65% TMD bed density.

A two-part gas-phase EOS sensitivity study was performed on the McAfee configuration with the
721-node fixed mesh. The TIGER EOS tables used by the DDT code are explosive-products tables,
each generated from a specified reactant using a chosen thermochemical library and gas-phase
EOS model. In the first study, the reactant was held at HMX while the table was regenerated
for three thermochemical libraries (JCZS, JCZS3, XDB) and three gas-phase EOS models (BKW,
JCZ3, HBBS); the DDT observables spread by only ∼3% in 𝑡DDT and 𝐷 2 , with the library choice
dominating the timing and the EOS-model choice dominating 𝐷 2 . In the second study, the DDT
model parameters were held fixed at their HMX-calibrated values and only the EOS table was
swapped— with the table regenerated from each of ten different reactants (HMX, RDX, CL-20,
PETN, TNT, Comp B, PBX-9501, PBX-9502, TATB, XTX-8003). This produced a much larger
spread: ∼70% in induction timing (𝑡 ign from 368 to 639 𝜇s) but only ∼10% in 𝐷 2 (6.24 to
6.88 km/s). Induction timing is therefore first-order controlled by which reactant underlies the EOS
table, whereas 𝐷 2 is constrained primarily by the Chapman–Jouguet state at the bed partial density
and is relatively insensitive to the choice of reactant. The EOS-table reactant is the largest EOS-
related uncertainty in DDT timing, an order of magnitude larger than the library-or-EOS-model
choice at fixed reactant, and will be a first-order consideration for the planned XCHEM–XDDT
coupling.

The primary validation case for XDDT is the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition experiment [2]
(digitized as Fig. 3 of Baer–Nunziato 1986 [3]), which most closely mirrors the thermal-source
scenarios that motivate the planned XCHEM–XDDT coupling. Chapter 6 documents the calibrated
configuration’s reproduction of the Price–Bernecker burn-front and detonation-front trajectories.
During that work, a post-DDT divergence between the SI and CGS builds at the calibrated con-
figuration was diagnosed as a floating-point conditioning artifact in the gas–solid energy-coupling
expressions and resolved by an architectural choice: XDDT internally uses CGS as the validated
compute kernel, with SI conventions applied only at the I/O boundary (input decks, output files,
and the XCHEM-handoff interface). The compute engine is now byte-identical between the two
builds, and a single canonical TIGER table serves both. The diagnostic chain and the resolution
are documented in Section 6.7; the broader methodology lessons from the SI conversion campaign
are recorded in Section 2.5.1.

Geometric Limitation and Planned Follow-on

The XDDT code, as developed here and in the preceding report, solves the Baer–Nunziato two-
phase equations in a one-dimensional planar coordinate system. This is appropriate for the Price–
Bernecker thermal-ignition experiment [2] that serves as the primary validation case for the present
report (Chapter 6), for the McAfee piston-driven DDT experiment [8] used as a secondary valida-
tion (Chapter 7), and for the Sandusky–Baer ball propellant experiment [6] validated in the first
report [1], all of which are nominally planar configurations. It is not appropriate, however, for the
accidental thermal-threat scenarios that motivate the XCHEM–XDDT coupling effort: a hot metal
fragment embedded in a granular explosive sets up a spherical thermal source (if the fragment is
approximately spherical) or a cylindrical thermal source (if the fragment is approximately cylindri-
cal). In such scenarios, geometric divergence strongly affects both the heating of the explosive and
the subsequent DDT wave propagation, and a planar approximation cannot capture the 1/𝑟 or 1/𝑟 2
energy spreading that governs whether thermal ignition leads to a mild fizzle or a full detonation.
Extending XDDT to spherical and cylindrical coordinate systems is therefore a necessary next step
for the coupled hot-fragment ignition analysis. The extension requires geometric source terms
in the conservation equations, modification of the mesh construction (spherical geometry has an

 106

origin singularity that the current planar code does not need to handle), and development of suitable
validation cases. This work is the subject of a planned follow-on SAND report, which will also
document the XCHEM–XDDT coupling that is the ultimate goal of the development effort.

Principal Findings

The principal findings of this work are:
 1. The TIGER EOS provides a viable first-principles alternative to the empirical JWL form for
 DDT simulations, with the advantage of self-consistent thermodynamics across all density–
 temperature states.
 2. The adaptive mesh refinement, when properly functioning, improves detonation velocity
 accuracy relative to a fixed mesh of comparable total node count.
 3. The XCHEM-compatible initial grid enables direct coupling between the prethermal ignition
 and DDT codes, a critical step toward the goal of predicting the complete response of a
 thermally damaged explosive from ignition through detonation.
 4. The reactant underlying the explosive-products EOS table is the largest EOS-related uncer-
 tainty in DDT timing: swapping the reactant shifts 𝑡 DDT by up to ∼280 𝜇s, whereas varying
 the thermochemical library or gas-phase EOS model at fixed reactant shifts it by only ∼15 𝜇s.
 The detonation velocity 𝐷 2 is relatively insensitive to the choice of reactant at typical bed
 partial densities.
 5. The present 1D planar implementation is adequate for piston-driven DDT experiments but
 cannot model hot-fragment ignition geometries. Extension to spherical and cylindrical
 coordinates is required for coupled XCHEM–XDDT analysis of accidental thermal-threat
 scenarios and is deferred to a planned follow-on report.
