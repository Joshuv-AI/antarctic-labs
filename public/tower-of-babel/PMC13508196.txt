Satisfaction towards virtual consultation services and its associated factors among patients in Malaysian public primary care
Authors: Ihsan Abdul Razak, Surianti Sukeri, Noor Aman A. Hamid, Siti Nurbaya Shahrir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Ministry of Health (MOH) has introduced the Virtual Consultation Service (VCS) in government health clinics in Malaysia since 2019, to connect patients and healthcare providers virtually using video conferencing platforms such as Google Meet, MySejahtera and Health Information Exchange. Despite being the largest rollout of telemedicine in the country and MOH’s planning to scale up the service in public primary care, little is known about patient satisfaction and its contributing factors towards the VCS. Therefore this study aimed to determine patient satisfaction with VCS in public primary care and its associated factors.MethodsA cross sectional study was conducted among patients who attended outpatient service at selected government health clinics in West and East Malaysia using online data collection. A total of 411 patients were recruited through stratified and systematic random sampling by using a Malay-validated Telehealth Usability, Acceptability, and Satisfaction Questionnaire (TUASQ). Data were analysed using simple and multiple linear regression.ResultsPatients reported a relatively high mean satisfaction score for VCS (20.87 out of 25; SD = 3.61), indicating generally positive perceptions of the service. Adjusted model revealed that acceptability has emerged as the strongest predictor of satisfaction (β = 0.410, p < 0.001), followed by usability (β = 0.382, p < 0.001), explaining 89.4% of the variance in satisfaction scores.ConclusionsPatients were highly satisfied with VCS in Malaysian public primary care using multiple virtual platforms. Acceptability and usability of VCS were determinants of satisfaction, indicating that patients value ease of use, convenience, privacy and quality interaction beyond their demographic characteristics. Policymakers should leverage these study findings to guide the scale-up and optimisation of this health service in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14710-z.

Background

Telemedicine represents the convergence of communication and information technology, biomedical engineering, and clinical medicine, utilising computer multimedia, internet connectivity, and related digital technologies to deliver healthcare services remotely [1, 2]. Telemedicine encompasses several modalities and is commonly classified into three main categories: store-and-forward services, where clinical information is recorded and transmitted asynchronously; interactive services, which involve real-time, synchronous communication between two or more users; and telemonitoring, whereby healthcare providers remotely monitor patients using wearable devices or sensors [3, 4]. Some studies have additionally classified mobile health (mHealth) as a telemedicine modality, referring to smartphone-based applications designed to support health promotion, disease management, and well-being [5].

Telemedicine interactions may occur between patients and healthcare providers or among healthcare professionals, using various communication channels such as video conferencing, audio calls, text messaging, mobile applications, picture archiving systems, and personalised digital platforms [1]. While telemedicine was initially designed to facilitate professional-to-professional communication, the COVID-19 pandemic substantially accelerated direct patient–provider interactions via telemedicine due to movement restrictions and infection-control measures [3].

Historically, telemedicine was predominantly implemented in hospital-based specialties such as teledermatology, teleradiology, and teleneurosurgery, with early adoption dating back to the 1960s [6, 7]. However, over the past decade - particularly in the post-pandemic era - telemedicine adoption in primary care has increased substantially worldwide [8–10]. This trend reflects growing recognition of telemedicine’s potential to reduce congestion in healthcare facilities, improve efficiency across levels of care, and enhance equitable access to health services. As primary care serves as the backbone of national healthcare systems and acts as the first point of contact and gatekeeper for patients, integrating telemedicine into primary care is critical for achieving universal health coverage [11, 12]. Nevertheless, global telemedicine uptake remains uneven and is heavily influenced by regional healthcare infrastructure, digital readiness, and the presence of supportive regulatory frameworks [13, 14].

Telemedicine satisfaction in primary care

Patient satisfaction with telemedicine in primary care has become a major focus of recent research, although findings vary across settings and populations [15, 16]. Satisfaction is often regarded as a key indicator of successful telemedicine implementation and is commonly used as an outcome measure. However, there is no universally accepted definition of what constitutes patient satisfaction in the context of telemedicine, leading to inconsistencies across studies [15, 17].

Numerous studies conducted in countries such as Bahrain, Sweden, the United States, Saudi Arabia, and Malaysia have reported high levels of patient satisfaction with telemedicine services [18–22]. Notably, the majority of these studies were conducted during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, when movement restrictions and social distancing were in place. This pattern is highlighted in a systematic review that found nearly all of the 51 included studies on telemedicine satisfaction were conducted in 2020 and 2021, reporting high satisfaction levels largely attributed to convenience and cost savings [23].

As healthcare systems transition into the post-pandemic period, it is increasingly important to reassess patient satisfaction with telemedicine, as perceptions may evolve and the initial novelty of telemedicine may diminish over time [23–25]. Continuous patient feedback is therefore essential to inform service improvement and ensure sustainable telemedicine adoption.

Various instruments have been developed to measure patient satisfaction with telemedicine. A review of 53 studies found that the most frequently used telemedicine-specific instruments were the Telehealth Usability Questionnaire (TUQ) (19%), Telemedicine Satisfaction Questionnaire (TSQ) (13%), and Service User Technology Acceptability Questionnaire (SUTAQ) (5.5%). In contrast, more general technology evaluation tools included the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ), Questionnaire for User Interaction Satisfaction (QUIS), System Usability Scale (SUS), Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire (PSQ), and Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), each used in approximately 3.5–5.5% of studies [26].

Many of these instruments have been culturally adapted to suit local healthcare contexts while maintaining their theoretical foundations. In Malaysia, a validated Malay-language instrument - the Telehealth Usability, Acceptability and Satisfaction Questionnaire (TUASQ) - was recently developed to assess telemedicine implementation from both patient and provider perspectives. This questionnaire integrates constructs from TAM, TUQ, and the Health Belief Model (HBM) and comprises three domains: usability, acceptability, and satisfaction, capturing essential dimensions of user experience in telemedicine [27].

Factors associated with patient satisfaction

Multiple factors have been associated with patient satisfaction in telemedicine. Demographic characteristics such as age, gender, income, and education level have been reported to significantly influence satisfaction, although findings remain inconsistent across studies. Mason et al. (2022) reported significant associations (p < 0.05) between these demographic variables and telemedicine satisfaction, suggesting that they moderate dimensions such as perceived health benefits, monetary and non-monetary costs, and patient-centred care [28]. Conversely, other studies found no significant associations with demographic factors, except for age [22, 29].

Convenience, reduced travel time, and lower travel-related costs have consistently emerged as key drivers of patient satisfaction and are closely related to cost savings and usability [16, 19, 30]. Technical reliability also plays a critical role, with fewer technical issues during telemedicine consultations being significantly associated with higher satisfaction (p < 0.001), alongside factors such as privacy, comfort, and cost savings [29]. A systematic review conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic reported similar findings, identifying convenience, comfort, ease of access, safety, and reduced waiting time as major determinants of patient satisfaction [31].

Given this complexity, some authors have proposed conceptualising telemedicine satisfaction as a multi-dimensional construct influenced by four domains: (1) health benefits or outcomes, (2) patient-centred care related to acceptability, (3) monetary costs reflecting cost savings, and (4) non-monetary costs, such as ease of use and usability [28]. Consequently, comprehensive evaluation of telemedicine satisfaction should incorporate all four dimensions from the patient’s perspective.

Telemedicine in Malaysia

In Malaysia, the telemedicine journey began with the introduction of the Telemedicine Blueprint in 1997, followed by the establishment of the Telehealth Unit under the Ministry of Health (MOH) and the rollout of the Teleprimary Care (TPC) system in government health clinics in 2000 and 2005, respectively [32, 33]. Despite these early initiatives, telemedicine implementation faced substantial challenges, including infrastructural limitations, financial constraints, low service demand, suboptimal system design and data governance, lack of interoperability and standardisation, change management issues, and user-related factors [33–36].

Notably, evaluation studies on telemedicine in Malaysia have largely focused on healthcare provider perspectives, with limited emphasis on patient experiences [37–39]. Given that Malaysia’s public healthcare system, including primary care services, is fully tax-funded, patient satisfaction with telemedicine represents a crucial indicator for policy decision-making - particularly in primary care, which manages the highest patient workload.

The MOH has progressively rolled out Virtual Consultation Services (VCS) in selected government health clinics as part of its telemedicine strategy. Initially introduced as a proof-of-concept in five clinics in 2019, the service expanded to an additional 35 clinics during the COVID-19 pandemic to enhance healthcare accessibility and continuity [40, 41]. This initiative was further reinforced through the MOH Digitalisation Strategic Plan 2021–2025, aligned with the Malaysia Digital Economy Blueprint, which outlines four pillars of healthcare digitalisation over a five-year period [42, 43].

In 2022, the Family Health Division of the MOH revised the VCS guidelines to include seven service scopes: outpatient care, maternal and child health, pharmacy, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, and dietetic services [40]. As of 2024, 387 out of 1093 government health clinics nationwide offer VCS, with plans to expand coverage to 900 clinics by the end of the year [44].

Three main platforms are currently utilised for VCS: Google Meet, MyVAS/MySejahtera, and the Health Information Exchange (HIE) platform. MyVAS serves as a web-based system for healthcare providers, while MySejahtera is a mobile application for patients; both were developed by the MOH in 2020 and later enhanced to support virtual consultations [44]. The HIE platform, piloted in Negeri Sembilan since 2024, aims to enable seamless electronic medical record exchange between clinics and hospitals while integrating virtual consultation capabilities [36, 45].

Despite the widespread rollout of VCS since 2019, there is currently no known patient satisfaction survey focusing specifically on outpatient virtual consultations in Malaysian government health clinics. This represents a critical evidence gap, particularly as the MOH plans nationwide expansion of VCS under its digitalisation agenda. Furthermore, limited data exist on factors influencing patient satisfaction with VCS, which may hinder large-scale implementation if left unaddressed. As patients are the direct recipients of care, their satisfaction can directly affect service utilisation and health outcomes.

In response to these gaps, this study aims to evaluate patient satisfaction with VCS in government health clinics by determining the mean satisfaction score and identifying associated factors. The findings are expected to inform service improvement strategies and support sustainable uptake of virtual consultation services in Malaysian primary care.

Methods

Study design and location

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from July 2025 to October 2025. The study was carried out in government health clinics across West and East Malaysia. At the time of the study, a total of 468 government health clinics nationwide were offering VCS under the supervision of the Family Health Division, Ministry of Health Malaysia.

Study participants

The study population comprised patients who attended virtual outpatient consultations at government health clinics during the study period and fulfilled the eligibility criteria. Eligible participants were Malaysian citizens aged 18 years and above, who were able to read and understand Malay or English, and had at least one prior experience using virtual consultation services in government health clinics in Malaysia.

Sampling method and sample size

This study employed stratified random sampling across all Malaysian states, with each state serving as a stratum. The sampling frame consisted of 468 government health clinics nationwide offering VCS. Malaysia comprises 14 states, each with a different number of government health clinics; therefore, proportional allocation was applied by selecting approximately 20% of VCS-providing clinics within each state, to ensure fair representation of patients from each state. This was followed by systematic random sampling of eligible patients within the selected clinics. The sampling was conducted using a fixed interval (every fifth eligible patient) over a four-month data collection period. The stratum-specific sampling details including the number of patients recruited by stratum were provided (see Additional file 3).

Study participants were recruited with the assistance of a co-researcher who provided a list of liaison officers from each selected clinic. These liaison officers were contacted and briefed by members of the research team regarding the study procedures. The liaison officers then identified potential respondents from daily VCS appointment lists using systematic random sampling until the required sample size for each clinic was achieved.

The calculated minimum sample size was 407, based on a 95% confidence level and allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%. A total of 435 respondents completed the online survey. However, 24 responses were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in a final analysed sample of 411 participants.

Study instrument and variables

Data were collected using a structured proforma (see Additional file 1) and a questionnaire adapted from a validated instrument, namely Telehealth Usability, Acceptability, and Satisfaction Questionnaire (TUASQ) (see Additional file 2), developed by Kannadasan et al. (2024) [27]. This Malay-validated TUASQ demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.975. The Composite Reliability (CR) values for all constructs exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, while the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were above 0.50, indicating good convergent validity [27]. Permission to use the questionnaire was obtained from the original author.

The TUASQ consists of 17 items across three domains: usability (3 items), acceptability (9 items), and satisfaction (5 items). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Domain scores were calculated by averaging item scores within each domain, yielding a mean score for usability, acceptability, and satisfaction.

Each domain was treated as a continuous variable in this study. Demographic variables included age, gender, education level, and income level. Usability and acceptability were analysed as independent variables, alongside demographic characteristics. Acceptability was defined as the degree to which patients are willing to use and endorse VCS for their healthcare needs, while usability refers to the extent to which patients can effectively and efficiently use VCS to achieve their healthcare goals. The primary outcome variable was patient satisfaction, consistent with the extended Technology Acceptance Model (extended TAM) proposed by Kissi et al. (2020) [46]. Satisfaction was defined as the degree to which healthcare services meet patients’ expectations across various aspects of care.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted online. The TUASQ, Patient Information Sheet, and informed consent form were integrated into an electronic survey using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system. A unique REDCap survey link was distributed to eligible participants in the form of a URL or QR code after they agreed to participate.

Participants were required to review the Patient Information Sheet and provide electronic informed consent before proceeding to the survey. The estimated time required to complete the survey was approximately 10–15 min. In instances of low response rates, reminders were communicated to the respective liaison officers to encourage participation. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) and the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Sains Malaysia (HREC USM) prior to the commencement of data collection.

Statistical analysis

Data collected via REDCap were exported into IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise respondents’ demographic characteristics and to compute mean scores for usability, acceptability, and satisfaction. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarised as means and standard deviations (SD), based on their distribution.

Simple and multiple linear regression analysis were performed to examine associations between independent variables and the dependent variable (satisfaction). The strength and direction of associations were reported using beta coefficients (β), adjusted beta coefficients (adjusted β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.01 (two-tailed). All regression analyses satisfied the assumptions of linearity, independence of observations, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, and absence of multicollinearity.

Interpretation of final model

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that usability and acceptability scores were significant predictors of satisfaction. The model explained 89.4% of variation in satisfaction scores within the study sample. The relationship can be explained as below:

Every one-unit increase in acceptability is associated with a 0.41-unit rise in satisfaction (95% CI: 0.382, 0.438), after controlling for usability. Age, gender, income and education had no significant relationship with satisfaction of using VCS (p > 0.01).

Discussion

The majority of patients who attended virtual consultations at government health clinics reported generally favorable satisfaction levels, with a mean score of 20.87 out of 25 (SD = 3.61). This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Malaysia. For example, patients attending teleconsultations for diabetes care at a teaching hospital reported high satisfaction, with an overall PSQ-18 score of 78% [21]. Similarly, patients attending telemedicine visits at two private medical institutions reported an average satisfaction score of 5.72 [47]. Collectively, these findings suggest that patients in Malaysia are generally receptive to telemedicine services, regardless of the healthcare setting in which they receive care.

Other local studies have also reported positive perceptions of telemedicine and recommended collaborative efforts among stakeholders to enhance adoption. However, these studies primarily focused on healthcare providers’ perspectives rather than patients’ experiences [38, 39]. In contrast, the present study provides direct patient-reported evidence from public primary care settings, thereby addressing an important gap in the Malaysian telemedicine literature.

The findings of this study are also consistent with international evidence from Bahrain, Sweden, the United States, and Saudi Arabia, where most patients reported satisfaction with telehealth platforms, expressed willingness to continue using telemedicine, and recommended its use to others [18–20, 22]. It is important to note, however, that many of these studies were conducted during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, when telemedicine utilisation was strongly influenced by movement restrictions and social distancing measures. The current study, conducted in a post-pandemic context, therefore provides important evidence that high patient satisfaction with telemedicine persists beyond crisis-driven adoption, as suggested by a review [48].

Demographic factors associated with patient satisfaction

The mean age of respondents in this study was 37.49 years, indicating that virtual consultations in government health clinics are mainly utilized by patients around the observed mean age. The majority of respondents were female (78.1%), which may reflect greater availability among women - particularly those not formally employed - to participate in scheduled virtual consultations. Despite this demographic distribution, age (p = 0.086) and gender (p = 0.796) were not significant predictors of satisfaction.

Although some studies have suggested that age influences acceptance and satisfaction with telemedicine [22, 29], the present findings suggest that technology adoption may be more closely related to individual digital capability rather than chronological age. This is plausible in the current digital era, characterised by high smartphone ownership and widespread digital exposure across age groups. Similarly, gender did not influence satisfaction, supporting findings from previous studies that reported no significant association between gender and telemedicine satisfaction [22, 49].

More than 80% of respondents in this study belonged to the low-income group, defined as households earning less than MYR5,249 per month and classified as the bottom 40% (B40) according to the latest Malaysian income classification [50]. However, income level was not a significant determinant of satisfaction (p = 0.610). While Liu et al. (2022) reported higher satisfaction among patients covered by private insurance [49], the Malaysian public primary care system is fully tax-funded, allowing citizens to access virtual consultations without direct payment. This likely explains why income level did not influence satisfaction in this context. Nevertheless, individuals from lower-income groups may derive greater benefit from subsidised healthcare services, which could explain their higher representation in this study.

More than half of the respondents had attained diploma or bachelor’s degree qualifications, which may reflect greater awareness of VCS availability. Although higher education levels have been associated with greater readiness to adopt telemedicine and higher satisfaction in some studies [51–53], education level was not significantly associated with satisfaction in this study (p = 0.148). This finding may be attributed to the increasing user-friendliness of contemporary telemedicine platforms, which are designed to be intuitive and accessible regardless of educational background.

Usability and acceptability of virtual consultation services

Respondents reported relatively high mean scores for usability (12.95 out of 15; SD = 2.31) and acceptability (37.21 out of 45; SD = 6.54). These findings indicate that patients were generally able to use virtual consultation platforms effectively and were willing to endorse VCS for their healthcare needs.

Multiple regression analysis revealed that acceptability was the strongest predictor of satisfaction (β = 0.410, p < 0.001), followed by usability (β = 0.382, p < 0.001). Together, these variables explained 89.4% of the variance in satisfaction scores. In contrast, demographic factors did not significantly predict satisfaction, highlighting that patient satisfaction with virtual consultations is primarily driven by experience-related attributes rather than socioeconomic characteristics.

High acceptability likely reflects perceived convenience, reduced travel time, and cost savings - factors consistently reported as key contributors to telemedicine satisfaction [16, 19, 30]. Other acceptability-related factors, such as privacy and comfort, have also been shown to significantly enhance satisfaction [29]. A systematic review conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic similarly identified convenience, comfort, ease of access, safety, and reduced waiting time as major determinants of patient satisfaction [31].

In terms of usability, fewer technical difficulties during virtual consultations have been associated with higher satisfaction [29]. The high usability scores observed in this study suggest that patients value platforms that are intuitive and easy to navigate. The familiar interface of the MySejahtera application in the Malay language, combined with the straightforward design of Google Meet and HIE platforms, likely contributed to positive user experiences. These usability features facilitate effective communication between patients and healthcare providers and, when supported by adequate infrastructure and appropriate health-seeking behaviours, may contribute to sustaining high levels of patient satisfaction, even in rural settings [54, 55].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of random sampling across government health clinics nationwide, enhancing representativeness, alongside a locally developed bilingual (Malay and English) questionnaire with strong reliability and validity, which supports the quality of patient-reported data. It is also among the first studies to evaluate telemedicine implementation in Malaysian public primary care using multiple platforms, including MySejahtera, HIE, and Google Meet. However, several limitations should be noted: the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and captures satisfaction at a single time point; clinical outcomes and cost savings were not assessed; and the focus on outpatient services may limit generalisability to other VCS components. Additionally, key variables such as digital literacy, comorbidities, and prior VCS experience were not examined, and the study did not differentiate satisfaction by platform or account for clinic-level variability (e.g., urban–rural differences). Finally, although the questionnaire was adapted from a validated tool, it was not formally re-validated in this specific population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, virtual consultation services in Malaysian public primary care were well received by patients, with satisfaction largely driven by usability and acceptability rather than sociodemographic factors. Acceptability and usability emerged as the strongest predictors of satisfaction, indicating that patients prioritise ease of use, convenience, privacy, and quality interaction over demographic characteristics. These findings address a critical evidence gap in telemedicine evaluation in Malaysia and provide valuable insights to inform policy decisions. Policymakers should leverage these findings to guide the scale-up and optimisation of Virtual Consultation Services in public primary care. Continued evaluation is essential to ensure sustained patient benefits and the long-term sustainability of telemedicine services in Malaysia. Future research should compare different telemedicine platforms, include a wider range of service scopes, and incorporate additional outcomes such as cost savings and health benefits. Longitudinal studies and qualitative approaches would also provide deeper insights into patient experiences and evolving perceptions of telemedicine.
