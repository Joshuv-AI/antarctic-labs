Sex-aware causal inference assessment of the immune system in complex neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Frida Lona-Durazo, Ross P Byrne, Marc-Olivier Pilon, Michael D Greicius, Marie-Pierre Dubé, Michael E Belloy, Russell L McLaughlin, Sarah A Gagliano Taliun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSex differences, in terms of prevalence, symptoms and disease progression, are established in the aetiology of complex neurodegenerative diseases, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease, but the underlying biology driving these differences remains poorly understood. There is emerging evidence from genetic and functional analyses affirming the role of the immune system in such diseases, but a thorough assessment of sex differences in the link between the immune system and neurodegenerative diseases remains lacking.Here, we applied a robust causal inference approach, two-sample Mendelian randomization, to evaluate the causal effect of immune-related protein levels on three neurodegenerative diseases with large-scale sex-stratified genome-wide association data available: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (females = 10 895 cases, 57 062 controls; males = 15 547 cases, 50 145 controls); Parkinson’s disease (females = 7947 cases, 90 662 controls; males = 13 020 cases, 89 660 controls); and Alzheimer’s disease (females = 18 822 cases, 281 415 controls; males = 17 293 cases, 213 339 controls). As exposures, we focused on 932 immune system-related proteins with significant protein cis-quantitative trait loci (false discovery rate cut-off < 0.01) from a large sex-combined plasma protein dataset (n = 33 477), for which corresponding genes were included in the Immunology Database and Analysis Portal gene list. We tested for a causal relationship between genetically predicted levels of each of these proteins and each neurodegenerative disease in sex-stratified and sex-combined data, followed by colocalization and estimation of sex-differential effects. We additionally performed exploratory analyses using sex-combined CSF protein cis-quantitative trait loci (n = 971) as exposures.We observed evidence for a sex-differential causal relationship between FCGR2A and Parkinson’s disease and between CD2AP, MAMDC2, PCDH17 or CSF3 and Alzheimer’s disease. We validated significant results using two independent protein cis-quantitative trait loci datasets for those plasma proteins available. After performing sensitivity analyses, we validated the potential causal relationships of OMG on Parkinson’s disease and of GRN, SERPINF2 and TREM2 on Alzheimer’s disease. Mendelian randomization with CSF protein cis-quantitative trait loci showed a potential causal effect of ADGRE2, GPNMB and COLEC11 on Parkinson’s disease and of CD33 on Alzheimer’s disease, without evidence of sex-differential effects. Finally, we substantiated our findings of protein-disease pairs using triangulation, specifically reporting independent supporting evidence from the literature and drug-related databases.Overall, our results point to potential causal effects of genetically predicted levels of immune system-related plasma and CSF proteins in Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, some of which may be considered as potential candidates for drug development.Keywords: sex differential effects, immune system, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Late-onset neurodegenerative diseases are influenced by a combination of genetic and non-genetic (environmental) factors. Differences between women and men, in terms of prevalence, symptoms and disease progression, are established in the aetiology of complex neurodegenerative diseases, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) and can be influenced by sex and gender. PD and ALS are more common in males than in females, whereas AD is more common in females than in males. In sporadic ALS, disease in men is more likely to begin in the spinal regions, whereas it is more common to begin in the bulbar region in women.1 Additionally, men are more likely to have earlier onset of ALS, compared to women.1 In the case of PD, the mortality rate is higher, and the disease progresses faster in females than in males.2 As for symptoms, in PD, tremors are more often presented in women, whereas cognitive decline is more often in men.3,4 Such differences may be explained in part by sex differing genetic effects.

Neurodegeneration is likely a result of contributions of multiple biological systems, including the immune system. Indeed, several lines of evidence support the role of the immune system in various neurodegenerative diseases.5-17 It is likely that the effect of the immune system is complex and dependent on various factors, including disease phase and immune cell type.7,18,19 In the context of neurodegeneration, immune influences may differ between females and males, which, for example, has been shown for AD.20-22

Identifying the relationship between neurodegenerative diseases and immune-related information, including immune cells and immune-related protein levels on a sex-aware basis, will be crucial to better understand disease progression and help identify potential drug targets, with the possibility of repurposing existing immune-related medications. To date, anti-inflammatory drug trials for PD have had limited success, but clinical trials involving immunotherapies are in progress.7 Similarly, ALS preclinical and clinical trials have attempted to repurpose neuroinflammatory drugs, but have not prospered.23,24 In contrast, there are several clinical trials targeting inflammation for AD, including a phase 3 study of NE3107, an anti-inflammatory metabolite that showed promising results in decreasing cognitive decline and improving cognitive function.25,26 Here, we aimed to further investigate the role of the immune system in neurodegenerative diseases using large multi-omics datasets alongside causal inference techniques, including in a sex-aware manner.

To do so, we employed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), an epidemiological approach, which, given the validity of certain assumptions, tests for a causal relationship between an exposure (e.g. levels of a protein of interest) and an outcome (e.g. a disease) by exploiting genetic variant-trait association summary-level data. We then performed complementary analyses to investigate the potential causal effect of the immune system on neurodegenerative diseases in a sex-aware framework. We focused on ALS,27 ad and PD,28 given the availability of large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) stratified by sex. Elucidating the sex-differential effects of the immune system in these diseases has the potential to guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide association studies summary statistics for neurodegenerative diseases

We obtained sex-stratified summary statistics for three neurodegenerative diseases: PD, ALS and AD. Sex-stratified PD summary statistics from autosomes, which included individuals of European genetic ancestry, were retrieved from the International Parkinson Disease Genomics Consortium,28 excluding UK Biobank, for our main analysis. Sex-stratified PD chromosome X summary statistics were retrieved from the GWAS catalogue (study accession numbers: GCST90104086 and GCST901040865), including UK Biobank clinical and proxy cases.29,30

Sex-stratified ALS GWAS summary statistics were obtained from a meta-analysis of GWAS conducted in European genetic ancestry individuals across autosomal variants, and across chromosome X for a subset of individuals, provided by co-authors R.P.B. and R.L.M.27

Sex-stratified AD GWAS summary statistics were obtained from a European genetic ancestry meta-analysis performed across autosomes,31 which included the FinnGen cohort, version 12 (Supplementary material, ‘Methods’ section). Chromosome X summary statistics are publicly available, including UK Biobank clinical and proxy cases.32

Our main analyses used summary statistics, excluding UK Biobank (i.e. for AD and PD), but we also report results using summary statistics that include UK Biobank. We chose to use summary statistics excluding the UK Biobank because the sex-stratified AD datasets were available only for both registry and proxy cases from the UK Biobank, and to maintain consistency, we adopted the same approach for PD. The consequences of including proxy cases for AD, in particular, have been thoroughly discussed elsewhere,33,34 and therefore, we present results from those datasets as secondary analyses.

To obtain sex-combined summary statistics for each neurodegenerative disease tested, we meta-analysed the sex-specific GWAS with METAL,35 using effect size estimates and their respective standard errors.

Genome-wide association studies summary statistics for protein levels

Summary statistics of plasma protein quantitative trait loci were obtained from analyses performed in the white British subset of the UK Biobank, which is currently the largest available cis-protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) dataset.36 Cis-pQTLs were identified across autosomes and chromosome X within a ±500 kb window of the transcription start site of the target protein-coding gene.37

We used two independent datasets of cis-pQTLs summary statistics for validation from: (i) 7213 individuals of European genetic ancestry from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort37; and (ii) 34 287 individuals of European genetic ancestry from deCODE.38 Protein levels were measured using SomaLogic technology in these two datasets (as opposed to Olink in the UK Biobank), resulting in a partial overlap of the proteins included across both technologies.

We retrieved summary statistics of CSF cis-pQTLs obtained from Yang et al.39 through the National Institute on Aging Genetics of Alzheimer’s Disease Data Storage Site (NIAGADS). cis-pQTLs were defined as variants within 1 Mb upstream and downstream of the gene start site from the respective protein, across autosomes.39

Sex-specific genetic correlations

We tested for pairwise global genetic correlations between sex-stratified PD, AD and ALS with cross-trait (bivariate) Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression (LDSC v1),40 using common HapMap autosomal variants [excluding the human leucocyte antigen (HLA) region] and the 1000 Genomes European super-population as the linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference. We defined a significant correlation using a Bonferroni-corrected P-value of 0.008. We also estimated regional genetic correlations with Local Analysis of (co)Variant Association (LAVA)41 (Supplementary material, ‘Methods’ section).

Cis-Mendelian randomization with plasma protein levels

To investigate sex-differential effects of genetically predicted plasma protein levels on neurodegenerative diseases, we performed a causal inference assessment using sex-stratified GWAS summary statistics for the tested neurodegenerative diseases, ALS, AD and PD. Specifically, we applied a two-sample cis-MR approach using the TwoSampleMR R package v0.6.0,42 with the sex-combined plasma cis-pQTLs dataset as the exposure and the sex-stratified ALS, AD or PD as the outcome. In addition to using the sex-stratified outcomes, we also performed the analysis using the sex-combined summary statistics. For the selection of exposures, we started with proteins for which there was at least one significant cis-pQTL [false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01] from the UK Biobank primary analysis cohort,37 and for which the corresponding genes were included in the Immunology Database and Analysis Portal (ImmPort) gene list, obtained from InnateDB 5.4.43 This list contained 4361 human protein-coding genes generated using automatic searches of EntrezGene and Gene Ontology records using immunology-related keywords, followed by manual curation by immunology experts to keep all genes with immune system-related functions (downloaded May 2024).43 Of the 2923 plasma proteins assessed in the UK Biobank, the corresponding genes of 1248 proteins were included in the ImmPort gene list, and from these, we selected 932 immune system-related proteins with significant cis-pQTLs (FDR < 0.01), and we then used these proteins as exposures in our MR analyses. Of note, 34 immune-related proteins with corresponding genes located on chromosome X were measured in the UK Biobank, but none had significant cis-pQTLs and were therefore not included in the cis-MR analyses.

MR has three core assumptions. First, the selected genetic instruments are robustly associated with the exposure. Second, there is no confounding, measured or unmeasured, influencing both the exposure and the outcome. Third, the genetic variants are associated with the outcome only through the exposure (i.e. no horizontal pleiotropy). To ensure that the MR assumptions were satisfied, we applied a quality control of the instrumental variables chosen as follows, we: (i) kept palindromic variants and inferred the allele strand based on the reported allele frequencies from the exposures and outcomes; (ii) kept FDR significant instrumental variables (FDR < 0.01); (iii) excluded genetic variants associated with smoking or body mass index (BMI) in GWAS of European populations available in the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog,44 given their possible confounding effect; (iv) clumped the data using the European super-population of the 1000 Genomes Project (phase 3) as the linkage disequilibrium reference (clumping parameters: r2 = 0.001; 10 Mb window); (v) excluded the chromosome 6 extended HLA region (chr6: 25 726 291–33,377,673, GRCh37) from the instrumental variables, given its widespread pleiotropy; (vi) excluded weak instrumental variables, F-statistic ≤ 10; and (vii) queried if any of the remaining instruments (or those in high linkage disequilibrium with the instrument, r2 > 0.8) were associated with other possibly confounding traits using Open Targets.45

We performed cis-MR with the TwoSampleMR R package v0.6.0,42 using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method or the Wald ratio test (in the presence of only one instrumental variable) in our primary analysis, but we also report results with MR Egger for comparison (in the presence of >2 instrumental variables), to consider horizontal pleiotropy. We then applied sensitivity analyses for nominally significant results (P-value < 0.05), namely: heterogeneity test (Cochran’s Q), horizontal pleiotropy (MR Egger intercept), detection of outliers with MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier test (MR-PRESSO v1.0)46 and directionality validation. For signals passing an FDR cut-off of 0.05 and which had no evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy, we performed colocalization analyses to distinguish likely true cis-MR signals from protein-disease pairs confounded by linkage disequilibrium. To do so, we used the coloc R package (v5.2.3)47 and applied slightly more relaxed prior probabilities (P1, P2 = 1 × 10−3; P12 = 1 × 10−4).48 We defined a colocalization signal as significant when the posterior probability of colocalization (PP H4) was ≥0.70. As a quality control check, we assessed whether our main cis-MR results were being driven by large differences in allele frequencies [minor allele frequency (MAF) difference ≥ 0.20] between the Finnish cohort included in the AD GWAS and other European genetic ancestry cohorts. Specifically, in the 1000 Genomes Project (phase 3) data, we compared the MAF of European ancestry (EUR) individuals (excluding Finnish individuals) to that of the Finnish subpopulation for each instrumental variable.

For the cis-MR significant proteins, we tested if the effect sizes for each protein were statistically different between males and females using the sexually dimorphic effects (SDE) Z-score statistic.49 We used an FDR cut-off of 0.05 to define an exposure-outcome pair with significant SDE.

To validate our findings, we repeated the analyses for signals with cis-MR and colocalization evidence using cis-pQTLs from the validation cohorts (ARIC37 and deCODE).38 We performed MR applying the same quality control steps and sensitivity tests as above, followed by colocalization analyses for the proteins available in the validation data.

We also performed two-sample genome-wide MR using sex-stratified immune cell traits as exposures and each sex-specific neurodegenerative disease GWAS summary statistics as the outcome (Supplementary material).

Cis-Mendelian randomization with cerebrospinal fluid protein levels

To complement our primary cis-MR analysis using plasma cis-pQTLs, we tested cis-pQTLs, from CSF, following the same procedure as described above. The profile of CSF cis-pQTLs may be a better representation of the proteins expressed across the brain, but this dataset is smaller compared to the plasma dataset. We tested 143 proteins, based on the overlap with the ImmPort gene list and the presence of significant cis-pQTLs.39,43 We then performed a causal inference assessment for ALS, AD and PD, using GWAS summary statistics, and applied similar quality control steps of the instrument variables as described above for the plasma cis-pQTLs, followed by colocalization of significant signals (FDR < 0.05) We also tested for SDE using the Z-score statistic and an FDR cut-off of 0.05.

Prioritization of potentially causal proteins through external evidence

For proteins with significant exposure-outcome cis-MR results that also showed evidence of colocalization and were validated, we used a triangulation approach to support our causal inference evidence to increase the robustness of our results.50,51 To do so, we mined previously published literature as well as drug-related databases for alternative lines of evidence supporting the exposure-outcome MR results. We first performed a literature search on PubMed for experimental studies that have shown evidence of a causal link between the significant proteins we identified and the outcome of interest, limiting the search criteria to the exposure’s official gene symbol AND outcome pairs (e.g. ‘TREM2’ AND ‘Alzheimer’s disease’) present in the title or abstract, and filtering to keep the following article types: clinical trial (all types), randomized control trial (all types) and research support (all types). We then searched databases to amass information on the targets for evidence of druggability. We used Open Targets (v25.03, last accessed: March 2025),45 which is a platform that provides association evidence between target-disease pairs, and the recently developed ML-GPS (last accessed: March 2025),52 a machine learning-assisted genetic priority score that incorporates genetic associations with predicted diseases to predict drug indications. Additionally, we looked up the targets in DrugBank (last accessed: March 2025),53 a widely used database for extracting drug-target information, and in DGIbd (last accessed March 2025),54 a drug-gene interaction database focused on the druggable genome, to extract information about available drugs that could be potentially repurposed.

Results

Sex-specific genetic correlations

As a first step to explore potential links across neurodegenerative diseases and differences between the sexes, we tested for global genetic correlations (rg) between sex-stratified ALS, AD and PD. As expected (and as previously reported for PD),28 the within-trait genetic correlations between males and females were strong (rg ≥ 0.85) and significant [P-value < 0.008 (ALS: rg = 0.95; P-value = 2.62 × 10−9; AD: rg = 0.93; P-value = 3.21 × 10−19; PD: rg = 0.85; P-value = 3.30 × 10−31)]. Additionally, we observed nominally positive significant correlations (0.05 > P-value ≥ 0.008) between female-specific ALS and PD (rg = 0.23; P-value = 0.03), but not between the corresponding male-specific summary statistics (rg = −0.02; P-value = 0.80). Similarly, there were nominally significant positive correlations between male-specific PD and AD (rg = 0.23; P-value = 0.01), but not between the corresponding female-specific summary statistics (rg = −0.01; P-value = 0.90) (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Of note, male-specific signals in the AD dataset used here may be driven by comorbid Lewy body dementia (LBD) and AD cases or by LBD cases misdiagnosed as AD cases (i.e. without pathology verification), which may be partially influencing the correlations between male-specific PD and AD observed (Supplementary material, ‘Results’ section). To further explore if there are specific shared loci driving the observed global genetic correlations, we performed regional genetic correlations with LAVA. These results are available as Supplementary material.

Cis-Mendelian randomization with plasma protein levels

The completed MR-STROBE55 checklist is found in Supplementary Table 2.

In our primary analysis, we evaluated a total of 932 immune system-related proteins measured in the UK Biobank36 to assess their potential causal relationships with sex-stratified or sex-combined ALS, AD and PD. Our primary analysis excluded the UK Biobank cohort from the AD and PD GWAS datasets (‘Materials and methods’ section). Cis-MR results for all genetically predicted plasma protein levels assessed across the three tested neurodegenerative diseases are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Given that the AD GWAS summary statistics were derived from non-Finnish and Finnish Europeans, we assessed the minor allele frequencies of the selected instrumental variables for the significant proteins comparing frequencies in the 1000 Genomes European ancestry super-population excluding Finnish individuals versus in the Finnish individuals. All had the same minor allele with similar frequencies (MAF difference ≤ 0.20) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

According to our cis-MR main results and subsequent validation in two independent cohorts, we categorized our findings in three tiers: Tier 1: significant plasma proteins that were successfully validated in independent cohorts; Tier 2: significant plasma proteins for which validation could not be performed in independent cohorts due to absence of the protein; Tier 3: significant plasma proteins for which validation failed in independent cohorts, i.e. no significant cis-MR estimate (P-value ≥ 0.0045) or no evidence of colocalization (PP H4 < 0.7) (Figs 2–5).

We identified a significant (FDR < 0.05) potential causal effect of TNFSF13 levels on sex-combined ALS [IVW odds ratio (OR) = 0.89; FDR = 0.03], supported by genetic colocalization (PP H4 = 0.85) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4). The sex-stratified analyses had similar effect sizes (IVW ORfemale = 0.91; IVW ORmale = 0.87) but were not significant (FDR > 0.05), and there was no evidence of SDE (FDR ≥ 0.05) (Supplementary Table 5). We also identified potential causal effects of four protein levels (FCGR2A, OMG and CTSB and NFKBIE) on PD in at least one of the sex-specific analyses (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4). Of these, OMG, FCGR2A and NFKBIE were significant in the male, but not female-specific analysis (IVW ORmale = 0.41; IVW ORmale = 1.18; IVW ORmale = 0.73, respectively), although only FCGR2A showed SDE (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5). In contrast, CSTB was significant in both sex-combined and sex-specific analyses, with similar effect size (IVW ORfemale = 0.75; IVW ORmale = 0.81), but with significant heterogeneity in the sex-combined and female-specific analyses (Fig. 3). For each of these four proteins, colocalization analyses also supported a shared causal signal (PP H4 > 0.70) with PD (Supplementary Table 4). Increased FCGR2A and decreased CTSB levels in CSF have previously been highlighted as causally associated with PD, although without colocalization evidence in the case of CTSB.56 We did not find prior evidence for a causal relationship between either OMG or NFKBIE with PD.

Cis-MR results with the MR Egger method were nominally significant (P-value < 0.05) for 10 out of 16 exposure-outcome pairs that were also FDR-significant with the IVW or Wald ratio method, further reinforcing the putative causal effects (Supplementary Table 5).

To further support the identified cis-MR and colocalized signals based on the primary cis-pQTL dataset (UK Biobank), we used two independent sets of plasma cis-pQTLs for validation (Supplementary Table 6).37,57 However, in our primary analysis (UK Biobank cis-pQTLs), protein levels were assessed using the Olink platform, whereas they were assessed using SomaScan in our two validation cohorts (deCODE and ARIC). For proteins assessed on both platforms, validation of some could have failed due to the technology differences in specificity and sensitivity, as previously benchmarked.30,58 To explore how possible technology differences may influence results, we extracted from Pietzner et al.30 the normalized Spearman correlation values of protein levels obtained between the two technologies (Supplementary data in Pietzner et al.30) of the significant proteins that we used for validation (Supplementary Table 7). Of the 14 proteins, seven were measured in the validation cohorts and had significant cis-pQTLs (i.e. FCGR2A, OMG, CTSB, CSF3, GRN, SERPINF2 and TREM2), leaving seven proteins (i.e. TNFSF13, NFKBIE, BLNK, CD2AP, MAMDC2, ORM1 and PCDH17) for which causal effect on AD, ALS or PD could not be validated. By applying a Bonferroni-corrected P-value threshold of 0.0045, to account for the number of cis-MR validation tests performed, followed by sensitivity analyses and genetic colocalization, we successfully validated the potential causal associations of OMG on male-specific PD, and of GRN, SERPINF2 and TREM2 on sex-combined AD (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 6).

For PD, we observed positive potential causal relationships of genetically predicted levels of three CSF-derived proteins (GPNMB, ADGRE2 and COLEC11), supported by colocalization evidence (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Fig. 7). The relationship between GPNMB levels and PD was significant in the male-specific and sex-combined PD cis-MR. This relationship was not, however, significant in the corresponding female-specific analysis, which could be a result of statistical power, as the causal inference effect size was in the same direction regardless of sex, and there was no evidence of SDE (IVW ORmales = 2.79, FDR = 1.32 × 10−7; IVW ORfemale = 1.98, FDR = 0.405). In contrast, ADGRE2 levels were significant only for female-specific PD (IVW ORfemale = 2.48, FDR = 0.039; IVW ORmale = 1.04, FDR = 0.975). Finally, COLEC11 had a significant potential causal effect on male-specific PD (IVW ORmale = 1.5, FDR = 0.043), whereas the female-specific analysis indicated no effect of the protein levels on PD (IVW OR = 1, FDR = 0.99). None of the significant proteins showed evidence of SDE (FDR ≥ 0.05). Validation of these results is warranted when independent large pQTL datasets become available in the future. Interestingly, a previous cis-MR study on CSF highlighted COLEC11 and ADGRE2 as potentially causal for PD, but without colocalization evidence.56

Prioritization of potentially causal proteins through external evidence

We triangulated our findings of the significant and colocalized plasma protein-neurodegenerative disease pairs that were validated in independent cohorts (i.e. Tier 1 proteins) by mining previously published literature and drug-related databases as additional evidence.49,50 We also included the significant and colocalized CSF protein-neurodegenerative disease pairs. As the outcome data used are from case/control GWAS (i.e. binary measure of presence/absence of disease), the potential targets identified in this study would be pertinent for disease prevention as opposed to treatment. Additionally, given that each of these individual proteins have modest effects on neurodegenerative diseases, potential drugs targeting them would likely have modest effects on disease development. Finally, our literature search is mostly limited to sex-combined studies, since sex stratification has rarely been considered.

Table 2 summarizes the literature search results of the protein-neurodegenerative disease pairs assessed. Among the plasma proteins, TREM2, GRN and CD33 have supporting evidence for AD, whereas among the CSF proteins, GPNMB has evidence for PD. Of note, the genes encoded by these proteins have, in the most part, been previously associated with AD or PD through GWAS, and have been followed up in targeted studies (e.g. TREM2, GRN, GPNMB).61,63,67 However, this is not the case for proteins encoding genes that have not been identified as genetic risk loci in previous GWAS (i.e. ADGRE2, COLEC11); although, we note that observational studies associating protein levels with disease status have not highlighted them either,68-73 which could be due to non-genetic factors influencing protein levels at the time measurements were taken in observational studies.

Our results suggested that genetically predicted decreased levels of oligodendrocyte myelin glycoprotein (OMG) in plasma increase PD risk. Additionally, our secondary cis-MR analysis (i.e. using neurodegenerative disease summary statistics including UK Biobank) also showed decreased levels of OMG as potentially causal for AD. OMG is uniquely and highly expressed in brain tissues (Human Protein Atlas version 24.0),59 but neither previous cis-MR studies in CSF nor our exploratory analyses using CSF protein levels have highlighted it as potentially causal.56,60 Through our literature search, we did not find direct links between OMG and PD, but OMG is a novel risk locus in the largest PD European ancestry GWAS to date.62 There is experimental evidence showing protein-protein interactions specific to AD brain samples, between OMG and Tau, a protein encoded by MAPT, which in turn is a genetic risk locus for PD and AD.64 These combined results encourage further consideration of OMG as a potential target for neurodegenerative diseases.

ADGRE2 and COLEC11 are two other proteins that lack direct links to PD and have not previously been highlighted in PD GWAS. There is experimental evidence indicating that ADGRE2 physically interacts with CD55, a protein which has been linked to PD (and which was not assessed on CSF).65,66,74,75 Similarly, there is evidence suggesting a protein-protein interaction between COLEC11 and α-synuclein, a crucial protein for synucleinopathies, including PD.76,77

All identified Tier 1 proteins, except for TREM2, are within the druggable genome gene set. From these, GPNMB, CD33 and SERPINF2 are drug targets currently under investigation for non-neurodegenerative diseases, and ADGRE2 and GRN are targets that interact with existing drugs. In summary, these results can inform future prioritization of potentially causal immune-related proteins for possible repurposing for neurodegeneration.

Discussion

Here, by applying Mendelian randomization and colocalization in large datasets, we interrogated possible causal links between the immune system and three neurodegenerative diseases in a sex-aware manner. These results have the potential to inform future research on drug repurposing of immune-related targets for neurodegenerative diseases influenced by sex.

Altogether, our two-sample cis-MR and colocalization results suggest a potential causal effect of genetically predicted plasma protein levels of TREM2, SERPINF2 and GRN on AD risk. We also observed a potential causal effect of TNFSF13 levels on ALS, which has previously been highlighted78 (although the protein was not present in our validation datasets), and a potential causal effect of decreased plasma levels of OMG on PD risk, without SDE. Furthermore, we observed evidence of sex-differential causal effects between CD2AP, MAMDC2 or PCDH17 levels and AD, although these proteins were unavailable in the validation cohorts to confirm results. Other potential causal relationships that warrant further investigation include the effects of BLNK or CSF3 levels on AD and of NFKBIE or FCGR2A on PD. Finally, for CSF protein levels, our results suggest a potential causal effect of GPNMB, ADGRE2 and COLEC11 on PD and of CD33 on AD.

We acknowledge that the present study has limitations. First, we were unable to assess immune-related proteins encoded by genes on the X chromosome, as there were no significant cis-pQTLs in the large dataset used in our primary analysis. We acknowledge this exclusion as an important limitation, particularly given that a main source of sex genetic differences can be attributed to the sex chromosomes49; a future solution would be to increase sample sizes to achieve higher power for pQTL detection. Similarly, our current approach interrogates sex-specific effects of the outcomes, but not at the level of the protein exposures, limited by existing data. Therefore, our approach can capture sex-specific effects at the level of outcome but not exposure. For instance, if a cis-pQTL is present in only one sex, or if it has opposite effects across the sexes, we may not be able to capture its effect by using sex-combined exposure datasets. As protein levels assessed in larger sample sizes become available (such as in the full UK Biobank cohort, currently in progress), there will be sufficient power to apply cis-MR to pQTLs from sex-stratified data. Likewise, future validation is needed for the CSF proteins identified here. Second, our study was restricted to cis-pQTLs, as their interpretation is more intuitive for cis-MR analyses, and they can be less prone to horizontal pleiotropy than trans-pQTLs. Future focus on trans-pQTLs may also provide additional insights into immune-related sex differential proteins. Third, although we applied a robust approach with appropriate sensitivity analyses, as with all MR studies, it is nevertheless possible that the core assumptions (such as assuming no horizontal pleiotropy due to unknown confounders) have not been entirely met across all exposure-outcome pairs. Finally, our study was limited to cohorts composed of individuals of European genetic ancestry, as sex-stratified GWAS in other genetic ancestries are currently unavailable or only available with smaller sample sizes, with limited statistical power for our investigations. In the future, our results should be tested for validation in large cohorts of various genetic ancestries to evaluate the extent of transportability.

We tested for causal relationships between immune system-related plasma protein levels and neurodegenerative diseases (i.e. ALS, AD and PD) using cis-MR, with a focus on exploring sex-differential effects. Assessing immune system-related plasma protein levels could help prioritize drug targets for relatively easier delivery, compared to assessing immune cell traits on a genome-wide basis (Supplementary material).50 In addition, we performed a complementary analysis using immune system-related CSF protein levels, given that CSF is potentially a more biologically relevant tissue than plasma in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, with the caveat of using a small cohort (971 participants). Previous cis-MR studies using genetically predicted CSF or plasma protein levels as exposures have identified several proteins with a potential causal effect on either ALS, AD or PD. Notably, these studies have highlighted immune system-related protein candidates, including proteins from the sialic-acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin (SIGLEC) family in the case of ALS,60,79 CD33, TREM2, GRN, SIGLEC9, among others for AD,80,81 and GPNMB, FCGR2A and FCGR2B for PD.56,60 Among these proteins with prior evidence, we observed potential causal effects of CD33 CSF levels and of GRN and TREM2 plasma levels on AD, as well as of GPNMB CSF levels on PD. All these protein-disease pairs were significant using sex-combined or sex-stratified AD as the outcome, in the same direction and with similar effect sizes as previously reported.60,80 Most of the aforementioned proteins were measured in the cohorts used in the current study. However, some did not show significant effects (e.g. SIGLEC7, SIGLEC9), another lacked colocalization evidence (FCGR2B), one showed evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (GPNMB, in plasma), and another failed the validation step (FCGR2A) (Supplementary Tables 3 and 6).

Differences in how protein levels are measured across the two proteomics platforms used (i.e. Olink versus SomaScan) could at least partially explain the failure to validate some cis-MR signals in our study.30,58,82 On the one hand, this is a limiting factor; it is important to note, however, that there are currently no other large datasets available that also use the Olink platform. However, it also provides robust and high-confidence results for those proteins with successful validation, minimizing the effect of platform-specific artefacts.

One of the identified causal relationships with a possible sexually dimorphic effect was with plasma protein levels of the granulocyte colony stimulating factor 3 (CSF3, also known as G-CSF) on AD risk. Specifically, our cis-MR results suggest that genetically predicted decreased levels of CSF3 in males, but not in females, increase the risk of AD. CSF3 is a common drug administered to chemotherapy patients to increase neutrophil counts (i.e. filgrastim). Prior work has shown that filgrastim stimulates neurogenesis and offers a neuroprotective effect..83,84 Although CSF3 was not successfully validated (deCODE PP H4 = 0.51; ARIC cis-MR P-value = 0.113), a lack of concordance in the proteomics technologies could partly explain this discrepancy (Supplementary Table 7). Previous studies using AD mouse models have linked AD and CSF3. For example, multiple AD mouse model studies have shown improved cognitive impairment following treatment with G-CSF and attenuation of brain chronic inflammation.85-88 Despite evidence in mice, the effect of administering G-CSF in a human pilot study was inconclusive.89 Among the aforementioned mouse studies, only Tsai et al.85 specified the use of male mice, whereas the sex was not reported in the other publications, although mouse studies have historically been limited to male organisms.90 Interestingly, the promoter of CSF3 overlaps a progesterone receptor transcription factor binding site predicted by JASPAR,91 suggesting possible sex hormone-dependent gene expression. Recently, Shue et al.92 showed that G-CSF levels in CSF were lower in APOE4 subjects compared to APOE3 subjects, hypothesizing that G-CSF treatment may differ by APOE status. Overall, these lines of evidence may warrant revisiting the therapeutic use of G-CSF considering sex or APOE status. Indeed, a recent study highlighted differences in lecanemab (phase 3 drug trial) response on AD patients, probably attributed to sex (i.e. less effective in females compared to males).93

To conclude, our primary goal was to investigate causal links, including sex-differential effects, of immune system-related exposures on neurodegenerative disease outcomes powered by large-scale datasets. Our cis-MR analyses revealed significant potential sex-differential causal effects between immune-related protein levels with either AD or PD, but not with ALS. The results could be due to various factors, including: (i) true lack of sex differences in the genetically predicted immune-related protein levels for ALS; (ii) inadequate power to detect modest signals with the current sample sizes; and (iii) sex differences stemming primarily from sex chromosomes, for which larger sample sizes are needed to identify significant pQTLs. Increasing sample sizes (statistical power) of the exposure and outcome datasets, alongside with expanding analyses to include the sex chromosomes, will certainly aid in increasing our understanding and creating a comprehensive roadmap of sex-differential immune system-related risk factors on neurodegenerative diseases. This knowledge will be crucial for future research to better understand the role of immune-related factors on neurodegeneration that accounts for sex differences.
