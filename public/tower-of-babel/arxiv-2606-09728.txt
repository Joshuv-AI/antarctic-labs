Quantum Cut Sparsifiers
Arpon Basu arpon.basu@princeton.edu Princeton University Joshua Brakensiek josh.brakensiek@berkeley.edu UC Berkeley Pravesh K. Kothari kothari@cs.princeton.edu Princeton University Aaron Putterman aputterman@g.harvard.edu Harvard University

Abstract
In this paper, we continue a line of research initiated by Basu, Brakensiek, and Putterman [2026] studying the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians. We focus particularly on the sparsifiability of the widely-studied Quantum Cut (QC) Hamiltonians. Our main result is that in an n n -qubit system, any n n -qubit QC Hamiltonian can be sparsified to O ~ ​ ( n / ε 2 ) \widetilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2}) many terms while preserving the energy of every state up to a factor of 1 ± ε 1\pm\varepsilon . Our result can be interpreted as giving an importance sampling scheme for the edges of an arbitrary graph G G such that the Kikuchi graph at level ℓ \ell of the sampled graph is a spectral approximation to the Kikuchi graph of G G . Importantly, the same sampling scheme works simultaneously for all ℓ \ell . The natural approach of leverage score sampling, analyzed via matrix concentration inequalities, yields a polynomially worse bound in our setting because the underlying matrices have dimension ∼ 2 n \sim 2^{n} . Instead, our approach relies on decomposing the action of these matrices into invariant subspaces. Then, by using an operator-valued inequality of Alon and Kozma [Ann. Henri Poincaré, 2020], itself building on an octopus inequality of Caputo, Liggett, and Richthammer [J. AMS, 2010], we extend our sparsification technique to all expander graphs. We then invoke expander decomposition to extend our sparsifier to all graphs.

Introduction

Sparsification is a fundamental algorithmic question, asking when large complex objects admit much smaller representations which still preserve key properties. In many settings it turns out that efficient, significant sparsification is possible, thereby dramatically reducing the size of objects. This paradigm was introduced in the seminal work of Benczúr and Karger

1.2
Quantum Cuts and Kikuchi Graphs

The quantum-cut Hamiltonian from
Eq.

1
has a particularly appealing combinatorial interpretation in terms of
Kikuchi graphs
, a.k.a as
token graphs
.

Towards elucidating the connection, first consider any function
\psi:\{0,1\}^{[n]}\to\mathbb{C}
(which can be interpreted as a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{\{0,1\}^{[n]}}
), and fix any
e=\{u,v\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
. Then

\langle\psi,\mathcal{L}_{e}\psi\rangle=\langle\psi,(M_{\text{QC}})_{e}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]\setminus e}\psi\rangle=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\setminus e}}\langle\psi_{z},(\lvert 01\rangle-\lvert 10\rangle)(\langle 01\rvert-\langle 10\rvert)\psi_{z}\rangle,

where
\psi_{z}:\{0,1\}^{e}\to\mathbb{C}
is the function defined as
\psi_{z}(y):=\psi(z,y)
, where
(z,y)\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\setminus e}\times\{0,1\}^{e}\cong\{0,1\}^{[n]}
is the concatenation of
z
and
y
. Simplifying further yields

\langle\psi,\mathcal{L}_{e}\psi\rangle=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\setminus e}}|\psi(z,01)-\psi(z,10)|^{2}\,.

The above expression naturally suggests a graph on
\{0,1\}^{[n]}
: For every
z\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\setminus e}
, connect
(z,01),(z,10)\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}
. If we call this graph
\mathcal{K}_{e}
, then note that
\langle\psi,\mathcal{L}_{e}\psi\rangle=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\setminus e}}|\psi(z,01)-\psi(z,10)|^{2}
is precisely

\sum_{\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\text{ is an edge of }\mathcal{K}_{e}}|\psi(x_{1})-\psi(x_{2})|^{2},

which is exactly equal to the quadratic form of
\psi
with the Laplacian of the graph
\mathcal{K}_{e}
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{e}
is the Laplacian of
\mathcal{K}_{e}
! The underlying graph
\mathcal{K}_{e}
also makes clear the nomenclature of the predicate
M_{\text{QC}}
being
quantum cut
.

Before we formalize this definition, it is convenient to rephrase it slightly: Note that we can naturally identify
\{0,1\}^{[n]}
with the power set
2^{[n]}
. Then the adjacency condition of
\mathcal{K}_{e}
becomes that
S,T\in 2^{[n]}
are connected if there exists
Z\in 2^{[n]\setminus e}
such that
S=Z\cup\{u\},T=Z\cup\{v\}
(or
S=Z\cup\{v\},T=Z\cup\{u\}
), i.e.
S,T
are connected if
|S|=|T|
and
S\oplus T=\{u,v\}=e
, where
\oplus
refers to the symmetric difference of sets. We thus formalize the definition of a Kikuchi graph as follows.

1.3
Challenges in Proving
Theorem

1.2

It is worth reflecting on
why

Theorem

1.2
is hard to prove, as it may seem that
[
12
]
has already introduced a robust framework for understanding the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians. Unfortunately, the results of
[
12
]
come in two forms:

1.

The first is a sequence of reductions from Pauli-Hamiltonians to classical CSP sparsification. Such a reduction is not possible in the setting of Quantum Cut: the
2
-qubit Hamiltonian
M_{\mathrm{QC}}
has rank
1
, and so any attempted reduction would yield a classical CSP where each constraint has
1
satisfying assignment. But, the theory of classical CSP sparsification
[
32
,
21
,
52
,
19
]
shows that it is impossible to get sparsifier sizes below
n^{2}
for these types of CSPs!

2.

The second type of result in
[
12
]
relies on basic invocations of Matrix Chernoff. Roughly speaking, they bound the “importance” scores for each local term in the Hamiltonian; in our setting these would be
p_{e}=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle}\frac{\langle\psi|\mathcal{L}_{e}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\mathcal{L}_{G}|\psi\rangle}
(that is, the
maximum
fraction of the energy that ever comes from that single term). In
[
12
]
, they show that for many natural choices of Hamiltonians, many of these importance scores are bounded below
1/n
; this is crucial for their application. Indeed, this is because they then
randomly subsample
those terms which have small importance scores, and use Matrix Chernoff to argue that the energy of the Hamiltonian concentrates. But, because the states
\lvert\psi\rangle
live in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}
, applying Matrix Chernoff gives concentration only with probability
1-2^{n}\cdot 2^{-1/R}
, where
R
is the
largest
importance score. For any value of
R>\frac{1}{n}
, this statement gives no meaningful bound, as then
2^{n}\cdot 2^{-1/R}>1
.

This turns out to be a major bottleneck in our setting. Indeed, even for a Quantum Cut Hamiltonian that arises from the complete graph, it is the case that every local term has importance
\geqslant 1/n
. This makes naively applying Matrix Chernoff impossible and is a barrier to even mild subsampling (say, at rate
1/2
). Similar barriers arise when using, for instance, black-box results for sparsifying sums of PSD matrices (see
[
14
]
). Here again, there is an extra logarithmic factor of the dimension which is paid. Because the dimension is
2^{n}
, this leads to sparsifiers of size
O(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
, which is trivial (as any graph contains at most
n^{2}
edges to begin with).

As we shall see,
Theorem

1.2
relies on techniques far more subtle than
[
12
]
. We explain these in greater detail below.

1.4
Our Techniques

Let us first describe our ideas for unweighted graphs
G
.
Our starting point is a recent result
[
58
]
that, as a special case, yields a near-linear QC-sparsifier for the complete graph. More generally, their work shows that Kikuchi graphs of random
2r
-uniform hypergraphs with appropriate density are spectral approximations to those of complete
2r
-uniform hypergraphs. The proof there is based on two ideas. First, they decompose the action of (the Laplacian of) the level
\ell
Kikuchi graph (on vertex set
{[n]\choose\ell}
) into a direct sum of “invariant subspaces”
E_{0}\oplus E_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{\ell}
, each of which is invariant under the action of matrices from the
Johnson scheme
; in particular, for the action of the Kikuchi graph of the complete
2r
-uniform hypergraph. They then observe that when restricted to any invariant subspace, the sum of the leverage scores of all the hyperedges is upper-bounded at a scale that just suffices for an application of a matrix concentration inequality to work! Unfortunately, the subspaces obtained are not invariant under the eigenspaces of Kikuchi graphs of arbitrary (and, in particular, sampled) hypergraphs. To tackle this, their main observation is that the action of Kikuchi graphs on any single
E_{i}
, while not invariant, is “band limited”. That is, any
\psi\in V_{i}
is sent to
\bigoplus_{j\leqslant O(r)}V_{i\pm j}
. This allows them to show that applying matrix concentration restricted to direct sums of contiguous blocks of
O(r)

V_{i}
s suffices to get a sparsifier. Here, we must deal with
arbitrary
graphs, where the results in
[
58
]
do not apply.
3
3

3

Indeed, an appropriate generalization of
[
58
]
to arbitrary hypergraphs will result in a resolution of the question of finding the correct density vs running time trade-offs for semirandom planted CSPs, a question that has now remained open for a few years

A direct proof for complete graphs

Our plan is to begin with an analogous decomposition of the action of the Kikuchi graph into subspaces that, unlike in the case of (higher arity) hypergraphs, are
invariant for all graphs
! Our decomposition is the standard “harmonic” decomposition of functions on slices of the
n
-dimensional Boolean hypercube. In this decomposition, there is a subspace for each
0\leqslant i\leqslant n/2
and the
i^{\text{th}}
subspace has dimension
\leqslant n^{i+2}
. When
G
is the complete graph, we can argue, analogously to
[
58
]
, that the sum of the leverage scores restricted to the
i^{\text{th}}
invariant subspace is proportional to
n/i
. This is because restricted to the subspace, the operator is in fact constant (i.e., has a single eigenvalue). We can thus apply matrix concentration inequalities and obtain a direct argument for sparsifying Kikuchi graphs of complete graphs.

For arbitrary graphs
G
, the associated operator can have widely varying eigenvalues on each invariant subspace and thus our argument above for bounding the sum of the leverage scores does not work. Our plan is to first tackle the case of expander graphs before generalizing.

Dealing with expanders

Since expander graphs are spectral approximations to complete graphs, one may expect the leverage scores to behave similarly. Proving this, it turns out, is quite difficult and we know of no elementary argument so far.

Our proof instead relies on establishing a new lower bound for the spectrum QC operators associated with expander graphs in terms of (appropriately scaled) the QC operator associated with a complete graph. Our key idea is invoking a powerful inequality of Alon and Kozma
[
5
]
. Indeed, in hindsight, one may view this inequality as proving precisely such a spectral lower bound in the more general setting of the so-called
interchange process
(see below). It turns out that this spectral lower bound suffices for bounding the sum of leverage scores on the
i^{\text{th}}
eigenspace by
\sim n/i
so our plan does succeed!

Our Key Tool: the Alon-Kozma Inequality

We believe that introducing this powerful tool is an important contribution of our work and include a brief overview of the setting that this inequality applies in.

The interchange process is a Markov chain defined on the elements of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
where the transitions are associated with a graph
G
on
[n]
. In the process of interchange for a given graph
G=([n],E,w)
, when a given element is present
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, a random edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E
is sampled proportional to the weights
w
and the transposition
(uv)
to
\sigma
. The Alon-Kozma inequality
[
5
]
implies that this interchange process operator for an expander graph always dominates the interchange process operator for the complete graph (up to an appropriate scaling). The proof makes crucial use of the so called “octopus inequality” developed by Caputo, Liggett, and Richthammer
[
22
]
in their breakthrough resolution of Aldous’ conjecture
[
3
, Open Problem 14.29]
–see
Section

1.5
for further details.

We use this spectral bound for the interchange process to derive a bound for Quantum Cut Hamiltonians by adapting an observation of
[
22
]
that relates the interchange process to an
exclusion process
that directly corresponds to a random walk on a Kikuchi graph, see
Section

1.2
and
Proposition

5.1
.

Generalizing to all graphs

Our idea for generalizing to all graphs is natural and inspired by several works in graph algorithms:
expander decomposition
. These results allow for the decomposition of any graph (with an average degree
\gg\log n
) into pieces that are
mild
expanders. That is, each piece has a spectral gap that grows at least as
1/\operatorname{polylog}(m)
where
m
is the number of vertices in the piece while the total fraction of edges that go between the edges is
o_{n}(1)
.

A priori, the spectral gap of the piece being only
1/\operatorname{polylog}(n)
could be concerning. But it turns out that our argument for expanders above naturally generalizes to such mild expanders with a loss of only a
\operatorname{polylog}(n)
factor in the size of the resulting sparsifier. Our proof is then completed by arguing that the error arising from the residual edges in the expander decomposition can be absorbed into an additional small relative error in our sparsification guarantee.

Handling Weighted Graphs

So far, we have discussed proving
Theorem

1.2

[
52
,
53
,
19
]
) where edges of similar weight (e.g., within a factor of

). For all the edges within a bucket, a simple modification of the unweighted approach results in

-sized sparsifiers. However, to aggregate between buckets, an additional idea is needed. Consider two graphs
G
and
H
for which the minimum weight of any edge of
G
is
n^{\Omega(1)}
-times larger than the maximum weight of any edge of
H
. Using an operator inequality due to Chen
[
25
]
(which also builds on the octopus inequality), we can prove that any edge

H
which connects two vertices in the same component of
G
is spectrally insignificant (that is, the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{G}+\mathcal{L}_{H}
is closely approximated by the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{G}+\mathcal{L}_{H-e}
). Furthermore, two edges of
H
which connect the same pair of connected components of
G
can be “aggregated” into a single edge without significantly changing the spectrum. As such, once a sparsifier of
G
is computed,
H
can be aggregated into a much smaller graph whose sparsifier size is proportional to the number of connected components of
G
. Thus, when aggregating a (potentially) large number of weight buckets, the total sparsifier size will still be near-linear.

Planted CSPs

Kikuchi sparsification has natural connection to solving (semirandom) planted instances of the

Harmonic Analysis on the Hypercube Slice

In this section we develop harmonic analysis on the slices of the boolean hypercube
\binom{[n]}{k}
. We stress that all the material in this section is standard, and we present it here for easy reference.

We do so by defining the
down
and
up

[
37
, Section 2]
. Harmonic analysis on the slice of the hypercube is a very well-studied field with connections to multiple areas, see
[

,
71
,
35
,
31
,

]
for other perspectives.

Definition 3.1

(Down and Up operators)
.

For
k\geqslant 1
, define the
down operator

\operatorname{D}_{k}:V_{k}\to V_{k-1}
, where for any
f\in V_{k}
and
S\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
, we define

(\operatorname{D}_{k}f)(S):=\sum_{\binom{[n]}{k}\ni T\supset S}f(T)\,.

Similarly, for any
k<n
, define the
up operator

\operatorname{U}_{k}:V_{k}\to V_{k+1}
, where for any
f\in V_{k}
and
S\in\binom{[n]}{k+1}
, we define

(\operatorname{U}_{k}f)(S):=\sum_{\binom{[n]}{k}\ni T\subset S}f(T)\,.

Finally, for any
0\leqslant k<\ell\leqslant n
, define the map
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}:V_{k}\to V_{\ell}
as
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}:=\operatorname{U}_{\ell-1}\circ\cdots\circ\operatorname{U}_{k+1}\circ\operatorname{U}_{k}
. We also define
\operatorname{U}^{(k)}_{k}:V_{k}\to V_{k}
to be the identity map
\operatorname{Id}_{V_{k}}
.

These operators allow us to define
harmonic functions
:

Definition 3.2

(Harmonic Function)
.

A function
f\in V_{k}
is called
harmonic
if
\operatorname{D}_{k}f=0
. More explicitly, if
f
is harmonic, then for any
T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
, we have

\sum_{t\in[n]\setminus T}f(T\cup\{t\})=0\,.

For
k>0
, define
H_{k}:=\ker(\operatorname{D}_{k})\subset V_{k}
to be the subspace of harmonic functions. For
k=0
, define
H_{0}:=V_{0}
. Finally, for any
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
define the subspace

E_{k}:=H_{k}\oplus\operatorname{U}^{(k+1)}_{k}(H_{k})\oplus\cdots\oplus\operatorname{U}^{(n-k)}_{k}(H_{k})\subset\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}\,.

(4)

Note that the direct sum in
Eq.

4
is orthogonal, since for any
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\in\{k,\ldots,n-k\}
, functions in
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}(H_{k}),\operatorname{U}^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{k}(H_{k})
are supported on disjoint sets (
\binom{[n]}{\ell}
and
\binom{[n]}{\ell^{\prime}}
respectively) and are thus orthogonal according to
Eq.

3
.

It is useful to record certain preliminary properties of the down and up operators:

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
k
such that the relevant symbols are defined, we have:

(1)

\operatorname{D}_{k}=\operatorname{U}^{*}_{k-1}
, i.e.
\operatorname{D}_{k}
and
\operatorname{U}_{k-1}
are adjoints of each other w.r.t. the inner product in
Eq.

3
.

(2)

\operatorname{D}_{k+1}\operatorname{U}_{k}-\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k}=(n-2k)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{V_{k}}
.

(3)

For
0\leqslant k<n/2
,
\operatorname{U}_{k}
is injective. Consequently,
\operatorname{D}_{k}
is surjective for
1\leqslant k<n/2+1
by adjointness.

(4)

For any
0\leqslant k\leqslant n/2
,
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}
is injective on
H_{k}
for any
k\leqslant\ell\leqslant n-k
.

Proof.

To show that
\operatorname{D}_{k}=\operatorname{U}_{k-1}^{*}
, we have to show that for any
f\in V_{k},g\in V_{k-1}
,
\langle\operatorname{D}_{k}f,g\rangle=\langle f,\operatorname{U}_{k-1}g\rangle
. To that extent,

\langle\operatorname{D}_{k}f,g\rangle=\sum_{T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}}(\operatorname{D}_{k}f)(T)\cdot\overline{g(T)}=\sum_{T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}}\sum_{S\supset T,|S|=k}f(S)\cdot\overline{g(T)}=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sum_{T\subset S,|T|=k-1}f(S)\cdot\overline{g(T)}

=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}f(S)\cdot\sum_{T\subset S,|T|=k-1}\overline{g(T)}=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}f(S)\cdot\overline{(\operatorname{U}_{k-1}g)(S)}=\langle f,\operatorname{U}_{k-1}g\rangle,

as desired.

For the second item, fix any
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}
and note that

\displaystyle(\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k}f)(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\subset S}(\operatorname{D}_{k}f)(T)=\sum_{T\subset S}\sum_{S^{\prime}\supset T}f(S^{\prime})=k\cdot f(S)+\sum_{S^{\prime}:|S\oplus S^{\prime}|=2}f(S^{\prime}),

(5)

\displaystyle(\operatorname{D}_{k+1}\operatorname{U}_{k}f)(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\supset S}(\operatorname{U}_{k}f)(T)=\sum_{T\supset S}\sum_{S^{\prime}\subset T}f(S^{\prime})=(n-k)\cdot f(S)+\sum_{S^{\prime}:|S\oplus S^{\prime}|=2}f(S^{\prime}),

(6)

as desired.

For the third item, note that
\operatorname{U}_{0}
is obviously injective. Thus assume
1\leqslant k<n/2
, and note that

\|\operatorname{U}_{k}f\|^{2}=\langle\operatorname{U}_{k}f,\operatorname{U}_{k}f\rangle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{item:adjoint}}}{=}\langle f,\operatorname{D}_{k+1}\operatorname{U}_{k}f\rangle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{item:commrel}}}{=}\langle f,((n-2k)\operatorname{Id}+\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k})f\rangle

=(n-2k)\|f\|^{2}+\langle f,\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k}f\rangle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{item:adjoint}}}{=}(n-2k)\|f\|^{2}+\langle\operatorname{D}_{k}f,\operatorname{D}_{k}f\rangle=(n-2k)\|f\|^{2}+\|\operatorname{D}_{k}f\|^{2}.

Consequently, if
\operatorname{U}_{k}f=0
, then
\|\operatorname{U}_{k}f\|=0\implies\|f\|=0\implies f=0
, since
n-2k>0
. Consequently,
\operatorname{U}_{k}
is injective.

Finally, consider
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}
for any
k\leqslant\ell\leqslant n-k
. Note that if
k=n/2
or
k=\ell
, then
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}=\operatorname{Id}_{V_{k}}
is injective. Similarly, if
k=0
, then
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}
is also injective since
\dim(V_{0})=1
. Thus assume
1\leqslant k<\min\{\ell,n/2\}
. Fix any
\psi_{k}\in H_{k}
, and write
\psi_{m}:=\operatorname{U}^{(m)}_{k}\psi_{k}
for all
k\leqslant m\leqslant n-k
. We claim that for all
k+1\leqslant m\leqslant n-k
,
\operatorname{D}_{m}\psi_{m}=c_{m}\psi_{m-1}
, for some
c_{m}>0
. Note that this proves that
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}
is injective since

\|\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}\psi_{k}\|^{2}=\langle\psi_{\ell},\psi_{\ell}\rangle=\langle\operatorname{U}_{\ell-1}\psi_{\ell-1},\psi_{\ell}\rangle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{item:adjoint}}}{=}\langle\psi_{\ell-1},\operatorname{D}_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}\rangle=c_{\ell}\langle\psi_{\ell-1},\psi_{\ell-1}\rangle=c_{\ell}\|\operatorname{U}^{(\ell-1)}_{k}\psi_{k}\|^{2}

\implies\|\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}\psi_{k}\|^{2}=\left(\prod_{m=k+1}^{\ell}c_{m}\right)\cdot\|\psi_{k}\|^{2},

and thus if
\operatorname{U}^{(\ell)}_{k}\psi_{k}=0
then
\psi_{k}=0
since
\prod_{m=k+1}^{\ell}c_{m}>0
. Thus, it only remains to prove that
\operatorname{D}_{m}\psi_{m}=c_{m}\psi_{m-1}
, which we prove inductively. For convenience set
c_{k}:=0
(since
\operatorname{D}_{k}\psi_{k}=0
) and
\psi_{k^{\prime}}:=0
for all integers

. Then for any

\operatorname{D}_{m}\psi_{m}=\operatorname{D}_{m}\operatorname{U}_{m-1}\psi_{m-1}\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{item:commrel}}}{=}\left((n-2(m-1))\operatorname{Id}+\operatorname{U}_{m-2}\operatorname{D}_{m-1}\right)\psi_{m-1}

and thus we get the recurrence relation

In this section we develop yet another point of view on the operator

Towards that end, let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts naturally on

\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
sends

\pi S:=\{\pi(s):s\in S\}

A_{\pi}:\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}
, where for any
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}

(A_{\pi}\psi)(S):=\psi(\pi S)\,.

Note that
A_{\pi}(V_{k})\subseteq V_{k}
for all

|\pi S|=|S|
for any
S\in 2^{[n]}
. Also note that
A_{\pi}
is unitary for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Now, for any
e=\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, denote by
(e):=(ij)
the transposition swapping

and

(e)S=\begin{cases}S&\text{if }|S\cap e|\in\{0,2\},\\
S\oplus e&\text{if }|S\cap e|=1\end{cases}.

We claim that
A_{e}
is Hermitian, where we simplify notation to write
A_{(e)}

A_{e}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
e=\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
,
A_{e}
is Hermitian and unitary. Consequently, all its eigenvalues are
\pm 1
.

Proof.

Fix any
\psi,\phi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}
. Then

\langle\psi,A_{e}\phi\rangle=\sum_{S\in 2^{[n]}}\psi(S)\cdot\overline{\phi((e)S)}=\sum_{|S\cap e|\in\{0,2\}}\psi(S)\cdot\overline{\phi(S)}+\sum_{|S\cap e|=1}\psi(S)\cdot\overline{\phi(S\oplus e)},

\langle A_{e}\psi,\phi\rangle=\sum_{S\in 2^{[n]}}\psi((e)S)\cdot\overline{\phi(S)}=\sum_{|S\cap e|\in\{0,2\}}\psi(S)\cdot\overline{\phi(S)}+\sum_{|S\cap e|=1}\psi(S\oplus e)\cdot\overline{\phi(S)}.

But note that
S\mapsto S\oplus e
is a bijection on the set
\{S\in 2^{[n]}:|S\oplus e|=1\}
since
(S\oplus e)\oplus e=S
, and thus
\langle\psi,A_{e}\phi\rangle=\langle A_{e}\psi,\phi\rangle
, thus showing that
A_{e}
is Hermitian, as desired.
∎

We now claim that
\operatorname{Id}-A_{e}

\mathcal{L}_{e}
defined in

\langle\psi,\mathcal{L}_{e}\psi\rangle=\sum_{\{S,S\oplus e\}\in E(\mathcal{K}_{e})}|\psi(S)-\psi(S\oplus e)|^{2}\,.

On the other hand,

\langle\psi,(\operatorname{Id}-A_{e})\psi\rangle=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\psi(S)\cdot\overline{(\psi(S)-(A_{e}\psi)(S))}=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}:|S\cap e|=1}\psi(S)\cdot\overline{(\psi(S)-\psi(S\oplus e))}

=\sum_{\{S,S\oplus e\}\in E(\mathcal{K}_{e})}\left(\psi(S)\cdot\overline{(\psi(S)-\psi(S\oplus e))}+\psi(S\oplus e)\cdot\overline{(\psi(S\oplus e)-\psi(S))}\right)

=\sum_{\{S,S\oplus e\}\in E(\mathcal{K}_{e})}\left|\psi(S)-\psi(S\oplus e)\right|^{2}=\langle\psi,\mathcal{L}_{e}\psi\rangle,

thus showing that
\mathcal{L}_{e}=\operatorname{Id}-A_{e}
.
6
6

6

Note that
A_{e}
is not exactly the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{K}_{e}
: Instead,
A_{e}
is the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{\prime}
, where

is the graph which has a self-loop for all vertices with zero degree in

is
the Laplacian for

since the

in the diagonal entry (corresponding to a degree

) of
\operatorname{Id}

in the diagonal of
A_{e}
representing the self-loop

We’ll now use this interpretation of

to prove that the spaces

\mathcal{L}_{e}
for any

, and then we shall subsequently also compute the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}
, where

Now, for any

define the linear operator

\mathcal{L}_{e}:=\operatorname{Id}-A_{e}
. Note that by

is a Hermitian PSD operator all of whose eigenvalues are

or
2
.

For any weighted graph

.

We now show that the spaces

defined in

.

Lemma 4.2

(Invariant Spaces)
.

For any
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, and any

, we have
\mathcal{L}_{e}(E_{k})\subseteq E_{k}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
A_{e}(E_{k})\subseteq E_{k}
. We first claim that
A_{e}
commutes with
\operatorname{U}_{k}
for any
0\leqslant k<n
: Indeed for any
\psi\in V_{k}
and
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}
, we have

(A_{e}\operatorname{U}_{k}\psi)(S)=(\operatorname{U}_{k}\psi)((e)S)=\sum_{T\subset(e)S}\psi(T)\overset{(\ast)}{=}\sum_{T\subset S}\psi((e)T)=\sum_{T\subset S}(A_{e}\psi)(T)=(\operatorname{U}_{k}A_{e}\psi)(S)\,,

where
(\ast)
uses the fact that
(e)^{2}=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
. Thus it suffices to show that
A_{e}(H_{k})\subseteq H_{k}
for all
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Since
H_{0}=V_{0}
and since
A_{e}(V_{k})\subseteq V_{k}
for any
k
, we’re done for

. Thus assume

, and recall that
\psi_{k}\in H_{k}
iff
\sum_{t\in[n]\setminus T}\psi_{k}(T\cup\{t\})=0
for all
T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}
. Now, fix any

and note that

\sum_{t\in[n]\setminus T}(A_{e}\psi_{k})(T\cup\{t\})=\sum_{t\in[n]\setminus T}\psi_{k}((e)(T\cup\{t\}))=\sum_{s\in[n]\setminus(e)T}\psi_{k}((e)T\cup\{s\})=0,

and thus
A_{e}H_{k}\subseteq H_{k}
, as desired.
∎

Finally, let
K_{n}=\left([n],\binom{[n]}{2}\right)
denote the complete (unweighted) graph on

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}|_{E_{k}}=k(n+1-k)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{E_{k}}
. Consequently, the decomposition in
Lemma

3.5
is the eigendecomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}
.

Proof.

Note that
E_{0}
is the subspace of all functions
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}
such that
\psi|_{V_{k}}
is a constant function for any
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
.
7
7

7

\psi|_{V_{k}}
and

need not assume the

though
For such functions the assertion can be seen to be true by direct computation. Thus assume

. Fix any
\psi_{k}\in V_{k}

(\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k}\psi)(S)=k\psi(S)+\sum_{S^{\prime}:|S\oplus S^{\prime}|=2}\psi(S^{\prime})=k\psi(S)+\sum_{x\in S,y\in[n]\setminus S}\psi(S\oplus\{x,y\})\,.

(8)

On the other hand, we also have

(\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}\psi)(S)=\sum_{e\in\binom{[n]}{2}}(\mathcal{L}_{e}\psi)(S)=\sum_{e\in\binom{[n]}{2}}(\psi(S)-\psi(S\oplus e))=\sum_{e:|S\cap e|=1}(\psi(S)-\psi(S\oplus e))

=k(n-k)\psi(S)-\sum_{x\in S,y\in[n]\setminus S}\psi(S\oplus\{x,y\})\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:udpsis}}}{=}k(n-k+1)\psi(S)-(\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k}\psi)(S),

and thus
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}|_{V_{k}}=k(n-k+1)\operatorname{Id}_{V_{k}}-\operatorname{U}_{k-1}\operatorname{D}_{k}
for all

8

8

recall that since

for all

\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}V_{k}\subseteq V_{k}

Now, to show that
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}|_{E_{k}}=k(n+1-k)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{E_{k}}
it suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}|_{\operatorname{U}_{k}^{(\ell)}(H_{k})}=k(n+1-k)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{\operatorname{U}_{k}^{(\ell)}(H_{k})}

5
Applying Spectral Bounds for the Interchange Process

To understand the spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}_{G}
for an arbitrary graph
G=([n],E,w)
, we find it useful to look at more general operators related to the
interchange process
on the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

[
3
,
28
,
22
]
.

5.1
The Interchange Process

In this section, we adapt the notation of Alon and Kozma
[
5
]
. We let
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
denote the ring of formal sums of the form
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}\sigma
where each
c_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}
is arbitrary. Multiplication in
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
is defined as follows

\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\right)\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c^{\prime}_{\sigma}\sigma\right)=\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}c^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\sigma\sigma^{\prime}),

where
\sigma\sigma^{\prime}
is multiplication in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We can think of multiplication as an operator in the function space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
(i.e., the space of functions
f:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
with the
L^{2}
norm), where
L=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
acts on
f\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
via

(Lf)(\sigma^{\prime})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{\sigma}f(\sigma\sigma^{\prime}).

As such, we can say that an operator
L\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
is PSD (i.e.,
L\succcurlyeq 0
) if
\langle f,Lf\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
.

For any edge
e=\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, we define
\nabla_{e}=\operatorname{id}-(ij)\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
, where
\operatorname{id}
is the identity permutation and
(ij)
is the transposition between
i
and
j
. Using these
\nabla_{e}
operators, we can build a corresponding operator
\nabla_{G}
for any weighted graph
G=([n],E,w)
as follows

\nabla_{G}:=\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}\nabla_{e}.

Crucially, any spectral inequalities on
\nabla_{G}
translate to spectral inequalities on
\mathcal{L}_{G}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
G=([n],E,w)
and
H=([n],E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
be weighted graphs and
\lambda\geqslant 0
a scalar. If
\nabla_{G}\succcurlyeq\lambda\nabla_{H}
then
\mathcal{L}_{G}\succcurlyeq\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\nabla_{G}\succcurlyeq\lambda\nabla_{H}
, we seek to prove that
\mathcal{L}_{G}\succcurlyeq\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H}
. To do this, it suffices to show for any
f\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{[n]}}
that

\langle f,(\mathcal{L}_{G}-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H})f\rangle\geqslant 0.

As a first observation, write
f=f_{0}+f_{1}+\cdots+f_{n}
where
f_{k}\in V_{k}=\mathbb{C}^{\binom{[n]}{k}}
for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Then, observe that

\langle f,(\mathcal{L}_{G}-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H})f\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\langle f_{k},(\mathcal{L}_{G}-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H})f_{k}\rangle,

so it suffices to prove that
\langle f_{k},(\mathcal{L}_{G}-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H})f_{k}\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
k\in[n]
. This later fact follows from an observation of Caputo, Liggett, and Richthammer
[
22
]
that the
exclusion process
is a subprocess of the interchange process. That is,
\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{V_{k}}
is a projection of
\nabla_{G}
. More explicitly, given
f_{k}\in V_{k}
define
g_{k}\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
by

g_{k}(\sigma)=c_{k}f_{k}(\{\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(k)\}),

where
c_{k}=\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}
. Then, for any
e=\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle g_{k},\nabla_{e}g_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}g_{k}(\pi)(\nabla_{e}g_{k})(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}g_{k}(\pi)\left[g_{k}(\pi)-g_{k}((ij)\pi)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c^{2}_{k}f_{k}(\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(k)\})\left[f_{k}(\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(k)\})-f_{k}((ij)\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(k)\})\right],

where
(ij)\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(k)\}
is the action of the transposition
(ij)
on the latter set. Observe for any set
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}
there are exactly
k!(n-k)!=1/c_{k}
choices of
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
S=\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(k)\}.
Therefore, the above sum can be simplified to

\displaystyle\langle g_{k},\nabla_{e}g_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}f_{k}(S)(f_{k}(S)-f_{k}((ij)S))

\displaystyle=\langle f_{k},\mathcal{L}_{e}f_{k}\rangle.

To finish, observe that

\displaystyle\langle f_{k},(\mathcal{L}_{G}-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{H})f_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\langle f_{k},(w_{e}-\lambda w^{\prime}_{e})\mathcal{L}_{e}f_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\langle g_{k},(w_{e}-\lambda w^{\prime}_{e})\nabla_{e}g_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle g_{k},(\nabla_{G}-\lambda\nabla_{H})g_{k}\rangle\geqslant 0,

as desired.
∎

5.2
Relating Expanders to the Complete Graph

A recent work by Alon and Kozma
[
5
]
, building off of the work of Caputo, Liggett, and Richthammer
[
22
]
, showed that
\nabla_{G}\succcurlyeq\lambda\nabla_{H}
often holds when
G
is an expander and
H
is the complete instance
K_{n}
. To discuss this relationship, we need to define a discrete Markov chain considered by
[
5
]
. For a graph
G=([n],E,w)
, for any
i\in[n]
define
w_{i}:=\sum_{e\in E,i\in e}w_{e}
. Then, let
M_{G}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be the transition matrix with probabilities

(M_{G})_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}\frac{w_{ij}}{2w_{i}}&\{i,j\}\in E\\
\frac{1}{2}&i=j\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
\pi_{G}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the unique stationary measure corresponding to
M_{G}
. That is,
\pi
is the unique probability distribution for which
\pi_{G}M_{G}=\pi_{G}
. In fact, one can show in this case that
\pi_{G}(i)=\frac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}
for all
i\in[n]
. Alon and Kozma define the discrete mixing time
\operatorname{dmix}G
to be

\operatorname{dmix}G:=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}\mid(M_{G}^{t})_{i,j}>\frac{3}{4}\pi(j)\right\}.

We also define an “error” parameter
\delta_{G}
as follows (see equation 4 of
[
5
]
)

\frac{1}{\delta_{G}}:=\prod_{k=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}\operatorname{dmix}G\rfloor}\max_{i\in[n]}\left[1+(M_{G}^{2^{k}})_{i,i}\right].

Theorem 5.2

(Theorem 1
[
5
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
c_{\mathsf{AK}}>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any graph
G=([n],E,w)
, we have that

\nabla_{G}\succcurlyeq\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\delta_{G}}{\operatorname{dmix}G}\cdot\frac{\min_{i\in[n]}w_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\nabla_{K_{n}}.

Furthermore,
\delta_{G}\geqslant\frac{1}{2\operatorname{dmix}G}
.

5.3
From Conductance to Discrete Mixing

Given a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
, we define
\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}
, where we recall that
w_{i}=\sum_{e\in E,i\in e}w_{e}
. Further define

\displaystyle\alpha_{G}=\frac{\min_{i\in n}w_{i}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}

(9)

to be the ratio between the minimum degree and average degree of
G
. We say that
G
is a
\phi
-conductance expander if for every cut
S\subseteq V
for which
\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(S)\leqslant\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(V)/2
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{w(S,\overline{S})}{\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(S)}\geqslant\phi,

(10)

where
w(A,B)=\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}w_{ij}
. Our goal is to show the following.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
G=(V,E,w)
is a
\phi
-conductance expander. Then,
\operatorname{dmix}G\leqslant\frac{8+8\log(n/\alpha_{G})}{\phi^{2}}
.

The proof of
Lemma

5.3
proceeds by using a fact of Morris and Peres
[
62
]
which connects the discrete mixing time of a Markov chain with its conductance. First, define a quantity for all
S\subseteq[n]
by

M_{G}(S,\overline{S}):=\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in\overline{S}}\pi_{G}(i)(M_{G})_{i,j}

to measure the density of edges leaving
S
. Let
\pi^{*}_{G}:=\min_{i\in[n]}\pi_{G}(i)
. For
r\in[\pi^{*}_{G},1/2]
define

\phi_{G}(r):=\inf\left\{\frac{M_{G}(S,\overline{S})}{\pi_{G}(S)}\mid S\subseteq[n],\pi_{G}(S)\leqslant r\right\}.

Further, let
\phi_{G}(r)=\phi_{G}(1/2)
for all
r\geqslant 1/2
. Then, using the fact that
(M_{G})_{i,i}\geqslant 1/2
for all
i\in[n]
, we have the following result due to Morris and Peres
[
62
]
.

Theorem 5.4

(Theorem 1
[
62
]
)
.

For any
i,j\in[n]
and any
\varepsilon>0
we have that

t\geqslant 1+\int_{4\min(\pi(i),\pi(j))}^{4/\varepsilon}\frac{4dr}{r\phi_{G}^{2}(r)}\implies\left|\frac{(M_{G}^{t})_{i,j}-\pi_{j}}{\pi_{j}}\right|\leqslant\varepsilon.

We can now prove
Lemma

5.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

First, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{M_{G}(S,\overline{S})}{\pi_{G}(S)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{G}(S)}\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in\overline{S}}\pi_{G}(i)(M_{G})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}{\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}}\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in\overline{S}}\frac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}\cdot\frac{w_{i,j}}{2w_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}}w(S,\overline{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{w(S,\overline{S})}{\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(S)}.

Furthermore,
\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(V)}=\pi_{G}(S)
. Therefore, by
Eq.

10
, we have that
\phi_{G}(r)\geqslant\frac{\phi}{2}
for all
r\in[\pi_{G}^{*},1/2]
. Observe by simple algebra for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
the implication that

\left|\frac{(M_{G}^{t})_{i,j}-\pi_{j}}{\pi_{j}}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{5}\implies(M_{G}^{t})_{i,j}>\frac{3}{4}\pi_{i,j}.

Thus, if we apply
Theorem

5.4
with
\varepsilon=1/5
we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dmix}G

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{i,j}\left[2+\int_{4\min(\pi(i),\pi(j))}^{4/\varepsilon}\frac{4dr}{r\phi_{G}^{2}(r)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2+\int_{4\pi^{*}_{G}}^{20}\frac{16dr}{r\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2+\frac{2}{\phi^{2}}\left[\log r\right]_{4\pi^{*}_{G}}^{20}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2+\frac{2(\log 20-4\log\pi^{*}_{G})}{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{8+8\log\frac{1}{\pi^{*}_{G}}}{\phi^{2}},

where the last line uses the fact that
\phi\leqslant 1
. Now, note that
\pi^{*}_{G}=\frac{\alpha_{G}}{n}
. Thus,
\operatorname{dmix}G\leqslant\frac{8+8\log(n/\alpha_{G})}{\phi^{2}}
, as desired.
∎

5.4
A Spectral Bound for Expanders

By combining the results from the previous sections, we can conclude with our key spectral bound.

Theorem 5.5
.

Assume that
G=(V,E,w)
with
|V|=n
is a
\phi
-conductance expander with
\alpha_{G}
the ratio between minimum and average degree. Then,

\mathcal{L}_{G}\succcurlyeq\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\phi^{4}\alpha_{G}^{2}}{128(1+\log(n/\alpha_{G}))^{2}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{G}(V)}{n^{2}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.3
, we have that
\operatorname{dmix}G\leqslant\frac{8+8\log(n/\alpha_{G})}{\phi^{2}}
. If we apply
Theorem

5.2
with the bound
\delta_{G}\geqslant\frac{1}{2\operatorname{dmix}G}
, we get that

\displaystyle\nabla_{G}

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}}{2(\operatorname{dmix}G)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\min_{i\in[n]}w_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\nabla_{K_{n}}.

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\phi^{4}}{128(1+\log(n/\alpha_{G}))^{2}}\cdot\frac{\frac{\alpha_{G}^{2}}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}\nabla K_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\phi^{4}\alpha_{G}^{2}}{128(1+\log(n/\alpha_{G}))^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}{n^{2}}\cdot\nabla_{K_{n}}.

To finish, we use
Proposition

5.1
to replace
\nabla_{G}
and
\nabla_{K_{n}}
with
\mathcal{L}_{G}
and
\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}
, respectively.
∎

6
Sparsifying Expanders

In this section, we consider sparsifying the Kikuchi-graph of unweighted expanders
G=(V,E)
.
Inspired by the above, we will assume that our graph
G=(V,E)
is a good
conductance expander
and that the minimum degree of any vertex is within a logarithmic factor of the
average degree
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
n^{\prime}\leqslant n
vertices and
m
edges such that
G
is a
\phi
conductance expander and has minimum-degree balance parameter
\alpha_{G}
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, let
m^{\prime}=\frac{100n^{\prime}\log(n)}{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}}
for
\lambda=\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\phi^{4}\alpha_{G}^{2}}{128(1+\log(n^{\prime}/\alpha_{G}))^{2}}
, and let
p=\min\left(1,\frac{m^{\prime}}{m}\right)
. Consider the random process of sampling each edge
e\in E
with probability
p
, and giving weight
1/p
to each sampled edge. Denote this resulting graph by
\widetilde{G}
. Then, with probability
\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{n^{8}}
over the random sampling, it is the case that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G},

and
\widetilde{G}
has
\leqslant 2m^{\prime}
many edges.

Proof.

Note that if
p=1
or
n=1
, the claim holds trivially. Thus, we assume
p<1
and
n\geqslant 2
going forward.

For each edge
e\in E
, we let
X_{e}
denote a random variable such that:

X_{e}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{e}\text{ with probability }p\\
0\quad\quad\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Note that by
Proposition

4.1
all of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{e}=\operatorname{Id}-A_{e}
lie in
[0,2]
.

Now, recall that our goal is to show

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}.

By
Lemma

3.5
and
Lemma

4.2
, it suffices to prove that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}

for
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n^{\prime}/2\rfloor
. To this end, let us now fix a single such
k
. Note that we can assume that
k>0
, as for any edge

(\mathcal{L}_{e})|_{E_{0}}=0
(thus the quadratic form on
E_{0}
is preserved automatically).

Importantly, we can then observe that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(X_{e}|_{E_{k}})\leqslant\frac{1}{p}\cdot\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{L}_{e}|_{E_{k}})\leqslant\frac{2}{p}.

(11)

At the same time, by
Lemma

4.3
, we know that

\mathcal{L}_{K_{n^{\prime}}}|_{E_{k}}=k(n^{\prime}+1-k)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{E_{k}}.

Plugging in our bound from
Theorem

5.5
(along with our definition of
\lambda
), we then see that

\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}\succcurlyeq\frac{2m}{n^{\prime 2}}\cdot\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_{K_{n^{\prime}}}.

Together, the preceding two claims then imply that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}\succcurlyeq\frac{2m}{n^{\prime 2}}\cdot\lambda\cdot k(n^{\prime}+1-k)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{E_{k}}.

(12)

Now, combining
Eq.

11
and
Eq.

12
, we then see that

\mathcal{L}_{e}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{R}\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}},

for

R=\frac{\frac{2m}{n^{\prime 2}}\cdot\lambda\cdot k(n^{\prime}+1-k)}{2/p}=\frac{p\cdot\frac{2m}{n^{\prime 2}}\cdot\lambda\cdot k(n^{\prime}+1-k)}{2}=\frac{m^{\prime}\cdot\frac{2m}{n^{\prime 2}}\cdot\lambda\cdot k(n^{\prime}+1-k)}{2m}

=\frac{\lambda\cdot k\cdot(n^{\prime}+1-k)\cdot 100n^{\prime}\log(n)}{n^{\prime 2}\cdot\lambda\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{100\log(n)\cdot k\cdot(n^{\prime}+1-k)}{n^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}.

In particular, we can observe that
n^{\prime}+1-k\geqslant n^{\prime}/2
, and thus

R\geqslant\frac{50\log(n)\cdot k}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

With this established, we now revisit
2.1
: we consider the sequence of variables
Y_{e}=X_{e}|_{E_{k}}\in E_{k}
. We then trivially have that
{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{e}Y_{e}]=\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}
. Thus,
2.1
implies that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}

with probability

\geqslant 1-2\cdot\dim(E_{k})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon^{2}\cdot R/3}\geqslant 1-2\cdot n^{\prime k+2}\cdot e^{-16k\log(n)}\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{n^{10k}}.

Note that in the inequality above, we have used the value of
R
computed above, along with
Proposition

3.4
to bound
\dim(E_{k})\leqslant(n^{\prime})^{k+2}
.

Now, we can take a union bound over all choices of
k
; in particular, the probability that for all
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor n^{\prime}/2\rfloor
we have
(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}|_{E_{k}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{k}}
is at least
1-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n^{\prime}/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{n^{10k}}\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{n^{9}}
.

Likewise, by a simple Chernoff bound, we see that with probability
1-1/n^{10}
, the desired bound on
|\widetilde{G}|
holds. Thus, we obtain our stated claim.
∎

7
Using Expander Decomposition to Sparsify All Kikuchi Graphs

To start, we use some basic extensions of the expander decomposition results of Saranurak and Wang
[
67
]
. Roughly speaking, these results show that for an arbitrary graph
G
, one can decompose it into smaller subgraphs each of which is a good expander. For our purposes, we will want the additional property that the
minimum-to-average
degree ratio is bounded.

Theorem 7.1
.

[See, for instance, Theorem 3.4 in
[
38
]
or Theorem 2.5 in
[
51
]
]
Let
G=(V,E)
be an unweighted graph with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m
. Then, there exists an edge-disjoint decomposition of
G
into smaller graphs
G_{1},\dots G_{k}
along with a universal constant
C
such that:

1.

Each vertex appears in
O(\log^{2}(m))
many of the graphs
G_{i}:i\in[k]
.

2.

Each of the graphs
G_{i}
is a
\geqslant\frac{1}{C\log(n)}
conductance expander.

3.

In each graph
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
, for all
v\in V_{i}
,
\mathrm{deg}_{G_{i}}(v)\geqslant\frac{1}{4C\log(n)}\cdot\frac{2|E_{i}|}{|V_{i}|}
. Equivalently,
\alpha_{G_{i}}\geqslant\frac{1}{4C\log(n)}
.

Intuitively, we will invoke
Theorem

7.1
to decompose the graph
G
into the expander components. Then, we will sparsify each of the individual expanders, and then the union of these sparsified expanders will be a sparsifier of the original graph. To do this, we first require a basic fact about the “composability” of sparsifiers:

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, let
G_{1},\dots G_{k}
be a (disjoint) partition of the edges of
G
, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Suppose that for each
i\in[k]
,
G^{\prime}_{i}
is a graph such that
(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G_{i}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{i}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G_{i}}
. Then, for
G^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in[k]}G^{\prime}_{i}
, it must be the case that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{i}}
. Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{i}}\succcurlyeq\sum_{i\in[k]}(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{L}_{G_{i}}
, and likewise
\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{i}}\preccurlyeq\sum_{i\in[k]}(1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{L}_{G_{i}}
.
∎

We also have the following proposition, which effectively shows that, whenever a base graph has “unused vertices” (in the sense that no edges are incident to these vertices), one can effectively
ignore
the unused vertices.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
H=(V_{H},E_{H})
denote a graph on vertex set
V_{H}
, and let
H^{\prime}=(V_{H},E^{\prime}_{H},w)
denote a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-Kikuchi sparsifier of
H
. Then, for the graph
\widehat{H}=(V,E_{H})
which lives on a larger vertex set
V_{H}\subseteq V
, it is the case that
\widehat{H^{\prime}}=(V,E^{\prime}_{H},w)
is a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-Kikuchi sparsifier of
H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

This follows because
\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{H}}=\mathcal{L}_{H}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{V-V_{H}}
, and likewise
\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{H^{\prime}}}=\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{V-V_{H}}
.
∎

7.1
Proving the Existence of Kikuchi Sparsifiers

With this in hand, we can prove our main theorem:

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an unweighted graph on
n
vertices and
m=\mathrm{poly}(n)
many edges, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a re-weighted subgraph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime},w)
such that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}

and
|E^{\prime}|\leqslant O(n\log^{11}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

To start, we invoke the expander decomposition statement of
Theorem

7.1
. This yields a decomposition of our graph
G
into
G_{1},\dots G_{k}
along with a universal constant
C
such that:

1.

Each vertex appears in
O(\log^{2}(m))
many of the graphs
G_{i}:i\in[k]
.

2.

Each of the graphs
G_{i}
is a
\geqslant\frac{1}{C\log(n)}
conductance expander.

3.

In each graph
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
, for all
v\in V_{i}
,
\mathrm{deg}_{G_{i}}(V_{i})\geqslant\frac{1}{4C\log(n)}\cdot\frac{2|E_{i}|}{|V_{i}|}
. Equivalently,
\alpha_{G_{i}}\geqslant\frac{1}{4C\log(n)}
.

Now, for each one of these graphs
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
, we invoke the expander sparsification statement of
Lemma

6.1
with
\alpha_{G_{i}}=\frac{1}{4C\log(n)}
, and
\phi=\frac{1}{C\log(n)}
. If
G_{i}
is a graph on
n^{\prime}_{i}=|V_{i}|
many vertices, then
Lemma

6.1
produces a sparsifier
G^{\prime}_{i}=(V_{i},E^{\prime}_{i},w)
with

|E^{\prime}_{i}|=\frac{100n_{i}^{\prime}\log(n)}{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}}

many edges, for
\lambda=\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\phi^{4}\alpha_{G_{i}}^{2}}{128(1+\log(n^{\prime}_{i}/\alpha_{G}))^{2}}
. Using our values of
\alpha_{G_{i}}
and
\phi
, we can see that
\lambda=\Omega(1/\log^{8}(n))
. Thus,
|E^{\prime}_{i}|=O(n^{\prime}_{i}\log^{9}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
. Note that because we are only proving an existential statement, we do not consider the failure probability in the sparsification process of
Lemma

6.1
; rather,
Lemma

6.1
suffices for proving that a sparsifier
G^{\prime}_{i}

exists
.

Finally, our resulting sparsifier
G^{\prime}
is simply
\sum_{i\in[k]}G^{\prime}_{i}
. The accuracy of the sparsifier follows from
Proposition

7.2
and
Proposition

7.3
. Note that the number of edges in
G^{\prime}
is bounded by

\sum_{i\in[k]}|E^{\prime}_{i}|\leqslant\sum_{i\in[k]}O(n^{\prime}_{i}\log^{9}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})\leqslant O(n\log^{11}(n)/\varepsilon^{2}),

where we have used the fact that
\sum_{i\in[k]}n^{\prime}_{i}=O(n\log^{2}(m))=O(n\log^{2}(n))
by the guarantees of the expander decomposition. This concludes the claim.
∎

7.2
An Efficient Sparsification Algorithm

To make
Theorem

7.4
algorithmic, we need an algorithmic analog of
Theorem

7.1
. For this, we use the expander decomposition of
[
17
]
:

Theorem 7.5
.

[See, for instance, Corollary 5.6 in
[
17
]
]
Let
G=(V,E)
be an unweighted graph with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m
. Then, there is a constant
C^{\prime}
such that for
\phi=\frac{1}{C^{\prime}\log^{4}(m)}
, one can construct in time
O(m\log^{10}(m))
an edge-disjoint partition of
G
into
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1}),\dots G_{k}=(V_{k},E_{k})
such that:

1.

\sum_{i\in[k]}|V_{i}|=O(n\log^{2}(m))
.

2.

Each of the graphs
G_{i}
is a
\phi
conductance expander.

3.

In each graph
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
, for all
v\in V_{i}
,
\mathrm{deg}_{G_{i}}(v)\geqslant\phi\cdot\frac{|E_{i}|}{4|V_{i}|}
. Equivalently,
\alpha_{G_{i}}\geqslant\frac{1}{8C^{\prime}\log^{4}(m)}
.

With this theorem, we can now prove a near-linear time algorithm for producing near-linear size Kikuchi sparsifiers:

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an unweighted graph on
n
vertices and
m=\mathrm{poly}(n)
many edges, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there is an
\widetilde{O}(m)
time algorithm which computes, with probability
1-\frac{1}{n^{6}}
over the algorithm’s randomness, a re-weighted subgraph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime},w)
such that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}

and
|E^{\prime}|\leqslant O(n\log^{29}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

To start, we invoke the expander decomposition statement of
Theorem

7.5
. For a universal constant
C^{\prime}
, this yields a decomposition in time
O(m\log^{10}(m))
of our graph
G
into
G_{1},\dots G_{k}
such that:

1.

\sum_{i\in[k]}|V_{i}|=O(n\log^{2}(m))
.

2.

Each of the graphs
G_{i}
is a
\phi=\frac{1}{C^{\prime}\log^{4}(m)}
conductance expander.

3.

In each graph
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
, for all
v\in V_{i}
,
\mathrm{deg}_{G_{i}}(v)\geqslant\phi\cdot\frac{|E_{i}|}{4|V_{i}|}
. Equivalently,
\alpha_{G_{i}}\geqslant\frac{1}{8C^{\prime}\log^{4}(m)}
.

Now, for each one of these graphs
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
, we invoke the expander sparsification statement of
Lemma

6.1
with
\alpha_{G_{i}}=\frac{1}{8C^{\prime}\log^{4}(m)}
, and
\phi=\frac{1}{C^{\prime}\log^{4}(m)}
. If
G_{i}
is a graph on
n^{\prime}_{i}=|V_{i}|
many vertices, then by random sampling in accordance with
Lemma

6.1
, we produce a sparsifier
G^{\prime}_{i}=(V_{i},E^{\prime}_{i},w)
with

|E^{\prime}_{i}|=\frac{100n_{i}^{\prime}\log(n)}{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}}

\lambda=\frac{c_{\mathsf{AK}}\phi^{4}\alpha_{G_{i}}^{2}}{128(1+\log(n^{\prime}_{i}/\alpha_{G}))^{2}}

\alpha_{G_{i}}
and
\phi

. Thus,
|E^{\prime}_{i}|=O(n^{\prime}_{i}\log^{27}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
. Moreover, the sparsification statement of

6.1
holds with probability
\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{n^{8}}

Finally, our resulting sparsifier
G^{\prime}

\sum_{i\in[k]}G^{\prime}_{i}

Proposition

7.2
and
Proposition

7.3

\sum_{i\in[k]}|E^{\prime}_{i}|\leqslant\sum_{i\in[k]}O(n^{\prime}_{i}\log^{27}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})\leqslant O(n\log^{29}(n)/\varepsilon^{2}),

where we have used the fact that
\sum_{i\in[k]}n^{\prime}_{i}=O(n\log^{2}(m))=O(n\log^{2}(n))
by the guarantees of the expander decomposition. Finally, we can take a union bound over all

(1\pm\varepsilon)

8
Extension to Weighted Graphs

So far, we have shown that
Theorem

1.2
holds for
unweighted
graphs. We now extend the sparsifer to cover weighted graphs as well.

8.1
Limited Aspect Ratio

As a first step, we show that we can always get a variant of
Theorem

1.2
if we are willing to pay a cost proportional to
\log\frac{\max w}{\min w}
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph on
n
vertices, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a re-weighted subgraph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
such that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}

and
|E^{\prime}|\leqslant O(n\log^{9}(n)\log^{2}\left(\frac{n\max w}{\min w}\right)/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

If
\varepsilon<1/n
, then we can return
G
itself and only have
n^{2}=O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
edges. Thus, assume
\varepsilon\geqslant 1/n
. Let
\widetilde{G}=(V,\widetilde{E})
be an unweighted graph such that for every
e\in E
, there are

\widetilde{w}(e)=\left\lfloor\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\min w}\right\rfloor

copies of
e
in
\widetilde{E}
. Observe that

m:=|\widetilde{E}|\leqslant\sum_{e\in E}\left\lfloor\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\min w}\right\rfloor\leqslant n^{2}\cdot\frac{3}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\max w}{\min w}\leqslant 3n^{3}\frac{\max w}{\min w}.

Upon careful inspection of the proof of
Theorem

7.4
, one can build a sparsifier
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}=(V,\widetilde{E}^{\prime},\widetilde{w}^{\prime})
of
\widetilde{G}
with

|\widetilde{E}^{\prime}|\leqslant O(n\log^{9}(n)\log^{2}(m)/\varepsilon^{2})=O(n\log^{9}(n)\log^{2}(n\max w/\min w)/\varepsilon^{2})

and

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}.

We now build
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
by setting
E^{\prime}=\widetilde{E}^{\prime}
and setting for all
e\in E^{\prime}
,

w^{\prime}(e)=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\widetilde{w}^{\prime}(e)\cdot\min w.

Thus,

\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}\preccurlyeq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}.

Note that for all
e\in E
, we have that

\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\widetilde{w}(e)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\min w}=w(e),

and since
\varepsilon<1
,

\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\widetilde{w}(e)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\left[\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\min w}-1\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\frac{3}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\min w}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)w(e).

Therefore,

(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)^{2}\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}

and

\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\cdot\min w\cdot\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{G}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{L}_{G},

as desired.
∎

8.2
An Effective Resistance Inequality

Given a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
, we let
R_{G}(u,v)
denote the effective resistance between vertices
u
and
v
in an electrical network with conductances indicated by
w:E\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
. More formally, for any edge
e=\{u,v\}
we define the effective resistance
R_{G}(e)
to be

R_{G}(e)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{V}\setminus\{0\}\\
\langle x,\mathbf{1}\rangle=0\end{subarray}}\frac{(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}}{\sum_{f=\{a,b\}\in E(G)}w_{f}\cdot(x_{a}-x_{b})^{2}}.

If
u
and
v
are in different connected components, then
R_{G}(u,v)=\infty
. We recall the following basic facts about effective resistance.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph. The following facts hold.

1.

For any edge
e\in E
,
R_{G}(e)\leqslant\frac{1}{w_{e}}
.

2.

[
70
, Lemma 12.9.1]

R_{G}:V\times V\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}\cup\{\infty\}
forms a metric.

Proof.

Since
Item

2
is proved in
[
70
, Lemma 12.9.1]
, we focus on proving
Item

1
, if
e=\{u,v\}\in E
then

R_{G}(e)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{V}\setminus\{0\}\\
\langle x,\mathbf{1}\rangle=0\end{subarray}}\frac{(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}}{\sum_{f=\{a,b\}\in E(G)}w_{f}\cdot(x_{a}-x_{b})^{2}}\leqslant\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{V}\setminus\{0\}\\
\langle x,\mathbf{1}\rangle=0\end{subarray}}\frac{(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}}{w_{e}(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}}=\frac{1}{w_{e}},

as desired.
∎

To relate effective resistances with the spectrum of the Quantum Cut Hamiltonian
\mathcal{L}_{G}
, we make use of the following theorem due to Chen
[
25
]
.

Theorem 8.3

(Theorem 1.1
[
25
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph. For any
u,v\in V
(for which it is not necessarily the case that
\{u,v\}\in E
), we have that

\mathcal{L}_{G}R_{G}(u,v)\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{u,v}.

Remark 8.4
.

Although Chen is not studying quantum cut, he built a linear operator identical to
\mathcal{L}_{G}
by studying a Markov chain on the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{V}
, where with probability proportional to
w_{e}
, the bits correspond to the edge
e
get swapped. Similar to the inequality of Alon-Kozma
[
5
]
, Theorem
8.3
crucially uses the octopus inequality of
[
22
]
in its proof.

Remark 8.5
.

Note that on its own
Theorem

8.3
is not sufficient to build Kikuchi sparsifiers. This is due to the fact that our sparsification results rely on the relative importance of an edge
u,v

decreasing
in the higher harmonic levels. As an example, in the complete graph
K_{n}
,
Theorem

8.3
roughly says that
\frac{1}{n-1}\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{u,v}
. While this on its own is tight, our sparsification argument roughly shows that, if we restrict to the orthogonal complement of a space of dimension
\binom{n}{i}
(for
i\leqslant n/2
), we can in fact show the much stronger inequality that
\frac{1}{i\cdot n}\mathcal{L}_{K_{n}}|_{E_{i}}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{u,v}|_{E_{i}}
.

Interestingly, this stronger inequality (properly normalized) fails to hold for general graphs. Indeed, let
G=([n],E)
be a tree on
n
vertices (with the weight of every edge being
1
), and fix any edge
e\in E(G)
. Note that the effective resistance of
e
is exactly
1
, and thus
Theorem

8.3
says that
\mathcal{L}_{G}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{e}
. We now claim that for any
\delta>0
,
(1-\delta)\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{i}}\not\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{e}|_{E_{i}}
for
every

1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
, i.e. the “relative importance/effective resistance” of
e
does not decay at all even as we go to “higher” eigenspaces! Indeed, first note that since
G
is a tree, deleting
e
disconnects
G
into exactly two connected components
C_{u},C_{v}\subset[n]
, where
u\in C_{u},v\in C_{v}
. Now consider the function
\psi\in V_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{\binom{[n]}{i}}
defined as
\psi(S):=\mathbf{1}(|S\cap C_{u}|\text{ is even})
. It is easy to see that
\langle\psi,(\mathcal{L}_{e}|_{E_{i}})\psi\rangle=\binom{n-2}{i-1}>0
, while
\langle\psi,(\mathcal{L}_{e^{\prime}}|_{E_{i}})\psi\rangle=0
for all
e^{\prime}\in E(G)\setminus\{e\}
, which proves our claim since
\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{i}}=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\mathcal{L}_{e}|_{E_{i}}
.

Intuitively, the idea is that for any graph
G
, if
e\in E(G)
is an “extremely important” edge, then the relative importance of
e
might not decay in the higher eigenspaces. However, one implication of expander decompositions is that
most
edges in a graph (with
\gg n\log^{O(1)}(n)
edges, say) can
not
be very important. For those edges, we indeed obtain some decay in the higher eigenspaces, and thus even though a decay of the sort
(1/i)\cdot R_{e}\mathcal{L}_{G}|_{E_{i}}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{e}|_{E_{i}}
might not hold for every edge
e
, it holds on “average” (upto some log factors), and that is enough for us to obtain a sparsifier.

8.3
An Edge-moving Lemma

Given weighted graphs
G=(V,E,w)
and
H=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
on the same vertex set
V
, we define
G\uplus H=(V,E^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime\prime})
to be the weighted graph on the edge set
E^{\prime\prime}=E\cup E^{\prime}
such that for all
e\in E^{\prime\prime}
, we have that

w^{\prime\prime}(e)=\begin{cases}w(e)+w^{\prime}(e)&e\in E\cap E^{\prime}\\
w(e)&e\in E\setminus E^{\prime}\\
w^{\prime}(e)&e\in E^{\prime}\setminus E.\end{cases}

From the definition, it follows that
\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H}=\mathcal{L}_{G}+\mathcal{L}_{H}
. Consider two graphs
G=(V,E,w)
and
H=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
on the same vertex set but their edge weights are widely different. For example, in
Section

8.4
, we consider the possibility that
\min w\geqslant n^{20}\max w^{\prime}
, where
n=|V|
. We would like to say that if an edge
e\in E^{\prime}
spans two connected components of
G
, then
e
can be “moved” to different vertices between the same two connected components with no consequence. We formalize this as follows

Definition 8.6
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph with connected components
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
. Let
H=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
and
H^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime\prime})
be weighted graphs on the same vertex set. We say that
H
and
H^{\prime}
are
equivalent
with respect to
G
if for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r
, we have that

\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}\cap(V_{i}\times V_{j})}w^{\prime}(e)=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime\prime}\cap(V_{i}\times V_{j})}w^{\prime\prime}(e).

Observe that the equivalence of
H
and
H^{\prime}
does not take into account edges which are within any connected component
V_{i}
of
G
. The goal of this section is to prove the following “Edge-moving Lemma.”

Lemma 8.7

(Edge-moving Lemma)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph, and let
H=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
and
H^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime\prime})
be weighted graphs which are equivalent with respect to
G
. Let
\lambda:=\frac{\min w}{\max w^{\prime}+\max w^{\prime\prime}}
. Then, for any
\eta\in(0,1/2]
, we have that

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}-\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}+\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H}.

(13)

As first step toward proving
Lemma

8.7
, we show that edges in
H
which are within a single connected component of
G
can be effectively ignored.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph with connected components
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
. Pick
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
as well as any distinct
u,v\in V_{i}
. We have that

\frac{n}{\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{u,v}.

Proof.

By
Theorem

8.3
, it suffices to prove that
\frac{n}{\min w}\geqslant R_{G}(u,v)
. To see why, by
Proposition

8.2
,
Item

1
we know that
R_{G}(e)\leqslant\frac{1}{\min w}
for all
e\in E
. Since
u
and
v
are in the same connected component, there is a chain of at most
n-1
edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}
connecting
u
and
v
. By
Proposition

8.2
,
Item

2
we have that

R_{G}(u,v)\leqslant R_{G}(e_{1})+\cdots+R_{G}(e_{\ell})\leqslant\frac{\ell}{\min w}\leqslant\frac{n}{\min w},

as desired.
∎

Second, we show that we can move one edge between connected components without affecting the sparsifier too much.

Lemma 8.9
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph with connected components
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
. Pick
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r
as well as
u_{i},v_{i}\in V_{i}
and
u_{j},v_{j}\in V_{j}
. For any
\delta>0
, we have that

\frac{(1+\delta)n}{\delta\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}+(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}_{u_{i},u_{j}}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{v_{i},v_{j}}.

Proof.

Define
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
to be a weighted graph where
E^{\prime}=E\cup\{(u_{i},u_{j})\}
and for all
e\in E^{\prime}
,

w^{\prime}(e)=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{\delta\min w}w(e)&e\in E\\
1&e=\{u_{i},u_{j}\}.\end{cases}

By definition, we have that
\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}=\frac{2n}{\delta\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}+\mathcal{L}_{u_{i},u_{j}}
. By
Theorem

8.3
, we thus have that

R_{G^{\prime}}(v_{i},v_{j})\left[\frac{n}{\delta\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}+\mathcal{L}_{u_{i},u_{j}}\right]\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{v_{i},v_{j}}.

Thus, it suffices to prove that
R_{G^{\prime}}(v_{i},v_{j})\leqslant 1+\delta
. Similar to the proof of
Lemma

8.8
, we make use of
Proposition

8.2
to argue this fact. In particular, by
Proposition

8.2
,
Item

1
we have for all
e\in E
that

R_{G^{\prime}}(e)\leqslant\frac{1}{w^{\prime}(e)}=\frac{1}{\frac{2n}{\delta\min w}w(e)}\leqslant\frac{1}{\frac{2n}{\delta\min w}\min w}=\frac{\delta}{n}.

Now, consider a shortest path from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
in
G^{\prime}
. This path will use at most
n-2
edges
e\in E
as well as the bridge edge
\{u_{i},u_{j}\}
. By
Proposition

8.2
,
Item

2
we can thus deduce that

R_{G^{\prime}}(v_{i},v_{j})\leqslant R_{G^{\prime}}(u_{i},u_{j})+(n-2)\cdot\frac{\delta}{n}\leqslant 1+\delta,

where the last step uses that
R_{G^{\prime}}(u_{i},u_{j})\leqslant 1
by
Proposition

8.2
,
Item

1
.∎

We are now equipped to prove
Lemma

8.7
.

Proof of
Lemma

8.7
.

If
n=1
, the inequality is trivial, so we assume that
n\geqslant 2
. First, write
H=H_{0}\uplus H_{1}
where
H_{0}=(V,E^{\prime}_{0},w^{\prime}_{0})
and
H_{1}=(V,E^{\prime}_{1},w^{\prime}_{1})
, where
E^{\prime}_{0}\subseteq E^{\prime}
is the set of edges of
H
within some component of
G
and
E^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq E^{\prime}
is the set of edges which cross two components of
G
. We define
H^{\prime}=H^{\prime}_{0}\uplus H^{\prime}_{1}
analogously. Observe that by
Lemma

8.8
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{0}}=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime\prime}_{0}}w^{\prime\prime}(e)\mathcal{L}_{e}\preccurlyeq\sum_{e\in E^{\prime\prime}_{0}}\frac{nw^{\prime\prime}(e)}{\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\frac{n^{3}\max w^{\prime\prime}}{\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\frac{n^{3}}{\lambda}\mathcal{L}_{G}.

(14)

We now seek to relate
\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}}
with
\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{1}}
. To do this, we first form what we call a
movement plan
between
H_{1}
and
H^{\prime}_{1}
. More precisely, we say that
\mathcal{M}=\{(\lambda_{i},e_{i},f_{i}):i\in[L]\}
is a movement plan between
H_{1}
and
H^{\prime}_{1}
if for all
i\in[L]
we have that
e_{i}\in E^{\prime}_{1}
,
f_{i}\in E^{\prime\prime}_{1}
and that
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
bridge the same pair of connected components of
G
. We further require that

\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{e_{i}}\text{\ \ \ and \ \ \ }\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}.

By
Definition

8.6
, such a movement plan always exists and may take it to have size
L\leqslant|E^{\prime}_{1}|+|E^{\prime\prime}_{1}|\leqslant 2\binom{n}{2}\leqslant n^{2}
by repeatedly picking arbitrary edges
e_{i}\in E^{\prime}_{1}
and
f_{i}\in E^{\prime\prime}_{1}
whose movements have not been fully assigned and setting
\lambda_{i}
to be as large as possible.

We now make crucial use of
Lemma

8.9
. Set
\delta=\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}
. For any
i\in[L]
, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\frac{(1+\delta)n}{\delta\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}+(1+\delta)\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{e_{i}}

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}.

Summing this inequality over all
i\in[L]
, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}\right)\frac{(1+\delta)n}{\delta\min w}\mathcal{L}_{G}+(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}_{H_{i}}

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{1}}.

Observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}_{1}}w^{\prime}(e)\leqslant n^{2}\max w^{\prime}
. Thus, we actually have that

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}\right)\frac{(1+\delta)n}{\delta\min w}\leqslant(1+\delta)\frac{n^{3}\max w^{\prime}}{\delta\min w}=(1+\delta)\frac{\eta\lambda\max w^{\prime}}{\min w}\leqslant(1+\delta)\eta.

That is,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n^{3}}{\lambda}+\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left(1+\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}\right)\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}}\geqslant\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{1}}.

(15)

We now seek to prove the RHS of
Eq.

13
. Observe that

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}+\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H}

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left(\frac{n^{3}}{\lambda}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\eta}-1\right)\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left(\frac{n^{3}}{\lambda}+\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left(1+\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}\right)\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}}

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left(\frac{n^{3}}{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left[\left(\frac{n^{3}}{\lambda}+\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G}+\left(1+\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}\right)\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle(\eta\leqslant 1/2)

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G}+\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{0}}+\mathcal{L}_{H^{\prime}_{1}}

\displaystyle(\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:H'_0}, \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:H'_1}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H^{\prime}}.

By symmetry, we know that
\left(1+\frac{n^{3}}{\eta\lambda}+\eta\right)\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H^{\prime}}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G\uplus H}
, from which we can deduce the LHS of
Eq.

13
by the identity that
\frac{1}{1+x}\geqslant 1-x
for all
x\geqslant 0
. Thus,
Eq.

13
holds.∎

8.4
Building the Sparsifier

We now prove our main weighted sparsifier theorem.

Theorem 8.10
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph on
n
vertices, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a re-weighted subgraph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
such that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{G}

and
|E^{\prime}|\leqslant O(n\log^{11}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

We may assume that
n
is at least a sufficiently large constant. Without loss of generality, we may normalize the weights of
G
so that
\max w=1
. We also may assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon\geqslant 1/n
. Set
\kappa=n^{-20}
, for integers
i\geqslant 0
, we define the
i
th weight bucket to be
B_{i}=[\kappa^{i},\kappa^{i+1}]
. Likewise, let
G_{i}
be
G
restricted to the weights in the
i
th bucket. Despite there being infinitely many buckets, at most
\binom{n}{2}
have an edge. Let
I
be the set of indices
i\in I
for which
G_{i}
is nonempty. Now define

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{even}}=\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}G_{i}\text{ \ \ \ and \ \ \ }G_{\mathrm{odd}}=\biguplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}G_{i}.

Clearly to sparsify
\mathcal{L}_{G}
it suffices to sparsify each of
\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even}}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{odd}}}
. We now demonstrate how to sparsify
\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even}}}
, with the sparsification of
\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{odd}}}
following by near-identical methods.

Given an even integer
t\geqslant 0
, define

G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}=\biguplus_{i\in I\cap\{0,2,4,\ldots,t\}}G_{i}.

Define
C_{t}
and
M_{t}
to be the number of connected components and edges which
G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}
has. We seek to prove by induction the following statement.

Proposition 8.11
.

For all
t
, we have that
G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}
has an
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{M_{t}}{n^{5}}
-sparsifier
G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}^{\prime}
with at most
f(n-C_{t})
edges, where
f(n)=\Theta(n\log^{11}(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
is a subadditive function.

As a base case of
t=0
, assume
G_{0}
has components
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
where
r=C_{0}
. We can apply
Theorem

8.1
on each component to get an
\varepsilon/2
-sparsifier with at most
f(|V_{i}|-1)
edges (using the fact that
\log(1/\kappa)=20\log n
). When these components are aggregated, the total sparsifier size is

\sum_{i=1}^{r}f(|V_{i}|-1)\leqslant f(n-r)=f(n-C_{0}),

as desired.

Now consider
t\geqslant 2
for which we assume by the inductive hypothesis that
Proposition

8.11
holds. If
G_{t+2}
is empty, then the inductive step is trivial. Thus, assume that
G_{t+2}
has at least one new edge. Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
(with
r=C_{t}
) be the connected components of
G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}
, and pick arbitrary representatives
v_{1}\in V_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in V_{r}
. Define a new graph
H_{t+2}=(V,E_{H},w_{H})
such that for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
where is an edge between
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E_{H}
with
w_{H}(v_{i},v_{j})
being the total sum of weights of edges between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
in
G_{t+2}
(unless
w_{H}(v_{i},v_{j})=0
in which case we do not include
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
in
E_{H}
). One can verify that
H_{t+2}
has exactly
C_{t+2}
connected components.

Let
H^{\prime}_{t+2}=(V,E^{\prime}_{H},w^{\prime}_{H})
be an
(\varepsilon/2)
-sparsifier of
H_{t+2}
with
f(C_{t}-C_{t+2})
edges. For every
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E^{\prime}_{H}
, let
\{u^{ij}_{i},u^{ij}_{j}\}\in E
be an edge of
G_{t+2}
which goes between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
.

Let
G^{\prime}_{t+2}=(V,E^{\prime}_{G},w^{\prime}_{G})
be a weighted graph where
E^{\prime}_{G}=\{\{u^{ij}_{i},u^{ij}_{j}\}\mid\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E^{\prime}_{H}\}
and
w^{\prime}_{G}(u^{ij}_{i},u^{ij}_{j})=w^{\prime}_{H}(v_{i},v_{j})
. We claim that
G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t+2}
can be chosen to be
G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G^{\prime}_{t+2}
. First, the edge count works out because
G^{\prime}_{t+2}
has the same number of edges as
H^{\prime}_{t+2}
which is
f(C_{t}-C_{t+2})
so
G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t+2}
has at most

f(n-C_{t})+f(C_{t}-C_{t+2})\leqslant f(n-C_{t+2})

edges, as desired. To check the accuracy of this sparsifier, we invoke
Lemma

8.7
multiple times with
\eta=1/n^{6}
and
\lambda\geqslant\kappa^{t}/(2n^{2}\kappa^{t+2})\geqslant n^{37}
. First, since
G_{t+2}
and
H_{t+2}
are equivalent, we may invoke the lemma to get

\left(1-\frac{2}{n^{6}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus H_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{2}{n^{6}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}.

Likewise, since
G^{\prime}_{t+2}
and
H^{\prime}_{t+2}
are equivalent, we may invoke the lemma again with respect to the graph
G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}
using
\eta=1/n^{6}
and
9
9

9

The choice of
\lambda
uses the fact that no sparsifier of
G_{t+2}
or
H_{t+2}
can have any edge weight exceed
(1+\varepsilon)n^{2}\kappa^{t+2}\leqslant 2n^{2}\kappa^{t+2}
.

\lambda\geqslant\kappa^{t}/(4n^{2}\kappa^{t+2})\geqslant n^{37}
to get that

\left(1-\frac{2}{n^{6}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus H^{\prime}_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G^{\prime}_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{2}{n^{6}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus H^{\prime}_{t+2}}.

Since
H^{\prime}_{t+2}
is an
(\varepsilon/2)
-sparsifier of
H_{t+2}
and
G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}
is an
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{M_{t}}{n^{5}}
-sparsifier
G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}
we also have that

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{M_{t}}{n^{5}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}\leqslant\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus H^{\prime}_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{M_{t}}{n^{5}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}.

Combining these three equations, we get that

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{M_{t}}{n^{5}}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{n^{6}}\right)^{2}\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G^{\prime}_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{M_{t}}{n^{5}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2}{n^{6}}\right)^{2}\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}

Since
M_{t}+1\leqslant M_{t+2}
and
n
is sufficiently large, we get that

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{M_{t+2}}{n^{5}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{L}_{G^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G^{\prime}_{t+2}}\preccurlyeq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{M_{t+2}}{n^{5}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{G_{\mathrm{even},\leqslant t}\uplus G_{t+2}},

completing the proof of the inductive hypothesis
Proposition

8.11
. From this,
Theorem

8.10
immediately follows.
∎

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we showed how to combine a harmonic decomposition of hypercube slices, a powerful operator inequality of Alon and Kozma, and an expander decomposition framework to efficiently compute near-optimal sparsifiers of all Quantum Cut Hamiltonians. We conclude with a few potential directions for future research.

Tightening
Theorem

1.2
.

Our current method of proving
Theorem

1.2
necessarily pays a number of
\log n
factors in the size of the sparsifier. On the other hand, in the classical setting, results like
[
15
]
show that
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
-sized sparsifiers are possible for spectral sparsifiers. Are
O_{\varepsilon}(n)
-sized quantum cut sparsifiers possible as well?

Sparsifiers for Broader Families of Local Hamiltonians.

As pursued in
[
12
]
, sparisfying quantum cut is one example of a large variety of local Hamiltonians which one could hope to sparsify. Our
Theorem

1.2
not only resolves Question 10.2 of
[
12
]
, but our methods also give partial insight into their Question 10.1 on relating the non-redundancy of systems of local Hamiltonians to their ultimate sparsifiability. As a first goal, we believe that with tools like the Alon-Kozma inequality
[
5
]
, it should be feasible to classify the sparsifiability of all 2-local Hamiltonian systems.

Further Applications of the Octopus Inequality.

The key technical tool toward proving the Alon-Kozma inequality was the octopus inequality designed by Caputo, Liggett, and Richthammer
[
22
]
. Given our paper is the first to apply this octopus inequality to sparsification, we hope that our work encourages the study of more applications of the octopus inequality to various problems in quantum physics and theoretical computer science.

Of note, if we wish to apply these ideas to
k
-local Hamiltonian systems with
k\geqslant 3
, we likely need to understand
hypergraph
generalizations of the octopus inequality. Recent works by Alon, Kozma, and Puder
[
4
,
6
]
have started laying the groundwork for understanding the octopus inequality in these hypergraph settings.
