Improved high-dimensional estimation with Langevin dynamics and stochastic weight averaging
Stanley Wei, Alex Damian, Jason D. Lee

Abstract
Significant recent work has studied the ability of gradient descent to recover a hidden planted direction θ ⋆ ∈ S d − 1 \theta^{\star}\in S^{d-1} in different high-dimensional settings, including tensor PCA and single-index models. The key quantity that governs the ability of gradient descent to traverse these landscapes is the information exponent k ⋆ k^{\star} [ 7 ] , which corresponds to the order of the saddle at initialization in the population landscape. 7 showed that n ≳ d max ⁡ ( 1 , k ⋆ − 1 ) n\gtrsim d^{\max(1,k^{\star}-1)} samples were necessary and sufficient for online SGD to recover θ ⋆ \theta^{\star} , and 6 proved a similar lower bound for Langevin dynamics. More recently, 14 showed it was possible to circumvent these lower bounds by running gradient descent on a smoothed landscape, and that this algorithm succeeds with n ≳ d max ⁡ ( 1 , k ⋆ / 2 ) n\gtrsim d^{\max(1,k^{\star}/2)} samples, which is optimal in the worst case. This raises the question of whether it is possible to achieve the same rate without explicit smoothing. In this paper, we show that Langevin dynamics can succeed with n ≳ d k ⋆ / 2 n\gtrsim d^{k^{\star}/2} samples if one considers the average iterate , rather than the last iterate. The key idea is that the combination of noise-injection and iterate averaging is able to emulate the effect of landscape smoothing. We apply this result to both the tensor PCA and single-index model settings. Finally, we conjecture that minibatch SGD can also achieve the same rate without adding any additional noise.

2.2.2
Single-Index Models

We mostly follow the setting of
14
. Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\}_{i\in[n]}
be the set of training data. The input data
x_{i}
are drawn i.i.d. from a standard
d

\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

y_{i}
are generated through a target or teacher function
f^{\star}
. In particular, we consider the setting where
f^{\star}
is a single index model, in which the label only depends on the input through a planted direction
\theta^{\star}\in S^{d-1}
. Formally, we have for each
i
:

\displaystyle y_{i}=f^{\star}(x_{i})+\xi_{i}=\sigma(\theta^{\star}\cdot x_{i})+\xi_{i},\quad x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\xi_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

where
\sigma
is a known link function. We will consider the setting where our learner is
f(\theta,x):=\sigma(\theta\cdot x)
, where
\theta\in S^{d-1}
is the learnable parameter.

Assumption 1
.

We will assume the following regarding the link function
\sigma
.

•

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma(x)^{2}]=1
(Normalization)

•

|\sigma^{(k)}(z)|\leq C
for
k=0,1,2
and for all
z
. (Lipschitzness)

We note the assumption on the boundedness of
\sigma^{(k)}
can be relaxed to it having polynomial tails
14
, but at the cost of increasing the complexity of the proof.

We consider training via the correlation loss; the loss on a specific sample
(x,y)
is:

\displaystyle L(\theta;x,y)=1-f(\theta,x)y

The empirical loss on our training set is therefore:

\displaystyle L_{n}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}L(\theta;x_{i},y_{i})

We also denote the population loss over
(x,y)
from the data distribution to be
L(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[L(\theta;x,y)]
.

In this setting,
7
showed that the sample complexity for learning depends on a quantity called the information exponent
k^{\star}
of the link function
\sigma
. To motivate this definition, consider first the probabilist’s Hermite polynomials.

Definition 1

(Probabilist’s Hermite polynomials)
.

For
k\geq 0
, the
k
th normalized probabilist Hermite polynomial
h_{k}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is:

\displaystyle h_{k}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}\gamma(x)^{-1}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\gamma(x)

where
\gamma(x):=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
is the probability density function of a standard univariate Gaussian.

Of importance is that the Hermite polynomials form an orthogonal basis in
L^{2}(\gamma)
(i.e. the space of square-integrable functions with respect to the standard Gaussian measure). Henceforth, for link function
\sigma\in L^{2}(\gamma)
, let
\{c_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
denote the Hermite coefficients of
\sigma
:

Definition 2

(Hermite coefficients)
.

Let the Hermite coefficients of
\sigma\in L^{2}(\gamma)
be
\{c_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. In other words,

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}h_{k}(x),\quad c_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma(z)h_{k}(z)]

This leads us to the key quantity, the information exponent.

Definition 3

(Information exponent)
.

We define the information exponent to be:

\displaystyle k^{\star}=\min\{k\geq 1:c_{k}\neq 0\}

In other words, this is the first Hermite coefficient with positive index that is nonzero. Some examples of information exponents are below:

Example 1
.

(Link functions and their information exponents)

•

\sigma(t)=t
and
\sigma(t)=\mathrm{ReLU}(t)
have information exponent 1.

•

\sigma(t)=|t|
and
\sigma(t)=t^{2}
have information exponent 2.

•

\sigma(t)=t^{2}e^{-t^{2}}
has information exponent 4.

•

\sigma(t)=h_{k}(t)
has information exponent
k.

7
showed that
n\gtrsim d^{\max(1,k^{\star}-1)}
samples were necessary and sufficient for online SGD to recover
\theta^{\star}
, mirroring the tensor PCA setting.
14
showed that this rate could be improved to
n\gtrsim d^{\max(1,k^{\star}/2)}
by running online SGD on a smoothed landscape. A number of papers have managed to circumvent the information exponent by applying a label transformation before running SGD
28
,
26
,
10
,
17
,
32
,
15
,
25
. These results apply a transformation
\mathcal{T}
to the labels
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to derive samples from the single index model defined by
\mathcal{T}\circ\sigma
. This link function can have smaller information exponent than
\sigma
, and the smallest exponent such a transformation can achieve is called the “generative exponent”

. For the purposes of this paper, we can assume that such a label transformation has already been applied so that the information exponent and the generative exponent coincide.

2.3
The Learning Algorithm

Definition 4

(Spherical gradient operator)
.

For
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, define the spherical gradient operator to be
\nabla_{\theta}g(\theta)=P_{z}^{\perp}\nabla g(z)|_{z=\theta}
, where
P_{\theta}^{\perp}:=I-\frac{\theta\theta^{\top}}{\|\theta\|^{2}}
is the orthogonal projection operator with respect to
\theta
and
\nabla
is the standard Euclidean gradient.

We now formally define our learning algorithm; here,
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Wiener process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Algorithm 1

Input:
Inverse temperature parameter
\epsilon
, number of time steps
T
, data points
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

Initialize
\theta_{0}\sim\mu
(e.g. uniform over
S^{d-1}
)

Run the following SDE up to time
T
:

\displaystyle d\theta=\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}\theta+ \epsilon b(\theta) )dt+P_{\theta}^{\perp}dW_{t},\quad b(\theta):=-\nabla_{\theta}L_{n}(\theta)

(1)

\hat{\theta}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{t}dt

\hat{M}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{t}\theta_{t}^{\top}dt

If
k^{\star}
is odd
, return
\hat{\theta}/\|\hat{\theta}\|

Otherwise if
k^{\star}
is even
, return the top eigenvector

of

It can be shown that when

follows the SDE in

Main Contributions

We now highlight our main contributions in this work.

•

We show that by combining Langevin dynamics with weight averaging, we can recover

in both the tensor PCA and single-index model settings with

One other feature of our algorithm is that it does not see the data in an online manner, unlike previous works

Main Results

\displaystyle d\beta=\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}\beta)dt+P_{\beta}^{\perp}dW_{t}

By ergodicity of Brownian motion, we can prove that the first term concentrates to zero. For the second term

with

Theorem 3

Let

1

succeeds in estimating

Overview of Proof Ideas

4.1
Ergodic Concentration

In showing a general ergodic concentration result, we first give some preliminaries on Markov processes on compact Riemannian manifolds.

Definition 5

(Markov semigroup)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a time-homogeneous Markov process. Then, its associated Markov semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the family of operators acting on bounded measurable functions
f
through:

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x):=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t})|X_{0}=x]

At this point, it is useful to define the infinitesimal generator of a Markov process.

Definition 6

(Infinitesimal generator)
.

Let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the associated Markov semigroup for a Markov process. Then, the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
associated with this semigroup is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f:=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{P_{t}f-f}{t}

for all functions
f
for which this limit exists.

Having these definitions introduced, consider the Brownian motion on
S^{d-1}
that we defined earlier:

\displaystyle d\beta=\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}\beta)dt+P_{\beta}^{\perp}dW_{t}

Note that by rotational invariance, the stationary distribution is
\mu
. Moreover, by classic results
[
31
]
, we know that the infinitesimal generator of this process is
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{S^{d-1}}
, where
\Delta_{S^{d-1}}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
S^{d-1}
. We now give a general lemma for ergodic averages of functions of a Brownian motion over the sphere.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
, where
\mu
is the stationary uniform measure over the sphere for the Brownian motion, and
\int_{S^{d-1}}fd\mu=0
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(\beta_{t})dt=\frac{\phi(\beta_{0})-\phi(\beta_{T})}{T}+\frac{M_{T}}{T}

where

\displaystyle\phi(\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}f(\beta)dt

and
M_{T}:=\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\phi(\beta_{t})^{\top}P_{\beta_{t}}^{\perp}dW_{t}
is a martingale.

The proof is deferred to the appendix, and it now remains to bound these terms, which depends on our choice of
f
. Recall that we need to make this ergodicity argument for
\beta_{t}
and
b(\beta_{t})
(defined in
Section

4.2
), as well as
\beta_{t}b(\beta_{t})^{\top}
for the even case. For the sake of exposition, we look at this function coordinate-wise in the main text; our full proofs in the appendix directly handle the tensorized version.

The bounds on these quantities are given by the following lemma, with full proof in the appendix.

Lemma 2
.

In the setting of
Lemma

1
the following holds:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\phi(\beta_{0})-\phi(\beta_{T})}{T}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sup\|\nabla f\|}{(d-2)T}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\quantity[\qty(\frac{M_T}{T})^2]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sup\|\nabla f\|^{2}}{(d-2)^{2}T}

The
d-2
term comes from the Ricci curvature of
S^{d-1}
being
\rho=d-2
, which leads to a bound on the gradient decay in the sense that
\|\nabla P_{t}f\|\leq e^{-\rho t}\|\nabla f\|

[
5
]
. A detailed discussion of this is included in the appendix. We now sketch the remainder of the ergodicity arguments in the main result. The previous lemmas tell us that the concentration happens at time
T
that depends on the function
f
.

4.2
Analyzing the Error Component
E

Recall in the previous section that the time average consists of a Brownian component that is averaged out to zero, and an error component
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}E_{t}dt
. First, let us recall our definition
b(\theta):=-\nabla_{\theta}L_{n}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}P_{\theta}^{\perp}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta\cdot x_{i})x_{i}
. By decomposing the time average of
E_{t}
even further, it turns out we can write the above as roughly:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}E_{t}dt\approx\frac{\epsilon}{d}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}b(\theta_{t})dt

From here, we derive the following:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}b(\theta_{t})dt=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}b(\beta_{t})dt+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}(b(\theta_{t})-b(\beta_{t}))dt

The first term concentrates to
\bar{b}:=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[b(z)]
using the ergodicity arguments from the previous section, and the second term can be controlled via upper bound on
\|E_{t}\|=\|\theta_{t}-\beta_{t}\|
due to Lipschitzness. Indeed, in the regime of
\epsilon
that we work in, we can further argue that with high probability,
\|\theta-\beta\|
remains order
O(\epsilon)
over all time, which we outline below. Recall the SDE’s for the coupled processes
\theta,\beta
:

\displaystyle d\theta

\displaystyle=\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}\theta+ \epsilon b(\theta))dt+P_{\theta}^{\perp}dW_{t}

\displaystyle d\beta

\displaystyle=-\frac{d-1}{2}\beta dt+P_{\beta}^{\perp}dW_{t}

This tells us that:

\displaystyle dE=\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}E + \epsilon b(\theta))dt+\quantity(P_\theta^\perp- P_\beta^\perp)dW_{t}

The key observation here is that the noise matrix
\Sigma^{1/2}:=P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp}
satisfies the property that
\tr\Sigma\leq 2\|E\|^{2}
. Intuitively, this means that the size of the noise scales with the norm of

, and this allows us to get a high probability uniform bound on
\|E\|
over all time. The following lemma makes this rigorous.

Lemma 3

(High probability uniform bound of
\sup\|E\|
)
.

With probability at least
1-dTe^{-d}
, there exists an absolute constant
C^{\prime}
such that:

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\leq T}\|E(t)\|\leq C^{\prime}\quantity[\frac{\epsilon\sup\|b\| }{d}]

The key idea of this uniform bound lies in a bijection between this Ornstein–Uhlenbeck-like process and a suitable subgaussian process. From there, we can apply the chaining method to obtain a uniform bound of
\sup\|E\|
over time. Indeed, the fact that

throughout training is key to both the proofs of odd and even

, since it heuristically reduces our process to a Brownian component plus an
\epsilon
signal component that can leverage the randomness in the Brownian component.

5.2
Extension to Mini-batch SGD

Our experimental results suggest that pure mini-batch SGD should have theoretically guarantees too. Consider mini-batch SGD with learning rate
\eta
and batch size 1:

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\frac{\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}}{\|\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}\|},\quad g_{t}:=\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{t};x_{i_{t}},y_{i_{t}}),\quad i_{t}\sim\mathcal{U}([n])

g_{t}
is approximately a standard Gaussian, since
\nabla L(\theta;x,y)=-y\sigma^{\prime}(\theta\cdot x)x
and
\theta\cdot x
is
O(1)
for the most part, and hence
\|g_{t}\|\approx O(\sqrt{d})
. For
\eta\ll d^{-1/2}
, we have the following approximation:

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\frac{\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}}{\|\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}\|}=\frac{\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}}{\sqrt{1+\eta^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}}}\approx(\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t})(1-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}(d-1))

Let
z_{t}:=g_{t}+b(\theta_{t})
be the mini-batch noise
2
2

2

By choosing batch size
B=1
, we maximize the scale of the noise without explicit noise boosting.
. Because we are in a noise-dominated regime,
z_{t}
is approximately isotropic so if we approximate this process by an SDE, we would heuristically get:

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle\approx\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}(d-1)\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\sqrt{\eta}\cdot\sqrt{\eta}z_{t}-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{2}\eta(d-1)\theta+\eta b(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle\implies d\theta

\displaystyle\approx\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}\eta\theta+ b(\theta) )dt+\sqrt{\eta}P_{\theta}^{\perp}dW_{t}

\displaystyle\implies d\theta

\displaystyle\approx\quantity(-\frac{d-1}{2}\theta+ \frac{1}{\eta}b(\theta))dt+P_{\theta}^{\perp}dW_{t}

which roughly recovers our Langevin setting with
\epsilon:=\frac{1}{\eta}
.
We therefore conjecture that there exists a learning rate regime for which this SGD argument holds even without the noise boosting that is present in Langevin dynamics.
The main technical challenge in extending our results in this direction is not just controlling the discretization error, but also the dependencies that arise between the noise covariance and the smoothing estimator. In particular, the stationary distribution for the pure-noise process will no longer be isotropic over the sphere and will have a data-dependent stationary distribution, which introduces additional complications. However, extending our results and techniques to the minibatch SGD setting is a promising direction for future work.

Lemma 6

1

\displaystyle d(E\beta^{\top})=\quantity(-(d-1) E\beta^\top+ \epsilon b(\theta) \beta^\top+ (P_\theta^\perp- P_\beta^\perp)P_\beta^\perp)dt+(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})dW_{t}\beta^{\top}+EdW_{t}^{\top}P_{\beta}^{\perp}

The SDE of
\beta E^{\top}
is just the transpose of the above, so we have:

\displaystyle d(E\beta^{\top})=\quantity(-(d-1) \beta E^\top+ \epsilon\beta b(\theta) ^\top+ P_\beta^\perp(P_\theta^\perp- P_\beta^\perp))dt+\beta dW_{t}^{\top}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})+P_{\beta}^{\perp}dW_{t}E^{\top}

Let
G:=E\beta^{\top}+\beta E^{\top}
. Then the SDE for
G
is:

\displaystyle d(G)

\displaystyle=\quantity(-(d-1)G + \epsilon(b(\theta) \beta^\top+ \beta b(\theta)^\top) + \qty[(P_\theta^\perp- P_\beta^\perp)P_\beta^\perp+ P_\beta^\perp(P_\theta^\perp- P_\beta^\perp)])dt

\displaystyle+(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})dW_{t}\beta^{\top}+EdW_{t}^{\top}P_{\beta}^{\perp}+\beta dW_{t}^{\top}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})+P_{\beta}^{\perp}dW_{t}E^{\top}

where the first line is the drift term, and the second line is the noise term. Moreover, we can further simplify the final term in the drift:

\displaystyle(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})P_{\beta}^{\perp}+P_{\beta}^{\perp}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})

\displaystyle=(-\beta E^{\top}-EE^{\top}+(E^{\top}\beta)(\beta\beta^{\top}+E\beta^{\top}))+(-E\beta^{\top}-EE^{\top}+(E^{\top}\beta)(\beta\beta^{\top}+\beta E^{\top}))

\displaystyle=-(\beta E^{\top}+E\beta^{\top})+\Xi

where
\Xi
is the remainder term satisfying
\|\Xi\|_{F}\lesssim\|E\|^{2}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}/d^{2}
. The last line follows from
Lemma

14
for simplification. Our SDE for
G
can therefore be rewritten as:

\displaystyle dG

\displaystyle=\quantity(-d G + \epsilon(b(\theta) \beta^\top+ \beta b(\theta)^\top) + \Xi)dt

\displaystyle+(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})dW_{t}\beta^{\top}+EdW_{t}^{\top}P_{\beta}^{\perp}+\beta dW_{t}^{\top}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})+P_{\beta}^{\perp}dW_{t}E^{\top}

This implies that:

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity(\epsilon(b(\theta_s)\beta_s^\top+ \beta_s b(\theta_s)^\top) + \Xi_s)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity[(P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) dW_t \beta^\top+ E dW_t^\top P_\beta^\perp+ \beta dW_t^\top(P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) + P_\beta^\perp dW_t E^\top]

We first analyze the time average of the second term, which is the noise term. Intuitively, the time average of it should concentrate around 0 as time increases.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity[(P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) dW_s \beta_s^\top+ E dW_s^\top P_\beta^\perp+ \beta dW_s^\top(P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) + P_\beta^\perp dW_s E^\top]dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})\int_{0}^{T-s}e^{-dt}dtdW_{s}\beta_{s}^{\top}+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}E\int_{0}^{T-s}e^{-dt}dtdW_{s}^{\top}P_{\beta}^{\perp}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\beta\int_{0}^{T-s}e^{-dt}dtdW_{s}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}P_{\beta}^{\perp}\int_{0}^{T-s}e^{-dt}dtdW_{s}E^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})\quantity(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}))dW_{s}\beta_{s}^{\top}+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}E\quantity(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}))dW_{s}^{\top}P_{\beta}^{\perp}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\beta\quantity(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}))dW_{s}(P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp})+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}P_{\beta}^{\perp}\quantity(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}))dW_{s}E^{\top}

It now suffices to bound the Frobenius norm of the time average of the top two terms of the last expression (since the latter two terms are just transposes). We again observe that
\|P_{\theta}^{\perp}-P_{\beta}^{\perp}\|_{F}\lesssim\sup\|E\|\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{d}
. For the first term, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\quantity[\left\| \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T (P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) \qty(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)})) dW_s \beta_s^\top\right\|_F^2]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\quantity[\qty(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}))^2 \|P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp\|_F^2]ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{d^{2}T}\sup\limits_{t\leq T}\|E_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{4}T}

where the second to last inequality follows from
Lemma

15
.

For the second term in the time average of the noise component, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\quantity[\left\| \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T E \qty(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)})) dW_s^\top P_\beta^\perp\right\|_F^2]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\quantity[\qty(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}))^2 \|E\|_F^2]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{4}T}

Combining all four noise terms together using
Lemma

21
and triangle inequality, we have that with probability
1-e^{-d}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity[(P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) dW_s \beta_s^\top+ E dW_s^\top P_\beta^\perp+ \beta dW_s^\top(P_\theta^\perp-P_\beta^\perp) + P_\beta^\perp dW_s E^\top]dt\right\|_{F}\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{Td^{3}}}

We now analyze the drift term of
G.
First, to isolate the Brownian motion, we once again do another decomposition:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity(\epsilon(b(\theta_s)\beta_s^\top+ \beta_s b(\theta_s)^\top) + \Xi_s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity(\epsilon((b(\beta_s) + v) \beta_s^\top+ \beta_s (b(\beta_s) + v)^\top) + \Xi_s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\epsilon(b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})^{\top})ds+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity(\epsilon(v \beta_s^\top+ \beta_s v^\top) + \Xi_s)ds

where here we define
v:=b(\theta)-b(\beta)
, which by Lipschitzness has norm bounded by
O(\|E\|)\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{d}
. Hence, for all
t\leq T
, this second term satisfies:

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\quantity(\epsilon(v \beta_s^\top+ \beta_s v^\top) + \Xi_s)ds\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sup\limits_{s\leq t}\|\epsilon(v\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}v^{\top})+\Xi_{s}\|_{F}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}/d^{2}

which means the time average over this component also has Frobenius norm
O(\epsilon^{2}/d^{2})
. For the time average of the first term, we have the following:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-d(t-s)}\epsilon(b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})^{\top})ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\quantity(\frac{1}{d}(1 - e^{-d(T-s)}) )\epsilon(b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})^{\top})ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{d}\epsilon(b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})^{\top})ds-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{d}e^{-d(T-s)}\epsilon(b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})^{\top})ds

For the second term, we can bound this in Frobenius norm by:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{d}e^{-d(T-s)}\epsilon(b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}+\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})^{\top})ds\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{Td}\sup\|b(\beta)\beta^{\top}+\beta b(\beta)^{\top}\|_{F}\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{Td}

Finally, for the first term, we have shown concentration to
\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim S^{d-1}}[b(z)z^{\top}+zb(z)^{\top}]
in the previous lemma. Combining everything through triangle inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}G(s)ds-\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}\quantity[z b(z)^\top+ b(z) z^\top]\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{d}\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\beta_{s}b(\beta_{s})+b(\beta_{s})\beta_{s}^{\top}ds-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}\quantity[z b(z)^\top+ b(z) z^\top]\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon}{Td}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{Td^{3}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{d\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon}{Td}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{Td^{3}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{d\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}

and the result follows.
∎

Theorem 5

(
Theorem

3
, restated)
.

Let
\epsilon=o(d^{-(k^{\star}-2)/2})
, and let
T\gtrsim d^{k^{\star}+2}/\epsilon^{2}
. Then, for
\Delta>0
, if
n\gtrsim d^{k^{\star}/2}/\Delta^{2}
, the algorithm succeeds in recovering
\theta^{\star}
up to error
\Delta
with probability at least
1-e^{-d^{c}}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\theta\theta^{\top}=\beta\beta^{\top}+E\beta^{\top}+\beta E^{\top}+EE^{\top}
. In the previous lemmas, we have analyzed each of these terms separately, and our goal is to prove ergodic concentration to
\frac{1}{d}I+\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim S^{d-1}}[zb(z)^{\top}+b(z)z^{\top}]
.

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{s}\theta_{s}^{\top}ds-\quantity(\frac{1}{d}I + \frac{\epsilon}{d}\E_{z\sim S^{d-1}} [z b(z)^\top+ b(z) z^\top])\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\beta_{s}\beta_{s}^{\top}ds-\frac{I}{d}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}(E\beta^{\top}+\beta E^{\top})ds-\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim S^{d-1}}[zb(z)^{\top}+b(z)z^{\top}])\right\|_{F}+\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}EE^{\top}ds\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon}{d\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon}{d\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}

Consider the stationary average of
M_{n}:=\frac{1}{d}I+\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim S^{d-1}}[zb(z)^{\top}+b(z)z^{\top}]
. By
Lemma

30
, with probability
1-e^{-d}
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim S^{d-1}}[zb(z)^{\top}+b(z)z^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim S^{d-1},x}[zb(z)^{\top}+b(z)z^{\top}]\right\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{d^{-k^{\star}/2}/n}

Therefore, we obtain via triangle inequality that:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{s}\theta_{s}^{\top}ds-\mathbb{E}_{x}[M_{n}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{s}\theta_{s}^{\top}ds-M_{n}\right\|_{2}+\left\|M_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{x}[M_{n}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon}{d\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{d}\sqrt{d^{-k^{\star}/2}/n}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{d}\sqrt{d^{-k^{\star}/2}/n}

where the last inequality follows from our regime of
\epsilon
and
T
. We now note that the eigengap for
\mathbb{E}_{x}[M_{n}]
is
\frac{\epsilon}{d}\Theta(d^{-k^{\star}/2})
. Then, when
n=\Theta(d^{k^{\star}/2}/\Delta^{2})
, when applying Davis-Kahan, we see that the top eigenvector can be recovered up to accuracy:

\displaystyle\sin(u_1, \theta^\star)\lesssim\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{d}\sqrt{d^{-k^{\star}/2}/n}}{\frac{\epsilon}{d}\Theta(d^{-k^{\star}/2})}\lesssim\Delta

where
u_{1}
denotes the top eigenvector of our time averaged matrix.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}
, and let
E=\beta-\beta^{\prime}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle E^{\top}\beta^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2}\|E\|^{2}

Proof.

\displaystyle\|\beta^{\prime}+E\|^{2}=\|\beta\|^{2}\implies 2E^{\top}\beta^{\prime}+\|E\|^{2}=0

since
\|\beta\|=\|\beta^{\prime}\|=1
. Rearranging gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\tr\quantity((P_\beta^\perp- P_{\beta^\prime}^\perp)(P_\beta^\perp- P_{\beta^\prime}^\perp)^\top)=2\|E\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|E\|^{4}

where
E=\beta-\beta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\tr\quantity((P_\beta^\perp- P_{\beta^\prime}^\perp)(P_\beta^\perp- P_{\beta^\prime}^\perp)^\top)=\tr\quantity(P_\beta^\perp(\beta^\prime\beta^{\prime\top}) + P_{\beta^\prime}(\beta\beta^\top))

Note that

\displaystyle P_{\beta^{\prime}}^{\perp}(\beta\beta^{\top})

\displaystyle=P_{\beta^{\prime}}^{\perp}(\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\top}+\beta^{\prime}E^{\top}+E\beta^{\prime\top}+EE^{\top})

\displaystyle=P_{\beta^{\prime}}^{\perp}(E\beta^{\prime\top}+EE^{\top})

\displaystyle=E\beta^{\prime\top}+EE^{\top}-\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\top}E\beta^{\prime\top}-\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\top}EE^{\top}

and similarly

\displaystyle P_{\beta}^{\perp}(\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\top})=-E\beta^{\top}+EE^{\top}+\beta\beta^{\top}E\beta^{\top}-\beta\beta^{\top}EE^{\top}

Summing these, we get the trace to be

\displaystyle 2\|E\|^{2}-1/2\|E\|^{4}

∎

Lemma 18
.

Let

where

is the
k
-th normalized Hermite polynomial and let

be the index of the first nonzero even coefficient. Then,

[

Lemma 22
.

Let
X:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy
X(0)=0
and

\displaystyle dX=-AXdt+\sigma(X)dW_{t}.

If
\sigma(X)\leq\sigma
for all
X
, then for all
0\leq s\leq t
,
it holds that
X(t)-X(s)
is
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{C}\quantity(1 - e^{-2C(t-s)})
-subgaussian.

Proof.

Let
Y(t):=e^{At}X_{t}
. Then,

\displaystyle dY(t)=e^{At}\sigma(X(t))dW_{t}

Thus,
Y(t)
is a martingale. Furthermore, the quadratic variation of
Y
satisfies

\displaystyle\langle Y\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{2At}\sigma(X(t))^{2}dt\leq\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2At}dt=\sigma^{2}\cdot\frac{e^{2At}-1}{2A}<\infty

Therefore, Novikov’s condition tells us that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\lambda Y)_{t}:=\exp\quantity(\lambda Y(t) - \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \langle Y \rangle_t)

is a martingale. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\lambda Y)_{s}=\mathbb{E}\quantity[\mathcal{E}(\lambda Y)_t | \mathcal{F}_s]=\mathbb{E}\quantity[\exp(\lambda Y(t) - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\langle Y \rangle_t) | \mathcal{F}_s]

Rearranging the above inequality gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda Y(t))|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\quantity[\exp\qty(\lambda Y(s) + \frac{\lambda^2 \sigma^2}{2} \frac{e^{2At} - e^{2As}}{2A}) \vert\mathcal{F}_s]

Now, converting back to
X
and replacing
\lambda\leftarrow\lambda e^{-At}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda(X(t) - X(s)))|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\quantity[\exp\qty( \lambda X(s) (e^{-A(t-s)} - 1) + \frac{\lambda^2 \sigma^2}{2} \frac{1-e^{-2A(t-s)}}{2A}) \vert\mathcal{F}_s]

Applying this for
(s,0)
instead of
(t,s)
gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda X(s))]\leq\exp\quantity(\frac{\lambda^2 \sigma^2}{2} \frac{1-e^{-2As}}{2A})\leq\exp\quantity(\frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{4A})

Plugging this in the previous equation upon taking expectation over
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda(X(t) - X(s)))]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\quantity(\frac{\lambda^2 \sigma^2 (e^{-A(t-s)} - 1)^2}{4A} + \frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2(1-e^{-2A(t-s)})}{4A})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\quantity(\frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2A} (1-e^{-2A(t-s)}))

where we substituted and used the fact that

\displaystyle(e^{-A(t-s)}-1)^{2}\leq 1-e^{-2A(t-s)}

∎

Lemma 23

(Chaining tail inequality
[
33
]
)
.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in T}
be a separable subgaussian process on the metric space
(T,d)
. Then for all
t_{0}\in T
and

,

\displaystyle\Pr\quantity[\sup_{t\in T}\{X_t-X_{t_0}\} \geq C\int_0^\infty\sqrt{\log N(T, d, \epsilon)} d\epsilon+ x]\leq Ce^{-\frac{x^{2}}{C\mathrm{diam}(T)^{2}}}

where
C<\infty
is a universal constant, and
N(T,d,\epsilon)
denotes the covering number of an
\epsilon
-net for
(T,d)
.

Corollary 3
.

In the setting of
Lemma

22
, there exists an absolute constant
C<\infty
such that for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\quantity[\sup\limits_{t\leq T} |X_t| \geq C \times\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{A}} \sqrt{\log\frac{1+AT}{\delta}}]\leq\delta

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle d(s,t):=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{A}(1-e^{-2A(t-s)})}

Then,
X_{t}-X_{s}
is
d(s,t)
-subgaussian from the
Lemma

22
. When we invert this distance, we obtain

\displaystyle N([0,T],d,\epsilon)\lesssim\frac{2AT}{-\log(1-\frac{A\epsilon^2}{\sigma^2})}

Note that for
\epsilon<\sigma/\sqrt{A}

and the diameter is upper bounded by

. Applying the chaining tail inequality in

, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr\quantity[\sup\limits_{t\leq T}\|X_t\| \geq C \times\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{A} } \sqrt{\log(1+ AT)} + x]\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}A}{C^{\prime}\sigma^{2}}}

where we used the fact that:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N([0,T],d,\epsilon)}d\epsilon\lesssim\frac{R}{\sqrt{A}}\sqrt{\log(1+AT)}

Rearranging gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Let
X(0)=0
and suppose
X
satisfies the following SDE.

\displaystyle dX=[-AX+b(X)]dt+\Sigma^{1/2}(X)dW_{t}

and that uniformly for all
X
,

\displaystyle\|b(X)\|\leq G,\quad\tr\Sigma(X)\leq B\|X\|^{2}

Then, there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any
\delta,T>0
, if
L:=1\vee\log\frac{1+AT}{\delta}
and
A\geq CBL
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\leq T}\|X(t)\|\leq\frac{CG}{A}.

Proof.

We begin by decomposing
X(t)=X_{1}(t)+X_{2}(t)
where
X_{1},X_{2}
follow:

\displaystyle dX_{1}=[-AX_{1}+b(X)]dt,\quad dX_{2}=-AX_{2}dt+\Sigma^{1/2}(X)dW_{t}

and
X_{1}(0)=X_{2}(0)=0
. Define
R:=\frac{G}{A}
. Observe that for all
t
,

\displaystyle X_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A(t-s)}b(X(s))ds\implies\|X_{1}(t)\|\leq G\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A(t-s)}ds\leq\frac{G}{A}=R.

For
X_{2}
, note that:

\displaystyle d\|X_{2}\|^{2}=[-2A\|X_{2}\|^{2}+\tr\Sigma(X)]dt+X_{2}^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}(X)dW_{t}

We now decompose
\|X_{2}\|^{2}=Y_{1}+Y_{2}
so that:

\displaystyle dY_{1}=[-2AY_{1}+\tr\Sigma(X)]dt,\quad dY_{2}=-2AY_{2}dt+X_{2}^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}(X)dW_{t}.

Define the stopping time
\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0~:~\norm{X_2(t)}\geq R\}
. Then

\displaystyle\tr\Sigma(X(t\wedge\tau))\leq B\norm{X(t \wedge\tau)}^{2}\leq 2B\quantity[\frac{G^2}{A^2} + R^2]=4BR^{2}.

Therefore
Y_{1}(t\wedge\tau)\leq 2BR^{2}/A
. Next, the noise term in the SDE for
Y_{2}
can be bounded by:

\displaystyle X_{2}(t\wedge\tau)^{T}\Sigma(X(t\wedge\tau))X_{2}(t\wedge\tau)\leq\norm{X_2(t \wedge\tau)}^{2}\tr\Sigma(X(t\wedge\tau))\leq 4BR^{4}.

Now, let
C
be a sufficiently large constant. Substituting into
Corollary

3
, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}\norm{Y_2(t \wedge\tau)}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{BR^{4}}{A}\log(\frac{2(1 + A T)}{\delta})}.

Under this event, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}\norm{X_2(t \wedge\tau)}^{2}\leq CR^{2}\quantity[\frac{B}{A} + \sqrt{\frac{B}{A} \log(\frac{2(1+AT)}{\delta})}].

Now since

is a sufficiently large constant then the right hand side is strictly less than

, which implies that with probability at least
1-\delta

and

.
∎

We now give the following standard definition of the Orlicz norm, which will be used extensively for our concentration results.

Definition 8
.

For
\alpha>0
, define the function
\psi_{\alpha}(t)=\exp(t^\alpha)-1
. Then, for a random variable
X
, we define the
\psi_{\alpha}
Orlicz norm of
X
to be:

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}=\inf\{\lambda>0:\mathbb{E}\psi_{\alpha}(|X|/\lambda)\leq 1\}

In particular, a mean zero random variable
X
is
\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}
-subexponential, and is
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}
-subgaussian.

Lemma 25

(Adapted from Theorem 4,

2

)
.

Suppose

.

Suppose

Next, we will control concentrate the norm of the deviation from this population expectation.

Lemma 28
.

With probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{z}b(z)-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}b(z)\|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k-1)/2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{n}}

\theta^{\star}

\displaystyle\left|\theta^{\star}\cdot\quantity(\E_z b(z) - \E_{z,Z} b(z))\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k-1)/2}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}

Proof.

We first note that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}b(z)-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}b(z)=kn^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{z}\quantity[P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}]]

which can be seen to be a linear functional of the Gaussian tensor
Z
, as well as rotationally invariant by symmetry. Hence, we obtain that
\|\mathbb{E}_{z}b(z)-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}b(z)\|^{2}\overset{d}{=}\tau^{2}\chi_{d}^{2}
, where
\tau^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\mathbb{E}_{z}b(z)-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}b(z)\|^{2}
. This can be calculated as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{z}b(z)-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}b(z)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left\|kn^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{z}\quantity[P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}]]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime},Z}\expectationvalue{P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}], P_{z'}^\perp Z[(z')^{\otimes k-1}]}

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[(z \cdot z')^{k-1} \ev{P_z^\perp, P_{z'}^\perp}]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[(z \cdot z')^{k-1} (d - 2 + (z\cdot z')^2)]

\displaystyle\asymp n^{-1}d^{-(k-3)/2}

Therefore,
\tau^{2}\asymp d^{-(k-1)/2}/n
. Finally, by
\chi_{d}^{2}
concentration, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
, the magnitude of a
\chi_{d}^{2}
random variable is bounded by
O(d+\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}+\log(1/\delta))=O(d+\log(1/\delta))
by the AM-GM inequality, and the result follows.

For the second equation, we note that
\theta^{\star}\cdot\quantity(\E_z b(z) - \E_{z,Z} b(z))
is also a linear functional of the Gaussian tensor
Z
, so we simply have to consider its variance for concentration. Using the previous calculation, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{Z}\quantity[\theta^\star\cdot\qty(\E_z b(z) - \E_{z,Z} b(z))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left|kn^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{z}\quantity[P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}]]\cdot\theta^{\star}\right|^{2}\asymp d^{-1}n^{-1}d^{-(k-3)/2}

where the
d^{-1}
factor follows from the covariance being isotropic. This gives that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\theta^{\star}\cdot\quantity(\E_z b(z) - \E_{z,Z} b(z))\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k-1)/2}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}

∎

Lemma 30
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\left\|G-\mathbb{E}_{Z}G\right\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k+2)/2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{n}}

Proof.

Note that
G-\mathbb{E}_{Z}G
is self-adjoint. Therefore, it holds that:

\displaystyle\|G-\mathbb{E}_{Z}G\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{1/4}}|v^{\top}(G-\mathbb{E}_{Z}G)v|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{1/4}}|v^{\top}(\mathbb{E}_{z}[zb(z)^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}[zb(z)^{\top}])v|+2\sup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{1/4}}|v^{\top}(\mathbb{E}_{z}[b(z)z^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{z,Z}[b(z)z^{\top}])v|

where
\mathcal{N}_{1/4}
denotes a
1/4
-net of
S^{d-1}

Let us start with the first term. Consider for a fixed

\displaystyle v^{\top}\quantity[\E_z[z b(z)^\top] - \E_{z,Z}[z b(z)^\top]]v=kn^{-1/2}\cdot v^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{z}\quantity[z (P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}])^\top]v

Since this quantity is a linear functional of a Gaussian tensor
Z
, it suffices to analyze just the variance to obtain a concentration.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{Z}\quantity[v^\top\qty[\E_z[z b(z)^\top] - \E_{z,Z}[z b(z)^\top]] v]

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\quantity[\E_z[v^\top\qty[z (P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}])^\top] v]^2]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\quantity[ \E_{z,z'} \qty[(v\cdot z)(v\cdot z') \qty[(P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}])^\top v] \qty[(P_{z'}^\perp Z[z'^{ \otimes k-1}])^\top v] ]]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[ (v\cdot z)(v\cdot z') \E_{Z} \qty[ (P_z^\perp Z[z^{\otimes k-1}])^\top v (P_{z'}^\perp Z[z'^{ \otimes k-1}])^\top v ]]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[ (v\cdot z)(v\cdot z') \cdot v^\top P_z^\perp\E_{Z} \qty[ ( Z[z^{\otimes k-1}]) ( Z[z'^{ \otimes k-1}])^\top] P_{z'}^\perp v]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[ (v\cdot z)(v\cdot z') \cdot v^\top P_z^\perp(z\cdot z')^{k-1} I_d P_{z'}^\perp v]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[(v\cdot z)(v\cdot z')(z\cdot z')^{k-1}]

\displaystyle-n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[(v\cdot z)^3(v\cdot z')(z\cdot z')^{k-1}]]-n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[(v\cdot z)(v\cdot z')^{3}(z\cdot z')^{k-1}]

\displaystyle+n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{z,z^{\prime}}\quantity[(v\cdot z)^2 (v\cdot z')^2 (z\cdot z')^{k}]

In the last expression, the first term is the main term, and the latter three are due to the at least one of the projection terms. For the main term, we can bound it by
\Theta(d^{-(k+2)/2}/n)
. For the latter three, we have that they are
O(d^{-(k+4)/2}/n)
. Hence, the entire variance expression is
\Theta(d^{-(k+2)/2}/n)
.

Therefore, we have that for an arbitrary
v\in S^{d-1}

1-\delta/9^{d}

\displaystyle\left|v^{\top}\quantity[\E_z[z b(z)^\top] - \E_{z,Z}[z b(z)^\top]]v\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k+2)/2}\log(9^d/\delta)}{n}}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k+2)/2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{n}}

We now consider a
1/4
-net
\mathcal{N}_{1/4}
over
S^{d-1}
, which has size at most
9^{d}
. Union bounding over

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{1/4}}\left|v^{\top}\quantity[\E_z[z b(z)^\top] - \E_{z,Z}[z b(z)^\top]]v\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k+2)/2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{n}}

By a similar argument, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{1/4}}\left|v^{\top}\quantity[\E_z[ b(z) z^\top] - \E_{z,Z}[b(z) z^\top]]v\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{-(k+2)/2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{n}}

Adding these yields the desired result.
