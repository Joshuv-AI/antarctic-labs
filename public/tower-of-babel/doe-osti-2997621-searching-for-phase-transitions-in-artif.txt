I. INTRODUCTION
Artificial Kagome spin ice is a metasurface consisting of small islands of polarizable magnets.
These islands can be oriented by an external magnetic field applied in the same plane as the
metasurface. By subsequently applying a relatively weaker field in the opposite direction of the
initial field, a portion of the magnets in the lattice can be repolarized or “flipped” the other way.
These flips cause instabilities in a previously uniformly aligned lattice, causing the system to
interact with itself and induce changes in the overall magnetization of the metasurface. This
Summer, my goal was to use this phenomenon to discern whether or not these systems could
undergo a phase transition caused only by the initial change of the external magnetic field.
Based on previous datasets that indicated that such a transition was observable, my task was to
find and reproduce this potential phenomena. This process was conducted by repeating
measurements on the system by using an external magnet to align the spin ice and then using
Magnetic Force Microscopy (MFM) to probe the surface of the material to understand the
changes in the magnetization over time.
This report will discuss the characteristics of artificial Kagome spin ice, the methodology used
to induce phase transitions, and the image processing methods used to understand the results.
Although no reproducible evidence of a phase transition was found during the Summer, we
were able to successfully reproduce short-term responses in the spin ice, as well as plot a
hysteresis curve of the material.

II. ARTIFICIAL KAGOME SPIN ICE
A. Geometry of Spin Ice metasurface
 A Kagome lattice is a repeating 2-dimensional pattern consisting of hexagons and
triangles. Artificial Kagome spice systems are created by mimicking this pattern on a thin
metasurface with small islands of polarizable material. To clarify some of the terms, a
metasurface is a 2-dimensional surface with periodic elements that are individually smaller than
the wavelength of light used to interact with the surface. Our individual magnets are the
periodic units making up the Kagome lattice, which we call “islands”. This surface can be

visualized simply as a honeycomb pattern, with each side of the hexagon being its own isolated
island. Although these entities create a honeycomb instead of a Kagome lattice, defining the
center of each island yields the exact configuration of a Kagome lattice.
 For the purposes of this experiment, all measurements were performed on spin ice
systems synthesized independently from my work. These lattices were present in nine
variations, each with their own island size. The vast majority of measurements were performed
on just one of these lattices, to ensure that the physical parameters of the spin ice were held
constant. The size of the lattice was on the order of tens of microns, and scans were made on a
range of anywhere between 5 to 15 micron grids.
B. Favored and unfavored junctions
​ Let us briefly discuss the rudiments of the theory motivating these systems. Artificial
Kagome spin ice systems are made to mimic naturally found (non-artificial) spin ice systems.
Spin ice systems are magnetic by nature (hence “spin”) and are characterized by their lack of a
single minimum energy state. This results in many possible configurations for the individual
elements of the spin ice, often influenced by external factors like temperature. Moving to our
case of artificial spin ice, the main distinguishing characteristic of the sample is the uniform
synthesis of the metasurface. Studying this idealized case of a spin ice material allows us to
probe for potential theory with less additional concerns.
​ In our Kagome lattice, the islands meet at junctions in the same way a honeycomb of
hexagons is arranged. These junctions are the meeting point of three islands and they have their
own effective magnetic “charge” due to the orientation of these islands. For simplicity, we
assume that each of these islands are either fully polarized in one direction or the other along the
direction of the respective hexagonal side of the honeycomb picture. If we call the positive end
to have a positive magnetic charge of +1, then the other side has a charge of -1. Looking then at
our junction, we see that there are a handful of different effective charges that can be localized
at these junctions. While most of these configurations are commonly observed in a system that
has been allowed to settle, there are two configurations that cause a point of stress in the system.
These occur when either all islands at a junction present their positive ends, or all present their
negative ends (+3 or -3 charge). Such a configuration is unstable, and often induces one or more
islands to flip to leave this high potential energy state. In this way, a system initialized to have
some of these unstable junctions, even without the subsequent influence of a magnetic field,
could be expected to evolve with time.
​ Combining these internal interactions with the presence of an external field, there are
theories suggesting a sort of avalanching behavior that occurs in artificial spin ice. This suggests
that the flipping of a few islands could cause a cascading sequence of flips that greatly alters the
overall magnetization of the metasurface. The potential of this phenomenon was precisely what
was studied during this Summer term.

C. External Field
 The final component of our system is the externally applied magnetic field. This field
lies in the plane of the metasurface, oriented such that its direction is parallel to two of the sides
of a hexagonal unit of islands. By tuning this field to a high strength (typically 60 mT), all of
the islands become aligned in the direction of the field. By subsequently flipping the direction
of the field, but at a smaller magnitude, a portion of the islands become flipped. This initial state
is not one of the local minimums, and begins to change with time. This was the fundamental
process used to study the artificial Kagome spin ice. Let us now discuss this methodology in
greater detail.

II. METHODOLOGY
A. Apparatus
​ The entirety of the experiment was integrated within a greater Atomic Force Microscopy
(AFM) system. Note that AFM (and similarly Magnetic Force Microscopy respectively) refers
both to the specific type of microscopy as well as the instrument used to conduct microscopy.
The sample was placed onto the moveable stage of the AFM, where it could be directly
measured by the AFM. On this same stage, a tunable magnetic field system was created, such
that the two bores creating the field were placed on opposing sides of the sample such that they
were properly aligned with the lattice. These bores were placed on moveable stages, which
allowed the tuning of the magnetic field by changing the distance between the bores. This
system included a probe near the position of the sample which could measure the strength of the
created field, so that field we could keep track of the strength of the magnetic field.
​ The AFM was then modified for use as a Magnetic Force Microscopy (MFM) system.
To provide some brief background, an AFM works by allowing a small needle attached to a
cantilever (this is the breakdown of the probe) to oscillate over the surface of a material. These
oscillations change as the AFM probe “taps” the surface of the sample, reacting to the
topography of the sample. The changes in oscillation are measured by a laser bouncing off the
reflective back surface of the probe, which then feeds into a sensor and allows a measurement to
occur.
​ To make this system into an MFM, it needed to become sensitive to magnetic fields.
This was accomplished by magnetizing the probe, which was simply done by placing the probe
in close proximity to a strong permanent magnet. By doing this, the probe now interacts with
the distinct fields created by regions of positive and electric magnetic charge on the islands.
Because this change is quite small compared to the changes used to measure the topography,
they do not impede the base function of the AFM. Rather, the changing regions of positive and
negative field gradients induce distinct phase changes in the oscillation of the probe. As such,

we are able to tell whether the area we are in is in the presence of a positive end, a negative end,
or the absence of an island entirely.
B. Experimental Process
​ Once this apparatus was completed, including routine maintenance throughout the week,
the design was ready to be used. Although a wide range of parameters were finely tuned and
used in the scanning process, many of the specifics are omitted here in consideration of
maintaining a reasonable scope. Most scans were conducted on a square grid of length of 15
microns on each side. Each scan allowed the MFM to trace the entirety of the grid, at a typical
resolution of 400 by 400 pixels. These scans took around 20 minutes each (varying with several
parameters).
​ To begin a run of the experiment, first the alignment of the MFM and the sample were
tuned and confirmed. Then, a field value of 60 mT was used to initialize the state of the spin ice
to be uniformly aligned. The system was given sufficient time under the initializing field to
reach complete uniformity. Then, the field was flipped to the opposite direction, at a lesser
magnitude. The magnitude that was flipped to was the primary controlled variable in this
experiment, and was modified to induce different behaviors in the system. Immediately after the
field was flipped using an external controller for the bores, the scans were initiated and
automated to repeat. Full scans were left to run overnight for approximately 1000 minutes
(around 16 hours).
​ Occasionally, scans were left over much longer periods of time (72+ hours) to verify that
the length of the experiment was suitable for our purposes. Once runs were completed, the
MFM was stopped and all components were reinitialized for a new run.

III. DATA PROCESSING
In this section, we will briefly cover the process used to analyze measurement data. Again, for
the sake of scope, this discussion is greatly simplified.
A. File conversion and noise reduction
​ The raw data files from the MFM are first extracted into individual data channels
(topography and phase). These files are converted into more accessible data files (.csv). These
are then processed by a MATLAB script which performs an initial cleaning of background noise
using typical gaussian filters and polynomial background removal. These are then converted
into text files (.txt) and are imported into a Python script. This may seem like an unnecessarily
segmented process, however it is the product of several contributors working for the same
project. Thankfully, this process does allow for more obvious troubleshooting options since
errors can be localized to an individual step.

B. Island identification and magnetization assignment
 Once the data is imported to the python script, both the topography and phase data are
used to create a map of the magnetization of the scanned grid. The topography image is passed
through a binary filter to precisely identify the position and geometry of the islands. The phase
data is then overlayed onto the corresponding islands, precisely aligning the two data files and
eliminating any extraneous data from empty regions outside of the islands.
 Each island is then identified as a magnet and is assigned an orientation depending on its
phase data. And with this final step, every magnet within the 15 by 15 micron grid is identified.
This data is then used to calculate a normalized magnetization of the sample area along the
direction of the magnetic field (+1 for uniformly with the initial field, and -1 for uniformly
opposing the initial field).
 To understand the behavior of our sample over time, this information was extracted for
every scan and the normalized magnetization was plotted as a function of time.
​
IV. ANALYSIS
A. Previous data and expected results
​ As briefly mentioned above, much of this Summer’s research was guided by a couple of
optimistic datasets taken prior to my arrival that indicated the possibility of a phase transition.
These runs showed that the net magnetization of the sample area initially decayed to a steady
value within approximately the first hour of scans. Then, several hours later in the scan, there
was a large, rapid transition in the net magnetization of the material that seemed to be an
avalanching behavior happening within the span of a single scan (around 20 minutes). This was
the proposed phase transition that we attempted to reproduce.
B. Actual results
​ Nearly all trials conducted over the Summer exhibited the initial decay into a steady
state that was previously demonstrated, but without a subsequent phase transition. These trials
were conducted repeatedly across a wide range of field values, primarily from 0 to 30 mT to
induce a variety of different initial states. Each trial yielded the same general plot for
magnetization over, with a loosely exponential decay into a steady state within the first hour of
scanning. Although trials yielded great consistency amongst themselves, no reproducible
evidence of a phase transition was found.
C. Hysteresis curve
​ While we were unsuccessful in reproducing a phase transition in the material, several
other smaller scans were conducted outside the hours used to run longer scans. Amongst these,
much time was spent understanding the hysteresis curve of the spin ice material (the relation

between external magnetic field and material magnetization). These scans were done in a
similar fashion as to what was outlined by the methodology, only omitting any subsequent scans
after the first measurement.
​ This process yielded a hysteresis curve in strong agreement with previous literature on
the same material, but with a greater number of data points and range. The motivation for
scanning for the hysteresis curve was not without relation to the search for the phase transition.
Certain regions of the hysteresis curve (specifically before the sign change of magnetization)
may be useful in future efforts in continuing to experiment for a phase transition. In addition,
the verification of the hysteresis with existing literature was a valuable check to ensure the
experimental apparatus was functioning as intended.

V. CONCLUSION
​ Although no phase transition was observed during my term at LANL, much of my work
served to develop and refine the experimental process used to interact with and observe the
sample. The data compiled throughout the Summer exhibited good self-consistency, and we
were able to better characterize the initial decay of the system to a steady state. Beyond the
experimental process, I was glad to have the opportunity to refine the data processing stream
and implement easily transferable Python scripts so that the data processing could be taken over
by any future researcher.
​ Moving forward, I hope to continue to study the motivating theory of artificial Kagome
spin ice. I would like to revisit the initial theories predicting a phase transition, perhaps
narrowing down the parameters necessary to achieve the transition or find a convincing case
against its presence entirely. Regardless, I am glad to have been able to contribute further to the
characterization of this fascinating material, especially in the generation of a hysteresis plot.
