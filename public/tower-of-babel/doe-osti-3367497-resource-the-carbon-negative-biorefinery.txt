Public Executive Summary
Recently published by the U.S. Department of Energy, the 2023 Billion-Ton Report estimates that
the current supply of total wet waste resources amounts to about 622 million tons annually[1].
All combined wet waste resources (which include animal manure, wastewater sludge, food
waste, and inedible FOG [fats, oils and grease]), possess an energy content equivalent to 4% of
the energy consumed in the transportation sector in 2023[2], representing an excellent feedstock
to provide additional domestic supply of alternative feedstocks to the refineries in the petrol and
chemical industries. For that, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), together with
Colorado State University (CSU) and Fluent Renewables, developed the first biorefinery that
funnels multiple organic waste feedstocks into a chemically consistent stream of volatile fatty
acids (VFAs) that can be upgraded to a suite of products. NLR’s Recirculating System for Optimal
Use of Refuse with Control and Efficiency (ReSOURCE) process operates via arrested anaerobic
digestion, and it accumulates and selectively isolates VFAs. The VFA products serve as robust
platform chemicals to subsequently produce aviation fuels, lubricants, and cleaning surfactant
substitutes.
The proposed system produced VFAs through the digestion of mixed waste feedstocks consisted
of two parallel upflow leachate bed reactors (ULBRs) packed with different organic solid wastes.
Leachate from these reactors flowed into a chain elongation bioreactor (CEBR) where VFAs were
produced. Next, a Rotating Ceramic Disk filter (RCD) was used as a cell and debris retention
device. Permeate from the RCD laden with VFAs was subjected to a liquid-liquid extraction using
an organic solvent mixture. Neat VFAs were distilled from the loaded organic phase and the
regenerated organic was recirculated.
In this project, we:
 • Designed and built modular hydrolysis units that enabled microbial management through
 tailored process control for maximum flexibility in feedstock variation. For that, we
 demonstrated the use of this technology for the digestion of multiple feedstocks
 including food waste, manure, grass clippings, and paper and paperboard.
 • Performed a comprehensive end-to-end demonstration of modular hydrolysis units,
 bioconversion, and separations where we obtained 49.5g of 97.9% pure VFAs from a
 feedstock mixture consisting of food waste and paper and paperboard. Each hydrolysis
 unit in the dual-ULBR system ran for 28 days, in a staggered sequence of 14 days to
 replicate continuous leachate production. The leachate was used together with food
 waste as feedstock for the CEBR operations. At steady state, the average concentration
 in both CEBR reactors was 25.8 g/L VFAs, the VFA production reached 2.56 g/L/day, with
 a net yield of 0.30 g VFAs / g VS.
 • Conducted TEA and LCA analysis to assess the implications of several feedstock scenarios
 focusing on the conversion of waste from agricultural and/or urban areas. High-level TEA
 results indicated the potential for cost-competitive SAF for urban and peri-urban
 scenarios. Agricultural scenarios showed more challenges due to limitations on CEBR
 feedstock. LCA results across all scenarios were generally promising due to favorable
 carbon intensity (CI) scores of waste feedstocks, driving low fuel CI.

end demonstration that funneled multiple organic waste feedstocks into a chemically consistent
stream of VFAs from an organic wet waste feedstock mixture.

 Concurrently to the laboratory scale works, TEA and LCA efforts were performed under Task
 1. To conduct TEA and LCA, we built a rigorous process model of the proposed process,
 encompassing the combined ULBR-CEBR bioconversion approach, recovery of VFAs via ISPR
 and distillation, and catalytic upgrading of VFAs to a SAF product. A process flow diagram is
 shown below in Figure 1. The process model was built using a combination of different
 software packages (Excel, Python, and Aspen Plus) which were integrated together to operate
 seamlessly. The mass and energy balance generated from the integrated process model were
 then used as a basis for TEA and LCA analyses to assess minimum fuel selling price (MFSP)
 and fuel carbon intensity (CI), in addition to other metrics such as capital requirement, fuel
 production potential, and energy efficiency. TEA methodology included a discounted cash
 flow rate of return (DCFROR) analysis, assuming a 10% internal rate of return and 40% equity
 financing. LCA methodology utilized data garnered from the Greenhouse Gases, Regulated
 Emissions, and Energy use in Technologies (GREET) model in order to assess life cycle GHG
 emissions of the process.

 Furthermore, Alder Renewable modeling efforts predicted that the most economically
 attractive scenario for SAF production was a centralized supply chain model with wet waste
 converted to VFAs in multiple locations, and then VFAs shipped to a single location for
 ketonization and hydrotreating to SAF. For the centralized VFA to SAF pathway at 1000 MTD
 (Zr-lined ketonization co-located with refinery hydrotreating at 90% uptime), the modeled
 MFSP was $6.11/GGE. The VFA feed cost remained the dominant contributor to MFSP. While
 the MFSP cost without incentives appeared high, the corresponding cost of CO2 abatement
 was very attractive at $160/MT CO2-eq. Closing the gap would rely on renewable energy
 credits and supply-chain optimization. The carbon intensity of -95 gCO₂e/MJ associated with
 converting VFAs to SAF was driven by the large negative upstream VFA credit assigned to wet
 waste feedstocks, and partially offset by hydrogen, natural-gas heat, and minor electricity
 loads. Together, these results indicate a pathway with a strong GHG advantage relative to
 fossil jet but with an economic gap that policy support and cost reductions would need to
 address.

Project Activities
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), together with Colorado State University (CSU) and
Fluent Renewables, developed the first biorefinery that funnels multiple organic waste
feedstocks into a chemically consistent stream of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) that can be upgraded
to a suite of products. The Recirculating System for Optimal Use of Refuse with Control and
Efficiency (ReSOURCE) process operates via arrested anaerobic digestion, and it accumulates and
selectively isolates VFAs. The VFA products serve as robust platform chemicals to subsequently
produce aviation fuels, lubricants, and cleaning surfactant substitutes. To summarize, in this
project, we: (1) Designed and built a modular hydrolysis units that enabled microbial
management through tailored process control for maximum flexibility in feedstock variation, and
we validated the use of this technology for the digestion of multiple feedstocks including food
waste, manure, grass clippings, and paper and paperboard; (2) Performed a comprehensive end-
to-end demonstration of modular hydrolysis units, bioconversion, and separations where we
obtained 49.5g of 97.9% pure VFAs from a feedstock mixture consisting of food waste and paper
and paperboard; and (3) Built a rigorous process model of the proposed process, and performed
TEA and LCA analysis to assess the implications of several feedstock scenarios focusing on the
conversion of waste from agricultural and/or urban areas.
