A degree version of the Burr–Erdős conjecture on trees
Jasmin Katz, Matías Pavez-Signé, Jozef Skokan

Abstract
An old conjecture of Burr and Erdős states that the Ramsey number of any n n -vertex tree T T is at most 2 ​ n − 2 2n-2 . In 2012, Schelp asked whether a degree version of the Burr–Erdős conjecture holds. More precisely, Schelp asked if is it true that for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 and Δ ≥ 2 \Delta\geq 2 , if G G is a graph on N ≥ ( 2 + ε ) ​ n N\geq(2+\varepsilon)n vertices and minimum degree δ ⁡ ( G ) ≥ ⌊ 3 ​ N / 4 ⌋ \delta(G)\geq\lfloor 3N/4\rfloor , then every blue/red colouring of the edges of G G yields a monochromatic copy of each n n -vertex tree with maximum degree at most Δ \Delta . We prove this conjecture in a strong form, showing that it is true even if one removes the extra ε ​ n \varepsilon n term in the size of the host graph.

Introduction

Ramsey theory is one of the most fundamental areas of research in extremal combinatorics. We say a graph
G
is Ramsey for a graph
H
(or
H
-Ramsey) if every blue/red colouring of the edges of
G
yields a monochromatic copy of
H
, in which case we write
G\rightarrow H
to denote that
G
is
H
-Ramsey. From an extremal graph theory perspective, the aim is to understand how large the
minimal

H
-Ramsey graphs are, where minimality typically refers to either the number of vertices or the number of edges among all
H
-Ramsey graphs. The classical problem in the area involves determining the Ramsey number of a graph
H
, denoted
R(H)
, which is the smallest integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
K_{n}
is Ramsey for
H
. In other words,
R(H)
is the least order among all
H
-Ramsey graphs, which is well-defined as a consequence of Ramsey’s theorem
[
32
]
from 1929. Determining the Ramsey number of a graph
H
is an extremely difficult problem in general, and even proving good bounds on
R(H)
is challenging. For instance, it took nearly 70 years to give the first exponential improvement on
R(K_{t})
(see e.g.
[
2
,
14
,
20
]
).

The progress on computing exact Ramsey numbers of graphs is also very modest. We know
R(H)
only for a few classes of graphs, such as factors
[
11
]
, paths
[
19
]
, and cycles
[
8
,
18
,
33
]
. For general trees, Burr and Erdős
[
12
]
conjectured in 1976 that any
n
-vertex tree
T
satisfies
R(T)\leq 2n-2
for even
n
and
R(T)\leq 2n-3
for
n
odd, which would be tight as witnessed by the
n
-vertex star
[
22
]
. Though this conjecture is still open, we know by a result of Yi Zhao
[
36
]
that
R(T)\leq 2n-2
holds for every
n
-vertex tree
T
, whenever
n
is sufficiently large.

In this paper, we are interested in understanding how resilient the property of being
H
-Ramsey is under adversarial local edge-deletion, a question that was first considered in 2010 by Li, Nikiforov, and Schelp
[
26
]
. Formally, the question we want to address is the following.

Question 1.1
.

For a graph
H
, does there exist a constant
c_{H}\in(0,1)
such that if
G
is a graph with at least
R(H)
vertices and minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq c_{H}|G|
, then
G\to H
?

In 2012, Schelp
[
34
]
asked for which graphs
H
one can find a non-trivial answer to Question
1.1
, and proposed a series of conjectures about which families of graphs are good candidates for solving it. This problem has received considerable attention since then. For example, Benevides, Luczak, Scott, Skokan, and White
[
5
]
showed that
c=3/4
is the answer for the
n
-vertex cycle
C_{n}
(see also
[
4
,
26
,
21
,
29
]
), and, very recently, Balogh, Freschi, and Treglown
[
3
]
made progress in the case when
H
consists of vertex-disjoint copies of
K_{3}
. In the asymmetric setting, Aragão, Marciano, and Mendonça
[
1
]
studied an analogue of Question
1.1
for the pair
(P_{n},K_{t})
, where
P_{n}
denotes the path of length
n
. In the case of trees, Schelp
[
34
]
conjectured that
c=3/4
would work for bounded-degree trees too, asking the following degree version of the Burr–Erdős conjecture on trees.

Question 1.2
.

Let
\Delta\geq 2
, let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and let
G
be a graph with
(2+\varepsilon)n
vertices and minimum degree
\delta(G)>3|G|/4
. If
T
is an
n
-vertex tree with maximum degree
\Delta(T)\leq\Delta
, does
G\rightarrow T
?

In this paper, we answer this question in the affirmative and show that, in fact, the
\varepsilon n
term is not necessary.

Theorem 1.3
.

For all
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is some
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all

the following holds.

If
G
is a blue/red edge-coloured graph with
|G|=2n-1
and
\delta(G)\geq\lfloor\frac{3|G|}{4}\rfloor
, then
G
contains a monochromatic copy of every
n
-vertex tree with maximum degree at most
\Delta
.

We also show that with an additional constant term in the minimum degree, we can embed a bounded degree tree in a graph on
2n-2
vertices, which is tight up to the constant term.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is some
C>0
and
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
the following holds.

If
G
is a blue/red edge-coloured graph with
|G|=2n-2
and
\delta(G)\geq\lfloor\frac{3|G|}{4}\rfloor+C
, then
G
contains a monochromatic copy of every
n
-vertex tree with maximum degree at most
\Delta
.

The extremal example of Theorem
1.4
is given by a graph

on

vertices with vertex set

, where

and
|V_{2}|=|V_{4}|=n/2-1
(assuming

is any blue/red coloured clique. Both

and

are complete bipartite graphs, with all edges coloured blue, and both

are complete bipartite graphs with all edges coloured red. The minimum degree of

(this is the degree of the vertices in

and

We made no attempt to optimise the constant

in Theorem
1.4
, though our proof gives
C=\text{poly}(\Delta)
. Moreover, if we allow a linear error term in the minimum degree, we can improve the maximum degree of the trees we can embed, as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

For all
\delta>0
, there are
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
the following holds.

If
G
is a blue/red edge-coloured graph with
|G|=2n-2
and
\delta(G)\geq(3/2+\delta)n
, then
G
contains a monochromatic copy of every
n
-vertex tree with maximum degree at most

.

The bound on the maximum degree in Theorem
1.5
might not be optimal, and we believe it might be pushed up to
o(n/\log n)
, which would be best possible. To see why this is a natural barrier, let us consider the following example, which is inspired by a famous construction of Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
25
]
. Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint sets of size
n
each, and let
G
be a graph with vertex set
V(G)=X\cup Y
defined as follows. For
p=0.9
, say, let
G[X]
and
G[Y]
be independent copies of
G(n,p)
, and between
X
and
Y
put a binomial bipartite random graph with edge probability
p
. It is clear that with high probability
G
has minimum degree roughly
0.9|G|
. Now, colour the edges in both
G[X]
and
G[Y]
with blue and colour the crossing edges between
X
and
Y
in red. For a sufficiently small constant
c\in(0,1)
, if
T
is the tree consisting of a vertex that has
c\log n
children and each grandchild has roughly
n/c\log n
children, then with high probability
T
is not a monochromatic subgraph of
G
, as otherwise there would be a dominating set of size
c\log n
in some colour, which does not exist with high probability.

For the proofs of Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
and
1.5
, we follow a stability approach as we briefly outline now. Given a graph
G
as in Theorem
1.3
, the first step is to prove a stability result (Theorem
4.2
) which states that either
G
contains each
n
-vertex tree with bounded degree in some colour or it is close to the extremal example depicted in Figure
1
. To prove this, we use the
regularity method
and, in particular, ideas around the
connected-matching method
introduced by Łuczak
[
37
]
(see Section
3
) in combination with structural results for graphs without large connected matchings (see Lemma
4.1
). The second step is thus to prove that if
G
looks similar to the extremal example, then it contains every
n
-vertex tree with bounded degree in some colour. For the extremal analysis, we assume that
G
contains pairwise disjoint sets
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3},V_{4}
such that
G[V_{i}]
is an almost complete graph of size
|V_{i}|\approx n/2
, for
i\in[4]
,
G[V_{1},V_{2}]
and
G[V_{3},V_{4}]
are blue almost complete bipartite graphs,
G[V_{1},V_{4}]
and
G[V_{2},V_{3}]
are red almost complete bipartite graphs, and there are only
o(n^{2})
crossing edges. For simplicity, assume that
V(G)=V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}\cup V_{4}
, in which case, since
|G|=2n-1
, at least one of the monochromatic graphs covers at least
n
vertices. The problem thus reduces to finding an
n
-vertex tree in a graph of high minimum degree, for which we use randomised embeddings and the
absorption method
via switchings.

Of course, the above outline is oversimplified. In particular, a lot of extra work is needed to refine the approximate extremal example we start with, as
V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}\cup V_{4}
might not cover
V(G)
and it is crucial to incorporate every single vertex in this structure. For Theorem
1.4
, we need some extra work as the components we find might have exactly
n-1
vertices, in which case we need to use the extra room in the minimum degree condition to find crossing edges between two of the monochromatic components. If the number of crossing edges is large enough (compared to the maximum degree of the tree), we show that this is enough to find a copy of each
n
-vertex tree with bounded degree. For trees with unbounded degree as in Theorem
1.5
, we follow a similar strategy, and we highlight that for the extremal analysis we need some sort of bipartite version of a celebrated result of Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
25
]
about minimum degree conditions forcing the containment of spanning trees.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we collect the necessary tools for reading this paper. In Section
3
we show how to embed bounded-degree trees using regularity and we use these results in Section
4
to prove the stability results. In Section
5
we prove four results needed to analyse the extremal situation and in Section
6
we finally prove Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
and
1.5
. We finish with some concluding remarks and questions in Section
7
.

For a graph

for the number of vertices of

and

for the number of edges of

for its neighbourhood in

d_{G}(x):=|N_{G}(x)|
is the degree of

in
G
. We let
\delta(G)
and

be the minimum and maximum degree of
G
, respectively, and write
d(G)=2e(G)/|G|
for the average degree of the vertices in
G
. Let
X
and
Y
be two disjoint subsets of
V(G)
. We write
N_{G}(X)=\bigcup_{x\in X}N_{G}(x)
to be the union of all neighbourhoods of vertices in
X
. We denote by

the subgraph of

induced by the vertices of
X
, and

the set of edges joining
X
and
Y
. We write

for the bipartite subgraph of

with bipartition

and edge set

. If
G
is bipartite, we write
G=(A,B)
to indicate
A
and
B

, with
|A|\geq|B|
. Say a set

Suppose

and

denote the graphs spanned by the red edges and the blue edges, respectively. We write

As usual, we write

for

and, for

, we use

to denote that

2.2
Probabilistic tools

Lemma 2.1

(Chernoff’s bound
[
23
, Corollary 2.3, Theorem 2.10]
)
.

Let
X
be either a binomial random variable or a hypergeometric random variable. Then, for all
0<\eps\leq 3/2
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\big|X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big|\geq\eps\mathbb{E}[X]\right)\leq 2\exp(-\eps^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]/3).

Lemma 2.2

(McDiarmid’s inequality
[
27
, Lemma 1.2]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
be independent random variables taking values in a set
\Omega
. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\geq 0
and suppose
f:\Omega^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function such that for every
i\in[m]
and every
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},x_{i}^{\prime}\in\Omega
, we have
\Big|f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{m})-f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{m})\Big|\leq c_{i}
.
Then, for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})]\Big|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

A sequence of random variables
(X_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is a submartingale if
\mathbb{E}[X_{i+1}\mid X_{i},\ldots,X_{0}]\geq X_{i}
for each
i\geq 0
. We will use the following Azuma-type bound for submartingales.

Lemma 2.3

(Azuma’s inequality
[
35
]
)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be a submartingale and let
c_{i}>0
for each
i\geq 1
. If
|X_{i}-X_{i-1}|<c_{i}
for each
i\geq 1
, then, for each
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}-X_{0}\leq t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

2.6

Let

We say that the pair

. Also, we say the graph

is

-regular if

is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair in

(\eps,d)
-regular. Say a subset

is
\eps
-significant
if
|X|>\eps|A|
, and say that a vertex

is
\varepsilon
-typical
with respect to a significant set

if

. We simply write

,

or

whenever

is clear from the context. The following result is a well-known fact about regular pairs.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
(A,B)
be an
\eps
-regular pair with density
d
.

(i)

For each
\eps
-significant set

, all but at most
\eps|A|
vertices from

are
\eps
-typical to
B^{\prime}
.

(ii)

For all
\alpha>\varepsilon
and every
\alpha
-significant sets

and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
,

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is
\frac{2\eps}{\alpha}
-regular with density

.

5
Embedding trees in very dense graphs

In this section, we prove several embedding results that we will need to analyse the extremal situations in Section
6
. The first result finds a spanning tree in a graph which is almost a clique, and the second result is some sort of bipartite version of Theorem
2.15
, which may be of independent interest. The last results are a bit different; these results find a large tree in a graph which is the union of an almost complete bipartite graph with either another almost complete bipartite graph or a relatively dense graph.
The first two of these results use an

approach inspired by the work of Bötcher, Montgomery, Parzyck and Person
[
10
]
and of Kathapurkar and Montgomery
[

]
. The basic idea in the proof is to embed a small proportion of the tree randomly so that it gives enough flexibility to incorporate the last bit of the tree by locally

the role of a vertex which is currently embedded so that a new vertex can be added at the time.

Now we prove the last two results of this section. In these results, we embed a tree into a graph which consists of two very dense bipartite graphs which some additional edges. The proofs use randomised semi-greedy arguments, where we first partition the tree into small subtrees and then assign those subtrees randomly to one of the bipartite graphs where most of the subtree is embedded. 7
Concluding remarks

While Theorem
1.3
resolves the conjecture of Schelp
[
34
]
, one could ask for additional improvements.

Improving the minimum degree in Theorem
1.4

From the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we see that we can take the constant
C
of the form
C=\text{poly}(\Delta)
. Ideally, one would show that
C=0
suffices, though even proving
C=\Delta^{1+o(1)}
would be interesting.

Larger maximum degree

As mentioned in the introduction, we cannot expect to improve the maximum degree condition in Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
beyond
o(n/\log n)
. In this direction, we believe that Theorem
1.5
should hold for trees of higher maximum degree.

Question 7.1
.

Prove that Theorem
1.5
holds for trees with maximum degree
cn/\log n
, provided
c
is sufficiently small.

Although our approach does not work for this question, because we rely heavily on controlling the maximum degree to prove Theorems
4.2
and
4.6
, we believe that the techniques we used for the stability analysis might still be useful.

Smaller host graph

Burr
[
13
]
noted that the Ramsey number of a tree
T
seems to depend not only on the number of vertices but rather on the sizes of the bipartition classes of
T
, and conjectured that if
T
has bipartition classes of sizes
t_{1},t_{2}
with
t_{1}\geq t_{2}\geq 2
, then
R(T)=\max\{t_{1}+2t_{2},2t_{1}\}-1
. Though Burr’s conjecture was known to be false for certain double stars (see e.g.
[
17
,
30
]
), Montgomery, Pavez-Signé and Yan
[
28
]
recently showed that Burr’s conjecture is indeed true for
n
-vertex trees with maximum degree at most
cn
, where
c\in(0,1)
is some small absolute constant.
As our extremal example has size
2n-2
and
2n-2\geq\max\{t_{1}+2t_{2},2t_{1}\}-1
holds for all
t_{1}\geq t_{2}\geq 2
with
t_{1}+t_{2}=n
, it might be possible to have a version of Theorem
1.3
for smaller host graphs.
