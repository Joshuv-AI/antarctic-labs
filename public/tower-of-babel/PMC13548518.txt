Fatal cardiogenic shock following inhalation of aluminum phosphide in a child requiring veno-arterial ECMO support: a rare case and review of extracorporeal strategies
Authors: Ebru Guney Sahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAluminum phosphide is a pesticide and rodenticide widely used in grain storage and pest management and is associated with high mortality due to refractory shock and multiorgan failure. Although severe cardiogenic shock requiring mechanical circulatory support has been predominantly reported following oral exposure, profound cardiac involvement after inhalational exposure is exceedingly rare.Case presentationWe report the case of a previously healthy 2-year-old girl who developed fulminant cardiogenic shock and malignant ventricular arrhythmias after inhalation of aluminum phosphide. Despite aggressive medical therapy, she experienced recurrent cardiac arrest within the first hours of hospitalization. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe left ventricular dysfunction, prompting emergent initiation of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) within the first 4 h. Multimodal extracorporeal supportive therapies, including continuous renal replacement therapy, therapeutic plasma exchange, and red blood cell exchange, were used as adjunctive measures to manage multiorgan failure. Cardiac function progressively recovered, and the patient was successfully weaned from VA-ECMO on day 11. However, she subsequently developed acute respiratory distress syndrome and suffered cardiac arrest on day 21 following progressive respiratory deterioration. The exact cause of the terminal cardiac arrest could not be determined.ConclusionWhile adjunctive extracorporeal therapies may contribute to metabolic stabilization and organ support, this case underscores that early recognition of toxin-induced cardiac failure and timely initiation of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is essential for initial stabilization in severe aluminum phosphide poisoning.

Introduction

Aluminum phosphide (AlP) is a highly toxic pesticide and rodenticide widely used in agricultural settings. Owing to its low cost and easy accessibility, it is a common agent of poisoning, particularly in South Asia, the Middle East, and Mediterranean regions, and represents a major public health concern [1]. Mortality remains extremely high, primarily due to refractory cardiogenic shock, severe metabolic acidosis, and multiorgan failure, with reported rates ranging from 30% to 100%, and most deaths occurring within the first 24 h [2].

AlP toxicity results from the release of phosphine (PH₃) gas upon contact with moisture or gastric acid. PH₃ inhibits mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation at the level of cytochrome c oxidase, leading to cellular hypoxia, anaerobic metabolism, and severe lactic acidosis [1]. In addition, increased oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation contribute to widespread cellular injury and the rapid development of multiorgan dysfunction [2].

Clinically, AlP poisoning manifests as profound circulatory collapse, cardiogenic failure, and metabolic derangements, often necessitating aggressive critical care support. In the absence of a specific antidote, management is largely supportive and focuses on early hemodynamic stabilization and organ support [3, 4].

We present the case of a previously healthy 2-year-old girl who developed fatal refractory cardiogenic shock following inhalational aluminum phosphide exposure despite multimodal extracorporeal support, including continuous renal replacement therapy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, therapeutic plasma exchange, and red blood cell exchange.

Case report

A previously healthy 2-year-old girl was brought to the emergency department with vomiting, altered mental status, and respiratory distress. Her mother reported a strong chemical odor in their home the previous night, followed by nausea, vomiting, and lethargy affecting both herself and the child. As the child’s condition progressively worsened overnight, she was brought to the hospital. The exact nature and route of exposure could not be determined at the time of admission. However, the presence of similar symptoms in the mother raised suspicion of a shared environmental toxic exposure, prompting a forensic investigation. Subsequent police inquiry confirmed the use of aluminum phosphide as a pesticide in a grain storage area located beneath the family’s residence. This finding, supported by witness statements and identification of the pesticide material, established inhalational exposure as the most likely route.

On admission, the patient had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 5–6 with preserved bilateral pupillary light reflexes. Oxygen saturation was 88–89% while receiving 10–15 L/min of oxygen via a non-rebreather mask. Vital signs demonstrated profound hemodynamic compromise, including a heart rate of 180 beats/min, respiratory rate of 45 breaths/min, blood pressure of 64/26 mmHg, and body temperature of 36.8 °C. Initial arterial blood gas analysis revealed severe metabolic and lactic acidosis (Table 1). While under observation in the emergency department, the patient developed progressive respiratory depression and signs of circulatory shock, prompting endotracheal intubation and transfer to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Because the toxic agent was not identified at the time of presentation, early decontamination measures such as gastric lavage, activated charcoal, or oil-based interventions were not performed.

Despite aggressive resuscitation, persistent hypotension necessitated escalating vasoactive and inotropic support with epinephrine (up to 1 µg/kg/min), norepinephrine (up to 1.5 µg/kg/min), and milrinone (up to 0.5 µg/kg/min) infusions. A hydrocortisone bolus was administered, and terlipressin infusion was added in response to worsening hemodynamic instability.

Within the second hour of PICU admission, the patient experienced two episodes of cardiac arrest due to ventricular tachycardia, occurring 15 min apart, both of which were successfully resuscitated with cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Lidocaine bolus therapy and an amiodarone loading dose were administered for recurrent ventricular tachycardia, followed by continuous amiodarone infusion for rhythm control. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 30%.

Given the rapidly progressive, catecholamine-refractory cardiogenic shock complicated by malignant ventricular arrhythmias and recurrent cardiac arrest within the first 4 h of hospitalization, emergent veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was initiated following multidisciplinary consultation with pediatric cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery teams. The primary objective of VA-ECMO support was to achieve immediate hemodynamic stabilization and provide myocardial rest in the setting of toxin-induced, potentially reversible myocardial dysfunction. Following ECMO initiation, systemic perfusion improved, vasopressor requirements gradually decreased, and serial echocardiographic assessments demonstrated progressive recovery of left ventricular function (Fig. 1). The patient was successfully weaned from VA-ECMO on day 11, confirming the reversibility of aluminum phosphide–associated myocardial injury under timely mechanical circulatory support. Left ventricular function remained stable in follow-up echocardiographic evaluations after ECMO weaning (days 11 and 14), confirming sustained myocardial recovery. Following ECMO decannulation, therapeutic-dose enoxaparin was initiated twice daily for thromboprophylaxis and continued during the remainder of the hospitalization.

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated to manage severe metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury, and fluid overload associated with multiorgan failure. Although CRRT does not directly eliminate phosphine, it was maintained as supportive therapy to achieve metabolic stabilization and fluid balance throughout the critical phase of illness.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a mildly hypoechoic and heterogeneous liver parenchyma with periportal edema, findings consistent with acute hepatitis. In response to severe acute liver failure, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and suspected cytokine-mediated multiorgan dysfunction, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) was initiated. Daily TPE sessions were performed using a membrane-based system with fresh frozen plasma as replacement fluid. Following TPE, a marked improvement in liver function tests and platelet counts was observed, allowing discontinuation of the procedure as organ function stabilized and inotropic support was no longer required.

Based on the proposed interaction between phosphine and erythrocytes, automated red blood cell exchange (RBCX) was performed on two consecutive days as an adjunctive therapy. Although the procedures were technically successful and free of complications, no significant clinical or laboratory improvement was observed, and RBCX was subsequently discontinued.

Temporal evolution of laboratory findings

Following decannulation from ECMO, the patient remained on mechanical ventilation, with relatively stable oxygenation in the early post-ECMO period and no evidence of severe hypoxemia. However, progressive respiratory deterioration developed around day 20, ultimately leading to acute respiratory distress syndrome, preceded by worsening oxygenation indices. Despite lung-protective mechanical ventilation strategies, hypoxemia continued to worsen.

On day 21, the patient experienced cardiac arrest following progressive respiratory deterioration and severe ARDS. Despite prolonged cardiopulmonary resuscitation, defibrillation, and advanced antiarrhythmic therapy, resuscitation efforts were unsuccessful, and the patient died on day 21 of hospitalization. The exact cause of the terminal cardiac arrest could not be definitively established.

Discussion

Aluminum phosphide poisoning is associated with high mortality, primarily due to refractory cardiogenic shock, malignant arrhythmias, and multiorgan failure [1, 2]. The cardiotoxic effects of phosphine result from profound mitochondrial dysfunction, myocardial depression, and oxidative stress, leading to rapid hemodynamic collapse [1, 3]. While severe myocardial involvement has been predominantly described following oral exposure, inhalational exposure is generally reported to cause milder cardiac dysfunction [2]. In contrast to these observations, our patient developed fulminant cardiogenic shock with early ventricular arrhythmias and cardiac arrest within the first hours of hospitalization, highlighting the potential severity of inhalational aluminum phosphide exposure.

Early initiation of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) played a pivotal role in the acute management of this case. Despite maximal medical therapy, the patient experienced recurrent cardiac arrest within the first 4 h, prompting emergent mechanical circulatory support. VA-ECMO provided immediate hemodynamic stabilization and myocardial rest, allowing recovery from toxin-induced but potentially reversible myocardial dysfunction. The successful weaning from ECMO on day 11, accompanied by progressive improvement in left ventricular function, supports existing literature suggesting that phosphine-related cardiotoxicity may be reversible when adequate circulatory support is instituted early [3, 4]. Importantly, no residual myocardial dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension was detected after ECMO decannulation, suggesting that persistent cardiac impairment was unlikely to be the primary cause of death.

Previous reports on ECMO use in aluminum phosphide poisoning have largely focused on oral ingestion, with favorable outcomes when ECMO is initiated before irreversible organ injury occurs [3, 4]. Our case expands this experience by demonstrating that inhalational exposure can similarly lead to profound cardiac failure requiring ECMO, reinforcing the importance of early cardiac assessment and timely consultation with cardiothoracic surgery teams. From a surgical perspective, this case underscores the value of ECMO as a bridge to myocardial recovery rather than as a rescue therapy instituted late in the disease course [3].

Adjunctive extracorporeal therapies, including continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), and red blood cell exchange (RBCX), were employed to address metabolic derangements, liver dysfunction, and systemic inflammation [5–8]. While these modalities contributed to metabolic stabilization and improvement in liver function, they did not appear to alter the underlying course of toxin-induced myocardial injury. Among these therapies, RBCX remains particularly experimental. Its rationale is based on evidence suggesting that phosphine may interact with erythrocytes, contributing to impaired oxygen delivery and systemic toxicity. In an experimental model of aluminum phosphide poisoning, Rahimi et al. demonstrated improved survival and correction of metabolic acidosis following fresh red blood cell transfusion [7]. Clinical evidence, however, remains limited to isolated case reports, including the report by Özkale et al. describing successful use of automated RBCX in severe aluminum phosphide poisoning [8]. In our patient, RBCX was technically successful and was not associated with any procedure-related complications; however, no clear clinical benefit could be demonstrated. Therefore, RBCX should currently be regarded as a promising but experimental adjunctive therapy requiring further investigation before routine clinical use can be recommended. ECMO, in contrast, remains the cornerstone of hemodynamic stabilization in patients with refractory cardiogenic shock due to aluminum phosphide poisoning [3, 4]. Furthermore, no temporal relationship was observed between RBCX administration and subsequent respiratory deterioration.

The development of ARDS in this case was likely multifactorial. Prolonged critical illness, systemic inflammatory response, fluid overload, extracorporeal support-related factors, and possible delayed pulmonary effects of phosphine toxicity may all have contributed. Because definitive diagnostic evidence was unavailable, the relative contribution of each factor could not be determined. Importantly, respiratory deterioration developed after recovery of left ventricular systolic function, making cardiogenic pulmonary edema a less likely explanation.

Despite successful cardiac recovery and ECMO weaning, the patient ultimately died after progressive respiratory deterioration and severe ARDS. Although anticoagulation was maintained during and after ECMO support, no definitive cause of the terminal cardiac arrest could be established. The fatal outcome likely reflected the cumulative effects of prolonged critical illness and multiorgan dysfunction.

This case highlights that successful cardiac recovery does not necessarily translate into overall survival in aluminum phosphide poisoning. Although VA-ECMO effectively reversed refractory cardiogenic shock, delayed complications such as ARDS and persistent multiorgan dysfunction may ultimately determine the outcome. These findings underscore the importance of continued vigilance and multidisciplinary management even after apparent myocardial recovery. In similar cases, early recognition of toxin-induced cardiac dysfunction, prompt echocardiographic assessment, and timely consideration of VA-ECMO in patients with refractory cardiogenic shock may be critical to improving survival.
