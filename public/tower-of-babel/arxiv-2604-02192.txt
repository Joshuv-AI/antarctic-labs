What can be computed in average anonymous networks?
Joel Rybicki, Oleg Verbitsky, Maksim Zhukovskii

Abstract
We study what deterministic distributed algorithms can compute on random input graphs in extremely weak models of distributed computing: all nodes are anonymous, and in each communication round, nodes broadcast a message to all their neighbors, receive a (multi)set of messages from their neighbors, and update their local state. These correspond to the 𝖲𝖡 \mathsf{SB} and 𝖬𝖡 \mathsf{MB} models introduced by Hella et al. [PODC 2012] and are strictly weaker than the standard port-numbering 𝖯𝖭 \mathsf{PN} and 𝖫𝖮𝖢𝖠𝖫 \mathsf{LOCAL} models. We investigate what can be computed almost surely on random input graphs. We give a one-round deterministic 𝖲𝖡 \mathsf{SB} -algorithm using O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) -bit messages that computes unique identifiers with high probability on anonymous networks sampled from G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) , where n ε − 1 ≤ p ≤ 1 / 2 n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2 and ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 is an arbitrarily small constant. This algorithm is inspired by canonical labeling techniques in graph isomorphism testing and can be used to “anonymize” existing distributed graph algorithms designed for the broadcast 𝖢𝖮𝖭𝖦𝖤𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{CONGEST} and 𝖫𝖮𝖢𝖠𝖫 \mathsf{LOCAL} models. In particular, we give a new anonymous algorithm that finds a triangle in O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) O(1/\varepsilon) rounds on the above input distribution. We also investigate computational power of natural analogs of “Monte Carlo” and “Las Vegas” distributed graph algorithms in the random graph setting, and establish some new collapse and hierarchy results. For example, our work shows the collapse of the weak model hierarchy of Hella et al. on G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) , as apart from a vanishingly small fraction of input graphs, the 𝖲𝖡 \mathsf{SB} model is as powerful as 𝖫𝖮𝖢𝖠𝖫 \mathsf{LOCAL} .

Introduction

Many graph problems cannot be solved by deterministic, anonymous distributed algorithms for the simple reason that symmetry cannot be broken in worst-case graphs
[
3
,
61
,
62
]
. Thus, the typical approach is to assume nodes are given some symmetry breaking information as input, such as unique identifiers (e.g., the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model
[
49
]
), a stream of random bits (e.g., the port-numbering model
\mathsf{PN}
with private random bits), or locally unique identifiers (e.g., a suitable graph coloring
[
28
]
).

But if we move beyond
worst-case
inputs, to which extent is such additional symmetry breaking information necessary?
Can anonymous, deterministic distributed algorithms perform well on
most
graphs?
To address these questions, we study what can be computed by anonymous, deterministic algorithms on
random input graphs sampled from the
Erdős-Rényi random graph distribution
G(n,p)
.
So far, even in the non-anonymous setting, the average-case complexity of distributed graph algorithms has remained largely unexplored apart from few isolated examples
[
48
,
46
,
19
,
58
]
.

Our results in a nutshell.

We investigate the question of what can be deterministically computed on “most” anonymous networks. We introduce and study the notions of asymptotically almost surely correct (“Monte Carlo”) and sound (“Las Vegas”) deterministic distributed algorithms on random input graphs.
It turns out
– somewhat surprisingly –
that apart from a vanishingly small fraction of graphs, anonymous deterministic algorithms in
very
weak models of computing can solve any problem solvable in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model
with only a small overhead in time and message length.

This is because on most graphs identifiers can be deterministically generated in only
one round
using short
O(\log n)
-bit messages, allowing simulation of both
\mathsf{LOCAL}
and (broadcast)
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithms in weak anonymous models of computing.
This further implies
that a range of non-trivial tasks, such as finding Hamiltonian cycles or triangles, can be done fast on anonymous random graphs.

1.1
The setting: weak models of distributed computing

We consider deterministic and anonymous distributed graph algorithms in the
set-broadcast

\mathsf{SB}
and the
multiset-broadcast

\mathsf{MB}
models. These models lie at the bottom of the weak model hierarchy introduced by Hella et al.
[
37
]
. In particular, both
\mathsf{SB}
and
\mathsf{MB}
models are strictly weaker than the standard port-numbering
\mathsf{PN}
and
\mathsf{LOCAL}
models of distributed computing.

In the distributed setting, the input graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
nodes describes the communication topology of the distributed system, where nodes represent processors and edges communication links between processors.
Each node
v\in V
is anonymous, but may additionally receive some local input
f(v)
, depending on the problem and the model of computing.
In the
set-broadcast model

\mathsf{SB}
, each node
v\in V
receives as local input its degree
\deg(v)
.
All nodes run the same algorithm and computation proceeds in synchronous rounds, where in each round every node
v\in V
in parallel

(1)

broadcasts
a single message to all its neighbors,

(2)

receives a
set
of messages broadcast by its neighbors, and

(3)

updates its local state.

Once a node enters a stopping state, it halts with some output
g(v)
.
In the
\mathsf{SB}
model, nodes cannot distinguish which neighbor sent which incoming message nor count the multiplicities of messages.
In the
multiset-broadcast model

\mathsf{MB}
, nodes receive
multisets
of messages instead of sets. That is, a node can also count the
multiplicity
of received messages. Otherwise, the two models are the same.
1
1

1

In particular, as in
\mathsf{SB}
, we assume that in
\mathsf{MB}
nodes know their degrees initially, although this could be learned in one communication round.

The standard
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model is equivalent to the setting in which each node is given a unique identifier as local input. We also consider the “congested” variants of these models, where in each round nodes broadcast only short
O(\log n)
-bit messages. While the congested version of
\mathsf{LOCAL}
is known as the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model, we denote the congested versions of
\mathsf{SB}
and
\mathsf{MB}
by
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
and
\mathsf{MB}^{*}
, respectively. Note that the
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
model with unique identifiers is (up to constant factors) equivalent to the
broadcast

\mathsf{CONGEST}
model (abbreviated as
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}
), where in each round each node broadcasts a single
O(\log n)
-bit message to all of its neighbors.

1.2
Distributed graph problems

Graph problems.

We consider problems on unlabeled graphs (i.e., the nodes or edges of the graph do not have input labels).
Let
\Gamma
be a (possibly infinite) set of output labels.
A
graph problem

\Pi
associates to each graph
G=(V,E)
a set
\Pi(G)\subseteq\Gamma^{V}
of solutions.
That is, a labeling
\sigma\colon V\to\Gamma
is a solution to
\Pi
on input
G
if
\sigma\in\Pi(G)
.
We say that a problem
\Pi
is
isomorphism-invariant
if for any isomorphic pair of graphs
G
and
H
with an isomorphism
\alpha
from
G
to
H
we have that

\Pi(G)=\{\sigma\circ\alpha:\sigma\in\Pi(H)\}.

We say that
\Pi
is a
component-wise
problem if for every graph
G
and labeling
\sigma\colon V\to\Gamma
we have that
\sigma\in\Pi(G)
if and only if
\sigma_{\restriction U}\in\Pi(H)
for every connected component
H=(U,F)
of
G
,
where
\sigma_{\restriction U}\colon U\to\Gamma
is the restriction of
\sigma
to the set
U\subseteq V
.
Throughout, we consider only isomorphism-invariant, component-wise graph problems. This definition covers many typical problems,
in particular, all problems on unlabeled graphs that are solvable in the
uniform

\mathsf{LOCAL}
model (where nodes do not a priori know
n
).

Solvability and time complexity.

For any model
\mathsf{M}
, we say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}

solves
a graph problem
\Pi
if on every input
G=(V,E)
, each node
v\in V
halts with an output
\sigma(v)
and
\sigma\in\Pi(G)
.
We often write
\mathcal{A}(G)
for the labeling
\sigma
output by the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on the input graph
G
.

The
time complexity

T_{\mathcal{A}}(G)
of an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on the input graph
G
is the number of steps until all nodes have reached a stopping state.
We say an algorithm is
non-uniform
if each node receives as local input also the value
n=|V|
. Otherwise, it is
uniform
.
Unless specified otherwise, all our algorithms are uniform.

For a given model
\mathsf{M}
, we use
\mathsf{M}
to denote the class of problems solvable in the model.
We write
\mathsf{M}[r]
to denote the class of problems solvable in
r
rounds in this model.
We write
\mathsf{ALL}
for the set of all isomorphism-invariant, component-wise problems.

in Appendix
A

compares and summarizes the different models we consider in this work.

1.3
Average-case distributed computation

We now introduce the formal framework we use to study anonymous distributed graph algorithms in the average case. We consider analogs of Monte Carlo (almost sure solvability) and Las Vegas (soundness) concepts for deterministic algorithms on random input graphs.

Random graphs.

An
Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
is an
n
-node graph,
where each pair of nodes is adjacent with probability
p=p(n)
, independently of all other pairs.
In particular,
G(n,1/2)
is a random graph chosen equiprobably from among all graphs on the node set
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
We say that an event happens for
G(n,p)

asymptotically almost surely

(
a.a.s.
for brevity) if the probability of this event tends to 1 as
n\to\infty
, i.e., with probability
1-o(1)
.
We write
G\sim G(n,p)
to emphasize that the graph
G
is sampled from the Erdős-Rényi distribution.

Asymptotically almost sure computation.

Let
\mathcal{D}(n)
be a probability distribution over
n
-node graphs with
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
solves a problem
\Pi

asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.)
on the distribution
\mathcal{D}(n)
if for the random graph
G\sim\mathcal{D}(n)
we have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}(G)\in\Pi(G))=1-o(1).

When we do not specify the distribution, we assume that the distribution is given by
G(n,1/2)
, i.e., the uniform distribution over all
n
-node graphs. In this case, we often simply say that
\mathcal{A}
solves the problem asymptotically almost surely. This means
\mathcal{A}
outputs a correct solution to
\Pi
on a fraction of the
n
-node graphs that approaches 1 as
n\to\infty
, that is, on
almost all

n
-node graphs.

The class of problems that are solvable a.a.s. on the distribution
\mathcal{D}(n)
by an
\mathsf{M}
algorithm is denoted by
\mathsf{M}_{a.a.s.}^{\mathcal{D}(n)}
.
Similarly, we write
\mathsf{M}_{a.a.s.}^{\mathcal{D}(n)}[r]
for the class of problems solvable on
\mathcal{D}(n)
a.a.s. in at most
r
rounds by an
\mathsf{M}
algorithm.
As before, we omit for brevity the distribution
\mathcal{D}(n)
from the notation when
\mathcal{D}(n)=G(n,1/2)
.
Note that algorithms that solve a problem a.a.s. are analogous to
Monte Carlo algorithms
, but the randomness is over the input graphs rather than the random choices of the algorithm (which deterministic algorithms cannot make).

Soundness.

Let
\Gamma
be a set of output labels and
failed
be a special label not in
\Gamma
.
We say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for a problem
\Pi
is
sound
if

(1)

\mathcal{A}
solves
\Pi
asymptotically almost surely,
and

(2)

if
\mathcal{A}(G)\notin\Pi(G)
, then
\mathcal{A}(G)
is the constant labeling
\sigma(\cdot)=\texttt{failed}
.

That is, if the algorithm fails to output a solution, which happens only with probability
o(1)
, then all nodes output the special value
failed
to indicate this. We write
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{snd}}
to denote the class of distributed problems that admit a sound algorithm in the model
\mathsf{M}
.

Note that sound algorithms can be seen analogous to
Las Vegas algorithms
, but the randomness is over the input graphs rather than the random choices of the algorithm: such algorithms always terminate, but they can terminate with the knowledge that no solution was found. In other words, a sound algorithm cannot output an incorrect solution without all nodes knowing this.

Expected time complexity.

A problem
\Pi
is solvable in
\mathsf{M}
in
expected time

T(n)
on the distribution
\mathcal{D}(n)
if there is an
\mathsf{M}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
solving
\Pi
correctly on every input graph such that

\E_{G\sim\mathcal{D}(n)}(T_{\mathcal{A}}(G))\leq T(n)
for all
n
.

Our contributions

1.4.1
Anonymization of
\mathsf{LOCAL}
and
\mathsf{Broadcast\ CONGEST}
algorithms

Our first result shows that unique identifiers can be generated asymptotically almost surely in one communication round.
The only communication needed is that each node broadcasts a single
O(\log n)
-bit message, namely its degree.
The algorithm
is based on a new succinct
graph canonical labeling
.
Such labelings have been widely studied in the context of graph isomorphism testing; see
Table

1
for a summary and
Section

1.5
for further discussion.
Unlike the existing labeling schemes, our new approach admits a communication-efficient implementation that produces short identifiers in the weak
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
model of distributed computing (i.e., set-broadcast with
O(\log n)
-bit messages).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a constant.
Then there exists a one-round
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
algorithm (depending only on
\varepsilon
) that
asymptotically almost surely assigns unique
O(\log n)
-bit identifiers on input
G(n,p)
for any

n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2
.

The algorithm in the above theorem is simple, but its analysis is non-trivial.
This result has several important consequences for weak models of distributed computing.
In particular, it readily implies the following inclusions:

\displaystyle\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}^{G(n.p)}_{a.a.s.}[r]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\mathsf{SB}^{*,\,G(n.p)}_{a.a.s.}[r+1]\text{ and}

(1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{LOCAL}^{G(n.p)}_{a.a.s.}[r]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\mathsf{SB}^{G(n.p)}_{a.a.s.}[r+1]

(2)

for any
n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2
and
r=r(n)
.

We can use the first of these inclusions to immediately “anonymize” algorithms for random graphs.
For example, Turau
[
58
]
gave a fast
\mathsf{Broadcast\ CONGEST}
-algorithm for finding Hamiltonian cycles on
G(n,p)
. Applying
Theorem

1.1
to Turau’s algorithm immediately yields the following result.

Corollary 1.2
.

There exists an
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
algorithm which
asymptotically almost surely finds a Hamiltonian cycle in
G(n,p)
within
O(\log n)
rounds
for any
p\geq\log^{3/2}n/\sqrt{n}

1.4.2
Anonymous triangle finding with short messages

As a further example, we investigate triangle finding in congested, anonymous networks. In triangle
detection
, at least one node outputs “yes” if and only if there is a triangle in the graph. In triangle
finding
, at least one node needs to output a witness for the existence of a triangle. In triangle
enumeration
, for each triangle of the graph, there has to be some node that outputs the triangle.
While all variants are trivial in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, the complexity of triangle detection and finding remains a long-standing open problem in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
and related models
[
24
,
25
,
13
,
40
,
31
,
1
,
15
,
4
]
.

The triangle detection problem is trivial on
G(n,p)
if nodes know the value of
p
, as there is a threshold at
p=\Theta(1/n)
for the existence of a triangle; see, e.g.,
[
41
, Theorem 3.4]
.
On the other hand, Izumi and Le Gall
[
40
]
showed that triangle
enumeration
requires
\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)
rounds in the (non-broadcast)
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model with inputs from
G(n,1/2)
.
We show that the “intermediate problem” of triangle
finding
can be solved substantially faster, in only diameter time, for a large range of
p
.

Theorem 1.3
.

1
.

There exists a sound
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}
algorithm which, for any constant
\varepsilon>0
and
any
p
satisfying
n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2
, asymptotically almost surely
finds a triangle on
G\sim G(n,p)
in
O(\diam(G))
rounds.

2
.

There exists a
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}
algorithm which, for any constant
\varepsilon>0
and
any
p
satisfying
n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2
,
finds a triangle on
G\sim G(n,p)
in
O(1/\varepsilon)
expected rounds.

This result is based on a new “eccentricity lemma” that identifies a useful combinatorial property for triangles in
G(n,p)
.
Using
Theorem

1.1
, these algorithms can be translated to the anonymous
\mathsf{SB}^{*}

1
).
That is, even when nodes do not have identifiers, nodes can quickly find and identify a triangle in the graph asymptotically almost surely.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
There exists an
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
algorithm which, for any

satisfying

1.4.3
Collapse of the weak model hierarchy in the average case

It is well-known that the port-numbering model
\mathsf{PN}
is strictly weaker than the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model.
Hella et al.
[
37
]
established the following strict hierarchy for even weaker models of distributed computing

\mathsf{SB}\subsetneq\mathsf{MB}\subsetneq\mathsf{VV}\subsetneq\mathsf{PN}\subsetneq\mathsf{LOCAL},

where
\mathsf{VV}
is a variant of the
\mathsf{PN}
model where the incoming and outgoing port are numbered independently.
First, we observe that the weak model hierarchy collapses in the average case; see (
2
).

Second, we show new
time hierarchy collapse results
for weak models.
We show that when
p
is above the connectivity threshold of
G(n,p)
,
any distributed problem can be solved on
G(n,p)

asymptotically almost surely by an anonymous
\mathsf{MB}
-algorithm in
o(\log n)
rounds.
If
p\geq n^{\varepsilon-1}
for
\varepsilon>0
,
the number of rounds is
O(1)
. Using Theorem
1.1
, we
obtain a similar result even for the weak model
\mathsf{SB}
.
In both cases, the time hierarchy collapses to the level equal to the expected diameter of
G(n,p)

up to a small additive constant.
Formally, we prove the following in
Section

4
.

Theorem 1.5
.

1
.

Let
\frac{(1+\delta)\ln n}{n}\leq p\leq\frac{1}{2}
for a constant
\delta>0
. Then

\mathsf{ALL}=\mathsf{MB}^{G(n,p)}_{a.a.s.}\left[(1+o(1))\frac{\ln n}{\ln(np)}+4\right].

2
.

Let
n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq\frac{1}{2}
for a constant
\varepsilon>0
. Then

\mathsf{ALL}=\mathsf{SB}^{G(n,p)}_{a.a.s.}\left[(1+o(1))\frac{\ln n}{\ln(np)}+3\right]=\mathsf{SB}^{G(n,p)}_{a.a.s.}\left[(1+o(1))/\varepsilon+3\right].

1.4.4
Limitations and power of sound algorithms

Finally, we investigate the difference between asymptotically almost surely correct (“Monte Carlo”) algorithms and sound (“Las Vegas”) algorithms.
One issue with the former is that although a correct solution is found on almost all graphs,
the nodes cannot be certain that this is the case for any particular input graph
G
.
As defined in
Section

1.3
, sound algorithms output a correct solution a.a.s.; if the output is not a correct solution, then all nodes declare that the algorithm failed.

It is not hard to see that sound and uniform
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms are (strictly) stronger than worst-case
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms (which must work on all inputs). This holds even for constant-round algorithms; see Appendix
C
for an illustrative example. However, in the
\mathsf{MB}
model, the power of sound and uniform algorithms is quite limited: they cannot solve problems such as leader election, triangle detection, or Hamiltonicity recognition; see Section
3.2
. In particular, Corollaries
1.2
and
1.4
are optimal in the sense that no sound, uniform
\mathsf{MB}
algorithms exist for Hamiltonicity or triangle detection.

Nevertheless, we show that anonymity is not a barrier for
non-uniform
sound algorithms, i.e., when nodes get the number
n
of nodes as input.
We write
\mathsf{M}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}
to denote the class of distributed problems solvable in the model
\mathsf{M}
by non-uniform sound algorithms. We study the round hierarchy

\mathsf{M}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[1]\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[2]\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[r]\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[r+1]\subseteq\cdots

for the classes
\mathsf{M}\in\{\mathsf{MB},\mathsf{SB}\}
.
We show that this hierarchy collapses
at the fourth level—for the remarkable reason that all problems are solvable by
non-uniform sound
\mathsf{SB}
algorithms in just four rounds.
Furthermore, we show that the collapse does not occur earlier. In summary, we get that

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}\mathsf{SB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[1]&\subseteq&\mathsf{SB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[2]&\subseteq&\mathsf{SB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[3]&\subsetneq&\mathsf{SB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[4]&=&\mathsf{ALL}\\
\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\subseteq$}&&\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\subseteq$}&&\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\subseteq$}&&\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$=$}&&\\
\mathsf{MB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[1]&\subseteq&\mathsf{MB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[2]&\subseteq&\mathsf{MB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[3]&\subsetneq&\mathsf{MB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[4].&&\end{array}

This result is formally captured by the following theorem we prove in
Section

4
.

Theorem 1.6
.

1
.

\mathsf{ALL}=\mathsf{SB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[4]
.

2
.

\mathsf{MB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[3]\subsetneq\mathsf{ALL}
.

1.5
Related work

Deterministic anonymous algorithms.

Foundational work in distributed computing has established that many symmetry-breaking problems, such as leader election and graph coloring, cannot be deterministically solved in anonymous networks in the port-numbering model
\mathsf{PN}

[
3
,
61
]
.
However, various optimization problems on graphs can be
approximated
fast even in the
\mathsf{PN}
model
[
5
,
55
,
57
]
.

Hella et al.
[
37
]
studied what can be computed in models that are strictly weaker than the port-numbering model
\mathsf{PN}{}
. They showed there exists a hierarchy of weak models of computing

\mathsf{SB}\subsetneq\mathsf{MB}=\mathsf{VB}\subsetneq\mathsf{SV}=\mathsf{MV}=\mathsf{VV}\subsetneq\mathsf{PN}\subsetneq\mathsf{LOCAL},

where in the model
\mathsf{VB}
nodes have incoming port-numbers but broadcast the same message to all neighbors, in
\mathsf{SV}
nodes receive a set of messages but have outgoing port numbers (and can send distinct messages to each neighbor), and
\mathsf{VV}

Distributed triangle detection, finding and enumeration.

Detecting, finding, and listing triangles are fundamental problems in distributed computing
[
15
]
.
In
\mathsf{LOCAL}
, these problems are solvable in constant time and a trivial
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm solves the problems in
O(n)
time.

Dolev et al.
[
24
]
gave a deterministic algorithm that runs in
O(n^{1/3}/\log n)
rounds in the congested clique model. In the same model, Censor-Hillel et al.
[
13
]
gave triangle detection and counting algorithms that run in
O(n^{\rho})
rounds, where
\rho
is the distributed matrix multiplication exponent.
Dolev et al.
[
24
]
also gave a sampling-based randomized algorithm that finds a triangle in
O(n^{1/3}/(t^{2/3}+1))
rounds with high probability when the input graph has
t
triangles.
Recently, Censor-Hillel et al.
[
12
]
gave a faster sampling algorithm that runs in
\tilde{O}(n^{0.1567}/(t^{0.3926}+1))
time.

Note that for
G(n,p)
, the number of triangles for
p=n^{\varepsilon-1}
is of order
n^{3\varepsilon}
. So for sufficiently large
\varepsilon>0
, the above randomized protocols run in (near) constant-time. However, our new algorithm finds a triangle in constant expected time for any constant
\varepsilon>0
and works in the anonymous
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
rather than the congested clique model.

Lower bounds for triangle detection and finding are notoriously challenging, as super-logarithmic lower bounds for triangle detection imply breakthroughs in circuit complexity
[
25
,
27
]
.
There has been substantial prior work on lower bounds for triangle detection in distributed models; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
31
]
.
In a recent breakthrough, Assadi and Sundaresan
[
4
]
showed that

rounds are needed for triangle

in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model using a round elimination argument.

Izumi and Le Gall
[
40
]
studied triangle finding
and triangle enumeration.
They gave the first sublinear time algorithms for the general
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model: a
O((n\log n)^{2/3})

O(n^{3/4}\log n)

They also gave a lower bound of
\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)
rounds for triangle enumeration in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
and congested clique. This lower bound uses an information theoretic argument on uniform random graphs.
Censor-Hillel et al.
[
14
]
gave a near-optimal
\mathsf{CONGEST}

rounds.
The lower bound was subsequently matched up to polylogarithmic factors by the algorithm of Chang et al.

Canonical labelings of graphs.

An important role in isomorphism testing is played by
color refinement
and related algorithms: they can be used to compute canonical
(i.e., isomorphism-invariant) labelings of graphs.
Interestingly, many of these canonization schemes admit distributed implementations, and can be used to compute unique identifiers on various graph distributions.

Table

1
summarizes prior work from the perspective of weak models of distributed computing.
Babai, Erdős, and Selkow
[
6
]
showed that a rather succinct version of color refinement a.a.s.
finds unique labels for nodes in a uniform random graph
G(n,1/2)
; see Section
4.1
for details.
Bollobás
[
11
, Ch. 3.5]
proved that
their approach works a.a.s. for
G(n,p)
whenever
p\gg n^{-1/5}\ln n
.
Czajka and Pandurangan
[
22
]
established that the color refinement algorithm computes a.a.s. canonical labeling of
G(n,p)
in the sparse setting, when
p\gg\frac{\ln^{4}n}{n\ln\ln n}
, which was later improved to
p\gg\frac{\ln^{2}n}{n}
by Mossel and Ross
[
52
]
.
Recently, Gaudio, Rácz, and Sridhar
[
32
]
suggested an algorithm that succeeds on
G(n,p)
for
p\geq\frac{(1+\delta)\ln n}{n}
, for any constant
\delta>0
.

Unfortunately, when translating these labeling schemes to the distributed setting, they incur a high cost in communication complexity, as the resulting identifiers are large, i.e., use super-logarithmically many bits.
As most distributed algorithms rely on communicating identifiers as part of their messages, the long identifiers can inflict in the worst-case a
polynomial slow-down
in the simulation of
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithms.
This is because the maximum degree
\Delta
of a random graph from
G(n,p)
is a.a.s.

\Delta=(1+o(1))\,np
if
p\gg\ln n/n
.

However, not all canonical labeling schemes suffer from a high communication complexity in the distributed setting.
Verbitsky and Zhukovskii
[
60
]
gave a succinct labeling scheme that generates identifiers and uses messages of at most logarithmic length, but their results only apply in uniform random graphs.

In contrast to prior work, our new labeling scheme uses messages and generates identifiers with only logarithmic length, and works for a much larger range of
p
. Moreover, the scheme is time-optimal, as it uses only one communication round.
Our work also answers the question recently raised by Gaudio et al.
[
32
]
: “can the assumptions on node identifiability be relaxed – or even removed – for local algorithms on random graphs”? Our work shows that this is possible even in far weaker models of distributed computing than the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, and even with short messages.

Generating unique identifiers with logarithmic communication

2.1

We write

We begin the proof of Theorem

The algorithm depends on an integer parameter

•

Each node

•

Algorithm 1

Generation of unique identifiers

\displaystyle=n^{-\varepsilon C/10}+\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathfrak{D}(s)}\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{\circ}=\mathcal{D}^{\circ}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\neg\mathcal{E}\mid\mathbf{a}=a\right)+o(n^{-10})

\displaystyle=n^{-\varepsilon C/10}+\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathfrak{D}(s)}\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{\circ}=\mathcal{D}^{\circ}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\neg\mathcal{E}\mid\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{\circ}=\mathcal{D}^{\circ}\right)+o(n^{-10})

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\varepsilon C/10}+\mathbb{P}(\neg\mathcal{E})+o(n^{-10})=o(n^{-10}).

The proof of Lemma

A
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}

1.3

The starting point of our approach can be easily explained by considering the particular case of
p=1/2
.
Assume that a node
u
of
G(n,1/2)
was elected as leader.
After receiving a message from
u
, all nodes in
N(u)
become aware that they are leader’s neighbors.
In the next round, every
x\in N(u)
sends its identifier to all
y\in N(x)
.
If a node
y\in N(u)
receives an identifier of some node

, it detects
a triangle
\{x,y,u\}
and informs
u
about this.
In this way, all triangles containing
u
are detected.
The procedure is sound: if
u
does not belong to any triangle, it
spreads the failure notifications throughout the network.

The algorithm finds a triangle asymptotically almost surely because every node
u
in
G(n,1/2)
is contained in a triangle
asymptotically almost surely.
This is no longer true for small

. Indeed, the expected number
of the triangles containing a fixed node of
G(n,p)
is equal to
{n-1\choose 2}\,p^{3}

and, hence, tends to 0 if
p=o(n^{-2/3})
. For this reason, the general case of Theorem
1.3

is far more complicated than the case of the uniformly random graph.
We need a much finer approach, which is presented in the following lemma.

The
eccentricity
of a node

is defined as the maximum distance from
v
to another node
and denoted by
\ecc(v)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and suppose that
p(n)\geq n^{\varepsilon-1}
for all
n
.
Then there exists a positive constant
c=c(\varepsilon)
such that

G(n,p)
has the following property with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{c})
:
for every node
u
there exists a triangle
\{x,y,z\}
and a value
d\in\{\ecc(u)-2,\ecc(u)-3\}
such that

(i)

the distance between
x
and
u
is equal to
d
, and

(ii)

x
is the unique neighbor of
y
at distance
d
from
u
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
p=p(n)\geq n^{\varepsilon-1}
, and
G_{n}\sim G(n,p)
. In what follows, we write
\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)
to denote the set of nodes at distance exactly
r
from a node
u
in
G_{n}
. We will need the following technical assertion.

Claim C.

With probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{\Theta(1)})
, for every node
u
,

(C1)

for every
r\geq 1
, if
|\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)|>\frac{1}{p}
, then
|\mathcal{S}_{r-1}(u)|>\frac{1}{2p(np)}
;

(C2)

if
r=\ecc(u)-2
, then
|\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)|>\frac{1}{2p(np)}
;

(C3)

if
0\leq r=\ecc(u)-\delta
for
\delta\geq 3
, then
|\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)|<\frac{1}{2p(np)^{\delta-3}}
.

Let us prove the lemma based on Claim
3.1
. Let
N_{\leq r}(v)
denote the set of nodes at distance at most
r
from the node
v
. By a ball of radius
r
around
v
we mean the subgraph of
G_{n}
induced on
N_{\leq r}(v)
.

Fix a node
u
. Expose the balls of radius
r
around
u
one by one starting from
r=1
. Let
d\geq 0
be the minimum integer such that
|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|>\frac{1}{2p(np)}
. Claim
3.1
implies that with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{\Theta(1)})

\ecc(u)-3\leq d\leq\ecc(u)-2,

where the first inequality follows from Condition
(C3)
and the second from Condition
(C2)
.
Furthermore, Condition
(C1)
implies that
|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|\leq 1/p
with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{\Theta(1)})
. By the Chernoff bound,
|\mathcal{S}_{d+1}(u)|\leq 0.8n
and, therefore,
|N_{\leq d+1}(u)|\leq 0.9n
with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(n))
. Suppose that we have exposed the ball of radius
d
around
u
. We may assume that

\frac{1}{2p(np)}<|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|\leq\frac{1}{p}.

It then suffices to prove that, with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{\Theta(1)})
, the layer
\mathcal{S}_{d+1}(u)
contains two adjacent nodes
y
and
z
such that
y
has a unique neighbor
x
in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)
and
z
is also adjacent to
x
.

Let
X
denote the complement of
N_{\leq d}(u)
. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
be an arbitrary ordering of the nodes in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)
. For every
i=1,\ldots,m
, expose the neighborhood
N_{i}
of
x_{i}
in
X\setminus N(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}\})
and split it into two subsets
N_{i}^{1}\cup N_{i}^{2}
of the same size, uniformly at random. For every node
y\in N_{i}^{1}
, check whether it has other neighbors in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)
and if not, then check whether it is adjacent to another node in
N_{i}^{2}
. Since at each step of this process, the set
X\setminus N(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}\})
has size
\Theta(n)
, the Chernoff bound ensures that, with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(np))
, each set
N_{i}
has size
\Theta(np)
. Therefore, the node
y
succeeds in fulfilling the above conditions with probability at least
(1-(1-p)^{\Theta(np)})(1-p)^{|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|}
. Then, the probability that all nodes fail in this process is bounded by

\displaystyle\biggl(1-(1-(1-p)^{\Theta(np)})(1-p)^{|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|}\biggr)^{|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|\Theta(np)}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\Theta(|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|\,np)\,(1-e^{-\Theta(np^{2})})\,(1-p)^{1/p}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\Theta(|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|\,np)\,(1-e^{-\Theta(np^{2})})\right).

If
p\geq n^{-1/2}
, then we use the bounds

and

, getting the probability bound
\exp\left(-\Theta(np)\right)
. If
p\leq n^{-1/2}
, then we use the bounds
|\mathcal{S}_{d}(u)|\geq\frac{1}{2p(np)}
and

. This yields the bound
\exp\left(-\Theta((1/p)np^{2})\right)=\exp\left(-\Theta(np)\right)
, completing the proof of the lemma.

Proof of Claim C.

Note first that Condition
(C1)
is immediate when
p(np)=\omega(1)
, i.e., when
p\gg n^{-1/2}
. Otherwise, fix
u
and a non-negative integer
r\leq n
, expose the ball of radius
r-1
around
u
, and assume that
s:=|\mathcal{S}_{r-1}(u)|\leq\frac{1}{2p(np)}
. Then
|\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)|
is stochastically dominated by
\mathrm{Bin}(n,1-(1-p)^{s})
. The last random variable has expectation
(1-(1-p)^{s})\,n\leq\frac{1}{2p}+O(1)
. The Chernoff bound implies that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)|>1/p)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(n,1-(1-p)^{s})>1/p)=\exp(-\Omega(1/p)),

and Condition
(C1)
follows by applying the union bound over

and
r
.

Consider the event that some set of size
k\leq\frac{1}{2p(np)}

.
Its probability is bounded by

\sum_{k\leq\frac{1}{2p(np)}}{n\choose k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(n(1-o(1)),1-(1-p)^{k})<\frac{npk}{2}\right)\leq\sum_{k\leq\frac{1}{2p(np)}}(ne/k)^{k}e^{-\Omega(npk)}=e^{-\Omega(np)}.

Therefore, we may assume that for every node
u
and every

|N_{\leq r}(u)|\leq\frac{1+o(1)}{2p(np)}

By the union bound, the probability that

contains a node adjacent to all other nodes is at most

. Therefore, we may assume that every node
u
has eccentricity
\ecc(u)\geq 2
. Since
\ecc(u)-2\geq 0
, also Condition
(C2)
is immediate when
p\gg n^{-1/2}
. Assume, therefore, that
p=O(n^{-1/2})
. Fix a node
u
and a non-negative integer
r\leq n

r
around
u

s:=|\mathcal{S}_{r}(u)|\leq\frac{1}{2p(np)}
. It suffices to prove that
|\mathcal{S}_{r+1}(u)\cup\mathcal{S}_{r+2}(u)|<0.9n
with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{\Theta(1)})
. Note that
|\mathcal{S}_{r+1}(u)|>1/p
with probability at most

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(n,1-(1-p)^{s})>1/p)=\exp(-\Omega(1/p)).

So, we expose
\mathcal{S}_{r+1}(u)
and assume that

. Now, the probability that
|\mathcal{S}_{r+2}(u)|>0.9n

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(n,1-(1-p)^{s^{\prime}})>0.9n)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(n,1-e^{-1+o(1)})>0.9n)=\exp(-\Omega(n)).

Condition
(C2)
follows by applying the union bound over

.

It remains to prove Condition
(C3)
. Assume first that

for all

\mathcal{S}_{2}(u)

. Therefore, it is enough to consider the case

and

It remains to consider

.

2.

\mathcal{A}

3.2

The limitations of anonymous networks are well known. The unsolvability of many natural problems can
typically be established using techniques based on covering maps (see, e.g.,
[

[

In particular, these results rule out the solvability by

\mathsf{MB}

\mathsf{MB}
-algorithms is often inherently difficult

A homomorphism from a graph

\mathcal{A}
and for every integer

, if
\mathcal{A}
is run on

and

,
then the processors

and

Theorem 3.5

Proof.

The bipartite cover used in the proof of Theorem

(or

In general, a lift of

be their copies in

form a matching between the node sets
\{u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}

and
\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}

of

is obtained by replacing this matching, for each

Note that a map
\alpha:V(F)\rightarrow V(G)

and
i=0,1,\ldots,k
is a covering map from

onto
G
.

The proof idea is the same as in the proof of Theorem

:
we need a lift of

which does not contain Hamiltonian cycles.
It is known
[
63
]
that a random graph

has the following property:
if
k
is large, then almost all lifts of

do contain a Hamiltonian cycle.
This, however, does not make our task complicated.

Indeed, assume that a graph

contains a triangle
\{a,b,c\}
and at least one additional vertex.
Denote the edges of this triangle by
e_{1}=\{b,c\}
,
e_{2}=\{a,c\}
, and
e_{3}=\{a,b\}
.
Consider the disjoint union of four copies
G_{0},G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
of
G
.
Construct a lift

of

as follows:
for each edge
e_{i}=\{x,y\}
, where

, we replace the edges
\{x_{0},y_{0}\}
and
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}

with two new edges
\{x_{0},y_{i}\}
and
\{x_{i},y_{0}\}
, which we will call

.
Thus,

has six twisted edges. Note that any Hamiltonian cycle in

must
contain all six of them. Indeed, for
e_{i}=\{x,y\}
, such a cycle

must contain
the twisted edges

and
\{x_{i},y_{0}\}
because they provide the only

to enter and exit the part

. Note also that each of
the vertices

is incident to two twisted edges; for example,

is incident to
\{a_{0},c_{2}\}
and
\{a_{0},b_{3}\}
. This, however, means
that once

enters the part

, which is only possible at one of the vertices

,
it must exit this part immediately, without visiting any other vertices.
This contradiction shows that

does not have any Hamiltonian cycle.

We are now prepared to prove the theorem. Assume that
\mathcal{A}
is a sound
\mathsf{MB}
-algorithm for
Hamiltonicity
.
A.a.s.,

has the following properties:

•

there exists a triangle
\{a,b,c\}
,

•

there exists a Hamiltonian cycle (see
[
11
, Ch. 8]
for combinatorial background
and
[
58
]
for algorithmic aspects),

•

\mathcal{A}
successfully detects the Hamiltonicity of
G(n,1/2)
, that is,
at least one of the nodes terminates in the accepting state

.

Fix
G
satisfying all three conditions and construct its lift

as described above.
By
Lemma

3.2
, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
terminates on

with at least one node
in state

does not contain any Hamiltonian cycle.
∎

Collapse of average-case
\mathsf{LOCAL}
/
\mathsf{MB}

We now consider anonymous networks with unbounded communication.
We will establish two collapse results:

•

All problems are solvable on

asymptotically almost surely in
\mathsf{MB}
using a small number
of communication rounds. If

•

If the processors know the network size

, all distributed
problems are solvable by sound
\mathsf{SB}
algorithms in just four rounds. Four rounds are optimal in this result.

We prove these collapse theorems in

and
4.4
, respectively.
The proof is based on a close relation between
\mathsf{MB}
model and the color refinement algorithm,
which is explained in
Section

.

4.1
Color Refinement

On an input graph
G
, the color refinement (CR) algorithm iteratively computes a sequence
C_{r}

of colorings of the node set
V(G)
.
The initial coloring is defined by node degrees:
C_{0}(x)=\deg x

for all
x\in V(G)
. For each
r>0
, the coloring
C_{r}
is defined inductively as

C_{r}(x)=\left\{\!\!\left\{C_{r-1}(y)\right\}\!\!\right\}_{y\in N(x)},

(7)

where
\left\{\!\!\left\{\right\}\!\!\right\}
denotes a multiset and
N(x)
is the neighborhood of a node
x
.
A simple induction on
r
shows that

\text{if }C_{r}(x)\neq C_{r}(x^{\prime}),\text{ then }C_{r+1}(x)\neq C_{r+1}(x^{\prime}),

(8)

that is, each subsequent color partition of the node set
V(G)
refines the preceding one.

As immediately seen from (
7
), the coloring
C_{r}
can be computed in
\mathsf{MB}
in
r
rounds:
in each round, the nodes send their neighbors all the information they have obtained so far.
That is, in the first round each node sends its degree, and in each subsequent round,
it sends the multiset of all messages received from its neighbors in the preceding round.
This is an
\mathsf{MB}
analog of a
full-information protocol
,
and we refer to it in the sequel as the
universal
\mathsf{MB}
algorithm
.

Color refinement can be used to characterize
\mathsf{MB}
-equivalent pairs of nodes with the next lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x\in V(G)
and
y\in V(H)
.

1
.

Assume that
C_{r}(x)=C_{r}(y)
.
If an
\mathsf{MB}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is run on
G
and
H
, then nodes
x
and
y

are in the same state up to the
r
-th round.

2
.

If
C_{r}(x)\neq C_{r}(y)
, then there is an
\mathsf{MB}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with the following property:
When
\mathcal{A}
is run on
G
and
H
, then nodes
x
and
y
enter distinct states
in the
r
-th round.

Proof.

1.

We use a close relationship between CR and the concept of universal cover.
Given a node

, let

denote the unfolding of

into a tree starting from

.
Formally,

can be defined as the graph whose nodes are non-backtracking walks in

where two walks are adjacent if one of them is a one-node extension of the other.
The unrooted version

of this tree is called the

of

because

covers every cover
H
of

.
It follows from Lemma

that if

and

are isomorphic as rooted trees,
then

and

are in the same state, whatever
\mathsf{MB}
algorithm is run on
G
and
H
.
This can be stated in a fairly useful, more general form.

For an integer

and a rooted tree

, let

be the truncation of

at depth
r
.
Let
U_{x}=U_{x}(G)
and
W_{y}=U_{y}(H)
and suppose that

and

are isomorphic rooted trees.
Even this weaker assumption ensures that if an
\mathsf{MB}

\mathcal{A}
is run on

and

then the processors

and

are in the same state up to the

It remains to note that

.
A proof of this well-known equivalence can be found, e.g., in
[

.

2.

The second claim follows by considering the universal
\mathsf{MB}
algorithm.
∎

If the
C_{r}
colors of all nodes
in a graph

are pairwise distinct, then they can be used to present

in a canonical form.
The approach succeeds a.a.s. on the uniformly random graph
G=G(n,1/2)
already for

, by the result of Babai, Erdős, and Selkow
[
6
]
. Their algorithm works as follows:

•

Set
m=\lceil 3\log_{2}n\rceil
and consider the set

consisting of the nodes with the

largest degrees in
G
.
It is known that the
m
largest degrees are a.a.s. unique, i.e.,
|U|=m

[

]
.

•

For each node

, compute its label as the bit string of length

consisting
of the adjacencies of

to the nodes in the set
U
.

As proven in
[
6
]
, these strings are a.a.s. pairwise distinct.

This canonical labeling scheme can be straightforwardly translated into a two-round
\mathsf{SB}
algorithm that asymptotically almost surely generates unique identifiers for all nodes.
In the first round, each node
v
sends its degree to all neighbors. After the message exchange,

v
knows the set of the degrees of all of its neighbors.
In the second round,
v
sends the
m
largest degrees it has heard about to its neighbors.
Since the diameter of

is a.a.s. equal to 2, every processor learns
the set

and, hence, is able to compute its identifier.
Note that while the identifiers have logarithmic size, the algorithm requires sending
messages of superlogarithmic length
\Omega(\log^{2}n)
.

Another
\mathsf{SB}
implementation of the Babai-Erdős-Selkow algorithm works in just one round,
requires sending messages of only logarithmic size, and produces identifiers of size
\Theta(\log^{2}n)
.
Having received the set of their neighbor degrees, each node

chooses the
m
largest elements
of this set and outputs its ordered list as its identifier

. These identifiers are pairwise distinct a.a.s.
because, as easily seen,
\id(v)\neq\id(v^{\prime})
whenever

and

have distinct adjacency patterns to

.
Along with part 1 of Lemma

, this has the following consequence, which we state for further reference.

Proposition 4.2

(Babai, Erdős, and Selkow
[
6
]
)
.

If CR is run on

, then a.a.s.
C_{1}(v)\neq C_{1}(v^{\prime})
for all
v\neq v^{\prime}
.

Subsequent work on CR on

is summarized in
Table

1
in
Section

.
Below, we use the following result from

]

.

Collapse of the time hierarchy for

4.3
Gathering all information a.a.s. in
\mathsf{SB}[4]

In the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, all nodes can learn the identifiers and structure of the graph
G
in
\diam(G)+1
rounds. Let
G_{v}
denote the rooted version
of
G
with
v
designated as root. Thus, by gathering the entire graph
G
, there is a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm that
allows each processor
v
to determine the isomorphism type of
G_{v}
in
\diam(G)+1
rounds.

Clearly, a similar result – in its general form – is impossible in the
\mathsf{MB}
model,
even in the average-case setting. Moreover, identifying the network graph
G
up to isomorphism
is impossible in
\mathsf{MB}
for any fixed
G
, because
G
is indistinguishable from any of its lift;
cf. the proofs of Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
.
However, if nodes know the size of
G
, then this task becomes possible a.a.s. even in the weaker
\mathsf{SB}
model.
This observation was at the core of the proof of
Theorem

1.5
. We now consider this issue in the case of the uniformly random graph
G(n,1/2)
in greater detail.

For a graph
G
, let

S_{r}(G)=\left\{C_{r}(v)\right\}_{v\in V(G)}

be the set of the node colors produced by the color refinement algorithm in the
r
-th round.
This is an isomorphism invariant of a graph, which is important for us by the following reason.
Assume that
S_{r}(G)\cap S_{r}(H)=\emptyset
for every non-isomorphic graph
H
with the same number of nodes.
By part 2 of Lemma
4.1
, this means that in the model
\mathsf{MB}
, all processors
can identify the isomorphism type of
G
in
r
rounds if they know the size of the network.
The proof of part 1 of Theorem
4.3
below shows that this works for
G=G(n,1/2)

asymptotically almost surely with

even in the
\mathsf{SB}
model.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G=G(n,1/2)
. Asymptotically almost surely,

1
.

S_{4}(G)\cap S_{4}(H)=\emptyset
for every
n
-node graph
H\not\cong G
;

2
.

S_{3}(G)\cap S_{3}(H)\neq\emptyset
for some
n
-node graph
H\not\cong G
;

3
.

S_{2}(G)\neq S_{2}(H)
for every
H\not\cong G
.

Theorem
4.3
shows how the graph invariant
S_{r}(G)
evolves in terms of its ability to identify
the random graph
G\sim G(n,1/2)
.
After two refinement rounds, the
entire
set
S_{r}(G)
suffices to identify the graph
G
.
Nevertheless, there exists a node whose individual color is still insufficient to identify
G
even after three rounds.
This changes after one more refinement: after four rounds, the color of any single node already identifies
G
.
To complete the picture, note that the number of rounds

in part 3 of the theorem is optimal.
Indeed, as shown in
[
54
, Lemma 6.2]
, a.a.s. there exists

.

We also remark that

is an average-case analog of the following worst-case result

]

. Note that

and

in

unless these graphs are CR-indistinguishable,

4.4
Computing everything in
\mathsf{SB}_{n\text{-}\mathrm{snd}}[4]

Recall that

5
Conclusions

We showed that unique identifiers can be generated on
G(n,p)
asymptotically almost surely
in
\mathsf{SB}^{*}[1]
whenever
n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2
. It is natural to ask
whether the range of
p
in this result can be made even closer to the connectivity threshold.
In a somewhat weaker form, the question is if unique identifiers can be generated in

\mathsf{MB}^{*}[O(1)]
whenever
(1+\delta)\ln n/n\leq p\leq 1/2
. We can prove that this is
possible in
\mathsf{MB}
within
O(\diam(G))
rounds. Whether this can be done in a constant time
seems to be a challenging question.

Generally, the average-case complexity of distributed problems on
G(n,p)

deserves further exploration. As we already mentioned in
Section

1
,
the Hamiltonian cycle problem has attracted a considerable attention

[
48
,
19
,
58
]
.
In
Section

3
, we addressed the triangle finding problem.
It is natural to consider also other problems in the average-case setting.
For example, let
p
be such that
\mathbb{P}(\diam(G(n,p))\leq 2)=1/2
. Note that
p=(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\ln n/n}
; see
[
9
]
.
Is there a
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm determining the diameter of
G\sim G(n,p)

asymptotically almost surely in a constant number of rounds?
Is there such
sound
algorithm?
Note that in the worst case, distinguishing graphs of diameter 2 from those of diameter 3 requires
\Omega(n)
rounds, even for randomized algorithms
[
39
]
.

While triangle finding is known to have complexity
\Omega(\log\log n)
in the worst-case
[
4
]
,
we showed that triangles can be found a.a.s. in constant-time on
G(n,p)
when
n^{\varepsilon-1}\leq p\leq 1/2
.
For many other natural subgraphs, such as larger cliques and longer cycles, the detection problem has polynomial-in-
n
worst-case lower bounds; see e.g.
[
25
,
43
,
23
,
15
,
31
]
.
Can some other hard-to-detect subgraphs still be found fast on
G(n,p)
in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model?

In
Section

4
, we proved that anonymous sound algorithms are very
powerful in the non-uniform setting, when the processors know the network size.
Specifically, all distributed problems solvable in
\mathsf{LOCAL}
can be solved in this model in just four rounds. This result assumes that the
message size is unbounded. An intriguing question is how powerful (or how weak)
are non-uniform sound algorithms in the
\mathsf{MB}^{*}
and
\mathsf{SB}^{*}
models
with logarithmically bounded communication.

Appendix A
Glossary of distributed computing models

In
Table

2
,
we give a concise glossary of the different models of computing discussed in this work.
We refer the reader to the book of Hirvonen and Suomela
[
38

for the formal definitions of the
\mathsf{PN}
,
\mathsf{LOCAL}
and
\mathsf{CONGEST}
models. The paper of Hella et al.
[
37
]
gives formal definitions for the weak models
\mathsf{SB}
,
\mathsf{MB}
,
\mathsf{SV}
,
\mathsf{MV}
, and
\mathsf{VV}
of computing; in this work, we only consider the first two models.

In the
\mathsf{PN}
model, each node
v\in V
has ports
1,\ldots,\deg(v)
. In each round, each node
v
sends (distinct) messages to each of its
\deg(v)
ports, receives a message from each of its ports,
and updates its state based on the vector of
\deg(v)
received messages in the round.
That is, the state transition can depend on the current state of a node and the vector of received messages.
The
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model is simply the port-numbering model
\mathsf{PN}
with unique
O(\log n)
-bit identifiers given as local input to all nodes. The
\mathsf{CONGEST}
is the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model with the additional restriction that messages have length at most
O(\log n)
bits. That is,
following our notational convention,
\mathsf{CONGEST}=\mathsf{LOCAL}^{*}
.

The weak models can be obtained as restrictions of the
\mathsf{PN}
model; see Hella et al.
[
37
]
for the formal details.
If the neighbors of node
v
send the messages
m_{1},\ldots,m_{\deg(v)}
to
v
in round
r
, then in
\mathsf{SB}
the state of node
v
at the start of round

is a function of its current state and the set

\{m_{i}\,:\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}1\leq i\leq\deg(v)\end{array}\}.

In the
\mathsf{MB}
model, the new state of node
v
is a function of its current state and the multiset

\{\!\!\{m_{i}\,:\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}1\leq i\leq\deg(v)\end{array}\}\!\!\}.

Note that the assumption that nodes know their degree is necessary in the
\mathsf{SB}
model.
Otherwise, without any inputs, only constant functions could be computed.
In the
\mathsf{MB}
model, this assumption could be lifted with the overhead of one additional communication round, as nodes can learn their degree using one round of communication (by counting how many messages they received).

Appendix B
A naïve algorithm for
Triangle Finding
in
\mathsf{CONGEST}

We make note of a natural algorithm idea that one could expect to find a triangle in
G(n,p)
:

•

Each node
x
sends its identifier
\id(x)
to all its neighbors. As a result, after one round, every node knows its neighborhood.

•

Having received the neighbor identifiers, each node
x
chooses the smallest two,
\id(y)
and
\id(z)
,
and sends the triple
(\id y,\id z,\id x)
.

•

Having received the message
(\id y,\id z,\id x)
, a node
y
checks if it is adjacent to
z
.
If so, then
\{x,y,z\}
is a triangle, and
y
outputs the triple
(\id y,\id z,\id x)
.

Recall that the node set of
G(n,p)
is
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Assume that
\id(x)=x
for all
x\in V
.
In this case, the above naïve triangle finding algorithm succeeds on
G(n,1/2)
with probability
1-n^{-\Omega(\log n)}
.
This still does not mean that this algorithm solves
Triangle Finding
in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
asymptotically almost surely.
Indeed, on almost all
G
the algorithm has to succeed for
all
identifier assignments,
in particular, for all
\id\in S_{n}
, where
S_{n}=\mathrm{Sym}(V)
is the symmetric group of all
permutations of
V
. It turns out that this is not the case. Below, we outline a proof for
a constant edge probability
p\in(0,1)
. This is likely true also if
p=o(1)
, but this case
requires much more intricate analysis.

A set
I\subset V
is called
2-dominating independent
set in a graph
if it is independent and every node from
V\setminus I
has at least two neighbors in
I
.

Claim D.

Let
p\in(0,1)
be a constant. Set
k=\lfloor 1.5\log_{1/(1-p)}n\rfloor
. Then a.a.s.
G\sim G(n,p)

contains a 2-dominating independent set of size
k
.

Proof sketch.

Let
X_{k}
be the number of independent sets
I
of size
k
in
G
such that every node that does not belong to
I
has at least two neighbors in
I
. It suffices to show that a.a.s.
X_{k}\geq 1
. To this end, we will prove
that
\mathbb{E}X_{k}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=0)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}X_{k}}{(\mathbb{E}X_{k})^{2}}.

The inequality
X_{k}\geq 1
will then follow from the equality
\mathbb{E}X_{k}(X_{k}-1)=o((\mathbb{E}X_{k})^{2})
.
The proof of the last fact is standard — see the proofs of similar facts (with almost verbatim computations)
in
[
26
,
51
,
42
]
—
but requires dense computations. Therefore, we omit it.

A fixed set
I
of size
k
is 2-dominating independent with probability

(1-p)^{{k\choose 2}}\left(1-(1-p)^{k}-kp(1-p)^{k-1}\right)^{n-k}=(1-p)^{{k\choose 2}}\left(1-n^{-3/2+o(1)}\right)^{n-k}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}X_{k}={n\choose k}(1-p)^{{k\choose 2}}\left(1-n^{-3/2+o(1)}\right)^{n-k}=\exp\left(k\ln n-k\ln k+k-{k\choose 2}\ln\frac{1}{1-p}+O(\ln\ln n)\right).

Since

\ln n-1+\ln k-\frac{k-1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{1-p}=\left(\frac{1}{4}+o(1)\right)\ln n,

we conclude that
\mathbb{E}X_{k}\to\infty
with
n
increasing, as desired.
∎

Let
I
be a 2-dominating independent set of size
k
in an input graph
G
,
which exists a.a.s. for
G\sim G(n,p)
by Claim
B
.
Consider
\id\in S_{n}
mapping
I
to
\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Then, for every
x\in V
, its two neighbors
with smallest identifiers belong to
I
and, hence, are not adjacent. Therefore, the algorithm does not find any triangle.

The

We here give a simple example showing that the class of problems
\mathsf{LOCAL}_{\mathrm{snd}}[O(1)]

is strictly larger than
\mathsf{LOCAL}[O(1)]
, and the same holds true as well for
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}_{\mathrm{snd}}[O(1)]

and
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}[O(1)]
. Both separations follow from the fact that

\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}_{\mathrm{snd}}[O(1)]\nsubseteq\mathsf{LOCAL}[O(1)].

(14)

To prove (
14
), we consider a natural problem,
which is a special version of
Leader Election
, namely:

Unique MaxDegree
—
If a processor
u
is a unique node in
G
with maximum degree, i.e.,
\deg(v)<\deg(u)

for all other
v\in V(G)
, then
u
must terminate in state
yes
.
If this is not the case,
u
must terminate in state
no
.

Note that the random graph
G(n,1/2)
contains a unique node of maximum degree a.a.s.
[
10
, Theorem 3.9]
.

Claim E.

Unique MaxDegree
is in
\mathsf{B\mbox{-}CONGEST}_{\mathrm{snd}}[9]
.

Proof of Claim E.

To show this, consider the following algorithm. For a node
v
, let
N[v]
denote the

v
, i.e.,
N[v]=N(v)\cup\{v\}
.
The case of a single processor is trivial, and we assume that the algorithm is run
on a connected graph
G
with at least two nodes.

1st round.
Node

sends the pair
(\deg v,\id v)
to the neighbors. After the message exchange,

v
computes the maximum node degree
D(v)=\max_{w\in N[v]}\deg(w)

of

2nd round.
Node

sends the message

(D(v),M(v),\id w)&\text{if }M(v)=1,\text{ where }w\in N[v]\text{ is such that }D(v)=\deg w.\end{cases}

A node

3rd round.
Every

For convenience, we suppose that

also receives this message (from itself).
Note that a node can receive such a message from at most one of the neighbors.

4th round.
Every node
v

The following action is repeated: All

Once a node receives this message, it becomes

After the message exchange is completed, all nodes enter their final states.
