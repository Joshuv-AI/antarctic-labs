A pilot study of the effect of 3D-Schwann-like cells derived from human adipose-derived stem cells on surgery-induced neurogenic bladder
Authors: Yukako Takehara, Tetsuya Ikemoto, Yuhei Waki, Takuya Tokunaga, Katsuki Miyazaki, Hiroki Teraoku, Shinichiro Yamada, Yu Saito, Yuji Morine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral nerve injury (PNI) is often associated with trauma and can cause various symptoms that decrease the quality of a patient’s life. However, PNI treatment is an unmet medical need. Nerve regeneration is a focus of PNI treatment and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) have recently garnered significant attention because of their multipotency and ease of production, and because they can differentiate into Schwann-like cells (SLCs) in vitro. We previously reported the production of three-dimensional (3D) SLCs derived from ADSCs and their potential for PNI treatment. The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of 3D SLCs on PNI in vitro and in vivo. First, the effect of 3D SLCs in an in vitro nerve injury model was investigated. The nerve injury model resulted in axon shortening; however, axon length was enhanced by co-culture with 3D SLCs. Next, we established bilateral pelvic nerve cut model rats and confirmed the development of neurogenic bladder. Transplantation of 3D SLCs at the nerve transection site resulted in functional and histological improvement of neurogenic bladder. Our findings indicate that 3D SLCs have therapeutic potential for PNI, especially neurogenic bladder caused by surgical trauma.Keywords: Schwann-like cells, Adipose-derived stem cells, Peripheral nerve injury, Regenerative medicine, Neurogenic bladderSubject terms: Medical research, Neuroscience, Stem cells

Introduction

The annual worldwide incidence of peripheral nerve injury (PNI) is over one million1. PNI is caused by loss of structure or function of peripheral nerves as a result of trauma, disease, or surgery2,3. In gastrointestinal surgery, injury to the pelvic autonomic plexus can be caused by retraction or high-frequency electrotome burns, which can result in urinary dysfunction4. Such PNI can decrease a patient’s quality of life. PNI treatment options are limited but include direct anastomosis, and when this is not possible, autologous nerve grafting is considered5,6. However, autografting has several disadvantages, including a limited supply of nerve tissue, mismatched nerve diameter, donor site morbidity, and the risk of tumor formation7,8. Furthermore, reconstruction of fine nerves, such as the pelvic nerve, is technically difficult. Therefore, nerve repair using regenerative medicine techniques is being intensively explored. After peripheral nerve damage, Schwann cells (SCs) promote axon repair through a variety of epigenetic and molecular mechanisms9–11. Numerous studies have focused on the effects of SCs on nerve regeneration. Moreover, autologous human SCs have been transplanted to treat PNI and spinal cord injury12,13. However, it is impossible to avoid damaging healthy nerves when harvesting autologous SCs and ethical concerns need to be considered.

We previously reported a protocol using RCP µ-piece to produce three-dimensional (3D) Schwann-like cells (SLCs) from adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) with the potential for peripheral nerve regeneration14,15. Briefly, 3D SLCs were superior to conventional SLCs in their ability to secrete neuronal growth factors and to express SC markers. Furthermore, our 3D SLCs showed better therapeutic potential than ADSCs in vitro and in vivo; our 3D SLCs promoted motor function and structural recovery in a rodent sciatic nerve injury model. Urinary disorder is caused by injury to the pelvic autonomic nerve16, which is much thinner than the sciatic nerve. Therefore, the potential of 3D SLCs for regeneration of such a thin automatic nerve was unknown. Here, we report in vitro and in vivo effects of SLCs on pelvic nerve injury.

Results

Effects of SLCs on nerve cell injury in vitro

First, we established an in vitro injury model by treating SH-SY5Y cells with semaphorin for 2 h to generate injured nerve cells (iNCs) (Fig. 1). The representative image was shown in Fig. 2a. One-way ANOVA showed a significant effect of treatment on axon length (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.92). Control and nerve injury model cells (iNCs) were immunostained for S100β. iNCs administered semaphorin showed significantly shorter axons than the control group (control group 25.40 ± 0.25 mm vs. iNC group 16.76 ± 2.54 mm; 95% CI of difference [3.86, 13.46], p = 0.002, Fig. 2b). However, the axon length of iNCs co-cultured with SLCs was similar to that of the control group (control group 25.40 ± 0.25 mm vs. iNC + 3D-SLC group 28.70 ± 2.43 mm; 95% CI of difference [− 1.52, 8.03], p = 0.210, Fig. 2b).

Transplantation of SLCs into a rat dysuria model improved bladder contraction

We established a rodent neurogenic bladder model by transecting the bilateral pelvic nerves. Rats that underwent bilateral pelvic nerve cut (BPNC) constituted the BPNC group. Rats in which 3D SLCs were transplanted at the nerve injury site constituted the SLCTx group. Rats whose pelvic nerves were not transected but rinsed with PBS only constituted the Sham group. The schema was shown in Fig. 3a and b.

Macroscopically, the bladder was more dilated and the wall was thinner in the BPNC group than in the Sham group. The bladder in the SLCTx group was similar to that of the Sham group (Fig. 3c). The BPNC group showed significantly lower maximal intravesical pressure (MVP) than the Sham group (Sham group 34.92 ± 10.18 mmHg vs. BPNC group 17.61 ± 2.57 mmHg; 95% CI of difference [2.93, 31.67], p = 0.021, Fig. 3d). However, the MVP of the SLCTx group was significantly improved (BPNC group 17.61 ± 2.57 mmHg vs. SLCTx group = 41.19 ± 6.74 mmHg; 95% CI of difference [10.95, 31.19] p = 0.001, Fig. 3d). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.76).

Histological investigation

Model rats were euthanized on post-operative day 14 and bladder specimens were investigated histologically. The thickness of the bladder wall muscle layer in the BPNC group was significantly greater than that of the control group (arrows in Fig. 4a) (Sham group: 545.91 ± 125.00 µm vs. BPNC group: 952.10 ± 229.79 µm; 95% CI of difference [81.25, 731.14] p = 0.014, Fig. 4b). However, the muscle layer thickness in the SLCTx group was significantly decreased compared with that of the BPNC group (SLCTx group: 668.40 ± 210.73 µm vs. BPNC group: 952.10 ± 229.79 µm; 95% CI of difference [18.38, 549.02] p = 0.035, Fig. 4b). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (p = 0.009, η2 = 0.43). Masson’s trichrome staining was then performed to determine collagen fiber abundance. It was not significantly increased in the BPNC group (Sham group: 27.47 ± 3.91% vs. BPNC group: 32.58 ± 1.93%; 95% CI of difference [− 2.80, 12.99], p = 0.217) but was significantly decreased in the SLCTx group (SLCTx group: 25.22 ± 3.71% vs. BPNC group: 32.58 ± 1.93%; 95% CI of difference [1.22, 13.45], p = 0.022, Fig. 5a,b). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (p = 0.03, η2 = 0.60). In addition, the S100β-positive area (arrows in Fig. 5a) was significantly increased in the SLCTx group compared with the BPNC group (SLCTx group: 2.27 ± 0.79% vs. BPNC group: 1.12 ± 0.68; 95% CI of difference [0.19, 2.10], p = 0.017, Fig. 6a,b). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (p = 0.01, η2 = 0.36).

Discussion

In this study, we established in vitro and in vivo nerve injury models of urinary dysfunction and investigated the effects of 3D SLCs. BPNC model rats also showed increased collagen abundance in the bladder wall and reduced MVP. Transplantation of 3D SLCs into BPNC model rats significantly improved MVP and reduced bladder wall collagen abundance.

We previously reported that our established 3D SLCs may have the potential to accelerate peripheral nerve regeneration14,15. These studies mainly focused on motor function; the present study extends our characterization of 3D SLCs to the regeneration of autonomic nerve injury. Our results indicate that 3D SLCs might be effective for regeneration after autonomic nerve injury and that 3D SLC transplantation might have potential to treat surgical PNI. In addition, we have previously reported that 3D SLCs have a greater capacity to secrete nerve growth factor and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor than conventional SLCs and ADSCs15. Mesenchymal stem cells and ADSCs can be effective for PNI repair17–20. However, SLCs derived from ADSCs or exosomes derived from SLCs are more efficient than stem cells21–23. This is because SLCs derived from ADSCs secrete more growth factors and neurotrophic factors than ADSCs. In addition, ADSCs have the potential to be tumorigenic24,25. Exosomes from SLCs are usually effective at promoting nerve regeneration; therefore, studies to compare the efficacy of cell transplantation and SLC-derived exosome administration are warranted.

The therapeutic effect on PNI of Bio 3D-printed nerve conduits has been reported26–28. This technology was more effective than conventional methods and has already been applied in clinical trials29. However, studies performed to date mainly confirmed the therapeutic effect on thick nerve fibers, such as the sciatic nerve. In this study, we targeted the pelvic plexus nerve, which is much thinner than the sciatic nerve. The conduit technique requires measurable nerve thickness, and we consider that implantation of SLCs might be more suitable than conduits for the thin nerves surrounding the bladder.

This study has several limitations. First, the in vivo experiments were conducted with a limited sample size. To evaluate the statistical reliability of our findings, we performed a post-hoc power analysis, which yielded values of 0.98 for MVP, 0,69 for bladder wall muscle thickness, and 0.78 for Masson’s trichrome staining. While some parameters demonstrated high power, the small cohort size inherently increases the risk of type I and type II errors. As this is a pilot study, these results should be interpreted as preliminary, and further validation with larger cohorts is essential. Moreover, this study lacked comparison groups treated with ADSCs alone, 2D SLCs, or vehicle at the lesion site. Consequently, the incremental benefits of 3D SLCs over these more conventional cell therapies could not be fully determined. In addition, since the cells used in this study were human-derived (xenotransplantation), the underlying mechanisms in a rat model may differ from those in human clinical applications. Second, the observation period was limited to two weeks post-transplantation. Therefore, the long-term fate, survival, and sustained efficacy of the transplanted SLCs remain unclear. Previously it was reported that mature cells derived from ADSCs have a low risk of carcinogenicity30, but the safety was not investigated in this study. It is possible that the therapeutic effects observed in the acute phase may diminish over time; thus, the durability of this treatment requires further investigation. Furthermore, this study did not identify the precise mechanisms underlying peripheral nerve regeneration. Future studies employing cell-tracking techniques in transplanted animals will be crucial to clarify the fate of SLCs and their specific contributions to the regenerative process.

Conclusions

3D SLCs improved axon length in an in vitro nerve injury model. Additionally, 3D SLC transplantation improved MVP and reduced the amount of collagen in the bladder wall of a surgically induced urinary bladder model. Therefore, 3D SLCs might have therapeutic potential for bladder dysfunction caused by surgical trauma.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and differentiation of 3D SLCs from human ADSCs

Human ADSCs were purchased from Invitrogen (Grand Island, NY, USA) and cultured in ADSC basal medium supplemented with Growth Supplement (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and GlutaMAX-I (Gibco). The SH-SY5Y cell line was purchased from ATCC (CRL-2266, Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 15% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For differentiation of ADSCs into 3D SLCs, ADSCs were cultured for three to five passages and then subjected to our previously described differentiation method14,15. Subsequently, 2 × 104 ADSCs/well were mixed with 0.2 mg/ml RCP µ-piece (16,629,004, FUJI FILM, Tokyo, Japan) and seeded in ultra-low-attachment 96-well plates (174,925, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After approximately 24 h, spheroid formation was observed and induced differentiation was initiated using our modified protocol with folic acid. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

In vitro nerve cell injury model

We established an in vitro nerve cell injury model based on our previous study15. SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into a 6-well plate (2 × 104cells/well) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. To establish the in vitro nerve injury model, cells were treated with 0.1 µg/ml semaphorin (#ab233670, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was for 2 h. Following this treatment, the medium was replaced with DMEM/F12 containing 15% fetal bovine serum. In this study, these semaphorin-exposed cells are explicitly defined as injured nerve cells (iNCs). Additionally, 3D SLCs were co-cultured with iNCs using a cell culture insert (#353,090, CORNING, NY, USA) for 48 h at 37 °C. For the control group, SH-SY5Y cells were cultured alone (Fig. 1). Three samples were prepared for each group, n = 3.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells on slides were washed twice with PBS, and fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (163–20,145, FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan) at 4 °C for 30 min. Permeabilization was performed with 0.1% Triton X-100 (HFH10, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min at room temperature. Next, slides were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS, prepared from MACS BSA Stock Solution (130-091-376, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were then incubated with an anti-S100β antibody (#90,393, 1:800, Cell Signaling Technology) at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times with PBS, slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (A-11008, 1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. After three PBS washes, performed in the dark to protect fluorescence, cells were stained with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (P36931, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or 10 min in the dark. Finally, cells were examined and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X700 KEYENCE, Tokyo, Japan). The primary antibody was replaced with BSA for negative controls. According to our previous report15, Image J 1.54 g software (National Institute of Mental Health, Bethesda, MD; https://imagej.net/ij/) with the NeurphologyJ plugin was used to analyze the immunofluorescence images and to calculate the number of branching neurites and the lengths of the neurites of each neuron. The results are expressed as the relative length in the experimental group compared with the control group.

Urinary dysfunction rat model

Eight-week-old female rats (NBRP Rat #0883, F344-Il2rgem1Iexas) were supplied by Tokyo University and bred at the animal facility of Tokushima University. The following series of animal experiments were conducted with all rats under anesthetized conditions with 2–3% isoflurane. All rats were made the abdominal midline incision under anesthesia, and the bilateral pelvic nerves were identified as shown in Fig. 2a.

Rats were randomly assigned to three groups:

Sham group (n = 4): Following a midline incision, PBS was applied to the bilateral pelvic nerves without transection.

BPNC group (n = 3): Bilateral pelvic nerves were identified and transected, followed by abdominal closure without cell transplantation.

SLCTx group (n = 5): After bilateral pelvic nerve transection, 5 × 106 3D SLCs were transplanted directly to the injury site.

All of the experiments and procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tokushima and performed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (approved number: T2024-105). We also confirmed that all animal experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (i.e. the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals).

Cystometry

Fourteen days after surgery, rats were anesthetized and a lower abdominal midline incision was made. The bladder was exposed, and a 50-PE tube was inserted (#01631-05, Nihon Bioresearch, Gifu, Japan). The tube was passed subcutaneously and brought out through the dorsal region of the body. Then, the 50-PE tube was connected to a pressure transducer (ADU-800, Star Medical, Tokyo, Japan). Saline was continuously infused into the bladder at a rate of 5 mL/h. Cystometry measurements were performed in awake animals to avoid the effects of anesthesia. Cystometry and MVP were analyzed using Eight Star software (Star Medical). The MVP was measured at least five times for each rat, and the average was calculated.

Histological analysis

CO2 euthanasia was performed according to the AVMA Guidelines. CO2 was introduced at a rate of approximately 30% of the chamber volume per minute. After respiratory arrest was observed, the CO2 flow was maintained for an additional minute to ensure deep anesthesia. Death was then formally confirmed by organ procurement as a secondary physical method. Then, the bladder was fixed in 10% formalin (Wako 066-03847), and embedded in paraffin. Tissue blocks were then sectioned at 4-µm thickness. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and the thickness of muscle layer measured. Additionally, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed and the amount of collagen fiber calculated using Image J 1.54 g software and the equation: collagen fiber area / bladder wall area × 100.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously reported31 to assess S100β, a Schwann cell marker. The primary antibody used was rabbit monoclonal anti-S100β (#90393, 1:800, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) and the secondary antibody was Anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (A-11008; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The S100β-positive area ratio was measured using ImageJ 1.54 g software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro software (version 17.0.0; SAS Campus Drive, NC, USA). For comparisons among three or more groups at a single endpoint, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. To account for multiple comparisons, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was applied to determine specific differences between groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
