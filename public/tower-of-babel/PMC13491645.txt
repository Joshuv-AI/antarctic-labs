Psychological distress and dyadic trust in Italian women awaiting colposcopy after positive cervical cancer screening: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Federica Bevilacqua, Eleonora Robba, Paola Berchialla, Mario Preti, Francesca Malandrone, Benedetta Pollano, Ilaria Barbierato, Zahra Bami, Niccolò Gallio, Luca Marozio, Luca Ostacoli, Sara Carletto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScreening programs have substantially reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality, yet abnormal results can generate considerable psychological distress even before a diagnosis is confirmed. Interpersonal resources such as dyadic trust—the expectation of reliability and support within intimate relationships—may buffer distress, but their role in the colposcopy context is understudied. Examining both individual and relational predictors of emotional well-being may inform targeted psychosocial interventions and improve patient-centered care in cervical cancer screening.ObjectivesTo estimate the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress among Italian women referred for their first colposcopy after a positive cervical cancer screening result, and to identify associated sociodemographic, clinical, and interpersonal factors, with particular focus on dyadic trust.Materials and methodsThis cross-sectional observational study was conducted from June 2022 to March 2024 at the Lower Genital Tract Clinic of the University of Torino, Italy. Women undergoing their first colposcopy after a positive cytology and/or human papillomavirus (HPV) DNA test within the “Prevenzione Serena” screening program completed three questionnaires at their appointment: a sociodemographic and clinical form, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21), and the Dyadic Trust Scale (DTS). Multivariable logistic regression models assessed associations between participant characteristics and psychological outcomes. Exploratory nomograms were developed to estimate individual risk probabilities; these were not internally validated and should not be used for clinical prediction.ResultsA total of 573 women participated (median age 36 years). Most were single (61%), university-educated (53%), employed (82%), and HPV-unvaccinated (83%). Mild to extremely severe symptoms of stress, depression, and anxiety were reported by 70%, 41%, and 31% of respondents, respectively. In multivariable analysis, higher dyadic trust was associated with lower risk of depression (OR 0.66, 95%CI 0.51–0.85), anxiety (OR 0.53, 95%CI 0.31–0.91) and stress (OR 0.46, 95%CI 0.25–0.85). Lower educational level was associated with higher risk of depression (OR 1.64, 95%CI 1.02–2.64), as HPV vaccination (OR 1.87, 95%CI 1.02–3.41). Younger age was associated with higher risk of anxiety (OR 1.88, 95%CI 1.25–2.86) and stress (OR 4, 95%CI 1.92–8.33) for 16-year increase. Exploratory nomograms allowed individualized risk estimation.ConclusionsPsychological distress—particularly stress—is common among women awaiting colposcopy after a positive screening result. Younger age, lower education, HPV vaccination status, and lower dyadic trust identify those at elevated risk. Integrating risk-stratified psychosocial support, enhancing relational coping, and tailoring communication may improve emotional outcomes. Background

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is among the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections worldwide, with an estimated global prevalence of around 12% [1]. Its incidence is particularly high among women between 15 and 25 years old [2, 3] however, it is estimated that nearly 80% of the sexually active population acquires an infection with one or more HPV strains at least once in their lifetime [3, 4] and it is reported a new infection rate of 14 million persons per year [5]. In most cases, the immune system effectively responds to the virus, resulting in the clearance of the infection within two years [3], however in case of immunodeficiency, co-infections and smoking, HPV can persist thus increasing the risk of developing pre-invasive and invasive diseases of the lower genital tract of men and women. HPV is responsible for about 5% of all carcinomas globally (vagina, vulva, anus, penis, oropharynx), but the strongest correlation exists with cervical cancer [2], the fourth most diagnosed cancer worldwide and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [6, 7].

Organized screening programs have played a crucial role in significantly reducing both incidence and mortality of cervical cancer over the last three decades. In Italy, the mortality rate among women under 50 has dropped by approximately 70% over the past 35 years, with a steady decline of 2.1% per year over the past 20 years [8]. This was achieved through the implementation of screening methods, such as HPV DNA testing and cytology, which enable the detection of precancerous lesions and early-stage invasive cancers, allowing for timely and effective treatment.

Piedmont was among the first regions in Italy to implement an organized cervical cancer screening program, starting in 1992. This program, which reflects the structure adopted at the national level wherever organized screening is in place, is organized into two levels.

At the first level women undergo a primary screening test (cytology from 25 to 29 years if HPV unvaccinated or HPV DNA test from 30 years, if vaccinated). If the screening test is negative, cytology is repeated after 3 years while HPV DNA testing after 5 years. When cytology indicates abnormalities, or a HPV test is persistently positive for two consecutive years even with a normal pap smearwomen are referred to colposcopy, the second level of the screening, where experienced gynecologists assess the need for biopsies or treatment.

The psychological impact of a positive Pap test or HPV test result is often overlooked, with limited attention given to the emotional distress it may cause patients. Additionally, healthcare providers are frequently unaware of the extent of these psychological effects. Heightened anxiety levels are observed not only during colposcopy examinations, but also when thepatient is informed of a positive screening result [9, 10]. Receiving the results can trigger significant emotional distress, leading to adverse psychosocial reactions. Compared to individuals who test negative for HPV, women with a positive HPV DNA result experience significantly poorer psychological well-being [11]. They often face heightened anxiety about the clinical implications of their condition, including fears of neoplastic progression, potential treatment outcomes, and the long-term impact on their health and reproductive future [9–11].

Patients’ concerns go beyond their own health and cancer risk, often extending to their partners—both past and present. They may experience anxiety about the implications for their relationships, uncertainty regarding disease transmission, and distress over potential perceptions of their sexual history [10].

With these premises, this study aims to examine women’s psychological and emotional responses when receiving a positive result after initial screening.

Methods

Design and participants

This observational study was conducted between June 2022 and March 2024.

Women enrolled in the organized screening program “Prevenzione Serena” and referred for their first colposcopy examination at the Lower Genital Tract Clinic within the Department of Surgical Sciences at the University of Torino, Italy, following positive cytology and/or HPV DNA testing, were invited to participate in the study. As part of the organized screening pathway, patients were invited to undergo colposcopy through an official letter informing them that, based on the result of their first-level screening test, a colposcopic examination was necessary. At the time of their appointment, and before entering the examination room, participants were asked to complete three electronic online questionnaires using their own devices. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the results were collected anonymously.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of the City of Health and Science of Torino (Reference Number: 0000629/29.04.2022) and was carried out in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent amendments.

Study measures

Three questionnaires were administered (supplementary material) : the first comprised nine personal questions regarding age, social status, level of education, employment status, number of pregnancies and miscarriages, and HPV vaccination. Both the second and third questionnaires included the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21) [12] to assess anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms experienced in the previous week, and the Dyadic Trust Scale (DTS) [13] to assess interpersonal trust within close relationships.

DASS-21 consists of 21 items, 7 for each of 3 subscales (i.e., depression, anxiety, and stress). Each item is scored from 0 to 3. The total score for each subscale is then doubled to align with the full version of the DASS, leading to a possible score range from 0 to 42 for each subscale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of symptoms. The DASS-21 demonstrates excellent internal consistency and robust psychometric properties across community and clinical samples, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients typically exceeding 0.70 and ranging from 0.76 to 0.94 for the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress subscales, and up to 0.93 for the total scale across different countries and cultural contexts (Antony, M. M., Bieling, P. J., Cox, B. J., Enns, M. W., & Swinson, R. P. (1998). Psychometric properties of the 42-item and 21-item versions of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales in clinical groups and a community sample. Psychological assessment, 10(2), 176. Henry, J. D., & Crawford, J. R. (2005). The short-form version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS‐21): Construct validity and normative data in a large non‐clinical sample. British journal of clinical psychology, 44(2), 227–239. Medvedev, O. N. (2023). Depression anxiety stress scales (DASS-21) in international contexts. In International handbook of behavioral health assessment (pp. 1–15). Cham: Springer International Publishing. Adu, P., Popoola, T., Iqbal, N., Medvedev, O. N., & Simpson, C. R. (2025). Validating the depression anxiety stress scales (DASS-21) across Germany, Ghana, India, and New Zealand using Rasch methodology. Journal of Affective Disorders, 383, 363–373).

The DTS [13] is an 8-item self-report measure assessing trust in close relationships; specifically, the respondent’s judgment of the significant other’s goodwill and honesty toward the respondent. Participants were asked to respond to each of the eight statements using a seven-point scale with the following anchors: 1 = definitely not; 2 = no; 3 = probably not; 4 = neutral; 5 = probably yes; 6 = yes; 7 = definitely yes. For this study, reverse scoring was applied to the necessary items so that a higher total score consistently indicated greater trust toward the partner, consistent with most of the previous literature concerning the DTS. The total score is obtained by summing the item scores, with a possible range from 8 to 56 points; a higher overall score indicates greater trust in the dyad. The DTS demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with an original reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 (Larzelere & Huston, 1980).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the characteristics of the study sample. Categorical variables, including sociodemographic data, clinical history, and DASS-21 severity categories, were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Continuous variables, specifically age and total Dyadic Trust Scale (DTS) scores, were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR).

To explore the associations between participant characteristics (sociodemographic, clinical, and DTS scores) and psychological outcomes, separate multivariable logistic regression models were developed for depression, anxiety, and stress, using the respective DASS-21 subscales as dependent variables. Candidate predictors were identified a priori based on clinical relevance and evidence from the literature. In addition, exploratory univariable analyses were conducted to examine crude associations with the outcomes. Variables considered clinically important were retained in the multivariable models regardless of their statistical significance, while additional variables showing statistical evidence of association in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion. The final set of covariates was determined through discussion among the investigators to ensure both clinical interpretability and model parsimony.

For these regression analyses, the DASS-21 subscale scores were dichotomized using established severity thresholds (moderate level and above): ≥14 for depression, ≥ 10 for anxiety, and ≥ 19 for stress to distinguish participants with clinically relevant symptom levels from those below the thresholds (Lovibond, S.H.; Lovibond, P.F. Manual for the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales, 2nd ed.; Psychology Foundation of Australia: Sydney, NSW, Australia, 1995). Although dichotomization may entail some loss of information from the continuous scale, this approach was adopted to model the risk of clinically meaningful psychological distress and to enhance the clinical interpretability of the results through the construction of nomograms.

Odds Ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (95%CIs) and p-values were calculated to assess the strength and statistical significance of these associations.

The functional relationship between DTS scores and the predicted probabilities of depression, anxiety, and stress derived from the models was graphically visualized. Furthermore, nomograms were constructed based on the final logistic regression models to serve as visual tools for estimating the individual probability of experiencing these psychological outcomes. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all inferential analyses. These nomograms are presented as exploratory and were not internally validated; they should not be used for individual risk prediction without further validation. Internal consistency of the DASS-21 total scale and its three subscales (Stress, Anxiety, Depression) and of the Dyadic Trust Scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha; 95% confidence intervals were estimated via bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.4.1 statistical software.

Results

During the study period, 682 women were referred to the clinic for a first colposcopy following a positive screening result (potentially eligible population). Among them, 573 women agreed to participate and were included in the final analysis, yielding a completion rate of 84.0%.

Most women were single (61%), had attained university or higher education (53%), and were employed (82%). 17% were HPV vaccinated, and 56.7% had no children.

Cronbach’s alpha indicated good internal consistency for both instruments in this sample: α = 95.5%, 95% CI 92.9–96.8%) for the DASS-21 total scale, and across all three subscales: Stress (α = 91.8%, 95% CI 87.7–94.2%), Depression (α = 90.8, 95% CI 85.7–93.5%), and Anxiety (α = 84.6, 95% CI 74.9–89.6). The DTS showed acceptable internal consistency (α = 73.4, 95% CI 69.0-77.1%).

Regarding psychological outcomes assessed via the DASS-21, 40.8% of women reported at least mild symptoms of depression (14.8% moderate, 5.1% severe, 2.6% extremely severe). Symptoms of anxiety were present in 31.3% of the sample (12.6% moderate, 2.4% severe, 1.7% extremely severe), while 70% reported at least mild stress symptoms (32.2% moderate, 7.9% severe, 7.8% extremely severe) this indicates that stress was the most prevalent psychological response, substantially higher than anxiety (31.3%) or depression (40.8%). The median score on the Dyadic Trust Scale (DTS) was 40 (IQR 33–44). Having non-university/pre-university education compared to university/higher education was associated with increased odds for depression, (OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.02–2.64; p = 0.04). Similarly, women who reported having received the HPV vaccination was significantly associated with higher odds of depression (OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.02–3.41, p = 0.04). This association should be interpreted cautiously as this study is cross-sectional and cannot establish causality. Conversely, higher dyadic trust was found to be a protective factor; an 11-point increase in the DTS score (comparison of 44 vs. 33 score) was associated with significantly lower odds of depression (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.51–0.85; p < 0.01). Employment status was not significantly associated with depression (p = 0.10).

For anxiety, older age was protective, with a 16-year increase (comparison of 46 vs. 30 years) associated with lower odds (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.35–0.80; p < 0.01). Higher dyadic trust was also significantly associated with lower odds of anxiety (OR 0.53 for an 11-point DTS increase, 95% CI 0.31–0.91; p = 0.04). No significant associations were found between anxiety and education level (p = 0.06), employment status (p = 0.32), or number of children.

For stress, both older age (OR 0.25 for a 16-year increase, 95% CI 0.12–0.52; p < 0.01) and higher dyadic trust (OR 0.46 for an 11-point DTS increase, 95% CI 0.25–0.85; p = 0.03) were significantly associated with lower odds. HPV vaccination status (p = 0.90), number of children, and menopausal status (p = 0.22) were not significantly associated with stress in the multivariable analysis.

Discussion

Sociodemographic characteristics of our cohort are aligned with existing literature: women aged 30–49 have significantly higher screening participation compared to those ≤ 30 or ≥ 50 years [14], as well as highly educated [14–16] and employed patients [14, 16]. Nulliparous women were more represented in our study. In high-income countries, the direct relationship between having multiple births and increased screening attendance is not well established. Access, education, and overall health orientation seem to be stronger predictors. In contrast, in lower-income regions, being multiparous is a consistent facilitator of screening, potentially due to increased contact with health services during and after pregnancy [17, 18]. Also, we enrolled only patients sent to colposcopy for the first time and this may have influenced our sample. The association between prior HPV vaccination and depression is exploratory and potentially confounded by cohort/age effects, expectations, and unmeasured factors (e.g., prior mental health). We avoid causal interpretations and recommend longitudinal and qualitative studies.

HPV vaccination rate was low; however, the median age of our patient population was above that of the cohorts targeted by the initial HPV vaccination campaigns in Italy [19]. Stress emerged as the most pervasive psychological response in this cohort, with approximately 70% of women reporting at least mild symptoms. Clinically significant anxiety and depression were also frequent, affecting approximately one third and two fifths of participants, respectively. These findings are in line with previous evidence showing that the experience of receiving an HPV-positive result or an abnormal cervical cytology can elicit intense psychological distress, especially during the interval preceding colposcopy [11, 20–22]. The stress of facing a diagnosis related to sexuality, future fertility, and potential surgical treatment—combined with the emotional weight of navigating ambiguity—can substantially impact women’s well-being, mental state, and sexual self-perception [23–25]. These reactions are theoretically grounded in the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping [26] which posits that high perceived threat and low control trigger strong psychophysiological arousal, and in Uncertainty in Illness Theory [27] which highlights how probabilistic information—such as that associated with HPV positivity—prolongs ambiguity and rumination. Recent contributions have emphasized that the psychological burden can persist across the diagnostic and follow-up trajectory [10, 28] and that psychosocial distress may also affect adherence to clinical recommendations [24, 28].

An interesting finding was the association between prior HPV vaccination and increased depressive symptomatology. One possible explanation is that of “vaccine-failure dissonance”: women who engaged in preventive behaviors may experience the positive result as a personal failure or breach of trust in the healthcare system. This dissonance could elicit emotional distress, especially in individuals with heightened health vigilance [29, 30]. Although no direct evidence supports this mechanism in the context of HPV, cognitive dissonance theory has been invoked in analogous scenarios, such as HIV diagnosis [30]. Interestingly, data from COVID-19 contexts suggest that preventive actions may instead buffer distress, raising the possibility that other mediators—such as trait-level anxiety or risk perception—may better account for the observed link [29]. These findings underscore the need for qualitative studies to explore how vaccinated women make sense of a positive screening result and how expectations about vaccine efficacy shape emotional reactions.

Anxiety and stress were primarily associated with younger age, with a sixteen-year difference (30 vs. 46 years) reducing the odds of anxiety by half and stress by three quarters. Younger women may interpret HPV positivity considering identity development, reproductive planning, and relationship dynamics, making them more vulnerable to psychological strain [31–33]. Previous literature also suggests that younger women, due to increased sexual activity and heightened concerns about fertility, perceive HPV as more threatening [34]. These findings point to the importance of developmentally sensitive communication and counseling strategies for younger subgroups.

Lower educational attainment was independently associated with increased risk of depressive symptoms. This finding reinforces the broader socio-ecological perspective that educational disadvantage interacts with health literacy, emotional resources, and access to support. Women with lower education levels may have greater difficulty understanding the prevalence and benign nature of HPV, leading to catastrophic interpretations and reduced coping capacity [24, 35].

Consistent with prior literature [36–38] limited or poorly tailored communication may exacerbate distress. Participants with lower educational backgrounds often report unmet informational and emotional needs and express a desire for more comprehensive psychological support during the screening process. These findings suggest that literacy-sensitive educational materials and standardized communication protocols should be prioritized as part of equitable care delivery.

Lower dyadic trust emerged as the only factor significantly associated with all three outcomes—depression, anxiety, and stress—across multivariable models. Each eleven-point decrease in Dyadic Trust Scale score was associated with a 34–54% increase in distress risk, underscoring the central, modifiable role of intimate relationships in psychosocial adjustment to screening outcomes. The relevance of dyadic trust aligns with attachment theory and dyadic coping models, which suggest that perceiving a partner as emotionally available facilitates threat reappraisal and supports adaptive coping [39]. Although specific studies on HPV and dyadic trust are lacking, evidence from oncology and HIV literature confirms that stable, supportive relationships buffer the psychological impact of health-related stigma and uncertainty [40]. dyadic trust is not merely a static trait but a dynamic process that can be fostered through collaborative coping behaviors and open communication. Fostering trust and shared coping may be particularly beneficial for women receiving HPV-positive results.

Implications

Integrating these insights into screening practice could involve encouraging partner attendance at colposcopy, providing couple-focused materials, or offering brief counseling sessions aimed at navigating stigma, intimacy, and sexual communication. Such approaches are feasible, low-cost, and potentially have a high impact.

Beyond explanatory insights, the nomograms developed in this study provide a practical tool for individualized risk estimation. By combining key variables such as age, vaccination status, and dyadic trust, the models generate probabilistic predictions of psychological distress, enabling early identification of high-risk profiles. Embedding these tools into digital scheduling systems and combining them with ultra-brief screeners (e.g., single-item stress thermometers) could facilitate targeted psychological support at the point of referral. This risk-stratified approach would align with ongoing efforts to personalize screening pathways, while avoiding unnecessary intervention in low-risk individuals. Moreover, such tools may promote equity by highlighting psychosocial vulnerability not captured by clinical risk factors alone. These nomograms are presented as exploratory and were not internally validated; they should not be used for individual risk prediction without further validation.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation of the results, and bidirectional relationships—particularly between dyadic trust and distress—cannot be excluded. The single-region Italian sample may also reduce generalizability to other sociocultural contexts. Furthermore, reliance on self-report data, particularly regarding sensitive constructions such as trust and sexuality, introduces potential bias.

Conclusions

These findings offer multiple points of clinical and policy relevance. Communication strategies should avoid ambiguous terminology, accompany results with clear absolute-risk information, and ensure that materials are adapted to different educational and developmental levels. Partner-inclusive formats may leverage existing relational resources, and the use of nomograms may streamline identification of women in need of psychosocial care. These nomograms are presented as exploratory and were not internally validated; they should not be used for individual risk prediction without further validation.

Prospective cohort studies and dyadic intervention trials are needed to confirm causal pathways and assess the feasibility of integrated, psychologically informed care models in cervical cancer screening programs. Literature on help-seeking behavior indicates that adverse emotional reactions can reduce screening adherence, particularly among women vulnerable to mental health difficulties [11]. Ultimately, addressing psychological distress early may not only improve individual well-being but also enhance the long-term efficacy of prevention programs.
