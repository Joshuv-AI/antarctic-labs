1.
Introduction

We consider the following parabolic-elliptic system with Dirichlet boundary conditions

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{t}-\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla u)=-\operatorname{div}(uM(x)\nabla\psi)+f(x,t)&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
-\operatorname{div}(M(x)\nabla\psi)=|u|^{\theta}&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
\psi(x,t)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u(x,t)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=0&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Here
N\geq 3
,
\Omega
is an open and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
T>0
and
\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
. Moreover,
A
is a measurable matrix-valued function in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
which is uniformly elliptic with respect to
x
and
t
, namely:

A(x,t)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\alpha\left|\xi\right|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\text{for a.e. }(x,t)\in\Omega_{T},

M
is a measurable matrix-valued function in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
which is uniformly elliptic with respect to
x
, namely:

M(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\alpha\left|\xi\right|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega,

and
f
is a function which belongs to
L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
,
f\geq 0
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{2}{N})
.

System (
1.1
) is loosely related to the minimal version of the Keller-Segel system described in
[
10
]
, which is a fully parabolic system used to describe the chemotaxis. Our system is a discontinuous-coefficient version of such model with an added source term
f\geq 0
and zero Dirichlet boundary conditions. Furthermore, the second equation of our system is elliptic and the dependence on
u
in the second term is sublinear (since
\theta\leq\frac{2}{N}<1
, recall that
N>2
), while the original model has a linear dependence on
u
in the second equation.

This system has been studied in
[
8
]
with
f=0
and Dirichlet boundary conditions of the type

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi(x,t)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u(x,t)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Under these assumptions, the authors proved the existence of distributional solutions and an ultracontractive estimate for the solution
u
, that is

\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\text{C}\,\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}{t^{\frac{N}{2}}}.

This suggests that the interplay between the two equations of (
1.1
) leads to an improvement in regularity of the solutions. It was indeed proved (see
[
7
]
) that, in general, for equations of the type

u_{t}-\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla u)=-\operatorname{div}(uE(x,t))\quad\text{with }E\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega_{T})\right)^{N}

it is only possible to find a solution
u\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
such that

\log(1+\left|u\right|)\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),

(which is consistent with the results of
[
3
]
in the elliptic case).

Moreover, it was shown in
[
6
]
that, for the stationary version of (
1.1
), the interplay between the two equations allows to prove the existence of solutions which obey the
L^{p}
-regularity theorems from Stampacchia and Boccardo-Gallouët (see
[
15
]
and
[
4
]
).

2.
Main results

Depending on the summability of
f
, we prove different existence results. From now on, we will write
p^{\star}
and
p^{\star\star}
as the real numbers which satisfy (when
p<N+2
and
p<\frac{N+2}{2}
respectively)

\frac{1}{p^{\star}}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{N+2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{p^{\star\star}}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{2}{N+2}.

This is due to the fact that the
L^{p}
regularity theorems in the parabolic case are, given
f\in L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
, formally equivalent to those in the elliptic case (up to writing
N+2
instead of
N
in the exponents), as shown in
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let

with

, then there exist

which solve (
1.1
). More precisely, there exist

and

\psi\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})

such that
u\geq 0
,
\psi\geq 0
and, for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and all

,

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}{}_{H^{-1}}\langle u_{t},\varphi\rangle_{H^{1}_{0}}+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega_{T}}uM(x)\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)\varphi\\
\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi(t)\cdot\nabla v=\int_{\Omega}u(t)^{\theta}v\quad\forall\,t\in(0,T).\end{cases}

Moreover, the initial condition is satisfied in the strong sense for both

and

If the summability of

]

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

with

such that

and

such that
u\geq 0
,
\psi\geq 0
and, for all

and all

,

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{t},\varphi\rangle+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega_{T}}uM(x)\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)\varphi\\

Finally, the initial condition for

is satisfied in the following weak sense: there exists a sequence

of functions in

, and
\psi(0)=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

Remark 2.3
.

The choice of test functions belonging to
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{0}(\Omega))
may seem somewhat restrictive since, arguably, the distributions that appear in the equation act on larger function spaces. Our choice, however, has the advantage of simplifying the notation while remaining in a Sobolev space context. Moreover, since the aforementioned larger spaces are of the form
L^{a}(0,T;L^{b}(\Omega_{T}))
, one can recover the complete set of test functions arguing by density.

To prove the existence of a distributional solution with, say,
C^{1}
test functions, we need the term

to belong to
L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
. For this to be possible, since we only know that

belongs to

, it is necessary for

to belong to (at least)
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
. Such requirement is equivalent to

m^{\star\star}=\frac{(N+2)m}{N+2-2m}\geq 2,

which means that

shall be greater than
\frac{2N+4}{N+6}
. We thus have the following result

Theorem 2.4
.

Let

with
\frac{2N+4}{N+6}\leq m<\frac{2N+4}{N+4}

and
\psi\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
such that
u\geq 0
,
\psi\geq 0
and for all
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{0}(\Omega))
and all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{t},\varphi\rangle+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega_{T}}uM(x)\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)\varphi\\
\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi(t)\cdot\nabla v=\int_{\Omega}u(t)^{\theta}v\quad\forall\,t\in(0,T).\end{cases}

Finally, the initial condition for

is satisfied in the following weak sense: there exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
of functions in

, and

almost everywhere in

For highly singular data, since

does not belong to

we cannot prove the existence of a distributional solution and we will need to use entropy solutions. The definition of entropy solutions was introduced in
[

]

From now on, we will use the notation

s\quad\text{if }\,\left|s\right|<k\\
-k\quad\text{if }\,s\leq-k\\
\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{k}(s)=s-T_{k}(s).

\quad\int_{\Omega_{t}}uM(x)\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla T_{k}(u-\varphi)+\int_{\Omega_{t}}f(x,s)T_{k}(u-\varphi)\\

3.
Existence of an approximating solution

To prove both the existence and regularity of solutions, we will proceed by approximation. Consider the approximating system:

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(u_{n})_{t}-\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla u_{n})=-\operatorname{div}(T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n})+T_{n}(f(x,t))&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
-\operatorname{div}(M(x)\nabla\psi_{n})=|T_{n}(u_{n})|^{\theta}&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
\psi_{n}(x,t)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u_{n}(x,t)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u_{n}(x,0)=0&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{array}\right.

We will use a fixed-point technique to prove the existence of a solution to (
3.1
). After that, we will prove a priori estimates on the solutions, which will allow us to pass to the limit as
n
tends to infinity, proving the existence of a solution for (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
. Then there exist
u_{n}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
such that
(u_{n})_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
and
\psi_{n}\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
which solve (
3.1
) in the sense that, for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
with
\varphi_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
and all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}{}_{H^{-1}}\langle(u_{n})_{t},\varphi\rangle_{H^{1}_{0}}+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega_{T}}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}T_{n}(f)\varphi\\
\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}(t)\cdot\nabla v=\int_{\Omega}\left|T_{n}(u_{n})\right|^{\theta}v\quad\forall\,t\in(0,T).\end{cases}

Remark 3.2
.

It can be shown, choosing test functions of the type
\rho(x)\eta(t)
and arguing by density, that an equivalent definition of solution to the first equation of (
3.1
) is

(3.3)

{}_{H^{-1}}\langle(u_{n})_{t},\varphi\rangle_{H^{1}_{0}}+\int_{\Omega}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(f)\varphi

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
.

Furthermore, to simplify the notation, we will write

\langle(u_{n})_{t},\varphi\rangle

instead of

{}_{H^{-1}}\langle(u_{n})_{t},\varphi\rangle_{H^{1}_{0}}

during computations.

Remark 3.3
.

Notice that
u_{n}
belongs to
C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
(see
[
9
]
, Section 5.9.2). This will play a crucial role in the proof.

Proof.

We start by fixing
w\in C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
such that
w(x,0)=0
and considering the system

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{t}-\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla v)=-\operatorname{div}(T_{n}(w)M(x)\nabla\eta)+T_{n}(f(x,t))&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
-\operatorname{div}(M(x)\nabla\eta)=|T_{n}(w)|^{\theta}&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
\end{array}\right.

with zero boundary conditions. We claim that there exists a unique choice of
v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
such that
v_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
and
\eta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
which solve (
3.4
).

The existence of a solution to the second equation follows from Lax Milgram’s theorem (from which we know that
\|\eta(t)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq Cn^{\theta}
) and Stampacchia’s Theorem (from
[
15
]
) implies that
\|\eta(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq Cn^{\theta}
for every
t\in[0,T]
.
Notice that
\eta\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
, since the datum of the second equation is continuous in
t
and the equation has a unique solution. More precisely, we have

\|\eta(t)-\eta(t^{\prime})\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\|w(t)-w(t^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\theta}

and the right hand side term goes to zero as
\left|t-t^{\prime}\right|\to 0
.

We now focus on the first equation: notice that

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left|T_{n}(w)M(x)\nabla\eta\right|\leq n\beta\left|\nabla\eta\right|\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\\
|T_{n}(f)|\leq n.\\
\end{array}\right.

This implies that there exists a unique solution
v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
of the first equation (see, for example,
[
11
]
). Such solution also satisfies the condition
v_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
and, since

\|T_{n}(w)M(x)\nabla\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|T_{n}(f)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\leq Cn^{\theta+1}

we have the estimate

(3.6)

\|v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq Cn^{\theta+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))}\leq Cn^{\theta+1}.

Notice that estimate (
3.6
)
implies (see
[
9
]
, Section 5.9.2) that
v\in C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and

(3.7)

\|v\|_{C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq Cn^{\theta+1}.

We now want to apply Schauder’s fixed point theorem to prove the existence of a solution to (
3.1
). Consider the nonlinear operator

\displaystyle S:C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))

\displaystyle\to C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\longmapsto v.

Estimate (
3.7
) implies that the closed ball of radius
R
is invariant for
S
. Furthermore, the same estimate, thanks to the compactness of the embedding given by Corollary 4 of
[
14
]
implies that
S
is compact.
To prove that
S
is continuous, let us consider a sequence
(w_{k})_{k}\subset C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
such that
w_{k}
strongly converges to some
w\in C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
. Let
\eta_{k}
and
\eta
be the corresponding solutions to the second equation of (
3.1
) (recall that both
\eta
and the
\eta_{k}
’s belong to
C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
. Fix
t\in(0,T)
: we have

\|\eta_{k}(t)-\eta(t)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C\|w_{k}(t)-w(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\leq C\|w_{k}-w\|_{C^{0}(L^{2})}^{\theta}

which implies, taking the supremum over
t
, that
\eta_{k}\to\eta
in
C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Now notice that, thanks to the compactness of the operator
S
, we know that any subsequence of
S(w_{k})
has a subsequence which converges to some
\tilde{v}
(which may depend on the chosen subsequence) in
C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
. At the same time, we have that

(3.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}T_{n}(w_{k})\to T_{n}(w)\text{ in }C^{0}([0,T];L^{p}(\Omega))\text{ for any }p\in(1,\infty)\text{ by Lebesgue's theorem }\\
\nabla\eta_{k}\to\nabla\eta\text{ in }C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)).\end{array}\right.

Thus,
\tilde{v}
satisfies the equation

\tilde{v}_{t}-\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla\tilde{v})=-\operatorname{div}(T_{n}(w)M(x)\nabla\eta)+T_{n}(f(x,t))

which implies, by uniqueness, that
\tilde{v}=S(w)
. This implies that
S
is continuous and, invoking Schauder’s fixed point theorem, we conclude the proof of the theorem.
∎

3.1.
A priori estimates

We will now prove several a priori estimates on the approximating solutions. We begin with a crucial estimate in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u_{n}
be a solution to the first equation of (
3.1
), then
u_{n}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
and

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}.

Proof.

We follow
[
6
]
,
[
3
]
and choose
T_{h}(u_{n})
as a test function at a fixed time
t
to obtain

\langle(u_{n})_{t},T_{h}(u_{n})\rangle+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{h}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\nabla T_{h}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)T_{h}(u_{n}).

Applying Young’s inequality to the right hand side (and recalling that
\left|T_{h}(u_{n})\right|\leq h
) we obtain

\langle(u_{n})_{t},T_{h}(u_{n})\rangle+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{h}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}h^{2}}{2\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{h}(u_{n})\right|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}h\left|f(x,t)\right|

After simplifying, we divide by
h
and pass to the limit as
h\to 0
, obtaining

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{n}(t)\right|\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|f(x,t)\right|.

Integrating from
0
to
t
leads to

\|u_{n}(t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|f(x,t)\right|\leq\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|f\right|=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}

which, taking the supremum over all
t\in(0,T)
, concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Notice that the previous estimate holds for any datum
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
.

Now, thanks to the positivity of

, we prove the positivity of the approximating solution.

Lemma 3.6
.

The solutions
u_{n}
,
\psi_{n}
of (

) are nonnegative.

Proof.

We will use the same technique used in the proof of the previous lemma.
First, we choose
\psi_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{\psi_{n}\leq 0\}}
as a test function in the second equation to obtain

\alpha\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}=\alpha\int_{\psi_{n}\leq 0}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\psi_{n}\leq 0}\left|T_{n}(u_{n})\right|^{\theta}\psi_{n}\leq 0

which, applying Poincaré’s inequality, implies that
\psi_{n}^{-}=0
.

We now prove that
u_{n}\geq 0
: choosing
T_{h}(u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}})
as a test function, we get (using Young’s inequality)

\int_{\Omega}(u_{n})_{t}T_{h}(u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}})+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{h}(u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}})\right|^{2}\leq

\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{-h\leq u_{n}\leq 0}T_{n}(u_{n})^{2}\left|M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{h}(u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}})\right|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)T_{h}(u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}}).

After cancellations (notice that the last term is negative, since
f\geq 0
), this leads us to

\int_{\Omega}(u_{n})_{t}T_{h}(u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}})\leq\frac{\beta^{2}h^{2}}{2\alpha}\int_{-h\leq u_{n}\leq 0}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}.

We now divide by
h
and take the limit

, obtaining

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\leq 0\}}\right|\leq 0.

Thus, integrating from
0
to

u_{n}(t)\geq 0
a.e. for every

.
∎

The following result enables us to choose powers of
u_{n}
as test functions (without requiring a truncation). To prove it, we will use the following Lemma from

.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a nonincreasing function such that

\psi(h)\leq\frac{M\psi(k)^{\delta}}{(h-k)^{\gamma}},\quad\forall\,h>k\geq 0,

for
M>0,\delta>1
and
\gamma>0
. Then
\psi
has a zero
d\in\mathbb{R}
, where

d^{\gamma}=M\psi(0)^{\delta-1}2^{\frac{\delta\gamma}{\delta-1}}.

We will also use the following parabolic version of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, which follows from the interpolation inequality for Lebesgue spaces.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
v\in L^{\infty}(0,T,L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
. Then
v\in L^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})
and

(3.9)

\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|v\right|^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}\leq\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{4}{N}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}.

Theorem 3.9
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{n}
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
.

Proof.

First of all, let us define the function

\Theta_{n}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}T_{n}(\sigma)d\sigma.

Let
k\geq 0
. We choose
\left[\Theta_{n}(u_{n})-\Theta_{n}(k)\right]^{+}
as a test function in the second equation of (
3.1
). This leads us to

\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla\left[\Theta_{n}(u_{n})-\Theta_{n}(k)\right]^{+}=\int_{\Omega}\left|T_{n}(u_{n})\right|^{\theta}\left[\Theta_{n}(u_{n})-\Theta_{n}(k)\right]^{+}.

Notice that

(3.10)

\nabla\left[\Theta_{n}(u_{n})-\Theta_{n}(k)\right]^{+}=T_{n}(u_{n})\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})

and that (recall that
u_{n}\geq 0
)

(3.11)

\left[\Theta_{n}(u_{n})-\Theta_{n}(k)\right]^{+}=\int_{k}^{u_{n}}T_{n}(\sigma)d\sigma{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\{u_{n}\geq k\}}\leq nG_{k}(u_{n}).

We now choose
G_{k}(u_{n})
as a test function in the first equation of (
3.1
) to obtain (integrating from
0
to
t
)

\int_{\Omega_{t}}(u_{n})_{t}G_{k}(u_{n})+\alpha\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega_{t}}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega_{t}}T_{n}(f)G_{k}(u_{n}),

that is (here we observe that
(u_{n})_{t}G_{k}(u_{n})=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}G_{k}(u_{n})^{2}
and apply (
3.10
)),

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}G_{k}(u_{n}(t))^{2}+\alpha\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|T_{n}(u_{n})\right|^{\theta}\left[\Theta_{n}(u_{n})-\Theta_{n}(k)\right]^{+}+\int_{\Omega_{t}}nG_{k}(u_{n}),

which implies, thanks to (
3.11
)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}G_{k}(u_{n}(t))^{2}+\alpha\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega_{T}}n^{\theta+1}G_{k}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega_{T}}nG_{k}(u_{n}).

We thus have, since both terms on the left hand side are positive,

(3.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq Cn^{\theta+1}\int_{\Omega_{T}}G_{k}(u_{n})\\
&\|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq Cn^{\theta+1}\int_{\Omega_{T}}G_{k}(u_{n}).\end{aligned}\right.

We now apply the parabolic Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (
3.9
) to obtain

\int_{\Omega_{T}}G_{k}(u_{n})^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}\leq\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{4}{N}}\|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C_{n}\left(\int_{u_{n}\geq k}G_{k}(u_{n})\right)^{\frac{N+2}{N}}.

We now define
A_{k}=\{u_{n}\geq k\}
and apply Hölder’s inequality to the last term, obtaining

\int_{A_{k}}G_{k}(u_{n})^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}\leq\tilde{C}_{n}\left(\int_{A_{k}}G_{k}(u_{n})^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|A_{k}\right|^{\frac{N+4}{2N+4}}.

Now choose
h>k
to obtain (after cancellations)

(h-k)^{\frac{N+2}{N}}\left|A_{h}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{n}\left|A_{k}\right|^{\frac{N+4}{2N+4}},

which implies

\left|A_{h}\right|\leq C_{n}\frac{\left|A_{k}\right|^{\frac{2N+8}{2N+4}}}{(h-k)^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}}.

Observing that
\frac{2N+8}{2N+4}>1
, we now invoke Stampacchia’s Lemma
3.7
with

\psi(k)=\left|A_{k}\right|

to conclude that
\left|A_{k}\right|=0
for some
k\geq 0
, which ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

The sequence

is bounded in

\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{n}^{\theta}\|_{L^{\frac{1}{\theta}}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}^{\theta}\quad\forall\,t\in(0,T).

We now take the supremum over

to obtain the

As for the gradient estimate, we choose

as a test function in the second equation of (

As before, taking the supremum over

3.2.
Existence and regularity of a solution with finite energy

We now focus on the finite energy case: that is, when
f\in L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
for some
m\geq\frac{2N+4}{N+4}
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
f\in L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
with
\frac{2N+4}{N+4}\leq m<\frac{N+2}{2}
, then the sequence of approximating solutions
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{m^{\star\star}}(\Omega_{T})
and in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\geq 1
. We define the function

F_{n}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{2\gamma-2}T_{n}(\sigma)d\sigma

and choose
F_{n}(u_{n})
as a test function in the second equation of (
3.1
) to get

\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma-2}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla u_{n}=\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla F_{n}(u_{n})=\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})^{\theta}F_{n}(u_{n})

\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})^{\theta}u_{n}^{2\gamma}

Now choose
u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}
(which belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
by Theorem
3.9
) as a test function in the first equation of (
3.1
) to get

\frac{1}{{2\gamma}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma}+\alpha(2\gamma-1)\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}u_{n}^{2\gamma-2}\leq\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma-2}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla u_{n}+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}.

Thus

(3.13)

\frac{1}{{2\gamma}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma}+\frac{\alpha(2\gamma-1)}{\gamma^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\gamma}u_{n}^{\theta}u_{n}^{2\gamma}+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}.

Now we split the integration domain in the third term and apply Hölder’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta+2\gamma}=\int_{u_{n}\leq k}u_{n}^{\theta+2\gamma}+\int_{u_{n}\geq k}u_{n}^{\theta+2\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|+\left(\int_{u_{n}\geq k}u_{n}^{\theta\frac{N}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}\left(\int_{u_{n}\geq k}u_{n}^{\gamma 2^{*}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}

\displaystyle\leq k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|+\tilde{C}\left(\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}}{k^{1-\theta\frac{N}{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\right|^{2}

Thus, choosing
k
large enough, we have

\frac{1}{{2\gamma}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma}+C\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\right|^{2}\leq k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}.

Integration from
0
to
t
leads to

\frac{1}{{2\gamma}}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma}(t)+C\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\right|^{2}\leq k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}.

It follows (since both terms on the left hand side are positive) that

(3.14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C\left(k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}\right).\\
&\|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C\left(k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

Hence, applying the Gagliardo-Nirenberg parabolic inequality to
v=u_{n}^{\gamma}
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}}\leq\|u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{4}{N}}\|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C\left(k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}\right)^{\frac{N+2}{N}}.

We now apply Hölder’s inequality to the last term, obtaining

(3.15)

\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}\leq\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega_{T})}\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{(2\gamma-1)m^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}}.

Setting
2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}=(2\gamma-1)m^{\prime}
leads to

\gamma=\frac{Nm}{2(N+2-2m)}\quad\text{and}\quad 2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}=\frac{(N+2)m}{N+2-2m}=m^{\star\star},

hence

\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{\frac{m(N+2)}{N+2-2m}}\right)^{\frac{N}{N+2}}\leq C\left(k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega_{T})}\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{\frac{m(N+2)}{N+2-2m}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}}\right)

for some
C>0
.
Moreover, we have

\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}=1-\frac{1}{m}<1-\frac{2}{N+2}=\frac{N}{N+2}.

This implies that the right-hand side grows at a slower rate than the left-hand side.
It follows that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{m^{\star\star}}(\Omega_{T})
.

Observe that, since
f\in L^{\frac{2N+4}{N+4}}(\Omega_{T})
, we can repeat the same calculations with

m=\frac{2N+4}{N+4}
and
\gamma=1
, which implies, thanks to (
3.14
), that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.

∎

•

T_{n}(f(x,t))
is bounded in
L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
.

This implies that the sequence
((u_{n})_{t})_{n}
in bounded in
L^{1}(0,T;W^{-1,1}(\Omega))
.
Thus, we can apply Corollary 4 of
[
14
]
to obtain that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
has a converging subsequence in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
. Let
u
be the limit of such subsequence: we have that
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
(since
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and since
\nabla u_{n}\rightharpoonup\nabla u
). Moreover,
u_{t}\in L^{1}(0,T;W^{-1,\frac{N}{N-1}}(\Omega))
.

By Lemma
3.11
, there exists a subsequence of
(\psi_{n})_{n}
that strongly converges in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
and weakly converges in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
to some function
\psi
.
Moreover, since
u_{n}^{\theta}
is strongly convergent in
L^{p}(\Omega_{T})
with
p>\frac{N}{2}
, we can invoke Stampacchia’s Lemma (from
[
15
]
) to prove that (up to subsequences):

•

\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
, which implies that
\psi\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
;

•

\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
, which implies that
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
.

We can now pass to the limit (up to subsequences) as
n\to\infty
in

(3.20)

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}\langle(u_{n})_{t},\varphi\rangle+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega_{T}}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}T_{n}(f)\varphi\\
\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}(t)\cdot\nabla v=\int_{\Omega}u_{n}(t)^{\theta}v\end{cases}

to conclude that the couple
(u,\psi)
is a solution to (
1.1
) in the sense (
2.2
).
∎

Remark 3.17
.

Recall that, due to (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we also have that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2\gamma}(\Omega))
and in
L^{2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})
with
2\gamma=\frac{Nm}{N+2-2m}
. We can interpolate between the two spaces with exponents
r
and
q
such that (letting
\lambda\in[0,1]
)

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{\lambda}{2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}}\quad\quad\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1-\lambda}{2\gamma}+\frac{\lambda}{2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}}

to obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{r}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2\gamma}(\Omega))}^{r(1-\lambda)}\|u_{n}\|_{2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}}^{r\lambda}=\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2\gamma}(\Omega))}^{r(1-\lambda)}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2\gamma\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})}.

It follows that
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{r}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
and thus, by Fatou’s Lemma,
u\in L^{r}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
.

3.3.
Existence of a solution with singular data

If the datum
f
belongs to
L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
with
m\in[1,\frac{2N+4}{N+4})
, we can still prove the existence of a solution, with a few adjustments.

Remark 3.18
.

We stress that all the properties of the approximating solutions
(\psi_{n})_{n}
still hold, since they only require
f
to belong to
L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
.

The next lemma deals with the “boundedness” of the truncates of the approximating solutions
(u_{n})_{n}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
. Even though this result is, strictly speaking, only needed to prove Theorem
2.6
(since it will allow us to work with entropy solutions), the fact that
u
“has infinite energy only where it is singular” holds whenever
f
belongs to
L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
, then the sequence
(T_{k}(u_{n}))_{n}
is bounded (with respect to
n
) in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Let us choose
T_{k}(u_{n})
as a test function in the first equation of (
3.1
)

\int_{0}^{t}\langle(u_{n})_{t},T_{k}(u_{n})\rangle+\alpha\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega_{t}}u_{n}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega_{t}}f(x,t)T_{k}(u_{n}).

Applying Young’s inequality to the right hand side (and recalling that
\left|T_{k}(u_{n})\right|\leq k
) we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\Theta_{k}(u_{n})+\alpha\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq k^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\alpha}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}+k\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t),

where, as before, we denote by
\Theta_{k}
the primitive of
T_{k}
such that
\Theta_{k}(0)=0
.
After simplifying the gradient term and dropping the first (positive) term, we end up with

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq\frac{k^{2}\beta^{2}}{2\alpha}T\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}+k\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}.

In view of Remark
3.18
we have

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})\right|^{2}\leq C(f)k^{2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now prove a boundedness result for the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
which extends Theorem
3.13
.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
f\in L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
with
1<m<\frac{2N+4}{N+4}
, then the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{m^{\star\star}}(\Omega_{T})
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
and
\varepsilon>0
. We define the function

F_{n}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}(\sigma+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-2}T_{n}(\sigma)d\sigma

and choose
F_{n}^{\varepsilon}(u_{n})
as a test function in the second equation of (
3.1
) to get

\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-2}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla u_{n}=\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla F_{n}^{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})^{\theta}F_{n}^{\varepsilon}(u_{n})

\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})^{\theta}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma}

where we used

F_{n}^{\varepsilon}(s)\leq\int_{0}^{s}(\sigma+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-1}d\sigma\leq(s+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma}-\varepsilon^{2\gamma}\leq(s+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma}.

Now choose

g_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=\frac{(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-1}-\varepsilon^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}

(which belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
by Theorem
3.9
) as a test function in the first equation of (
3.1
) to get

\langle(u_{n})_{t},g_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-2}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla u_{n}

\leq\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-2}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla u_{n}+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)g_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}).

Thus

\langle(u_{n})_{t},g_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-2}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla u_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma}+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)g_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}).

Integration from
0
to
t
leads to

\int_{\Omega}G_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}(t))+\int_{\Omega_{t}}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma-2}A(x,s)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla u_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{\Omega_{t}}u_{n}^{\theta}(u_{n}+\varepsilon)^{2\gamma}+\int_{\Omega_{t}}f(x,s)g_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}),

where
G_{\varepsilon}
is the primitive of
g_{\varepsilon}
such that
G_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
.

We can now apply Fatou’s Lemma (notice that
G_{\varepsilon}(s)\to\frac{1}{2\gamma(2\gamma-1)}s^{2\gamma}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and that
G_{\varepsilon}(s)\geq 0
for
s\geq 0
) to obtain

\frac{1}{{2\gamma(2\gamma-1)}}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{2\gamma}(t)+\frac{\alpha(2\gamma-1)}{\gamma^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{\Omega_{t}}u_{n}^{\theta}u_{n}^{2\gamma}+\int_{\Omega_{t}}f(x,s)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}.

At this point, it suffices to follow the proof of Theorem
3.13
to conclude.
∎

Remark 3.21
.

The previous result also implies that

(3.21)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C\left(k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}\right).\\
&\|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C\left(k^{\theta+2\gamma}\left|\Omega\right|T+\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)u_{n}^{2\gamma-1}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

Since
(2\gamma-1)m^{\prime}=m^{\star\star}
, (
3.21
) implies that the sequence
(u_{n}^{\gamma})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.

As in the elliptic case, we cannot expect our solution to have finite energy when we deal with singular data. Recall that, in the elliptic case, the sequence of approximating solutions is bounded in
W^{1,m^{*}}_{0}(\Omega)
whenever
1<m<\frac{2N}{N+2}
. Since the parabolic equivalent of Stampacchia’s theorems are often analogous to the elliptic ones in dimension
N+2
, we expect that approximating sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
to be such that the sequence
(\nabla u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
\left(L^{m^{\star}}(\Omega_{T})\right)^{N}
. This is precisely what happens.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
f\in L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
, with
1<m<\frac{2N+4}{N+4}
. Then the sequence of approximating solutions is bounded in
L^{m^{\star}}(0,T;W^{1,m^{\star}}_{0}(\Omega))
(where
m^{\star}=\frac{(N+2)m}{(N+2)-m})
.

Proof.

To prove this result, we use the same technique employed (in the elliptic case) in
[
3
]
. Our objective is to exploit Hölder’s inequality, Theorem
3.20
and the fact that
\frac{1}{2}<\gamma<1
(see Remark
3.16
) to gain an estimate on the
L^{q}
norm of
\nabla u_{n}
.
More precisely, we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{q}=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{q}}{u_{n}^{q(1-\gamma)}}u_{n}^{q(1-\gamma)}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{u_{n}^{2(1-\gamma)}}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{\frac{2q}{2-q}(1-\gamma)}\right)^{1-\frac{q}{2}}.

Now observe that, choosing
\gamma
as in the proof of Theorem
3.20
, we get

\frac{2q}{2-q}(1-\gamma)=\frac{(N+2)m}{2N+4-4m-Nm}\frac{2N+4-4m-Nm}{(N+2)-2m}=\frac{(N+2)m}{(N+2)-2m}=m^{\star\star},

which implies that, by Theorem
3.20
,

\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{\frac{2q}{2-q}(1-\gamma)}=\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{n}^{m^{\star\star}}\leq C.

On the other hand, notice that

\int_{\Omega_{T}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{u_{n}^{2(1-\gamma)}}=\|\nabla u_{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}

which is bounded by Remark
3.21
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the existence of a solution for (some) singular data. To apply Corollary 4 of
[
14
]
, we need a uniform bound on the time derivatives of the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
, which requires the divergence term

-\operatorname{div}(T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n})

to be bounded in (at least)
L^{1}(0,T;W^{-1,1}(\Omega))
. For this to be possible, since we only know that
(\nabla\psi_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
\left(L^{2}(\Omega_{T})\right)^{N}
, it is necessary for
(u_{n})_{n}
to be bounded in (at least)
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
. Such requirement is equivalent to

m^{\star\star}=\frac{(N+2)m}{N+2-2m}\geq 2,

which means that
m
shall be greater than
\frac{2N+4}{N+6}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We write the first equation of (
3.1
) as

(u_{n})_{t}=\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla u_{n})-\operatorname{div}(T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n})+T_{n}(f(x,t))

and observe that, by Theorem
3.22
:

•

\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla u_{n})
is bounded in
L^{q^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{-1,1}(\Omega))
;

•

T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}
is bounded in

;

•

T_{n}(f(x,t))
is bounded in
L^{m}(\Omega_{T})
.

This implies that the sequence
((u_{n})_{t})_{n}
in bounded in
L^{1}(0,T;W^{-1,1}(\Omega))
.
Thus, we can apply Corollary 4 of
[
14
]
to obtain that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
has a converging subsequence in
L^{m^{\star\star}}(\Omega_{T})
. Let
u
be the limit of such subsequence: we have
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{Nm}{N+2-2m}}(\Omega))
,
u\in L^{m^{\star}}(0,T;W^{1,m^{\star}}_{0}(\Omega))
(since
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{m^{\star}}(0,T;W^{1,m^{\star}}_{0}(\Omega))
and since
\nabla u_{n}\rightharpoonup\nabla u
), and
u\in L^{m^{\star\star}}(\Omega_{T})
by Fatou’s Lemma and Theorem
3.20
. Moreover,
u_{t}\in L^{1}(0,T;W^{-1,1}(\Omega))
.

By Lemma
3.11
, there exists a subsequence of
(\psi_{n})_{n}
that strongly converges in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
and weakly converges in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
to some function
\psi
.

Moreover, since
u_{n}^{\theta}
is strongly convergent in
L^{p}(\Omega_{T})
(by Vitali’s Theorem, with
p>\frac{N}{2}
) we can invoke Stampacchia’s Theorem (from
[
15
]
) to prove that (up to subsequences):

•

\psi_{n}\to\psi
in

, which implies that
\psi\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
;

•

\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
, which implies that
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
.

We can now pass to the limit (up to subsequences) as
n\to\infty
in

(3.22)

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}\langle(u_{n})_{t},\varphi\rangle+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega_{T}}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}T_{n}(f)\varphi\\
\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}(t)\cdot\nabla v=\int_{\Omega}u_{n}(t)^{\theta}v\end{cases}

to conclude that the couple
(u,\psi)
is a solution to (

).
∎

Remark 3.23
.

Notice that
\frac{2N+4}{N+6}>1
if and only if
N>2
, which is our assumption. This implies that the techniques we have employed up until now fail for any
m\in[1,\frac{2N+4}{N+6})
. In such cases, we resort to entropy solutions.

3.4.
Existence of entropy solutions

Since Theorem
3.20
does not include the case when
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
, we will need the following result, whose proof follows
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.24
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
, then the sequence of approximating solutions
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega_{T})
for every
p<\frac{N+2}{N}=1^{\star\star}
and the sequence of gradients
(\nabla u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{q}(\Omega_{T})
for every
q<\frac{N+2}{N+1}=1^{\star}
.

Proof.

Let
1<\lambda<2
and choose
\frac{1-(1+u_{n})^{1-\lambda}}{\lambda-1}
as a test function in the second equation of (
3.1
). Repeating the same steps as in the proof of Theorem
3.20
, we define the function

F_{n}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}(\sigma+1)^{-\lambda}T_{n}(\sigma)d\sigma

and choose
F_{n}(u_{n})
as a test function in the second equation of (
3.1
) to get

\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+1)^{-\lambda}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla u_{n}=\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla F_{n}(u_{n})=\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})^{\theta}F_{n}(u_{n})

\leq\int_{\Omega}T_{n}(u_{n})^{\theta}\frac{(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda}\leq\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}\frac{(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda}

Now choose

g(u_{n})=\frac{1-(1+u_{n})^{1-\lambda}}{\lambda-1}

(which is bounded by
\frac{1}{\lambda-1}
) as a test function in the first equation of (
3.1
) to get

\langle(u_{n})_{t},g(u_{n})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+1)^{-\lambda}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla u_{n}

\leq\int_{\Omega}(u_{n}+1)^{-\lambda}T_{n}(u_{n})M(x)\nabla\psi_{n}\nabla u_{n}+\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)g(u_{n}).

Which implies

(3.23)

\langle(u_{n})_{t},g(u_{n})\rangle+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{(u_{n}+1)^{\lambda}}\leq\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}\frac{(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda}+\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\int_{\Omega}f(x,t).

We now focus on the term

\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}\left((u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1\right).

As usual, we let
k\geq 0
and apply Hölder’s inequality along with Lemma
3.4
to get

\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}\frac{(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda}\leq\int_{\left|u_{n}(t)\right|\leq k}u_{n}^{\theta}\frac{(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda}+\int_{\left|u_{n}(t)\right|>k}u_{n}^{\theta}\frac{(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda}

\leq k^{\theta}\frac{(k+1)^{2-\lambda}}{2-\lambda}\left|\Omega\right|+\left(\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}}{k^{1-\theta\frac{N}{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left((u_{n}(t)+1)^{2-\lambda}-1\right)^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}.

Since for every
\eta\geq 0
we have that

\left[(1+\eta)^{2-\lambda}-1\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq(1+\eta)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}=\left[(1+\eta)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right]+1,

we have

\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{2-\lambda}-1\right]^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}}\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\{\left[\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right]+1\right\}^{2^{*}}\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right]^{2^{*}}+C.

It follows, since
2^{*}\geq 2
, that

\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{\theta}\left((u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1\right)\leq k^{\theta+2-\lambda}\left|\Omega\right|+\left(\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}}{k^{1-\theta\frac{N}{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}\left(C\left[\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right]^{2^{*}}\right]^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}+C\right).

On the other hand, by Sobolev’s inequality, we have

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\lambda}}=\frac{4}{(2-\lambda)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left[\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right]\right|^{2}\geq\frac{4\mathcal{S}}{(2-\lambda)^{2}}\left[\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right]^{2^{*}}\right]^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}.

Notice that, if we define
G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}g(\sigma)d\sigma
, the first term becomes
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}G(u_{n})
.

Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}G(u_{n})+\left(\alpha-C\frac{(2-\lambda)^{2}}{4\mathcal{S}}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{(2-\lambda)k^{\frac{2}{N}-\theta}}\right)\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)+k^{\theta}\frac{(k+1)^{2-\lambda}}{2-\lambda}\left|\Omega\right|+\frac{C}{2-\lambda}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{k^{\frac{2}{N}-\theta}}.

Choosing
k=k_{0}
such that

\alpha-C\frac{(2-\lambda)^{2}}{4\mathcal{S}}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{(2-\lambda)k^{\frac{2}{N}-\theta}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}

we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}G(u_{n})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\lambda}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)+k_{0}^{\theta}\frac{(k_{0}+1)^{2-\lambda}}{2-\lambda}\left|\Omega\right|+\frac{C}{2-\lambda}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{k_{0}^{\frac{2}{N}-\theta}}.

Integrating from
0
to
t
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}G(u_{n})(t)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\lambda}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\int_{\Omega_{T}}f(x,t)+k_{0}^{\theta}\frac{(k_{0}+1)^{2-\lambda}}{2-\lambda}\left|\Omega\right|T+\frac{C}{2-\lambda}\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{k_{0}^{\frac{2}{N}-\theta}}T,

That is

\int_{\Omega}G(u_{n})(t)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{\left(1+u_{n}\right)^{\lambda}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})},\lambda\right),

where
C\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})},\lambda\right)\to\infty
as
\lambda\to 1
.

Since
G(s)=s+(1+s)^{2-\lambda}-1
(which, for
s\geq 0
, is greater than
s^{2-\lambda}
) and
u_{n}\geq 0
, we have

(3.24)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})},\lambda\right)\\
&\|\nabla\left((u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})},\lambda\right).\end{aligned}\right.

Applying the parabolic Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality
3.9
, we have

\|(u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\|_{L^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\|(u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{4}{N}}\|\nabla\left((u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\right)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}.

Since
\frac{2-\lambda}{2}\in(0,1)
and
u_{n}\geq 0
we have, by concavity

(u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\leq u_{n}^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}

thus,

\|(u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|u_{n}^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|u_{n}^{2-\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By convexity, since
2-\lambda>1
, we get

\|(u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|u_{n}^{2-\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|(u_{n}+1)^{2-\lambda}-1\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

To sum up, we have proved that, for any
\lambda\in(1,2)
,

\|(u_{n}+1)^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}}-1\|_{L^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})},\lambda\right)^{1+\frac{2}{N}}

which implies, by comparison, that the sequence
(u_{n}^{\frac{2-\lambda}{2}})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})
. This means that
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{{2-\lambda}\frac{N+2}{N}}(\Omega_{T})
for any
\lambda\in(1,2)
, i.e.
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega_{T})
for any
p<\frac{N+2}{N}
.

We now follow
[
3
]
: as before, choosing
q<2
and using Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{q}=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{q}}{(1+u_{n})^{\frac{q\lambda}{2}}}(1+u_{n})^{\frac{q\lambda}{2}}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}\frac{\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{2}}{(1+u_{n})^{\lambda}}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}(1+u_{n})^{\frac{q\lambda}{2-q}}\right)^{1-\frac{q}{2}},

thus

\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|\nabla u_{n}\right|^{q}\leq\tilde{C}\left(\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{T})},\lambda\right)\left(\int_{\Omega_{T}}(1+u_{n})^{\frac{q\lambda}{2-q}}\right)^{1-\frac{q}{2}}.

Setting
\frac{q\lambda}{2-q}={2-\lambda}\frac{N+2}{N}
, we obtain that
(\nabla u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{q}(\Omega_{T})
with,

q=q(\lambda)=\frac{2(2-\lambda)(N+2)}{\lambda N+(2-\lambda)(N+2)}

which is always smaller that
\frac{N+2}{N+1}
and tends to
\frac{N+2}{N+1}
as
\lambda\to 1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\langle(\mathcal{T}_{k}(u_{n}))_{t},\varphi\rangle+&\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{k}(u_{n})\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}_{k}(u_{n})\nabla u_{n}\varphi\\

(3.26)

\begin{split}(\mathcal{T}_{k}(u_{n}))_{t}-&\operatorname{div}(A(x,t)\nabla\mathcal{T}_{k}(u_{n}))+A(x,t)\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla u_{n}\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}_{k}(u_{n})\\
