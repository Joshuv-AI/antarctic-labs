Deep learning analysis of single-lead electrocardiograms enables pragmatic heart failure risk assessment in the general population
Authors: Meraj Neyazi, Jan P Bremer, Jan Brederecke, Marius S Knorr, Ferdinand Seum, Carla Reinbold, Stefan Gross, Dora Csengeri, Stefan Blankenberg, Marcus Dörr, Marcus Vollmer, Renate Bonin-Schnabel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsEffective heart failure (HF) prevention requires early identification of high-risk individuals, yet population-wide stratification remains difficult. We evaluated whether deep learning using single-lead (lead I) electrocardiograms (ECGs), obtainable from medical systems and wearables, enables population-scale risk assessment. We developed AI-HF to estimate incident clinical HF risk using UK Biobank (UKB) data, validating in the prospective SHIP-START and SHIP-TREND cohorts.Methods and resultsThe analysis included 31 740 UKB participants (median age 64, 5.2 year follow-up, 243 events), 3025 SHIP-START participants (age 50, 15 year follow-up, 166 events), and 1342 SHIP-TREND participants (age 51, 9 year follow-up, 84 events). Participants with prevalent HF were excluded. Performance was evaluated at a harmonized 5-year prediction horizon. C-indices for incident clinical HF were 0.693 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.654–0.732] in UKB, 0.715 (0.652–0.777) in SHIP-START, and 0.791 (0.749–0.833) in SHIP-TREND. Hazard ratios per standard deviation increase in AI-HF output were 1.67 (1.56–1.79), 1.43 (1.25–1.65), and 1.46 (1.34–1.59), respectively (all P < 0.001). Adding biometric variables improved discrimination modestly (C-indices: 0.714, 0.718, and 0.77).ConclusionAcross cohorts, AI-HF identified individuals at elevated 5-year incident clinical HF risk using single-lead ECGs. Given the ubiquity of wearables, this method may enable population-scale assessment to support targeted prevention and early intervention.Keywords: Chronic heart failure, Prevention, Cardiovascular risk assessment, Wearables, Artificial intelligence, Machine, Deep learning

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a major public health issue, affecting approximately 26 million individuals worldwide.1,2 Identifying individuals at high risk for HF can enable targeted preventive measures or timely diagnosis, facilitating earlier treatment and potentially slowing disease progression.3,4 This is underlined by the observation that screening-detected HF patients exhibit improved survival rates.5 However, identifying patients at high risk for developing HF in community settings remains challenging, because symptoms are often nonspecific or absent, which frequently leads to a diagnosis only after an initial HF decompensation in a large proportion of patients.6–8 Multiple risk prediction models have been developed to support selection of individuals who would benefit most from preventive measures.9–11 Nonetheless, their routine application has been hampered by moderate discrimination ability and lack of external validation.12

To address this issue, a pragmatic approach could involve artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted large-scale population screening to identify individuals at risk for incident clinical HF. Modern AI models have derived clinically relevant information from 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), such as for screening for prevalent cardiac dysfunction13 or atrial fibrillation14 in selected populations. Wearable-based screening for atrial fibrillation via pulse irregularity has also proven feasible.15 With the rising popularity of modern wearables like smartwatches capable of recording single-lead ECGs, AI-assisted HF risk assessment could be scaled to broader populations. Users of such wearables could be encouraged to seek medical attention, while medical professionals could use the risk assessment as an additional pillar to guide further diagnostic steps.

The possibility of pragmatic risk assessment for incident clinical HF in the general population using single-lead ECGs remains uncertain. To explore this opportunity, we trained a deep learning-driven (DL) model, AI-HF, using single-lead ECG data extracted from medical-grade multi-lead recordings from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large, prospective population-based cohort, and externally validated the model in two external cohorts. Additionally, we explored whether the incorporation of readily available biometric and lifestyle parameters could improve discrimination.

Methods

The European Hearth Rhythm Association AI checklist

We followed the European Hearth Rhythm Association (EHRA) AI reporting guidelines16 and provide a completed checklist in the Supplementary material.

Study cohorts

The UKB is a large, prospective observational cohort study that recruited 500 000 individuals aged 37 to 73 years between 2006 and 2010 across 22 assessment centres in the United Kingdom. At recruitment, biological samples, anthropometric measures, and questionnaires were collected.17

For external validation, we used two independent prospective observational cohorts within the Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP), SHIP-START, and SHIP-TREND. SHIP-START included 4308 individuals from Western Pomerania, Germany, aged 20 to 81, through a two-step stratified random sampling procedure between 1997 and 2001 (SHIP-START-0). Follow-up examinations were performed on 3300 participants between 2002 and 2006 (SHIP-START-1), 2333 participants between 2008 and 2012 (SHIP-START-2), and 1718 participants between 2014 and 2016 (SHIP-START-3). Median follow-up times from baseline were 5.0, 10.9, and 15.8 years, respectively. The second cohort, SHIP-TREND, also recruited randomly selected individuals from Western Pomerania. Baseline examinations were conducted on 4420 individuals between 2008 and 2011 (SHIP-TREND-0). A follow-up study was conducted between 2016 and 2019 with 2507 individuals (SHIP-TREND-1) with a median follow-up period of 7.3 years. All examinations of SHIP participants were conducted according to the SHIP protocol.18

In the UKB, resting ECGs were recorded immediately before cycle ergometry from 4-lead and 12-lead ECGs. Lead I from available ECGs was extracted (CAM-USB 6.5, Cardiosoft v6.51, GE Healthcare, Chalfont St Giles, Buckinghamshire, UK). In SHIP, resting 12-lead ECGs were recorded and lead I was extracted for analysis (SHIP-START-0 to −1: Personal-120-LT, Esaote Biomedica, Genova, Italy; SHIP-START-2 to −3 and SHIP-TREND-0 to −1: Welch Allyn SE-PRO-600 Cardioperfect Pro Recorder/Hill-Rom Holdings, Batesville, Indiana, USA). We focused on lead I because it closely approximates the signal obtainable from wearable devices. In UKB, lead I was directly measured rather than synthesized, with electrodes placed at the right and left wrists (RA, LA) and both antecubital fossae in a true Einthoven lead I configuration (LA–RA), which is comparable to standard 12-lead systems and analogous to consumer wearable electrode configurations.

HF diagnosis in the UKB was algorithmically defined based on hospital in-patient records or primary care records. In SHIP, HF was defined according to the Rotterdam Study criteria.19 A diagnosis of HF was considered if one symptom was present [dyspnoea at rest or on exertion, leg oedema, or chest pain or discomfort when walking uphill or in a hurry (angina pectoris)] with evidence of cardiac disease (prior heart surgery, prior myocardial infarction, definite ECG anterior or inferior myocardial infarction, atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation or ECG left ventricular hypertrophy), while symptoms could not be attributed to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (self-reported chronic bronchitis or use of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease medication—ATC code R03). This approach allows us to apply consistent criteria in large, population-based cohorts and to define an outcome of incident clinical HF. However, because echocardiography is not available for most participants, these criteria do not permit differentiation between ejection-fraction–based HF phenotypes. Individuals with prevalent HF at baseline were excluded from the analysis.

The UK Biobank Research Ethics Committee (reference 11/NW/0382) approved the UKB. The University Medicine Greifswald Ethics Committee approved the SHIP studies (approval number BB 39/08). All participants provided written, informed consent.

Model development and testing

We used a one-dimensional ResNet convolutional neural network to predict incident clinical HF by analysing single-lead ECG data.20 The UKB cohort with available digital ECGs initially comprised 98 520 individuals; after excluding participants with HF events prior to baseline or loss to follow-up, 31 740 remained for analysis. The target variable for model training was incident HF, defined as new-onset HF during the observational period. At the participant level, the UKB cohort was randomly split into a 66% development set (n = 20 945) and an independent 33% hold-out internal validation set (n = 10 795), with each participant uniquely assigned to one subset to avoid overlap. Within the 66% development set, we used six-fold cross-validation for model selection and early stopping: in each iteration, five-folds were used for training and one-fold for validation, yielding out-of-fold performance estimates that were used to tune learning rate, ECG segment length, and augmentation strength. After hyperparameter optimization, the final network was retrained on all development data (66% of UKB) using the optimal configuration, and this single model was then applied to the 33% hold-out set for internal validation; the hold-out set was not used at any stage of model training or hyperparameter tuning. To facilitate model training on single-lead ECGs, lead I was computationally extracted from all available 12-lead ECGs sampled at 250 Hz. These signals were preprocessed with a fifth-order Butterworth filter and augmented during training using random baseline shifts, random cropping, voltage scaling, and dropout.21

Because of class imbalance arising from the low HF event rate in UKB, we applied 10-fold oversampling of positive cases within the training batches to ensure that HF events were adequately represented. To obtain stable estimates and confidence intervals (CIs) of Fine–Gray model C-indices, we additionally performed 10-fold cross-validation of the C-index within the evaluation cohort, always computing C-indices from out-of-fold predictions only; this procedure affected only the estimation of performance metrics and did not involve any further neural network training, thereby keeping all reported discrimination and calibration metrics strictly out-of-sample. External validation was conducted in the independent SHIP-START and SHIP-TREND cohorts, in which lead I of resting 12-lead ECGs was similarly extracted for analysis; SHIP-START initially included 3545 individuals with digital ECGs, of whom 3025 remained after excluding participants with events prior to baseline or loss to follow-up, and SHIP-TREND included 4396 individuals, of whom 2650 were retained after the same exclusions. Among the participants included in these analyses, none had ECG recordings excluded for technical reasons. Training details, including network architecture and optimization settings, are provided in Supplementary Methods 1. The model was developed for research purposes only, and code and trained weights are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (25th, 75th percentile) and binary variables as absolute and relative frequencies. We used complete-case data.

We first developed AI-HF, a deep-learning network that generates an ECG-based risk score for incident clinical HF. To assess its prognostic value, we fitted Fine–Gray sub-distribution hazard models to assess the association between the AI-HF score and incident HF, accounting for death without HF as a competing event. Deaths with HF as underlying cause (ICD-10: I11.0, I13.0, I13.2, I50.−) were reclassified as HF events at the midpoint between the last HF-free follow-up visit and death. Three model specifications were evaluated: (i) AI-HF score only; (ii) AI-HF score with the biometric and lifestyle parameters [sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking]; and (iii) biometric and lifestyle parameters only. These covariates were selected because they are established cardiovascular risk factors and are straightforward to obtain in routine care, providing a practical comparator to an automatically derived single-lead ECG risk score. For each model, we report sub-distribution hazard ratios (sdHRs) per 1 SD increase in the predictor(s) along with their 95% CIs. All analyses were performed separately within each cohort.

All performance metrics [C-indices, sdHRs, and net reclassification improvement (NRI)] were evaluated at a harmonized 5-year prediction horizon; individuals with follow-up beyond 5 years were censored at 5 years. C-indices from the Fine–Gray model were computed using 10-fold cross-validation. Categorical NRI was computed at risk thresholds of 5% and 10%, comparing the Fine–Gray cumulative incidence function predictions of the enhanced models against the base model, with CIs from bootstrap resampling (SHIP: 1000 samples; UKB: 200 samples), following Pencina et al.22 To evaluate potential clinical utility, we performed 5-year decision curve analysis (DCA) for all three models across threshold probabilities ranging from 0% to 5%. Model-based strategies were compared with ‘treat all’ and ‘treat none’ reference strategies within each cohort. A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.0.5; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/).

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 31 740 participants (52% women) from the UKB were included in the analysis. These individuals had a median age of 64, a median BMI of 26.8 kg/m2, and 3.7% were active smokers. During a median follow-up of 5.2 years, 243 participants (0.8%) developed HF, and 645 participants (2.0%) died. (Table 1).

For SHIP-START, we analysed 3025 participants (52% women). For SHIP-TREND, we analysed 2650 participants (51% women). The participants in these cohorts were younger, with median ages of 50 and 51 years, respectively. Additionally, the median BMIs were slightly elevated (26.9 and 27.2 kg/m2), and there were some differences in smoking prevalence, with a rate of 29.0% for SHIP-START and 23.8% for SHIP-TREND. Over a median follow-up of 10.9 years for SHIP-START and 7.3 years for SHIP-TREND, 166 participants in SHIP-START (5.5%) and 84 participants in SHIP-TREND (3.2%) developed clinical HF.

No participants were excluded on the basis of age, ethnicity, or gender. Participants were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, interpretation, or dissemination of this particular study.

Single-lead ECG DL model for incident HF prediction

In the UKB, we developed the DL-model AI-HF and tested its performance in an internal validation subset of the UKB using Fine–Gray sub-distribution hazard models, yielding a C-index of 0.693 (CI 0.654–0.732) for single-lead ECG prediction of incident clinical HF within 5 years (Figure 1, Table 2). External validation of AI-HF in the SHIP cohorts confirmed consistent results for incident clinical HF prediction over 5 years (SHIP-START: C-index 0.715 (CI 0.652–0.777); SHIP-TREND: C-index 0.791 (CI 0.749–0.833). Furthermore, the model was robust with a sdHR per SD increase of 1.67 (CI 1.56–1.79, P < 0.001) in the UKB and positive but reduced sdHRs in SHIP-START (1.43, CI 1.25–1.65, P < 0.001) and SHIP-TREND (1.46, CI 1.34–1.59, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Combination of single-lead DL information with basic biometric variables and risk factors

In the UKB, adding basic biometric parameters, including sex, BMI, and smoking status to AI-HF’s single-lead ECG analysis increased the C-index from 0.693 (CI 0.654–0.732) to 0.714 (CI 0.678–0.750) over 5 years. This modest improvement was replicated in the external validation cohorts, with C-index gains of 0.003 in SHIP-START and 0.021 in SHIP-TREND (Figure 1, Table 2).

When compared to a model based solely on the aforementioned predictors without incorporating AI-HF, we observed a trend towards a weaker discriminatory ability over 5 years in the UKB (C-index 0.605, CI 0.567–0.644) and SHIP-TREND (C-index 0.689, CI 0.633–0.744) but a similar discriminatory ability in SHIP-START (C-index 0.687, CI 0.612–0.763; Figure 1, Table 2).

Further analysis comparing the covariate-only model with the combined AI-HF and covariate model revealed modest reclassification gains in the UK Biobank but not in the external cohorts. In the UK Biobank, the overall NRI was 0.018 (CI −0.006–0.042) over 5 years, driven by improved event reclassification of 0.022 (CI −0.002–0.046) despite a slight decline in non-event reclassification of −0.004 (CI −0.005–0.004). In SHIP-START, the overall NRI was 0.052 (CI −0.053–0.157), indicating no meaningful change, and in SHIP-TREND it was 0.156 (CI 0.048–0.264), reflecting improved event reclassification (0.132; CI 0.024–0.24) and positive non-event reclassification (0.024; CI 0.011–0.037). When biometric and lifestyle covariates were added to AI-HF in the UK Biobank, the overall NRI was 0.028 (CI 0.001–0.055), while SHIP-START showed no significant change (−0.003; CI −0.103–0.097) and SHIP-TREND showed a positive but non-significant trend (0.119; CI −0.019–0.257) (see Supplementary material online, Table S1).

Decision curve analysis

Across all three cohorts, the net benefit of AI-HF–based strategies was small within the clinically relevant 0–5% 5-year risk range (Figure 2). In SHIP-START, all three models demonstrated a positive but modest net benefit at low thresholds (≈0.01–0.015). Net benefit gradually declined with increasing thresholds and approached the ‘treat none’ strategy at ≈5%, 10% and 2.5% for SHIP-START, SHIP-TREND, and UKB respectively.

In SHIP-TREND, which had the highest event rate, net benefit peaked around 0.02–0.03. Within this range, Base + DL and DL generally did not show high absolute differences to Base model (<0.004 net benefit units).

In UK Biobank, the very low 5-year incidence (<1%) led to uniformly low net benefit across all models. Although the curves were slightly above zero at thresholds <1%, none of the models provided a clinically meaningful advantage over ‘treat none’ across the prespecified decision range.

The ‘treat all’ strategy performed substantially worse than all model-based strategies in every cohort, becoming markedly negative even at thresholds around 1%, confirming that population-wide HF screening would not be beneficial in low-risk settings.

Discussion

In this study, we explore the potential for pragmatic risk assessment and identification of individuals at high risk for developing clinical HF in the general population using single-lead ECGs analysed by a DL model, which can be applied for screening.

Despite advances in treatment, incident HF is associated with a poor prognosis, including a 5-year mortality rate of 67%.23 Current guidelines emphasize the significance of targeted prevention.3 However, this approach necessitates the identification of individuals at high risk for incident HF.

To address this challenge, we developed AI-HF, a convolutional neural network-based model capable of extracting clinical HF-risk in unselected, community-based cohorts using single-lead ECGs.

The model showed good discriminatory performance across three independent cohorts, and its prognostic performance was moderately improved when combined with simple biometric and lifestyle measures. These findings support the generalizability of AI-HF and demonstrate that clinically meaningful HF risk information can be obtained from a minimal-input ECG-based model.

It is important to contextualize AI-HF relative to existing risk stratification tools. Traditional HF risk scores, such as the Framingham HF and Health ABC scores, typically achieve C-statistics of approximately 0.70–0.75 in external, predominantly older cohorts, but require multiple clinical input variables.24,25 In the domain of AI, performance varies by input complexity: convolutional neural networks applied to standard 12-lead ECGs have reported AUCs around 0.75 for long-term HF,26 while single-lead models trained to detect left ventricular systolic dysfunction yield C-statistics between 0.72 and 0.83.27 Other recent frameworks, such as the AIRE-HF 12-lead model (C-index 0.79)28 or multi-modal approaches combining ECG with approximated heart rate variability (C-index 0.85),29 demonstrate high discrimination but often rely on data sources not easily obtained from consumer devices.

Against this backdrop, our single-lead AI-HF model achieved C-indices of 0.693 in UKB, 0.715 in SHIP-START, and 0.791 in SHIP-TREND. These results demonstrate that AI-HF provides discrimination that is at least comparable to traditional multivariable scores and contemporary 12-lead AI models. Crucially, this performance was sustained across independent, prospective cohorts, including two with median ages below 50 years, suggesting utility in younger, lower-risk populations where traditional scores are less validated. Unlike models serving as surrogates for structural disease (e.g. detecting left ventricular systolic dysfunction), AI-HF directly models incident clinical HF, offering a parsimonious, single-lead solution suitable for population-scale implementation.

While traditional scores rely on echocardiography, serum biomarker levels, or the diagnosis of preexisting conditions such as diabetes mellitus or arterial hypertension, which are often underdiagnosed in the general population,30–33 AI-HF requires only a single-lead ECG and optional basic biometric and lifestyle parameters. The single-lead ECG signal can be produced by an estimated 200 million smartwatch users34 and can be recorded in any primary-care office; even the elderly population, which is at higher risk for HF, is showing increased interest and confidence in wearable technology.35 Enabling accessible AI-based HF risk assessment has also been explored by others, such as Dhingra et al.36 who applied an image-based deep-learning model to 12-lead ECGs within routine clinical workflows. While this approach suits opportunistic screening in healthcare settings, our single-lead, wearable-compatible model is designed to extend risk assessment into the general population. Dynamic DL competing-risk models represent an additional promising direction for future work in cohorts with repeated ECG ascertainment.37

Prior studies have repeatedly demonstrated that DL algorithms can extract clinically relevant information from latent signatures in ECGs, which may not be interpretable to the human eye. These include models for detecting asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction, valvular defects, and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, as well as predicting serum biomarker levels; this further supports the notion that AI-HF carries biological plausibility.13,14,38,39

To explore a potential translation of this model to clinical utility, we performed a DCA. This analysis demonstrated that AI-HF provides a positive net benefit over default strategies at low thresholds (approximately 1–5%), a range clinically relevant for population screening. While the low absolute incidence of HF in the general population limits the magnitude of this benefit, the analysis confirms that a ‘screen-all’ approach is unwarranted. Consequently, AI-HF is best positioned not as a diagnostic instrument, but as a pragmatic triage tool. In practice, individuals exceeding a defined risk threshold could be prompted to consult their general practitioner for targeted downstream testing, such as NT-proBNP measurement or referral for echocardiography. This approach allows for earlier clinical contact and optimized resource allocation while leaving final diagnostic decisions to physician judgement.

Given that risk scores that are easy to obtain are more likely to be adopted40 and that AI-HF couples high discrimination with minimal data requirements, our results suggest that a model like AI-HF could have a significant impact on population-wide risk evaluation and public health.

Our study has some limitations. We did not have access to smartwatch- or other wearable-derived ECGs for training and validation, as such data are not yet available at sufficient scale with detailed phenotyping and long-term follow-up. Instead, all single-lead inputs were derived from medical-grade multi-lead systems by extracting lead I from available 4-lead or 12-lead ECGs (UKB: 4-lead GE Cardiosoft; SHIP: 12-lead Esaote/Welch Allyn). Single-lead ECGs recorded by current wearables may have a lower signal-to-noise ratio than medical-grade devices,41 and, even if available in large numbers, would not represent a uniform data source because hardware, firmware, and signal-processing pipelines differ across manufacturers and device generations. Nonetheless, within our datasets AI-HF performed consistently across different ECG systems, suggesting some robustness to variation in signal processing and noise. In addition, prospective studies have demonstrated the general usability of wearable-derived single-lead ECGs for extracting medically relevant information.42 It has been shown that neural networks that were originally trained on medical-grade data are adaptable for use with wearable-derived ECGs.43 Taken together, these observations support the potential transferability of AI-HF to consumer wearables, but dedicated, device-level validation studies will be required before any screening applications can be recommended in that context.

We acknowledge that the Rotterdam Study criteria used in SHIP do not fully align with HF diagnosis according to current ESC guidelines. Retrospective reclassification based on ESC criteria is not feasible in these cohorts because key data, such as LVEF, natriuretic peptide levels, detailed echocardiography (including E/E′ ratio and TR Vmax), and systematically recorded symptoms at the time of diagnosis, were not consistently available. This is a limitation of our outcome definition, as it does not allow differentiation between ejection-fraction–based HF phenotypes, although it still captures incident clinical HF. At the same time, this approach enables the use of large, well-characterized cohorts with long-term follow-up as external validation datasets, which is a major strength of our study.

Another limitation is that the cohorts we studied are all based in Europe. More diverse cohorts from different regions could further validate the generalizability of AI-HF. Additionally, DL models in general are considered ‘black boxes’ in that they lack so-called explainability. This makes it challenging to comprehend their decision-making processes and identify potential biases or errors. Therefore, the application of these models requires caution and extensive testing, especially in high-risk domains such as health care.44 In the context of this study, it is important to note that a model such as AI-HF would not be used to diagnose HF, but rather to prompt the consideration of obtaining confirmatory diagnostics or implementing preventive measures.

The strength of our analysis is the robust discrimination in the independent validation studies which increases trust in its predictive ability. Prospective trials are certainly needed to elucidate whether a model like AI-HF can improve HF prevention in the general population.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that AI-HF performs well for risk assessment of incident clinical HF in the community. By analysing single-lead ECGs, which are becoming increasingly available in the general population due to growing popularity of smartwatches and other wearable devices, a DL model such as AI-HF may represent a pragmatic and highly scalable tool to effectively screen for HF risk and expand HF prevention.
