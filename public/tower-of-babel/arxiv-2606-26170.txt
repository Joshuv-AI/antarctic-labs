Sensitivity of evolutionary entropy in Lefkovitch matrices
Henrique M. Oliveira

Abstract
Evolutionary entropy, introduced by Demetrius, is a demographic invariant that quantifies the temporal organization of structured populations. Explicit sensitivity expressions for this quantity were derived by Demetrius, Gundlach and Ziehe for age-structured Leslie matrices, establishing the foundations of entropy-based perturbation theory. In this paper we develop a complete sensitivity theory for evolutionary entropy in irreducible Lefkovitch matrices. Using the Perron–Frobenius representation of the associated Markov chain, we derive explicit closed-form expressions for the stationary distribution, generation time, evolutionary entropy and its partial derivatives with respect to fertility, transition and retention parameters. The resulting identities are expressed directly in terms of demographic coefficients, Perron eigenvectors, the dominant eigenvalue and the reproductive potential. The entropy representation obtained here gives a natural decomposition into transition and retention components and clarifies the distinct mechanisms through which demographic uncertainty is generated in stage-structured populations. We further show that the theory specializes immediately to open-group Leslie matrices, a class that has been shown to comprise a large fraction of empirical demographic models. The results extend the entropy sensitivity theory of Demetrius–Gundlach–Ziehe from age-structured to general stage-structured populations and provide practical tools for comparative demographic analysis, perturbation studies, demographic robustness, and the investigation of life-history strategies. Several biological examples are presented, illustrating how entropy decomposition and sensitivity analysis reveal complementary aspects of population organization.

Introduction

Structured population models constitute one of the most successful
applications of matrix theory to biology. Since the pioneering works
of Leslie
[
13
,
14
]
, matrix population models have
provided a mathematical setting for describing population growth,
stable demographic distributions, reproductive value and long-term
population dynamics. To overcome the limitations of purely
age-structured descriptions, Lefkovitch
[
12
]

introduced stage-structured matrices allowing individuals to remain
within the same developmental stage for variable periods of time.
These models have become standard tools in ecology, conservation
biology, forestry, plant demography and evolutionary biology

[
3
,
21
,
15
,
20
,
17
,
16
,
19
,
11
]
.
Today, matrix population models form the mathematical core of large
demographic repositories such as COMADRE and COMPADRE and are
routinely used to analyse structured populations across a broad range
of ecological contexts.

A complementary line of research was initiated by Demetrius

[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, who introduced
evolutionary entropy as a quantitative measure of demographic
organisation. Whereas the dominant eigenvalue describes asymptotic
population growth, evolutionary entropy characterises the temporal
organisation of demographic trajectories generated by the underlying
life cycle. Through its interpretation in terms of Markov chains, it
connects demographic structure, robustness, adaptation and
organisation, thereby providing information complementary to that
contained in the population growth rate.

An important aspect of this theory concerns sensitivity analysis.
Demetrius
[
7
]
derived sensitivity expressions for the
dominant eigenvalue, relating population growth to reproductive value and age-specific vital rates. This theory was subsequently developed by Caswell and collaborators

[
3
,
4
]
. Subsequently, Demetrius, Gundlach and
Ziehe
[
6
]
obtained explicit sensitivity formulae for
evolutionary entropy in age-structured Leslie matrices. Their results
established evolutionary entropy as a natural object for
perturbation analysis and provided formulae directly expressed in
terms of biologically meaningful demographic parameters.

The theory of
[
6
]
, however, is restricted to Leslie
matrices. This limitation is significant because many contemporary
applications involve stage-, size- or state-structured populations
for which Lefkovitch matrices provide a more realistic description.
Although sensitivity and elasticity theory for population growth is
well developed in the Lefkovitch setting, explicit sensitivity formulae for evolutionary entropy do not appear to have been obtained.

A key observation motivating the present work is the similarity
theorem established in
[
1
]
, which shows that
every irreducible Lefkovitch matrix is similar to a suitable
pseudo-Leslie matrix. This result establishes a bridge between
age-structured and stage-structured population models and suggests
that an entropy sensitivity theory for Lefkovitch matrices should
exist.

The purpose of this paper is to derive explicit closed-form expressions for evolutionary entropy, generation time, the associated Markov representation and the corresponding sensitivity coefficients in irreducible Lefkovitch matrices.
The resulting formulae are expressed directly in terms of fertility,
transition and retention parameters, the dominant eigenvalue and the
associated reproductive potential.

The extension from Leslie to Lefkovitch matrices is not obtained by a
simple replacement of variables. Stage-retention effects introduce
new demographic quantities, additional sources of uncertainty and new
sensitivity directions. Table

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 develops an explicit
Markov representation for irreducible Lefkovitch matrices and derives
closed-form expressions for the stationary distribution, generation
time and evolutionary entropy. Particular attention is given to the
important class of open-group Leslie matrices, which arise
frequently in demographic applications. Section 3 introduces a
natural decomposition of evolutionary entropy into transition and
retention components. Section 4 contains the main sensitivity
formulae. Section 5 presents illustrative biological examples.
Finally, Section 6 discusses the implications of the results and
directions for future research.

2
Entropy representation for Lefkovitch matrices

Let

L=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}&\cdots&m_{d}\\
b_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&0\\
0&b_{2}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&c_{d}\end{pmatrix}

(1)

be an irreducible pure Lefkovitch matrix, where

m_{j}\geq 0,\qquad b_{j}>0,\qquad c_{j}\geq 0.

Denote by
\lambda
the Perron eigenvalue of
L
, and let

u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})^{T}

be the corresponding positive right Perron eigenvector.

Remark 2.1
.

Here and throughout the paper, empty sums are interpreted as zero and
empty products are interpreted as
1
. Furthermore, following the
standard convention in information theory and entropy theory, terms of
the form

0\log 0

are interpreted as zero, since

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x\log x=0.

This convention is classical in the definition of Shannon entropy and
related entropy functionals

[
18
,
5
]
.

Our first objective is to derive explicit expressions for the
probability distribution associated with
L
, the stationary
distribution of the corresponding Markov chain, and the resulting
formula for evolutionary entropy.

Theorem 2.2

(Generalized Euler–Lotka representation)
.

Define

R_{1}=1,\qquad R_{j}=\prod_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{b_{h}}{\lambda-c_{h+1}},\qquad j=2,\ldots,d.

(2)

Then

u_{j}=u_{1}R_{j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,d,

(3)

and the quantities

q_{j}=\frac{m_{j}R_{j}}{\lambda},\qquad j=1,\ldots,d,

(4)

satisfy

\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}=1.

(5)

Equivalently,

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{m_{j}R_{j}}{\lambda}=1,

(6)

which may be regarded as a generalized Euler–Lotka equation for
irreducible Lefkovitch matrices.

The proof is given in
A.2
.

The quantities
q_{j}
define a probability distribution associated
with the demographic transitions generated by the matrix
L
. In
the Leslie case, they coincide with the classical reproductive
distribution of Demetrius, Gundlach and Ziehe.

Let

U=\operatorname{diag}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}),

(7)

and define

P=\frac{1}{\lambda}U^{-1}LU.

(8)

The matrix
P
is stochastic and determines the Markov chain
naturally associated with the Lefkovitch matrix.

Theorem 2.3

(Stationary distribution)
.

Define

\eta_{i}=\frac{\lambda-c_{i}}{\lambda},\qquad\rho_{i}=\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

(9)

and

Q_{i}=\sum_{j=i}^{d}q_{j},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d.

(10)

Then the stationary distribution

\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})

of
P
is given by

\pi_{1}=\frac{1}{T},\qquad\pi_{i}=\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}T},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

(11)

where

T=1+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}.

(12)

The proof is given in
A.3
.

The stationary distribution provides the bridge between the
demographic dynamics generated by
L
and the information-theoretic
quantities introduced by Demetrius.

Theorem 2.4

(Explicit entropy representation)
.

The evolutionary entropy of the irreducible Lefkovitch matrix
L

is

H=\frac{S}{T},

(13)

where

S=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}\left(\eta_{i}\log\eta_{i}+\rho_{i}\log\rho_{i}\right),

(14)

and
T
is given by
(
12
).

The proof is given in
A.4
.

Remark 2.5
.

The entropy formulae remain well defined when some retention
probabilities satisfy
\rho_{i}=0
, since

\lim_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\rho\log\rho=0.

Naturally, the entropy representation extends continuously to
Leslie and open-group Leslie matrices.

The quantity S may be interpreted as the Shannon entropy associated with the demographic trajectories of the underlying Markov chain, whereas evolutionary entropy is obtained after normalization by the generation time T.

The entropy functional S naturally separates into two distinct components. The first describes uncertainty from transitions among stages, whereas the second describes uncertainty generated by residence within stages.

Corollary 2.6

(Open-group Leslie matrices)
.

Consider the open-group Leslie matrix

L=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}&\cdots&m_{d-1}&m_{d}\\
b_{1}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&b_{2}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&b_{d-1}&\rho\end{pmatrix}.

This matrix is the particular Lefkovitch matrix obtained by setting

c_{2}=\cdots=c_{d-1}=0,\qquad c_{d}=\rho.

Therefore,

\eta_{i}=1,\qquad\rho_{i}=0,\qquad i=2,\ldots,d-1,

and

\eta_{d}=\frac{\lambda-\rho}{\lambda},\qquad\rho_{d}=\frac{\rho}{\lambda}.

The Shannon entropy functional reduces to

S=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}-q_{d}\log\!\left(\frac{\lambda-\rho}{\lambda}\right)-\frac{q_{d}\rho}{\lambda-\rho}\log\!\left(\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right).

The generation time is

T=1+\sum_{i=2}^{d-1}Q_{i}+\frac{\lambda q_{d}}{\lambda-\rho}.

Hence the evolutionary entropy is

H=\frac{-\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}-q_{d}\log\!\left(\frac{\lambda-\rho}{\lambda}\right)-\dfrac{q_{d}\rho}{\lambda-\rho}\log\!\left(\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right)}{T}.

Therefore the entropy formula for open-group Leslie matrices is
obtained directly from the general Lefkovitch entropy representation,
without requiring a separate derivation.

Open-group Leslie matrices play an important role in empirical applications.
In
[
2
]
, an analysis of the COMADRE animal
demographic database revealed that approximately
74\%
of the
matrices considered in that study belonged to the open-group class.
Thus, the open-group case represents one of the most common
demographic structures encountered in practice. The previous
corollary shows that the entropy representation specializes
immediately to this important class of models.

3
Decomposition of evolutionary entropy

The similarity transformation established in

[
1
]
shows that every irreducible Lefkovitch
matrix admits an associated pseudo-Leslie representation.
However, evolutionary entropy is not preserved by this
transformation, and the pseudo-Leslie matrix may contain negative
pseudo-fertilities. Hence, entropy cannot in general be
identified with an entropy associated with the pseudo-Leslie
representation.

Nevertheless, the explicit entropy formula obtained for Lefkovitch
matrices reveals a natural decomposition into two conceptually
distinct contributions. The first is associated with the transition
structure encoded by the distribution
q_{j}
, whereas the second is
generated by the retention mechanisms characteristic of Lefkovitch
models.

Theorem 3.1

(Entropy decomposition)
.

The evolutionary entropy of an irreducible Lefkovitch matrix may be
written as

H=H_{\mathrm{tr}}+H_{\mathrm{ret}},

(15)

where

H_{\mathrm{tr}}=-\pi_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}

(16)

and

H_{\mathrm{ret}}=-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\pi_{i}\left(\eta_{i}\log\eta_{i}+\rho_{i}\log\rho_{i}\right).

(17)

The proof is given in
A.5
.

The quantity
H_{\mathrm{tr}}
measures uncertainty associated
with the transition structure of the demographic process. It depends
only on the probabilities
q_{j}
and therefore reflects how
demographic trajectories are distributed among the possible pathways
of the underlying Markov chain. In the Leslie case, this term
reduces to the classical entropy contribution considered by
Demetrius, Gundlach and Ziehe.

The quantity
H_{\mathrm{ret}}
measures uncertainty generated by
stage retention. Each term

-\eta_{i}\log\eta_{i}-\rho_{i}\log\rho_{i}

is the binary entropy associated with the choice between remaining
in stage
i
and advancing to the next stage.

Substituting

\pi_{1}=\frac{1}{T},\qquad\pi_{i}=\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}T},

into
(
15
)
immediately gives the explicit formula

H=\frac{1}{T}\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}\left(\eta_{i}\log\eta_{i}+\rho_{i}\log\rho_{i}\right)\right].

(18)

The decomposition highlights a fundamental distinction between
Leslie and Lefkovitch models. In Leslie matrices, uncertainty is
generated entirely by the transition structure. In Lefkovitch
matrices, additional uncertainty is introduced through stage
retention, yielding a second contribution that has no analogue in
the classical age-structured setting.

Corollary 3.2

(Leslie limit)
.

As the retention coefficients satisfy

c_{i}\to 0,\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

the Lefkovitch entropy converges to

H_{L}=-\frac{1}{T_{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}^{(0)}\log q_{j}^{(0)},

(19)

which is precisely the entropy formula for the corresponding Leslie
model.

The proof is in
A.6
.

4
Sensitivity of evolutionary entropy

A fundamental result in the entropy-based theory introduced by
Demetrius is the decomposition

r=\Phi+H,

(20)

where
r
denotes the Malthusian parameter,

\Phi
is the reproductive potential,
and
H
is the evolutionary entropy.

For discrete-time population models, the relation takes the form

\log\lambda=\Phi+H.

(21)

The quantity
\Phi
, known as the reproductive potential,
measures the contribution of reproductive opportunities to the
overall population growth rate, whereas
H
measures the temporal
organization of demographic trajectories.

Differentiating (
21
) one gets

\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda}=d\Phi+dH.

(22)

Hence any demographic perturbation may be decomposed into two
components: a variation in reproductive potential and a variation in
demographic organization.

To study the latter contribution, define

h_{i}=-\eta_{i}\log\eta_{i}-\rho_{i}\log\rho_{i},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d.

(23)

Then the Shannon entropy
(
14
)
may be written as

S=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}h_{i}.

(24)

The first step consists in expressing the differential of entropy in
terms of the variables
q_{j}
and
\eta_{i}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Differential representation)
.

The differential of evolutionary entropy admits the representation

dH=\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}\,dq_{j}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}D_{i}\,d\eta_{i},

(25)

where

C_{j}=\frac{1}{T}\left[-(1+\log q_{j})+\sum_{i=2}^{j}\frac{h_{i}-H}{\eta_{i}}\right],\qquad j=1,\ldots,d,

(26)

and

D_{i}=\frac{Q_{i}}{T\eta_{i}^{2}}\left(H+\log\rho_{i}\right),\qquad i=2,\ldots,d.

(27)

The proof is given in
A.7
.

The next result expresses
dq_{j}
and
d\eta_{i}
in terms of the
demographic parameters.

Lemma 4.2

(Parameter differentials)
.

Define

A_{j}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{\lambda-c_{h+1}},\qquad j=1,\ldots,d.

(28)

Then

dq_{j}=q_{j}\left[\frac{dm_{j}}{m_{j}}+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{db_{h}}{b_{h}}+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{dc_{h+1}}{\lambda-c_{h+1}}-A_{j}\,d\lambda\right],

(29)

and

d\eta_{i}=\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda^{2}}d\lambda-\frac{1}{\lambda}dc_{i},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d.

(30)

The proof is given in
A.8
.

The preceding results lead to an explicit expression for the entropy
differential.

Theorem 4.3

(Entropy differential)
.

Define

B=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}A_{j}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}D_{i}\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda^{2}}.

(31)

Then

\displaystyle dH

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}\frac{dm_{j}}{m_{j}}+\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\left(\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}\right)\frac{db_{r}}{b_{r}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r=2}^{d}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda-c_{r}}\sum_{j=r}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}-\frac{D_{r}}{\lambda}\right]dc_{r}+B\,d\lambda.

(32)

The proof is given in
A.9
.

We may now eliminate
d\lambda
using the classical
Perron–Frobenius perturbation formula.

Theorem 4.4

(Explicit sensitivity formulas)
.

Let
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
be the positive left Perron eigenvector of

L
, normalized by

vu=1.

Then

\displaystyle dH

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\frac{\partial H}{\partial m_{r}}\,dm_{r}+\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\frac{\partial H}{\partial b_{r}}\,db_{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r=2}^{d}\frac{\partial H}{\partial c_{r}}\,dc_{r},

(33)

where

\frac{\partial H}{\partial m_{r}}=\frac{C_{r}q_{r}}{m_{r}}+Bv_{1}u_{r},\qquad r=1,\ldots,d,

(34)

\frac{\partial H}{\partial b_{r}}=\frac{1}{b_{r}}\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}+Bv_{r+1}u_{r},\qquad r=1,\ldots,d-1,

(35)

and

\frac{\partial H}{\partial c_{r}}=\frac{1}{\lambda-c_{r}}\sum_{j=r}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}-\frac{D_{r}}{\lambda}+Bv_{r}u_{r},\qquad r=2,\ldots,d.

(36)

The proof is given in
A.10
.

Remark 4.5
.

The sensitivity coefficients consist of two distinct contributions.
The first term in
(
34
)–(
36
)
describes the direct effect of a perturbation on the demographic
organization of the population.

The second term, involving products of Perron eigenvectors,
arises indirectly through the perturbation of the dominant
eigenvalue. Therefore, evolutionary entropy responds both to
local demographic changes and to the global spectral structure of
the population matrix.

Corollary 4.6

(Leslie reduction)
.

As the retention coefficients satisfy

c_{i}\to 0,\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

the Lefkovitch entropy and sensitivity theory reduce to the
classical Demetrius–Gundlach–Ziehe theory for Leslie matrices.

The proof is given in
A.11
.

Corollary 4.7

(Sensitivity expressions for open-group Leslie matrices)
.

For an open-group Leslie matrix, with

c_{2}=\cdots=c_{d-1}=0,\qquad c_{d}=\rho,

the entropy sensitivities are obtained from
Theorem
4.4

by the same substitution. In particular, the sensitivities with
respect to fertility and transition coefficients are

\frac{\partial H}{\partial m_{r}}=\frac{C_{r}q_{r}}{m_{r}}+Bv_{1}u_{r},\qquad r=1,\ldots,d,

and

\frac{\partial H}{\partial b_{r}}=\frac{1}{b_{r}}\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}+Bv_{r+1}u_{r},\qquad r=1,\ldots,d-1.

The sensitivity with respect to the terminal open-group retention
coefficient is

\frac{\partial H}{\partial\rho}=\frac{1}{\lambda-\rho}C_{d}q_{d}-\frac{D_{d}}{\lambda}+Bv_{d}u_{d}.

Thus the open-group Leslie sensitivities are precisely the
Lefkovitch sensitivities restricted to the case in which the only
non-zero retention coefficient is the terminal one.

5
Examples

The theoretical results obtained in the previous sections transform
evolutionary entropy into a directly computable demographic
quantity. For any irreducible Lefkovitch matrix, one may determine
the associated Markov chain, generation time, reproductive
potential, entropy decomposition and sensitivity coefficients.

The examples considered below illustrate these concepts in a variety
of biological settings. Particular attention is given to the
comparison between population growth and evolutionary entropy, the
role of stage retention, and the specialization of the theory to
open-group Leslie matrices, a demographic structure frequently
encountered in empirical applications.

The decomposition admits a particularly transparent interpretation.

Conclusions

The explicit entropy sensitivity formulae obtained by Demetrius,
Gundlach and Ziehe for Leslie matrices extend naturally to the
broader class of irreducible Lefkovitch matrices. The resulting
expressions provide closed-form representations for evolutionary
entropy, its decomposition, and its sensitivities with respect to
fertility, transition and retention parameters.

The analysis shows that stage retention introduces a distinct source
of demographic uncertainty which has no counterpart in purely
age-structured models. This contribution appears explicitly through
the retention component of entropy and may dominate the demographic
organisation of stage-structured populations, as illustrated by the
biological examples considered here.

The theory specializes immediately to open-group Leslie matrices, a
class that encompasses a large proportion of the animal population
models currently available in COMADRE and many demographic models
used in human population studies. The expressions derived here therefore
apply to a substantial part of the structured populations encountered
in practice.

By placing Lefkovitch and open-group Leslie models within a common
entropy-based perturbation context, the present results enlarge the
scope of evolutionary entropy as a quantitative tool for demographic
analysis and provide a foundation for future large-scale studies of
the COMADRE and COMPADRE databases and beyond.

Data availability

The demographic matrices used in the illustrative examples are
available from the public COMPADRE and COMADRE matrix population
databases. All theoretical results are derived analytically in the
article, and all numerical values required to reproduce the examples
are either reported in the manuscript or can be obtained from these
public repositories.

The Wolfram Mathematica code used to perform the computations is
available from the author upon reasonable request.

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{m_{j}R_{j}}{\lambda}=1.

Using the definition

\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}=1,

Proof of Theorem
2.3

Proof.

For
i=2,\ldots,d
, consider the partition separating the states

\{1,\ldots,i-1\}

from

\{i,\ldots,d\}.

The stationary probability flux crossing this partition from left to
right is determined by the transition distribution
q_{j}
and equals

\pi_{1}\sum_{j=i}^{d}q_{j}=\pi_{1}Q_{i}.

Here

Q_{i}=\sum_{j=i}^{d}q_{j}

denotes the cumulative transition probability associated with the
states
i,\ldots,d
.

The opposite flux is generated by transitions from state
i
to
state
i-1
and equals

\pi_{i}\eta_{i}.

Since the chain is stationary, the two fluxes must coincide.
Therefore

\pi_{i}\eta_{i}=\pi_{1}Q_{i}.

(39)

Hence

\pi_{i}=\frac{\pi_{1}Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}.

(40)

Using the normalization condition

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i}=1,

together with (
40

1=\pi_{1}\left(1+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}\right).

By definition,

and therefore

\pi_{1}=\frac{1}{T}.

Substituting this expression into
(
40
) we get

\pi_{i}=\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}T},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

which is exactly
(
11
).

A.11
Proof of Corollary
4.6

Let
L(c)
be the Lefkovitch matrix and let
L_{0}
be the Leslie
matrix obtained by setting

c_{i}=0,\qquad i=2,\ldots,d.

Since the entries of
L(c)
converge entrywise to those of
L_{0}
,
and since the Perron eigenvalue of an irreducible non-negative matrix
is simple, the Perron root depends continuously on the entries of the
matrix. Hence

\lambda(c)\to\lambda_{0}>0,

where
\lambda_{0}
is the Perron eigenvalue of
L_{0}
. Moreover,
the associated Perron eigenvectors may be chosen continuously, with
the normalization

v(c)u(c)=1.

Thus

u(c)\to u^{(0)},\qquad v(c)\to v^{(0)},\qquad v^{(0)}u^{(0)}=1.

We first record the limiting behaviour of the quantities entering
the entropy and sensitivity formulae. From

(\ref{RjDefinition})
,

R_{j}(c)=\prod_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{b_{h}}{\lambda(c)-c_{h+1}}.

Since

\lambda(c)-c_{i}\to\lambda_{0},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

we obtain

R_{j}(c)\longrightarrow\frac{b_{1}\cdots b_{j-1}}{\lambda_{0}^{j-1}}=:R_{j}^{(0)}.

Therefore, by
(\ref{qjDefinition})
,

q_{j}(c)=\frac{m_{j}R_{j}(c)}{\lambda(c)}\longrightarrow\frac{m_{j}b_{1}\cdots b_{j-1}}{\lambda_{0}^{j}}=:q_{j}^{(0)}.

Therefore,

Q_{i}(c)=\sum_{j=i}^{d}q_{j}(c)\longrightarrow Q_{i}^{(0)}:=\sum_{j=i}^{d}q_{j}^{(0)}.

Furthermore, from
(\ref{EtaRhoDefinition})
,

\rho_{i}(c)=\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)}\to 0,\qquad\eta_{i}(c)=1-\rho_{i}(c)\to 1.

Hence

\frac{Q_{i}(c)}{\eta_{i}(c)}\longrightarrow Q_{i}^{(0)}.

It follows from
(\ref{TDefinition})
that

T(c)=1+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}(c)}{\eta_{i}(c)}\longrightarrow 1+\sum_{i=2}^{d}Q_{i}^{(0)}=:T_{L}.

By Corollary
3.2
,

H(c)\to H_{L},

where

H_{L}=-\frac{1}{T_{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}^{(0)}\log q_{j}^{(0)}.

We now study the coefficients
C_{j}
,
D_{i}
,
A_{j}
and
B

appearing in the sensitivity formulae.
First, from
(\ref{EntropyKernel})
,

h_{i}(c)=-\eta_{i}(c)\log\eta_{i}(c)-\rho_{i}(c)\log\rho_{i}(c).

Since

\eta_{i}(c)\to 1,\qquad\rho_{i}(c)\to 0,

and since

\eta\log\eta\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\eta\to 1,

while

\rho\log\rho\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\rho\to 0^{+},

we have

h_{i}(c)\to 0.

Therefore, using
(\ref{CjDefinition})
,

C_{j}(c)=\frac{1}{T(c)}\left[-(1+\log q_{j}(c))+\sum_{i=2}^{j}\frac{h_{i}(c)-H(c)}{\eta_{i}(c)}\right].

Passing to the limit gives

C_{j}(c)\longrightarrow C_{j}^{(0)},

where

C_{j}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{T_{L}}\left[-(1+\log q_{j}^{(0)})-(j-1)H_{L}\right],\qquad j=1,\ldots,d.

Next, by
(\ref{AjDefinition})
,

A_{j}(c)=\frac{1}{\lambda(c)}+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{\lambda(c)-c_{h+1}}.

Hence

A_{j}(c)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}=\frac{j}{\lambda_{0}}=:A_{j}^{(0)}.

We now control the coefficient
B
. By
(\ref{BDefinition})
,

B(c)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}(c)q_{j}(c)A_{j}(c)+\sum_{i=2}^{d}D_{i}(c)\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)^{2}}.

The first sum converges to

-\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}^{(0)}q_{j}^{(0)}A_{j}^{(0)}=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}^{(0)}q_{j}^{(0)}\frac{j}{\lambda_{0}}.

It remains to show that the second sum tends to zero. By

(\ref{DiDefinition})
,

D_{i}(c)=\frac{Q_{i}(c)}{T(c)\eta_{i}(c)^{2}}\left(H(c)+\log\rho_{i}(c)\right).

Thus

D_{i}(c)\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)^{2}}=\frac{Q_{i}(c)}{T(c)\eta_{i}(c)^{2}}\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)^{2}}\left(H(c)+\log\rho_{i}(c)\right).

The factor

\frac{Q_{i}(c)}{T(c)\eta_{i}(c)^{2}}\frac{1}{\lambda(c)^{2}}

remains bounded, because

Q_{i}(c)\leq 1,\qquad T(c)\to T_{L}>0,\qquad\eta_{i}(c)\to 1,\qquad\lambda(c)\to\lambda_{0}>0.

Moreover,

c_{i}H(c)\to 0,

since
H(c)\to H_{L}
, and

c_{i}\log\rho_{i}(c)\to 0.

Indeed,

\rho_{i}(c)=\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)},

and
\lambda(c)\to\lambda_{0}>0
, so

c_{i}\log\rho_{i}(c)=c_{i}\log c_{i}-c_{i}\log\lambda(c)\to 0.

Therefore

D_{i}(c)\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)^{2}}\to 0.

Since the number of terms is finite,

\sum_{i=2}^{d}D_{i}(c)\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda(c)^{2}}\to 0.

Thus,

B(c)\to B^{(0)},

where

B^{(0)}=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}C_{j}^{(0)}q_{j}^{(0)}\frac{j}{\lambda_{0}}.

We now pass to the limit in the sensitivities with respect to the
fertility coefficients. From
(\ref{SensitivityM})
,

\frac{\partial H}{\partial m_{r}}=\frac{C_{r}q_{r}}{m_{r}}+Bv_{1}u_{r}.

Using the limits already obtained, together with

u(c)\to u^{(0)},\qquad v(c)\to v^{(0)},

we get

\frac{\partial H}{\partial m_{r}}\longrightarrow\frac{C_{r}^{(0)}q_{r}^{(0)}}{m_{r}}+B^{(0)}v_{1}^{(0)}u_{r}^{(0)}.

This is the Leslie sensitivity of entropy with respect to
m_{r}
.
Equivalently,

\frac{\partial H_{L}}{\partial m_{r}}=\frac{C_{r}^{(0)}q_{r}^{(0)}}{m_{r}}+B^{(0)}v_{1}^{(0)}u_{r}^{(0)},\qquad r=1,\ldots,d.

Similarly, from
(\ref{SensitivityB})
,

\frac{\partial H}{\partial b_{r}}=\frac{1}{b_{r}}\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}C_{j}q_{j}+Bv_{r+1}u_{r}.

Passing to the limit we obtain

\frac{\partial H}{\partial b_{r}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{b_{r}}\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}C_{j}^{(0)}q_{j}^{(0)}+B^{(0)}v_{r+1}^{(0)}u_{r}^{(0)}.

Thus

\frac{\partial H_{L}}{\partial b_{r}}=\frac{1}{b_{r}}\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}C_{j}^{(0)}q_{j}^{(0)}+B^{(0)}v_{r+1}^{(0)}u_{r}^{(0)},\qquad r=1,\ldots,d-1.

This proves that
the Lefkovitch sensitivity formulae converge to the corresponding
Leslie sensitivity formulae.

Finally, the coefficients

\frac{\partial H}{\partial c_{r}},\qquad r=2,\ldots,d,

measure perturbations in the retention directions. These directions
are absent from the Leslie model. Hence they do not correspond to
Leslie sensitivity coefficients, and they are not part of the
reduction to the Leslie subspace.

This proves the corollary.

Appendix B
Computational implementation

The analytical formulae derived in Sections 4 and 5 were implemented
in Wolfram Mathematica. The computational procedure consists of the
following steps.

1.

Given an irreducible Lefkovitch matrix

L=(a_{ij}),

compute the Perron eigenvalue
\lambda
and the corresponding right
and left Perron eigenvectors
u
and
v
, normalized by

v^{\!T}u=1.

2.

Extract the fertility, transition and retention coefficients

m_{j}=L_{1j},\qquad b_{j}=L_{j+1,j},\qquad c_{j}=L_{jj}.

3.

Compute the generalized survivorship coefficients

R_{1}=1,\qquad R_{j}=\prod_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{b_{h}}{\lambda-c_{h+1}},\qquad j\geq 2.

4.

Compute the reproductive distribution

q_{j}=\frac{m_{j}R_{j}}{\lambda},

and the tail probabilities

Q_{i}=\sum_{k=i}^{d}q_{k}.

5.

Compute the transition and retention probabilities

\eta_{i}=\frac{\lambda-c_{i}}{\lambda},\qquad\rho_{i}=\frac{c_{i}}{\lambda}.

6.

Compute the local retention entropy

h_{i}=-\eta_{i}\log\eta_{i}-\rho_{i}\log\rho_{i}.

7.

Compute the generation time

T=1+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}.

8.

Compute the total entropy

H=\frac{S}{T},

where

S=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}h_{i}.

9.

Compute the decomposition

H=H_{\rm tr}+H_{\rm ret},

with

H_{\rm tr}=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}\log q_{j},

and

H_{\rm ret}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{Q_{i}}{\eta_{i}}h_{i}.

10.

Compute the entropy sensitivity coefficients

C_{j}=\frac{1}{T}\left[-(1+\log q_{j})+\sum_{i=2}^{j}\frac{h_{i}-H}{\eta_{i}}\right],

D_{i}=\frac{Q_{i}}{T\eta_{i}^{2}}\left(H+\log\rho_{i}\right).

11.

Compute the auxiliary quantities

W_{j}=q_{j}C_{j},

A_{j}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{\lambda-c_{h+1}},

and

B=\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{D_{i}c_{i}}{\lambda^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}W_{j}A_{j}.

12.

Compute the growth sensitivities

\frac{\partial r}{\partial a_{ij}}=\frac{v_{i}u_{j}}{\lambda},

the entropy sensitivities

\frac{\partial H}{\partial a_{ij}},

and finally

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial a_{ij}}=\frac{\partial r}{\partial a_{ij}}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial a_{ij}}.

13.

Compute the corresponding elasticities

e_{ij}^{(r)}=a_{ij}\frac{\partial r}{\partial a_{ij}},

e_{ij}^{(H)}=a_{ij}\frac{\partial H}{\partial a_{ij}},

e_{ij}^{(\Phi)}=a_{ij}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial a_{ij}}.

The implementation was independently validated against finite differences.
For all tested matrices, including the examples presented in this
paper, the two implementations produced identical results up to
machine precision.
