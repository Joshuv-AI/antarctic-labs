Resveratrol derivative restores macrophage cholesterol homeostasis via stabilizing xanthine oxidoreductase in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Guojun Liang, Lin Zeng, Xiaohui Huang, Qingqing Ma, Hongyu Wang, Yi Wang, Ting Liu, Xiaoxin Tu, Jianxin Peng, Huijie Huang, Wendao Liu, Peifeng Ke, Jun Yan, Min He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key determinants of the immunosuppressive microenvironment in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and critically influence the efficacy of immunotherapy. However, how metabolic regulators shape TAM immunophenotypes and subsequent CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction in HCC remains incompletely understood.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing data and primary tumor samples from patients with HCC were used to characterize xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) expression on TAMs, and to clarify the underlying mechanisms mediating the effects of XOR⁺ monocytes/macrophages on CD8⁺ T cells. An in-house small-molecule library was screened to identify compounds capable of modulating XOR activity, followed by mechanistic and therapeutic validation in vivo.ResultsWe identified a marked downregulation of XOR expression in TAMs within HCC tumors, which was significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, loss of XOR disrupted PPARγ signaling and cholesterol homeostasis in macrophages, driving their polarization toward an alternatively activated, immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. XOR-deficient TAMs exhibited an impaired capacity to support CD8⁺ T cell activation through enhancing PD-L1 expression, thereby facilitating tumor progression. Notably, a resveratrol derivative, Res616, directly bound to and stabilized the XOR protein, restoring cholesterol metabolic balance and reversing the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs. Therapeutically, targeting XOR with Res616 significantly enhanced intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell responses and synergized with anti-PD-L1 therapy to suppress tumor growth in murine HCC models.ConclusionsOur study identified XOR as a pivotal metabolic checkpoint governing TAM-mediated immunosuppression in HCC. Pharmacological stabilization of XOR to restore macrophage cholesterol homeostasis represented a previously unrecognized strategy to remodel the tumor immune microenvironment and improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08433-2.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Tumor-associated macrophages, Xanthine oxidoreductase, Cholesterol metabolism, Resveratrol derivative, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths with a rising global incidence [1, 2]. The development of effective systemic therapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, has revolutionized HCC treatment, but limited clinical benefits remain a critical challenge [3]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent the dominant immune cell population within the tumor microenvironment (TME), and their presence is associated with an unfavorable prognosis [4, 5]. Macrophages are educated by the TME, acquiring a pro-tumoral phenotype that promotes tumor progression, invasion, and immune evasion [6]. The mechanisms driving TAM polarization remain elusive, but once identified, they may serve as key therapeutic targets to block the tumor-promoting functions of TAMs and restore their anti-tumor capabilities [7].

Aberrant cholesterol metabolism is a prominent feature of HCC [8]. Cholesterol serves a dual role in both energy metabolism and immune regulation in immune cells [9, 10]. For example, studies have suggested that cholesterol deficiency driven by cholesterol efflux is associated with the generation of immunosuppressive macrophages in HCC [11]. Meanwhile, research by Li et al. revealed that the lipid raft content in TAMs is negatively correlated with the expression level of CD206 [12]. Given the crucial role of cholesterol metabolism in modulating the survival and function of macrophages, it is imperative to further uncover the intricacies of restoring cholesterol homeostasis in TAMs to enhance HCC immunotherapy efficacy [13, 14].

Xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) is a ubiquitous, rate-limiting enzyme that is responsible for the terminal steps of purine catabolism, which is transcribed and translated as xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) and converted to xanthine oxidase (XO) [15]. Dysregulated XOR expression and activity have been observed in various types of cancer and are closely associated with clinical outcomes [16]. Beyond its established role as a metabolic enzyme, XOR has recently been shown to possess immunomodulatory functions. Specifically, XOR is reported to promote M1 macrophage polarization by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [17]. By contrast, reduced XOR expression has been observed in TAMs, which is negatively correlated with immunosuppressive activity [16]. Nevertheless, the specific mechanism by which XOR regulates the polarization of TAMs in HCC remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we identified XOR as a critical modulator of cholesterol metabolism in HCC-associated TAMs. The reduction of XOR impaired PPARγ expression, caused cholesterol deficiency, and promoted pro-tumoral polarization of TAMs, which in turn enhanced HCC progression. Moreover, through screening of an in-house compound library, we identified the resveratrol derivative Res616 as a potent agent that reversed TAM polarization. Mechanistically, Res616 stabilized XOR by inhibiting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, restored cholesterol homeostasis, and mobilized systemic CD8⁺ T cell responses. In hepatoma-bearing mice, Res616 also elicited antitumor immunity and synergized with anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy. Therefore, these findings defined a purine catabolism-independent role of XDH in TAM polarization, and highlighted its potential as a therapeutic target in HCC immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Patients and specimens

Peripheral blood samples (n = 20) and paired peritumoral and tumor tissues (n = 5) were collected from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. Patients were pathologically confirmed to have HCC and had not received anticancer therapy prior to sampling. Individuals with concurrent autoimmune diseases, HIV, or syphilis were excluded. The clinical stage of tumors was determined according to the TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors, 8th Edition. Tumor differentiation was graded by the Edmondson grading system. Sex- and age-matched healthy controls (HCs, n = 20) were recruited from the Physical Examination Center of the same hospital. The demographic information for the peripheral blood and HCC tumor tissue samples was presented in Table S1. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: YE2025-147-01). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Isolation of mononuclear cells from human peripheral blood and tissues

Monocytes were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using anti-CD14 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-050-201), with a purity of over 95%. Monocytes were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% human AB serum in the presence or absence of 30% Huh7-TSN, Hep-3B-TSN, SK-Hep-1-TSN, and THLE-2-SN. In some cases, Res616 or siRNA was added as indicated. To obtain mononuclear cells, fresh HCC biopsy specimens or mouse biopsy samples were cut into 1–2 mm pieces and digested for 1 h at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, #C11875500BT) containing 0.1% type IV collagenase (Biosharp, #BS165), 0.005% DNase I (YESEN, #10607ES15), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell Bio, #FSD500). The digested cells were filtered through a 300-mesh sieve, and mononuclear cells were isolated by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation. These cells were then used for further experiments.

Cell culture and tumor culture supernatant preparation

Human normal liver cell line (THLE-2) and human hepatoma cell lines (HuH7, Hep3B and SK-Hep-1) were obtained from ATCC. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, #C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Cells were used for the study at passages below 10 and were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination. For culture supernatants (SN) preparation, 1 × 107 tumor cells were plated in 10 mL complete medium in a T75 culture flask for 24 h, and then the medium was changed to DMEM supplemented with 10% human AB serum. After 72 h, the SNs were collected, centrifuged, and stored in aliquots at −80 °C.

RNA interference and overexpression

Briefly, monocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in a 24-well plate, and then the cells were left untreated or transfected with 300 nM of si-NC (negative control), si-XDH, or si-PPARG using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #13778100). Cells were then incubated for 48 h before analysis. All siRNA duplexes were purchased from RiboBio, and the sequences were listed in Table S2.

The lentiviral vector for XDH overexpression (LV-XDH) and the negative control vector (LV-CON) were constructed and titered by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For transduction, monocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates. Lentivirus was applied at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, together with the transduction enhancer HitransG A + P to improve transduction efficiency. 48 h post-transduction, transduction efficiency was confirmed under a fluorescence microscope, followed by validation using western blot.

Coculture of monocytes with T cells

T cells were isolated from the PBMCs of healthy donors using a Pan T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-096-535) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sorting purity of CD3⁺ T cells was routinely greater than 95%. T cells were co-cultured with monocytes at a ratio of 1:4 in the presence of 20 IU/mL IL-2 (R&D Systems, #202-IL-010), 2.5 µg/mL anti-CD3 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #16-0037-85) and 5 µg/mL anti-CD28 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #16-0289-85). After 3 days, the cells were collected for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry

Monocytes/macrophages and T cells from peripheral blood, tissues, or in vitro cultures were analyzed using flow cytometry. To detect intracellular cytokines, T cells were stimulated with leukocyte activation cocktail (BD Biosciences) at 37 °C for 6 h. For intracellular staining, cells stained with surface markers were fixed and permeabilized using transcription factor buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #00-5523-00), and then stained with intracellular markers. Isotype control monoclonal antibodies were used as negative controls. Cell phenotype analysis was performed using a CytoFLEX Lx flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Miami, Florida, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, Treestar, Inc., San Carlos, CA). The fluorochrome-conjugated Abs were listed in Table S3.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

The paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 4 μm thick slices, which were processed for immunohistochemistry as previously described [18]. After dewaxing, hydration, and antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated with Ki-67 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #MA5-14520) or Abs against mouse CD8 (Invitrogen, #14-0081-82), and then stained with the DakoCytomation Envision system.

For immunofluorescence analysis of patient samples, deparaffinized sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, and subsequently stained with anti-human CD68 (Santa Cruz, #sc-20060, 1:200), anti-human XOR (Abcam, #ab109235, 1:200), or anti-human PPARγ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #MA5-1489, 1:200), followed by Goat anti-mouse IgG-AF488 (Abcam, #ab150113) and Goat anti-rabbit IgG-AF594 (Abcam, #ab150080). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Immunofluorescence staining images were captured using a ZEISS microscope. Positive cells were quantified with ImageJ software and expressed as the mean percentage of positive cells ± SD in high-powered fields. All antibodies were listed in Table S3.

Filipin-III and lipid raft staining

Monocytes/macrophages cultured in vitro were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with either 50 µg/mL Filipin-III (Sigma-Aldrich, #SAE0087) or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated CTB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C34775) at 4 °C. The samples were then analyzed using flow cytometry or immunofluorescence.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C as described previously [19]. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit. Protein samples were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 1% BSA, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against human XOR, PPARγ, β-actin, GAPDH, or Ubiquitin (1:1000), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000). Protein blots were detected using the Bio-Rad gel imaging system and analyzed with ImageJ software. The antibodies used were listed in Table S3.

Immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry

Potential XOR-interacting proteins were identified by immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (IP-MS). Briefly, antigen-antibody complexes were immunoprecipitated using protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, #HY-K0202), and the IP-MS analysis was performed by Shanghai Baipu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15596026CN) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA with HiScript III Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, #R223-01). Quantitative PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green real-time PCR kit in a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Gene expression was calculated relative to β-actin. The data were analyzed using the comparative Ct method. The primers were listed in Table S4.

DARTS

Monocyte cell lysates were incubated with the indicated concentrations of Res616 for 50 min, followed by the addition of the indicated concentrations of Pronase and continued incubation at room temperature for 30 min. The reactions were then terminated by adding loading buffer, followed by heat denaturation for western blot analysis.

CETSA-MS and CETSA

Monocytes and Huh7 tumor cells were treated with Res616 (50 µM) or DMSO (0.5%) for 24 h, and then the cells were collected and resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors. The cell suspensions were rapidly cooled in liquid nitrogen following heating and subjected to 5 freeze-thaw cycles to ensure complete lysis. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min to collect the supernatants.

Samples were mixed with SDT lysis buffer, reduced and alkylated, and then filtered through UA and NH4HCO3 buffers. Proteins were digested with trypsin for 16–18 h, and the resulting peptides were purified using C18 StageTips, dissolved in 0.1% FA, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Mass spectrometry data were searched against the UniProt database using Proteome Discoverer 2.4, and candidate targets were identified.

Monocytes were treated with Res616 (50 µM) or DMSO (0.5%) for 24 h, and then the cells were collected and resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors. Each cell suspension was heated at 37 °C, 42 °C, 47 °C, 52 °C, 57 °C, 62 °C, 67 °C, or 72 °C for 3 min, then rapidly cooled in liquid nitrogen and subjected to 5 freeze-thaw cycles to ensure complete lysis. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatants were collected. XOR protein expression was analyzed using western blot.

Surface plasmon resonance

XOR and Res616 interactions were analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) using a Biacore 8 K instrument. XOR (50 µg/mL) was immobilized on the sample channel (Fc2), while the reference channel (Fc1) was treated with buffer only. Following immobilization, unreacted sites were blocked with 1 M ethanolamine at a flow rate of 10 µL/min. Res616 was prepared at a series of concentrations (0.3125 µM, 0.625 µM, 1.25 µM, 2.5 µM, 5 µM, and 10 µM) and injected over the sensor surface at 30 µL/min for a 150 s association phase, followed by a 300 s dissociation phase. The sensor surface was regenerated between injections with 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.0) for 5 min. Sensorgrams were recorded and globally fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model in Biacore Insight software to determine kinetic parameters.

Cell viability

The cell viability of Res616-treated monocytes was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (APExBIO, #K1018), and the optical density at 450 nm was read with a microplate reader.

Cholesterol uptake

Cholesterol uptake ability was measured using a Cholesterol Uptake Assay Kit (Abcam, #ab236212). Monocytes (1 × 106 cells/well) were incubated in serum-free medium with 20 µg/mL NBD-cholesterol at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 24 h. Cellular fluorescence intensity was detected by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry.

Animals and animal models

C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (Approval number: 20240417001).

In a subcutaneous tumor model, Hepa1-6 cells (8 × 10⁵) were inoculated into the flanks of mice. From day 6 onward, mice received either intraperitoneal injections of anti-PD-L1 antibody (10 mg/kg/3 days) or oral administration of Res616 (50 mg/kg/2 days), or a combination of both Res616 and anti-PD-L1 antibody. Six days after inoculation, the growth of tumors was monitored every 3 days. Tumor dimensions were measured using a caliper, and tumor volume was calculated as follows: volume = (length) × (width)² / 2.

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 79 hepatocellular carcinoma samples (PRJCA007744) were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information of China (CNCB) and analyzed using the Seurat package in R [20]. Cells with > 20% mitochondrial gene content were excluded. The remaining data were normalized, log-transformed, and subjected to variable gene selection, principal component analysis, and clustering. Data visualization was performed using ggplot2. Additionally, 545 lipid metabolism-related genes were obtained from GeneCards (Relevance score > 20). For 7 macrophage clusters in scRNA-seq, the AUCell algorithm was used to calculate the activity score (AUC value) of the gene set on the standardized expression matrix, and descriptive statistics of the AUC score were calculated across distinct macrophage clusters.

Spatial transcriptomic data (HRA000437) were analyzed as previously described [21]. Spatial feature scores for XDH⁺ macrophages across three tumor regions were calculated using 20 scRNA-seq-derived marker genes, representing the average expression of each gene set at individual capture spots in the SCT matrix. Spatial distributions were visualized using SpatialFeaturePlot.

Transcriptomic sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from CD14⁺ cells using TRIzol reagent, quality controlled, and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using edgeR, with genes meeting p < 0.05 and fold change > 1.3 defined as differentially expressed. Results were visualized by volcano plots. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using ClusterProfiler, and pathway enrichment was further validated by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA).

Molecular docking

The structure of Res616 was optimized using Chem3D. The XOR structure (PDB ID: 2E1Q) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and prepared in PyMOL by removing water molecules and ligands and adding hydrogen atoms. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina, and the lowest-energy binding conformation was visualized in PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

Statistical test methods were detailed in the figure legends. Results were presented as mean ± SD. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Prognostic factors were identified through univariate and multivariate analyses using the Cox proportional hazards model. All data were tested for normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences between two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test, while differences among three or more groups were assessed with analysis of variance (ANOVA). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

XOR was selectively downregulated in TAMs and correlated with poor prognosis in HCC patients

To delineate macrophage heterogeneity and characterize lipid metabolic states, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of HCC tissues (PRJCA007744) identified 7 distinct macrophage subsets, with the TAM_C4 subset exhibiting the highest lipid metabolic signature and concurrent elevation of XOR expression (Fig. 1A-B and Fig. S1A-C). Subsequent Cox regression treating macrophage XDH expression as a continuous variable yielded HR < 1, indicating higher XDH associates with lower mortality (Fig. S1D). Stratification of 79 HCC patients by median XOR⁺ TAM levels further revealed that low XOR expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS) (Fig. 1C), confirmed by both univariate (Fig. 1D) and multivariate (Fig. 1E) analyses. These findings were independently validated in external cohorts, including OEP000321 and TCGA-LIHC (Fig. S1E-J).

Aimed to profile XOR levels in tumor-infiltrating monocytes/macrophages, CD14⁺ cells were isolated from peripheral blood and tumor tissues of HCC patients. XOR expression in circulating monocytes/macrophages was markedly lower in patients (20.28% ± 11.68%) than in healthy controls (64.63% ± 12.53%) (Fig. 1F and Fig. S2A). Similarly, macrophages from tumor tissues exhibited reduced XOR and XDH expression compared with paired non‑tumoral liver tissues, and this decrease was validated by flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and RT-qPCR in tumor‑derived monocytes/macrophages (Fig. 1G-J and Fig. S2A). Consistent with these findings, proteomic data from Yang et al. further supported the reduction of XOR in HCC (Fig. S1M). Analysis of the TCGA-LIHC dataset also revealed that low XDH expression was associated with advanced clinical stages (Fig. S1N). Collectively, these data established XOR as a macrophage-intrinsic protective factor associated with favorable prognosis in HCC.

XOR⁺ macrophages spatially associated with CD8⁺ T cells and shaped peritumoral immune distribution

We next examined the role of XOR in TAM differentiation and function. Heatmap analysis showed that XDH⁺ TAMs were enriched in M1-like genes (CD86 and IL1B), whereas XDH⁻ TAMs exhibited higher expression of M2-like genes (CD163, SPP1, and CD274) (Fig. S3A). To model this in vitro, healthy monocytes were treated with tumor supernatant (TSN) from hepatoma cells (Huh7) for 1, 3, or 6 days. Whereas XOR expression remained stable during monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation in the medium control group, it progressively decreased in TSN-treated cells (Fig. 2A-B and Fig. S2A). This effect was also observed with TSN from other hepatoma cells (Hep3B, SK‑Hep‑1), but not with supernatant from normal hepatocyte THLE‑2 cells (Fig. S3B). Consistently, XOR⁺ TAMs exhibited higher FSC and SSC values by FC, indicating enhanced cellular differentiation (Fig. S3C-E). To functionally validate this polarization, we assessed CD163, PD-L1, Arginase-1 (Arg-1) and CD86 as canonical markers. Notably, XOR expression inversely correlated with the immunosuppressive markers CD163 and PD‑L1 in TSN-treated monocytes (Fig. 2C-D). In parallel, silencing XOR expression in monocytes led to a significant upregulation of CD163, PD-L1, and Arg-1, along with the downregulation of CD86 (Fig. 2E-I). Conversely, XOR overexpression markedly increased CD86 while reducing CD163, PD-L1, and Arg-1 levels (Fig. 2E-I). These findings indicated that XOR critically regulated macrophage differentiation and restrained the acquisition of an immunosuppressive phenotype.

In order to further assess the correlation between XDH⁺ TAMs with immune infiltration, XDH-immune cell marker relationships were analyzed via TIMER2.0. XDH⁺ TAMs showed a higher correlation with the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells compared with CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. S3F). Spatial transcriptomic analysis further revealed that XDH⁺ macrophages and CD8⁺ T cells were co-localized within tumor sections (Fig. 2J). Next, we employed a mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) to assess the functional impact of XDH on T cells (Fig. 2K). Following XDH knockdown in TAMs, both the frequency and cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells were impaired, as evidenced by reduced production of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and granzyme B (GzmB), accompanied by upregulation of the exhaustion marker T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain containing-3 (TIM-3). Conversely, XDH overexpression restored CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxic activity, increasing IFN-γ and GzmB levels while reducing TIM-3 expression (Fig. 2L-N). Notably, Lymphocyte Activation Gene‑3 (LAG‑3) expression remained unaffected by either XDH knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 2O). In contrast, CD4⁺ T cell responses remained largely unaffected (Figs. S2B and S3G-L). Interestingly, we found that treatment with a PD-L1 inhibitor partially rescued the loss of CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxic function and the increased exhaustion induced by XOR deficiency (Fig. S3M-P). Collectively, these data indicated that loss of XOR drove TAMs toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, which in turn impaired CD8⁺ T cell effector function—a process driven, at least in part, by the PD-L1 axis, and ultimately promoted tumor progression.

XOR functioned as a metabolic checkpoint controlling macrophage cholesterol homeostasis via the PPARγ-CD36 axis

Given that metabolic reprogramming is essential for immune cell lineage differentiation and function, we sought to determine whether XOR is involved in this process in TAMs by performing GSEA. As expected, XDH⁺ TAMs were enriched in multiple lipid metabolic pathways, with cholesterol metabolism being the most prominently affected (Fig. 3A and Fig. S4A). Similarly, KEGG analysis revealed a significant downregulation of cholesterol metabolism in Huh7-TSN-treated macrophages (Fig. S4B-C). In support of these findings, we subsequently analyzed lipid rafts and cholesterol levels in either CD14⁺ cells from HCC patients or in vitro differentiated TAMs. In both models, XOR⁺ cells displayed higher cholesterol levels and lipid rafts compared with XOR⁻ cells (Fig. 3B-D and Fig. S4E-F). Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown of XOR in normal monocytes markedly reduced membrane cholesterol and lipid raft content, whereas XOR overexpression produced the opposite effect (Fig. 3E-F). Notably, supplementing purine metabolites (xanthine/uric acid) did not affect macrophage immunosuppression or cholesterol levels, suggesting that the immunomodulatory function of XOR operated independently of its classical role in purine catabolism (Fig. S5A-D).

Interestingly, GSEA analysis indicated that the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) signaling pathway was robustly enriched in XDH⁺ TAMs (NES = 2.256, Fig. S6A). XOR knockdown specifically downregulated PPARG, but not PPARA or PPARD, among the three PPAR isoforms (Fig. S6B). To further validate the regulatory role of XOR in PPARγ stability, monocyte-derived macrophages were treated with cycloheximide (CHX) and LV-XDH for the indicated time points and found that PPARγ was rapidly degraded in cells treated with CHX, an inhibitor of eukaryotic translational elongation and protein synthesis, whereas XOR overexpression markedly slowed its degradation (Fig. S6C-E). Accordingly, IP/MS analysis identified PPARγ as a direct XOR interactor (Fig. 3G-H), which was further validated by computational docking (Hdock) analysis (Fig. 3I). Immunofluorescence confirmed XOR and PPARγ co‑localization in TSN‑treated monocytes, with a positive correlation in their MFI (Fig. 3J-L). Their direct interaction was further validated by co‑immunoprecipitation (Fig. 3M-N). Besides, XOR knockdown reduced the levels of both XOR and PPARγ, whereas PPARγ knockdown did not affect XOR expression. This unidirectional regulatory relationship positioned XOR as an upstream regulator of PPARγ signaling (Fig. 3O-R).

We next focused on identifying the downstream PPARγ target genes that mediate its metabolic effects. By intersecting predicted PPARγ targets (CHEA/GTRD databases) with known cholesterol metabolism genes, we identified a set of candidate regulators (Fig. 3S). Further screening based on correlation (R > 0.3) and significance (p < 0.05) yielded two high-confidence candidates: CD36 and SQLE (Fig. S7A-B). However, subsequent knockdown experiments revealed that only CD36 expression was robustly suppressed upon PPARγ depletion, whereas SQLE remained largely unaffected (Fig. 3T-U). Moreover, strong positive correlations were observed between XOR and CD36 (R = 0.8652) and between XOR and lipid raft content (R = 0.8003) in the clinical samples (Fig. S6F). Together, these results indicated that XOR primarily regulated cholesterol metabolism via the PPARγ-CD36 axis.

Pharmacological stabilization of XOR by Res616 suppressed ubiquitin-dependent degradation

Given that the multifaceted phenotype of TAMs is shaped by cholesterol metabolism, we next sought to explore potential immunomodulatory agents that restore cholesterol homeostasis. An in-house library containing 50 compounds with 3 distinct skeletal types was screened, including resveratrol derivatives [22], benzoselenazolestilbene hybrids [23] and indole-indanone derivatives [24]. Among them, Res616 (S6) exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity on the polarization of TAMs, marked by decreased CD163 expression in monocytes/macrophages (Fig. 4A-B and Fig. S8A). Based on dose-response optimization, 10 µM was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. S9A-C). Additionally, to confirm whether this concentration acts specifically on macrophages, Huh7 tumor cells and T cells were similarly treated in vitro. No significant impact was observed on cell viability, tumor cell proliferation (Ki67), or CD8⁺ T cell function (IFN-γ, GzmB, TIM-3, and LAG-3), indicating that Res616 primarily targeted macrophages (Fig. S9D-J).

To systematically validate the high-confidence functional targets of Res616 and elucidate its cell-compartment selectivity, we performed cellular thermal shift assay-combined mass spectrum (CETSA-MS) in both primary CD14⁺ cells and HCC cells (Fig. 4C), and implemented a multi-dimensional screening criteria on the newly generated CETSA-MS datasets. Specifically, a candidate protein must be identified with high fidelity based on more than 3 unique characteristic peptides, exhibit a pronounced thermal stabilization with a Fold Change > 2 upon Res616 treatment in the macrophage group, and simultaneously show no distinct susceptibility in hepatoma cells (Fig. 4D-E). Remarkably, XOR emerged as a top-three candidate across the entire proteome with 6 high-confidence characteristic peptides (Fig. 4F), thereby demonstrating its exceptional target selectivity within the macrophage compartment. Furthermore, through combining CETSA‑MS data with virtual target screening across multiple databases (including Wemol, PharmMapper, SuperPred, and SEA), XOR stood out as a target of Res616 (Fig. S10A), which further demonstrated its potential target selectivity within the macrophage compartment. Molecular docking revealed that Res616 forms hydrogen bonds with XOR residues I696, P694, H615, and A905, along with a carbon‑hydrogen bond with L903, yielding a binding free energy of -8.56 kcal/mol (Fig. S10B). Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) and the CETSA were then used to validate direct binding. DARTS confirmed that Res616 enhanced XOR stability and protected it from proteolytic degradation (Fig. 4G-J), while CETSA demonstrated that Res616 shielded XOR from thermal and lysis‑induced denaturation (Fig. 4K-L). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis further demonstrated that Res616 bound directly and effectively to XOR (KD = 6.25 µM), as evidenced by dose-dependent changes in response units (Fig. 4M).

The regulatory mechanism was further explored by treating TSN-exposed monocytes with Res616. Res616 restored XOR protein levels, as shown by western blot and flow cytometry (Fig. 4N-O and Fig. S10C), without altering XDH mRNA expression (Fig. 4P). GSEA suggested that Res616 protected protein from ubiquitination (Fig. S10D). Consistent with this, immunoprecipitation of XOR revealed that Res616 reduced its ubiquitination and restored protein levels by decreasing K48-linked ubiquitination, with no effect on K63-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 4Q-R and Fig. S10E). Together, these results identified Res616 as a stabilizer of XOR, acting by inhibiting its K48-linked ubiquitin-mediated degradation.

Res616 reversed immunosuppressive TAM reprogramming and restored cholesterol metabolic balance

Next, we further explored the effects of Res616 on TAMs by analyzing the transcriptional profiles of these cells by RNA sequencing (PRJCA051289). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of 260 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, > 1.3‑fold change) showed enrichment in immune‑related pathways such as immune response, inflammatory response, and macrophage differentiation (Fig. 5A-B). Consistent with this transcriptional shift, Res616 reversed Huh7‑TSN‑induced immunosuppression by downregulating CD163 and upregulating CD86 (Fig. 5C-E), and partially restored the expression of PD‑L1, Arg‑1, and HLA‑DR (Fig. 5F-G and Fig. S11A). Res616 also improved macrophage morphology and CD14 levels (Fig. S11B-D).

In MLR, Res616 reversed the suppressive effect of TSN-treated macrophages on IFN-γ and GzmB expression in CD8⁺ T cells and reduced the upregulation of the exhaustion marker TIM-3, while LAG-3 expression remained unaffected (Fig. 5H-K and Fig. S11F). Importantly, this effect was independent of antioxidant activity. Although both Res616 and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) reduced ROS levels, NAC was unable to restore XOR expression in macrophages or rescue CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxicity and exhaustion (Fig. S11G-L). Together, these data indicated that Res616 alleviated TSN-driven immunosuppressive reprogramming in macrophages through a mechanism distinct from its antioxidant effects. To assess XOR specificity, XOR-knockdown macrophages were treated with Res616. As expected, XOR knockdown completely abolished the effects of Res616, as evidenced by the sustained immunosuppressive phenotype of macrophages and the persistent low cytotoxicity and exhausted state of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. S11M-U).

Given the tight coupling between macrophage phenotype and metabolism, we next assessed metabolic alterations induced by Res616. GSEA demonstrated significant enrichment of the PPAR signaling pathway and cholesterol metabolism following Res616 treatment in macrophages (Fig. 6A and Fig. S11E), as validated by RNA-seq (Fig. 6B), RT-qPCR (Fig. 6C), and flow cytometry (Fig. 6D). The effect was PPARγ-dependent, as siRNA‑mediated PPARG knockdown abolished Res616‑induced PPARG restoration (Fig. 6E). Res616 also restored CD36 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6F-G). Consistent with CD36’s role in cholesterol uptake, Res616 rescued the Huh7‑TSN‑induced defect in cholesterol internalization (Fig. 6H-I) and replenished intracellular cholesterol, shown by increased cholera toxin subunit B (CTB) and Filipin-III staining (Fig. 6J-M). These data demonstrated that Res616 restored cholesterol homeostasis in monocytes/macrophages by reactivating the PPARγ-CD36 axis.

Compared with controls, Res616 alone or in combination with anti‑PD‑L1 markedly suppressed tumor growth, as reflected by reduced tumor volume, weight, and size (Fig. 7B-E). Consistent with our in vitro findings, Res616 upregulated XOR and PPARγ expression in tumor-infiltrating macrophages (Fig. 7H-I). Analysis of immune infiltration showed that Res616 decreased CD206⁺ macrophages while increasing CD86⁺ macrophages, boosted CD8⁺ T cell accumulation, and raised the frequencies of IFN‑γ⁺CD8⁺ and GzmB⁺CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 7F-G and J). These effects were further enhanced when Res616 was combined with anti‑PD‑L1 (Fig. 7H-J and Fig. S12A-B). Moreover, Res616 elevated Filipin‑III staining and upregulated PPARG and CD36 mRNA in tumor‑derived macrophages (Fig. 7K-L).

Taken together, these results demonstrated that Res616 elevated macrophage cholesterol levels via the XOR/PPARγ-CD36 axis and inhibited macrophage polarization toward an M2‑like phenotype. This remodeling of the TME promoted CD8⁺ T cell activation and cytotoxicity, thereby potentiating the anti‑tumor activity of anti‑PD‑L1 therapy.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified XOR as a novel modulator of cholesterol metabolism in TAMs. The loss of XOR in TAMs not only contributed to the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs, but also correlated with poor prognosis in HCC. Of note, Res616 could effectively reverse the polarization and metabolic dysfunction of TAMs through stabilizing XOR, thereby increasing cholesterol uptake through the PPARγ-CD36 axis. This effect also benefited anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy in hepatoma-bearing mice. Our study provided mechanistic and therapeutic insights into TAM metabolic reprogramming for HCC therapy. TAMs are the predominant population in HCC and act as key modulators of immune suppression due to their rapid recruitment and high plasticity [25–27]. Using scRNA-seq data, we systematically mapped the heterogeneity of TAMs in human HCC and identified XOR⁻ TAMs subset as a tumor promoter that is positively correlated with advanced disease stage and poor prognosis in HCC. Loss of XOR expression in TAMs was observed in both peripheral blood and tumor tissues of HCC patients, and a gradual decline in XOR expression was found with tumor progression. To date, the role of XOR in tumorigenesis remains paradoxical, with its function as a tumor promoter or suppressor being contingent on the particular cancer type and cellular context [16]. Consistent with our results, reduced XOR expression has been observed in TAMs in HCC [28]. Thus, our study comprehensively assessed the protective role of XOR in HCC progression. Regarding the mechanisms that drive metabolic reprogramming, an important advancement made in this study is that we identified XOR as a previously unknown master regulator of cholesterol metabolism. This study showed that loss of XOR contributed to the generation of immature and immunosuppressive macrophages by mediating cholesterol metabolism. This conclusion was supported by the following observations: (i) scRNA-seq data analysis of HCC tissues revealed significant enrichment of cholesterol metabolism in TAMs, accompanied by reduced XDH expression. XOR⁻ TAMs exhibited significantly lower cholesterol levels in both in situ detection and in vitro induction models. TSN treatment significantly downregulated XOR expression, which was aligned with decreased cholesterol levels and lipid rafts. (ii) XOR⁻ macrophages had markedly lower cell size and granularity but retained CD14 expression, indicating that XOR may limit the differentiation of monocytes into TAMs. (iii) The MFI of XOR was negatively correlated with CD163 and PD-L1 expression in TSN-treated monocytes. Knockdown of XOR in monocytes increased CD163, PD-L1, and Arg-1 expression, which were critical for monocytes to exert their immunosuppressive function. Consequently, XOR-deficient TAMs exhibited a reduced capacity to support CD8⁺ T cell activation by enhancing PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression, thereby facilitating tumor progression. (iv) Spatial transcriptomics showed that XDH⁺ macrophages were co-localized with CD8⁺ T cells. MLR also confirmed that ablation of XOR in TAMs aggravated the exhaustion of CD8⁺ T cells, as shown by impaired production of IFN-γ and GzmB and increased TIM-3 expression. Conversely, XOR overexpression reversed these effects. Moreover, we found that neither xanthine nor uric acid had any effect on the polarization of TAMs. These results raised a very interesting issue that XOR rewired the polarization of TAMs independently of its classical purine metabolic pathway.

Macrophage cholesterol metabolism is closely regulated by coordinated metabolic signaling networks [29]. PPAR pathway has been widely recognized as a major branch of cholesterol catabolism [30, 31]. This study identified that PPARγ is regulated by XOR, but not PPARα or PPARβ/δ, and its interaction have also been confirmed by IP-MS and Co-IP results. Although we have validated XOR stabilized PPARγ through protecting it from degradation, whether XOR fulfills the criteria of a classical molecular chaperone remains to be fully elucidated in our further study. Our findings were consistent with a role of PPARγ in the maintenance of cholesterol homeostasis in NKT cells, possibly by regulating cholesterol synthesis in HCC [32]. Specifically, among the signaling cascades downstream of PPARγ, CD36 was significantly involved. CD36 is the predominant receptor affecting lipid uptake [33]. Qin et al. also found that overexpression of CD36 enhanced Flotillin-1 expression [34].

Given the fact that loss of XOR in TAMs contributed to their suppressive phenotype, we sought to determine the therapeutic potential of targeting XOR for HCC [14]. We screened an in-house library comprising 50 compounds based on three distinct, well-studied anticancer scaffolds. Among them, Res616 most potently suppressed immunosuppressive TAM differentiation, demonstrating the highest CD163 inhibition rate. Res616 was designed by incorporating resveratrol with the pharmacophore of clioquinol (CQ), both of which are well known to exhibit remarkable antitumor effects [35–37]. Res616 possessed dual antioxidant and immunomodulatory activities, coupled with minimal toxicity to immune cells, and showed high biosafety in vivo [22]. Moreover, we observed that Res616 had no significant effect on the viability or function of tumor cells and T cells, suggesting that its primary action was mediated through macrophage regulation. Consistently, functional analyses demonstrated that Res616 reprogrammed macrophages into an M1-like phenotype. This reprogramming downregulated immunosuppressive markers (PD-L1 and Arg-1) and restored the ability of macrophages to potentiate CD8⁺ T cell effector function, an effect that was independent of its antioxidant activity. CETSA-MS combined with virtual screening, DARTS, CETSA, and SPR experiments confirmed that Res616 bound directly to XOR with high affinity. Importantly, Res616 failed to exert its effects in XOR-deficient macrophages, further validating the efficacy of the target. Interestingly, Res616 didn’t reverse the mRNA expression of XOR but protected it from ubiquitination, specifically by reducing its ubiquitination on the K48-linked chain, with no effect on the K63-linked chain. Most importantly, Res616 restored cholesterol homeostasis through the XOR-PPARγ-CD36 axis. It has been reported that the local immune landscape determines tumor PD-L1 heterogeneity and sensitivity to therapy [38]. In line with these in vitro findings, in vivo mice experiments confirmed that Res616 synergized with anti-PD-L1 to increase the cholesterol levels in macrophages, and that the infiltration and activation of CD8⁺ T cells were also enhanced within HCC tumor tissues.

We acknowledge that this study has several limitations. It lacks a macrophage-specific XDH knockout mouse model, and the in vitro co-culture system does not fully recapitulate the physiological tumor immune microenvironment. While our study identifies the XOR-PD-L1 axis as a major driver of CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion, the immunosuppressive microenvironment of HCC is inherently multifactorial. Other factors such as Arg-1 and various inhibitory cytokines may act in concert with PD-L1 [39, 40]. However, PD-L1 inhibition significantly rescued T cell function in our XOR-deficient co-culture model underscored the therapeutic potential of targeting this specific metabolic-immune checkpoint. Nevertheless, our findings still provided a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for HCC. However, the specific ubiquitination sites of XOR and the E3 ubiquitin ligases involved remain to be elucidated. To identify the E3 ligases involved in XOR degradation, we re-mined our IP-MS results for XOR shown in Fig. 3G. Among the 122 interacting proteins identified across independent replicates, proteins related to E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase activity was screened. Two high-confidence candidates, TRIM21 and TRIM28, were consistently detected in the XOR interactome. Notably, TRIM21 and TRIM28 have been previously reported in the metabolic reprogramming of immune cells and the regulation of various redox-related enzymes [41, 42]. Hence, in our further study, TRIM21 and TRIM28 will be prioritized as the primary candidates for investigating the specific E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination of XOR. We are currently designing more comprehensive experiments to systematically validate the role of XOR in HCC immune regulation and its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we proposed that the loss of XOR in TAMs impaired the PPARγ expression, remodeled cholesterol homeostasis, and caused TAMs to polarize toward the alternatively activated M2 phenotype. Furthermore, the resveratrol derivative Res616, which directly bound to and stabilizes the XOR protein, increased PPARγ activity, mediated cholesterol uptake, reverted the tumor-promoting functions of TAMs and reduced tumor progression. These studies revealed an unexpected role for XOR in driving TAM-mediated tumor progression while pointing to a potentially novel anti-tumor therapeutic strategy.
