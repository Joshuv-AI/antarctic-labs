Refined Detection for Gumbel Watermarking
Tor Lattimore

Abstract
We propose a simple detection mechanism for the Gumbel watermarking scheme proposed by 1 . The new mechanism is proven to be near-optimal in a problem-dependent sense among all model-agnostic watermarking schemes under the assumption that the next-token distribution is sampled i.i.d.

Introduction

We are interested in the problem of watermarking the output of large language models.
The token space is a finite set
\Sigma=\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Let
\Sigma^{*}
be the space of all finite sequences over alphabet
\Sigma
.
A language model is a function
p:\Sigma^{*}\to\Delta(\Sigma)
where
\Delta(\Sigma)
is the space
of probability distributions over the tokens. Equivalently,
p
is a probability kernel from
\Sigma^{*}
to
\Sigma
.
A sequence of tokens
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
is sampled autoregressively from
p
in the usual way:

\displaystyle A_{t}\sim p(\cdot|A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})=P_{t}\,.

Watermarking is a mechanism for sampling from the language model (preferably without distortion) in such a way that the owner
of the model can later distinguish those sequences that have been sampled from the model from those that have not.
The Gumbel watermarking scheme proposed by
1
works as follows (see
Algorithm

1
):

1.

Let
key
be a secret key known to the language model designer.

2.

When sampling
A_{t}
from the model, use a hash function to produce a random seed
using
key
and
A_{t-1},\ldots,A_{t-k}
where
k
is some small integer. For example,
k=3
.

3.

Use this random seed to generate pseudorandom uniform variables
(U_{t,a})_{a\in\Sigma}
in
[0,1]^{\Sigma}
and
then select

\displaystyle A_{t}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,max}_{a\in\Sigma}\frac{P_{t}(a)}{-\log(U_{t,a})}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,max}_{a\in\Sigma}\left[-\log(-\log(U_{t,a}))+\log(P_{t}(a))\right]\,.

Setting aside the issue that the
(U_{t,a})
are only pseudorandom, the conditional law of
A_{t}
is
P_{t}
by the Gumbel trick and recalling that

-\log(-\log(U))
is Gumbel distributed when
U
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
.

Detection

Detection works by noting that, when given the secret key,
the sequence of noise random variables
(U_{t})
can be reconstructed from the text
(A_{t})
. Then let
V_{t}=U_{t,A_{t}}
.
When the text is not watermarked one would expect
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
to behave like an i.i.d. sequence of random variables that are uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
.
On the other hand, when the text is watermarked, then
V_{t}
is not uniformly distributed unless
P_{t}
is a Dirac.
Hence, the detector can identify the watermark by proving statistically that the observed
(V_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
were not sampled i.i.d. from the uniform distribution.
Importantly, detection is possible without access to the model.
The generic detection mechanism is given in

Contribution

We study the Gumbel watermarking procedure (
Algorithm

1
), but replace the detection test proposed by
1
with a new test that is theoretically more statistically efficient.
We prove nearly matching problem-dependent upper and lower bounds on the number of tokens needed to detect watermarked text.
Our bounds are in terms of an entropy-like quantity of the distribution of next-token distributions.
The lower bound applies to all watermarking schemes in the same framework (to be explained) and hence the Gumbel scheme combined with the new
detector is (in a certain sense) nearly optimal.

Related work

There are now a huge variety of schemes for watermarking language models, with a range of desiderata
and analysis objectives
(
19
;
20
)
.
A relatively recent summary of the current state-of-the-art can be found in the review by
12
, but the field is growing incredibly rapidly.
Some schemes are distortionary in the sense that they change the distribution of the model
(
13
;
25
;
18
)
while
others are non-distortionary
(
1
;
5
)
.
Our work is based on the non-distortionary Gumbel watermarking scheme proposed and analysed by
1
.

1
used a detection mechanism based on a different statistic than the one proposed here and proved that
detection occurs with high probability once
n=\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\bar{H}^{2})
with
\bar{H}
the average entropy
(see
Eq.

1
).
This is the detection time that seems to be obtained most naturally when the concentration analysis used is
not variance-aware
(
4
)
.

11
theoretically study watermarking procedures that are both distortion free and
distortionary. Perhaps the most relevant of their results is a proof that when
P_{t}=p
is constant, then
detection is possible if
n=\Omega(\log(1/H(p))\log(1/\delta)/H(p))
and that this is optimal. But this result is not obtained
using a simple practical watermarking procedure.

21
ambitiously tackle the problem of designing exactly optimal statistical watermarking/detection schemes
but focus principally on the single-shot case where only a single token is sampled from the model, with preliminary results
in the sequential setting with a constant token distribution (
P_{t}=p\,\forall t
).
Like us,
16
also examine the problem of refining the detection of Gumbel watermarking (and the scheme by
14
). They propose a model-agnostic detector that is optimal in an asymptotic minimax framework where
P_{t}
is assumed to
satisfy
\max_{a\in\Sigma}P_{t}(a)\leq 1-\Delta
for some user-provided constant
\Delta\in(0,1)
.
On the one hand, they are able to establish a kind of exact optimality. On the other hand, thanks to the minimax nature of their
optimality definition, it is unclear if their method achieves the same kind of near-optimal problem-dependent detection efficiency
as our proposal.
Our statistical test is inspired by the classical literature on goodness-of-fit tests, and particularly the Anderson–Darling test
(
2
)
.
Goodness-of-fit tests have been used for Gumbel watermarking by
9
.

17
also use goodness-of-fit tests for detection. They primarily focus on a more complicated version of the problem where some of the text has been generated by an LLM
but other parts are human-generated. Nevertheless, they prove a kind of optimality in certain scenarios. At the same time, their results seem incomparable to ours.

Notation

The sequence length
n>1
and confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
are fixed throughout.
When
\mu
and
\nu
are probability measures on the same space and
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
, then
the relative entropy and
\chi^{2}
-divergence between
\mu
and
\nu
are

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mu,\nu)=\int\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu}{\operatorname{d}\!\nu}(x)\right)\operatorname{d}\!\mu(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\int\left(\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu}{\operatorname{d}\!\nu}(x)-1\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\nu(x)\,.

The uniform probability measure on
[0,1]
is
\mathcal{U}([0,1])
, which is also denoted by
\lambda
.
The
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(U_{s})_{s=1}^{t}
and
(A_{s})_{s=1}^{t}
is

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(U_{1},A_{1},\ldots,U_{t},A_{t})
.

Null and alternative hypotheses

Our results on the correctness and detection time for the Gumbel watermarking scheme
depend on two probability measures. Under the null hypothesis
\mathbb{P}_{0}
we assume that

(U_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
and
(A_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
are independent
so that
(V_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is a sequence of independent uniformly distributed random variables.
On the other hand, under the alternative hypothesis
\mathbb{P}_{1}
we assume that

(U_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is a sequence of independent random elements sampled from
\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{\Sigma})
and

A_{t}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,max}_{a\in\Sigma}P_{t}(a)/(-\log(U_{t,a}))
where
P_{t}=p(\cdot|A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})

is the next-token distribution generated by the model.
The expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{k}
with
k\in\{0,1\}
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{k}
.
These assumptions are not unusual for the analysis of watermarking schemes
(
11
;
16
;
5
)
.
Care is needed, however, since the statistical assumptions are not strictly-speaking correct because
the
(U_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
are generated using pseudorandomness and with seeds constructed from the observed tokens.
The statistical assumption could in principle be justified using cryptographic arguments and with
certain modifications to the procedure for generating
(U_{t})
.
Empirical evidence suggests that
the distortion due to pseudorandomness does not impact model performance on downstream tasks
(
5
)
.

Entropy-like quantities

Given a distribution
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
let

p_{\epsilon}(a)=p(a)\bm{1}(p(a)\leq\epsilon)
and
p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)=p(a)\bm{1}(p(a)>\epsilon)
.
The entropy of a distribution
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
is defined by

H(p)=-\sum_{a\in\Sigma}p(a)\log p(a)

with
0\log 0=0
.
We also need another entropy-like quantity, defined for
q\in(0,1)
by

\displaystyle G^{q}(p)=\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\left(p(a)(1-p(a))\right)^{q}\,.

Lastly, define
G_{\epsilon}(p)=G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(p_{\epsilon})/\sqrt{\epsilon}+G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(p_{\epsilon}^{c})
.

Exponential detection

The Gumbel scheme was proposed by
1
, who suggested the following simple detection mechanism.
Let
S_{t}=\log(1/(1-V_{t}))
.
Under the null hypothesis
\mathbb{P}_{0}
,
(S_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is a sequence of independent exponentially distributed random variables.
The detector can proclaim a sequence is watermarked if
\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}
is unusually large relative
to what would be expected under the null hypothesis.
In the language of
Algorithm

2
,

\displaystyle\left((V_{t})_{t=1}^{n},\delta\right)=\bm{1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}\geq\tau_{\star}\right)\,,

where the critical threshold
\tau_{\star}
is chosen so that
\mathbb{P}_{0}(\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}\geq\tau_{\star})=\delta
.

1
showed that under the alternative hypothesis
\mathbb{P}_{1}
,
S_{t}
has a larger mean than under the null and
that detection occurs with probability at least
1-\delta
if
n=\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\bar{H}^{2})
where

\displaystyle\bar{H}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}H(P_{t})

(1)

is the average entropy.

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(S_{t}-R_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(a)}}}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[S_{t}-R_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[(S_{t}-R_{t})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]}{2}-\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(b)}}}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[S_{t}-R_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[S_{t}-R_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]-\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[S_{t}-R_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(c)}}}}}{{\geq}}\frac{c}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})-\frac{cn\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}-\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(d)}}}}}{{\geq}}\frac{c}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})-\frac{cn\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}-\tau\,,

where
(a)
follows from
Lemma

23
,

(b)
since
S_{t}-R_{t}\in[0,1/\sqrt{\epsilon}]
,

(c)
from
Lemma

4
and

(d)
from the definition of
\tau
.
The law of
R_{t}
under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is the same as the law of
S_{t}
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
.
Hence, repeating the argument in
Lemma

5
shows that
with
\mathbb{P}_{1}
-probability at least
1-\delta
,
\sum_{t=1}^{n}R_{t}\geq-\tau
.
Combining the above with a union bound shows with probability at least
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(S_{t}-R_{t})+\sum_{t=1}^{n}R_{t}\geq\frac{c}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})-\frac{cn\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}-2\tau\geq\frac{c}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})-3\tau\,.\qed

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Let
C=8/c
where
c
is defined in
Lemma

4
and
define events
E
and
F
by

\displaystyle E=\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})\geq C\tau\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad F=\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}\geq\frac{c}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})-3\tau\right\}\,.

By
Lemma

6
,
\mathbb{P}(F)\geq 1-2\delta
.
Suppose that
E
and
\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}<\tau_{\star}
. Then

\displaystyle\tau\geq\tau_{\star}>\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}-\frac{c}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})+4\tau\,,

which after arranging implies that
F^{c}
holds.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})\geq C\tau,\,\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{alg:detect} returns }\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(E\text{ and }\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}<\tau_{\star}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(F^{c}\right)\leq 2\delta\,.\qed

Theorem

2
can be used to bound the detection time for
various synthetic representations of a language model.
By definition
\tau=\Theta(\sqrt{n\log(n)\log(1/\delta)})
, so detection is likely to occur whenever

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})=\Omega\left(\sqrt{n\log(n)\log(1/\delta)}\right)\,.

Example 7

(
no entropy
)
.

Suppose that
P_{t}
is a Dirac for all
t
.
Then
G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})=0
for all
t
and detection does not occur with high probability.
Distortion-free watermarking is not possible without entropy.

Example 8

(
constant low entropy
)
.

Suppose that
P_{t}=p
where
p=(1-\beta,\beta)
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. Then

\displaystyle G_{\epsilon}(p)=\Theta\left(\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}\right)\,.

(8)

Hence, by
Theorem

2
, detection occurs with probability at least
1-2\delta
if

\displaystyle nG_{\epsilon}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})=\Omega\left(\sqrt{n\log(n)\log(1/\delta)}\right)\,,

which after substituting
Eq.

8
is equivalent to

n=\Omega\left(\frac{\log(n)\log(1/\delta)}{\beta(1-\beta)}\right)
.

Example 9

(
rare high entropy
)
.

Suppose that
P_{t}
is a Dirac with probability
1-\beta\in(0,1)
and otherwise
P_{t}=p=(1/2,1/2)
.
Then
G_{\epsilon}(p)=1
and with high probability

\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})=\Omega(\beta n)
and detection occurs once

\displaystyle n=\Omega\left(\frac{\log(n)\log(1/\delta)}{\beta^{2}}\right)\,.

Example 10

(
many rare tokens
)
.

Suppose that
d\geq 2
and
\beta\in(0,1/2)
and
P_{t}=p
is constant with
p=(1-\beta,\beta/(d-1),\beta/(d-1),\ldots,\beta/(d-1))
.
Then

\displaystyle G_{\epsilon}(p)

\displaystyle\approx\begin{cases}\sqrt{d\beta}&\text{if }\beta/(d-1)\geq\epsilon\\
\sqrt{\beta}+\beta/\sqrt{\epsilon}&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Then detection occurs once
n=\Omega\left(\frac{\log(n)\log(1/\delta)}{\beta}\right)
.
Note that this is roughly the time it takes to see
\Omega(\log(n)\log(1/\delta))
rare tokens.

The previous examples are specialisations of the general setting that
(P_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is sampled from
a product measure
\xi^{n}
.

Example 11

(
iid setting
)
.

Suppose that
P,P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}
are independent and identically distributed with law
\xi
and
G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})=O(1)
almost surely and that
\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{t})]\ll 1
is small, which is
the interesting case.
By Hoeffding’s bound, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})

\displaystyle\geq n\mathbb{E}[G_{\epsilon}(P)]-O\left(\sqrt{n\log(1/\delta)}\right)\,.

Hence detection occurs with high probability once

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{\epsilon}(P)]\approx\sqrt{\frac{\log(n)\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\,.

(9)

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\epsilon}(P)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})/\sqrt{\epsilon}+G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})\right]\geq\max\left(\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]/\sqrt{\epsilon},\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})]\right)\,.

Suppose that
n
is the smallest value such that
Eq.

9
holds.
By the definition of
\epsilon=\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}
,

\displaystyle n\approx\min\left(\frac{\sqrt{-\log(\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})])}}{\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]},\,\frac{-\log(\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})])}{\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})]^{2}}\right)\log(1/\delta)

This bound is not fully explicit, since
\epsilon
on the right-hand side depends on
n
. Nevertheless, it serves as a useful comparison for our lower bound.

4
Lower bounds

The Gumbel watermarking procedure combined with the detection mechanism described above is nearly optimal in the setting where the detection process
cannot use the model.
We will formalise this by assuming that
(P_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is an independent
and identically distributed sequence sampled from a suitably
symmetric distribution
\xi
on
\Delta(\Sigma)
.
Before this, however, we provide a more-or-less known lower bound
that holds even if the model is known.
This result is essentially known already, though with a different proof
(
11
)
.
The bound holds for any sequential watermarking scheme, which we now formalise.

Definition 12
.

A sequential watermarking scheme is characterised by a probability kernel
\kappa
from
[0,1]\times\Delta(\Sigma)\to\Delta(\Sigma)
such that for all
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(\cdot|u,p)\operatorname{d}\!u=p\,.

Kernels
\kappa
satisfying the above will be called valid selection kernels.

This definition is intended to be used with secret source of noise
(U_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
that is i.i.d. with law
\mathcal{U}([0,1])
.

Example 13
.

The Gumbel scheme can be recovered by letting
\kappa(\cdot|u,p)
be a Dirac on

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,max}_{a\in\Sigma}\frac{p(a)}{-\log\phi_{a}(u)}\,,

where
(\phi_{a})_{a\in\Sigma}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a collection of functions such that
(\phi_{a}(U))_{a\in\Sigma}
has law
\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{\Sigma})
whenever
U
has law
\mathcal{U}([0,1])
.

Remark 14
.

The kernel
\kappa(\cdot|u,p)=p
for all
u\in[0,1]
corresponds to ignoring the source of randomness. That is, no watermarking.

Assumption 15
.

Suppose that
(U_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is an i.i.d. sequence with law
\mathcal{U}([0,1])
and
\kappa
is a valid selection kernel (
Definition

12
).
Under the null distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
we assume that
A_{t}
has law
p(\cdot|A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})
and is independent of
(U_{t})
.
Under the alternative distribution
\mathbb{P}_{1}
it is assumed that
A_{t}
is sampled from
\kappa(\cdot|U_{t},P_{t})
where
P_{t}=p(\cdot|A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})
.

Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(A_{1},U_{1},\ldots,A_{t},U_{t})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the information available to the detector.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}
be the null and alternative measures defined in
15
.
Suppose that
T\in\{0,1\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable and

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=0)\geq 1-\delta
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}(T=1)\geq 1-\delta
.
Then
n\geq\mathbb{E}[\bar{H}]^{-1}\log(1/(4\delta))
.

Proof.

Let
I_{t}(X;Y)
be the mutual information between
X
and
Y
under
\mathbb{P}_{1}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t})
.
By the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality,

\displaystyle 2\delta

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)+\mathbb{P}_{1}(T=0)\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{t-1}(U_{t},A_{t}\in\cdot),\mathbb{P}_{t-1}(U_{t}\in\cdot)\otimes\mathbb{P}_{t-1}(A_{t}\in\cdot))\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}I_{t-1}(U_{t};A_{t})\right]\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}H(P_{t})\right]\right)=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-n\mathbb{E}[\bar{H}]\right)\,.

Rearranging shows that
n\geq\mathbb{E}[\bar{H}]^{-1}\log(1/(4\delta))
.
∎

The Gumbel watermarking scheme combined with the new detection mechanism is nearly optimal when the model is not known and
when
(P_{t})
has law
\xi^{n}
for some distribution
\xi
on
\Delta(\Sigma)
.
The assumption that the model is not known is expressed by making a symmetry assumption on
\xi
.

Definition 17
.

A probability measure
\xi
on
\Delta(\Sigma)
is symmetric if when
P
has law
\xi
, then
P
and
P\circ\sigma
have the same law for any
permutation
\sigma
of
\Sigma
.

Assumption 18
.

\xi
is a symmetric probability measure on
\Delta(\Sigma)

and
\kappa
is a valid selection kernel and
\kappa_{0}(\cdot|u,p)=p(\cdot)
. Let
\mathbb{Q}_{0}=\lambda\otimes\xi\otimes\kappa_{0}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{1}=\lambda\otimes\xi\otimes\kappa
.
Given
k\in\{0,1\}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{k}
be the law of
U_{1},A_{1},\ldots,U_{n},A_{n}
when
(U_{t},P_{t},A_{t})
has law
\mathbb{Q}_{k}^{n}
.
That is
\mathbb{P}_{0}
is the law of
(U_{1},A_{1},\ldots,U_{n},A_{n})
under the null hypothesis and
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is the law of the same under the alternative hypothesis.

Remark 19
.

The symmetry assumption on
\xi
ensures that
\mathbb{P}_{1}(A_{t}\in\cdot)=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
. Hence the marginals of both
U_{t}
and
A_{t}
are the same
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}
. But for
\mathbb{P}_{0}
they are independent, which is not true for
\mathbb{P}_{1}
.

Theorem 20
.

Suppose that
\xi
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}
are defined as in
18
and that

T\in\{0,1\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable and

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=0)\geq 1-\delta
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}(T=1)\geq 1-\delta
.
Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
and
P
with law
\xi
,

\displaystyle n\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{2\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]},\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{16\delta}\right)}{16\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})\right]^{2}}\right)\,.

(10)

Proof of
Theorem

20
.

We proceed in three steps.

Step 1: Setup and entropy inequalities

Suppose that
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
and
Eq.

10
does not hold.
Then
\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]<\frac{1}{2n}
.
Let
E^{n}=\cap_{t=1}^{n}\{P_{t}(A_{t})>\epsilon\}
.
Note that the law of
E^{n}
is the same under both
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.
Let
P
have law
\xi
and
A\sim P
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{0}(E^{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{0}(P(A)>\epsilon)^{n}=(1-\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{0}(P(A)\leq\epsilon))^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a\in\Sigma}P(a)\bm{1}(P(a)\leq\epsilon)\right]\right)^{n}\geq\left(1-2\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})\right]\right)^{n}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\geq\frac{1}{4}\,,

(11)

where the second inequality follows from the assumption that
\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]<1/(2n)
and the first
from the fact that
\epsilon\leq 1/2
so that

\displaystyle G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})=\sum_{a:p(a)\leq\epsilon}p(a)(1-p(a))\geq\sum_{a:p(a)\leq\epsilon}p(a)/2\,.

From
Eq.

11
it follows that
\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{0}(T=1|E^{n})=\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{0}(T=1,E^{n})/\mathbb{Q}_{0}(E^{n})\leq 4\delta
.
Similarly,
\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{1}(T=0|E^{n})\leq 4\delta
.
Therefore, by the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality,

\displaystyle 8\delta

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{0}(T=1|E^{n})+\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{1}(T=0|E^{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\operatorname{KL}\right)\,,

where
\operatorname{KL}=\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}^{n}(U_{1},A_{1},\ldots,U_{n},A_{n}\in\cdot|E^{n}),\mathbb{Q}_{0}^{n}(U_{1},A_{1},\ldots,U_{n},A_{n}\in\cdot|E^{n}))
.
Now
\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{k}(\cdot|E^{n})
is a product measure for both
k=0
and
k=1
.
Therefore, abbreviating
U=U_{1}
and
A=A_{1}
and
\lambda=\mathcal{U}([0,1])
and
E=\{P(A)>\epsilon\}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}

\displaystyle=n\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}(U,A\in\cdot|E),\mathbb{Q}_{0}(U,A\in\cdot|E))

\displaystyle=n\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Q}_{1}(A=a|E)\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}(U\in\cdot|A=a,E),\mathbb{Q}_{0}(U\in\cdot|A=a,E))

\displaystyle=n\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Q}_{1}(A=a|E)\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}(U\in\cdot|A=a,E),\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{d}\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\chi^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}(U\in\cdot|A=a,E),\lambda)\,,\

(12)

where the inequality follows from
Lemma

22
and because
\mathbb{Q}_{1}(A=a|E)=1/d
by the symmetry assumption.

Step 2: Bounding the chi-squared divergence

We start by introducing a variety of conditional measures (regular versions):

•

\xi(\cdot|a)=\mathbb{Q}_{1}(P=\cdot|A=a,E)
.

•

\mu(\cdot|a)=\mathbb{Q}_{1}(U=\cdot|A=a,E)
.

•

\mu(\cdot|a,p)=\mathbb{Q}_{1}(U=\cdot|P,A=a,E)
evaluated at
p
.

By Bayes’ law, when
p(a)>\epsilon
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\xi(\cdot|a)}{\operatorname{d}\!\xi}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{dp_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}{\mathbb{Q}_{1}(E)}\,.

(13)

Next, if
p(a)>\epsilon
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu(\cdot|a,p)}{\operatorname{d}\!\lambda}(x)=\frac{\kappa(a|x,p)}{\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(a|y,p)\operatorname{d}\!y}\leq\frac{1}{p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}\,.

(14)

Let
Q
be independent of
P
and have law
\xi
.
Then:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu(\cdot|a),\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu(\cdot|a)}{\operatorname{d}\!\lambda}(x)-1\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu(\cdot|a)}{\operatorname{d}\!\lambda}(x)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!x-1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(a)}}}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu(\cdot|a,p)}{\operatorname{d}\!\lambda}(x)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(p|a)\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!x-1

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu(\cdot|a,p)}{\operatorname{d}\!\lambda}(x)\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\mu(\cdot|a,q)}{\operatorname{d}\!\lambda}(x)\operatorname{d}\!x\right]\operatorname{d}\!\xi(p|a)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(q|a)-1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(b)}}}}}{{\leq}}\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\min\left(\frac{1}{p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)},\frac{1}{q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}\right)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(p|a)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(q|a)-1

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\min\left(\frac{1-p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}{p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)},\frac{1-q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}{q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}\right)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(p|a)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(q|a)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(c)}}}}}{{=}}\frac{d^{2}}{\mathbb{Q}_{1}(E)^{2}}\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\int_{\Delta(\Sigma)}\min\left(\frac{1-p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}{p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)},\frac{1-q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}{q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)}\right)p_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(p)\operatorname{d}\!\xi(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{\mathbb{Q}_{1}(E)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(Q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)),P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-Q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a))\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(d)}}}}}{{\leq}}16d^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)),\,Q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-Q_{\epsilon}^{c}(a))\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{\tiny{\color[rgb]{0.6,0,0}(e)}}}}}{{\leq}}16d^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a))}\right]^{2}\,.

where
(a)
follows from the definition of
\mu(\cdot|a)
,
\mu(\cdot|a,p)
and
\xi(p|a)
,

(b)
from
Eq.

14
,

(c)
from
Eq.

13
,

(d)
since
\min(p(1-q),q(1-p))\leq\min(p(1-p),q(1-q))
and
\mathbb{Q}_{0}(E)\geq\mathbb{Q}_{0}^{n}(E^{n})\geq 1/4
by
Eq.

11
. Finally,

(e)
holds because
\min(p(1-p),q(1-q))\leq\sqrt{p(1-p)q(1-q)}
.

Step 3: Finishing up

Combining the result in the previous step with
Eq.

12
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}

\displaystyle\leq 16nd\sum_{a=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a))}\right]^{2}=16n\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{d}\sqrt{P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a)(1-P_{\epsilon}^{c}(a))}\right]^{2}=16n\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})\right]^{2}\,.

Therefore

16\delta\geq\exp(-16n\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})]^{2})
,
which implies that

\displaystyle n\geq\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{16\delta}\right)}{16\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})\right]^{2}}\,,

which contradicts our assumption that
Eq.

10
does not hold.
∎

5
Comparing upper and lower bounds

The upper and lower bounds are nearly matching in the i.i.d. setting of
Example

11
.
The upper bound shows that detection occurs with high probability with Gumbel watermarking and power law detection after observing
n\geq N_{\textsc{upper}}
tokens where

N_{\textsc{upper}}
is the smallest natural number such that

\displaystyle N_{\textsc{upper}}\approx\min\left(\frac{\sqrt{-\log\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]}}{\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]},\frac{-\log\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})]}{\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})]^{2}}\right)\log(1/\delta)

where
\epsilon=\log(1/\delta)/N_{\textsc{upper}}\leq 1/2
with the inequality holding by the assumption in
Example

11
that
\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P)]
is sufficiently small.
The lower bound (
Theorem

20
) shows that detection cannot occur unless
n\geq N_{\textsc{lower}}
where

\displaystyle N_{\textsc{lower}}\approx\min\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$1$}}(P_{\epsilon})]},\,\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\mathbb{E}[G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{\epsilon}^{c})]^{2}}\right)\,.

Note that the lower bound holds for any
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, and in particular for
\epsilon=\log(1/\delta)/N_{\textsc{upper}}
.
Hence the upper and lower bounds are separated by logarithmic factors.

Discussion

Experiments

You surely noticed the absence of experiments.
Our brief tests showed the power law detector indeed outperforms the exponential detector in synthetically crafted examples where
the theory says it should (e.g.,
Example

8
).
Unfortunately, however, its performance on language data seems to be slightly worse, presumably due to the additional logarithmic factor and/or constant factors.
There may be ways to improve the proposed statistic to get the best of both worlds. Most naively one can combine both detectors with a union bound, but better methods
might be possible.

Detecting non-watermarked text

Algorithm

3
either rejects the null hypothesis and returns or returns
. The latter result does not correspond to a high-probability guarantee that the text is not
watermarked. Such an assertion is not possible in the standard frequentist setting considered here because the signal
provided by watermarking can be arbitrarily small when the entropy of the conditional distributions
(P_{t})
is small.
However, if there is a known (probabilistic) lower bound on
\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})
under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, then it is possible
to accept the null hypothesis if
\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}
does not grow suitably fast.

Intuition for statistic

The intuition behind the definition of
S
in
Eq.

2
is as follows.
The key statistical approach is based on testing whether or not
(V_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
could reasonably be sampled from
a uniform distribution, which falls into the goodness-of-fit class of statistical tests
(
7
)
.
Many of these tests are based on some kind of comparison between the empirical CDF of
(V_{t})
and the CDF of the uniform
distribution, which for
x\in[0,1]
is
F(x)=x
. The empirical CDF is
F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\bm{1}(V_{t}\leq x)
.
Classical statistics include the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Anderson–Darling statistics:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\left(F(x)-F_{n}(x)\right)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{F(x)-F_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!x\,.

These statistics cannot be written as sums of functionals of
V_{t}
. Moreover, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic is not making
use of the fact that under the null hypothesis the variance of
F(x)-F_{n}(x)
is
x(1-x)/n
.
This suggests a weighted Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,1)}\frac{F(x)-F_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\,,

(15)

which was introduced and analysed by
2
as a goodness-of-fit test.
6
also studied this statistic in the context of multiple hypotheses
as we do here, but with a less complex structure for the alternative.
As it turns out, this statistic would yield about the same theory as our choice.
The statistic used here is based on the observation that
F(x)-F_{n}(x)
is expected to be large for values of
x\in[1/2,1]
,
so the
\sqrt{x}
term in the denominator has little impact. Moreover, rather than taking a supremum we average ‘uniformly’ on buckets

[0,1/2],[1/2,3/4],[3/4,7/8],\ldots
, which corresponds to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1-\epsilon}\frac{F(x)-F_{n}(x)}{(1-x)^{3/2}}\operatorname{d}\!x=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\min\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\,,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-V_{t}}}\right)-4+2\sqrt{\epsilon}\,,

which except for a scale and a shift is the proposed statistic.
The truncation is needed to ensure the statistic has well-controlled tails under the null hypothesis.

Weighted detectors

The proposed detection mechanism is entirely independent of the model.
Detection can be improved enormously given access to a low-fidelity surrogate model.
Consider the situation in
Example

9
where most tokens are nearly deterministic.
If the detector has access to a classifier
\phi:\Sigma^{*}\to\{0,1\}
such that

\phi(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})=0
if
P_{t}
is close to a Dirac, then a better test statistic
is
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\phi(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})S_{t}
, which can then be compared to the critical
value for sample size
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\phi(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})
.
Assuming that
\phi
is accurate, then this approach reduces the detection time for
Example

9
from
\tilde{O}(1/\beta^{2})
to
\tilde{O}(1/\beta)
.
Alternatively,
\phi
can be real-valued (and even learned). Concretely, suppose that
\phi:\Sigma^{*}\to[0,1]
and detection has the form

\displaystyle T=\bm{1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\phi(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})S_{t}\geq\tau_{\star}\right)\,,

where
S_{t}=S(V_{t})
is defined as in
Eq.

2
.
Since
\alpha_{t}=\phi(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1})
only depends on
(A_{t})
, under the null hypothesis
(\alpha_{t})
and
(S_{t})
are independent. Hence, you can
still use Monte-Carlo to calculate a data-dependent critical threshold
\tau_{\star}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\alpha_{t}S_{t}\geq\tau_{\star}\bigg|(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{n}\right)=\delta\,.

Theoretically, by repeating the argument in
Lemma

5
, with
\mathbb{P}_{0}
-probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\alpha_{t}S_{t}=\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\alpha_{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\,.

Repeating also the proof of
Lemma

6
shows that with
\mathbb{P}_{1}
-probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\alpha_{t}S_{t}=\Omega\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\alpha_{t}G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})\right)-\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\alpha_{t}^{2}}\right)\,.

This is a complicated expression to optimise because
G_{\epsilon}(p)
depends on
\epsilon
. A reasonable approximation in many practical scenarios is

G_{\epsilon}(p)\approx G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(p)
for some constant
\epsilon
(e.g.,
\epsilon=1/10
). Whenever this approximation is reasonable and
\alpha_{t}=G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{t})
, then detection occurs once

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{t})^{2}=\tilde{\Omega}(1)\,.

Of course, this choice of
\alpha_{t}
depends on
G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{t})
that is generally not known. An interesting question is how to choose
\alpha_{t}
robustly when
G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{t})
can only
be estimated; and also in the case that
G_{\epsilon}(P_{t})\approx G^{\text{\scriptsize$\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$}}(P_{t})
is a poor approximation.

Asymptotics of the critical statistic

The Lindeberg–Feller central limit theorem provides an asymptotic approximation of
\tau_{\star}
defined in
Eq.

3
.
When
U
has law
\mathcal{U}([0,1])
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S(U)]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[S(U)^{2}]=\log(1/\varepsilon)-O(1)\,.

Hence, with
\varepsilon=\log(1/\delta)/n
the central limit theorem approximation of
\tau_{\star}
for large
n
is

\displaystyle\tau_{\star}\sim\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta)\sqrt{n\log n}\,,

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard Gaussian.

Non-optimality of subexponential statistics

A random variable
X
is called
\sigma
-subexponential if
\mathbb{E}[\exp(|X/\sigma|)]\leq 2

(
24
)
.
Our choice of
S_{t}
is a truncated power law under the null hypothesis, which is not subexponential for any
\sigma=O(1)
.
On the other hand,
1
chose
S_{t}
to be exponentially distributed
and
5
essentially chose the statistic to be Gaussian (more precisely, a convolution of Bernoulli’s).
Both of these choices are
\sigma
-subexponential with
\sigma=\Theta(1)
.
Suppose that the statistics
S_{t}
are scaled and shifted so that under the null hypothesis
\mathbb{E}_{0}[S_{t}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{0}[S_{t}^{2}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}_{0}[\exp(|S_{t}/\sigma|)]\leq 2

for some
\sigma=O(1)
.
We argue under these conditions that using a detector of the form

\displaystyle T=\bm{1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}\geq\tau\right)

cannot lead to near-optimal detection.
To see why, under the alternative hypothesis, using
Lemma

21
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}[S_{t}|P_{t}]=\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\mathbb{E}_{1}[S_{t}\bm{1}(A_{t}=a)|P_{t}]\leq\frac{5}{2}\sigma\sum_{a\in\Sigma}h(P_{t}(a))\leq 5\sigma H(P_{t})\,,

where
h(p)=-p\log(p)-(1-p)\log(1-p)
is the binary entropy.
Since we assumed that
\mathbb{E}_{0}[S_{t}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{0}[S_{t}^{2}]=1
, the central limit theorem suggests the critical threshold satisfies
\tau_{\star}=\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})
,
it follows that detection occurs only when
\sigma\sum_{t=1}^{n}H(P_{t})=\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n})
, which when
\sigma=O(1)
implies that
n=\tilde{\Omega}(1/\bar{H}^{2})
.
We showed in
Example

8
, however, that the power law statistic yields detection once

n=\tilde{\Omega}(1/\bar{H})
.

22
also noted the importance of using a heavier-tailed statistic for more efficient detection.

Logarithmic factors

There is a logarithmic factor gap between the upper and lower bounds.
The upper bound is probably not improvable for the proposed power law detection scheme.
In certain cases (see
Example

9
) the bound on the detection time
for the newly proposed detection scheme is worse than the bound provided by
1
.
Since the watermarking scheme is the same for both, one can of course combine the tests proposed here
with that suggested by
1
at the cost of a union bound.

Appendix A
Technical inequalities

Lemma 21
.

Suppose that
\sigma>0
and
X
is a random variable with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\exp(|X/\sigma|)]\leq 2
and
E\in\{0,1\}
be
such that
\mathbb{P}(E=1)=p
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[XE]\leq\frac{1+\log(2)}{\log(2)}\sigma h(p)\,.

where
h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
is the binary entropy.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=x\log x-x
, which has Fenchel dual
f^{\star}(u)=\exp(u)
.
Hence, by Fenchel–Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[XE]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|X|E]=p\sigma\mathbb{E}[|X/\sigma|Ep]\leq p\sigma\left(\mathbb{E}[f^{\star}(|X/\sigma|)]+\mathbb{E}[f(E/p)]\right)\leq p\sigma\left(1+\log(1/p)\right)\,.

On the other hand,
\mathbb{E}[XE]=\mathbb{E}[X(E-1)]\leq\mathbb{E}[|X|(1-E)]
and hence the same argument shows that

\mathbb{E}[XE]\leq(1-p)\sigma\left(1+\log(1/(1-p))\right)
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[XE]

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\min\left(p(1+\log(1/p)),\,(1-p)(1+\log(1/(1-p)))\right)\leq\frac{1+\log(2)}{\log(2)}\sigma h(p)\,.\qed

p

q

p

.

Lemma 23

(
15
)
.

Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
is a sequence of random variables adapted to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=1}^{n}

and
\eta>0
is a constant such that
\eta|X_{t}|\leq 1
almost surely.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\eta}\,.

Lastly we supply for completeness a proof of
Lemma

3
.

Proof of
Lemma

3
.

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V\leq x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}(U_{a}\leq x,A=a)=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{x}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{p(b)}{-\log(U_{b})}<\frac{p(a)}{-\log(y)}\text{ for all }b\neq a\right)\operatorname{d}\!y

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{x}\mathbb{P}\left(U_{b}<y^{p(b)/p(a)}\text{ for all }b\neq a\right)\operatorname{d}\!y=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{x}\prod_{b\neq a}y^{p(b)/p(a)}\operatorname{d}\!y

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{x}y^{\sum_{b\neq a}p(b)/p(a)}\operatorname{d}\!y=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{x}y^{1/p(a)-1}\operatorname{d}\!y=\sum_{a=1}^{d}p(a)x^{1/p(a)}\,.\qed
