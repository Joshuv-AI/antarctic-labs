The melanocytic transcriptomic state is independently associated with poor overall survival in patients with metastatic melanoma
Authors: Ziyin Huang, Kristen E. Rhodin, Rami Al-Rohil, Viviana Geron, Arul M. Chinnaiyan, Margaret H. O’Connor, Christina V. Angeles, Smita K. Nair, Matthew K. Iyer, Georgia M. Beasley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Melanomas display distinct transcriptomic states, but it remains unclear how they associate with clinical outcomes. We performed digital spatial RNA profiling (DSP-RNA) of metastatic tumors from patients to investigate how transcriptomic states correlate with melanoma specific survival (MSS) and acral melanoma (AM). We performed DSP-RNA across a tissue microarray constructed from 111 patients with in-transit metastatic melanoma (ITM) diagnosed from 1990 to 2020. Data quality control, noise correction, and normalization yielded high-quality profiles from 105 patients, including 30 (36%) who received immune checkpoint inhibitors and 20 (24%) with AM. We performed principal component (PC) analysis and correlated the results with published gene signatures: The PC1 axis differentiated transitory from undifferentiated melanoma, PC2 reflected immune cell infiltration, PC3 corresponded to stromal cells and neural crest–like melanoma, and PC4 associated with melanocytic melanoma. Across a cohort of treatment-naïve ITM, high expression of the melanocytic state conferred a median MSS difference of 7.72 years (melanocytic “high” = 5.16 years versus “low” = 12.88 years, log-rank P = 0.0061) and independently associated with poor survival in multivariate analysis. AMs showed higher melanocytic state gene expression compared to nonacral. Findings were validated in external datasets, supporting that the melanocytic state predicts poor prognosis. The melanocytic state is associated with poor prognosis and may be enriched in AM, implying that identifying patients with melanocytic melanoma may be important for therapeutic decisions. Unlike other gene expression predictors proposed for prognostic stratification, the melanocytic state characterizes a biological subtype, suggesting that it may have specific therapeutic vulnerabilities.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with advanced cutaneous melanoma can experience a wide spectrum of clinical outcomes, even among individuals classified within the same clinical stage. While current staging systems remain the cornerstone for risk assessment and prognostication in clinical practice, they do not fully account for the underlying biological variability among patients (1–3). As a result, staging alone may fall short in explaining the considerable heterogeneity in outcomes observed in advanced melanoma.

Gene expression is increasingly being used in conjunction with traditional clinical cancer staging to more precisely estimate risk and guide treatment decisions, including for patients with breast cancer and uveal melanoma (4, 5). Several studies have nominated gene signatures predictive of melanoma outcomes, particularly to assess risk of metastases in patients diagnosed with clinical early-stage disease (6–9). However, despite efforts to commercialize gene expression profile (GEP) tests, robust validation and broad implementation of prognostic gene signatures remains limited. The published prognostic signatures include different sets of genes with different biologic functions, suggesting susceptibility to confounding variables or technical biases.

In parallel with efforts to improve melanoma staging by using prognostic gene signatures, several studies have unveiled that malignant melanoma cells can be classified into distinct transcriptomic states (10, 11). In 2015, the Cancer Genome Atlas Network (TCGA) study of bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from cutaneous melanomas (67 primary cutaneous melanoma and 266 metastases) defined “immune,” “keratin,” and “microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF)-low” gene expression phenotypes that associated with overall survival (OS) (12). Building on this study, Tirosh et al. (13) used single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) from 18 melanoma metastatic lesions and 1 primary acral melanoma to identify heterogeneous populations of MITF-high and MITF-low malignant cells within and across tumors (13). Subsequently, Tsoi et al. (14) used 53 patient-derived melanoma cell lines to propose an expanded four-state model consisting of undifferentiated, neural crest–like, transitory, and melanocytic states (14). A recent meta-analysis of scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq datasets by Balderson et al. (15) proposed a consolidated four-state transcriptomic model that associated Tsoi’s neural crest–like and undifferentiated states with activity in AP1 and AP2 transcription factor complexes, respectively (15). For clarity, this manuscript will refer to “neural crest/AP2” and “undifferentiated/AP1” states to unify the Tsoi et al. (14) and Balderson et al. (15) nomenclature. While these studies demonstrate progress, the characterization of transcriptomic states remains somewhat imprecise, and their biological and clinical importance remains unclear.

The potential of transcriptomic subtypes to inform prognosis and guide management has not been adequately explored in the context of in-transit metastatic melanoma (ITM)—a challenging metastatic presentation characterized by variable patterns of progression and survival. While an estimated 80% of patients with ITM will develop lymph node or distant metastases, the remaining 20% may experience long-term survival with localized disease, highlighting the need for a molecular explanation for such divergent outcomes. Similarly, acral melanoma represents a distinct clinical subtype with heterogeneous outcomes, and the transcriptomic composition of acral melanomas remains poorly understood (16, 17). Here, we performed digital spatial profiling (DSP-RNA) of a tissue microarray (TMA) constructed from tumor specimens from patients with ITM, aiming to associate transcriptomic states with acral melanoma status and melanoma specific survival (MSS).

RESULTS

Digital spatial profiling of ITM

We constructed a TMA containing 189 tissue samples from 111 patients with ITM (Fig. 1B) and profiled the entire TMA on six GeoMx DSP slides. The DSP slides contained partially overlapping tissue sections resulting in 80 technical duplicates (269 total spatial transcriptomic profiles). Quality control criteria retained 242 of 269 regions of interest (ROIs) (fig. S1A). Gene filtering yielded 12,902 of 18,815 (69%) probes with detectable expression across the cohort (fig. S1B). The estimated false-positive rate for gene versus background expression was 14% and the area under the curve (AUC) for gene detection was 0.827 (fig. S1, C and D). The highest quality replicate was chosen from technical duplicates, resulting in 170 ROIs from 105 distinct patients for analysis. These included in-transit lesions (116 ROIs from 84 patients) and other metastases (53 additional ROIs) from patients with ITM (table S1).

Melanogenesis and tumor immunity associate with OS in patients with metastatic melanoma

Initially, we stratified patients into “poor” and “good” survival groups (N = 46 with OS <5 years, N = 59 with OS ≥5 years) for differential expression (DE) analysis (fig. S2A and table S2A). Although no genes met our significance criteria (adjusted P value <0.05), we noted that genes involved in melanin production (PMEL, DCT, TYRP1, and TRPM1) were among the most differentially expressed in patients with poor OS. We then performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using a curated compilation of 229 gene sets including published melanoma gene sets, Hallmark gene sets, and Curated Cancer Cell Atlas (3CA) gene sets (detailed in Materials and Methods). Gene sets related to the melanocytic and transitory states, skin pigmentation, and MITF expression were associated with poor prognosis, whereas gene sets related to tumor immunity were associated with good prognosis (fig. S2B and table S2B). The proliferation of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a well-established positive prognostic factor (18, 19). Although immune infiltration of the TME leading to a relative decrease in melanin-producing cells could explain the DE and GSEA results, we hypothesized that the melanocytic program could potentially represent an independent prognostic factor.

Unsupervised identification of melanoma transcriptional states

We further interrogated the association between gene expression and poor OS in our dataset using principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1C). Each of the first four PCs accounted for more than 5% of the total gene expression variation, collectively explaining nearly 40% (fig. S3A). Visualization of top genes contributing to the first two PCs revealed a nearly orthogonal relationship between melanocyte genes (S100B, MITF, PAX3, and DCT) and immune cell markers (ITGB2, LYZ, CD74, CD3E, immunoglobulin genes, major histocompatibility complex class II complex genes) (fig. S3B). This pattern suggested that tumor and immune cell expression was being stratified along the first two PCs. PCA plots colored by lymph node or distant metastasis did not show a clear separation pattern (fig. S3C). Sample date also did not appear to be a major driver of the PCA structure (fig. S3D). Although background AUC was modestly correlated with sample date (fig. S3E), suggesting a possible technical or quality-related effect, this association did not explain the dominant transcriptional variation captured by the first PCs. To expand upon this observation, we explored the correlation between PCs and curated gene sets, resulting in 183 significant PC-gene set associations (absolute Spearman ρ > 0.5; adjusted P value <0.001; Fig. 2A, table S3, and Materials and Methods).

We inferred the biological relevance of each PC by examining the gene sets with the highest absolute correlation, focusing on melanoma transcriptional states and found first that PC1 was strongly correlated with the transitory state described by Balderson et al. (BALDERSON_TRANSITORY_UP, ρ = 0.802, Padj < 0.001) (15) and negatively correlated with the undifferentiated/AP1 state from Tsoi et al. (TSOI_UNDIFFERENTIATED, ρ = −0.857, Padj < 0.001) (14) (Fig. 2, B and C). We henceforth referred to PC1 as PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated. Second, PC2 was positive correlated with gene sets related to immune infitration and activation, including B cells, T cells, and interferon gamma response genes (e.g., 3CA_B_CELLS_MEMORY). Visual inspection of CD20 and CD3 staining across PC2-high and PC2-low ROIs confirmed that PC2 effectively differentiated immune “hot” from “cold” tumors (Figs. 2B and 3A). We therefore referred to PC2 as PC2-Immune. Third, PC3 was negatively correlated with gene sets describing cancer-associated fibroblasts, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), (POZNIAK_MESENCHYMAL) (10) (Fig. 2A), and the neural crest/AP2 melanoma state (Fig. 3). We labeled PC3 as PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest, acknowledging the ambiguity between the stromal compartment and neural crest/AP2 tumor cells delineated by this PC. Last, PC4 was highly correlated with melanocytic state signatures (TSOI_MELANOCYTIC, BALDERSON_MELANOCYTIC) (14, 15) and melanogenesis (3CA_MALIGNANT_32_SKIN_PIGMENTATION) (Fig. 2, B and C). This included genes such as tyrosinase, crucial in melanin production, and premelanosome protein, crucial in formation of melanin polymerization. Accordingly, this PC was labeled PC4-Melanocytic. Considering the PCA results and their association with external gene sets, together with the findings from unsupervised analysis, we noted that the PC4 axis (melanogenesis genes) stratified samples independently from PC2 (immune genes), suggesting that melanocytic gene expression might independently predict poor prognosis.

Validation of unsupervised analysis using segmented DSP-RNA data

Our study design did not segment ROIs using melanocyte or immune cell markers. A recent GeoMx DSP-RNA study by Aung et al. (20) applied segmentation using S100B (tumor cell marker), CD68 (macrophage marker), and CD45 (leukocyte marker) to differentiate tumor from immune compartments, and thus analyzed transcriptomics from specific cells and not the entire tumor (20). We obtained, analyzed, and projected this dataset onto our existing PCA axes for validation purposes (Fig. 3B). PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated and PC4-Melanocytic expression were prominent in S100B segmented regions, whereas immune cell expression (e.g., 3CA_B_CELLS_MEMORY) was enriched along the negative axis of PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated and the positive axis of PC2-Immune. This supported that the PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated and PC4-Melanocytic expression patterns arose from malignant cells. However, this external validation dataset did not segment stromal cells and could not resolve the ambiguity between stromal cells and neural crest/AP2 tumor cells within PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest. To further address compartment composition, we performed deconvolution analysis of our TMA areas of interest (AOIs) using CIBERSORTx (fig. S4A). PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated showed strong correlation with tumor purity, while PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest correlated with stromal infiltration. PC4-Melanocytic demonstrated only weak associations with malignant, immune, or stromal fractions. We observed consistent results with MCPcounter (fig. S4B).

Extraction of melanoma gene signatures from PCA

As described in Materials and Methods, we derived gene sets from the PCA results by extracting genes with the greatest contribution to the positive and negative axes of each PC, enforcing that each gene could be assigned to one and only one gene set; the genes are listed in Table 1 and table S4. Hypergeometric testing of gene set overlap showed that the several PCA-derived gene sets had significant overlap with published melanoma state signatures (Fig. 2D). In addition, other gene sets also showed enrichment in Hallmark, 3CA, and GO biological pathways (Fig. 3C).

Development of an ITM melanoma survival cohort

To further explore the prognostic implications of melanoma transcriptional state signatures, we selected ROIs from ITM lesions obtained from treatment-naïve patients at their initial presentation (N = 84). Table 2 summarizes demographics and tumor characteristics for these 84 patients. Patient level detail can be found in table S1. Median age at diagnosis was 62 years [interquartile range (IQR) 52, 72], 51% were female, and 24% had acral histology. Median Breslow depth of the initial primary tumors was 2.5 mm (IQR 1.7, 4.0), and 39% were ulcerated. The median time to first ITM diagnosis was 16.7 months (IQR 9.2, 30.8) from initial melanoma diagnosis. More than 95% of patients had at least one additional ITM recurrence during median follow-up of 5.5 years (IQR 3.3, 10.5). During the follow-up period, there were 51 deaths. Median MSS was 8.32 years [95% confidence interval (CI) 5.45 to 16.1], and 5-year MSS was 64.72% (95% CI 54.8 to 76.4) (Fig. 4A). As expected, given the heterogeneity in outcomes for patients with ITM, at 15 years, more than 25% of patients are alive (N = 36). Further, patients with ITM only (ITMO) without lymph node or distant metastases had significantly superior MSS compared to those with lymph node (ITMN) or distant disease (ITMD) (fig. S6A). Accordingly, patients received multiple different treatments. During their subsequent disease course, 85% of patients underwent isolated limb infusion, 36% received immune checkpoint inhibitors, and 14% underwent intralesional injections with Talimogene laherparepvec. As expected, receipt of immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) was associated with improved MSS for the entire cohort (Fig. 4B).

Association between melanoma gene signatures and OS

We associated the expression of each gene set with MSS by stratifying patients into the optimal high and low expression groups that maximized the log-rank test (Materials and Methods and table S5). Of the gene sets derived from our DSP-RNA dataset, PC2-Immune positive, PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated negative, and PC4-Melanocytic positive were significantly associated with MSS (Fig. 4D and fig. S5A). High expression of PC4-Melanocytic conferred a median MSS difference of 7.72 years [melanocytic high = 5.16 years versus low = 12.88 years, hazard ratio (HR) = 2.47 (1.36 to 4.49), log-rank P = 0.0061]. We confirmed that PC4-Melanocytic expression scores were not directly related to our other gene set scores (Fig. 4E). PC2-Immune positive predicted improved MSS (HR = 0.37 (0.19 to 0.73), log-rank P = 0.003) (fig. S5A). PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated negative was also associated with improved MSS [HR = 0.24 (0.09 to 0.67), log-rank P = 0.003] (fig. S5A). There was no clear association observed between other PCA signatures and prognosis (fig. S5B).

Association between melanocytic melanoma and clinical variables

Melanocytic high was associated with worse MSS among groups of patients with ITMO, ITMN, or ITMD (fig. S6B). No significant differences in expression of PC4-Melanocytic positive were observed across patients with ITMO, ITMN, and ITMD (fig. S6C). Next, we examined receipt of immune checkpoint therapy. As expected, receipt of immune checkpoint inhibitors was associated with improved MSS for the entire cohort (Fig. 4B). There was no difference in expression of PC4-Melanocytic positive based on receipt of ICI (Fig. 5A). We performed a Cox hazards model including an interaction term between PC4-Melanocytic positive expression and ICI treatment and found the interaction term was not statistically significant [HR = 1.24 (0.35–4.44), P = 0.7442], indicating PC4-Melanocytic positive was a prognostic predictor of poor prognosis independent of ICI treatment. ICI treatment was significantly associated with improved MSS in “melanocytic-low” patients and showed a trend toward benefit in the melanocytic-high group (Fig. 5B). Last, we performed multivariate survival analysis and found PC4-Melanocytic positive was still significantly associated with poor prognosis [HR = 2.48 (1.01 to 6.05), log-rank P = 0.047] after adjusting for effects of other clinical variables (Fig. 5D). Further, there was no difference in Melanocytic- positive between patients with and without a BRAFV600E mutation (fig. S7).

Of the ITM patients, 24% of samples were acral melanoma patients. There was no significant difference in MSS between acral and nonacral patients (Fig. 5C). To study the association between melanocytic state and acral subtype, we compared acral versus nonacral patients from our ITM cohort (fig. S8 and table S6). Although no genes met significance criteria for DE, GSEA of genes ranked by fold change in acral versus nonacral tumors showed a significant enrichment for melanocytic gene sets, including PC4-Melanocytic positive, TSOI_MELANOCYTIC, BALDERSON_MELANOCYTIC, and POZNIAK_MELANOCYTIC (Fig. 5E) (13–15). In our study, the enrichment score of PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest positive was more significant than PC4-Melanocytic positive (Fig. 5E). The PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest positive signature did not significantly overlap with other gene sets, yet it may harbor genes related to acral melanoma (Fig. 5E).

Validation of association between melanocytic subtype and poor prognosis

Last, we validated the prognostic value of melanocytic gene signatures in TCGA, which included 358 metastatic tumors with survival information. We stratified patients in TCGA using the same proportion as identified in the ITM cohort and performed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (table S7). The results reflected that PC4-Melanocytic–high patients had significantly worse survival than PC4-Melanocytic–low patients [HR = 1.9 (1.42 to 2.55), log-rank P = 0.00000105] (Fig. 6A). Among all gene sets evaluated [TCGA (12), Tirosh (13), Tsoi (14), Balderson (15), Pozniak (10), and Hu (11)], PC4-Melanocytic positive demonstrated the highest hazard ratio for predicting worse OS (Fig. 6B). We also examined melanocytic signatures in an external spatial transcriptomics dataset from Nirmal et al. (21). The described exophytic and invasive melanomas exhibited significantly higher expression of PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated positive and PC4-Melanocytic positive genes than melanoma in situ, while melanoma in situ presented significantly higher enrichment of PC1-Transitory-Undifferentiated negative genes. Notably, PC4-Melanocytic positive showed the highest enrichment among all PCA signatures in the exophytic and invasive melanomas, suggesting a possible association between melanocytic differentiation and invasive phenotype (Fig. 6C). Last, we validated that the PC4-Melanocytic positive signature is derived from the tumor compartment using an independent scRNA-seq melanoma dataset, where the signature showed strong enrichment specifically in malignant cells (fig. S9) (22).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we generated whole-transcriptome tumor profiles from a TMA constructed from patients with ITM melanoma, a heterogeneous disease with variable presentation, progression, and outcomes. Our goal was to link the variability in metastatic melanoma outcomes to transcriptomic patterns, producing a robust prognostic biomarker with potential biological and clinical relevance. Initially, through a supervised approach involving DE analysis and GSEA, we observed that melanocytic signatures were enriched in patients with poor OS. To explore the significance of these findings, we used PCA to identify dominant sources of gene expression variation, considering whether melanocytic or other transcriptional states could emerge from the data. This revealed that published melanoma state signatures were highly correlated with the first four PCs and could be recapitulated in our dataset. To validate our PCA results, we projected third-party DSP-RNA data onto the PCs defined by our dataset and showed that our PCs effectively separated tumor cells (S100B positive) from immune cells (CD68 or CD45 positive). Recognizing the potential for broader applicability, we derived gene signatures representative of the PCs that could be generalized across different datasets or platforms for validation and further study.

When stratifying patients by gene signature score for survival analysis, we found that PC2-Immune positive and PC4-Melanocytic positive were significantly associated with MSS (Fig. 4D and fig. S5A). Our finding that a high immune expression score represents a positive prognostic factor is consistent with multiple reports that hot tumors, characterized by high immune activation and inflammation, are known to be associated with improved outcomes and disease control (18, 23, 24). In addition, the strongest predictor of decreased MSS was high expression of melanocytic genes (PC4-Melanocytic positive), which conferred a median MSS difference of 7.59 years between high- and low-expressing patients (Fig. 4D). Apart from its compelling performance on training and validation datasets, several aspects of our study design suggest that the melanocytic signature (PC-4 melanocytic positive) will be a robust biomarker for melanoma outcomes. First, we leveraged the capability of GeoMx DSP to measure and subtract the effects of background noise from the transcriptomic signal. Second, we limited our unsupervised analysis to the most highly variable genes in the dataset to prioritize patterns arising from biological variability rather than technical noise. Third, PCA showed that melanocytic genes account for a substantial portion of total expression variation. Fourth, published melanocytic gene signatures produced by orthogonal platforms and analyses support the results. Fifth, we showed that high melanocytic gene expression predicted poor prognosis in the TCGA SKCM dataset, which was generated using an entirely different platform (bulk RNA-seq) (Fig. 6, A and B). Last, the melanocytic signature was associated with tumor invasiveness in a third-party spatial transcriptomics study (Fig. 6C). Further supporting tumor invasiveness and treatment resistance, an additional study has shown that in a cohort of patients with stage IV melanoma treated with anti–PD-1 therapy, high pigmentation scores were associated with treatment resistance (https://biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.01.679282v1.full). For these reasons, we suggest that estimating melanocytic expression using the PC4-Melanocytic–positive signature could potentially be a useful and generalizable prognostic biomarker.

Notably, 20 of the samples from our cohort were from patients with acral melanomas. We observed that melanocytic gene expression was significantly enriched in acral relative to nonacral tumors. Recent transcriptional profiling of acral melanomas also showed that expression of pigmentation genes showed the worst survival (25). Acral tumors were also highly enriched for PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest positive, a PC axis that was not highly correlated with published melanoma state signatures (Table 1). Some of the genes appear to be related to protein deubiquitination, a posttranslational process implicated in therapeutic resistance (26). Further study of the genes in PC3-Stroma-Neural-Crest positive is warranted to characterize their biological relevance and relationship with acral melanoma.

The use of GeoMx DSP to profile a TMA serves as a powerful and cost-effective modality for generating high-quality transcriptomic profiles from archival tissues, in this case enabling the study of long-term survival (27). We did not incorporate segmentation by cell type markers into our study design because of concerns over cell counts. The addition of segmentation could potentially target distinct cell types within the TME at the expense of a decrease in overall signal quality due to cell sparsity. In our dataset, PCA effectively distinguished malignant expression programs corresponding to the transitory (PC1+) and melanocytic (PC4+) states from immune (PC2+) and stromal (PC3−) expression patterns. The undifferentiated/AP1 and neural crest/AP2 melanoma states were highly correlated with immune and stromal signatures, respectively, and should be interpreted as a mixture of malignant and TME compartment expression.

These results must be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, there is selection bias for which we cannot fully adjust for. For instance, we cannot account for patient or provider preferences in their treatments and decision to pursue care at a tertiary care center, which likely influence the makeup of our TMA. Second, we did not fully account for genomic (driver oncogenes and tumor mutational burden) that could account for PCA subgroups or differences in outcome. Third, while our TMA ultimately captured high-quality data from 105 unique patient specimens, this may not embody the breadth of disease across ITM. Despite these limitations, this is the largest cohort of ITM samples to have undergone whole-transcriptomic analysis and provides further insight into prognostic gene pathways that may help to inform treatment decisions.

In summary, we showed that the melanocytic state was independently associated with MSS, implying that transcriptomic characterization of patient tumors may be informative for clinical decision making. The degree of melanocytic expression may have implications for current clinical conundrums, such as when to offer locoregional therapies and spare patients the potential toxicities of ICI. Unlike other gene expression predictors proposed for prognostic stratification, the melanocytic state characterizes a biological subtype, suggesting that it may account for the prognostic shortcomings of standard histopathological and clinical staging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection and clinical outcomes

The Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board waived informed consent and approved this study (Pro00091710 and Pro00090678). Our institutional registry was queried for patients with first occurrence of ITM diagnosed from 1990 to 2020 with at least one pathology specimen in storage (Fig. 1A). Patients with concurrent distant disease at first ITM diagnosis were excluded, while patients with regional nodal or a history of regional nodal disease were included. Chart review was performed for patient demographics, tumor characteristics, and treatments received. Descriptive statistics were obtained for the entire cohort of patients with at least one sample in the TMA. Patient variables including receipt of immune checkpoint therapy were included. Oncologic outcomes collected included treatment response, recurrences, and MSS. MSS was defined from the time of first ITM diagnosis; death not related to melanoma was treated as a censored observation.

TMA and spatial transcriptomics

A TMA was assembled from patients with at least one archive tissue block from an ITM lesion available in storage. A pathologist selected the tumor region from the block to be cored and included in the TMA. Eligible tissue blocks included in transit lesions and subsequent nodal and distant metastases from patients diagnosed with ITM. In some cases, multiple tissue blocks were available from a single patient. A pathologist confirmed the presence of tumor for each sample from a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain of the TMA. Spatial transcriptomics was performed on the TMA using the Nanostring GeoMx DSP with the human Whole-transcriptome Atlas (WTA) probe panel (28). The TMA underwent staining for CD20 (B cells), CD3 (T cells), and DNA to guide ROI selection. ROIs were drawn to maximize tumor content within each tissue spot on the TMA (Fig. 1B). Full ROIs were profiled without the use of segmentation to avoid inadequate cell counts. The DSP plates were dried down and rehydrated in 10 μl of nuclease-free water. Libraries were prepared and pooled as specified in the protocol for WTA RNA samples. The final pool was assessed for quality using the Tapestation D1000 assay and Qubit. Sequencing was performed using NovaSeq 6000 S4 flowcell run as 27 base pairs (bp) by 8 bp by 8 bp by 27 bp (Illumina). The GeoMx DSP Analysis Pipeline version 2.3.3.10 performed trimming, alignment, and deduplication of ultraviolet-cleaved probe barcodes. A tabulated matrix of probe counts for each AOI was exported from the DSP server for subsequent analysis.

Newly generated DSP-RNA data have been deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with accession number GSE325123. In addition, newly generated code has been deposited in Dryad under the following URL: https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.kh18932pf.

Quality control and normalization

Quality control, background noise correction, and quantile normalization were performed using the R package stgeomx (https://github.com/mkiyer/stgeomx) as previously described (29). Criteria used to filter ROIs were as follows: (i) >500,000 aligned deduplicated total counts, (ii) signal-to-noise area under the curve (snAUC) >0.60, and (iii) >20% of gene probes above the limit of detection. For technical duplicates, the ROI with the highest snAUC was used. Gene probes with undetectable expression in >90% of ROIs were removed.

DE analysis

DE analysis was conducted using LIMMA (RRID:SCR_010943) (30). Criteria for calling DE genes included absolute log2 fold change >1.0 and adjusted P value <0.05.

External gene set curation

TCGA melanoma subtype signatures (Immune, Keratin, and MITF-low), were obtained from table S4A of the published manuscript (12). Tirosh et al. (13) MITF and AXL expression programs were obtained from tables S7 and S8, respectively, of the manuscript (13). Tsoi et al. (14) melanoma subtype signatures (undifferentiated, neural crest like, transitory, and melanocytic) were obtained from table S3 of the manuscript (14). Balderson et al. (15) melanoma states markers (AP1, AP2, transitory, and melanocytic) were obtained from table S2 of the manuscript (15). Suresh et al. (8) gene signatures associated with metastasis in tumors from the AVAST-M trial were obtained from table S1 of the manuscript (8). Bakr et al. (7) prognostic signature (“MPS_45”) was obtained from table S6 of the manuscript (7). Garg et al. (9) e9 expression signature (“CAM_121”) was obtained from data S1 of the manuscript. Gerami et al. (6) prognostic signature was obtained from the Table 1 of the manuscript (6). Hallmark and Curated Cancer Cell Atlas (3CA) gene sets were obtained from the molecular signatures database (MSigDB) version 2023.2 (31, 32). Human Protein Atlas gene signatures were extracted from the pathology data available online (https://proteinatlas.org/download/pathology.tsv.zip) (33).

Gene set enrichment analysis

GSEA was conducted using the Bioconductor package FGSEA (RRID:SCR_020938) (34). Gene ranks were computed by taking the product of the log2-fold change and the negative base-100 logarithm of the P value from DE analysis, e.g. rankgene = −log2(fold change) * log100(P value). Results with adjusted P value <0.05 were considered significant.

PCA and correlation analysis

PCA was performed on normalized data in R (https://r-project.org/) using the top 1000 most highly variable genes across samples. The first four PCs were extracted for downstream analysis. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed to quantify enrichment scores of external gene sets in samples using the R package GSVA (version 1.52.3). Spearman correlation coefficients between PC values and GSVA scores were computed to associate PCs with external gene sets. Significance was computed from 1000 random permutations of the samples and adjusted using the false discovery rate approach. Correlation coefficients with magnitude greater than 0.5 and adjusted P values less than 10−3 were considered significant.

Deconvolution analysis

Malignant, immune, and stromal cell composition was estimated using CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) with Tirosh (13) melanoma scRNA-seq reference. Immune and stromal scores were additionally calculated using MCPcounter (version 1.2.0). These estimates were used for correlation analyses with PCs.

Extraction of PCA signatures

Gene level coordinates, cos (2), and contribution were computed from each PC. Genes with greater than expected contribution—assuming that every gene contributes equally to the PC—were included in gene sets. Each gene was assigned to one single gene set based on its overall cos (2) rank and coordinate sign to generate nonredundant gene sets for each PC. Gene sets with fewer than 10 genes were excluded. GSEA was applied to PCA signatures using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.12.16) and gene ratios were used to assess the enrichment in external gene sets.

Survival analysis

Association of genes, gene sets, and melanoma subtypes with MSS was performed using the survminer package (version 0.4.9) (https://rpkgs.datanovia.com/survminer/index.html). For each gene or gene set, we determined the cutoff that would produce the most significant survival difference using the “surv_cutpoint” function. This optimal cutoff was used to stratify patients into low and high expression groups, requiring at least 20% of patients in the smaller of the two groups to avoid overfitting. A Cox proportional hazards regression models were also fit with gene set scores treated as continuous variables to assess their association with MSS.

External validation datasets

Raw data from the published DSP-RNA study by Aung et al. (20) was downloaded from GEO (GSE233305) and processed using stgeomx as described above (20). Normalized GEPs were projected onto the PCA axes defined by our original data using the predict.prcomp function (20). TCGA bulk RNA-seq data from the skin cutaneous melanoma cohort was downloaded from Genomic Data Commons (https://gdc.cancer.gov/). Reads were processed by fastp and aligned using STAR. Spliced RNA reads were counted and normalized to transcripts-per-million values. Gene set scoring was computed using GSVA. Processed gene expression data from Nirmal et al. (21) was downloaded from GEO (GSE171888). Gene set scores were computed using GSVA. Statistical comparison of GSVA scores between melanoma in situ and invasive melanoma samples was performed using t tests (21). scRNA-seq data from Zhang et al. (22) was downloaded from GEO (GSE215121) and analyzed using Scanpy (version 1.9.5). To distinguish malignant from nonmalignant cells, large-scale copy-number variation patterns were inferred using infercnvpy (version 0.4.2). Malignant cells were identified by combining inferred CNV profiles with the expression of melanoma lineage markers. Nonmalignant cell populations were annotated on the basis of canonical lineage marker expression. Module scores for each PCA signature were calculated using Scanpy’s “score_genes” function and were visualized on the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection embedding (22).
