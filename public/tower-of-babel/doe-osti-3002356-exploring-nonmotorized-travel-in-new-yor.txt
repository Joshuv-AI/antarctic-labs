 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents systematic analysis of nonmotorized travel behavior within New York State (NYS)
utilizing 2017 National Household Travel Survey data. As walking and biking assume increasingly
prominent roles in advancing active transportation objectives, accessible mobility, and sustainable
transportation systems, comprehensive understanding of these modal patterns becomes essential for
evidence-based policy development and strategic planning initiatives. Recognizing the substantial
geographic, demographic, and socioeconomic heterogeneity characterizing NYS—spanning from the
concentrated urban fabric of New York City (NYC) to dispersed suburban and rural contexts—this
analysis employs the structured "4Ws" analytical framework to examine participant demographics, spatial
and temporal distributions, and motivational factors underlying active transportation choices relative to
motorized alternatives. This methodological approach captures modal behavior patterns, user
characteristics, trip purposes, and temporal variations across the state's diverse contexts. Additionally, the
research examines behavioral differences among distinct user classifications, including walk-only and
bike-only travelers, while conducting comparative analysis of nonmotorized travel patterns between
ALICE (Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed) and non-ALICE household categories.

The “4Ws” of Nonmotorized Travel

 • Nonmotorized trips are found to constitute 25% of all travel within NYS, substantially exceeding
 the national average of 17%. NYC is observed to generate 71% of all nonmotorized trips
 statewide, reflecting the city's pronounced walkability orientation and extensive active travel
 infrastructure.

 • Middle-aged adults (40–64 years) are found to represent 36% of nonmotorized users statewide
 and comprise 45% of all biking participants. Conversely, seniors (65+) and children under 16 are
 observed to have lower overall nonmotorized trip rates.

 • Self-reported health status is found to exhibit positive correlation with nonmotorized trip
 frequency. Individuals engaging in light-to-moderate and vigorous physical activity are observed
 to have higher nonmotorized travel rates compared to those with sedentary lifestyles.

 • While automobile and transit modes are found to exhibit characteristic bimodal peaks during
 morning and evening commute periods, nonmotorized travel is observed to show gradual
 morning increases with peak activity around midday before declining through evening hours.

 • Weekend nonmotorized travel patterns are observed to begin later in the morning, maintain
 elevated levels until 3:00 p.m., then decline while remaining consistent between 3:00 p.m. and
 9:00 p.m. This temporal distribution suggests increased discretionary travel for recreational,
 commercial, and social purposes.

 • Recreational purposes are found to constitute the predominant motivation for nonmotorized trips
 across all geographic regions, accounting for 29% of weekday trips and 39% of weekend trips.

Comparison of Nonmotorized Travel by User Group

 • Among walk-only travelers, women constitute a slight majority, representing 54% of trips.
 Conversely, the bike-only category demonstrates pronounced male dominance, with males
 accounting for 68% of bike-only trips compared to 32% for females.

 • Households with children represent the largest demographic segment across all user categories
       (42%–50%).

 • Regarding trip frequency, the bike-only group exhibits a distinct peak among middle-aged adults,
 with rates increasing from 2.5 among youth (5–15 years) to 4.1 and 4.3 for adults aged 25–39 and
 40–64 years, respectively, before declining to 3.0 for seniors.

 • Zero vehicle households are found to have significantly elevated walking trip frequencies in both
 NYC and the rest of NYS.

 • Individuals with shorter travel times (under 30 minutes) are found to have higher biking
 frequencies.

Comparison between ALICE and non-ALICE Households

 • ALICE households are found to exhibit consistently higher reliance on nonmotorized travel
 compared to non-ALICE households. In NYC, ALICE households are observed to make 48%
 nonmotorized trips of all trips compared to 40% for non-ALICE households. This difference
 becomes more pronounced in remaining NYS regions, where nonmotorized trips are found to
 account for 20% of total trips for ALICE households versus only 9% for non-ALICE households.

 • Zero vehicle households are found to generate the highest proportion of nonmotorized trips
 statewide regardless of ALICE status. While this pattern holds for NYC, ALICE households are
 observed to have substantially higher nonmotorized trip shares (56%) compared to non-ALICE
 households (36%) in remaining NYS regions.

 • Trip purposes are found to vary between ALICE and non-ALICE households in NYS. For
 school/church purposes, ALICE households are observed to have higher concentrations of
 nonmotorized trips during morning hours, whereas non-ALICE households are found to show a
       higher midday spike (12:00–2:00 pm).

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND

Walking and biking, collectively known as nonmotorized or active transportation, are increasingly
recognized as essential components of a balanced and inclusive transportation system. These modes offer
economically accessible alternatives to automobile travel, promote public health through physical
activity, and contribute to reduced congestion on roadways. Data from the 2017 National Household
Travel Survey (NHTS) indicate that walk and bike trips represent approximately 17% of all trips
nationally, equivalent to about 42 billion trips annually (FHWA, 2020). However, national aggregates
obscure substantial spatial variation in active transportation utilization. Certain states and metropolitan
regions demonstrate significantly elevated rates of nonmotorized travel, likely reflecting the influence of
built environment characteristics, transit connectivity, population density, cultural factors, and active
transportation infrastructure quality (Piatkowski et al., 2019).

Examining nonmotorized transportation patterns in New York State (NYS) demands particular attention
due to the state's considerable geographic, socioeconomic, and demographic heterogeneity. The state
contains diverse built environments ranging from the concentrated urban fabric of New York City (NYC)
to dispersed rural communities throughout upstate regions, each generating distinct travel behaviors and
modal preferences. In metropolitan areas such as NYC, walk and bike infrastructure supports these modes
as principal transportation options, whereas in suburban and rural contexts, walking and biking frequently
function as access modes for longer-distance travel or serve recreational purposes. Beyond mobility
provision, nonmotorized travel contributes to public health outcomes and transportation network
adaptability. Comprehensive analysis of these phenomena requires systematic investigation extending
beyond simple participation metrics to examine the demographic characteristics of users, spatial
distribution of activities, temporal patterns of trip-making, and underlying motivational factors driving
mode selection.

The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical importance of active transportation modes within urban
mobility systems. During periods of reduced motorized travel and transit service disruptions, walking and
biking demonstrated remarkable resilience as viable transportation alternatives, particularly in dense
urban environments. Zhang and Fricker (2021) observed that nonmotorized travel not only maintained
pre-pandemic levels but exhibited growth in areas with established walking and biking infrastructure.
This finding aligns with broader empirical evidence demonstrating that infrastructure provision and
design quality, including protected cycling facilities, continuous pedestrian networks, and traffic calming
measures, serve as primary determinants of active transportation adoption and sustained usage patterns
(Dill & McNeil, 2013).

Active transportation patterns exhibit pronounced associations with socioeconomic status and mobility
access. Economically burdened households, including those categorized as ALICE (Asset Limited,
Income Constrained, Employed) (United Way, 2025), could have higher dependence on walking and
biking for essential travel due to constrained mobility options rather than discretionary choice. These
populations frequently confront structural mobility barriers including limited vehicle ownership, elevated
transportation cost burdens relative to household income, and spatially dispersed travel patterns requiring
complex trip-chaining behaviors. Consequently, examining active transportation utilization among
ALICE households provides critical insights into the broader spectrum of nonmotorized travel
motivations and constraints throughout NYS.

 3

This analysis utilizes data from the 2017 NHTS, a nationally representative cross-sectional dataset
capturing daily travel behavior across US households. NYS's participation as an add-on partner involved
supplemental sampling investments to achieve statistically robust representation at the state level. This
enhanced sampling design facilitates granular examination of travel patterns across geographic regions,
sociodemographic strata, and trip types within the state. The survey instrument captures trip-level
attributes including modal choice, trip purpose, temporal characteristics, and trip duration, complemented
by individual and household socioeconomic and demographic variables, thereby providing
comprehensive coverage of daily mobility patterns.

To examine the behavioral dynamics of nonmotorized travel within NYS, the analysis employs the
systematic "4Ws" framework to address fundamental questions regarding nonmotorized travel patterns:
Who engages in active transportation modes, Where these trips occur spatially, When they are undertaken
temporally, and Why individuals select walking or biking over motorized alternatives. The study
incorporates modal segmentation analysis, categorizing the population into distinct user groups, such as
walk-only users, bike-only users, and those who use a mix of motorized and nonmotorized modes,
thereby revealing behavioral heterogeneity across travel patterns. Additionally, the research conducts
comparative analysis of nonmotorized travel behavior between ALICE and non-ALICE household
classifications.

This study is a continuation of a series of special population analyses that use the 2017 NHTS data to
investigate distinct demographic and mobility segments. Previous installments examined rural residents,
older adults, low‐income communities, millennial populations, and zero vehicle households (Liu et al.,
2022; Pan et al., 2024; Patwary et al., 2025a,b; Uddin et al., 2024). A comprehensive study is also
conducted to analyze travel patterns, vehicle ownership trends, and overall travel behaviors in NYS
regions and compare with the rest of US (Pan et al., 2025). Building upon this established analytical
framework, the present study advances understanding of active transportation patterns within NYS while
providing analytical inputs for enhanced transportation planning and policy development.

1.2 OBJECTIVES

This study aims to advance the understanding of nonmotorized travel behavior in NYS across different
population groups. The analysis provides comprehensive characterization of nonmotorized mobility
utilizing detailed trip-level data from the 2017 NHTS. The specific research objectives include:

 • Examine the “4Ws” of nonmotorized travel: identify who makes nonmotorized trips, where these
 trips occur across the state, when they happen during the day and week, and why they are
 undertaken
 • Analyze characteristics of nonmotorized travelers by gender, age, income, and household
 composition
 • Explore key patterns and characteristics of nonmotorized travel, in terms of daily trip rate
 • Compare nonmotorized travel behavior between ALICE and non-ALICE households.

1.3 DATA

1.3.1 NHTS

The NHTS is a comprehensive dataset that captures detailed information on travel behavior across the
US. It includes data on trip purpose, mode, distance, duration, and socio-demographic characteristics of
both individuals and households. NYS participated in the 2017 NHTS as a state add-on, resulting in a
substantial sample size that allows for robust disaggregated analyses. This enables a focused examination
of how daily travel behaviors differ across population subgroups within NYS (NHTS, 2017).

In 2017, the survey included more than 129,000 households: 26,000 from the national sample plus
additional respondents from 13 state or metropolitan planning organization add‐ons. As part of the NYS
add-on, more than 17,000 households across the state were surveyed, making it one of the largest
subsamples within the 2017 dataset. This enables us for a more granular look at nonmotorized travel
behavior in the region. Notably, the 2017 survey was the first NHTS cycle to fully adopt an online survey
platform, with enhanced GIS capabilities for household and trip-level attributes. This provides a rich
foundation for examining behavioral trends, multimodal patterns, and emerging mobility dynamics.

1.3.2      ALICE

The ALICE household classification has garnered increasing scholarly and policy attention as a
framework for examining economic vulnerability and transportation accessibility challenges. This
analysis incorporates data from the United Way's ALICE research initiative (United Way, 2025) to
examine socioeconomic influences on travel behavior. The ALICE classification identifies households
earning above federal poverty thresholds yet having trouble affording basic living expenses including
housing, transportation, childcare, healthcare, and food costs. Within NYS, approximately half of all
households meet ALICE criteria. These populations frequently experience distinctive transportation
constraints stemming from limited financial resources, restricted vehicle ownership, and constrained
modal alternatives, rendering their mobility patterns critical for comprehensive transportation planning
and policy development.

1.4 USER GROUPS

To better understand the diversity of daily travel patterns and participation in nonmotorized
transportation, individuals were classified into five user groups based on transportation modes they used
on their assigned travel day. This modal segmentation methodology facilitates detailed analysis of
multimodal travel behavior while enabling comparative assessment across demographic and
socioeconomic variables.

 • Walk-only. Individuals who completed at least one walking trip but did not report any bike trips
 on their travel day. These individuals may have also used motorized modes (e.g., driving or
 transit) in combination with walking.
 • Bike-only. Individuals who made at least one bike trip but no walking trips. As with the walk-
 only group, bike-only users may have also used motorized transportation on the travel day.
 • Nonmotorized. A broader category that includes individuals who made at least one walk or bike
 trip, regardless of whether they also used motorized modes. This group captures all forms of
 active travel participation.

 5

 • Motorized. Individuals who completed one or more trips using motorized modes (e.g., private
 vehicle, public transit, taxi/rideshare). This group includes those who may have also walked or
 biked on the travel day.
 • All. The complete set of survey respondents who reported making any trip on the travel day,
 regardless of mode.

1.5 REPORT ORGANIZATION

This report presents a systematic assessment of nonmotorized travel behavior in NYS, examining modal
patterns, user demographics, spatiotemporal distributions, and socioeconomic disparities in travel
behavior through analysis of 2017 NHTS data. Section 2 investigates the fundamental dimensions of
nonmotorized travel: spatial distribution of trip origins and destinations, temporal patterns across daily
and weekly cycles, demographic characteristics of participants, and underlying trip motivations. Section 3
examines user segmentation and behavioral heterogeneity through comparative analysis of travel
characteristics across five modal classifications (All users, Motorized, Nonmotorized, Walk-only, and
Bike-only); thereby revealing variation in daily mobility patterns and multimodal integration throughout
the state. Section 4 conducts detailed comparative analysis of nonmotorized travel behavior between
ALICE and non-ALICE household categories, examining socioeconomic influences on active
transportation choices. The report concludes with synthesis of primary findings, discussion on
implications for transportation planning practice, and identification of future research priorities.

 2. THE 4WS OF NONMOTORIZED TRAVEL

The analysis in this Section employs a systematic "4Ws" framework to investigate four fundamental
dimensions of nonmotorized travel: Who engages in nonmotorized trips, Where these trips occur, When
they are undertaken, and Why they are made. This comprehensive framework enables a multidimensional
examination of nonmotorized travel behavior across demographic, spatial, temporal, and motivational
domains, providing insights into the complex factors that influence active transportation choices.

2.1 Who?

2.1.1 Age of Persons Reporting Nonmotorized Travel

Who participates in nonmotorized travel is a critical first step to understanding the dynamics in active
travel. It has been observed from 2017 NHTS data that one in every four trips (25%) is nonmotorized
(either by walking or biking) in NYS. This rate is significantly higher than the national average of 17%
(FHWA, 2020). Figure 2-1 shows the age distribution of persons reporting nonmotorized trips, either
walk, bike or both modes. Among NYS residents reporting at least one nonmotorized trip, middle-aged
adults (40–64 years) constitute the largest demographic segment, representing approximately 36% of all
nonmotorized trip-makers. This predominance likely reflects multiple contributing factors: sustained
physical mobility and health status within this age cohort, discretionary travel choices for utilitarian and
recreational purposes, and increased residential proximity to walkable built environments. The
prominence of this demographic becomes more pronounced when examining biking behavior specifically,
with adults aged 40-64 comprising nearly 45% of all bike trip-makers statewide.

Conversely, seniors aged 65 and older and children under 16 make up the smallest share of nonmotorized
trip-makers. For seniors, this could be attributed to physical limitations, safety concerns, or lack of
accessible infrastructure. For children, trip dependency and adult supervision needs may limit their ability
to engage in independent walking or biking. Interestingly, younger adults aged 16 to 24 show lower
participation than expected. While they do contribute meaningfully to nonmotorized travel, their share is
still outpaced by the 25–39 and 40–64 age groups.

2.1.2 Health Status

Figure 2-2 illustrates the share of nonmotorized trips across different self-reported health statuses. The
relationship is clear: better health is strongly associated with higher levels of nonmotorized travel.
Individuals who rated their health as “Excellent” account for the largest share of nonmotorized trips
(38%), followed by those reporting “Very good” (31%) and “Good” (22%) health. In contrast, the share
of nonmotorized trips drops significantly among those with “Fair” health (7%) and is nearly negligible
among those who rate their health as “Poor” (1%). This distribution highlights the role of physical ability
in nonmotorized travel choices. Healthier individuals may be more capable and confident in walking or
biking, while those with poorer health may face limitations that restrict such travel. It also underscores a
potential loop: active modes like walking and biking may help individuals maintain or improve their
physical well-being, while those in poor health may be excluded from the benefits of active transportation
due to existing health barriers.

2.1.3 Physical Activity

Figure 2-3 demonstrates a clear association between lifestyle physical activity levels and nonmotorized
travel participation. Individuals reporting light to moderate physical activity account for 60% of all
nonmotorized trips, while those engaging in vigorous physical activity contribute 34% of trips.
Conversely, individuals with minimal or no regular physical activity represent only 6% of nonmotorized
trips. This distribution indicates a strong positive association between existing physical activity
engagement and the propensity to utilize active transportation modes. The findings suggest a bidirectional
relationship wherein active lifestyles predispose individuals toward nonmotorized travel choices, while
regular walking and biking for transportation may reinforce and sustain active lifestyle patterns.

walking activity begins to drop significantly, especially for those whose travel takes over an hour.

their travel with walk or bike trips. Overall, the data suggests a clear inverse relationship between average
travel time and nonmotorized travel activity. As total daily travel time increases, the number of walking
and biking trips per person tends to decrease. Shorter travel time seems to enable and possibly encourage
greater engagement in nonmotorized travel across all groups.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

Nonmotorized travel (walking and biking) constitutes a substantial portion of all US travel and is
recognized as an integral component of comprehensive mobility systems. While active transportation
modes promote accessibility, affordability, and public health outcomes, these benefits are found to vary
significantly across geographic, demographic, and socioeconomic contexts. To examine these dynamics
within NYS, this analysis utilized 2017 NHTS add-on data. The primary research objectives were to
address the systematic "4Ws" framework of nonmotorized travel—Who participates, Where trips occur,
When they are undertaken, and Why they are made—while conducting user segmentation analysis and
comparative examination of travel behavior between ALICE and non-ALICE household categories.

The findings reveal that nonmotorized trips are found to constitute one-quarter of all travel in NYS, a
share substantially exceeding the national average. These trips are observed to concentrate strongly in
NYC, which accounts for more than 70% of the state's walking and biking activity. Demographically,
middle-aged adults (40–64 years) are found to represent the largest participant group, particularly in
biking, where they comprise nearly half of all users. Seniors and children, by contrast, are observed to
report substantially fewer trips. Health and physical activity status also play critical roles—those with
higher self-reported health and more frequent exercise are found to exhibit higher rates of walking and
biking. Temporal patterns highlight that nonmotorized travel differs from commuting modes, with
midday peaks on weekdays and extended afternoon activity on weekends, often tied to discretionary or
recreational purposes. Recreational activities are found to represent the most common purpose for
nonmotorized trips across all regions and home geographies.

Segmentation analysis of user groups reveals different participation patterns by sex: women are found to
dominate walk-only travel, while biking is observed to be disproportionately male-dominated.
Households with children consistently represent the largest share across all groups, suggesting that family
responsibilities shape active travel behavior. Zero vehicle households are found to report significantly
higher walking frequencies across both NYC and remaining NYS regions, underscoring the relationship
between vehicle availability and reliance on nonmotorized modes. The comparison of ALICE and non-
ALICE households highlights notable differences in travel reliance: ALICE households are observed to
consistently make more nonmotorized trips, with particularly substantial gaps outside NYC, where non-
ALICE households are found to report far fewer walking or biking trips. These differences extend into
trip purposes, with ALICE households observed to show higher concentrations of morning school- and
church-related trips, while non-ALICE households are found to exhibit midday peaks associated with
family and personal activities.

The implications of these findings extend beyond descriptive statistics into transportation planning and
modeling applications. By documenting who walks and bikes, when these trips occur, and for what
purposes, this study provides analytical inputs that can inform four-step travel demand models, activity-
based models, and discrete choice modeling frameworks. Active transportation has historically been
underrepresented in forecasting methodologies, often treated as residual trips rather than integral travel
behavior. The evidence presented here demonstrates the necessity of incorporating nonmotorized trips as
fundamental components of regional travel demand, both in terms of person-trip generation and
spatiotemporal distribution patterns. Enhanced representation of walking and biking in modeling
frameworks can improve system-level assessments of accessibility, mode choice calibration, and overall
network performance across both dense urban and dispersed non-urban contexts.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on a single cross-sectional dataset
(2017 NHTS), which does not capture temporal shifts in behavior that may have occurred in subsequent
years, including changes accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, while the survey provides
robust statewide representation, smaller subgroups (such as bike-only users) have relatively limited

 46

sample sizes, constraining the statistical power of detailed disaggregated analysis. Third, the survey
depends on self-reported information, which may be subject to recall bias or underreporting of short,
incidental walk trips. Future research could address these limitations by combining survey data with
passive mobility data sources such as smartphone GPS or fitness applications and applying longitudinal
methods to track behavioral shifts over time. Additionally, incorporating built environment measures and
spatial accessibility indicators would enable robust linkages between land use, infrastructure, and
nonmotorized travel patterns.

Transportation A means of transportation includes private and public transit modes, as well as
 walking.
 The following transportation modes, grouped by major mode, are included in the
 NHTS data.
 Active modes—include modes by which a person must actively move from one
 place to the next.
 • Walk: This category includes walking and jogging.
 • Bicycle: This category includes bicycles of all speeds and sizes that do
 not have a motor.
 Private vehicle—a stipulation for being a private vehicle is that the vehicle is
 privately operated, including rental cars.
 • Car: Includes cars and station wagons. Leased and rented cars are
 included if privately operated and not used for picking up passengers in
 return for fare.
 • Sport utility vehicle: Includes vehicles that are a hybrid of design
 elements from a van, pickup truck, and station wagon. Examples include a
 Ford Explorer, Jeep Cherokee, or Nissan Pathfinder.
 • Van: Includes vans or minivans designed to carry 5–13 passengers or to
 haul cargo.
 • Pickup truck: Includes vehicles with an enclosed cab that usually
 accommodates two to three passengers and has an open cargo area in the
 rear. Late-model pickups often have a back seat that allows for total
 seating of four to six passengers. Pickup trucks usually have a wheelbase
 the same size as that of a full-size station wagon. This category also
 includes pickups with campers.
 • Motorcycle/moped: This category includes large, medium, and small
 motorcycles and mopeds.

 • RV (motor home, ATV, snowmobile): An RV or motor home includes a
 self-powered recreational vehicle that is operated as a unit without being
 towed by another vehicle (e.g., a Winnebago motor home). This category
 also includes ATVs and snowmobiles.
 Rental car—Includes Zipcar and Car2Go, in addition to commercially rented cars
 for private use.
 • Golf cart/Segway: This includes all electric or gas-operated vehicles
 designed for use on a golf course, but whose use has recently extended to

 Public transportation—as used in Federal Highway Administration publications
 and analysis of NHTS data, typically includes the following (indicated in italics in
 the following text): public or commuter bus, commuter rail, and subway/elevated
 rail/light rail/streetcar.
 Bus—This category includes:
 • Public or commuter buses: local public transit buses available to the
 public and buses used for short-distance public transport purposes (e.g.,
 city bus or public bus), school buses
 • Private/charter/tour/shuttle buses: private buses operating on a fixed
 schedule between population centers and buses that shuttle passengers
 from one fixed place to another (e.g., airport shuttles)
 • City-to-city buses: buses that run from one urban center to another (e.g.,
 Greyhound)
 Train—This category includes:
 • Amtrak/commuter rail: trains that run from one urban center to another
 • Subway/elevated rail/light rail/streetcar (also known as rail rapid transit):
 a high-capacity system operated on a fixed-rail or guideway system on a
 private right of way, and vehicles that run on a fixed-rail system powered
 by electricity obtained from an overhead power distribution system
 Other modes—This category includes:
 • School buses
 • Paratransit/dial-a-ride
 • Taxi/limo: Use of a taxicab by a passenger for fare, including
 limousines. In 2017, this category also includes ridesharing such as
 Uber and Lyft.
 • Airplane: includes commercial airplanes and smaller planes available
 for use by the public in exchange for a fare. Private and corporate
 planes and helicopters are also included.
 • Boat/ferry/water taxi: includes travel by passenger line ferries
 • Something else: includes any type of transportation not previously
 listed (e.g., skateboards, roller blades, sailboats, cruise ships)

Travel Day A travel day is a 24 h period from 4:00 a.m. to 3:59 a.m. designated as the
 reference period for studying trips and travel by members of a sampled household.
Travel Day Trip A travel day trip is defined as any time the respondent went from one location to
 another by private motor vehicle, public transportation, bicycle, walking, or other
 means during the NHTS-assigned reporting travel day. However, a separate trip is
 not counted in two instances:
 1. When the sole purpose for the trip is to get to another vehicle or mode of
 transportation to continue to the destination.
 2. Travel within a shopping center, mall, or shopping areas of four to five
 blocks is to be considered as travel to one destination.
