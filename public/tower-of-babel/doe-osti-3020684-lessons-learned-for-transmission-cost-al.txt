Executive Summary
Expanding electric transmission can facilitate generator interconnection and improve grid
reliability. Assigning costs for new transmission infrastructure within a transmission planning
region and between regions is highly contentious because these costs can have a direct impact on
energy prices and ratepayer bills. In this report, we evaluate what factors influence successful
transmission cost allocation agreements. Through a review of legal disputes, existing cost
allocation practices, and regional case studies, we identify cost allocation trends and potential
strategies to minimize cost allocation disputes for future projects.

Transmission projects advance most effectively when regions use flexible, easy-to-
apply methods, evaluate benefits relative to costs across portfolios, and actively engage
stakeholders to ensure broadly distributed benefits and support.
The six regional project case studies revealed common trends and lessons learned for
transmission cost allocation. These include lessons on the planning approach used to identify and
evaluate projects and the actual cost allocation method design. Figure ES-1 summarizes six
lessons learned for transmission cost allocation.

transmission projects, reflecting regional preferences and system conditions. To allow

transmission projects and cost allocation methods into six project types and five cost allocation
approaches. Figure ES-2 compares the cost allocation approaches used by each region. Usage-

However, even when regions use the same approach, they are often implemented differently. For

Successful cost allocation methods strike a balance between precision and practicality.
The review of legal disputes over transmission cost allocation and project case studies reveals
successful cost allocation methods are both detailed enough to ensure costs are allocated to
project beneficiaries and feasible to implement in a real system. This tension appears primarily in
relation to (1) identifying project beneficiaries and (2) quantifying project costs and benefits.
Current regulations allow discretion in implementing cost allocation methods, provided these
decisions are evidence based, cost and benefits are relatively balanced, costs are assigned to
project beneficiaries, and the analytic cost allocation methods roughly identify project
beneficiaries given future uncertainties and imperfect analytical tools. The most successful
application of benefit evaluation in our case studies was for the purposes of project selection and
communication with stakeholders, not cost allocation.

Regions have multiple pathways to update their cost allocation methods, but the
process remains complex and requires stakeholder engagement.
The report also highlights the processes by which regions can update their cost allocation
methods, including through compliance filing to federal regulations and exercising filing rights
from Sections 205 and 206 of the Federal Power Act. As the summary highlights, implementing
changes is not simple because each region has split authority among multiple stakeholder groups
to file cost allocation changes.

This report focuses on the seven U.S. grid operators, either regional transmission organizations
or independent system operators. The case studies are focused on projects identified through the

regional transmission organization/independent system operators regional planning process
rather than those developed through a local planning process or independently with negotiated,
rather than regulated, service agreements. Although we do not consider cost allocation methods
currently under development for compliance with FERC Order 1920, the trends and lessons
learned identified in this report can inform discussions on effective cost allocation methods to
reduce barriers for transmission development.

1 Introduction
Expanding electric transmission infrastructure can facilitate generator interconnection and
improve grid reliability. Recent national studies published by federal government agencies,
national laboratories, and research organizations demonstrate that significant transmission
expansion is the lowest-cost option to meet growing demand and maintain grid reliability (U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) 2024; Larson et al. 2021; Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI)
2021; Denholm et al. 2022; U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) 2023).

Despite these findings, transmission capacity additions across the United States have declined
steadily over the last 10 years. Focused primarily on incremental reliability needs, only 3,300
circuit-miles of new or upgraded transmission and 70 circuit-miles of interregional transmission
were added annually, on average, from 2011 to 2020 (U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) 2023).
By comparison, studies such as the U.S. Department of Energy’s National Transmission
Planning Study estimate annual additions of more than 4,000 circuit-miles of new high-voltage
transmission can unlock the lowest-cost mix of transmission, generation, and storage resources to
meet growing system needs through 2035 (U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) 2024). The
slowing of additions of transmission has led to increasing congestion across the grid and a
backlog of interconnection requests. The result has been nearly 12,000 projects and over 2,500
gigawatts waiting in interconnection queues by the end of 2023, rising interconnection costs,
longer generation project development timelines, and an increasing number of project
cancellations (Rand et al. 2024).

The challenges with developing bulk transmission can be broadly characterized by the “three Ps”
—planning, permitting, and paying (cost allocation) (Gavan and Gramlich 2021). Planning is a
challenge because transmission exhibits large economies of scale and identifying least-cost
transmission investments requires long-term, multivalue planning and coordination across
multiple jurisdictions and wide geographic areas. Permitting a bulk transmission project is a
multiyear process that typically requires numerous authorizations and compliance with a
complex set of U.S. federal, state, and local requirements (U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)
2023).

Paying, or cost allocation, for transmission—the focus of this report—is often cited as the
hardest of the “three Ps” (Gavan and Gramlich 2021). Assigning the costs of transmission
projects to network users is highly contentious because these costs can have a direct impact on
energy prices and ratepayer bills. Across U.S. regional markets, transmission charges accounted
for over 20% of wholesale power costs, on average, during 2019 through 2022 (Figure 1).

Unlike merchant transmission projects—where transmission costs are paid by interconnecting
generator-customers with negotiated service agreements—regionally planned transmission
project costs are allocated to project beneficiaries. Determining who benefits from a project can
be challenging because the benefits of a high-voltage transmission project are often distributed
across a broad geographic area and a diverse set of network users. Further, the benefits and
beneficiaries of a transmission project change over the multidecadal lifetime of the project. As a
result, there is generally no single network user willing to pay for a regional project because they
would receive only a fraction of the benefits. Across the United States, different regions have
developed a multitude of cost allocation methods designed to assign costs to project
beneficiaries. Legal disputes over the methods used to calculate project benefits have led to
litigation between utilities, regulators, and customer groups.

In this report, we evaluate what factors influence successful transmission cost allocation
agreements and identify lessons learned to minimize cost allocation disputes for future projects.
These findings will inform potential opportunities for federal, regional, and state regulators and
project developers to reduce cost allocation barriers for bulk transmission development.

1.1 Regulatory and Legal Background of Transmission Cost
 Allocation
Transmission cost allocation is the assignment of costs among network users for building and
operating a transmission facility. These costs are ultimately paid by the end consumer through
direct charges on ratepayer bills or in the form of higher per-unit energy rates. The objectives of
transmission charges are to guarantee cost recovery for prudent network investments and to send
efficient locational signals to network users regarding the costs of installing their facilities in

 2

different parts of the grid (Olmos 2013). In Order 1000, the Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission (FERC) requires that transmission costs “must be allocated to those within the
transmission planning region that benefit from those facilities in a manner that is at least roughly
commensurate with benefits” (FERC 2011). Order 1000 was purposefully designed to allow
transmission providers flexibility to develop their own cost allocation methods in accordance
with this “beneficiary pays” principle. As a result, there exist a variety of transmission cost
allocation methods across the United States.

The beneficiary pays principle is a simple concept—ubiquitous in cost-of-service rate regulation
—that is difficult to implement in practice. 1 Although transmission costs are usually well
defined, the benefits of a transmission facility are more difficult to quantify. There is no
universal catalog of transmission benefits. Chang, Pfeifenberger, and Hagerty developed a
comprehensive list of transmission benefits, including more than 30 possible items (Chang et al.
2013). The challenge, as their list demonstrates, is that some benefits (e.g., production cost
savings) are relatively straightforward to measure but others (e.g., increased market liquidity,
improved reliability) are difficult to measure and quantify. Even standard metrics such as
production cost savings are subject to disagreements over input assumptions and model scenarios
used to quantify the benefits. In addition, the estimated benefits quantified ex ante through
various models can vary across different future scenarios and be very different from realized
benefits ex post in operations (Shu and Mays 2025). Further, the nature and magnitude of
benefits may change over the lifetime of the project. One recommended approach to address
these challenges is to allocate costs “roughly commensurate” with, but not formulaically based
on, quantified benefits (Pfeifenberger, 2025). Network usage is generally accepted as a proxy for
how much each network user benefits from a transmission facility. Here, too, there are
implementation challenges because there is no universally accepted method to measure how
much each agent uses the network (Olmos 2006).

In 2024, FERC passed Order 1920, which focuses on long-term regional transmission planning
and cost allocation (FERC 2024a). Similar to Order 1000, the regulations for cost allocation
reinforced the principle of “beneficiary pays.” These were focused on high-level principles 2 and
process improvements for developing cost allocation methods rather than methods themselves,
and these regulations leave transmission providers with the flexibility to design their own
approach for transmission cost allocation. Notably, Order 1920 removed the principle that
regions could have different regional cost allocation methods for different types of transmission

At a high level, these disputes center around the balance between precision and practicality; the
cost allocation method must be detailed enough to ensure costs are allocated to project
beneficiaries but also feasible to implement in a real system and under long-term uncertainties.
This tension between precision and practicality appears throughout the legal disputes in relation
to (1) identifying project beneficiaries and (2) quantifying project costs and benefits. Figure 2
summarizes the key findings we identified from these cases.

 Figure 2. Key lessons from transmission cost allocation legal disputes

Overall, these cases indicate there is discretion in implementing cost allocation methods,
provided these decisions are evidence based, cost and benefits are relatively balanced, costs are

 5

assigned to project beneficiaries, and the analytic cost allocation methods roughly identify
project beneficiaries. Courts have ruled benefits do not have to be calculated “to the last penny
or, for that matter to the last million or ten million or perhaps hundred million dollars” for the
purposes of cost allocation so long as there is more than “no benefits.” (U.S. Court of Appeals
for 7th Circuit, August 2009). And regions do not have to use a particular formula as long as they
can justify the method allocates costs “roughly commensurate” with the benefits received (U.S.
Court of Appeals for D.C., August 2018).

Emerging Cost Allocation Issues on the Horizon
Growing interconnection requests from large loads may raise new cost allocation disputes. In
November 2024, FERC rejected an amended interconnection service agreement in the PJM
region that sought to facilitate a co-location arrangement between existing generation and a large
load (PJM’s Susquehanna Co-Location Proposal 2024). In its ruling and a separate docket from
a technical conference to address issues raised by this type of arrangement (FERC 2024b), FERC
commissioners and participating panelists agreed large loads must pay their share of transmission
costs, but there was disagreement as to what these costs entail and how they should be evaluated.
As demand for data centers continues to grow, new methods may be needed to evaluate
transmission cost responsibility from large loads.

1.2 Analysis Scope and Analytical Approach
In this report, we evaluate what factors influence successful transmission cost allocation
agreements. Specifically, we investigate whether the following factors influence the outcome of
a cost allocation agreement:

• Method complexity: Are simpler methods such as those based on load ratio share more
 successful than more complex methods based on power flows or quantified benefits?
• Regional flexibility: Are cost allocation methods that allow intraregional differentiation
 more successful than those that apply uniformly across the region?
• Project portfolio: Are multivalue projects or bundled projects more successful to cost
 allocate than single-purpose or single projects?
• Project champion: Does the existence of an external project “champion,” such as a state,
 lead to more successful project outcomes?
To answer these questions, we review current cost allocation practices across different
transmission planning regions to identify common trends and key differences in approaches. We
use a selection of six case studies to identify drivers and contextual factors that influence project
outcomes.

This report focuses on the seven U.S. grid operators, either regional transmission organizations
(RTOs) or independent system operators (ISOs). These RTOs and ISOs oversee delivery of two-
thirds of electricity used in the country (FERC 2023). All but the Electric Reliability Council of
Texas (ERCOT) are under FERC jurisdiction and therefore must comply with FERC’s
transmission cost allocation requirements. The case studies are focused on projects identified
through the RTO/ISO regional planning process rather than those developed through a local
planning process or independently with negotiated, rather than regulated, service agreements. We
do not consider cost allocation methods currently under development for compliance with FERC
Order 1920.

Historically, these project types have been identified through different planning mechanisms and,
at times, by different planning entities. FERC Order 1000 classifies reliability, economic, and
public policy projects as those identified by the regional transmission provider as necessary to
satisfy reliability standards, reduce congestion, and meet transmission needs driven by public
policy requirements established by state or federal laws and regulations, respectively (FERC
2011). Multivalue projects provide some combination of these benefits. These categories of
projects are identified through regional transmission planning processes. By contrast, local and
generator interconnection projects have generally been identified outside of the regional planning
process. Local transmission projects are identified by incumbent transmission providers to meet

In comparison of the cost allocation methods used by different regions, several trends emerge.

 1. The costs for local projects are allocated primarily using license plate approaches.
 Costs for these projects are allocated to the local pricing zone in which the project is
 being built (i.e., SPP, PJM, MISO) or to the local transmission owner (i.e., CAISO,
 NYISO, ISO-NE). The philosophy behind these approaches is that local projects will
 have localized benefits and should not be paid for by the broader planning region. The

 9

 sole exception is ERCOT, which allocates costs based on network usage across the
 region.
 2. Generator interconnection projects are almost exclusively paid for by direct
 assignment to the interconnecting generator. In all regions, except ERCOT and MISO,
 transmission upgrades to meet generator interconnection requests are fully cost allocated
 to the interconnecting customer. In MISO, the costs for interconnection projects below
 345 kilovolts (kV) are allocated to the interconnecting generator. At or above 345 kV,
 90% of costs are assigned to the generator, and 10% are allocated across the region based
 on usage. This approach assumes high-voltage lines provide broader benefits across the
 region, not just to the interconnecting customer. In ERCOT, interconnecting generation
 customers pay a limited interconnection cost to cover certain direct costs of connecting to
 the transmission system, and the remaining costs are passed to customers based on usage
       (Wilson et al. 2024).
 3. Usage-based methods are the most common approach for regional cost allocation.
 Outside of local 8 and generator interconnection projects, most regions and project types
 use some type of usage-based method to allocate costs. The most common usage-based
 approach is a postage stamp method that uses electricity load as a proxy for usage. Some
 regions, such as SPP, combine usage-based methods with other methods, such as license
 plate allocation, to limit the usage-based cost responsibility to specific subregions or
 zones. While usage-based allocation is primarily based on load ratio share in most
 regions, PJM also uses power flow modeling to approximate network usage.
 4. Direct cost allocation based on quantified benefits or costs is rarely used. Most
 regions use network usage as a proxy for benefits rather than allocating costs based on
 quantified benefits or costs. MISO and the NYISO use estimated benefits to allocate costs
 among load zones for economic projects, which account for a small share of total
 transmission investments. Within each load zone a usage-based approach is then used to
 allocate costs. PJM’s framework uses a cost-causer approach for its multi-driver projects
 that meet multiple needs such as reliability or economic or public policy. Costs for this
 project type, which has not been utilized to date, would be allocated by dividing the
 project costs between the individual drivers (i.e., reliability, economic) and assigning
 costs using the method developed for the project type under that driver.
 5. Even when regions use the same method, it is often implemented differently. Regions

 regions has some form of postage stamp method that allocates costs in proportion to

3 Impact of Cost Allocation on Project Development:
  Case Studies
As shown in the previous section, transmission cost allocation approaches vary across regions.
This section details six project case studies to identify potential drivers and contextual factors
that may influence successful cost allocation and project outcomes.

3.1 Case Study Selection
The case studies selected for investigation are designed to capture diverse factors that may
influence the outcome of a cost allocation agreement. These include the complexity of the cost
allocation method, the regional flexibility with which costs are assigned, the portfolio of the
transmission project(s), and the existence of a state project champion. In addition, the case
studies include a range of different RTOs/ISOs, including single-state and multistate systems.

 Figure 4. Summary of project case studies classified by factors that may influence the cost
 allocation outcome
Figure 4 classifies our selected case studies according to these factors.

• Method complexity: The complexity of the cost allocation method refers to the input data,
 modeling, and calculations required to identify cost responsibility. Methods that require
 production cost or power flow modeling are considered more “complex.” We consider
 “simple” cost allocation methods to be those that do not require economic- or reliability-
 based power system modeling. This includes variations of the load ratio share, whereby costs
 are allocated in proportion to electricity load.

 12

• Regional flexibility: The regional flexibility refers to the ability to assign costs differently
 within a given region. Methods that spread project costs across an entire planning region
 based on a single formula are considered to have “regional scope.” Methods that allow for
 intraregional variation in cost responsibility are considered to have “intraregional scope.”
• Project portfolio: Transmission planning regions take varied approaches in how they bundle
 and categorize approved projects for cost allocation. “Single project/driver” examples refer to
 cases in which costs are allocated for a single transmission project with a specific driver (i.e.,
 reliability). “Multi-project/driver” projects refer to cases in which costs are allocated for
 projects that provide multiple grid services or multiple projects have been bundled into a
 portfolio for a purpose of cost allocation.
• Project champion: A “project champion” is an external (i.e., not the project sponsor) state
 entity that advocates for the development of a transmission project or portfolio of projects.
 These entities may be responsible for identifying the transmission need and supporting the
 transmission planning region in the identification and cost allocation of transmission
 solutions to meet that need.

3.2 Case Study Summaries
The following sections provide details on each case study, highlighting the project details and
cost allocation outcomes. More information on each case study is provided in Appendix C.

3.2.1 MISO Multi-Value Project Planning Portfolio
The MISO Multi-Value Project (MVP) portfolio demonstrates the application of a portfolio
approach (i.e., multi-project/driver) to project planning and cost allocation and a balance of
rigorous technical analysis for project benefits evaluation combined with a simple method for
cost allocation.

The initial MVP portfolio includes 17 projects identified through MISO’s 2011 Transmission
Expansion Plan (MTEP 11). 9 These projects were initially championed by the governors of
seven member states with growing concerns over the “crisis” generation developers face because
of a lack of transmission for interconnection (MISO 2011; Boyd and Garvey 2021). Various
regional initiatives organized by the states assisted MISO in developing cost allocation proposals
as well as model inputs and assumptions that were used for the MTEP 11 (MISO 2011).

Figure 5 shows the projects included in the 2011 MVP portfolio. The projects consist of high-
voltage alternating current (AC) transmission upgrades at 345 kV or higher and are distributed
throughout MISO’s Midwestern planning footprint. 10 As of March 2025, all but 1 of the 17
proposed projects have been constructed and are in service (MISO 2025).

However, the quantified benefits are not used for cost allocation. Instead, MISO uses a simple
load ratio share method to allocate costs for the entire portfolio. MISO’s load ratio share is
calculated based on each transmission owner’s monthly energy use (i.e., withdrawals) rather than
peak demand to allocate costs among load zones. The energy-based load ratio share method was
selected to reflect changing load profiles and network usage that may arise as a result of growth
in industrial output, electrification, or energy efficiency, among others (MISO 2023). MISO
does, however, calculate each zone’s ratio of benefits to zonally-allocated costs to show that
every zone’s benefits exceed its assigned costs, even though the benefit-to-cost ratios vary by
zone and depend on uncertain future conditions and modeling assumptions.

3.2.3 SPP Priority Projects
SPP applies a unique cost allocation approach to its “Priority Projects” and “Integrated
Transmission Plan” (ITP) projects based on a voltage threshold to allocate costs on a load ratio
share basis between regions and local zones, also known as the Highway/Byway method.

The SPP Priority Projects consisted of five 345-kV single-circuit and double-circuit transmission
lines in Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, Nebraska, and Missouri (Figure 7). These projects were
identified in 2009 by the Synergistic Planning Project Team (SPPT) to relieve congestion across
the network, integrate new generation resources, and connect the east and west portions of SPP’s
footprint (SPP 2009). The SPPT estimated the Priority Projects would provide $3.7 billion in
benefits across the region through a range of quantified benefits, including production cost
savings, reduced losses, deferred capacity investments, and improved reliability (SPP 2010b).
After approval by the SPP Board of Directors in 2010, all Priority Projects were completed in
2017 (SPP 2025a).

 Figure 7. SPP Priority Projects. Source: (SPP 2010b)

 16

Costs for SPP’s Priority Projects and subsequent ITPs are allocated through the region’s unique
Highway/Byway method. The philosophy behind this method is that higher-voltage lines provide
benefits across the entire region, whereas lower-voltage lines provide more localized benefits. As
a result, costs for higher-voltage lines should be borne by the entire region, and those for lower-
voltage lines should be paid by the local zones where the project is located. The SPP’s Regional
State Committee (RSC) developed a Highway/Byway approach to replace SPP’s three existing
methods at the time, which included separate rates for zonal projects and regional projects, a load
ratio share rate for Balance Portfolio projects, and a new yet-to-be-determined rate for extra-
high-voltage projects. The SPPT noted that members felt these disparate approaches were
“fragmented, confusing, and difficult to administer.” SPP used power flow modeling to support
the establishment and application of voltage thresholds for cost allocation (SPP 2010c). The
resulting Highway/Byway method allocates 100% of costs for facilities greater than 300 kV to
the region on a load ratio share basis. For lower-voltage lines greater than 100 kV, one-third of
costs are allocated regionally on a load ratio share basis, and two-thirds are allocated to the local
transmission-owner zone where the facility is located. Facilities less than 100 kV are cost
allocated 100% to local zones (Table 5).

3.2.4 PJM Artificial Island Project
The PJM Artificial Island Project provides an example of the challenges to using detailed power
system modeling for cost allocation.

The $266.5 million Artificial Island Project was proposed in 2015 to resolve the stability issues
identified by PJM at the Salem and Hope Creek nuclear plants in New Jersey and to relieve
congestion on the Delmarva Peninsula (PJM 2015a). The project consisted of 12 transmission
enhancements—5 would operate above a threshold of a 500-kV or 345-kV double circuit, and
the remaining 7 were planned below this threshold, including a 3-mile 230-kV underwater cable
beneath the Delaware River (Figure 8). These projects were the first to be planned under PJM’s
Order 1000 transmission tariff as part of the Regional Transmission Expansion Planning process
(PJM Inside Lines 2021).

 17

 Figure 8. PJM Artificial Island Project. Source: (PJM 2015a)
PJM’s transmission tariff combined electricity load and power flow metrics to approximate
network usage and, by extension, network benefits. Under the tariff, the costs for facilities
greater than 500 kV or double-circuit 345 kV are allocated 50% across the region based on load
ratio share and 50% using a method called distribution factor analysis, or DFAX (PJM 2010).
The DFAX method determine cost allocations based on simulated power flows over a new
transmission line to customer zones using hourly production cost simulations of network
operations for the existing system with the added transmission upgrade (PJM 2024). For lower-
voltage facilities—defined as facilities operating below 500 kV, or below 345 kV in the case of
double-circuit 345-kV lines—100% of costs are allocated using the DFAX method.

In August 2015, PJM filed the cost allocation for the Artificial Island Project with FERC in
accordance with its transmission tariff (PJM 2015b). Under this method, 50% of the costs for the
five high-voltage lines were assigned based on load ratio share, and all remaining costs were
assigned based on the DFAX method, including the underwater cable. The resulting cost
allocation assigned nearly 98% of the costs to Delmarva Power and Light, which serves
customers in Delaware and Maryland. State regulators filed a complaint with FERC, citing the
DFAX method results were unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory or preferential
(Public Service Electric and Gas Company, Petitioner v. Federal Regulatory Commission 2021).
Although FERC originally denied the complaint, it later reversed this decision and, after
rehearing, found the cost responsibility assigned by the DFAX method to be unjust and
unreasonable for transmission enhancements that address stability-related reliability issues for
the broader PJM region. FERC found the beneficiaries of transmission enhancements to solve
stability issues are not captured by the DFAX method, which is designed to identify flow-based
beneficiaries.

 18

In March 2020, after years of proceedings and litigation over alternative cost allocation
approaches, FERC accepted PJM’s reallocation of costs for the Artificial Island Project using a
new stability deviation method. Using the stability deviation method, Delmarva Power and Light
was allocated only 13% (PJM 2020) of the project’s costs compared to 98% under the DFAX
method. The 230-kV transmission line running under the Delaware River, along with the
upgraded substations and new transformer, were completed in fall 2020 with the remaining
upgrades completed in spring 2021 (PJM Inside Lines 2021).

3.2.5 ISO New England’s Greater Boston Transmission Project
This project illustrates project portfolio planning designed to identify reliability needs and cost
allocation based on project costs rather than project benefits.

The ISO-NE’s Greater Boston Transmission Project, selected in 2015, is a reliability project
consisting of a portfolio of new transmission lines, reconductoring of existing lines, new
transformers, and other transmission-related upgrades. The project was identified in the ISO-
NE’s Regional System Plan to address numerous thermal and voltage violations and increase
transfer capacity into Boston. The final work on an underground 115 kV line was completed in
December 2024 and Hudson station work was completed in 2025 was completed in 2025 (ISO-
NE 2025b).

Although reliability projects are the primary driver for transmission investments across the
United States, ISO-NE is notable in that the region has never constructed a transmission project
based on economic/system efficiency needs or a public policy goal (ISO-NE 2025b). This is due
to a combination of planning rules and methodologies. A report from the External Market
Monitor notes that ISO-NE has “aggressive local reliability planning criteria” and experiences
10% to 20% less congestion than other RTOs, both of which would favor transmission
investments for reliability over economic needs (Potomac Economics 2023). The process to
identify a reliability need takes fewer process steps than for a system efficiency project, which
requires demonstrating a reduction in production costs. While production cost is used as the main
metric, the ISO also considers transmission construction avoided by proposed system efficiency
projects, avoided transmission losses, avoided capital costs, and reduced unserved energy as
other metrics. Finally, the ISO’s tariff defines selection criteria for approving projects based on
project cost (ISO-NE 2025a). There is no mechanism to evaluate or communicate quantified
benefits for reliability projects.

The Greater Boston Transmission Project was one of the largest and most expensive reliability
projects planned in the ISO-NE, originally estimated to be $822 million (ISO-NE 2023b). These
costs are allocated regionally based on load ratio share. In September 2020, an additional $188.6
million was requested to underground a 115-kV transmission line originally designed as an
overhead line but was unable to secure property rights. The Reliability Committee did not
recommend approval of this request, and the additional cost for the undergrounding project
remains under review by ISO-NE (ISO-NE 2020).

3.2.6 MISO-SPP Joint Targeted Interconnection Queue Projects
The MISO-SPP Joint Targeted Interconnection Queue (JTIQ) projects demonstrate interregional
planning and cost allocation for generator interconnection projects that would not otherwise be
selected as part of the regional planning process in either participating region.

 19

The JTIQ concept originated from developers that wanted to build and interconnect generation
along the MISO-SPP seam but were facing prohibitive interconnection costs. In response, MISO
and SPP conducted a joint study that identified five projects along the seam totaling
approximately $1 billion (Figure 9). The projects are expected to come online in 2031.

 Figure 9. MISO-SPP JTIQ projects. Source: (MISO 2024b)
The initial JTIQ study (SPP and MISO 2022b) established a cost allocation methodology for
JTIQ projects similar to MISO’s generator interconnection cost allocation scheme. In this
method, 90% of the engineering and construction costs are paid for by the interconnecting
generators, and 10% is paid for by load in SPP and MISO in proportion to each region’s
calculated adjusted production cost benefit. Of the 10% allocation to load, 7% was allocated to
SPP and 3% to MISO-based production cost modeling to estimate the benefit to each region.
Costs to interconnection generators would be spread over generator interconnection projects
across multiple queue clusters to reduce the impact on any single interconnecting customer (SPP
and MISO 2023). Electricity load between MISO and SPP would continue to pay for operations
and maintenance costs and would serve as a backstop for undersubscribed capacity. In November
2024, FERC approved the JTIQ cost allocation proposal (FERC 2024c).

The JTIQ transmission projects do not meet the criteria as a reliability or economic project as
defined in the MISO or SPP tariffs and would not have been identified as transmission solutions
in either region’s transmission planning process (FERC 2024c). The joint planning and cost
allocation approach taken by MISO and SPP helped identify projects that support generator
interconnection and provide regional benefits that may not have otherwise been built.

3.3 Lessons Learned
The literature reviews, legal outcomes, and project case studies reveal several common trends
and lessons learned for transmission cost allocation. These include lessons on the planning
approach used to identify and evaluate projects and the actual cost allocation method design.
These lessons are summarized in Figure 10 and explained in more detail in the following
sections.

 Figure 10. Lessons learned for transmission cost allocation

3.3.1 Planning Approach
The planning approach refers to the transmission study, project evaluation process, and
contextual factors eventually leading to project selection and the calculation of cost
responsibility. Three planning trends emerged that led to more successful transmission
development outcomes:

 1. A portfolio approach can identify a suite of projects that provide broad benefits across a
 region and reduce the need for project-by-project evaluations and cost allocation. This
 approach was seen across many of the case studies. In PJM, ISO-NE, and the NYISO, the
 portfolios were designed for a single type of transmission need (e.g., reliability, generator
 interconnection) or benefit to a specific subregion. By contrast, the MISO MVP and SPP
 ITP portfolios of projects were driven by a combination of system needs, and the JTIQ
 projects provided benefits across multiple subregions in both MISO and SPP.
 A benefit of the portfolio approach is the mix of projects can be designed so that each
 zone within the region or subregion receives some benefit. In addition, the benefits could
 be more evenly distributed among zones and, for multi-benefit portfolios, the range of
 benefits may be more stable over time compared to the benefits of individual projects
 (Pfeifenberger and DeLosa 2022).

 21

 2. Evaluating project benefits alongside costs can help identify projects that provide the
 greatest net benefit, communicate project impacts, and inform which costs are justified
 for regional cost recovery. In MISO and the NYISO, the tariff requires each region to
 evaluate project benefits as part of the MVP and PPTPP processes, respectively. Both
 regions’ tariffs allow for additional benefit metrics to be included through stakeholder
 processes such as the Organization of the MISO States in MISO and NYPSC in the
 NYISO. The flexibility to include alternative benefit metrics allows each region to be
 responsive, as benefits may change over time and new tools and methods are developed
 to quantify benefits that were previously difficult to evaluate.
 In MISO, the evaluation of benefits for individual cost allocation zones allows it to
 transparently communicate the costs and benefits of a given transmission portfolio to
 member states and show that MISO’s load-ratio approach yields cost allocations that are
 roughly commensurate with received benefits. This information can be used by state
 regulators, policymakers, and other stakeholders when deciding whether to support a
 given portfolio. In the NYISO, the board is empowered to choose public policy
 transmission solutions based on qualitative as well as quantitative criteria defined by the
 NYPSC, such as maximizing the use of existing right-of-way. This holistic approach to
 selection criteria can support project selection that reflects the complexity of the
 underlying public policy need and goal. For example, the selected solutions for the AC
 Transmission Needs project were higher cost than some alternatives but provided greater
 benefits such as infrastructure resilience and operational flexibility that were deemed
 important by the NYPSC. By contrast, the ISO-NE tariff does not provide a specific
 mechanism to evaluate or communicate monetized project benefits. As a result, highly
 beneficial projects may not be selected because there is no defined means to evaluate
 whether the additional benefits to the region could outweigh additional cost.

 Quantifying project benefits is useful for evaluating projects and communicating impacts,
 but quantified benefits are difficult to use for cost allocation. In general, it is easier to
 calculate estimated project costs over the multidecade lifetime of a transmission project
 than the estimated benefits (Shu and Mays 2025). Current tools are designed to quantify a
 narrow set of benefits, and those benefits can change over the lifetime of the facility
 (FERC 2024a). The most successful application of benefit evaluation in our case studies
 was for the purposes of project selection and communication with stakeholders, not cost
 allocation.

 3. Stakeholder engagement and strong champions can increase confidence and support
 for the project. These groups can build external support for transmission projects among
 stakeholders; directly inform planning through scenario design, initial studies, and
 analysis; and provide input on evaluation criteria and cost allocation.
 In two case studies, stakeholders outside of the RTO/ISO or project developer team
 initiated the transmission planning process that ultimately led to regional projects. The
 most influential project champions tend to be state-related stakeholders. In MISO, the
 governors from Midwest states led efforts to develop more transmission to meet their
 state needs. This led to studies of transmission expansion in the region. In the NYISO, the
 state legislature sets state policy goals that inform public policy transmission needs

 22

 identified by the NYPSC. The NYPSC also has a defined role to identify additional
 benefit criteria that will be used for project selection.

3.3.2 Cost Allocation Method Design
The design of the cost allocation method itself will determine the cost responsibility among
network users. Three lessons in method design were identified:

 1. Simple methods that can identify project beneficiaries but do not require detailed power
 system modeling can improve consistency and predictability in cost allocation outcomes,
 reduce cost allocation disputes, and strike a balance in method usability and accuracy. As
 discussed in Section 2.2, methods that consider network usage a proxy for benefits are the
 most common approach for cost allocation.
 Among the case studies, MISO, SPP, and ISO-NE all use a form of load ratio share to
 approximate network usage and to allocate costs. The benefit of using load ratio share is
 the calculation does not require power system modeling and cost allocation is not strictly
 based on calculated benefits, which can reduce disputes over study assumptions and
 benefit metrics. Rather, input data on load forecasts or historic load can be readily
 obtained to validate and predict cost allocation outcomes. In addition, the approach is
 flexible and can be implemented in different ways, including allowing for regular updates
 to reflect changing system conditions. For example, ISO-NE uses monthly peak demand,
 MISO uses monthly energy use, and ERCOT focuses on peak demand during the summer
 months.

 PJM’s Artificial Island Project illustrates some of the challenges using a more complex
 cost allocation approach. The DFAX and stability deviation methods used by PJM
 require power system modeling to determine each zone or customer’s contribution to
 power flows or angle deviations, respectively. As seen in the case study, the results are
 sensitive to the modeling assumptions and type of model used, reducing the consistency
 and predictability of the results. In addition, quantifying different types of transmission
 benefits with detailed modeling may require a range of power system models, increasing
 the complexity and effort required.

 The JTIQ and the NYISO’s AC Transmission Needs projects combine simple and
 complex methods. Similar to most generator interconnection projects, the JTIQ portfolio
 is funded primarily by interconnection customers with 10% of the capital cost originally
 allocated to load. Although the cost share to load is small, it requires not only production
 cost modeling to assess the economic benefits to each region but agreement between SPP
 and MISO on modeling assumptions and methods to reach a common solution. Similarly,
 the NYISO’s AC Transmission Needs project used a combination of quantified economic
 benefits and load ratio share. However, the backstop method that would be used in the
 absence of an alternative is a simple 100% allocation based on load ratio share.

 2. Regional flexibility that allows for intraregional differentiation in cost responsibility can
 enable more granular cost allocation to users that bear the greatest cost responsibility or
 receive the greatest benefits from the project. The SPP and MISO tariffs illustrate two
 approaches to subregional cost allocation.

 23

 SPP’s Highway/Byway method uses a voltage threshold to determine the distribution of
 project costs among local and regional ratepayers. The underlying philosophy is lower-
 voltage projects primarily benefit local network users, whereas higher-voltage projects
 have regional benefits. The MISO footprint consists of a diverse mix of states divided
 into two subregions, MISO Midwest and MISO South. Although the case study took
 place before MISO South was added, subsequent MISO MVP tranches allow costs to be
 allocated to one or both subregions based on the benefits the projects provide.
 Subregional cost allocation can enable MISO to focus cost responsibility on the
 subregion driving the transmission need and receiving the most. The regional flexibility
 also allows MISO to use different cost allocations for other types of transmission
 projects.

 Regional flexibility enables more granular cost allocation without introducing a complex
 method based on power system modeling. In both MISO and SPP, the actual cost
 allocation of the regionally-allocated project is based on a simple load ratio share. Power
 system models are used prior to cost allocation to evaluate project benefits in MISO and
 to validate the Highway/Byway method in SPP but are not part of the cost allocation
 calculation. In addition, both MISO and SPP evaluate and quantify a broad range of
 benefit types, which has the added advantage of producing a set of benefits that is more
 evenly distributed and more stable over time than any single metric–thereby facilitating
 cost allocation. In contrast, when only individual metrics are used, such as power flows in
 PJM, the resulting uneven distribution of benefits can more readily lead to disputes.

 3. A backstop or default cost allocation method allows flexibility for project-specific cost
 allocation agreements while also ensuring selected projects can move forward if an
 alternative agreement is not reached. The NYISO’s PPTPP and SPP-MISO JTIQ projects
 illustrate the application of a backstop method to reduce the risk of projects stalling as a
 result of disputes about cost allocation methods.
 For public policy projects in the NYISO, the NYPSC can recommend an alternative cost
 allocation method tailored to the characteristics of the project. For the NYISO’s AC
 Transmission Needs projects, the NYPSC identified an alternative cost allocation method
 that assigns more cost responsibility to the zones that receive economic benefits from the
 project. If an alternative is not used, project costs are applied across the entire region
 based on load ratio share in line with the view that policy-driven projects benefit all
 network users.

 The JTIQ projects present a different approach to backstop cost allocation. In this case,
 the projects are driven by interconnecting customers across multiple states and
 transmission planning regions rather than the policy of a single state. The costs to
 interconnecting customers are spread across multiple queue clusters to avoid
 prohibitively expensive costs for the first generators and free ridership for generators that
 connect later. To reduce the risk to project developers, electricity load in SPP and MISO
 serves as the backstop in case the transmission capacity is undersubscribed by generators.
 This approach enables the projects to move forward through the planning and permitting
 processes without the need to wait for generators to fully subscribe to the transmission
 capacity.

4 Cost Allocation Changes: Regulatory Paths
  Forward
The previous sections outline existing methods and lessons learned for transmission cost
allocation. This section presents the regulatory options for regions to update their cost allocation
methods and compares the processes different regions have adopted to implement these options.

4.1 Regulatory Options To Update Cost Allocation Methods
Implementing new or alternative cost allocation methods requires navigating formal procedures
to revise RTO/ISO governing documents and/or transmission owner tariffs. 12 These documents
are institution-specific rules and operating procedures, and changes must be reviewed and
approved by FERC. The three pathways to submit proposed amendments to these governing
documents for FERC review are (1) compliance with FERC rulemaking, (2) exercising Federal
Power Act (FPA) Section 206 filing rights, and (3) exercising FPA Section 205 filing rights.

Compliance With Federal Energy Regulatory Commission Rulemaking
FERC can require RTO/ISOs to revise their governing documents through a broad-based
regulatory order. In general, FERC issues regulatory requirements or principles in the
rulemaking order that allow for regional flexibility in each RTO/ISO’s compliance plan. To date,
FERC rulemaking related to cost allocation has been limited. Order 1000 required regions to
include a cost allocation method for new facilities selected through regional planning according
to six principles (FERC 2011). Order 1920 builds on this but adds more structural and procedural
guidelines related to developing a default or ex ante cost allocation method, state involvement,
and engagement periods (Building for the Future Through Electric Regional Transmission
Planning and Cost Allocation 2024).

Exercising Federal Power Act Section 206 Filing Rights
Section 206 filing rights are broadly available to different stakeholder groups, giving them the
right to challenge existing RTO/ISO governance document provisions and give FERC the
authority to investigate and modify these rates if they are found to be unjust, unreasonable,
unduly discriminatory, or preferential (“Federal Power Act” 2018). However, FERC is not
required to respond to Section 206 filings. Compared to Section 205 rights, Section 206 requires
a higher burden of proof for the filer. Under these rights, the existing rules must be found to be
unjust, unreasonable, or unduly discriminatory, and the new proposal must be found to be just,
reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory.

Exercising Federal Power Act Section 205 Filing Rights
FPA section 205 gives the holder of the filing rights the ability to propose changes to certain
governing documents (“Federal Power Act” 2018). When filing for changes, the filer must prove
their proposed revisions are just and reasonable. FERC has a passive and reactive role to 205
filings, meaning it can approve, reject, make minor modifications, or make modifications with

Beyond changing RTO/ISO governing documents, Section 205 filing rights are also important

5 Summary and Conclusions
Aging infrastructure and increasing electricity demand are driving the need for increased
investment in transmission infrastructure. Accelerating transmission investment depends, in part,
on reaching successful cost allocation agreements to pay for new infrastructure.

By examining legal disputes, current practices for allocating costs, and examples from different
regions, this report highlights several trends and lessons that can support successful cost-
allocation agreements. The legal cases show that cost allocation works best when it balances
precision with practicality. It’s important to identify who benefits from a project and ensure they
contribute to project costs. Giving regions flexibility in how they allocate costs within the region
can also assist in obtaining broader stakeholder support and allow costs to be more accurately
assigned to areas that receive the most benefit. However, given the complexities of trying to
estimate project benefits and beneficiaries for individual stakeholders—especially over the long
life of a transmission project–cost and benefit estimates do not have to be perfectly precise.

Several strategies can reduce potential opposition to new projects, including quantifying and
communicating the full range of project benefits, not just costs, and engaging with stakeholders
and state champions to build external support. Bundling projects into a portfolio can identify a
suite of projects that provide a broader distribution of benefits across the region, facilitating
regional cost allocation. Finally, establishing a backstop method can help ensure cost-effective
projects can go forward in case project-specific cost allocation agreements cannot be reached.

The process to implement any of these lessons through changes to cost allocation methods is not
simple. Each region has split authority to file cost allocation changes among multiple stakeholder
groups. However, transmission providers now have an opportunity to engage stakeholders and
implement changes as part of their compliance filing for FERC Order 1920. The trends and
lessons identified in this report can inform discussions on effective cost allocation methods.

C.1 MISO Multi-Value Project Planning Portfolio
MISO’s approach to multivalue planning and cost allocation is based on the philosophy that the
benefits of transmission will change over time but coordination among states to deploy regional
transmission infrastructure will ensure broad-based shared benefits (Doner 2023). The MISO
Multi-Value Project (MVP) portfolio includes 17 projects identified through MISO’s 2011
Transmission Expansion Plan (MTEP 11) (MISO 2011). The MVP portfolios were originally
championed by the governors of Minnesota, Iowa, Wisconsin, North Dakota, and South Dakota
interested in transmission development to enable increased generator interconnection toward
their respective state policy mandates. In 2008, these governors announced the creation of the
Upper Midwest Transmission Development Initiative to identify wind generation resources and
necessary transmission infrastructure (State of Minnesota 2008). The initiative would also
develop a cost allocation proposal for the new transmission to propose to MISO and contribute
alongside state public utility commissions and the Organization of MISO States to MISO’s
analysis of wind energy in the region as part of the “Regional Generation Outlet Study”
published in 2010 (MISO 2010). This study was followed by the MTEP 11 that included the first
tranche of MVP projects. The MVP portfolio includes projects distributed throughout MISO’s
footprint at the time and what is today the MISO Midwest subregion.

MISO defines the qualifying criteria, benefit metrics, and cost allocation methods for MVP
projects in its OATT (MISO 2024a). The underlying principle for MVP regional cost allocation
is the assumption that MVP projects provide regional benefits in excess of costs. To qualify for
MVP cost allocation, projects must meet at least one of three criteria roughly aligned with
providing public policy, economic, and reliability benefits 15:

 1. Public policy: Reliably and economically deliver energy under the constraints of existing
 state and federal energy policy mandates, laws, and regulatory requirements.
 2. Economic: Provide multiple types of economic benefits across multiple pricing zones.
 Benefit-to-cost ratio must exceed 1.0.
 3. Reliability: Address at least one reliability issue in violation of a NERC or local utility
 standard and provide at least one economic benefit that provides value across multiple
 pricing zones.
The MISO Board approved the MVP portfolio in 2011 under Criterion 1 because the projects
helped MISO states meet their renewable portfolio standards. 16

C.2 New York Independent System Operator AC Transmission Needs
The PPTPP is a joint planning process conducted by the NYISO and NYPSC as part of the
NYISO’s Comprehensive System Planning Process (NYISO, n.d.-a). The process represents an
innovative partnership between the ISO and regulator, establishing a role for the NYPSC to
identify transmission needs and cost allocation solutions for projects to meet public policy
requirements. The PPTPP starts with the NYISO identifying and submitting transmission needs
to NYPSC for consideration to be included as a public policy transmission need. After approval
from NYPSC, the NYISO then solicits input from developers and stakeholders to identify and
evaluate transmission solutions to meet the public policy transmission need.

 57

The AC Transmission Needs project was initiated through an order from NYPSC in December
2015 identifying a public policy need to increase transfer capacity along two corridors from
upstate to downstate New York (Proceeding on Motion of the Commission To Examine
Alternating Current Transmission Upgrades 2015). The identified need followed a State
Transmission Assessment and Reliability Study (NYISO Board of Directors 2019) led by the
NYISO and Energy Highway Blueprint released by Governor Andrew Cuomo (New York
Energy Highway Task Force 2012). The order required no less than 350 megawatts (MW) of
transfer capability for Segment A and 900-MW transfer capability for Segment B. The NYISO
issued a solicitation for solutions to meet this need in February 2016 followed by an order from
the NYPSC in January 2017 affirming the NYISO’s determination of AC Transmission Needs
and directing the NYISO to evaluate and select transmission solutions to meet this need. The
PPTPP allows for flexibility in designing the scenarios and sensitivities used for project
evaluation based on the project context and stakeholder input. It also authorizes the NYPSC to
identify additional benefit criteria—both quantitative and qualitative—and the NYISO Board to
select projects based on multivalue criteria rather than solely on project cost. 17

The selected solutions consist of upgrades to existing lines from 230 kV to 345 kV between
central and east New York (Segment A) and replacing 115-kV and 69-kV lines with new double-
circuit 345-kV lines between Albany and downstate New York (Segment B) (NYISO 2019c).
The proposed solutions for both segments had higher costs than alternatives but were selected
based on the projects’ benefits including increased transfer capacity, deferred transmission
upgrades, greater operational flexibility, and more resilient structure design.

The PPTPP allows the NYPSC and project developers opportunities to develop a project-specific
cost allocation but retains a backstop cost allocation method based on load ratio share to prevent
project delays in cases in which an alternative agreement cannot be reached. For the AC
Transmission Needs study, the NYPSC recommended the NYISO adopt an alternative cost
allocation scheme in its December 2015 order (State of New York Public Service Commission
Order Finding Transmission Needs Driven by Public Policy Requirements 2015). The adjusted
method assigns 75% of costs to load zones based on reduced energy payments estimated over a
10-year period and 25% of costs to all load zones in the New York Control Area based on load
ratio share of the forecasted summer peak demand. The adopted approach allows for more
granular cost allocation to load downstate, which bears the largest cost responsibility.

C.4 PJM Artificial Island Project
The PJM Artificial Island Project was recommended to the board in a white paper to resolve
stability issues identified by PJM at the Salem and Hope Creek nuclear plants in New Jersey and
to relieve congestion on the Delmarva Peninsula. LS Power was awarded the competitive bid to
build the transmission line, and Public Service Electric & Gas and Pepco Holdings were tasked
with upgrading the substations on either end (RTO Insider 2020). The project consisted of 12
transmission enhancements, 5 would operate above a threshold of 500-kV or 345-kV double
circuit, and the remaining 7 were planned below this threshold, including a 3-mile 230-kV
underwater cable beneath the Delaware River.

PJM opened its first Order 1000 Regional Transmission Expansion Planning (RTEP) process
window in April 2013. Under its approved Order 1000 tariff for cost allocation for regional
facilities, the costs of transmission facilities greater than 500 kV or double-circuit 345 kV would
be allocated 50% to the region on a load ratio share basis and 50% using the distribution factor
analysis method, or DFAX method (PJM 2010). For lower-voltage facilities, 100% of the costs
are allocated using the DFAX method. The DFAX method is intended to measure each
transmission zone’s use of the upgrade to serve zonal load through changes in power flows.

The first regional project to be planned under PJM’s Order 1000 rules was the New Jersey
Artificial Island transmission need (PJM Inside Lines 2021). PJM identified stability issues and
requested proposals for improved operational performance in the Artificial Island area where the
Salem 1 and 2 and Hope Creek 2 nuclear power plants are located. Of the 12 total transmission
enhancements that made up the Artificial Island Project, 5 of the projects were planned to
operate at 500 kV (345-kV double circuit) or higher with a cost allocation of 50% allocated to
the region via load ratio share and 50% via solution-based DFAX method. The remaining 7

 60

projects were planned to operate below the voltage thresholds, and 100% of the cost of these
projects was to be allocated via the solution-based DFAX method, including the 3-mile 230-kV
underwater cable beneath the Delaware River.

This original cost allocation using DFAX and load ratio share allocated the majority of the cost
of the transmission project, including nearly all the cost for the 230-kV underground
transmission line, to Delmarva Power and Light, which serves customers in Delaware and
Maryland. After requests for rehearing from state regulators, FERC found the DFAX method
was not suitable to identify beneficiaries of stability-based upgrades because it is based on flow-
based calculations. PJM proposed two alternative ex ante cost allocation approaches for
identifying the beneficiaries of transmission projects that address stability-related reliability
issues—the stability deviation method and the stability interface DFAX method. FERC found the
stability deviation method to be just and reasonable and included the tariff provisions in the
February 28, 2019, order and directed PJM to make a compliance filing with the additional
provisions. A group of other state regulators and transmission owners sued and took FERC to
court, arguing the commission changed its decision without proper evidence. The case went to
the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia (Public Service Electric and Gas
Company, Petitioner v. Federal Regulatory Commission 2021). The D.C. Appeals Court denied
the petition for review of FERC orders by Public Service Electric and Gas and other petitioners,
deciding FERC made the right decision in adopting the alternate cost allocation method for
stability issues. In March 2020, FERC accepted PJM’s reallocation of costs for the Artificial
Island Project. Using the stability deviation method, Delmarva Power and Light was allocated
only 13% (PJM 2020) of the costs, compared to nearly 98% (PJM 2015b) using the DFAX
methods. The 230-kV transmission line running under the Delaware River, along with the
upgraded substations and new transformer, were completed in fall 2020, with the remaining
upgrades completed in spring 2021 (PJM Inside Lines 2021).

C.5 ISO New England’s Greater Boston Transmission Project
The Greater Boston Transmission Project is a reliability project consisting of a portfolio of new
transmission lines, reconductoring of existing lines, new transformers, and other transmission-
related upgrades. It illustrates the ISO’s approach to planning transmission projects around
project costs rather than project benefits.

Costs for regional reliability projects are allocated on a postage stamp basis to the region using
load ratio share. Reliability projects must satisfy evaluation factors defined in Attachment K and
reliability standards in Attachment N of the ISO’s tariff (ISO-NE 2025a). The tariff defines the
criteria that ISO-NE may use in selecting proposals to satisfy reliability needs, but there is no
benefit quantification for reliability projects. The ISO’s Transmission Cost Allocation Review
process can be summarized in four steps (ISO-NE 2023a):

 1. Transmission owners submit a transmission cost allocation application, requesting
 regional rate recovery for their project(s).
 2. ISO reviews project costs and determines if costs are “reasonable and in accordance
 with good utility practice.”
 3. The Reliability Committee provides advisory input to ISO-NE on whether the
 proposed project includes localized costs.

 61

 4. ISO determination of how much of project’s costs are applicable for regional cost
 recovery.
The Greater Boston Transmission Project was one of the largest and most expensive reliability
projects planned in the ISO-NE, originally estimated to be $822 million (ISO-NE 2023b). The
Reliability Committee voted to recommend the project in June 2017. Costs for reliability projects
are generally based on load ratio share. The final cost allocation for this project has not been
determined yet. In September 2020, an additional $188.6 million was requested to underground a
115-kV transmission line originally designed as an overhead line but was unable to secure
property rights. The Reliability Committee did not recommend approval of this request, and the
additional cost for the undergrounding project remains under review by ISO-NE (ISO-NE 2020).

C.6 MISO-SPP Joint Targeted Interconnection Queue Projects
The JTIQ concept originated from developers that wanted to build and interconnect generation
along the MISO-SPP seam but were facing prohibitive interconnection costs. A joint study by
MISO and SPP originally identified seven projects with a total cost of roughly $1.6 billion
(MISO 2022). Two of the initial 7 JTIQ projects were found to have benefits to load and qualify
as MVP projects in MISO’s tariff and were removed from JTIQ and placed in MISO’s Long
Range Transmission Planning Tranche 1 portfolio (SPP and MISO 2022a).

The initial JTIQ study (SPP and MISO 2022b) established a cost allocation methodology for
JTIQ projects similar to MISO’s generator interconnection cost allocation scheme, whereby 90%
of the engineering and construction costs are paid for by the interconnecting generators and 10%
is paid for by load in SPP and MISO in proportion to each region’s calculated adjusted
production cost benefit. Of the 10% allocation to load, 7% was allocated to SPP and 3% to
MISO-based production cost modeling to estimate the benefit to each region. After the projects
received $464.5 million in grants through the Department of Energy’s Grid Resilience and
Innovation Partnership program, the cost allocation was updated to 100% of the remaining
engineering and construction costs would be paid for by interconnection generators (MISO
Planning Advisory Committee 2023). These costs would be spread over generator
interconnection projects across multiple queue clusters to reduce the impact on any single
interconnecting customer (SPP and MISO 2023). Electricity load between MISO and SPP would
continue to pay for operations and maintenance costs and would serve as a backstop for
undersubscribed capacity. In November 2024, FERC approved the JTIQ cost allocation proposal
(FERC 2024c).

The JTIQ transmission projects do not provide reliability or economic benefits as defined in the
MISO or SPP tariffs and would not have been identified as transmission solutions in either
region’s transmission planning process (FERC 2024c). The joint planning and cost allocation
approach taken by MISO and SPP helped identify projects that support generator interconnection
and provide regional benefits that may not have otherwise been built.
