A structured teaching module for postoperative follow-up in urology residency training: development and evaluation of an IMDB-based educational tool
Authors: Xin Sun, Qirui Zhou, Weiwei Qian, Hao Geng, Dengdian Wang, Jiaxing Ma, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTeaching postoperative follow-up for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is challenging during brief urology rotations. We evaluated a structured module based on a simplified risk model (IMDB: venous invasion, tumor size ≥ 7 cm, systemic symptoms, renal sinus fat invasion) to improve residents’ knowledge and clinical decision-making.MethodsForty residents who completed the 8–10 h module (2022–2025) were enrolled in this retrospective pre-post study. The module used a Postoperative Health Management Passport integrating lectures, simulations, outpatient practice, and debriefing. Knowledge was assessed by validated questionnaire (pre/post). Decision-making was scored on follow-up card assignments (0–15).Satisfaction was measured by 5-point Likert scale.ResultsKnowledge scores increased from 65.75 ± 5.13 to 90.88 ± 4.92 (mean gain 25.13 ± 4.00; t = 39.71, P < 0.0001). Decision-making scores: IMDB accuracy 4.52 ± 0.64, plan rationality 4.19 ± 0.65, patient education 3.93 ± 0.81. Inter-rater reliability was excellent (ICC = 0.97), pass rate 92.5% (37/40).Satisfaction was 96.1%; 100% would recommend the module. Knowledge gain did not correlate with decision-making scores (all r ≤ 0.07, P > 0.05). Background

Kidney cancer—renal cell carcinoma (RCC), to be precise—is one of those diseases where getting the surgery right is only half the battle. The other half is the postoperative follow-up: recurrence and metastasis can derail even the most successful operation, and early detection is critical for improving patient outcomes [1–3]. All major clinical guidelines emphasize that RCC follow-up must be systematic, standardized, and tailored to the patient’s individual recurrence risk [4, 5].

However, the established prognostic models for RCC—including University of California Los Angeles Integrated Staging System (UISS) [6], the Mayo Clinic SSIGN score [7], and the International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium (IMDC) model [8]—were developed for oncologists and clinical researchers, not for urology residents in short-term rotations. These models incorporate multiple complex variables (e.g., IMDC includes Karnofsky performance status, time to treatment, and six laboratory indicators) that create a high cognitive load for novice learners, making it difficult for residents to master and apply them independently in clinical practice.

Urology rotations for standardized residency training are typically only 2–3 months in duration, during which residents must learn to manage a wide spectrum of urological pathologies. To help international readers interpret our findings, a brief description of the residency training context in China is warranted.

Standardized residency training in China typically lasts three years. Residents rotate through multiple specialties, with urology rotations lasting approximately 2–3 months. Teaching is largely delivered through bedside apprenticeship, didactic lectures, and case discussions. Formal simulation-based training and structured assessment of clinical decision-making are not yet universally implemented. Our study was conducted at a large tertiary teaching hospital in eastern China, which has a high volume of RCC surgeries and dedicated urology oncology faculty. The training environment is broadly representative of major academic medical centers in China, but may differ from community hospitals or institutions in other countries.

The traditional apprenticeship model of teaching is often fragmented and lacks systematicity, leading to a common outcome: many residents complete their rotation without the ability to make evidence-based, independent follow-up decisions for RCC patients [9]. To address this gap, we developed a simplified risk stratification model (IMDB) specifically for educational purposes, based on four core, teachable indicators closely linked to RCC recurrence (venous invasion [I], tumor diameter ≥ 7 cm [M], systemic symptoms [D], renal sinus fat invasion [B])—all derived from the European Association of Urology (EAU) RCC guidelines [4, 5]. The IMDB model was developed specifically for educational purposes and is not intended to replace clinically validated prognostic models such as IMDC, UISS, or SSIGN.

To operationalize this simplified model, we developed a tangible clinical tool: the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys. This passport is a one-page visual aid that guides users through the entire decision pathway: it displays the IMDB scoring criteria, provides a risk-stratified follow-up schedule, lists red-flag symptoms for patient education, and includes a tracking section for documenting completed follow-up visits. Originally designed for patient self-management, we repurposed the passport as the core instructional scaffold for our teaching module.

We then transformed this clinical patient management tool into the cornerstone of a structured teaching module. This module integrates three key educational theories: cognitive load theory [10, 11], dual process theory [12, 13], and metacognitive theory [14, 15], and adopts a “learn-practice-reflect” framework to compress the learning curve for residents. In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of this IMDB-based teaching module in enhancing urology residents’ knowledge of RCC postoperative follow-up and their clinical decision-making abilities, and explored the relationship between knowledge acquisition and procedural competency in medical education.

Methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective pre-post self-controlled study design was used to enroll 40 urology residents who completed the IMDB-based teaching module during their rotation at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University from January 2022 to December 2025.

The Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University is a tertiary referral center in Hefei, China, performing approximately 200 RCC surgeries annually. The residency training program follows the national standardized curriculum mandated by the Chinese government.

Sample size calculation

A power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. For a paired t-test with a medium effect size (d = 0.5), α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and power = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 34. We enrolled 40 residents to account for potential dropouts and incomplete data.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Rotation duration in the urology department of at least 2 months; (2) Completion of the entire IMDB-based teaching module; (3) Availability of complete pre-test and post-test knowledge questionnaire data; (4) Submission of three valid follow-up decision-making simulation assignments covering different IMDB risk levels (see Additional file 1).

Exclusion criteria

(1) Absence from more than 50% of the teaching module sessions; (2) Incomplete key study data; (3) Submission of fewer than three valid simulation assignments.

The cohort comprised 29 males (72.5%) and 11 females (27.5%), aged 22–28 years. Postgraduate year distribution: first-year (n = 15, 37.5%), second-year (n = 18, 45.0%), and third-year (n = 7, 17.5%). Mean rotation duration was 2.83 ± 0.50 months, with a median of 0.5 prior RCC cases encountered (IQR: 0–8).

Most residents had minimal prior experience with RCC, and over half (52.5%) had never heard of the IMDB model (Table 1).

Teaching module development and implementation

Case library construction

A teaching case library was established using de-identified clinical data from RCC patients who underwent surgical treatment at our center from January 2019 to December 2025. Inclusion criteria for cases: (1) Pathologically confirmed RCC; (2) Complete clinical and pathological data for IMDB scoring; (3) Written informed consent from patients for the use of their anonymized data in medical education research. The final library included 200 cases, evenly covering all five IMDB risk levels (0 = very low, 1 = low, 2 = intermediate, 3 = high, 4 = very high).

IMDB risk stratification model development

The IMDB model was developed through a two-step process: (1) A systematic literature review to identify clinical and pathological indicators strongly associated with RCC postoperative recurrence; (2) Two rounds of Delphi expert consultation (10 urology experts with ≥ 10 years of clinical and teaching experience) to screen for teachable, clinically accessible indicators. The Delphi panel was explicitly instructed to select indicators that are teachable and clinically accessible, prioritizing educational utility over maximal predictive accuracy. Four core indicators were finalized, each assigned 1 point for a positive finding (total score 0–4): I (Venous invasion): Microscopic or macroscopic invasion of the renal vein or inferior vena cava (pathologically confirmed); M (Tumor size): Maximum tumor diameter ≥ 7 cm (preoperative imaging or postoperative pathology); D (Systemic symptoms): Presence of systemic symptoms at diagnosis (ECOG performance status ≥ 1, unexplained weight loss > 10%, anemia, or hypercalcemia); B (Renal sinus fat invasion): Pathologically confirmed invasion of the renal sinus fat.

Risk stratification was defined as: Very low risk (0 points), Low risk (1 point), Intermediate risk (2 points), High risk (3 points), Very high risk (4 points). Corresponding follow-up schedules and examination recommendations for each risk level were compiled into the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys (Additional file 2).

It is important to emphasize that the IMDB model was developed for teaching, not for clinical prediction. Established models such as IMDC, UISS, and SSIGN have been rigorously validated for prognostication and treatment selection in RCC; IMDB does not and is not intended to replace them. Rather, it serves as a simplified cognitive scaffold to help novice learners grasp the principles of risk-stratified follow-up before transitioning to more complex, evidence-based guidelines.

Module structure

The 8–10 h module was built around the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys and divided into three sequential phases (Learn-Practice-Reflect), with educational theory integrated into each phase (Fig. 1). As introduced in the Background, the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys is a one-page visual tool that operationalizes the IMDB model.

Phase 1: theoretical introduction and tool orientation (2.5 h)

Didactic lectures covered IMDB risk stratification criteria, the correlation between risk levels and recurrence probability, and individualized follow-up strategies for RCC. The core teaching activity was a step-by-step demonstration of using the Health Passport: extracting case data → IMDB scoring → risk grouping → formulating a follow-up plan → identifying red flag symptoms for patient education.

Phase 2: case simulation and immersive clinical practice (5–6 h)

Case simulation workshop (3 h)

Residents worked in small groups to complete independent IMDB scoring, follow-up plan design, and red flag symptom identification for 5 cases of different risk levels. Instructors provided real-time one-on-one feedback, corrected errors, and explained the clinical rationale for each decision.

Outpatient immersive practice (2–3 h)

Residents observed and, when ready, supervised application of the Health Passport with real RCC follow-up patients. Instructors intervened to guide decision-making and patient communication as needed.

Phase 3: structured debriefing and feedback (2 h)

Two senior urology faculty led a group debriefing guided by three core reflective questions: (1) Was the IMDB risk stratification accurate, and what errors were made in scoring/grouping? (2) Did the follow-up plan align with the assigned risk level, and were the examination items and time points rational? (3) Were key patient education points (red flag symptoms and health instructions) adequately addressed? The debriefing included exemplary case presentations, common error pattern analysis, and peer comparison, with the goal of helping residents reflect on their decision-making processes and consolidate learning.

Outcome measures

Knowledge acquisition

A 20-item multiple-choice knowledge questionnaire (Additional file 3) was developed to assess residents’ understanding of RCC postoperative follow-up, with a total score of 100 points (5 points per correct answer). The questionnaire was validated through: development by three RCC specialists based on EAU/NCCN guidelines and recurrence risk literature; two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, yielding a content validity index (CVI) of 0.92; internal consistency reliability testing, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.86.

The questionnaire covered four domains: (1) IMDB scoring criteria (6 items, 0–30 points); (2) Risk stratification and follow-up frequency association (5 items, 0–25 points); (3) Selection of follow-up investigations (5 items, 0–25 points); (4) Recognition and management of red flag symptoms (4 items, 0–20 points).

The questionnaire was administered as a pre-test (T0) on the first day of the module and a post-test (T1) on the last day of the module. Knowledge improvement was defined as T1 score minus T0 score.

Clinical decision-making ability

Clinical decision-making ability was evaluated using the Follow-up Card Quality Assessment Scale (Additional file 1), a 15-point scale (5 points per domain) assessing residents’ performance on three simulation assignments:

IMDB stratification accuracy (0–5 points): 5 points for complete accuracy of 4 indicators and risk grouping; 4 points for 1 error; 3 points for 2 errors; 0–2 points for ≥ 3 errors or no answer. Follow-up plan rationality (0–5 points): 5 points for a completely rational follow-up plan based on self-assigned risk; 4 points for 1 incorrect/missing time point; 3 points for 2 incorrect/missing time points; 0–2 points for a completely wrong plan or no answer. Completeness of patient education points (0–5 points): 5 points for correctly listing at least 2 core red flag symptoms; 4 points for 2 items with 1 inaccurate/unclear; 3 points for only 1 correct symptom; 0–2 points for incorrect symptoms or no answer.

Two senior associate chief physicians, blinded to resident identities, independently scored all assignments. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

A pass was defined as a mean total score ≥ 9 points (60% of the maximum score).

Teaching satisfaction

An anonymous 5-point Likert satisfaction questionnaire (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) was administered to all residents after module completion. The questionnaire included 9 items across three dimensions: (1) Module design and logic (2 items); (2) Learning gains and ability improvement (4 items); (3) Overall experience and promotion value (3 items). Agreement rate was defined as (number of strongly agree + agree responses) / total responses × 100%. Overall satisfaction score was the mean of all 9 items, with an overall satisfaction rate defined as an agreement rate ≥ 80%.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Normality of continuous data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and non-normal data as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical data are presented as n (%). Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare pre-test and post-test knowledge scores; one-way ANOVA was used to compare knowledge improvement across different postgraduate years; two-way mixed-effects model ICC was used to assess inter-rater reliability of the Follow-up Card Quality Assessment Scale (ICC benchmarks [Koo & Li]: <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, > 0.90 = excellent); Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the relationship between knowledge improvement and clinical decision-making scores. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

The 40 residents had a mean age of 24.78 ± 1.29 years (range 22–28 years), with 29 males (72.5%) and 11 females (27.5%). Postgraduate year distribution: 15 first-years (37.5%), 18 s-years (45.0%), 7 third-years (17.5%). The mean rotation duration was 2.83 ± 0.50 months. Most residents had minimal prior experience with RCC (median number of RCC cases encountered: 0.5, IQR: 0–8). Over half (52.5%) had never heard of the IMDB model, and only 20.0% reported being able to understand and apply it (Table 1).

Knowledge acquisition

Total knowledge scores increased significantly from pre-test (65.75 ± 5.13) to post-test (90.88 ± 4.92), with a mean improvement of 25.13 ± 4.00 points (t = 39.71, P < 0.0001). All four subdomains showed statistically significant improvements, with the largest gain in IMDB scoring criteria (10.00 ± 3.40 points) and the smallest gain in recognition and management of red flag symptoms (1.13 ± 4.47 points) (Table 2).

Clinical decision-making ability

The mean total score for clinical decision-making was 12.63 ± 1.86 points (out of 15), with an overall pass rate of 92.5% (37/40). Teaching satisfaction

All 40 residents completed the satisfaction questionnaire, with an overall satisfaction score of 4.51 ± 0.48 and an overall satisfaction rate of 96.1%. Subgroup analysis of knowledge improvement

Knowledge improvement varied significantly across postgraduate years (F = 4.307, P = 0.0208).

Second-year residents had the largest mean knowledge improvement (27.00 ± 4.14 points), followed by first-year residents (24.72 ± 3.63 points) and third-year residents (22.14 ± 2.67 points) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated an IMDB-based structured teaching module for RCC postoperative follow-up in urology residency training, demonstrating that the module significantly improves residents’ specialized knowledge and clinical decision-making abilities, with high teaching satisfaction and a 92.5% competency pass rate. The key findings—disproportionate knowledge gains across subdomains, varied improvement by postgraduate year, and no correlation between knowledge acquisition and clinical decision-making—provide critical insights for competency-based medical education, especially in short clinical rotations.

Drivers of significant knowledge gains: cognitive load optimization

The 25.13-point improvement in total knowledge scores (P < 0.0001) is consistent with the module’s adherence to cognitive load theory [16]. Traditional RCC follow-up teaching requires residents to integrate fragmented information from multiple complex guidelines and models (IMDC, UISS, SSIGN), creating high extraneous cognitive load that disperses cognitive resources and impairs learning [17]. In contrast, the IMDB model simplifies risk stratification to four teachable indicators, and the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys chunks information into a visual, step-by-step decision pathway (assess → stratify → plan → educate). This design may have reduced extraneous load, freed up cognitive resources, and allowed residents to focus on the core clinical logic of follow-up decision-making (Fig. 4). However, because this was a pre-post study without a control group, we cannot rule out other influences such as testing effects or increased motivation.

The largest knowledge gain in IMDB scoring criteria (10.00 points) and the smallest gain in red flag symptom recognition (1.13 points) highlight a critical distinction: factual knowledge is easily acquired through didactic teaching, while practical, patient-centered knowledge requires experiential learning. The relatively modest improvement in red flag symptom recognition warrants careful interpretation. The pre-test score for this domain was already 85% of the maximum, leaving minimal room for improvement.

Thus, the small gain is largely explained by a ceiling effect rather than a failure of the module.

Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that this domain also involves elements of communication, empathy, and clinical judgment – skills that may be less readily taught through a visual tool alone. Future module iterations could explore whether adding dedicated communication training (e.g., role-playing with standardized patients) yields additional benefit, but such efforts should be evaluated with more sensitive, non-ceilinged assessments.

Improvement in clinical decision-making: dual process theory in practice

Residents’ high scores in clinical decision-making (mean total 12.63/15) and excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.97) demonstrate that the module effectively cultivates procedural decision-making skills. This aligns with dual process theory [18], which posits that clinical decision-making relies on two systems: System 1 (fast, intuitive, expert) and System 2 (slow, analytic, novice). Novice residents rely almost exclusively on System 2, and the module’s repetitive practice (case simulation + outpatient immersion) allows them to automate the analytic decision-making pathway for RCC follow-up (IMDB scoring → risk grouping → follow-up planning). The structured debriefing further reinforces this pathway by helping residents reflect on and correct their analytic errors, building a foundation for the development of intuitive System 1 thinking in future clinical practice [19, 20].

The high post-module decision-making scores are compatible with the hypothesis that the “learn-practice-reflect” framework helps residents translate knowledge into procedural skills. Alternative explanations include repeated practice with the same case format (practice effect) and the Hawthorne effect, as residents were aware of being assessed. The lowest score in completeness of patient education points (3.93/5) and the highest error rate in omission of red flag symptoms (65%) are consistent with the knowledge acquisition results. Patient education is a complex clinical skill that integrates knowledge, communication, and empathy—skills that are not fully addressed by the module’s current design. Future revisions will add a dedicated patient communication training component (e.g., standardized patient role-playing) to address this gap.

Varied knowledge improvement by postgraduate year: implications for individualized teaching

The significant difference in knowledge improvement across postgraduate years (P = 0.0208) shows that one-size-fits-all teaching is suboptimal for residency training. Second-year residents had the largest improvement because they have a basic clinical foundation (unlike first-years) but are not yet burdened by graduation, board examinations, or job hunting (unlike third-years). First-year residents had moderate improvement, likely due to their limited clinical background and slower integration of theoretical knowledge into practice. Third-year residents had the smallest improvement, reflecting a ceiling effect—their higher pre-test score left less room for improvement, and their focus on other training and career goals may have reduced their engagement with the module.

This finding highlights the need for individualized, tiered teaching based on postgraduate year: First-years: Focus on foundational knowledge (IMDB indicators, basic follow-up schedules) and guided case analysis; Second-years: Emphasize independent decision-making and outpatient practice; Third-years: Integrate RCC follow-up with other urological pathologies and assign peer teaching roles to consolidate their own learning.

A methodologically limited null finding: the uninterpretable correlation between knowledge gain and decision-making

A methodologically limited null finding of this study is the lack of a statistically significant linear correlation between knowledge improvement and clinical decision-making scores (all r ≤ 0.07, P > 0.05). However, because the decision-making assessment showed near-ceiling performance (mean total score 12.63/15, pass rate 92.5%), this restricted range severely limits the statistical power to detect any true correlation. Therefore, this null finding should not be interpreted as evidence that knowledge and competence are unrelated; rather, it is uninterpretable with respect to that question. This result does, however, highlight the need for more sensitive, graded assessments of clinical decision-making ability in future research on this topic.

A more sensitive assessment tool—one that avoids ceiling effects—will be required before the relationship between knowledge acquisition and clinical decision-making competence can be meaningfully examined in this population. This null finding does not imply that knowledge and competence are unrelated; rather, the current decision-making measure lacks sufficient range to test that relationship. The module’s success in cultivating clinical decision-making ability lies in its “learn-practice-reflect” framework, which prioritizes application over passive knowledge acquisition. The Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys acts as a scaffold for clinical practice, and the combination of simulation and real outpatient experience allows residents to translate knowledge into action.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its single-center, retrospective design and the restriction of the sample to residents at our tertiary hospital may limit the generalizability of our findings to other institutions (e.g., community hospitals or healthcare systems with different training structures). We have provided detailed contextual information in the Background to help readers assess applicability. Second, because this was a pre-post study without a control group, we cannot establish causation; the observed improvements could be partly due to testing effects, recall bias, or the Hawthorne effect. A randomized controlled trial would be needed to definitively attribute the gains to the module itself. Third, the same 20-item multiple-choice questionnaire was used for both pre-test and post-test assessments. This raises the possibility of testing effects, such as recall of specific answers or increased familiarity with item wording, which could have contributed to the observed knowledge gains.

To mitigate this bias in future studies, alternative or parallel forms of the questionnaire could be developed; nevertheless, given that the primary goal was to evaluate immediate learning within a short module, the pre-post design with identical items is common practice in medical education research, and readers should interpret the magnitude of improvement with this limitation in mind.

Fourth, the near-ceiling performance on the decision-making assessment (mean total score 12.63/15, pass rate 92.5%) created a restricted range of outcomes, which severely limits the statistical power of the Pearson correlation and renders the null finding uninterpretable with respect to the true relationship between knowledge gain and clinical decision-making ability. Fifth, the pre-test score for “recognition and management of red flag symptoms” was already 85% of the maximum, leaving minimal room for improvement; this ceiling effect largely explains the small observed gain and should be considered when interpreting that result. Finally, the module’s success relies on a curated case library and trained faculty with dedicated teaching time, which may pose challenges for replication in resource-limited institutions.

Future research directions

Building on these findings, several avenues for future research merit exploration. Multicenter validation studies across diverse urology departments are needed to test the generalizability of the IMDB-based module in different clinical and educational contexts. A prospective randomized controlled trial would help establish causality. Longitudinal follow-up at 6 to 12 months would help determine whether knowledge gains and decision-making skills are retained and whether they translate into improved real-world RCC follow-up practice. The module itself could be strengthened by incorporating dedicated patient communication training—such as standardized patient encounters or role-playing—to better address the identified gap in red flag symptom education and patient counseling skills. Given the variation in knowledge gains by postgraduate year, developing tiered teaching materials tailored to different learner levels may further optimize outcomes. A digital version of the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys (e.g., a mobile app or WeChat mini-program) could enhance accessibility and scalability [21]. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses would help determine whether the module reduces unnecessary follow-up examinations or missed recurrences, potentially generating savings in clinical practice.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the IMDB-based structured teaching module effectively enhances urology residents’ knowledge and clinical decision-making abilities in RCC postoperative follow-up, with high teaching satisfaction and a 92.5% competency pass rate. The module’s “learn-practice-reflect” framework, grounded in cognitive load, dual process, and metacognitive theories, optimizes learning for short clinical rotations by reducing cognitive load and prioritizing clinical application over passive knowledge acquisition.

The critical finding of no correlation between knowledge improvement and clinical decision-making scores, however, must be interpreted with caution: the near-ceiling performance of the decision-making assessment restricts the range of outcomes and reduces statistical power. Therefore, this null result does not provide meaningful evidence about the relationship between knowledge and competence. Future studies should employ more sensitive, graded assessments of clinical decision-making ability before drawing conclusions about this relationship.

The IMDB model, with its high teachability and clinical applicability, and the Postoperative Health Management Passport for Kidneys provide a reproducible scaffold for teaching RCC follow-up, and the framework can be extended to other urological pathologies and medical specialties. Whether this approach can be adapted to other specialties and other clinical problems remains an open question—one we hope others will explore.
