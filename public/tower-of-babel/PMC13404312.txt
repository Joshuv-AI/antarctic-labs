The association of mobile phone addiction on campus-based physical activity among college students: the mediating role of rest intolerance and the moderating role of psychological resilience
Authors: Kaixin Li, Dingyou Zhang, Hu Lou, Huanyu Huang, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of mobile phone addiction campus-based physical activity among college students, examining the mediating role of rest intolerance and the moderating role of psychological resilience.MethodsA stratified cluster sampling method was employed to conduct a questionnaire survey among 24,345 college students. Measurements were taken using the Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale (MPATS), the Rest Intolerance Scale (RIS-8), the Adolescent Psychological Resilience Scale, and the Physical Activity Rating Scale (PARS-3). Data analysis was conducted through SPSS 27.0 and the Process macro plugin, and a bias-corrected Bootstrap method was used for 5,000 repeated samplings to test the moderated mediation model.ResultsMobile phone addiction showed a significant negative predictive effect on campus-based physical activity (β = -0.288, p < 0.05). Rest intolerance emerged as a significant partial mediating role between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity, with a mediation effect value of 0.203(95%CI [0.124,0.282]). Psychological resilience significantly moderated the path from rest intolerance to campus-based physical activity (interaction term β = -0.003, p < 0.001). The results of the moderated mediation analysis indicated that, under conditions of low psychological resilience, the indirect effect of mobile phone addiction on campus-based physical activity transmitted through rest intolerance was significant (effect = 0.300). As the level of psychological resilience increased, this indirect effect weakened and became no longer significant at high levels of psychological resilience.ConclusionMobile phone addiction is directly negatively associated with college students’ participation in campus-based physical activity but also shows indirect associations through increased rest intolerance. Psychological resilience serves as a key protective factor that buffers this negative impact. The findings suggest that enhancing psychological resilience and improving the management of phone-related rest intolerance may effectively promote their engagement in physical activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-05025-z.

Introduction

Campus-based physical activity refers to various forms of physical education, extracurricular exercise and sporting competitions conducted within the school environment, with the aim of improving physical fitness, fostering a habit of exercise and developing teamwork skills; the level of participation in these activities is directly linked to the physical and mental well-being of pupils and the effectiveness of school sports policies [1]. However, previous studies have suggested that the current level of participation in campus-based physical activity among university students may be suboptimal [2]. A long-term trend analysis of Chinese university students indicates that, over the past two decades, the decline in students’ physical fitness and the rise in cardiovascular risks have been closely linked to insufficient levels of physical activity [2]. To systematically address this global challenge and improve the health of students in China, In 2025, the Ministry of Education and four other departments jointly issued the ‘Opinions on Implementing the Student Physical Fitness Enhancement Programme’, which stipulates that higher education institutions must meet minimum requirements of 144 contact hours for undergraduate students and 108 contact hours for vocational college students in physical education [3].;The ‘Opinions on Strengthening School Physical Education to Promote the Comprehensive Physical and Mental Development of Students’, issued by the General Office of the State Council in 2016, also established the overarching requirement to ensure a seamless transition between classroom teaching and extracurricular activities and to guarantee one hour of campus-based physical activity on campus for students every day [4]. However, there remains a significant gap between the policy framework and the actual level of campus-based physical activity among university students. This suggests that, in addition to macro-level institutional arrangements, the micro-level behavioural and psychological mechanisms influencing individual participation require systematic investigation. Further research has revealed that mobile phone addiction is a potential key factor in this issue [5, 6].

Mobile phone addiction, also known as pathological mobile phone use, refers to a behavioural addiction in which university students lose the ability to exercise self-control over their smartphone use. It is characterised by key features such as increased tolerance and pronounced withdrawal symptoms, and results in persistent harm to academic performance, health and social functioning [7, 8], Its definition is highly consistent with the diagnostic framework for behavioural addiction set out in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders [9]. In empirical research, mobile phone addiction is commonly assessed using standardized self-report scales, and individuals scoring above a predefined cut-off are classified as exhibiting addictive tendencies [10]. Although the DSM-5 does not formally recognize mobile phone addiction as a distinct disorder, its diagnostic framework for behavioral addictions is widely used to conceptualize this construct [11]. The problem has become widespread and persistent among university students. Research by Flores Moscoso et al. shows that 48.72% of the university students surveyed use their mobile phones for more than four hours a day, a figure that is consistent with the global trend of rising mobile phone addiction [12]; A cross-sectional study conducted in 2025 found that the prevalence of mobile phone addiction among Spanish university students stood at 33.8%, and was closely linked to daily usage duration and the habit of using mobile phones before bed [13].Persistent mobile phone addiction has had a significant negative impact on the growth and development of university students. A cross-lagged panel model study based on three months of longitudinal data from 376 university students confirmed that mobile phone addiction significantly inhibits physical activity (β = −0.32, p < 0.01) and creates a vicious cycle through a ‘time-crowding effect’ [5]. Based on survey data from 7,479 undergraduates at a Chinese university, a one-standard-deviation increase in mobile app usage is associated not only with a decline of approximately 36.2% in the average GPA for compulsory courses and a drop of approximately 2.74 marks in physical education grades, but also with a reduction of approximately 2.3% in graduates’ starting salaries [14]. At the same time, excessive use of mobile phones at night can directly disrupt sleep patterns. A cross-sectional study in Norway involving 45,202 university students aged 18 to 28 found that for every hour spent using screens such as mobile phones in bed, average sleep duration was reduced by approximately 24 minutes, whilst the risk of insomnia increased by around 63 per cent. This indicates that screen use at night has a significant negative impact on both sleep duration and sleep quality [15].

As a systematic and organised form of in-person activity, campus-based physical activity has been shown to be an effective means of mitigating mobile phone addiction; it reduces individuals’ dependence on their phones by diverting their attention and enhancing face-to-face social interaction [1]. A longitudinal study conducted in 2025 found that a positive physical activity environment significantly and negatively predicts the level of mobile phone addiction among university students, mediated by psychological resilience [16]; A questionnaire survey of 559 university students at Wuhan University of Science and Technology and Central China Normal University found that for every one-standard-deviation increase in physical activity levels, the degree of mobile phone addiction decreased significantly, with a regression coefficient of β = 0.038 (p < 0.001); that is, physical activity has a direct negative predictive effect on mobile phone addiction [17]; Another study focusing on university students majoring in physical education found that levels of physical activity were significantly negatively correlated with mobile phone addiction; the mechanism by which this occurs is thought to be an indirect alleviation of addictive behaviour through a reduction in individuals’ tendency to feel bored [18]. Although a negative correlation between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity has been widely established, existing research has largely focused on the direct effects of these two factors, whilst there remains a lack of exploration into the underlying psychological pathways linking them and individual differences. The question of exactly how mobile phone addiction influences participation in campus-based physical activity through psychological mechanisms has yet to be clearly answered and urgently requires further in-depth investigation.

To address this gap, the present study draws on established theoretical frameworks. According to the Conservation of Resources theory [19], individuals strive to protect their limited psychological resources (e.g., time, attention, self-control). Persistent mobile phone use depletes these resources, which may trigger negative emotional responses to perceived time waste——conceptualized in this study as rest intolerance. Rest intolerance is therefore proposed as a mediating mechanism linking mobile phone addiction to reduced physical activity. Furthermore, the Stress and Coping Theory [20] suggests that personal resources such as psychological resilience can moderate the impact of stressors on behavioral outcomes. Accordingly, psychological resilience is proposed as a moderator that may buffer the negative emotional and behavioral consequences of rest intolerance. This integrated framework provides a theoretical basis for the moderated mediation model examined in this study. Against this backdrop, this study introduces two variables—rest intolerance and psychological resilience—in an attempt to uncover the underlying mechanisms through which mobile phone addiction influences participation in campus-based physical activity.

From the perspective of conservation of resources theory, individuals’ psychological and behavioral resources such as time and energy are finite, and protecting and acquiring these resources constitute a fundamental motivation [21]. Mobile phone addiction is essentially a process of continuous resource depletion; excessive immersion in mobile phone use directly encroaches on the time that could otherwise be devoted to physical exercise. Existing research has confirmed that this behavioural pattern directly leads to an increase in sedentary time and a reduction in physical activity among university students [22]. Furthermore, numerous cross-cultural studies have confirmed that prolonged screen use is significantly associated with reduced physical activity and increased sedentary behaviour [23]. Another study based on structural equation modelling also indicated that mobile phone addiction has a significant negative predictive effect on physical activity among university students, with an overall effect on physical activity of approximately β≈−0.35 (p < 0.001) [6]. Furthermore, research has shown that among more than 3,500 university students in western China, participation in physical activity is a significant protective factor against mobile phone addiction (odds ratio OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.59–0.82, p < 0.001) [24]. Taking the above studies into account, the findings suggest that mobile phone addiction shows a significant negative association with campus-based physical activity, which may be explained by the depletion of both time and psychological resources. Therefore, based on the aforementioned theoretical and empirical research, this study proposes the following direct effect hypothesis H1: Mobile phone addiction significantly and negatively predicts participation in campus-based physical activity.

The impact of mobile phone addiction on campus-based physical activity may not be solely due to the diversion of resources, but may also exert an indirect influence by triggering specific emotional experiences. Rest intolerance refers to the feelings of guilt and anxiety experienced by individuals when taking necessary rest or relaxation in a socio-cultural environment that values efficiency and diligence, arising from the perception of being ‘in a non-task state’. This is typically accompanied by cognitive evaluations such as ‘I don’t deserve a rest’ or ‘I’m wasting time’. As a measurable psychological trait, its core manifestation is a sense of aversion and discomfort towards states of rest, which can easily lead to emotional distress and impaired regulatory functions [25, 26]. It is important to note that rest intolerance differs from general restlessness or inability to relax; whilst the latter two primarily manifest as physical unease or a general difficulty in relaxing, rest intolerance specifically refers to the guilt and self-criticism triggered by the perception of being in a non-task state, and is closely linked to cultural values that prioritise hard work and achievement [25]. From the perspective of psychological capital theory, positive psychological resources such as self-efficacy, psychological resilience and hope are key to maintaining an individual’s mental well-being [27], Excessive mobile phone use not only undermines self-control and leads to the depletion of psychological capital, but also encroaches on time that should be devoted to rest and recovery, giving rise to a negative perception that ‘time is being wasted’ [17]. This cognitive dissonance exacerbates feelings of guilt and self-doubt, thereby giving rise to ‘rest intolerance’—a sense of shame triggered by mobile phone use—which exerts a complex, bidirectional influence on university students’ participation in physical activity, according to frustration-coping theory [28], Rest intolerance constitutes an emotional setback experienced by individuals in terms of the allocation of time resources and their perception of self-worth. When this sense of shame is positively regulated, individuals tend to adopt an approach-oriented coping strategy, viewing physical activity as a valuable endeavour capable of restoring their sense of self-efficacy, thereby promoting participation in physical activity; Conversely, if shame is not effectively regulated, individuals are prone to psychological avoidance and behavioural withdrawal. To avoid the negative experiences associated with unproductive labels, they may instead focus on academic pursuits or other activities perceived as more productive, leading to a further reduction in participation in campus-based physical activity. Based on the aforementioned theoretical and empirical evidence, this study proposes Hypothesis H2: Rest intolerance mediates the relationship between mobile phone addiction and participation in campus-based physical activity.

The indirect effect of mobile phone addiction on campus-based physical activity via rest intolerance may vary depending on differences in an individual’s level of psychological resilience. Psychological resilience is an individual’s dynamic psychological capacity to maintain positive adaptation and achieve personal growth in situations of stress and adversity [29–31]. From the perspective of resource conservation theory, psychological resilience serves as a core protective psychological resource that acts as a buffer when an individual faces threats to or depletion of resources. It helps individuals effectively manage and protect their limited time, energy and emotional resources when confronted with stressors such as academic workload and social evaluation, thereby preventing them from falling into a spiral of resource depletion [19]. In situations where rest intolerance is viewed as shameful, individuals with high psychological resilience tend to interpret this rest intolerance as a signal to adjust their time allocation and energy management, thereby replenishing their resources by sensibly planning the boundaries between rest and activity, rather than succumbing to cognitive biases that lead to self-denial [31]. Related research has also confirmed that individuals with high psychological resilience are more likely to view rest as a necessary means of replenishing their mental energy when faced with academic or life pressures, rather than as a meaningless waste of time [32]. Conversely, individuals with low psychological resilience often lack the ability to regulate their emotions effectively or reframe their thoughts when faced with rest intolerance; they are easily overwhelmed by negative emotions, and their coping strategies may take one of two extremes: either they engage excessively in physical activity to compensate for their sense of shame, or they abandon physical activity altogether to avoid situations that might exacerbate their negative experiences [33, 34]. Psychological resilience therefore plays a key moderating role in the relationship between rest intolerance and campus-based physical activity. Based on this, this study proposes moderating hypothesis H3: psychological resilience moderates the effect of rest intolerance on campus-based physical activity.

Method

Study design

This study adopted a cross-sectional correlational design to examine the associations between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity among college students. Based on Hayes’ conditional process framework, the present study further explored whether rest intolerance was indirectly associated with the relationship between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity, and whether this association varied across different levels of psychological resilience.

Specifically, rest intolerance was hypothesized to function as a potential mediator in the association between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity, while psychological resilience was hypothesized to moderate the association between rest intolerance and campus-based physical activity. Accordingly, the indirect association between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity through rest intolerance was expected to differ across levels of psychological resilience.

The proposed conceptual framework therefore integrates mediation, moderation, and moderated mediation (conditional process) analyses within a unified correlational model.

Survey population and sampling

The data for this study are drawn from the 2024 survey of the ‘China College Students’ Physical Activity and Health Tracking Survey’ (CPAHLS-CS). The CPAHLS-CS aims to collect a high-quality set of individual microdata representative of physical activity and health-related behaviours among Chinese university students, with a view to analysing the interrelationships between physical activity and health among this population and promoting interdisciplinary research into student health issues. The CPAHLS-CS is widely used to analyse the health status of Chinese university students [35–45], A stratified, cluster and multi-stage sampling method was employed to select the sample, taking into account school type, geographical distribution and socio-economic development levels; the questionnaire survey was conducted at the level of administrative classes. The list of ordinary higher education institutions was drawn from the Ministry of Education’s ‘List of Ordinary Higher Education Institutions Nationwide (as of 20 June 2024)’, with the following exclusion criteria: (1) Questionnaires where the full name of the institution could not be identified were removed (2). Questionnaires with at least 21 consecutive identical entries were excluded (3). Given that the average completion time was 6 min and 12 s, questionnaires with completion times falling within the ranges (0, 0.5%) and (99.5%, 100%) were excluded [46], A total of 24,345 valid questionnaires were ultimately included. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. The consent form clearly outlined the study’s objectives, methodology, potential risks, and the rights of the subjects, ensuring that participation was entirely voluntary and based on full ethical compliance and informed awareness. All participants were informed that the questionnaire would take approximately 12 min to complete, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences.

Scale

Mobile phone addiction

In this study, mobile phone addiction among college students was measured using the Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale (MPATS), which was developed by Chinese scholar Xiong Jie and colleagues. The scale consists of four dimensions—withdrawal symptoms, salience behavior, social comfort, and mood change—with a total of 16 items [10]. Dimension classification for the retest: Dimension 1: Withdrawal symptoms (6 items), item numbers: 1, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12; Dimension 2: Prominent behaviours (4 items), item numbers: 5, 9, 13, 15; Dimension 3: Social Comfort (3 items), item numbers: 2, 7, 16; Dimension 4: Mood Changes (3 items), item numbers: 3, 11, 14. A 5-point Likert scale is used, ranging from ‘Strongly Disagree’ to ‘Strongly Agree’, scored from 1 to 5; The maximum score is 80 points and the minimum is 16 points; a higher score indicates a greater tendency towards mobile phone addiction, whilst a lower score indicates a lower tendency. Exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis indicate that the scale possesses good reliability and validity, with an internal consistency coefficient of 0.83 and a test-retest reliability of 0.91. In this study, the Cronbach’s α value for the scale was 0.895 [10].

Rest intolerance

In this study, ‘rest intolerance’ among university students was measured using the ‘Rest Intolerance Scale-8’ (RIS-8), developed by Chinese scholars including Wang Fei in 2025 [47]. This scale measures four dimensions of rest intolerance: negative emotions, obsessive thoughts, social comparison and cognitive distortions [47]. The scale comprises eight items, ranging from ‘When I rest or engage in leisure activities, I feel guilty’ to ‘I believe that holiday time should be spent on more worthwhile activities’. A 5-point Likert scale is used, with scores ranging from 1 for ‘strongly disagree’ to 5 for ‘strongly agree’. Higher scores on the items indicate a higher level of rest intolerance in the participant. The Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale is 0.89, with CFI = 0.99 (greater than 0.9), GFI = 0.98 (greater than 0.8) and RMSEA = 0.063 (less than 0.08). The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity, effectively reflects rest intolerance, and can serve as a useful research tool [47].

Campus-based physical activity

The level of physical activity among university students is measured using the Physical Activity Rating Scale-3 (PARS-3), which was developed by the Japanese scholar Kimio Hashimoto in 1990 and translated and revised by the Chinese scholar Liang Deqing in 1994 [48]. To align with the concept of campus-based physical activity, we have included contextual qualifiers such as ‘during school hours’ or ‘participating in school-organised activities’ in both the introductory instructions and the specific items of the original questionnaire. The PARS-3 assesses physical activity levels across three dimensions: intensity, frequency and duration of a single exercise session. Each dimension comprises five options used to measure the level of physical activity participation, with a maximum score of 100 and a minimum of 0; a higher score indicates a greater level of physical activity. The Chinese adult normative standards for the PARS-3 are as follows: low physical activity ≤ 19 points, moderate physical activity 20–42 points, and high physical activity ≥ 43 points [48]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the PARS-3 is 0.78, with a GFI of 0.93, an AGFI of 0.92, a CFI of 0.88, an RMSEA of 0.06, and a test-retest reliability of 0.82. The scale has now undergone reliability and validity testing in China and has been found to possess good reliability and validity, making it a suitable general-purpose tool for research.

Formula for calculation: Physical activity score = Intensity × (Duration − 1) × Frequency.

Psychological resilience

In this study, psychological resilience among university students was measured using the Adolescent Psychological Resilience Scale developed by Hu Yueqin. The scale comprises 27 items across five dimensions: goal orientation, emotional regulation, positive cognition, family support and interpersonal support. It employs a 5-point rating scale, where 1 = ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 = ‘strongly agree’, with a total score ranging from 27 to 135 points [49]. In the current sample, the Cronbach‘s alpha for this scale was 0.775. Calculate the average score across all items; the higher the score, the greater the individual’s psychological resilience. This scale has been shown to have good reliability and validity in previous studies.

Covariate

This study measured and controlled for gender, ethnicity, year group and body mass index (BMI) as covariates; all data were collected via a demographic questionnaire. The specific measurement methods are as follows:

Gender was collected via the question “What is your gender?”, with the options set as ‘Male’ and ‘Female’, and was included in the model as a binary control variable during data analysis. Ethnicity was collected via the question “What is your ethnicity?”, and in accordance with the ethnic classifications set out in the Law of the People’s Republic of China on Regional Ethnic Autonomy, was grouped into two categories—Han Chinese and ethnic minorities—for statistical analysis. Year of study was collected via the question “What is your year of study?”, covering four options: first year, second year, third year and fourth year, reflecting the characteristics of university students at different academic stages. The World Health Organisation (WHO) definition of BMI was used as the standard for assessing overweight and obesity [50]. Data was collected via the question “What are your height and weight?”, with participants self-reporting their height and weight; BMI was calculated using the formula BMI = weight (kg)/height² (m²). To align with the assessment standards for the Chinese university student population, this study employed the BMI scoring table from the National Student Physical Fitness Standards for classification: a BMI of ≤ 18.5 was classified as underweight, 18.5–23.9 as normal weight, and ≥ 24.0 as overweight. In the statistical analysis, considering the characteristics of the sample distribution, BMI was treated as a continuous variable.

Statistical methods

In this study, two software programs, SPSS 27.0 and Excel, were primarily used for data processing. The entire analysis process can be divided into five key stages:

(1) The raw data were preliminarily processed using Excel, including the re-measurement or removal of incomplete or outlier data, and the completion of variable coding (2). The Harman single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. The results showed that eight principal components were extracted in this study, with eigenvalues greater than 1; the largest factor explained 24.527% of the variance, which is below the commonly set threshold of 40%. In addition, a number of procedural controls were implemented during the questionnaire design phase to minimise common-method bias: the questionnaire was completed anonymously, reverse-scored items were included, the order of items within each scale was randomised, and different instructions were provided for each scale. This indicates that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study (3). Prior to the main analyses, the normality of the data was examined using skewness and kurtosis. For mobile phone addiction, campus-based physical activity, psychological resilience, and rest intolerance, the absolute values of skewness ranged from 0.146 to 1.922, and the absolute values of kurtosis ranged from 0.027 to 3.690. Given the large sample size (N = 24,345), the standard errors of skewness and kurtosis were very small (0.016 and 0.031, respectively). According to Kim, for large samples (n > 300), absolute skewness values greater than 2 or absolute kurtosis values greater than 7 indicate substantial non-normality [51]. The observed skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable ranges, suggesting that the data approximate a normal distribution, which supports the use of the subsequent statistical analyses in this study. (4) The collected student data were analysed, and independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were employed to investigate differences in campus-based physical activity, psychological resilience, mobile phone addiction and rest intolerance among students of different genders, ethnicities, year groups and BMIs. η² was used to measure the degree to which the independent variables explained the dependent variables. According to Cohen’s criteria, η² ranges from 0 to 1, with 0.01 indicating a small effect, 0.06 a moderate effect, and 0.14 a large effect [52]. (5) Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between the four core variables: mobile phone addiction, psychological resilience, rest intolerance, and campus-based physical activity. (6) Using the Process plugin, a moderated mediation model was tested to examine the relationships between mobile phone addiction, rest intolerance, psychological resilience and campus-based physical activity. Model 14 was selected, and gender, ethnicity, year group and BMI were included as control variables in the analysis. The confidence interval was set at 95%, with a bootstrap sample size of 5,000.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and the PROCESS macro developed by Hayes. Descriptive statistics, independent-samples t tests, one-way ANOVA, and Pearson correlation analyses were first performed. Subsequently, PROCESS Model 14 was used to test the proposed moderated mediation model. Gender, ethnicity, academic year, and BMI were included as covariates. Indirect effects were estimated using a bias-corrected bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals.

Although the remaining variables showed statistical differences in certain dimensions, the effect sizes were all small, and their practical significance was limited. Although the p-values for the effects of gender on psychological resilience and mobile phone addiction, ethnicity on various variables, and year group on campus-based physical activity were significant, the proportion of variance explained was extremely low; and although the effect of BMI on participation in campus-based physical activity was significant, the effect size was merely 0.010. Overall, gender is the most critical demographic variable influencing university students’ participation in campus-based physical activity; therefore, it is necessary to control for it as a covariate in subsequent analyses.

Before testing the model, we first use scatter plots to visually examine the linear relationships between variables. Figure 3 presents scatter plots for the regression analysis of key variables, showing the simple linear regression relationships between rest intolerance and mobile phone addiction, as well as the partial regression relationships between campus-based physical activity and each predictor variable. These findings are consistent with the results of the regression models and provide a basis for the subsequent tests of the moderated mediation model.

Discussion

The impact of mobile phone addiction on campus-based physical activity

The findings of this study are consistent with the view that mobile phone addiction is significantly negatively associated with university students’ participation in campus-based physical activity (β = −0.288, p < 0.05), which supports Hypothesis H1. This discovery aligns with existing research conclusions. Lin et al. confirmed that heightened mobile phone addiction further diminishes physical activity engagement by reducing exercise self-efficacy, constituting a significant barrier to participation in physical activities [6].A large-scale cross-sectional survey involving 53,534 South Korean adolescents revealed that individuals classified as potential sufferers of mobile phone addiction and those diagnosed with addiction exhibited significantly lower levels of physical activity compared to the general user group [53]. Research among medical students in China has reported similar findings: mobile phone addiction is not only directly associated with lower levels of physical activity but also intertwined with health issues such as reduced sleep duration [54]. A large-scale study involving university students in Shanghai, China (n = 6294) similarly confirmed that physical activity is significantly negatively correlated with mobile phone addiction and irrational procrastination [55]. Han et al.‘s research similarly showed a significant negative correlation between mobile phone addiction and physical exercise behaviour (r = −0.279, p < 0.001) [56]. These data collectively suggest an association: excessive mobile phone use may encroach on time and energy that should be devoted to physical activity, creating a vicious cycle in which the mind becomes engrossed in the digital world while the body remains sedentary. This may be indirectly related to lower participation levels in sporting activities.

The negative correlation between the two can be explained from the perspective of resource conservation theory [57]. When core resources such as time, self-control, and attention are depleted through sustained digital immersion, individuals often actively avoid activities that require additional resource investment, with physical exercise being a prime example [58]. Concurrently, self-determination theory offers an additional layer of explanation for this process: persistent distractions and emotional exhaustion may interfere with the fulfilment of autonomy, competence, and relatedness within sporting activities. When these psychological needs remain unmet, physical exercise ceases to be experienced as a meaningful endeavour and instead feels like an additional burden, thereby diminishing willingness to participate [59].

The mediating role of rest intolerance between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity

This study employed the Bootstrap method to examine the mediating role of rest intolerance in the relationship between mobile phone addiction and campus-based physical activity. Results showed a significant mediating effect (Effect = 0.2029, 95% CI [0.124, 0.283]), which is consistent with the hypothesis that rest intolerance serves as a mediator in the relationship between mobile phone addiction and health behaviours, thereby providing support for Hypothesis H2. This indicates that the association between mobile phone addiction on campus-based physical activity is not a straightforward direct relationship, but involves complex psychological internal processes.

This finding is highly consistent with the conclusions of numerous recent studies examining psychological mechanisms. Tong and Meng et al. found that physical activity can indirectly reduce mobile phone addiction by lowering negative emotions among university students, thereby establishing the importance of negative emotions as a mediating variable [60]; Research by Yuanyuan Ke et al. indicates that physical activity can reduce mobile phone dependency by enhancing university students’ self-esteem levels [61]. However, unlike studies adopting a resource gain perspective that treat physical activity as a positive antecedent variable, this research is framed within resource conservation theory. It takes a resource depletion perspective, conceptualizing mobile phone addiction as a stressor that may consume an individual’s psychological resources (such as attention and self-regulatory capacity) [19]. Whilst being negatively associated with participation in physical activity, the psychological resource depletion associated with mobile phone addiction shows a significant positive association with rest intolerance (β = 2.883, p < 0.001). This composite negative cognitive emotion, blending guilt, self-criticism, and social discipline, may be conceptualized as a potential compensatory motivator. It shows a positive association with physical activity (β = 0.335, p < 0.001) which could be interpreted as an attempt to regain self-control, restore psychological resource equilibrium, and thereby counteract guilt [62].

This study identifies a positive pathway from rest intolerance to physical activity, whose underlying psychological mechanisms appear to be rooted in China’s specific sociocultural context. This provides a novel perspective for understanding the complex relationship between mobile phone addiction and physical activity, which differs from the mediating pathways proposed by scholars such as Bai Yang that involve negative health consequences like sleep disorders [63]. Chinese society emphasises a cultural tradition in which Heaven rewards diligence and collective achievement is prioritised, thereby constructing unremitting effort as a noble personal virtue and social responsibility. Conversely, rest and enjoyment are imbued with negative moral connotations of laziness and a lack of ambition [64]. This may elevate rest intolerance beyond ordinary anxiety or depression, potentially transforming it into a complex cognitive-emotional experience that reflects internalized societal discipline and moral judgement. When mobile phone addiction is associated with individuals perceiving time as wasted, which may conflict with the principle of sustained effort, it may be accompanied by intense moral anxiety and cognitive dissonance [65]. In this context, participation in sporting activities widely acclaimed by society may serve as a potential means of moral compensation and self-affirmation [66]. Through this visible act of engagement, individuals may attempt to counteract guilt, restore a sense of control, and repair their self-image. Thus, the core motivation behind exercise in the context of rest intolerance may be characterized as a guilt-associated, instrumental form of controlled motivation—aimed at alleviating moral discomfort rather than originating from intrinsic interest in physical activity or health pursuits [67]. This stands in stark contrast to the pathway identified in studies by Tong and Meng, among others, in which physical activity reduces mobile phone dependency by enhancing resources [60]. The findings of this study suggest that, within specific cultural disciplines, compensatory behaviours aimed at restoring psychological resource balance may operate through complex moral-emotional dynamics. This highlights the possibility that non-autonomous motivational sources may be relevant to health-promoting behaviours health-promoting behaviours.

The regulatory role of psychological resilience

This study provides evidence that psychological resilience significantly moderates the latter half of the pathway from rest intolerance to “campus-based physical activity” (interaction term β = −0.003, p < 0.001), which is consistent with Hypothesis H3. The absolute value of this coefficient is relatively small, which is related to the wide range of the scales for psychological resilience and rest intolerance; however, it exhibits a significant pattern in terms of conditional indirect effects (Table 3). Moreover, a buffering pattern of psychological resilience as a protective resource was observed. As psychological resilience levels were higher, the indirect effect of mobile phone addiction on physical activity via rest intolerance was progressively smaller, and was non-significant at high resilience levels (effect size was 0.300 at low resilience versus 0.081 at high resilience). For individuals with low psychological resilience, rest intolerance shows a stronger positive association with compensatory physical activity. Conversely, for those with high psychological resilience, this path of instrumental behaviour associated with negative emotions is less evident. Their participation in physical activity may be more reflective of autonomous motivations, such as interest and health [68].

This study aligns with the prevailing consensus that psychological resilience moderates the adverse effects of general negative emotions on behaviour. Previous research has consistently demonstrated that psychological resilience effectively buffers the detrimental impact of various stresses on mental health. Asaduzzaman M’s study among medical undergraduates revealed that psychological resilience significantly moderated the relationship between academic stress and depressive symptoms. Within the low-resilience group (M-1SD), academic stress exhibited a high predictive coefficient for depression (β = 0.52), whereas within the high-resilience group (M+1SD), this coefficient markedly decreased to β = 0.28. This directly quantifies the magnitude of psychological resilience’s effect as a ‘stress buffer’ [69]. Similarly, a structural equation modelling analysis of a study on internet addiction among university students revealed that psychological resilience not only exerted a direct negative predictive effect on depressive symptoms (β = −0.24, p < 0.001), but also exerted a significant indirect effect via hope (indirect effect value = − 0.12, 95% CI [− 0.15, − 0.09]). This demonstrates that psychological resilience enhances resilience by boosting hope. p < 0.001), but also exerted a significant indirect effect via hope (indirect effect value = −0.12, 95% CI [−0.15, −0.09]). This revealed its complex mechanism for forming a chain of protective pathways by enhancing positive psychological resources [70]. These specific data collectively point to one conclusion: psychological resilience, as a core component of psychological capital, serves the fundamental function of significantly mitigating the intensity and impact pathways of negative emotional experiences when individuals face challenges, thereby safeguarding psychological adaptation and well-being. Therefore, from the perspective of universal logic and effect patterns, the finding that psychological resilience moderates the association between rest intolerance induced by mobile phone addiction on subsequent behaviour is highly consistent with the widely accepted consensus that psychological resilience plays a protective moderating effect within the emotion-behaviour pathway.

However, this consistency primarily is observed in the universality of the regulatory function, whereas the present study differs in the specific targets and directions of regulation. Most research focuses on how psychological resilience buffers the inhibitory effects of general negative emotions on behaviour [71]; This study found that psychological resilience moderates the positive association between rest intolerance on physical activity. Specifically, lower levels of psychological resilience are associated with a stronger role of rest intolerance in compensatory physical participation through feelings of guilt; conversely, higher levels of psychological resilience are associated with a weaker expression of this pathway, which corresponds to more autonomous motivation for physical activity [72]. Research by Shanshan Xu et al. indicates that psychological resilience not only helps students withstand pressure without becoming overwhelmed, but also enhances their autonomous motivation to participate in physical activities by satisfying fundamental psychological needs outlined in self-determination theory [73]. It is evident, therefore, that psychological resilience is not only associated with a weaker link between emotions and behaviour. Rather, its function may involve managing the guilt arising from this complex cognitive process, which may allow individuals to move beyond a simplistic response pattern of immediately engaging in exercise to compensate for feelings of shame. This may be conducive to the development of more stable, intrinsically motivated habits of physical activity participation.

The root cause of this discrepancy lies in the particular nature of the mediating variable known as “rest intolerance”. Unlike basic emotions, rest intolerance constitutes a composite cognitive evaluation that integrates time anxiety, self-criticism, and social conditioning. Analysing this unique regulatory mechanism can benefit from multiple perspectives: from cognitive and emotional regulation viewpoints, higher psychological resilience is associated with stronger cognitive reappraisal abilities. This may help individuals deconstruct irrational beliefs that equate rest with laziness, which may be related to a lower intensity of the behavioural drive associated with shame [74]; From the perspective of emotion and motivation, this drive can be viewed as a controlled motivational behaviour associated with guilt. Psychological resilience is closely linked to the fulfilment of fundamental psychological needs, such as autonomy and competence [73], Enhancing psychological resilience increases the likelihood that individuals derive intrinsic enjoyment from sporting activities, which may contribute to more internalized motivation for autonomous health pursuits. Crucially, when examined through a sociocultural lens within the Chinese cultural context, the protective function of psychological resilience manifests as assisting individuals in strategically navigating internalised social conditioning. This involves employing more adaptive cultural-cognitive strategies to balance societal expectations with personal needs, which may contribute to more internalized emotional conflict [75]. Thus, psychological resilience may facilitate the transition from guilt-associated behaviour to autonomous pursuit through the aforementioned multi-level cognitive and motivational transformations. This is consistent with the observation that the pathway from rest intolerance to “campus-based physical activity” is weaker when psychological resilience is higher.

Implications for practice

Given the moderating role of psychological resilience, higher education institutions can design tiered intervention programmes tailored to students with mobile phone addiction. Specifically, the Psychological Resilience Scale can be used to assess students during the freshers’ intake, thereby identifying those with different levels of resilience. For those with lower levels of psychological resilience, a six-week group intervention programme could be established, covering cognitive restructuring, emotional regulation and low-intensity group physical activities. The cognitive restructuring component guides students to examine automatic thoughts such as ‘resting is a waste of time’; the emotional regulation component introduces mindfulness breathing and stress reappraisal strategies; and the physical activity component focuses primarily on low-intensity activities such as walking and yoga. Following each activity, a brief group sharing session is arranged, during which the facilitator assists students in establishing personalised connections between their physical activity experiences and emotional management. For students with high psychological resilience who have mobile phone addiction, it is advisable to provide environmental interventions centred on autonomy support; for example, setting up self-discipline challenges regarding mobile phone use and encouraging participation in non-competitive sports clubs, allowing students to independently regulate the allocation of time between mobile phone use and physical activity. The effectiveness of the aforementioned intervention programme can be further tested through a controlled experimental design.

Although this study included a sample of 24,345, the participants were predominantly concentrated in specific regions and academic years, making it difficult to fully represent the characteristics of the national university student population. Mobile phone addiction and physical activity levels were assessed using self-reported measures. Self-reported mobile phone use may lead students to underestimate their actual usage, whilst self-reported levels of physical activity may be subject to recall bias or a tendency to overestimate actual activity levels. As these two types of measurement bias act in opposite directions, they may, to some extent, have reduced the effect size of the association between the variables. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes rigorous causal inferences, and the exploration of psychological resilience as a moderating mechanism remains insufficient. At the cultural level, the concept of rest intolerance in this study is embedded within the Chinese cultural context; its four-dimensional structure is closely linked to social values that emphasise diligence and collective achievement. Consequently, caution is required when generalising the findings to other cultures, particularly individualistic ones. The generalisability of the findings warrants further validation. Future research may employ longitudinal tracking or experimental intervention designs to clarify causal sequencing. Objective indicators such as mobile screen usage duration and fitness tracker data could enhance measurement validity. Expanding sample coverage, including cross-cultural samples to test the stability of the model, and incorporating contextual variables like academic pressure and social support would refine theoretical models. Latent profile analysis could identify heterogeneous response subgroups exhibiting rest intolerance. and delve deeper into the specific cognitive and emotional mechanisms through which psychological resilience exerts its moderating effect. This will provide more robust empirical support for the development of precision health interventions that balance behavioural promotion with psychological counselling, thereby empowering university students to achieve a more autonomous and healthy equilibrium between digital engagement and physical activity, ultimately fostering their holistic physical and mental development.

Conclusion

This study constructed a moderated mediation model to examine the psychological mechanisms associated with mobile phone addiction and university students’ participation in campus-based physical activities. Findings suggest that mobile phone addiction is negatively associated with participation in campus-based physical activities and may be related to increased rest intolerance, which in turn shows a complex association with physical activity participation that may reflect culturally shaped moral compensation processes. Psychological resilience plays a significant moderating role within this mechanism. It is associated with cognitive reappraisal and emotion regulation, which may reduce compensatory motivation related to rest intolerance and correspond to a shift towards autonomous, health-oriented participation in physical activities. These findings suggest that psychological resilience may serve as an important internal resource for mitigating the negative correlates of digital addiction behaviours and may support students in developing rational and healthy perspectives.
