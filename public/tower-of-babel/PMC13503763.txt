Clinical application of AI for lung lobe segmentation and functional quantification with SPECT/CT: open-source versus custom models
Authors: Elaheh Amini, Maria Goemans, Daniel Kyrollos, Oscar Osorio Cruz, Jose Aquino, Farzad Abbaspour, Karine Provost, Ran Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung lobe quantification with SPECT/CT is essential for treatment planning but remains clinically limited by inadequate tools. Although AI-based approaches show promise, their clinical validation is limited. This study compares the clinical accuracy of SPECT/CT-based lobar quantification using open-source and in-house developed segmentation models. We curated a dataset of 200 diverse CT and SPECT scans with expert lobar annotations, graded by segmentation difficulty (Easy, Moderate, Hard). We trained a local AI model on these data and included a novel anatomy-aware softmax modification at inference to improve robustness in missing-lobe cases. Lobar function error was computed as the difference between model- and ground truth-derived function after automated SPECT/CT coregistration. Results were compared across the local model, its anatomy-aware variant, three open-source models, and a commercial tool. Across 40 test cases (10 Easy, 10 Moderate, 20 Hard), no systematic bias was found in lobar function error across models. However, error standard-deviation increased significantly with case complexity for open-source models (0.57 to 5.79%, p < 0.005), particularly in missing-lobe cases (1.61 vs. 7.57%, p < 0.001). In contrast, the local model and commercial tool maintained high accuracy across difficulty (0.51 to 1.4%, p > 0.24). The anatomy-aware softmax modification eliminated false-positive lobe predictions, further improving quantification accuracy. AI-based tools can streamline routine lobar quantification with minimal but necessary physician oversight. SPECT/CT lobar quantification benefited from improved robustness through training with representative data and anatomy-awareness. Introduction

Single-photon emission computed tomography/x-ray computed tomography (SPECT/CT) lung function assessment is routinely used for evaluating a patient’s functional reserve in several clinical settings1, including pre-surgical or radiation therapy planning, and selecting target lobes for bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)2. The derived lobar function information plays an important role in guiding these treatments and, therefore, improving patient outcomes.

However, the limited spatial resolution of SPECT and the lack of anatomical landmarks in functional imaging3 make it necessary to use co-registered CT images for accurate lung lobe delineation, which are then applied to SPECT. Although commercial CT-based lung lobe segmentation tools are available, semi-automated workflows such as Hybrid3D tool used in this study, can be time-consuming, requiring specialized training, and be prone to inter-observer variability4–6. These drawbacks may increase the workload of radiologists or technologists who perform the task and, therefore, limit the routine clinical use of lobar function quantification. Furthermore, access to dedicated software may be limited by their cost and the clinical workload considerations.

On the other hand, advances in AI and the growing availability of annotated datasets have recently made fully automated lung lobe segmentation tools widely available, offering improvements in reproducibility, processing time, and accuracy, particularly in cases where traditional image-processing techniques struggle. However, their performance depends heavily on the quantity, quality, and diversity of the data used for training7,8. While outperforming the traditional methods, many of the existing open-source tools were developed using large annotated general radiology datasets, and therefore, they lack the full diversity of lung pathology and anatomy seen in clinical practice9–11. As a result, these models may not perform reliably in segmenting more complex cases, defined as those with altered anatomy (e.g., post-surgical changes, lobar collapse, pneumothorax, or pleural effusion) or significant parenchymal disease (e.g., fibrosis or emphysema), which potentially affects the accuracy of downstream measurements of lung function from SPECT.

All prior studies evaluating SPECT/CT lung lobe segmentation and quantification have primarily focused on assessing the relative validity of SPECT/CT-driven measurements against planar or manually derived quantification1,2,4–6, or on the clinical utility of SPECT/CT lobar quantification for guiding surgical or bronchoscopic interventions12,13. However, none have examined the overall failure scenarios of segmentation models and the impact of data complexity on model performance, and consequently, their impact on lobar function quantification in a clinical setting. Another limitation of these studies lies in their small and narrowly scoped datasets, typically restricted to patients with a specific pathology, thereby limiting the robustness of their evaluation14. Only two studies to date have evaluated an AI-based segmentation tool for SPECT/CT lobar quantification. Verrecchia-Ramos et al.6 and Subramanian et al.15 both assessed the AutoLung3D algorithm (Siemens Healthineers) to validate its feasibility for lobar function quantification, but limited their analyses to small single-scanner cohorts.

In our previous work, we extensively benchmarked the lung lobe segmentation performance of several open-source models against a locally trained model, identifying the strengths and weaknesses of each model on our task16. However, how the differences in segmentation accuracy of these tools impact downstream applications, such as measuring lung lobe function in the context of lower resolution (~ 10 mm) SPECT imaging, has not yet been established. This gap may be largely attributed to the lack of well-curated SPECT/CT datasets with accurate lobar annotations. Consequently, the integration of AI-based segmentation tools into nuclear medicine (NM) workflows for lobar quantification has not yet been widely adopted.

In this study, we address these gaps by leveraging a locally curated, highly variable dataset of lung perfusion SPECT and CT studies to examine differences in lung lobe segmentation accuracy across models. We further evaluate whether these segmentation differences lead to statistically and clinically meaningful differences in lobar function quantification. We compared the accuracy and precision of lobar function values derived from several general radiology-trained models, a locally trained ad-hoc AI model, and a semi-automated commercial software tool. These comparisons are stratified across pre-determined task difficulty classes based on image quality, pathology, and missing lobes. In addition, we introduce a novel anatomy-aware softmax modification strategy at inference to correct false-positive lobe predictions in the cases with missing lobes. Finally, we further share our experience integrating our locally developed software into our clinical workflow. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the feasibility and validity of using open-source AI models for lung lobe segmentation and functional quantification in routine clinical practice.

Materials and methods

This work explores the feasibility and accuracy of employing open-source and locally trained AI models for SPECT/CT-based lung lobar quantification, with an emphasis on their clinical utility and integration. The study is based on a curated SPECT/CT dataset with meticulous, expert lobar annotations as the reference ground-truth. A novel inference-level, anatomy-aware softmax modification strategy was developed to address false-positive lobe predictions in cases with missing lobes. The local software was subsequently deployed within the clinical workflow to streamline and standardize SPECT/CT lung lobe quantification.

Study cohort

The database was created with approval from the institutional Research Ethics Board (Protocol ID: 20220303–01 H) for secondary use of clinical data. We retrospectively collected data from patients referred to our department for pre-operative lung function assessment prior to February 2025. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. For patients undergoing this assessment, ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) lung scintigraphy is routinely used to demonstrate the distribution of ventilation and perfusion in both tomographic and planar projections. According to our departmental protocol, each patient undergoes both ventilation and perfusion planar scintigraphy, as well as a perfusion SPECT study. Since ventilation SPECT is not routinely performed at our department, only perfusion SPECT were used in this study.

All patients who were referred for V/Q quantification had established pulmonary disease and were undergoing evaluation for surgery. Consequently, our study cohort was broadly representative of the pulmonary conditions encountered in clinical practice16.

Imaging acquisition

SPECT acquisition

Perfusion scans were performed following intravenous administration of 23–290 MBq of technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin, after which SPECT imaging was acquired on Siemens Symbia T, Symbia ECAM, or Symbia Intevo dual-head gamma cameras using low-energy, high-resolution parallel-hole collimator. Imaging was conducted using a 15% energy window centered at 140 keV, with patients in the feet first supine position. Acquisition parameters included a 128 × 128 matrix using the full field of view (FOV), 180˚ rotation (360˚ coverage with dual heads), 64 views per head, and 8 s per view.

CT acquisition

While some of the NM scans were performed on hybrid SPECT/CT systems, the CT component of the study was acquired only when no recent diagnostic chest CT or whole-body CT from a PET study was available for the patient, in accordance with departmental protocol. This was done to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure and the use of lower-quality hybrid CT, which is limited by lower resolution and breathing artifacts. In this study, our dataset primarily consists of diagnostic chest CT studies obtained within 0–859 days (median: 60 days) of the NM study, unless patients had undergone a procedural or experienced pathological changes between the CT and the SPECT acquisition dates that altered lung anatomy (e.g., lung transplant, wedge resection, lobectomy, pneumonectomy, radiotherapy, severe pneumothorax, or large pleural effusion). In such cases, the treating physician may order a new CT to be performed post-SPECT, or the hybrid CT may be used. Only one case in our dataset included a low-dose CT attenuation correction (CTAC) scan, while all remaining CT scans were acquired using the standard diagnostic clinical protocols.

CT images were acquired on various scanners, using various clinical protocols, with patients in the feet first supine position. Image parameters included a matrix size of 512 × 512, slice thickness ranging from 0.8 to 3 mm, tube voltage of 80–130 kVp, and tube current of 49–1558 mA, with or without intravenous contrast. Details of CT scanners, acquisition protocols and parameters for a subset of cases were reported previously16.

Ground truth annotations and difficulty grading

All CT studies were initially segmented by a trained annotator using Hybrid3D, a clinically validated semi-automated software tool (Hermes Medical Solutions, Stockholm, Sweden)4, and any inaccuracies were manually corrected using 3D Slicer software17. The corrected annotations for the initial subset of the dataset were then used to train deep learning models, which were used iteratively to segment new cases; predicted segmentations were manually refined and incorporated into subsequent training rounds to progressively improve model performance on new cases. All segmentations were reviewed by a medical physicist and a nuclear medicine physician. Complex cases were reviewed with radiologists’ input, and uncertainties were resolved by consensus.

Upon completion of dataset preparation, we applied a three-point segmentation difficulty scale (Easy, Moderate, Hard) to each CT study, to better stratify and assess models’ performance under varying imaging and pulmonary conditions. Scoring was based on expected model accuracy in fissure detection and parenchyma tissue segmentation, with scoring criteria informed by image quality, lung anatomy, radiology reports, and observed segmentation performance by prior AI models16.

Segmentation models

In this study, we evaluated lobar function accuracy derived from segmentations generated by three open-source medical image segmentation tools (TotalSegmentator9, MOOSE10, and Vista3D11), a clinically validated commercial tool (Hybrid3D), and a locally trained model both with and without anatomy-aware probability adjustments. TotalSegmentator and MOOSE were built on the nnU-Net framework, whereas Vista3D employed a SegResNet-based architecture. All public models were trained on large, general radiology datasets comprising both in-house and publicly available sources.

Unlike the AI-based models, the semi-automated clinical software is based on conventional image processing techniques, including thresholding and region growing with manual seeding. Using this method, lung segmentation was performed using user-defined thresholds for the airways and lungs, which was then followed by manual placement of control points along the main fissures. Lobar boundaries were then obtained by interpolating these points across sagittal slices.

To assess the impact of dataset diversity on lung lobe segmentation and quantification with the objective of routine use within our institute, we trained an nnU-Net model on our curated dataset (LocalModel), with manual stratification by segmentation difficulty to optimize training18,19. We used the 3D full-resolution nnU-Net configuration for our training, as it empirically demonstrated the best performance on our dataset. The model was trained using the stochastic gradient descent optimizer with initial learning rate of 10− 2 and a weight decay of 3 × 10− 5. Training was performed for 8000 epochs with 250 iterations per epoch, a batch size of 2, and patch size of (96, 160, 160), with a median image size of (258, 511, 511) in the training dataset. We employed the nnU-Net compound loss function combining cross-entropy and Dice loss to optimize segmentation performance20.

The results reported for all AI-based segmentation models used in this study reflect their complete inference pipelines, including any post-processing steps applied to the model predictions. This also applies to our LocalModel and its anatomy aware variant, where largest connected component analysis was used as a post-processing step. In doing so, the results presented herein represent expected performance of these models without modification.

Anatomy-aware softmax modification

In clinical practice, some cases involve patients who have undergone surgical resection of one or more lobes or where lobes have collapsed, resulting in fewer than the typical five lobes. Standard segmentation models may erroneously predict these non-existing classes, introducing substantial segmentation and quantification errors largely due to rarity of such cases in clinical datasets. To mitigate this, we can leverage prior anatomical knowledge from clinical reports to suppress false-positive lobe predictions.

Training and validation

Of the whole dataset (200 cases), 20% (40 cases; 10 Easy, 10 Moderate, and 20 Hard) were reserved as a test set. Of the remaining cases, 15% (24 cases; 6 Easy, 6 Moderate, and 12 Hard) were used for validation and 85% (136 cases; 35 Easy, 32 Moderate, and 69 Hard) for training20. Only the LocalModel was trained using these data. The open-source models were used as provided by their developers, without further training.

Quantification of lobar function

Segmentation results (from the CT) obtained from each model were automatically co-registered to the corresponding SPECT images using affine transformations (including translation and rotation) to extract lobar counts and lobar function (%), calculated as the fraction of total lung counts contributed by each lobe. An overview of the study workflow is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Evaluation metrics

We computed lobar function error for each model as the difference between the model-predicted lobar function and that of the ground truth segmentation. For segmentation evaluation, we computed the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), the 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (rHd95), and the normalized surface distance (NSD) segmentation metrics21,22 against the ground truth segmentation. Metrics were compared between competing models, and within the same model between difficulty categories.

Computational considerations

Segmentation models were trained and evaluated on a local workstation with an Intel Core i9 3.0 GHz CPU, 128 GB RAM, and an Nvidia GeForce RTX 3090 Ti GPU on Microsoft Windows 10. All models were developed using the PyTorch deep-learning library23. All analysis was performed in Python.

The LocalModel was trained using the standard nnU-Net framework. Detailed training resource recommendations are available in the nnU-Net documentation. During workflow testing, inference on a representative chest CT scan with dimensions of 512 × 512 × 256 required approximately 4 GB RAM and 3 GB GPU VRAM, with an average inference time of 1.6 min.

Statistical analysis

We assessed the normality of lobar function error distributions across lobes and difficulty categories using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Bias in lobar function error was evaluated with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test overall and stratified by lobe, difficulty category, and missing-lobe status. Because lobar function errors within ± 5% were considered clinically insignificant, equivalence testing (two one-sided tests) with a ± 5% margin was applied when statistically significant bias was detected to determine whether it remained clinically negligible24,25. Error variability differences in lobar function error across difficulty categories were examined using Levene’s test, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons when significant. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm–Bonferroni correction method, and adjusted p-values are reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Segmentation performance in terms of DSC, rHd95, and NSD by TotalSegmentator, MOOSE, and LocalModel was previously published16. Briefly, segmentation quality was similar for Easy and Moderate cases for all models, with mean DSC scores above 0.94. The LocalModel performed better with DSC above 0.96. Performance deteriorated for Hard cases with mean DSC of approximately 0.8 for TotalSegmentator and MOOSE, and 0.89 for LocalModel. rHd95 and NSD trends corroborated those of DSC.

Error Bias

Across the 40 test cases (10 Easy, 10 Moderate, and 20 Hard), we found no evidence of systematic bias in median lobe function error when models were assessed overall (p = 1.0) or stratified by difficulty categories (p = 1.0) (Fig. 1a). Small but statistically significant biases were detected for right middle lobe (RML) in all open-source tools (p < 0.02), the right upper lobe (RUL) with TotalSegmentator (p = 0.016), and the right lower lobe (RLL) with Hybrid3D (p = 0.033) (Fig. 1b). However, equivalence testing with a ± 5% margin confirmed that these differences were clinically negligible (both p < 0.001). This indicates that, although minor statistical biases were present, their magnitudes were not clinically meaningful. Nevertheless, no significant biases were detected for any lobe with the LocalModel.

Error variability

Variability analysis across the 40 test cases showed no significant differences in lobar function error variability across difficulty groups for Hybrid3D and LocalModel (p > 0.244) (Fig. 1a). In contrast, error variability significantly increased from Easy to Moderate and Easy to Hard for TotalSegmentator (p < 0.005), MOOSE (p < 0.001), and Vista3D (p < 0.003). Among these, Vista3D exhibited the largest increase in lobar function error spread between Easy and Hard cases (standard deviation (SD) = 0.57 vs. 5.79, respectively), indicating the highest sensitivity to case difficulty, followed by MOOSE (SD = 0.82 vs. 4.98), and TotalSegmentator (SD = 0.63 vs. 4.89).

Missing lobe effect

Of the 40 test cases, 10 were missing-lobe cases: 9 cases with one missing lobe each and 1 case with two missing lobes, all of which were classified as Hard cases. None of the models demonstrated a systematic bias in lobar function error in either groups of with or without missing lobes (p > 0.581) (Fig. 1c). In contrast, error variability was markedly higher in missing-lobe cases for all models (p < 0.001), except for our LocalModel, which showed no significant difference between groups (p = 0.96). Contrary to other models, for Hybrid3D, variability in lobar function error was higher for cases with all lobes present compared to missing-lobe cases, although both variances were small in magnitude (1.25 vs. 0.18, respectively; p < 0.001).

In these scenarios, spatial misallocation of parenchyma to false-positive lobes leads to misassignment of function that should have been attributed to other existing lobes. Among the AI-based models, our LocalModel correctly identified the missing-lobe scenario in 2 of 11 cases, where other models failed in all cases. Furthermore, the LocalModel produced the smallest absolute function errors with regards to false-positive lobes, indicating that although perfect exclusion of missing lobes was challenging, the false-positive lobes were relatively small and were below the clinically significant threshold (5% function error) in 10 of 11 cases. Impact of Segmentation Accuracy on Absolute Lobar Function Error

As expected, across all models, absolute lobar function error was strongly associated with segmentation accuracy metrics (DSC, rHd95, NSD) (p < 0.001): higher DSC and NSD values corresponded to lower errors, whereas higher rHd95 values corresponded to greater error (Fig. 5). It is important to note that this relationship was most evident in cases with high segmentation accuracy, whereas at lower accuracy levels, the association weakened, suggesting that different types of large segmentation errors may affect lobar function quantification in less predictable ways.

Discussion

In our previous work, we had evaluated the segmentation performance of various AI models for lung lobe segmentation; however, we did not assess how the segmentation inaccuracies resulting from these models translate into downstream lung lobar function quantification. In this study, we therefore focused on evaluating the feasibility and limitations of using these AI-based lung lobe segmentation tools—both publicly available and locally trained—for clinical lung lobe quantification and proposed an anatomy-aware strategy for addressing complex cases involving missing lobes. We found that all AI models, regardless of whether they were trained on general radiology or thoracic-specific datasets, performed clinically well and achieved segmentation performance similar to a semi-automated commercial tool (Hybrid3D), producing segmentations that result in clinically reliable lobar function measurements in cases with typical lung anatomy.

However, as we previously demonstrated in16, the performance of open-source models significantly declined in cases with fewer than five lobes, where lung anatomy deviated from the standard configuration. In addition, the open-source models exhibited the largest function error variations with respect to RUL and RML. These limitations likely stemmed from the scarcity of challenging cases involving missing lobes and displaced or obscured right horizontal fissures within their training datasets. In contrast, our locally trained model, which incorporated higher anatomical and pathological variability, achieved the most consistent and accurate lobar function results, with 5–20 times faster inference time than the commercial tool16.

Our secondary objective was to mitigate the consequences of the limited representation of missing-lobe cases in the training data. Because such cases are underrepresented, models are unable to adequately learn these anatomical variations, resulting in poorer segmentation performance and, consequently, larger errors in lobar function measurements. To address this limitation, we proposed to integrate prior anatomical knowledge about the missing lobes during inference. We showed that a simple softmax adjustment—zeroing the probability of anatomically absent lobes— enabled selection of the next most probable class, which in the majority of instances corresponded to the valid class. This straightforward adjustment improved segmentation accuracy and enhanced the reliability of lobar function quantification.

Clinical integration

AI-based image analysis models can be integrated into clinical workflows through PACS using the DICOM standard, where image data can be manually or automatically routed to a processing server executing the AI application and the results such as images or quantitative reports are transmitted back for review. This approach allowed for models to be integrated on premises without a commercial entity and the associated regulatory requirements within the scope of this research project.

At our institution, we have integrated the entire lung lobe segmentation and quantification process into the clinical setting in an end-to-end workflow (Fig. 6). Any user can initiate the process by sending the CT and corresponding SPECT studies via a pre-configured DICOM node to a processing server, which executes our software, performing lobar segmentation and quantification. The resulting segmentations and a final report, including quality control images, are returned to the patient directory within the patient browser application. Users must review and validate the segmentation quality; approved reports are forwarded to PACS, otherwise the segmentations are refined, and the quantification is repeated. To improve performance, we periodically retrain and validate our model (AA_LocalModel) as new cases are added to our data cohort.

Overview of the clinical integration of an automated end-to-end workflow for lung lobe segmentation and functional quantification. Reconstructed CT and SPECT images are retrieved from PACS and transferred to the Medical Image Processing Automation Server (MIPAS) via an input DICOM node. After data structuring, segmentation may be performed using any available AI model (e.g., LocalModel, TotalSegmentator, MOOSE, Vista3D, etc.), with optional specification of missing-lobe class labels. The resulting segmentations are applied to the co-registered SPECT images to compute lobar function, followed by NIfTI-to-DICOM conversion. The outputs, including segmentation maps and quantification reports, are sent back to PACS via an output DICOM node, enabling visualization, quality control, and physician reporting.

It is worth noting that deploying our software in the clinical setting has supported a more efficient and reliable lung lobe quantification. Processing time per case was reduced from 10 to 60 min to about 3 min, while automation eliminated the need for specialized training and minimized inter-operator variability. In our clinical experience, after our clinical integration, cases with typical five-lobe anatomy did not generally require manual correction, while manual corrections were mainly needed in missing-lobe cases. However, introduction of the AA_LocalModel reduced false-positive missing-lobe errors and produced more accurate segmentation and quantification in these complex cases. Furthermore, anecdotally, our software enabled processing of complex cases that were difficult or even impossible to process using the semi-automated Hybrid3D software. For example, in the first 100 cases in our database, Hybrid3D failed entirely to segment the lungs in 8 cases due to severe fibrotic and emphysematous disease.

Despite the lower performance of open-source models compared to our LocalModel, they demonstrated a clinically acceptable accuracy for the majority of cases encountered in routine practice. Using the same integration pipeline, all these models can be readily deployed in any clinical setting.

The commercial segmentation and quantification tool

As shown in Fig. 1c, unlike the other models, Hybrid3D showed significantly higher functional error spread in the All-Lobes-Present group compared to Missing-Lobe cases. This is due to two factors: first, Hybrid3D is a semi-manual algorithm where users manually delineate the fissures. Therefore, the missing lobes are accounted for, which prevents function misallocation. Second, its reliance on thresholding and region-growing methods can hinder inclusion of diseased parenchyma, particularly in severe pathology cases, which are mostly represented in the All-Lobes-Present group, leading to greater variability in functional error.

Under severe pathology, Hybrid3D may either fail to segment the lungs entirely or omit the diseased, high-density regions. In the latter scenario, the incomplete segmentations can still be used for lobar quantification; in the former, segmentation failure prevents quantification. In our dataset, eight cases could not be segmented by Hybrid3D, but these were subsequently processed with our in-house AI models, which successfully generated accurate segmentations.

As noted, when dense parenchymal regions (e.g., consolidations, fibrotic or emphysematous tissue, and extra-pulmonary regions) were excluded, we proceeded with quantification using the incomplete segmentations. This approach was justified by the strong correlation between diseased tissues and reduced function, meaning that these regions were typically photopenic and thus contributed little to no measurable activity. Consequently, their exclusion did not meaningfully affect lobar function quantification (RLL mass and fibrotic tissue examples in Fig. 7). Moreover, the relative insensitivity of lobar function quantification to minor segmentation errors can further be attributed to the limited spatial resolution of SPECT. Therefore, only gross errors such as large false-positive class predictions (RUL lobectomy example in Fig. 7) or major fissure misdelineations are likely to yield clinically unreliable quantification results. This distinction is crucial when interpreting the accuracy of lobar segmentation outputs from conventional or AI-based tools in the context of Nuclear Medicine, as it determines their potential to be integrated in the clinical setting.

Figure 7 demonstrated the association between segmentation inaccuracies and lobar function across four representative clinical scenarios for all evaluated models. In the RLL mass case, only TotalSegmentator and the LocalModel correctly included the mass within the RLL segmentation; however, its low uptake made this inclusion clinically irrelevant. Similarly, undersegmentation by MOOSE and Vista3D in the fibrotic tissue case excluded largely nonfunctional regions. In contrast, right horizontal fissure misdelineation by MOOSE led to RUL function overestimation due to misallocated activity. The RUL lobectomy case highlights gross false-positive predictions by all open-source models, reflecting their overfitting to normal anatomy and yielding implausible function estimates.

Finally, in the RUL collapse case, even large segmentation errors had minimal impact on function when occurring outside the parenchyma, as seen with TotalSegmentator; false-positives from MOOSE and Vista3D similarly showed negligible effect.

Our findings demonstrated that while segmentation accuracy varies significantly across different models, these differences, only in limited instances, translate into clinically significant discrepancies (greater than ± 5%) in lobar function measurements. This means that, in most typical clinical cases, variation in segmentation accuracy has negligible impact on functional quantification. Among open-source AI models, a meaningful decline in performance is primarily seen in lobectomy cases, where lung anatomy is altered, or in severe fibrotic changes involving displaced fissures. This change in performance reflects the absence of such cases in their training data. Therefore, incorporating these challenging representations, as done in our LocalModel, mitigates this limitation to a great extent.

Anatomy-aware softmax modification

By introducing the inference-time correction for missing lung lobes, we addressed these clinically significant cases. We leveraged a simple yet computationally efficient method that could be readily employed in a clinical setting where prior anatomical information about the patients is available. To that end, our proposed anatomy-aware softmax correction strategy effectively prevents false-positive class predictions by our LocalModel, hence improving the accuracy of lobar function measurements. The same concept may be applied to any segmentation model, including the open-source models herein. This approach, however, requires software engineering consideration of how to practically provide the missing lobe information (or lobe inventory) to the model at time of inference.

The use of the softmax correction proved to be successful (Figs. 2 and 3) despite the scarcity of missing lung lobe cases on which to train a predictive model. While missing lung lobe cases in our dataset were not all that rare (10 of 40 tests cases), each missing lobe was only represented by no more than 3 cases (missing RUL) in our training set. Only a single case had two missing lobes (RLL and LUL). Perhaps with training sets with more missing lobe examples, a model may learn to better account for missing lobes, but the anatomy-aware remains a simple correction to improve on inaccurate model predictions.

Limitations

This was a single-center study, and these results need to be verified on larger external datasets including low-dose CTAC images. The primary analysis in this study was based on diagnostic quality CT data (acquired at separate imaging session from SPECT), reflecting a clinical practice, where the additional radiation of CTAC is avoided in the presence of a recent diagnostic CT (which is of higher quality). Nevertheless, the use of hybrid-SPECT/CT is common in many practices, which begs the question, how our methodology generalizes to CTAC images for anatomical segmentation of the lungs and lobes. We thus included an additional exploratory evaluation on an external set of low-dose CTAC images (n = 20) in the Supplementary Material. These results indicate similar performance trends in CTAC compared to our previously reported metrics in diagnostic-CT16, across difficulty classes (Supplementary Table S1) and in cases of missing lobes (Supplementary Table S2). Likewise, quantification errors were similar in magnitude to those reported herein – perhaps marginally higher in Moderate cases. In the missing lobe cases, the anatomy aware modification did not improve performance as well as seen on diagnostic CT. This preliminary analysis highlights the need for further evaluation of generalizability to CTAC images and of the potential benefit of training the LM on such data. It is perhaps worth noting that the CTAC images in the supplemental are not only lower quality CT, but also consist of frequent respiratory motion artifacts.

A second limitation is the absence of an established clinical standard for what may be considered as negligible function error. The ± 5% threshold used to define clinically negligible lobar function error was selected a priori based on clinical experience and as a conservative margin that is smaller than typical inter-method and inter-operator differences reported for SPECT/CT lobar quantification measurements4,12.

Another limitation of our work is the relatively limited effective sample size. Although the test set included 40 cases with approximately 200 lobe-level measurements per model, lobe-level measurements from the same patient are not fully independent. This limitation becomes more relevant after stratifying the cohort by difficulty category or missing-lobe status. Therefore, the subgroup statistical analyses should be interpreted with caution and viewed as preliminary findings. Larger external datasets are needed to confirm these findings.

It is important to note that, our LocalModel was trained on a data representative of our clinical task, whereas the open-source models were evaluated in their publicly available configurations. Therefore, observed performance differences should not be interpreted as evidence of methodology superiority of the LocalModel. Furthermore, demonstrating a superior performance was not the objective of our study. Nevertheless, fine-tuning open-source models on the same local training data would help separate the effect of task-specific training data from model design and may be explored in future work.

Conclusion

We developed and clinically deployed a locally trained AI model for lung lobe segmentation and quantification on SPECT/CT. Our locally trained model outperformed several open-source segmentation models on complex clinical cases, likely due to the use of representative cases. Nevertheless, for all models, derived lobar function estimates were mostly within clinically acceptable limits. With the introduction of a novel anatomy-aware softmax modification to the prediction, we improved robustness to cases with missing lobes. These findings suggest that AI-based lung lobe segmentation models are mature enough for clinical adoption to streamline lung lobe quantification, so long as physician oversight remains. Manual corrections or processing may be applied in the few cases where automated segmentation is deemed unacceptable.
