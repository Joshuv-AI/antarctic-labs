Bizarre Parosteal Osteochondromatous Proliferation Arising From the Second Rib: A Case Report
Authors: Yuji Nojima, Hiroki Sugiyama, Takashi Matsutani, Ai Maeda, Shinsuke Saisho, Katsuhiko Shimizu, Masao Nakata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP) is a rare benign lesion, first described by Nora et al. in 1983, that typically affects the small bones of the hands and feet. We report a rare case of BPOP arising from the left second rib in a 41‐year‐old man, which was incidentally detected as a “pulmonary mass” on the chest X‐ray during a routine health checkup. CT imaging revealed a lesion measuring 4.1 × 2.2 cm in size in the second left rib, and we performed surgical resection for both diagnosis and treatment. Postoperative histopathological examination revealed the diagnosis of BPOP. This case underscores the importance of including BPOP in the differential diagnosis of rib lesions, especially when the imaging findings suggest a benign osteocartilaginous lesion. Given the potential of the lesion for recurrence, long‐term follow‐up of persons with BPOP is recommended.Keywords: bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation, differential diagnosis, Nora's lesion, rib, surgical excision

Introduction

Bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP) is a rare proliferative bone lesion that was first described by Nora et al. in 1983 [1]. It typically arises from the small bones of the extremities, and definitive diagnosis often requires surgical excision. BPOP is also known to show a high risk of local recurrence in cases where the lesion is not adequately excised. Herein, we report a rare case of BPOP arising from a rib.

Case History/Examination

A 41‐year‐old man presented to his local doctor with a “pulmonary mass” that had been incidentally detected on the chest X‐ray performed during a health checkup. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a tumor involving the left second rib, and the patient was referred to our institution for further examination and treatment. Past medical history of the patient included alcoholic liver disease and dyslipidemia; and there was no history of trauma.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The chest X‐ray revealed a mass lesion measuring 4 cm in diameter in the peripheral region of the left upper lung field (Figure 1). Chest CT revealed a mass measuring 4.1 × 2.2 cm in size arising from the cortical bone of the left second rib. The lesion showed an attenuation value similar to that of bone, with no evidence of cortical destruction or invasion of the surrounding tissues (Figure 2A,B). Chest magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a well‐defined mass abutting the left second rib that was visualized as a low signal intensity on T1‐weighted imaging and as a high signal intensity on diffusion‐weighted imaging (Figure 3A,B). Positron emission tomography (PET) demonstrated fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the lesion, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) in the range of 4.4–5.5 (Figure 4). There was no FDG accumulation suggestive of lymph node or distant metastasis. Based on these imaging findings, the lesion was considered as being confined to the rib, and we planned surgery for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Surgery was performed through a 6‐cm skin incision made directly over the lesion. The lesion was found to be in contact with the left second rib, with no evidence of invasion of the surrounding tissues. The pectoralis major and minor muscles were preserved. Therefore, we resected the lesion with only a segment of the left second rib underlying the lesion, leaving a 1 cm margin from the lesion, along with partial resection of the first and second intercostal muscles. Even though an approximately 6‐cm segment of the second rib was resected, chest wall reconstruction was not needed.

Tissue Diagnosis

Gross examination of the resected surgical specimen revealed the lesion protruding from the second rib (Figure 5A), and histopathological examination revealed the lesion as being composed of abundant fibrous connective tissue with a spongy bone‐like architecture (Figure 5B). In addition, trabeculae lined by osteoblasts and regions of lamellar bone formation were also identified (Figure 5C). Immunohistochemistry showed negative staining for MDM2 and a low proliferative activity (low Ki‐67 value), leading us to rule out malignancy. Based on these findings, the tumor was diagnosed as BPOP.

Discussion

BPOP, also referred to as Nora's lesion, is a rarely encountered benign bone lesion. Edoardo et al. reviewed 322 cases of BPOP diagnosed between 1983 and 2021 [2], and none of these cases showed the lesion arising from a rib. Furthermore, Table 1 summarizes the case reports of adult BPOP published over the past decade that we reviewed. None of the reports described the lesion as arising from a rib, indicating that the present case of BPOP arising from a rib is extremely rare. BPOP commonly occurs in the metacarpal, metatarsal, and phalangeal bones in young to middle‐aged adults [3]. Although the etiology remains unknown, a possible association with prior trauma has been suggested [5]. In the present case, the patient had no history of trauma, and the chest CT revealed no evidence of rib fractures, indicating that a traumatic etiology was unlikely in this case. Radiologically, BPOP typically presents as a well‐demarcated mass with heterotopic mineralization arising directly from the cortical surface of the parent bone that may show infiltration into the adjacent soft tissues [10]. While increased uptake on bone scintigraphy has been reported [8], there are very few reports describing the PET/CT findings of BPOP. In the present case, PET/CT demonstrated moderate FDG uptake with an SUV max of 4.4 to 5.5, which is likely attributable to the proliferative nature of BPOP. In regard to treatment, surgical excision is the first‐line treatment in persons with suspected BPOP, serving both as a therapeutic intervention and as a means for establishing a definitive histopathological diagnosis [10]. However, a remarkably high rate of local recurrence—in the range of 23% to 55%—after resection has been reported [4, 10]. To reduce the risk of local recurrence, it has been suggested that en‐bloc excision of the lesion along with decortication of the underlying bone be performed. In the present case, because the lesion originated from a rib rather than a bone of the hands/ft and complete resection including the underlying bone was feasible, we speculate that the risk of recurrence may be low. In regard to the protocol for follow‐up, Heidi et al., who analyzed 10 cases of BPOP over a mean follow‐up period of 7 years, recommended postoperative imaging every 6 months during the first year and annual surveillance from the second year onwards [11]. Accordingly, long‐term follow‐up is also proposed in the present case.

Conclusion

We report an extremely rare case of BPOP originating from a rib, a site of origin of the lesion that has not previously been reported in the literature. This case highlights the importance of including BPOP in the differential diagnosis of rib lesions, particularly when the radiologic findings suggest a benign osteocartilaginous tumor. We performed complete surgical resection of the lesion with the underlying bone segment, which we speculate could reduce the likelihood of local recurrence. Nevertheless, we propose to conduct long‐term follow‐up of the patients due to the known potential for recurrence of BPOP.
