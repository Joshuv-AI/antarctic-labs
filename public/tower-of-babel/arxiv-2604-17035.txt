Fundamental temperature in the superstatistical description of non-equilibrium steady states Journal: Physica A
Sergio Davis

Abstract
Among the statistical mechanical frameworks able to describe systems in non-equilibrium steady states such as collisionless plasmas, self-gravitating systems and other complex systems, superstatistics have gained recent attention. Superstatistics postulates a superposition of canonical systems with inverse temperatures β \beta described by a probability distribution depending on the external conditions. Unfortunately, the uncertainty about β \beta cannot be attributed to fluctuations of a phase space function, and this suggests that the distribution of β \beta is purely of statistical nature and must be inferred rather than measured. This lack of direct observability of the superstatistical temperature then becomes a conceptual issue in need of resolution. In this work we address this issue, showing that a mapping exists between functions of the superstatistical temperature and functions of the recently proposed fundamental temperature, a model-dependent function of the energy, in such a way that their expectation values coincide. We illustrate the use of this mapping by computing the conditional distribution of inverse temperature given energy for the q q -canonical ensemble, as well as the full inverse temperature distribution, without the use of Laplace inversion.



1
Introduction

The appropriate extension of the concept of temperature to non-equilibrium states remains an open problem in statistical mechanics. One rather elegant path towards solving this problem involves the theory of
superstatistics
[
1
,
2
]
, which has been recently successful in describing plasmas
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
, self-gravitating systems
[
17
]
and other complex systems out
of equilibrium
[
11
,
19
]
. In superstatistics, systems in non-equilibrium steady states are described as superpositions of equilibrium states at different temperatures, thus temperature in these systems
is treated as a random variable with its own probability density. As is well known, a system in equilibrium at temperature
T
is described by the canonical ensemble,

P(\bm{\Gamma}|\beta)=\frac{\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})\big)}{Z(\beta)},

(1)

which is the probability (density) of observing a microstate
\bm{\Gamma}
at inverse temperature
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=1/(k_{B}T)
. Here
\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})
is the Hamiltonian of the system and
Z(\beta)
is the partition function.
In superstatistics, the canonical ensemble in (
1
) is replaced by the joint distribution of microstates
\bm{\Gamma}
and inverse temperature
\beta
, which we will write as

P(\bm{\Gamma},\beta|\bm{\lambda})=P(\bm{\Gamma}|\beta)P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})=\frac{\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})\big)}{Z(\beta)}P(\beta|\bm{\lambda}),

(2)

under external conditions that will be represented by the set of parameters
\bm{\lambda}
. This joint distribution is given simply by the product rule of probability theory
[
23
]
, where
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
is
the superstatistical distribution of inverse temperature
\beta
. By integrating out the variable
\beta
, the superstatistical microstate distribution is given by

P(\bm{\Gamma}|\bm{\lambda})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,\frac{P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})}{Z(\beta)}\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})\big).

(3)

In this way, each choice of
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
leads to a microstate distribution different from (
1
). Superstatistical states then belong to the wider class of
energy steady states
(ESS), that is,
non-equilibrium steady states where the microstate probability (density) is completely determined by the microstate energy. In other words, an ESS has a microstate distribution of the form

P(\bm{\Gamma}|\bm{\lambda})=\rho\big(\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma});\bm{\lambda}\big),

(4)

where
\rho
is called the
ensemble function
. By defining the
superstatistical weight function

f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})
as

f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})}{Z(\beta)},

(5)

we directly see from (
3
) that

\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta E),

(6)

i.e. the superstatistical ensemble function
\rho
is the Laplace transform of
f
.

Despite the success of the theory of superstatistics, there are at least two difficulties in this approach. The first issue is that not every ESS can be described using superstatistics, that is, not every
non-negative function
\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})
can be expressed as the Laplace transform of a non-negative function
f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})
. Notable exceptions to superstatistics include the Gaussian ensemble
[
4
,
5
,
12
,
20
]
, the
q
-canonical ensemble of Tsallis statistics
[
21
,
22
]
with
q<1
and the microcanonical ensemble.

The second is perhaps a more profound issue. It has been shown
[
7
]
that in superstatistical states, the inverse temperature
\beta
cannot be directly measured as the value of a phase-space observable

B(\bm{\Gamma})
. This implies that there is no function
B(\bm{\Gamma})
such that

\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\big<G(B)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}

(7)

for every function
G
. In particular, this implies that
P(\beta|\bm{\lambda})
cannot be estimated by computing a histogram of a function
B
, and instead has to be inferred indirectly. In contrast, the energy
E
can
always be measured directly using the Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}(\bm{\Gamma})
, so for any function
G(E)
the equality

\big<G(E)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\big<G(\mathcal{H})\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}

(8)

always holds.

In this work we provide a powerful result that compensates the lack of direct observability of
\beta
. We prove here that, for every superstatistical ESS and function
G(\beta)
, there exists a function

G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta_{F})
such that

\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\big<G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta_{F})\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}

(9)

holds, where
\beta_{F}
is the
fundamental inverse temperature function

[
8
,
10
]
, defined for any ESS by

\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\ln\rho(E;\bm{\lambda}),

(10)

and
G_{\bm{\lambda}}
is a linearly-transformed version of
G
.

2
Properties of the fundamental temperature in superstatistics

The main connection point between the fundamental inverse temperature
\beta_{F}
in (
10
) and the inverse temperature
\beta
of the superstatistical formalism is the identity

\big<\beta\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})\beta=\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda}),

(11)

where the conditional distribution of
\beta
given
E
is

P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})=\frac{f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta E)}{\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})}.

(12)

The joint distribution of energy
E
and inverse temperature
\beta
is obtained from (
2
) as

P(E,\beta|\bm{\lambda})=f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta E)\Omega(E),

(13)

and from it, the conditional distribution of
\beta
given
E
is

P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})=\frac{P(E,\beta|\bm{\lambda})}{\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(E,\beta|\bm{\lambda})}=\frac{f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta E)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta E)}

(14)

which is (
12
). Its moments are given by

\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})\beta^{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})}\frac{\partial^{n}\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})}{\partial E^{n}}

(15)

for all integers
n\geq 0
and all allowed energies
E
, and, as was shown in Ref.
[
6
]
.

Figure 1:
Three examples of functions
\beta_{F}
. In the case (a), the function is strictly decreasing, therefore
{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}<0
and, furthermore,
\beta_{F}
is invertible. The case (b) has an interval where
{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}=0
,
so
\beta_{F}
is decreasing but not strictly, and therefore not invertible. In both cases (a) and (b), the value of
\beta_{F}
determines the value of its derivative, in agreement with (
19
). Finally, in case (c) the
derivative
{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}
does change sign, breaking the requirement in (
18
) and thus is not compatible with superstatistics.

From (
15
) with
n=2
we obtain the second moment of
\beta
given
E
as

\big<\beta^{2}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})^{2}-{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}(E;\bm{\lambda}),

(16)

and combining (
11
) and (
16
) we can write the variance of (
12
) as

\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=-{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}(E;\bm{\lambda}).

(17)

Now, because the left-hand side of (
17
) is a variance, it is non-negative by definition, so we must have

{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}(E;\bm{\lambda})\leq 0

(18)

for all allowed energies
E
. This means that
\beta_{F}
must be a decreasing function of
E
, and this condition implies that we can always construct a function
A(\beta_{F})
such that

{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}=A(\beta_{F}),

(19)

that is, the value of
\beta_{F}
by itself fixes the value of the derivative
{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}
, without making reference to the energy
E
. Please note that the condition (
19
) can be fulfilled even for functions

\beta_{F}
that are not invertible, thus (
19
) is true even if there are intervals of energy where
\beta_{F}
is constant. These cases are depicted in Fig.
1
.

The ordinary differential equation (ODE) in (
19
) belongs to the class of
autonomous
ODEs. Therefore we can understand superstatistical models as solutions of autonomous ODEs. From (
19
)
we can show by induction that the higher-order derivatives of
\beta_{F}
also depend only on
\beta_{F}
, that is, there is a sequence of functions
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\ldots
such that

{\beta_{F}}^{(n)}=A_{n}(\beta_{F})

(20)

for all integer
n\geq 1
. Clearly (
20
) for
n=1
reduces to (
19
) with
A_{1}=A
, so it is true. Assuming (
20
) for
n
we easily verify that (
20
) for

n+1
follows, as

{\beta_{F}}^{(n+1)}=\frac{\partial{\beta_{F}}^{(n)}}{\partial E}=\frac{\partial A_{n}(\beta_{F})}{\partial E}=A_{n}^{\prime}(\beta_{F}){\beta_{F}}^{\prime}=A_{n}^{\prime}(\beta_{F})A(\beta_{F})=A_{n+1}(\beta_{F}),

(21)

where we have identified

A_{n+1}(\beta_{F})=A_{n}^{\prime}(\beta_{F})A(\beta_{F}).

(22)

3
A new perspective on the observability of the inverse temperature

Now we can proceed with the central result of this work, namely, the proof that for any superstatistical model, the conditional distribution
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
in (
12
) only depends on
E
through the
function
\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})
, even when this function is not in general invertible. In order to show why this is true, let us first consider the moment-generating function (MGF) of
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
, defined by

R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<\exp(-\beta t)\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^{n}}{n!}\,\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}},

(23)

which is such that

\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=(-1)^{n}\left[\frac{\partial^{n}R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)}{\partial t^{n}}\right]_{t=0}.

(24)

From its definition, the function
R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)
is also equal to the Laplace transform of
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta t)=R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E),

(25)

and, replacing (
15
) into the right-hand side of (
23
), it can be written as the
ratio function

R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)=\frac{1}{\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})}{\partial E^{n}}=\frac{\rho(E+t;\bm{\lambda})}{\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})}.

(26)

Taking logarithm of the rightmost-hand side of (
26
) and expanding it in a Taylor series, we have

\ln\rho(E+t;\bm{\lambda})-\ln\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial E^{n}}\ln\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}{\beta_{F}}^{(n-1)}(E;\bm{\lambda})=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}A_{n-1}\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big),

(27)

which means

R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}A_{n-1}\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big)\right),

(28)

thus
R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)
depends on
E
only through the function
\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})
. From here on, for simplicity we will let
\beta^{\prime}
denote one of the allowed values of
\beta_{F}
, and using this notation
we can replace
R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)
by a new function
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\lambda}}(t,\beta^{\prime})
given by

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;\beta^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}A_{n-1}(\beta^{\prime})\right),

(29)

such that

R_{\bm{\lambda}}(t;E)=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\lambda}}\big(t;\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big).

(30)

Replacing (
30
) into (
25
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})\exp(-\beta t)=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\lambda}}\big(t;\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big)

(31)

and this in turn implies that
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
can only depend on
E
through
\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})
. Therefore we can write

P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})=\mathcal{P}\big(\beta;\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda}),\bm{\lambda}\big)

(32)

with
\mathcal{P}
a function whose role we will clarify shortly. In fact, the joint distribution of
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
can be computed as

\begin{split}P(\beta,\beta^{\prime}|\bm{\lambda})&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,P(\beta,E|\bm{\lambda})\delta\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})-\beta^{\prime}\big)\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,P(E|\bm{\lambda})P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})\delta\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})-\beta^{\prime}\big)\\
&=\mathcal{P}(\beta;\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,P(E|\bm{\lambda})\delta\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})-\beta^{\prime}\big)\\
&=\mathcal{P}(\beta;\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})P(\beta^{\prime}|\bm{\lambda})\end{split}

(33)

and it follows that the conditional distribution of
\beta
given
\beta^{\prime}
is

P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})=\frac{P(\beta,\beta^{\prime}|\bm{\lambda})}{P(\beta^{\prime}|\bm{\lambda})}=\mathcal{P}(\beta;\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda}).

(34)

Therefore, without ambiguity, we can encode the fact that
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
depends on
E
only through
\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})
into the shortcut notation

P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})=P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})

(35)

provided we interpret
\beta^{\prime}
as the value of
\beta_{F}
corresponding to the energy
E
. This is an important result for superstatistics, as it implies that the only information about the energy that is relevant to

\beta
is the value of
\beta_{F}
. From (
35
) we can derive our main result.

Theorem
.

Let
G
be any function of the superstatistical inverse temperature
\beta
such that its expectation value is well defined. Then it is possible to define a transformed function
G_{\bm{\lambda}}
of the fundamental inverse
temperature
\beta_{F}
such that

\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\big<G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta_{F})\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}

(36)

holds. Furthermore, the transformed function
G_{\bm{\lambda}}
is defined by

G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,G(\beta)\,P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda}),

(37)

i.e., it is the conditional expectation of
G(\beta)
given a fixed value of
\beta_{F}
.

Proof.

We compute the expectation of
G
given
E
and
\bm{\lambda}
as

\big<G(\beta)\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,G(\beta)P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,G(\beta)\Big[P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})\Big]_{\beta^{\prime}=\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})}=\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,G(\beta)\,P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})\right]_{\beta^{\prime}=\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})}

(38)

where we have used (
35
) to replace
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
by
P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},\bm{\lambda})
. Using the definition of
G_{\bm{\lambda}}
in (
37
), we have

\big<G(\beta)\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=G_{\bm{\lambda}}\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big),

(39)

and by taking expectation of
\big<G(\beta)\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}
given
\bm{\lambda}
and using (
39
), we have

\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-7.0ptdE\,P(E|\bm{\lambda})\big<G(\beta)\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-7.0ptdE\,P(E|\bm{\lambda})G_{\bm{\lambda}}\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big).

(40)

Finally, introducing a factor of 1 as

1=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta^{\prime}\,\delta\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})-\beta^{\prime}\big)

(41)

in the rightmost-hand side of (
40
) and changing the order of integration, we obtain

\begin{split}\big<G(\beta)\big>_{\bm{\lambda}}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-7.0ptdE\,P(E|\bm{\lambda})\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta^{\prime}\,\delta\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})-\beta^{\prime}\big)\right]G_{\bm{\lambda}}\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})\big)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-7.0ptd\beta^{\prime}\,G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-7.0ptdE\,P(E|\bm{\lambda})\delta\big(\beta_{F}(E;\bm{\lambda})-\beta^{\prime}\big)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta^{\prime}\,G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta^{\prime})P(\beta^{\prime}|\bm{\lambda})=\big<G_{\bm{\lambda}}(\beta_{F})\big>_{\bm{\lambda}},\end{split}

(42)

which is (
36
).
∎

4
Conditional distribution of inverse temperature in the
q
-canonical ensemble

The
q
-canonical ensemble is a generalization of (
1
) commonly used in the context of Tsallis’ non-extensive statistical mechanics
[
22
]
, where the parameter
q
is known as the
entropic index. Within the superstatistical framework,
q\geq 1
is required. The ensemble function for the
q
-canonical ensemble in that case is

\rho(E;q,\beta_{0})=\frac{1}{Z_{q}(\beta_{0})}\Big[1+(q-1)\beta_{0}E\Big]^{\frac{1}{1-q}}

(43)

with
\beta_{0}>0
. The corresponding fundamental inverse temperature function is

\beta_{F}(E;q,\beta_{0})=\frac{\beta_{0}}{1+(q-1)\beta_{0}E},

(44)

so that
0\leq\beta_{F}\leq\beta_{0}
, therefore the parameter
\beta_{0}
can be understood as the upper bound for the fundamental inverse temperature. The derivative of
\beta_{F}
is given by

{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}(E;q,\beta_{0})=(1-q)\beta_{F}(E;q,\beta_{0})^{2},

(45)

and we see that the function
A(\beta)
in (
19
) must be

A(\beta)=(1-q)\beta^{2}.

(46)

As an application of the formalism developed in the previous section, we will compute the conditional distribution
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
for the
q
-canonical ensemble without using the Laplace inversion of
\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})

to obtain
f(\beta;\bm{\lambda})
. In order to do this, we will first use (
24
) to compute the conditional moments of
\beta
given
\beta^{\prime}
, as

\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{\beta^{\prime},q,\beta_{0}}=(-1)^{n}\left[\frac{\partial^{n}\mathcal{R}_{q}(t;\beta^{\prime})}{\partial t^{n}}\right]_{t=0}.

(47)

Using (
43
) we can compute the ratio function
R_{q,\beta_{0}}(t;E)
in (
26
) as

R_{q,\beta_{0}}(t;E)=\left[\frac{1+(q-1)\beta_{0}\big(E+t\big)}{1+(q-1)\beta_{0}E}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-q}}=\left[1+\frac{1+(q-1)\beta_{0}t}{1+(q-1)\beta_{0}E}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-q}},

(48)

which can be written as a function of
\beta_{F}
as

\mathcal{R}_{q}(t;\beta^{\prime})=\Big[1+\beta^{\prime}(q-1)t\Big]^{\frac{1}{1-q}}=\exp_{q}\big(-\beta^{\prime}\,t\big)

(49)

where
\exp_{q}
is the
q
-exponential function
[
13
]
,

\exp_{q}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big[1+(1-q)x\big]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-q}}.

(50)

Here we immediately note that, in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
, the explicit dependence on
\beta_{0}
disappears. Interestingly, by taking expectation of (
31
) given
(q,\beta_{0})
and replacing (
49
)
it follows that

\Big<\exp(-\beta t)\Big>_{q,\beta_{0}}=\Big<\exp_{q}\big(-\beta_{F}t\big)\Big>_{q,\beta_{0}}.

(51)

By replacing (
49
) into (
47
) and using the
n
-th derivative of the
q
-exponential function

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}\exp_{q}(x)=\exp_{q}(x)^{1-n(1-q)}\,(q-1)^{n}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)},

(52)

we obtain

\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{\beta^{\prime},q}=(q-1)^{n}\frac{\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\,(\beta^{\prime})^{n}

(53)

Here we note that the
q
-canonical ensemble is the only superstatistical model where

\big<\beta^{n}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=f_{n}(\bm{\lambda})\,(\beta^{\prime})^{n}

(54)

for all integers
n\geq 1
. In fact, From (
54
) with
n=2
we have that

\big<\beta^{2}\big>_{E,\bm{\lambda}}=f_{2}(\bm{\lambda})(\beta^{\prime})^{2},

(55)

and, replacing it into (
16
),

{\beta_{F}}^{\prime}=\big[1-f_{2}(\bm{\lambda})\big](\beta^{\prime})^{2},

(56)

which is (
46
), whose unique solution is (
44
), i.e. the
q
-canonical ensemble.

Now we will use the conditional moments in (
53
) to recover
P(\beta|E,\bm{\lambda})
without using the inversion of a Laplace transform. For this we consider a function
G(\beta)
with power series

G(\beta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\beta^{n},

(57)

whose transformed function
G_{q}
is given by

G_{q}(\beta^{\prime})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}(q-1)^{n}\frac{\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\,(\beta^{\prime})^{n}.

(58)

We can then rewrite (
58
) in terms of the function
G
by using the integral form of the gamma function, as

\begin{split}G_{q}(\beta^{\prime})&=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\,\Gamma\Big(n+\frac{1}{q-1}\Big)\big[\beta^{\prime}(q-1)\big]^{n}=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,s^{n+\frac{1}{q-1}-1}\exp(-s)\right]\big[\beta^{\prime}(q-1)\big]^{n}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{s^{\frac{1}{q-1}-1}\exp(-s)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\big[\beta^{\prime}(q-1)s\big]^{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{s^{\frac{1}{q-1}-1}\exp(-s)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}G\big(\beta^{\prime}(q-1)s\big),\end{split}

(59)

and performing the change of variables from
s
to
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta^{\prime}(q-1)s
we can write (
59
) as

G_{q}(\beta^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-7.0ptd\beta\left[\frac{1}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}-1}\hskip-12.0pt\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)}\right)\right]G(\beta).

(60)

It follows by comparison with (
37
) that the function in brackets must be the conditional distribution
P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},q,\beta_{0})
. That is, we have

P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},q)=\frac{1}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}-1},

(61)

result which is verified in
A
using the Laplace inversion of (
6
). This is a gamma distribution with mean and variance

\displaystyle\big<\beta\big>_{\beta^{\prime},q,\beta_{0}}

\displaystyle=\beta^{\prime},

(62a)

\displaystyle\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{\beta^{\prime},q,\beta_{0}}

\displaystyle=(q-1)(\beta^{\prime})^{2},

(62b)

in agreement with (
11
) and (
17
), respectively.

5
Inverse temperature distribution for the
q
-canonical ensemble

In this section we will use the distribution of
\beta
given
\beta^{\prime}
obtained in (
61
) to compute
P(\beta|q,\beta_{0})
in the case of a system with constant heat capacity
C_{E}=\alpha k_{B}
.
The density of states for this kind of system is given by

\Omega(E)=\omega_{0}\,E^{\alpha}

(63)

and the energy distribution
P(E|q,\beta_{0})
can be computed by replacing (
43
) and (
63
) into

P(E|\bm{\lambda})=\rho(E;\bm{\lambda})\Omega(E).

(64)

The result can be written as

P(\varepsilon|q,\beta_{0})=\frac{\big(1+\varepsilon\big)^{\frac{1}{1-q}}\,\varepsilon^{\alpha}}{B\left(\alpha+1,\frac{1}{q-1}-\alpha-1\right)},

(65)

which is an inverted beta distribution for the reduced variable
\varepsilon\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta_{0}(q-1)E
. Now invoking the marginalization rule over the variable
\beta^{\prime}
we have

P(\beta|q,\beta_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta^{\prime}\,P(\beta,\beta^{\prime}|q,\beta_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta^{\prime}\,P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},q)P(\beta^{\prime}|q,\beta_{0}),

(66)

and we can obtain the distribution
P(\beta^{\prime}|q,\beta_{0})
from
P(\varepsilon|q,\beta_{0})
in (
65
) as

P(\beta^{\prime}|q,\beta_{0})=\left<\delta\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{1+\varepsilon}-\beta^{\prime}\right)\right>_{q,\beta_{0}}=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\Big<(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\,\delta\big(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{F}(\beta^{\prime})\big)\Big>_{q,\beta_{0}}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{(\beta^{\prime})^{2}}P\big(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{F}(\beta^{\prime})\big|q,\beta_{0}\big)

(67)

where
\varepsilon_{F}
is given by

\varepsilon_{F}(\beta^{\prime})=\frac{\beta_{0}}{\beta^{\prime}}-1.

(68)

Replacing (
65
) and (
68
) into (
67
) then yields

P(\beta^{\prime}|q,\beta_{0})=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}B\left(\alpha+1,\frac{1}{q-1}-\alpha-1\right)}\,\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\beta^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-q}+2}\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\beta^{\prime}}-1\right)^{\alpha},\qquad 0\leq\beta^{\prime}\leq\beta_{0},

(69)

which means, using (
66
), that

P(\beta|q,\beta_{0})=\frac{\big[\beta_{0}(q-1)\big]^{1-\frac{1}{q-1}}\,\beta^{\frac{1}{q-1}-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}-\alpha-1\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-10.0pt\frac{d\beta^{\prime}}{(q-1)(\beta^{\prime})^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}(q-1)}\right)\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\beta^{\prime}}-1\right)^{\alpha},

(70)

then we finally obtain

P(\beta|q,\beta_{0})=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(q-1)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}-\alpha-1\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\beta_{0}(q-1)}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta_{0}(q-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}-\alpha-2}.

(71)

This is a gamma distribution with mean and variance given by

\displaystyle\beta_{S}=\big<\beta\big>_{q,\beta_{0}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}\Big(1+(1-q)(\alpha+1)\Big),

(72a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\big<(\delta\beta)^{2}\big>_{q,\beta_{0}}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{0})^{2}(q-1)\Big(1+(1-q)(\alpha+1)\Big),

(72b)

in agreement with Eqs. (83) and (85) of Ref.
[
9
]
. The reduced inverse temperature covariance, defined by

u\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mathcal{U}}{(\beta_{S})^{2}},

(73)

depends only on
q
, and is given by

u=\frac{q-1}{1+(1-q)(\alpha+1)}.

(74)

By replacing
\beta_{0}
and
q
in terms of
u
and
\beta_{S}
we can simplify the distribution of inverse temperature, which now reads

P(\beta|u,\beta_{S})=\frac{1}{u\beta_{S}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{u\beta_{S}}\right)^{\frac{1}{u}-1}.

(75)

We emphasize here that this distribution leads to the
q
-canonical ensemble only in systems where the partition function
Z(\beta)
is such that the weight function
f(\beta;q,\beta_{0})
in (
5
)
is proportional to a gamma distribution.

q

In the
q
-canonical ensemble, as seen from (

has a universal statistical distribution which depends solely on

. In fact, we can explicitly compute the distribution of

P(z|\beta^{\prime},q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,P(\beta|\beta^{\prime},q)\delta\left(z-\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{1}{(q-1)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{z}{q-1}\right)\left(\frac{z}{q-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}-1},

Q(\bm{\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left<\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta_{F}}\right)^{2}\right>_{\bm{\lambda}}

Q(q,\beta_{0})=q

is normalizable for

7
Concluding remarks

We have established a central property of the fundamental inverse temperature functions
\beta_{F}
of superstatistical models, namely that they are solutions of autonomous ordinary differential equations, and this
implies that higher-order derivatives of
\beta_{F}
are functions of
\beta_{F}
as well. From this property, it follows that a mapping exists between functions of the inverse temperature and functions of the fundamental
inverse temperature, such that their expectation values are preserved. This result may provide future insights into the nature of the superstatistical and fundamental inverse temperatures, as well as practical
theoretical tools for the analysis of superstatistical systems.
