Comparative effectiveness of different inhaler technique education modalities on clinical outcomes in patients with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a protocol for a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Xun Yang, Xinrong Chen, Cui Yang, Mei Feng, Ying Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAsthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) collectively affect an estimated 475 million individuals worldwide, yet only 31% of patients achieve correct inhaler technique, a proportion that has not meaningfully improved over four decades of clinical practice. Critical technique errors are independently associated with poor disease control, increased exacerbation risk and greater healthcare utilisation. Although multiple educational modalities exist, no systematic review has applied network meta-analysis (NMA) to simultaneously compare and rank all six principal training approaches. This protocol describes a systematic review and NMA to generate comparative effectiveness evidence for inhaler technique training modality selection in adults with asthma or COPD.Methods and analysisA Bayesian random-effects NMA will be conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015, PRISMA-NMA and PRISMA 2020, with prospective PROSPERO registration (systematic search: June 2026; completion anticipated: February 2027). Seven databases will be searched from inception to June 2026 without language restriction: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science Core Collection, CINAHL, CNKI and Wanfang Data, supplemented by clinical trial registry searches. Eligible randomised controlled trials will enrol adults aged 18 years or older with confirmed asthma (Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) criteria) or COPD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria). Six intervention nodes defined a priori using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) framework will be compared against usual care and each other: face-to-face physical demonstration, video and digital training, teach-to-goal training, pharmacist-led training, nurse or clinician-led training and sensor-assisted technique feedback training. Primary analyses comprise two disease-specific NMAs (asthma: Asthma Control Test; COPD: COPD Assessment Test) with a pooled exploratory NMA as secondary analysis, a prespecified sparsity contingency and Bayesian meta-regression for transitivity (four covariates in disease-specific NMAs: device category, baseline severity, number of training sessions and participant age; disease type is added as a fifth in the pooled NMA). Risk of bias will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2; certainty of evidence will be evaluated using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework.Ethics and disseminationFormal ethical approval is not required. Findings will be published in a peer-reviewed respiratory or evidence synthesis journal, presented at ERS and ATS annual congresses and communicated to GINA, GOLD and NICE guideline development groups.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261347918.

Introduction

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) collectively affect an estimated 475 million individuals worldwide, with asthma accounting for 262 million prevalent cases and COPD for 212 million, and together rank among the leading causes of chronic respiratory morbidity, premature mortality and healthcare expenditure.13 According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, chronic respiratory diseases were the third leading cause of death globally, responsible for approximately 4.0 million deaths annually.1 Inhaled pharmacotherapy, comprising inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting β₂-agonists, long-acting muscarinic antagonists and their fixed-dose combinations, is the cornerstone of treatment for both conditions.4 5

Correct inhaler technique is a prerequisite for adequate pulmonary drug delivery, yet its attainment in clinical practice remains consistently poor. Technique error rates are high across all device types,6 and a landmark systematic review of 144 studies encompassing more than 54 000 subjects over four decades found that only 31% of patients demonstrated correct technique, with no improvement observed over time.7 The clinical consequences are well-established: suboptimal disease control, increased exacerbation frequency, diminished health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and greater healthcare resource utilisation.8 Critical errors have been independently associated with uncontrolled asthma and increased exacerbation rate in real-world practice.9 A further challenge is technique decay: errors re-emerge in many patients within 1–3 months of successful training, with some evidence of deterioration beginning as early as 24 hours after a training session.10 The associated economic burden has been estimated at €782 million in annual direct and indirect costs (direct costs only: €105 million) attributable to poor inhalation technique across three European countries for two widely prescribed dry-powder inhaler products.11

Multiple training modalities have been developed to address this problem, differing in delivery format, provider role, interactivity, resource requirements and scalability. Six distinct approaches are supported by randomised controlled trial (RCT) evidence. Face-to-face physical demonstration (FTF) involves structured in-person training by a healthcare professional comprising verbal instruction, device demonstration and observed patient practice without a predefined mastery threshold.12 Video and digital training (VID) delivers standardised instruction via prerecorded video, computer-based module, smartphone application or augmented reality platform without real-time provider feedback and has been shown to be non-inferior to FTF in a multicentre RCT.13 Teach-to-goal training (TTG) is an iterative mastery-based approach defined exclusively by its criterion-referenced mastery threshold, independent of delivery medium or provider type.14 15 Pharmacist-led training (PHM) encompasses structured instruction, counselling and reinforcement delivered primarily by a registered pharmacist; a meta-analysis of 12 RCTs confirmed significant improvements in inhalation technique and medication adherence.16 Nurse or clinician-led training (NLD) covers structured training delivered primarily by a respiratory nurse or allied health clinician, excluding pharmacists.12 Sensor-assisted technique feedback training (SAF) integrates an electronic inhaler monitoring device to objectively measure inhalation parameters and provide real-time or asynchronous corrective feedback.17 18 Standalone written materials are classified as usual care (UC).

Despite substantial evidence that structured modalities improve technique, no systematic review has generated comparative effectiveness estimates across modalities. The most comprehensive synthesis to date confirmed that structured training significantly reduces both dry powder inhaler (DPI) critical errors and pressurised metered-dose inhaler (pMDI) technique errors and demonstrated that the educational effect for pMDI varies by training approach, whereas no such differential effect was observed for DPIs.19 20 No registered or published systematic review has employed network meta-analysis (NMA) to simultaneously compare all six modalities, leaving clinicians and guideline developers without the comparative ranking data required to inform training programme selection.

This systematic review and NMA therefore aims to: (1) compare the effectiveness of six inhaler training modalities (FTF, VID, TTG, PHM, NLD and SAF) on disease control and other clinical outcomes in adults with asthma or COPD; (2) rank modalities by comparative effectiveness using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking curve (SUCRA) analysis; (3) explore potential effect modifiers including disease type, inhaler device type, training frequency and intervention duration and (4) assess the certainty of evidence using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework.

Methods and analysis

Study design and registration

This protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015,21 22 PRISMA extension for NMA (PRISMA-NMA)23 and PRISMA 2020.24 The review has been prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261347918); any subsequent amendments will be documented with dates and rationale. The systematic search is planned to commence in June 2026. Data extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, NMA and manuscript preparation are anticipated to be completed by February 2027.

Eligibility criteria

Types of studies

Only RCTs will be included. Quasi-randomised designs, controlled before-and-after studies and non-randomised evaluations will be excluded. No language, date or publication status restrictions will be applied.

Types of participants

Adults aged 18 years or older with a physician-confirmed diagnosis of asthma (Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) criteria)4 or COPD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria; postbronchodilator FEV₁/FVC below 0.70)5 will be eligible. Studies enrolling mixed asthma-COPD populations will contribute to the pooled exploratory NMA; they will contribute to a disease-specific primary NMA only where disease-stratified outcome data are reported separately. Where a disease-specific subnetwork is disconnected or any active modality node has fewer than two independent direct pairwise comparisons within that subnetwork, the primary analysis for that disease will revert to the pooled exploratory NMA with disease type modelled as a metaregression covariate, and the sparse sub-network will be characterised descriptively. Studies exclusively enrolling children or patients whose primary diagnosis is other than asthma or COPD will be excluded.

Types of interventions

Seven nodes will be defined a priori for the NMA: six active intervention nodes and one comparator node (UC). Node definitions were developed through systematic application of the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) framework25 to the published trial literature, ensuring that each node is empirically grounded, reproducibly classifiable and mutually distinguishable on the basis of a single primary differentiating characteristic, in accordance with Cochrane guidance on node definition.26

Eligibility principle

For a trial to contribute a valid pairwise comparison to the NMA, the two arms must differ solely in the inhaler technique education modality under evaluation; all other cointerventions must be delivered identically across arms or absent from both. Arms incorporating non-technique educational content not balanced in the comparator will be classified as ineligible for that pairwise comparison. In multiarm trials, individual pairwise comparisons will be evaluated separately.

Node classification hierarchy

Where a trial arm combines features of more than one node, it will be classified by its single primary differentiating characteristic according to the following precedence, ordered by degree of clinical distinctiveness: (1) sensor-based objective measurement of inhalation technique parameters (SAF); (2) predefined mastery criterion with iterative reassessment, irrespective of delivery medium or provider type (TTG); (3) pharmacist as the primary training provider (PHM); (4) nurse or non-pharmacist allied health clinician as the primary training provider (NLD); (5) standardised video or digital content without real-time personalised provider feedback (VID) and (6) in-person physical demonstration with supervised practice and no mastery criterion (FTF). Sensitivity analyses will explore alternative classifications for arms plausibly satisfying the criteria for more than one node. A symmetric-hybrid rule applies when both arms of a trial share the same higher-priority node feature: the shared feature is then constant across the comparison and is not the primary differentiating characteristic; each arm is classified by the next-highest feature in the hierarchy that distinguishes it from the comparator, and the shared feature is recorded as a study-level covariate.

Face-to-face physical demonstration

In-person training by a healthcare professional using a placebo or patient-owned inhaler, comprising verbal instruction, physical device demonstration and supervised patient practice with immediate error correction, without a predefined mastery criterion. Arms may additionally provide a technique-specific written or pictorial guide as a supplementary memory aid without altering node classification.

Video and digital training

Training delivered exclusively through standardised prerecorded content without real-time personalised provider feedback. This node encompasses prerecorded video (on any device format), multimedia touchscreen systems, web-based or smartphone applications and interactive computer-based adaptive learning modules. Standalone static written or pictorial materials, regardless of quality or specificity, are classified as UC.

Teach-to-goal training

Iterative training in which standardised assessment against a predefined mastery criterion (zero critical errors or correct performance of all checklist steps) is repeated until that criterion is met, irrespective of the number of training cycles required. TTG is defined exclusively by its criterion-referenced mastery threshold, independent of delivery medium and provider type. Prespecified sensitivity analyses will subclassify TTG arms by delivery medium (FTF-based vs VID-based) and by provider type; each subclassification analysis will be executed only when the relevant subcategory contains at least three eligible trials. Where this threshold is not met, TTG node heterogeneity will be characterised descriptively and reported as a limitation of the SUCRA estimates for that node.

Pharmacist-led training

Structured inhaler technique training delivered primarily by a registered pharmacist (or a pharmacy student under direct pharmacist supervision) in any healthcare setting. The PHM node is defined by the pharmacist provider role as the primary differentiating feature; the training may incorporate FTF, TTG-style iterative correction or supplementary materials, provided that equivalent non-pharmacist contact is absent in the comparator arm.

Nurse or clinician-led training

Structured inhaler technique training delivered primarily by a respiratory nurse, clinical nurse specialist, nurse practitioner, physician, allied health clinician (eg, physiotherapist) or trained clinical researcher with a nursing background, excluding pharmacists. NLD encompasses both individual and small-group delivery formats.

Sensor-assisted technique feedback training

Training integrating objective, sensor-based measurement of inhalation technique parameters (including peak inspiratory flow rate, inspiratory volume, inhalation duration, hand-breath coordination and breath-hold duration) with real-time or asynchronous corrective feedback. This node encompasses electronic inhaler monitoring devices (INCA, Hailie, Smart AeroChamber, Digihaler), acoustic flow-rate feedback trainers (Clip-Tone, 2Tone Trainer) and computer optical or visual feedback systems. SAF is distinguished from electronic adherence monitoring alone, which records actuation timing or frequency without assessing inhalation quality.27

Usual care

The reference node comprises standard local practice without structured inhaler technique training: provision of a manufacturer’s patient information leaflet or other written self-instruction materials without FTF; unstructured brief verbal advice from routine staff without observed patient practice or error correction; assessment-only arms and delayed training. designs in which the comparator arm receives no training during the measurement period. Head-to-head comparisons between any two active modality nodes are eligible regardless of whether a UC arm is present.

Types of outcome measures

Primary outcome

Disease control at the longest reported follow-up, assessed by the Asthma Control Test (ACT; scale 5 to 25; minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 3 points) in asthma, or the COPD Assessment Test (CAT; scale 0 to 40; MCID of 2 points) in COPD.

Information sources

Seven databases will be searched from inception to June 2026: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science Core Collection, CINAHL, CNKI and Wanfang Data. The following clinical trial registries will be searched: ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP, EU Clinical Trials Register and ISRCTN. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews will be hand-searched. Conference abstracts from ERS and ATS (past 10 years) and correspondence with content experts will supplement database searches.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy will be developed in collaboration with an information specialist and peer-reviewed using the PRESS 2015 checklist.29 Terms will be organised across three concept domains: population (asthma, COPD); intervention (inhaler technique training, inhalation instruction, teach-to-goal, pharmacist-led training, video training, digital inhaler, sensor-assisted feedback) and study design (RCT). The complete PubMed search strategy is provided in the online supplemental table 1.

Study selection

Records will be imported into EndNote for deduplication and blinded dual-reviewer title and abstract screening. Full-text eligibility assessments will be performed independently by two reviewers (XY and XC). Discrepancies will be resolved by discussion, with unresolved disagreements referred to a third reviewer (YW) for arbitration. Inter-rater agreement will be quantified using Cohen’s κ. Data extraction

Two reviewers (XY and XC) will independently extract data into a standardised, piloted REDCap electronic form. Trial authors will be contacted for missing numerical data. Means and SDs will be estimated from medians and IQRs using the Wan et al method30 where required. Data presented in graphical format will be extracted using WebPlotDigitizer software.31

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias in each included RCT will be assessed independently by two reviewers (XY and XC) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool26 32 across five domains. Blinding of participants and training providers is inherently impracticable in educational trials and is expected to be uniformly rated as ‘some concerns’. Blinding of outcome assessors will be documented for each study. A pre-specified sensitivity analysis will examine the impact of excluding studies rated as overall high risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analysis

For treatment pairs with two or more direct RCT comparisons, a preliminary pairwise random-effects meta-analysis will be conducted using the DerSimonian-Laird method. Between-study heterogeneity will be quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test.

Network meta-analysis

A Bayesian random-effects NMA will be conducted using the generalised linear modelling framework of Dias et al,33 implemented in R (V.4.3.0 or later) with the gemtc package34 via JAGS. Primary analyses will be conducted as two separate disease-specific NMAs: one restricted to asthma-only trials (primary outcome: ACT) and one restricted to COPD-only trials (primary outcome: CAT). A pooled exploratory NMA incorporating all eligible trials will be conducted as a secondary analysis, with disease type entered as a metaregression covariate. Trials enrolling mixed populations will contribute to the pooled analysis; they will contribute to a disease-specific analysis only where disease-stratified outcome data are reported separately. Where a disease-specific subnetwork is disconnected, or any active modality node has fewer than two independent direct pairwise comparisons within that subnetwork, the primary analysis for that disease will revert to the pooled exploratory NMA with disease type modelled as a metaregression covariate, and the sparse subnetwork will be characterised descriptively. All estimates will be reported with 95% credible intervals. Non-informative priors will be specified: N(0, 10⁴) for basic treatment effect parameters and HN(0, 1) for the between-study heterogeneity SD.26 MCMC sampling: 20 000-iteration burn-in followed by 100 000 sampling iterations across four independent chains, with a thinning interval of 10. Convergence will be assessed using the Brooks-Gelman-Rubin statistic (R̂ ≤1.05) and trace plot inspection. Treatment rankings will be estimated using SUCRA values and rankograms for all seven nodes.35

Assessment of network geometry and assumptions

A network plot will be constructed with node sizes proportional to the number of participants and edge widths proportional to the number of trials per comparison. Transitivity will be evaluated qualitatively by comparing the distributions of key potential effect modifiers (disease type, inhaler device type, baseline disease severity, training frequency and follow-up duration) across comparison subsets. Local inconsistency will be assessed using the node-splitting method36 and global inconsistency using the design-by-treatment interaction model.33

To formally evaluate potential violations of the transitivity assumption,37 a prespecified Bayesian random-effects meta-regression will be conducted for the primary outcome in accordance with established NMA methodology.26 In each disease-specific NMA, disease type is constant within the subnetwork; four study-level covariates will be modelled: inhaler device category (pMDI, DPI or SMI), mean baseline disease-severity score (ACT or CAT), number of training sessions and mean participant age. In the pooled exploratory NMA, disease type is added as a fifth covariate. Where the number of studies per comparison is insufficient to support multivariable regression without overfitting, covariates will be examined individually. Findings will be presented alongside the main NMA results to inform interpretation of SUCRA rankings.

Subgroup analyses

Two prespecified subgroup analyses will be conducted: (1) inhaler device type (pMDI vs DPI vs SMI), given evidence that the educational effect for pMDI/MDIs varies by training approach, whereas no such differential effect has been observed for DPIs19 and (2) overall risk of bias (low vs some concerns or high). Disease type is addressed at the level of the primary analysis and is not repeated as a subgroup stratification. Number of training sessions and intervention duration, which are highly collinear, are examined as continuous study-level covariates within the Bayesian meta-regression rather than as binary subgroup analyses. An exploratory subanalysis within the SAF node will contrast acoustic-only feedback trainers, electronic inhaler monitoring devices and fully integrated digital inhaler systems; this analysis will be executed only when each subcategory contains at least three eligible trials to ensure reliable SUCRA estimation.

Sensitivity analyses

Four prespecified sensitivity analyses will be conducted: (1) exclusion of studies rated as overall high risk of bias; (2) replication of the primary NMA using the frequentist netmeta package38 for cross-validation of Bayesian estimates; (3) a device-type-stratified sensitivity analysis restricting the critical-error-rate outcome to trials involving a single device category (pMDI only, DPI only or SMI only) and applying a critical error definition endorsed by the manufacturer or a national or international guideline committee (GINA, GOLD or equivalent) to minimise heterogeneity from cross-device pooling of non-equivalent definitions and (4) a chronological sensitivity analysis for the SAF node, examining whether treatment effect estimates have changed across calendar periods (before 2015, 2015 to 2020 and 2021 onwards) to detect technology-generation effects on pooled estimates. Studies with a follow-up shorter than 4 weeks and studies without a validated primary disease control outcome will be excluded in additional exploratory analyses.

Assessment of reporting bias

For comparisons with 10 or more contributing studies, comparison-adjusted funnel plots will be constructed and visually inspected for asymmetry to detect potential small-study effects.26

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each treatment comparison will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach adapted for NMA through the CINeMA framework,39 appraising six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence. Overall certainty will be classified as high, moderate, low or very low. The CINeMA web application (https://cinema.ispm.unibe.ch) will be used.

Patient and public involvement

This protocol was developed without direct patient or public involvement. Patient-centred, guideline-endorsed outcomes were selected to ensure clinical relevance. Findings will be disseminated via plain-language summaries to relevant patient organisations.

Ethics and dissemination

As this review will synthesise only previously published aggregate data, formal ethical approval is not required. Findings will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed respiratory medicine or evidence synthesis journal and presented at ERS and ATS annual congresses. Key findings will be communicated to GINA, GOLD and NICE guideline development groups.

Discussion

Clinical significance

This will be the first systematic review and NMA to simultaneously compare six inhaler training modalities in adults with asthma and COPD. Inhaler technique errors affect the majority of patients, are causally linked to poor disease control and preventable exacerbations and have not meaningfully improved over four decades of clinical practice.7 8 Existing meta-analyses confirm that structured inhaler technique education consistently improves technique and clinical outcomes compared with UC or pretraining performance, yet no comparative ranking across modalities exists to guide selection.12 16 The NMA framework addresses this gap by generating SUCRA-based rankings for all six modalities, including pairs never previously compared head-to-head, thereby enabling policy makers and guideline committees to move beyond general recommendations towards evidence-based specification of preferred training approaches.

The comparative effectiveness question has direct resource implications. FTF training requires trained staff and appointment time; PHM offers a scalable community model; TTG demands iterative contact but may optimise outcomes for patients with persistent errors; VID can be disseminated at negligible incremental cost; and SAF provides objective biometric feedback at the expense of device acquisition costs. The cost structure of competing modalities differs substantially. Pharmacist-led training has demonstrated net cost savings of €227 per patient over a 1-year modelled horizon from a 3-month community pharmacy programme in COPD,40 while digital inhaler technologies in difficult-to-treat asthma yielded cost savings of €3207 per patient at a 1-year horizon, driven primarily by reduced add-on biologic prescriptions.41 The direct cost burden attributable to poor inhalation technique has been estimated at €105 million annually across three European countries for two widely prescribed DPI products.11 A systematic review of adherence-promoting interventions in asthma and COPD found that multidisciplinary care involving one-to-one advice and digitally enhanced communications appears to offer the greatest benefit.42 The comparative effectiveness evidence generated by this NMA is intended as a necessary precondition for, rather than a substitute for, formal health-economic modelling incorporating local unit costs, healthcare system structure and patient volume.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Four prospectively identified methodological challenges have been addressed. Intervention classification heterogeneity is managed through a priori TIDieR-based node definitions with a six-level precedence hierarchy, ordered by clinical distinctiveness, and prespecified rules for hybrid arms. Inconsistent critical-error definitions will be handled by a device-type-stratified sensitivity analysis restricted to device-validated standardised checklists. Transitivity will be evaluated qualitatively through effect-modifier distribution mapping and statistically using node-splitting and the design-by-treatment interaction model; a prespecified Bayesian metaregression will formally model study-level covariates as potential effect modifiers,37 and where unresolved inconsistency is detected, SUCRA rankings will be interpreted with explicit caution. Follow-up duration variability is addressed through time-point stratification and inclusion of technique retention at 3 months or more as a distinct endpoint.

Key limitations are acknowledged. Blinding of participants and training providers is inherently impracticable; performance bias (RoB 2 domain 2) is expected to be uniformly rated as ‘some concerns’. Potential network sparsity for SAF and head-to-head active modality comparisons may constrain the precision of indirect estimates; the prespecified sparsity contingency ensures that disease-specific analyses are not attempted when the evidence base is insufficient. Secondary outcome instruments are likely to vary across trials, limiting cross-study pooling for HRQoL outcomes. Application of the classification hierarchy will reclassify pharmacist-delivered trials that employ a formal mastery criterion from PHM to TTG; the PHM node will consequently comprise lower-intensity pharmacist-led training programmes without a mastery criterion, and this compositional constraint should be considered when interpreting the PHM versus TTG comparison. Within the SAF node, substantial technological heterogeneity is anticipated; chronological sensitivity analyses and device subcategory subanalyses will probe this source of heterogeneity. A formal health-economic analysis is beyond the scope of this protocol; cost-effectiveness conclusions will require future modelling integrating these NMA estimates with resource-use and unit-cost data.114042 Residual publication bias cannot be excluded despite comprehensive database and registry coverage.
