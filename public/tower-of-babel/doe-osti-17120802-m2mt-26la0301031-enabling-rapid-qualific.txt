1 Executive Summary
This report summarizes the FY26 development of a physics-based constitutive modeling
framework to predict the mechanical response of additively manufactured (AM) 316H stain-
less steel (SS) under thermal, post-irradiation, and active irradiation conditions. The objec-
tive is to establish a pathway for enabling rapid qualification of AM 316H through irradiation-
response simulations by leveraging complementary non-prototypical experimental data and
irradiation defect-kinetics calculations within a common modeling framework. The start-
ing point is the advanced microstructure-sensitive constitutive model developed in previous
fiscal years and implemented within the full-field fast Fourier transform crystal-plasticity
framework, LApx. In FY26, this framework is further refined to describe the evolution of
the AM dislocation-cell structure and extended to couple experimentally characterized high
order irradiation defects with irradiation-generated point defects during creep. Together,
these developments establish an integrated strategy for combining thermal-mechanical data,
experimentally characterized AM microstructures, post-irradiation examination (PIE) mea-
surements, and physics-based defect-kinetics calculations to predict irradiation response. The
present irradiation-creep calculations are not yet fully calibrated or validated, and several
irradiation-specific inputs remain incompletely constrained, including the continuous evolu-
tion of the relevant sink populations within the rate-theory treatment. Nevertheless, the
FY26 work establishes the modeling pipeline needed to leverage non-prototypical informa-
tion, progressively incorporate higher-fidelity irradiation physics, and identify the irradiation
conditions for which targeted experiments are most necessary to support rapid qualification
of AM 316H SS.

 • The first step focuses on establishing the unirradiated constitutive response and trans-
 ferring it from conventionally processed to AM 316H SS. The FY25 constitutive model
 was first assessed using experimentally characterized LPBF 316H microstructures,
 which highlighted the need to better distinguish the evolution of dislocations within
 the cell interiors and cell walls. The FY26 formulation therefore separately evolves
 these dislocation populations through dislocation generation, trapping, and recovery.
 The refined model was calibrated using tensile and thermal-creep data for solution-
 annealed wrought 316H SS and subsequently applied to an experimentally character-
 ized AM 316H microstructure. The resulting calculations capture the experimentally
 observed tensile anisotropy and demonstrate the effects of AM grain morphology, crys-
 tallographic texture, and cellular dislocation evolution on the thermal-creep response.

 • The second step incorporates experimentally characterized irradiation microstructures
 to assess the post-irradiation mechanical response. PIE measurements of dislocation
 loops, black dots, and cavities are introduced into the constitutive framework as ad-
 ditional barriers to dislocation motion. The calculations capture irradiation-induced
 changes in the tensile response and predict substantial suppression of subsequent ther-
 mal creep. The effects of He are additionally incorporated through the experimentally
 characterized cavity populations, while radiation-induced segregation is examined as a
 sensitivity study. This approach provides a direct connection between the measured ir-

 than representing irradiation effects solely through accumulated dose.
 • The third step extends the framework to creep during active irradiation (irradiation
 creep) by introducing the non-equilibrium vacancy and self-interstitial populations that
 control irradiation-enhanced dislocation climb and recovery. An analytical steady-state
 point-defect treatment is first used together with the PIE-informed high order defect

 point-defect-enhanced climb and recovery. Vacancy and self-interstitial concentrations
 obtained from ORNL rate-theory calculations are then incorporated into the same con-
 stitutive framework to provide a more detailed treatment of the point-defect sink envi-

 defect kinetics propagate into the predicted irradiation-creep response and the relative
 contributions of dislocation glide and climb.

 The following report corresponds to two manuscripts. The first applies the constitutive
framework developed in the previous fiscal year to experimentally characterized AM 316H
microstructures as part of a collaborative study published in Materials & Design. The second
focuses on the FY26 recalibration of the 316H constitutive model and its application to AM
316H irradiation creep using complementary non-prototypical irradiation data and defect-
kinetics calculations. In the next fiscal year, the framework will be further (i) extended
to improve the description of dislocation cell-wall evolution and (ii) calibrated with AM
thermal-creep data, PIE characterization, and rate-theory calculations generated through
complementary efforts within the AMMT program.

2 Introduction

The long-term performance of structural components operating under elevated temperature,
mechanical loading, and irradiation is strongly influenced by their creep response and the
associated evolution of microstructure. Austenitic stainless steels (SS), particularly 316H,
are attractive for high-temperature structural applications because of their combination of
creep strength and corrosion resistance [1, 2, 3, 4]. Additive manufacturing (AM) provides
additional opportunities for fabricating complex three-dimensional component geometries
that are difficult or impossible to produce using conventional manufacturing routes. However,
the layer-by-layer deposition, repeated melting, and rapid solidification associated with laser
powder bed fusion (LPBF) subject the material to complex thermal histories and produce
microstructures that differ substantially from those of conventionally processed alloys [5, 6].
 Indeed, the microstructure of LPBF 316 SS can span a wide range of states depend-
ing on both the initial processing conditions and subsequent thermal exposure as shown in
Fig. 2-1. As-built LPBF 316 SS typically exhibits columnar and fine grains, high dislocation
densities, and pronounced cellular dislocation structures, while wrought 316 SS generally con-
tains equiaxed grains with more homogeneous dislocation distributions and no comparable
cellular substructure [7]. Moreover, rapid solidification during LPBF process also promotes
chemical heterogeneity, including segregation of solute elements such as Cr and Mo along
the cellular substructure, as well as the formation of oxide particles [8, 9]. These features
can subsequently influence the nucleation and growth of secondary phases, including M23 C6
carbides and Laves phase, further differentiating the AM microstructure from its wrought
counterpart [10, 11]. Importantly, these processing-induced microstructural features are un-
stable during elevated-temperature heat treatment. At sufficiently high temperatures (above
approximately 1100°C) , recrystallization can transform the as-built microstructure by dis-
solving the dislocation cell structure and producing more equiaxed grains, which results in
a microstructure that more closely resembles the wrought condition [10, 12, 13]. On the
other hand, at intermediate temperatures of approximately 650–900°C, the cellular struc-
ture undergoes progressive dislocation rearrangement and recovery, accompanied by changes
in cell size, wall thickness, and dislocation density [10]. These processes are further coupled
to redistribution of solute species and evolution of secondary-phase populations. Thus, the
mechanical response of AM 316 SS depends not only on its initial processing-induced mi-
crostructure but also on the evolution of that microstructure during heat treatment or in
service.
 This evolving microstructure is particularly important under irradiation because many
of the same features that govern the thermal-mechanical response also interact strongly with
irradiation-induced defects. Irradiation generates primary knock-on atoms (PKAs) that
initiate displacement cascades, producing excess vacancies and self-interstitial atoms and
driving the point defect population far from thermal equilibrium [14, 15]. Under applied
stress, preferential absorption of irradiation-generated point defects by dislocations biases
the climb process, thereby modifying dislocation recovery and contributing to irradiation
creep [16, 17, 18]. At the same time, the point defects can also cluster into higher-order
defects, including black dots, prismatic dislocation loops, and cavities (voids and He bub-

 In FY 25, we extended a calibrated polycrystal plasticity framework to investigate how
processing-induced microstructural features influence the tensile and creep responses of AM
316H SS. The initial analysis used synthetic microstructures with different grain aspect ra-
tios and systematically varied dislocation and precipitate populations to establish bounds on
mechanical performance. A key development was the implementation of a voxel-level seeder
module within the full-field fast Fourier transform framework, LApx, enabling explicit rep-
resentation of dislocation-rich cell walls and cell interiors. This capability incorporated both
grain-to-grain variations linked to grain size and localized concentrations of dislocations and
precipitates along cell walls. Comparisons among uniform, grain-dependent, and explic-
itly cellular distributions enabled assessment of how the initial spatial distribution of these
features affects mechanical behavior. Although different initial dislocation densities were
assigned to cell walls and interiors, both regions followed the same dislocation evolution
laws; the formulation did not distinguish the dislocation mechanisms operating in the two
regions. Precipitate populations remained fixed, and static dislocation recovery was not in-

 Introduction

 Extending this capability to irradiation creep introduces a major experimental challenge.
Direct mechanical-property measurements under the combinations of temperature, stress,
accumulated dose, dose rate, and He production relevant to service remain extremely lim-
ited. As shown in Fig. 2-2, motivated by potential applications of AM 316H in Generation
IV reactor structural components, the operating conditions span temperatures of approx-
imately 500–650°C, accumulated doses of approximately 1–10 dpa, and dose rates on the
order of 10−7 –10−6 dpa/s. Available irradiation experiments generally probe only subsets
of this parameter space. The experimental database is even more limited for AM 316H,
for which available measurements must also account for variations in processing history
and the resulting microstructural state. Ion irradiation can provide detailed microstruc-
tural characterization at relevant temperatures and doses but is commonly conducted at
dose rates several orders of magnitude higher than expected during service [9]. Existing ion-
and neutron-irradiation datasets also consist largely of post-irradiation tensile measurements
rather than direct irradiation-creep measurements [21]. As a result, prototypical irradiation-
creep data are not presently available over the parameter space required for qualification of
AM 316H SS.

during irradiation provide different information about the deformation response. PIE char-
acterizes retained irradiation defects, such as dislocation loops and cavities, and, together
with mechanical testing and hardening models, helps quantify their contributions to irra-
diation hardening [22]. However, the non-equilibrium vacancy and interstitial populations
generated during irradiation are no longer continuously sustained once irradiation ceases.
Their production, migration, and absorption at sinks influence dislocation climb and irradia-
tion creep [16]. Consequently, a constitutive description constrained only by retained defect
populations and post-irradiation hardening does not, by itself, capture the additional point-
defect-mediated deformation occurring during irradiation. This limitation is particularly
important when translating high-dose-rate ion-irradiation measurements to lower-dose-rate
service conditions. Whereas irradiation dose measures the accumulated exposure in displace-
ments per atom, dose rate governs the production of point defects relative to their removal
by recombination, absorption at sinks, and incorporation into defect clusters. Different
dose rates can therefore produce different vacancy and interstitial concentrations and mi-
crostructural evolution histories even at the same accumulated dose. Because these concen-
trations directly influence dislocation climb, the irradiation-creep response cannot generally
be characterized by accumulated dose alone [16]. Predicting creep under service conditions
therefore requires accounting for dose-rate-dependent defect kinetics, while translation from
ion-irradiation experiments must also account for differences in helium production [23].
 To address the lack of prototypical irradiation-creep data, the present work extends the
previously developed microstructure-sensitive constitutive framework to predict the irradiation-
creep response of AM 316H SS using complementary experimental PIE data and rate-theory
calculations. Tensile and thermal-creep data for wrought 316H constrain the baseline con-
stitutive response, which is transferred to AM 316H using experimentally characterized mi-
crostructures. PIE characterization and post-irradiation mechanical testing constrain the
retained irradiation-defect populations, including dislocation loops and cavities, and their
contributions to hardening under different dose and He conditions. Rate-theory calculations
describe the coupled evolution of point defects and higher-order defect populations, with the
point-defect concentrations coupled directly to the dislocation-climb formulation to account
for irradiation-enhanced climb and recovery.
 The framework thus distinguishes the hardening associated with retained irradiation de-
fects from the point-defect-mediated deformation occurring during irradiation, while account-
ing for their coupled evolution. By using each dataset to constrain the corresponding physical
mechanisms, this approach provides a mechanistic basis for translating non-prototypical ex-
perimental information into predictions of AM 316H irradiation creep over the temperature,
dose, and dose-rate ranges of interest, supporting future qualification efforts.

3 Approach
We hypothesize that the irradiation-creep response of AM 316H SS is governed by the com-
bined effects of persistent high-order irradiation-induced defects and transient irradiation-
generated point defects. High-order defects, including dislocation loops, black dots, and
cavities, provide additional resistance to dislocation motion, whereas non-equilibrium va-
cancies and self-interstitial atoms modify dislocation climb and recovery during irradiation.
Direct irradiation-creep measurements spanning the relevant AM microstructures, tempera-
tures, doses, He contents, and service-relevant dose rates are presently unavailable. Conse-
quently, the present work develops a strategy to combine complementary non-prototypical
datasets, each of which constrains a different component of the response as seen in Fig.3-1.
First, thermal-creep data for wrought 316H are used to establish the underlying constitu-
tive behavior and transfer it to experimentally characterized AM microstructures (ORNL).
Second, PIE measurements (ANL) are used to constrain persistent irradiation-induced de-
fect populations and their hardening effects. Finally, analytical point-defect supersaturation
calculations and rate-theory calculations (ORNL) are used to provide the transient vacancy
and interstitial populations required for in-situ irradiation creep. Because these datasets do
not individually reproduce the complete service environment and several irradiation-specific
parameters remain incompletely constrained, the primary objective is to demonstrate an
integrated modeling pipeline for translating complementary non-prototypical information
toward the prediction of AM 316H irradiation creep.

3.4 Irradiation-generated point defects and in-situ creep calculations
Prediction of in-situ irradiation creep additionally requires the non-equilibrium vacancy and
self-interstitial populations generated during irradiation. Unlike the high-order defects re-
tained in the post-irradiation microstructure, these continuously sustained point-defect pop-

3-6 Page Los Alamos National Laboratory

 Approach

ulations are not directly available from PIE. Their concentrations govern the defect fluxes
to dislocations and therefore influence dislocation climb and the evolution and recovery of
the cellular dislocation structure. Two complementary approaches are used to supply this
information. In the first, the analytical defect-supersaturation formulation described in the
Methods section estimates vacancy and interstitial concentrations from the irradiation defect-
production rate and the assumed microstructural sink strengths. In the second, steady-state
point-defect concentrations are obtained from rate-theory calculations performed at ORNL
for selected irradiation conditions.
 The ORNL rate-theory calculations use experimentally characterized cavity and dislocation-
loop distributions as microstructural inputs. The dislocation-loop population is prescribed
and held fixed during the rate-theory calculations, while the cavity population can evolve
during irradiation. For conditions containing He, the rate-theory treatment additionally
accounts for He migration, trapping, detrapping, and its interaction with vacancies. The
cavity-evolution treatment was evaluated using ion-irradiation measurements by initializing
the calculations with the measured cavity distribution at 5 dpa and predicting its evolution to
10 dpa for comparison with the corresponding experimental distribution. Calculations were
performed both without additional void nucleation and with an empirical void-nucleation
rate of approximately 9.4 × 1019 voids cm−3 s−1 , obtained from a linear projection of the
experimentally measured cavity populations at multiple doses. This nucleation rate is pre-
scribed as an input and does not evolve during the calculation. For the prototypical neutron-
irradiation condition considered here, the damage rate is reduced from 10−3 to 10−6 dpa/s
and a He production rate of 1 appm He/dpa is introduced, while the remaining rate-theory
parameters are retained from the ion-irradiation calculations. The resulting matrix vacancy
and self-interstitial concentrations are extracted from the rate-theory calculations and sup-
plied to the constitutive model as the point-defect inputs governing irradiation-enhanced
dislocation climb and recovery. These approaches provide alternative inputs through which
irradiation conditions, including dose rate, affect the constitutive response.
 Both point-defect treatments use the same constitutive parameters and dislocation-
climb and recovery formulations. They are used to assess how alternative descriptions of
irradiation-defect kinetics propagate into the mechanical response, rather than as indepen-
dently calibrated irradiation-creep models. In both treatments, irradiation-generated point
defects act concurrently with persistent high-order defects constrained using ANL PIE data.

remain incompletely constrained. These include parameters governing point-defect produc-

experimental constraints are unavailable. These choices influence the balance between re-

reactor service conditions; this requires further calibration and validation against irradiation-

tally characterized AM microstructures, persistent high-order defects constrained by ANL
PIE data, and irradiation-generated point-defect populations to the mechanical response.

and two alternative point-defect treatments, with dose-rate effects providing a specific appli-

irradiation measurements become available, both the point-defect concentrations and the
high-order defect-population histories can be updated, enabling the framework to incorpo-

and physical constraints.

In this work, we utilize the full-field EVPFFT framework, LApx, together with a physics-
based constitutive model to predict the mechanical response of AM 316H SS for different mi-
crostructures and loading conditions. The original FFT-based formulation was developed to
study the micromechanical response of composite materials [28, 29] and was later adapted to
polycrystals [30] to capture the effective and local response arising from spatial variations in
crystal orientation and the associated anisotropic mechanical behavior. The FFT-based for-
mulation for polycrystals has subsequently been extended to different deformation regimes,
including elasticity [31], incompressible viscoplasticity [30, 32], dilatational viscoplasticity
[33], infinitesimal elasto-viscoplasticity [34, 35], and finite elasto-viscoplasticity [36]. The
constitutive formulation employed here extends the frameworks developed in [37, 38, 18]
and leverages recent constitutive-modeling developments under the NEAMS program for
Grade 91 [39]. The formulation is adapted here for AM 316H SS by explicitly account-
ing for its cellular dislocation substructure, M23 C6 precipitation and solute strengthening,
and irradiation-induced defect populations. These microstructural features are represented
through evolving internal state variables that directly affect the resistance to dislocation
glide, the internal stress distribution, and the kinetics of dislocation climb and recovery.
Their coupled evolution therefore allows the constitutive response to change with the un-
derlying microstructural state during both thermal and irradiation creep. The individual
deformation mechanisms and corresponding evolution laws are summarized below.

We first assess the ability of the constitutive model available at the beginning of the fis-
cal year to predict the mechanical response of experimentally characterized AM 316H SS
microstructures provided by ORNL [24]. The limitations identified from this assessment
motivate the adoption of a refined constitutive model that explicitly tracks dislocation evo-
lution within the cell interiors and cell walls. This refined model is subsequently used to
establish the thermal-creep response of 316H SS, predict the response of a selected AM mi-
crostructure, incorporate persistent irradiation-induced defects characterized through ANL
PIE data, and assess the additional effects of irradiation-generated point defects on the
in-situ irradiation-creep response.
 Figure 5-1 compares the simulated and experimental tensile responses for the three AM
316H SS microstructures [24]. The simulations use an initially uniform dislocation density of
1014 m−2 and a strain rate of 10−3 s−1 , without recalibration of the constitutive parameters
to the LPBF tensile data. Although the model underpredicts the experimentally measured
yield strength and early strain hardening, it reproduces several qualitative trends. For the 43
J/mm3 microstructure, the predicted vertical and horizontal responses are nearly identical,
consistent with the relatively small experimental directional differences and the fine, weakly
textured grain structure. For the 71 J/mm3 condition, the model captures the emergence of
mechanical anisotropy, including higher yield strength and stronger early hardening under
horizontal loading than under loading along the vertical build direction. For the strongly
textured 135 J/mm3 microstructure, however, the model predicts a crossover in the stress–
strain curves at approximately 0.4% strain, beyond which the vertical response exceeds the
horizontal response. This crossover is not observed experimentally and persists when the
initial dislocation density is reduced to 1012 m−2 . Our analysis associates this behavior
with the strong ⟨001⟩ texture aligned with the build direction, which promotes simultaneous
activation of multiple slip systems during vertical loading [24]. Within the initial constitutive
formulation, the resulting dislocation storage exceeds the compensating dynamic recovery,
causing dislocation density and slip resistance to increase more rapidly under vertical loading
than under horizontal loading. The persistence of the crossover at both initial densities
indicates that changing the initial dislocation density alone does not resolve the discrepancy.
In addition, the coarse grains in the 135 J/mm3 condition reduce the number of grains
sampled within the cropped simulation domain, limiting its statistical representativeness
and introducing an additional consideration when interpreting the predicted anisotropy.
 These results indicate that the initial model captures the principal directional trends
associated with grain morphology and crystallographic texture for the 43 and 71 J/mm3
conditions, but does not reproduce the experimentally observed hardening response of the
strongly textured 135 J/mm3 microstructure. A key feature absent from these calculations is
the heterogeneous cellular dislocation structure characteristic of LPBF 316H SS. Dislocation-
rich cell walls provide a preexisting obstacle network, while the relatively dislocation-poor
interiors provide a distinct environment for mobile dislocations. Representing these regions
through a single, initially uniform dislocation population does not distinguish storage within

 The experimental characterization provides additional evidence of dislocation hetero-

spatial distribution vary with processing conditions, providing further motivation to move
beyond a uniform initial-density description.
 These findings motivated the adoption, early in the present fiscal year, of a refined consti-
tutive formulation that represents cell-interior and cell-wall dislocations as separate evolving
populations. The formulation explicitly accounts for dislocation generation within cell in-

for the two populations. Cell-wall recovery is coupled to the wall-dislocation climb velocity,
allowing temperature and irradiation-generated point-defect concentrations to influence the
persistence of the cellular structure. The evolving populations, in turn, modify glide resis-
tance, internal stress distributions, and climb kinetics. This treatment provides a basis for
describing the role of the heterogeneous cellular structure beyond its initial contribution to
strength and is adopted for the subsequent thermal and irradiation-creep calculations.

6.1 Calibration against NIMS data for solution-annealed wrought 316H SS

Figure 6-1 shows the experimental tensile and thermal creep data for solution-annealed
wrought 316H SS from the National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS), reported and
analyzed in many previous modeling works [75, 76, 77, 78]. They are used to calibrate the
FY26 constitutive model. Specifically, Figure 6-1(a) shows the experimental yield strength
and ultimate tensile strength of wrought 316H SS from room temperature to 750°C. Al-
though strength generally decreases with increasing temperature, the yield strength reaches
a local minimum near 500°C, followed by an increase between 500 and 600°C and a sub-
sequent decrease at 650°C. This departure from monotonic thermal softening is consistent
with dynamic strain aging, which has been observed in related 316 austenitic stainless steels
over a similar temperature range [79, 80]. Dynamic strain aging arises when solute redis-
tribution occurs on a timescale comparable to the waiting time of dislocations at obstacles,
allowing solutes to strengthen the pinning of temporarily arrested dislocations. The result-
ing increase in resistance to dislocation motion can partially offset thermal softening and
modify the temperature dependence of strength. This behavior motivates the inclusion of
time-dependent solute aging in the present constitutive formulation. Moreover, Fig.6-1 (b)
presents the thermal creep data at 600°C under applied stresses of 108, 127, 137, 157, and
177 MPa, with the longest tests extending beyond 105 h. The experimental creep-rate his-
tories exhibit primary-creep deceleration followed by a minimum and tertiary acceleration,
with some conditions showing additional changes in slope or local minima rather than a
prolonged secondary stage. These features illustrate the complexity of the creep response
and the potential interplay among dislocation evolution, solute strengthening, precipitation,
and damage.
 In FY26, the model calibration focuses on establishing the interplay between dislocation
evolution (cell and cell-wall) and solute-strengthening mechanisms. Although precipitation
has been proposed to contribute to the complex creep-rate histories [76], the physical pre-
cipitate size and number density are frozen to isolate the mechanisms of interest. Neverthe-
less, the effective precipitate obstacle size can change through the climb-assisted depinning
formulation described in Section 4, representing a change in obstacle effectiveness rather
than physical precipitate growth or dissolution. Moreover, damage evolution responsible for
tertiary-creep acceleration and failure is also excluded. Therefore, the model calibration tar-
gets (i) the tensile responses as a function of temperature and (ii) the evolution of creep rate
during primary creep and its approach to the minimum-rate regime, without attempting to
reproduce the complete creep-to-rupture history.

6.3 Post-irradiation tensile response of AM 316H SS
Next, the model is used to predict the PIE mechanical response of AM 316H SS under
tensile and thermal creep loading. The analysis evaluates the effects of irradiation dose,
He-dependent irradiation defect populations, and radiation-induced segregation (RIS), with
the unirradiated AM responses established in the previous section as the baselines. The PIE
tensile calculations first examine irradiation-induced changes in strength across temperature,
build orientation, and irradiation dose, then followed by the effects of He concentration
and RIS. The PIE thermal-creep simulations then evaluate how irradiation dose and He
concentration, through their effects on the retained irradiation defect populations, modify
the time-dependent deformation response over a range of applied stresses. Together, these
calculations provide a systematic assessment of how retained irradiation-induced high order
defects and RIS modify the predicted PIE mechanical response of AM 316H SS.

in creep rate was associated with irradiation-induced voids and dislocation loops retained

This behavior is qualitatively consistent with the present calculations, where the retained
prismatic-loop and cavity populations provide additional resistance to creep deformation. In
contrast, Lovell and Barker reported that Type 316 SS irradiated at approximately 482°C

thermal creep response. Instead, the PIE response depends on the irradiation-induced mi-
crostructure retained following irradiation and its subsequent evolution at the creep-test

temperature. The present calculations specifically evaluate the contribution of the retained
prismatic-loop and cavity populations and therefore predict creep suppression associated
with these defect obstacles.
 Importantly, the post-irradiation thermal creep response considered here is distinct from
irradiation creep. During PIE creep, irradiation has ceased and the retained irradiation-
induced defects modify the subsequent thermal creep response. Under irradiation, continu-
ous production and migration of nonequilibrium point defects provide additional pathways
for time-dependent deformation, particularly through their influence on dislocation climb
and the evolution of the dislocation structure. The PIE tensile and thermal creep calcula-
tions therefore provide a means of constraining the mechanical contribution of the retained
irradiation-induced defect populations independently of these irradiation-driven point-defect
processes. Together with the corresponding microstructural characterization, future PIE
tensile and thermal creep measurements of the AM 316H specimens within the AMMT
program can be used to further calibrate and validate these contributions. The resulting
description of the retained irradiation-induced defect populations can then be coupled with
the point-defect production and evolution mechanisms required to predict creep under active
irradiation, as discussed in the following section.

7.1 Effect of irradiation dose rate on creep response

Figure 7-1 compares the irradiation creep response of AM 316H SS at 600°C for irradiation
dose rates of 10−8 , 10−6 , and 10−3 dpa/s with the corresponding thermal creep response
over applied stresses ranging from 78 to 137 MPa. In general, the irradiation dose rate
has a strong influence on both the magnitude and evolution of the creep rate. At 10−8
and 10−6 dpa/s, the irradiation creep rates generally remain lower than the corresponding
thermal creep responses over substantial portions of the simulated history, indicating an
overall suppression of creep. Specifically, at 10−6 dpa/s, the creep rate typically reaches a
pronounced minimum between approximately 5×102 and 2×103 h before partially recovering,
while similar changes at 10−8 dpa/s are shifted toward approximately 105 h. In contrast,
the 10−3 dpa/s calculations exhibit an early minimum followed by a rapid increase in creep
rate, eventually approaching a long-time rate on the order of 10−8 s−1 across the investigated
stress range. This response is approximately one to two orders of magnitude higher than the
corresponding thermal creep rates at long times.
 Importantly, the relationship between irradiation and thermal creep is in qualitative
agreement with experimental observations for conventionally manufactured austenitic stain-
less steels [93]. Both enhancement and suppression of the creep rate under active irradiation
relative to the corresponding thermal-creep response have been documented experimentally
in Type 316 and 321 SS. Jung et al. summarized irradiation-creep measurements showing
conditions under which irradiation produced creep rates higher than the corresponding ther-
mal response, including measurements for 321 SS [94], as well as conditions under which
irradiation reduced the creep rate when thermal creep became dominant [93, 95]. The latter
behavior was attributed to irradiation-induced dynamic hardening associated with defect
clusters acting as additional obstacles to dislocation motion. These contrasting observations
are qualitatively consistent with the competition captured in the present calculations, where
irradiation-induced defect populations increase the resistance to dislocation motion while
irradiation-generated point defects simultaneously enhance dislocation climb and recovery.
The relative contributions of these mechanisms therefore determine whether the irradiation
creep rate is lower or higher than the corresponding thermal-creep response.
 Moreover, the increase in irradiation creep rate seen in Fig. 7-1 with increasing dose
rate is qualitatively consistent with the point-defect kinetics proposed for conventionally
manufactured 316 SS [93, 96]. Nagakawa et al. [96] compared their low dose-rate proton-
irradiation experiments at approximately 2.5×10−8 dpa/s with previous high-dose-rate light-
ion measurements [93] at approximately 0.5–4.5 × 10−6 dpa/s. Their rate-theory analysis
showed that the steady-state point-defect concentration increases with displacement rate
and, under recombination-dominated conditions, scales approximately with the square root
of the displacement rate. This behavior provides a physical basis for increasing absolute
irradiation creep rate with increasing dose rate. Nagakawa et al. [96] also showed that
the irradiation creep compliance, defined as the creep rate normalized by applied stress and
displacement rate, was approximately one order of magnitude larger under the low-dose-rate

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 7-1

condition. The compliance represents the creep deformation produced per unit applied stress
and accumulated displacement damage. Thus, a lower dose rate can produce more creep per
unit dpa even though the absolute irradiation creep rate can still increase with increasing
dose rate.

mobile vacancies and interstitials that enhance dislocation climb and climb-mediated recov-

formation of irradiation-induced defect clusters suppresses dislocation glide, whereas the el-
evated concentrations of mobile point defects enhance mass transport and dislocation climb

through the stress-dependent precipitation model. More generally, the occurrence of local
minima in the creep-rate response appears to be associated with changes in the evolving

additionally include the point-defect populations and the dose-dependent evolution of loops,

mechanisms to change with accumulated dose.

of the local minima and changes in the predicted irradiation creep response of AM 316H

calculated creep-rate curves. These features should therefore not necessarily be interpreted
as physical discontinuities in the irradiation creep response, but rather as a consequence of
the currently available sparse description of the dose-dependent irradiation microstructure.

description of these populations, while cluster-dynamics or rate-theory calculations could
predict their continuous evolution with irradiation condition and accumulated dose. Incor-
porating such information would allow the evolution of the high-order defect populations
and their interactions with the point-defect populations to be described more continuously
and physically, such that changes in the irradiation creep response emerge directly from
the evolving irradiation microstructure rather than from interpolation between discrete PIE
conditions.

7.3 Rate-theory-informed irradiation creep response

To provide a more complete description of the point-defect kinetics, vacancy and self-
interstitial concentrations obtained from the ORNL rate-theory calculations are incorpo-
rated into the constitutive framework. In the current analytical treatment in LAPx, the
high-density dislocation-cell walls are assumed to dominate the point-defect sink strength,
whereas the ORNL rate-theory calculations account for a broader population of irradiation-
induced sinks and reactions, including the evolving loop and cavity populations. The re-
sulting vacancy and self-interstitial concentrations are supplied directly to the same LAPx
constitutive description of dislocation glide, climb, and recovery, allowing the influence of the
more detailed sink-strength treatment on the predicted mechanical response to be assessed
without changing the underlying constitutive parameters. Two ORNL irradiation conditions
are considered: 10−3 dpa/s without He production and 10−6 dpa/s with 1.0 He/dpa.
 Figure 7-6 compares the irradiation creep responses with the corresponding thermal creep
calculations at 600°C over applied stresses ranging from 78 to 137 MPa. Both irradiation con-
ditions initially exhibit creep rates below the corresponding thermal response, consistent with
the additional resistance to dislocation motion introduced by the irradiation-induced high-
order defect populations. As irradiation proceeds, however, the creep rates reach minima and
subsequently increase, producing a transition from initial creep suppression to irradiation-
enhanced deformation. For the 10−6 dpa/s condition with 1.0 He/dpa, this transition occurs
over tens to hundreds of hours depending on the applied stress. Following the minimum,
the creep rates increase toward values on the order of 10−8 s−1 , substantially above the cor-
responding long-time thermal creep rates. The transition shifts to later times with decreas-
ing applied stress, indicating that the evolving irradiation microstructure remains strongly
coupled to the stress-dependent dislocation response. On the other hand, the 10−3 dpa/s
condition without He exhibits a much more rapid transition. Following an initial reduction
in creep rate, the calculated response begins to increase within the first fraction of an hour
and reaches values approaching 10−5 s−1 for the higher applied stresses. Although the two
ORNL irradiation conditions cannot be compared solely in terms of dose rate because of
their different He production rates, the calculations demonstrate the strong sensitivity of the
predicted creep response to the irradiation-dependent point-defect populations supplied to
the constitutive model.
 The evolution at 108 MPa is shown separately in Fig. 7-7. At 10−6 dpa/s with 1.0 He/dpa,
the irradiation creep rate initially decreases below the thermal response and reaches a min-
imum of several 10−9 s−1 after approximately several tens of hours, corresponding to an
accumulated dose of approximately 0.1–0.2 dpa. The creep rate subsequently increases by
approximately a factor of two to three and approaches values on the order of 10−8 s−1 . In
contrast, the thermal creep rate continues to decrease and approaches approximately 10−10
s−1 at longer times. The ORNL-informed calculation therefore predicts a transition from an
initially suppressed response to a long-time irradiation creep rate approximately two orders

8 Conclusion

The FY26 effort established an integrated physics-based framework for predicting the ther-
mal, post-irradiation, and in-situ irradiation-creep responses of AM 316H SS using comple-
mentary non-prototypical datasets. The constitutive formulation was refined to explicitly
evolve the dislocation populations within the cell interiors and cell walls and to couple their
evolution to dislocation glide, climb, dynamic and static recovery, and time-dependent solute
strengthening. Calibration against solution-annealed wrought 316H tensile and thermal-
creep measurements provided the baseline constitutive response, which was subsequently
transferred to an experimentally characterized LPBF 316H microstructure without recali-
bration to the AM tensile data.
 Application of the calibrated model to the AM microstructure demonstrated that grain
morphology, crystallographic texture, and the evolving cellular dislocation structure can
substantially influence both tensile and creep deformation. The model reproduced the mag-
nitude and orientation dependence of the room-temperature tensile response for the selected
43 J/mm3 LPBF microstructure and predicted higher AM creep rates during the early stages
of thermal creep compared with the idealized wrought microstructure. The subsequent evo-
lution of the cell-interior and cell-wall dislocation populations showed that these differences
are strongest during primary creep and decrease as the competing storage and recovery
mechanisms evolve.
 The PIE calculations provided a complementary means of constraining the mechanical ef-
fects of persistent irradiation-induced defects. Experimentally informed loop, black-dot, and
cavity populations produced irradiation hardening in the tensile calculations and substantial
suppression of post-irradiation thermal creep. The predicted minimum PIE creep rates at
600°C were approximately 23–31% of the corresponding unirradiated values, while the He-
dependent cavity populations produced additional suppression. In contrast, the approximate
RIS treatment considered here produced only a small change in the predicted macroscopic
tensile response. These results demonstrate that the retained irradiation microstructure,
rather than accumulated dose alone, is a critical input for describing the post-irradiation
mechanical response.
 The in-situ irradiation-creep calculations further demonstrated that the response cannot
be described solely through irradiation-induced hardening. Irradiation-generated vacancies
and self-interstitials enhance dislocation climb and recovery while the simultaneously evolv-
ing high order defects increase the resistance to dislocation glide. The balance between
these mechanisms changes with irradiation condition and accumulated dose, producing non-
monotonic creep-rate histories and transitions between creep suppression and enhancement.
Under the analytical point-defect treatment, lower dose-rate conditions remain predomi-
nantly glide-mediated and exhibit creep suppression over substantial portions of the sim-
ulated histories, whereas the 10−3 dpa/s condition develops substantially enhanced climb
kinetics and a mixed glide–climb response.
 Coupling vacancy and self-interstitial concentrations from the ORNL rate-theory cal-
culations demonstrated how a more detailed treatment of point-defect sinks and reactions
can be incorporated without changing the underlying constitutive formulation. The ORNL-

informed calculations show that the representation of the point-defect environment can sub-
stantially affect both the magnitude of the predicted irradiation-creep response and the
relative contributions of glide and climb. The present rate-theory-informed results should
nevertheless be interpreted as a demonstration of the coupled modeling strategy rather than
as fully calibrated irradiation-creep predictions. The dislocation-loop population is presently
prescribed rather than evolved continuously within the rate-theory calculations, the evolution
of all competing sink populations is not yet treated self-consistently, several irradiation-
specific parameters remain incompletely constrained, and direct in-situ irradiation-creep
measurements for AM 316H SS are still required for quantitative validation.
 The principal outcome of the FY26 effort is therefore the establishment of a model-
ing pathway for enabling rapid qualification of AM 316H SS through irradiation-response
simulations. Thermal-mechanical measurements can constrain the baseline constitutive re-
sponse, PIE measurements can constrain persistent irradiation-induced microstructures and
their hardening effects, and defect-kinetics calculations can provide the nonequilibrium point-
defect environment required for active irradiation. The constitutive framework then provides
the connection between these non-prototypical sources of information and the resulting me-
chanical response. Continued development should focus on more self-consistent evolution
of the point-defect and high order defect sink populations, improved representation of the
initial AM dislocation heterogeneity and precipitation evolution, and validation against tar-
geted irradiation-creep measurements. These developments will progressively strengthen the
physical and experimental constraints of the framework while reducing the portion of the
irradiation-condition space that must be explored through direct prototypical testing.
