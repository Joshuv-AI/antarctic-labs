Bioreactor-derived EVs from placental MSCs show context- and donor-specific immunomodulatory trends in human 3D lung inflammatory models
Authors: Karolina Soroczynska, Magdalena Dlugolecka, Justinas Mačiulaitis, Rūta Insodaitė, Paulius Valiukevičius, Ieva Čiapienė, Ugnė Kuzaitytė, Mindaugas Kliucinskas, Romaldas Mačiulaitis, Malgorzata Czystowska-Kuzmicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have garnered attention as cell-free therapeutics due to their regenerative and immunomodulatory potential. Human placenta-derived stromal cells (hPSCs) are a particularly promising source owing to their accessibility, scalability, and superior proliferative capacity—yet the functional behavior of their EVs, especially in inflammatory disease contexts, remains poorly defined. This study introduces a novel integrated approach, combining a 3D microcarrier bioreactor for scalable EV production with advanced 3D human airway disease models, to resolve how donor variability and inflammatory context shape the immunomodulatory activity of hPSC-EVs.MethodshPSCs were expanded under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant conditions in both conventional 2D cultures and 3D stirred-tank bioreactors using microcarrier technology. EVs were isolated from conditioned media via tangential flow filtration and characterized by fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA), flow cytometry and protein content. Functional effects of hPSCs and EVs were assessed in PBMC co-cultures and two human 3D airway models—cystic fibrosis (CF) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)—based on the Epithelix SmallAir™ platform, integrating primary airway epithelium and macrophages at the air–liquid interface. Inflammation was induced with TNF-α (CF) or LPS (ARDS), and EVs or hPSCs were administered to both apical and basal compartments.Results3D culture significantly increased EV yield without compromising quality. A key finding was a substantial donor-dependent variability in both hPSC and EV activity, which translated into distinct, model-specific immunomodulatory profiles. Notably, EV- and hPSC-mediated responses diverged across immune and epithelial compartments, indicating that EVs do not simply recapitulate parental cell function. In the 3D models, despite substantial heterogeneity, induction of IL-10 and Arginase-1 (up to 25-fold) in the macrophage compartment emerged as a consistent trend across experimental conditions. In contrast, parental hPSCs showed broader but less predictable cytokine modulation, including variable TNF-α suppression and context-specific effects across donors.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that hPSC- and EV-mediated immunomodulation is highly context-dependent and cannot be predicted solely from donor identity or culture format. Rather than identifying a single optimal condition, this study highlights the need for larger donor cohorts and functional profiling in advanced human models and supports the use of EVs as distinct, cell-free immunomodulatory entities with compartment-specific activity. Together, this work provides a translational framework linking GMP-compliant EV manufacturing with functionally relevant human disease modeling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05102-5.

Background

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as key paracrine effectors of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and are increasingly recognized as the principal mediators of their regenerative potential. Stem cell-derived EVs retain therapeutic properties of their parental cells while offering lower immunogenicity and higher stability, making them attractive for a wide range of therapeutic applications beyond traditional cell therapy [1, 2]. However, the clinical translation of EV-based therapies remains limited by inconsistencies in isolation protocols, culture conditions, and challenges in producing high-quality EVs at scale [3].

Three-dimensional (3D) culture systems – such as spheroids, microcarriers, or hollow-fiber bioreactors—have been shown to enhance EV yield and bioactivity relative to conventional two-dimensional (2D) monolayers [4–8]. However, the degree to which 3D culture modulates EV phenotype and function depends strongly on cell source and microenvironment.

The human placenta represents an ethically accessible and abundant source of MSCs—obtained from both fetal (e.g., Wharton's jelly) and maternal (e.g., decidua basalis) regions—with superior proliferative and regenerative capabilities compared to adult MSCs. [9, 10]. When produced under GMP conditions, placenta-derived MSCs (hPSCs) and their EVs hold strong potential for clinical translation [11]. Yet, hPSCs and their EVs remain underexplored compared with adult MSCs, particularly regarding the influence of donor variability and 3D culture on EV potency.

Most published work on MSC-EVs evaluates immunomodulation in simplified monolayer or rodent models, which fail to recapitulate human airway microenvironments or the complex epithelial–macrophage interactions that drive lung inflammation. Further, while many studies have shown that MSC/MSC-EVs from 3D cultures yield more vesicles and may have enhanced immunomodulatory potential, none have combined: GMP-compatible, scalable MSC-EV production, donor heterogeneity as a biological variable, and functional testing in fully human, disease-relevant 3D models. This gap limits the translational relevance of preclinical findings.

Here, we address these limitations by: (i) establishing GMP-compatible 2D and 3D microcarrier culture systems for scalable production of hPSCs and their EVs; (ii) performing a systematic side-by-side comparison of 2D vs 3D microcarrier culture of placenta-derived MSCs (hPSCs) and their EVs, including quantitative yield and molecular phenotyping, across multiple donors, and (iii) assessing the immunomodulatory activities of both hPSCs and their EVs in advanced, fully human 3D airway models of cystic fibrosis (CF) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

This is, to our knowledge, the first study to integrate GMP-compatible EV manufacture with human 3D airway disease models of CF and ARDS, enabling donor- and compartment-specific evaluation of EV immunomodulation. These data provide new insights into the context-dependent nature of hPSC/EV immune regulation and highlight donor heterogeneity as a key determinant for EV potency screening in clinical translation.

Methods

Placenta procurement and quality assurance

Placentas were collected from consenting healthy donors (n = 8) undergoing cesarean delivery, in compliance with Lithuanian Bioethics Committee guidelines (BE-2-105) and European Pharmacopoeia standards. Donors completed a medical survey, and blood samples were tested for infections (HBV, HCV, HIV I/II, T. pallidum). All reagents used were of clinical grade, with traceability maintained throughout. hPSCs were monitored for cell count, viability, doubling time (PDT, formula: PD = ln(2) × culture time (days)/ln[cell harvest number/initial cell number seeded]), and cumulative population doublings (cPD). Sterility (Ph. Eur. 2.6.27, automated system BD BACTECTM), mycoplasma (Ph. Eur. 2.6.14, validated real-time PCR (qPCR) TaqMan®-based qPCR assay Venor®GeM qEP), and endotoxin tests (Ph. Eur. 2.6.14, automated kinetic chromogenic Limulus Amoebocyte Lysate test Endosafe NexGen-PTS) were conducted as per Ph. Eur. guidelines. Chromosomal stability from the second and fourth passages was verified via G-banding.

hPSCs isolation and culture

To extract hPSCs following cGMP guidelines, the amniotic and chorionic layers, as well as the umbilical cord, were discarded, and the remaining placental tissue was sliced into 2–4 mm pieces and washed in Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Gibco). Tissue was enzymatically digested for 2 h at 37 °C in 0.1% collagenase (NB6, Nordmark) and DNase solution (Pierce Nuclease, Thermo Fisher), filtered, and seeded in MEM with 10% FBS. Cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, with medium changes every 3 days. Subconfluent cells were passaged using TrypLE Select.

Initialization and maintenance of hPSCs in 3D microcarrier cultures

Cytodex 1 microcarriers (MCs, Cytiva) were sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min and preconditioned by incubating with hPSC culture medium and incubated for at least 4 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Only siliconized (Sigmacote, Sigma-Aldrich) glass equipment was used during hPSC handling to prevent hPSC adsorption. Bioreactor cultures were inoculated with 25 × 10⁶ hPSCs (3-cell/bead ratio) and subjected to intermittent agitation ranging from 5 to 25 min. After 24 h, medium was replenished and agitation increased. Culture was maintained with regular medium changes and sampling.

Cellular and metabolic assessments of hPSCs in 3D microcarrier cultures

Trypan Blue exclusion and DNA-based counts assessed proliferation. Metabolic Analysis was performed at the same time as the media change. Glucose consumption (Accu-Check Active glucometer) and osmolarity (SN:15111109 B, Advanced Micro-Osmometer, Model 3320, Advanced Instruments) were monitored. Flow cytometry was used to phenotype cells. Briefly, 1 × 106 hPSCs were washed in FACS buffer consisting of DPBS supplemented with 1% BSA; and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark with the following fluorochrome-conjugated primary antibodies: anti-CD31-APC, anti-CD45-FITC, anti-CD73-PE, anti-CD90-PE, and anti-HLA-DR-FITC (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). After incubation, the cells were washed with FACS buffer and analyzed using a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer. Unstained cells (shown in green) were used do determine positivity and autofluorescence cell background. Data acquisition and analysis of relative fluorescence intensity were performed using BD FACSDiva software version 8.0.1 (BD Immunocytometry Systems, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Analysis of PBMC proliferation and cytokine secretion upon hPSC co-incubation

The peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) co-culture assay was performed as described by Oliver-Vila et al. [12]. PBMCs from healthy donors (n = 3) isolated by density gradient centrifugation were CFSE-labeled using the CellTrace™ CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (#C34554, Invitrogen™) and co-cultured with hPSCs at ratios of 1:10, 1:5, and 1:2.5 (hPSCs:PBMCs). Proliferation was stimulated using PMA/ionomycin (at 0.250 μg/ml and 0.5 μM, respectively) and assessed by flow cytometry after 5 days. Fluorescence was analyzed using FlowJo™ software version 10.10.0. Gating was used to remove debris on the FSC/SSC scatter plot and to select single cells on FSC-H/FSC-A scatter plot. Chondrocytes were used as negative controls. Normalized proliferation was calculated as described by Vila et al. [12]. Cell-free supernatants from PBMC assays were collected and tested for TNF-α and IL-6 by ELISA according to the manufacturer's protocol (TNF-α: #NBP-83741, and IL-6: #QK206, both Biotechne).

hPSC harvest

Harvest of the hPSCs from the microcarriers was performed by incubation of the cell-coated beads for 15 min in Tryple Select solution with agitation at 100 rpm and additional accelerations every 5 s. After neutralization, the cell/microcarrier suspension was filtered through a 150 µm filter and centrifuged for 5 min at a speed of 300 g. Cell pellet was washed twice with PBS to remove microcarrier debris and medium components prior to further analysis.

EV harvest

The culture medium was changed to EV-free serum-conditioned medium 48 h before harvesting and subsequent EVs purification. Regular or EV-free serum-conditioned medium was collected and centrifuged for 15 min. at 2000 g at 4 °C to remove eventual microcarrier debris, filtered through a 0.22 µm mPES filter and concentrated 30 times using a 500 kDa tangential flow filtration filter (Sartorius AGy) in the KrosFlo KR2i system (Repligen Corporation). The flow rate was set at 36 ml/min. with a transmembrane pressure of 3 psi. Concentrated medium was diafiltered with five volumes of DPBS, sterilized through a 0,22 µm mPES filter, further concentrated using 100 kDa cut-off Amicon filters between 40–50 times, and stored at -80 °C until further analysis or in vitro use.

f-NTA analysis of EVs

EVs size distribution profiles and concentration were measured on the ZetaView PMX220 (Particle Metrix) instrument equipped with a 488 and 640 nm laser and ZetaView 8.05.11 SP4 Software, according to a previously established protocol [13] In brief, the measurements in scatter mode were performed at RT at 11 positions in 2 cycles with the following settings: – Sensitivity: 80, Shutter: 100, Minimal Brightness: 30, Tracelength: 15 Min Area: 10 Max Area: 1000 nm/Class: 5, Classes/Decade: 64, Resolution: Medium. Fluorescence labeling was performed using prediluted EVs and prediluted antibody or the lipid membrane-specific dye CellMask Deep Red (CMDR, Invitrogen) in a total volume of 10 µL for 2 h at RT in the dark. Then the EVs were further diluted in PBS (usually 1: 1000) and measured on NTA at RT at 11 positions in 1 cycle with the following settings: for staining with antibodies—F488, Sensitivity: 95, Shutter: 100, Minimal Brightness: 25, Tracelength: 7 Min Area: 10 Max Area: 1000 nm/Class: 5, Classes/Decade: 64, Resolution: Medium; for staining with CMDR—F640, Sensitivity: 91, Shutter: 100, Minimal Brightness: 25, Tracelength: 7 Min Area: 10 Max Area: 1000 nm/Class: 5, Classes/Decade: 64, Resolution: Medium. All immuno-labeled samples were first evaluated in the fluorescence mode with the function "low bleach" on, immediately followed by evaluation in the scatter mode to minimize photo-bleaching. At least 3 measurements of each sample were performed. For following functional assays, EV doses were normalized to CD81⁺ particle numbers as quantified by fluorescence NTA; however, CD81⁺ vesicles were not physically purified, and EV preparations therefore contained heterogeneous vesicle populations including CD81⁻ EVs and vesicles with variable size, marker expression, and membrane integrity.

Analysis of PBMC proliferation and T-cell activation upon hPSC and EVs co-incubation

hPSCs were plated in 48-well flat-bottom plates in RPMI-medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin/streptomycin and 2% glutamine, at a cell density of 3 × 104 or 6 × 104 cells per well, to ensure a 1:5 and 1:2.5 hPSC to PBMC ratio (after adding 1.5 × 105 PBMCs per well), respectively. The next day PBMCs were labeled with Cell Trace Violet dye (5 μM final concentration, #C10044, Invitrogen™) according to the manufacturer’s manual and added to the plated hPSCs or to an empty well (EV treatment only) and stimulated with Dynabeads® Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). hPSCs-derived EVs of the corresponding placenta donors were normalized based on the f-NTA quantification of CD81 + particles, which we considered as “true” EVs. Based on these measurements, we added EVs at a ratio of 4.5 × 107 EVs per 1 × 106 PBMCs (1:300 PBMC/EV ratio). The 3 (PL3-PL5 for hPSCs co-incubation) to 5 (PL1-PL5 for EV co-incubation) tested placenta donors were biological replicates that were measured in 2 technical replicates each. T-cell activation and proliferation were analyzed by flow cytometry after 5 days. A different antibody mixture was prepared for each staining panel, assessing T cell activation and Treg induction, respectively. The PBMCs were incubated in a total volume of 100 μl of flow buffer (PBS/1% BSA) with the appropriate antibody mixture for 20 min in the dark at RT. Then, the cells were centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at RT. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed with 2 mL of flow buffer and centrifuged again at 310 × g for 5 min at RT, resuspended in 100 μl of flow buffer and analyzed on the Cellstream instrument. In preliminary experiments, the percentage of dead cells was assessed as being marginal, using the Zombie Aqua dye (#423102, Biolegend) according to manufacturer’s manual. Therefore, in the following experiments this step was omitted since the dye impeded correct fluorochrome compensation on the CellStream instrument. All used antibodies and dyes are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For the analysis of T cell activation, the cells were first gated on lymphocytes on the FCS/SSC dot plot, then on single cells (FSC-H/FCS-A plot), then on CD3-positive T cells and finally on CD4- and CD8-positive T cells. For the analysis of Treg induction, the cells were first gated on lymphocytes on the FCS/SSC dot plot, then on single cells (FSC-H/FCS-A plot), then on CD3-positive T cells and CD4-positive T cells and then on CD25high and CD127low T-cells, which were considered as Tregs. Percentages of proliferating cells or percentages of selected populations and MFIs of selected markers were calculated using the FlowJo Software v10.0.0 (Becton Dickinson).

Analysis of the immunomodulatory potential of hPSCs and their EVs in a human 3D cystic fibrosis or ARDS model the 3D (SmallAir™-M-EV)

The SmallAir™ model (Epithelix, Geneva, Switzerland) is a fully humanized 3D in vitro model of a fully differentiated and functional lower airway epithelium containing ciliated, goblet, club and basal cells cultured at an air‒liquid interface [14] and was used by us to model inflammatory lung diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis and ARDS. In the case of the cystic fibrosis (CF) model the airway epithelial cells (AECs) were obtained from a CF patient (homozygous F508del mutation), whereas in the case of the ARDS models the AECs were from a healthy donor. A coculture of both of these commercially available in vitro cell models with healthy primary human airway epithelium-alveolar macrophages (SmallAir™-M) was established by seeding 20 000 macrophages on the apical side of each epithelial tissue and incubating them (37 °C, 5% CO2, 100% humidity) for 24 h to recapitulate the in vivo conditions even more closely. Following macrophage seeding, the basal medium in each insert was replaced with 600 µL of fresh medium supplemented with TNF-α (15 ng/mL, for the CF model) or LPS (5 µg/mL for the ARDS model) for 12 h. 100 000 hPSCs cells were seeded in 24-well plates in 600 µl of SmallAir™ culture medium (#EP65SA, Epithelix) 24 h before the co-incubation. For co-incubation cultures of both models, the transwells containing the AECs and macrophages were transferred to the plates containing the seeded hPSCs and corresponding EVs were administered to the apical and basal side of the cultures in amounts normalized based on the f-NTA quantification of CD81, what corresponded approximately to the median total EV amounts released in 48 h by approximately 100 000 hPSCs in the bioreactor culture (as measured by NTA) The 3 (PL6-PL8) tested placenta donors were biological replicates, that were measured in 4 technical replicates each. The cultures were incubated in SmallAir™ medium for 60 h. hPSC- or EV-untreated SmallAir™-M cultures served as the control. (Fig. 11A). After the co-culture, the basal medium from each sample was collected and the apical side was washed with 200 µl of SmallAir medium for 20 min at 37 °C (apical medium).

Multiplex cytokine assay and cytotoxicity test

Cytokine concentrations in PBMC and 3D airway model supernatants were quantified using the LEGENDplex™ bead-based immunoassay (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Supernatants from the PBMC/hPSC/EV-co-cultures were analyzed via the LegendPlex Human CD8/NK Panel (#740809, LOT B409342, 13-plex) and supernatants from the CF and ARDS models were analyzed via the LegendPlex Human Inflammation Panel (#740809, LOT B370664, 13-plex) and the LegendPlex Human Macrophage/Microglia Panel (only apical medium, #740503, LOT B347870, 13-plex). LOT- and assay-specific LODs (limit of detection values) for each tested cytokine are listed in tables in Supplementary Materials. For each condition, four (PBMC assay) to six (3D models) technical replicates were measured and used to calculate mean cytokine levels after exclusion of technical outliers (see details in Statistical analysis and heatmap generation). Biological replicates were defined as independent donor placentas (hPSC lines), each expanded and processed separately. All samples were run on the Cytek CellStream cytometer (Cytek, software version 1.5.17), acquiring raw bead fluorescence using standard LEGENDplex settings. Cytokines were quantified by analyzing the obtained fcs files with the dedicated BioLegend’s LEGENDplex™ data analysis software (Version 9.0). The software automatically performed bead identification, standard curve fitting (5-parameter logistic regression), and concentration calculation for each analyte. Quality control steps included removal of values below the LOD and visual inspection of bead separation and standard curve fit in the software. Mean concentrations were exported for downstream analysis.

Cell cytotoxicity was evaluated by measuring LDH activity in the basal medium via an LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (#04 744 926 001; Roche Diagnostics) following the manufacturer’s instructions (n = 3 placenta donors, 3 technical repetitions). The low control was medium from the SmallAir™-M coculture treated with PBS instead of EVs. The positive control consisted of a sample of an equal number of lysed airway epithelial cells. The level of specific cytotoxicity was calculated according to the following formula: ([abs 492 nm experimental—abs 492 nm low control])/[abs 492 nm positive control- abs 492 nm low control]) × 100.

Statistical analysis and heatmap generation

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software). For the quantitative analysis of the NTA data, mean particle numbers of 3 technical replicates of n = 5 placenta donors were assessed. The mean peak size (Mode) was chosen as the particle size for most samples. Where there was no mode, the median (X50) was used for calculations. Group differences of independent samples were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, while Dunn’s post-hoc correction was applied for pairwise comparisons.

According to the functional assays, biological replicates corresponded to independent hPSC donors and their matched EV preparations (i.e., different placentas), while technical replicates referred to repeated flow cytometry measurements within each biological sample. Technical outliers were identified using the ROUT method with Q = 1% in GraphPad Prism. Outliers corresponded to clear assay artifacts (e.g., bead count < 300, standard curve failure, values below LOD or raw concentration values outside expected assay performance). Outliers were removed prior to calculating means and fold-change values.

For heatmap generation, cytokines were included only if they were detectable in all biological replicates in any group and were above LOD. Fold changes were calculated relative to the hPSC/EV untreated control: bead-stimulated PBMCs (PBMC co-incubation assay), TNFα stimulated control (CF model) and LPS-stimulated control (ARDS model), respectively—measured within the same experimental plate. When cytokine ranges spanned orders of magnitude that impeded visualization within a single heatmap, log2-transformed fold changes were used. Because the heatmaps served as descriptive summaries of cytokine directionality rather than inferential comparisons, no statistical testing or multiple-comparison correction was applied to fold-change values. For statistical analysis, raw cytokine concentrations (pg/mL) were analyzed instead. Four to six technical replicates were averaged to generate one value per biological replicate (donor). Because cytokine measurements were obtained from matched biological donors across all conditions, technical replicates were averaged and statistical differences were assessed using the non-parametric Friedman test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction. When some values were missing due to technical outlier removal, a mixed-effects model (REML) was used with Holm-Šídák's multiple comparisons test. Group analyses of the multiplex cytokine assay were performed separately for each cytokine. Corrected p-values are reported in Supplementary Tables 2S-9S.

At passage six, morphology remained unchanged. The viability of cells across passages P0 to P6 underscored the consistently high viability throughout the early passages, with a slight decrease observed at P6. Increase cell yield in 3D microcarrier systems compared to 2D cell factories

The initial cell densities in the 2D Cell Factories were consistent across replicates at 0.135 × 105 cells/mL from day 0 (Fig. 3B). By Day 3, we recorded a modest increase in cell densities to an average of 0.25 ± 0.02 × 105 cells/mL. By Day 6, the average density had risen to 0.73 ± 0.03 × 105 cells/mL; by Day 9, it had reached 1.375 ± 0.06 × 105 cells/mL. In contrast, the 3D microcarrier culture, allowing higher initial cell loading in comparison to 2D culture, was initiated with approximately 3.7 times higher cell densities on Day 0, at a uniform 0.5 × 105 cells/mL across all replicates. Thus, the increased cell numbers observed in 3D cultures represent an increased cumulative expansion capacity rather than an acceleration of cell cycle progression.

In our assessment of cellular glucose consumption, we observed distinct patterns between cells cultivated in 2D factories and those cultivated in 3D microcarriers (Fig. 3C). At the onset (Day 0), cells in 2D environments displayed glucose levels predominantly ranging between 1 and 1.4 mmol/L, settling at an approximate mean of 1.1 mmol/L. By Day 3, a discernible dip was noted, with consumption values at 0.82 ± 0.1 mmol/L. By Day 6, the consumption spectrum contracted, ranging from 0.6 to 0.9. On Day 9, the glucose levels in 2D cultures had substantially declined, averaging 0.42 ± 0.08 mmol/L. Conversely, the 3D microcarriers began with an almost parallel glucose consumption pattern on Day 0, averaging approximately 1.1. However, by Day 3, a more pronounced reduction was evident, with values at 0.57 ± 0.12 mmol/L. This downward trend persisted on Day 6, centering near the mean consumption of 0.5. By Day 9, the glucose consumption in these 3D cultures declined sharply, averaging 0.1 ± 0.09 mmol/L. Similarly, on Day 0, the osmolality for 2D cell factories was recorded at an average of 288.7 mOsm/kg, with a range of 278–295 mOsm/kg (Fig. 3D). On Day 3, the osmolality for 3D culture was comparable to 2D results, averaging 291.7 mOsm/kg. However, by Day 6, 3D culture exhibited a significantly higher concentration of solute particles at 312.5 mOsm/kg, and on Day 9, the osmolality readings ranged between 329–355 mOsm/kg.

3D hPSCs retain inhibitory effects on T-cell proliferation and inflammatory cytokine production

We examined the inhibitory effect of hPSCs on T-cell proliferation induced by polyclonal stimulation compared to untreated cells and chondrocyte controls. 3D-expanded hPSCs retained the robust inhibitory effects on T-cell proliferation and inflammatory cytokine production of 2D hPSCs, reported by us before [15, 16]. Addition of hPSCs inhibited the proliferation of PBMC treated with PMA/ionomycin in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A). The inclusion of chondrocytes did not affect T-cell proliferation (not shown). In addition, we investigated the ability of hPSCs to inhibit the generation of TNFα and IL-6 by T cells in response to stimuli. hPSCs displayed dose-dependent inhibition of TNFα and IL-6 production, with superior TNF- and IL-6 protein secretion-suppressing capacities (p = 0.0001) and enhanced capacity to reduce inflammation across all tested groups ( Fig. 4C and D). Discussion

Overview and novelty

This study offers an integrated comparison of placenta-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (hPSCs) and their extracellular vesicles (EVs), produced under 2D and 3D conditions, with a particular focus on immunomodulatory potential and donor variability. Our approach is novel in combining GMP-compatible 3D microcarrier culture with human disease-relevant 3D airway models that incorporate both epithelial and macrophage compartments. This platform enabled direct testing of EV function in physiologically relevant lung contexts, advancing beyond prior work restricted to rodent or single-cell-type systems. While prior studies have demonstrated the capacity of MSC-EVs to mimic many of their parent cells' therapeutic functions, our findings highlight notable functional heterogeneity and unexpected effects, particularly regarding pro-inflammatory cytokine induction.

EV yield and characterization: 2D vs. 3D cultures

While 2D culture for EV production fails to mimic the native cell environment, thereby probably impacting secretome composition, culturing MSCs in a 3D microenvironment enhances EV production, yielding vesicles with properties similar to in vivo EVs and improved therapeutic potential. For instance, human placental MSC-derived EVs [6] or umbilical cord MSC-derived EVs from a 3D spheroid [7] or hollow-fiber bioreactor culture [10] showed increased yields and anti-inflammatory and protective effects. Similarly, both cells and EVs from a human amnion-derived MSCs 3D culture secreted or contained higher amounts of angiogenic and immunosuppressive factors than their 2D counterparts [17]. Consistent with these studies, we demonstrated that 3D culture on microcarriers significantly increases cumulative hPSC yield and EV production (by approximately tenfold compared with 2D systems) while maintaining general vesicular identity. Importantly, the 3D microcarrier platform was operated under conditions optimized for scalable production, resulting in higher initial seeding densities relative to 2D monolayers. While this configuration yielded greater cumulative cell output, intrinsic proliferation rates were comparable between systems, indicating that dimensionality per se did not enhance cell cycle kinetics [10, 18, 19]. The higher metabolic rate and osmolality observed in 3D cultures probably reflect elevated cell density and nutrient consumption, underlining the need for process monitoring (glucose levels, osmolality) during scale-up. We therefore emphasize that the advantage of the 3D system lies in its capacity for scalable expansion rather than altered cellular proliferation behavior. Future studies incorporating density-matched reseeding strategies could further dissect the specific contributions of culture dimensionality independent of surface area availability; however, such experiments were beyond the scope of the current platform-focused comparison. Most importantly, we confirmed that 3D-cultured hPSCs retained immunosuppressive potency in PBMC co-incubation assays, supporting efficient cell proliferation and metabolic activity, thus offering a promising platform for large-scale MSC and EV production—an area still scarcely explored, particularly for hPSC-derived EVs.

Interestingly, although total EV particle numbers increased in 3D culture, the relative proportion of true EVs (CD81 + or CMDR +) was lower in 3D cultures, suggesting a larger fraction of heterogeneous or non-vesicular particles. Due to higher particle count in the 3D EV samples, the detection of a CD9 + population in all EV samples became possible. An open question remains whether the emergence of this new population of CD9-positive EVs is solely due to surpassing the lower detection limit of the equipment (as suggested by isolated cases of very low-level detection of CD9 + EVs in individual 2D-derived EV samples), or if it truly indicates that changes in culture conditions can influence the phenotype and cargo of secreted EVs. The proportions of CMDR + , CD81 + , CD63 + and CD9 + vesicles also varied among donors. Although isolating a homogeneous, functionally optimized EV subpopulation would represent an important translational advance, such refinement was beyond the scope of the present study. Limited remaining material and the unavailability of the original 3D airway and alveolar macrophage systems precluded additional refinement experiments without introducing non-comparable model systems. Notwithstanding, our findings demonstrate that clinically relevant, bulk EV preparations display substantial donor- and context-dependent functional diversity in complex human airway models. This highlights the necessity of future efforts aimed at EV subpopulation refinement and functional selection, ideally guided by advanced multicellular models that capture epithelial–immune cross-talk. Importantly, our data suggest that EV optimization strategies cannot rely on particle number or single-marker normalization alone, but must account for qualitative EV features shaped by culture conditions and donor-specific variability. This heterogeneity reinforces the importance of rigorous EV characterization prior to therapeutic application.

Donor variability as a biological determinant

A key observation was the pronounced donor-to-donor divergence not only in EV phenotype, but also in hPSCs and EV immunomodulatory activity. Only one (PL3) of five donor lines significantly inhibited PBMC proliferation, and cytokine modulation profiles varied markedly. Such donor variability—often overlooked in MSC research—represents a key challenge in MSC-based cell and EV therapies, influencing treatment outcomes across diseases [20]. MSCs and EVs from different donors or tissues can vary in growth, immune modulation, and therapeutic efficacy [21, 22], complicating standardization of treatment parameters such as dose and timing [23]. Rather than being random noise, such variability likely reflects intrinsic biological differences—possibly in placental region of origin, metabolic state, or baseline immunoregulatory gene expression. These findings argue that donor heterogeneity is a biologically meaningful variable that should inform donor screening and potency assays in EV manufacturing pipelines.

The stronger inhibition of PBMC proliferation observed with 2D-expanded hPSCs compared to 3D-expanded cells may reflect culture-induced priming effects rather than intrinsic differences in immunomodulatory capacity. MSCs are highly sensitive to substrate mechanics, and exposure to rigid 2D culture surfaces induces sustained cytoskeletal tension and YAP/TAZ signaling [24], which has been linked to enhanced IDO-mediated immunosuppression [25]. In contrast, microcarrier-based expansion may preferentially support proliferative expansion, rapid metabolic activity and EV production, potentially shifting cells toward a growth-optimized phenotype. Additionally, culture configuration is known to influence EV cargo composition, with 2D expansion enriching stress- and inflammation-associated miRNAs, whereas 3D bioreactor conditions may promote more trophic or regenerative EV profiles [26]. Finally, differences in harvesting procedures may affect surface expression of contact-dependent immunoregulatory molecules like ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 [27], further contributing to functional divergence in PBMC assays. In fact, Kusuma et al. have shown that EVs from 3D MSC-cultures did not enhance, as expected, the immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory MSC-EV properties expected from 2D cultures [28]. Importantly, these interpretations are mechanistically plausible but not experimentally validated within the present study.

Regarding EVs specifically, although EV dosing was normalized to CD81⁺ particle numbers, our data indicate that EV bioactivity cannot be attributed to particle number alone. Instead, functional outcomes are likely shaped by the combined properties of heterogeneous EV subpopulations, including qualitative features such as membrane integrity and surface marker composition. Due to the limited number of biological donors, we did not perform multivariate analyses linking individual EV characteristics to functional readouts; however, the observed donor-to-donor variability highlights the need for future subpopulation-resolved and systems-level approaches to EV characterization.

Context-dependent immunomodulation

While numerous in vivo studies support MSC and MSC-EV immunomodulatory functions, in vitro effects on PBMCs remain controversial. Some reports show MSC-EVs suppress T-cell proliferation, enhance Treg generation, and inhibit Th1 polarization [29, 30], whereas others describe weaker effects than parental MSCs [31–33] or no suppression at all [34, 35]. Papait et al. demonstrated that the EV-free fraction of hAMSC secretome, but not EVs, inhibited T-cell proliferation and Th1 polarization and induced Tregs and M2 monocytes despite confirmed EV uptake [36]. Similarly, Andrews et al. found that MSCs suppressed T-cell activation regardless of priming condition, while MSC-EVs had modest effects except for Treg induction after acidosis priming [37]. Such discrepancies highlight the complexity of EV immunobiology, likely driven by donor variability and microenvironmental influences on EV cargo.

Consistent with these reports, our hPSC-derived EVs seem to exert their immunosuppressive effects on T cells differently from their parental hPSCs, namely by inhibiting T-cell activation (CD69 expression) and promoting Treg expansion. The stronger impact of hPSCs manifested by suppression of T cell proliferation may implicate broader immunomodulation mechanisms. Direct T-cell–MSC contact, mediated for example by ICAM-1, PD-L1, HLA-G1, CD40, and VCAM-1 [38], has been shown to be necessary for Treg induction and CTLA-4 expression [39, 40].

Co-culture of activated PBMCs with placenta-derived MSCs (pMSCs) increased Granzyme A, sFas, and sFasL, along with a strong IL-6 upregulation. This profile suggests that pMSCs do not merely suppress immune activity but modulate it through activation-induced cell death (AICD) and cytokine-driven regulation. Elevated sFas and sFasL indicate engagement of apoptotic pathways that restrain overactivated T cells. Akiyama et al. showed FasL on MSCs induces transient T-cell apoptosis via Fas/FasL signaling [41], while Yu et al. reported high FasL expression on pMSCs and partial reversal of their suppressive effect by anti-FasL antibodies [42] FasL-overexpressing MSCs induced extensive splenocyte apoptosis, enhancing immunosuppressive efficacy [43]. Increased Granzyme A likely reflects persistent cytotoxic activity. Cytokines such as IFNγ and TNFα from activated T cells may “license” MSCs, boosting IL-6 secretion—a pleiotropic cytokine with both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects. IL-6 from decidua-derived MSCs was shown to enhance PD-L1/PD-L2 expression and immunomodulatory potency in a GVHD model [44]. Our strong IL-6 induction aligns with these findings, potentially contributing to Treg expansion and monocyte polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, as indicated by elevated IL-10. Collectively, pMSCs appear to shape immune responses dynamically rather than uniformly suppressing them, balancing inflammation and repair through IL-6–mediated regulation and Fas/FasL–dependent apoptosis.

In contrast, hPSC-derived EVs induced a mixed cytokine response—upregulating both pro-inflammatory (IL-2, IFNγ, TNFα) and anti-inflammatory (IL-4, IL-6, IL-10) mediators while downregulating cytotoxic factors such as granulysin and granzyme B. This pattern suggests immune modulation rather than suppression: attenuating cytotoxicity while promoting Th2/regulatory pathways. Such balanced regulation may be beneficial in disorders like GVHD or autoimmunity, where excessive immune activation is pathogenic but complete suppression is undesirable.

Compartment-specific effects in 3D airway models

Using advanced 3D co-culture models, we observed donor-, compartment- and disease-specific divergences between hPSCs and their EVs. In CF, hPSCs slightly reduced epithelial TNFα secretion, whereas EVs modestly increased it; both treatments elevated IL-6 and IL-8, indicating mild epithelial activation. In macrophages, however, EVs—but not hPSCs—induced strong cytokine secretion, particularly of IL-10 and ARG1 (up to 25-fold), consistent with possible M2 polarization [45–47].

In ARDS, differences were even more pronounced. hPSCs upregulated epithelial IL-6 and IL-10, whereas EVs suppressed TNFα, IFNγ, and IL-6. In macrophages, EVs promoted IL-10, IL-1RA, and ARG1, while hPSCs induced only modest changes. Thus, EVs exerted stronger anti-inflammatory activity in ARDS, whereas hPSCs showed broader but less predictable effects.

These findings highlight disease- and compartment-specific divergence in MSC versus EV signaling, likely reflecting fundamental biological differences between whole-cell and vesicl-mediated immunoregulation. Unlike hPSCs, which continuously sense inflammatory cues and secrete both soluble mediators and vesicular cargo, isolated EVs represent a selectively packaged and temporally fixed subset of the MSC secretome. Consequently, EV-driven signaling may amplify specific pathways while lacking soluble suppressive factors present in co-culture with live cells, resulting in distinct or even opposing cytokine signatures. For example, MSCs may neutralize TNFα through soluble receptors or contact-dependent mechanisms, while EVs might deliver pro- or anti-inflammatory cargoes, including miRNAs or proteins. Prior reports confirm that EV phenotypes can differ substantially from their parent cells [20, 33, 48, 49].

Co-administration of hPSCs and EVs did not yield additive or synergistic effects and occasionally attenuated individual responses, possibly due to competing signaling mechanisms. Sequential rather than simultaneous administration may improve efficacy. These findings highlight that EV effects are microenvironment-dependent—potentially beneficial in hyperinflammatory macrophage settings but variable in epithelial compartments. While such dichotomy complicates universal claims of “superior” EV activity, it also underscores their precision-modulating potential in targeted contexts. While hPSCs dynamically respond to their microenvironment through cell–cell contact and inducible cytokine secretion, EVs represent a snapshot of the donor- and culture-condition–specific molecular cargo at the time of release. As a result, EVs may elicit more compartment-restricted or cell-type-specific responses, such as the pronounced ARG1 induction observed in macrophages, without recapitulating the broader and more variable effects of live cells.

EV-mediated ARG1 induction in macrophages: plausible molecular pathways

The pronounced induction of ARG1 in the macrophage compartment following exposure to hPSC-derived EVs represents one of the most consistent trends observed in this study. While mechanistic interrogation was beyond the scope of the present work, several EV-associated pathways known to promote alternative macrophage activation may plausibly contribute to this phenotype [50]. MSC-derived EVs have been shown to transfer immunoregulatory miRNAs targeting TLR4/NF-κB, PTEN/STAT5, and PI3K/AKT signaling. Notably, MSC-EVs enhanced STAT5 phosphorylation and ARG1 expression via the miR-181a-5p–PTEN–pSTAT5–SOCS1 axis, attenuating inflammatory cytokine production in ARDS-relevant macrophage models and in vivo lung injury [51]. Additional miRNAs implicated in EV-driven M2 polarization include let-7b [52] and miR-146a [53]. Beyond miRNA transfer, EVs can induce metabolic reprogramming through mitochondrial delivery, enhancing oxidative phosphorylation and fatty-acid oxidation—metabolic states required for M2 polarization and ARG1 expression [54, 55]. MSC-EVs may also act via direct protein cargo, including TGF-β, C1q, and semaphorins [56] as well as ARG1 itself. We previously demonstrated that ovarian cancer–derived EVs contain enzymatically active ARG1 that can be transferred to recipient immune cells, where it suppresses dendritic cell antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell proliferation through local L-arginine depletion [57]. A conceptually similar mechanism has been described independently in metabolic disease, where erythrocyte-derived EVs transfer ARG1 to endothelial cells, contributing to endothelial dysfunction in type 2 diabetes [58]. Together, these observations raise the possibility that hPSC-derived EVs may directly deliver ARG1 protein to macrophages, thereby contributing to the ARG1 upregulation observed here—a hypothesis that will require targeted EV cargo profiling and functional validation in future studies.

Functional limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the number of biological replicates was limited (n = 3–5 placenta donors), resulting in low statistical power; therefore, most functional observations should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than definitive effects. Second, our functional conclusions rely primarily on cytokine profiling. Although these readouts are informative, they do not directly confirm downstream functional consequences such as macrophage polarization, epithelial barrier restoration, or Treg suppressive capacity. Mechanistic analyses of EV cargo and culture-induced cellular states were beyond the scope of the present study. The combination of limited sample availability and resource constraints restricted comprehensive molecular profiling, precluding causal attribution to specific molecular determinants. Furthermore, although hPSCs were separated from microcarriers prior to downstream assays and EV preparations underwent standard purification steps, future manufacturing-validation studies should include acellular microcarrier-conditioned medium controls to fully exclude potential background effects from culture materials. Although several outcomes displayed coherent directional changes, the study is inherently underpowered, and thus most results should be interpreted as exploratory trends rather than conclusive effects. Future work integrating flow cytometric quantification of macrophage M1/M2 surface markers, functional Treg suppression assays and omics analyses will be necessary to delineate the mechanisms underlying these immunomodulatory patterns. Third, EV preparations were heterogeneous by design, reflecting clinically realistic manufacturing conditions rather than purified subpopulations. Finally, while the 3D airway models provide high physiological relevance, their cost and complexity limited experimental scalability. Although we observed opposing (or divergent) effects of hPSCs and EVs under certain conditions, these cannot be interpreted as universal rules. Together, these constraints underscore that the present study is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, aimed at identifying context-dependent patterns rather than establishing deterministic therapeutic rules.

Conclusions and translational implications

In summary, this study provides a systematic comparative analysis of hPSCs and their EVs across donors, culture formats, and target cell compartments using physiologically relevant human immune and airway models. Rather than identifying a universally optimal immunomodulatory condition, the data reveal pronounced donor variability, strong context dependence, and clear divergence between cell- and EV-mediated effects. While most cytokine changes should be interpreted as trend-level observations due to limited statistical power, the robust and consistent induction of ARG1 in the macrophage compartment emerged as a biologically meaningful finding that warrants further mechanistic investigation. While broader conclusions must be made cautiously, the pronounced ARG1 upregulation provides a compelling indication of EV-driven macrophage polarization.

Importantly, this work represents the first integrated evaluation of hPSC-derived EVs produced under GMP-compatible 3D conditions and functionally tested in human 3D airway disease models. The divergent cytokine signatures elicited by hPSCs versus their EVs underscore that these entities constitute distinct immunomodulatory modalities: hPSCs combine soluble factor secretion with direct cell–cell interactions, whereas EVs act as acellular, cargo-dependent signaling units whose effects are highly target- and context-specific. Collectively, our findings argue against one-size-fits-all assumptions of immunosuppressive efficacy and emphasize the need for donor-aware, model-appropriate functional testing.

From a translational perspective, our results emphasize three key messages: Scalability and GMP compliance: The 3D microcarrier system supports efficient hPSC and EV production suitable for clinical manufacturing. Donor screening necessity: EV compositional heterogeneity, together with donor-dependent functional divergence, must be addressed through potency assays and standardized selection criteria. Context-dependent efficacy: EV-based immunomodulation is highly model-specific, underscoring the need for physiologically relevant human testing platforms. By linking scalable EV manufacturing to human-relevant functional readouts, this study contributes to a more nuanced and translationally grounded framework for EV-based immunotherapies.
