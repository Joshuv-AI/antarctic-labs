ECAS-Based Neuropsychological Phenotyping in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: A Retrospective Study Comparing Different Algorithms

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to compare different algorithms based on the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS) to classify patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) according to their neuropsychological phenotype to identify possible discrepancies among these systems.MethodsECAS-Cognitive and -Carer Interview (ECAS-C/-CI) scores of N = 901 patients with ALS without a formal diagnosis of dementia were retrospectively retrieved. Patients were classified, pursuant to Strong et al.’s criteria, as cognitively and behaviourally normal (ALScbn), cognitively and/or behaviourally impaired (ALSci/bi/cbi), or Possible ALS-FTD, according the following ECAS-based algorithms: (1) Abrahams’, solely addressing ECAS-C total and ALS-Specific subtotals; (2) Poletti et al.’s, addressing single task-level ECAS-C scores; (3) “Subscale”, addressing ECAS-C subscales (i.e., Language, Executive, Fluency, Memory and Visuospatial). Introduction

The Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS) [1] is the most widespread disease-specific screening method for frontotemporal-spectrum neuropsychological disorders in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [2]. The ECAS has been proposed as a useful tool for classifying patients with ALS according to Strong et al.’s [3] revised criteria, which identify four classifications: cognitively and behaviourally normal (ALScbn), cognitively and/or behaviourally impaired (ALSci/bi/cbi) and ALS with frontotemporal dementia (ALS-FTD).

To date, two algorithms have been proposed for operationalizing Strong’s criteria via the ECAS: the first is by Abrahams [4] and only addresses the total and subtotal level scores on the cognitive section of the ECAS for identifying cognitive impairment (i.e., besides the total score, the ALS-Specific and ALS-Nonspecific subtotals); the second, by Poletti et al. [5], classifies patients’ cognitive status by addressing single-item-level scores on the cognitive section of the ECAS. For behavioural classifications, both systems employ the ECAS-Carer Interview (ECAS-CI) [6], a semi-structured interview addressed to patients’ caregivers that focuses on FTD-like behavioural changes. A third, intermediate possibility, which, however, to the best of the authors' knowledge, has not been explored yet, is to address subscale-level scores on the cognitive section of the ECAS for classifying patients’ cognitive status (i.e., the Language, Fluency, Executive, Memory and Visuospatial subscales).

Theoretically, these three classification systems might have advantages and disadvantages in two respects, namely, (1) the ease of use and (2) the degree of information provided. Whilst Abraham’s algorithm is probably the easiest to apply, as users only need to look at three cognitive scores, it might also yield the least detail about patients’ cognitive profiles. On the other hand, Poletti’s system provides detailed information on patients’ cognitive status, requiring users to consider each cognitive task from the cognitive section of the ECAS (N = 15). The Subscale approach, however, provides a good balance, being easy to use, addressing several cognitive measures (N = 5), and providing information on patients’ cognitive profiles, allowing the determination of which specific sets of cognitive domains/functions are involved. The ECAS is structured to assess ALS-related cognitive domains. Compared with global or composite scores, subscale-level analysis provides more domain-specific information by identifying which cognitive functions are selectively impaired while remaining more interpretable than single-test level analyses. Importantly, ECAS subdomains have been shown to map onto the regional distribution of TDP-43 pathology in extramotor brain areas, supporting their neurobiological relevance [7].

Despite these considerations, to our knowledge, no study to date has compared the classifications yielded by the three algorithms. This study thus aimed to compare three different ECAS-based approaches to classify patients with ALS according to Strong’s phenotypes. Therefore, we addressed the Italian version of the cognitive section of the ECAS, given that it comes with cut-offs not only for the total and subtotals but also for each of the subscales and individual tasks [5].

Methods

This retrospective cohort comprised 901 patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) without a concurrent, formal clinical diagnosis of frontotemporal dementia (FTD) or other dementias who were consecutively referred to three Northern Italian centers (IRCCS Istituto Auxologico Italiano, Milan; Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta, Milan; City of Health and Science University Hospital of Turin, Torino) between 2016 and 2023 and for whom the results of the cognitive section of the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS) [8, 9] and the ECAS-Carer Interview (ECAS-CI) [6] were available. Participants provided informed consent, and data were treated according to current regulations. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of IRCCS Istituto Auxologico Italiano (ID: 2013_06_25), the Ethics Committee of Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta (ID: 71/2015; 2017/06/07, Excerpt 6/41; 2020/06/17, Excerpt 01/73; 2023/01/18, Excerpt 06/11) and the Ethics Committee of the ALS Expert Center of Torino, Azienda Ospedaliero Universitaria Città della Salute e della Scienza (ID: #0038876). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments.

The three algorithms (Abrahams, Poletti and Subscale) were applied to ECAS/ECAS-CI scores, and the frequencies and percentages for each Strong’s classification were calculated. The three algorithms were compared with each other via Bonferroni-corrected Cohen’s k coefficients (i.e., Poletti vs. Abrahams, Poletti vs. Subscale and Abrahams vs. Subscale).

Analyses were run via IBM® SPSS® Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., 2021) and jamovi 2.3 (the jamovi project, 2022).

Results

Agreement rates among these classifications were moderate-to-high (Poletti vs. Abrahams: Cohen’s k = 0.78, z = 42.42, p < 0.001, with an agreement rate of 84%; Poletti vs. Subscale: Cohen’s k = 0.78, z = 42.93, p < 0.001, with an agreement rate of 84%; Abrahams vs. Subscale: Cohen’s k = 0.81, z = 44.80, p < 0.001, with an agreement rate of 86%).

We then compared each of the proposed classification tools to find discrepancies (Fig. 2). Comparing the Poletti vs. Abrahams algorithms, categorizations were substantially consistent between the two regarding Possible ALS-FTD and ALSbi, with disagreement rates of ~ 5% and ~ 7%, respectively. In contrast, ~ 24% of patients classified as ALSci by Poletti’s algorithm were assigned to the ALScbn category by Abraham’s algorithm. Similarly, ~ 11% of patients classified as ALScbn by Poletti’s algorithm were assigned to the ALSci category by Abraham’s algorithm. The highest percentage of disagreement (~ 43%) was found for the ALScbi category, with 27% of patients classified as such by Poletti’s algorithm being classified in the Possible ALS-FTD category by Abraham’s algorithm and another ~ 16% being re-classified by Abraham’s algorithm as ALSbi.

Comparing the Poletti and Subscale algorithms, agreement rates across the five categories were high (from ~ 81% to ~ 92%), with the following disagreements being found: (1) ~ 15% of Poletti’s ALSbi patients were re-classified by the Subscale algorithm as ALScbi; (2) ~ 19% of Poletti’s ALScbn patients were re-classified as ALSci by the Subscale algorithm; similarly, ~ 13% of Poletti’s ALSci patients were allotted to the ALScbn category by the Subscale algorithm; (3) ~ 11% and ~ 7% of Poletti’s ALScbi patients were re-classified by the Subscale algorithm as ALSbi and Possible ALS-FTD, respectively.

Finally, the Abraham and Subscale algorithms showed excellent agreement for the ALSci and ALScbi categories (~ 94% and ~ 92%, respectively). Contrarily, ~ 23% and 1% of Abrahams’ Possible ALS-FTD patients were re-classified by the Subscale approach as ALScbi and ALSbi, respectively. Approximately 17% of patients classified as ALScbn by Abrahams’ algorithms were assigned to the ALSci category by the Subscale approach. Finally, ~ 12% of patients classified as having ALSbi by Abrahams’ algorithms were assigned to the ALScbi category by the Subscale approach.

Discussion

The present study provides practitioners and clinical researchers with data on how different Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen-based algorithms categorize patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) neuropsychological status based on Strong’s criteria for frontotemporal-spectrum disorders in this population.

This study suggests that different ECAS-based algorithms for operationalizing Strong’s categories, despite yielding overall consistent classifications on a large scale, might have slight discrepancies at the individual level. These can be summarized as follows: (1) “ALS cognitively and behaviourally normal (ALScbn)-to-ALS cognitively impaired (ALSci)” and “ALS cognitively impaired (ALSci)-to-ALS cognitively and behaviourally normal (ALScbn)” re-classifications occurred across the three comparisons, ranging from ~ 11% to ~ 24%; (2) the most classification disagreements (~ 43%) occurred in the ALS cognitively and behaviourally impaired (ALScbi) category when comparing single task-level (Poletti) to total-level (Abrahams) algorithms, with patients being re-classified as either ALS behaviourally impaired (ALSbi) or Possible ALS-frontotemporal dementia (ALS-FTD); (3) ~ 24% of Abraham’s Possible ALS-FTD cases were re-classified as either ALScbi or ALSbi by the Subscale approach. In contrast, the ALSbi classification yielded the least disagreement across the three algorithms, probably because the same ECAS-Carer Interview (ECAS-CI)-based algorithm was employed to determine behavioural impairment in each of the three approaches.

These differences in the three algorithms can be attributed, with a sufficient degree of certainty, to the obvious differences in the operationalization of cognitive impairment. Whilst Poletti’s algorithm looks at individual tasks from the cognitive section of the ECAS, the Subscale approach addresses ECAS-Language, -Fluency, -Executive, -Memory and -Visuospatial subscores, and Abrahams’ focuses on both the ECAS-Total and the two subtotals (ECAS-ALS-Specific and -ALS-Nonspecific).

The most noticeable discrepancies among those above may also have practical implications. First, the single task-level approach could detect cognitive impairment (i.e., ALSci) in ~ 24% patients who were classified as free of cognitive dysfunction (i.e., ALScbn) by Abrahams’ approach. This suggests that Poletti’s algorithm might be more sensitive to subtle cognitive changes, which, in contrast, might go undetected when only addressing the ECAS-Total and its ALS-Specific subtotal scores. Second, the ALScbi category yielded the most re-classifications in Poletti’s and Abraham’s algorithms, with these patients being classified by Abrahams’ approach as either ALSbi or Possible ALS-FTD. Given that behavioural status was classified in the same way by all the algorithms, the only possible explanation for this is the different operationalizations of Rascovsky et al’s [10] cognitive criterion, i.e., executive dysfunction with relatively spared memory and visuo-spatial functioning. In fact, in Abrahams’ approach, this criterion is operationalized as the presence of an abnormal ECAS-ALS-Specific score together with a normal ECAS-ALS-Nonspecific score, whilst Poletti’s relies on the performances on individual cognitive tasks (as described in Table 2).

Different algorithms may thus be employed for different purposes, namely, clinical vs. research-related. Within specialized clinical settings, we advocate that the Subscale approach be used, given that, due to its intermediate nature compared with Abrahams’ and Poletti’s algorithms, it is likely to provide an optimal balance between information and ease of use. The Subscale algorithm provides information on which cognitive domains are most involved, as in Poletti’s algorithm, but does not require examiners to look at each individual task from the cognitive section of the ECAS, thus possibly preventing both under- and overestimating the prevalence of a given category. In contrast, for research purposes, Poletti’s algorithm might be of interest, given that it faithfully applies Strong’s criteria by treating each task in the cognitive section of the ECAS as an individual test. Moreover, Abrahams’ algorithm is a highly practicable and clinically feasible approach that can be used whenever in-depth information on patients’ cognitive impairment patterns is not required, such as in non-specialized clinical settings.

This study has limitations. First, no gold-standard comparator was employed to explore the performance of each algorithm to correctly stratify patients with ALS based on their neuropsychological status. Hence, future investigations should compare ECAS-based classifications to those yielded by a second-level neuropsychological battery, which should include both domain- and function-specific cognitive measures and more detailed, caregiver-reported behavioural interviews/questionnaires that account for patients with ALS verbal-motor disabilities. Also, results may not be directly generalizable to other ECAS language versions, where differences in linguistic structure and subtest difficulty may influence performance. Moreover, the current study relied on a 2-SD-based normative framework for the cognitive section of the ECAS [8, 9], thus precluding inferences about how alternative approaches (e.g., regression-based methods) would have performed. Given that different statistical techniques for deriving norms have been shown to yield heterogeneous prevalence estimates of cognitive impairment in ALS [11, 12], further studies are needed to compare normative approaches across languages. In addition, as previously mentioned, Possible ALS-FTD classifications solely relied on psychometric outcomes and not on clinical history and/or information on functional independence in daily life. This represents a critical issue given that an ALS-FTD diagnosis requires evidence of progressive behavioural and/or cognitive deterioration (based on longitudinal observation or clinical history), adversely affecting patients’ functional independence. Finally, our ALS-FTD classification approach does not consider less frequent FTD variants, such as non-fluent or semantic variants of primary progressive aphasia.

Conclusions

Albeit overall consistent among each other on a large scale, different Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS)-based algorithms to derive Strong’s phenotypes might yield slight discrepancies that under- or overestimate a given classification. Users are therefore advised to exert caution when operationalizing Strong’s categories via the ECAS based on their specific needs.

Datasets associated with the present study cannot be made publicly available because they include sensitive information, but can be made available upon reasonable request of interested researchers to the Corresponding Author, who will forward a data transfer agreement request to the relevant Ethics Committees. Raw data from the Corresponding Author's primary Institution have been stored on an online repository with restricted acess (10.5821/zenodo.20097944).
