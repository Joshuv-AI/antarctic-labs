Information anxiety across populations in the VUCA era: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Yuan Jiang, Normah Mustaffa, Jamaluddin Aziz, Xiaochun Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionInformation anxiety (IA) describes the distress caused by the gap between the information individuals have and what they feel they should possess. In the current digital environment—marked by volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity—IA has expanded beyond traditional academic and workplace contexts to become a pervasive concern across populations. Mapping the empirical evidence on IA is critical to understanding its prevalence, determinants, impacts and coping strategies.Methods and analysisThis protocol outlines a scoping review guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). We will systematically search EBSCOhost, Scopus and Web of Science for peer-reviewed empirical studies published from 1 January 2000 to the planned end date of 5 November 2025. Two reviewers will independently screen records, with a third resolving discrepancies. Data extraction will be conducted using a customised tool, and results will be synthesised narratively and visually, structured around bibliometric characteristics, the Population, Concept, Context framework and a Stimulus-Organism-Response model. Subgroup analyses will be conducted across populations, disciplines and regions.-ScREthics and disseminationAs this study is based on secondary analysis of published data, ethical approval is not required. Introduction

The concept of information anxiety (IA) was first introduced by Wurman,1 who defined it as the distress arising from the gap between the information individuals possess and the information they feel they should possess.2 With the proliferation of the internet and the acceleration of digitalisation, this phenomenon has evolved substantially. In the VUCA era—marked by volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity—particularly following the global COVID-19 pandemic, individuals’ exposure to vast, often contradictory information has amplified both the prevalence and intensity of IA.3

Empirical studies indicate that IA is shaped by both situational factors and individual characteristics.4 5 Excessive information exposure and high task demands have been consistently linked to heightened IA, often resulting in avoidance behaviours.6 7 Digital platforms, particularly social media, can further intensify IA by facilitating the rapid dissemination of misinformation and disinformation, thereby increasing users’ uncertainty and cognitive load.8 Moreover, IA manifests differently across demographic and occupational groups. For instance, female students generally report higher information-seeking anxiety than their male counterparts,9 10 and patterns of IA vary across age cohorts.11 Front-line healthcare workers, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, experienced distinct IA profiles due to elevated uncertainty and overwhelming information demands.12 Collectively, these findings suggest that IA not only affects cognitive processing and emotional responses but also has broader implications for self-efficacy, learning outcomes and professional performance.13 14

Over the past three decades, research across multiple disciplines has examined IA through diverse lenses. Early studies primarily focused on library anxiety and information-seeking anxiety, which fall under the broader IA umbrella.2 Contemporary perspectives increasingly define IA as ‘the discomfort or distress experienced when interacting with information in academic, professional and everyday contexts’,15 the stress or discomfort that typically occurs when individuals are unable to access, understand or effectively use the information they require.5 16 17 The types of information that trigger IA have continuously expanded alongside the evolution of the media environment. Transitioning from early, relatively singular sources primarily focused on library literature and traditional media content,1 18 it has evolved into a broad spectrum of information inputs in the modern context19—ranging from massive volumes of daily news and complex health-related data20 to conflicting workplace directives and academic materials,15 21 as well as rapidly disseminating social media content.22

Given that IA can manifest at various challenging stages of information processing, Naveed conceptualised it across six dynamic dimensions: task conceptualisation, need recognition, information retrieval, information evaluation, as well as information access and utilisation.5 This expanded conceptualisation underscores IA as an increasingly prominent, complex and multidimensional phenomenon with wide-ranging implications.

Since 1986, research on information-related anxiety has steadily increased, especially in the 21st century.23 To assess the overall scope of existing literature and to inform the development of this review protocol, a pilot search was conducted in July 2024 across Scopus and Web of Science, using titles, abstracts and keywords containing ‘information anxiety’, yielded 258 relevant articles. These records were imported into VOS viewer 1.6.20 (CWTS, Leiden, Netherlands) for bibliometric analysis. To ensure the timeliness and methodological rigour of the final review, a comprehensive and updated search will be conducted prior to formal data extraction. The search will cover the period from January 2000 to the month in which the search is conducted, in order to systematically capture and include the most recent studies published in this field.

To provide a clear theoretical boundary and resolve conceptual ambiguity, it is crucial to differentiate IA from related constructs. For instance, while ‘information overload’ primarily refers to the cognitive burden caused by excessive information,19 IA emphasises the emotional and psychological responses—such as stress, confusion and fear—arising from perceived information inadequacy, poor quality or high uncertainty during information exposure.1 5 Similarly, 'information-seeking anxiety’ represents a task-specific manifestation of IA,24 whereas anxiety related to misinformation and disinformation reflects context-specific triggers within digital environments.25

Based on these distinctions, this review operationally defines IA as a multidimensional construct that captures the emotional distress associated with information exposure across the stages of seeking, processing, evaluating and using information.23 This encompasses both general and context-specific forms of anxiety triggered by various types of information (eg, massive data volumes, complex technical knowledge or misinformation). This broader conceptualisation enables the integration of distinct yet related constructs into a unified analytical framework while maintaining the conceptual clarity necessary for a systematic evidence synthesis.

Furthermore, to systematically understand this multidimensional phenomenon, this review adopts a stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) conceptual framework.26 Within this model, the diverse and often overwhelming information inputs characteristic of the VUCA era serve as the environmental stimuli (S).27 The organism (O) represents the individual’s internal cognitive and emotional state—specifically, the operationally defined experience of IA across various processing dimensions.28 Finally, the response (R) encompasses the subsequent behavioural, psychological and professional outcomes, such as information avoidance, altered self-efficacy or the deployment of specific coping mechanisms.7

Despite the growing literature, considerable heterogeneity exists in the conceptualisation, measurement and analysis of IA across disciplines, populations and contexts. Existing reviews have narrowly focused on students’ information-seeking anxiety,29 psychometric properties of IA scales30 or bibliometric mappings of anxiety related to information tasks.23 However, no comprehensive review has synthesised evidence spanning populations, professions and cultural contexts in the VUCA era. In particular, knowledge gaps remain regarding how IA is experienced by diverse groups, which contextual factors exacerbate or alleviate it and what coping strategies are most effective in different environments.

Review questions

This scoping review aims to systematically map the current status, characteristics and development trends of research in the field of IA. Specifically, it will address research questions across the following three dimensions:

1. Basic characteristics and research foundations (bibliometric and methodological dimensions)

RQ1: Trends and distribution: What are the characteristics of existing IA research in terms of time span, geographical distribution of researchers and affiliated academic disciplines?

RQ2: Research methods and theories: What are the predominant research methodologies and underlying theoretical frameworks employed in existing empirical studies?

Analysis of research elements (based on the PCC framework)

RQ3: Population: What are the profiling characteristics of the target populations in existing studies?

RQ4: Concept: What are the core issues and specific research topics focused on in studies related to IA?

RQ5: Context: In what contexts or specific environments is IA research typically conducted?

Mechanisms and Interventions (Based on the S-O-R Theoretical Framework)

RQ6: Stimuli (antecedents): What external environmental stimuli and internal personal factors contribute to or trigger IA?

RQ7: Organismic states and consequences: How does IA manifest across different populations and what are its overall negative impacts on individuals?

RQ8: Responses (interventions): What effective individual-level coping strategies and organisational/societal-level interventions have been identified in existing research to address IA?

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this scoping review are structured according to the ‘Participants–Concept–Context (PCC)’ framework.31

Participants

This review considers studies involving individuals of any age, gender, profession or nationality. By imposing no restrictions on participants, the review aims to capture the full spectrum of IA experiences across diverse populations.

Concept

IA is a broad, interdisciplinary construct encompassing both classical and contemporary perspectives. To ensure a comprehensive examination, this review is guided by the IA frameworks proposed by Wurman1 and Naveed and Anwar,5 and informed by a preliminary mapping of relevant literature from the Web of Science and Scopus databases. The review includes studies addressing the following IA-related constructs:

Context

This review adopts the VUCA world as its contextual framework. As there is no universally established starting point for the ‘VUCA era’, the review focuses on studies conducted in the information age, particularly following the widespread adoption of the Internet, where VUCA are most salient. To operationalise this contextual boundary, the literature search will be restricted to studies published from 1 January 2000, onwards. The year 2000 serves as a critical milestone marking the widespread global commercialisation of the Internet, the dawn of highly interactive digital environments, and the subsequent exponential growth of digital information—all of which are primary catalysts for IA in the modern era. Studies addressing exclusively non-digital contexts, such as traditional library anxiety, will be excluded. No geographical or cultural restrictions will be applied.

Types of sources

This review will include only peer-reviewed empirical studies that examine IA within internet-enabled VUCA contexts, published after the advent of the Internet (1 January 2000 onwards). The specific exclusion criteria are: (1) conceptual papers, review articles, conference proceedings, dissertations, government reports and other forms of grey literature; (2) studies addressing exclusively non-digital contexts (eg, traditional library anxiety without a digital component) and (3) non-empirical methodologies. The emphasis on empirical research is justified because such studies provide systematically collected, verifiable evidence, ensuring reliability, comparability and validity. By concentrating on empirical studies, the review aims to generate robust, evidence-based insights into the prevalence, determinants, impacts and coping strategies of IA across diverse populations and contexts.

Methods

This review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Scoping Review methodology31 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines.32 A comprehensive search will be conducted independently by two reviewers across three distinct databases chosen for their complementary strengths: Scopus and Web of Science for their multidisciplinary breadth in capturing the complex technological and management contexts of the VUCA era, and EBSCOhost for its deep coverage of behavioural and social sciences, which is essential for analysing the psychological impacts of IA across diverse populations. Any discrepancies will be resolved through consultation with a third communications expert. Following study selection, data will be extracted using a standardised instrument and synthesised through both narrative and visual approaches. This protocol has been prospectively registered with the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/TGHXS).

Search strategy

The search procedure was organised in two phases to retrieve all studies and reviews relevant to the topic. In the first phase, a comprehensive search strategy (see online supplemental appendix A) was formulated after preliminary searches in Web of Science and Scopus. This strategy was refined through consultations with two communication studies experts and developed using the titles, abstracts, and keywords of relevant articles identified during the initial searches. During the second phase, the strategy will be tailored to each database to ensure proper alignment during retrieval. There will be no language restriction. The publication date will be limited to studies published from 1 January 2000 to the planned search end date.

Study selection

The final selected articles will be uploaded to EndNote V.21 (Clarivate Analytics, Pennsylvania, USA) to remove duplicates. Subsequently, all records will be organised and managed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Washington, USA) for screening and documentation. An initial set of 20 articles will be pilot tested, followed by screening of titles and abstracts by two independent reviewers according to the inclusion criteria. Studies that meet the eligibility criteria will then be retrieved in full text for further evaluation. The rationale for excluding studies at the full-text review stage will be recorded and presented. Any conflicts between the two reviewers will first be addressed through discussion; unresolved cases will involve consultation with a third communication expert. Articles published in English, Chinese and Malay will be reviewed directly by the research team. For articles published in other languages, assistance from language experts will be sought, supplemented by online translation tools. The completed search results and study selection process will be presented using a PRISMA flow diagram.33

Discrepancies in data extraction will be addressed through discussion, and a third reviewer will adjudicate any unresolved issues.

Data analysis and presentation

Data will be analysed following the JBI Scoping Review Framework.34 All included studies will be systematically tabulated, and findings will be presented using tables and graphical illustrations. Descriptive analysis will be performed to summarise study characteristics, outcomes and key themes, accompanied by a critical discussion to contextualise the results. To account for heterogeneity, studies will be stratified and compared based on participant characteristics, geographic location and other dimensions. Both quantitative and qualitative approaches will be employed: quantitative heterogeneity will be explored through frequency distributions and crosstabulations, while qualitative heterogeneity will be examined by identifying thematic patterns and variations across study contexts. This comprehensive approach allows for the identification of patterns, divergences and potential sources of variability across studies. The final comprehensive results will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines.

Study status and timeline

Data extraction had begun in August 2025 and was ongoing, with completion anticipated by November 2025. Data charting and analysis were scheduled to start in October 2025, and preliminary findings were expected by November 2025; no results were available at the time of submission.

Discussion

This scoping review protocol outlines a comprehensive and theoretically grounded approach to mapping the multidimensional construct of IA across diverse populations in the VUCA era.

Strengths of the study design

The primary strength of this study design lies in its robust theoretical foundation. By integrating the S-O-R framework, the review elevates the analysis from a simple descriptive summary to a highly structured synthesis of the antecedents, manifestations and consequences of IA. Furthermore, the operationalisation of the contextual boundary—focusing strictly on the post-2000 digital environment—ensures that the synthesised evidence accurately reflects the rapid information growth and interactive complexities defining the modern VUCA landscape. Methodologically, the study strictly adheres to the JBI framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, using a robust, multidatabase search strategy (Scopus, Web of Science and EBSCOhost). This rigorous approach, combined with the absence of geographical or cultural restrictions, guarantees high transparency, reproducibility and a broad mapping of the phenomenon across diverse disciplines.

Limitations of the study design

Despite these methodological strengths, several anticipated limitations of the proposed design must be acknowledged. First, in alignment with the fundamental objectives of scoping reviews, this study will not perform a formal quality appraisal or risk of bias assessment of the included literature; the focus remains on mapping the breadth of the evidence rather than evaluating its methodological quality. Second, the explicit exclusion of grey literature and non-empirical studies (eg, conceptual papers and policy documents) is a methodological trade-off. While this ensures the reliability of empirical evidence, it may result in the omission of valuable qualitative and theoretical insights. Third, given the interdisciplinary nature of the topic, significant variability in how IA is conceptualised and measured across different fields is anticipated, which may limit the direct comparability of the extracted data. Finally, although the review aims for global coverage and accommodates literature in English, Chinese and Malay directly, reliance on translation tools for other languages may introduce a minor degree of language bias.
