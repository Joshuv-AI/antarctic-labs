EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
There is growing interest in high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) for use in advanced nuclear
reactors as a high-energy fuel source. The primary objectives of this report are to identify the security
risks that directly result from HALEU and to identify the gaps and challenges it presents from a theft
and sabotage perspective. This study focuses on HALEU security risks for the front end of the fuel
cycle and includes a review of the supply chain, fuel fabrication, and transport for terrestrial reactors.
The higher fissile isotope content of HALEU is likely to result in increased material attractiveness and
proliferation concerns. From a theft perspective, the fuel’s material enrichment, form, size, and
portability are all key factors that can contribute to the overall security risk. Similarly, sabotage risks
can include potential energy sources for heat, pressure, chemical conversion, criticality, and other
hazardous materials used. Fuel processes, HALEU fuel forms and their associated fuel fabrication
processes were considered as a part of this work to qualitatively assess the security risks. Although
additional security challenges for wide-scale deployment of HALEU were identified, security risks for
HALEU were not found to be significantly different than those identified for low-enriched uranium
(LEU).
Wide-scale deployment of HALEU is expected to result in an increase in the number of fuel cycle
facilities globally that will in turn lead to an increase of HALEU in the global market. Current size
limitations for HALEU storage and transport containers will increase the number of material
shipments and result in increased material handling. Because of HALEU’s increased enrichment,
differentiating the processing of HALEU versus highly enriched uranium (HEU) is more difficult and
will also require enhanced monitoring and detection capabilities. In this work, opportunities
identified to improve HALEU security and to reduce risks were primarily focused in the areas of
material transport, storage, and nuclear material accounting and control.

1. INTRODUCTION
There is growing interest in high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) for use in advanced nuclear
reactors as a high-energy fuel source. The active development of fuel designs with increased fissile
content necessitates additional review to ensure that the highest standards for nuclear security are
endorsed and sustained globally. Understanding the potential for nuclear material theft and sabotage
is a key component for evaluating the overall security risk. The commercialization and widespread
use of HALEU across international markets is expected to increase with the deployment of advanced
reactors. There is growing concern that increased nuclear material within the global marketplace
could lead to increased availability and accessibility of fissile material, thus resulting in unintended
consequences for the nuclear industry. This study seeks to objectively review the security risks
associated with expanded use of HALEU in the global market.

1.1. OBJECTIVES
Ensuring the use of HALEU for peaceful purposes is integral to the successful deployment of
advanced reactors. The primary objectives of this report are to identify the security risks that directly
result from HALEU and to identify the gaps and challenges it presents from a theft and sabotage
perspective. This work builds upon past efforts to characterize the nuclear security impacts of HALEU
fuels and is intended as a supplemental resource. Continued periodic review of the HALEU market is
necessary to address the changes in demand, supply chain, material movement, and material
quantities to ensure effective monitoring and protection strategies. By identifying and mitigating the
security risks early, actionable steps can be taken to enhance the security of HALEU domestically and
abroad.

1.2. SCOPE
This work is part of a broader effort to characterize the global HALEU market and its potential
impacts from a nuclear security perspective. This study focuses on HALEU security risks for the front
end of the fuel cycle through operation (reactors) and includes a review of the supply chain, fuel
fabrication, and transport, focusing on a traditional open, once-through fuel cycle for terrestrial
reactors. Risks associated with reprocessing and back-end fuel cycle stages of irradiated fuel—
including spent fuel storage, irradiated material transport, and spent fuel waste streams—are not
included in this study.

2. BACKGROUND
Uranium fuel is enriched with uranium-235 (U-235), which is the primary fissile isotope used for
uranium-based fuel cycles. The fissile content of U-235 is used to discern whether the material is low-
enriched uranium (LEU) or highly enriched uranium (HEU). The International Atomic Energy Agency
(IAEA) considers fuel enriched with less than 20% U-235 to be LEU. Highly enriched uranium (HEU) is
defined as uranium enriched with 20% or more U-235. Most commercial power reactors in operation
today are light-water reactors (LWRs) that use LEU fuel comprised of less than 5% U-235.

 2

2.1. HALEU FUEL
HALEU is a uranium-based fuel enriched from 5% to less than 20% U-235. Many advanced reactor
developers are pursuing the use of higher fissile content to enable longer operating cycles, better fuel
utilization, and reduced waste streams. Operators of existing Generation III and Generation III+
reactor technologies are also pursuing higher energy fuel options with HALEU enriched with up to
10% U-235 for improved fuel cycle economics. Whereas manufacturing HALEU fuel typically comes at
higher cost to achieve the higher enrichment, cost savings can be realized through enhanced
operational strategies that include enabling higher fuel burn-ups and longer operating cycles in both
advanced reactors and existing LWRs.

The amount of U-235 (fissile content) in the fuel is used to define the material category as it relates to
its proliferation potential and possible use in weapons. Although nuclear material regulation for
HALEU is the subject of an independent study and outside the scope of this report, insights into the
security risks for theft and sabotage can also be gleaned from the fuel’s material categorization. In
general, HALEU is recognized both domestically and internationally as a higher category of material
with more physical security requirements than traditional LEU [1],[2]. As a result, nuclear facilities
that process HALEU are expected to have an elevated security posture that should be factored into
the overall risk profile.

2.2. REGULATORY FRAMEWORKS
The primary legal instrument that will drive physical protection requirements for HALEU material
stems from the Convention for the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material and its Amendment
(A/CPPNM). This cornerstone instrument defines the legal obligations for domestic and international
use, storage, and transportation of certain quantities and enrichment of uranium and other nuclear
materials [4]. Domestically, in the United States, the primary drivers for regulating physical
protection of HALEU will be 10 CFR 53 (currently being drafted) and 10 CFR 73 [5][6]. The IAEA’s
publication, Regulations for the Safe Transport of Radioactive Material, will also provide key guidance
for safely transporting HALEU that must be considered in addition to security requirements [7]. For
packaging requirements, 10 CFR Part 71 will also play a role when designing future packages that
allow for higher capacities of HALEU materials to support commercial-scale production and transport
of HALEU feed materials and fuel [8]. Various instruments that may help provide guidance for
operators to implement requirements include Nuclear Security Series (NSS) No. 13, Nuclear Security
Recommendations on Physical Protection of Nuclear Materials and Facilities, NSS No. 26-G, Security of
Nuclear Material in Transport, and NSS No. 27-G, Physical Protection of Nuclear Material and Nuclear
Facilities [9],[10],[11]. A detailed analysis of regulatory frameworks for HALEU is outside the scope of
this report; however, the aforementioned instruments are referenced throughout this report because
they are key factors to ensure the physical protection of nuclear materials at facilities and during
transportation and are an integral part of assessing the overall security risk.

3. HALEU FUEL CYCLE
The fuel cycle includes the steps required to prepare, use, and dispose of nuclear fuel. This study
focuses on the risks associated with the front-end fuel cycle stages that include enrichment, fuel

 3

fabrication, transport, and operations. Back-end fuel cycle stages for storage, waste streams, or
reprocessing are not included in the scope of this review. This section briefly describes each of the
front-end fuel cycle stages for HALEU and the associated security risks at each stage.

3.1. ENRICHMENT
HALEU is produced either through enrichment or downblending (de-enrichment). Material is enriched
in the form of uranium hexafluoride (UF 6 ). Uranium enrichment processes and the associated
technologies are of general security concern because the same process/technology is used for
enrichment of LEU, HALEU, and HEU. There are several methods for enrichment, including gaseous
diffusion, gas centrifuge, or laser separation technologies.

HALEU is considered to be of higher strategic significance because it takes less material to achieve a
goal quantity. However, in terms of the SWUs required for enrichment, most of the effort required is
in the initial enrichment of up to 5% from natural uranium. Achieving enrichment levels beyond 5%
requires significantly less effort, as shown in Figure 1. In terms of attractiveness for theft, HALEU
enriched up to 20% is not likely to be much more attractive than LEU enriched to 5% given that both
could be enriched further with similar effort. However, if achieving higher enrichment is the goal,
then starting the process from HALEU material would reduce the time and work required.

Risks associated with theft of HALEU vs. LEU can be closely associated with the material storage
aspects (and less associated with the enrichment process or the technology used). Even though
diversion activities that lead to theft are of concern, the enriched material must still be collected and
containerized. HALEU has the potential to be a more attractive target for theft because it is packaged
in smaller container sizes and volumes than those used for LEU. HALEU containment vessels will
require larger safety margins to minimize the potential for a criticality event resulting from increased
fissile content. Smaller containers will be used for HALEU to enhance safety, but they are also more
portable and contain more enriched material. Traditional uranium hexafluoride (UF 6 ) storage
cylinders for LEU enriched to less than 5% are 30 inches in diameter, whereas those required for
HALEU are likely to be 5 inches in diameter, which is similar to that currently used for HEU [3]. Use of

 4

smaller cylinders will increase the number of containers and handling operations. However, HALEU-
specific storage and transport containers have not yet been qualified, so this risk may be reduced if
larger containers are able to be licensed.
The sabotage potential and associated risks for HALEU enrichment facilities are likely to be similar to
that for LEU enrichment facilities because the same process and technologies are expected to be
used. The two primary hazards at enrichment facilities include the potential hazards associated with a
UF 6 release and the criticality hazards associated with the enriched uranium. Any additional sabotage
risk introduced by HALEU is likely to be offset by the more stringent security requirements required
of higher category II facilities. The ability to willfully circumvent safety controls to achieve a
criticality event at an enrichment facility is likely to be the same as that required for LEU where the
enrichment equipment is designed to minimize the potential for a criticality event.

3.2. FUEL FABRICATION
Traditional fuel fabrication processes begin with the UF 6 cylinders provided by enrichment facilities.
The manner in which the enriched UF 6 material is processed at the fuel fabrication facility is
dependent on the final desired fuel form and fuel type. Like enrichment facilities, the primary hazards
for fuel fabrication facilities include chemical and radiological hazards. This section describes
different fuel forms and the associated security risks with a focus on theft and sabotage.

3.2.1. Fuel Forms
Advanced reactor developers are exploring new fuel options that include oxide, metal, carbide,
nitride, salt, and coated particle fuel forms. Although the final fuel forms are considerably different,
the manufacturing processes remain similar to those used with current LWR fuel, beginning with
chemical conversion of the enriched UF 6 . The following subsections provide an overview of the
different HALEU fuel forms currently in use or in development and the security risks associated with
each type.

3.2.1.1. Oxide Fuel
Uranium oxide fuel is the most common fuel form currently in use today with LWRs. Ceramic pellets
are produced by converting UF 6 into uranium dioxide (UO 2 ) that is then pressed into pellets and
sintered at high temperature. The pellets are then loaded into fuel rods (mainly zirconium alloy),
sealed, and placed into fuel assemblies. Oxide fuel is likely to be the most susceptible to theft or
diversion when it is in a UO 2 powder or pelletized fuel form because of the small size and portability
of the enriched material. Once the fuel is loaded into fuel rods and subsequent fuel assemblies, it is
much less accessible and is not easily moved without specialized equipment. Fuel is closely tracked
through each step of the fuel fabrication process, with added material controls for all areas in which
the fuel can be accessed.
Chemical conversion of the UF 6 using either wet or dry methods involves removing the fluoride and
impurities to create the UO 2 . The same processes are used regardless of material enrichment, so
sabotage potential remains similar to that of LEU fuel forms outside of the additional criticality
controls required for HALEU. Consequences of sabotage are likely to be highest during the conversion
of UF 6 to UO 2 because of the hazards associated with the chemical processing. Additionally, there is
added risk for dispersal while the UO 2 is in a powdered form after conversion and prior to being

 5

formed into pellets. However, the same chemical and radiological risks apply for LEU conversion and
are not unique to processing HALEU.

3.2.1.2. Uranium Nitride Fuel
Uranium nitride (UN) fuel is another ceramic HALEU fuel form that can be manufactured using a
variety of methods as detailed in open literature. These methods include carbothermic nitriding,
hydriding–nitriding, and sol-gel methods that begin with uranium metal feedstock derived from
enriched UF 6 . UN can also be created with ammonolysis of uranium tetrafluoride. Added process
controls are required to ensure that UN does not come in contact with air, water, or steam during
manufacturing because of the potential to result in an exothermic reaction. Like oxide fuels, the
nitride powder is compacted and sintered at high temperature (above 2,000°C) [12]. After the
sintering process is complete, the final fuel form is stable in air and is loaded into fuel rods. Like oxide
fuels, UN fuel is likely to be most susceptible to theft or diversion during fuel fabrication when it is in
a nitride powder or pelletized fuel form because of the small size and portability of enriched material.
Some UN fuel designs include an inert plenum gas with liquid sodium that serves as an intermediate
heat conducting medium between the fuel and the cladding. Sodium is also highly reactive, requiring
additional controls during the manufacturing process. Sabotage and consequence potential for UN
fuel fabrication remains similar to that of oxide fuel forms, but with the added hazards associated
with the reactivity of UN and sodium. Heat, pressure, chemical processes, and criticality safety are
energy release mechanisms that could be exploited in a sabotage scenario but are generally of no
greater risk than those seen in existing LEU facilities utilizing similar chemical processes and
equipment.

3.2.1.3. Metal Fuel
Metal fuel is comprised of uranium metal and typically zirconium formed into an alloy that is cast into
the desired fuel shape, which is generally pellets or cylindrical pins. The metal fuel pellets or pins are
loaded into fuel rods made of corrosion-resistant steel, sealed, and placed into assemblies. Sodium is
used inside the fuel rods as a thermal bond. Similar to UN fuel designs, fabrication must be done in
an inert environment. Fuel fabrication of metallic fuel forms typically begins with enriched UF 6 that is
converted to UF 4 , although UO 2 can also be used. The UF 4 is then further reduced chemically using
either calcium or magnesium to separate the uranium metal from the fluoride [1]. At this stage in the
fabrication process, the separated uranium metal is most susceptible to theft prior to being loaded
into sealed fuel rods. However, strict accounting of the material throughout the process can be used
to minimize the potential for theft or diversion during fuel fabrication.
Sabotage and consequence potential for metallic fuel fabrication is also similar to that of other
ceramic fuel forms previously described. Heat, pressure, chemical processes, and criticality safety
comprise the primary hazards that could be exploited in a sabotage scenario, but they are similar to
those of existing LEU facilities utilizing similar processes and equipment.

3.2.1.4. Coated Particle Fuel Forms
Coated particle fuel forms consist of uranium fuel as a kernel coated with several layers of ceramic
materials designed to protect the fuel and retain the fission products. The primary coating materials
are pyrolytic carbon and silicon carbide. Early coated particle fuel forms include the buffer-isotropic
(BISO) fuel design used in pebble-bed modular reactors consisting of a UO 2 kernel surrounded by two

 6

layers of pyrolytic carbon. More modern TRISO fuel consists of either a UO 2 or UCO kernel with four
coating layers consisting of two inner pyrolytic carbon layers, a silicon carbide layer for added
strength, and an outer pyrolytic carbon layer. Specific properties of each coating layer are carefully
controlled and specific to each fuel design. Typical TRISO particles are less than a millimeter in
diameter. Thousands of coated fuel particles are combined with a matrix material such as graphite to
create spherical fuel pebbles, cylinders, or blocks, often referred to as compacts, that are then
pressed and heated to obtain the final fuel form. The compacts can take multiple physical forms, with
some reactor designs employing hundreds of thousands of compacts.
Coated particle fuel forms are most susceptible to theft or diversion while the fuel particles are being
manufactured because of the extremely small kernel sizes. Material attractiveness is reduced further
with each processing step as each protective carbon layer adds obstacles and protections against
nefarious use. Measurement uncertainty for material control must be taken into consideration when
evaluating the potential for diversion of small amounts of material over prolonged periods of time.
Accurate material accountancy remains an important part of reducing the risk of theft [3] for this fuel
form.
A benefit of coated particle fuel forms is that the silicon carbide layer acts as a protective cladding
layer for each fuel kernel. In addition to the operational benefits, the coating layers also help protect
the fuel from sabotage. Thus, the potential for sabotage is likely to be highest when the fuel is in its
purest form prior to coating.

3.2.1.5. Molten Salt Fuel Forms
Molten salt reactors (MSRs) are another promising advanced reactor technology that may utilize
HALEU. Fuel options for MSRs can include either traditional solid fuel forms such as those described
above, or fuel salt. When fuel salt is used, the HALEU material is dissolved in the coolant salt. Unlike
conventional solid fuel that is manufactured at an independent fuel fabrication facility and
transported to reactor sites, MSRs with fuel salts are anticipated to include localized salt processing
capabilities in which either UF 6 or UF 4 will be used to manufacture the fuel salt at the point of use
[17]. Online or batch processing salt clean-up systems will be used to separate and remove fission
products to extend fuel life with higher burnup. Some salt clean-up systems being proposed are
similar to reprocessing systems because of the separation of special nuclear material, but that area is
beyond the scope of this study. Risks related to theft and sabotage of UF 6 or UF 4 feedstocks are
expected to be similar to those described for enrichment facilities (see Section 3.1). Once the fuel has
been dissolved into the salt, the fissile material is in a much less attractive form, so the risks for theft
or diversion are significantly reduced. The majority of sabotage risks associated with fresh fuel salts
can be attributed to the chemical hazards associated with the salt and are less associated with
radiological risks of HALEU. Liquid fuel salts are likely to be more dispersible in a sabotage scenario,
but most MSRs operate at much lower pressures when compared to traditional LWR technologies,
thus limiting the release potential. Sabotage of irradiated fuel salts or salt clean-up systems that
process actinide waste streams can be expected to have a higher consequence potential, but this is
outside the scope of this review.

3.2.2. Fuel Fabrication Security Summary
The HALEU manufacturing market is in ongoing development to support the growing global demand.
Enrichment providers and fuel manufacturers are working on methods for bulk manufacturing of

 7

HALEU. Currently, manufacturing and fuel fabrication equipment is under development, and
continued innovation in this area can be anticipated. In addition to the theft potential for the various
fuel forms described in the previous sections, the main security risks for HALEU fuel fabrication can
generally be associated with the energy storage and release mechanisms that could lead to facility
damage or release of radiation.
These risks are defined as follows:
 1) Chemical reactions (heat release, chemical hazard)
 2) Thermal heat transfer (temperature gradient or phase change)
 3) Pressure release (structural storage or pressure gradient)
 4) Electromagnetic discharge (electric discharge, magnetic force)
 5) Nuclear reactions (criticality, dose consequence)
 6) Gravitational (falling)
With the introduction of new fuel forms, differing manufacturing processes, and different
intermediate forms of material storage, the protection and retention of fissile material in each
manufacturing step must be evaluated to assess an adversary’s ability to gain access to the material
and the potential theft or sabotage risks. Although this study highlights the general qualitative
security risks related to HALEU, more detailed analysis of the specific fuel fabrication facilities and
equipment will be required to quantify individual site risks.
Future manufacturing technologies such as 3D printing are rapidly evolving and are expected to be
deployed to support fuel fabrication. These technologies may introduce additional security risks. How
they may be applied to the HALEU market is yet to be seen, but research on 3D printed fuel forms and
fuel components has already been demonstrated at bench scale. Understanding new and evolving
technologies and the potential new security risks will be another aspect of the evolving HALEU
market to be evaluated in future work.

3.3. TRANSPORT
Transportation for HALEU fuel will connect each of the primary steps within the fuel cycle, from
mining to waste disposal, and will play a key role in future HALEU supply chains. Transportation is
one of the most vulnerable steps in the fuel cycle because the material transits in the public domain.
Transport of new fuel forms, the advent of new technologies, and evolving threat landscapes will
likely present new and unique challenges for HALEU shipments compared to transport of more
traditional LEU shipments. Regarding transportation of HALEU materials, the greatest risks currently
come in the form of UF 6 sabotage, sustainability of material movements caused by the lack of
certified packages to support commercial-scale HALEU production, and novel transportation
challenges associated with emerging technologies such as FNPPs and factory-fueled and sealed
nuclear reactors. Additionally, the increase in the category of materials associated with HALEU
shipments will require more stringent security measures when moving material between sites. This
may create operational constraints around shippers and ports where material transits occur. Although
the general security risks of theft and sabotage for transporting HALEU are likely not drastically
different from those associated with LEU shipments, the operational risks for sustaining a HALEU
supply chain will have direct links to national and energy security.

4.1. FRESH FUEL STORAGE AND PREPARATION
Advanced reactors are expected to use various fuel forms, as discussed in Section 3.2.1. The storage
and preparation of solid fuel forms are likely to be similar to those used for LWR technologies that
use cladded fuel. Broadly, the fuel assembly or fuel compact will be transported to the reactor site,
where it will be stored locally in dry storage casks or in a controlled storage space until it is inserted
into a reactor core during refueling. Upon fuel receipt at the site, the fresh fuel will be inspected and

 9

cleaned in preparation for use. The quantity of fresh fuel on site to support start-up or refueling will
be dependent on the reactor design and size.
Molten salt reactors (MSRs) can use either traditional solid fuel forms or fuel salt. If fuel salts are
used, then MSR reactors will include localized salt processing systems to manufacture fuel salt at the
point of use. This will require onsite storage of enriched UF 6 or UF 4 feedstocks and bulk storage
capabilities for both fresh and irradiated fuel salts. Security risks associated with fuel salts are
addressed in Section 3.2.1.5.
In general, the risks for material theft, diversion, or sabotage of fresh HALEU fuel for advanced
reactors during storage and preparation will likely remain similar to those associated with LEU fuel.
At this stage in the fuel cycle, the fuel is less accessible and is likely to be less attractive. Radiological
risks of fresh HALEU fuel also remain low.

4.2. REFUELING
Reactors with open, once-through fuel cycles will require periodic refueling. During refueling, access
to the reactor core is required to remove and replace traditional fuel assemblies. Advanced reactor
technologies are likely to require different steps or specialized processes than typically required to
replace the fuel. For example, sodium fast reactors (SFRs) require that fresh fuel be inerted before it
is exposed to the highly reactive sodium coolant [31]. Although the hazards associated with sodium
are not unique to HALEU, the reactivity of sodium to air and water is another potential energy release
mechanism that must be taken into consideration from a sabotage perspective. Similarly, MSR fuel
salt systems designed to enable online refueling, actinide separation, and salt cleanup may further
contribute to potential theft or sabotage risks.

4.3. OPERATION
Operational security risks associated with the use of HALEU are dependent on reactor design, fuel
form, and fuel type. Risks for theft or diversion of HALEU in solid fuel forms are minimized during
operation by the fuel containment structure (cladding or matrix material) that provides a barrier to
the fuel. Molten salt reactors that utilize liquid fuel salts and include online fuel processing and/or
fuel separation and storage capabilities could be at higher risk for theft as a result of direct access to
the fuel. However, this is not unique to HALEU and can be managed through robust nuclear material
accounting and control (NMAC) programs and effective physical protection.
From a sabotage perspective, preventing the potential for a radiological release is the primary
consideration during reactor operations. Independent of the fuel enrichment, there are several
barriers that reduce sabotage risks to fuel, including the fuel form and containment mechanism
(cladding, matrix or coating), reactor components, and reactor containment structure [31]. The use of
HALEU does not impact the effectiveness of those barriers positively or negatively.

4.4. SPENT FUEL STORAGE
Once spent fuel is removed from the reactor core, it is stored locally, where it is allowed to “cool”
(typically for several years) before it is prepared for long-term storage on site, at an interim spent
fuel storage facility, or at another long-term waste repository. With traditional LWR designs, spent

fuel is initially stored in water-based spent fuel pools supported by decay heat removal systems. After

4.5. OTHER OPERATIONAL CONSIDERATIONS
Advanced reactors are being developed to support new operational strategies beyond those seen in
traditional power generation. The pairing of reactors with energy-intensive industrial processes is
being explored to help reduce greenhouse gases and support improved process efficiencies. Co-
location of reactors at large industrial sites may also present new security challenges and risks that
will require additional review from a security perspective.
Small modular reactors and microreactor concepts currently in development may also implement new
operational strategies in which developers assume the role of the designer, fabricator, owner, and
operator responsible for the entire reactor lifecycle. Under this type of scenario, some developers
plan to utilize centralized processing facilities for manufacturing, fueling, and maintenance activities,
and new methods for transporting fully fueled reactor cores may also be proposed. Whereas the
safety and regulatory challenges must still be formally addressed, review of these operational
pursuits should also be considered as a part of future HALEU risk studies.
Floating nuclear power plants (FNPPs) are another deployment pathway for advanced reactors that
will also present unique operational differences and security risks. Establishing secure shipyards for
manufacturing, maintenance, or refueling-related activities will be necessary. Use of HALEU will
require added physical protections in nontraditional maritime environments that have the potential
to introduce new threat vectors.

4.6. ADVANCED REACTOR SECURITY RISK SUMMARY
Advanced reactor technologies will include different hazards and energy release mechanisms
compared to those presented by traditional LWRs. These unique factors could be exploited in a
sabotage scenario. Research on advanced reactor security risks and methods for evaluating the
consequence potential of several advanced reactor concepts is currently in progress [31]. Future work
to characterize the security risks for each reactor type is recommended to further evaluate the
potential impacts resulting from the use of HALEU.

5. HALEU SECURITY RISKS
The material attractiveness and proliferation concerns for HALEU fuel are key contributors to its
security risks. The security risks of theft and sabotage for HALEU are not significantly different than
the security risks for LEU. The specific risks associated with the theft and sabotage of HALEU for the
front end of the fuel cycle as addressed herein are summarized in the following sections.

5.1. MATERIAL THEFT
In general, the primary contributors to the risks for theft of HALEU are as follows:
 1) Material enrichment: smaller quantity needed to achieve a goal quantity
 2) Material portability: smaller containers required for criticality safety for storage and transport
 3) Material handling: increased handling of material resulting from an increased number of
 storage and transport containers across the supply chain
 4) Material form: most attractive and susceptible to theft during enrichment, transport, and
 initial stages of fuel fabrication, when the material is in a pure, stable state (UF 6 , UO 2 , or
 U3O8)
 5) Material size: potential to contribute to risks of theft or diversion because of its smaller size
 (i.e., coated particle fuel forms)
 6) Material accountancy: likelihood of being more difficult for accountancy with some advanced
 reactor fuel forms, thus requiring more accurate measurement techniques and specially
 designed material balance areas

5.2. SABOTAGE
The risks for sabotage of HALEU, although similar to LEU, can be derived from the fundamental
potential energy sources of manufacturing HALEU. Sabotage risks include the following:
 1) Industrial processes: heat, pressure, chemical conversion
 2) Criticality: higher enrichment combined with mass, volume, shape, and material composition
 3) Hazardous materials: fluorides, acids, and other flammable or reactive materials used during
 the enrichment and fuel fabrication processes
From a consequence perspective, the radiological risk for HALEU is low and does not present a risk to
the public. The hazards associated with the chemicals and industrial processes used to support
enrichment and fuel fabrication are likely to have the greatest consequence potential for HALEU
sabotage. However, the same processes and chemicals are used for LEU manufacturing and are
independent of the increased level of enrichment.

6. RECOMMENDATIONS
Ensuring the highest standards for nuclear security will be a key requirement for all reactor
technologies, regardless of fuel cycle, fuel type, or reactor design. HALEU fuel has increasingly
become a key part of the design strategy for advanced reactors and will play a critical role in the
success of advanced reactor technology deployment. Security challenges resulting from HALEU can
be overcome with effective security plans, monitoring, and physical protection strategies.

6.1. HALEU FUEL SECURITY CHALLENGES
The following is a summary of security challenges expected with HALEU:
  Increased material attractiveness because of higher enrichment
  Expanded development of HALEU fuel cycle facilities domestically and internationally
  Increased amounts of HALEU in the global market
  Increased transport of Category II material shipments with added security requirements
  HALEU processing similar to HEU processing, with difficulty to differentiate
  More precise measurement systems for accurate NMAC needed to address advanced reactor
 fuel forms
  Differing deployment strategies for advanced reactors utilizing HALEU fuels to include
 factory fueled reactors (TNPPs), FNPPs, and submersible technologies
  Differing threat vectors, to include hijacking of floating or transportable reactors and
 underwater attacks
  New and emerging technologies, to include new manufacturing methods and autonomous
 operating systems

6.2. OPPORTUNITIES
Future opportunities to enhance the nuclear security of HALEU and reduce risks include the
following:
  Development of HALEU-specific storage and transport containers qualified for larger
 commercial quantities that will help reduce material handling and transport risks
  Development of new or enhanced detection capabilities to measure key fuel characteristics,
 to include enrichment level / isotope content
  Continued development of enhanced fuel insult testing capabilities to support consequence
 analysis and risk evaluation of advanced fuel forms
  Enhanced NMAC capabilities
Efforts to evaluate HALEU security risks for individual reactor designs were limited in this study.
Further research is recommended to investigate the potential HALEU security risks for reactor
technologies under development. Additionally, more in-depth review of vendor deployment and
operational strategies is proposed to further characterize the HALEU security risks related to
cogeneration, transportable reactors, and nontraditional operating models in which developers
design, build, own, and operate reactors.

6.3. KEY FINDINGS
This study found that the wide-scale deployment of HALEU is likely to result in new security
challenges. While the risks associated with sabotage for the front end of the fuel cycle were found to
be similar to those of LEU, key findings related to the risk of theft include the following:
  The amount of material required to obtain a target quantity of fissile material combined with
 the level of effort to achieve weapons-grade HEU is less with HALEU when compared to LEU,
 which could impact the risk of theft attempts.
  HALEU has the potential to be a more attractive target for theft due to the smaller container
 sizes and volumes when compared to LEU. However, HALEU specific storage and transport
 containers have not yet been qualified, so this risk may be less if larger containers are able to
 be licensed.
  For both HALEU and LEU, material is likely to be most attractive and susceptible to theft
 during enrichment, transport, and the initial stages of fuel fabrication.
  For both HALEU and LEU, fuel type can contribute to risks of theft (e.g., coated particle fuel
 forms with smaller size could be easier to steal). Material accountancy, which is important for
 deterrence and detection of theft, is likely to be more difficult with some advanced reactor
 fuel forms.
  For both HALEU and LEU, novel deployment scenarios are being considered by reactor
 developers, including co-locating reactors at large industrial sites and deploying floating
 reactors. These scenarios present new security challenges that will require additional review.
