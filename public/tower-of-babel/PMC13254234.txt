Low‐Income Preschool Children's Free Sugar Consumption and Its Association With the Home Environment
Authors: Maryam Yuhas, Katherine M. Kidwell, Xeuzhu Hua, Lynn S. Brann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHigh consumption of sugary foods and beverages by young children has negative health effects; however, few studies have examined multiple domains of the home environment simultaneously or included sugars from 100% fruit juice. This study hypothesized that less favorable socio‐cultural, built, and economic home environments would be associated with higher free sugar intake among low‐income preschool children.MethodsCaregivers (n = 170) of 3–5‐year‐old children enrolled in Head Start programs completed questionnaires assessing food and drink intake, home environment, and food parenting practices. Statistical analyses included bivariate and multivariable linear regression with sensitivity analyses.ResultsChildren consumed an average of 4.05 (SD = 3.12) sugary foods/drinks per day, while caregivers consumed 3.78 (SD = 3.49); intake was strongly correlated (r = 0.67, p < 0.001). In bivariate analyses, higher child intake was associated with parental intake, permissive food parenting practices, number of caregivers, home availability, and lowest household income. In the adjusted regression model, parental intake (β = 0.333, p < 0.001), allowing sugary foods on a “bad day” (β = 0.397, p = 0.030), and home availability (β = 1.872, p < 0.001) remained significant predictors.ConclusionsChildren and caregivers exceeded national and global recommendations for free sugar intake. Findings highlight the importance of multilevel interventions addressing caregiver intake, permissive parenting practices, home availability, and socioeconomic barriers.

Introduction

Preschool‐aged children (3–5 years) in the United States consume excessive amounts of sugary foods and beverages, contributing to excess weight gain, dental caries, and cardiometabolic risk [1, 2]. Current recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO), Dietary Guidelines for Americans, and the American Heart Association advise limiting added sugars to < 10% of total energy and discouraging routine consumption of sugar‐sweetened beverages and 100% fruit juice [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. However, added sugars contribute approximately 13% of energy in preschoolers' diets, and fruit juice remains widely consumed, especially in low‐income households [1, 8, 9]. These disparities underscore the central role of the home environment, where socio‐cultural, built, and economic factors interact to shape children's intake [10, 11, 12].

Most prior studies examined isolated aspects of the home environment, such as parental feeding practices or food availability, and operationalized dietary exposure as added sugar, excluding the sugars naturally present in 100% fruit juice [13, 14]. Though these drinks provide important nutrients, they are also a source of concentrated sugar without the fiber. Among low‐income children participating in federal nutrition programs such as Women, Infants and Children (WIC) and Head Start, juice consumption was disproportionately high and frequently exceeded age‐specific recommendations [15, 16], yet its contribution to free sugar intake was rarely captured in home environment research. This study addressed both gaps by applying the WHO definition of “free sugars” (encompassing added sugars as well as sugars naturally present in fruit juices, honey, and syrups [3]) and simultaneously examining socio‐cultural, built, and economic domains of the home environment [10]. To the authors' knowledge, no previous study has tested a multi‐domain home food environment model inclusive of 100% fruit juice in a low‐income preschool sample.

Head Start is a federally funded program that serves families below the federal poverty level. Children enrolled in this program face elevated risk for excess sugar intake due to the convergence of economic constraints, food environment exposures, and caregiver stress [17, 18]. Early patterns of free sugar intake established during this period have been shown to track into later childhood, with daily sugary beverage consumption before age 2.5 associated with over four times the odds of continued intake at ages 5–9 [19], underscoring the urgency of intervening during the preschool years. Despite this risk, few studies have simultaneously examined parenting practices, home food availability, family composition, and economic indicators in a Head Start sample. The present study addressed this gap by testing a multi‐domain model of the home food environment in a racially diverse sample of Head Start caregivers, with the goal of informing multilevel, equity‐oriented interventions.

Several hypotheses guided the analyses. Based on the socio‐cultural factors, higher parental free sugar intake and more permissive food parenting practices were expected to predict higher child intake. Based on the built environment, greater home availability of sugary foods and beverages was expected to be a significant positive predictor. Based on economic factors, lower household income and the presence of multiple caregivers were expected to be associated with higher intake. Taken together, these hypotheses reflect the expectation that no single factor would fully explain child intake, and that a multi‐domain model would better capture the complexity of the home food environment in low‐income families.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study collected data from parents, legal guardians, or caregivers (hereby referred to as caregivers) whose child attended a Head Start program in the Central New York region from February to April of 2021. Convenience sampling was used to recruit caregivers via an internal Head Start system that sent a text message to each child's caregiver. Caregivers were contacted up to three times each. The message invited the caregiver to join the study and provided a secure link that took them directly to the informational consent form hosted on Qualtrics. Caregivers provided their consent by clicking “next” and continuing to the survey that took about 15–20 min to complete. Participants received a $10 grocery store gift card for completing the survey. All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Syracuse University.

Survey Design

The survey was designed in collaboration with Head Start key staff who provided feedback on an initial draft. Questions were added, removed, or modified based on feedback to improve cultural sensitivity and capture data of interest to staff. The finalized survey was then built into the Qualtrics software. To support caregiver completion of the online or paper survey, Head Start home visitor staff were available. Some caregivers used home visitor assistance to complete the survey (n = 10), but no paper surveys were used.

Measures

Sugary Food and Beverage Intake

Caregiver and child free sugar food and beverage intake was measured by assessing the frequency of each sugary food (candy and chocolate; syrups and add ins; baked desserts; frozen desserts; sugary cereals) and beverage (regular soda; 100% fruit juice; sweetened sports, energy, and fruit drinks; sweetened coffee and tea; flavored cow's milk; flavored alternative milk) over the past week. These questions were adapted from the 2009–2010 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) Dietary Screener Questionnaire to ask frequency of food and drink intake over the past week instead of the past month [20]. Amounts consumed at each time were not assessed; therefore, a frequency per day variable was the primary outcome of interest. Each sugary food and beverage was assessed separately and then combined to create a total free sugar food and beverage frequency per day variable. The weekly time frame was selected in place of the original monthly recall period to reduce cognitive burden and improve accuracy among caregivers managing multiple household responsibilities. Total intake frequency was calculated by summing the reported frequency of each individual item across all 11 food and beverage categories and dividing by seven to yield a per‐day score. Serving size was not assessed; intake frequency, rather than gram‐level quantification of free sugar content, was therefore the primary dietary outcome. This approach does not allow for biochemical distinction between added and free sugars, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Socio‐Cultural Home Environment

The child's socio‐cultural home environment was defined by the caregiver's reported parenting behaviors and family context. Parenting behaviors included (1) caregiver intake of sugary foods and beverages (as described above) and (2) caregiver's food parenting practices. Food parenting practices questions were adapted from the Family, Life, Activity, Sun, Health, and Eating (FLASHE) survey sponsored by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), that assessed the intake of both junk food and sugary foods [21]. Caregivers responded to the following statements on a five‐point scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree: (1) If my child has a bad day, I let him/her have junk food and sugary drinks to feel better, (2) I don't buy a lot of junk food or sugary drinks for my child, (3) I try to avoid eating junk food or drinking sugary drinks when my child is around, (4) My child and I decide together how much junk food and sugary drinks he/she can have, (5) I have to make sure that my child doesn't eat too much junk food or drink too many sugary drinks, (6) I decide how much junk food or sugary drinks my child can have, (7) It's okay for me to make rules about how much junk food or sugary drinks my child can have. Each item was scored on a five‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Items reflecting restrictive or monitoring behaviors (items 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7) were reverse‐coded so that higher scores consistently indicated greater permissiveness. Items were examined individually rather than as a composite score, as they were not designed to form a single scale and reflect conceptually distinct parenting behaviors. Family context was measured using the number of caregivers making food decisions for the child, primary caregiver's marital status, caregiver's age, total number of children in the household, and if the child had older siblings.

Built Home Environment

The built home environment was operationalized as the home availability of each sugary food and beverage category assessed in the dietary screener. Caregivers reported how often each item was available in the home on a five‐point scale ranging from “Never” [1] to “Always” [5]. These questions were adapted from the FLASHE survey [21]. A composite home availability score was calculated by averaging responses across all items, with higher scores indicating greater availability of sugary foods and beverages in the home.

Economic Home Environment

Economic home environment was measured using household income (coded as a categorical variable with the following categories to capture the variation in low‐income groups: ≤ $9,999, $10,000–24,999, $25,000–44,999, ≥ $45,000), whether the family participated in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistant Program (SNAP), and the Women, Infant and Children (WIC) programs.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics (means, SDs, frequencies) were calculated for all variables. Normality was assessed using Shapiro‐Wilk tests, and assumptions for parametric analyses were met. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. First, bivariate analyses were conducted (Pearson correlations, t‐tests, and one‐way ANOVAs with Tukey post hoc tests) to identify socio‐cultural, built, and economic home environment variables associated with child sugary food and beverage intake. Variables significant at p < 0.05 in bivariate analyses were retained for multivariable modeling.

A final multivariable linear regression model was then estimated, adjusting for demographic covariates (caregiver age, number of children in the household, and household income). Significant predictors from each home environment domain were entered simultaneously. Sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of findings using negative binomial regression, alternative SES indicators (SNAP, WIC).

Results

Sample

From the 181 caregivers who participated, data from 170 caregivers were analyzed, who had complete data. Caregivers were on average 21 years of age (SD = 6.8). The sample included Black/African American (36.7%), White (34.7%), Hispanic/Latino (12.2%), Asian (8.8%), Mixed race (4.8%), and American Indian/Alaskan Native (2.7%) caregivers. Bivariate Associations

Children consumed an average of 4.05 (SD = 3.12) sugary foods and beverages per day, while caregivers consumed 3.78 (SD = 3.49). Parent and child intake were strongly correlated (r = 0.67, p < 0.001). In bivariate analyses, higher child intake was significantly associated with parental intake (p < 0.001), permissive parenting practice of allowing sugary foods on a “bad day” (F = 6.09, p < 0.001), less restrictive purchasing (“don't buy,” F = 5.94, p < 0.001), more caregivers in the household (F = 4.86, p = 0.003), higher home availability of sugary foods (r = 0.52, p < 0.001), and lower household income (F = 3.72, p = 0.013). SNAP and WIC participation, caregiver age, marital status, and number of children in the household were not significantly associated with child intake (all p > 0.05).

Multivariable Analysis

In the final adjusted model (Table 2), child sugary food and beverage intake was significantly associated with parental intake (B = 0.333, 95% CI [0.195, 0.470], p < 0.001), permissive parenting practice of allowing sugary foods on a “bad day” (B = 0.397, 95% CI [0.038, 0.756], p = 0.03), and home availability of sugary foods and beverages (B = 1.872, 95% CI [1.188, 2.557], p < 0.001). Other predictors, including the parenting practice of “not restricting purchases,” number of caregivers, household income, caregiver age, and number of children, were not significantly associated with child intake.

Discussion

Consistent with prior evidence [14], both children and caregivers in this sample exceeded national and global recommendations, consuming approximately four instances of sugary foods and beverages daily. By applying Rosenkranz and Dzewaltowski's (2008) home food environment model, socio‐cultural, built, and economic factors significantly associated with higher free sugar consumption were identified, including the novel contribution of assessing “free sugars” with 100% fruit juice, within a multi‐domain framework applied to a low‐income Head Start sample.

Parental intake was a strong independent predictor of child intake, reinforcing evidence that caregiver dietary behaviors are closely mirrored in children [6, 22]. The parenting practice of allowing sugary foods on a “bad day” also remained significant, highlighting the influence of emotional feeding on child diet [11, 12]. In contrast, the parenting practice of “not restricting purchases” and the number of caregivers were not significant after adjustment, suggesting that their influence may be less robust when considered alongside stronger predictors. Although not significant in the final model, the number of caregivers was independently associated with higher intake, suggesting that inconsistent feeding norms across multiple adults may undermine efforts to establish healthier habits [18, 23].

Although household income was not significant in the adjusted models, bivariate analyses indicated differences between lowest‐ and highest‐income groups. Nearly all participants faced similar economic constraints, which likely reduced between‐group variability in both income and dietary opportunities. In this context, factors such as home availability and caregiver feeding practices may serve as more proximal determinants of intake than absolute household income. Nonetheless, socioeconomic constraints remain important contextual influences, with substantial evidence linking low socioeconomic status to less healthy dietary patterns [19, 24, 25, 26]. While SNAP and WIC participation were not independent predictors, financial hardship likely shapes purchasing behaviors and reliance on low‐cost, sugar‐dense foods, a structural reality that individual behavior change alone cannot address.

Home availability of sugary foods and beverages was the strongest predictor in the final model, aligning with prior research showing that availability is a proximal determinant of child dietary intake [27, 28]. Reducing availability and increasing the visibility and accessibility of healthier options may therefore be one of the most powerful levers for intervention. This is inseparable from broader market and structural forces: aggressive marketing of sugar‐dense products, coupled with price advantages over healthier alternatives, makes home availability difficult to modify through individual caregiver action alone [29]. Food pricing policies, sugar‐sweetened beverage taxes, and clearer front‐of‐pack labeling have been proposed to address these structural barriers and are critical tools for sustainable dietary change in low‐income communities [30, 31].

Limitations

Several important correlates were not measured, including caregiver education, nutrition knowledge, food insecurity, and neighborhood food access, all of which may confound the observed associations. Dietary intake was assessed using caregiver self‐report, introducing recall and social desirability bias. As noted, the frequency‐based screener does not capture portion size or allow biochemical distinction between added and free sugars, which limits precision in estimating absolute sugar intake. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the temporal direction of observed associations remains uncertain. Finally, the sample was drawn from a single geographic region, which may limit generalizability to other low‐income Head Start populations.

Future Research

Intervening during the preschool years may have enduring benefits for establishing healthy sugar‐related habits. Future research should evaluate scalable, technology‐supported strategies that reduce home availability and promote consistent caregiver feeding practices using longitudinal designs to capture developmental change. Notably, data for this study were collected in 2021, prior to the 2024 federal rule phasing in added sugar limits for the Child and Adult Care Food Program governing Head Start meal patterns. These findings therefore represent a pre‐policy baseline against which future studies can assess whether changes to the institutional food environment moderate the home environment factors identified here.
