Universal Complex Quantum-Like Bits from Hermitian Weighted Graphs
Ethan Dickey, Sabre Kais

Abstract
We study when block-coupled regular graphs can realize prescribed complex quantum-like bit states as exact synchronized eigenstates. Two regular subgraphs G A G_{A} and G B G_{B} supply normalized all-ones eigenvectors V A V_{A} and V B V_{B} , and algebraically regular bipartite couplings reduce the full graph-supported operator exactly to a 2 × 2 2\times 2 effective block on 𝒮 = span { | 0 ⟩ , | 1 ⟩ } \mathcal{S}=\Span\{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle\} . Within this reduction we prove that two natural symmetric complexifications are not universal under a real-spectrum requirement: complex symmetric coupling with real diagonal regularities forces the target computational basis amplitude ratio r = ω 2 / ω 1 r=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1} , for | ψ ⟩ = ω 1 | 0 ⟩ + ω 2 | 1 ⟩ \lvert\psi\rangle=\omega_{1}\lvert 0\rangle+\omega_{2}\lvert 1\rangle , to satisfy r 2 ∈ ℝ r^{2}\in\mathbb{R} , while real symmetric coupling with complex diagonal regularities forces r + 1 / r ∈ ℝ r+1/r\in\mathbb{R} . Replacing complex symmetry by Hermitian coupling removes this phase obstruction. For any nonbasis target state, any prescribed real eigenvalue, and any prescribed nonzero signed spectral gap, a Hermitian weighted coupling realizes the target exactly. Additionally, an independently tuned directed-coupling model gives a second universality mechanism. We then pass from continuous effective parameters to finite weighted graphs with entries in { 0 , ± 1 , ± i } \{0,\pm 1,\pm i\} (the fourth roots of unity and zero), characterize the balanced discrete coupling lattice by perfect matchings, and show that exact discrete Hermitian realizations are dense in the synchronized pure-state space. These results give a universality taxonomy for complex QL-bits and identify Hermitian conjugate pairing as the robust structural mechanism that supports arbitrary complex amplitudes with real two-level spectra.

For quantum-like scholarship to mature, however, it is not enough to show that some two-level sector exists or that certain real-amplitude states can be constructed. The central structural question is sharper: given a target normalized state

\lvert\psi\rangle=\omega_{1}\lvert 0\rangle+\omega_{2}\lvert 1\rangle,\quad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,

In particular, once one allows genuinely complex amplitudes, the design problem is no longer only about state synthesis; it is also about spectral reality and stability. A construction may reproduce the desired eigenvector algebraically while still failing to define a viable two-level model if the associated eigenvalues become complex or if the target sector is not spectrally isolated. The purpose of this paper is to resolve that structural problem for a broad family of block-coupled graph constructions.

Allowing complex edge weights broadens the design space but also raises a central spectral issue. In this work, we study block-coupled regular subgraphs whose synchronized modes define an effective qubit basis. We first derive a common synchronized-space reduction that identifies the induced

We then prove obstruction theorems showing that two natural symmetric complexifications fail to realize arbitrary complex nonbasis states once a real-spectrum condition is imposed. More broadly, the paper shows that the synthesis of complex two-level states in graph-supported operators is governed not by graph size alone, but by a precise interplay among regularity, reciprocity, conjugation symmetry, and arithmetic realizability. In that sense, the work contributes both to the developing theory of quantum-like state spaces and to the broader study of exact low-dimensional sectors engineered inside large structured networks.

Preliminaries

Following prior QL-bit work, we begin from the picture of a single QL-bit as a pair of emergent graph states that are spectrally separated from the bulk and can be identified with a two-level Hilbert space. In our setting, those states are built from two coupled subgraphs
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
. The symmetric construction singles out the analogs of
|+\rangle
and
|-\rangle
; the later sections then ask how complex weights, detuning, and asymmetric coupling enlarge the set of realizable states. The purpose of this section is to fix the common graph-theoretic setup and isolate the two-dimensional sector used throughout the paper.

We work at the adjacency-matrix level. Let
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
have adjacency matrices
A
and
B
, where
G_{A}
is
k_{A}
-regular on
n
vertices and
G_{B}
is
k_{B}
-regular on
m
vertices. In this regular setting, the top eigenvector of each subgraph is the normalized all-ones vector (their Perron-Frobenius eigenvectors
[
27
]
),

V_{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\mathbf{1}_{n},\qquad V_{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\mathbf{1}_{m}

(1)

with eigenvalues

AV_{A}=k_{A}V_{A},\qquad BV_{B}=k_{B}V_{B}.

(2)

We regard
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
as the
synchronized modes
of the two subgraphs. Here “synchronized” means that the amplitude is constant across all vertices of the corresponding subgraph. Regularity makes this block-constant vector invariant under the internal adjacency: because every row of
A
has sum
k_{A}
, the action of
A
on
V_{A}
only rescales
V_{A}
, and similarly for
B
and
V_{B}
. Thus the internal graph dynamics do not create variation within either subgraph when the system is initialized on these modes. Once the coupling block also has constant weighted row- and column-sums, the full operator maps block-constant vectors back into the two-dimensional span generated by
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
. These vectors are the building blocks from which QL-bit states are assembled.

Embed these into the full space by defining

\lvert 0\rangle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
\mathbf{0}_{m}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lvert 1\rangle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}\\
V_{B}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\Span\{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle\}.

(3)

Equivalently, the Hadamard basis on
\mathcal{S}
is

\psi_{+}\equiv\lvert+\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert 0\rangle+\lvert 1\rangle)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
V_{B}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\psi_{-}\equiv\lvert-\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert 0\rangle-\lvert 1\rangle)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
-V_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

Thus any normalized synchronized state may be written as

\psi=a\,\lvert+\rangle+b\,\lvert-\rangle=\omega_{1}\lvert 0\rangle+\omega_{2}\lvert 1\rangle,\qquad|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,

where

\omega_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a+b),\qquad\omega_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a-b).

The later sections ask which choices of coupling and detuning make a prescribed state in
\mathcal{S}
an eigenvector.

In the original real-valued QL-bit construction
[
18
]
, the two subgraphs are coupled by a bipartite block
C
, giving

R_{\mathrm{real}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}.

The complex-weighted extensions considered below keep the same diagonal blocks but vary the relation between the off-diagonal blocks:
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
use

\begin{pmatrix}A&-C\\
-C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix},

Section

3.3
uses

H\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-C\\
-C^{\dagger}&B\end{pmatrix},

and
Section

4
uses

R_{\mathrm{asym}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-C_{A}\\
-C_{B}&B\end{pmatrix}.

What matters in every case is the induced action on
\mathcal{S}
.

2.1
Algebraic regularity of the coupling block

For complex edge weights, combinatorial degree is no longer the right notion of regularity, because phase cancellations can make a combinatorially regular block act nonuniformly on the synchronized mode. The correct condition is constant weighted row- and column-sums.

We call
C\in\mathbb{C}^{\,n\times m}

algebraically regular
if

C\mathbf{1}_{m}=s_{A}\,\mathbf{1}_{n},\qquad C^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}=s_{B}\,\mathbf{1}_{m}

(5)

for some
s_{A},s_{B}\in\mathbb{C}
. These scalars are not independent: both equations encode the total weighted sum of the entries of
C
, so necessarily

ns_{A}=ms_{B}.

Relative to (
1
), this means that
C
acts by a single effective scalar on the synchronized sector:

CV_{B}=l\,V_{A},\qquad C^{T}V_{A}=l\,V_{B},\qquad l\coloneqq s_{A}\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}=s_{B}\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}.

If the lower off-diagonal block is
C^{\dagger}
instead of
C^{T}
, then the same computation gives

C^{\dagger}V_{A}=l^{*}V_{B}.

Thus algebraic regularity is precisely the condition that a coupling block preserve synchronization. In the discrete constructions of
Section

6
, writing
l=c+di
records the net real and imaginary weight seen by the synchronized mode.

2.2
Synchronized subspace and effective reduction

Once the diagonal blocks preserve
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
, and the off-diagonal blocks act on them by scalar multiplication, the full
(n+m)
-dimensional problem closes on the two-dimensional space
\mathcal{S}
.

Proposition 2.1

(General synchronized reduction)
.

Let

R\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-X\\
-Y&B\end{pmatrix},

where
A\in\mathbb{C}^{\,n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
,
X\in\mathbb{C}^{\,n\times m}
, and
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
. Assume

AV_{A}=k_{A}V_{A},\qquad BV_{B}=k_{B}V_{B},\qquad XV_{B}=l_{A}V_{A},\qquad YV_{A}=l_{B}V_{B}

for some
k_{A},k_{B},l_{A},l_{B}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
\mathcal{S}
is
R
-invariant, and relative to the ordered basis
\{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle\}
the restriction
R|_{\mathcal{S}}
is represented by

M_{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l_{A}\\
-l_{B}&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Equivalently, for any
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
,

R\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(k_{A}\omega_{1}-l_{A}\omega_{2})V_{A}\\
(k_{B}\omega_{2}-l_{B}\omega_{1})V_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular,
\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix}
is an eigenvector of
R
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if

(k_{A}-\lambda)\omega_{1}=l_{A}\omega_{2},\qquad(k_{B}-\lambda)\omega_{2}=l_{B}\omega_{1}.

Proof.

For any
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
,

R\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}AV_{A}-\omega_{2}XV_{B}\\
-\omega_{1}YV_{A}+\omega_{2}BV_{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(k_{A}\omega_{1}-l_{A}\omega_{2})V_{A}\\
(k_{B}\omega_{2}-l_{B}\omega_{1})V_{B}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}.

This proves invariance and identifies the coordinate matrix of
R|_{\mathcal{S}}
. The final statement is exactly the eigenvalue equation for
M_{\mathcal{S}}
.
∎

This proposition is the common engine behind the rest of the paper. In the symmetric settings,
X=C
and
Y=C^{T}
, so algebraic regularity yields
l_{A}=l_{B}=l
. In the Hermitian setting,
X=C
and
Y=C^{\dagger}
, so
l_{A}=l
and
l_{B}=l^{*}
. In the asymmetric setting,
X=C_{A}
and
Y=C_{B}
, so
l_{A}
and
l_{B}
can be tuned independently (although
k_{A}=k_{B}
). The question in
Sections

3
,
4
and
5
is therefore not whether the reduction exists, but which target states in
\mathcal{S}
and which real two-level spectra these different reduced blocks can realize.

Complex Edge Weights in Symmetric Coupling and Detuning

Extending the constructions in
[
18
]
, we demonstrate how complex coefficients can be effectively represented by the adjacency matrices of complex synchronized networks. We first treat
k_{A},k_{B},l
and, in the asymmetric setting,
l_{A},l_{B}
, as continuously tunable effective regularities under several structural restrictions; finite-alphabet and finite-graph constraints are imposed in
Section

6
.

3.1
Symmetric coupling and the global block matrix.

We consider two disjoint undirected regular subgraphs
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
with adjacency blocks
A
and
B
and allow
detuning

k_{A}\neq k_{B}
. We couple them via an undirected bipartite block
C
. To match the eigenvalue relations used throughout this work (cf. the form
\lambda_{R}=k_{A}-rl
), we absorb a global sign into the off-diagonal blocks and define the complex-symmetric matrix

R\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-C\\[2.84526pt]
-C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

Because
R^{T}=R
but generally
R^{\dagger}\neq R
, the model is complex symmetric but not Hermitian; we therefore do not invoke Hermitian spectral theorems in this section.

Applying
Proposition

2.1
with
X=C
and
Y=C^{T}
, the synchronized restriction of
R
is

M_{\mathrm{sym}}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, in the symmetric setting, the problem is not the existence of the synchronized reduction, but rather which target states and real spectra this reduced block can realize.

3.2
Why symmetric complexifications are insufficient

We now separate two requirements for a viable QL-bit construction on the synchronized subspace. The first is
state synthesis
: realizing a prescribed target state

\lvert\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\[2.84526pt]
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix},\qquad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1.

The second is
spectral stability
: keeping the corresponding effective two-level spectrum real and separated.
The reality condition is essential because, given a matrix and its eigenvector

RV_{R}=\lambda_{R}V_{R}\,,

if we take
R
as the generator of a continuous time evolution via

\psi(t)=e^{-itR}\psi(0)\,,

and take
\psi(0)=V_{R}
, then

\psi(t)=e^{-itR}V_{R}=e^{-it\lambda_{R}}V_{R}\,.

It is desirable for
V_{R}
to only be rotated by a phase. However, if the corresponding eigenvalue is complex, say
\lambda_{R}=\alpha+\beta i
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

e^{-it\lambda_{R}}V_{R}=e^{-it\alpha}e^{t\beta}V_{R}\,.

The factor
e^{-it\alpha}
gives the expected phase rotation, but the nonzero imaginary part
\beta
produces exponential growth or decay of the model amplitude. Accordingly, throughout this subsection we require the relevant effective eigenvalues to be real, and, when possible, strictly separated by a positive gap.

In particular, the requirement that the relevant eigenvalues be real is not merely a formal convenience. In the present setting, the reduced
2\times 2
synchronized block is intended to encode a quantum-like two-level sector, so its eigenvalues play the role of effective frequencies or energies. Real eigenvalues therefore correspond to stationary oscillatory modes, while complex eigenvalues introduce built-in amplification or dissipation. From this perspective, allowing complex target amplitudes is not itself problematic; the issue is whether those amplitudes can be embedded into a reduced operator whose dynamics remain phase-like rather than exponentially unstable. This is the reason for separating the problem of state synthesis from the problem of spectral realization: a construction may reproduce a desired eigenvector algebraically, yet still fail to provide a physically meaningful or stable two-level model if the associated spectrum is not real.

For nonbasis states, i.e.
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0
, define the amplitude ratio

r\coloneqq\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

(7)

As in
Section

2.2
,
the block constructions considered below reduce to
2\times 2
scalar systems in the variable
r
. The next two propositions show that neither of the natural symmetric placements of the complex degrees of freedom yield arbitrary complex target states under a real-spectrum constraint.

Proposition 3.1

(Obstruction for complex symmetric coupling with real detuning)
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and consider the effective reduced matrix

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{csym}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l&k_{B}\end{pmatrix},

(8)

\displaystyle k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{R},\quad l\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

(9)

If
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
, with
r=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}
, is an eigenvector of
M_{\mathrm{csym}}
with a real eigenvalue, then

r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

Equivalently,
r
is either purely real or purely imaginary,

r\in(\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}\,.

(11)

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be the eigenvalue corresponding to
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
. Then

\lambda=k_{A}-lr=k_{B}-\frac{l}{r}.

Since
k_{A},k_{B},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows that

lr=k_{A}-\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{l}{r}=k_{B}-\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

As
l\neq 0
and
r\neq 0
, dividing gives

r^{2}=\frac{lr}{\,l/r\,}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

The equivalence of
Equations

10
and
11
is immediate: if
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
r
is either real or purely imaginary, and conversely both cases clearly satisfy
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Before turning to the realization statement, it is convenient to separate two remaining spectral parameters once the target ratio
r
is fixed: the location
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
of the distinguished eigenvalue, and the signed offset
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
locating the second eigenvalue relative to it. For an isolated
2\times 2
sector, the absolute value of one eigenvalue is often not essential, since adding a scalar multiple of the identity shifts both eigenvalues by the same amount and contributes only a global phase to the evolution. However, keeping
\lambda
explicit is stronger than normalizing it to, for example,
0
: it shows that the target synchronized mode can be placed at any prescribed real spectral location, not merely somewhere on the real axis, and that the second eigenvalue can then be placed at a prescribed signed offset. This is also the natural formulation when the reduced block is viewed as part of a larger operator, where the absolute spectral location may matter.

Now fix
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
satisfying
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. The eigenvector relations

k_{A}-lr=\lambda,\quad k_{B}-\frac{l}{r}=\lambda

show that the family of admissible parameters has only one remaining real degree of freedom. Although
l
a priori carries two real degrees of freedom, requiring
k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{R}
forces
lr\in\mathbb{R}
, and then

\frac{l}{r}=\frac{lr}{r^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}

automatically because
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. It is therefore natural to set

\tau\coloneqq lr\in\mathbb{R},\quad\text{equivalently}\quad l=\frac{\tau}{r},

which parametrizes the feasible set by its single intrinsic real parameter. In this parametrization,

k_{A}=\lambda+\tau,\qquad k_{B}=\lambda+\frac{\tau}{r^{2}}.

(12)

Let
\lambda_{2}
denote the other eigenvalue of
M_{\mathrm{csym}}
(
8
). Since the trace of any square matrix equals the sum of its eigenvalues (counted with algebraic multiplicity), we have

\tr(M_{\mathrm{csym}})=\lambda+\lambda_{2}.

Therefore,

\lambda_{2}=\tr(M_{\mathrm{csym}})-\lambda.

The trace is then

\tr(M_{\mathrm{csym}})=k_{A}+k_{B}=2\lambda+\tau(1+r^{-2}),

hence the second eigenvalue is

\lambda+\tau(1+r^{-2}).

Prescribing a signed offset
\delta
is therefore equivalent to choosing

\delta=\tau\bigl(1+r^{-2}\bigr).

(13)

The next proposition shows that this necessary condition is also sufficient: whenever
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, the complex-symmetric model realizes the target ratio with an arbitrary prescribed real eigenvalue and, except in the degenerate case
r=\pm i
, an arbitrary prescribed signed offset.

Proposition 3.2

(Realization for complex symmetric coupling with
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
)
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
with
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exist
k_{A},k_{B},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
l\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that

M_{\mathrm{csym}}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}

has a real spectrum and satisfies

M_{\mathrm{csym}}\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}=\lambda\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}.

Moreover, if
r\neq\pm i
, then for any prescribed target eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and any prescribed nonzero signed offset
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, one may choose

\tau\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{1+r^{-2}},\qquad l\coloneqq\frac{\tau}{r},\qquad k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+\tau,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{\tau}{r^{2}},

so that the eigenvalues are
\lambda
and
\lambda+\delta
.

Proof.

Choose any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, the quantities

l\coloneqq\frac{\tau}{r},\qquad k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+\tau,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{\tau}{r^{2}}

satisfy
k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{R}
and
l\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
whenever
\tau\neq 0
. A direct calculation gives

k_{A}-lr=\lambda,\qquad k_{B}-\frac{l}{r}=\lambda,

so
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
.

The trace equals

k_{A}+k_{B}=2\lambda+\tau\left(1+r^{-2}\right),

so the second eigenvalue is

\lambda+\tau\left(1+r^{-2}\right).

If
r\neq\pm i
, then
1+r^{-2}\neq 0
, and choosing

\tau=\frac{\delta}{1+r^{-2}}

gives second eigenvalue
\lambda+\delta
.
∎

One can place the complex degree of freedom differently by keeping the coupling real and allowing the detunings to be complex,
l\in\mathbb{R}
and
k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{C}
. This changes the form of the obstruction, but not the conclusion: the realizable set of quantum states remains a strict subset of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. The next proposition identifies the exact condition in this second symmetric model.

Proposition 3.3

(Obstruction for real symmetric coupling with complex detuning)
.

Let

M_{\mathrm{rsym}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l&k_{B}\end{pmatrix},\quad l\in\mathbb{R}^{\times},\quad k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{C}.

Assume that
M_{\mathrm{rsym}}
has real spectrum, and let
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
be an eigenvector with
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0
. Define

r\coloneqq\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

Then

r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}.

Equivalently,

r\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{or}\quad|r|=1.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
be the eigenvalue corresponding to
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
. Then

\lambda_{1}=k_{A}-lr=k_{B}-\frac{l}{r}\,,

so

k_{A}=\lambda_{1}+lr,\qquad k_{B}=\lambda_{1}+\frac{l}{r}\,.

Relating the trace to the eigenvalues of a square matrix in the standard way,

\tr(M_{\mathrm{rsym}})=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=k_{A}+k_{B}\,,

the second eigenvalue is then

\lambda_{2}=(k_{A}+k_{B})-\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}+l\left(r+\frac{1}{r}\right).

(14)

Since the spectrum is assumed real and
l\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, it follows that

r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}.

Now write
r=\rho e^{i\phi}
with
\rho>0
. Then

r+\frac{1}{r}=\left(\rho+\rho^{-1}\right)\cos\phi+i\left(\rho-\rho^{-1}\right)\sin\phi.

This is real if and only if

\left(\rho-\rho^{-1}\right)\sin\phi=0,

i.e. if and only if either
\rho=1
or
\sin\phi=0
. Equivalently, either
|r|=1
or
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Parameterization.

Unlike the complex coupling case (
Proposition

3.2
), where a real auxiliary parameter
\tau
is introduced to enforce the constraints
k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{R}
, the present setting imposes the realness condition directly on the coupling
l\in\mathbb{R}
. As a result, it is more natural to work directly with
l
. Introducing an auxiliary parameter analogous to
\tau
would amount to a redundant reparameterization and does not simplify the algebra.

The next proposition shows that the obstruction of
Proposition

3.3
is also sufficient. Thus, within the real-coupling symmetric model, the exact realizable set of states is

\left\{r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}:r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

and, away from the degenerate case
r=\pm i
, the second eigenvalue may again be placed at an arbitrary prescribed signed offset.

Proposition 3.4

(Realization for real symmetric coupling with complex detuning)
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that
r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}.
Then there exist
l\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
,
k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{C}
, and a real eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

M_{\mathrm{rsym}}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}

has a real spectrum and satisfies

M_{\mathrm{rsym}}\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}=\lambda\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}.

Moreover, if
r+\frac{1}{r}\neq 0
(equivalently
r\neq\pm i
), then for any prescribed target eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and any prescribed nonzero signed offset
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, one may choose

l\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{\,r+\frac{1}{r}\,},\qquad k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+lr,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{l}{r},

so that the eigenvalues are
\lambda
and
\lambda+\delta
.

Proof.

Choose any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

First suppose
r+\frac{1}{r}\neq 0
. Set

l\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{r+\frac{1}{r}}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+lr,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{l}{r}.

Since
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
and
r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, we have
l\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. Moreover,

k_{A}-lr=\lambda,\qquad k_{B}-\frac{l}{r}=\lambda,

so
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and the second eigenvalue (as in
Equation

14
) is

(k_{A}+k_{B})-\lambda=\lambda+l\left(r+\frac{1}{r}\right)=\lambda+\delta\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence the spectrum is real.

If instead
r+\frac{1}{r}=0
(equivalently
r=\pm i
), choose any
l\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
and define

k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+lr,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{l}{r}.

Then
\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}
is again an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and the second eigenvalue also equals
\lambda
, again giving that the spectrum is real. This is a zero-gap realization; it is not covered by the prescribed-offset assertion, since the trace identity forces
\delta=l\left(r+\frac{1}{r}\right)=0.

∎

Corollary 3.5

(Non-universality of the two symmetric complexifications)
.

Under a real-spectrum requirement, neither of the two symmetric complexifications above yield arbitrary complex QL-bit states. Indeed, their exact state spaces are

\{r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}:r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}=(\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\{r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}:r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\mathbb{R}^{\times}\cup\{r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}:|r|=1\},

respectively, whose union is a strict subset of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. In particular, the generic complex ratio
r=2e^{i\pi/4}
is not realizable by either symmetric model.

Proof.

The first identity is
Proposition

3.1
, and the second is

Proposition

3.3
. Their union is a strict subset because, for example,
r=2e^{i\pi/4}
is neither real nor purely imaginary and does not satisfy
|r|=1
.
∎

3.3
The Hermitian Remedy

The preceding obstructions show that, within the synchronized two-level ansatz, relocating the complex degree of freedom within a
symmetric
reduced matrix does not suffice to obtain arbitrary complex target states while maintaining a real, separated spectrum. The minimal successful modification is instead to replace complex symmetry by Hermitian symmetry.

Theorem 3.6

(Hermitian coupling realizes arbitrary complex nonbasis states with prescribed real spectral gap)
.

Interpret
k_{A},k_{B},
and
l
as continuously tunable effective parameters. Assume
A=A^{\dagger}
,
B=B^{\dagger}
, and
k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{R}
, and consider the Hermitian block matrix

H\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-C\\[2.84526pt]
-C^{\dagger}&B\end{pmatrix}.

(15)

Assume

AV_{A}=k_{A}V_{A},\qquad BV_{B}=k_{B}V_{B},\qquad CV_{B}=l\,V_{A},\qquad C^{\dagger}V_{A}=l^{*}\,V_{B}.

Let

\lvert\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\[2.84526pt]
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix},\qquad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,\qquad\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0,

and define
r\coloneqq\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}.
Then for any prescribed eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and any prescribed nonzero signed offset
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, choosing

\tau\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{1+|r|^{-2}},\qquad l\coloneqq\frac{\tau}{r},\qquad k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+\tau,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}}

(16)

makes
\lvert\psi\rangle
an eigenvector of
H
and
H|_{\mathcal{S}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and the other eigenvalue of
H|_{\mathcal{S}}
equals
\lambda+\delta
.

(Thus the target state may be chosen as either the lower or upper synchronized eigenstate by taking
\delta>0
or
\delta<0
, respectively.)

Proof.

By
Proposition

2.1
applied with
X=C
and
Y=C^{\dagger}
, the synchronized subspace

\mathcal{S}=\Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
V_{B}\end{pmatrix}\right\}

is
H
-invariant, and

H|_{\mathcal{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l^{*}&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if

\lambda=k_{A}-lr\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda=k_{B}-\frac{l^{*}}{r}\,.

With the prescribed choice of (
16
),

\tau,k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{R}\qquad lr=\tau,\qquad\frac{l^{*}}{r}=\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}},

so indeed

k_{A}-lr=\lambda+\tau-\tau=\lambda,\qquad k_{B}-\frac{l^{*}}{r}=\lambda+\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}}-\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}}=\lambda.

Thus
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenvector of
H
with eigenvalue
\lambda
.

The restriction of
H
to
\mathcal{S}
(
Proposition

2.1
) is the
2\times 2
Hermitian matrix

\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\[2.84526pt]
-l^{*}&k_{B}\end{pmatrix},

so its eigenvalues are real. Since one eigenvalue is
\lambda
, the other is determined by the trace:

\lambda_{2}=(k_{A}+k_{B})-\lambda=\lambda+\tau+\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}}=\lambda+\delta.

This proves the claim.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

For the continuous weighted-graph model, the coupling regularity assumption is nonempty. Given any
l\in\mathbb{C}
, one may take

C=l\,V_{A}V_{B}^{\dagger},

which has entries
l/\sqrt{nm}
and satisfies

CV_{B}=lV_{A},\qquad C^{\dagger}V_{A}=l^{*}V_{B}.

Thus the effective parameter
l
can always be realized by a weighted complete bipartite coupling; sparser algebraically regular couplings may be substituted when available.

The ratio parametrization in
Theorem

3.6
(
r=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}
) is convenient for proving existence, but in the Hermitian case one can also write the required effective regularities directly in terms of the target amplitudes. The next lemma records this amplitude-space parameterization and shows that, once the target state and the two spectral parameters
\lambda
and
\delta
are fixed, the Hermitian reduced block is uniquely determined.

Lemma 3.7

(Direct amplitude-space realization of the Hermitian effective block)
.

With the notation of
Theorem

3.6
, write the target synchronized state on the reduced basis of
\mathcal{S}
as

\psi\coloneqq\binom{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}},\qquad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,\qquad\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0,

and define its orthogonal complement direction by

\psi_{\perp}\coloneqq\binom{-\omega_{2}^{*}}{\omega_{1}^{*}}.

For prescribed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, consider the Hermitian reduced block (
Proposition

2.1
)

M_{H}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\[2.84526pt]
-l^{*}&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

M_{H}\psi=\lambda\psi,\qquad M_{H}\psi_{\perp}=(\lambda+\delta)\psi_{\perp}

if and only if

k_{A}=\lambda+\delta|\omega_{2}|^{2},\qquad k_{B}=\lambda+\delta|\omega_{1}|^{2},\qquad l=\delta\,\omega_{1}\omega_{2}^{*}.

(17)

In particular, once the target amplitudes and the spectral gap
(\lambda,\delta)
are fixed, the effective Hermitian parameters are uniquely determined.

Proof.

Because
|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1
, both
\psi
and
\psi_{\perp}
have unit norm, and a direct computation shows
\langle\psi|\psi_{\perp}\rangle=0
; hence they are orthonormal. Writing out the eigenvector equations for each eigenvector gives

k_{A}\omega_{1}-l\omega_{2}=\lambda\omega_{1},\qquad k_{A}\omega_{2}^{*}+l\omega_{1}^{*}=(\lambda+\delta)\omega_{2}^{*},

(18)

and

k_{B}\omega_{2}-l^{*}\omega_{1}=\lambda\omega_{2},\qquad k_{B}\omega_{1}^{*}+l^{*}\omega_{2}^{*}=(\lambda+\delta)\omega_{1}^{*},

(19)

respectively. Multiply the first pair (
18
) by
\omega_{1}^{*}
and
\omega_{2}
, respectively, and add. The
l
-terms cancel, yielding

k_{A}\bigl(|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}\bigr)=\lambda|\omega_{1}|^{2}+(\lambda+\delta)|\omega_{2}|^{2},

hence

k_{A}=\lambda+\delta|\omega_{2}|^{2}.

Similarly, multiplying the second pair (
19
) by
\omega_{2}^{*}
and
\omega_{1}
and adding eliminates
l^{*}
and gives

k_{B}=\lambda+\delta|\omega_{1}|^{2}.

Substituting this into the first eigenvector equation gives

l=\frac{(k_{A}-\lambda)\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}=\delta|\omega_{2}|^{2}\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}=\delta\,\omega_{1}\omega_{2}^{*}.

This proves necessity.

Conversely, with these choices,

k_{A}\omega_{1}-l\omega_{2}=(\lambda+\delta|\omega_{2}|^{2})\omega_{1}-\delta\,\omega_{1}\omega_{2}^{*}\,\omega_{2}=\lambda\omega_{1},

and

-l^{*}\omega_{1}+k_{B}\omega_{2}=-\delta\,\omega_{1}^{*}\omega_{2}\,\omega_{1}+(\lambda+\delta|\omega_{1}|^{2})\omega_{2}=\lambda\omega_{2},

so
M_{H}\psi=\lambda\psi
. Also,

\tr(M_{H})=k_{A}+k_{B}=2\lambda+\delta.

Hence the other eigenvalue is
\lambda+\delta
(as the trace also equals the sum of the eigenvalues). Since
M_{H}
is Hermitian and
\delta\neq 0
, the corresponding eigenspace is orthogonal to
\psi
, namely
\Span\{\psi_{\perp}\}
. Therefore

M_{H}\psi_{\perp}=(\lambda+\delta)\psi_{\perp}.

Uniqueness is immediate from the necessity argument.
∎

Using
r=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}
, this is equivalent to the parameter choice (
16
). More importantly, it already reveals the basic Hermitian mechanism: the diagonal terms depend only on the population weights
|\omega_{1}|^{2}
and
|\omega_{2}|^{2}
, while the off-diagonal coupling stores the relative phase through the product
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}^{*}
. We now explain why that mechanism removes the obstructions present in the two symmetric models.

Why the Hermitian construction works.

The two symmetric models fail for the same structural reason: the same scalar
l
appears in both off-diagonal positions, so the reduced eigenvalue equations involve the pair
lr
and
l/r
without complex conjugation. As a result, the phase of
r
survives in the consistency conditions. In the complex-symmetric model with
k_{A},k_{B},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, requiring

\lambda=k_{A}-lr,\qquad\lambda=k_{B}-\frac{l}{r}

forces both
lr
and
l/r
to be real, hence
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. In the real-coupling symmetric model with
l\in\mathbb{R}
, the second eigenvalue is

\lambda_{2}=\lambda+l\left(r+\frac{1}{r}\right),

so the real spectrum forces

r+\frac{1}{r}\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, in both symmetric placements of the complex degree of freedom, the target phase encoded in
r
is not fully absorbed by the matrix structure; instead it reappears as an additional reality constraint on
r
.

The Hermitian reduction

M_{H}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l^{*}&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}

changes exactly this feature. Its eigenvalue equations are

\lambda=k_{A}-lr,\qquad\lambda=k_{B}-\frac{l^{*}}{r}.

If one chooses

l=\frac{\tau}{r},\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R},

then

lr=\tau,\qquad\frac{l^{*}}{r}=\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}}.

The phase of
r
is therefore absorbed into the conjugate pair
l
and
l^{*}
, and the consistency conditions depend only on
|r|^{2}
, not on
r^{2}
or on
r+1/r
. Since
|r|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for every
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, no residual phase obstruction remains. Moreover, the spectral gap set to

\delta=\tau\left(1+|r|^{-2}\right)

is solvable for every prescribed
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, because

1+|r|^{-2}>0.

At the scalar level, this is the key change: Hermitian symmetry replaces the problematic quantities
r^{2}
and
r+1/r
by the always-real quantity
|r|^{2}
.

Equivalently, on the synchronized two-level space, Hermitian symmetry allows the target state and its orthogonal complement to serve as an eigenbasis. The relative phase is then stored in a conjugate pair of off-diagonal entries, while the effective eigenvalues remain real by construction.

The preceding universality statements are formulated for nonbasis states because the ratio
r=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}
is only defined when both amplitudes are nonzero. The omitted endpoints are not difficult graph-theoretic cases; they are boundary points of the same two-level state space where the coupling between the two synchronized modes must vanish. We record this separately before turning from the reduced
2\times 2
block to the full graph-supported operator.

Remark 3.2

(Basis states)
.

The singular cases
\omega_{1}=0
or
\omega_{2}=0
correspond to basis states and lie outside the ratio parametrization
r=\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}
. In both the symmetric and Hermitian coupled models, these states occur exactly in the decoupled case
l=0
; with nonzero coupling they are obtained only as limits
|r|\to 0
or
|r|\to\infty
.

Further structural consequences.

Theorem
3.6
concerns the synchronized restriction
H|_{\mathcal{S}}
. The next proposition and corollary show that, in the Hermitian setting, this restriction is not merely a computational device: it defines an exact orthogonally decoupled two-level subsystem of the full network. The proofs are deferred to
Appendix

A
.

Proposition 3.8

(The Hermitian construction defines an exact closed two-level subsystem)
.

Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

3.6
, let

\mathcal{S}:=\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
V_{B}\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

Then both
\mathcal{S}
and its orthogonal complement

\mathcal{S}^{\perp}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}:\langle V_{A}|x\rangle=0,\ \langle V_{B}|y\rangle=0\right\}

are invariant under
H
. Equivalently, relative to the orthogonal decomposition

\mathbb{C}^{\,n+m}=\mathcal{S}\oplus\mathcal{S}^{\perp},

the Hermitian matrix
H
is block diagonal:

H=H|_{\mathcal{S}}\oplus H|_{\mathcal{S}^{\perp}}.

(In operator-theoretic language,
\mathcal{S}
is a reducing subspace for
H
.)

Corollary 3.9

(The designed synchronized eigenpair is unique in the full operator)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.6
, suppose in addition that

\{\lambda,\lambda+\delta\}\cap\sigma(H|_{\mathcal{S}^{\perp}})=\varnothing,

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the spectrum. Then
\lambda
and
\lambda+\delta
are simple eigenvalues of the full matrix
H
, and every eigenvector of
H
with either of these eigenvalues lies entirely in
\mathcal{S}
.

Interpretation and practical implications.

Proposition

3.8
clarifies the status of the synchronized reduction. The two-component form
\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A},\,\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
is not merely a trial restriction to block-constant vectors. In the Hermitian model,
\mathcal{S}
is a reducing subspace: both
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
are invariant. Equivalently, after choosing an orthonormal basis adapted to

\mathbb{C}^{\,n+m}=\mathcal{S}\oplus\mathcal{S}^{\perp},

the full matrix
H
becomes block diagonal, so the unitary time evolution splits as

e^{-itH}=e^{-itH|_{\mathcal{S}}}\oplus e^{-itH|_{\mathcal{S}^{\perp}}}.

Thus an initial state in the synchronized sector never leaks into nonsynchronized modes, and nonsynchronized modes never feed amplitude back into the synchronized sector. In this precise sense, the Hermitian construction embeds an exact QL-bit inside the larger graph, rather than providing only an approximate two-level description.

The corollary gives the corresponding spectral interpretation. If
H|_{\mathcal{S}^{\perp}}
does not contain either designed eigenvalue
\lambda
or
\lambda+\delta
, then the two synchronized eigenstates are uniquely identifiable in the full operator. This matters for numerical extraction, spectral addressing, and state preparation: the designed QL-bit eigenvectors cannot be confused with nonsynchronized eigenvectors at the same eigenvalue. This also means that one may enlarge or modify the rest of the network while preserving the intended synchronized QL-bit, provided no spectral collision with
\lambda
or
\lambda+\delta
is introduced in the complementary sector.
For example, a Hermitian perturbation

\Delta H=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta A&-\Delta C\\
-\Delta C^{\dagger}&\Delta B\end{pmatrix}

leaves the synchronized block unchanged if

\Delta AV_{A}=0,\qquad\Delta BV_{B}=0,\qquad\Delta CV_{B}=0,\qquad\Delta C^{\dagger}V_{A}=0.

These conditions say that the perturbation has zero action on the synchronized modes, or equivalently that it changes only the nonsynchronized degrees of freedom. Designing
H|_{S^{\perp}}
is therefore a separate structured spectral problem: local changes in
A
,
B
, or
C
can move many complementary eigenvalues, and avoiding collisions with
\lambda
and
\lambda+\delta
generally requires either explicit spectral estimates or direct spectral verification for the chosen finite graph.

4
Complex Edge Weights in Asymmetric Coupling and Detuning

The Hermitian construction restores universality by pairing the off-diagonal entries by conjugation. A second route is to drop the symmetry requirement altogether and allow the two coupling directions to be tuned independently. We first isolate the genuinely asymmetric mechanism by keeping a common synchronized regularity on both sides, i.e. by imposing
k_{A}=k_{B}=k
while allowing the two coupling blocks to differ. This shows that universality can already be recovered from directed coupling alone, without any diagonal detuning. We then pass to the fully generalized setting (
Section

5
) in which the diagonal regularities are also allowed to differ, so that asymmetry and detuning are both present.

On the synchronized subspace, we replace the single effective coupling
l
of
Section

2.2
by two independent scalars
l_{A}
and
l_{B}
in the reduced
2\times 2
block.

Throughout
Sections

4
and
5
,
\lambda
denotes the eigenvalue assigned to the target state, and the other synchronized eigenvalue is written as
\lambda+\delta
, where
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
is a signed offset. Thus
\delta>0
makes the target state the lower synchronized eigenstate, while
\delta<0
makes it the upper synchronized eigenstate. In the common-regularity parametrization of
Theorem

4.1
,
\tau=\delta/2
and
k=\lambda+\tau
, so the two synchronized eigenvalues are

k-\tau=\lambda,\qquad k+\tau=\lambda+\delta.

Theorem 4.1

(Asymmetric coupling with common regularity realizes arbitrary complex nonbasis states with prescribed real spectral gap)
.

Interpret
k
,
l_{A}
, and
l_{B}
as continuously tunable effective parameters, and consider the block matrix

R_{\mathrm{asym}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-C_{A}\\[2.84526pt]
-C_{B}&B\end{pmatrix}.

Assume

AV_{A}=kV_{A},\qquad BV_{B}=kV_{B},\qquad C_{A}V_{B}=l_{A}V_{A},\qquad C_{B}V_{A}=l_{B}V_{B}.

Let

\lvert\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\[2.84526pt]
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix},\qquad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,\qquad\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0,

and define
r\coloneqq\omega_{2}/\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Then for any prescribed eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and any prescribed nonzero signed offset
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, choosing

\tau\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{2},\qquad k\coloneqq\lambda+\tau,\qquad l_{A}\coloneqq\frac{\tau}{r},\qquad l_{B}\coloneqq\tau r

makes
\lvert\psi\rangle
an eigenvector of
R_{\mathrm{asym}}
and
R_{\mathrm{asym}}|_{\mathcal{S}}
(
Section

2.2
) with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and the other eigenvalue of
R_{\mathrm{asym}}|_{\mathcal{S}}
equals
\lambda+\delta
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

2.1
applied with
X=C_{A}
and
Y=C_{B}
, the synchronized subspace

\mathcal{S}=\Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
V_{B}\end{pmatrix}\right\}

is
R_{\mathrm{asym}}
-invariant, and

R_{\mathrm{asym}}|_{\mathcal{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}k&-l_{A}\\
-l_{B}&k\end{pmatrix}.

Hence
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if

\lambda=k-l_{A}r,\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda=k-\frac{l_{B}}{r}.

With the prescribed choice,

l_{A}r=\tau,\qquad\frac{l_{B}}{r}=\tau,

so indeed

k-l_{A}r=\lambda+\tau-\tau=\lambda,\qquad k-\frac{l_{B}}{r}=\lambda+\tau-\tau=\lambda.

Thus
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenvector of
R_{\mathrm{asym}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
.

The trace of
R_{\mathrm{asym}}|_{\mathcal{S}}
is
2k=2\lambda+2\tau=2\lambda+\delta
. Since one eigenvalue is
\lambda
, the other is

2k-\lambda=\lambda+\delta.

This proves the claim.
∎

Thus, imposing
k_{A}=k_{B}
does not destroy universality so long as the common value

Generalized Construction and Unification Theorem

The preceding theorem shows that the essential gain from asymmetry already appears in the common-regularity case

and

We therefore now pass from the purely asymmetric common-regularity model to the fully generalized asymmetric-detuned reduction, in which both coupling directions and both diagonal levels are treated as independent effective parameters. We proceed using the subspace reduction derived in

Theorem 5.1

(Generalized coupling realizes arbitrary complex nonbasis states with prescribed real spectral gap)
.

Let

\lvert\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\[2.84526pt]
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix},\qquad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,\qquad\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0,

The obstruction disappears here for the same reason it disappears in the Hermitian model: the two scalar conditions are no longer tied to a single off-diagonal parameter. Indeed,

6
Discrete Realizations and Density of Representation

Sections

3
,
4
and
5
are effective-parameter results: the reduced quantities
k_{A},k_{B},l
(and, in the asymmetric setting,
l_{A},l_{B}
) are treated as continuously tunable scalars. For actual finite weighted graphs, however, these parameters are discrete.
The goal of this section is therefore to identify the exact arithmetic row- and column-sum constraints within a balanced model and then show that, as the graph size grows, the resulting exact finite realizations become dense in the synchronized pure-state space.

6.1
Admissible Discrete Coupling Phases

Definition 6.1

(Admissible coupling phases)
.

Define the fourth roots of unity as

\mu_{4}\coloneqq\{\pm 1,\pm i\}=\left\{e^{i\pi m/2}\;\middle|\;m\in\{0,1,2,3\}\right\}.

(20)

We write
\mu_{4}^{0}\coloneqq\{0\}\cup\mu_{4}
for the set of admissible weighted adjacency entries.

We now choose a concrete finite alphabet of allowed coupling weights. The set
\mu_{4}^{0}
should be read as a unit-weight four-phase discretization:
0
denotes an absent edge, while
1
,
-1
,
i
, and
-i
denote present edges with phases
0
,
\pi
,
\pi/2
, and
3\pi/2
. This alphabet is small but structurally natural for the Hermitian setting: it is closed under complex conjugation and supplies positive and negative real and imaginary contributions separately. Consequently, row and column sums of such coupling blocks lie in the Gaussian integers
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

The fourth-root alphabet is not meant to be the only discrete phase alphabet that could ever be studied; rather, it is the minimal canonical one for the Hermitian construction used here. The real alphabet
\{0,\pm 1\}
is too small, since it forces every coupling row-sum to be real and therefore cannot encode a generic Hermitian coherence. Odd root alphabets such as
\mu_{5}
are conjugation-closed but not centrally symmetric: if
\zeta\in\mu_{5}
, then
-\zeta\notin\mu_{5}
, so signed cancellation is not available edge-by-edge and row-sum admissibility is governed by cyclotomic relations rather than by independent positive and negative counts. Even alternatives such as
\mu_{6}
are possible in principle, but they produce an Eisenstein-integer triangular lattice rather than the Cartesian Gaussian-integer lattice; this would give a different arithmetic theory without strengthening the density result. Larger alphabets similarly add cyclotomic arithmetic and extra exact finite cases, but are unnecessary for universality. By using
\mu_{4}^{0}
, every effective coupling has the form
l=c+di\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, the edge budget is exactly
|c|+|d|\leq q
, and the perfect-matching construction realizes this condition by assigning disjoint matchings to the four signed coordinate directions. Thus
\mu_{4}^{0}
is a minimal canonical alphabet for the Gaussian-integer construction used here: it provides Hermitian conjugation symmetry, independent real and imaginary coupling control, a simple exact admissibility condition, and a Gaussian-rational dense set of target ratios.

6.2
Balanced Discrete Coupling

We work throughout in the balanced case
n=m=q
, with
q
even. This parity assumption removes the usual obstruction for ordinary simple regular graphs: when
q
is even,
qk
is even for every
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}
, so
q
-vertex
k
-regular diagonal blocks exist for all admissible degrees.
Thus, the only genuinely nontrivial discrete issue is the coupling block: the diagonal degrees can be realized by standard regular graphs, but achieving a prescribed complex effective coupling requires a row- and column-sum construction with entries restricted to
\mu_{4}^{0}
.

Necessarily, if a balanced
\mu_{4}^{0}
-valued coupling block
C
has common weighted row and column sum
l=c+di
, then
l\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
and
|c|+|d|\leq q
, since each row sum is a sum of
q
terms from
\{0,\pm 1,\pm i\}
. The next proposition shows that this necessary condition is also sufficient: it characterizes the discrete set of balanced effective couplings
l
(and hence conjugate couplings
l^{*}
) available to the Hermitian QL-bit model, and it gives an explicit realization via disjoint perfect matchings.

Proposition 6.1

(Balanced discrete coupling realization via perfect matchings)
.

Fix
q\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

\mathcal{L}_{q}\coloneqq\{\,c+di\in\mathbb{Z}[i]:|c|+|d|\leq q\,\}.

(21)

If
l=c+di\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
, then there exists a matrix
C\in(\mu_{4}^{0})^{q\times q}
such that

C\mathbf{1}_{q}=l\,\mathbf{1}_{q},\qquad C^{T}\mathbf{1}_{q}=l\,\mathbf{1}_{q}.

Equivalently, every
l\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
is realizable as a balanced effective coupling.

Proof.

Let
P_{0},\dots,P_{q-1}
be permutation matrices arising from a
1
-factorization of the complete bipartite graph
K_{q,q}
on two parts of size
q
; for example, one may take the cyclic shift matrices on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. Thus the
P_{j}
are pairwise disjoint, and each row and each column of each
P_{j}
contains exactly one
1
.

Define

C\coloneqq\sign(c)\sum_{j=0}^{|c|-1}P_{j}+i\,\sign(d)\sum_{j=|c|}^{|c|+|d|-1}P_{j}\,,

where
\sign(0)\coloneqq 0
, and empty sums are understood as zero. Equivalently, assign
|c|
values of each row and column to
1
, and then
|d|
values of each row and column to
i
(modulo a sign). Since
|c|+|d|\leq q
, the chosen matchings are disjoint, so every entry of
C
lies in
\{0,\pm 1,\pm i\}=\mu_{4}^{0}
. Moreover, each row and each column meets exactly
|c|
terms in the first block and exactly
|d|
terms in the second, hence every row-sum and every column-sum equals

\sign(c)\,|c|+i\,\sign(d)\,|d|=c+di=l.

Therefore
C\mathbf{1}_{q}=l\,\mathbf{1}_{q}
and
C^{T}\mathbf{1}_{q}=l\,\mathbf{1}_{q}
, as claimed.
∎

Hermitian implication.

With
V_{A}=V_{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\mathbf{1}_{q}
, any such coupling block satisfies

CV_{B}=l\,V_{A},\qquad C^{\dagger}V_{A}=l^{*}V_{B},

since

C^{\dagger}V_{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\,\overline{C^{T}}\mathbf{1}_{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\,l^{*}\mathbf{1}_{q}=l^{*}V_{B}.

Hence every
l\in\mathcal{L}_{q}
yields an exact balanced discrete Hermitian off-diagonal block, and after choosing
A
and
B
so that
AV_{A}=k_{A}V_{A}
and
BV_{B}=k_{B}V_{B}
, one obtains a discrete realization of the Hermitian effective matrix

\begin{pmatrix}k_{A}&-l\\
-l^{*}&k_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
Proposition

6.1
resolves the coupling-level arithmetic problem. The next corollary is the descent step: it combines this coupling realization with
Theorem

3.6
and the existence of regular graphs, showing that admissible effective parameters produce an actual finite Hermitian QL-bit.

6.3
From Arithmetic Admissibility to Density of Discrete Representation

We continue to use the ratio parameter

r\coloneqq\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\qquad|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}=1,\qquad\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\neq 0.

Since
\mathbb{Q}(i)
is dense in
\mathbb{C}
, density will follow once we show that every Gaussian-rational ratio admits an exact balanced discrete Hermitian realization. The next corollary gives the required descent statement from the continuous Hermitian model to the discrete setting; we then verify that every Gaussian-rational ratio satisfies it.

Corollary 6.2

(Balanced discrete Hermitian realization under arithmetic admissibility)
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, and define

\tau\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{1+|r|^{-2}},\qquad l\coloneqq\frac{\tau}{r},\qquad k_{A}\coloneqq\lambda+\tau,\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lambda+\frac{\tau}{|r|^{2}}

as in
Theorem

3.6
. Suppose there exists an even integer
q
such that

l\in\mathcal{L}_{q},\qquad k_{A},k_{B}\in\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}.

Then there exist simple
q
-vertex regular graphs with adjacency matrices
A
and
B
, and a coupling block
C\in(\mu_{4}^{0})^{q\times q}
, such that

H\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&-C\\
-C^{\dagger}&B\end{pmatrix}

has an eigenvector in the synchronized subspace
\mathcal{S}
of the form

\lvert\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix},

with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and the other eigenvalue of
H|_{\mathcal{S}}
equals
\lambda+\delta
.

Proof.

Since
q
is even and

, there exist simple

-vertex

- and
k_{B}
-regular graphs (existence requires

to be even and trivially

AV_{A}=k_{A}V_{A},\qquad BV_{B}=k_{B}V_{B}.

Since

,

6.1
yields a matrix

such that

C\mathbf{1}_{q}=l\,\mathbf{1}_{q},\qquad C^{T}\mathbf{1}_{q}=l\,\mathbf{1}_{q}.

Hence, with

CV_{B}=l\,V_{A},\qquad C^{\dagger}V_{A}=l^{*}V_{B}.

The claim now follows from

3.6

∎

Thus

is the exact discretization step for

3.6

and

), they are realized by an actual finite weighted graph. In particular, every Gaussian-rational ratio is exactly realizable: if
r=z/w\in\mathbb{Q}(i)^{\times}
with

, choose

and
\tau=|z|^{2}
(equivalently

), so that

, and

. Hence, for sufficiently large even

We now show that, allowing the balanced size

to vary and working modulo global phase, the exact balanced discrete Hermitian realizations form a dense subset of the projective synchronized state space.
Any normalized nonbasis synchronized state is determined, up to global phase, by its ratio

r\coloneqq\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

Indeed,

\lvert\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}V_{A}\\
\omega_{2}V_{B}\end{pmatrix}=\omega_{1}\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
rV_{B}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
\binom{V_{A}}{rV_{B}}
is the unnormalized representative of ratio

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\

Therefore normalization forces

so

|\omega_{1}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|r|^{2}}}.

Writing
\omega_{1}=e^{i\theta}|\omega_{1}|

\lvert\psi\rangle=e^{i\theta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|r|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\

for some

.

Corollary 6.3

(Density of exact discrete Hermitian realizations)
.

Allowing the balanced size

to vary, the set of normalized synchronized states exactly realizable by balanced discrete Hermitian QL bits is dense, modulo global phase, in the projective synchronized state space
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

Proof.

Every normalized nonbasis synchronized state may be written as

e^{i\theta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|r|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\

Since

is dense in

with

. The map

r\longmapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|r|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}V_{A}\\
rV_{B}\end{pmatrix}

is continuous on

, so the corresponding projective synchronized states converge to the target state. By the preceding observation, each ratio

is exactly realizable by some balanced discrete Hermitian QL bit, and multiplying an eigenvector by a global phase preserves the eigenvector property. Thus the exactly realizable nonbasis synchronized states are dense.

The basis states are exactly realizable as well, by taking

(equivalently

\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.
∎

7
Conclusion

We have characterized the exact synchronized two-level states that can be realized by block-coupled regular graph operators with complex edge weights. The central result is that complex amplitudes are not obtained merely by allowing complex parameters in a symmetric reduced block: under a real-spectrum requirement, complex-symmetric coupling and real symmetric coupling with complex detuning obey the phase constraints
r^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
r+r^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}
, respectively. Hermitian conjugate pairing removes these constraints. In the Hermitian model, every nonbasis target ratio
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
can be realized with an arbitrary prescribed real eigenvalue and nonzero signed spectral gap, and the synchronized subspace is a reducing subspace of the full operator.

The asymmetric construction shows that universality can also be obtained by independently tuning the two coupling directions, although without the automatic orthogonal decoupling and spectral guarantees supplied by Hermitian symmetry. Finally, the balanced
\mu_{4}^{0}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm i\}
construction shows that this universality is not only a continuous-parameter phenomenon: every Gaussian-rational amplitude ratio has an exact finite weighted-graph realization, and these exact realizations are dense, modulo global phase, in the projective synchronized pure-state space. Thus, the expressive power of complex QL-bits is governed by the structural relation between reciprocal couplings, conjugation symmetry, and arithmetic row-column regularity, with Hermitian pairing providing the most robust mechanism for universal real-spectrum state synthesis.
