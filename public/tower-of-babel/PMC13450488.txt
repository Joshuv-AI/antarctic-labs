Drug-induced psoriasis secondary to cannabis use
Authors: Sophia Nem, Etan Marks, McKenna Abercrombie
Published: 2026

Introduction

Psoriasis is an inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by scaly, well-demarcated pruritic papules and plaques typically located on the extensor surfaces, trunk, and scalp.1 The pathogenesis of psoriasis is multifactorial and can be influenced by a number of intrinsic and extrinsic factors including genetics, mechanical/psychologic stress, infections, altered skin/gut microbiome, medications, lifestyle, environment, and hormonal/metabolic dysregulation.1 Among these factors, drug-induced psoriasis is well documented with commonly implicated agents including lithium, beta blockers, antimalarials, antibiotics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and interferons.1 However, reports of cannabis-induced cutaneous reactions remain limited. Furthermore, the relationship between cannabis’ various cannabinoid (CBN) compounds and the mechanism underlying these reactions is poorly understood. As recreational and medicinal cannabis use becomes more widespread, recognition of its role as a trigger is of growing clinical significance. Here, we describe a case of drug-induced psoriasis secondary to cannabis use.

Case report

A 37-year-old African American male presented with a 1-week history of a pruritic, scaly rash involving the arms, legs, axillae, trunk, and buttocks. The eruption developed within 24-48 hours of smoking cannabis and had occurred previously, with the initial episode 6 months earlier following chronic recreational use. A recurrence 1 month after the initial episode was noted after use of a different cannabis strain from another dispensary and responded to topical corticosteroids.

A comprehensive history excluded other potential triggers, including medications, mechanical stress, infection, environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, psychological stress, and hormonal or metabolic changes.

Physical examination revealed multiple hyperpigmented papules and plaques with overlying white scale, symmetrically distributed on the bilateral upper extremities, trunk, buttocks, and thighs (Fig 1, Fig 2, Fig 3). The patient was diagnosed with drug induced psoriasis and was subsequently treated with topical Clobetasol 0.05% cream twice daily for 2 weeks, with complete resolution of the rash. Counseling was provided on avoidance of marijuana consumption to prevent recurrence of his psoriasis flares.

Drug-induced psoriasis discussion

The present case highlights a rare but clinically relevant phenomenon: drug-induced psoriasis following cannabis exposure. While cannabinoids, particularly cannabidiol (CBD), have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects in preclinical and early clinical studies, their impact on skin health remains incompletely understood.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Cannabinoid receptors are widely expressed in the skin, and the endocannabinoid system is implicated in the regulation of inflammation, cell proliferation, and immune responses relevant to psoriasis pathogenesis.2, 3, 4

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that cannabinoids may modestly reduce pruritus and have potential benefits in inflammatory skin diseases, including psoriasis.4,5 However, the clinical efficacy for other outcomes, such as erythema, skin dryness, and quality of life, remains unproven, and larger randomized trials are needed.5 Notably, the pharmacologic complexity of cannabis, with over 125 phytocannabinoids and numerous terpenes, raises the possibility of idiosyncratic or strain-specific adverse reactions, including paradoxical induction or exacerbation of psoriasis.7

Drug-induced psoriasis is a well-established entity, with several classes of medications known to trigger or worsen psoriatic lesions in susceptible individuals including but not limited to beta-blockers, antibiotics, antimalarials, non-steroidal anti-inflammatories, and psychotropic drugs.8 Cannabis, though not traditionally included among these agents, is increasingly recognized as a potential contributor, especially given its widespread recreational and medicinal use.9,10 The mechanisms underlying cannabis-induced psoriasis are not fully elucidated, but may involve immune modulation, oxidative stress, and alterations in cytokine profiles, as well as individual genetic susceptibility.3,6,9

Dose-response relationships and onset of cutaneous manifestation in drug-induced psoriasis is poorly characterized. İnan et al demonstrated dose-dependent epidermal thickening, increased serum IgE, and reduced filaggrin in a murine model following topical exposure of CBD and β-caryophyllene, supporting the role of cannabinoids in barrier disruption and dermatitis-like changes.10 However, this study does not address alternative modes of cannabis exposure (inhaled, ingested, sublingual, transdermal) and their role in drug-induced psoriasis. In our case report, inhalation was the primary mode of exposure. The patient’s history of chronic cannabis use, combined with a temporal pattern of recurrent cutaneous findings and biopsy proven psoriasis, strongly suggests repeated inhalational exposure as the etiology of our patient’s psoriasis and rapid onset of the most recent eruption. These findings highlight the need to further investigate non-topical routes of cannabinoid exposure in development of psoriasis.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for drug-induced or drug-triggered psoriasis in patients presenting with new or recurrent eruptions temporally associated with cannabis use. Detailed history-taking regarding cannabis exposure, including strain, route, and frequency, is essential. Notably, non-drug triggers of psoriasis should be carefully evaluated and excluded. In the case of our patient, an extensive history was obtained, and common drug and non-drug triggers such as mechanical stress, infection, environmental/pollution, lifestyle, psychological stress, and hormonal/metabolic alterations were ruled out. Further research is needed to clarify the risk factors, mechanisms, and clinical spectrum of cannabis-induced cutaneous disease, as well as to guide safe use of cannabinoid-based therapies in dermatology.3,5,6,8

In summary, this case underscores the importance of recognizing cannabis as a potential trigger for psoriasis, balancing its therapeutic promise with the possibility of adverse cutaneous effects. Comprehensive patient education and ongoing research are warranted to optimize care for individuals with psoriatic disease in the context of increasing cannabis use.
