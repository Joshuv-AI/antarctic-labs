AI literacy and digital psychological states: social-behavioral implications for FOMO and NOMOPHOBIA over time (2019–2025)
Authors: Mehmet Emre Sezgin, Nazire Burcin Kaya, Ugur Basarmak, Serap Sisman Ugur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAI literacy has become increasingly relevant in digitally mediated environments, yet its relationship with fear of missing out (FOMO) and nomophobia remains underexplored.MethodThe study used an exploratory design to examine associations between AI literacy measured in 2025 and nomophobia and FOMO assessed at two time points (2019 and 2025).ResultsAlthough descriptive trends indicated that individuals with higher AI literacy tended to report lower nomophobia and higher FOMO scores, these interaction effects did not reach statistical significance. Accordingly, the findings should be considered preliminary and warrant further investigation.ConclusionsThe study provides exploratory and cross-sectional evidence on how AI literacy may be associated with AI literacy and digital psychological experiences at the 2025 measurement point, alongside longitudinal trends in nomophobia and FOMO. Accordingly, AI literacy was measured cross-sectionally and the sample is small (N = 30), the findings cannot be interpreted as causal, predictive, or differential effects over time, and should be read as preliminary patterns that require confirmation in larger, multi-wave studies.Keywords: AI literacy, FOMO, Nomophobia, Digital well-being, Learning approaches, Exploratory study

Introduction

Social media platforms, mobile devices, and ubiquitous connectivity have transformed individuals from passive consumers into active content producers, reshaping their roles in digital ecosystems and influencing both cognitive engagement and emotional experience [10]. While digitalization increases access and participation, intensive and unregulated use has been associated with psychological outcomes such as depression, anxiety, and stress [11], and recent findings suggest that technologically mediated interventions may also enhance socio-emotional skills, indicating a complex bidirectional relationship between technology and affect [30]. As artificial intelligence becomes embedded in everyday decision-making, communication, and social participation, AI literacy is increasingly discussed in the literature as a competency that may extend beyond technical skills to include cognitive and affective dimensions relevant to digital environments. Understanding AI literacy in connection with emerging psychological states, particularly fear of missing out (FOMO) and nomophobia, offers a meaningful analytical frame for examining digital adaptation. Nomophobia refers to anxiety experienced when separated from mobile devices [15], while FOMO reflects a compulsive need to remain online due to concerns about missing out on social information [37], both of which have been linked to distraction, lowered academic performance, and heightened emotional distress, especially among younger users and under pandemic-driven conditions [23, 37, 37]. In parallel, learning approaches constitute another determinant shaping digital experiences: deep learning, which is characterized by critical thinking and conceptual understanding, supports selective and conscious engagement with digital content, whereas surface learning prioritizes memorization and performance-oriented strategies [4, 37]. Collectively, these strands of research point to an underexplored intersection in which AI literacy, learning approaches, and digital psychological anxieties may be related to individuals’ adaptive behaviours in technology-mediated environments.

This study examines relationships among AI literacy, nomophobia, and FOMO across two time points, contributing to the field of educational technologies from an affective and behavioural perspective by examining AI literacy as a variable associated with digital adaptation. Rather than considering educational technologies solely as tools for teaching and learning, this perspective highlights their role in emotional and behavioural experiences in human–computer interaction. Recent studies emphasise the transformative impact of generative AI on cognition, metacognition, and epistemic agency [37], affecting not only cognitive strategies but also levels of trust, anxiety, and dependence in digital environments [35]. Critical engagement with AI appears to mitigate uncritical trust or overreliance [20], yet the place of digital behavioural anxieties in this transformation remains underexplored. Digital anxiety types such as FOMO and nomophobia influence participants’ engagement in collaborative problem-solving, social interaction, and digital ethics [24], while variations in AI literacy across learner groups indicate the need for resilience-oriented frameworks in policy and teacher education [5]. In line with evidence that digital competencies can support psychological well-being in online learning, the present study extends this discussion by exploring how AI literacy is associated with these variables and how they relate to learning approaches [22, 26, 31]. These considerations point to the value of empirical research that goes beyond technical skill acquisition to address emotional well-being, digital fatigue, technostress, and social isolation as dimensions of AI-mediated educational experience.

Therefore, this study addresses a gap in the literature by exploring the relationship between digital competence and digital anxiety across two time points. While AI literacy is often considered a protective factor against digital anxiety, the relationship between this competency and excessive cognitive arousal and social vigilance has not been sufficiently researched. Building upon prior research that highlights both the educational implications of digital transformation [37] and the well-being consequences of technology-mediated behaviors [8], the present study contributes to the discussion on digital psychological experiences by examining how AI literacy interacts with two psychosocial indicators nomophobia and FOMO across time.

Whereas earlier studies have focused separately on educational perspectives or national-level ICT factors, this study integrates micro-level behavioral indicators and digital competence across two time points, providing an exploratory perspective on the relationships among digital well-being learning orientations, and technological adaptation. While Medina-Gual and Parejo [33] conceptualise AI literacy through learner profiles emphasising engagement, responsibility, and faculty modelling, the present study extends this line of inquiry by examining the psychological correlates of AI literacy namely nomophobia and FOMO within a time-dependent framework. By examining descriptive patterns in digital well-being within a period of rapid AI integration, this study deepens our comprehension of the temporal evolution of technology-related psychological risks. In the broader literature, nomophobia, FOMO, and AI literacy may serve as exploratory variables that reflect how individuals navigate and adapt to the evolving demands of technology in the digital age. By tracking their temporal dynamics and interactions with learning behaviours, the current study contributes to the growing body of knowledge concerning the interaction between anxiety and digital competence in influencing subjective well-being. This exploratory approach yields preliminary insights that may inform subsequent empirical inquiry into digital adaptation and psychological experiences in AI-mediated environments. At the same time, it offers an interdisciplinary perspective at the intersection of educational technology, psychological well-being, and learning sciences, offering insights that may inform future research and practice participants programs, digital literacy education, and evidence-based policy-making processes aimed at enhancing societal well-being. Similar to the cross-national findings of Dev et al. [8], which emphasize the differentiated impact of mobile and computer technologies on national well-being, our study highlights how AI literacy is associated with psychosocial outcomes of digital device usage particularly nomophobia and FOMO suggesting that future research may examine whether different patterns of AI literacy are associated with different forms of digital engagement and emotional experience. As artificial intelligence systems increasingly mediate participants’ interactions with digital content, the construct of AI literacy has evolved from a set of technical competencies into a multidimensional learning disposition that may be associated with how individuals interpret, navigate, and regulate technology-rich environments. Recent research in technology-enhanced learning highlights that effective engagement with digital systems depends not only on procedural knowledge but also on participants’ cognitive, affective, and metacognitive capacities. Within this context, AI literacy may be associated with how participants engage with algorithmically mediated information environments. Understanding the interaction between AI literacy, digital behavioral anxieties such as fear of missing out (FOMO) and nomophobia, and learning approaches therefore provides an important lens for examining how participants adapt to technology-mediated learning environments. This study contributes to the literature by shifting the focus from technology adoption toward a perspective that considers learning processes, digital psychological experiences, and learner experience in AI-supported educational contexts.

In this context, digital behavioural anxiety and AI literacy may be conceptualized as intersecting psychological categories that are meaningfully relevant to individuals' technology-mediated experiences and subjective well-being and ability to adapt to technology-mediated situations. FOMO (fear of missing out) and nomophobia (the fear of being without a mobile phone) carry potential implications for understanding the heterogeneous nature of individuals' digital experiences and the factors that may shape them and their impact on subjective well-being, as they are closely related to perceived social connectedness and access to information. Recent research demonstrates that digital behavior and psychosocial outcomes are shaped by the intersection of technological access, social context, and individual dispositions. Studies show that increased online exposure heightens vulnerability to negative psychological states such as cyberbullying and emotional strain, particularly in pandemic-driven digital learning patterns [37]. Digital literacy emerges as a central determinant of technology use, social media frequency, and motivational engagement online, indicating that competence predicts both opportunity and risk [37]. Parallel research links digital immersion with behavioral dependency, where technology use becomes an identity-shaping practice rather than a functional habit [36]. Beyond usage patterns, human factors and value orientation explain how digital environments co-construct psychological vulnerability and cybersecurity awareness, particularly among younger users [37]. Building on this trajectory, the present study extends the discussion by examining how AI literacy may be associated with nomophobia and FOMO over time, suggesting that digital proficiency may relate differently to various forms of digital anxiety, including algorithmic vigilance and social monitoring pressure. This frames AI literacy as a potentially relevant correlate of digital experiences, although its psychological associations remain to be examined empirically and are not yet established by the current data.

Literature review

Psychosocial dynamics of nomophobia and FOMO

With the rapid integration of digital technologies into everyday life, new forms of psychological conditions have emerged, most notably nomophobia and FOMO, which are closely linked to digital dependence, social media engagement, and the need for continuous online presence [12]. Nomophobia denotes the anxiety experienced when access to mobile devices is restricted, while FOMO reflects the distress caused by the possibility of missing social updates [16, 37]; these anxieties often reinforce each other, driving individuals to repeatedly check their phones and triggering discomfort when the device is unavailable. Research shows that higher FOMO is associated with social media addiction, lower academic achievement, and reduced psychological well-being [25], with individuals reporting depression, anxiety, and distress, particularly as remote education prolonged digital exposure [21, 27]. These states therefore represent not only behavioral reactions but new forms of digital attachment structured by technologically mediated lifestyles. Recent literature also highlights the psychological risks associated with digitally intensive environments. Problematic engagement with digital platforms has been linked to increased anxiety, reduced academic performance, and difficulties in attention regulation [13, 23]. In digitally mediated environments, social comparison processes and continuous connectivity play a central role in shaping psychological outcomes, particularly in relation to fear of missing out (FOMO) and nomophobia [37, 37].

Recent empirical studies further indicate that user motivations, emotional attachment, and cognitive factors are associated with problematic or excessive use of digital and AI-supported systems. For example, conversational AI usage has been linked to information and entertainment motivations, while individual differences such as mindfulness may shape these relationships [37]. Similarly, depressive mood and self-disclosure have been associated with problematic mobile application use and reduced academic performance [37]. Studies on live-streaming and short-video platforms also suggest that emotional attachment, immersive experiences, and perceived benefits contribute to compulsive use behaviours [37, 37].

Furthermore, digitally mediated experiences are influenced not only by individual characteristics but also by interactional and platform-related mechanisms. Research suggests that human–system interactivity, user gratification, and platform attachment play a role in continued use intentions and sustained engagement [28, 29, 37]. In social media environments, emotional attachment and perceived service quality have been linked to user identification and satisfaction [37, 37]. These findings indicate that digital experiences emerge from a complex interaction of cognitive, affective, and socio-technical factors. In this respect, FOMO and nomophobia can be interpreted as manifestations of ongoing social monitoring, attentional regulation challenges, and difficulties in disengaging from digitally mediated environments.

In parallel, Dev, Kumar, and Saha [8] demonstrate that mobile phone proliferation can paradoxically diminish subjective well-being despite improving connectivity, a dynamic our study extends by analyzing how AI literacy interacts with mobile-induced anxieties to shape digital psychological resilience. This aligns with work highlighting that critical literacy, cognitive control, and learning strategies mitigate technology-related stress and support healthier digital habits [37]. From an educational standpoint, Sarra et al. [37] show that participants’ beliefs about digital technologies reflect post-pandemic “digital abundance,” encompassing both innovation-oriented optimism and resistance grounded in overload and low self-efficacy; such attitudes operate as qualitative indicators of how educational systems negotiate accelerated digitalisation. By focusing on participants’ AI literacy and digital anxieties over time, the present study contributes to this discussion by quantifying longitudinal psychosocial adjustment in AI-mediated environments, situating nomophobia and FOMO not as isolated symptoms but as psychological experiences observed within technology-mediated learning environments.

Time dimension of digital awareness: AI literacy

The integration of artificial intelligence into daily life and educational processes has created a need for competencies that extend beyond technical proficiency to include ethical, social, and psychological awareness. AI literacy, positioned as a cognitive–affective skill set that supports adaptation to evolving technological systems, enhances learning efficiency and fosters lifelong learning [37]. Rather than being framed solely as operational knowledge, it is increasingly conceptualized as an epistemic and critical capacity that enables individuals to navigate AI-mediated environments confidently, ethically, and autonomously [5, 37]. Empirical evidence demonstrates that critical thinking mediates desirable forms of AI use by encouraging reflective, cautious, and collaborative engagement, mitigating risks of overconfidence and uncritical acceptance [20], while participants’ trust in AI-supported systems depends on transparency and an understanding of underlying decision-making mechanisms [35]. At the same time, digital anxieties such as FOMO and nomophobia can emerge from reactive and superficial technology use [1], suggesting that AI literacy may be related to psychological experiences in digital environments by strengthening ethical awareness and cognitive control [19]. However, higher literacy may also increase sensitivity to algorithmic stimulation and real-time social comparison, indicating potentially different or complex associations in which individuals gain control yet become more vulnerable to digital overstimulation and emotional vigilance, a pattern interpretable through Cognitive Load Theory [37] and digital resilience frameworks [28, 29]. European survey research further indicates that variations in AI knowledge predict trust, fear, and willingness to pursue training [9], highlighting the broader relevance of AI literacy in AI-mediated environments. Overall, AI literacy has been associated with technical competence, resistance to misinformation, and indicators of digital well-being, suggesting that it may play an important role for learning, psychological coping, and participation in contemporary educational and social ecosystems [14, 37].

Digital reflections of learning tendencies: the role of deep and surface approaches

The learning approaches individuals adopt not only influence academic outcomes but also shape their interaction with digital environments, their relationship with information, and their digital psychological experiences. Deep and surface learning approaches, frequently discussed in the literature, explain how participants construct knowledge and what mental strategies they mobilize in the process: deep learning relies on interpretation, critical thinking, and holistic understanding, whereas surface learning is grounded in rote memorization, exam-oriented strategies, and fragmented knowledge acquisition [4]. The development of AI-based instructional systems and deep learning techniques has been proposed as a potential contributor to changes in these approaches and to pedagogical reform in digital environments [6]. Growing evidence indicates that learning approaches also intersect with digital well-being, as enhancing digital awareness through deeper learning tendencies may reduce digital anxieties such as FOMO and nomophobia [37], while surface-oriented participants report greater cognitive disruption and heightened anxiety linked to continuous monitoring and performance pressure online [2]. Empirical work similarly suggests that learning strategies may be associated with the relationship between these anxieties and emotional responses to digital spaces [17]. Thus, learning approaches should be understood not only as pedagogical constructs but as cognitive tendencies that may be related to psychological experiences and behavioural tendencies in technology-mediated contexts, offering a critical lens for interpreting digital learning experiences and emotional responses in the age of AI.

Significance of the study

The integration of digital technologies into everyday life has made individuals’ relationships with technology increasingly complex, positioning AI literacy as a competence that encompasses not only technical operation but also the ability to evaluate social, ethical, and psychological consequences. Although research acknowledges emerging digital anxieties, particularly nomophobia (fear of being without a mobile phone) and FOMO (fear of missing out), their connection to AI literacy remains largely unexplored. While ICT diffusion is generally linked to well-being, Dev et al. [8] demonstrate that treating diverse digital tools as a single category masks contrasting affordances and effects: mobile phones and mobile broadband foster fragmented, high-pressure engagement that may harm well-being, whereas household computers and fixed broadband afford structured activities that enhance it. Our study parallels this disaggregated logic by distinguishing nomophobia as dependence driven by constant connectivity and FOMO as socially conditioned vigilance and comparison. Existing findings on participants’ digital beliefs [37], AI-related training needs [9], and critical engagement with AI systems [20, 35] indicate that AI literacy varies across contexts and can influence trust, autonomy, and emotional regulation. The significance of this study lies in its exploratory investigation of the concurrent associations between AI literacy and digital psychological states within a longitudinal cohort. Rather than establishing AI literacy as a definitive predictor of behavioral change, this work provides a descriptive baseline for how technical competence and digital anxieties coexist in an increasingly AI-mediated environment. By identifying these preliminary patterns, the study offers a heuristic framework for future research to examine the potential role of cognitive-affective dispositions in digital adaptation. These insights are provisional and intended to generate hypotheses regarding the evolving nature of human–computer interaction, rather than providing conclusive evidence for policy or design interventions.

The main purpose of this research is therefore to investigate the concurrent associations of AI literacy with nomophobia and FOMO at the final measurement point, alongside exploring the temporal trajectories of these digital anxieties from 2019 to 2025 and their relationships with learning approaches. In light of this framework, the present study empirically examines these variables within AI-mediated educational contexts through the following research questions:

Method

Research design

This study adopts a purposive and context-dependent sampling strategy, not with the intention of achieving population representativeness, but to identify socially meaningful patterns within a group directly experiencing the behavioral consequences of AI-mediated environments. A non-probabilistic structure is therefore appropriate to the research aim, which is exploratory and focused on identifying relational patterns rather than predictive or generalizable. The analytical focus is placed on associations, trajectories, and behavioral tendencies observable across a fixed time period (2019–2025), rather than on estimating population parameters. A longitudinal design involves collecting data from the same individuals repeatedly over time, allowing for the analysis of both the direction and structure of change [34]. This approach is preferred in the social sciences because it is more conducive to making causal inferences [37]. In this respect, the methodological orientation aligns with social science research traditions that prioritize theoretically relevant cases and experiential depth when examining emerging forms of digital adaptation and psychosocial response.

Study group and data collection

The study group consisted of a total of 30 participants (15 male, 15 female). Data on nomophobia and FOMO were collected from the participants in both 2019 and 2025, while data on learning approaches and AI literacy were collected only in 2025. Artificial intelligence literacy was assessed only in 2025 for contextual and construct-validity reasons. At the time of the baseline data collection in 2019, generative AI tools had not yet become widely accessible or educationally salient in everyday life. Following the broad public diffusion of generative AI in late 2022, AI literacy became a more meaningful and measurable construct. Accordingly, the 2025 follow-up wave was considered the most appropriate point to assess participants’ AI-related competence and awareness. While the sample is non-probabilistic, the longitudinal exploratory design aligns with research traditions that prioritize pattern identification over population inference. The small sample size (N = 30) limits statistical power and increases the likelihood of Type II error. All data collection procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring voluntary participation and adherence to ethical principles. The 2025 data collection represents a follow-up measurement with the same participants who participated in the initial 2019 data collection. Informed consent was obtained again prior to the follow-up data collection. Nomophobia was measured using the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q; [37]). While the original validation study reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92, the internal consistency for the current sample was calculated as α = 0.97. FOMO was assessed with the FOMO Scale [18], which had an original alpha of 0.81,in the present study, the reliability was α = 0.89. For the Learning Approaches Scale [3], the original reliability was reported as 0.79, whereas the current sample yielded an alpha of α = 0.85. Finally, the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale [7] originally reported an alpha of 0.85, and the internal consistency for our participants was found to be α = 0.83.

Data analysis

Since the longitudinal data in this study involved 2 × 2 Mixed-Design ANOVA and correlation analysis were used. The SPSS and JMP statistical softwares were employed to test differences between measurement times. Given the non-probabilistic nature of the sample, the findings are interpreted as context-specific patterns of association rather than as population-level generalizations. The analyses aim to illuminate relationships among AI literacy, digital anxiety, and learning approaches within technology-mediated learning environments. AI literacy was measured only at the second time point; therefore, it cannot be interpreted as a predictor of change over time. Emphasis is placed on effect sizes, descriptive trends, and relational patterns relevant to learning processes, in line with methodological approaches commonly adopted in educational technology and learning sciences research. The analyses were not intended to identify causal mechanisms but rather to describe associations and temporal patterns observed within the study cohort.

Regarding the third research question, the correlation analysis revealed that NOMO2019 scores did not significantly correlate with AI literacy, deep learning, or surface learning approach scores. In contrast, NOMO2025 scores showed a significant positive correlation with both deep learning (r = 0.39, p < 0.05) and surface learning approach scores (r = 0.43, p < 0.05). Discussion

Empirical findings

At the 2025 assessment, participants classified as having higher AI literacy descriptively showed lower nomophobia and higher FOMO compared with their 2019 baseline levels. Nevertheless, the absence of a significant Time × Group interaction in the Mixed-Design ANOVA indicates that these patterns likely reflect common temporal trends across groups rather than changes uniquely associated with AI literacy. Furthermore, because AI literacy was assessed only in 2025, it should be interpreted as a concurrent correlate of psychological digital outcomes at the endpoint rather than as a longitudinal predictor of changes occurring between 2019 and 2025.

Descriptively, individuals with higher AI literacy scores exhibited lower device-based dependence and higher FOMO scores at the 2025 endpoint, which aligns with Hou et al.’s [20] broader argument that cognitive awareness may coexist with a heightened visibility of social dynamics in AI environments. Accordingly, this study extends educational technology research by illustrating that technological competence may be associated with different emotional experiences rather than uniformly reducing stress. Thus, AI literacy may be considered in relation to socio-emotional processes rather than a mere technical proficiency.

Exploratory interpretations

One possible theoretical interpretation of these descriptive findings can be considered in relation to Cognitive Load Theory [37], although the present study did not directly assess cognitive load. The observed co-occurrence of AI literacy, nomophobia, and FOMO may be discussed in light of theoretical perspectives concerning cognitive demands in technology-mediated environments. However, the present data do not permit conclusions regarding the mechanisms underlying these associations, and such interpretations should be regarded as exploratory hypotheses for future research. These findings may help interpret the observed pattern of decreasing nomophobia and increasing FOMO over time. The results are consistent with prior research suggesting that digital anxieties are associated with usage patterns and dispositional factors [11, 12]. In our data, individuals with higher AI literacy showed descriptively lower nomophobia levels and higher FOMO levels; however, these differences were not statistically supported. This suggests that AI literacy may be associated with different forms of digital experience rather than uniformly reducing digital stress. In line with Distefano et al. [9], who report that higher AI-specific knowledge relates to readiness for AI training, our findings point to a more nuanced relationship in which digital competence is linked to both adaptive and potentially demanding aspects of digitally mediated environments. Although descriptive patterns suggest differences between groups, the non-significant interaction effects indicate that these differences should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, these interpretations should be considered exploratory given the non-significant interaction effects.

Affective and cognitive components emerge as descriptively relevant: higher AIL scores co-occurred with lower nomophobia and higher FOMO scores at the 2025 measurement point. These descriptive patterns may warrant further investigation regarding the psychological correlates of AI literacy. These observations are consistent with a tentative interpretation in which AI literacy may be considered alongside other individual differences when discussing participation, well-being, and perceived connectivity demands; however, our cross-sectional 2025 data do not provide evidence that AI literacy shapes these outcomes. Integrating Nazaretsky et al. [35], these findings highlight that trust, anxiety, and literacy should be evaluated together, as transparency and decision-making clarity influence users’ emotional regulation. These observations suggest that educational technology design may benefit from attending to affective and behavioral dimensions alongside technical proficiency, though this remains a hypothesis rather than an evidence-based prescription.

Learning approaches further shape these dynamics. The deep learning approach was positively related to AIL and, in 2025, correlated with nomophobia, suggesting that the observed association between deep learning and nomophobia may indicate that these constructs co-occur within this sample, although the nature of this relationship remains unclear. This complements Yan et al. [37], who show that metacognition supports epistemic agency in GenAI environments; here, we add that the co-occurrence of analytical engagement and digital anxiety observed in this sample warrants further investigation. Thus, while deep learning is often associated with technological autonomy in prior literature, it may also be associated with increased interpretive monitoring, cognitive vigilance, and information-tracking tendencies, which are factors associated with FOMO. These observations may provide a basis for future research exploring how AI-supported learning environments address both cognitive and affective dimensions of learning.

The findings converge with Law et al. [24] by showing that collaborative digital engagement is associated with emotional states such as FOMO, which may be related to social interaction patterns and perceived responsibility to remain updated. Similarly, parallels with Dev et al. [8] suggest that digital affordances matter: as mobile connectivity is associated with fragmented engagement and household ICT with more structured use, AI literacy may be concurrently associated with distinct patterns of affordance utilisation. These remain descriptive observations that cannot be interpreted causally within the present design. The concurrent sample-wide decrease in nomophobia and increase in FOMO over time may be discussed in relation to changing patterns of digital engagement and shifting visibility pressures, though these trends occurred independently of statistical interaction with AI literacy levels.

Future research hypotheses

Although descriptive trends indicate that individuals with higher AI literacy may exhibit distinct patterns of digital anxiety, these findings should be interpreted within an exploratory framework given the absence of statistically significant interaction effects. The descriptive coexistence of lower nomophobia and higher FOMO scores among individuals with higher AI literacy warrants validation through adequately powered, multi-wave longitudinal designs. Accordingly, AI literacy is conceptualized as a correlate of digital experience rather than a validated social indicator or a causal driver of digital inequality. Future research should examine whether these patterns are cohort-specific or more broadly observable in other populations. While higher literacy scores co-occur with technical competence, they are also concurrently associated with specific emotional considerations, reinforcing the hypothesis that AI education may benefit from incorporating emotional regulation, boundary-setting, and critical digital awareness, a proposition that warrants testing in future research.

In summary, the descriptive coexistence of decreasing nomophobia and increasing FOMO among high-literacy individuals suggests a nuanced picture of digital experience that merits further empirical investigation. Rather than implying that digital competence resolves or causes psychological tension, these concurrent patterns indicate that different forms of digital anxiety may co-occur alongside higher AI literacy. These observations are consistent with prior scholarship suggesting that digital competence may be associated with distinct patterns of identity performance, immersion, and perceived connectivity demands [36, 37, 37], though the direction and mechanism of these associations remain to be established. These patterns are exploratory and do not establish causal links. Future policy and teacher training discussions may wish to consider emotional resilience, algorithmic awareness, and cognitive boundary-setting alongside technical skill development—hypotheses suggested by the present findings but requiring confirmation in larger studies.

An additional and exploratory reading of these descriptive observations is that AI literacy might be associated with qualitatively different forms of digital engagement; whether it is causally related to engagement patterns cannot be determined from the present design. The descriptive patterns suggest that higher digital competence is concurrently associated with different modes of relating to digital environments, characterised by higher monitoring and interpretive engagement rather than passive dependence. Whether this co-occurrence reflects a directional relationship remains an open question for future longitudinal inquiry. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The present findings are exploratory and describe concurrent associations observed within this cohort. They should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence for broader policy, governance, or societal conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this exploratory study suggests a parallel six-year shift toward reduced nomophobia and elevated FOMO within the cohort. Its principal limitation lies in the single-wave assessment of AI literacy and learning approaches in 2025, which prevents these constructs from being examined as antecedents of change over time. Accordingly, any claim that AI literacy meaningfully shapes trajectories of digital anxiety remains speculative. Future longitudinal research using repeated multi-wave assessments of AI literacy is needed to establish whether increasing competence fosters digital resilience or amplifies sensitivity to algorithm-driven comparison processes. These results align with recent research emphasizing training needs [9], participants’ digital beliefs [37] and the differentiated well-being effects of ICT affordances [8]. They also complement studies highlighting the role of critical thinking in AI use [20] and the importance of transparency in shaping trust [35].

Learning approaches further clarify these patterns. The association between deep learning and higher AI literacy suggests increased metacognitive engagement; however, whether this co-occurs with heightened awareness of missed information and social comparison cues—potentially reflected in higher FOMO—remains an exploratory interpretation that cannot be confirmed from the present data. Thus, the co-occurrence of reflective tendencies and higher FOMO may be a pattern worth examining in future longitudinal work, rather than a demonstrated causal pathway. This tension suggests that emotionally informed AI-supported learning environments that balance cognitive challenge with regulation of attention and affect may merit investigation as a future design hypothesis.

Overall, the findings show that AI literacy may be associated with both reduced and increased forms of digital anxiety. Descriptively, higher AI literacy scores co-occurred with lower nomophobia and higher FOMO scores at the final wave. These outcomes indicate that familiarity with AI co-occurs with complex psychological patterns in digital life, rather than uniformly tracking with lower digital stress. These findings suggest that educational technology research may benefit from considering emotional regulation, digital resilience, and boundary-setting as design dimensions worth investigating, rather than treating them as peripheral concerns.

This study contributes a longitudinal perspective to emerging scholarship by showing how digital anxieties evolve over time in relation to literacy and learning approaches. While not intended for population-level generalization, the findings offer insight into how AI mediates behavioral norms and participation within educational contexts. Future research should expand this work with larger samples, behavioral indicators beyond self-report, and analysis of how AI literacy intersects with broader questions of well-being, teacher workload, and governance of digital learning ecosystems.

In conclusion, the descriptive observation that declining nomophobia and rising FOMO co-occurred among participants with higher AI literacy suggests that effective technology integration may involve not only what individuals know and can do, but also how they manage attention, emotion, and engagement—though this remains a hypothesis to be tested rather than a demonstrated effect. These concurrent patterns are consistent with the view that AI-mediated participation involves cognitive and emotional dimensions, and that designing learning environments mindful of both autonomy and emotional sustainability may be worth exploring. AI literacy, as observed in this study, is not straightforwardly linked to reduced digital anxiety and may instead co-occur with different forms of digital experience, from device-dependence (nomophobia) to algorithmic social vigilance (FOMO). Rather than functioning as a purely protective factor, AI literacy is descriptively associated with complex concurrent patterns of digital anxiety, underlining the hypothesis—to be confirmed in future studies—that digital competence frameworks may benefit from attending to emotional as well as technical dimensions.

This study contributes to the literature by providing one of the few exploratory longitudinal perspectives on the relationship between AI literacy and digital psychological states, highlighting that AI literacy may be concurrently associated with different forms of digital psychological experience. Whether digital competence, in the absence of emotional self-regulation, has any link to broader inequalities (such as differential capacity to disconnect versus remain visible online) is a substantive societal question that goes well beyond what the present small-sample, exploratory study can address; we mention it only as a possible avenue for future inquiry. The findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than evidence of causal or predictive relationships, given the timing of measurements and the small sample size.

Practical implications

This study is among the few longitudinal investigations examining how AI literacy is considered a factor associated with digital adaptation. The descriptive cross-sectional pattern is one in which higher AI-literacy scores co-occurred with comparatively lower nomophobia and comparatively higher FOMO scores; the corresponding Time × Group interactions, however, were not statistically significant. The data therefore do not show that AI literacy reduces nomophobia or increases FOMO, and any "redistribution" of psychological strain is speculative. Accordingly, AI literacy initiatives may benefit from attending not only to technical skills but also to emotional self-regulation, attentional balance, and resilience in AI-mediated environments—a design direction worth exploring in future intervention research. Similarly, technology-enhanced learning designs may consider integrating affective scaffolding such as reflective prompts, pacing mechanisms, and critical engagement activities, with the hypothesis that these may help moderate heightened vigilance and social comparison; this proposition requires empirical testing.

Recommendations

For practitioners and policymakers, the descriptive patterns suggest that AI-focused training initiatives may benefit from including components addressing digital anxiety awareness, boundary management, and psychosocial sustainability, though this recommendation is exploratory given the non-significant interaction effects and small sample size. Deep learning approaches, while positively associated with AI literacy, also appear descriptively linked to increased sensitivity to digital stressors; future instructional design research may therefore explore how cognitive challenge and emotional support can be balanced in AI-mediated learning contexts. At a policy level, the findings are consistent with the hypothesis that digital transformation frameworks might consider psychological sustainability alongside device access and infrastructure, and that emotional regulation may be a relevant dimension of responsible AI literacy—propositions that remain to be confirmed in larger, adequately powered studies. Future interventions may benefit from exploring the integration of affective competencies such as critical social media use, reflective engagement, and algorithmic awareness alongside traditional skill-based outcomes.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution due to several methodological and contextual limitations.

The small sample size (n = 30) constrains generalizability. External conditions between 2019 and 2025 particularly the pandemic and the expansion of remote/hybrid learning could not be systematically controlled within the scope of the study and may have influenced digital behaviors. In addition, reliance on self-report measures limits the precision of behavioral inference. Future research should incorporate larger and more diverse samples, behavioral metrics (e.g., screen-time or platform usage data), and multi-source evidence to more accurately capture the dynamics of AI-mediated psychological experience. A significant limitation of this study is the timing of the measurement for the independent variables. While nomophobia and FOMO were assessed at two time points (2019 and 2025), AI literacy and learning approaches were only measured in 2025. Although AI literacy was measured only at the 2025 follow-up, this timing also reflects the rapid societal emergence of generative AI after late 2022. Nevertheless, because repeated measures were unavailable, the findings should be interpreted as concurrent associations rather than longitudinal effects.

The dichotomization of AI literacy scores, while useful for exploratory comparison, may have reduced variability and statistical sensitivity, and should be interpreted as a heuristic rather than a definitive classification. Although the study includes repeated measures for nomophobia and FOMO, the single-time measurement of AI literacy and learning approaches limits the ability to fully model longitudinal relationships.

In addition, the non-probabilistic sampling strategy limits the extent to which the observed patterns can be generalized beyond the study group, reinforcing the exploratory nature of the findings.

A central interpretive limitation of this study concerns the temporal misalignment of measurement across key constructs. While nomophobia and FOMO were tracked longitudinally from 2019 to 2025, AI literacy and learning approaches were assessed only cross-sectionally at the endpoint. As a result, no inference can be made regarding whether AI literacy exerted a longitudinal influence on changes in these outcomes. Accordingly, the observed relationships should be understood as concurrent associations at the final wave rather than as causal or predictive mechanisms of change. Future longitudinal research using repeated assessments of AI literacy is required to establish whether developing digital competencies function as causal drivers in the progression of technology-mediated anxieties.
