A standardized fecal microbiota transplantation protocol enables consistent, microbiota-driven colitis in IL-10-deficient mice
Authors: Prabhdeep Kaur, Jesús Rivera-Nieves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gut microbiota dysbiosis is a central feature of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), yet experimental systems that enable controlled investigation of microbiota-driven inflammation remain limited. In interleukin-10-deficient (Il10−/−) mice, intestinal inflammation is strictly dependent on the presence of commensal microbiota; however, disease onset and severity are highly variable, reflecting differences in microbial composition across environments. To overcome this limitation, pharmacologic approaches such as piroxicam administration have been widely used to synchronize disease, but these methods introduce epithelial injury and non-microbiota-dependent inflammatory pathways that confound mechanistic interpretation. Here, we describe a standardized fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) protocol that enables controlled microbiota-driven induction of colitis in Il10−/− recipient mice without the use of chemical triggers. In this model, recipient mice aged 8–10 weeks receive fecal microbiota via oral gavage from either colitic Il10−/−; Itgb7−/− double knockout (DKO) donor mice or non-colitic young Il10−/− controls. The DKO donors exhibit impaired mucosal immune regulation and reduced IgA responses, features associated with the emergence of a colitogenic microbial community. Repeated FMT administration over 9 weeks promotes uniform disease induction and reduces variability in disease kinetics across experimental cohorts. Importantly, this approach preserves microbiota-driven disease mechanisms while improving experimental consistency compared with conventional spontaneous Il10−/− models and avoids the confounding effects of pharmacologic synchronization. The protocol is compatible with downstream histological, immunological, and microbiome analyses and provides a practical platform for investigating host-microbiota interactions and microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies in IBD.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD), comprises chronic inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract driven by complex interactions among host genetics, environmental factors, and the intestinal microbiota. A central feature of IBD pathogenesis is dysbiosis of the gut microbial community and the loss of immune tolerance to commensal organisms (Sartor, 2008). Experimental models have been instrumental in defining these relationships, demonstrating that intestinal inflammation can arise from aberrant host responses to otherwise non-pathogenic microbiota.

Seminal work established that interleukin-10-deficient (Il10−/−) mice develop spontaneous colitis that is strictly dependent on the presence of commensal bacteria (Kühn et al., 1993). Germ-free Il10−/− mice remain disease-free, whereas colonization restores susceptibility to intestinal inflammation (Sellon et al., 1998; Berg et al., 2002). These studies provided direct evidence that the microbiota is necessary for disease initiation in a genetically susceptible host. Subsequent studies further demonstrated that disease severity is influenced by microbial composition rather than the presence of a single pathogen, supporting the concept that colitogenicity reflects properties of the microbial community as a whole (Sartor, 2008).

Despite these advances, a major limitation of the Il10−/− model is the variable and asynchronous development of colitis under conventional housing conditions (Sellon et al., 1998; Berg et al., 1996; Kullberg et al., 1998). Disease penetrance, onset, and severity differ substantially across facilities and experimental cohorts, largely reflecting differences in microbial exposure and community structure. Disease severity is also influenced by genetic background, with BALB/c mice typically exhibiting more severe inflammation than C57BL/6 mice (Sartor, 2008; Berg et al., 2002; Ikenoue et al., 2005). This variability has limited the consistency of the model and constrained its utility for mechanistic studies. More broadly, many experimental colitis models introduce confounding mechanisms that complicate interpretation of host-microbiota interactions (Strober et al., 2002). In particular, models that rely on chemical injury or pharmacologic triggers often combine epithelial damage, immune activation, and microbial responses, making it difficult to isolate microbiota-specific contributions to disease pathogenesis.

To overcome the variability of the Il10−/− model, pharmacologic approaches such as administration of the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) piroxicam have been widely adopted to synchronize disease onset. Initial studies demonstrated that NSAID treatment induces rapid and highly penetrant colitis in Il10−/− mice (Berg et al., 2002). However, subsequent work has shown that piroxicam accelerates disease through mechanisms that include epithelial barrier disruption, increased epithelial apoptosis, and enhanced bacterial translocation across the mucosa (Berg et al., 2002; Holgersen et al., 2014). These findings indicate that the piroxicam-accelerated model reflects a combination of microbiota-dependent inflammation and chemically induced barrier injury. While effective in synchronizing disease, this approach introduces non-microbiota-dependent pathways that confound interpretation of microbiota-specific effects. Together, these limitations highlight the need for experimental systems that preserve microbiota-driven disease mechanisms while improving experimental consistency.

The composition of the donor microbiota is a key determinant of disease induction in transfer-based models. In prior work, we characterized interleukin-10 and integrin β7 double-knockout (Il10−/−; Itgb7−/−; hereafter referred to as double knockout [DKO] mice) as a model of severe intestinal inflammation associated with impaired mucosal immune regulation and defective lymphocyte trafficking (Tyler et al., 2022). These mice exhibit markedly reduced IgA responses, reflecting the combined effects of IL-10 deficiency on immunoglobulin class switching and β7 integrin deficiency on intestinal immune cell homing. The resulting disruption of mucosal immune homeostasis is associated with the emergence of a highly colitogenic microbial community. Notably, fecal material derived from these mice induces colitis when transferred into Il10−/− recipients, suggesting that this microbial community possesses enhanced inflammatory potential. However, the precise microbial or functional features that confer this heightened colitogenicity remain incompletely defined.

Here, we describe a standardized fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) protocol that enables controlled transfer of defined microbial communities into Il10−/− recipient mice. By using microbiota derived from DKO donors, this approach provides a microbiota-driven method to induce reproducible colitis while avoiding the confounding effects of pharmacologic synchronization. This model improves consistency of disease induction while preserving microbiota causality and provides a robust platform for investigating host-microbiota interactions and microbiome-targeted interventions in IBD.

Step-by-step protocol: fecal microbiota transplantation for inducing colitis

Mouse breeding and housing

Estimated time: Ongoing.

Il10−/− and Itgb7−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (stock numbers 002251 and 002965, respectively) and bred in-house. Il10−/−; Itgb7−/− double knockout (DKO) mice were generated by intercrossing and breeding to homozygosity for both alleles. Genotypes were confirmed by PCR-based genotyping (Transnetyx Inc., Cordova, TN, USA). Donor mice (Il10−/− and DKO) and recipient mice (Il10−/−) were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a controlled environment (20-23 °C; 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to irradiated chow (Envigo Teklad Global 2920X; Envigo, Indianapolis, IN, USA) and autoclaved water. Bedding and enrichment materials were standardized across cages, and cage changes were performed weekly under a hood. Recipient mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Both male and female mice were used in experiments; sex was balanced across treatment groups when possible. No consistent sex-specific differences in disease severity were observed. Mice were housed at a density of 5 mice per cage. Donor and recipient mice were maintained on separate racks to minimize unintended microbial transfer prior to FMT.

Donor fecal sample collection and storage

Estimated time: 1–2 h.

Fecal pellets were collected from genotypically confirmed donor mice:

Non-colitic donors

Non-colitic donor mice were defined as Il10−/− mice younger than 12 weeks of age with no clinical evidence of intestinal inflammation. These mice exhibited normal stool consistency with well-formed pellets, stable body weight relative to age-matched controls, and normal activity and grooming behavior. Mice with loose stool, diarrhea, weight loss, rectal prolapse, reduced activity, or poor body condition were excluded from the non-colitic donor group.

Colitic donors

Colitic donor mice were Il10−/−; Itgb7−/− double knockout (DKO) mice older than 12 weeks of age with clinical evidence of colitis as reported earlier (Tyler et al., 2022). Donors were identified based on the presence of clinical signs consistent with intestinal inflammation, including loose stool or diarrhea, weight loss, rectal prolapse, or reduced body condition and activity. Only mice with confirmed Il10−/−; Itgb7−/− genotype and clear clinical signs of disease were used as colitic donors, while mice without clinical evidence of colitis were excluded.

To ensure that donor mice had active disease, colitis was confirmed by histologic evaluation in a subset of donor mice from each cohort prior to fecal collection and use as microbiota donors. To reduce donor-to-donor variability, fecal pellets were collected from multiple mice meeting the inclusion criteria within the same donor group and pooled. Specifically, freshly voided fecal pellets were collected from 2–3 cages per donor group and combined into a single preparation using sterile forceps. To preserve obligate anaerobic bacteria, fecal material was processed rapidly on ice with minimized oxygen exposure using sealed tubes. All sample preparation was performed under standard laboratory conditions; consistent disease induction suggests preservation of functionally relevant microbial communities. For FMT administrations, aliquots from pooled donor material were prepared and stored to minimize freeze-thaw cycles and maintain consistency across time points. Samples were snap-frozen on dry ice and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation of donor fecal slurry

Estimated time: 30–45 min.

Fecal pellets were thawed on ice and homogenized in sterile, cold PBS at a ratio of approximately 100 mg feces per 1 mL PBS using vortexing or mechanical disruption. Homogenates were centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS to concentrate the microbial fraction. The resulting suspension was adjusted to a final volume of 200 μL per original fecal pellet and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain a uniform slurry. To standardize inoculum density across preparations, optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured. Preparations with OD600 values between 1.5 and 2.0 were used for FMT administration.

Expected outcomes

FMT from colitic DKO donors results in consistent induction of colitis in Il10−/− recipient mice. In contrast to conventional Il10−/− models, in which disease onset is variable and often asynchronous, this protocol promotes uniform disease onset and reduces variability in disease kinetics across experimental cohorts.

Clinical signs typically emerge within 2–4 weeks following initiation of FMT and include progressive weight loss, loose or unformed stools, reduced activity, and, in some cases, rectal prolapse. Disease progression can be monitored longitudinally using body weight and clinical scoring systems, including disease activity indices. Histologically, colonic tissue demonstrates characteristic features of inflammatory colitis, including epithelial hyperplasia, crypt architectural distortion, mucosal ulceration, and infiltration of inflammatory cells (Figure 1). These features can be quantified using established histopathological scoring systems (Dieleman et al., 1998). At the immunological level, increased mucosal immune activation is typically observed, including changes in immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels in stool and serum, as well as expansion of immune cell populations within intestinal and lymphoid tissues. These phenotypic and immunological changes are quantitatively reflected by differences in body weight, histological inflammation scores, and fecal and serum IgA levels between experimental groups (Figure 2). The magnitude of these responses may vary depending on donor microbiota composition and host factors. Notably, variability in disease severity may still occur due to differences in donor microbiota composition, host factors, and housing conditions; however, this variability is generally reduced relative to conventional Il10−/− models. In our experience, this protocol results in reproducible induction of colitis in nearly 100% of recipient mice, as defined by histological inflammation and associated clinical and immunological parameters.

Discussion

Here, we describe a standardized FMT protocol that enables controlled and consistent induction of colitis in Il10−/− recipient mice through transfer of defined microbial communities. This approach addresses a central limitation of the Il10−/− model, the variable and asynchronous development of disease under conventional housing conditions, while preserving the microbiota-dependent nature of intestinal inflammation (Sellon et al., 1998; Berg et al., 2002).

A major challenge in studying microbiota-driven disease has been the trade-off between biological relevance and experimental consistency. While Il10−/− mice provide a physiologically relevant model in which inflammation is dependent on commensal microbiota, disease onset and severity are highly influenced by environmental microbial exposure and community composition (Sartor, 2008). This variability limits reproducibility and complicates mechanistic studies of host-microbiota interactions. Compared with germ-free or spontaneous Il10−/− models, the present approach enables controlled and reproducible disease induction without requiring specialized housing or pharmacologic triggers, thereby improving experimental accessibility and consistency.

To overcome this limitation, pharmacologic approaches such as piroxicam administration have been widely adopted to synchronize disease onset (Berg et al., 2002; Holgersen et al., 2014). However, these models introduce epithelial injury and non-microbiota-dependent inflammatory pathways, including disruption of barrier integrity and enhanced bacterial translocation, thereby complicating interpretation of microbiota-specific effects (Holgersen et al., 2014). More broadly, many experimental colitis models combine epithelial injury, immune activation, and microbial responses, making it difficult to specifically examine the role of microbiota-driven mechanisms (Table 3) (Strober et al., 2002).

The present protocol provides a microbiota-driven alternative to pharmacologic synchronization. By transferring fecal microbiota derived from colitic donors, this approach enables uniform disease induction while maintaining the central role of microbial communities in driving inflammation. In this protocol, histologic inflammation serves as the primary endpoint to confirm successful disease induction. However, additional analyses, including flow cytometry, immunoglobulin measurements, gene expression analysis, and microbiome sequencing, can be incorporated depending on the experimental objectives. In this context, FMT can be viewed as a biologic approach to standardize microbial exposure and reduce variability in disease kinetics in Il10−/− mice.

An important feature of this model is the use of Il10−/−; Itgb7−/− double knockout mice as donors. These mice exhibit impaired mucosal immune regulation and defective lymphocyte trafficking, resulting in markedly reduced IgA responses and disruption of intestinal immune homeostasis (Tyler et al., 2022). IgA plays a critical role in immune exclusion and containment of commensal bacteria at the mucosal surface, and its deficiency may permit expansion of microbial communities with enhanced inflammatory potential (Sartor, 2008). Consistent with this concept, fecal material from these donors induces colitis when transferred into non-colitic Il10−/− recipients. However, the precise microbial or functional features responsible for this increased colitogenicity remain incompletely defined and represent an important area for future investigation. While direct microbiota profiling was not performed in this study, prior work has demonstrated that microbiota derived from Il10−/−; Itgb7−/− donors exhibits altered composition and colitogenic potential (Tyler et al., 2022). The consistent transfer of disease phenotype across experiments further supports successful microbial engraftment.

FMT has also been translated into clinical practice, where it is now widely used for the treatment of recurrent Clostridium difficile infection, demonstrating high efficacy in restoring microbial balance and resolving disease (van Nood et al., 2013; Cammarota et al., 2017). In contrast, the therapeutic application of FMT in inflammatory bowel disease, particularly ulcerative colitis, has yielded variable results across clinical studies, with differences in donor selection, treatment protocols, and host factors contributing to heterogeneous outcomes (Paramsothy et al., 2017; Costello et al., 2019). These observations highlight both the central role of the microbiota in modulating intestinal inflammation and the challenges associated with controlling microbial composition in clinical settings. In this context, experimental systems that enable defined and controlled transfer of microbial communities are essential for dissecting the mechanisms underlying microbiota-driven disease and therapeutic response. Compared with chemically induced models such as dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) or 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS), and pharmacologically accelerated models such as piroxicam-treated Il10−/− mice, this approach maintains a primary role for microbial community structure in disease initiation while improving experimental control (Strober et al., 2002; Holgersen et al., 2014). Standardization of donor material, pooling strategies, and repeated FMT administration further contribute to improved consistency across experimental cohorts. DSS, TNBS, and piroxicam-treated Il10−/− mouse models introduce epithelial injury or exogenous inflammatory stimuli that may confound interpretation of microbiota-specific effects. In contrast, the FMT-based Il10−/− model described here enables controlled transfer of donor microbial communities, providing a microbiota-driven approach to disease induction while improving experimental consistency relative to conventional spontaneous Il10−/− models.

Several limitations should be considered. As with all microbiota transfer approaches, donor-to-donor variability in microbial composition can influence disease severity (Sartor, 2008). Housing conditions and inter-cage microbial transfer may also impact experimental outcomes, necessitating strict control of animal housing. In addition, while the Il10−/− model captures key aspects of microbiota-driven inflammation, it does not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of human IBD (Strober et al., 2002). Finally, the microbial taxa or functional pathways responsible for disease transfer remain to be defined. Despite these limitations, this protocol provides a practical and consistent framework for studying microbiota-driven intestinal inflammation.

In summary, this FMT-based Il10−/− colitis model provides a controlled and microbiota-driven platform for inducing colitis in genetically susceptible hosts. By enabling standardized transfer of colitogenic microbial communities, this approach facilitates mechanistic studies of host-microbiota interactions and supports the development of microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies in IBD.
