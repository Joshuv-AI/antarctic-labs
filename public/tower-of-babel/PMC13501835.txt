Functional characterization of TONSOKU in Cyathula officinalis reveals its role in DNA damage and root development
Authors: Ji Chen, Yao Liu, Xin Luo, Mengzhu He, Jin Xiong, Wenjun Jiang, Jin Huang, Xiaowan Jin, Rui Li, Mengliang Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCyathula officinalis Kuan. (C. officinalis), a perennial medicinal herb endemic to Sichuan, holds significant pharmacological value in traditional medicine, yet its key functional genes governing physiological traits remain unexplored.ResultsCoTSK encodes a nuclear protein containing conserved tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains that phylogenetically clusters with Amaranthaceae homologs. Introduction

Cyathula officinalis Kuan (Chuan Niuxi), a perennial herb endemic to Sichuan Province, China, is renowned in traditional medicine for its dried roots, which have effects on anti-inflammation [1], antioxidation [2], and immune-enhancing [3], etc. While its pharmacological properties have been well documented, the molecular mechanisms governing key developmental traits remain unexplored, particularly the regulatory networks controlling root morphogenesis, a critical determinant of medicinal biomass accumulation. In C.officinalis, suboptimal root development directly reduces the yield and quality of the medical root material, yet no systematic study has addressed the genetic basis of root morphogenesis in this species. Identifying key regulators of root growth is therefore a prerequisite for molecular breeding aimed at improving root biomass.

In Arabidopsis thaliana, the TONSOKU (TSK) gene encodes a nuclear protein containing tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains, which plays a central role in maintaining meristem homeostasis and genomic stability [4]. Genetic studies demonstrate TSK mutants exhibit characteristic developmental defects including disorganized quiescent center cells and shortened primary roots (50% length decrease vs. wild-type (WT)) [5]. Despite this mechanistic understanding in model plants, no TSK homologs have been functionally characterized in medicinal species, creating a critical knowledge gap in how these developmental regulators evolve to shape specialized plant architectures. Studies demonstrate that TSK mutants exhibit aberrant quiescent center (QC) cell organization in root apical meristems, accompanied by disordered cell division planes and disrupted epidermal cell file patterning [5, 6]. Additionally, these mutants display hypersensitivity to DNA-damaging agents, characterized by transcriptional desilencing and elevated genomic instability [5], suggesting TSK’s role in chromatin remodeling during damage repair. However, no TSK homologs have been functionally characterized in medicinal plants, limiting the translation of mechanistic insights to crop improvement.

This study aims to bridge this gap by elucidating the biological functions of CoTSK in C. officinalis. We address three objectives. First, molecular characterization of CoTSK structural features through multi-species alignment and phylogenetic reconstruction. Second, functional validation via phenotypic rescue of Arabidopsis AtTSK mutants. Third, mechanistic exploration of DNA damage response specificity using prokaryotic expression systems.

Materials and methods

A schematic overview of the experimental workflow is provided in Fig. S1. Briefly, the study proceeded through four phases: (1) gene cloning and bioinformatic characterization of CoTSK; (2) subcellular localization and heterologous expression in E. coli for DNA damage assays; (3) tissue‑specific expression profiling in C. officinalis; (4) complementation of Arabidopsis AtTSK mutants to assess functional conservation. The full-length sequence assembly of the CoTSK gene in Cyathula officinalis

For total RNA extraction, EASYpin Plus Plant RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd, Beijing) was used to extract total RNA from the leaves of C. officinalis. Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to check the quality of RNA. 1000 µg total RNA was used as the template for cDNA synthesis by using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai) following the instruction of the manufacturer. Primers were designed using Primer 3.0 and DNAMAN. The primers used in this study are shown in (Table. S1). Subsequently, expand the sequence, the PCR program consisted of initial denaturation at 94℃ for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94℃ for 20 s, 54–60℃ for 30 s, annealing at 68℃ for 0.5–4 min, and extension at 68℃ for 7 min, followed by melt curve analysis. Three biological replicates were used for each sample.

The purified PCR products were sequenced via commercial services (Sangon Biotech) to obtain core sequence fragments. Subsequently, rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) was performed using the core sequence as a template. The RACE products were then gel-extracted, sequenced, and assembled to obtain the full-length CoTSK cDNA (The sequence has been deposited in the GenBank database under accession number PZ011813). Overlapping regions of all sequenced PCR fragments were aligned and merged with bioinformatics tools such as DNASTAR Lasergene, completing the full-length genomic sequence of the C. officinalis TSK gene.

Bioinformatics analysis of the full-length CoTSK gene from C.officinalis

The cloned full-length CoTSK gene was subjected to various biological analyses. Firstly, the CoTSK genomic data was analyzed using GeneWise (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/psa/genewise/). Subsequently, the ProtParam tool (http://web.expasy.org/cgi-bin/protparam) was employed to predict the molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, protein stability, and hydrophobicity of the protein. The secondary structure of the protein was predicted online using SOPMA (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/). The conserved domains of the protein were predicted using BLAST (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and the three-dimensional modeling of the domains was performed using SWISS-MODEL (http://www.swissmodel.expasy.org/Interactive). Next, the open reading frame (ORF) of the spliced full-length gene sequence was identified using ORF Finder (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gorf/gorf.html), and the sequence was translated into an amino acid sequence using ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/translate/). The determined gene sequence was aligned with homologous amino acid sequences using BLAST and ESPript 3 software. Finally, the homologous amino acid sequences of plant TSK were aligned using ClustalX and MEGA 11.0 software, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the alignment results using the Neighbor-Joining method.

Subcellular location analysis of CoTSK protein

To investigate the subcellular localization, a transient expression vector was constructed for transient transformation and observation in tobacco leaves. The CDS sequence of the CoTSK gene from Sichuan Achyranthes bidentata was amplified using the primers Pcb2300 (F) -BamHI-TSK and Pcb2300 (R) -SpeI-TSK. The PCR amplification conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 5 min; 94 °C for 20 s; 58 °C for 3.5 min, 30 cycles; 68 °C for 7 min; and 4 °C hold. The constructed pCAMBIA2300-TSK-GFP expression vector was transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 competent cells using the freeze-thaw method. After transformation, colony PCR was performed to detect the transformants and verify the successful introduction of the vector. Following confirmation, the transformed Agrobacterium strains were stored at -80 °C for future use.

The Agrobacterium was cultured until the OD600 reached 1.5-2.0, then transferred to 200 mL of liquid medium containing 10 mM 2- (N-Morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES) and 2 µM Acetosyringone (AS). After 24 h of culture, the cells were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES). The Agrobacterium was centrifuged again at 4000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 8 mL of infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, 10 µM AS). The bacteria were diluted to an OD600 of 0.5 and incubated at room temperature for 3–6 h. Small holes were made on the underside of tobacco leaves using a 1 mL syringe, and the Agrobacterium suspension was injected into the leaf tissue through these holes. The tobacco plants were cultured in greenhouse conditions for 2–3 days for observation of CoTSK-GFP protein subcellular localization.

The transiently expressed tobacco seedling leaves were cut into 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm pieces, immersed in 10 µg/mL DAPI solution for 10 min of staining, and then washed several times with PBS solution. The leaves were then placed flat on a clean slide with the underside facing up, covered with a cover slip, and observed under a laser scanning confocal microscope with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 520–550 nm. The distribution of GFP green fluorescence in the tissue and the nuclear staining were observed to determine the subcellular localization of the target gene expression product.

Analysis of CoTSK protein expression effects on bacterial growth

The pGEX-TSK recombinant vector was constructed as follows: PCR amplification was performed using primers pGEX6t (F) -BamHI-TSK and pGEX6t (R) -XhoI-TSK under the following thermal cycling conditions: 94 °C for 5 min (initial denaturation); 30 cycles of 94 °C for 20 s (denaturation), 58 °C for 3.5 min (annealing/extension); followed by a final extension at 68 °C for 7 min. The amplified CoTSK fragment was subsequently cloned into the BamHI/XhoI-digested pGEX-6t-1 vector using the One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Recombinant plasmids were extracted from E. coli cells using the Plasmid MiniPrep Purification Kit II (GenMark Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai), with restriction enzyme verification performed using BamHI and XhoI (0.5 µL each) in a 10 µL reaction system containing 5 µL pGEX-TSK and 1 µL CutSmart buffer. The digestion products were incubated at 37 °C for 2–4 h and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

For protein expression analysis, BL21 cells harboring the recombinant plasmid were retrieved from − 80 °C storage and cultured until OD600 reached 0.5. Protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM IPTG (2.3 µL added to 5 mL culture) at 30 °C for 5 h with shaking. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (1 mL culture) and subjected to trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation: 15 µL TCA was added to the pellet followed by 2–3 freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen/37°C water bath. Proteins were extracted using 500 µL ice-cold acetone solution containing 10% TCA and 0.07% β-mercaptoethanol, incubated overnight at -20 °C, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed with 1 mL acetone containing 0.07% β-mercaptoethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in 50 µL denaturing buffer (8 M urea, 0.07% β-mercaptoethanol). Samples were mixed with 15 µL SDS-PAGE loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and analyzed by 12% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 for 5 h and destained until clear protein bands were visible, followed by imaging using a gel documentation system.

To assess the effects of TSK protein expression on bacterial growth, E. coli BL21 strains expressing either pGEX-vector or pGEX-CoTSK were cultured to mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.5). Serial dilutions (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁷) were prepared in sterile YEP medium, and 2 µL aliquots of each dilution were spotted onto agar plates containing varying IPTG concentrations (0, 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, and 0.1 mM). Time courses were monitored after 24 h incubation at 30 °C.

Effect of CoTSK protein on DNA damage stress response

E. coli BL21 strains harboring pGEX-vector or pGEX-CoTSK were cultured to mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.5) and induced with 0.1 mM IPTG for 4 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3, 500 ×g for 2 min, washed three times with sterile YEP medium, and resuspended in 1 mL fresh sterile YEP. Cell density was normalized by spectrophotometric measurement prior to preparing 10-fold serial dilutions (10− 1 to 10− 7) in sterile YEP medium.

To assess DNA damage tolerance, 2 µL aliquots of each dilution were spotted onto: (1) YEP agar plates containing methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) at concentrations of 0, 0.01%, and 0.02% (v/v). (2) YEP agar plates containing hydroxyurea (HU) at concentrations of 0, 5, and 10 mM. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Colony morphology and time courses were analyzed by comparative measurement of colony diameter between vector control and CoTSK-expressing strains. This experimental design enabled systematic evaluation of TSK protein’s physiological function in enhancing bacterial survival under DNA-damaging agent stress.

Tissue-specific expression profiling of CoTSK gene

The β-actin reference gene was identified through annotation of the Cyathula officinalis genome database, and specific primers were designed following quantitative PCR criteria (amplicon length 80–200 bp, Tm = 60 ± 2 °C, GC content 40–60%). Field-collected C. officinalis specimens were acclimatized for 7 days in controlled growth chambers (Conviron®) under 12/12 h light/dark photoperiod, 20–25 °C thermocycle, 70% relative humidity, and 70 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ photosynthetic photon flux density. Root, stem, leaf, and floral tissues were synchronously harvested from equivalent developmental stages, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for RNA extraction.

Total RNA was isolated from each tissue using the method described above, followed by cDNA synthesis with the ReverTra Ace-α First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Toyobo Life Science). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using gene-specific primers for CoTSK (forward: 5’→3’; reverse: 5’→3’) and β-actin (forward: 5’→3’; reverse: 5’→3’) in a 10 µL reaction system containing 5 µL 2× SsoFast™ EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad), 2 µL cDNA template, 0.2 µL each primer (10 µM stock), and 2.6 µL nuclease-free water.

Thermal cycling parameters included: Initial denaturation: 95 °C for 30 s. Amplification: 35 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 55 °C for 5 s (combined annealing/extension). Melt curve analysis: 95 °C for 10 s followed by 0.5 °C increments (65–95 °C range) with 5 s plateaus. Three biological replicates were analyzed using the CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Specificity was confirmed through single-peak melt curves and linear amplification plots (R² > 0.98). Relative expression levels were calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method normalized to β-actin, with tissue-specific expression patterns visualized using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Genotypic and phenotypic characterization of transgenic plants

Homozygous Arabidopsis mutant screening

Genomic DNA was extracted from Arabidopsis thaliana leaves using a modified alkaline lysis protocol. Briefly, leaf tissue (50–100 mg) was homogenized in liquid nitrogen and lysed in 750 µL extraction buffer (0.2 M Tris-HCl pH 9.0, 0.4 M LiCl, 25 mM EDTA, 1% SDS). After centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 6 min at 4 °C, 500 µL supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol and incubated at -20 °C for 30 min. The DNA pellet was washed with 700 µL 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 100 µL TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0).

Homozygous SALK_057330 T-DNA insertion mutants were identified using a tri-primer PCR strategy. Primers: SALK_057330-LP (P1, forward), SALK-LBb1.3 (P2, reverse), SALK_057330-RP (P3, reverse). PCR conditions: 94 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C/30 s, 58 °C/45 s, 72 °C/1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons were resolved on 1% agarose gels: WT: P1/P3 product (1.2 kb). Heterozygote: P1/P2 (0.8 kb) + P1/P3 (1.2 kb). Homozygote: P1/P2 (0.8 kb).

Validation of CoTSK-overexpressing transgenic lines

Using pCAMBIA2300-eGFP as the target vector, the recombinant vector pCAMBIA2300-CoTSK was constructed. Agrobacterium tumefaciens was cultured to prepare the infection solution. The culture was expanded to an OD600 of 1.5-2.0, with a volume of 200 mL. The culture was divided into four 45-mL portions, centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 15 min to remove the supernatant completely. The infection solution was used to wash twice, and the bacteria were resuspended in the infection solution, diluted to an OD600 of 0.8-1.0. Arabidopsis thaliana with heterozygous mutations was immersed in the infection solution twice, with the entire flower buds submerged under the surface of the infection solution for 10–20 s. After dark treatment for 24 h, the plants were cultured normally until Arabidopsis seeds were harvested. Seeds obtained from the transformed plants were sown on 1/2 MS medium containing Kan resistance (50 mg/L) to select positive seedlings. Positive transgenic plants that grew normally were transferred to soil when they had four true leaves. Subsequently, genomic DNA was extracted from the transgenic plants for PCR genotyping. The phenotypes of different genotypes of transgenic Arabidopsis were analyzed to investigate the effects of CoTSK on the root, stem, and rosette leaf morphogenesis of different genotypes of Arabidopsis plants, the phenotypic recovery of homozygous mutants of Arabidopsis by CoTSK, and the functional homology of CoTSK and AtTSK in regulating the morphogenesis of Arabidopsis.

Cultivation conditions of transgenic lines

The identified transgenic plants of various types were used for seed collection and subsequent phenotypic observation experiments. Seeds of Arabidopsis thaliana wild type and SALK_057330 (CoTSK) were sown on 1/2 MS medium and vertically placed in the greenhouse for cultivation. After seed germination, the root growth and development and other phenotypes of each line were observed.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using IBM SPSS 27.0 statistical software, and the multiple comparisons of the means were conducted by one-way ANOVA test (p = 0.05), and plotted using Microsoft Excel 2017 software, and using the Graph Pad Prism software (https://www.graphpad.com/). Three biological replicates were required for each treatment or measurement.

Results

CoTSK exhibits conserved structural motifs and evolutionary relationships with amaranthaceae species

To characterize the sequence and structural features of CoTSK, bioinformatics analysis was conducted on CoTSK. Secondary structure prediction using SOPMA (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/) revealed that CoTSK comprises four structural elements: α-helix (55.23%), β-strand (11.54%), β-turn (7.96%), and random coil (25.27%) (Fig. S2). Comparative analysis with Cyathula capitata CcTSK showed nearly identical secondary structure composition, indicating structural conservation between the two species.

Homology analysis of TSK amino acid sequences from C. officinalis, Cyathula capitata (Wall.) Moq. (C. capitata), Arabidopsis thaliana (BAD04041.1), and Nicotiana tabacum (XP_016454753.1) via NCBI BLASTp demonstrated 69.41% global sequence identity (Fig. S3). Notably, the TPR (tetratricopeptide repeat) and LRR (leucine-rich repeat) domains were highly conserved across all species, with CoTSK and CcTSK each containing five TPR and ten LRR repeats. Domain annotation using NCBI CDD confirmed these motifs as signature features of the TSK protein family.

To resolve the evolutionary relationships of CoTSK, a phylogenetic tree was reconstructed from 24 plant TSK homologs (16 dicots, 8 monocots) using the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA 11.0 with JTT substitution model (bootstrap = 1000 replicates) (Fig. 1). CoTSK and CcTSK formed a sister clade with 100% bootstrap support, reflecting their close evolutionary relationship. Divergence between Amaranthaceae and other dicots/monocots aligned with known taxonomic boundaries, supporting the monophyletic origin of TSK proteins in angiosperms.

Phylogenetic relationship of CoTSK. The Neighbor-Joining tree was constructed using 24 plant TSK homologs (16 dicots, 8 monocots). CoTSK from C. officinalis and CcTSK from C. capitata form a sister clade with 100% bootstrap support, indicating their close evolutionary relationship within Amaranthaceae. The tree resolves monophyletic origin of TSK proteins in angiosperms, with clear separation between dicots and monocots

CoTSK protein is predominantly localized to the nucleus

The subcellular localization of a protein is closely associated with its biological functions. Subcellular localization analysis revealed nuclear targeting of CoTSK, consistent with its predicted role in DNA damage repair. Transient expression of the CoTSK-eGFP fusion protein in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium GV3101 infiltration showed strong GFP fluorescence exclusively overlapping with DAPI-stained nuclei (Fig. 2). In contrast, control leaves expressing free eGFP displayed diffuse cytoplasmic and nuclear fluorescence (Fig. 2), confirming the nuclear specificity of CoTSK localization. This nuclear enrichment aligns with the subcellular distribution of Arabidopsis AtTSK, which mediates chromatin remodeling during DNA repair [7]. The conserved nuclear targeting suggests that CoTSK may function analogously to AtTSK in maintaining genome stability, though its direct interaction with DNA repair machinery requires further validation.

CoTSK expression exhibits dose-dependent growth inhibition and confers specific tolerance to alkylation damage in E. coli

To investigate the functional impact of CoTSK on bacterial growth, recombinant E. coli BL21 (DE3) strains expressing pET28a-CoTSK were constructed.

To evaluate growth effects, E. coli strains expressing pGEX-CoTSK or empty vector were serially diluted (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁷) and spotted onto agar plates containing 0–1.0 mM IPTG. After 12 h incubation at 37 °C, CoTSK-expressing strains exhibited dose-dependent growth inhibition, with colony formation reduced at 1.0 mM IPTG (Fig. 3A). In contrast, vector controls showed no growth defects across IPTG concentrations (Fig. 3A). These results demonstrate that CoTSK expression specifically impedes bacterial proliferation, likely through interference with essential cellular processes.

Serial dilutions of E. coli expressing pGEX-CoTSK or empty vector were spotted on IPTG-containing plates. At 1.0 mM IPTG, colony formation of CoTSK-expressing strains is strongly reduced compared to vector control, indicating that CoTSK overexpression interferes with essential cellular processes. B CoTSK confers specific tolerance to alkylation damage. Under 0.02% MMS, CoTSK-expressing strains show less repressed growth relative to control, whereas no difference is observed under HU treatment. This suggests CoTSK specifically mitigates alkylation-induced DNA damage but not replication fork stalling

To assess the functional conservation of CoTSK in DNA damage response, E. coli BL21 (DE3) strains expressing pGEX-CoTSK or empty vector were exposed to methyl methanesulfonate (MMS, 0-0.02%) or hydroxyurea (HU, 0–10 mM). CoTSK-expressing strains exhibited less repressed growth under 0.02% MMS compared to controls (Fig. 3B). In contrast, no significant difference in colony formation was observed under HU treatment (Fig. 3B), indicating that CoTSK specifically mitigates alkylation-induced DNA damage but not replication fork stalling.

This functional specificity parallels the known role of Arabidopsis AtTSK in double-strand break (DSB) repair but not replication stress responses [8, 9], suggesting evolutionary conservation of TSK-mediated DNA repair pathways in plants. The lack of HU tolerance further implies that CoTSK does not interact with replication-associated factors, aligning with its predicted nuclear localization and chromatin-modulating activity.

CoTSK exhibits stem-predominant expression diverging from leaf-biased CcTSK in Cyathula officinalis

To resolve the spatiotemporal expression pattern of CoTSK, we quantified its transcript levels in roots, stems, leaves, and flowers of C. officinalis at two developmental stages: seedling (30 days post-germination) and flowering (120 days post-germination). qRT-PCR analysis revealed stage-dependent expression patterns (Fig. 4).

Tissue-specific expression patterns of CoTSK and CcTSK. qRT-PCR analysis at seedling stage (A) and flowering stage (B). CoTSK transcripts are highest in stems at seedling stage, whereas its paralog CcTSK from C. capitata is leaf-biased. At flowering, CoTSK expression peaks in floral tissues (8.2-fold vs. stems), followed by roots (3.5-fold) and leaves (1.8-fold). The stem-predominant expression of CoTSK contrasts with the leaf-biased pattern of CcTSK, indicating species-specific regulatory divergence

At the seedling stage, CoTSK transcripts were higher in stems compared to leaves, suggesting a role in stem vascular development. In contrast, CcTSK (the homolog from C. capitata) exhibited higher expression in leaves than stems, indicating species-specific regulatory divergence (Fig. 4). During the flowering stage, CoTSK expression peaked in floral tissues (8.2-fold vs. stems), followed by roots (3.5-fold vs. stems) and leaves (1.8-fold vs. stems). This shift aligns with Arabidopsis AtTSK, which is highly expressed in nascent floral organs to regulate cell division [10–12]. The conserved expression enrichment in reproductive tissues implies a shared role in meristem maintenance across plant species.

CoTSK partially rescues root elongation defects in AtTSK mutants, supporting functional conservation

Previous studies have demonstrated that the loss of the AtTSK gene in Arabidopsis thaliana results in characteristic phenotypes including flattened stems, shortened roots, and excessive tillerin [5, 13, 14]. To validate the functional conservation of CoTSK, we introduced the CoTSK gene into Arabidopsis TSK mutants (SALK_057330), which exhibit severe root shortening and disorganized meristems. Homozygous TSK mutants were confirmed by PCR genotyping, revealing a 208-bp T-DNA insertion in the second intron of AtTSK (Fig. S5).

Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh Arabidopsis thaliana leaves using standard protocols. Gene-specific primers were designed, and PCR amplification was performed to genotype the plants. The resulting PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis and analyzed to distinguish homozygous mutants. Based on the electrophoresis profiles, homozygous mutant lines were selected and propagated under controlled conditions to generate sufficient seeds for downstream experiments.

Following genotypic confirmation, the homozygous TSK mutant line was identified. A comparative phenotypic analysis between WT plants and the homozygous TSK mutant was subsequently conducted to assess morphological differences (Fig. S6).

WT, homozygous TSK mutant, and CoTSK-complemented transgenic plants were cultured on 1/2 MS agar plates to evaluate growth phenotypes (Fig. 5A). Phenotypic analysis on 1/2 MS agar plates demonstrated that CoTSK-OE plants restored root length to 30–42% of WT levels, while TSK mutants retained stunted roots (Fig. 5B-D). Notably, stem flattening and rosette proliferation phenotypes in TSK mutants were not rescued, indicating that CoTSK specifically complements root developmental defects.

B-D Whole-plant phenotypes at 30, 25, and 50 days after germination. CoTSK-OE restores root length to 30–42% of WT levels, but stem flattening and rosette proliferation (arrows) are not rescued. This indicates that CoTSK specifically complements root developmental defects while other aspects of TSK function have diverged

This partial functional conservation aligns with the shared nuclear localization and TPR/LRR domains between CoTSK and AtTSK, suggesting that CoTSK may act through similar chromatin-modulating pathways to regulate root meristem activity. However, the incomplete rescue implies divergent regulatory mechanisms in stem and floral development, warranting further investigation.

Discussion

Structural conservation and potential functional divergence of CoTSK

The CoTSK and CcTSK genes encode 1284-amino acid proteins sharing 98.5% sequence identity, yet seven amino acid substitutions distinguish them, including two in TPR and two in LRR domains—key regions for protein interactions (Fig. S3). While both proteins lack predicted nuclear localization signals (NLS), their nuclear localization in N. benthamiana (Fig. 2) suggests alternative mechanisms for nuclear import, possibly through piggybacking with interacting partners [15, 16]. Structural modeling revealed that substitutions in TPR/LRR domains alter surface charge distribution (Fig. S3), which may affect binding to chromatin remodelers like AtBRU1 [13].

Heterologous expression of CoTSK in Arabidopsis TSK mutants partially restored root elongation (Fig. 5A), demonstrating conserved roles in root development. However, the failure to rescue stem flattening implies lineage-specific functional divergence, potentially linked to differential expression of interacting factors (e.g., Cyathula lacks AtBRU1 homologs). Similarly, CoTSK conferred MMS tolerance in E. coli (Fig. 3B) but not HU resistance, mirroring AtTSK’s specificity for double-strand break repair [13, 17, 18]. These findings highlight conserved core functions (DNA repair, root development) while underscoring species-specific regulatory adaptations.

Evolutionary implications of tissue-specific expression divergence

The preferential expression of CoTSK in the stem contrasts sharply with the leaf-biased expression of CcTSK (Fig. 4), suggesting that its function may have undergone tissue-specific differentiation. In C. officinalis, the high expression of CoTSK in the stem may indirectly enhance root biomass accumulation by promoting vascular tissue development, which is directly related to its medicinal value based on root use. Phylogenetic analysis shows that CoTSK/CcTSK clusters with Amaranthaceae plants (Fig. 1), indicating that the differentiation of this expression pattern occurred after speciation and may have been accompanied by functional adaptive reshaping. Notably, AtTSK in Arabidopsis is mainly expressed in floral organs [19, 20], while Cyathula species exhibit co-expression in stems and flowers. This tissue preference difference implies that TSK may have undergone neofunctionalization in reproductive strategies, that is, from regulating floral development to co-regulating vegetative and reproductive processes, supporting the optimization of its medicinal traits under specific ecological or cultivation conditions.

Toward molecular breeding: challenges and opportunities

Although the functions of CoTSK in promoting root elongation and participating in DNA repair have hinted at its potential in stress-resistant breeding, its actual role in enhancing crop stress resistance has not been confirmed. The current key bottlenecks include: (1) the lack of tissue-specific expression evidence at the protein level, which limits the precise localization of its functional sites in stress responses; (2) the failure to verify its direct contribution to stress tolerance in the target crop background through CRISPR knockout; (3) the absence of agronomic trait evaluation under field stress conditions, which prevents the transformation of its molecular functions into breeding indicators. Future research should prioritize the establishment of a transformation system for Cyathula itself to systematically dissect the functional coupling of CoTSK on root architecture, genome stability, and ultimately stress-resistant yield in its native genetic background, thereby achieving a functional closed loop from gene to trait to breeding application.

Conclusions

This first functional study of a medicinal-plant TSK homolog shows that C. officinalis CoTSK, despite 98.5% identity with leaf-biased CcTSK and a 100%-bootstrap placement alongside it within Amaranthaceae, is instead stem-enriched. Nuclear-localized CoTSK (Fig. 3) partially rescues Arabidopsis TSK root elongation and, like AtTSK, confers MMS but not HU tolerance in E. coli (Fig. 5), implicating the conserved TPR domain in alkylation-specific double-strand-break repair. Stem-predominant expression pinpoints CoTSK as a target to boost root biomass and medicinal metabolite yield; CRISPR knockout or overexpression in C. officinalis can now dissect its metabolic and stress-resilience roles.
