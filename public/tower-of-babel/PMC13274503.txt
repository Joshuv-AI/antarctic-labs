Vicarious trauma and burnout: the role of self-compassion
Authors: Grace Woolway, Susannah Jenner, Dorothy King, Hjördis Lorenz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Psychologists are frequently exposed to distressing trauma stories which may increase the likelihood of experiencing vicarious trauma. Vicarious trauma can cause changes in core beliefs and cognitive schemas, ultimately changing an individual’s view of self, others and the world.Objective: The current study aims to determine the extent of vicarious trauma and burnout psychologists in the United Kingdom experience, whether they are associated with each other, and to understand their relationship with self-compassion.Method: Practitioner psychologists were invited to participate in an anonymous online survey if they had been exposed to clients’ trauma stories through their work. The survey explored psychologists’ personal and workplace characteristics and assessed vicarious trauma, burnout and self-compassion.Results: Two hundred and fifty-five participants completed the survey and were included in data analysis. Psychologists experienced moderate levels of vicarious trauma. High vicarious trauma scores were associated with higher levels of personal, work and client-related burnout. Higher levels of vicarious trauma and burnout were also associated with lower self-compassion scores. Self-compassion did not significantly moderate the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout. However, a sensitivity analysis suggests self-kindness may weaken the association between vicarious trauma and burnout, but the effect size is small.Conclusions: The findings from the present study highlight the importance of screening psychologists for vicarious trauma and burnout. As higher self-compassion was significantly associated with lower vicarious trauma and burnout, compassion focused approaches may be beneficial to reduce negative consequences associated with exposure to distressing trauma content disclosed by clients.

Introduction

Psychologists and other health professionals who engage in therapeutic relationships are often exposed to narratives of trauma. This is especially likely for professionals working in specialist trauma services, but it also extends to professionals working in more generic mental health services, due to high prevalence rates of psychopathology in individuals who have experienced a traumatic event (Lewis et al., 2019). This suggests that most psychologists are regularly exposed to other people’s trauma whether that is in individual therapy, clinical supervision, triaging or referrals. Psychologists are also likely to hear detailed trauma stories (e.g. in trauma memory processing work) if specific trauma treatment is required.

Research has established that mental health professionals who work with a higher number of trauma survivors on a regular basis are emotionally affected by their therapeutic work (Hensel et al., 2015), with 70% of therapists working in the United Kingdom (UK) considered at increased risk of experiencing negative consequences (Sodeke-Gregson et al., 2013). Several terms have been used to describe the negative consequences of exposure to indirect trauma including vicarious trauma and secondary traumatic stress (STS) (Guitar & Molinaro, 2017). Vicarious trauma is distinct from STS in that it is a pervasive cognitive change following repeated exposure to traumatic events experienced by another person (McCann & Pearlman, 1990). While vicarious trauma is characterised by changes to an individual’s cognitive schemas (thinking shortcuts) and core beliefs (e.g. the world is a dangerous place), individuals with STS do not experience such cognitive changes but rather experience intrusive imagery, avoidance, and physiological arousal akin to Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) (Sutton et al., 2022). In addition to the cognitive changes, vicarious trauma can lead to changes in identity and experiencing feelings of distrust, helplessness, overwhelm or numbness (Evces, 2015). In the current study, we focus on vicarious trauma due to a dearth of evidence understanding this phenomenon in psychologists (Kim et al., 2022) despite its relevance to the work in this population (Possick et al., 2015). Previous research has shown that psychologists are at an increased risk of STS but it is unclear whether this extends to the cognitive changes associated with vicarious trauma (Kounenou et al., 2025; Makadia et al., 2017; Pearlman & Mac Ian, 1995; Sui & Padmanabhanunni, 2016). Therefore, research investigating vicarious trauma in psychologists is important to consider due to the potential negative impact on their own psychological distress and wellbeing and also their professional functioning.

In addition to being exposed to trauma stories, psychologists are also at a high risk of burnout, demonstrating much higher rates of emotional burnout than other mental health professionals (Dreison et al., 2018; McCormack et al., 2018; Mills & Huebner, 1998). A recent review found that 42% of British National Health Service (NHS) staff experienced emotional exhaustion, with clinical psychologists being the least represented in the literature (Sideri et al., 2025). Burnout is a psychological phenomenon characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and a reduced sense of professional efficacy (Maslach et al., 1997). It is thought to develop as a result of high job demands (such as high pressure and emotional content) and insufficient job resources (such as organisational support) (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). Research has suggested that vicarious trauma could also contribute to the development of burnout (Butler et al., 2017; Fansher et al., 2020; Hazen et al., 2020; Laster et al., 2026). This could be because cognitions associated with vicarious trauma such as ‘the world is unsafe’ and ‘most people do bad things’ exacerbate feelings of emotional exhaustion and becoming detached, indifferent, or self-critical about work. In addition to burnout affecting the individual, it also affects organisations by impacting delivery of care, staff turnover, and absenteeism (von Hippel et al., 2019).

Both vicarious trauma and burnout have the capability to affect an individual’s ability to function at work and provide care to others, however a potential mitigating factor for both vicarious trauma and burnout is compassion. Compassion has been defined as ‘a sensitivity to suffering of self and others with a commitment to try and alleviate and prevent it’ (Gilbert, 2013). Compassion is a motivation that supports one to face and alleviate difficulties in life and can protect individuals from mental health difficulties (Gilbert, 2013; Gilbert & Irons, 2005). Research has shown that self-compassion can benefit therapists’ mental health and prevent negative consequences from work-related stressors (Crego et al., 2022). Preliminary research suggests that self-compassion training decreases symptoms of STS and burnout in healthcare professionals but no research has yet found whether this extends to vicarious trauma (Eriksson et al., 2018; Rushforth et al., 2023).

A recent study of 266 Greek mental health professionals has started to explore the relationship between vicarious trauma, burnout and self-compassion. The findings suggest a negative correlation between self-compassion and (i) vicarious trauma and (ii) burnout, however, the authors state that self-compassion did not moderate the relationship (but no data was presented) (Kounenou et al., 2025). It remains unclear whether these findings extend to psychologists working in the UK.

The current study aims to improve our understanding of the relationship between experiences of vicarious trauma, burnout and self-compassion in UK-based practitioner psychologists. Methods

Ethical approval was gained from the University of Oxford Medical Sciences Interdivisional Research Ethics Committee (MS IDREC:584165) in June 2025. This study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/dkgyb/overview).

Participants

From July to October 2025, participants were invited to take part online via email, social media and psychology platforms such as psychology training programmes and charities psychologists might work in. Practitioner psychologists who reported indirect exposure to traumatic experiences through their work were invited to participate. Practitioner psychologists include clinical, counselling and educational psychologists as defined by the Health & Care Professions Council (HCPC) (2023). Psychologists working in the NHS, private practice, and third sector (such as charities) alike were invited to participate. Trainee psychologists in their second and third year were also invited to take part, based on the assumption that trainees would have been exposed to similar trauma narratives as qualified practitioners by their second year of training. Psychologists were required to work in the UK, where it is standard to have obtained a doctoral level degree to practice as a practitioner psychologist. Participants were also required to have access to the internet, be English speaking, and have capacity to consent. Participants were initially directed to an information sheet which outlined the study aims and what taking part would involve. If participants consented to participate, they were required to complete two screening questions to check eligibility (confirming they currently work as practitioner psychologist in the UK and exposure to trauma at work) and then proceeded to complete the online anonymous survey, which took approximately 15 min to complete. The survey included personal and professional information and three questionnaires. Upon completion of the survey participants were provided with signposting information for further support if required.

A priori power analyses using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009) revealed a sample size of at least 128 participants (for 2 groups) or 159 participants (for 3 groups) was required to achieve sufficient statistical power with an alpha level of .05, power .80 and effect size of .25 (medium effect size) for the ANCOVA analysis. Furthermore, a sample size of 55 participants was required to achieve 80% power for the moderation analysis, assuming a medium effect.

A total of 341 participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. However, 11 individuals were deemed ineligible at screening, and 75 participants did not complete the full survey leaving a final sample size of 255.

Materials

To gain an overview of professional and personal characteristics of the participants we gathered information on age, gender, qualification status, working hours, sector and area of work, exposure and length of time exposed to trauma stories, current and past burnout, hours clinical contact, therapeutic approach, hours individual, group and peer supervision, trauma training, perceived support by management, supervisor and peers, and number of personal adverse childhood experiences. The following validated measures were used to assess vicarious trauma, burnout and self-compassion: The Vicarious Trauma Scale (VTS) (Vrklevski & Franklin, 2008) is an eight-item scale which assesses distress associated with working with clients who experience trauma on a Likert scale from 1 (strongly agree) to 7 (strongly disagree), with a good reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α = 0.88). Scores are categorised into low (8–28), moderate (29–42), and high (43–56) vicarious trauma (Aparicio et al., 2013). Scores are categorised into low (<50), moderate (50–74), and high (75–99) burnout. The scale is widely used across several countries and has shown good internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.85–0.87) and validity across the subscales.The Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) (Neff, 2003) is a 26-item measure of self-compassion that has been widely used and validated. Procedure

Data was collected via Qualtrics, an online data collection platform. Following data exportation screening questions were reviewed to determine eligibility. Eleven individuals seemed to answer a screening question incorrectly; participants responded ‘no’ to ‘Do you work as a practitioner psychologist in the UK? Or are you in training to become one (2nd and 3rd year only)?’ but subsequently identified that they were in training (n = 8) or provided the date they qualified as psychologists (n = 3). These participants were excluded from the main analysis and only included in an ad-hoc sensitivity analysis. Data was checked for consistency across repeated questions, patterns of responding and response time to highlight potential fake participants.

Statistical analysis

A linear multiple regression was used to test whether vicarious trauma score was associated with burnout and self-compassion scores. Following this, a fixed effects ANCOVA was used to compare burnout and self-compassion scores across low, moderate and high vicarious trauma groups, to account for four demographic covariates identified by previous research including age, training, perceived support and supervision (Sodeke-Gregson et al., 2013). However, due to the majority of participants scores falling within the moderate range and a small number of participants falling into the low VTS group (n = 19) data was analysed using a median split, removing 1 standard error from the middle of the distribution (n = 18) to create two distinct groups. This method has been suggested to allow identification of individuals who are at a higher risk (Aparicio et al., 2013). A moderation analysis using a linear regression model with an interaction term was also performed to assess whether self-compassion moderates the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout. All analyses were conducted in R Studio (Posit team, 2025).

The ANCOVA results indicated a significant main effect of VTS group on CBI scores (F(1,231) = 69.14, p < .001) when controlling for gender and perceived support from management and supervisor. Estimated marginal means demonstrated that the high VTS group had significantly higher adjusted CBI scores (M = 46.1, SE = 4.07, p < .0001) than those in the low VTS group (M = 35.2, SE = 3.93). The results also showed a significant main effect of VTS group on SCS scores (F(1,231) = 21.24, p < .001) when controlling for gender and perceived support from management and supervisor. Estimated marginal means demonstrated that the low VTS group had significantly higher adjusted SCS scores (M = 3.59, SE = 0.24, p = .0001) than those in the high VTS group (M = 3.23, SE = 0.24).

Total SCS scores did not significantly moderate the relationship between VTS and CBI (β = −0.85, SE = 0.67, p = 0.21), nor did the SCS subdomains (see Supplementary Table 1). Despite finding no evidence of a moderating effect, post-hoc analysis using a single hierarchical regression suggests that adding self-compassion significantly improved the model fit (adjusted R2 = 0.12, p < .001) (See Supplementary Table 2). Trend level data suggests that as self-compassion scores increase the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout weakens. When including the 11 participants who were likely screened inappropriately in a sensitivity analysis (see Methods for details), the self-compassion subdomain of self-kindness becomes significant, but the effect size remains small (β = −1.44, SE = 0.67, p = .03) (see Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

The current findings suggest that practitioner psychologists based in the UK experience moderate levels of vicarious trauma. High vicarious trauma scores were associated with higher burnout and lower self-compassion scores. High burnout scores were also associated with lower self-compassion scores. Although the results of the moderation analysis were non-significant, the results suggest a potential small moderation effect of self-compassion weakening the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout.

Contrary to our hypothesis, we did not find psychologists to experience high levels of vicarious trauma. However, we did find higher levels of vicarious traumatisation as measured by the VTS in UK-based psychologists (mean = 38.14) than Greek mental health professionals (mean = 29.23) (Kounenou et al., 2025). The mean score on the VTS is consistent with the original publication in legal attorneys (mean = 41.50) who were deemed to be at an increased risk for vicarious trauma (Vrklevski & Franklin, 2008), suggesting that the high, moderate and low thresholds may not be truly representative of those who experience VT. There was a much lower prevalence of burnout in psychologists in the current study than reported in psychologists in previous literature (Hannigan et al., 2004; McCormack et al., 2018; Mills & Huebner, 1998) which might be explained by heterogenous burnout measures and studies measuring different dimensions of burnout. For example, in a 2018 systematic review only two studies out of 29 assessed work and client related burnout (McCormack et al., 2018). Self-compassion scores were consistent with previous literature (Eriksson et al., 2018).

The current findings replicate recent research in Greek mental health professionals, similarly finding that higher vicarious trauma was associated with higher burnout and lower self-compassion scores (Kounenou et al., 2025). We were also able to demonstrate that the findings extend to doctoral-level psychologists who undergo standardised training. Despite both outcomes being significantly associated with self-compassion, self-compassion did not significantly moderate the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout, also mirroring Kounenou et al. (2025) findings. This could be because both studies found relatively low levels of burnout, because the sample size was not large enough or because the moderation effect was so small that we were underpowered to detect it. A post-hoc power analysis indicated insufficient power (1-β = .61; f2 = .02, α = .05, n = 255) to detect a small moderation effect (a priori power analysis assumed a medium moderation effect). When the additional 11 participants were included in the analyses power marginally improved (1-β = .63) and self-kindness significantly moderated the association between vicarious trauma and burnout, but the effect was small. Therefore, we cannot conclude there is a true absence of effect but rather that more research with a larger sample is required to test small moderation effects.

Furthermore, it is also worth considering that positive changes resulting from exposure to client’s trauma stories may be playing a part in the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout. Post-traumatic growth, which can lead to recognising personal strength and experiencing a newfound appreciation for life and vicarious resilience, could be experienced by clinicians reducing the impact of trauma stories on their wellbeing (Iqbal, 2015; Tedeschi & Calhoun, 2004).

Whilst we acknowledge our findings cannot provide causal evidence, the results demonstrate that lower scores on all the self-compassion subdomains (except common humanity) were associated with higher vicarious trauma and burnout scores, highlighting the potential of promoting self-compassion to reduce the negative consequences of hearing trauma stories whilst working as a psychologist. Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT) could help clinicians to develop a compassionate mindset and help individuals to engage their soothing system, which subsequently may lead to feelings of safeness and calmness and allow psychologists to give and receive care from others (Gilbert, 2020). Common humanity, which emphasises the notion that suffering, imperfection and failure is a shared human experience, was not associated with vicarious trauma. We could hypothesise that due to the nature of the work psychologists do and the exposure to their clients suffering, they are able to see that difficulties are a part of life everyone goes through. Therefore, developing ‘self’ concepts (e.g. self-kindness) rather than normalising suffering may be more beneficial for psychologists. While promoting self-compassion is one option to explore, an alternative could be to prevent and reduce vicarious trauma and burnout in psychologists, which may in turn increase self-compassion, which we also encourage researchers to investigate. A compassionate approach could also be incorporated systemically in teams by engaging in compassionate leadership, which acknowledges that stress (resulting from high workloads, increasing job demands and difficult working conditions) can be addressed by attending to, understanding and empathising with suffering and enabling systems to work towards shared solutions. Future research should consider the effectiveness of such compassion focused approaches within psychology teams in the UK.

In line with previous literature, lower perceived support from both management and an individual’s supervisor as well as being female were associated with both vicarious trauma and burnout (Kounenou et al., 2025; Sodeke-Gregson et al., 2013). This, once again, emphasises the importance of organisational support as a resource to protect against poor staff wellbeing (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). Gender differences should be interpreted with caution due to the large proportion of female participants in the current sample.

It is worthwhile considering the conceptualisation of vicarious trauma and burnout and the convergence of the two concepts. Vicarious trauma can lead to changes in personal identity and world view, feelings of distrust and helplessness, and emotional overwhelm or numbness (Evces, 2015). Despite the cognitive changes being the defining feature of experiencing vicarious trauma, individuals who are feeling burnt out may also experience the emotional changes associated with vicarious trauma, which may explain why one is so highly associated with the other (Newell & MacNeil, 2010). Furthermore, due to the short ‘screening style’ of the VTS it is hard to tease apart the experiences associated with vicarious trauma and burnout and explore the nuanced cognitive changes associated with vicarious trauma. Future research should consider developing a more comprehensive scale to better understand the specific cognitive changes associated with vicarious traumatisation.

Limitations

It is important to note limitations of the current study. Our inclusion criteria did not include other psychological practitioners, such as cognitive behavioural therapists, who also work with traumatised clients. This criterion was used to ensure consistency across training and practice and to create a homogenous sample. Our sample mostly consisted of young female psychologists working in the NHS, whilst this is somewhat reflective of the psychology profession, we are unable to generalise our results to older psychologists, male psychologists and psychologists that work in other settings. Due to the low prevalence of males in our sample, we are unable to determine if there are gender differences in vicarious trauma, burnout or self-compassion. Future research should further explore gender differences and their associations with different coping strategies. Due to anonymous data collection, we were unable to obtain participants’ geographical information and determine how representative our sample is of UK practitioner psychologists. Participants may have been subject to self-selection and ascertainment biases, recruiting those who were less burnt out (which therefore could lead to an underestimation of burnout among psychologists) or psychologists who are interested in self-compassion and thus more likely to participate in this research. We were also unable to recruit participants to the low VTS group (when initially trying to sample by low, moderate and high VTS) suggesting that those who experienced slightly more vicarious trauma may have been more interested or motivated to participate. Additionally, as a result of the study documentation outlining the study aims participants may have been subjected to social desirability bias. The cross-sectional study design prevented us from drawing conclusions about causality and the direction of effect. It remains unclear whether high vicarious trauma leads to increased burnout or whether psychologists experiencing burnout are more susceptible to vicarious trauma. A more detailed longitudinal design may be more appropriate to draw out the directionality and the nuances of the relationship between vicarious trauma, burnout and self-compassion. There may also have been other confounding variables that were not assessed. This could have included personal stress (e.g. those with caring responsibilities/illnesses), working multiple jobs (potentially across different sectors), and other factors associated with the wider context of working in the NHS (such as commissioning and barriers to accessing treatment for themselves).

The brief nature of the VTS meant we were unable to explore whether specific cognitions associated with vicarious trauma (such as changes in world view) were related to burnout and self-compassion. Furthermore, the Cronbach’s α of the VTS in the current sample was in the ‘acceptable’ range (Taber, 2018), highlighting lower reliability in UK psychologists than the population it was validated in. Initially, we planned to use three distinct vicarious trauma risk groups (low/moderate/high) for data analysis. However, due to difficulty recruiting participants with low vicarious trauma scores the data was analysed using a median split. Therefore, the results may not replicate in another dataset as a median is sample dependant. However, the primary analysis using vicarious trauma score as a continuous variable suggests this would not be the case. A median split was chosen for clinical utility, to identify clinicians who are at an elevated risk of vicarious trauma and burnout as suggested by previous research (Aparicio et al., 2013). This method could be employed within psychology teams to identify clinicians who could benefit from compassion focused approaches and increasing support from their supervisor and/or management.

Conclusion

Psychologists based in the UK experience moderate levels of vicarious trauma. Higher scores on the vicarious trauma scale were associated with higher burnout and lower self-compassion scores. High levels of burnout were also associated with lower levels of self-compassion. Trend level data suggest that self-compassion may moderate the relationship between vicarious trauma and burnout to a small extent, but further research in larger, well-powered samples is required to strengthen our understanding. These findings highlight the importance of screening psychologists to identify those who are likely to experience vicarious trauma and burnout (potentially simultaneously). This is especially important given the likelihood of exposure to client’s trauma stories and its potential to affect psychologists’ ability to provide care to others. Future research should consider developing a more comprehensive measure of vicarious trauma to better understand the cognitive changes associated with the phenomenon and explore the use of compassion focused approaches in supervision, training and leadership.
