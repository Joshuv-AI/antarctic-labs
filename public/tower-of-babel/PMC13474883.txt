Effects of metaverse-based education on motivation and academic achievement in undergraduate physiotherapy students: a randomized three-arm study
Authors: Mesut Ergan, Tahir Keskin, Ferdi Başkurt, Zeliha Başkurt, Faruk Durna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional synchronous online education may be less engaging than face-to-face instruction in physiotherapy education, particularly for practice-oriented content. Metaverse-based environments may offer a more interactive alternative, but comparative evidence in undergraduate physiotherapy students remains limited. This study compared metaverse-based, synchronous online, and face-to-face education for the theoretical component of palpation-related content.MethodsThis randomized three-arm controlled trial used a pre-test/post-test design. Ninety undergraduate physiotherapy students were randomized to metaverse-based, synchronous online, or face-to-face education; 83 completed the study (metaverse n = 28, online n = 26, face-to-face n = 29). All groups received the same theoretical palpation content, differing only in delivery mode. Motivation was assessed using the Instructional Materials Motivation Survey (IMMS), including its Attention-Relevance and Confidence-Satisfaction subscales, and academic achievement was assessed using a 15-item multiple-choice quiz after the intervention.ResultsMotivation increased in all groups after instruction. Significant group-by-time interactions were found for IMMS total (p = 0.001) and Attention-Relevance (p < 0.001), with greater improvement in the metaverse group than in the online group for IMMS total, and greater improvement than both the online and face-to-face groups for Attention-Relevance. Quiz scores also differed across groups (p = 0.048), with significantly higher post-test quiz scores in the metaverse group than in the online group. No significant group-by-time interaction was observed for Confidence-Satisfaction (p > 0.05).ConclusionsMetaverse-based education may provide motivational advantages and higher post-test quiz performance over conventional synchronous online education in undergraduate physiotherapy students. Its clearest benefit was observed in the Attention-Relevance dimension of motivation toward the instructional materials. These findings suggest that more interactive virtual learning environments may enrich online physiotherapy education when theoretical content is delivered remotely.

Background

Physiotherapy aims to promote functional recovery and improve quality of life in individuals with health-related limitations. Accordingly, entry-level physiotherapy education is expected to equip students not only with clinical knowledge and practical skills, but also with the professional behaviours required for a safe transition into clinical practice [1]. Within this context, palpation is a fundamental component of physiotherapy practice, particularly in musculoskeletal assessment and treatment planning [2–6]. Developing competence in palpation depends not only on repeated practice but also on a sound theoretical understanding that supports accurate clinical application [7–11].

In recent years, synchronous online education has become increasingly common in health professions education and offers important advantages in terms of flexibility, continuity, and accessibility. However, concerns remain regarding how effectively it supports student engagement when teaching content that is closely linked to later practical application. In physiotherapy education, studies conducted during the COVID-19 period reported reduced engagement, concerns about educational quality, and perceived disadvantages of online learning compared with face-to-face education, particularly for practice-oriented components [12, 13].

The metaverse has been described as a shared three-dimensional virtual environment in which users, represented by avatars, interact with one another and with digital objects in real time [14, 15]. The literature indicates that the concept has been inconsistently operationalised across studies, with many studies equating it solely with virtual reality [16]. In contrast, the metaverse has been conceptualised as a networked, persistent virtual environment that supports synchronous interaction and co-presence [16]. In healthcare education, metaverse-based learning has been associated with interactive, immersive, and collaborative learning experiences and may support experiential learning and theory-to-practice connections [14, 17]. However, the educational value of metaverse-based learning is unlikely to depend on the technology alone. Reviews and conceptual papers emphasize that outcomes may vary according to instructional design, implementation quality, learner readiness, and the way educational outcomes are assessed [16, 18–20]. Published metaverse-based educational research also suggests that potential benefits may be selective rather than universal [19]. Accordingly, metaverse-based education should be evaluated as a pedagogical approach whose added value needs to be demonstrated empirically rather than assumed on the basis of technological novelty alone.

Although technology-enhanced approaches such as virtual reality, simulation, and virtual patient tools have been explored in physiotherapy education, evidence specifically examining metaverse-based education in physiotherapy students remains limited. In particular, direct comparisons between metaverse-based education, conventional synchronous online education, and face-to-face instruction are still scarce in physiotherapy education when teaching palpation-related theoretical content. This gap is important because, when such content is delivered in virtual formats, educators need evidence on whether richer and more interactive learning environments offer advantages over standard online delivery. Therefore, the aim of this randomized three-arm study was to compare metaverse-based, conventional synchronous online, and face-to-face education for the theoretical component of palpation-related content in undergraduate physiotherapy students. We hypothesized that all groups would show improvement, but that the metaverse-based format would yield greater gains than conventional synchronous online education.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a three-arm randomized controlled trial with a pre-test/post-test repeated-measures structure to investigate the effects of metaverse-based, synchronous online, and face-to-face educational modalities on learning motivation and academic achievement. The educational content focused on palpation-related theoretical content, including anatomical landmarks and conceptual understanding that support palpation practice, without hands-on practical training. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement (Fig. 1).

Setting and study period

The study was conducted at the Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation, Faculty of Health Sciences, Süleyman Demirel University, Türkiye, during the fall semester of the 2025–2026 academic year. All educational sessions were delivered by the same instructor in order to standardize content delivery across groups.

Participants

Eligibility criteria

Participants were second-year undergraduate physiotherapy students enrolled in the relevant course during the study period. Students were eligible if they were enrolled in the course, agreed to participate, and were able to access the educational platforms using a personal computer or mobile device. Students who declined participation were not included. Students with incomplete post-intervention outcome data were excluded from the final analysis.

Recruitment and informed consent

All students enrolled in the course were informed about the study by a researcher other than the course instructor. Students were clearly informed about the study aims, procedures, confidentiality of data, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without academic consequences. They were also informed that non-participation would not affect their course attendance, academic evaluation, or course standing. To further minimise perceived pressure, students who did not participate were provided with face-to-face instruction separately, and routine course achievement was evaluated through the standard course assessment process using separate questions and methods. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before baseline assessment.

Sample size

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.4). Assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and statistical power of 95%, the minimum required sample size was estimated as at least 16 participants per group, based on effect size assumptions informed by prior metaverse-based education research [21]. Because recruitment was limited to a single course cohort, all eligible students were invited to participate.

All 97 students enrolled in the course were invited; 90 provided consent and were randomized equally (30/30/30). After post-test non-completion (e.g., absence from the final assessment or incomplete questionnaires), 83 students completed the study (metaverse n = 28, online n = 26, face-to-face n = 29).

Randomization and allocation

After completion of the baseline assessments, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups: metaverse-based education, synchronous online education, or face-to-face education. A computer-generated randomization sequence was created using Random.org. Group allocation was implemented by an independent researcher who was not involved in outcome assessment or data analysis, and assignment was carried out only after completion of the baseline assessments. Because of the nature of the educational interventions, blinding of participants and the instructor was not feasible.

Educational interventions

All groups received identical learning objectives, theoretical content, instructional materials, and planned instructional sequence. The same visual resources, videos demonstrating how relevant structures are palpated, and discussion points were used across groups, and all sessions were delivered by the same instructor with the same planned duration. Students in all groups were given opportunities to ask questions and participate in discussion during the sessions. The interventions differed primarily in the mode of delivery and in the interactive and spatial features of the learning environment, including the use of embedded three-dimensional instructional supports within the metaverse environment.

Metaverse-based education

Participants in the metaverse group attended the sessions in a virtual classroom created on the Spatial.io platform [22], accessed via personal computers or mobile devices. All groups received the same learning objectives, the same core theoretical content, and the same instructional duration; however, the metaverse group experienced this content within a shared virtual environment. This environment enabled avatar-based participation, peer and instructor co-presence, and spatial navigation within the virtual classroom. Standardized instructional materials were embedded directly into the environment and included presentation panels, visual resources, videos demonstrating how relevant structures are palpated, and three-dimensional instructional elements used to support the explanation of palpation-related theoretical content. Students were able to move through the virtual space, examine the embedded materials, and then join the designated classroom area, where the instructor delivered the lesson, answered questions, clarified unclear points, and reinforced key concepts through interactive discussion. Real-time technical support was available when needed for access, navigation, or connectivity problems.

Synchronous online education

Participants in the synchronous online group attended live sessions through Microsoft Teams, accessed via personal computers or mobile devices. Sessions were delivered by the same instructor and followed the same schedule and duration as the other groups. Students were able to ask questions and participate in discussion in real time during the videoconferencing session. In contrast to the metaverse group, this format did not include avatar-based participation, spatial navigation, or a shared virtual classroom environment with embedded three-dimensional instructional elements.

Face-to-face education

Participants in the face-to-face group attended the sessions in a conventional classroom setting. Sessions were delivered by the same instructor and followed the same instructional plan, schedule, and duration as the metaverse-based and synchronous online groups. Students were able to ask questions and participate in discussion during the classroom sessions.

Metaverse environment development and access

The metaverse-based educational environment was implemented using Spatial.io [22], a multi-user digital platform that supports synchronous interaction across different devices. The environment was designed as a shared virtual classroom in which students could participate through avatars, navigate the space, and engage with the shared instructional content in a spatially organised format. In this group, the learning environment enabled avatar-based participation, navigation, and access to embedded three-dimensional instructional supports aligned with the theoretical learning objectives of the course (Fig. 2). Although the core educational content was kept consistent across groups, the metaverse group experienced this content in a more interactive and spatially organised learning environment.

Personal information form

A brief Personal Information Form developed by the researchers was used to describe participant characteristics. The form included items related to age, sex, study-supporting technology use, satisfaction with distance education during the COVID-19 period, time spent on social media, grade point average, and prior experience with metaverse or virtual reality technologies.

Instructional Materials Motivation Survey (IMMS)

Learning motivation was assessed using the IMMS, which is grounded in Keller’s ARCS model (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction) [23]. The Turkish adaptation and psychometric properties of the IMMS were reported by Kutu and Sozbilir [24]. In that study, the IMMS comprised 24 items with a two-factor structure (Attention-Relevance and Confidence-Satisfaction) and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.83 for the total scale). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), producing total scores ranging from 24 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater motivation toward the instructional materials. In the present study, both the IMMS total score and its two subscale scores were analysed.

Academic achievement quiz

Academic achievement was evaluated using a 15-item multiple-choice quiz developed by the research team to reflect the learning objectives of the course. The same quiz was administered to all participants after completion of the educational program (post-test only). Scores were calculated according to the number of correct responses and converted to a score out of 100, with higher scores indicating better academic achievement. The quiz items were generated by the researchers through a consensus-based process and were designed to represent the theoretical content covered during the educational sessions in accordance with the predefined instructional objectives. The quiz included multiple-choice items addressing anatomical landmarks, vertebral level identification, recognition of structures relevant to palpation, and interpretation of basic positional and directional principles used in palpation, including visually based identification tasks. Because the quiz was intended to assess post-intervention performance on the targeted instructional content, a baseline quiz was not administered.

Assessment procedure and timeline

At baseline, participants completed the Personal Information Form and the IMMS before randomization. After completion of the educational sessions, the IMMS was re-administered as the post-test measure of learning motivation. Academic achievement was assessed at post-test using the 15-item multiple-choice quiz.

Ethical approval and trial registration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Süleyman Demirel University Health Sciences Ethics Committee, Türkiye (Meeting No: 96; Decision No: 7; Date: 12 May 2025). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07166705).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as number and percentage. Distributional assumptions were evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q-Q plots, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. For descriptive comparisons of baseline continuous variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when parametric assumptions were met, and Welch’s ANOVA was used when homogeneity of variance was violated. Changes in IMMS total and subscale scores were analysed using a 3 × 2 mixed ANOVA, with group (metaverse-based, synchronous online, face-to-face) as the between-subject factor and time (pre-test, post-test) as the within-subject factor. Effect sizes for the ANOVA results were reported as partial eta squared (ηp²). Partial eta squared (ηp²) values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively, based on commonly used benchmarks in the literature [25, 26]. When a significant group-by-time interaction was identified, change scores (Δ = post-test - pre-test) were calculated and compared between groups using pairwise Welch’s t-tests with Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes for pairwise comparisons were reported as Hedges’ g. Within-group pre–post changes in IMMS total and subscale scores were examined using paired-samples t-tests. Because academic achievement was assessed only at post-test and the score distribution did not meet parametric assumptions, between-group differences in quiz scores were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Effect size was reported as epsilon squared (ε²). When the omnibus test was significant, Bonferroni-adjusted Mann-Whitney U tests were used for pairwise comparisons, and rank-biserial correlation was reported as the effect size. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline participant characteristics and pre-instruction IMMS scores are presented in Table 1 for descriptive comparison only. Age and sex distributions appeared broadly similar across groups, although variation was observed in study-supporting technology and prior metaverse/VR experience. In particular, mobile phone use was more frequent in the face-to-face group, tablet use was more frequent in the metaverse group, and prior metaverse/VR experience was lower in the metaverse group than in the other groups. Post-intervention IMMS total and subscale scores, together with change scores (Δ = post–pre) and the primary between-group analyses, are presented in Table 2. Median post-test quiz scores were 73.26 [53.28–79.92] in the face-to-face group, 59.94 [53.28–66.60] in the online group, and 66.66 [66.65–73.26] in the metaverse group.

The primary analysis (3 × 2 mixed ANOVA) demonstrated significant group-by-time interactions for IMMS total (F(2,80) = 7.27, p = 0.001, ηp²=0.154, large effect) and Attention–Relevance (F(2,80) = 10.52, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.208, large effect), indicating that motivational changes over time differed between groups (Table 2). Post-hoc comparisons of change scores (Δ = post–pre) showed greater improvement in the metaverse group than in the online group for IMMS total (adjusted p < 0.001; Hedges’ g = 1.14). For Attention–Relevance, improvements were greater in the metaverse group than in both the face-to-face group (adjusted p = 0.013; g = 0.78) and the online group (adjusted p < 0.001; g = 1.34). Although Confidence–Satisfaction scores increased significantly within each group, the group-by-time interaction was not statistically significant (F(2,80) = 2.55, p = 0.084, ηp²=0.060), indicating that the magnitude of change did not differ significantly between groups. For academic achievement, quiz scores differed across groups (Kruskal–Wallis H(2) = 6.07, p = 0.048, ε²=0.051). Post-hoc comparisons showed higher quiz scores in the metaverse group than in the online group (adjusted p = 0.019; rrb = 0.43), whereas other pairwise differences were not significant (Table 2).

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that a metaverse-based, device-accessible virtual classroom may enhance students’ motivation toward instructional materials and may support learning outcomes compared with a conventional synchronous online format. Across groups, motivation increased from pre to post, but the magnitude of improvement differed meaningfully by educational modality. In the primary analysis, the metaverse group showed the largest gains, particularly for overall IMMS scores and the Attention-Relevance dimension, whereas improvements were smaller in the online group and generally intermediate in the face-to-face group. Quiz performance also varied by group, with higher scores observed in the metaverse group than in the online group. Taken together, these results indicate that the added features of a shared, interactive virtual learning space beyond simply delivering the same content online may have contributed to more interactive learning experiences and may be associated with modest advantages in post-test quiz performance.

A key question raised by these findings is why a metaverse-based education session might lead to larger motivational gains than a conventional synchronous online class when the instructional content is comparable. Metaverse learning environments are often described as providing a shared virtual space in which learners participate via avatars and can interact in real time (e.g., peer-instructor communication within the same virtual classroom), which may support more active participation during synchronous sessions [15, 21]. Consistent with this rationale, prior randomized work in nursing education reported that instructional-materials motivation (IMMS) was comparable between groups at baseline but was higher in the metaverse group after the intervention, and that IMMS scores also increased from pre to post within the metaverse group [21]. Evidence syntheses similarly suggest that well-designed metaverse-based instruction may support engagement and positive learning-related outcomes, although the strength and consistency of effects can vary [27]. Nevertheless, effects can vary across studies, partly because motivational impact may depend on specific instructional design features (e.g., integrating interactive scenarios or collaborative activities), exposure may be brief and difficult to sustain over time, and outcome measures and evaluation tools are not consistent [18, 19, 27]. In our study, this interpretation is supported by the domain-specific pattern of change: the metaverse group showed the clearest advantage in Attention-Relevance, consistent with a setting that invites exploration and interaction rather than passive attendance. Taken together, our results suggest that any advantage is less about the technology label itself and more about the instructional conditions enabled by the environment namely, a shared learning space, immediate interaction, and structured facilitation which may make theoretical content feel more engaging and relevant than standard video-based delivery. Although this interpretation is consistent with the observed pattern, engagement was not measured directly in this study.

The metaverse advantage can also be interpreted in light of challenges reported for conventional synchronous online education in physiotherapy education. In a comparative study of a virtual classroom versus face-to-face instruction, participants reported lower perceived motivation and lower perceived efficiency in the virtual classroom condition, and the authors indicated that face-to-face delivery may be preferred because it allows stronger interaction cues such as eye contact, while technical problems in distance education can negatively affect motivation [28]. Importantly, physiotherapy and rehabilitation students’ evaluations of online learning during the COVID-19 period similarly indicate motivational and engagement challenges (e.g., a subgroup reporting difficulty motivating themselves and concerns about educational quality, particularly for practice-oriented components) [13]. UK physiotherapy student survey data collected during the COVID-19 pandemic also reported dissatisfaction and reduced engagement with online-learning approaches compared with traditional face-to-face delivery, while emphasizing the need to adopt pedagogical strategies that maximize learning and engagement opportunities when content is delivered online [12]. In our study, the smaller motivational gains observed in the online group are compatible with these reports and support a pragmatic inference: unless interaction is deliberately strengthened and technical/communication barriers are managed, standard video-based delivery may offer fewer opportunities for sustained engagement than more interactive formats.

Alongside the motivational findings, the quiz results should be interpreted with caution because academic achievement was assessed only at post-test and no baseline knowledge measure was available. Therefore, although the metaverse group showed higher quiz scores than the synchronous online group, it cannot be determined with certainty to what extent this difference reflects learning gained during the intervention rather than pre-existing differences in topic-specific knowledge. Nevertheless, the pattern of results suggests that online education may be more effective when it is intentionally designed to be interactive rather than delivered as a standard videoconferencing session. Evidence from physiotherapy education shows that immersive or interactive digital approaches can improve short-term knowledge outcomes; for example, a single-blind randomized trial with physiotherapy students found that an immersive VR supplement yielded higher immediate knowledge scores than traditional atlas/textbook-based learning, although the advantage was not sustained at later follow-up [29]. At the same time, physiotherapy-focused synthesis work comparing VR/AR with traditional education emphasizes that effects on academic performance are not uniform, and that value is most likely when these technologies are used as complements that increase interactivity and active learning rather than as stand-alone replacements [30]. In our study, the metaverse group’s higher post-test quiz performance than the conventional synchronous online group, together with larger gains in Attention-Relevance, is consistent with this interpretation. Future studies should incorporate pre–post testing and longer follow-up to better quantify learning gains and retention over time.

In contrast to IMMS total and Attention-Relevance, the group-by-time interaction for Confidence-Satisfaction was not statistically significant in our study. A plausible explanation is that confidence- and satisfaction-related responses may depend more on how the learning experience is designed and how outcomes are assessed than on delivery mode alone. In support of this, metaverse education work recommends strengthening interactive programme elements such as gamified learning scenarios, real-life case simulations, and collaborative virtual activities to enhance learner engagement and experience [19]. Broader metaverse design frameworks likewise emphasize that educational effects are shaped by fundamental design choices and implementation features [18], and evidence syntheses highlight the importance of an explicit assessment dimension when evaluating metaverse-based training [20]. Moreover, umbrella-level evidence indicates that outcomes across metaverse studies can be heterogeneous and sometimes mixed [27]. Applied to our results, the lack of a differential effect on Confidence-Satisfaction may therefore reflect that our implementation and measurement approach was more sensitive to attention and perceived relevance than to confidence-building or satisfaction. Future studies could test this directly by embedding structured practice and feedback components and pairing them with competence-oriented assessments aligned with Confidence-Satisfaction outcomes.

From an implementation perspective, our metaverse approach was deliberately designed to be device-accessible (PC/mobile) without head-mounted displays, which may improve feasibility and reduce common adoption barriers. However, reviews in health-sciences education emphasize that successful metaverse integration depends on practical considerations such as cost and access barriers, connectivity requirements, usability, and data-privacy/security concerns, all of which may influence engagement and learning outcomes [15, 18]. In line with these considerations, we provided a brief orientation and real-time technical support during the sessions to minimize avoidable access and navigation problems and to keep students’ attention focused on the instructional goals. These implementation components should be considered part of intervention fidelity, and they are likely important for translating metaverse-based learning from small-group studies to routine physiotherapy curricula where variability in devices, connectivity, and digital literacy is expected. In physiotherapy programmes, where teaching time is shared between theoretical instruction and hands-on laboratory practice, scalable digital learning formats, are most valuable when they remain equitable and technically reliable across students.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the intervention targeted palpation-related theoretical content, including knowledge used to support palpation practice, rather than hands-on practical skill performance; therefore, the results cannot be generalized to hands-on palpation competence, which requires supervised practice and objective performance assessment. Second, quiz performance was assessed only post-intervention, limiting our ability to quantify within-person learning gains and retention over time. Because no baseline knowledge assessment was performed, baseline differences in topic-specific knowledge between groups cannot be ruled out. In addition, although the quiz was developed to align with the predefined instructional objectives, it was researcher-developed and was not subjected to a formal psychometric validation process. Third, the study was conducted in a single course and institution, and blinding of participants and the instructor was not feasible due to the nature of the educational modalities. Although the use of a single instructor helped standardize content delivery across groups, it may also have introduced instructor-related bias, including the possibility of unconscious differences in emphasis or interaction between educational modalities. Student preference for learning environment was also not assessed; therefore, it was not possible to determine whether alignment between assigned educational modality and individual preference may have influenced motivation or performance. As student engagement was not measured directly, the mechanisms underlying the observed differences remain speculative. Because some instructional elements used in the metaverse environment could also be implemented through other digital tools, the observed effects cannot be attributed exclusively to metaverse use itself, but should be interpreted in relation to the broader combination of interactive and spatial features of the learning environment.

Future studies should examine whether the observed motivational advantages translate into objective skill performance and should include pre–post knowledge testing with longer follow-up to evaluate retention. Given that metaverse outcomes can vary with design and implementation, future work should identify which instructional components drive change and assess feasibility at scale across diverse student contexts. Our results support metaverse-based education as a potentially useful approach to enrich synchronous online physiotherapy education for theoretical content when implemented with intentional instructional design and robust facilitation, while recognizing that hands-on competence development will continue to require complementary in-person practice.

Conclusion

This randomized three-arm study suggests that a device-accessible metaverse classroom can enhance physiotherapy students’ motivation toward instructional materials when teaching palpation-related theoretical content. Its clearest advantage was observed in the Attention-Relevance dimension of motivation, with greater improvement than the conventional synchronous online format. Post-test quiz scores were also higher in the metaverse group than in the synchronous online group. Taken together, these findings suggest that when palpation-related theoretical content must be delivered in a virtual format, more interactive learning environments may offer advantages over standard videoconferencing-based online instruction. Future studies should examine retention and whether motivational gains translate into objective practical skill performance.
