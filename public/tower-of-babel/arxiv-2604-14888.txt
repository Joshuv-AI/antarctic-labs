Reasoning Dynamics and the Limits of Monitoring Modality Reliance in Vision-Language Models
Danae Sánchez Villegas, Samuel Lewis-Lim, Nikolaos Aletras, Desmond Elliott, University of Copenhagen, Department of Computer Science, University of Sheffield, School of Computer Science {davi, de}@di.ku.dk{slewis-lim1, n.aletras}@sheffield.ac.uk

Abstract
Recent advances in vision language models (VLMs) offer reasoning capabilities, yet how these unfold and integrate visual and textual information remains unclear. We analyze reasoning dynamics in 18 VLMs covering instruction-tuned and reasoning-trained models from two different model families. We track confidence over Chain-of-Thought (CoT), measure the corrective effect of reasoning, and evaluate the contribution of intermediate reasoning steps. We find that models are prone to answer inertia , in which early commitments to a prediction are reinforced, rather than revised during reasoning steps. While reasoning-trained models show stronger corrective behavior, their gains depend on modality conditions, from text-dominant to vision-only settings. Using controlled interventions with misleading textual cues, we show that models are consistently influenced by these cues even when visual evidence is sufficient, and assess whether this influence is recoverable from CoT. Although this influence can appear in the CoT, its detectability varies across models and depends on what is being monitored. Reasoning-trained models are more likely to explicitly refer to the cues, but their longer and fluent CoTs can still appear visually grounded while actually following textual cues, obscuring modality reliance. In contrast, instruction-tuned models refer to the cues less explicitly, but their shorter traces reveal inconsistencies with the visual input. Taken together, these findings indicate that CoT provides only a partial view of how different modalities drive VLM decisions, with important implications for the transparency and safety of multimodal systems.



1
Introduction

Chain-of-Thought prompting (CoT) improves reasoning in large language models
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, often resulting in large gains in downstream task accuracy
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
. However, recent work argues that final accuracy alone provides a limited view of reasoning behavior
(
Guan et al. 2025
)
. Instead, analyzing how predictions evolve across intermediate reasoning steps can reveal whether reasoning progresses incrementally, or simply justifies an early hypothesis. For example,
Lewis-Lim et al. 2025
show that tracking the temporal evolution of model confidence across CoT steps helps distinguish genuine reasoning from post-hoc rationalization. Similarly,
Madsen et al. 2024
find that LLM self-explanations are often unfaithful, misrepresenting the true basis of model predictions. However, these analyses are limited to text-only models.
In vision-language models (VLMs), reasoning requires the integration of textual explanations with visual evidence. This can introduce additional uncertainty and potential failure modes
(
Parcalabescu & Frank 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2025a
)
. Prior work shows that models often struggle to reconcile internal parametric knowledge with external inputs, leading to
knowledge conflict
, where provided evidence is overridden by prior beliefs
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
. More broadly, models may rely on textual priors over visual signals, misinterpret visual structures, or produce explanations that are weakly grounded in the image
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Aghzal et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. Consequently, it remains unclear how confidence evolves during multimodal reasoning, how intermediate reasoning varies across modality conditions, and whether the modality driving the final decision can be reliably recovered from CoT traces.

Specifically, we investigate whether intermediate steps correct early mistakes, and how text interacts with visual evidence.
We analyze how the model’s prediction evolves across these steps by measuring the confidence assigned to the final answer conditioned on each prefix.

Our contributions are:

(1) a large-scale analysis of multimodal reasoning dynamics.

We track predictive confidence across CoT prefixes for 18 VLMs across different training paradigms: instruction-tuned and reasoning-trained. We show that VLMs are
inertial
, forming final predictions early and subsequently reinforcing them.

(2) Quantifying the corrective effect of reasoning.

Using net-gain and truncation analyses, we find that reasoning behavior is shaped by both training paradigm and the level of modality dependence, ranging from text-dominant to vision-only settings.

(3) Monitoring modality reliance under conflicting signals.

We introduce misleading textual cues that conflict with visual evidence to study modality reliance and its observability in reasoning traces (monitorability). Models often follow these cues without explicitly reflecting this modality influence in their CoT.

2
Related Work

Reasoning Dynamics and Monitorability of CoT.

CoT prompting improves reasoning accuracy in large language models
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
, but how intermediate reasoning steps relate to the model’s underlying decision process remains unclear. Prior work examines the faithfulness of CoT explanations, i.e., whether reasoning traces reflect the factors driving a model’s final prediction.
Turpin et al. 2023
show that biasing features such as suggested answers can shift predictions without being acknowledged in the reasoning trace. Subsequent work examines faithfulness through training paradigms, hint framing, and step-wise analyses
(
Lanham et al. 2023
;
Chua & Evans 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Lewis-Lim et al. 2025
)
, though different evaluation methods often yield conflicting conclusions
(
Parcalabescu & Frank 2024
)
.
Guan et al. 2025
formalize monitorability and propose metrics to asses whether specific intervention effects are recoverable from reasoning traces. These analyses are limited to text-only models; we extend them to multimodal settings.

Multimodal Reasoning and Modality Bias.

Multimodal reasoning introduces additional challenges, as models must integrate visual and textual inputs into coherent explanations. Surveys and benchmarks on multimodal CoT highlight persistent limitations in grounding and modality integration
(
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Cheng et al. 2025
)
. A central concern is unimodal bias, where models over-rely on textual cues despite the presence of informative visuals. Several methods quantify this effect using causal analyses or modality attribution metrics
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Parcalabescu & Frank 2023
)
, while others study how modality preferences affect robustness
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
.

Monitoring Modality Reliance through Perturbations.

Robustness and modality reliance in multimodal reasoning are often studied through controlled perturbations. Benchmarks such as MME-CoT and MathVerse show that models often fail to rely on visual information, instead exploiting textual redundancies or dataset biases
(
Jiang et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. Other work probes alignment between visual descriptions and reasoning
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
or examines consistency across decoding strategies
(
Parcalabescu & Frank 2025
)
. Calibration-aware evaluations further show that multimodal models struggle to recognize the limits of their knowledge
(
He et al. 2025
)
.
Balasubramanian et al. 2025
show that visual and subtle biases often influence predictions without being articulated, and that models can shift answers late in the CoT, particularly in perception-driven tasks.

Our focus.

While prior work focuses on robustness, bias, and final outputs, it leaves open how predictions are formed and reinforced during reasoning, whether intermediate steps contribute to correcting errors under different modality conditions, and whether the influence of misleading cues can be detected from reasoning traces. Our work analyzes reasoning dynamics in multimodal models by (i) tracking confidence trajectories across CoT steps, (ii) quantifying the corrective effect of reasoning through net gain across modality conditions, and (iii) evaluating the monitorability of misleading hints and modality attribution.

4
Reasoning Dynamics in Multimodal Models

Confidence Trajectories.

We first quantify how predictive confidence evolves over CoT reasoning in VLMs. By contrasting instruction-tuned and reasoning-trained models, we assess whether confidence is progressively revised or remains largely stable until the final answer. Models are prompted to produce step-by-step reasoning followed by a final answer using a fixed template (Appendix
A
).
For each reasoning step
t
(i.e., each CoT prefix
\text{CoT}_{1:t}
), we measure confidence as the probability assigned to the gold answer letter after the prefix
Answer:
, conditioned on the input
x
and the reasoning prefix
\text{CoT}_{1:t}
. We obtain this by extracting next-token log-probabilities for all candidate answer letters, normalizing them over the answer set, and taking the resulting probability assigned to the correct answer. For final-incorrect cases, we additionally track the probability assigned to the model’s predicted answer.
The resulting sequence
[P_{0},P_{1},\dots,P_{T}]
defines a per-example confidence trajectory over the reasoning process. For comparability across examples and models, we normalize these trajectories to the range
[0,1]
prior to aggregation.

Contribution of Intermediate Steps.

We evaluate the extent to which intermediate CoT steps contribute to final prediction accuracy by analyzing model performance under progressive truncation of the reasoning sequence. This analysis complements confidence-based dynamics by directly measuring whether additional reasoning steps improve task performance. For each example, we first generate a full reasoning sequence
\mathrm{CoT}_{1:T}
. We then iteratively truncate the sequence to retain only the first
t
steps, where
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
. At each truncation depth, the model is prompted to produce a final answer conditioned on the partial reasoning prefix and the original multimodal input. We compute accuracy at step
t
:

\mathrm{Acc}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}\!\left[\arg\max_{y}P_{M}(y\mid x_{i},\mathrm{CoT}_{1:t}^{i})=y_{i}^{*}\right],

(1)

where
x_{i}
denotes the multimodal input for example
i
and
y_{i}^{*}
is the gold answer. Aggregating across examples yields an accuracy curve over reasoning steps, which reveals whether later reasoning steps lead to systematic improvements in predictive performance.

Net Gain of CoT Reasoning

We assess the corrective effect of CoT reasoning by comparing model predictions before and after CoT generation. For each example, we record whether the initial prediction without CoT is correct and whether the final prediction after CoT is correct. We define
net gain
as:

\text{Net Gain}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[b_{i}=0\land f_{i}=1]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[b_{i}=0]}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[b_{i}=1\land f_{i}=0]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[b_{i}=1]}.

(2)

where
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
indexes evaluation examples
x_{i}
.

b_{i}=\mathbb{1}[\text{initial prediction is correct}]\quad\text{and}\quad f_{i}=\mathbb{1}[\text{final prediction is correct}]
. The net gain lies in the range
[-1,1]
.
Intuitively, net gain measures how often CoT corrects initially incorrect predictions minus how often it overturns correct ones, thus, a positive net gain indicates that CoT more often corrects incorrect predictions than it overturns correct ones.

Intermediate reasoning often reinforces early predictions rather than correcting them.

When predictions are correct, the probability of the gold answer increases over reasoning steps; when incorrect, it declines while the probability of the chosen incorrect option remains high or increases. This divergence emerges early, suggesting that predictive signal is largely determined in the initial steps, with later reasoning primarily amplifying the model’s current hypothesis rather than correcting it.

5
Monitorability of Visual Reliance

In this section, we complement our step-wise analysis with a monitorability evaluation, assessing whether modality influences are reflected in the CoT.

5.1
Monitorability Framework

Monitorability refers to the degree to which a property of a model’s behavior can be reliably inferred from its reasoning trace by an external monitor
(
Guan et al. 2025
)
. In our setting, the property of interest is which input modality (text or vision) influences the model’s prediction. To study this,
we adapt the framework of
Guan et al. 2025
, which uses controlled interventions to test whether their influence is recoverable from the model’s CoT. While originally proposed for text-only settings, we adapt it to multimodal inputs by using interventions that create conflicts between textual cues and visual evidence. This enables us to assess both whether models prioritize one modality over the other and whether that reliance is reflected in the CoT. We operationalize monitorability through two complementary evaluations: detecting whether injected hints influence reasoning, and attributing predictions to textual or visual information.

Misleading Hint Interventions.

We inject misleading hints as textual interventions in the prompt
(
Turpin et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Guan et al. 2025
)
that suggest a specific incorrect answer, following prior intervention-based analyses in text-only LLMs. We consider three framing variants: sycophancy, reward-hacking, and unethical information (Table
1
).
All experiments are conducted on the
Vision-Only
subset of MathVerse, where the full problem is contained in the image. This provides a clean setting for intervention: because the correct answer must be recovered from visual content, any effect of injected text reflects reliance on textual cues rather than missing task information.
We sample 10 generations per prompt to estimate intervention effects and restrict evaluation to
baseline-correct
examples, those answered correctly in more than 50% of generations without a hint, to ensure these effects reflect cue reliance rather than task competence.

Monitor tasks.

Given that hints may influence model behavior, we test whether this influence is detectable from the CoT. An external monitor reads the reasoning trace and judges whether the intervention affected the model’s prediction. We define two progressively stricter tasks:

(i) Hint influence detection (
G^{2}_{H}
)
, where the monitor is told a hint may have been present and asked whether it influenced the reasoning, measuring whether explicit hint reliance is recoverable from the CoT; and
(ii) Modality attribution (
G^{2}_{M}
)
, where the monitor is asked whether the model primarily relied on textual or visual information, with no mention of any hint. Because the only textual content is the injected hint, this provides a stricter test of whether the CoT reveals the modality that drove the prediction.
Comparing these two tasks enables us to disentangle intervention-specific detection from general modality attribution. In particular, it assesses whether a monitor that considers only image-versus-text reliance can recover intervention effects as effectively as one explicitly instructed to detect the hint. Monitorability metric.

We quantify monitor performance using
G^{2}_{\text{mean}}
from
Guan et al. 2025
. This metric measures the extent to which the model’s CoT contains sufficient information for an external monitor to detect whether the intervention influenced the model’s prediction.
G^{2}_{\text{mean}}
combines the monitor’s sensitivity and specificity into a single score, where a value of 1.0 indicates perfect monitorability while 0.25 corresponds to random performance. To account for the fact that the model may select the hinted answer by chance even without the intervention, the metric incorporates the total effect,

\mathrm{TE}=\Pr(Y{=}1\mid X{=}1)-\Pr(Y{=}1\mid X{=}0),

(3)

where
X\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether the hint is present and
Y
denotes whether the model selects the hinted answer. These probabilities
are estimated by sampling 10 responses per example under each condition. A positive total effect indicates that the injected text causally shifts the model toward the hinted option, establishing a lower bound on the proportion of positive outcomes attributable to the intervention, against which the monitor’s detection rate is calibrated. We further use the aggregated total effect to contextualize the strength of each hint’s influence across models. We refer to
Guan et al. 2025
for the full derivation of these metrics.

Implications for monitoring CoTs in multimodal LLMs.

These results suggest that monitorability of modality reliance depends both on the model behavior and on the information available to the monitor. A monitor that knows the form of the intervention can search for specific signals, whereas a monitor tasked only with assessing modality grounding must determine whether the reasoning is genuinely derived from the image, a substantially harder judgment, particularly when the CoT is long and internally coherent. This gap has practical implications: in deployment, one typically does not know the form of potential confounding cues. The fact that modality attribution is harder than hint detection, especially for larger models, suggests that CoT monitoring alone may be insufficient to ensure visual grounding in multimodal systems.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we analyze reasoning dynamics in VLMs and find that predictions are often formed early, with later CoT steps primarly reinforcing rather than revising them. While reasoning-trained models show stronger corrective behavior, these improvements depend on modality conditions, with the largest gains in vision-only settings. Using controlled interventions, we show that models are consistently influenced by misleading textual cues. This influence is easier to monitor in shorter CoTs from instruction-tuned models, where inconsistencies with visual content are apparent,
but becomes increasingly obscured in longer ones from multi-step reasoning models, making modality reliance difficult to recover without prior knowledge of influencing factors. These findings highlight important implications for the transparency and safety of multimodal systems. Future work should focus on improving CoT monitorabiliy to reliably recover influences on predictions, regardless of reasoning length or model type.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

CoT prompt

For Section
4
, we prompt the model to produce step-by-step reasoning and a final answer using a fixed template:

You are a careful reasoning assistant that
answers multiple-choice questions
about an image.
First, think step by step.
Then, give the final answer.
Format EXACTLY as:
Reasoning: <step 1>
Reasoning: <step 2>
...
Answer: <final letter>
Use at most 5 reasoning steps.
Each step should be one or two sentences.
Do NOT use ’1.’, ’2.’ or bullet points.
Do NOT literally write
<step 1> or <final letter>.

We first parse explicit
Reasoning step
markers; when unavailable, we fall back to splitting free-form reasoning by non-empty lines. We treat each
Reasoning
segment as a reasoning step, corresponding to a prefix
\text{CoT}_{1:t}
in our analysis. Although this setup may introduce minor ambiguities (e.g., formatting inconsistencies or implicit step boundaries), in practice it provides a sufficiently reliable approximation of step boundaries.

Generation Protocol.

For the reasoning-dynamics experiments (Section
4
), we use one-shot prompting with deterministic decoding (temperature
=0
), generating a single reasoning trace per example. This allows us to analyze step-wise confidence trajectories conditioned on a fixed CoT.
For the intervention experiments (Section
5
), we keep the prompt fixed and sample ten independent generations per example and condition (control and intervention) using temperature
=0.8
to capture stochastic variation in cue reliance. For the monitor model, we use deterministic decoding (temperature
=0

Across model families, two common patterns emerge.
