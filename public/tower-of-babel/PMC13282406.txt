Chloroplast genome editing of Rubisco boosts photosynthesis and plant growth
Authors: Wataru Yamori, Issei Nakazato, Yuchen Qu, Yukina Sanga, Tomoko Miyata, Ryo Uehara, Yuma Noto, Keiichi Namba, Hiroshi Fukayama, Hiroyoshi Matsumura, Shin-ichi Arimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotosynthetic inefficiencies limit the productivity and sustainability of crop production and the resilience of agriculture to future societal and environmental challenges. Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) has inherently low catalytic efficiency, making it a key target for photosynthesis and crop improvement. However, introducing mutations to the chloroplast-encoded Rubisco large subunit (rbcL), which contains the enzyme’s catalytic sites, is technically challenging. In this study, we successfully generate a range of chloroplast-genome-edited Arabidopsis thaliana plants targeting rbcL by a targeted base editor, ptpTALECD. The M309I and D397N substitutions in rbcL result in an increased Rubisco catalytic rate (kcat) without any reductions of Rubisco content, thereby enhancing photosynthetic rates and plant growth under both current atmospheric CO2 concentrations (i.e., 381 μmol mol−1) and projected future concentrations (i.e., 549 μmol mol−1). Cryo-electron microscopy structural analysis shows that the M309I and D397N substitutions, although located far from the catalytic site, induce structural alterations in the catalytic (60 s) loops. Our findings highlight the potential of Rubisco engineering to improve plant photosynthesis and growth, and underscore the unique opportunities that chloroplast genome editing offers for enhancing photosynthesis and crop productivity and reducing atmospheric CO2 levels in a non-GMO context.Subject terms: Plant breeding, Agricultural genetics, Rubisco

Introduction

Climate change has been accelerating, with rising atmospheric CO₂ levels contributing to higher global temperatures since the Industrial Revolution1,2 and exacerbating the food crisis. Global food production is projected to need to increase by more than 30% by 2050 to meet growing demands3,4. Plants, as the foundation of the global food supply, are susceptible to climate change and play a crucial role in regulating atmospheric CO₂. Rubisco (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) is the primary enzyme responsible for carbon fixation in the Calvin–Benson cycle, catalyzing the addition of CO₂ to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. Rubisco is inefficient, making its catalytic reactions the limiting step in photosynthetic CO₂ fixation under physiologically relevant conditions. Engineering Rubisco with a high carboxylation rate into plant chloroplasts is a promising approach to increasing global crop yields and reducing atmospheric CO₂ levels4–6. Mathematical modeling suggests that introducing Rubisco with improved kinetic properties—such as higher CO₂ specificity and lower Michaelis constant (Kc)— could potentially result in over a 25% increase in crop yields7,8. However, efforts to engineer Rubisco are fundamentally constrained by kinetic trade-offs, particularly between catalytic rate and CO₂/O₂ specificity, which limit the simultaneous improvement of both parameters.

Engineering Rubisco in vascular plants has traditionally been conducted exclusively in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), where well-established procedures for chloroplast transformation, the foreign gene insertion, are available9,10. Rubisco is a hexadecameric complex composed of eight large subunits encoded by the rbcL chloroplast genome-encoded gene and eight small subunits encoded by a family of rbcS nuclear genes. Efforts have been made to identify new Rubisco variants from diverse natural sources or to develop hybrid enzymes that exhibit not only higher turnover rates (kcat) but also improved carboxylation efficiency (kcat/Kc), which is considered more relevant for enhancing photosynthetic performance and plant growth6,8,11–18. However, attempts to engineer vascular plants with these Rubisco variants have largely been unsuccessful, at best growing slower than wild type even in enhanced 1% (= 10,000 μmol mol−1) CO2 conditions, primarily due to issues with inefficient assembly and poor solubility of heterologously expressed Rubisco. These methods, even if succeeded, would pose another hurdle of GM regulation in many countries, so here, we tried to carry out genome editing also for future social acceptance.

In recent years, directed evolution approaches using Rubisco-dependent Escherichia coli selection19 and evolutionary studies of Rubisco from C3 to C4 plants of the genus Flaveria20 have identified several candidate residues in RbcL that could potentially improve Rubisco’s carboxylation rate. Among these, M309 and D397 were of particular interest: M309 has been shown to act as a C4 catalytic switch residue in Flaveria, and D397 lies within a conserved structural domain implicated in catalytic regulation. However, how the structural changes within Rubisco enhance its characteristics remains still unexplored. The next challenge is to introduce these residues directly into different plant species under the natural expression levels. In this study, we successfully generated genome-edited A. thaliana lines with stable point mutations in rbcL to assess their effects on Rubisco function/structure, photosynthesis, and plant growth.

Results

Amino acid modification in RbcL

We initially focused on six amino acid substitutions in the Rubisco large subunit (RbcL) that have been suggested to enhance Rubisco’s carboxylation rate, as shown in Fig. 1. The M309I substitution has been reported as a natural variant potentially contributing to the higher catalytic activity of Rubisco in C4 compared to C3 photosynthesis in Flaveria species20. The other five substitutions—L74M, I393M, D397N, A414T, and P415A—were selected based on a report of cyanobacterial Rubisco mutation screening conducted in vitro using an E. coli expression system19. Of these, M309I, D397N, and A414T could be introduced by C:G-to-T:A base editing21. By this base editing, L74 and P415 could be substituted for the amino acids Phe and Ser, respectively. The residues L74, D397, A414, and P415 are conserved across all 32 investigated crop and tree species, while M309 is conserved in 26 of these species (Supplementary Data S1). Consequently, we attempted to introduce a total of five mutations into A. thaliana: L74F, M309I, D397N, A414T, and P415S (Fig. 1b).

The binary vectors encoding chloroplast base editors, ptpTALECD or ptpTALECD_v2mod (Supplementary Fig. S1a), were integrated into the nuclear genome of A. thaliana Col-0. All the target substitutions seemed to be homoplasmically introduced [i.e., introduced across all chloroplast genomes, which are present in hundreds to thousands of copies per cell22] in one or more T1 plants (Supplementary Fig. S1b–g and Supplementary Data S2). In the T2 generation, only null-segregant plants, which lacked T-DNA, were selected to avoid additional mutations induced by the base editors (Supplementary Data S3). Including the unintended byproducts (D72N, R312C, and P415L mutants), a total of seven null-segregant single mutants were isolated (Supplementary Data S3). All the T3 plants carried the homoplasmic mutation at the targets. Two T3 lines of M309I and D397N mutants were subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) to investigate off-target mutations in the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes. There were no off-target mutations detected in three lines (Supplementary Data S4), and a line rbcL_M309I-3_DddA11_11-1 had an off-target mutation of seemingly no effect, an intergenic region, a C to T conversion located 65 bp upstream of the initiation codon of the rpoB gene. These results suggest that null-segregant lines with the homoplasmic mutation at the chloroplast-targeted bases were generated. Subsequent analyses were conducted using T4 plants for the A414T mutants and T3 plants for the remaining mutants. For readability, the mutant lines will hereafter be referred to by shortened names, like “M309I Line 1” (listed in Supplementary Data S5).

Plant growth at low, ambient, and high CO2 concentration

A range of chloroplast-genome-edited A. thaliana plants targeting rbcL were cultivated at different CO2 concentrations, including low (286 μmol mol−1), ambient (381 μmol mol−1), and high concentration (549 μmol mol−1) (Fig. 2a–c). No significant difference in shoot dry weight or leaf area was observed on M309I and D397N cultivated under low CO2 concentration, compared with Col-0 (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. S2a, b). On the other hand, both at the ambient and high CO2 concentration, M309I and D397N substitutions in rbcL exhibited significantly higher total leaf area and total shoot dry weight compared to Col-0 at 48 days after sowing (Fig. 2f–i), whereas at 24 or 25 days after sowing, they did not show any significant differences in total leaf area and total shoot dry weight (Supplementary Fig. S2c–f). All other lines exhibited either comparable or lower leaf area and dry weight relative to Col-0 irrespective of the growth CO2 concentration and timing after sowing (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S2). Also, at ambient CO2 concentration, M309I and D397N exhibited significantly higher growth not only in a large-scale 5-week cultivation experiment but also throughout a 90-day cultivation, with higher shoot dry weight, root dry weight, harvested seeds weight compared with Col-0 (Supplementary Figs. S3, S4, and supplementary video).

Rubisco kinetics and photosynthetic components

M309I exhibited a significantly higher catalytic turnover rate (kcat) compared to Col-0 (Fig. 3a), but it also showed higher Michaelis-Menten constants for CO2 (Kc), lower catalytic efficiencies for carboxylation (kcat/Kc), and a lower ratio of carboxylation to oxygenation efficiencies (Sc/o) (Fig. 3b–d). Moreover, D397N displayed a significantly higher kcat and a lower Sc/o compared to Col-0 (Fig. 3a, d), while Kc and kcat/Kc were comparable (Fig. 3b, c). The extent to which photorespiration suppresses net CO₂ assimilation was also evident from the significantly lower ratio of photosynthesis at 21% O₂ to that at 2% O₂ in the M309I and D397N mutants compared to Col-0 (Supplementary Fig. S6a). On the other hand, all other lines showed lower kcat and kcat/Kc relative to Col-0 (Supplementary Fig. 3a, c). Furthermore, for other lines, Kc was comparable to or higher than that of Col-0, whereas Sc/o was comparable to or higher than Col-0 (Fig. 3b, d).

D72N, M309I, D397N, and A414T exhibited Rubisco content comparable to that of Col-0, while other lines showed lower levels (Fig. 3e). Similarly, D72N, L74F, M309I, and D397N showed total chlorophyll (Chl) contents comparable to Col-0, whereas other lines had lower contents (Fig. 3f). CO2 response of photosynthetic rate

The CO2 responses of several photosynthetic parameters were determined in all the chloroplast-genome-edited plants (Fig. 4a, b, and Supplementary Fig. S9). At a CO2 concentration of 400 μmol mol−1, the M309I and D397N substitutions in rbcL exhibited a significantly higher CO2 assimilation rate and electron transport rate, and tended to have a lower NPQ compared to Col-0, whereas all other lines showed lower CO2 assimilation rates and electron transport rates relative to Col-0 (Fig. 4c–e). Furthermore, since stomatal conductance and intercellular CO2 concentrations for M309I and D397N were comparable to Col-0 (Fig. 4f, g), the intrinsic water-use efficiency (WUEi), calculated as the CO2 assimilation rate divided by stomatal conductance, was higher in M309I and D397N than in Col-0 (Fig. 4h). This finding indicates that overcoming photosynthetic limitations to improve Rubisco function resulted in increased water-use efficiency.

Structures of M309I and D397N Rubisco

To address how M309I and D397N mutations of RbcL affect the Rubisco activity, we purified Col-0, M309I, and D397N Rubisco from leaves in Col-0 and chloroplast-genome-edited A. thaliana, and collected cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) data in the presence of 50 mM sulfate ions. The motivation for solving the structure of Rubisco in the presence of sulfate ions arises from the weak binding affinity of sulfate ions, and sulfate binding could partially mimic a ligand bound to the active site without inducing loop 6 (i.e., active site) closure. Several studies have reported that amino acid mutations at Rubisco subunit interfaces, distant from the active site, can influence its catalytic rate19,20. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that changes in Rubisco dynamics may contribute to its enhanced catalytic rate. Since the tightly binding analogs such as a transition-state analog 2-carboxyarabinitol 1,5-bisphosphate (2-CABP) may stabilize (or rigidify) the Rubisco structure, we sought to determine whether structural differences reflecting dynamic changes may be observed in the presence of sulfate ions. Such an approach has also been employed in previous structural studies of hybrid Rubisco, with the replacement of rice Rubisco RbcS by sorghum counterparts with increased kcat14.

The structure of Rubisco is related to D4 symmetry, and the cryo-EM density maps imposed with D4 symmetry exhibited the highest resolution (Supplementary Figs. S11–13, and Supplementary Data S6). During molecular fitting, we carefully considered the contour level of the density map (see the Methods section).

Expressed in leaves of A. thaliana are four distinct isoforms of RbcS (RBCS1A, RBCS1B, RBCS2B, and RBCS3B), which possibly correlate with the accumulation of Rubisco in chloroplasts within the leaves23 (Supplementary Fig. S14). Growth temperature affects the expression levels of these RbcS isoforms with distinct kinetics24. Given our growth conditions (see Methods), RBCS1A is expected to be the most abundant, and RBCS3B and RBCS2B are the second and the third23. Our SDS-PAGE analysis also shows that the RBCS1A level accounted for approximately half of the total (Supplementary Fig. S15), consistent with the previous report23. Notably, the RBCS2B and RBCS3B isoforms have identical mature protein sequences, differing only in their transit peptides. Together, the RBCS2B and RBCS3B make up about half of Rubisco; thus, the amount of RBCS1A is roughly equal to RBCS2B/3B under these conditions. We first evaluated which amino acid side chains fit better into the cryo-EM density maps with C1 symmetry. Although this map is expected to possess D4 symmetry (as mentioned below), we refer to it as a C1 density map hereafter because the D4 symmetry averaging has not been applied. However, we were unable to fit either isoform exclusively into the C1 density map. We then generated models incorporating alternative amino acids corresponding to the amino acid sequences of RBCS1A and RBCS2B/3B, and calculated their occupancy using the C1 density maps of WT, M309I, and D397N (see Methods). As a result, the occupancy of the alternative amino acids was 0.45–0.51 for RbcS1A and 0.49–0.55 for RbcS2B/3B across all eight RbcS subunits, indicating no significant bias in amino acid occupancy. This suggests likely heterogeneity within the Rubisco sample purified from Arabidopsis thaliana leaves, where particles containing RBCS1A, RBCS2B/3B, or mixtures of these species have been averaged together regardless of their symmetry, and that the C1 density map was already averaged to D4 symmetry during reconstruction. Therefore, we decided to refine the structures using the D4 density map, applying half-occupancy models for alternative amino acids (Supplementary Fig. S16). RBCS1A and RBCS3B/2B differ at residues 2, 22, 24, 34, 58, and 96, with Q/K, T/S, S/V, I/L, S/T, and N/G substitutions, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S14). Residues 2, 22, 24, and 34 are on the molecular surface of Rubisco, residue 96 is at the solvent channel entrance, and residue 58 is inside the channel (Supplementary Fig. S17). The structure of this solvent channel has been suggested to be associated with Rubisco kinetics25. Subtle variations in amino acid hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity may influence Rubisco’s enzymatic kinetics.

The folding of M309I and D397N Rubisco is almost the same as that of Col-0 Rubisco (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S18). The cryo-EM density map of M309I clearly shows the substitution of M309 with I, and this amino-acid replacement alters the orientation of the Phe117 side chain (Supplementary Fig. S19a, b). On the other hand, the substitution of D397 with N cannot be judged by density (Supplementary Fig. S19c, d). The Col-0, M309I, and D397N Rubisco structures contain sulfate ions at the RuBP phosphate group binding sites, due to the presence of 50 mM sulfate ions in the cryo-EM specimen solutions. In the Col-0 Rubisco active site, two sulfate ions were observed at the P1 distal site and P2 upper site (Fig. 5c, d), whereas in M309I and D397N, only one sulfate ion was present in the P2 upper site (Fig. 5e, f, and Supplementary Fig. S20a). In Col-0 Rubisco, the side chain of W66 interacts with the sulfate at the P1 distal site, but such interactions are not observed in M309I and D397N. The absence of a sulfate ion at the P1 site in M309I and D397N is likely due to the W66 side chain being oriented in a way that does not accommodate substrate or product binding at the P1 site.

The two catalytic loops of Rubisco, namely 60 s loop (residues 64-67) and loop 6 (residues 332-337), typically undergo a transition from disorder to an ordered state upon binding of the sugar-phosphate substrate or the transition state analog 2CABP26. In our condition with 50 mM sulfate ions, all of Rubisco’s loop 6 were structurally not resolved with an open conformation (Fig. 5c–f). In contrast, Col-0 and D397N Rubisco’s 60 s loop were structurally ordered, but not resolved in M309I (Supplementary Figs. S20, S21). Notably, D397N has a 60 s loop with a distinct conformation (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. S20b). Thus, differences in the 60S loop structure were observed in both mutants.

Discussion

The transformation of chloroplast genomes in plants offers a promising approach to enhancing photosynthesis and crop productivity, as many chloroplast genome-encoded genes are involved in photosynthesis. Despite extensive research over the past fifty years aimed at improving Rubisco’s catalytic properties, efforts to engineer vascular plants by replacing them with “super Rubisco” enzymes have largely been unsuccessful and often resulted in reduced plant growth. These imply that enhancing the function of Rubisco would be fundamentally difficult in plants. Against these background, this study demonstrates that Rubisco kinetics can be significantly enhanced through the least change, single amino acid substitutions at M309I and D397N, each of which increases kcat of Rubisco (Fig. 3). The 309th amino acid shows natural variation among plant species. Both isoleucine and methionine are found at this position in members of the genus Flaveria, and isoleucine was suggested to enhance Rubisco’s carboxylation rate20. The isoleucine at 309th is also found in members of the Solanaceae family (Supplementary Data S1), so their Rubisco may exhibit higher catalytic activity than if this position were occupied by methionine. In contrast, the 397th amino acid is highly conserved, with aspartic acid consistently present across all 33 species analyzed (Supplementary Data S1). The D397N substitution was originally identified during a directed evolution experiment of cyanobacterial Rubisco in E. coli. In that study, the L74M and D397N double mutant was shown to enhance enzymatic activity19. However, it was neither evident nor anticipated that D397N alone would lead to an increased carboxylation rate. The reason why the D397N has not yet been observed in nature, and whether it imposes any biological constraints under natural environmental conditions, remains to be clarified. M309 and D397 are located at the RbcL-RbcL and RbcL-RbcS interfaces, respectively, both of which are distant from the active site (Fig. 5b). The molecular mechanisms by which these mutations at sites distant from the active site affect Rubisco activity have remained unclear so far. In the present structural analysis, we observed structural differences in the 60 s loop between the Col-0, M309I, and D397N. Because Rubisco is famously resistant to mechanistic interpretation, the following mechanistic description should be regarded as a hypothesis.

The open and closed mechanism of two catalytic loops of Rubisco (loop 6 and the 60 s loop) was previously proposed26. In the model, the distance between the P1 and P2 phosphates acts as a trigger. The P1 phosphate interacts with Thr65, causing a rotation of its main chain φ torsion angle, which is key to switching the catalytic loops between the open and closed states. Thr65 is centrally located within the 60 s loop. Here, we found that in M309I and D397N, which have higher kcat, the 60 s loop adopts a more flexible or distinct conformation compared to Col-0. Furthermore, in M309I and D397N, no sulfate ion was found at the P1 site. This observation suggests a potential correlation between higher kcat and increased dynamics of the 60 s loop, whereas other amino acid mutations appear to have reduced this flexibility. Previously, we also proposed a similar correlation based on structural analyses of hybrid Rubisco with higher kcat14, in which slight amino acid differences (Ile102 to Leu) in RbcS were discussed as a potential factor influencing 60 s loop dynamics. These shared features may provide insights for future improvements in Rubisco engineering; however, predicting and designing mutations to achieve such structural alterations remains challenging (see Supplementary Text for a more detailed discussion). The cryo-EM structure of the cyanobacterial Rubisco activase (Rca)-Rubisco complex (PDB id: 6Z1F) has revealed three binding regions (residues 91–96 of RbcL, residues 66 and 128-129 of RbcL, and residues 72–75 of RbcS, in Col-0 numbering) between Rca and Rubisco27. M309 and D397 are located more than 15 Å away from these regions. The Rubisco activation mechanism by Rca and the M309I/D397N mutations may be independent of each other (Supplementary Fig. S8).The structural changes caused by the M309I and D397N substitutions improved Rubisco kinetics (Fig. 3), and the engineering Rubisco with a high carboxylation rate into plant chloroplasts presents a viable strategy for improving photosynthesis (Fig. 4c), WUEi (Fig. 4h), and plant growth (Fig. 2) under both ambient and elevated CO₂ concentrations, which is an adaptation with clear evolutionary significance. Although both M309I and D397N exhibit increased kcat compared to Col-0, D397N additionally shows a lower Kc and a slightly higher carboxylation efficiency (kcat/Kc) (Fig. 3a–c), suggesting a potentially greater catalytic advantage. However, this kinetic difference did not translate into a clear difference in biomass accumulation between the two lines at day 48 (Fig. 2a), although a trend of higher photosynthesis in D397N was observed under lower CO₂ concentrations (Fig. 4a), in agreement with its improved kinetics. These findings suggest that even small changes in Rubisco kinetics, particularly under non-limiting CO₂, can lead to cumulative physiological advantages that may become more pronounced over time. Indeed, the absence of significant growth differences at 25 days, followed by a significant biomass increase at 48 days, supports the idea that modest early advantages in carbon assimilation can compound over developmental time, resulting in larger effects on final plant biomass. At a high CO₂ concentration (549 μmol mol⁻¹), photosynthesis is expected to remain at least partially Rubisco-limited, such that improvements in Rubisco function can contribute to enhanced plant growth. At the same time, photosynthesis under elevated CO₂ conditions may, in some cases, be limited by RuBP regeneration rather than by Rubisco28. Notably, the Rubisco-edited plants still exhibited enhanced growth under these conditions (Fig. 2), suggesting that factors beyond Rubisco carboxylation kinetics also contribute to growth stimulation at elevated CO₂. One possible explanation is that higher Rubisco catalytic efficiency reduces the amount of Rubisco protein required, allowing nitrogen to be reallocated toward RuBP-regeneration processes such as electron transport and Calvin-cycle enzymes. Such redistribution may enhance photosynthetic capacity even when Rubisco is not the primary limiting factor. Historically, atmospheric CO₂ concentrations remained relatively stable at around 280–290 µmol mol⁻¹ for much of the geological past, prior to the Industrial Revolution29. As a result, Rubisco in most plants evolved to function optimally under these lower CO₂ levels. However, current atmospheric CO₂ concentrations have risen to approximately 420 µmol mol⁻¹, and projections suggest it may reach 550 µmol mol⁻¹ in the near future30. Under elevated CO₂, the necessity for a high CO₂/O₂ specificity factor Sc/o is reduced, while an increased catalytic turnover rate kcat becomes more advantageous for maximizing carbon assimilation. However, rising temperatures will reduce CO₂ solubility, indicating that the selective pressure on Sc/o under future climates will depend on both CO₂ concentration and temperature. Both M309I and D397N showed a tendency toward improved growth compared with Col-0 even under elevated CO₂ conditions combined with a + 3 °C warming treatment (Supplementary Fig. S5). Therefore, Rubisco variants with enhanced kcat, such as those characterized in this study, may offer a competitive advantage over the wild-type enzyme under future climatic conditions. We also observed that several mutants exhibiting poor growth under ambient CO₂ conditions showed significant recovery when grown under elevated CO₂ concentrations (Fig. 2f–i). This suggests that higher CO₂ levels can buffer the negative effects of suboptimal Rubisco kinetics, providing a more permissive physiological environment for future efforts aimed at engineering Rubisco function.

Rubisco has evolved to enhance Sc/o, kcat, and kcat/Kc in angiosperms. However, the rbcL gene has evolved slowly and is among the most evolutionarily conserved protein-coding genes in plants31, which limits the range of natural sequence variation available for adaptive changes. As a result, there remains substantial potential to improve Rubisco function through targeted artificial mutations. In this study, we utilized the ptpTALECD genome editing tool21 for chloroplast genome editing to accelerate Rubisco evolution, introducing properties adapted to high CO₂ environments, similar to C4 Rubisco. While both isoleucine and methionine are found at position 309 across plant species, D397 is well conserved in diverse plant species even at the nucleotide sequence level (Supplementary Data S1). If the target plant species carries isoleucine at position 309, the D397N substitution can be a promising candidate for introduction. Consequently, the introduction of M309I and D397N mutations may improve biomass production in most herbaceous and woody plants, including agriculturally important crops. This approach, not a drastic change but the least change of base editing, led to improvements in photosynthesis and plant growth (Figs. 2 and 4).

Many of the previous approaches aim to overcome Rubisco’s limitations through various bioengineering strategies, either by enhancing chloroplast CO₂ levels32,33 or by improving Rubisco’s catalytic properties3,34,35. As, in recent years, CO2 levels have been rising more rapidly than humanity had anticipated, Rubisco modification itself, even without concentrating CO2 within the chloroplasts, may be able to boost photosynthetic capacity some more. Chloroplast transformation offers several advantages, including precise genome manipulation via homologous recombination, avoidance of transgene silencing, and better control over the escape of transgenes into the environment36. As chloroplast genome editing can be performed in plant species that allow nuclear genome-targeted transformation, this transformation strategy could also be applied to introduce point mutations in other chloroplast-encoded genes. While GMOs are tightly regulated, genome-edited plants—such as the null-segregants in this study—that contain minimal and precise changes without foreign gene integration, are increasingly being accepted under evolving non-GMO policies in many regions. Thus, chloroplast genome editing could play a role in a more acceptable and feasible new green revolution.

Method

Sequence analysis of RbcL

rbcL sequences were extracted from reference genomes (Supplementary Data S1) and compared using Geneious Prime v2024.0.7.

Development and Introduction of Rubisco Mutants

The sequences that the TALE arrays bind to (listed in Supplementary Data S7) were settled based on the positional tendencies of edited cytosines21,37. All of the mutants except for M309I were obtained by ptpTALECD. The M309I mutation could not be achieved with ptpTALECD; therefore, ptpTALECD_v2mod, which has a cytidine deaminase domain with a higher base-editing activity than that of ptpTALECD, was used to introduce this mutation. Binary vectors to express ptpTALECD and ptpTALECD_v2mod were assembled as described previously21,37,38, but the vector of ptpTALECD_v2mod was assembled using intermediate plasmids (Addgene ID #191599-191602, and 191607-191610) instead of those employed to assembly ptpTALECD vectors (Addgene ID #171724-171735, 191579-191598). The binary vectors were integrated into the nuclear genome of A. thaliana Col-0 by floral dipping39. T1 seeds were selected based on the presence of GFP fluorescence40. DNA was roughly extracted from a T1 leaf by incubating it with 50 µL of TE Buffer [100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.5) and 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (pH 8.0) in diluted water] at 98 °C for 15 minutes. Targeted sequences were amplified from the DNA using KOD one PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO). PCR products were purified with FastGene Gel/PCR Extraction Kit (Nippon Genetics) and subsequently subjected to Sanger sequencing. PCR primers were synthesized by Merck, and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data S8. Null-segregant T2 seeds were selected based on the absence of GFP fluorescence. Genotyping of T2 plants was conducted using the same methods as for the T1 plants.

NGS analyses

To assess off-target mutations in chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes, NGS analyses were performed by using total DNAs extracted from a leaf of T3 null-segregant plants with the Maxwell RSC Plant DNA Kit (Promega). Siblings of these plants were subjected to the phenotypic assays in this study. We ordered Macrogen Japan to prepare paired-end libraries using a Nextera Flex DNA library Prep Kit (Illumina) and to sequence the libraries using the Illumina NovaSeq X platform. NGS genome data of Col-0 was obtained from our previous study37. The subsequent processes to analyze the NGS data, including trimming of low-quality and adapter sequences in the reads, mapping of the reads to the reference chloroplast and mitochondrial genome sequences (AP000423.1 and BK010421.1, respectively), and SNP calling, were conducted using Geneious Prime v2024.0.7. We filtered out inadequately mapped reads with mapping identities ≤ 97% or alignment cover rates ≤80%. Finally, we listed base positions where the difference between the substitution rate of a T3 sample and the average substitution rate of the two Col-0 samples was ≥ 5%.

Plant material and cultivation conditions

To establish genome-edited lines, seedlings of A. thaliana ecotype Col-0, and T2 and T3 plants were grown on 1/2 Murashige-Skoog (MS) agar plates for 2-3 weeks before being transferred to Jiffy-7 (Jiffy Products International). The plants were grown under 22 °C under long-day conditions (a daylight/dark period of 16/8 h). T1 seeds were sown on 1/2 MS agar plates containing 125 mg L-1 Claforan, and T1 plants were grown under similar conditions. The composition of the 1/2 MS plates was described previously41. For the phenotyping assays, the seeds of Arabidopsis were sown in a 1:1 mixture of MetroMix (Metromix 350, Hyponex) and vermiculite, and cultivated in several environment-controlled growth chambers. The growth chambers were operated with a daylight/dark period of 10/14 h, and photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) in the daylight period was set to 150 μmol photons m-2 s-1. Air temperature and relative humidity were set to a constant 22 °C under normal conditions, and to 25 °C or 30 °C under high-temperature cultivation, with relative humidity maintained at 60% in all cases. Chamber CO2 concentrations were set to low concentration (286 µmol mol-1 ± 14), ambient concentration (381 ± 14 μmol mol−1), and high concentration (549 ± 23 μmol mol−1). To eliminate potential biases arising from positional differences within the growth chamber, the plants were repositioned every other day in a randomized manner to ensure uniform growth conditions.

Plant growth

Plants were cultivated under three different CO₂ conditions: low, ambient, and high CO₂ concentration. For early-stage growth determinations, plants were harvested after 25 days at both ambient and high CO₂ concentrations, whereas under low CO₂ conditions, harvesting was performed at 32 days. For later-stage growth determinations, plants were harvested after 48 days at all CO₂ concentrations. During harvest, all above-ground tissues were separated, and the leaves were scanned using a scanner. Leaf area was measured using ImageJ software. After scanning, all above-ground tissues were collected and dried at 60 °C for 48 h to determine their dry weight. Below-ground tissues were carefully uprooted without damaging the root systems, rinsed in running tap water, and gently rubbed to remove any soil residues. Cleaned root tissues were then dried at 60 °C for 48 h, and their dry weight was measured.

Photosynthetic responses to CO2 concentrations

The gas exchange rate and chlorophyll fluorescence were measured simultaneously in fully expanded young leaves attached to 6- to 8-week-old plants grown at ambient CO2 concentration with a portable gas exchange system (LI-6400XT, LI-6400-40 leaf chamber fluorometer, LI-COR). The CO2 assimilation rate, stomatal conductance, and intercellular CO2 concentration, together with chlorophyll fluorescence parameters including electron transport rate at Photosystem II and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), were measured at a PPFD of 1000 μmol photons m − 2 s − 1, with relative humidity of 60–70%, temperature of 25 °C, and CO2 concentrations of 50 µmol mol−1 to 1500 μmol mol−1. The CO₂ assimilation rate under ambient (21%) and low (2%) O₂ concentrations was also measured at a CO₂ concentration of 400 µmol mol⁻¹, with 60% relative humidity and a leaf chamber temperature maintained at 25 °C.

Rubisco, Rubisco activase, and photosynthetic pigments

Leaf tissues from the same leaf used for gas-exchange measurement were sampled immediately after gas exchange measurements, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. The Rubisco content was determined by formamide extraction of SDS-PAGE-separated, Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250–stained bands corresponding to the large and small subunits of Rubisco, with Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) as the standard42. The Rubisco activase content was quantified by immunoblotting of SDS-PAGE-separated proteins with Primary anti-activase antibody originally produced as described in the previous study43, and secondary anti-rabbit IgG HRP-linked antibody (Cytiva, NA934, Amersham ECL IgG, HRP-linked whole Ab from donkey, Polyclonal), on a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane43. Chlorophyll was extracted in 80% v/v acetone, and chlorophyll content was determined as described previously44.

Rubisco kinetics

The leaves were homogenized in extraction buffer containing 100 mM Bicine-NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM NaH2PO4, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 20 mM ascorbate, 4 mM amino-n-caproic acid, 0.8 mM benzamidine, 0.4% (w/v) bovine serum albumin, and 1% (w/v) polyvinylpolypyrrolidone, pH 8.0, using a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C. Rubisco in the supernatant was activated by pre-incubation with 15 mM MgCl2 and 5 mM NaHCO3 on ice for 15 min. Rubisco activity was determined at 25 °C using [14 C] NaHCO3 (specific activity, 37 MBq mmol-1) by assaying the incorporation of 14 C into acid-stable products, as described previously14. The reaction was started by the addition of activated Rubisco to a reaction mixture containing 100 mM Bicine-NaOH, 20 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 0.5 mM RuBP, 1.0 W-A units carbonic anhydrase, and 0.5-20 mM NaH14CO3, pH 8.2. After 1 min, 1/2 volume of 1 N HCl was added to the reaction solution to stop the reaction. The acidified reaction mixes were dried, and acid-stable 14 C was measured by liquid scintillation. The Rubisco catalytic site concentration was determined by measuring the stoichiometric binding of [14 C] 2-carboxyarabinitol bisphosphate (specific activity, 1.85 GBq mmol-1) as described previously45. The in vitro kcat and Kc were calculated by direct fitting of the Rubisco activities at six different NaH14CO3 concentrations (0.5–20 mM) to the Michaelis-Menten equation using the KaleidaGraph data analysis software (Synergy Software).

To determine in vivo Rubisco CO2/O2 specificity (Sc/o), the gas exchange rates were measured with two different light intensities at a PPFD of 125 and 500 μmol photons m − 2 s − 1. The Sc/o was calculated using the CO2 compensation points independent of photorespiration estimated by the intersection of these two regression lines46.

The activation state of Rubisco was assayed using leaf samples collected from the same position as those used for photosynthesis measurements and stored in liquid nitrogen and subsequently in a deep freezer43.

Protein purification, cryo-EM specimen preparation, and data collection

Col-0, M309I, and D397N Rubisco were purified from leaves in Col-0 and chloroplast-genome-edited A. thaliana as described in the previous studies for rice Rubisco47, with the following modifications. Rubisco fractions were applied twice to a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200 prep grade column (Cytiva). In the final step of the gel filtration, we used a buffer containing 80 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 8.0 at 20 °C), 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT. The purified enzyme was concentrated by centrifugation (Vivaspin 20, MWCO: 50,000, Sartorius) up to 15 mg mL-1 for Rubisco, frozen with liquid N2, and stored at − 80 °C until the cryo-EM experiments. The amount of Rubisco was estimated spectrophotometrically by assuming an extinction coefficient of 1.67 absorbance units for 1 mg mL-1 at 280 nm.

A 3 µL aliquot of Rubisco (1 mg mL–1) in the buffer containing 80 mM Hepes-KOH pH 8.0, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, and 50 mM ammonium sulfate was applied to Quantifoil Cu R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grids (200 mesh) and frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot IV system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4 °C and 100 % humidity, 3 s blotting time, 0 blot force, 5 s waiting time). The grids were inserted into a CRYO ARM 300 transmission electron microscopy (JEOL Ltd. Japan) equipped with a cold field-emission electron gun operated at 300 kV and an Ω-type energy filter with a 20 eV slit width. Cryo-EM images were recorded with a K3 direct electron detector camera (Gatan, USA) at a nominal magnification of × 60,000, corresponding to an image pixel size of 0.87 Å with a setting defocus range from − 0.7 to − 2.2 μm, using Serial-EM48. The holes were detected using YoneoLocr49. Movie frames were recorded in CDS counting mode with a total exposure of 3 s and a total dose of 70-80 e–/Å2, which varies due to a slight decay in the beam intensity from the cold field emission electron gun over several hours between its flashing. Each movie was fractionated into 40 frames. A total number of 2732, 3258, and 3509 movies were collected for Col-0, M309I, and D397N of the Rubisco, respectively.

Cryo-EM image processing and model building

The process of single particle analysis of the Col-0 Rubisco was performed using RELION 4.050. Image processing procedures of Col-0 Rubisco are described in Supplementary Fig. S11. After performed motion correction to align all micrographs, followed by the estimation of parameters of the contrast transfer function (CTF), the selected particles were extracted into a box of 80 × 80 pixels with binning × 4. A total of 183 micrographs with resolution lower than 5 Å were excluded. The coordinate data used for particle picking (1,487,277) was obtained by BoxNet’s particle picking of Warp51 performed during data collection. 1,214,112 particle images from good 2D class average images were selected for the initial 3D model with C1 symmetry into three classes. After the initial 3D model, a good initial volume class with 737,173 particles was re-extracted into a box of 320 × 320 pixels (unbinned). After 3D refinement with C1 symmetry, CTF refinement and Polish process, particle images were performed 3D refinement with D4 symmetry. Following 3D refinement with D4 symmetry, the map resolution reached 1.76 Å [the Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143].

The process of single particle analysis of the M309I and D397N Rubisco was also performed using RELION 4.0 in the same way as for Col-0 Rubisco (Supplementary Figs. S12, S13, respectively)50. Totals of 256 and 219 micrographs with resolution lower than 5 Å were excluded for M309I and D397N, respectively. Particle picking (2,074,623 for M309I and 2,302,408 for D397N) was initially obtained by BoxNet’s particle picking of Warp51. In M309I and D397N, following 3D refinement with D4 symmetry, the map resolutions reached 1.75 and 1.74 Å, respectively (FSC = 0.143). The models were built using the crystal structure of sulfate-bound rice Rubisco (PDB code 6KYI14) as the initial model. In this study, cryo-EM density maps of Col-0, M309I, and D397N were obtained using the same protocol, yielding nearly identical resolutions of 1.76, 1.75, and 1.74 Å, respectively. Since the average density maps are normalized within the mask during image analysis, they can be compared at the same contour level. We also compared the cryo-EM density and crystallographic electron density of Rubisco structures from green algae, red algae, spinach, and rice (PDB IDs: 1IR2, 1IWA, 1WDD, 3AXK, 3AXM, 6KYI, and 6KYJ), which we had previously determined. Based on these comparisons, we fitted the structures using a 0.040 contour level, examined blurred regions at 0.026, and considered not resolved areas with no density at 0.026. When comparing structures in the paper, we carefully evaluated the density maps of Col-0, M309I, and D397N at the same contour levels to ensure consistency. For a comprehensive discussion on the contour level of cryo-EM maps, please refer to a previous paper52. After the initial models were manually fitted into the map using UCSF Chimera ver. 1.1653 and modified in Coot ver. 0.9.8.9654, real-space refinement was performed in PHENIX ver. 1.20.155 and reciprocal-space refinement against unsharpened half maps was performed in Servalcat ver. 0.4.126. Occupancy refinement of the RbcS1A- or RBCS2B/RBCS3B-specific residues were performed using Servalcat ver. 0.4.12656. The refinement was carried out under the condition that all RBCS1A-specific amino acids shared the same occupancy value, and similarly for RBCS2B/RBCS3B-specific amino acids. To achieve convergence of occupancy values, 30 refinement cycles were applied. As a result, the occupancy for RbcS1A ranged from 0.45 to 0.51 and for RbcS2B/3B from 0.49 to 0.55 across all eight RbcS subunits, indicating no significant bias in amino acid occupancy. The model was validated using MolProbity ver. 4.5.257 in PHENIX ver. 1.20.155, and this cycle was repeated several times. Cryo-EM data collection, refinement, and validation statistics are summarized in Data S6.

The stained bands were scanned and processed using ImageJ ver. 1.53q58. Figures were prepared using UCSF Chimera ver. 1.1653, CLUSTALX ver. 2.059, ESPript ver. 3.060 (https://espript.ibcp.fr), and PyMOL ver. 2.5.0 (Schrödinger, LLC, USA).

Statistical analysis

The significance of variations between means of multiple lines and the control group was evaluated by Dunnett’s two-tailed test. Multiplicity-adjusted p-values were calculated to control the family-wise error rate. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism v. 8.0.1 software.
