A Complete Classification of 2-Linear Neighborhood Complexes
Mohammed Rafiq Namiq

Abstract
. The neighborhood complex 𝒩 ⁡ ( G ) \mathcal{N}(G) and the dominance complex 𝒟 ⁡ ( G ) \mathcal{D}(G) are fundamental simplicial complexes associated with a graph G G . We characterize precisely when the Stanley–Reisner ring k ⁡ [ 𝒩 ⁡ ( G ) ] k[\mathcal{N}(G)] admits a 2 2 -linear resolution, thereby answering an open question posed by Fröberg. We prove that this occurs if and only if G G is neighborhood conformal and its common neighbor graph is chordal. Equivalently, G G is a bipartite graph whose only induced cycles are 4 4 -cycles. As a consequence, we show that Katzman’s lower bound becomes an equality for this class, yielding reg ⁡ ( S / I ⁡ ( G ) ) = im ⁡ ( G ) \operatorname{reg}(S/I(G))=\operatorname{im}(G) . Using recent results on glued clique complexes, we derive explicit combinatorial formulas for the exact graded Betti numbers of these neighborhood complexes. Finally, utilizing combinatorial Alexander duality, we obtain a corresponding classification of Cohen–Macaulay dominance complexes, and prove that the dominance complex of a graph without isolated vertices admits a 2 2 -linear resolution if and only if the graph is a star graph.

1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite simple graph. The neighborhood complex
\mathcal{N}(G)
, originally introduced by Lovász to prove Kneser’s conjecture
[
7
]
, is defined as the abstract simplicial complex whose faces are all subsets
A\subseteq V
such that all vertices in
A
share a common neighbor in
G
. Formally, it is written as:

\mathcal{N}(G)=\{A\subseteq V\mid\exists v\in V\text{ such that }A\subseteq N_{G}(v)\}.

The topological connectivity and homotopy type of
\mathcal{N}(G)
have been extensively studied to provide lower bounds on chromatic numbers and to relate the complex to other combinatorial structures, such as dominance complexes
[
8
]
.

The dominance complex
\mathcal{D}(G)
, introduced by Ehrenborg and Hetyei
[
2
]
, encodes the dominating sets of a graph and plays a central role in the interplay between domination theory, simplicial topology, and combinatorial algebra. Together, neighborhood and dominance complexes form fundamental objects of study in combinatorial commutative algebra, where their structural properties are evaluated via Stanley–Reisner theory and its associated graded rings.

The algebraic study of simplicial complexes is carried out through Stanley–Reisner theory, which translates combinatorial information into algebraic invariants of graded rings.
One of the central problems in this area is the characterization of simplicial complexes whose Stanley–Reisner rings admit linear resolutions.
Fröberg’s classical theorem characterizes graphs whose edge ideals have linear resolutions in terms of the chordality of their complement graphs
[
3
]
.
Later, Eagon and Reiner proved that a squarefree monomial ideal has a linear resolution if and only if the Stanley–Reisner ring of its Alexander dual complex is Cohen–Macaulay
[
1
]
.
This result established Alexander duality as a fundamental tool in the study of linear resolutions and Cohen–Macaulay simplicial complexes.

Neighborhood and dominance complexes are also closely related to threshold graphs and shifted complexes. Klivans showed that several simplicial complexes associated with graphs are shifted precisely when the underlying graph is threshold
[
6
]
.
In particular, he proved that

\mathcal{N}(G)=\mathcal{D}(G)

if and only if
G
is a threshold graph.
This characterization reveals a remarkable connection between neighborhood complexes, dominance complexes, and one of the most important graph classes in extremal combinatorics.
More recently, Matsushita and Wakatsuki established a combinatorial form of Alexander duality connecting neighborhood and dominance complexes:

\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G}).

This identity
[
8
]
provides a direct relationship between the two constructions and creates a natural bridge for transferring algebraic and topological information.
Together with the theorem of Eagon and Reiner, it offers a powerful framework for studying Cohen–Macaulay dominance complexes through the homological properties of neighborhood complexes.

Motivated by these developments, Fröberg recently began a systematic investigation of the Stanley–Reisner rings associated with neighborhood complexes
[
4
]
.
Using the theory of fat forests, he proved that
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution if and only if
\mathcal{N}(G)
is a fat forest.
Although this result provides a complete simplicial characterization, it does not describe the underlying graph structures that produce such complexes.
This naturally leads to the question of determining which graph theoretic conditions on
G
guarantee that its neighborhood complex admits a
2
-linear resolution.

At the same time, recent work has made it possible to compute homological invariants of these complexes more explicitly.
Namiq
[
9
]
showed that fat forests can be realized as glued chordal clique complexes obtained by successively attaching simplices along common faces.
This construction yields explicit formulas for the
f
-vectors, Hilbert series, and graded Betti numbers of the associated Stanley–Reisner rings, providing an effective combinatorial framework for homological computations.

The main objective of this paper is to resolve the graph theoretic classification problem arising from Fröberg’s characterization and to investigate its algebraic and topological consequences.
To achieve this, we introduce the common neighbor graph and the notion of neighborhood conformality.
These concepts allow us to translate the homological condition of
2
-linearity into explicit graph theoretic conditions.

Organization of the paper.
Section 2 reviews the algebraic and graph theoretic concepts used throughout the paper. In Section 3, we develop the notion of neighborhood conformality and establish the tools needed for our classification.
Section 4 contains the main theorem, which characterizes graphs whose neighborhood complexes admit
2
-linear resolutions.
We prove that this occurs precisely when the graph is bipartite and all of its induced cycles are squares.
We then apply this characterization to several important graph families and present examples that demonstrate its limitations, including grid graphs and strongly regular graphs.
Section 5 investigates the resulting homological consequences.
We show that this class of graphs satisfies

\operatorname{reg}(S/I(G))=\operatorname{im}(G),

and we derive explicit formulas for the graded Betti numbers using the theory of glued clique complexes.
Section 6 combines our classification with the duality of Matsushita and Wakatsuki to obtain corresponding characterizations for dominance complexes. We also examine the special case of threshold graphs, where the relationship between neighborhood complexes, dominance complexes, and Alexander duality leads to additional structural and homological symmetries. Consequently, for a threshold graph
G
, we show that
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
admits a linear resolution if and only if
G
is a star forest. Finally, we prove that for any graph
G
without isolated vertices,
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
admits a strictly
2
-linear resolution if and only if
G
is a star graph
K_{1,n}
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we review the fundamental topological, algebraic, and graph theoretic definitions required for the subsequent classifications.

2.1.
Simplicial Complexes and Alexander Duality

Let
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
be a finite vertex set.
An
abstract simplicial complex

\Delta
on
V
is a collection of subsets of
V
that is closed under inclusion (i.e., if
F\in\Delta
and
G\subseteq F
, then
G\in\Delta
).
The elements of
\Delta
are called
faces
. The dimension of a face
F
is defined as
|F|-1
, and the dimension of
\Delta
is the maximum dimension of its faces.
A maximal face under inclusion is called a
facet
.
A subset
N\subseteq V
is a
non-face
of
\Delta
if
N\notin\Delta
.
The minimal non-faces dictate the algebraic constraints of the complex.

For any simplicial complex
\Delta
on the vertex set
V
, its combinatorial
Alexander dual
, denoted
\Delta^{\vee}
, is defined on the identical vertex set
V
by taking the complements of the non-faces of the original complex:

\Delta^{\vee}=\{V\setminus F\mid F\notin\Delta\}.

2.2.
Stanley–Reisner Rings and Linear Resolutions

Let
S=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the standard graded polynomial ring over a field
k
.
The
Stanley–Reisner ideal

I_{\Delta}
associated with a simplicial complex
\Delta
is the ideal generated by squarefree monomials corresponding to the minimal non-faces of
\Delta
:

I_{\Delta}=\bigl(x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{m}}\mid\{v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}},\dots,v_{i_{m}}\}\text{ is a minimal non-face of }\Delta\bigr).

The
Stanley–Reisner ring
of
\Delta
is the quotient ring
k[\Delta]=S/I_{\Delta}
.

The ring
k[\Delta]
possesses a minimal graded free resolution of the form:

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{\beta_{p,j}}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{\beta_{1,j}}\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow k[\Delta]\longrightarrow 0,

where the exponents
\beta_{i,j}(k[\Delta])
are the
graded Betti numbers
.
The
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity
of
k[\Delta]
is defined as
\operatorname{reg}(k[\Delta])=\max\{j-i\mid\beta_{i,j}\neq 0\}
.

The ring
k[\Delta]
(and equivalently its defining ideal
I_{\Delta}
) is said to admit a
d
-linear resolution
if the ideal is generated in a single uniform degree
d
, and all syzygies occur in purely sequential degrees, such that
\beta_{i,i+j}(k[\Delta])=0
for all
j\neq d
.
In this paper, we specifically study
2
-linear resolutions, meaning
I_{\Delta}
is generated by quadrics.
A standard graded
k
-algebra is
Cohen–Macaulay
if its depth equals its Krull dimension.
The celebrated Eagon–Reiner theorem links these algebraic properties via topological duality.

Theorem 2.1

(Eagon–Reiner
[
1
]
)
.

A Stanley–Reisner ideal
I_{\Delta}
admits a linear resolution if and only if the Stanley–Reisner ring of its Alexander dual,
k[\Delta^{\vee}]
, is Cohen–Macaulay over
k
.

2.3.
Graph Associated Complexes

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite simple graph.
The
neighborhood complex

\mathcal{N}(G)
is the simplicial complex on
V
where a subset
S\subseteq V
forms a face if and only if all vertices in
S
share at least one common neighbor in
G
.
A subset
D\subseteq V
is a
dominating set
if every vertex
v\in V\setminus D
is adjacent to at least one vertex in
D
.
The
dominance complex

\mathcal{D}(G)
is the simplicial complex on
V
whose faces are the exact complements of the dominating sets of
G
.
The
clique complex

Cl(G)
is the simplicial complex whose faces correspond to the subsets of vertices that induce complete subgraphs (cliques) in
G
.
A simplicial complex whose minimal non-faces consist of subsets of cardinality
2
is called a
flag complex
.
Every flag complex is identically the clique complex of its
1
-skeleton.

2.4.
Graph Structural Classes

The
complement graph

\overline{G}
has the identical vertex set
V
, where two distinct vertices are adjacent in
\overline{G}
if and only if they are not adjacent in
G
.
A graph is
chordal
if every induced cycle of length
4
or greater contains a chord (an edge connecting two non-adjacent vertices of the cycle).
A graph is
co-chordal
if its complement is a chordal graph.
A graph is
bipartite
if its vertex set can be partitioned into two disjoint independent sets.
Equivalently, a graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no odd cycles.

A
threshold graph
is a graph that can be constructed from a single isolated vertex by repeatedly adding either an isolated vertex or a universal vertex (a vertex connected to all existing vertices).
Threshold graphs belong to a structured subclass of chordal graphs that excludes induced cycles of length
4
or greater (
C_{\geq 4}
) and induced paths of length
4
(
P_{4}
).

A
chain graph
(also known as a difference graph) is a bipartite graph
G=(V_{1}\sqcup V_{2},E)
in which the open neighborhoods of the vertices within each respective partition are totally ordered by set inclusion.
Mathematically, the vertices of
V_{1}
can be ordered as
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}
such that
N_{G}(x_{1})\subseteq N_{G}(x_{2})\subseteq\dots\subseteq N_{G}(x_{m})
.

For any integer
n\geq 1
, the
ladder graph

L_{n}
is defined as the Cartesian product of two path graphs,
L_{n}\cong P_{2}\times P_{n}
.
Geometrically, it consists of two parallel paths on
n
vertices connected by
n
corresponding edges, equivalent for
n\geq 2
to a linear concatenation of exactly
n-1
squares (
C_{4}
).

A graph
G
is
strongly regular
with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
if every vertex has degree
k
, every adjacent pair has
\lambda
common neighbors, and every non-adjacent pair has
\mu
common neighbors.
It is non-trivial if it is neither a disjoint union of cliques nor its complement.

3.
The Algebraic and Structural Translation

To classify the homological properties of
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
, we translate the geometric structure of the neighborhood complex into precise graph theoretic conditions that encode its faces and
1
-skeleton.

Definition 3.1

(Common Neighbor Graph)
.

For any finite simple graph
G=(V,E)
, we define the
common neighbor graph

CN(G)
as the graph on the vertex set
V
where an edge
\{u,w\}
exists if and only if
u
and
w
possess at least one common neighbor in
G
.
Formally,
\{u,w\}\in E(CN(G))\iff N_{G}(u)\cap N_{G}(w)\neq\emptyset
.

By definition, the
1
-dimensional faces (edges) of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{N}(G)
are exactly the pairs of vertices sharing a common neighbor.
Consequently,
CN(G)
is precisely the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{N}(G)
.

Definition 3.2

(Neighborhood Conformal)
.

A finite simple graph
G
is
neighborhood conformal
if, for every subset
S\subseteq V
, the following holds: if every pair of vertices in
S
shares a common neighbor in
G
, then the entire subset
S
shares a single common neighbor in
G
.
Mathematically, if
N_{G}(u)\cap N_{G}(w)\neq\emptyset
for all
u,w\in S
, then
\bigcap_{v\in S}N_{G}(v)\neq\emptyset
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph. The Stanley–Reisner ring
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution if and only if
G
is neighborhood conformal and the common neighbor graph
CN(G)
is chordal.

Proof.

We establish the equivalence by proving both directions independently.
Let
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}
denote the Stanley–Reisner ideal of the neighborhood complex.
By the Stanley–Reisner correspondence, the minimal generators of
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}
correspond bijectively to the minimal non-faces of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{N}(G)
.

(\Rightarrow)
Assume
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution. By definition of a linear resolution, the ideal
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}
must be generated in degree
2
.
Consequently, all minimal non-faces of
\mathcal{N}(G)
have a cardinality of exactly
2
, meaning
\mathcal{N}(G)
is a flag complex.
A flag complex is identically the clique complex of its
1
-skeleton.
Since the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{N}(G)
is precisely
CN(G)
, we have the equality
\mathcal{N}(G)=Cl(CN(G))
.
This equality states that a subset of vertices forms a face in
\mathcal{N}(G)
(sharing a single common neighbor) if and only if all pairs within the subset form edges in
CN(G)
(sharing pairwise common neighbors).
This is the exact definition of
G
being neighborhood conformal.
Furthermore, because
\mathcal{N}(G)
is a flag complex, its minimal non-faces are precisely the non-edges of
CN(G)
.
Thus, the ideal is exactly the edge ideal of the complement graph:
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}=I(\overline{CN(G)})
.
Since we assumed
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}
has a linear resolution, Fröberg’s theorem
[
3
]
implies that the common neighbor graph
CN(G)
is chordal.

(\Leftarrow)
Conversely, assume
G
is neighborhood conformal and
CN(G)
is chordal. The neighborhood conformal condition implies the equality
\mathcal{N}(G)=Cl(CN(G))
. Because
\mathcal{N}(G)
is the clique complex of a graph, it is a flag complex. Consequently, its minimal non-faces are
1
-dimensional edges, meaning its Stanley–Reisner ideal
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}
is generated by quadrics. Specifically, these quadrics correspond to the non-edges of
CN(G)
, establishing the algebraic identity
I_{\mathcal{N}(G)}=I(\overline{CN(G)})
.

Because
CN(G)
is assumed to be chordal, Fröberg’s theorem guarantees that the edge ideal of its complement,
I(\overline{CN(G)})
, admits a linear resolution.
Since we already established that this ideal is generated in degree
2
, its linear resolution is
2
-linear, completing the proof.
∎

4.
The Complete Graph Theoretic Characterization

We now apply Theorem
3.3
to obtain a complete characterization of graphs whose neighborhood complexes admit
2
-linear resolutions.

Theorem 4.1
.

The Stanley–Reisner ring
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution if and only if
G
is a bipartite graph whose only induced cycles are
C_{4}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.
By Theorem
3.3
, the common neighbor graph
CN(G)
is chordal and
G
is neighborhood conformal.
We claim that
G
is triangle free. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
G
contains a triangle (
C_{3}
).
Let
K
be a maximal clique in
G
such that
|K|\geq 3
.
Consider any pair of distinct vertices
u,w\in K
. Because
|K|\geq 3
, there exists a third vertex
z\in K
distinct from
u
and
w
.
Since
K
is a clique,
z
is adjacent to both
u
and
w
.
Thus, every pair of vertices in
K
pairwise shares a common neighbor within
K
.
Consequently,
K
forms a clique in the common neighbor graph
CN(G)
.
By the neighborhood conformal property, there must exist a single common neighbor
v^{*}\in V
adjacent to every single vertex in
K
.
Because a graph is simple and a vertex cannot be adjacent to itself,
v^{*}\notin K
.
However, since
v^{*}
is adjacent to all vertices in
K
, the union
K\cup\{v^{*}\}
forms a clique of size
|K|+1
in
G
.
This contradicts the maximality of
K
. Therefore,
G
must be triangle free.

Next, suppose
G
contains a subset of vertices
S\subseteq V
inducing a cycle
C_{k}
for
k\geq 5
.
Within
CN(G)
, the connections between vertices in
S
occur at distance
2
.
We consider the following three specific sub-cases based on the cycle length
k
:

Case 1:
k
is odd (
k\geq 5
).
The distance
2
connections of an odd cycle traverse all vertices, forming an induced cycle of length
k
in
CN(G)[S]
.
Because
k\geq 5
,
CN(G)[S]
contains a hole of length
\geq 5
.
Because
CN(G)
must be chordal, this hole must be triangulated by external vertices
v\in V\setminus S
.
Triangulating an induced cycle of length
\geq 5
necessitates the formation of at least one
K_{3}
in
CN(G)
.
The vertices of this
K_{3}
pairwise share common neighbors.
However, because
S
is chordless in
G
, no single vertex can connect to three alternating vertices of
C_{k}
without violating the chordless nature of
S
.
This yields an empty global intersection (
\bigcap N_{G}(v)=\emptyset
), forming a non-conformal core and contradicting the neighborhood conformal property.

Case 2:
k
is even (
k\geq 8
).
The distance
2
connections decompose into two disjoint induced cycles of length
k/2
.
Since
k/2\geq 4
, the induced subgraph
CN(G)[S]
contains a hole of length at least
4
.
Enforcing chordality requires triangulating this hole, which necessarily produces a
K_{3}
in
CN(G)
.
This again leads to the same empty global intersection contradiction as in Case 1.

Case 3:
k=6
.
The distance
2
connections form
K_{3}\sqcup K_{3}
, which is chordal.
However, consider one triangle in
CN(G)
, say
S^{\prime}=\{x_{1},x_{3},x_{5}\}
.
Each pair of vertices in
S^{\prime}
shares a common neighbor (namely
x_{2}
,
x_{4}
, and
x_{6}
).
However, there is no single vertex in
G
adjacent to all three simultaneously, since such a vertex would introduce an internal chord and contradict the assumption that
C_{6}
is induced.
Hence
S^{\prime}
forms a non-conformal core, violating neighborhood conformality.

Since
G
contains neither triangles nor induced odd cycles of length at least five, it is bipartite.
Consequently, the only possible induced cycles are squares
C_{4}
.

Conversely, assume that
G
is bipartite and every induced cycle is a
C_{4}
.
Let
V(G)=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
be its bipartition. Two distinct vertices
u,v\in V(G)
share a common neighbor if and only if there exists a path of length
2
between them.
In a bipartite graph, paths between vertices in different parts have odd length, while paths between vertices in the same part have even length.
Hence,
u
and
v
can share a common neighbor only when they lie in the same partition.
Consequently, the common neighbor graph decomposes as

CN(G)=CN(G)[V_{1}]\sqcup CN(G)[V_{2}].

We first show that
CN(G)
is chordal.
Suppose for contradiction that it contains an induced cycle
C_{k}
with
k\geq 4
on vertices
S=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}\}
(indices taken modulo
k
).
By the decomposition from above, all vertices of
S
lie in a single part of the bipartition, say
V_{1}
.
Since adjacent sequence vertices
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
form an edge in
CN(G)
, each consecutive pair shares a common neighbor
y_{i}\in V_{2}
in
G
.
This produces a closed alternating walk in
G
,

W=x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\dots,x_{k},y_{k},x_{1}.

Because
W
alternates between
k
vertices in
V_{1}
and
k
vertices in
V_{2}
, its total length is exactly
2k
.
Since
k\geq 4
, the length of the walk is
\geq 8
.
We now show that
W
has no chords in
G
.
Because
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are independent sets, no edges can exist between any
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, nor between any
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
.
Moreover, since
S
is an induced cycle in
CN(G)
, nonconsecutive vertices
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
share no common neighbor in
G
, which forces all
y_{i}
to be distinct and prevents any
y_{i}
from being adjacent to nonconsecutive vertices of the sequence.
Hence
W
is an induced cycle in
G
of length
2k\geq 8
.
This contradicts the assumption that
G
has no induced cycles other than
C_{4}
.
Therefore,
CN(G)
contains no induced cycle of length at least
4
, so it is chordal.

To prove neighborhood conformality, let
S^{\prime}
be a clique in
CN(G)
.
By bipartiteness,
S^{\prime}
lies entirely in one part, say
V_{1}
, so every neighborhood
N_{G}(v)
for
v\in S^{\prime}
is contained in
V_{2}
.
Since
S^{\prime}
is a clique in
CN(G)
, every pair of vertices in
S^{\prime}
has a common neighbor in
G
.
It remains to show that there is a single vertex in
G
adjacent to all vertices of
S^{\prime}
, that is,

\bigcap_{v\in S^{\prime}}N_{G}(v)\neq\emptyset.

Assume for contradiction that no such common neighbor exists. Then there exists a minimal subset
S^{*}\subseteq S^{\prime}
with empty total intersection.
Because every pair in
S^{\prime}
successfully shares a common neighbor, minimality implies
|S^{*}|\geq 3
.
Take distinct vertices
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in S^{*}
.

By minimality, each pair in
S^{*}
admits a common neighbor after removing one vertex.
Thus we obtain vertices
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in V_{2}
such that:

•

y_{1}
is adjacent to
x_{2},x_{3}
but not
x_{1}
(otherwise
y_{1}
would be a common neighbor for the entirety of
S^{*}
),

•

y_{2}
is adjacent to
x_{1},x_{3}
but not
x_{2}
,

•

y_{3}
is adjacent to
x_{1},x_{2}
but not
x_{3}
.

Because
y_{1},y_{2},
and
y_{3}
are not adjacent to
x_{1},x_{2},
and
x_{3}
respectively, these conditions force
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
to be distinct.
Consider the six vertices
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
such that
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}\subset V_{1}
and
\{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\}\subset V_{2}
.
The adjacencies yield the cycle

x_{1},y_{2},x_{3},y_{1},x_{2},y_{3},x_{1}.

Because
G
is bipartite, there are no edges within
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
or within
\{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\}
, and the defining non-adjacencies
x_{i}\not\sim y_{i}
ensure that this cycle has no chords.
Hence it is an induced
C_{6}
, contradicting the assumption that the only allowed induced cycles in
G
are
C_{4}
.
Therefore, the assumption is false, and we conclude

\bigcap_{v\in S^{\prime}}N_{G}(v)\neq\emptyset,

so
G
is neighborhood conformal.
Therefore
CN(G)
is chordal and
G
is neighborhood conformal. By Theorem
3.3
,
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.
∎

The following corollaries show that our main theorem recovers several recent results of Fröberg
[
4
]
and identifies new classes of graphs with
2
-linear neighborhood complexes.

Corollary 4.2
.

The cycle graph
C_{4}
is the unique cycle graph whose neighborhood complex admits a
2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

Let
C_{n}
satisfy that
k[\mathcal{N}(C_{n})]
has a
2
-linear resolution. By Theorem
4.1
,
C_{n}
is bipartite and all induced cycles are
C_{4}
. Hence
n
is even and
n<5
, so
n=4
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Every forest
T
satisfies that
k[\mathcal{N}(T)]
has a
2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

Forests are bipartite graphs with no cycles, so they trivially satisfy the condition that all induced cycles are
C_{4}
. The result follows from Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
G
be a chain graph. Then
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
has a
2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

Chain graphs are bipartite with nested neighborhoods, which forbid induced
2K_{2}
configurations and hence exclude induced cycles of length at least
6
.
Thus all induced cycles are
C_{4}
. The result follows directly.
∎

Corollary 4.5

(Compare to
[
4
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.4]
)
.

Paths
P_{n}
and star graphs
K_{1,n}
satisfy that
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

Both are forests, so the result follows immediately from the previous corollary.
∎

Corollary 4.6

(Compare to
[
4
, Theorem 3.12]
)
.

For the ladder graph
L_{n}\cong P_{2}\times P_{n}
, the ring
k[\mathcal{N}(L_{n})]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

Ladder graphs are bipartite and any longer cycle necessarily contains a chord via a rung edge, so all induced cycles are
C_{4}
.
The claim follows.
∎

Corollary 4.7

(Compare to
[
4
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

For the complete bipartite graph
K_{m,n}
, the ring
k[\mathcal{N}(K_{m,n})]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

In a bipartite graph, induced cycles have length at least
4
.
In
K_{m,n}
, any longer cycle contains chords between the two parts, so the only induced cycles are
C_{4}
.
∎

Corollary 4.8

(False Twin Inflations)
.

Let
G
be a graph satisfying Theorem
4.1
.
The operation of false twin multiplication (replacing a vertex
v
with a set of non-adjacent vertices sharing the identical open neighborhood as
v
) preserves bipartiteness and does not introduce induced cycles of length greater than
4
.
Consequently, the resulting graphs also admit
2
-linear neighborhood complexes.

Example 4.9

(The
3\times 3
Grid Graph Obstruction)
.

Although ladder graphs admit
2
-linear neighborhood complexes, Theorem
4.1
shows this fails for
m\times n
grids with
m,n\geq 3
.
The
3\times 3
grid
G_{3\times 3}
is bipartite, but its four corner vertices form an induced
C_{4}
in
CN(G_{3\times 3})
with no chords, while the outer boundary forms an induced
C_{8}
.
Hence
CN(G_{3\times 3})
is not chordal, so the neighborhood complex is not
2
-linear.

Remark 4.10
.

Let
G
be a non-trivial strongly regular graph with
\lambda>0
and
\mu>0
.
Then every pair of vertices has a common neighbor, so
CN(G)
is complete and hence chordal.
However,

\bigcap_{v\in V}N_{G}(v)=\emptyset,

so
G
fails neighborhood conformality.
By Theorem
3.3
, no such graph admits a
2
-linear neighborhood complex.

5.
Homological Equality and Exact Betti Numbers

The
2
-linear structure leads to sharp homological consequences for both the edge ideal and the neighborhood complex.
Katzman’s lemma
[
5
]
gives the general bound
\operatorname{reg}(S/I(G))\geq\operatorname{im}(G)
, where
\operatorname{im}(G)
denotes the induced matching number of
G
.
An induced matching is a set of pairwise disjoint edges with no additional edges connecting distinct edges in the set, and
\operatorname{im}(G)
is the maximum cardinality of such a set.
In our case, the structural conditions force this inequality to become an equality.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G
be a graph such that
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution. Then

\operatorname{reg}(S/I(G))=\operatorname{im}(G).

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
,
G
is a bipartite graph whose only permitted induced cycles are squares (
C_{4}
).
Consequently,
G
contains no induced cycles of length
\geq 5
.
To analyze
\overline{G}
, observe that an induced cycle of length
k\geq 5
in
\overline{G}
corresponds to an induced anti-cycle
\overline{C_{k}}
in
G
.
We evaluate this in two distinct cases:

(1)

For
k=5
, we have
\overline{C_{5}}\cong C_{5}
, which cannot occur in
G
since bipartite graphs forbid odd cycles.

(2)

For any integer
k\geq 6
, the cycle graph
C_{k}
possesses an independence number of at least
3
.
Consequently, its complement
\overline{C_{k}}
contains a clique of size
3
(a triangle,
K_{3}
).
Because
G
is bipartite, it is triangle free, making it impossible for
G
to contain an induced
\overline{C_{k}}
for any
k\geq 6
.

Hence,
\overline{G}
has no induced cycles of length
\geq 5
.
Because neither
G
nor
\overline{G}
possesses induced cycles of length
5
or greater, both graphs are weakly chordal.
The homological equality follows directly from Woodroofe’s theorem
[
10
, Theorem 14]
, giving

\operatorname{reg}(S/I(G))=\operatorname{im}(G).

∎

Theorem 5.2

(Explicit Betti Numbers via Glued Complexes)
.

Let
G
be a graph such that
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.
Let
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{e}\}
denote the sizes of the maximal cliques of
CN(G)
ordered along a valid clique tree, and let
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{e-1}\}
denote the sizes of their sequential intersections.
Then the graded Betti numbers of
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
are given by
\beta_{0,0}=1
, all
\beta_{i,j}=0
for
j\neq i+1
, and for
i\geq 1
:

\beta_{i,i+1}(k[\mathcal{N}(G)])=\sum_{t=0}^{i+1}(-1)^{t-1}\binom{N_{r}-t}{i+1-t}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{e}\binom{n_{m}}{t}-\sum_{m=1}^{e-1}\binom{r_{m}}{t}\right),

where
N_{r}=\sum_{m=1}^{e}n_{m}-\sum_{m=1}^{e-1}r_{m}
.

Proof.

Since
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
possesses a
2
-linear resolution, Theorem
4.1
implies that
CN(G)
is chordal and
\mathcal{N}(G)
is a flag complex determined by its
1
-skeleton.
Hence, the maximal simplices of
\mathcal{N}(G)
correspond bijectively to the maximal cliques of
CN(G)
, possessing sizes
n_{1},\dots,n_{e}
, and sequential intersections of size
r_{1},\dots,r_{e-1}
.
This gives a total vertex count of:

N_{r}=\sum_{m=1}^{e}n_{m}-\sum_{m=1}^{e-1}r_{m}.

The chordal structure allows the complex to be realized topologically as a sequential gluing of cliques, so the Betti numbers reduce to the combinatorial skeleton formula of Namiq
[
9
, Theorem 4.3]
.
In the
2
-linear case, only the linear homological strand survives, so all
\beta_{i,j}
vanish for
j\neq i+1
.
Substituting
j=1
into the skeleton Betti formula yields:

\beta_{i,i+1}(k[\mathcal{N}(G)])=\sum_{t=0}^{i+1}(-1)^{t-1}\binom{N_{r}-t}{i+1-t}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{e}\binom{n_{m}}{t}-\sum_{m=1}^{e-1}\binom{r_{m}}{t}\right),

completing the computation.
∎

6.
Dominance Complexes, Alexander Duality, and Threshold Symmetries

The classification of neighborhood complexes immediately yields corresponding results for dominance complexes through combinatorial Alexander duality.

Lemma 6.1

(Matsushita–Wakatsuki
[
8
]
)
.

For any graph
G
, the dominance complex satisfies
\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})
.

Combining this identity with Theorem
4.1
gives a complete characterization of dominance duals with
2
-linear resolutions.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then
k[\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}]
admits a
2
-linear resolution if and only if
\overline{G}
is bipartite and all induced cycles are
C_{4}
.

Proof.

Using
\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})
, we reduce to
k[\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})]
. The equivalence follows directly by applying Theorem
4.1
to the complement graph
\overline{G}
.
∎

Remark 6.3

(Cohen–Macaulay Dominance Complexes and Higher Linearities)
.

By the Eagon–Reiner theorem
[
1
]
, Theorem
6.2
exactly characterizes which dominance complexes are Cohen–Macaulay via
2
-linear duals.
Graphs outside this class may still yield higher linearities, but these remain largely open.

We next describe a profound structural symmetry applicable to threshold graphs.

Lemma 6.4

(Klivans
[
6
]
)
.

A graph
G
is a threshold graph if and only if
\mathcal{D}(G)=\mathcal{N}(G)
.

Since threshold graphs are closed under complementation, Lemmas
6.1
and
6.4
imply the following symmetry.

Theorem 6.5
.

If
G
is threshold, then

\mathcal{N}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{D}(\overline{G}).

Proof.

Let
G
be a threshold graph. By Lemma
6.4
, we have
\mathcal{D}(G)=\mathcal{N}(G)
. Since the class of threshold graphs is closed under complementation,
\overline{G}
is also threshold, and hence
\mathcal{D}(\overline{G})=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})
. We now apply Lemma
6.1
, which states that

\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G}).

Substituting
\mathcal{D}(G)=\mathcal{N}(G)
yields

\mathcal{N}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{N}(\overline{G}),

establishing neighborhood symmetry. Substituting
\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})=\mathcal{D}(\overline{G})
instead gives

\mathcal{D}(G)^{\vee}=\mathcal{D}(\overline{G}),

which establishes dominance symmetry.
∎

Thus, for threshold graphs, neighborhood and dominance complexes coincide and Alexander duality corresponds exactly to graph complementation.
A direct homological consequence follows.

Corollary 6.6
.

If
G
is threshold, then
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
has a linear resolution if and only if
\mathcal{N}(\overline{G})
is Cohen–Macaulay.

We now turn to 2-linear resolutions of dominance complexes. In the threshold setting, the classification follows directly from the correspondence between neighborhood and dominance complexes.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
G
be a threshold graph. Then
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution if and only if
G
is a star forest (at most one star graph
K_{1,n}
and a set of isolated vertices).

Proof.

By Lemma
6.4
, because
G
is a threshold graph, we have the topological equality
\mathcal{D}(G)=\mathcal{N}(G)
.
Consequently, their Stanley–Reisner rings are isomorphic, reducing the problem to the
2
-linear resolution of
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
.

(\Rightarrow)
Assume
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution. By Theorem
4.1
,
G
must be a bipartite graph with no induced cycles larger than
C_{4}
.
However, threshold graphs are chordal, which forbids any induced cycles of length
4
or greater.
A bipartite graph (which contains no odd cycles) that is also chordal (which contains no cycles
\geq 4
) must be entirely cycle-free, meaning
G
is a forest.
Furthermore, threshold graphs forbid induced paths of length
4
(
P_{4}
).
A forest that is
P_{4}
-free mathematically collapses into a star forest consisting of at most one non-trivial star graph
K_{1,n}
and a set of isolated vertices.

(\Leftarrow)
Conversely, assume
G
is a star forest.
A star forest is a bipartite graph that does not have any cycles.
It trivially satisfies the structural conditions of Theorem
4.1
, implying that
k[\mathcal{N}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.
Because
G
is a threshold graph,
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
consequently admits a
2
-linear resolution.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

If
v
is an isolated vertex of
G
, then
N_{G}[v]=\{v\}
and the Stanley–Reisner ideal
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}
contains the linear generator
x_{v}
. Consequently, although
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}
may have a linear resolution, it cannot be generated uniformly in degree
2
. Thus the resolution is linear but not strictly
2
-linear.

The existence of a
2
-linear resolution for dominance complexes is highly restrictive, and in fact occurs only for a single graph family. To obtain a classification of strictly
2
-linear resolutions, isolated vertices must be excluded.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph without isolated vertices. Then
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
admits a strictly
2
-linear resolution if and only if
G
is a star graph
K_{1,n}
for some
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

By definition, the minimal non-faces of the dominance complex
\mathcal{D}(G)
correspond exactly to the closed neighborhoods of the vertices of
G
. Thus, the Stanley–Reisner ideal is generated by:

I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}=\bigl(\prod_{y\in N_{G}[v]}x_{y}\mid v\in V(G)\bigr).

(\Rightarrow)
Assume
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution. Then
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}
must be generated in degree
2
. For each vertex
v\in V(G)
, the generator

u_{v}=\prod_{y\in N_{G}[v]}x_{y}

has degree
2
if and only if
v
is a leaf (a vertex of degree
1
). Because
G
has no isolated vertices, every connected component must contain at least one edge.

Let
L
denote the set of leaves of
G
. For each
l\in L
, let
w_{l}
be its unique neighbor, yielding the quadratic generator

u_{l}=x_{l}x_{w_{l}}.

Since
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}
is generated by quadratics, every generator
u_{z}
corresponding to a non-leaf vertex
z
must be redundant. Thus
u_{l}\mid u_{z}
for some
l\in L
, equivalently

N_{G}[l]\subseteq N_{G}[z].

Because
l
is a leaf, this forces
z=w_{l}
. Consequently, every non-leaf vertex is the unique neighbor of at least one leaf, and

I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}=\bigl(x_{l}x_{w_{l}}\mid l\in L\bigr).

Therefore
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}
is the edge ideal of the graph

H=(V,\{\{l,w_{l}\}:l\in L\}),

which is a star forest. Since
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}=I(H)
has a linear resolution, Fröberg’s theorem
[
3
]
implies that
\overline{H}
is chordal. A star forest has chordal complement if and only if it contains no induced
2K_{2}
. Hence
H
consists of a single star, so all leaves share a common neighbor
c
. Since every non-leaf vertex is the neighbor of a leaf,
c
is the unique non-leaf vertex, and therefore

G\cong K_{1,n}.

(\Leftarrow)
Conversely, let
G=K_{1,n}
with center
c
and leaves
l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}
. Then

I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}=(x_{c}x_{l_{1}},\ldots,x_{c}x_{l_{n}})=x_{c}(x_{l_{1}},\ldots,x_{l_{n}}).

Since
(x_{l_{1}},\ldots,x_{l_{n}})
has a linear resolution, multiplication by
x_{c}
shifts all homological degrees by one and yields a
2
-linear resolution of
I_{\mathcal{D}(G)}
. Consequently,
k[\mathcal{D}(G)]
admits a
2
-linear resolution.
∎
