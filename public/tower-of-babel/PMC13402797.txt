Nitrate-reducing bacteria bridge nitrogen cycling and antibiotic resistance in river ecosystems
Authors: Chunfang Deng, Hetong Cai, Kongyan Luo, Songfeng Liu, Qian Chen, Weiling Sun, Jinren Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRiver ecosystems, crucial components of the global nitrogen cycle, are increasingly affected by antibiotic pollution. However, the mechanistic interplay between nitrogen cycling and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) dissemination remains poorly understood, limiting effective ecological risk assessments. Here, we identify nitrate-reducing bacteria (NRBs), key drivers of denitrification and greenhouse gas mitigation, as dual-functional hubs that co-regulate nitrogen turnover and ARG dissemination under antibiotic stress. By integrating 173 metagenomes and 10 metatranscriptomes from the Yangtze River, we reconstruct 4200 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and find that NRBs harbor ~69% of actively transcribed ARGs in river microbiomes, with antibiotic pressure as the dominant ecological driver. Simulated microcosms exposed to antibiotic gradients reveal a hormetic response, where environmentally relevant concentrations enhanced both NRB-driven denitrification efficiency and ARG dissemination. Multi-omics analyses further reveal antibiotic-driven horizontal gene transfer as the predominant selective force co-shaping ARG and nitrate reduction gene dynamics, accelerating both nitrogen cycling and ARG spread. These findings establish NRBs as central hubs bridging antibiotic resistance and nitrogen metabolism, providing a mechanistic framework for predicting co-selection dynamics and mitigating cascading ecological impacts. Our work highlights the need to integrate microbial co-metabolic functions into pollution control strategies and redefine ecological risk assessments in antibiotic-polluted ecosystems.Subject terms: Microbial ecology, Freshwater ecology, Element cycles

Introduction

River ecosystems are fundamental regulators of the global nitrogen cycle, where diverse microbial communities mediate the transformation and flux of nitrogen species1,2. Among these, nitrate-reducing bacteria (NRBs) constitute a key functional group responsible for nitrate reduction, converting nitrate into gaseous forms such as N2 and N2O, thereby contributing to eutrophication mitigation and greenhouse gas regulation3,4. However, the stability and functionality of NRBs in river ecosystems are increasingly compromised by anthropogenic disturbances, with antibiotic contamination emerging as a global crisis with profound ecological and public health implications5–8. While antibiotics are known to exert potent selective pressures, fueling the proliferation and dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs)9–12, their specific impacts on microbially mediated nitrogen cycling, particularly under environmentally relevant concentrations in complex river ecosystems, remain critically understudied.

Emerging evidence suggests strong ecological and genetic synergies between nitrogen cycling and antibiotic resistance13–15. Co-occurrence patterns of ARGs and nitrate reduction genes (NRGs) have been consistently observed in NRBs across engineered systems (e.g., wastewater treatment plants)16,17 and natural riverine habitats18,19. Notably, NRBs have been identified as ARG reservoirs, with genomic analyses revealing co-localization of NRGs and ARGs on the same contigs18,20. This co-localization may enable the simultaneous transfer of both ARGs and NRGs via horizontal gene transfer (HGT), thereby establishing a co-selection process that links biogeochemical cycles with resistome dynamics21.

However, fundamental questions remain unresolved: Do NRBs actively transcribe ARGs in situ, functioning as potential vectors of HGT? How does antibiotic pressure specifically enrich and mobilize ARGs within NRBs compared to other microbial communities? And critically, does such stress concurrently reshapes the activity of NRBs and influence the evolutionary trajectory of ARG propagation? Collectively, these questions converge on a central hypothesis: NRBs act as keystone taxa that mechanistically bridge nitrogen cycling and antibiotic resistance under antibiotic stress, thereby regulating environmental risks through co-selection pressures.

To address these questions, we selected the Yangtze River, one of the world’s largest rivers, which exhibits distinct longitudinal gradients in geophysical characteristics, climate regimes, and socioeconomic activities from the headwater to estuary22. This environmental gradient results in a systematic increase in antibiotic concentrations from upstream to downstream regions22,23, forming an ideal natural laboratory for studying the interplay between nitrogen cycling and antibiotic resistance. By integrating 173 metagenomes and 10 metatranscriptomes, we reconstructed over 4200 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and identified NRBs as the primary reservoir of transcriptionally active ARGs, accounting for ~69% of total ARG expression. Simulated microcosm experiments under antibiotic gradients revealed a hormetic response, where environmentally relevant antibiotics simultaneously stimulated both denitrification activity and ARG propagation. Multi-omics integration further unveiled antibiotic-driven HGT as a dominant selective force, revealing genetic connections between nitrogen cycling and resistance functions. These findings establish NRBs as dual-function hubs that mediate both biogeochemical stability and evolutionary risks in antibiotic-polluted ecosystems, providing a framework to predict and mitigate the cascading effects of anthropogenic contaminants on riverine ecosystem stability and microbial adaptive landscapes.

Results

NRBs represent the predominant reservoir of ARGs in river systems

We performed comprehensive metagenomic binning to reconstruct genomes from water and sediment samples across the Yangtze River. From 173 metagenomic datasets, we recovered 4232 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) with completeness ≥ 70% and contamination <10%, exceeding the medium quality thresholds of the MIMAG criteria24 (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data 1). Among these, 215 MAGs harbored ARGs, 1253 MAGs contained NRGs, and 149 MAGs carrying both ARGs and NRGs (Fig. 1a). Notably, 69.3% of ARG-carrying MAGs co-carried NRGs, representing the most prevalent functional category among ARG-carrying lineages (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3), which could be further supported by a strong positive correlation between ARG and NRG abundances at metagenomic read level (R2 = 0.765, p < 0.001, n = 173; Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings implicate nitrate-reducing bacteria (NRBs) as the predominant reservoir for ARGs in the Yangtze River ecosystem.

Microbial hosts carrying ARGs, NRGs, or both displayed divergent functional configurations. Across 215 ARG-carrying MAGs, we identified 67 distinct ARG subtypes associated with resistance to 12 antibiotic classes. The most prevalent ARG subtypes were those conferring resistance to bacitracin (107 MAGs, 49.8%), followed by multidrug resistance (64 MAGs, 29.8%), sulfonamide (21 MAGs, 9.8%) and β-lactam (16 MAGs, 7.4%) (Fig. 1b). Among NRG-carrying MAGs, nirB (encoding nitrite reductase) was the most abundant (detected in 431 MAGs, 34.4%), followed by nirD (388, 31.0%), narG (384, 30.6%), narH (368, 29.4%), and narI (334, 26.7%) (Fig. 1c). For NRG-ARG co-hosts, 69.1% of MAGs encoded a single ARG subtype (103 MAGs), while 30.9% carried multiple ARG subtypes (47 MAGs). The most common ARG and NRG combination was bacitracin resistance genes co-occurring with nirB, nasAC, and nirD (Fig. 1d). Notably, 12 NRG-ARG co-host MAGs carried both NRGs and ARGs on the same genomic contig (Supplementary Fig. S5), suggesting potential co-evolutionary mechanisms that reinforce their functional linkage.

Taxonomic analysis revealed distinct distributions among the three host categories (ARG-hosts, NRG-hosts, and NRG-ARG co-hosts; see “Methods” for definitions, Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 2). NRG-host spanned 31 phyla, dominated by Proteobacteria (44.8%), Bacteroidota (15.0%), and Verrucomicrobiota (6.0%). ARG-host were restricted to six phyla, with Proteobacteria (77.9%) as the primary contributor, supplemented by Bacteroidota (7.4%), Verrucomicrobiota (4.4%), and Actinobacteriota (4.4%). NRG-ARG co-hosts were distributed across six phyla, with Proteobacteria being predominant (89.9%), followed by Bacteroidota (4.7%), Desulfuromonadota (2.0%), Actinobacteriota (0.67%), and Firmicutes (0.67%).

Distribution analysis further revealed distinct partitioning of NRG-hosts, ARG-hosts, and NRG-ARG co-hosts between water and sediments (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Supplementary Data 3). The proportion of ARG-hosts were significantly higher in water (28.6%) than in sediments (17.6%), whereas NRG-ARG co-hosts exhibited a significantly greater proportion in sediments (16.7%) than in water (14.0%; p < 0.05, Wilcoxon test) (Fig. 1f). Notably, NRG-ARG co-hosts accounted for 94.3% of total ARG-hosts in sediments, highlighting the preferential genomic integration of ARGs into NRBs. Moreover, sediment microbiomes exhibited greater phylogenetic breadth, dominated by Proteobacteria, with significant contributions from Verrucomicrobiota, Acidobacteriota, and Actinobacteriota (Fig. 1g).

NRBs account for ~69% of expressed ARGs in river microbiomes

Metatranscriptomic profiling of Yangtze River water samples (n = 10 sites) revealed distinct transcriptional profiles among microbial functional groups (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 4). While 58% (126/215) of ARG-carrying and 50% (631/1253) of NRG-carrying MAGs showed detectable expression, NRG-ARG co-hosts demonstrated significantly higher transcriptional activity. Among these co-hosts, 58% (87/149) expressed ARGs, 71% (106/149) expressed NRGs, and 47% (70/149) co-expressed both functional gene types. Notably, NRG-ARG co-hosts contributed approximately 69% of all expressed ARGs in riverine microbiomes, while the remaining 31% of expressed ARGs were largely associated with MAGs encoding sulfur oxidation genes (22 MAGs) and thiosulfate oxidations (15 MAGs) (Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8), suggesting potential linkages between antibiotic resistance and key biogeochemical cycles.

NRG and ARG expression were disproportionately enriched in specific microbial lineages (Fig. 2b). Among NRG-hosts, Nitrospirota exhibited the highest expression level (12,081 reads per kilobase per million RPKM), followed by Proteobacteria (3245), Thermoproteota (832), and Campylobacterota (443). ARG-hosts were dominated by Proteobacteria (354 RPKM), Verrucomicrobiota (109 RPKM), and Planctomycetota (41 RPKM). For NRG-ARG co-hosts, Nitrospirota (NRGs: 1815 RPKM, ARGs:141 RPKM) and Proteobacteria (NRGs: 750 RPKM, ARGs: 950 RPKM) exhibited the highest transcriptional activity. Notably, within Proteobacteria, the genus Rhodoferax (with only one genome assigned to a known species) emerged as the dominant co-hosts carrying both ARGs and NRGs on the same contig (Supplementary Fig. S9). This pattern was consistent in metagenomic datasets (10 out of 12 MAGs) and metatranscriptomic datasets (9 out of 10 MAGs), suggesting that physical proximity may facilitate coordinated expression under environmental selection.

Functional analysis identified narH (354 transcript ORFs, 6362 RPKM) and narG (418 ORFs, 6251 RPKM) as the highest transcriptional activity within NRG-hosts, followed by nirK (277 ORFs, 2802 RPKM), while other NRGs showed expression levels below 400 RPKM (Supplementary Fig. S10). In ARG-hosts, bacitracin resistance genes were most highly expressed (208 RPKM), succeeded by sulfonamide (6 ORFs, 138 RPKM), unclassified (2 ORFs, 61 RPKM), macrolide (2 ORFs, 46 RPKM), and aminoglycoside (4 ORFs, 41 RPKM) resistance genes. In NRG-ARG co-host, narG (63 transcript ORFs, 1034 RPKM) and narH (56 transcript ORFs, 968 RPKM) consistently exhibited the highest transcriptional activity, while sulfonamide resistance genes had the highest total expression (13 ORFs, 402 RPKM), followed by bacitracin (73 ORFs, 318 RPKM).

Analysis of 70 co-expressing MAGs revealed distinct prevalence patterns (Fig. 2c). nirB (42 MAGs) and nasAC (26 MAGs) were the most prevalent NRGs, followed by narG (23 MAGs) and narH (22 MAGs), while bacitracin resistance genes were the most common (29 MAGs) among ARGs, followed by multidrug resistance (19 MAGs), beta-lactam (11 MAGs), and sulfonamide (10 MAGs). The most frequent co-expression pattern involved 19 MAGs harboring nirB and bacitracin resistance genes (27% of all co-expression hosts), followed by nasAC with bacitracin (15 MAGs), and nirB with multidrug (14 MAGs), suggesting a strong genomic linkage between nitrogen cycling and antibiotic resistance.

Antibiotics drive the dynamics of NRG-hosts, ARG-hosts, and co-hosts

To elucidate the environmental drivers shaping the abundance dynamics of NRG-hosts, ARG-hosts, and NRG-ARG co-hosts, we analyzed a suite of 16 environmental factors encompassing major antibiotic classes (MLs, TCs, QNs, SAs, βLs), heavy metal(loid)s (Cr, Cu, As, Cd, Pb), pH, and nutrients (TN, TP, TOC, NH₄⁺, NO₃−). Our analyses identified antibiotic concentrations as the most influential factor for all three host types, a conclusion supported by both metagenomic and metatranscriptomic data (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S11). Specifically, SAs and MLs showed significant positive correlations with the abundances of all three host types in water, whereas multiple antibiotic classes (MLs, TCs, QNs, and SAs) were positively correlated with NRG-hosts and/or NRG-ARG co-hosts in sediments. Linear regression quantified these relationships, revealing that total antibiotic concentration correlated positively with NRG-hosts (R = 0.31, p = 0.003), ARG-hosts (R = 0.27, p = 0.011), and NRG-ARG co-hosts (R = 0.27, p = 0.011) in water samples (Fig. 3b). In sediment, significant positive correlations were observed for NRG-hosts (R = 0.31, P = 0.0035) and NRG-ARG co-hosts (R = 0.37, P < 0.001). At the transcriptional level, elevated total antibiotic concentrations were associated with increased transcriptional activity of NRG-hosts and NRG-ARG co-hosts in both water and sediment (Fig. 3c).

Nutrients and heavy metal(loid)s exerted additional, context-dependent selective pressures on host dynamics (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S11). Ammonium (NH₄⁺-N) negatively correlated with NRG-host and ARG-host abundances in water, while nitrate (NO₃−-N) showed negative correlations with NRG-hosts and NRG-ARG co-hosts in sediment. Associations with heavy metal(loid)s (Cr, Cu, As, Cd, and Pb) were highly variable. In water, ARG-hosts were negatively correlated with As and positively correlated with Pb, and NRG-ARG co-hosts correlated positively with Cd and Pb. In sediment, Cr showed positive correlations with both NRG-hosts and NRG-ARG co-hosts, while As negatively correlated with NRG-ARG co-hosts.

Variance partitioning analysis confirmed antibiotics as the strongest independent driver for all host types in both water and sediment (Fig. 3d), with SAs demonstrating the strongest independent contribution. This emphasizes the central role of SAs in structuring these host communities. Further analysis revealed that total antibiotic concentrations explained a significant portion (~ 10%) of the overall microbial community variation (PERMANOVA: water, R² = 0.096, p = 0.001; sediment, R² = 0.073, p = 0.001), confirming the significance of antibiotics as the primary environmental stressor in the Yangtze River ecosystem.

Microcosm validation of antibiotic-driven hormetic effects on nitrate reduction

Given the widespread occurrence and ecological significance of SAs in the Yangtze River, we selected SDZ as a representative compound to explore its potential effects on nitrate reduction processes. Microcosms experiments were conducted over four consecutive cycles using sediment samples collected from the upper, middle, and downstream reaches of the Yangtze River. Intriguingly, our results revealed that SDZ consistently enhanced nitrate removal efficiency across all treated groups, highlighting a significant interaction between SDZ and microbial communities.

In the first experimental cycle, near-complete nitrate removal with minimal nitrite accumulation was achieved at SDZ concentrations of 100, 1000, and 10,000 ng L−1, whereas the control group (0 ng L−1) showed residual nitrate and nitrite. Over the subsequent three cycles, nitrate removal efficiency declined across all the experimental groups but remained higher in SDZ-treated groups compared to the control (Fig. 4a–c). Variations in nitrous oxide levels confirmed the occurrence of dissimilatory nitrate reduction (Fig. 4d–f). In the SDZ-treated groups, nitrous oxide levels initially increased, followed by a significant decline, indicative of accelerated denitrification. In contrast, the control group exhibited either a delayed peak (e.g., C3 in CT) or no peak (e.g., C4 in WH), suggesting slower nitrate reduction in the absence of SDZ.

Interestingly, no clear dose-dependent trend was observed in nitrate removal efficiency (Fig. 4a–c). At the lower environmentally relevant concentration of SDZ (100 ng L−1), nitrate reduction was consistently promoted across all four cycles. However, at higher concentrations (1000 and 10,000 ng L−1), a more complex pattern emerged, with fluctuating nitrate removal observed in the third and fourth cycles, although it remained higher than the control. This variability may be attributed to the increasing accumulation of SDZ in both the suspension water (Fig. 4h) and sediments (Fig. 4i).

Furthermore, 15N tracer analysis confirmed enhanced denitrification rates in the SDZ-treated groups, with distinct patterns observed in the relationship between denitrification rates and SDZ concentrations across the study sites (Fig. 4g). At sites CT and WH, denitrification rates positively correlated with increasing SDZ concentrations. In contrast, at site MAS, denitrification efficiency followed a non-linear trend, with a decrease at the highest concentration (10,000 ng L−1) compared to lower concentrations, although still higher than the control group. These findings suggest that environmentally relevant concentrations may sustain enhanced nitrate reduction, whereas prolonged exposure to high concentrations may led to more complex effect.

Antibiotic-induced co-selection of ARGs and NRGs

Exposure to escalating SDZ concentrations (0, 100, 1000, 10,000 ng L−1) significantly reshaped microbial community structure (Supplementary Data 5). All three host categories showed increased relative abundances under SDZ pressure, including ARG-hosts represented by Firmicutes lineages, NRG–ARG co-hosts affiliated with Proteobacteria, and NRG-hosts represented by Bacteroidota (Supplementary Figs. S12 and 13 and Supplementary Data 6). Interestingly, no significant differences were observed among the SDZ treatment groups, aligning with the trends observed in nitrate removal efficiency.

Functional analysis revealed a co-selection pattern between ARGs and NRGs. SDZ exposure led to a notable enrichment of ARGs in ARG-hosts, particularly genes conferring macrolide resistance (Fig. 5a). Concurrently, NRG-hosts exhibited increased relative abundances of key nitrogen cycling genes (e.g., nar, nrf, and nir) (Fig. 5b), indicating that nitrogen transformation capacity was maintained or even enhanced under antibiotic pressure. Notably, NRG-ARG co-hosts displayed simultaneous enrichment of both NRGs (e.g., nar, nrf, nor) and ARGs (e.g., multidrug, sulfonamide, and aminoglycoside resistance genes), indicating functional coupling under antimicrobial pressure (Fig. 5a, b). These patterns were further corroborated by metatranscriptomic data, which revealed significant upregulation of narGHI together with sulfonamide- and multidrug-resistance genes.

Moreover, transcriptional analysis of the entire microbial community revealed enhanced expression of genes involved in energy production, carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, translation, and other core cellular processes under SDZ exposure (Fig. 5c). This global upregulation may contribute to the enhanced nitrate reduction observed under antibiotic stress. Taken together, these results support the notion that antibiotic stress not only selects for resistant microorganisms but also reshapes ecological functions, enabling certain host types to sustain or even enhance their roles in biogeochemical cycling under anthropogenic pressure.

Horizontal gene transfer facilitates co-dissemination of ARGs and NRGs

The capacity for HGT among microbial groups was assessed through mobile genetic element (MGE) abundance analysis. Natural samples revealed significant differences in MGE content across host types (P < 0.001, Kruskal–Wallis test), with NRG-ARG co-hosts harboring the highest MGE density per genome (21.4), followed by ARG-hosts (10.6) and NRG-hosts (10.3) (Fig. 6a). This pattern was corroborated by controlled microcosm experiments, demonstrating robustness across environmental and experimental contexts (Fig. 6b). Notably, plasmids dominated MGE composition, accounting for over 60% of annotations across host types, and their abundance followed the same host-type ranking as total MGEs, being highest in NRG-ARG co-hosts, intermediate in ARG-hosts, and lowest in NRG-hosts (Fig. 6c, d). Under escalating antibiotic concentrations, total MGEs and plasmid abundances increased, with the most pronounced increase observed in NRG-ARG co-hosts, implying selection pressure favors MGE-rich taxa and enhances their genetic stability (Fig. 6e, f). Plasmid prevalence further highlighted this trend, with 87.2% of NRG-ARG co-host genomes containing plasmids compared to 77.9% of ARG-hosts and 61.1% of NRG-hosts (Fig. 6g, h), underscoring antimicrobial-driven retention of plasmid-mediated traits.

To quantify HGT dynamics, 561 ARG sequences were analyzed for similarity. Out of the total, 446 (79.5%) showed near-identical matches (≥ 99% nucleotide identity and 100% alignment coverage) with at least one other gene, indicating frequent ongoing transfer events (Fig. 6i). These ARG sequences were classified into 83 distinct groups, and taxonomic analysis revealed that NRG-ARG co-host pairs shared clusters more frequently than pairs composed exclusively of ARG-hosts. The largest cluster comprised 32 nearly identical multidrug resistance genes from diverse Rhodocyclaceae genera, including a plasmid-borne variant, evidencing plasmid-mediated dissemination among these co-host strains. Cross-phylum ARG transfer was further supported by 69 sequences matching across bacterial phyla, 32 of which resided on plasmids (Fig. 6j). These cross-phylum ARGs were mainly enriched in co-host genomes (70%), positioning them as central hubs for genetic exchange. Functionally, sulfonamide resistance genes showed the highest transfer frequency (18 instances), followed by tetracycline (17) and macrolide–lincosamide–streptogramin (15) resistance genes.

In addition, we identified 337 NRGs from 181 plasmids, with 50% of the plasmids carrying at least two NRGs and 27% encoding three or more (Supplementary Fig. S14). Notably, five plasmids harbored more than four NRGs, and one plasmid encoded nearly the entire denitrification pathway (napAB, nirK, and norC) except for the terminal step (Supplementary Fig. S15). These findings highlight the capacity of plasmids to mobilize multi-step nitrogen cycling modules and broaden the recognized role of mobile genetic elements in spreading core biogeochemical functions. Moreover, we found that the number of plasmids carrying NRGs increased in NRG-ARG co-hosts under antibiotic pressure, whereas NRG-hosts exhibited a progressive decline in such plasmids with rising antibiotic concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S16). This pattern suggests that hosts harboring both ARGs and NRGs have a competitive advantage under antibiotic selection, potentially due to co-selection dynamics.

Discussion

NRBs as active hubs of resistance gene expression in aquatic microbiomes

The continuous influx of municipal, agricultural, and industrial pollutants into riverine ecosystems has led to the co-occurrence of antibiotics and nitrogen compounds5,6, significantly reshaping microbial communities and fostering the persistence of both ARGs and NRGs25,26. While previous studies have linked ARG-NRG co-occurrence through abundance correlations20 or genomic co-localization16,18, our integrated metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses spanning the 6030-km continuum of the Yangtze River provide strong evidence that NRBs serve as central reservoirs and active hubs for ARGs, expressing nearly 69% of all detectable ARGs (Figs. 1 and 2). Furthermore, the residual 31% of ARGs were specifically associated with microbe involved in sulfur oxidation and thiosulfate oxidation. These findings indicate that the coupling between biogeochemical cycling and ARG dissemination is not a random background process, but is highly selective and concentrated in specific functional guilds, with NRBs being the most dominant reservoir and hub. This establishes a critical principle: core functionally essential microbes involved in biogeochemical cycles are central players of ARG persistence and dissemination in riverine ecosystems. Consequently, environmental conditions or anthropogenic perturbations that promote the activity and dominance of these key biogeochemical players may simultaneously amplify the ecological risks of antibiotic resistance dissemination.

The functional coupling between nitrogen metabolism and antibiotic resistance represents a physiological integration likely driven by stress-induced metabolic specialization27,28. This integration exemplifies a coherent adaptive strategy29, wherein NRBs strategically allocate energy resources between nitrogen metabolism and resistance mechanisms, thereby enhancing their ecological resilience in transitional niches defined by low oxygen, antibiotic accumulation, and nitrogen loading. Importantly, ARG–NRG co-hosts, predominantly Proteobacteria, frequently exhibit co-expression of ARGs and NRGs on the same contigs in both natural river environments and controlled microcosms (Figs. 1 and 2). Such co-localization and coordinated expression confirm that the coupling is not a localized phenomenon but a widespread ecological strategy. By maintaining close associations with other microbes through metabolic handoffs, NRBs may act as ecological bridges that facilitate the dissemination of resistance genes across communities30,31, and mediate the synergistic effects of pollutants on microbial adaptive evolution in riverine systems.

Antibiotics as the dominant driver for ARG and NRG host dynamics

While antibiotics are widely recognized as drivers of ARG dissemination11,32, our findings reposition them as principal ecological regulators governing community assembly dynamics in large river ecosystems. Beyond selection of resistance, antibiotic exposure emerged as a dominant and consistent environmental driver shaping both ARG-host and NRG-ARG co-host communities (Fig. 3), highlighting their role in co-selecting nitrogen cycling functions alongside resistance traits18.

In contrast, heavy metal(loid)s (e.g., As, Cd, and Pb) acted as context-dependent modulators rather than primary drivers. Although capable of imposing persistent selective pressures33, their effects were inconsistent across habitats and host categories, highlighting their role as conditional stressors whose impact is contingent upon local geochemical conditions and interspecific interactions. These contaminants likely reshape community structure through physiological filtering, preferentially enriching taxa with greater tolerance or adaptive capacity34,35.

The ecological dynamics are further clarified by the observed negative correlations between inorganic nitrogen species (NH₄⁺-N and NO₃−-N) and specific host groups. This pattern suggests active biological consumption rather than substrate-driven enrichment, indicating that thriving NRG-host communities are a cause, rather than a consequence, of reduced nitrate concentrations. Collectively, these findings highlight antibiotics as the dominant driver, with metal(loid)s and nutrients playing complementary roles in shaping ARG and NRG host dynamics.

Antibiotic-induced hormesis effect on nitrate reduction

Our study provides ecosystem-scale evidence of environmentally realistic antibiotic exposure in a large natural river system. We observed a hormetic effect of antibiotics on nitrogen reduction in the Yangtze River, which was further supported by microcosm experiments showing enhanced denitrification rates and accelerated depletion of NO₃− and NO₂− at environmentally relevant concentrations (Fig. 4). This pattern contrasts with the inhibitory responses typically reported under short-term, high-concentration exposure (typically in the µg·L−1 to mg·L−1 range), which are characterized by acute metabolic suppression and elevated N₂O production36. While such studies delineate toxicity thresholds, they may overlook microbial adaptation under prolonged, low-level stress. Emerging evidence further suggests that antibiotic effects are strongly dependent on exposure regime and ecological context, where transient inhibition can be followed by functional recovery as microbial communities undergo compositional turnover or metabolic reallocation37,38. These findings highlight the necessity of employing scale-appropriate, long-term experimental designs when evaluating antibiotic effects on nitrogen cycling, as adaptive community restructuring under sustained low-level stress may not be captured in short-term experiments.

The observed hormesis effect may directly arise from co-selection of ARGs and NRGs under antibiotic stress, leading to the enrichment of NRG–ARG co-hosts predominantly affiliated with Proteobacteria, a group recognized both as established ARG reservoirs and as taxa with high nitrogen transformation potential39 (Fig. 5). Metatranscriptomic data further demonstrated coordinated upregulation of resistance and nitrate reduction pathways within NRG–ARG co-hosts, alongside increased expression of oxidative stress response genes under elevated antibiotic concentrations. This coordinated response suggests that the coupling of resistance and nitrogen metabolism traits represents an adaptive strategy under chronic selection. This phenomenon advances our understanding of how antibiotic-driven shifts can restructure nitrogen-transforming microbial guilds, influencing both resistance dissemination and biogeochemical cycling in river ecosystems.

Furthermore, the observed positive correlations between antibiotic concentrations, NRG-hosts, and total microbial abundance point to an antibiotic-mediated community filtering mechanism. In this process, the suppression of antibiotic-sensitive taxa reshapes competitive dynamics40,41, thereby favoring NRG-host microbes and amplifying their role in biogeochemical cycling under anthropogenic pressure. This aligns with patterns observed in Qinghai-Tibet Plateau urban rivers18. Such reproducibility across different river systems confirms it as a reproducible community-level response to antibiotic disturbance42. Therefore, beyond direct co-selection, antibiotics may indirectly modulate biogeochemical processes like denitrification by reconfiguring microbial networks that integrate antibiotic resistance to ecosystem functions.

Although the microcosm experiments focused on SDZ as a representative sulfonamide and the field investigation spanned broad spatial gradients and two seasons, the generality and long-term stability of the observed hormetic and co-selection patterns remain to be established, warranting broader antibiotic coverage and multi-year monitoring.

MGEs drive the evolutionary coupling of antibiotic resistance and nitrate reduction

Metagenomic evidence of antibiotic-treated sediments revealed a pronounced enrichment of MGEs, with both their abundance and diversity markedly exceeding those in untreated controls (Fig. 6). This expansion was most notable within NRG-ARG co-hosts, where MGEs and plasmids increased substantially under rising antibiotic concentrations, providing genomic platforms for ARG acquisition, maintenance, and transcriptional activation in situ. These findings reinforce the well-established role of HGT as a principal driver of ARG dissemination across bacterial lineages43. The elevated plasmid prevalence observed here, coupled with a higher number in NRG-ARG co-hosts, underscores their pivotal role in mediating resistance gene flow44,45, facilitating not only intra-species but also cross-phylum transfer events.

Importantly, NRGs were also detected on plasmids, suggesting that key biogeochemical functions may be mobilizable across taxonomically and functionally distinct hosts46. In natural environments, denitrification pathways are often incomplete, leading to the accumulation of undesirable intermediates, including toxic nitrite (NO₂−) and the greenhouse gases nitric oxide (NO) and nitrous oxide (N₂O). These compounds pose significant risks for both environmental toxicity and climate change47,48. The dissemination of NRGs via plasmids might be the main reason for the truncation of denitrification processes in natural systems. This finding highlights a critical, yet often overlooked, coupling of ARGs and NRGs on mobile genetic elements. By extending the concept of co-selection, our results suggest that plasmid-mediated transfer may facilitates the concurrent dissemination of resistance traits and crucial metabolic functions, such as nitrate reduction. This coupling promotes the persistence of these functions and enhances microbial community resilience under antibiotic stress. While the observed genomic co-localization and coordinated transcriptional responses are consistent with co-selection, they do not directly demonstrate co-transfer or regulatory coupling. Further experimental validation through targeted approaches, such as conjugation assays or gene-level perturbation, will be required to clarify the mechanistic basis of this functional coupling.

In conclusion, this study highlights the critical role of NRBs as central hubs for the co-occurrence and co-expression of ARGs and NRGs in riverine ecosystems. Our findings demonstrate that antibiotics not only drive the proliferation of ARGs but also influence nitrogen cycling by accelerating denitrification processes. Furthermore, mobile genetic elements, particularly plasmids, facilitate the simultaneous dissemination of both ARGs and NRGs, enhancing microbial community resilience under environmental stress. These insights underscore the need to consider the integrated impacts of antibiotics on microbial resistance and biogeochemical processes when assessing the ecological risks in aquatic environments.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

To capture the multi-scale heterogeneity of the Yangtze River, we designed a comprehensive sampling strategy that incorporated spatial, seasonal, and inter-annual dimensions: (1) sampling along the full longitudinal gradient (from upstream to downstream) to capture environmental and biogeochemical gradients across the 6300-km river continuum; (2) collecting samples across seasons to account for hydrological and physicochemical dynamics; (3) conducting multiple-year replications to distinguish persistent community patterns from short-term stochastic fluctuations. In total, 86 water samples and 87 sediment samples were collected from 43 sites along the Yangtze River (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data 1).

Water and sediment samples were collected and pretreated synchronously within one week, during which no extreme weather events were reported. For each water sample, pH, total nitrogen (TN), nitrate-nitrogen (NO3−-N), ammonium-nitrogen (NH4+-N), total phosphorus (TP), and total organic carbon (TOC) were measured according to the Environmental Quality Standards for Surface Water (GB3838-2002) of China49,50. For sediment samples, pH, TN, NO3−-N, NH4+-N, TP, and TOC were determined following the standards HJ 962–2018, HJ 717–2014, HJ 634–2012, HJ 632–2011, and HJ 695–2014, as recommended by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China. The analysis encompassed five major antibiotic classes, including macrolides (MLs), tetracyclines (TCs), quinolones (QNs), sulfonamides (SAs), and β-lactams (βLs). The concentrations of heavy metal(loid)s (Cr, Cu, As, Cd, and Pb) in water and sediment samples were measured following the standard methods issued by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China (HJ 700–2014 for water, HJ 1315–2023 for sediments). Environmental DNA was extracted from each sample, using either filter membranes or 0.5 g of sediment, through multiple extractions with the FastDNA® SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality and concentration were determined through 260/280 nm absorbance measures using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer ND-2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). DNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform using the paired-end (2 × 150 bp) strategy (Majorbio Company, Shanghai, China).

Moreover, metatranscriptomic sequencing was performed on 10 representative sites selected from the 43 sampling locations (Supplementary Fig. S1). 20 L of surface water were collected from the selected sites and filtered through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes (Millipore, USA). The filters were immediately transferred to sterile 10 mL centrifuge tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then homogenized. Total RNA was extracted in triplicate for each sample using the FastRNA® Pro Soil-Direct Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The triplicate extracts were pooled to form a composite RNA sample. To eliminate residual DNA, the PureLink®DNase set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed. The concentration and purity of the RNA were quantified with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and Nanodrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), respectively. RNA integrity was verified on an Agilent 4200 system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was subsequently removed using the Ribo-Zero Magnetic Kit (Epicenter, USA). The resulting rRNA-depleted RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) library construction with the TruSeqTM RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). Finally, paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Majorbio Company, Shanghai, China).

Metagenome quality control, assembly, and binning

To ensure data quality, raw reads from each sample were subjected to quality control using FastQC (v0.11.8)51. Quality-filtered sequencing reads from all 173 metagenomes were then de novo assembled into contigs for each sample using MEGAHIT (v1.2.8)52. Binning was performed using the Binning module of the metaWRAP v1.3.12653 software with default parameters. Refined bins were generated with the MetaWRAP-Bin_refinement module. The completeness and contamination of the genomes were estimated with CheckM v1.0.1154 with the “lineage_wf” workflow. Only genomes that met the following criteria were retained: >70% completeness and <10% contamination.

Taxonomic annotation and relative abundance calculation of MAGs

Taxonomic annotation of each Metagenome-Assembled Genome (MAG) was conducted using GTDB-Tk v2.1.055 (release R207) with the “classify_wf” workflow, applying default parameters. The phylogenetic tree for the MAGs was constructed using FastTree (version 2.1.11)56 with default settings, based on alignments of protein sequence derived from 120 bacterial or 122 archaeal marker genes, as generated by GTDB-Tk. The relative abundance of each MAG in the metagenomic samples was quantified using CoverM software (genome -m relative abundance, https://github.com/wwood/CoverM). CoverM calculates relative abundance by determining the ratio of aligned read bases on the genome’s total length, adjusted for the proportion of total reads mapped to the genome.

Functional annotation of MAGs

Open Reading Frames (ORFs) in the MAGs were predicted using MetaGeneMark v3.3857. The identification of MAGs harboring antibiotic resistance genes was performed using the Structured Antibiotic Resistance Genes (SARG) database58. A gene sequence was assigned as an ARG if it satisfied thresholds of >80% identity and >70% coverage. All identified ARGs were then categorized into types and subtypes based on the ARGs-OAP database, and their resistance mechanisms were annotated through the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD)59. Nevertheless, methodological challenges remain in accurately distinguishing broad-spectrum transporters from genuine ARGs within current databases60.

NRGs in MAGs were filtered based on KEGG annotations and supported by metabolic pathway reconstruction using the Metabolic software61. To compare hosts that carrying ARGs or NRGs alone with those harboring both, particularly with respect to taxonomic composition, functional gene repertoires, and the enrichment of mobile genetic elements, genomes were classified into three categories: ARG-hosts (containing ARGs only), NRG-hosts (containing NRGs only), and NRG-ARG co-hosts (containing both). Subsequent analyses were conducted based on this classification.

Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) within each MAG were identified by BLASTp (e-value < 1e-20, identity ≥ 90%, coverage ≥ 90%) against the mobileOG-db, a curated database of mobility-associated protein families62. Hits were annotated using mobileOG-db functional categories, including plasmid-associated proteins, transposases, integrases, and conjugation-related factors. In addition, plasmids and viruses were further identified using geNomad63. The potential transfer of ARGs was assessed using a widely used approach to detect nearly identical full-length ARG sequences across different hosts21,64,65. Candidate ARGs were extracted from genomes and subjected to all-versus-all BLASTn alignments (E-value ≤ 1 × 10−5). Putative transfer events were defined as ARG pairs from distinct host taxa exhibiting >99% nucleotide identity over their full length. To illustrate potential transfer patterns of ARGs across host taxa, we constructed a host–ARG linkage network and visualized it with Cytoscape66.

Metatranscriptomic data processing

Raw metatranscriptomic data were first filtrated using Trim Galore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore, default parameters) to remove low-quality bases and 3’ end adapters. Subsequently, SortMeRNA (v4.3.7)67 (default parameters) was employed to remove prokaryotic biological and eukaryotic rRNA sequences. Only sequencing data that passed the above quality control steps were used for subsequent gene expression calculations. Transcript expression of target genes was estimated using RSEM (v1.3.3)68 by mapping the quality-filtered dataset to the functional gene sequences predicted from the MAGs. Transcript expression was quantified as RPKM (reads per kilobase per million). Additionally, the transcript expression for each MAG in each sample was calculated as the sum of the RPKM values of all gene transcripts present within that MAG.

Microcosm design, physicochemical, and microbial analysis

Freshwater-sediment microcosms were established to explore the effects of antibiotic stress on ARG and NRG host communities. Sediment samples for the microcosm setup were collected from three locations along the Yangtze River: Cuntan (upstream, site CT), Wuhan (midstream, site WH), and Maanshan (downstream, site MAS). These locations were selected due to the higher transcriptional activities of host communities observed there. Sulfadiazine (SDZ), a representative sulfonamide, was selected as the focus antibiotic for the study due to its common detection in the Yangtze River, significant environmental concentrations, and well-documented impact on host communities18.

For the microcosm setup, 50 g of homogeneous fresh sediment was submerged in 100 mL of in-situ overlying water in a 200 mL serum bottle, which was then sealed with a rubber stopper and aluminum cap. To capture both common background and moderately elevated conditions, we employed a broad concentration gradient of SDZ (0, 100, 1000, and 10,000 ng L−1) in our microcosm experiments. This range is informed by a global monitoring dataset5, which indicates that median riverine concentrations typically fall below 100 ng L−1, yet extreme levels can reach μg L−1 near intense anthropogenic sources. Accordingly, each treatment group comprised: (1) a low antibiotic stress group with 100 ng L−1 sulfadiazine, (2) a medium antibiotic stress group with 1000 ng L−1 sulfadiazine, and (3) a high antibiotic stress group with 10000 ng L−1 sulfadiazine. The control group received no antibiotic addition.

Each microcosm was incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 5 days per cycle. At the end of each cycle, both sediment and overlying water were collected for physicochemical and microbial community analysis. A total of 48 sediment metagenomic datasets (3 sites × 4 treatments × 4 cycles) were acquired utilizing the same sequencing methods outlined in section 2.1. Additionally, original 12 sediment samples (3 sites × 4 treatments) collected from three sites performed metagenomic sequencing as controls.

Sulfadiazine concentrations in both water and sediment samples were extracted using a solid-phase extraction (SPE) method and analyzed by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC; Dionex UltiMate 3000 Series), coupled with a TSQ Endura triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS; Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) source. N2O concentrations were measured using a gas chromatograph (7890B, Agilent, USA) at an oven temperature as of 50 °C69. All other physicochemical analyses followed the protocols described in the previous sections and were performed in triplicate. After four cycles, triplicated microcosms were sacrificed for sampling. Metatranscriptomic sequencing was performed, yielding 12 metatranscriptomic datasets (3 sites × 4 treatments). Additionally, 50 g of mixed sediment samples from each treatment were used to measure microbial denitrification rates, following the methodologies referenced in refs. 70,71. The processing methods for both the metagenomic and meta transcriptional data followed the same protocols in previous sections.

Statistical analysis

Microbial community dissimilarities were calculated using Bray–Curtis distances and visualized by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) implemented with the metaMDS function in the vegan package in R. Differences in community structure among treatments and sites were tested using analysis of similarity (ANOSIM). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between 16 environmental factors and the abundances of the three host types in water and sediment samples. Significance levels were defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. The relative independent contributions of environmental variables to host abundances were estimated using multiple linear regression coupled with LMG-based variance decomposition. Differences in the numbers of MGEs and plasmids among NRG-hosts, ARG-hosts, and NRG–ARG cohosts were assessed separately using two-sided Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction.
