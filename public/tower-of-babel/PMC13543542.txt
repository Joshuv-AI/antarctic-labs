Endoscopic incision and debridement for presumed delayed postoperative anastomotic submucosal abscess of the rectum: a case report
Authors: Hai-Hua Zhou, Jian-Ping Xu, Shuai Yuan, Ya-Mei Ma, Sheng-Li Shi, Wei Zhang, Rui-Qiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnastomotic lesions after colorectal cancer surgery are uncommon and usually signify tumor recurrence or early complications. Delayed submucosal abscess occurring several years after curative resection is extremely rare. Due to the lack of specific clinical manifestations, imaging studies may mimic tumors or other subepithelial lesions, making the diagnosis challenging. Moreover, such lesions are located deep within the intestinal wall at the anastomotic site, which further complicates their management. Herein, we report a rare case of presumed delayed postoperative anastomotic submucosal abscess of the rectum successfully treated by endoscopic incision and debridement, highlighting diagnostic pitfalls and therapeutic feasibility.Case descriptionA 43-year-old woman presented with an incidentally discovered submucosal mass at the rectal anastomotic site during routine surveillance colonoscopy, eight years following laparoscopic low anterior resection for rectal cancer. She was completely asymptomatic (no abdominal pain or distension) with unremarkable laboratory findings, including normal tumor markers. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) revealed a slightly hyperechoic solid lesion originating from the submucosa, measuring approximately 1.61 cm × 0.81 cm, with relatively clear borders and heterogeneous internal echoes. Given the diagnostic uncertainty and the lesion’s deep intramural location, endoscopic treatment was undertaken for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. The procedure involved a longitudinal incision through the muscularis propria at the central part of the lesion elevation, which unexpectedly revealed a sinus cavity containing purulent fluid and yellow plaque-like solid material, suggesting the presence of a chronic abscess. The cavity was irrigated, and the defect was closed with clips. Post-procedure EUS confirmed complete resolution of the mass, and the patient recovered uneventfully without complications.ConclusionsThis case shows that a presumed delayed anastomotic submucosal abscess, although rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of mass-like lesions occurring years after rectal cancer surgery. However, in the absence of microbiological and pathological confirmation, the exact nature of the lesion remains uncertain. Alternative explanations, including a chronic postoperative cavity, anastomotic sinus formation, implantation cyst, or other benign postoperative changes, cannot be completely excluded. Nevertheless, endoscopic incision and debridement may represent a safe, minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic option in selected cases of presumed postoperative abscess.Keywords: Delayed anastomotic abscess, Endoscopic mucosal incision (EMI), Submucosal lesion, Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), Case report

Introduction

The delayed development of new lesions within the anastomosis site after colorectal cancer surgery is uncommon. These lesions are most often associated with tumor recurrence or postoperative inflammatory changes. The delayed appearance of a submucosal mass at an anastomotic site several years after radical resection is rare and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. The extended application of ESD technology have expanded the treatment options for complex colorectal lesions. This report describes a submucosal mass identified at an anastomotic site eight years after rectal cancer surgery and discusses the diagnostic process and rationale for selecting the endoscopic treatment strategy.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old woman was admitted for evaluation after the incidental discovery of a rectal submucosal mass 10 days earlier. Colonoscopic examination demonstrated a submucosal lesion measuring approximately 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm located adjacent to the anastomotic site, with no evidence of tumor recurrence; consequently, the lesion was not resected at that time. The patient had no clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, or anal discomfort. The patient had undergone laparoscopic low anterior resection (LAR) for rectal cancer, with reconstruction performed using a stapled end-to-end anastomosis 8 years before presenting with the new lesion. Her medical history was notable for the placement of an intrauterine device. Physical examination revealed no abnormal findings.

Investigations

Preliminary diagnosis included a rectal submucosal mass and status post radical resection for rectal cancer. Laboratory investigations, including carcinoembryonic antigen, alpha-fetoprotein, carbohydrate antigen 125, carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9, complete blood count, and infection-related markers, were all within normal limits. EUS demonstrated a slightly hyperechoic solid lesion arising from the submucosa, measuring approximately 1.61 cm × 0.81 cm, with relatively clear borders and heterogeneous internal echoes (Fig. 1A and B). Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT revealed multiple linear hyperdense shadows in the upper rectal wall, along with a small patchy hyperdense area measuring approximately 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm. No enhancement was observed on the contrast-enhanced imaging. These findings were linked with postoperative changes rather than malignancy (Fig. 1C).

Treatment

Preoperative evaluation

In this patient, EUS demonstrated a slightly hyperechoic solid lesion arising from the submucosa, while abdominal CT revealed a localized hyperdense area within the rectal wall. These findings suggested a benign lesion, with chronic organized rectal abscess and lipoma considered the primary differential diagnoses. Notably, the lesion lacked the hypoechoic or anechoic features typically associated with an acute abscess cavity, indicating minimal fluid or pus content. Under these circumstances, EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) was considered unlikely to provide effective drainage of the organized hyperechoic material within the cavity [1]. Therefore, based on a preliminary diagnosis of chronic organized rectal abscess, endoscopic mucosal incision using ESD-based techniques was planned for further diagnostic and therapeutic management.

Procedure

The patient underwent endoscopic mucosal incision of the rectal subepithelial lesion under anesthesia. A submucosal lesion was identified approximately 5 cm from the anal verge. A mucosal incision knife was used to make a C-shaped incision on the anal side of the lesion, followed by gradual submucosal dissection until the muscularis propria was exposed; however, no definite subepithelial tumor was identified (Fig. 2A). During the procedure, the surgical strategy was modified: a longitudinal incision approximately 2 cm in length was made from the anal side to the oral side along the plane of the muscularis propria (Fig. 2B), but the lesion was still not visualized. Subsequently, an exploratory incision was made through the muscularis propria at the central part of the lesion elevation. During this maneuver, a small amount of purulent material was observed. After further dissection of the muscular layer, a sinus cavity was identified containing a large amount of yellow plaque-like and debris-like solid material (Fig. 2C). The sinus cavity was repeatedly irrigated with metronidazole solution, which flushed out a small amount of pus and a large volume of yellow plaque-like and debris-like solid material (Fig. 2D). Post-irrigation EUS showed complete resolution of the previously observed slightly hyperechoic submucosal lesion (Fig. 2E). The wound was closed with titanium clips (Fig. 2F).

Follow-up

Postoperative management consisted of bowel rest, intravenous fluid replacement, and antibiotic therapy for three days. The patient experienced no abdominal pain, fever, hematochezia, or other postoperative complications. The patient was followed clinically for 8 months after the endoscopic procedure, during which no symptoms, such as abdominal pain, abdominal distension, or fever, and signs of reinfection were observed. The patient was advised to undergo a follow-up colonoscopy or EUS within a year for further evaluation. Any relevant long-term findings will be reported in a future update.

Discussion

Postoperative anastomotic submucosal abscess of the rectum refers to a localized abscess formation within the submucosal layer at the anastomotic site. This abscess usually develops within a few days after rectal surgery. Rectal submucosal abscesses typically present initially with symptoms such as anal heaviness and pain, often accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers. On colonoscopy, EUS, and other imaging studies, they can resemble submucosal tumors, necessitating differentiation from neuroendocrine tumors, rectal cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), and other lesions [2]. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a well-established method for assessing perirectal infections. Delayed postoperative anastomotic submucosal abscesses can arise from several factors. Persistence of viable bacteria deep within the rectal wall after anti-infective therapy may lead to chronic irritation and abscess formation. Incomplete resolution of a local hematoma from incisional bleeding can become infected and develop into an abscess. Additionally, poor blood supply or excessive tension at the anastomotic site may result in local infection and subsequent abscess formation [3, 4]. Additionally, studies have reported that a low anastomosis may compromise local blood supply and is associated with the occurrence of delayed anastomotic complications [5]. This report describes an unusual case of a female patient diagnosed with a presumed delayed anastomotic submucosal abscess eight years after laparoscopic low anterior resection for rectal cancer. The patient was asymptomatic, and inflammatory markers were within normal limits. EUS revealed a slightly hyperechoic solid lesion originating from the submucosa, measuring approximately 1.61 cm × 0.81 cm, with relatively clear borders and heterogeneous internal echoes. This atypical appearance may reflect the specific pathological stage of the abscess. Long-standing chronic inflammation can lead to extensive fibrous tissue formation and accumulation of necrotic debris, rendering the pus viscous and increasing its solid components. As a result, on EUS, the lesion may appear as a slightly hyperechoic mass resembling a benign tumor rather than the anechoic cystic cavity typically observed in abscesses. Furthermore, erosive changes associated with chronic inflammation may extend into the muscularis propria, complicating differential diagnosis. These imaging features underscore the insidious and deceptive nature of presumed delayed anastomotic submucosal abscess. EUS is an indispensable tool for the evaluation of gastrointestinal subepithelial lesions; however, it has limitations in the diagnosis of inflammatory subepithelial lesions. Previous studies have reported a sensitivity of only 3.39%–4.0%, with a misdiagnosis rate as high as 96.61% [6]. EUS-FNA/FNB is an important minimally invasive modality for the evaluation of subepithelial lesions. However, in the present case, the lesion location, atypical EUS features, and the contrast-enhanced CT finding of a small patchy hyperdense area collectively favored a benign process. Therefore, an ESD-based mucosal incision technique was selected as a combined diagnostic and therapeutic strategy. ESD and its derivative techniques are commonly used for treating early gastrointestinal cancers and submucosal tumors [7–10]. In this case, we initially adopted an ESD-based approach, performing a mucosal incision on the anal side of the lesion followed by stepwise submucosal dissection to expose the lesion and establish a diagnosis. However, even after exposure of the muscularis propria, the lesion could not be identified. Accordingly, the intraoperative strategy was modified to a longitudinal exploratory incision at the center of the lesion elevation. This modification was based on two considerations. First, the subepithelial lesion was located at the anastomotic site. A conventional marginal incision would likely have created a relatively large mucosal defect predominantly involving the anastomosis. In such a setting, postoperative closure with endoscopic clips could be technically challenging because of metallic staples within the deeper anastomotic layer. In contrast, the longitudinal fenestration-type incision adopted in this case enabled effective wound closure while minimizing interference from the staples. The successful closure of the approximately 2-cm longitudinal incision further supported the feasibility of this strategy.Second, based on the findings of endoscopic ultrasound, white-light colonoscopy, and abdominal computed tomography, a chronic rectal abscess was considered the most likely preoperative diagnosis. Therefore, a fenestration-type incision was considered both diagnostically and therapeutically appropriate. Using this approach, we were ultimately able to penetrate the muscular layer and successfully identify the lesion. As shown in Fig. 2D, the lesion was located posterior to the muscularis propria. During irrigation, a large amount of irrigant and yellow debris-like material could overflow through the incision opening without evidence of extravasation into the extraluminal space. The post-irrigation EUS findings further confirmed that the lesion may have been associated with a dead space created by folding of the muscular layer during anastomotic suturing, which subsequently led to occult infection. In contrast to the reports by Mao Xet al. [11], Zhang et al. [12] and Hu et al. [13], who described endoscopic fenestration for delayed anastomotic abscesses, the present case is distinguished by the lesion’s two-muscle-layer deep location. Whereas fenestration alone may be sufficient for superficial abscesses, the endoscopic incision and debridement technique we adopted in this case enabled targeted exploration of the muscular layers and precise drainage, thereby minimizing tissue trauma while preserving mucosal integrity. Drainage combined with antibiotic therapy is considered a safe and effective strategy for abdominopelvic abscesses. However, guidelines for the use of this technique to treat intramural intestinal abscesses are lacking [14]. Previous studies have also reported successful treatment of delayed postoperative submucosal abscesses using endoscopic fenestration combined with antibiotics [12, 13]. Intraoperative irrigation of the abscess cavity with metronidazole solution was performed for several reasons. Post-anastomotic infections following rectal surgery are commonly associated with mixed microbial flora, particularly anaerobic bacteria. Metronidazole is a well-established anti-anaerobic agent. The National Drug Standard issued by the National Medical Products Administration of China specifically states that metronidazole may be used for continuous lavage of deep abscess cavities, postoperative intracavitary and incisional irrigation, and topical treatment of wound infections. Currently, an increasing number of clinical studies have evaluated the role of metronidazole in intraoperative wound irrigation and peritoneal lavage, with favorable outcomes reported in the prevention and control of postoperative infection [15, 16]. When managing an intramural abscess, both leaving the cavity open and primary closure have respective advantages and disadvantages, and the choice should be individualized. While open drainage allows continuous evacuation and may reduce the risk of abscess reaccumulationm [17], primary closure offers the advantages of protecting the bowel wall, reducing the risk of delayed perforation or fistula formation, limiting luminal contamination, and promoting faster mucosal healing [18]. In this case, primary closure was selected based on both anatomical and clinical considerations. The lesion was located at the anastomotic site, and the depth of dissection suggested possible involvement of the muscular layer, raising concern for delayed perforation or fistula if the defect were left open. In addition, the lesion was consistent with a chronic abscess without clinical or laboratory evidence of active infection, indicating a low likelihood of ongoing purulent drainage. In this setting, closure with clips was considered a safer approach to protect the bowel wall while maintaining effective treatment following irrigation and debridement. Intraoperative irrigation and postoperative antibiotic therapy were administered. No recurrence has been observed during follow-up, supporting the safety and feasibility of this approach. The endoscopic intervention provided useful diagnostic information. In particular, the intra-procedural finding of purulent material and debris supported the clinical impression of an abscess-like lesion. Although this result was not sufficient to definitively confirm the diagnosis in the absence of microbiological or pathological evidence, it helped guide clinical management and made alternative, more serious diagnoses, such as tumor recurrence, less likely. Although delayed postoperative abscess and endoscopic drainage have been reported in previous studies, the present case of presumed anastomotic submucosal abscess following rectal cancer surgery presented several unique challenges for diagnosis and treatment. The extremely late onset, absence of clinical symptoms, atypical EUS features mimicking a solid subepithelial tumor, and multiple therapeutic difficulties were encountered during the endoscopic management of the submucosal lesion at the anastomotic site, including wound closure and complete resection.

This report has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The diagnosis of presumed abscess was based solely on the intraoperative finding of purulent material and yellowish debris, without microbiological culture or histopathological examination of the cavity contents. Consequently, the exact etiology and pathological nature remain unconfirmed. Alternative pathologies, including chronic postoperative cavity, anastomotic sinus formation, implantation cyst, or other benign postoperative changes, may also present with similar endoscopic and ultrasonographic features. The absence of symptoms, normal inflammatory markers, and the very late onset of the lesion eight years after surgery are not typical of an acute or subacute infectious process. Therefore, the lesion is best appropriately described as a chronic presumed abscess. The diagnostic uncertainty highlighted in this case does not diminish the clinical value of the endoscopic intervention, which successfully eliminated the lesion and reduced the risk of future complications. However, whenever possible, microbiological culture or pathological examination is still highly recommended to confirm the diagnosis of similar lesions.

Conclusion

A delayed postoperative anastomotic submucosal abscess-like lesion of the rectum is rare and diagnostically challenging, as it may mimic tumor recurrence or a subepithelial tumor. Although the exact pathological nature of the lesion in this case was not objectively confirmed via microbiological or pathological analysis, the endoscopic incision and debridement successfully resolved the mass without complications. This case suggests that in selected patients with chronic presumed abscess involving the deep muscle layer at the anastomosis, endoscopic incision and debridement combined with short-term antibiotic therapy may represent a safe, minimally invasive option that offers both diagnostic and therapeutic value. However, the lack of definitive confirmation of infection should be acknowledged, and future studies with rigorous diagnostic workup are needed.
