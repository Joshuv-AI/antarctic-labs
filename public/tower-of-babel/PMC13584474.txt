The effect of boarding on students’ noncognitive skills: evidence from rural education in Northwest China
Authors: Bin Tang, Siyi Xiao, Xiaoli Lin, Zeming Cheng, Yingzhe Qin, Yaojiang Shi, Fang Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA significant proliferation of boarding schools and boarding students has been observed in rural areas of China due to the challenges associated with “school consolidation” policies and parental migration from rural to urban areas for employment purposes. Hence, boarding has emerged as a prominent characteristic within the present-day rural student community. However, there remains a lack of consensus regarding the impact of boarding on noncognitive development among rural students. This study investigates the impact of boarding on students’ noncognitive development using a two-period panel dataset collected during the research conducted on rural elementary schools in 2019 and 2020.MethodsUsing a two-period panel dataset comprising 10,923 students from 195 rural elementary schools in 21 counties, we employ both the propensity score matching method and instrumental variable approach to establish a causal relationship between boarding arrangements and students’ noncognitive development.ResultsThis study finds that boarding has detrimental effects on students’ noncognitive skills, negatively impacting certain aspects such as openness and grit, and affinity for schools. The study also reveals that the impact of boarding varies across different student subgroups, with girls, younger students, those from affluent families, and students with more educated mothers benefiting more from the boarding experience. The study also examines pathways through which boarding influences students’ noncognitive development.ConclusionsThis paper proposes the following policy recommendations: (1) At the familial level, parents should allocate additional time and effort towards nurturing their children’s emotional well-being and academic performance. (2) Schools ought to incorporate curriculum training programs aimed at enhancing students’ noncognitive skills, including self-care, resilience, and interpersonal abilities. (3) The government should ensure the cultivation of students’ non-cognitive skills by implementing policies, allocating funds, as well as promoting and raising awareness about the significance of these skills.Keywords: Boarding, Noncognitive development, Rural students, Human capital, Quasi-experimental methods

Background

Traditional human capital theory has predominantly emphasized cognitive abilities such as intelligence, memory, and academic performance as key indicators of human capital. Typically, measures like IQ test scores and standardized tests are utilized for such evaluations. However, recent theoretical advancements recognize noncognitive skills as crucial components of human capital [1, 2]. Noncognitive skills encompass a wide array of personal attributes, attitudes, behaviors, and psychological tendencies that significantly influence students’ future success, including academic achievement, income, and occupational levels [3–8]. Additionally, strong noncognitive skills can mitigate the negative impacts of adverse family socioeconomic conditions, particularly during crucial developmental periods in early childhood and adolescence [7].

Research has identified various familial and educational factors influencing noncognitive skill development. Family income, parenting styles, and parent-child interactions are primary familial determinants. Studies indicate significant disparities in noncognitive skills linked to income levels, with children from lower-income families generally exhibiting weaker skills [9]. Additionally, parenting practices, such as supervision and communication, differentially influence noncognitive skills across genders [10]. In rural China, parental migration for employment significantly contributes to declines in children’s noncognitive skills [11]. At the educational level, factors such as class size, school type, and teacher behavior significantly affect noncognitive skills [12–14]. Rural students are particularly disadvantaged, given their typically marginalized circumstances and limited access to positive reinforcement from teachers [14].

Boarding schools, defined as educational institutions where students reside on campus and return home periodically, have become increasingly prevalent worldwide. Specifically, three types of boarding schools have emerged: private elite institutions, public boarding schools catering to disadvantaged populations, and specialized boarding schools serving distinct educational objectives [15–18]. In China, boarding schools primarily fall into the second category, significantly expanding due to policies such as school consolidation and mergers, combined with rural-urban labor migration. Over the past two decades, China has witnessed a profound urbanization process, accompanied by large-scale rural-to-urban labor migration. These demographic and economic shifts have significantly reduced the number of school-age children in many rural areas. In response, the government introduced a series of school consolidation policies aimed at improving the efficiency of educational resource allocation. As a result, small village schools were merged into larger township boarding schools. This restructuring has made boarding a common educational arrangement for students in remote or depopulated regions. Boarding schools have thus become not only a response to educational challenges but also an institutional adaptation to the broader urbanization and demographic changes reshaping rural China. By 2017, rural boarding students constituted 14.1% of rural compulsory education enrollments [19]. Theoretically, boarding schools provide structured learning environments, prolonged study hours, and close teacher supervision. However, practical challenges, including inadequate facilities, nutritional deficiencies, and emotional isolation due to limited parental interaction, significantly affect students’ emotional well-being and psychological health, particularly during crucial formative years [20–23]. The strict scheduling, regulatory environments, and reduced familial interactions prevalent in boarding schools create unique ecological contexts that shape students’ socialization processes distinctly from their nonboarding peers [15–29].

Boarding schools have been widely used as a policy instrument in various low- and middle-income countries to address geographical and socioeconomic barriers to education. Empirical studies examining the effects of boarding schools present mixed results regarding cognitive and noncognitive outcomes. For example, studies have demonstrated both positive and negative impacts on academic performance among boarding students [30–34]. Concerning noncognitive skills, Behaghel et al. (2017) observed negative impacts on students’ school well-being over prolonged boarding periods, particularly among academically weaker students [30]. Conversely, some scholars argue boarding provides structured environments fostering life skills and self-reliance, beneficial particularly for students from unstable family backgrounds [15, 35]. However, evidence also highlights detrimental outcomes such as higher risks of depression, anxiety, stress, lower emotional intelligence, reduced self-esteem, and resilience among boarding students compared to nonboarding peers [36–37]. For instance, Yao and Gao (2018) found boarding associated with lower emotional intelligence among rural middle school students in China [38]. Similarly, Zhu et al. (2019) reported increased depressive symptoms and lower resilience among boarding students [39].

While these studies offer valuable insights, several critical gaps remain. First, much of the literature relies on cross-sectional data, limiting causal inference. Second, studies often lack rigorous identification strategies to address potential endogeneity in boarding school assignment. Third, research focusing on primary school students, particularly in rural Chinese contexts, remains sparse. Lastly, little is known about how boarding effects vary across subgroups or through what mechanisms boarding impacts noncognitive development.

This study addresses these gaps by employing a quasi-experimental design using a two-period panel dataset collected from rural elementary schools in Northwest China. We use both propensity score matching and a class-level instrumental variable to estimate the causal impact of boarding on multiple noncognitive dimensions, including the Big Five personality traits, grit, locus of control, academic efficacy, and school-related attitudes. Our analysis also explores heterogeneity by student background and examines potential mechanisms such as parental engagement, peer interactions, and behavioral outcomes. By providing robust empirical evidence and new theoretical insights, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of boarding’s role in shaping noncognitive development and informs policy efforts to improve rural education quality and equity.

Methods

Aim, design, and setting of the study

The primary aim of this study is to rigorously investigate the causal impact of boarding on students’ noncognitive skill development in rural Northwest China. The research employs a two-period panel dataset collected in 2019 and 2020, utilizing quasi-experimental methodologies, specifically propensity score matching (PSM) and an instrumental variable (IV) approach, to ensure robust causal inferences. The baseline survey was conducted in December 2019, and the follow-up survey took place in December 2020, allowing for a 12-month interval between waves. The study setting includes three municipalities within Northwest China, where a total of 21 counties were randomly selected for participation. Within each county, several elementary schools were systematically chosen, ultimately including 195 rural elementary schools with six complete grades.

Characteristics of participants

Participants in this study comprised students attending 195 rural elementary schools in Northwest China. In each participating school, the research team randomly selected one third-grade class and one fourth-grade class, creating an initial baseline sample of 10,923 students.

To ensure data integrity, several exclusion criteria were applied sequentially. First, samples lacking complete boarding status information or with missing values on any noncognitive skill indicators at either the baseline (2019) or follow-up (2020) survey were excluded, resulting in a reduced dataset of 5,988 students. Second, based on students’ boarding trajectories between the two survey waves, we identified three groups: (1) students who remained non-boarders at both baseline and follow-up (4,832 students), (2) students who changed from non-boarding to boarding status (261 students), and (3) other transitions (e.g., boarding to non-boarding or keep boarding). After excluding the latter category to maintain consistency in longitudinal comparisons, the final analytic sample comprised 5,093 students.

Among these 5,093 students, approximately 15% were boarding students at follow-up. Students were systematically categorized according to changes in their boarding status between baseline and follow-up to delineate the intervention (newly boarded) and control (non-boarding) groups for subsequent analyses.

In the propensity score matching (PSM) stage, a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching procedure was implemented based on baseline covariates. This yielded 261 treated (new boarders) successfully matched with 261 comparable non-boarders, producing a balanced matched sample of 522 students used in the matching-based regression analysis.

In the study areas of rural Northwest China, “non-boarding” students are those who do not reside on campus during the semester. According to school interviews and field observations from the baseline survey, approximately 85–90% of these students live with their families in nearby villages and commute daily, usually within 1–5 km, reflecting China’s “nearby enrollment” policy and the presence of small village schools (cunxiao). A smaller share (10–15%) live with parents who rent temporary housing in township centers where the schools are located. This proportion remains limited because most rural migrant workers seek employment in county or prefecture-level cities, enrolling their children in urban or suburban schools; such urban migrant children fall outside the scope of this rural-school study.

Although our survey did not directly record the living arrangements of non-boarding students, boarding students’ reported reasons for boarding provide indirect evidence. Among the 895 boarding students, the most common reasons were: school is far from home (61.9%), no one can take care of me at home (11.5%), my parents asked me to board (30.3%), and I want to live with my classmates (25.4%). These results suggest that boarding is mainly driven by distance and caregiving constraints, implying that non-boarding students typically live close to school and/or have caregivers available at home.

This stepwise clarification enhances the transparency of sample construction, provides a clearer understanding of students’ living arrangements, and helps interpret the educational contexts of both boarding and non-boarding students within the rural school system.

Theoretical framework

This paper primarily draws upon the following three theoretical frameworks. The human capital theory underscores that education enhances individual productivity and socioeconomic returns by nurturing both cognitive and non-cognitive skills, such as responsibility and perseverance [40]. As a domain of intensive human capital investment, the closed management system in boarding schools may reinforce students’ non-cognitive skills through time accumulation effects (e.g., extended study hours) and environmental screening effects (e.g., discipline). However, it may also result in skill development heterogeneity due to resource constraints, such as insufficient emotional investment from families. The theory of educational socialization further highlights that the institutionalized school environment transmits social norms and behavioral patterns via implicit curricula, such as collective life rules and role divisions [41–42], thereby shaping students’ self-identity and social adaptability. The characteristic of boarding schools may expedite the internalization of socioemotional skills, such as cooperation and emotion management, but it may concurrently diminish the effectiveness of transmitting official school norms due to the dominance of peer subcultures. Additionally, the theory of the educational production function conceptualizes non-cognitive skills as the core outcome variable of school production from an input-output dynamics perspective [43]. Their formation relies on collaborative inputs from families and schools, such as teacher-student interactions and extracurricular activity resources, as well as substitution relationships, such as teachers partially assuming parental roles under the boarding system.

Description of measures

Independent variables

This study focuses on boarding, with the boarding status of students functioning as the independent variable. This paper utilizes the questionnaire query “Are you boarding this semester?” to determine a student’s boarding status during a particular semester. To estimate the dynamic intervention process, a newly constructed independent variable representing changes in the student’s boarding status is introduced using data from two periods. The classifications are as follows: (1) students who did not board in both the baseline and follow-up periods, (2) students who did not board at baseline but boarded during the follow-up period, (3) students who boarded in both the baseline and follow-up periods, and (4) students who boarded at baseline but did not board during the follow-up period. The students in category (2) are designated the intervention group in this paper; they are referred to as the “boarding” group. In contrast, students in category (1) constitute the control group. By comparing the noncognitive skills of these two groups, the effect of boarding on students can be determined. The newly generated dichotomous independent variable is designated a value of 1 for the intervention group and 0 for the control group.

Dependent variables

Consistent with prior literature on noncognitive skills, we conceptualize noncognitive skills as encompassing both stable personality-related traits and context-specific socioemotional attitudes that shape learning behaviors and well-being. Accordingly, the Big Five Personality traits, grit, and locus of control are treated as core personality-based noncognitive skills, while academic efficacy, self-efficacy in enlisting social resources, school liking, and school avoidance are classified as school-related socioemotional and attitudinal outcomes, representing context-dependent expressions of noncognitive competencies in school settings.

Core personality-based noncognitive skills are measured as follows:

Big Five Personality Traits: This scale comprises 46 items designed to measure openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Students rated statements reflecting various characteristics such as being talkative, relaxed, trusting, or nervous on a 5-point scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” [44]. Higher scores indicate a stronger presence of each respective trait. Notably, high scores on neuroticism indicate lower levels of noncognitive skills.

Covariates

Several covariates were controlled to address potential confounding factors influencing noncognitive skill development. These covariates included students’ demographic characteristics such as gender, grade level (third vs. fourth grade), socioeconomic status (captured through family economic condition categories), ethnicity (minority status), whether the student was an only child, and parental education levels (whether parents had completed junior high school).

All of the aforementioned variables are regarded as binary variables for analysis, with values of 0 and 1. The gender variable is specifically coded as 0 for boys and 1 for girls. Another variable is created on the basis of grade level, delineating between third graders (coded as 1) and fourth graders (coded as 0). There are five levels of family economic status: extremely difficult, relatively difficult, moderate, relatively affluent, and extremely wealthy. A new variable, titled poverty, is created to capture the “very difficult” (coded as 1) option while 0 is assigned to the other categories. In addition, a variable titled “low education” distinguishes parents who have not completed junior high school (coded as 1) from those with a higher level of education (coded as 0).

Clear description of processes and interventions

Data collection involved administering structured questionnaires during school hours under the supervision of trained researchers. Students provided self-reported data on boarding status, noncognitive skills, and demographic characteristics at baseline and follow-up time points. The core intervention examined was the change from nonboarding to boarding status between these two measurement points, thus clearly defining the intervention group.

Comparisons

The main comparison in this study was between students who transitioned into boarding status (intervention group) and students who consistently did not board (control group). To ensure robust comparability between these two groups, a one-to-one matching process was implemented using the Mahalanobis distance method based on students’ baseline noncognitive skill scores and demographic characteristics.

Research methods and models

Propensity score matching

The matching method identifies appropriate control group samples for the intervention group based on covariates. This approach can be categorized into propensity score matching (PSM) and Mahalanobis distance matching according to the calculation rules, or one-to-one/one-to-many matching depending on the sample proportions. Originating from statistical experimental design, this method has become widely adopted in policy evaluation and intervention effect estimation [53–58]. In quasi-experimental designs, self-selection behavior may introduce systematic differences between the intervention and control groups (i.e., selection bias [59]), thereby preventing the observed differences from accurately reflecting the intervention effects. Matching addresses this issue by filtering out comparable samples using covariates such as socioeconomic characteristics or baseline scores, thereby excluding individuals who are not representative of the counterfactual scenario. PSM calculates the propensity score (i.e., the probability of receiving the intervention [60]) using relevant variables for matching purposes.

Conversely, Mahalanobis distance matching enhances accuracy by calculating distances in multi-dimensional covariate space and is frequently combined with one-to-one matching to effectively mitigate potential limitations of PSM [61]. In this study, 261 students experiencing changes in boarding status were matched one-to-one using Mahalanobis distance, with the control group constructed using baseline personal characteristics and non-cognitive skills as covariates.

Instrumental variable approach

To address potential endogeneity in boarding status, we adopt an instrumental variable (IV) approach using the proportion of students in each class whose boarding status changed between the baseline and follow-up surveys. This variable is plausibly exogenous because, in rural China, students are typically randomly assigned to classes at the start of primary school, limiting systematic correlation between class composition and unobserved family characteristics.

The instrument meets the standard IV conditions: it is strongly correlated with the endogenous regressor (boarding status), as students’ decisions to board tend to shift together within classes, and it is unlikely to affect noncognitive outcomes directly, except through boarding. The F-statistic from the first-stage regression (208.81) strongly rejects concerns about weak instruments (Table 9). This involves regressing the noncognitive skill scores for the baseline and follow-up periods, as well as the boarding status in the follow-up period. After matching the covariates, the research sample is generated, and regression analysis is performed. The dependent variable is noncognitive skill scores in the follow-up period, and the independent variable is the newly created variable “becoming boarding”. The covariate variables include the students’ basic personal characteristics and their noncognitive skill scores at the baseline of the study. Finally, the robustness of the effect of boarding will be examined via instrumental variable analysis, and the heterogeneity of the effect will be analyzed on the basis of students’ fundamental personal characteristics, such as gender and age.

Robustness check: instrumental variables approach

This paper employs an instrumental variable regression design within a quasi-experimental framework to further assess the robustness of the study’s findings regarding the impact of boarding on students’ noncognitive skills. According to the requirements for instrumental variables, the chosen variable should not be correlated with known covariates but rather with the independent variable of becoming boarding. This ensures that the variable influences students’ noncognitive skills solely via its effect on their boarding status, thereby satisfying the aforementioned requirements. Previous studies have utilized school-to-home distance as an established instrumental variable [62–63]. However, because this study lacks data on students’ school-to-home distance, it is not possible to use the instrumental variable from previous research. In this study, the proportion of students whose boarding status has changed in each class relative to the class size serves as the independent variable. This variable has been utilized frequently in studies examining similar topics, such as the effects of private tutoring on students’ national college entrance exam performance and the effects of boarding on obesity among secondary school students [64–65].

The variable meets the criteria for an instrumental variable. After careful consideration, this paper chooses the proportion of students whose boarding status has changed in relation to the total number of students in the class as an instrumental variable. A meticulous analysis revealed that there was no correlation between the proportion of boarding students in each class and the students’ baseline characteristics or noncognitive skills. According to our interviews, current elementary school class assignments are predominantly arbitrary, in accordance with Ministry of Education regulations. Therefore, there is no correlation between the percentage of boarding students in a class and covariate variables such as academic performance and family socioeconomic status. Moreover, changes in the boarding status of individual students have a direct effect on the proportion of boarding students relative to the total number of students in each class. Therefore, changes in the boarding status of individual students correlate directly with the proportion of boarding students at the class level.

To empirically support the assumption of random class assignment, we estimated logit regressions using classroom dummies as the dependent variable and a full set of student background characteristics as independent variables. The resulting pseudo R² values are extremely low (baseline model: 0.0045; with county fixed effects and clustered standard errors: 0.018;due to the constraints of space, the detailed regression table would not be presented here), suggesting that classroom composition is not systematically predicted by family or demographic covariates. These findings support the validity of our instrumental variable strategy and align with Ministry of Education regulations promoting random assignment in rural public schools.

One potential concern is that the instrument, class-level changes in boarding status, may affect students’ noncognitive outcomes not only through their own boarding transition, but also via peer effects or changes in classroom environment. If students are influenced by the boarding status of their classmates (e.g., through changes in group norms or behavior), then the exclusion restriction may be partially violated. While we cannot completely rule out this possibility, we believe such peer effects are likely limited for two reasons. First, boarding activities primarily take place outside of classroom hours (e.g., in dormitories), and the structure of classroom teaching and curriculum remains consistent across students regardless of their boarding status. Second, the boarding decision is implemented at the individual or household level, and the program does not involve coordinated group-based boarding initiatives that would systematically change class dynamics. Nevertheless, we acknowledge this as a limitation and encourage future research to further investigate the potential interaction between boarding and peer environments.

Table 9 displays the results of the weak instrumental variable test for the chosen instrumental variable, which indicates that the F statistic of 208.810 exceeds the critical value of 10. This finding indicates that the instrumental variable effectively passes the weak instrumental variable test.

The effects of boarding estimated via the instrumental variable method are presented in Row 1 of Table 10. In openness and agreeableness, boarding students score 0.191 and 0.257 lower than the control group does, as shown in Columns 1 and 4 of Panel A, Row 1. These differences are statistically significant at the 5% and 1% levels, respectively. The data in Columns 6 of Panel A, Row 1 indicate that boarding students score 0.197 lower than the control group in terms of grit. The statistical significance of these differences is 1%. In addition, the data in Table 11, Panel B, Row 1, Column 3–4 reveal that boarding students score 0.801 lower and 0.655 higher than the control group in terms of school liking and school avoidance, a difference that is statistically significant at the 5% level. The results of the instrumental variable regression largely corroborate the above findings concerning the effect of boarding on noncognitive skills. The direction of the effect, the magnitude of the coefficients, and the significance levels all indicate that boarding has a significant negative effect on the noncognitive skills of students. As a consequence, the primary regression results of this study are regarded as robust.

Despite some variation in significance levels across the unmatched, matched, and instrumental variable models, the overall pattern consistently indicates that boarding exerts negative effects on multiple dimensions of noncognitive development. These findings are further reinforced by results from multiple hypothesis testing using the Romano-Wolf stepdown procedure. After adjusting for family-wise error rates, boarding is shown to significantly impair students’ openness, locus of control, school liking, and school avoidance. Although some dimensions, such as grit and self-efficacy, exhibit weaker or model-dependent effects, the general direction and consistency of the results across methods and correction procedures underscore the robustness of the main conclusion: boarding has measurable and adverse impacts on students’ noncognitive skill formation (Table 11).

Four groups were defined based on students’ boarding trajectories between baseline (2019) and follow-up (2020): (1) consistently non-boarding, (2) newly boarded, (3) consistently boarding, and (4) stopped boarding. A supplementary comparison between Groups 3 and 4 showed no significant differences in any noncognitive outcomes, likely because of the small sample size and short observation period, suggesting that the negative effects observed for newly boarded students are not merely attributable to environmental change.

From the first perspective of gender, we find that boarding has a more significant impact on girls’ conscientiousness (Row 1, Column 2) and agreeableness (Row 1, Column 4) of the Big Five personality traits, which is consistent with previous research on gender differences [66–67]. This may be attributed to gendered personality traits and the role of gender as a moderating factor influencing individual responsiveness to external expectations [68]. Social role theory also suggests that individuals of different genders develop distinct group identities, leading them to behave differently in collective settings [69]. Moreover, girls may be more inclined to adapt to group life through cooperation and emotional expression, which enhances their agreeableness. Group home living increases peer contact and emotional exchange, providing girls with more opportunities for companionship and social support [15]. Therefore, girls’ stronger identification with group norms may explain why they are more positively influenced by the structured, communal learning environment and external discipline provided by boarding schools.

Second, we find that boarding reduces school avoidance by 0.594 SDs (p < 0.05) among third graders compared to fourth graders. This suggests that younger students may benefit more from structured routines and peer support provided in boarding environments, which may buffer against psychological stress or family instability common in rural areas. The more positive influence of boarding for younger students may stem from their greater developmental plasticity and adaptability to school-based norms and support systems [70–72].

Third, we find that boarding has a relatively smaller impact on openness and agreeableness among economically disadvantaged students, with reductions of 0.357 SDs and 0.260 SDs respectively (Row 3, Column1 and Column4). Conversely, boarding significantly enhances school liking for these students, with an increase of 0.863 SDs (Row 3, Column 10). These contrasting results may reflect the dual role of boarding for students from low-income families. While boarding schools provide a safer and more structured environment that enhances school affinity, they may not offer sufficient emotional or social resources to support the development of traits like openness and agreeableness, which depend more heavily on high-quality interpersonal interactions and psychological security [73].

Fourth, for only children, transitioning from a non-boarding to a boarding status is associated with a large but statistically insignificant reduction in self-efficacy in enlisting social resources (− 1.690 SDs, Row 4, Column 9), while no significant impacts are observed across other non-cognitive dimensions, such as school liking, locus of control, and Big Five personality traits. Although this effect is not significant at conventional levels, its magnitude is notable. This pattern may reflect the role of siblings in providing an informal support system during early socialization. Through daily interactions, siblings often help children develop cooperation, communication, and emotional regulation skills [74–76]. In contrast, only children, lacking such intra-family peer interactions, may find it more difficult to adapt to the unfamiliar social dynamics of boarding environments. This absence of early, sibling-based social learning may hinder their ability to establish supportive peer networks, consequently lowering their perceived capacity to seek help or resources from others.

Fifth, among ethnic minority students, transitioning from non-boarding to boarding status significantly decreased self-efficacy in enlisting social resources by 0.980 SDs (Row 5, Column 9), while effects on other noncognitive dimensions remained largely insignificant. For minority students, boarding in Han-culture-dominated environments may involve substantial cultural adaptation pressures, including differences in language, customs, dietary habits, and social interactions [77]. Prior studies have shown that ethnic minority students often experience cultural marginalization in mainstream educational settings, which may elevate psychological stress and emotional vulnerability [78, 79]. Moreover, standardized boarding school management and limited teacher resources may offer insufficient cultural accommodation and emotional support [80], exacerbating low self-efficacy among minority students.

Finally, we observe distinct patterns between fathers and mothers with education levels below junior high school. For students whose fathers have lower educational attainment, the transition from non-boarding to boarding is not significantly associated with changes in any of the noncognitive skill dimensions. This may indicate a generally weak baseline level of parental involvement, especially given that fathers in rural China are more likely to migrate for work and be physically absent from their children’s daily lives [81]. In contrast, for students whose mothers have similarly low education levels, boarding has significant adverse effects: it reduces openness to experience by 0.368 SDs (Row 7, Column 1). These findings suggest that boarding may deprive students of the limited but essential emotional support provided by less-educated mothers, who often serve as primary caregivers in rural households and may lack the resources to help children adjust to institutional environments [82, 83]. The adverse effect on openness underscores the role of maternal presence in fostering emotional expressiveness and adaptability.

In addition, the heterogeneity analysis provides supporting evidence for the mechanisms discussed above. The impact of boarding varies across subgroups, reflecting differences in developmental needs, family background, and social context. Younger students benefit more from structured routines and teacher supervision, showing reduced school avoidance. Girls respond more positively in traits like conscientiousness and agreeableness, possibly due to stronger identification with group norms. In contrast, only children experience declines in social self-efficacy, likely due to limited early peer interaction. Economically disadvantaged students show improved school liking but diminished openness and agreeableness, suggesting emotional support remains insufficient. Boarding decreases self-efficacy in enlisting social resources among ethnic minority students, possibly due to cultural adjustment stress, and has adverse effects on openness for students whose mothers have low education levels, highlighting the importance of caregiver support.

Overall, the empirical patterns point to the conclusion that boarding influences noncognitive development not only through structural exposure to school routines, but also by reshaping students’ daily environments and social contexts in ways that affect emotional well-being, behavioral norms, and interpersonal relationships.

Conclusions and policy implications

Main findings and contribution

This paper uses data from a two-period panel study conducted in 2019 and 2020 in rural elementary schools in Northwest China. The purpose of this study is to examine the impact of boarding on students’ noncognitive skills. To address the issue of endogeneity, quasi-experimental designs such as matching and instrumental variable regression are utilized, substantially mitigating the likelihood of bias. The primary results and conclusions of this investigation are provided below.

This study initially examined the prevalence of boarding students and the fluctuations in their boarding status in rural regions of China. Approximately 15% of the 5,093 students included in the sample are boarding students. In addition, between 2019 and 2020, a total of 261 students, or approximately 5%, changed their status from nonboarding to boarding. These numbers suggest a sizeable proportion of boarding students in rural boarding schools and a relatively stable boarding status for students.

Second, this study investigates the impact of boarding on the noncognitive skills of students. This study classifies noncognitive skills into eleven dimensions. In general, boarding has a significant negative effect on certain indicators of noncognitive skills but has no effect on others. Specifically, boarding decreases students’ openness by 0.159. The characteristics of openness include imagination, aesthetics, emotional sensitivity, a willingness to attempt new things, critical thinking, and the acceptance of diverse values. The negative effect of boarding on openness suggests a shift in students’ cognitive tendencies, from imaginative and idealistic tendencies to a more pragmatic and practical outlook. In addition, boarding has a negative effect on students’ school liking and school avoidance, thereby diminishing their overall preference for school and increasing their emotions of aversion and resistance. The findings suggest that boarding may be associated with lower openness and reduced emotional engagement with school, which could reflect more limited social and emotional responsiveness among students. The instrumental variable analysis yields results largely consistent with the main findings, supporting the robustness of the observed associations.

Third, the effects of boarding on students’ noncognitive skills are heterogeneous. With respect to the Big Five personality traits, boarding has a greater positive effect on girls than boys. Additionally, the impact is more beneficial for third graders than for fourth graders. With respect to a few indicators, children with siblings and Han students have a more positive response to boarding than do children without siblings and students from ethnic minorities. Similarly, students whose mothers have completed at least junior high school benefit more from boarding than do those whose mothers have not attained this level of education.

Compared with current studies, this research makes three key contributions to the literature. First, while most prior studies on boarding schools focus on academic outcomes, relatively little attention has been paid to non-cognitive skills, particularly in under-resourced rural settings. This study contributes to the literature by providing a systematic empirical evaluation of how boarding school experiences shape students’ non-cognitive abilities such as grit, self-control, and interpersonal skills. Second, we employ a rigorous identification strategy using PSM and IV based on large-scale panel data from rural areas. This enables us to more credibly estimate the causal effects of boarding status on non-cognitive development and to explore heterogeneous impacts across gender, age and socioeconomic background. Third, we go beyond average treatment effects by exploring underlying mechanisms through which boarding may affect non-cognitive outcomes, such as parent-child interaction, peer environment, and risky behaviors. Moreover, in resource-constrained regions, where access to supportive learning environments is limited, boarding schools may amplify both the effects on child development. This study provides empirical evidence that may inform policies aimed at enhancing human capital formation and reducing educational inequality among vulnerable student populations. The insights derived from this context also hold value for policymakers in other developing countries facing similar structural constraints in educational delivery.

Policy implications and limitations

The specific policy implications of this paper are as follows, on the basis of the above findings, to increase the level of noncognitive skills of students in rural areas and promote the accumulation of human capital in rural areas:

First and foremost, it is essential for parents to increase their focus and guidance on the noncognitive development of their children. In addition to focusing on their children’s daily lives and academic progress, parents should pay attention to their children’s emotional well-being and mental states reflected in their daily lives. Observing their children’s responses to adversity, challenges, collaborative situations, and interpersonal interactions is also essential [90, 91]. Parents who are able to do so should facilitate the development of their children’s noncognitive skills in all aspects, encourage their participation in competitive sports [92], foster proactive communication and task-taking initiatives, and guide their children to approach problem solving independently. By fostering a resilient disposition during adolescence, students are better equipped to handle academic pressures and navigate the obstacles of school life.

Second, schools should prioritize the training and education of noncognitive skills, especially in boarding school contexts. Noncognitive skills encompass an extensive array of socioemotional competencies, such as adaptability, interpersonal interaction, resiliency in the face of adversity, self-esteem and self-confidence. Opportunities for students to engage in socioemotional training are scarce in the educational landscape of rural China, which typically places a preponderant emphasis on test performance [93]. Social-emotional competence should be incorporated into the elementary school curriculum, with particular attention given to its implementation in boarding schools, according to the findings of this study. For example, to enhance the development of students’ noncognitive skills in boarding school settings, we recommend integrating structured socio-emotional learning (SEL) programs into the regular school curriculum [94]. Social emotion courses can be provided to students regularly, courses in classroom practice can be adjusted to achieve better teaching results, and an appropriate ability assessment system to strive for students to improve their social emotion ability significantly after receiving course training can be explored [95]. Since there is a correlation between noncognitive skills and students’ mental health, these pertinent socioemotional courses can also enhance students’ mental health. The classroom should serve as the primary venue for delivering such training, assist students in acquiring knowledge and skills related to mental health, foster an awareness of self-help and mutual support, instill the ability to approach challenges and setbacks rationally, and integrate various forms of life education and frustration education [96, 97]. The development of social-emotional competencies, a significant component of noncognitive skills, should be bolstered to achieve the goal of fostering intellectual, physical, and aesthetic growth that is well rounded.

Third, the government should increase the value of noncognitive skills and increase public awareness of their significance. This can be accomplished by implementing policies and allocating funds at the government level, thereby guaranteeing the holistic development of students’ noncognitive skills. Moreover, the significance of noncognitive skills to all sectors of society should be promoted. The government, colleges, universities, and other educational institutions can collaborate to provide schools with noncognitive ability-related curriculum resources. In addition, the government should actively promote the significance of noncognitive skills within the community. This will allow schools, students, parents, and other stakeholders to recognize the importance of developing socioemotional competencies during adolescence.

Fourth, the selection of a large-scale sample (5,093 students from 195 rural schools across 21 counties) ensures the representativeness of this study for Northwestern China, a region where boarding schools are prevalent due to geographic dispersion and labor outmigration. Although the data primarily pertain to rural Northwest China, the findings may offer useful implications for other rural regions with similar socioeconomic contexts, such as labor outflow and a relatively underdeveloped economy. For instance, studies by Chen et al. (2020) and Chang et al. (2023) have shown that boarding arrangements affect students’ emotional development and peer relationships, a finding that aligns with the conclusions drawn in this study [62, 67]. From an international perspective, economically disadvantaged regions are typically confronted with the dual challenges of disrupted family support systems and inadequate socialization functions within schools. Consequently, these results may serve as a valuable policy reference for other developing countries facing similar challenges. This is particularly relevant in the design of policies aimed at balancing the scale of educational resources with the mental health needs of children.

While this paper provides a comprehensive analysis of the effects of boarding on students’ noncognitive skills, it is crucial to note that the shorter follow-up time may limit the estimated impact of boarding in this study. The sample of the intervention group in this paper consists of students who did not board in 2019 but board in 2020 and whose boarding duration was between one and two semesters. Consequently, it is necessary to consider whether such a duration is adequate for observing the complete effects of boarding. It is also possible that the initial decline in noncognitive skills is due to temporary maladjustment to boarding, which is likely to improve over time. To investigate how the duration of boarding affects students’ noncognitive skills, it is necessary to conduct additional follow-up studies with extended follow-up periods. Furthermore, although we control for baseline boarding status, our two-wave panel design limits our ability to assess long-term cumulative effects of boarding experiences. Future studies with extended longitudinal data are encouraged to examine this dimension more thoroughly. To delve deeper into the discussion of these mechanisms, future studies should include additional research periods and relevant questionnaire items to investigate potential mechanisms, thereby allowing for more robust research conclusions. Finally, it is important to note that the “non-boarding” category includes both daily commuters from nearby villages and a smaller group of township-residing students living with their parents. Although this within-category variation is limited, it may influence family supervision and socioemotional support. Future research could further distinguish these subgroups to better understand the mechanisms underlying boarding effects.
