Uncertainty Principles for the Number Theoretic Transform
Giulio Malavolta, Alon Rosen

Abstract
Motivated by polynomial identity testing with exponentials (Li and Wu, ITCS’26), we study uncertainty principles for the number-theoretic transform (NTT). We show that the NTT satisfies strong sparsity tradeoffs: For every fixed prime q q and for all but finitely many primes p ≡ 1 ( mod q ) p\equiv 1\pmod{q} every nonzero f ∈ 𝔽 p ℤ q f\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}} and its number-theoretic transform f ^ \hat{f} satisfy | Supp ⁡ ( f ) | + | Supp ⁡ ( f ^ ) | ≥ q + 1 . |\mathrm{Supp}(f)|+|\mathrm{Supp}(\hat{f})|\geq q+1. Thus, a k k -sparse function has transform support at least q − k + 1 q-k+1 . As our main technical contribution, we prove a probabilistic version of the above uncertainty principle, averaged over primes p p , in the regime p = q O ⁡ ( 1 ) p=q^{O(1)} . As an application, we obtain a black-box identity test for k k -sparse exponential polynomials of degree at most d d with vanishing soundness error, for q q moderately larger than k k .



1
Introduction

Polynomial identity testing (PIT) asks whether a given algebraic expression is identically zero.
For ordinary low-degree polynomials over a field, this problem is well understood: One can
evaluate the polynomial at a random point, and the Schwartz–Zippel lemma
[
10
,
17
,
4
]
shows that every
nonzero degree-
d
polynomial vanishes with probability at most
d/|S|
over a finite test set
S
.

In a recent work
[
7
]
Li and Wu introduced the analogous problem for expressions that contain exponentials. A
typical object has the form

P(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(x)\exp\left(\frac{g_{j}(x)}{h_{j}(x)}\right),

where the
f_{j},g_{j},h_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
are polynomials. The identity-testing problem is to decide whether
P
is identically zero on its domain. This differs from standard PIT in two basic ways: First, the expression is no longer a polynomial, so the Schwartz–Zippel lemma does not apply directly. Second, there is no canonical notion of evaluating
\exp(\cdot)
over a finite field, so even defining a useful black-box testing model requires care.

This problem is motivated by recent advances in machine-learning. In optimization compilers for tensor programs, such as Mirage
[
15
]
one often needs to test whether two subprograms are functionally equivalent. For purely algebraic fragments, randomized finite-field testing is already useful. The next
natural step is to handle fragments that involve exponentials, since operations such as
normalization and softmax introduce this type of structure. Li and Wu
[
7
]
formalized a finite-field
evaluation model for such expressions by using two primes
p
and
q
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
: Arithmetic inside the
exponent is carried out modulo
q
, arithmetic outside the exponent is carried out modulo
p
,
and exponentials are interpreted using the unique subgroup of order
q
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
. This gives
a mathematically clean black-box model and matches the algebraic structure used by the verifier in Mirage
[
15
]
.

The central challenge is in proving soundness. The main result of
[
7
]
shows that, for a sum of
k

exponential terms of degree
d
, the acceptance probability on a nonzero polynomial is at most
q^{-1/(k-1)}+O(dk^{2}/q)
. In the setting of interest where
q=k^{O(1)}
, the soundness error is therefore
1-\Theta({\log q}/{k})
. It is left as an open problem to prove a constant soundness error for the same test, which is conjectured to hold in light of empirical evidence
[
15
]
.

Li and Wu
[
7
]
suggested that this conjecture is related to
uncertainty principles
over finite fields. More specifically, let
G\leq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
be the unique subgroup of order
q
, they define a linear map from polynomials modulo
x^{q}-1
to their evaluations on
G
. In cryptography, this is known as (the inverse of) the
number-theoretic transform
(NTT) which, among other things, is routinely used for fast implementations of lattice-related cryptographic schemes
[
6
,
8
,
9
]
. The conjecture can be interpreted as a
strong
uncertainty principle of the NTT and it can be equivalently stated as follows:
For sufficiently large
q
and
p
, any univariate non-zero polynomial

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}z^{\alpha_{j}},

with
k\leq q^{O(1)}
distinct non-zero coefficients, has at most
O(q)
roots in
G
. A formal version of this conjecture is stated in
4.1
.

1.1
Our Results

Motivated by the above algorithmic applications, we prove a series of uncertainty principles for the NTT. Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
and let
\hat{f}
be its NTT, with
\mathrm{Supp}(f)
denote the non-zero coordinates of
f
. Our first result is a
strong
uncertainty principle for the NTT for infinitely many
p
and
q
.

Theorem 1.1

(Additive Uncertainty, Informal)
.

For every fixed prime
q
, for all but finitely many primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, every non-zero
f\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
satisfies

|\mathrm{Supp}(f)|+|\mathrm{Supp}(\hat{f})|\geq q+1.

A formal version of is given in
Theorem

3.1
. This implies, as an immediate corollary, a non-uniform version of
4.1
, in the settings where
p
is allowed to grow with
q
. In fact, we obtain a much stronger and essentially optimal bound in these settings.

We stress that the well-known Donoho–Stark
[
5
,
14
]

multiplicative
uncertainty principle over finite fields (i.e., where the bound is on the product of the cardinalities of the supports) does
not
yield any meaningful bound in our context. To bear any implication on the soundness of the identity tester, one needs
additive
uncertainty principles, such as
Theorem

3.1
.

A limitation of the above result is that it does not offer a good control on the growth of
p
with respect to
q
. A quantitative analysis shows that the theorem holds for any
p
larger than
\approx q^{q^{2}}
, which is quite far from what is used in practice
[
15
]
. In the more realistic settings where
p=q^{O(1)}
,
Theorem

3.1
does not apply.

Motivated by this, we prove a weaker
approximate
uncertainty relation, which also applies to the settings where
p
is polynomially bounded in
q
. Such a relation holds for any fixed input vector, but only on average over the random choice of
p
from the appropriate interval. The following theorem is an informal version of
Theorem

3.6
.

Theorem 1.2

(Approximate Uncertainty, Informal)
.

There exist infinitely many primes
q
such that, for any non-zero
f\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
with
|\mathrm{Supp}(f)|<q
, then

\E_{p\leq q^{4}}\bigl(|\mathrm{Supp}(\hat{f})|\bigr)\geq q-1-\frac{32(q-1)\log q}{q^{2}}\log_{2}\|{f}\|_{1}.

While this bound does not have any direct implication on the tester proposed in
[
7
]
, we show how a comparatively simple modification of their test allows us to prove much stronger soundness bounds. In short, if one is willing to additionally randomize the choice of
p
(sampled from the appropriate interval), then one obtains a test where every nonzero input is accepted with probability at most
\approx{dk^{2}}/{q}
. In particular, once
q
is moderately larger than
dk^{2}
, the test has constant soundness.

Theorem 1.3

(PIT with Exponentials, Informal)
.

There exists a randomized test such that, for all integer-coefficient
k
-sparse exponential polynomials
P\not\equiv 0
of degree at most
d
and coefficients bounded by
w
, the test accepts with probability at most

O\!\left(\frac{dk^{2}}{q}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{\log(kw)\log q}{q^{2}}\right).

We refer the reader to
Theorem

4.3
for a precise statement. We highlight that, if
q=k^{C}
with
C>2
and
kw
is polynomially bounded, then the soundness error is
o(1)
, directly improving over
[
7
]
, where the bound is dominated by the term
q^{-1/(k-1)}=1-o(1)
.

Besides testing equivalence of program fragments that contain exponentials, this test may be useful for verifying arithmetic circuits with exponentiation gates and may provide a soundness primitive for proof systems built for such circuits.

1.2
Proof Overview

Recall that the number-theoretic transform (NTT) is the finite-field analogue of the discrete Fourier transform. Fix primes
q
and
p
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, and let
g\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
have order
q
. Then evaluation on the subgroup
G=\{1,g,g^{2},\dots,g^{q-1}\}
defines an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear isomorphism
E:\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{q}-1)\mapsto\mathbb{F}_{p}^{q}

E(f)=\bigl(f(1),f(g),\dots,f(g^{q-1})\bigr)

whose inverse
\mathcal{F}=E^{-1}
is the NTT. Writing
f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}c_{j}x^{j}
, the evaluation map is represented by the Vandermonde matrix

M=(g^{tj})_{t,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}},

so uncertainty statements for the NTT naturally translate to statements about sparse polynomials and their evaluations on
G
. A good strategy in this context
[
11
]
, is to show
hyperinvertibility
of
M
, i.e., that every square submatrix of
M
must be invertible. A routine calculation shows that this implies the strong (additive) uncertainty principle of the associated Fourier transform.

When the co-domain of the map is
\mathbb{C}
, one can show that
M
is hyperinvertible
[
11
]
, so we seek a finite-field analogue of this statement. The main difficulty is that such hyperinvertibility does not automatically translate over finite fields, and the direct analogue is in fact false
[
16
]
. Fortunately, a recent result of Caragea et al.
[
3
]
shows that, if
p
is sufficiently large compared to
q
, then the hyperinvertibility of
M
holds. We can directly use this fact to establish our first uncertainty principle. In
Section

3.1
, we also provide a somewhat more direct proof of this fact, using properties of the resultant of polynomials.

Yet the above proof is not useful for algorithmic applications, since a conservative bound on the size of
p
results into
p\approx q^{q^{2}}
, which is not realistic. To handle the regime where
p=q^{O(1)}
, we instead prove a weaker
probabilistic
property, averaging over primes
p
over a bounded interval. The idea is as follows, fix a nonzero

f(z)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}c_{j}z^{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]

with
|\mathrm{Supp}(f)|<q
, then
f
cannot vanish at a primitive
q
-th root of unity: Otherwise the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{q}(z)=1+z+\cdots+z^{q-1}
would divide
f
, forcing
f
to be a scalar multiple of
\Phi_{q}
, which has full support
q
. It follows that their resultant
\mathrm{Res}(f,\Phi_{q})
is a nonzero integer. Poisson’s formula for the resultant gives

|\mathrm{Res}(f,\Phi_{q})|=\prod_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{*}}|f(\omega^{b})|\leq\|{f}\|_{1}^{q-1}.

If
f
has an order-
q
root modulo some prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, then
f
and
\Phi_{q}
have a common root modulo
p
, and hence
p\mid\mathrm{Res}(f,\Phi_{q})
. Thus there are at most
(q-1)\log_{2}\|{f}\|_{1}
such bad primes.
To make this useful in the polynomial-size regime, we sample
p
from the interval

\mathcal{P}(q)=\{p\leq q^{4}:\ p\text{ prime and }p\equiv 1\pmod{q}\}.

We then appeal to the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem
[
1
,
13
]
to show that there are infinitely many primes
q
for which

|\mathcal{P}(q)|\geq\frac{q^{3}}{32\log q}.

Combining this estimate with the above bound, we obtain our approximate uncertainty principle.

This directly suggests a natural one-query zero-tester for polynomials
P
with exponentiations. Given a polynomial
P
we sample:

•

A random admissible prime
p\in\mathcal{P}(q)
.

•

A random point
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
for the exponents.

•

A random point
u\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
for the coefficients.

•

A generator
g
of the order-
q
subgroup
G\leq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
.

•

A random
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, and set
a=g^{r}
.

We then evaluate the algebraic extension

P_{p,q,a}(u,v)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(u)\,a^{g_{j}(v)h_{j}(v)^{-1}},

declaring the value undefined if some denominator vanishes, and accept iff the value is either
0
or undefined.

The soundness proof is as follows. First, by Schwartz–Zippel and a union bound, we bound the probability that all denominators are nonzero and the exponents
\alpha_{j}=g_{j}(v)h_{j}(v)^{-1}

are pairwise distinct. Conditioned on this event, the test evaluates the ordinary polynomial

H_{a,v}(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(X)a^{\alpha_{j}}

at a random point
u\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. If
H_{a,v}\not\equiv 0
, Schwartz–Zippel gives error at most
d/(q+1)
. If
H_{a,v}\equiv 0
, then some nonzero monomial coefficient vector
\eta=([X^{\mu}]f_{1},\ldots,[X^{\mu}]f_{k})
, with
\|\eta\|_{1}\leq kw
, satisfies

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}g^{r\alpha_{j}}=0.

This is exactly the event that a random coordinate of the evaluation vector of a sparse integer polynomial with
\ell_{1}
-norm at most
kw
vanishes. The approximate uncertainty principle is precisely a bound on the probability of this event. The bound on the soundness error of the test is then obtained by combining the above estimates.

2
Preliminaries

We write
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
for
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. If
S
is finite,
x\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)
means that
x
is sampled uniformly from
S
. For a vector
v=(v_{a})_{a\in A}
, write

\mathrm{Supp}(v):=\{a\in A:v_{a}\neq 0\}.

All logarithms are natural unless the base is displayed explicitly.

2.1
Resultants

We recall the elementary properties of the resultant that will be used throughout. If
K
is a field and
f,g\in K[z]
are nonzero, then

\mathrm{Res}(f,g)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\text{ and }g\text{ have a common root over }\overline{K}.

(1)

Equivalently,
\gcd(f,g)\neq 1
in
K[z]
. If
g
is monic, Poisson’s product formula gives

|\mathrm{Res}(f,g)|=\prod_{\alpha:g(\alpha)=0}|f(\alpha)|,

(2)

where the product is taken in an algebraic closure, with multiplicities.

Lemma 2.1

(Modular Reduction)
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
, and let
p
be prime. If the reductions

f_{p},g_{p}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[z]
have a common root over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, then
p\mid\mathrm{Res}(f,g)
.

Proof.

Let
m=\deg f
and
n=\deg g
, and form the Sylvester matrix of
f
and
g
using these original degrees. Its determinant is
\mathrm{Res}(f,g)
. Reducing this matrix modulo
p
gives the corresponding Sylvester matrix with the same prescribed sizes for
f_{p}
and
g_{p}
, possibly with zero leading coefficients if degrees drop.

If
f_{p}
and
g_{p}
have a common root over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, then they are not coprime in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[z]
. Hence there exist nonzero polynomials
A,B\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[z]
, with
\deg A<n
and
\deg B<m
, such that

Af_{p}+Bg_{p}=0.

Equivalently, the reduced Sylvester matrix has a nontrivial kernel. Its determinant is therefore zero in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{Res}(f,g)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
∎

2.2
Schwartz–Zippel

We recall the Schwartz–Zippel lemma
[
10
,
17
,
4
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Schwartz–Zippel)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, let
H\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most
d
, and let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be finite and nonempty. Then

\Pr_{u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S^{n})}\left(H(u)=0\right)\leq\frac{d}{|S|}.

2.3
The Number-Theoretic Transform

Let
q
be prime, let
p
be prime with
q\mid p-1
, and choose an element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of exact order
q
. The order-
q
subgroup is

G_{p,q}:=\{1,\gamma,\gamma^{2},\ldots,\gamma^{q-1}\}\leq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}.

Since
G_{p,q}
is the unique subgroup of order
q
, it is independent of the choice of
\gamma
.
Define the evaluation map

E=E_{p,q,\gamma}:\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{q}-1)\mapsto\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}},\qquad E(f):=\bigl(f(\gamma^{t})\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}.

(3)

If
f(x)=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}a_{c}x^{c}
, with exponents represented by
0,\ldots,q-1
, then

E(f)(t)=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}a_{c}\gamma^{tc}.

Thus, relative to the monomial basis on the domain and the standard basis on the codomain,
E
is represented by the Fourier matrix

M=M_{p,q,\gamma}:=(\gamma^{tc})_{t,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}.

(4)

Proposition 2.3
.

The map
E
in
Equation

3
is a well-defined
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear isomorphism.

Proof.

Because
\gamma^{q}=1
, evaluation at
\gamma^{t}
depends only on the class of a polynomial modulo
x^{q}-1
. Moreover,

x^{q}-1=\prod_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(x-\gamma^{t})

splits over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
into distinct linear factors. The Chinese remainder theorem gives

\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{q}-1)\cong\prod_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x-\gamma^{t})\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}},

and this is exactly the evaluation map
E
.
∎

Definition 2.4

(NTT)
.

The number-theoretic transform (NTT) associated with
(p,q,\gamma)
is

\mathcal{F}:=E^{-1}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}\mapsto\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{q}-1).

When convenient, we identify
\mathcal{F}(v)
with its coefficient vector in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
.

The inverse is explicit. For
v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
,

(\mathcal{F}v)_{c}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}v_{t}\gamma^{-tc}

(5)

where
q^{-1}
is taken in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. This follows from the orthogonality relation

\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}\gamma^{t(c-c^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}q&\text{ if }c=c^{\prime}\\
0&\text{ if }c\neq c^{\prime}.\end{cases}

3
Uncertainty Principles

For
v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
,
\mathrm{Supp}(v)
denotes the nonzero coordinates of
v
. For
f=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}a_{c}x^{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{q}-1)
,
\mathrm{Supp}(f):=\{c:a_{c}\neq 0\}
. Under our identification of
\mathcal{F}v
with its coefficient vector,
\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}v)
is well-defined.

3.1
Additive Uncertainty

We prove the first version of the additive uncertainty principle in the following: We show that there are infinitely many pairs of primes
p
and
q
that satisfy the stronger (additive) uncertainty principle.

Theorem 3.1

(Additive uncertainty)
.

Fix a prime
q
. For all but finitely many primes
p
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, the following holds. For every element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of order
q
and every nonzero
v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
,

|\mathrm{Supp}(v)|+|\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}v)|\geq q+1.

To prove
Theorem

3.1
we adopt the strategy from
[
11
]
, where it is shown that additive uncertainty is equivalent to invertibility of every square submatrix of the Fourier matrix
M
(see
Section

2.3
). This statement was shown in the complex settings, but the same proof works for finite-fields.

Lemma 3.2

(Hyperinvertibility and Additive Uncertainty)
.

Let
q
and
p
be primes with
q\mid p-1
, let
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
have order
q
, and let
M=(\gamma^{tc})_{t,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
. The following are equivalent.

(i)

Every square submatrix of
M
is nonsingular over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

(ii)

Every nonzero
v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
satisfies
|\mathrm{Supp}(v)|+|\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}v)|\geq q+1
.

Proof.

Assume first that every square submatrix is nonsingular. Let
w:=\mathcal{F}v
, and set
S:=\mathrm{Supp}(w)
,
k:=|S|
. Write
v=Mw
. Let
Z:=\mathbb{Z}_{q}\setminus\mathrm{Supp}(v)

be the zero set of
v
. Restricting the identity
v=Mw
to rows in
Z
and columns in
S
gives

0=M_{Z,S}w_{S}.

If
|Z|\geq k
, choose
Z^{\prime}\subseteq Z
with
|Z^{\prime}|=k
. Then
M_{Z^{\prime},S}
is a nonsingular
k\times k
matrix, so
w_{S}=0
, contradicting the definition of
S
unless
v=0
. Hence
|Z|\leq k-1
, which is equivalent to

q-|\mathrm{Supp}(v)|\leq|\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}v)|-1.

Conversely, suppose that some
k\times k
submatrix
M_{R,S}
is singular. Choose
0\neq a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{S}
with
M_{R,S}a=0
, extend it by zero outside
S
, and set
v:=Ma
. Then
\mathcal{F}v=a
, so
|\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}v)|\leq k
, while
v
vanishes on all rows in
R
, so
|\mathrm{Supp}(v)|\leq q-k
. Thus

|\mathrm{Supp}(v)|+|\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}v)|\leq q,

contradicting additive uncertainty.
∎

We shall use the following classical theorem of Chebotarev, in the form used in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3.3

(Hyperinvertibility of Fourier Matrices)
.

Let
q
be prime and let
\omega=e^{2\pi i/q}
. For every
1\leq k\leq q
and every pair of
k
-element subsets
R,C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}
,

\det(\omega^{rc})_{r\in R,c\in C}\neq 0.

One may hope to prove a finite-field analogue of
Theorem

3.3
, unfortunately such a result cannot be true. It was shown by Zhang
[
16
]
that in the finite field settings there exists a Fourier matrix with a singular submatrix.

To circumvent this obstacle, we show that the finite-field analogue of
Theorem

3.3
holds for all but finitely-many primes
p
. We shall mention that this fact also follows as a consequence of a theorem proven in a very recent work

[
3
]
. Here we choose to give an explicit proof, since it serves as a good warm-up for subsequent arguments.

Lemma 3.4

(Finite Field Hyperinvertibility)
.

Fix a prime
q
. There is a finite set
B_{q}
of primes such that, whenever
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
and
p\notin B_{q}
, every square submatrix of
M_{p,q,\gamma}
is nonsingular over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for every element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of order
q
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq k\leq q
and
k
-element sets
R,C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. Choose integer representatives in
\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
and define

D_{R,C}(z):=\det(z^{rc})_{r\in R,c\in C}\in\mathbb{Z}[z],

where each exponent
rc
is represented modulo
q
by an integer in
\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
. Let

\Phi_{q}(z):=1+z+\cdots+z^{q-1}

be the
q
-th cyclotomic polynomial.
For every
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}
, multiplication by
b
permutes
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, so
bC
is again a
k
-element subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. By
Theorem

3.3
,

D_{R,C}(\omega^{b})=\det(\omega^{brc})_{r\in R,c\in C}=\det(\omega^{rc^{\prime}})_{r\in R,c^{\prime}\in bC}\neq 0.

Thus
D_{R,C}
and
\Phi_{q}
have no common complex root, and
\mathrm{Res}(D_{R,C},\Phi_{q})

is a nonzero integer.
Let

\Delta_{q}:=\prod_{k=1}^{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}R,C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}\\
|R|=|C|=k\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Res}(D_{R,C},\Phi_{q})\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

and let
B_{q}
be the set of prime divisors of
\Delta_{q}
. Suppose
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
,
p\notin B_{q}
, and
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
has order
q
. Then
\Phi_{q}(\gamma)=0
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. If some minor
M_{R,C}
were singular, then

D_{R,C}(\gamma)=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{F}_{p}.

Hence the reductions of
D_{R,C}
and
\Phi_{q}
modulo

would have the common root
\gamma
. By
Lemma

2.1
, this implies

\mathrm{Res}(D_{R,C},\Phi_{q})\equiv 0\pmod{p},

so
p\mid\Delta_{q}
, a contradiction.
∎

The proof of our main theorem is now straightforward.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

For fixed
q
, take
B_{q}
from
Lemma

3.4
. If
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
and
p\notin B_{q}
, the matrix

is hyperinvertible for every order-

gives the claimed uncertainty inequality.

3.2
Approximate Uncertainty

The proof above is qualitative because it multiplies all submatrices. We next keep a single resultant at a time and bound the number of primes that can divide it.

Lemma 3.5

(Bad Primes)
.

Let
q
be prime, let
F(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
, and
\omega=e^{2\pi i/q}
. Suppose that, for some real
B\geq 1
,

F(\omega^{b})\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad|F(\omega^{b})|\leq B\qquad\text{for every }b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}.

Then the number of primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
for which there exists an element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of order
q
with

F(\gamma)=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{F}_{p}

is at most
(q-1)\log_{2}B
.

Proof.

The hypotheses imply that
F
and
\Phi_{q}
have no common complex root. Hence

\mathrm{Res}(F,\Phi_{q})\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

If
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
and
F(\gamma)=0
for an element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of order
q
, then also
\Phi_{q}(\gamma)=0
. Thus the reductions of
F
and
\Phi_{q}
modulo
p
have a common root. By
Lemma

2.1
,
p\mid\mathrm{Res}(F,\Phi_{q})
.

By
Equation

2
(Poisson’s formula),

|{\mathrm{Res}(F,\Phi_{q})}|=\prod_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}}|{F(\omega^{b})}|,

and therefore
|\mathrm{Res}(F,\Phi_{q})|\leq B^{q-1}
. If a nonzero integer
N
has
m
distinct prime divisors, then
2^{m}\leq|N|
. Consequently
\mathrm{Res}(F,\Phi_{q})
has at most
\log_{2}(B^{q-1})=(q-1)\log_{2}B
distinct prime divisors.
∎

Let

\mathcal{P}(q):=\{p\leq q^{4}:p\text{ prime and }p\equiv 1\pmod{q}\}.

(6)

We shall use primes
q
for which

|\mathcal{P}(q)|=\pi(q^{4};q,1)\geq\frac{q^{3}}{32\log q}.

(7)

We prove an approximate uncertainty principle for a fixed integer sparse vector, but averaged over the random choice of primes
p\in\mathcal{P}(q)
.
To prove the approximate uncertainty principle, all needs to be done is to show that infinitely many such
q
exist. Here we simply postulate that such
q
exists, and we refer to
Section

3.3
for a proof of this fact.

Theorem 3.6

(Approximate Uncertainty)
.

Let
q
be prime and suppose
Equation

7
holds. Let
0\neq c\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
satisfy

|\mathrm{Supp}(c)|<q\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|c\|_{1}:=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}|c_{a}|\leq W

where
W\geq 2
. Then

\E_{p}\bigl(|\mathrm{Supp}(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(c))|\bigr)\geq q-1-\frac{32(q-1)\log q}{q^{2}}\log_{2}W.

Proof.

Let
C(z):=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}c_{a}z^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
. We first show that
C
does not vanish at any primitive
q
-th root of unity. If
C(\omega)=0
for a primitive root
\omega
, then the minimal polynomial
\Phi_{q}
of
\omega
over
\mathbb{Q}
divides
C
in
\mathbb{Q}[z]
. Since
\deg C\leq q-1=\deg\Phi_{q}
, this forces
C=\lambda\Phi_{q}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
. But
\Phi_{q}=1+z+\cdots+z^{q-1}
has support
q
, whereas
0\neq C
has support strictly smaller than
q
. This is impossible. The same argument applies to every primitive root
\omega^{b}
, for
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}
.

Moreover for all
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}
,

|C(\omega^{b})|\leq\sum_{a}|c_{a}|\leq W.

By
Lemma

3.5
, the number of primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
for which there exists an order-
q
element
h\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
with
C(h)=0
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is at most
(q-1)\log_{2}W
. Call these primes bad.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}(q)
is not bad, then for every
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}
, the element
\gamma_{p}^{t}
has order
q
, and hence

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(c)(t)=E_{p,q,\gamma_{p}}(c)(t)=C(\gamma_{p}^{t})\neq 0.

Thus, for non-bad
p
, the evaluation vector can vanish only at
t=0
, and the zero fraction is at most
1/q
. For bad
p
we use the trivial bound
1
. Therefore

\E_{p}\left(\frac{1}{q}\bigl|\{t:E_{p,q,\gamma_{p}}(c)(t)=0\}\bigr|\right)\leq\frac{1}{q}+\frac{(q-1)\log_{2}W}{|{\cal P}(q)|}.

Using
Equation

7
gives the claimed bound. The desired statement follows by multiplying the zero-fraction inequality by
q
and subtracting from
q
.
∎

3.3
Bounds on Admissible Primes

For
n\geq 1
, let
\Lambda(n)
denote the von Mangoldt function. For
(a,q)=1
, define

\psi(x;q,a):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\,(q)\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n),\qquad\theta(x;q,a):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(q)\end{subarray}}\log p.

We use the following standard form of Bombieri–Vinogradov
[
1
,
13
,
12
]
.

Theorem 3.7

(Bombieri–Vinogradov)
.

For every
A>0
there are constants
B=B(A)
and
C=C(A)
such that, for all
x\geq 2
and all
Q\leq x^{1/2}(\log x)^{-B}
,

\sum_{q\leq Q}\max_{(a,q)=1}\max_{2\leq y\leq x}\left|\psi(y;q,a)-\frac{y}{\varphi(q)}\right|\leq C\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}.

The next lemma shows that there are infinitely many
q
with the desired property.

Lemma 3.8

(Admissible Primes)
.

There are infinitely many primes
q
satisfying
Equation

7
.

Proof.

First compare
\psi
and
\theta
. For
y\geq 3
,

\displaystyle 0\leq\psi(y;q,1)-\theta(y;q,1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}y\rfloor}\sum_{p\leq y^{1/m}}\log p

\displaystyle\leq(\log y)\sum_{m=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}y\rfloor}\frac{y^{1/m}}{m},

using the elementary bound
\sum_{p\leq t}\log p\leq t\log t
. With
y=q^{4}
, this gives, for
q\geq 3
,

0\leq\psi(q^{4};q,1)-\theta(q^{4};q,1)\leq 2q^{2}\log q+4q^{4/3}\log q\,(1+\log(4\log q)).

(8)

Indeed, the term
m=2
contributes at most
2q^{2}\log q
, and the remaining terms are bounded by

4q^{4/3}\log q\sum_{m=3}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(q^{4})\rfloor}\frac{1}{m}\leq 4q^{4/3}\log q\,(1+\log(4\log q)).

Fix
A>2
, and let
B,C
be as in
Theorem

3.7
. For a large parameter
N
, set

x:=(2N)^{4},\qquad Q:=x^{1/2}(\log x)^{-B}.

For all sufficiently large
N
, the interval
[N,2N]
is contained in
[1,Q]
. Let
E_{N}
be the set of integers
q\in[N,2N]
for which

\left|\psi(q^{4};q,1)-\frac{q^{4}}{\varphi(q)}\right|>\frac{q^{4}}{2\varphi(q)}.

For
q\in[N,2N]
, the right-hand side is
\gg N^{3}
. Bombieri–Vinogradov and Markov’s inequality therefore give

|E_{N}|=O\!\left(\frac{N}{(\log N)^{A}}\right).

By the prime number theorem, the interval
[N,2N]
contains
\gg N/\log N
primes. Since
A>2
, for all sufficiently large
N
there is a prime
q\in[N,2N]\setminus E_{N}
. Taking
N\to\infty
gives infinitely many such primes. For any such prime
q
, since
\varphi(q)=q-1
,

\psi(q^{4};q,1)\geq\frac{q^{4}}{2(q-1)}\geq\frac{q^{3}}{4}

for
q\geq 2
. Combining this with
Equation

8
, and taking
q
sufficiently large along the infinite sequence, gives

\theta(q^{4};q,1)\geq\frac{q^{3}}{8}.

Finally,

\theta(q^{4};q,1)\leq\pi(q^{4};q,1)\log(q^{4})=4\pi(q^{4};q,1)\log q,

so
Equation

7
follows.
∎

Theorem 3.9

(Multiplicative Uncertainty)

Let

We now apply the preceding uncertainty principles to the finite-field identity-testing model of Li and Wu

, which we recall in the following. Let

P(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(x)\exp\!\left(\frac{g_{j}(x)}{h_{j}(x)}\right)

(9)

with

is

Thus the rational functions

We say that

.

Let
p,q
be primes with
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}

G_{p,q}\leq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
be the order-
q

\gamma\in G_{p,q}
,

, and

, define the algebraic evaluation

P_{p,q,\gamma}(u,v):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(u)\,\gamma^{\,g_{j}(v)h_{j}(v)^{-1}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p},

(12)

where the exponent is computed in

4.1
A Strong Descartes Rule over Finite Fields

Li and Wu
[
7
]
formulate the following finite-field analogue of Descartes’ rule as a sufficient route to improved soundness.

Conjecture 4.1

(Strong finite-field Descartes rule)
.

There exist constants
\varepsilon,\delta<1
such that the following holds. Let
p,q
be sufficiently large primes with
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, let
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
have order
q
, and let
G\leq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
be the subgroup of order
q
. If
k\leq q^{\delta}
, if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
are distinct, and if
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then

A(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}z^{\alpha_{j}}

has at most
\varepsilon q
roots in
G
, unless all
\beta_{j}
are zero.

Our additive uncertainty theorem gives a stronger conclusion for fixed
q
and all but finitely many admissible
p
.

Theorem 4.2

(Nonuniform Strong Descartes Rule)
.

Fix a prime
q
. For all but finitely many primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, the following holds. Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
have order
q
, and let
G\leq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
be the subgroup of order
q
. If

A(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}z^{\alpha_{j}},

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
are distinct and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
, then
A
has at most
k-1
roots in
G
.

Proof.

Choose
p
so that
Theorem

3.1
holds. Equivalently, by
Lemma

3.2
, the matrix
M=(\gamma^{ta})_{t,a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
is hyperinvertible.
Let
S:=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}
and define
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}
by
c_{\alpha_{j}}=\beta_{j}
and
c_{a}=0
for

. Then

(Mc)_{t}=A(\gamma^{t}).

If
A
vanished at
k
or more elements of

, there would be a

such that

Assume

satisfies

7

P
. Sample:

•

p\sim\mathrm{Unif}({\cal P}(q))
.

•

.

•

•

of order

.

Theorem 4.3

(Soundness)
.

Let
q
be a prime satisfying
Equation

7
. Let
P
be a condensed
k
-sparse exponential polynomial of degree at most
d
and height at most
w
, with
w\geq 2
, and suppose
q>2kw
.
If
P\equiv 0
, the one-query randomized-

test accepts with probability

. If
P\not\equiv 0
, the test accepts with probability at most

\frac{kd}{q}+\frac{2d\binom{k}{2}}{q}+\frac{d}{q+1}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{32(q-1)\log q}{q^{3}}\log_{2}(kw).

(13)

Proof.

Completeness is immediate: If
P\equiv 0
, every defined algebraic evaluation is zero, and undefined evaluations are accepted by convention.

Assume
P\not\equiv 0
. Define the good denominator event

E_{h}:=\{h_{j}(v)\neq 0\text{ in }\mathbb{F}_{q}\text{ for all }j\in[k]\},

and the good separation event

E_{g}:=\{g_{j}(v)h_{t}(v)-g_{t}(v)h_{j}(v)\neq 0\text{ in }\mathbb{F}_{q}\text{ for all }j\neq t\}.

Because
q>2kw\geq w
, every nonzero integer polynomial in
Equation

11
remains nonzero after reduction modulo
q
. By
Lemma

2.2
(Schwartz–Zippel) and the union bound,

\Pr\bigl(\neg(E_{h}\cap E_{g})\bigr)\leq\frac{kd}{q}+\frac{2d\binom{k}{2}}{q}

(14)

since
\deg(g_{j}h_{t}-g_{t}h_{j})\leq 2d
.

Condition on
E_{h}\cap E_{g}
and set

\alpha_{j}:=g_{j}(v)h_{j}(v)^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

The
\alpha_{j}
are pairwise distinct. For fixed
p,\gamma,r
, with
a=\gamma^{r}
, the algebraic evaluation is the value at
u
of

H_{a,v}(X):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(X)a^{\alpha_{j}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}].

If
H_{a,v}\not\equiv 0
, again

2.2
(Schwartz–Zippel) gives

\Pr_{u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n})}\bigl(H_{a,v}(u)=0\bigr)\leq\frac{d}{p}\leq\frac{d}{q+1}.

(15)

Since

and is condensed, not all coefficient polynomials

are zero. Choose a monomial

for which

\eta:=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}):=([X^{\mu}]f_{1},\ldots,[X^{\mu}]f_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}

is nonzero. The height bound gives

\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\eta_{j}|\leq kw.

Since every sampled

satisfies

, the vector
\eta
remains nonzero modulo

.
If
H_{\gamma^{r},v}\equiv 0
, then the coefficient of
X^{\mu}
in this polynomial vanishes in
\mathbb{F}_{p}

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\gamma^{r\alpha_{j}}=0.

(16)

Define

by placing
\eta_{j}
at the coordinate

.
Because the
\alpha_{j}
are distinct,

c\neq 0,\qquad|\mathrm{Supp}(c)|\leq k<q,\qquad\|c\|_{1}\leq kw.

Moreover,

E_{p,q,\gamma}(c)(r)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\gamma^{r\alpha_{j}}.

Therefore

16
is the event

3.6
, applied with

\Pr_{p,r}\bigl(H_{\gamma^{r},v}\equiv 0\bigr)\leq\Pr_{p,r}\bigl(E_{p,q,\gamma}(c)(r)=0\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{q}+\frac{32(q-1)\log q}{q^{3}}\log_{2}(kw).

Appendix A
Approximate Hyperinvertibility

In the following, we prove an approximate version of the hyperinvertibility theorem of Fourier matrices.
For
q
prime and
1\leq k\leq q
, let

\mathcal{K}_{q,k}:=\{(R,C):R,C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q},\ |R|=|C|=k\}.

For a prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
, choose any element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of order
q
and define

F_{q,k}(p):=\Pr_{(R,C)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{K}_{q,k})}\bigl(\det(\gamma^{rc})_{r\in R,c\in C}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{F}_{p}\bigr).

(18)

This value is independent of the chosen order-
q
element
\gamma
: Replacing
\gamma
by
\gamma^{s}
with
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}
replaces
R
by
sR
, and
R
is uniform over all
k
-subsets.

Lemma A.1

(Bad Primes for One Submatrix)
.

Fix a prime
q
, an integer
1\leq k\leq q
, and
(R,C)\in\mathcal{K}_{q,k}
. The number of primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
for which there exists an element
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
of order
q
such that

\det(\gamma^{rc})_{r\in R,c\in C}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{F}_{p}

is at most
\frac{(q-1)k}{2}\log_{2}k
.

Proof.

Define

D_{R,C}(z):=\det(z^{rc})_{r\in R,c\in C}\in\mathbb{Z}[z],

again using representatives of
rc\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
in
\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
. For
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ast}
,

D_{R,C}(\omega^{b})=\det(\omega^{brc})_{r\in R,c\in C}\neq 0

by
Theorem

3.3
. Also
D_{R,C}(\omega^{b})
is the determinant of a
k\times k
complex matrix whose entries all have absolute value
1
. Hadamard’s inequality gives

|D_{R,C}(\omega^{b})|\leq k^{k/2}.

Apply
Lemma

3.5
with
B=k^{k/2}
.
∎

Next, we obtain an average bound over all submatrices.

Lemma A.2

(Averaging over Submatrices)
.

Let
q
be prime, let
1\leq k\leq q
, and let
x\geq 2
. For at least half of the primes
p\leq x
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{q}
,

F_{q,k}(p)\leq\frac{(q-1)k}{\pi(x;q,1)}\log_{2}k,

where
\pi(x;q,1):=|\{p\leq x:p\text{ prime and }p\equiv 1\pmod{q}\}|
.

Proof.

For each admissible prime
p
, fix one order-
q
element
\gamma_{p}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
. By linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 1\,(q)\end{subarray}}F_{q,k}(p)

\displaystyle=\E_{(R,C)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{K}_{q,k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 1\,(q)\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}\{\det(\gamma_{p}^{rc})_{r\in R,c\in C}=0\}.

For each fixed
(R,C)
,

bounds the inner sum by
\frac{(q-1)k}{2}\log_{2}k
. Hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv 1\,(q)\end{subarray}}F_{q,k}(p)\leq\frac{(q-1)k}{2}\log_{2}k.

Markov’s inequality applied to the nonnegative values

proves the claim.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem A.3

(Approximate Hyperinvertibility)
.

There exist infinitely many primes
