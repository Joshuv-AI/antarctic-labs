Destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value drive tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism
Authors: Mingliang Chu, Musen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional village tourism plays a vital role in rural development, with tourist loyalty being essential for preservation and sustainability. However, research on its determinants and mechanisms remains limited. This study constructs a Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) model to examine how tourists’ perceptions and behaviors shape loyalty. Based on 330 valid responses, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA) were applied. Results indicate that objective authenticity, co-creation, e-word-of-mouth, and service quality significantly enhance destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value, except for service quality’s nonsignificant effect on satisfaction. Destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value strongly influence loyalty, with satisfaction and perceived value identified as necessary conditions. This study identifies the key drivers of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism and provides theoretical and practical implications for its sustainable development.

Introduction

With the acceleration of urbanization, rural tourism has become a major option for leisure and affective experience, with traditional villages serving as core carriers that embody unique cultural value1. Traditional village tourism not only integrates culture, architecture, and art but also serves as a vital channel for cultural heritage preservation2. Since the launch of China’s Rural Revitalization Strategy in 2018, a new wave of tourism development has positioned traditional village tourism as a focal area of attention3. The substantial market potential has stimulated numerous projects, reshaping the rural tourism landscape. However, rapid growth has also intensified competition and created critical challenges. On one hand, “homogenization” and “over-commercialization” distort tourist perceptions4; on the other, excessive development has led to village degradation and cultural erosion5. Balancing tourist perceptions with heritage protection has therefore become a pressing necessity for the sustainable development of traditional villages.

Against this backdrop, the question of how to enhance tourist loyalty within traditional village contexts has emerged as a central issue in sustainable tourism management. On the one hand, tourist loyalty directly reflects visitors’ overall evaluations of their travel experiences and constitutes a critical driver of revisit intentions, positive word-of-mouth communication, and the stable development of destinations6. On the other hand, loyal tourists tend to demonstrate stronger identification with and support for traditional culture, and are more likely to engage in cultural preservation and the co-creation of social value7. Accordingly, systematically identifying the key psychological mechanisms that shape tourist loyalty in traditional villages is of substantial importance for fostering long-term destination competitiveness and cultural sustainability. Tourist loyalty is influenced by a range of psychological factors, including perceived value, satisfaction, and destination image, all of which have been empirically shown to exert significant effects on loyal tourism behaviors8. Nevertheless, existing studies remain limited in terms of theoretical depth and the explanatory power of their analytical models.

However, despite these important insights, the existing literature still shows three major gaps. First, tourist loyalty has often been examined through perceived quality, satisfaction, or other isolated behavioral factors9,10, with limited efforts to integrate destination image, perceived value, and satisfaction within a unified S–O–R framework. Second, the mechanisms through which these factors jointly shape tourist loyalty remain underexplored, as most prior studies focus mainly on linear relationships11,12, while overlooking nonlinear effects and necessary condition mechanisms. Third, existing research is largely grounded in general tourism settings, with insufficient contextualized attention to traditional village tourism, where cultural symbolism, heritage value, and spatial experience may shape loyalty formation in distinctive ways. Taken together, these gaps indicate the lack of a context-sensitive and methodologically integrated explanation of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism.

In response to these gaps, this study develops an integrated model grounded in the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) theory and combines three complementary analytical approaches—Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA)—to simultaneously capture linear relationships, nonlinear mechanisms, and necessary condition effects among the variables. It should be noted that the S–O–R model itself is not a new theoretical framework, as it has been widely applied in tourism and consumer behavior research. However, in the context of traditional village tourism, the application of this model remains relatively fragmented and insufficiently contextualized. Existing studies have often treated destination image, perceived value, and satisfaction as separate antecedents in explaining tourist loyalty, while paying limited attention to how external destination-related perceptions are systematically transformed into tourists’ internal evaluations and subsequently influence their loyalty intentions through a coherent theoretical process.

Against this background, this study introduces the S–O–R framework into the context of traditional village tourism as a form of heritage-based rural destination and employs it to explain the formation of tourist loyalty in a more integrated and mechanism-oriented manner. Specifically, destination image is conceptualized as the external stimulus, perceived value and satisfaction as internal organismic evaluation states, and tourist loyalty as the behavioral response. In doing so, the study more clearly reveals the progressive formation path of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism, from external perceptions to internal judgments and ultimately to behavioral intentions. Accordingly, this study advances tourist loyalty research from relatively fragmented factor-based explanations toward a more systematic, mechanism-based, and context-sensitive theoretical account. At the same time, the combined use of PLS-SEM, ANN, and NCA further strengthens this contribution by enabling the study not only to identify the net effects among variables, but also to uncover nonlinear influence patterns and the key necessary conditions underlying the formation of high levels of tourist loyalty.

In summary, tourist loyalty is of critical importance to the sustainable development of traditional village tourism. Accordingly, this study proposes the following research questions:

RQ1: How do destination image, perceived value, and satisfaction jointly influence tourist loyalty in traditional village contexts through the S–O–R model?

RQ2: Do nonlinear or necessity-based relationships exist among these psychological factors beyond linear effects?

RQ3: Can an integrated multi-method analytical approach provide a more comprehensive explanation of tourists’ decision-making mechanisms?

This study focuses on destination image, perceived value, and tourist satisfaction as three core variables, and systematically examines their combined effects on tourist loyalty in traditional villages. The contributions of this research are threefold: theoretically, it extends the applicability of the S–O–R model to traditional village contexts; methodologically, it represents the first application of an integrated SEM–ANN–NCA strategy in this domain; and practically, it offers actionable implications for the planning and sustainable management of traditional village tourism.

Literature review and hypothesis development

Traditional village tourism

Traditional villages refer to settlement forms that originated in earlier historical periods, preserve prominent tangible and intangible cultural resources, and embody scientific, artistic, social, and economic value2. Unlike rural spaces in a general sense, traditional villages preserve historically specific architectural forms, spatial patterns, production practices, social relations, customs, and cultural memory. They are therefore not static heritage carriers but “living cultural landscapes” that combine historical continuity with contemporary life. This distinguishes traditional village tourism from general rural tourism, as tourists consume not only natural scenery or leisure experiences but also the authenticity, cultural atmosphere, and heritage value embedded in the destination13.

From a broader tourism studies perspective, traditional village tourism lies at the intersection of rural tourism, cultural tourism, and heritage tourism. On the one hand, it shares core attributes with rural tourism, such as ecological environments, rural lifestyles, and pastoral experiences. On the other hand, it also possesses distinctive heritage tourism characteristics due to its historical continuity, cultural symbolism, and conservation value. With the accelerating pace of urbanization, together with tourists’ growing demand for natural, cultural, and place-based experiences, traditional village tourism has gradually become an important tourism choice14. At the same time, it has been widely regarded as an important pathway for rural revitalization, because it not only helps stimulate local economic development and improve village environments, but also plays a significant role in cultural heritage preservation and the sustainable transformation of rural areas15.

However, the development of traditional village tourism is not without tension. As related tourism projects continue to expand, some destinations have gradually experienced ecological imbalance, cultural loss or excessive commercialization, and infrastructure overload. These problems may weaken tourists’ perceptions of destination authenticity and further reduce their emotional attachment and loyalty intentions13,16. This suggests that, in the context of traditional village tourism, tourist loyalty cannot be adequately explained solely by general destination management variables; rather, it should be examined in relation to the distinctive attributes of heritage-based rural destinations, with greater attention paid to the interconnections among destination perception, value judgment, and experiential response.

Existing studies have explored the determinants of tourist loyalty from multiple perspectives. Zhu et al.17 argued that, in the context of rural tourism, destination image and experience serve as mediating variables in the positive effect of destination recovery on destination loyalty. Zhao and Li18 pointed out that, in rural tourism settings, both object-based authenticity and existential authenticity can enhance place attachment and further strengthen tourist loyalty. Qiu et al.19 found that, in industrial heritage tourism, perceived value and satisfaction exert significant positive effects on loyalty, with satisfaction playing a mediating role. Similarly, Choo et al.20 demonstrated in the context of local food festivals that participation can influence loyalty both directly and indirectly through satisfaction. Marinao-Artigas et al.21 argued that perceived quality, reputation, and satisfaction exert direct or indirect effects on tourist loyalty. Although prior studies have advanced the understanding of tourist loyalty, explanations remain fragmented and traditional village tourism remains underexplored. This study therefore conceptualizes traditional village tourism as a heritage-based rural tourism context and develops an integrated S–O–R framework to identify key determinants of tourist loyalty.

Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR)

The S–O–R model, proposed by Mehrabian and Russell22, is rooted in behaviorism’s “stimulus–response” theory, introducing the organism as an intermediary and thereby forming a tripartite structure of Stimulus (S), Organism (O), and Response (R). The model posits that external environmental stimuli influence an organism’s cognitive and emotional states (e.g., pleasure, arousal, dominance), which in turn shape individual behavioral responses23. Stimuli may include both internal and external environmental cues, while responses can manifest as either positive approach behaviors or negative avoidance behaviors.

At present, the S–O–R model is regarded as an important framework for explaining the antecedents, interactions, and outcomes of tourism behavior24. Studies have shown that the S–O–R model has been widely applied in tourist behavior research across heritage tourism, film-induced tourism, and VR tourism25,26. It emphasizes how environmental stimuli influence consumer behavioral responses through internal psychological processes, including satisfaction, loyalty, and visit intentions27. Building on this, Zhu et al.17 examined how external stimuli such as compatibility and lifestyle atmosphere perceptions influence tourist loyalty via destination image. Ciki and Tanrıverdi28 not only verified that Muslim-friendly tourism characteristics positively influence tourists’ destination loyalty and subjective well-being, but also further revealed their effect on tourists’ revisit intention. Similarly, Wei and Zhang29 identified physical factors, social activities, environmental atmosphere, and information and services as environmental stimuli that affect heritage tourism behavior intentions through perceived value as the organism. These studies confirm the applicability and effectiveness of the S–O–R model in predicting tourist behavior within tourism contexts. Nevertheless, three limitations remain. First, prior research has focused mainly on single mediating paths, such as destination image or perceived value, rather than integrated psychological mechanisms. Second, most S–O–R studies rely on linear models, leaving nonlinear effects and necessary conditions underexplored. Third, evidence remains limited on traditional villages as culturally symbolic and immersive tourism settings.

It is worth noting that Hoang et al.30, drawing on expectation–confirmation theory, empirically examined the relationships among destination image, tourist satisfaction, and loyalty, demonstrating that destination image indirectly influences loyalty through satisfaction and underscoring the central mediating role of satisfaction in the loyalty formation process. While this study provides important theoretical insights into the image–satisfaction–loyalty linkage, it does not further investigate the relative importance of different psychological variables or the conditional constraints under which they operate in shaping tourist loyalty.

In response to these limitations,this study applies the S–O–R framework to examine how objective authenticity, co-creation, electronic word of mouth, and service quality influence tourist loyalty through destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value. It further integrates linear, nonlinear, and necessary condition analyses to explain loyalty formation in traditional village tourism.

Hypotheses development and conceptual model construction

Objective authenticity

Objective authenticity, as the core attribute of traditional village cultural heritage, exerts a systematic influence on tourist cognition and behavior31. Objective authenticity primarily relies on the intrinsic attributes of cultural objects themselves as stimulus variables. In other words, objective authenticity does not depend on tourists’ participation, but rather on the “authentic presentation” of the cultural entity. Consequently, it mainly enhances the destination’s cultural credibility, cultural symbolic value, and forms of passive affective resonance32. Kim and Jamal33 further emphasized that when tourists recognize that cultural presentations are not distorted by commercialization, they develop stronger trust and identification, thereby forming more positive evaluations of the destination. This process stems from authenticity reinforcing both cognitive and affective connections: it provides credible cultural symbols for interpretation while also evoking emotional resonance with traditional cultural values, ultimately shaping the overall destination image in a positive manner34.

Within the satisfaction formation mechanism, objective authenticity drives tourist satisfaction through two distinct pathways. First, it fulfills tourists’ intrinsic motivations to seek genuine cultural experiences35, such as participating in indigenous agricultural practices or intangible cultural heritage performances. Second, through the mediating effect of cultural identification, when tourists perceive cultural expression as authentic, their sense of identity and belonging is significantly strengthened, thereby enhancing their satisfaction36.

Objective authenticity also constitutes a fundamental basis for tourists’ perceived value. Tourists’ perception of authenticity in cultural experiences enhances their value judgments of services or products, thereby increasing both perceived value and satisfaction37. Yang et al.38 confirmed that tourists’ recognition of objective authenticity not only directly and positively influences perceived value but also mediates the relationship between authenticity and revisit intention. Moreover, in the case of rural tourism souvenirs derived from intangible cultural heritage, higher levels of objective authenticity have been shown to significantly improve tourists’ perceived value39.

Prior research has shown that objective authenticity positively affects destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value, but its role remains underexplored in culturally intensive contexts such as traditional villages. Grounded in the S–O–R framework, this study examines objective authenticity as a key stimulus shaping these psychological responses. Based on this rationale, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1a: Objective authenticity in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on destination image.

H1b: Objective authenticity in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist satisfaction.

H1c: Objective authenticity in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on perceived value.

Co-creation

Galvagno and Dalli40 define co-creation behavior as “a joint, collaborative, simultaneous, and peer-like process of materially and symbolically producing value.” This behavior exhibits strong contextual dependence, with different modes of co-creation emerging across diverse settings, while individual imagination constitutes a potential resource41. Value co-creation consists of two dimensions: customer participation behavior and customer citizenship behavior32.

In the context of rural tourism, value co-creation exerts a profound influence on the formation and evolution of destination image. Destination image represents the composite of tourists’ beliefs, ideas, and impressions of a destination42. The impact of co-creation on destination image does not stem from the cultural entity itself, but rather from tourists’ attribution of meaning to their own contributions, thereby endowing the image with a stronger sense of “subjective ownership”43. Similarly, enhancements in satisfaction arise from the depth of participation—namely, tourists’ opportunities for self-expression through co-creation—which generate a form of gratification fundamentally distinct from the “cultural authenticity satisfaction” derived from authenticity-based experiences.

Studies indicate that tourists construct their initial cognitive frameworks of destinations through online interactions such as photo sharing and posting reviews, and such co-creation behaviors can significantly enhance perceptions of destination image44. In particular, when tourists capture photos with cultural representations, their creative outputs directly participate in the construction of destination image, thereby generating a positive cycle of image reinforcement34.

Value co-creation also serves as a core driving mechanism of tourist satisfaction. Satisfaction is essentially a comprehensive evaluation of service quality, depth of participation, and emotional experience45. This aligns with Oliver’s46 “confirmation–disconfirmation” paradigm. When tourists engage in co-creation activities such as service design or cultural practices, their perceived value and emotional identification are significantly enhanced, thereby elevating overall satisfaction. Ghali et al.47 further confirmed that consumer co-creation positively affects consumer satisfaction; in other words, the higher the level of customer participation in co-creation, the greater the satisfaction.

At the level of perceived value formation, value co-creation provides a foundational theoretical framework48. The interactive process not only satisfies tourists’ functional needs but also stimulates deeper hedonic experiences and aesthetic cognition through shared practices. Xu et al.49 argued that such immersive collaboration enhances psychological fulfillment and exerts a positive influence on perceived value, which is particularly evident in cultural experience contexts within traditional villages. In addition, Uslu and Tosun50 revealed the positive effect of consumer behavior on tourist loyalty.

Existing studies rarely compare authenticity and co-creation within the same S–O–R framework. This study therefore examines their parallel effects on destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value in traditional village tourism. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H2a: Co-creation in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on destination image.

H2b: Co-creation in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist satisfaction.

H2c: Co-creation in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on perceived value.

E-WOM

Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) is defined as the informal exchange of information among consumers based on digital platforms, encompassing core dimensions such as service quality and product experience51. On social media platforms (e.g., WeChat, TikTok, and Xiaohongshu), tourists share their experiences and receive interactive feedback, making eWOM a crucial source of information for destination choice. This form of communication plays a significant role in constructing destination image—conceived as a multidimensional mapping of tourists’ perceptions—where its formation process is strongly shaped by eWOM52. Case studies from Indonesian tourism further validate the universality of eWOM’s impact on destination image.

As an “external informational stimulus,” electronic word of mouth operates by providing social evaluative cues that shape tourists’ prior expectations of a destination. In essence, it constitutes a cognitive heuristic process grounded in others’ experiences and functions as an anticipatory informational intervention for tourists, belonging to the pre-trip stage of value formation53. Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) influences tourist satisfaction through two main pathways. First, as a reference basis for consumer decision-making, the information it conveys directly shapes tourists’ judgments. Second, as a social certification mechanism for service quality, the positive or negative content of eWOM significantly alters tourist preferences54. Empirical studies have shown that the more satisfied tourists are with the sufficiency of eWOM information, the higher their overall satisfaction. Moreover, positive eWOM exerts a favorable influence on tourist satisfaction, while negative eWOM induces adverse effects55.

Perceived value arises from tourists’ overall evaluation of their travel experiences and interactions, with its core reflected in their cognitive judgment of the “price–quality ratio”56. Research indicates that eWOM influences perceived value by triggering a value re-evaluation mechanism: in the hotel industry, both positive and negative reviews significantly alter tourists’ value perceptions, leading to systematic evaluation biases57. Studies on smart tourism further reveal that eWOM not only directly affects consumption intentions but also indirectly shapes decision-making through the mediating role of perceived value—when tourists consult others’ reviews on social platforms, they reassess product cost-effectiveness, resulting in dynamic adjustments in perceived value58.Existing studies rarely compare authenticity and co-creation within the same S–O–R framework. This study therefore examines their parallel effects on destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value in traditional village tourism. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H3a: Electronic word-of-mouth in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on destination image.

H3b: Electronic word-of-mouth in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist satisfaction.

H3c: Electronic word-of-mouth in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on perceived value.

Service quality

Service quality can be understood as an individual’s overall perception of the strengths and weaknesses demonstrated by a service provider during the service process, serving as a core medium in the construction of destination image59. Research has shown that high-quality service enhances the tourist experience and thereby positively reinforces destination image, while destination image itself carries implicit expectations of service quality60. In other words, when perceived service quality aligns with image-based expectations, it significantly strengthens cognitive consistency; conversely, when expectations are unmet, it leads to cognitive distortions of destination image. Voorhees et al.61 conceptualize service quality as an on-site experiential stimulus that encompasses immediate evaluations across multiple dimensions, including service outcomes, interactions, and the service environment, and characterizes it as a systematic process-based pathway.

Service quality constitutes a core driving factor of tourist satisfaction. Based on motivation and experience demand theory, service quality enhances satisfaction by fulfilling tourists’ functional needs62. Cham et al.60 confirmed the positive impact of service quality on tourist satisfaction. Rahman et al.63 further argued that service quality and tourist satisfaction are not only significantly correlated but that satisfaction also serves as a mediating variable regulating the relationship between service quality and tourist loyalty. This suggests that satisfaction is not merely a direct outcome variable but also a crucial transmission hub in the formation of loyalty. Moreover, Qin and Zhang64 in the context of cultural heritage tourism, demonstrate that perceived cultural distance functions as a psychological expectation boundary that significantly moderates the transformation pathways through which experiential quality influences tourists’ satisfaction and sense of identification.

Perceived value is essentially a cognitive assessment of the trade-off between service quality and price65. As a key antecedent variable, service quality enhances perceived value by optimizing the “cost–benefit” ratio: when tourists perceive that the resources they invest (time/money) are outweighed by the experiences they gain, perceived value increases significantly66. Habibi and Rasoolimanesh67, in their study on the impact of service quality on medical tourists, found that service quality has a significant positive effect on perceived value, suggesting that this mechanism is even more explanatory in cultural consumption contexts. Although electronic word of mouth has been widely examined as an informational cue, its effects have rarely been compared with on-site experiential stimuli such as objective authenticity and value co-creation. This study therefore examines its relative effects on destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value within the S–O–R framework. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H4a: Service quality in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on destination image.

H4b: Service quality in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist satisfaction.

H4c: Service quality in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on perceived value.

Destination image

Destination image, as a core element of tourists’ cognition and emotion, profoundly shapes their attitudes and behaviors. Tasci et al.68 define it as “an interactive system of thoughts, opinions, feelings, visualizations, and intentions toward a destination” (p. 200). In the context of rural tourism, destination image is not only a key driver in attracting tourists but also an important factor in strengthening place attachment, significantly influencing emotional perceptions and attitudinal evaluations69. Moreover, destination image is crucial for tourism promotion and directly affects tourist satisfaction70. In specific contexts such as ancient village tourism and Muslim-friendly tourism, the positive impact of destination image on tourist satisfaction has been empirically validated71.

Tourist loyalty refers to tourists’ continuous intention to revisit a destination, their willingness to return, and their propensity to recommend it to others72. Oliver73 emphasized the close relationship between destination image and loyalty, noting that loyalty is crucial for the sustainable development of destinations. Empirical studies have confirmed that destination image is one of the most influential factors on tourist loyalty and a key predictor of recommendation intention—where a positive destination image guides tourists’ decisions and behavioral intentions in a favorable direction74. Likewise, multiple studies consistently demonstrate the significant positive effect of destination image on tourist loyalty75,76. Tasci et al.77 verified, through a comparison between place-oriented and people-oriented approaches, that destination image is a stronger predictor of destination loyalty. Existing studies often treat destination image as a direct antecedent of tourist loyalty. From an S–O–R perspective, however, it may serve as a bridging mechanism between external stimuli and internal evaluations such as satisfaction and perceived value. Its role within this causal chain remains insufficiently validated. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H6: Destination image in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist satisfaction.

H7: Destination image in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist loyalty.

Tourist satisfaction

Tourist satisfaction refers to the overall level of feelings aroused after a travel experience, derived from the comparison between tourists’ expectations and their actual perceptions78. It serves as a key antecedent variable in the formation of tourist loyalt. In tourism contexts, satisfaction represents a comprehensive evaluation of pre-travel expectations and post-travel perceived experiences: when perceptions meet or exceed expectations, tourists feel satisfied; otherwise, dissatisfaction arises79. Ciki et al.28 revealed that tourists’ perceptions, particularly in terms of quality and atmosphere, have a positive effect on tourist satisfaction, which in turn mediates the relationship between quality and word-of-mouth communication. Chi et al.80 confirmed that tourist satisfaction exerts a direct positive impact on loyalty. Although satisfaction is a key antecedent of loyalty, its role remains unclear when perceived value and destination image are considered together. This study therefore examines their relative and mediating roles in tourist loyalty formation. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H8: Tourist satisfaction in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist loyalty.

Perceived value

Perceived value is defined as the overall assessment of a product or service made by consumers based on the comparison between perceived benefits and costs81. Simply put, it reflects whether consumption is considered “worth the money” or not. As an antecedent variable of satisfaction, satisfaction essentially represents an evaluation of perceived value after consumption19. Research has demonstrated that perceived value not only has a positive effect on tourist satisfaction but also that satisfaction serves as a mediator between perceived value and tourist loyalty19. Kim and Park82 further confirmed that value judgments have a significant impact on both satisfaction and tourist loyalty.

It is noteworthy that merely meeting tourists’ basic or functional needs is insufficient to directly establish high levels of loyalty83. Studies have shown that emotional value dimensions—such as pleasure, enjoyment, quality, and interaction perceived during the tourism experience—more directly drive loyal behaviors84. Researchers have further validated that in special tourism contexts (e.g., industrial heritage and marine tourism), fulfilling tourists’ psychological needs through emotional value is a key factor in promoting satisfaction and loyalty19. In the context of tourism and hospitality, Uslu and Caber85 argued that perceived value has a significant influence on tourist satisfaction and behavioral intention.

Overall, although prior studies have confirmed the positive effects of perceived value on satisfaction and loyalty, its role has rarely been examined alongside destination image and satisfaction within a unified model. In traditional village tourism, its nonlinear effects and necessary conditions for loyalty formation also remain underexplored. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H5: Perceived value in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist satisfaction.

H9: Perceived value in traditional village tourism has a positive impact on tourist loyalty.

Research model

Based on the above literature review and analysis, this study constructs a research model of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism (see Fig. 1) and proposes the following hypotheses: Objective authenticity has a positive effect on destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value (H1a–H1c); co-creation exerts a positive effect on destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value (H2a–H2c); electronic word-of-mouth positively influences destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value (H3a–H3c); and service quality has a positive impact on destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value (H4a–H4c). In addition, destination image and perceived value positively influence tourist satisfaction (H5–H6), while destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value all positively affect tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism (H7–H9).Fig. 1Research model of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism.

Research method

This study was conducted in six stages (see Fig. 2). First, relevant literature was reviewed to identify the core factors influencing tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism and to construct an S–O–R-based theoretical model. Second, a representative case site was selected, the questionnaire was developed and refined through a pilot survey, and the formal field survey was conducted. Third, PLS-SEM was used to test the linear path relationships and research hypotheses. Fourth, ANN analysis was applied using latent variable scores from PLS-SEM to examine nonlinear prediction and predictor importance. Fifth, NCA was conducted to identify necessary conditions for tourist loyalty and to complement the SEM and ANN results. Finally, the integrated SEM-ANN-NCA findings were discussed to derive the theoretical, methodological, and practical implications of the study.Fig. 2Research process of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism.

Research process of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism.

Study case and subject

This study selects Fangyu Village in Jinan, Shandong Province as the case site. Nestled in a valley between the Taishan mountain range and Jinxiuchuan Reservoir, the village enjoys a favorable ecological environment (see Fig. 3). Established during the Song Dynasty, Fangyu Village still preserves intact ancient dwellings, stone-paved alleys, and traditional village layouts from the Ming and Qing dynasties, earning it the reputation of a “Museum of Ming and Qing Stone Architecture.” In 2016, it was included in the fourth batch of China’s List of Traditional Villages86. As a typical traditional village rich in both tangible and intangible cultural resources, Fangyu Village demonstrates remarkable representativeness in terms of cultural value, social influence, and recognition. Therefore, its selection as the research site not only provides a prototypical context for traditional village tourism studies but also comprehensively covers the measurement scope of related variables, thereby ensuring the scientific rigor and validity of this research.Fig. 3Cultural heritage of Fangyu village.

Data collection

Instrumentation

The measurement scales in this study were adapted from established instruments, with appropriate modifications made to fit the context of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism (see Supplementary Material, Table A1). A seven-point Likert scale was employed (1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree”) to assess respondents’ level of agreement87. The scale for objective authenticity was adapted from Zhou et al.88; co-creation from Caru and Cova89 and Prebensen and Foss90; electronic word-of-mouth and tourist satisfaction from Martinez-Navalon et al.91; service quality from Yen et al.92; destination image from Ramseook-Munhurrun et al.8; perceived value from Kim et al.93; and tourist loyalty from Mechinda et al.94.

Formal data collection

In June 2025, a pilot survey was conducted with 50 Chinese tourists who had prior traditional village tourism experience to evaluate the questionnaire’s applicability and validity. Based on the refined questionnaire, the formal survey was conducted using a mixed online and offline approach, which is widely used in Chinese rural tourism research13. Offline data were collected at the Fangyu Village scenic area from July 10 to 15, 2025, where two trained surveyors randomly distributed questionnaires, provided on-site clarification, and offered small beverages as tokens of appreciation. The online survey was administered through Sojump (Wenjuanxing) to improve sample diversity95. Screening questions were used to exclude respondents without rural tourism experience, and a 5 RMB incentive was provided for valid responses. A total of 398 questionnaires were collected; after removing 52 responses with outliers and 16 with missing key information, 330 valid samples were retained for analysis (see Fig. 4).Fig. 4Data collection process.

Respondent profile

As shown in Table 2, the 330 valid samples included in the final analysis were subjected to descriptive statistical analysis using SPSS software. The demographic characteristics of the sample indicate the following: in terms of gender distribution, males accounted for 53.03% and females for 46.97%. Age was concentrated within the prime working group of 26–45 years (26–35 years: 25.15%; 36–45 years: 23.94%), together comprising 49.09% and reflecting strong consumption potential. The overall education level was relatively high, with 38.79% holding a bachelor’s degree or above. Monthly income was highest in the 7001–9000 RMB range (29.70%), suggesting solid economic capability among respondents. SEM–ANN–NCA hybrid research method

To examine tourist loyalty formation in traditional village tourism, this study adopts a sequential hybrid approach of “PLS-SEM → ANN → NCA.” . PLS-SEM was first used to validate the structural model and test linear path relationships, although its linear assumptions limit its ability to capture nonlinear effects and predictor importance97.ANN analysis was then conducted in SPSS 27 using a Multilayer Perceptron model, with SEM-significant predictors as input neurons and tourist loyalty as the output; RMSE and sensitivity analysis were used to evaluate model performance and predictor importance98. Finally, NCA was applied to identify necessary conditions for high tourist loyalty through CE-FDH ceiling lines and bottleneck analysis99. By integrating linear, nonlinear, and necessity-based perspectives, this study provides a comprehensive explanation of tourist loyalty formation.

Findings

Common method bias

Common method bias refers to a form of systematic error arising from the measurement process rather than from the constructs under investigation. It may artificially inflate or attenuate the statistical associations among variables, thereby distorting inferences about their true relationships. Such bias is typically induced by factors related to questionnaire design, administration procedures, and respondents’ response styles during survey completion, and thus may undermine the robustness of validity and reliability in empirical studies relying on self-reported data100. To detect and control for this potential bias, the present study conducted Harman’s single-factor test as an initial diagnostic to examine whether a single latent factor accounts for the majority of the measurement variance101. Exploratory factor analysis performed using SPSS 27.0 extracted eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, explaining a cumulative variance of 73.896%. The first principal component accounted for 31.277% of the total variance, which is well below the commonly accepted threshold of 40%. These results indicate that common method bias is not a significant concern in the present study.

Findings of PLS-SEM

Following the research approach of Hair et al.102 and Sarstedt et al.103, this study employed SmartPLS 4.1 software to conduct a two-stage modeling analysis104. In the first stage, the measurement model was validated using the PLS algorithm; in the second stage, the research hypotheses were tested and evaluated through bootstrapping.

According to Nunnally and Bernstein105, when both CA and CR exceed 0.7, the measurement model can be considered to have high reliability. As shown in Table 3, the CA and CR values of all latent variables in this study were greater than 0.8, reaching an excellent level. At the same time, to avoid path coefficient distortion caused by high correlations among variables, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was used to diagnose multicollinearity. Based on the criterion of Neter et al.106, VIF values below 5 indicate no severe multicollinearity risk. Building on the SEM results (see Fig. 5), we employed the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) algorithm in SPSS to construct and train ANN models2. Comparative analysis of SEM-ANN results

Based on the results reported in Table 9, this study applies a sequential comparison approach to contrast the path coefficients derived from Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) with the normalized relative importance obtained from Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). PLS-SEM is grounded in linearity and additivity assumptions; thus, its path coefficients represent average linear effects. Findings of NCA

In sufficiency analysis, the core focus of PLS-SEM lies in verifying the “sufficient” condition—namely, whether the predictor variable (X) promotes and enhances the outcome variable (Y). In contrast, Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA) emphasizes the “indispensable” condition, treating X as a prerequisite constraint for the occurrence of Y110. NCA plots predictors on the x-axis and outcomes on the y-axis, using the Ceiling Envelopment-Free Disposal Hull (CE-FDH) technique to construct a stepwise ceiling line (see Fig. 6). The ceiling line separates feasible and infeasible observation areas and identifies the minimum level of X required to achieve a given level of Y. By integrating PLS-SEM and NCA, this study examines both sufficient effects and necessary conditions. To ensure robustness, a randomization test with 10,000 permutations was conducted to assess the significance of the effect size d, and ceiling accuracy was calculated to reduce pseudo-ceiling effects.Fig. 6Relationship of condition (X) for outcome (Y) through CE-FDH.

Effect size and significance testing

In Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA), the effect size (d) is used to measure the minimum level of a condition required to produce a specific outcome, ranging between 0 and 1, with values closer to 1 indicating stronger effects. When d < 0.1, the effect is generally considered small. Since the present study aims to validate the core necessary conditions, the effect size must meet the threshold of d > 0.1, and the significance test results must satisfy P < 0.05111.

Discussion

This study examines the psychological mechanisms underlying tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism. It tests how destination-related stimuli, including objective authenticity, co-creation, electronic word-of-mouth, and service quality, influence destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value, thereby shaping loyalty formation. To enhance methodological rigor, a hybrid SEM–ANN–NCA approach was adopted, with ANN cross-validation used to improve predictive robustness.

The results show that destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value all exert significant positive effects on tourist loyalty, which is consistent with previous studies23. This finding further suggests that loyalty in traditional village tourism is jointly rooted in cognitive evaluation, affective response, and overall value judgment, rather than being driven by a single post-visit attitude. Existing studies have similarly argued that destination loyalty is formed through the cumulative interaction of image perception, emotional confirmation, and value assessment, especially in experience-intensive tourism contexts113,114.

Among these factors, perceived value emerged as the strongest driver of loyalty. This may reflect the experiential nature of traditional village tourism, where tourists value cultural depth, immersive settings, and emotional engagement more than conventional service excellence. When cultural learning, emotional fulfillment, or aesthetic appreciation is perceived as worth the cost, loyalty is substantially enhanced.19,115. This interpretation is in line with prior literature suggesting that perceived value in heritage and rural tourism is not limited to functional utility, but also includes emotional, symbolic, and epistemic returns derived from local culture and authentic experience. The dominance of younger, well-educated visitors in the sample further reinforces this pattern, as these groups tend to favor knowledge-oriented and value-driven travel experiences. Together, these findings indicate that traditional village tourism constitutes a value-dominant consumption context, where loyalty is grounded more in experiential returns than in service performance alone17.

Tourist satisfaction ranks second in its influence on loyalty, which is consistent with Chi and Han69, although its effect is weaker than that of perceived value. This result may be explained by the nature of satisfaction in traditional village tourism. Satisfaction is often generated through immediate emotional responses to cultural immersion, spatial atmosphere, service encounters, and on-site experiences. Therefore, it mainly reflects whether the visit meets or exceeds tourists’ short-term expectations. By contrast, perceived value represents a more comprehensive post-experience judgment, involving tourists’ assessment of whether the cultural, emotional, aesthetic, and educational benefits of the trip justify their monetary, temporal, and psychological investment. Compared with satisfaction, perceived value is more likely to be retained as spatial memory and transformed into cultural identification, thereby exerting a more enduring influence on loyalty116. In this sense, satisfaction functions as an affective evaluation of the immediate travel experience, whereas perceived value operates as a more stable value-based judgment formed through retrospective assessment. This distinction helps explain why perceived value shows stronger predictive power for loyalty than satisfaction. Similar differences between transient affective appraisal and more durable value-oriented evaluation have also been emphasized in tourism research. Destination image also shows a significant but relatively weaker impact. This can be explained by the fact that destination image in traditional village tourism is often externally constructed through media and promotional narratives, and its influence may be moderated by on-site authenticity perceptions. Moreover, deeper cultural understanding typically requires direct engagement, making destination image alone insufficient to directly drive loyal behavior.

The study further reveals that the effects of objective authenticity and co-creation are stronger than those reported in some prior rural tourism studies, reflecting the distinctive contextual attributes of traditional village tourism. Both constructs exert direct and positive effects on destination image, satisfaction, and perceived value, consistent with Hua et al.117 and Zhao et al.34. These strong effects highlight the essence of traditional villages as culturally participatory destinations, where tourists seek not merely consumption, but proximity to cultural authenticity and participation in meaning construction. Through such processes, deeper cultural identification and a stronger sense of belonging are fostered, leading to more enduring emotional outcomes118. In environments rich in cultural symbols and historical narratives—such as traditional architecture, folk crafts, and customary practices—authenticity experiences generate heightened emotional rewards, amplifying their effects on perceived value and loyalty119. Younger and more educated tourists, who tend to prefer culturally deep experiences, are particularly responsive to authenticity and co-creation, further explaining the stronger effects observed. Compared with general rural tourism settings, traditional villages usually possess denser historical symbolism and more visible material carriers of culture, which may make authenticity easier to perceive and co-creation more meaningful to tourists. This helps explain why the explanatory power of these two variables is especially pronounced in the present context.

Regarding experiential value, both electronic word of mouth (eWOM) and service quality positively influence destination image, perceived value, and satisfaction, consistent with Zheng et al.120. All eWOM-related paths are significant, underscoring the critical role of online evaluations in shaping tourists’ cognitive and affective appraisals. This is especially salient in traditional village tourism, where information asymmetry is pronounced and tourists rely heavily on online experience sharing. In contrast, the direct effect of service quality on satisfaction is not statistically significant (p > 0.05), echoing Seow et al.121. This suggests that traditional village tourism is fundamentally culture-driven rather than service-driven. Satisfaction is more strongly derived from cultural immersion, emotional resonance, and meaning construction than from standardized service performance.

This non-significant effect reflects the distinctive nature of service provision in traditional villages, which is often characterized by low standardization, high contextual dependence, and community-based hospitality. Such environments prioritize locality, interpersonal warmth, and everyday authenticity over professional consistency, resulting in greater tolerance toward service variability107,122. As long as basic expectations are met, service quality does not function as a primary benchmark for satisfaction. Instead, deficiencies in cultural experience are more likely to trigger negative evaluations. Consequently, in cultural-expectation—dominant contexts, the marginal effect of service quality on satisfaction is substantially weakened123. The demographic profile of the sample—dominated by younger, well-educated tourists—further reinforces this pattern, as these visitors show higher acceptance of “simple” and “down-to-earth” service styles while prioritizing cultural depth and authenticity. This explanation is consistent with studies arguing that in place-based and culture-led destinations, tourists often interpret modest or non-standardized services as part of the local atmosphere rather than as service failure, provided that such services do not obstruct the core experiential process.

These findings collectively indicate that tourist satisfaction in traditional village tourism is driven more by perceived value and emotional attachment than by service provision alone. Destination image and perceived value also exert significant positive effects on satisfaction, consistent with Zhao et al.34, as favorable images raise psychological expectations and perceived value strengthens experiential identification. Accordingly, integrating local culture and interactive experiential activities into tourism planning is essential for enhancing loyalty.

Furthermore, the ANN analysis provides complementary insights into the relative nonlinear importance of destination image, perceived value, and tourist satisfaction in shaping tourist loyalty. By capturing complex and nonlinear relationships, ANN strengthens the robustness of the SEM results and offers a deeper understanding of the psychological mechanisms underlying loyalty formation108. The ANN results show that perceived value has the strongest nonlinear influence on loyalty, followed by tourist satisfaction and destination image. This suggests that loyalty formation is not driven solely by linear causal paths but by asymmetric effects of cognitive and affective evaluations. The dominant role of perceived value indicates that when tourists perceive traditional village experiences as worthwhile, loyalty may increase disproportionately. Satisfaction further reinforces this process, while destination image indirectly supports value perception and satisfaction formation. Overall, the ANN findings corroborate the SEM results and reveal the hierarchical and nonlinear structure of loyalty formation. Practically, destination managers should prioritize value creation by enhancing cultural authenticity, interpretive depth, and interactive engagement.

Finally, NCA uncovers a necessary condition structure underlying tourist loyalty formation. Co-creation, eWOM, and service quality emerge as necessary conditions for a positive destination image, indicating that minimum thresholds of information quality, interaction, and basic service assurance must be met34,124. Moreover, eWOM is identified as a necessary condition for satisfaction, while co-creation is necessary for perceived value, highlighting the foundational roles of information credibility and participatory depth55.These findings show that some factors function as irreplaceable prerequisites rather than variables following a simple “more is better” logic. Sufficient participation is needed for value formation, positive informational cues are needed for satisfaction, and loyalty depends on the joint fulfillment of value and emotional needs. The NCA results therefore complement SEM and ANN by revealing the minimum levels that antecedents must reach before favorable psychological responses can occur.Overall, the integrated SEM–ANN–NCA analysis confirms that co-creation and electronic word-of-mouth influence tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism through perceived value and satisfaction. This provides a theoretical basis for improving protection strategies and development models in traditional village tourism.

In conclusion, the integrated SEM–ANN–NCA analysis confirms that co-creation and electronic word-of-mouth influence tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism through the mediating roles of perceived value and satisfaction. This finding offers a solid theoretical basis for developing protection strategies and optimizing development models in traditional village tourism.

Conclusions

Grounded in the S–O–R (Stimulus–Organism–Response) framework, this study investigates the formation mechanism of tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism. To enhance explanatory power and address the limitations of single-method analyses, an integrated SEM–ANN–NCA approach was adopted. SEM was used to test linear path relationships, ANN to capture potential nonlinear effects, and NCA to identify necessary conditions underlying key outcomes such as tourist loyalty. The results show that objective authenticity, co-creation, user evaluations, and service quality significantly enhance destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value, although the effect of service quality on satisfaction was not statistically significant. Destination image and perceived value further exert significant positive effects on tourist satisfaction, while perceived value and satisfaction emerge as particularly important drivers of tourist loyalty. ANN identifies perceived value and satisfaction as the strongest predictors of loyalty, whereas NCA highlights the indispensability of authenticity, co-creation, electronic word of mouth, and service quality through different mediating paths. Overall, these findings reveal a progressive formation mechanism of tourist loyalty and provide an integrated basis for strengthening sustainable development in traditional village tourism.

Theoretical implications

Theoretically, this study extends the S–O–R framework by explaining tourist loyalty formation in traditional village tourism. Objective authenticity, co-creation, electronic word-of-mouth, and service quality are conceptualized as key stimuli, while destination image, tourist satisfaction, and perceived value serve as organismic evaluations linking stimuli to loyalty. The findings highlight the central roles of perceived value and satisfaction and show that eWOM and co-creation influence loyalty indirectly through these evaluations. Thus, this study enhances the explanatory power and contextual applicability of the S–O–R framework in traditional village tourism.

Methodologically, this study proposes an integrated SEM–ANN–NCA hybrid model to overcome the limitations of single-method approaches. SEM tests hypothesized linear paths, ANN captures nonlinear relationships, and NCA identifies indispensable prerequisites for high-level outcomes. This integrated analytical strategy broadens the application of hybrid modeling in tourism research and offers a useful paradigm for examining complex behavioral mechanisms across diverse tourism contexts.

Managerial implications

Managerially, this study translates the SEM–ANN–NCA findings into strategies for strengthening tourist loyalty in traditional village tourism. First, cultural authenticity should be prioritized by preserving traditional architecture, presenting folk practices, and displaying intangible cultural heritage to enhance destination image and trust. Second, participatory experiences, such as handicraft co-creation and interactive folk activities, should be developed to deepen emotional engagement and perceived value. Third, given the role of electronic word of mouth, managers should improve online communication through user-generated content touchpoints and responsive review mechanisms. Although service quality is not the strongest direct driver, basic services such as cleanliness, signage, and safety remain necessary for a positive destination image. Finally, cultural value recognition should be strengthened through transparent pricing, value-oriented experience design, and post-experience feedback, thereby promoting revisit intention and positive word-of-mouth. Through collaboration among governments, local communities, enterprises, and tourists, traditional villages can better balance heritage preservation with tourism development while enhancing tourists’ emotional attachment and loyalty.

Limitations and future work

Although this study develops an integrated SEM–ANN–NCA framework and offers theoretical, methodological, and practical contributions, several limitations remain. First, the data were collected from a specific regional context, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to traditional villages with different cultural or developmental conditions. Second, although the model includes key constructs from prior literature, other factors such as seasonality, crowding, and unexpected events were not considered. Third, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to capture dynamic causal relationships over time. Methodologically, SEM depends on model specification, ANN improves nonlinear prediction but has limited interpretability, and NCA results may vary according to ceiling line techniques and threshold estimation. Future research could expand the sample across different regions and village types, adopt longitudinal or mixed-method designs, and use advanced techniques such as virtual reality experiments or eye-tracking to better examine the psychological mechanisms underlying tourist loyalty.
