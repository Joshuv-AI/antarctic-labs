The financial implications of selective disenrollment from Medicare advantage
Authors: Richard Kronick, Logan Johnson, Ramona Krauss, Shangbin Tang, Daniel Waldo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrior studies show that beneficiaries with relatively high health needs are more likely to disenroll from Medicare Advantage (MA) and return to Traditional Medicare (TM). However, the financial implications for MA plans of selective disenrollment have not been quantified.MethodsWe calculated annual disenrollment rates from MA to TM, and compared TM spending in months 1-12 and 13-24 following disenrollment with the expected capitation payments that MA plans would have received.ResultsFor 2019 disenrollees, TM spending in the first 12 months following disenrollment averaged 30% higher than the expected MA capitation. However, disenrollment was relatively rare—1.7% of MA members in 2019, much lower than the 4.3% in 2007, and declining further to 1.2% in 2022. Because disenrollment is rare, its effect on plan finances is limited, increasing spending on MA by approximately 0.6%.ConclusionAlthough the effect of selective disenrollment on plan finances is limited, the consistent direction and magnitude of selection suggest that a measured policy response is warranted.Keywords: medicare advantage, medicare health policy

Introduction

Beneficiaries with greater health needs disenroll from Medicare Advantage (MA) to Traditional Medicare (TM) at higher rates than other enrollees, a pattern documented in multiple studies.1-11 These studies consistently find that beneficiaries with higher spending, greater morbidity, or both are more likely to disenroll from MA than those with lower health care needs. This pattern is often discussed as part of a broader literature on favorable selection in MA, which has focused primarily on differences in risk-adjusted spending at enrollment. However, prior analyses have not assessed the financial implications of selective disenrollment for MA plan and Medicare program finances.

Overall disenrollment rates from MA to TM are low—1.2% in 2022.12 This raises an important question: even if disenrollment is selective, does it have a meaningful effect on plan payments or program spending? The answer is not obvious. Low rates of disenrollment suggest that any financial effects may be small, but differences in spending between disenrollees and stayers could still have meaningful effects on MA plan and Medicare program finances.

In this paper, we answer the questions: How much higher are the costs to Medicare of disenrollees than the capitation MA plans would have received had these beneficiaries remained enrolled? Do these differences translate into a material effect on MA plan revenue or profit, on Medicare program spending, or on the supplemental benefits available to beneficiaries? We address these questions for the MA program overall and for major parent organizations.

Data and methods

We measure TM spending on beneficiaries who disenroll from MA in the 24 months following disenrollment, and compare post-disenrollment TM spending to the capitation payments that the MA plan would have received had the beneficiaries stayed enrolled in MA. If the post-disenrollment TM spending is greater than the capitation payment that the MA plan would have received, then we conclude that the MA plan received a financial advantage from selective disenrollment. Our assumption is that post-disenrollment TM spending is similar to the spending for which the MA plan would have been responsible had the beneficiary stayed enrolled in MA. This assumption is discussed further in the Supplemental Material Section A1.

We use TM claims and MA encounter data for 2018 through 2021 from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW), and monthly enrollment, dual eligibility status, and annual demographic information from the Master Beneficiary Summary Files (MBSF) for calendar years 2007 through 2022. We utilize CMS's public use data files covering MA plan enrollment, payment, rebate, contract, and parent organization information. All CCW data analyses were conducted at the CMS Virtual Research Data Center under the terms of a CMS data use agreement.

We use the Master Beneficiary Summary File to identify Medicare beneficiaries who were enrolled in MA and switched to TM, remaining in TM for at least 13 months (or until their death, whichever was sooner). We use 2 cohorts of disenrollees—first, those who disenrolled during 2019, and second, those who disenrolled in 2021. We use TM claims for these beneficiaries to measure how much was spent on them in the months following disenrollment. Details are provided in the Supplemental Materials, Sections A1 and A2.

As shown in the Supplemental Material Section D, substantial numbers of beneficiaries disenroll from MA and then re-enroll within a 13-month period. Because we are interested in measuring the difference between TM spending and capitation payments in the 12 months following disenrollment, and because most of these short-term disenrollees were in TM for only a few months, we exclude these beneficiaries from the analytic sample.

We use CMS Plan Payment Data from 2019-2021 to estimate the amount that the plan would have been paid if the beneficiary had remained enrolled. Details on methods are provided in the Supplemental Material Section A1.

The COVID-19 pandemic both lowered TM spending during 2020 and lowered MA risk scores in 2021 because fewer diagnoses were recorded in 2020. As shown in the Supplemental Material Section E, those 2 effects largely balance each other, and we do not make an adjustment to our estimates for a COVID-19 effect.

Our primary analysis focuses on disenrollees in 2019 because we are able to analyze 24 months of post-disenrollment TM spending and compare that to 24 months of expected capitation payments for beneficiaries who disenrolled in 2019. To provide estimates based on more recent data, we also analyze beneficiaries who disenrolled in 2021. However, at the time we conducted the analysis, plan payment data for 2022 were not available, and for 2021 disenrollees, our analysis is restricted to comparing TM spending and expected capitation payments during calendar year 2021. In order to compare 2019 and 2021 results, we conduct a parallel analysis on 2019 disenrollees, restricting that analysis to TM spending and expected capitation payments during calendar year 2019.

To analyze whether selective disenrollment varies across insurers, we estimate 3 contract-level regressions. In each regression, the unit of analysis is one of the 491 contracts in 2019. In the first regression, the dependent variable is the 2019 disenrollment rate; in the second it is the average monthly difference between TM spending on 2019 disenrollees in the 12 months following disenrollment minus the expected capitation payment that the contract would have received had the member stayed enrolled; in the third it is the aggregate value of the quantity in the second (ie, aggregated across all disenrollees and across 12 months) divided by the total revenue the contract received during those 12 months. Key independent variables are indicator variables for each of the 10 largest insurers. We also include variables for the star rating of the contract, the fraction of members in the contract who are dually eligible for Medicaid, and the number of years that the contract participated in Medicare. Details are in the Supplemental Material Section A3.

Results

Disenrollment rates declined substantially from 2007 through 2022 (Figure 1). In 2022, 1.2% of MA enrollees disenrolled to TM and did not return to MA within 13 months, much lower than the 4.3% disenrollment rate in 2007. The 2022 disenrollment rate was higher among beneficiaries eligible for full Medicaid benefits (2.3%) than among other Medicare beneficiaries, although that rate also declined substantially from 2007 to 2022 (Figure 2). The disenrollment rate among beneficiaries eligible for partial Medicaid benefits was much higher than average in 2007, but by 2022, it was no different than for the average Medicare beneficiary.

Disenrollment rates are highest among beneficiaries who had recently enrolled in MA. Among both 2019 and 2021 disenrollees, beneficiaries who had been enrolled in MA for less than 12 months had substantially higher disenrollment rates than those enrolled for longer periods of time (Figure S1). The finding of higher disenrollment among more recent MA enrollees may reflect, in part, early sorting or mismatch at enrollment. Some beneficiaries may initially select plans that do not align well with their health care needs or preferences and subsequently switch after gaining experience with coverage.

In 2021, MA members disenrolling in January accounted for 23% of all disenrollees, with 20% disenrolling in February and March, and the remaining 56% disenrolling in April through December (Table S2). As expected, given their greater opportunity to disenroll outside of open enrollment, dual eligibles are more likely than Medicare-only members to disenroll later in the year.

TM spending on 2019 disenrollees in the 12 months after they disenroll ($1431/month) was 30% greater than the capitation payments that MA plans would have received ($1102/month) (Table 1). The difference between TM spending and expected MA capitation payments is much larger for dually-eligible beneficiaries ($605/month, or 39% of expected capitation payments) than for Medicare-only members ($191/month, or 22% of expected capitation payments). TM spending on disenrollees in months 13-24 following disenrollment was 13% greater than the capitation payments that MA plans would have received during those months, substantially smaller than the 30% difference in months 1-12 (Table 1).

While these per-beneficiary differences are substantial, the aggregate financial impact is modest because relatively few beneficiaries disenroll. Aggregated across all disenrollees, the difference between TM spending and expected capitation payments for 2019 disenrollees in the 12 months following disenrollment was $1.5 billion, which was 0.6% of the $249.2 billion in total capitation payments that MA plans received during those 12 months (Table 1). The aggregate difference in months 13 through 24 following disenrollment was 0.15% of total capitation payments (Table 1).

Because the disenrollment rate is higher for dually-eligible enrollees than for Medicare-only beneficiaries, and because the per-member-per-month difference between TM spending and estimated capitation payments is greater for those enrollees than for Medicare-only, the financial benefit to MA plans from disenrollment is relatively greater for dual eligibles than for Medicare-only beneficiaries. For dual eligibles, the aggregate difference between TM spending and expected capitation payments in the first 12 months following disenrollment was 1.4% of total capitation payments, compared to 0.3% for Medicare-only beneficiaries (Table 1).

Disenrollees in 2021 were more highly selective than 2019 disenrollees: for 2021 disenrollees, the difference between TM spending and expected capitation payments in 2021 averaged $708 per month, or 64.2% of expected capitation payments, with the comparable figures being $415 per month, or 39.6% of expected capitation payments for 2019 disenrollees (Table S2). The lower disenrollment rate in 2021 compared to 2019 largely balances the somewhat greater selectivity of the 2021 disenrollees: the aggregate difference between the TM spending on 2021 disenrollees in 2021 and the capitation payments that would have been received as a share of the total capitation payments received by MA in 2021 was 0.66%; the comparable statistic for 2019 was 0.56% (Table S2).

The aggregate difference between TM spending and expected capitation payments of 0.6% of premium likely does not result in greater profit for the MA plans, but rather in greater rebates that are used to provide supplemental benefits, lower cost sharing, or lower premiums for members. If selective disenrollment is anticipated—as it likely is given that MA plan bids are based on historical claims experience, and that the historical experience includes selective disenrollment—then the average MA bid was approximately 0.6% lower than it would have been in the absence of selective disenrollment. In addition, county-level benchmarks are slightly higher than they would have been if disenrollment were not selective. A rough estimate is that selective disenrollment causes the benchmarks to be 0.3% higher than they would have been had high-cost beneficiaries not selectively disenrolled (total TM spending in 2019 was $488 billion, and $1.5 billion is 0.3% of that total). If selective disenrollment does cause bids to be 0.6% lower than they would have been in the absence of selective disenrollment (as opposed to simply resulting in greater profit to the MA plan if the bid did not adjust downward), and if selective disenrollment causes benchmarks to increase by 0.3%, then the difference between the average MA plan bid and the benchmark is about 0.9% greater than it would have been if disenrollment had not been selective. The rebate is calculated as a percentage of the difference between the bid and the benchmark, with the percentage depending on the MA plan's star rating, and averaging approximately 65%.12 Under these assumptions, the average rebate in 2021 was approximately 0.6% of the benchmark ($6/month) greater than it would have been had disenrollment not been selective, accounting for about 4% of the average 2021 rebate of $140 per month.

To examine whether disenrollment from MA to TM accumulates over a longer horizon, we followed beneficiaries enrolled in MA in April 2018 and measured the share who were in TM in April of each subsequent year. Disenrollment was concentrated in the first 2 years: 1.6% had disenrolled by April 2019, an additional 1.0% by April 2020, and only 0.2% in each subsequent year, for cumulative disenrollment to TM of 3.2% over 5 years (Table S4).

Insurer level results

Controlling for star rating, dual eligibility, and the number of years the contract participated in Medicare, there were few notable differences across insurers in disenrollment rates in 2019 (Table 2). Disenrollment rates in 2019 were higher in contracts with lower star ratings: compared to the average disenrollment rate in 2019 of 1.7%, disenrollment in 3-star contracts was more than double the average. The association of star ratings and disenrollment rates is similar to the findings in Meyers et al.5 Because plan all-cause disenrollment is included as a measure in the Star Ratings system, higher disenrollment may contribute to lower star ratings, raising the possibility of reverse causality. However, this measure receives relatively low weight, so changes in disenrollment rates are likely to have only a modest effect on overall star ratings. Consistent with the results in Figure 2, disenrollment was higher in contracts with a larger fraction of dually-eligible members: for each 10% point increase in the proportion of members who were dually eligible, the disenrollment rate increased by 0.16%.

Similarly, with a few exceptions, the extent to which disenrollment is selective does not vary substantially across insurers (columns 3 and 4 of Table 2). The difference between TM spending in the 12 months following disenrollment and the expected capitation payments that MA plans would have received is significantly less at Kaiser, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan, and Highmark Health than the average for insurers that are not one of the top 10 insurers (the omitted category).

As would be expected given relatively small insurer-level differences in disenrollment rates and in the extent to which disenrollment is selective, the financial benefit of selective disenrollment does not vary widely among the 10 largest insurers (columns 5 and 6 of Table 2). As a share of total premium, the aggregate difference between TM spending and expected capitation payment was significantly less for Kaiser and Highmark Health than the average for insurers not in the top 10 (the omitted category), and not significantly greater than average at any of the 10 largest insurers.

These insurer-level comparisons are descriptive and do not adjust for potential unobserved differences across contracts and markets that may influence both disenrollment patterns and spending.

Limitations

There are 3 main limitations to our analysis. First, we do not know how much would have been spent by MA plans on disenrollees had those members remained in MA; we assume that MA plan spending would have been similar to the TM spending on these disenrollees. As discussed in the Supplemental Material Section A1, it seems unlikely that this assumption has much effect on our conclusions. Second, our analysis is focused on long-term disenrollees—beneficiaries who remain in TM for at least 13 months—and we do not analyze the TM spending of short-term disenrollees who reenrolled in MA within 13 months. As shown in the Supplemental Material (Section D and Figures S2-S4), this omission does not materially affect our results. Third, we do not analyze members who disenroll from one MA plan and reenroll in another MA plan. This omission should not have any effect on our estimates of the MA industry-wide benefit from selective disenrollment, but may have an effect on insurer-specific estimates. To the extent that high-cost members are selectively leaving some insurers and enrolling with others, some insurers may benefit (and others may be hurt) by selective disenrollment.

Discussion and conclusion

Relatively few MA members disenrolled to TM in 2022: excluding members in cost, PACE, demonstration contracts, and contracts that terminated, only about 1.2% of MA members disenrolled and stayed in TM for at least 13 months—much lower than the 4.3% rate in 2007.

Declining disenrollment may reflect several factors, although our analysis does not directly test these mechanisms. Declining disenrollment may reflect increased financial benefits of staying in MA, made possible in part by more intensive coding.13,14 However, average rebates changed little from 2007 to 2016, while disenrollment rates were falling, so increased rebates cannot be the entire explanation. Financial incentives to brokers to retain beneficiaries may also play a role.15 The difficulty and cost of obtaining Medigap coverage likely discourage some dissatisfied enrollees from leaving MA,16 but these constraints have changed little over time and cannot fully explain the downward trend in disenrollment. Disenrollment rates are not much different in the 4 states that guarantee issue to Medigap than in the remaining states that do not, calling into question the extent to which the “Medigap trap” accounts for low disenrollment rates.17 Further, the low and declining disenrollment rates among dually-eligible beneficiaries—who face no Medigap barrier—suggest Medigap availability is not the primary driver. Higher disenrollment in the first 12 months of enrollment, followed by lower and declining rates thereafter, may reflect growing satisfaction with MA, although it could also indicate that some beneficiaries feel locked in once Medigap becomes inaccessible. Additional work is needed to understand these dynamics.

MA members who disenroll are costly: TM spending on 2019 disenrollees in the 12 months after disenrollment was 30% higher than the capitation payments plans would have received. Our results align with those of Biniek et al., who found TM spending on disenrollees was 27% higher than on comparable continuously enrolled TM beneficiaries (61% higher for dually-eligible, 20% for Medicare-only), similar to our estimates (39% and 20%).10

Yet because disenrollment is rare, the aggregate financial impact is small. The difference between TM spending and expected capitation payments in the first 12 months after disenrollment was $1.5 billion for 2019 disenrollees—only 0.6% of total capitation payments that year—and similar in 2021.

While per-beneficiary differences are substantial, only a small share of beneficiaries disenroll and remain in TM, and not all of these are among the highest-cost members. Many Medicare beneficiaries incur substantial costs, and only a small minority of high-need members disenroll annually. These patterns likely reflect a combination of factors. Some transitions may represent routine switching or evolving beneficiary preferences. At the same time, higher disenrollment among higher-need beneficiaries may reflect dissatisfaction with plan networks, prior authorization requirements, or other features of MA coverage. Even in the absence of any plan-driven behavior, higher-need beneficiaries may be more likely to leave MA because they place greater value on flexibility and are more sensitive to care management restrictions. Even if the aggregate financial effects are modest, these patterns may have important implications for beneficiary experience and access to care.

Selective disenrollment also affects MA plan bids and rebates. Because plan bids are informed by historical claims experience, and that experience incorporates the effects of prior disenrollment, bids are likely somewhat lower—and rebates somewhat higher—than they would be in the absence of selective disenrollment. However, the extent to which the effect of selective disenrollment is reflected in bids, rebates, or plan profits depends on plan behavior and cannot be directly observed.

Selective disenrollment makes only a minor contribution to overall favorable selection in MA. To place our estimates in context, MedPAC estimates that favorable selection increased payments to MA by $23 billion in 2019 and $31 billion in 2021 relative to a counterfactual of neutral selection. In comparison, our estimate that selective disenrollment increased payments to MA by approximately $1.5 billion in each of those years suggests that selection at enrollment is the more important driver of favorable selection.

Selective disenrollment of relatively high-cost MA members increases the average cost of beneficiaries in TM and, as a result, increases the TM benchmarks against which MA plans bid. Our estimates suggest that this effect is small but real. A straightforward policy response would be to adjust county-level benchmarks downward by approximately 0.9% to offset the effects of selective disenrollment.
