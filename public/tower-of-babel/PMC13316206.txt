Exploratory compositional profiling of metabolite and antioxidant properties in green perilla (Perilla frutescens) seeds at maturation stages
Authors: Tae Joung Ha, Jin Hwan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen perilla has been utilized as food and medicinal resources in East Asia. This research employed an exploratory compositional profiling approach to investigate variations in metabolite constituents and antioxidant capacities of green perilla seeds at three maturation stages: unripe (UR), mid-ripe (MR), and full ripe (FR). Twelve phytochemicals identified by UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS showed distinct stage dependent patterns. Six isolated phenolics exhibited significant differences, with the highest level at MR (28,697.8 μg/g), followed by UR (16,132.6 μg/g) and FR (7820.0 μg/g). Specifically, luteolin-7-O-glucoside exhibited remarkable fluctuations with decreasing levels (13,161.9 → 28,192.6 → 152.9 μg/g). Protein and oil contents increased progressively, with oil content rising 13.5 → 46.1%, accompanied by marked increases in linolenic acid (31.0 → 60.8%) and γ-tocopherol contents (10.4 → 265.4 μg/g). Antioxidant properties were highest at MR, demonstrating 87.6% (DPPH), 100% (ABTS), 5.64 OD593nm (FRAP) at 200 μg/mL, and 87.6% (DNA protection) at 250 μg/mL, followed by UR > FR. Additionally, chemometric analysis (PCA and heatmap) revealed clear stage-associated differences in the metabolic profiles. These findings provide preliminary yet insightful data on the maturation-related chemical shifts in green perilla seeds, establishing a descriptive baseline for optimizing harvest times based on desired functional constituents.Keywords: Green perilla seeds, Maturation, Phytochemicals, UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS, Luteolin-7-O-glucoside, Antioxidant activity, TocopherolChemical compounds: Caffeoylquinic acid (PubChem CID: 1794427), Caffeic acid (PubChem CID: 689043), p-Coumaric acid (PubChem CID: 637542), Luteolin-7-O-glucoside (PubChem CID: 5280637), Apigenin-7-O-glucoside (PubChem CID: 5280704), Rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (PubChem CID: 131751481), Chrysoeriol-7-O-glucoside (PubChem CID: 11294177), Rosmarinic acid (PubChem CID: 5281792), N-Octanoyl sucrose (PubChem CID: 447970), Luteolin (PubChem CID: 5280445), Apigenin (PubChem CID: 5280443), Chrysoeriol (PubChem CID: 5280666)

Introduction

Plant-derived foods contain diverse phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, and tocopherols (Ahirwar & Singh, 2023; Andarwulan et al., 2021; Gonçalves et al., 2019; Kaur et al., 2024; Massarioli et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2018), which exhibit broad biological activities, including anticancer, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects (Cooney et al., 1993; Deng et al., 2024; Kim & Choi, 2001; Lee & Cho, 2021; Sabahi et al., 2024). The profiles of these metabolites vary substantially depending on genetic background, developmental stage, environmental conditions, processing techniques, and maturation times (Assefa et al., 2018; Bhaswant et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2022; Lee & Cho, 2021; Tsao et al., 2006). Understanding how maturation dynamically influences phytochemical composition is essential for optimizing harvest timing and enhancing applications in foods, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceuticals (Lee et al., 2013; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Sabahi et al., 2024; Serrano-García et al., 2024).

Perilla (Perilla frutescens L. Britt), an economically important herb of the Lamiaceae family, traditionally used in East Asia for culinary and medicinal purposes (Chen et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2014; Saini et al., 2020). This crop is known for its treatment of depression, cough, nausea, and digestive disorders (Kaur et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023). Modern studies have confirmed its antioxidant, anticancer, anti-obesity, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral properties (Kosuna & Haga, 1997; Lee et al., 2017; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). These effects are attributed to abundant phenolic metabolites, volatile oils, triterpenes, and polysaccharides (Assefa et al., 2018; Kaur et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023). Perilla is commonly divided into red and green chemovarietal forms (Chen et al., 2015; Lee & Cho, 2021; Saini et al., 2020), with green perilla particularly enriched in phenolics and widely utilized in foods, cosmetics, and traditional medicine due to its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Kosuna & Haga, 1997; Lee et al., 2017). While the leaves are known to be rich in essential oils and phenolic compounds (Lee et al., 2017; Lee & Cho, 2021; Saini et al., 2020), the seeds accumulate substantial amounts of lipophilic components such as α-linolenic acid, linoleic acid, and tocopherols, which play important roles in preventing lipid peroxidation and regulating metabolic functions (Kaur et al., 2024; Kim & Choi, 2001; Kosuna & Haga, 1997; Peiretti et al., 2011). Among seed metabolites, phenolic compounds are recognized as key contributors to antioxidant and disease-preventive activities due to their hydroxylated aromatic structures (Assefa et al., 2018; Olennikov et al., 2018; Rahman et al., 2018; Sabahi et al., 2024). Recent evidence shows that certain flavonoids can exert dose-dependent adverse effects, including hepatotoxicity under specific exposure conditions (Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025), highlighting that potential harms should be considered alongside their benefits. In addition, tocopherols and unsaturated fatty acids from perilla seeds have gained considerable attention because of their nutritional significance and protective effects against oxidative stress (Cooney et al., 1993; Kim & Choi, 2001; Peiretti et al., 2011; Ponciano et al., 2024). Notably, γ-tocopherol exhibits distinctive antioxidant properties through its ability to neutralize reactive nitrogen species, which enhances its physiological relevance (Cooney et al., 1993; Lee et al., 2018). Based on the above considerations, many investigations emphasize the need to characterize metabolite diversity and elucidate the functional and therapeutic potential of perilla and related edible plants (Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Nooshkam et al., 2019; Sabahi et al., 2024). Despite extensive interest in perilla, little is known about the dynamic variation of phenolic metabolites, tocopherols, and antioxidant properties in perilla seeds during maturation, particularly in green chemovarietal forms that accumulate high phenolic levels. Previous studies have reported major metabolites or biological activities in perilla leaves and seeds (Assefa et al., 2018; Guan et al., 2014; Kosuna & Haga, 1997; Lee & Cho, 2021; Saini et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023), but systematic, stage-specific profiling of green perilla seeds has not been conducted. While some reports have initiated discussions on the safety and bioactivity of flavonoids (Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025), there remains a persistent need to evaluate how these constituents fluctuate in maturation stages.

Furthermore, no research has clarified how maturation stage influences metabolite accumulation or which harvest stage maximizes metabolite content and antioxidant activity. These gaps indicate the need for comprehensive exploratory profiling linked with functional antioxidant evaluation.

To overcome these limitations, this work provides an exploratoy analysis of phenolic metabolites, tocopherols, and antioxidant capacities in green perilla seeds through maturation stages. Using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole Orbitrap mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS), we elucidated the phenolic profiles throughout seed development, while HPLC facilitated quantitative analysis of key metabolites in different accessions. Multivariate approaches, including principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical heatmap analysis, were used to investigate metabolic correlations and maturation dependent compositional shifts. In addition, antioxidant potential was evaluated using radical scavenging assays (DPPH and ABTS), ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), DNA protection assays, and measurements of total phenolic and flavonoid contents (TPC and TFC). This study identifies maturation stage-dependent metabolic patterns and provides a descriptive baseline for optimizing harvest timing and developing nutraceutical applications of green perilla seeds.

Materials and methods

Plant sources and sampling methodology

Perilla (cv. Dayu) was cultivated during the 2022 growing season at the experimental field of the National Institute of Crop Science, Rural Development Administration, located in Miryang (35.493°N, 128.744°E), Gyeongsangnam-do, Republic of Korea. All plants were grown under open-field conditions following the institute's standardized agronomic practices to minimize spatial and management-related variability. The experimental plot consisted of well-drained sandy loam soil, a typical soil type for perilla cultivation in this region. To ensure uniform soil conditions, all samples were collected from the same experimental plot, where consistent management was applied, including a standardized pre-planting fertilization (N:P2O5:K2O = 40:30:30 kg/ha), regular overhead irrigation to prevent water stress, and systematic pest control following the institute's protocols. While continuous environmental monitoring such as soil pH, moisture content, temperature, and light intensity was not conducted, field-derived variability was minimized through these controlled management practices and a systematic sampling design. Within this exploratory approach, the present study was designed to provide a descriptive investigation of maturation stage dependent metabolic changes under consistent field conditions. Sampling was conducted based on predefined morphological and physiological criteria representing three maturation stages: unripe (UR; green husk with soft seed texture), mid-ripe (MR; partially browned husk with intermediate firmness), and fully ripe (FR; brown husk with hardened seeds). These characteristics are commonly used in agronomic and phytochemical studies to define early, transitional, and final maturation stages. Perilla seeds were harvested on 19 Sep, 29 Sep, and 9 Oct, corresponding to the UR, MR, and FR stages, respectively. To ensure biological representativeness within each stage, sampling was not limited to a single collection. On each date, samples were collected at two time points (10:00 AM and 3:00 PM), with three independent collections performed at each point from multiple positions within the plot, resulting in six biological collections per maturation stage (n = 6). Importantly, as noted in the study's exploratory scope, these six collections per stage were pooled prior to compositional analysis. This pooling strategy was employed primarily for compositional stabilization and to ensure analytical feasibility for the initial exploratory profiling to the respective maturation stages. While we previously suggested this was to reduce variability, we acknowledge that characterizing biological variability is essential for a definitive interpretation of maturation-dependent effects. Therefore, it should be noted that this pooling approach results in the loss of information regarding within stage variation and reduces the statistical power for inferential analysis. The harvested seeds were washed with sterile water and air-dried at room temperature for 2 days under natural light, and their appearances are illustrated in Fig. 1. The seeds were then freeze-dried and stored at −78 °C for further analysis.

Chemicals

Metabolite standards were extracted from perilla seeds using 50% methanol at room temperature for 7 days, followed by filtration and rotary evaporation to obtain the crude extract. The concentrated extract was then applied to a silica gel column and fractionated following previously published methods (Lee et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2017; Lee & Cho, 2021) to isolate individual metabolite standards. The isolated compounds were validated through HPLC purity analysis and by confirming their molecular ions and fragmentation patterns using TOF-MS. Their identities were further verified by comparison with previously reported data of well-known perilla metabolites. Analytical grade water and acetonitrile were obtained from J.T. Baker (Philipsburg, NJ, USA). Silica gel 60Rp-18 and TLC aluminum RP-18 F254 plates were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich for assessing the purity of the isolated metabolites. 2,2-Diphenyl-1-pycrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) (ABTS), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and 6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemicals used in the current research were of analytical grade (Sigma).

Evaluation of antioxidant effects

Antioxidant activities were measured using an Agilent BioTek spectrophotometry (EPOCH2, Winooski, VT, USA) by evaluating absorbance values. A 50% methanol solution was selected to maximize the extraction of phenolic constituents and their associated antioxidant effects. The antioxidant abilities of perilla seeds at different maturation stages were assessed using ABTS, DPPH, and FRAP assays, following previously published methods, with minor modifications (Deng et al., 2024; Gonçalves et al., 2019; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023). Pulverized seeds (1.0 g) were extracted with 50% MeOH (20 mL) at 25 °C for 24 h. The supernatant was filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper and immediately used for antioxidant properties. For the ABTS•+ assay, the scavenging effect was evaluated using Trolox as a positive control (Lee et al., 2023). A 7 mM ABTS•+ solution was prepared in ethanol, and the radical cation was generated by reacting it with 2.45 mM K2S2O8 followed by incubation for 12 h to achieve a stable absorbance. The ABTS•+ stock solution was diluted to an absorbance of 0.70 at 734 nm. The diluted ABTS•+ solution (0.9 mL) was mixed with sample solution (0.1 mL), and absorbance was measured after 3 min. The scavenging ability (%) was calculated using the formula:

ABTS•+ scavenging capacity (%) = [1 – absorbance of sample / absorbance of control] × 100.

The DPPH radical scavenging effect was determined using a modified method based on Gonçalves et al. (2019), Lee, Cho, et al. (2024) and Lee, Kwak, et al. (2024). In brief, sample extract (0.1 mL) at various concentrations was mixed with methanol (0.49 mL) and 1 mM DPPH methanolic solution (0.39 mL). The mixtures were vortexed and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer, with BHT (positive control). The scavenging activity was determined as a percentage by the following equation:

DPPH radical scavenging capacity (%) = [1 – absorbance of sample / absorbance of control] × 100.

The FRAP assay was carried out using a reaction mixture consisting of 2.5 mL of 10 mM TPTZ solution, 25 mL of 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), and 2.5 mL of 20 mM FeCl3 solution, which was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, 0.05 mL of the diluted sample was added to 0.95 mL of the FRAP solution and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 15 min. Absorbance was evaluated at 593 nm, and the reducing power of the sample or ascorbic acid (positive control) was assessed in comparison with the established procedure (Deng et al., 2024; Gonçalves et al., 2019).

Evaluation of DNA protection

The DNA cleavage protection assay against metal-catalyzed oxidative damage performed according to previous reported methods (Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2018). The 50% methanol extracts at eight different concentrations (10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, 750, and 1000 μg/mL) were combined with 5 μL of 3.3 mM dithiothreitol and 5 μL of 15.4 μM FeCl3, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2.5 h. Subsequently, 1 μg of pUC18 supercoiled plasmid DNA was added to the reaction mixture. After incubation, 5 μL of the mixture was combined with 1 μL of loading buffer and electrophoresed on a 0.8% agarose gel in TAE buffer (40 mM Tris-acetate, 1 mM EDTA). Electrophoresis was conducted at 85 V for 30 min, and DNA bands were visualized and documented using a Gel Doc XR+ imaging system equipped with UV illumination. Band intensities were analyzed using Image Lab software. The percentage inhibition of DNA damage was calculated using the equation described by Rahman et al., 2018, with minor modifications:

DNA protection (%) = (SF DNA band intensity / pUC18 plasmid DNA band intensity) × 100.

SF: Super-coiled form.

DNA protection assay was performed using three independent experiments with triplicate samples (n = 3), and results are presented as mean of percentage inhibition of DNA damage.

Measurement of TPC and TFC

TPC and TFC were determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent BioTek EPOCH 2, Winooski, VT, USA), following previously reported procedures (Deng et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024). TPC was quantified colorimetrically using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. Briefly, 500 μL of appropriately diluted sample extract or gallic acid (positive control) was mixed with 250 μL of 1 N Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, followed by the addition of 500 μL of 20% sodium carbonate. After incubation at 35 °C for 5 min, absorbance was measured at 750 nm and compared with a calibration curve (0.01–1 mg/mL). The results were expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE/g). TFC was also assessed using a colorimetric method. A sample or rutin (positive control) (250 μL) was mixed with 50% ethanol (2 mL), followed by the addition of 1 mL of diethylene glycol (90 g/100 mL) and 1 mL of 4 N NaOH. After incubation at 35 °C for 10 min, the crude solution was recorded at 420 nm and compared with a calibration curve (rutin, 0.01–1 mg/mL). The results were expressed as rutin equivalents (RE/mg).

Evaluation of protein, oil, fatty acid, and tocopherol contents

Oil and protein contents were demonstrated using a Buchi Extraction System (B-811, Buchi, Schweiz) and an Auto Kjeldahl Analyzer (B-339, Buchi, Schweiz). Tocopherols were quantified using a UPLC Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo, Dionex) equipped with a fluorescence detector (Dionex) and an Eclipse XDB C18 column (5 μm, 150 mm × 4.6 mm). Fatty acid composition was analyzed using a GC (Agilent 7890 A, Boeblingen, Germany) coupled with a flame ion detector and an SP-2560 capillary column (0.25 μm, 100 mm × 0.25 mm). Protein content was measured using the Kjeldahl nitrogen method. Ground seeds (0.2 g) were digested in 20 mL H₂SO₄ with a catalyst mixture (K₂SO₄:CuSO₄ = 9:1, w/w) using a Buchi B-435 digestion system and B-412 scrubber. The digested solution was distilled using a Buchi 339 unit, and protein content was calculated as nitrogen percentage multiplied by 6.25 (Lee et al., 2023). Oil content was determined using a Buchi B-811extraction system (Lee et al., 2023). Pulverized seed (2.0 g) were extracted with hexane (200 mL) by boiling for 2 h at 105 °C. After cooling to 25 °C in a desiccator, oil content was expressed as a percentage of dry seed mass. Fatty acid contents were evaluated by methylating the extracted fat using a toluene:MeOH:water (10:20:1, v/v/v) solution. The crude mixture was heated in a water bath (100 °C) and cooled at 25 °C. After being filtered through a syringe filter, the organic layer was injected into a gas chromatography (GC) following established protocols (Lee et al., 2023), and the results were expressed as percentages of total fatty acids. For tocopherol analysis, the perilla seeds (0.5 g) were extracted with 2-propanol (10 mL) at 25 °C for 3 h in a shaking incubator (Andrés et al., 2011; Lee et al., 2018). The supernatant was filtered through a syringe filter (0.45 μm, Whatman Inc., Maidstone, UK) prior to UPLC analysis.

Arrangement of sample and calibration curve for quantification of phenolic metabolites

To demonstrate the phenolic metabolite contents, the dried perilla seeds (1.0 g) were extracted with 20 mL of 50% MeOH for 24 h at 25 °C in a shaking incubator. The supernatant was centrifuged for 5 min at 3000g and subsequently filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter (Whatman Inc., Maidstone, UK) prior to HPLC analysis. The calibration curves were generated by integrating the peak areas of isolated phenolic metabolites from the HPLC chromatogram at 330 nm with the Agilent ChemStation software. The stock solutions of each phenolic metabolite were prepared in DMSO at a concentration of 1000 μg/mL. Calibration curves were constructed in triplicate (n = 3) by diluting the stock solution with DMSO to eight different concentrations: 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL. Curves exhibiting high linearity (r2 > 0.998) were selected from these replicates and used to determine the retention times of individual phenolics. The preparation of curves was conducted as reported in earlier studies with minor modifications (Lee et al., 2013; Lee & Cho, 2021).

Measurement of phenolic metabolite contents

Six phenolic contents were analyzed by HPLC Dionex Ultimate 3000 series (Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with a diode array detector, autosampler, quaternary gradient pump, and Eclipse XDB C18 column (5 μm, 125 mm × 4 mm). A 50% methanol extract (20 μL) of perilla seeds was injected into the analytical reverse phase C18 column. Gradient elution was carried out with two solvents: elution A (0.1% acetic acid in water) and elution B (0.1% acetic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: 5% B from 0 to 3 min, increased to 10% B at 5 min, 30% B at 20 min, and 50% B at 30 min, and then maintained for 10 min before reverting to the initial conditions. The column temperature was consistently kept at 25 °C and the total running time was 40 min at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Phenolic metabolites were detected at a wavelength of 330 nm.

UPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap-MS conditions

Phenolic profiling was conducted using a UPLC Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to a hybrid quadrupole Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive™, Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization source and a Halo C18 column (2.7 μm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm). The injection volume was 10 μL, and the column temperature was maintained at 25 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (elution A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (elution B). The gradient elution program was applied as follows: 5% B (0–3 min), 5–30% B (3–10 min), 30–50% B (10–20 min), 50–70% B (20–30 min), and 70–90% B (30–40 min). Orbitrap mass spectra were acquired in the negative ionization mode with a scan range of m/z 50–550, a resolution of 70,000 FWHM, and a scan rate of 2.0 s/cycle. The instrument was operated in full MS (MS1) scan mode combined with data-dependent MS/MS (MS2) acquisition for the most intense precursor ions, enabling fragmentation-based characterization of phenolic compositions. The MS source parameters were set as follows: capillary temperature, 270 °C; heater temperature, 400 °C; spray voltage, +3.5 kV; auxiliary gas pressure, 10 arb; sheath gas pressure, 40 arb. Instrument operation and data acquisition were performed using Xcalibur Qual Browser software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). These conditions were prepared with minor modifications from a previously reported method (Lee et al., 2017).

Statistical analysis and data processing

Phenolic metabolite, antioxidant effects, TPC, and TFC were obtained from three independent experiments with triplicate sample (n = 3), and the results were presented as mean ± SD (standard derivation). Contents of protein, oil, fatty acid, and tocopherol were determined in three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data are shown as mean ± SD. Their differences in maturity times of perilla seeds were analyzed using Duncan's multiple range test at a significant level, based on the 0.05 probability level by the statistical analysis software (SAS) (9.2 PC package, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Heatmap and clustering analyses were conducted using mean values through MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca) (Chong et al., 2019). Heatmaps were generated using the ComplexHeatmap package in R (version 4.3.1) after z-score normalization of variables. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward's linkage method, and distance between variables were calculated using Pearson correlation distance (1 − Pearson correlation). PCA was performed using OriginLab 10.0 (Northampton, MA, USA), producing score plots for PC1 and PC2 with 95% confidence intervals.

Results and discussion

Variations of antioxidant properties in green perilla seeds at maturation times

To assess the antioxidant potential of perilla seed extracts, we employed widely used in vitro spectrophotometric assays, including DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging tests and the FRAP assay (Assefa et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2013; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Sabahi et al., 2024). Although these chemical assays do not fully reflect the complexity of biological antioxidant mechanisms, they are standardized, reproducible, and internationally recognized for evaluating the radical quenching and reducing capacities of plant metabolites. This approach provides a preliminary basis for comparison and is consistent with methods used in previous studies. In addition, a DNA protection assay was incorporated to provide a complementary descriptive perspective by examining the extract's ability to prevent oxidative DNA damage. To ensure accurate functional evaluation, 50% methanol was selected as the extraction solvent; aqueous methanol consistently extracts phenolic compounds more effectively than ethanol (Chen et al., 2022; Guan et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2013), thereby improving the accuracy of both compositional analysis and antioxidant assessment. The DPPH and ABTS methods were conducted by measuring the percentage inhibition of radical formation, comparing the results with those of positive controls (BHT and Trolox), as reported for natural plants and crops in earlier studies (Deng et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023). While recent reports have discussed potential dose-dependent effects of flavonoids in specific models (Deng, Zhao, Cao, et al., 2025; Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025), in vitro assays serve as essential initial indicators for screening antioxidant potency. In the initial DPPH radical scavenging experiments, both perilla seed extracts and BHT showed increased inhibition with rising concentrations (100 → 200 → 300 → 500 → 1000 μg/mL). Although complete (100%) inhibitions were achieved at higher extract concentrations (300 → 1000 μg/mL), the study focused on 200 μg/mL to reflect dose-dependent variations more clearly. These results were similar patterns to our previous findings (Lee et al., 2013; Lee & Cho, 2021). As shown in Fig. 2A, radical scavenging percentages differed significantly across both concentration levels (5–200 μg/mL) and maturation stages. The order of antioxidant activity was MR > UR > FR. Seeds collected on MR indicated the highest inhibition rates were as follows in all concentrations: 87.6% at 200 μg/mL, 77.5% at 100 μg/mL, 45.5% at 50 μg/mL, 30.8% at 25 μg/mL, 17.4% at 10 μg/mL, and 14.2% at 5 μg/mL (Fig. 2A). The UR sample exhibited intermediate levels (71.7, 61.7, 36.1, 19.2, 9.8, and 4.1% at 200 → 5 μg/mL), while the FR source showed the lowest activity (61.6, 50.6, 30.5, 12.7, 7.0, and 3.6% at 200 → 5 μg/mL) (Fig. 2A). Although all samples displayed lower DPPH scavenging than BHT (100% at 200 μg/mL) (Lee & Cho, 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2017), the results indicate that seeds harvested on MR possess the highest antioxidant potential via this mechanism. In the ABTS assay, the same rank order (MR > UR > FR) was observed (Fig. 2B). The MR sample showed strong concentration-dependent ABTS inhibition (24.6 → 30.2 → 44.5 → 58.0 → 89.2 → 100%) at extract levels of 5 → 200 μg/mL. The UR seeds followed with 11.1 → 20.0 → 27.5 → 44.3 → 78.9 → 81.5%, while the FR sample exhibited 4.6 → 10.2 → 20.5 → 40.4 → 60.4 → 69.2%. Although most values were lower than the Trolox control (100% at 100–200 μg/mL), all extracts showed pronounced dose dependency. The difference between ABTS and DPPH values, approximately 15% higher ABTS inhibition at comparable concentrations, suggests differing sensitivities to the associated antioxidant mechanisms (Fig. 2A and B). To further validate the antioxidant properties of perilla seeds, the FRAP assay was performed to measure Fe3+-reducing capacity (Fig. 2C). As with the radical scavenging assays, FRAP activity increased in proportion to extract concentration and differed significantly between maturation stages. Seeds harvested on MR showed the highest FRAP values (5.64 → 2.41 → 1.62 → 0.86 → 0.29 → 0.13 OD593 nm, at 200 → 100 → 50 → 25 → 10 → 5 μg/mL), followed by the UR seeds (3.75 → 1.91 → 0.50 → 0.31 → 0.17 → 0.11) and the FR source (2.23 → 1.18 → 0.31 → 0.15 → 0.08 → 0.05) (Fig. 2C), maintaining the same rank order in all assays. These consistent patterns in DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays indicate that antioxidant potential peaks at the mid-maturation stage represented by MR. This trend is primarily attributed to maturation stage dependent changes in the composition and concentration of phenolic metabolites (Cheng et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2021). Specifically, the MR stage represents the peak period of secondary metabolite biosynthesis in seed tissues, during which phenolic compounds accumulate at maximum levels prior to degradation or conversion at full maturity. This trend is likely associated with changes in TPC, TFC, and phenolic metabolite accumulation, which are known to have strong correlations with antioxidant capacity in many natural sources (Andarwulan et al., 2021; Assefa et al., 2018; Deng et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2014; Sabahi et al., 2024). To clarify this relationship, Pearson correlation analysis was performed, revealing strong positive correlations between TPC/TFC and DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP activities (p < 0.05), suggesting that phenolic accumulation significantly contributes to antioxidant capacity during seed maturation. The decline in antioxidant activity in fully ripe seeds (9 Oct) reflects physiological changes occurring during late seed maturation. As seeds approach full ripeness, two metabolic processes likely contribute to this reduction: (1) oxidative degradation of phenolic compounds induced by maturation-related oxidative processes, and (2) enzymatic conversion of free phenolics into bound or polymerized forms with lower antioxidant bioavailability. Previous studies have reported similar relationships between phenolic composition and radical scavenging or reducing power in plant tissues (Andarwulan et al., 2021; Assefa et al., 2018; Deng et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2021), and the reduction in antioxidant activity observed in fully ripe seeds (9 Oct) may reflect phenolic conversion or dilution effects during late maturation stages, a pattern also reported in other crop species (Kumar et al., 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Tsao et al., 2006). Furthermore, late maturation is generally characterized by decreased biosynthetic activity and increased catabolism, resulting in reduced formation of new phenolics and oxidative modification of existing compounds. The generally higher ABTS values suggest that this method captures both hydrogen-donating and chain-breaking antioxidant properties, consistent with earlier observations in perilla and other plants (Assefa et al., 2018; Ha et al., Lee et al., 2013). In summary, these findings support the conclusion that perilla seeds harvested at MR exhibit the most favorable antioxidant profile, making them strong candidates for nutraceutical applications. However, considering the descriptive nature of these findings, further integrated studies are required to characterize the metabolic safety and physiological interactions before clinical nutraceutical utilization (Deng, Zhao, Cao, et al., 2025; Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025). Thisstudy provides a comparative analysis illustrating how antioxidant capacity fluctuates during key maturation stages of green perilla seeds. Although the antioxidant and DNA protection assays were conducted under in vitro conditions, they are widely used as preliminary indicators of radical scavenging and biomolecular protective capacities of plant metabolites. Because in vivo effects depend on complex bioavailability and metabolic interactions, these results primarily reflect the intrinsic redox properties within an exploratory framework. However, the correlations among phenolic composition (TPC and TFC), antioxidant activity, and DNA protection suggest that maturation stage dependent phenolic accumulation may help reduce ROS-induced biomolecular damage. Future integrated studies building upon this baseline are needed to evaluate the broader physiological relevance and safety profiles.

Measurement of DNA protection in green perilla seeds at maturation times

Our research assesses the super-coiled DNA protection rates of recombinants in green perilla seeds at maturation times, based on radical scavenging and FRAP capacities. The DNA protection assays were performed to provide an exploratory evaluation of the ability of seed extracts to protect super-coiled plasmid DNA (pUC18) from hydroxyl radical induced damage (Lee et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2018). Using the MCO system, DNA protection ratios were quantified at eight concentrations (1000, 750, 500, 250, 100, 50, 10, and 1 μg/mL), and the band intensities of super-coiled versus nicked forms were assessed by gel electrophoresis (Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023). While the pooled sample design limits the assessment of biological variability, the resulting protection patterns differed markedly in the three maturation stages, following the same order observed in DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays: MR > UR > FR (Fig. 2F–2H). The extracts obtained at the MR stage exhibited the most pronounced protective effect, showing 100% DNA protection at 500, 750, and 1000 μg/mL. Lower concentrations also indicated clear dose-dependent responses: 87.6% at 250 μg/mL, 76.3% at 100 μg/mL, 67.2% at 50 μg/mL, 48.2% at 10 μg/mL, and 21.2% at 1 μg/mL (Fig. 2G). Samples harvested at the UR and FR stages also protected DNA in a concentration-dependent manner, though with lower efficiencies. Their protection rates were UR: 8.6 → 17.9 → 26.7 → 35.6 → 58.5 → 64.5 → 81.8 → 90.9% (Fig. 2F), and FR: 1.9 → 5.4 → 7.3 → 10.5 → 18.8 → 21.7 → 25.5 → 33.9% (Fig. 2H). The maturation stage dependent variation in DNA protection capacity is closely associated with differences in phenolic metabolite abundance and composition. The MR sample showed the strongest DNA protective effect due to its higher phenolic content, which enhances ROS scavenging through electron- and hydrogen-donating mechanisms (Lee et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2018). This preliminary finding is significant, given recent animal studies showing that certain flavonoids can exacerbate xenobiotic-induced hepatotoxicity via gut microbiota and liver metabolism modulation (Deng, Zhao, Cao, et al., 2025; Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025). The decline from MR to FR likely reflects reduced phenolic biosynthesis and increased oxidative conversion during late seed maturation, resulting in lower levels of free phenolic antioxidants. Collectively, these results indicate that all extracts preserved super-coiled DNA mobility to varying degrees, with substantial differences in maturation times. Within this exploratory framework, a clear pattern can be seen: seeds harvested earlier (particularly at MR) exhibit superior functional properties. This trend is consistent with previous studies reporting that DNA protection is greatly influenced by the amount and types of phenolic metabolites (Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2018). The similar patterns observed between DNA protection activity and TPC/TFC levels further support that phenolic metabolites play an important role in reducing ROS-mediated DNA damage in plant systems (Lee et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2018). Pearson correlation analysis also showed a significant positive association between phenolic content (TPC and TFC) and DNA protection rates (p < 0.05), which is consistent with the role of phenolic metabolites in mitigating ROS-induced DNA damage. In summary, green perilla seeds collected at MR showed the strongest DNA protective capacity among the samples tested, reflecting their higher phenolic content and antioxidant potential. These results suggest that perilla seeds harvested at the MR stage could potentially be used as valuable materials for future nutraceutical development, breeding programs, and future applications designed to support human health. It is important to note that the plasmid DNA protection assay used in this study represents a simplified in vitro oxidative damage model and does not fully reflect cellular DNA repair processes or intracellular antioxidant environments. However, it serves as a practical descriptive indicator linking chemical antioxidant capacity to potential biomolecular protection. The maturation-associated differences observed here, while preliminary due to the pooled design, suggest a possible role in mitigating ROS-induced damage. Future integrated validation using independent biological replicates and in vivo models is required to further investigate these observations.

Distributions of TPC and TFC in green perilla seeds at maturation times

To characterize the phenolic profiles of green perilla seeds within an exploratory context, the TPC and TFC were quantified in 50% methanol extracts collected at three maturation stages. As indicated in Fig. 2D and E, both TPC and TFC exhibited clear and significant variations in maturation stages. Seeds harvested at MR showed the highest levels, with 5.20 GAE mg/g for TPC and 1.68 RE mg/g for TFC, followed by seeds collected at UR and FR. More specifically, TPC values followed the order: MR (5.20 GAE mg/g) > UR (3.85 GAE mg/g) > FR (1.29 GAE mg/g) (Fig. 2D). Similarly, TFC values were consistent with this pattern: MR (1.68 RE mg/g) > UR (1.10 RE mg/g) > FR (0.61 RE mg/g) (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that both phenolic and flavonoid accumulation decrease progressively as seed maturation advances from late Sep to early Oct, reflecting stage dependent metabolic transitions. The MR represents the peak of secondary metabolite biosynthesis, when phenolic biosynthetic enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, cinnamate 4-hydroxylase, and flavonoid synthases are most active and phenolic precursors are abundant (Cheng et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2021). These patterns are consistent with previous findings that phenolic accumulation in perilla and other plants is strongly influenced by growth stage, environmental conditions, and metabolic changes during maturation (Cheng et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2017; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). The decline in TPC and TFC during the FR stage reflects a metabolic shift from biosynthesis to catabolism. During late maturation, the activity of phenolic biosynthetic pathways decreases, whereas oxidative and hydrolytic enzymes (e.g., peroxidases and polyphenol oxidases) increase, promoting degradation, polymerization, and oxidative modification of phenolics. Previous studies have also reported that TPC and TFC are positively associated with antioxidant activity and overall biofunctional quality (Deng et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024), supporting our interpretation that the higher phenolic levels on MR are likely to contribute to the enhanced antioxidant capacities observed in this study. Consistent with these reports, Pearson correlation coefficients calculated in the present study indicated significant positive correlations between TPC/TFC levels and all measured antioxidant parameters (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and DNA protection) (p < 0.05). While these results provide insightful stage-level profiles, the pooled sample design means they should be considered descriptive rather than definitive. Differences in TPC and TFC may reflect macromolecules degradation and the increased release of hydroxyl containing phenolics during specific maturation stages, consistent with earlier reports (Lee & Cho, 2021; Nooshkam et al., 2019). During the transition from UR to MR, enhanced glycosidase and esterase activities promote hydrolytic cleavage of sugar moieties from flavonoid and phenolic conjugates, releasing free phenolic aglycones that contribute to increased TPC values. As discussed above, these two values may serve as an important factor in perilla quality for developing functional food agents, owing to the clear differences in contents based on growth times (Ha et al., 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Tsao et al., 2006). Notably, while higher TPC/TFC correlate with antioxidant capacity, recent in vivo evidence indicates potential for flavonoid-mediated adverse interactions at specific exposures, underlining the need for safety testing (Deng, Zhao, Cao, et al., 2025; Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025). Overall, the phenolic profiles measured in this work indicate that seeds harvested at MR possess the most favorable phytochemical characteristics for potential future applications. To our knowledge, this study provides a preliminary quantitative comparison of TPC and TFC fluctuations in green perilla seeds during key maturation periods, establishing a foundation for further detailed studies. Our research suggest that while high phenolic levels enhance bioactivity, they may also cause dose-dependent adverse effects, necessitating careful safety and bioavailability assessment.

Fluctuations of protein, oil, and fatty acid contents in green perilla seeds at maturation times

As shown in Table 1, the protein, oil, and fatty acid compositions of green perilla seeds exhibited clear fluctuations in the three maturation periods. Protein contents increased modestly, ranging from 18.7 to 22.8%, whereas oil contents displayed a much larger accumulation, rising from 13.5 to 46.1% as maturation progressed from UR to FR. Specifically, protein levels increased sequentially (18.7 → 21.5 → 22.8%), while oil levels expanded markedly (13.5 → 34.4 → 46.1%) (Table 1). These results suggest that oil accumulation appears to increase more rapidly than protein accumulation during seed maturation under the conditions studied, potentially reflecting maturation dependent shifts in biosynthetic priorities and the allocation of carbon resources. While the lack of independent biological replicates limits statistical inference, these patterns provide a descriptive overview of reserve accumulation. During UR, protein synthesis is dominant to establish storage capacity for germination. As seeds progress to MR and FR, metabolic resources are increasingly redirected toward lipid accumulation via enhanced activities of fatty acid synthase and acetyl-CoA carboxylase, which convert carbohydrate precursors into long chain fatty acids for storage (Abdelghany et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2023). Fatty acid profiling also revealed distinct patterns (Table 1). Among the individual fatty acids, C18:3 consistently remained the dominant component, accounting for approximately 51% of total fatty acids and exhibiting substantial increases during maturation (31.0 → 60.6 → 60.8%). In contrast, saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids such as C16:0, C18:0, and C18:2 showed slight declines (22.6 → 8.0 → 7.3%; 7.6 → 2.6 → 2.8%; 28.9 → 13.0 → 13.1%, respectively), while C18:1 exhibited a moderate increase (9.9 → 15.4 → 16.1%) (Table 1). The pronounced accumulation of C18:3 during maturation reflects activation of polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, particularly delta-12 and delta-15 desaturases that introduce double bonds into C18 fatty acids. These enzymes become more active during mid-to-late seed maturation. The final fatty acid distribution followed the order: C18:3 (50.8%) > C18:2 (18.3%) > C18:1 (13.8%) > C16:0 (12.6%) > C18:0 (4.3%), indicating that perilla seeds are consistently enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acids throughout maturation. These findings are in accordance with previously reported nutritional characteristics of perilla seeds, which typically contain 15.7–23.9% protein and 30–45% oil, with fatty acid distributions dominated by linolenic acid, followed by oleic and linoleic acids (Kaur et al., 2024; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Peiretti et al., 2011). Earlier investigations have shown that lipid metabolism, particularly the accumulation of C18:3, is strongly influenced by harvest timing and environmental conditions rather than genetic factors (Abdelghany et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2023). The pronounced increase in oil and C18:3 contents during later maturation may also reflect enhanced lipolysis and oxidative modifications in membrane lipids, consistent with mechanisms reported in related plant systems (Silva et al., 2013). Although environmental conditions influence these compositional changes, previous research has reported that oil content generally exhibits only minor variation across cultivation years, growth durations, and other production-related factors (Lee et al., 2023; Mirdoraghi et al., 2024). Although the current design is more appropriately suited to exploratory profiling, total fatty acid content showed relatively minor variation across maturation stages compared to individual fatty acid proportions. These patterns suggest C18:3 may serve as a key parameter for assessing perilla quality within the scope of this study. These observations are consistent with previous reports describing fatty acid distribution in both perilla seeds and germinated seeds (Bhaswant et al., 2024; Kaur et al., 2024). Moreover, our findings suggest that the fatty acid contents in the perilla plant may be influenced by environmental parameters, consistent with an exploratory framework (Abdelghany et al., 2020; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Mirdoraghi et al., 2024). This work provides a detailed yet preliminary comparison of protein, oil, and fatty acid fluctuations in green perilla seeds. While these shifts offer insightful data for functional food development, the exploratory nature of the dataset necessitates caution in drawing generalized conclusions, and further validation with independent biological replication is required to further investigate these maturation-dependent effects.

Fluctuations of tocopherols in green perilla seeds at maturation times

To obtain more detailed compositional information on the metabolites in green perilla seeds within an exploratory approach, we investigated the profiles and distributions of tocopherols in three maturation periods. Although tocopherols play considerable roles in various biofunctional effects (Andrés et al., 2011; Cooney et al., 1993; Kim & Choi, 2001; Lee et al., 2018), there is currently no available data on the fluctuations and comparisons of tocopherol contents in perilla plant. As presented in Fig. 3A, the representative HPLC chromatogram of three tocopherol standards was obtained within 12 min, with retention times of δ-tocopherol (tR = 8.2 min), γ-tocopherol (tR = 9.8 min), and α-tocopherol (tR = 11.6 min). The quantitative analysis revealed substantial variations in both individual and total tocopherol contents in the three harvesting dates (UR → MR → FR) (Table 1 and Fig. 3B–3D). While the pooled sample design limits the assessment of biological variability, the observed highest total tocopherol level was in seeds harvested at the MR stage (317.0 μg/g), followed by those collected at FR (274.0 μg/g) and UR (26.4 μg/g). The 12-fold increase in total tocopherols from UR to MR offers a descriptive insight into the maturation stage dependent upregulation of tocopherol biosynthetic enzyme genes, indicating coordinated activation during mid-seed development. This increase may be associated with enhanced expression of key tocopherol biosynthetic genes, including HPT, tocopherol cyclase, and γ-tocopherol methyltransferase, which regulate metabolic flux from homogentisate toward γ- and α-tocopherol formation (Deng, Zhao, Hu, et al., 2025; Ponciano et al., 2024). Notably, γ-tocopherol was the predominant component in the MR sample, accounting for 311.3 μg/g (98.2% of the total), followed by δ-tocopherol (4.9 μg/g; 1.5%) and α-tocopherol (0.8 μg/g; < 0.3%) (Table 1 and Fig. 3C). This γ-tocopherol enrichment at MR, coinciding with peak phenolic metabolite content (28,697.8 μg/g), reflects a preliminary pattern of coordinated activation of multiple antioxidant defense pathways in response to maturation stage specific oxidative stress signals, likely mediated by ROS-responsive transcription factors and abscisic acid signaling. (Cooney et al., 1993; Kumar et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2018). FR samples showed γ-tocopherol (265.4 μg/g) > α-tocopherol (2.1 μg/g) > δ-tocopherol (1.8 μg/g) (Fig. 3D), while UR seeds displayed δ-tocopherol (16.0 μg/g) > γ-tocopherol (10.4 μg/g) with undetectable α-tocopherol (Fig. 3B). These stage-specific tocopherol distributions serve as descriptive indicators of differential regulation of γ-tocopherol methyltransferase activity, which controls the conversion of γ-tocopherol to α-tocopherol, thereby explaining the strong γ-tocopherol predominance observed at the MR stage (Deng, Zhao, Hu, et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2018; Ponciano et al., 2024). This regulatory shift suggests that metabolic flux is preferentially maintained toward γ-tocopherol accumulation during mid-seed maturation. The partial decline from MR to FR (317.0 → 274.0 μg/g) reflects a transition from active biosynthesis to oxidative metabolism, with increased tocopherol degradation through side-chain oxidation pathways during late maturation and seed desiccation (Kumar et al., 2021; Ponciano et al., 2024). Compared with γ-tocopherol, both δ- and α-tocopherol remained at relatively low levels throughout maturation. While higher tocopherol levels suggest potential functional benefits, form-specific safety assessment is advisable within a future validated scope because flavonoids and tocopherols can interact with drug transporters (Deng, Zhao, Cao, et al., 2025). Taken together, tocopherol contents did not increase consistently with extended growth but followed the order MR > FR > UR, showing a pattern similar to that of phenolic accumulation. Overall, seeds harvested during late Sep may represent a favorable source for preliminary nutraceutical and functional food developments. As far as we know, this study provides an exploratory investigation of tocopherol fluctuations in perilla seeds. The results suggest that while different forms of tocopherols provide antioxidant benefits, their physiological relevance requires further validation through independent biological replication and safety evaluation.

Fluctuations of phenolic metabolites in green perilla seeds at maturation times

To clarify the phenolic metabolite dynamics in green perilla seeds using an exploratory profiling approach, we examined the phenolic profiles from 50% methanol extracts in three maturation periods. HPLC chromatograms (Fig. 3E–3G) detected six major peaks within 15 min at 330 nm, identified by retention time comparison with standards (Lee et al., 2013; Lee & Cho, 2021): luteolin-7-O-glucoside (peak 4, tR = 6.3 min), rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (peak 6, tR = 6.7 min), rosmarinic acid (peak 8, tR = 8.6 min), luteolin (peak 10, tR = 12.8 min), apigenin (peak 11, tR = 13.9 min), and chryseriol (peak 12, tR = 14.1 min) (Fig. 3G and Table 2). Phenolic content showed substantial stage-dependent variations: MR (28,697.8 μg/g) > UR (16,132.6 μg/g) > FR (7820.0 μg/g). At UR, two major and three minor phenolics were detected (Fig. 3E): luteolin-7-O-glucoside dominated at 13,161.9 μg/g (81.6%), followed by rosmarinic acid (2430.4 μg/g; 15.1%), with minor compounds including apigenin (216.4 μg/g; 1.3%), luteolin (171.0 μg/g; 1.1%), and chryseriol (152.9 μg/g; 0.95%). Luteolin-7-O-glucoside dominance at UR reflects early-stage biosynthetic prioritization, where moderately active phenolic biosynthetic enzymes generate products rapidly glycosylated by UDP-glucose: flavonoid glucosyltransferase into soluble storage metabolites serving as early oxidative stress buffers and developmental signaling molecules. (Cheng et al., 2022; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). From UR to MR, total phenolics increased nearly two-fold (16,132.6 → 28,697.8 μg/g) (Table 1). This change was largely attributed to marked differences in luteolin-7-O-glucoside (13,161.9 → 28,192.6 μg/g) and rosmarinic acid (2430.4 → 391.8 μg/g), indicating divergent trends in phenolic subclasses (Table 1; Fig. 3E and F). During this period, luteolin remained detectable at 113.4 μg/g, whereas rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside, apigenin, and chryseriol were not detected. These findings suggest rapid metabolic rearrangements occurring during the 10 day interval from UR to MR. This dramatic surge represents peak secondary metabolite biosynthesis when transcriptional activation of phenolic biosynthetic genes reaches maximum levels in response to developmental signals and oxidative stress from accelerating lipid biosynthesis (Ali & McNear Jr., 2014). These observations represent peak secondary metabolite biosynthesis within the observed samples. While the absence of analytical replicates from independent biological sources limits statistical power, the surge suggests peak phenylpropanoid pathway activity (Ali & McNear Jr., 2014; Deng, Zhao, He, et al., 2025). Mechanistically, phenolic/flavonoid interactions with xenobiotic transporters and inflammatory pathways, as reported in recent animal studies, support inclusion of gut-liver axis considerations in interpreting these exploratory bioactivity data (Deng, Zhao, Cao, et al., 2025). In particular, the preferential luteolin-7-O-glucoside accumulation indicates metabolic flux redirection through the phenylpropanoid pathway toward the flavonoid biosynthetic branch, reducing flux toward phenolic acid synthesis (Ali & McNear Jr., 2014). Such coordinated transcriptional regulation and enzyme activation may explain the elevated accumulation of phenolic compounds observed at the MR stage. This substrate channeling directly coordinates with peak lipid biosynthesis, ensuring synchronous accumulation of lipophilic and hydrophilic antioxidants to protect newly synthesized polyunsaturated fatty acids from peroxidative damage during the energy intensive lipogenesis phase (Cheng et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2021; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). The 73% reduction in rosmarinic acid during this transition reflects decreased activity of rosmarinic acid synthase and increased competitive substrate utilization by downstream flavonoid biosynthetic enzymes, consistent with known phenylpropanoid pathway regulation in plant tissues. By FR, phenolic composition underwent dramatic reorganization (Fig. 3G). Rosmarinic acid exhibited the highest level (4625.6 μg/g), followed by rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (1430.3 μg/g), showing a marked shift compared to the earlier harvests. Other phenolics were present at the following levels: luteolin (944.0 μg/g) > apigenin (563.6 μg/g) > luteolin-7-O-glucoside (152.9 μg/g) > chryseriol (103.6 μg/g) (Table 1). All six metabolites became detectable only at FR, indicating late-stage enzymatic remodeling. Total phenolics declined 73% (28,697.8 → 7820.0 μg/g). This compositional shift serves as a preliminary indicator of physiological transition from biosynthetic accumulation to metabolic degradation and oxidative modification during seed desiccation and dormancy preparation. This transition appears to be associated with the downregulation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes as developmental signals redirecting metabolic resources from growth to dormancy, along with increased activity of β-glucosidase and other hydrolytic enzymes that remove glucose moieties from flavonoid glycosides to produce free luteolin (Ali & McNear Jr., 2014). The released luteolin is then further oxidized through peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase activities, contributing to the formation of rosmarinic acid derivatives via O-methyltransferase related reactions, while additional non-enzymatic oxidation may occur under moisture changes during late seed maturation (Abdelghany et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2022; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Ponciano et al., 2024). The exclusive FR appearance of rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (ND → ND → 1430.3 μg/g) suggests that its accumulation is strongly dependent on specific maturation related environmental conditions. Extending the maturation period from MR to FR resulted in a substantial decrease in total phenolics (28,697.8 → 7820.0 μg/g), indicating that phenolic accumulation peaks around late Sep and declines thereafter. After presenting these results, we compared them with previous findings. Earlier studies have documented a wide range of phenolic classes such as phenolic acids, anthocyanins, flavonoids, stilbenes, and tannins in perilla leaves and seeds (Assefa et al., 2018; Guan et al., 2014; Saini et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023). However, the present results did not reveal certain metabolite types reported previously, consistent with differences arising from plant tissue type and environmental influences. The observed exploratory fluctuations correspond with established concepts that phenolic biosynthesis is shaped by environmental parameters, including temperature, light exposure, soil properties, and growth status, as reported in various crops (Bhaswant et al., 2024; Kaur et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Serrano-García et al., 2024; Tsao et al., 2006). Likewise, the divergent behavior of individual phenolics is consistent with known transformations, degradation processes, and dynamic biosynthetic pathways documented in natural plants (Abdelghany et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2021; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Ponciano et al., 2024). In summary, the descriptive data indicates that phenolic metabolite accumulation does not increase linearly but instead peaks at the MR stage. This non-linear pattern reflects the fundamental physiological transition from biosynthetic to catabolic seed metabolism, where enzymatic activity shifts from the activation of biosynthetic pathway toward the upregulation of degradative enzymes. The increased phenolic level at the MR stage may therefore be associated with a transient upregulation of biosynthetic gene expression and enhanced catalytic activity of key enzymes prior to the downregulation that typically accompanies later maturation (Ali & McNear Jr., 2014; Cheng et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2021; Tsao et al., 2006). Therefore, seeds harvested in late Sep may represent the optimal period for potential phenolic yields. This study provides a preliminary comparative evaluation of phenolic fluctuations. Pearson correlation analysis supported the functional contribution of phenolic accumulation, although these findings must be interpreted with caution due to the pooled sample design. This variability may be attributed to minor instrumental fluctuations during HPLC analysis, differences in sample preparation, and natural biological variation in individual metabolite accumulation during seed maturation (Assefa et al., 2018; Serrano-García et al., 2024; Tsao et al., 2006). The results suggest that while phenolic accumulation supports functional potential, the exploratory nature of this study necessitates further validated follow-up research to fully characterize maturation-dependent mechanisms and safety profiles.

Correlation analysis of metabolite constituents in green perilla seeds at maturation times

To examine how metabolite constituents (protein, oil, tocopherols, and phenolic compounds) varied over maturation times, PCA was performed using the data obtained from green perilla seeds. The PCA score plots (Fig. 3H) revealed distinct groupings of the three maturation stages along the PC1 axis. Notably, PC1 explained 99.92% of the total variance, whereas PC2 accounted for only 0.05% (Fig. 3H). Given that the contribution of PC2 is negligible, the interpretation of the PCA was focused exclusively on the PC1-driven variation. The overwhelming dominance of PC1 demonstrates that seed maturation is the primary and singular factor influencing the overall metabolic trajectory. While the lack of independent biological replicates limits the assessment of within-stage variability, the unidimensional structure of the PCA indicates that metabolite composition follows a highly coordinated developmental program rather than independent or multidimensional metabolic fluctuations (Cheng et al., 2022; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024). A heatmap using z-score normalization was further constructed to visualize the quantitative differences in metabolite levels and their hierarchical clustering patterns (Fig. 3I) (Chong et al., 2019; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024). This heatmap provides the necessary resolution to understand the variables driving the separation observed in the PCA PC1 score space, effectively compensating for the absence of loading plots. The resulting clustering grouped metabolites largely according to maturation stage, forming two major metabolic clusters corresponding to anabolic (UR and MR) and catabolic (FR) phases. Within the heatmap, metabolites displayed strong positive (red) or negative (blue) correlations that were highly consistent with the PCA results, particularly along the PC1 axis. The transition along PC1 from UR to FR is primarily driven by the progressive accumulation of storage reserves (oil and protein) and the maturation-dependent shift in phenolic profiles, as visualized by these colour-coded correlation patterns. Specifically, protein and oil contents varied markedly depending on collection time, increasing progressively over the maturation period (UR → FR). This tendency is consistent with earlier reports indicating that protein and oil accumulation generally rises during seed development (Saini et al., 2020). The strong positive correlations (red) observed between these components along the PC1 axis indicate synchronized biosynthetic activation of protein and lipid storage pathways, reflecting a coordinated allocation of carbon resources and metabolic precursors during reserve accumulation. Fatty acid composition also shifted significantly: saturated fatty acids (C16:0 and C18:0) decreased notably from UR to FR, whereas unsaturated fatty acids such as C18:1 and C18:3 increased. In contrast, C18:2 exhibited a gradual decline relative to other unsaturated fatty acids. These inverse relationships observed in the heatmap suggest maturation-dependent activation of fatty acid desaturases (delta 12 and delta 15), which introduce double bonds into saturated precursors, with environmental conditions during seed maturation likely further modulating these patterns (Abdelghany et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2013). Phenolic metabolites showed clear stage dependent fluctuations during seed maturation. Luteolin-7-O-glucoside (4) increased sharply from UR to MR, reaching its maximum level at MR, and then declined markedly at FR. In contrast, other phenolics, including rosmarinic acid (8), luteolin (10), apigenin (11), and chrysoeriol (12), decreased from UR to MR but increased again from MR to FR. These inverse patterns observed in the heatmap reflect stage-specific enzymatic regulation, where enhanced glucosyltransferase activity during the UR → MR transition promotes flavonoid glycoside accumulation, while increased glycosidase and oxidative enzyme activities during MR → FR generate and subsequently oxidize free aglycones. Such maturation dependent phenolic transitions are consistent with previous reports indicating that phenolic metabolism is strongly influenced by developmental timing and environmental conditions (Cheng et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2021; Lee, Cho, et al., 2024; Lee, Kwak, et al., 2024; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Tsao et al., 2006). Consistent with the phenolic variation patterns and multivariate analysis results, tocopherol composition also exhibited clear maturation stage dependent changes, with γ-tocopherol remaining the predominant isoform throughout seed development. Although δ-, γ-, and α-tocopherol were detected at all sampling times, their peak levels occurred at different stages, indicating dynamic regulation of tocopherol biosynthesis during maturation (Fig. 3I). Notably, the strong positive correlation between γ-tocopherol and peak phenolic accumulation at the MR stage suggests coordinated activation of antioxidant defense systems during mid-maturation. This relationship implies that maturation associated oxidative stress signals may simultaneously stimulate phenolic and tocopherol biosynthetic pathways through shared ROS-responsive regulatory mechanisms, thereby contributing to the stabilization of polyunsaturated lipids and the protection of developing seed tissues (Kumar et al., 2021; Ponciano et al., 2024). The exploratory correlation patterns in the heatmap further provided a biological foundation for the unidimensional separation observed in the PCA score space. At UR, strong positive correlations among chrysoeriol, δ-tocopherol, and saturated fatty acids (C16:0, C18:0, and C18:2) indicate early-stage metabolic prioritization: δ-tocopherol preferentially accumulates due to low γ-tocopherol methyltransferase expression, while saturated fatty acids predominate because desaturase enzyme expression is minimal. At the MR stage, luteolin-7-O-glucoside (4) emerged as the central metabolic hub, showing strong correlations (red) with protein, oil, γ-tocopherol, and polyunsaturated fatty acids (C18:1 and C18:3), indicating a metabolic shift toward coordinated reserve accumulation and antioxidant biosynthesis. This pattern reflects the peak biosynthetic phase in which flavonoid antioxidants and lipid-associated metabolites are synchronized to protect newly synthesized polyunsaturated fatty acids. These associations persisted from MR to FR, suggesting continued interaction among major storage and antioxidant components during late maturation. At the FR stage, strong correlations among rosmarinic acid (8), apigenin (11), α-tocopherol, rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (6), and luteolin (10) indicate late-maturation metabolic reorganization driven by catabolic transformation and oxidative adjustment. The selective association of α-tocopherol at this stage suggests its increasing role in protecting seed longevity and viability during desiccation (Fig. 3I) (Ponciano et al., 2024). While these metabolic shifts provide a descriptive baseline for perilla seed development, the exploratory nature of this research, stemming from the pooled analytical design, necessitates further follow-up research with independent biological replicates to fully characterize these maturation-dependent mechanisms and their functional significance.

Comparisons of metabolite profiles using UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS analysis in green perilla seeds at maturation times

Analyses performed in negative ion mode with UV detection at 330 nm allowed the identification of twelve metabolites based on retention time, accurate mass, and MS/MS fragmentation, with their structural characteristics confirmed through full scan spectra. Although previous studies using UPLC–Orbitrap analysis have reported a larger number of metabolites (Chen et al., 2025), the present exploratory study focused on twelve high confidence compounds to provide a clear descriptive baseline. As illustrated in Fig. 4, both individual and total metabolite profiles differed markedly in the three maturation times. In the seeds collected at the UR stage (Fig. 4A), a wide array of metabolites, including both major and minor peaks, appeared within 35 min, from which ten compounds were tentatively identified. The product ion mass spectrum of peak 1 (tR = 6.8 min, mass error − 0.56 ppm) revealed a deprotonated ion [M – H]− at m/z 353.0879 and a characteristic fragment at m/z 191.0552 ([M – H]− – 162 amu), consistent with the loss of a caffeonyl moiety (Fig. 4D). Therefore, it was assigned as chlorogenic acid (1) (Assefa et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2024). Peak 2 (tR = 7.3 min, mass error − 0.77 ppm) displayed a [M – H]− ion at m/z 179.0349 and a fragment ion at m/z 135.0431 (Fig. 4E). The fragmentation at m/z 135.0431 ([M – H]− – 44 amu) indicated the loss of a carboxylic acid (–COOH) group, corresponding to caffeic acid (m/z 179) (Lee & Cho, 2021; Lee et al., 2013; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). Peak 3 (tR = 8.1 min, mass error − 0.65 ppm) possessed an identical molecular ion [M – H]− at m/z 163.0395 with the same fragmentation pattern as peak 2 (m/z 163. 0395 → m/z 119.0480; [M – H]− – 44 amu), corresponding to the loss of a carboxylic acid molecule (Fig. 4F). Thus, peak 3 was confirmed as p-coumaric acid, consistent with previously reported data (Assefa et al., 2018; Massarioli et al., 2023). The MS spectrum of peak 4 (tR = 15.7 min, mass error 1.11 ppm) presented [M – H]− at m/z 447.0921 with a fragment at 285.0402, corresponding to the loss of a glucose unit (162 amu) (Fig. 4G) (Lee et al., 2013). The fragment ion matched the characteristic luteolin aglycone, and based on previously reported perilla data, peak 4 was tentatively identified as luteolin-7-O-glucoside (Chen et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2013; Lee & Cho, 2021; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). The product ion mass spectrum of peak 8 (tR = 22.8 min, mass error 0.27 ppm) showed [M – H]− at m/z 359.0784 with fragment ions at m/z 197.0435, m/z 179.0349, and m/z 161.0218 (Fig. 4K). The ion at m/z 197.0435 resulted from the loss of a 162 amu (m/z 359 → m/z 197; caffeoyl moiety), while the ions at m/z 161.0218 and 179.0349 correspond to deprotonated caffeoyl and caffeic acid fragments, respectively. In particular, the fragmentation observed at m/z 179.0349 corresponds to the pattern of peak 2, identified as caffeic acid. Based on these diagnostic ions, peak 8 was identified as rosmarinic acid (Gonçalves et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2017). The UPLC-Orbitrap-MS profile of peak 9 (tR = 27.1 min, mass error 0.00 ppm) revealed a deprotonated ion [M – H]− at m/z 467.2111 with two fragments at m/z 289.1564 and m/z 161.0410 (Fig. 4L). The ion at m/z 289.1564 resulted from the loss of a methyl and glucose unit (178 amu; [M – H]− – (methyl + glucose)), while m/z 161.0410 corresponded to the deprotonated glucose residue (Ha et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2013). Notably, the fragment ion at m/z 289.1564 is likely associated with the deprotonated fructose lipid moiety (Lee et al., 2017). Based on these fragmentation patterns and previous perilla studies, peak 9 was tentatively identified as N-octanoyl sucrose (Lee et al., 2017; Ha et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2013). Peak 10 (tR = 27.4 min, mass error − 0.69 ppm) exhibited a deprotonated ion [M – H]− at m/z 285.0402 (Fig. 4M), consistent with previous literature reports (Lee & Cho, 2021; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). Thus, this peak was assumed to luteolin (10) (Chen et al., 2025; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). Subsequently, the perilla seeds collected at the MR stage exhibited notable changes in metabolite distributions compared to the UR sample (Fig. 4B). While the pooled design limits our ability to statistically investigate maturation effects, the descriptive results showed that both individual and total metabolite profiles differed markedly after 10 days of maturation (19 Sep → 29 Sep). Eight components were identified in the MR sample, five of which (peaks 3, 4, 8, 9, and 10) overlapped with the UR profile. The Orbitrap-MS data of peak 5 (tR = 18.6 min, mass error − 0.23 ppm) displayed a molecular ion [M – H]− at m/z 431.0965 and a major fragment at m/z 269.0456 (Fig. 4H), corresponding to the loss of a glucose unit (162 amu, m/z 431 → m/z 269) and characteristic of apigenin (Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). Based on these observations, peak 5 was assigned as apigenin-7-O-glucoside (5), in agreement with previously reported data (Chen et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2024). Peak 6 (tR = 19.8 min, mass error − 0.57 ppm) displayed a molecular ion [M – H]− at m/z 521.1302, with fragments at m/z 323.0841, m/z 197.0450, and m/z 161.0217 (Fig. 4I). The major fragment at m/z 161.0217 (−360 amu) corresponded to the loss of a glucose residue, while the minor ion at m/z 197.0450 indicated the loss of deprotonated caffeoyl and glucose moieties (Lee et al., 2013). Moreover, the ion at m/z323.0841 was identified as the deprotonated caffeoyl (m/z 162) and glucose (m/z 162) residues (Guan et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2013). These patterns resembled those of peak 8 and matched earlier reports, supporting the identification of peak 6 as rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (Guan et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2013; Liu, Jin, et al., 2024; Liu, Liang, et al., 2024). The UPLC-Orbitrap-MS analysis of peak 7 (tR = 20.1 min, mass error − 0.43 ppm) revealed the molecular ion [M – H]− at m/z 461.1123 with two fragments at m/z 299.0601 and m/z 284.0348 (Fig. 4J). The major fragment ion at m/z 299.0601 (m/z 461–162) resulted from the loss of a glucose moiety (162 amu), and the remaining ion at m/z 284.0348 (m/z 461 – [162 + 15] amu) was generated by the losses of glucose (162 amu) and methyl ([M – H]− – 15 amu) moieties (Deng et al., 2024; Olennikov et al., 2018). The summarized fragmentation characteristics were consistent with those reported for chryseriol-7-O-glucoside (7). The perilla seeds harvested at the FR stage also showed significant differences compared with those collected on UR and MR (Fig. 4C), with five metabolites elucidated, including three peaks (6, 8, and 10) that were present in the earlier samples. Peaks 11 (tR = 31.9 min, mass error 0.70 ppm) and 12 (tR = 33.3 min, mass error 0.99 ppm) exhibited pseudomolecular ions at m/z 269.0432 ([M – H]−) (Fig. 2N) and m/z 299.0586 ([M – H]−) (Fig. 4O). A fragment ion for peak 12 was found at m/z 284.0347 (Fig. 4O), corresponding to a loss of 15 amu regarding methyl moiety ([M – H]− – 15 amu), consistent with previous reports (Lee et al., 2013). Based on the described ions and published perilla data, peaks 11 and 12 were assigned as apigenin (11) and chryseriol (12) (Guan et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2013; Olennikov et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2024). Table 2 summarizes the retention times, UV maxima, elemental compositions, and mass spectral data used for metabolite identification by comparison with the relevant research references. In accordance with the exploratory nature of this research, we focused on analytical reliability over comprehensive untargeted metabolite profiling, thereby limiting our analysis to metabolites that could be confirmed with clear and conclusive MS/MS evidence. While the absence of independent biological replicates necessitates caution in generalizing these maturation-dependent changes, the observed metabolite distributions changed markedly over the maturation period. These findings provide preliminary insights into the time-dependent fluctuations of secondary metabolites and offer a valuable descriptive foundation for developing functional foods derived from green perilla. Future follow-up research with independent replicates is required to further validate these maturation-dependent metabolic shifts.

Limitations

Our work has certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, samples collected within each maturation stage were pooled prior to compositional analysis, resulting in a single composite sample per stage. While this approach provides a stabilized overall representation of metabolite composition at each stage, it cannot capture the natural biological variation that occurs within maturation stages. This significantly limits the statistical power of our findings and prevents the assessment of individual-level biological variability. Second, the absence of independent biological replicates restricts the strength of conclusions regarding maturation-dependent changes. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory rather than definitive. Third, since sampling was conducted within a single experimental field, the potential influence of environmental and soil-related factors cannot be entirely excluded. Although we maintained consistent agronomic conditions through standardized management, the absence of high-resolution environmental data (such as daily temperature fluctuations or detailed soil nutrient analysis) means that site-specific influences on the metabolite profiles cannot be fully quantified. Finally, the limited number of maturation stages and sampling time points may not fully reflect the continuous nature of the maturation process. Future studies should include independent replicates, sampling from different environments, and more frequent sampling to better validate these findings.

Conclusion

This research provides an exploratory investigation of metabolite fluctuations and biofunctional properties in green perilla seeds across three maturation stages during the peak harvest period. Antioxidant activities, including DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP, as well as DNA protection ratios, varied markedly with maturation, with the highest values observed in seeds harvested on 29 Sep, emphasizing the crucial role of phenolic metabolites. Protein and oil contents increased progressively during maturation, ranging from 18.7 to 22.8% and 13.5 to 46.1%, respectively, while fatty acid profiling revealed C18:3 as the predominant component (31.0 → 60.8%), accompanied by changes in saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids. Tocopherols also showed significant variations throughout maturation stages (26.4 → 317.0 μg/g), with γ-tocopherol reaching the highest concentration (311.3 μg/g) in the MR sample. Through UPLC-Orbitrap-MS analysis of 50% methanol extracts, twelve phenolic metabolites were identified, with significant accumulations varying as follows: MR (28,697.8 μg/g) > UR (16,132.6 μg/g) > FR (7820.0 μg/g). Luteolin-7-O-glucoside (152.9–28,192.6 μg/g) showed the most pronounced accumulation, followed by rosmarinic acid (391.8–4625.6 μg/g), rosmarinic acid-3-O-glucoside (nd–1430.3 μg/g), luteolin (113.4–944.0 μg/g), apigenin (nd–563.6 μg/g), and chrysoeriol (nd–152.9 μg/g). Overall, seeds harvested at MR consistently showed the highest antioxidant activities, DNA protection, and phenolic concentrations, suggesting that late Sep may represents the optimal harvest period for maximizing the quality and biofunctional potential of green perilla seeds under these experimental conditions. While these findings serve as a descriptive framework due to the exploratory nature of the study, they offer valuable baseline insights for the development of perilla seeds as functional food ingredients and nutraceutical resources. Future studies should build upon these preliminary data to further examine the validated health-promoting effects and potential pharmaceutical applications of these bioactive compounds.
