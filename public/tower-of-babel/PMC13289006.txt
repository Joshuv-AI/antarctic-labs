Association of 24-hour movement guideline adherence with mental health symptoms among Colombian university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gonzalo Romero-Martínez, Juan Carlos Urriago-Fontal, Héctor Mario Quirama-García, Julián David Ortiz-López, Carolina Arias-Álzate, Gustavo Adolfo Gutierrez-Quintero, Luis Hebert Palma Pulido, Juan Carlos Calderón-González, Robinson Ramírez-Vélez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the associations between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and symptoms of anxiety and depression among Colombian university students.Study designCross-sectional study.Participants1125 individuals (mean age 20.2±2.5 years; 56.7% female).SettingStudents sampled from a single public university.Primary and secondary outcome measuresParticipants completed validated self-report instruments: the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form to assess physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviour (SB) and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index to assess sleep duration. Symptoms of depression and anxiety were measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, with a score of ≥11 used to classify elevated symptoms. Binary logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines (meeting all three, two, one or none) and mental health outcomes, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsOnly 15.5% of students met all three components of the 24-hour movement guidelines. Meeting a greater number of components was significantly associated with lower odds of depressive and anxiety symptoms. In fully adjusted analyses, students who met all three guidelines were less likely to report anxiety symptoms (OR=0.26; 95%CI 0.13 to 0.54) and depressive symptoms (OR=0.42; 95%CI 0.22 to 0.79) compared with those who met none. Among individual behaviours, sufficient PA and adequate sleep were independently associated with lower odds of both outcomes, whereas high SB was associated with higher odds of elevated symptoms.ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional study, adherence to a greater number of 24-hour movement guideline components was associated with lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms in a graded manner. However, the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding directionality or causality, and bidirectional associations or residual confounding remain possible. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine whether integrated daily movement behaviours influence mental health outcomes in young adults, particularly in Latin American populations.Keywords: Exercise, Anxiety disorders, Depression & mood disorders, Young Adult, SLEEP MEDICINE, Health

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are among the most common and debilitating mental health symptoms affecting university populations globally.1 College students encounter numerous stressors, including academic pressure, life transitions and social changes, which increase their susceptibility to psychological distress.2 High levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms among students are linked to impaired academic performance, diminished quality of life and long-term health risks, making them a critical public health concern.3 A recent meta-analysis including 64 studies and more than 100 000 participants estimated pooled prevalence rates of 33.6% for depressive symptoms and 39.0% for anxiety symptoms in college populations.4 Notably, depression levels reached up to 42.5% in economically disadvantaged regions, and anxiety prevalence exceeded 48% in North American cohorts.3 In a large observational study, university students from 26 low-, middle- and high-income countries reported prevalence rates of 24.0% for moderate depression and 12.8% for severe depression.5 For instance, Ahmed et al6 (World Mental Health International College Student Survey) and others7 8 have shown that global anxiety symptoms affect roughly 39%–40% of college students, with depression prevalence similarly elevated, with pooled estimates ranging from 21% to 31% in previous studies.8

In October 2020, Canada introduced the first-ever 24-hour movement guidelines for adults, establishing a comprehensive framework for daily movement behaviours.9 These guidelines provide specific recommendations on physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviour (SB) and sleep duration, representing a novel approach to examining the integrated impact of these behaviours on adult health. The recommendations advise adults to engage in at least 150 min of moderate- to vigorous-intensity PA per week, limit sedentary time to no more than 8 hours per day, and obtain 7–9 hours of high-quality sleep each night.9 However, given the recent introduction of these guidelines, further research is warranted to examine their associations with key health outcomes, such as depression, and to inform future refinements.10

For example, adherence rates of 27.8% and 31.7% have been reported in separate studies conducted in China,10 11 while only 26.9% of students in Visegrad Group countries (Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland and Slovakia) met all components of the 24-hour movement guidelines.12 Previous studies among university students have consistently reported low adherence to all three components, underscoring the need for targeted investigations in this population to better understand behavioural patterns and their mental health implications.13 In addition, most research remains cross-sectional and focused on individual behaviours in high-income countries, with limited attention to the full 24-hour composition. In this context, a recent cross-sectional study highlighted the lack of evidence regarding the relationship between the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health indicators among college students.10 Other studies have indicated that movement behaviours—including PA, SB and sleep—are independent correlates of mental health symptoms in university students. Xu et al14 found that Chinese undergraduates with higher self-reported PA had significantly lower depression and anxiety scores. A recent meta-analysis likewise confirmed a modest but significant negative association between college students’ PA and depressive symptoms (pooled r≈−0.238, p<0.001).15 Conversely, prolonged sedentary behaviour—including screen time—predicts poorer mental health outcomes. Lee and Kim16 reported that each additional hour of daily sitting was associated with significantly higher stress, anxiety and depression among Korean university students, even after adjusting for demographics. Sleep also plays a key role: inadequate or poor-quality sleep has been consistently associated with higher anxiety and depressive symptoms in students.8 17 However, the combined effects of sufficient PA, limited ST/SB and appropriate sleep duration on mental health outcomes remain unclear. Investigating the associations between the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health in young adults can support the development and broader adoption of these guidelines across diverse populations.16

Recognising that these behaviours occur within a finite 24-hour period and compete for limited time, current guidelines adopt a comprehensive ‘24-hour movement’ approach. To our knowledge, no prior study conducted in Latin America has directly examined whether meeting the combined adult movement guidelines is associated with lower anxiety or depression among university students. This gap is particularly evident in Latin America. Existing Colombian studies have reported mental health prevalence or examined individual lifestyle factors (eg, social capital or isolated sleep measures), but none have assessed adherence to the 24-hour movement recommendations.18 Addressing this gap is important given the high burden of mental health symptoms among students and the potential value of lifestyle-based interventions. The present study therefore examined the association between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health symptoms in a sample of university students, with particular attention to this understudied Latin American context.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study, part of the EpiHealth-UCEVA project, examined the relationships among lifestyle habits, physical fitness and mental health outcomes in university students using a cross-sectional design. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.19 Participants were recruited from a single public university using convenience sampling through institutional email distribution lists, the university website, social media announcements and on-campus posters and flyers. Eligible participants were undergraduate students aged 16–35 years enrolled in any academic programme during the 2024–2025 academic period; students from all semesters and disciplines were eligible. A total source population of 5113 enrolled undergraduate students across 12 academic programmes was considered. The EpiHealth-UCEVA project was conceived as a population-based surveillance initiative aiming to recruit the largest feasible number of eligible students rather than to meet a prespecified sample size target; therefore, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Under conventional assumptions for finite populations (two-sided α=0.05, 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error and maximum variance (p=0.50)), the minimum required sample would have been 358 participants. The final analytical sample included 1125 students, representing 22.0% of the target undergraduate population and exceeding the minimum sample size estimated for descriptive precision under these assumptions.

Anthropometric and body composition measures

Standing height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a calibrated stadiometer (Seca, Chino, CA, USA). Body weight was measured using a multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis system (InBody 770; InBody Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea). Body mass index was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Body fat percentage (%) was assessed using a validated multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis method20 with a segmental tetrapolar analyser (InBody 770; InBody Co. Ltd). Measurements were obtained under standardised conditions, including a 2- to 3-hour fast,21 bladder voiding before assessment, avoidance of vigorous PA for at least 12 hours and controlled ambient temperature, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Mental health measures

This study used an online survey format. Before the study began, all research team members received standardised training. Students were also instructed on how to complete the survey prior to participation. To ensure individual responses, each participant was assigned a unique survey code. The survey could only be submitted once all items were completed. In addition, data were monitored in real time to ensure accuracy and reliability. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), a widely used instrument for detecting these symptoms in both medical and general populations.22 The HADS consists of two subscales, each with seven items: one for anxiety (HADS-A) and one for depression (HADS-D). Each item is rated on a scale from 0 to 3, yielding subscale scores ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Scores of 8–10 on the HADS-A or HADS-D indicate subclinical anxiety or depressive symptoms, whereas scores of ≥11 suggest moderate to severe symptoms. In this study, a threshold of ≥11 was used to identify probable or definite cases for population categorisation following the initial descriptive analyses, consistent with prior research.23 24 Both continuous and dichotomised HADS variables were used in the statistical analyses.

Movement behaviours

Movement behaviours, including PA, SB and sleep, were assessed using two self-report questionnaires: the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF) and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The reliability and validity of the IPAQ-SF have been established across various populations, with acceptable performance for assessing PA and SB in Latin American contexts, including Colombia.25 Briefly, participants were asked to recall their PA (vigorous, moderate and walking) and SB (sitting during leisure and transportation) over the previous 7 days. Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was calculated following the standard IPAQ scoring protocol. Total weekly minutes were calculated for each PA domain. SB was assessed using IPAQ-SF items on average weekday and weekend sitting time. A weighted mean (five weekdays and two weekend days) was used to estimate overall daily sedentary time. Data processing for PA and SB variables followed the published guidelines of the IPAQ expert group.26 Sleep duration (in hours) was assessed using the PSQI, which has been applied in Spanish-speaking populations.27 In accordance with the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for Adults,9 participants were classified as meeting the guidelines if they reported ≥150 min per week of MVPA, ≤8 hours per day of SB and 7–9 hours of sleep per day.

Measures of lifestyle behaviours, demographics and covariables

Diet quality was assessed using the validated 14-item Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener, developed for the PREDIMED study.28 The questionnaire evaluates adherence to key dietary components and habits using binary (yes/no) responses, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating greater adherence. Consistent with prior literature, a score of ≥8 was used to define adequate adherence to the Mediterranean diet.29 Sociodemographic characteristics were collected via self-report using a standardised questionnaire. Variables included marital status (single or common-law union, married and separated), socioeconomic status classified according to national criteria (low (levels I–II), middle/high (levels III–VI)), residence (urban or rural), race/ethnicity (Mestizo, Afro-descendant or Indigenous/other), smoking status, and alcohol consumption, recorded as binary responses (yes/no). Self-reported disease status was assessed using a standardised questionnaire and classified dichotomously as any physician-diagnosed condition (respiratory, cardiovascular, metabolic, mental health or other) versus none. Participants could report more than one condition.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated to report frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables and means (SD) for continuous variables. For continuous variables, sex differences were assessed using independent t-tests. For categorical variables, comparisons were conducted using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. No imputation of missing data was performed; analyses were based on available cases. Associations between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health outcomes were examined using multivariable regression models. Guideline adherence was operationalised as a categorical variable representing meeting 0 (reference), 1, 2 or 3 recommendations, in accordance with the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for Adults.9 For binary outcomes (clinically significant anxiety (HADS-A≥11) and depressive symptoms (HADS-D≥11)), logistic regression models were constructed hierarchically. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was fully adjusted and additionally included diseases, body fat percentage, alcohol intake, residence (urban/rural) and race/ethnicity. Model fit was evaluated using deviance, Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion and pseudo-R² indices (McFadden, Nagelkerke, Tjur and Cox & Snell). Incremental model improvement was assessed using likelihood ratio χ² tests. ORs and 95% CIs were derived from log-odds coefficients, and Wald statistics were used to assess statistical significance.

For continuous mental health symptom severity (HADS total score), hierarchical linear regression models were performed using the same block structure. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, and model 2 was additionally adjusted for alcohol intake, body fat percentage, residence, race/ethnicity and self-reported disease status. Statistical significance of model improvement was tested using F tests.30 Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor statistics. In fully adjusted models, variance inflation factor values were below 2.0, indicating no evidence of problematic collinearity. For linear regression, residual and collinearity diagnostics (condition index and variance proportions) were examined to verify model assumptions. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. Analyses were conducted using standard regression procedures.

Results

Study population characteristics

Among the 1125 participants (638 women and 487 men), most sociodemographic characteristics were similar between the sexes (table 1). Women were slightly younger than men and had lower body weight, height, but a higher body fat percentage (all p<0.001). No significant difference was observed in body mass index. The prevalence of self-reported diseases differed by sex (p=0.003), with women reporting more metabolic and mental health conditions, whereas a greater proportion of men reported no diseases. Women engaged in less MVPA than men (p<0.001), whereas sedentary time and sleep duration were similar between sexes. Women also had higher HADS scores and a greater prevalence of anxiety symptoms (both p<0.001).

Adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines by sex

Sex differences were observed in adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines. Men were more likely than women to meet all three recommendations (23.3% vs 11.8%; p<0.001). By contrast, women were more likely to meet only one guideline (37.1% vs 27.5%; p=0.001), to meet the SB recommendation only (25.1% vs 14.3%; p<0.001) and to meet none of the recommendations (9.7% vs 5.3%; p=0.007). No significant sex difference was found for meeting exactly two guidelines overall (41.1% vs 46.0%; p=0.11), although the specific combinations differed (table 2). These patterns are illustrated in figure 1. Among women, 11.3% met all three recommendations, and 9.7% met none. The highest prevalence of single-component adherence was observed for SB only (25.2%), followed by PA only (7.8%) and sleep duration only (4.3%). For combined adherence, 25.1% met both the PA and SB recommendations, 12.4% met the SB and sleep duration recommendations and 3.6% met the PA and sleep duration recommendations. Among men, 23.3% met all three recommendations, whereas 5.3% met none. Single-component adherence was 12.1% for PA only, 14.3% for SB only and 1.8% for sleep duration only. For combined adherence, 32.0% met both the PA and SB recommendations, 8.6% met PA and sleep duration recommendations and 5.3% met SB and sleep duration recommendations.

Associations between number of 24-hour movement guideline components met and mental health symptoms

In logistic regression analyses, adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines was associated with lower odds of clinically significant anxiety symptoms after adjustment for covariates. Meeting two (OR, 0.42; 95% CI 0.25 to 0.71; p=0.001) or all three guidelines (OR, 0.23; 95% CI 0.11 to 0.45; p<0.001) was associated with significantly lower odds of anxiety symptoms compared with meeting none (reference group). These associations remained statistically significant after adjustment for age and sex (model 1) and persisted in the fully adjusted model (model 2), which additionally included diseases, body fat percentage, alcohol intake, residence and race/ethnicity (two guidelines: OR, 0.46; 95% CI 0.27 to 0.79; p=0.005; three guidelines: OR, 0.26; 95% CI 0.13 to 0.54; p<0.001; Nagelkerke R²=0.040). For depressive symptoms (HADS-D≥11), adherence to two (OR, 0.56; 95% CI 0.33 to 0.95; p=0.031) or three guidelines (OR, 0.42; 95% CI 0.22 to 0.79; p=0.008) was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms in fully adjusted analyses. Although statistically significant, the proportion of variance explained was modest (Nagelkerke R²=0.010) (table 3).

Discussion

This study examined adherence to the Canadian 24-hour movement guidelines for adults and evaluated the associations between different combinations of movement behaviours and mental health symptoms, assessed using the HADS questionnaire, in a sample of Colombian university students. Overall, 15.5% of participants met all three guideline components, and adherence to a greater number of recommendations was associated with lower anxiety and depressive symptoms. It should be noted that the proportion of variance in mental health symptoms explained by guideline adherence was modest in logistic models, suggesting that movement behaviours represent one component within a broader multifactorial aetiology of anxiety and depression in university students.

In this sample of Latin American university students, adherence to the integrated 24-hour movement guidelines was low. Only 15.5% of participants met all three recommendations, with a higher prevalence among men (23.3%) than among women (11.8%). This proportion is substantially lower than that reported in Chinese (approximately 27.8%) and Central European (approximately 26.9%) university populations.10 12 The low prevalence observed in our study aligns with broader global trends, in which only 9.6% of youth meet all three guidelines.31

Differences in measurement tools and study contexts likely contribute to variability across studies. Our study relied on self-reported PA (IPAQ-SF) and sleep (PSQI), whereas prior research in children and adolescents has frequently used the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children survey or objective measures, such as accelerometers, to assess MVPA, SB and sleep.31 32 These methodological differences may have influenced prevalence estimates. In addition, data were collected during an active academic period, which may have involved increased academic demands, potentially contributing to lower PA levels, shorter sleep duration and greater sedentary time.

Consistent with Zhang et al10 and other studies,33 34 students who met a greater number of movement recommendations tended to report better mental health. In our study, individuals who met two or all three guidelines had significantly lower odds of anxiety and depressive symptoms than those who met none, with evidence of a graded association. Notably, the magnitude of the association appeared stronger for anxiety symptoms than for depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted models. These findings are in line with Zhang et al,10 who reported progressively higher odds of depressive symptoms among Chinese university students meeting fewer guidelines (ORs of 3.4 for meeting none vs all and 2.7 for one vs all). Similarly, Guimarães et al35 observed that Brazilian students with higher physical fitness—a proxy for an active lifestyle—had a substantially lower prevalence of depressive symptoms (prevalence ratio of approximately 0.53 comparing high vs low fitness). Although Guimarães et al35 did not find an independent association with anxiety, our cross-sectional results indicate that adherence to integrated movement behaviours is associated with lower odds of both anxiety and depressive symptoms, with a somewhat stronger association for anxiety in this sample. We caution, however, that the temporal direction of this relationship cannot be established with these data.

Our findings extend the existing international evidence to the Latin American context. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to examine adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines in relation to mental health among Latin American university students. The relatively low adherence observed (approximately 15%, compared with approximately 27% in some European and Asian samples) suggests that insufficient compliance with integrated movement recommendations may represent a broader cross-cultural challenge among young adults. Moreover, the observed graded association in which meeting two or more recommendations was associated with progressively lower mental health symptom burden—supports the hypothesis that integrated movement behaviours may confer additive benefits. For example, López-Gil et al36 reported that US adolescents who met all three 24-hour movement guidelines (PA, ST and sleep) had the lowest probability of depressive symptoms (21.3%, 95% CI 17.3% to 25.3%) compared with those who met none (37.6%, 95% CI 33.4% to 41.8%). Although our study focused on university students and used different instruments, the convergence of findings across populations is consistent with the notion that combined PA, reduced SB and adequate sleep may represent a relevant focus for mental health promotion, although causal mechanisms—and the possibility of reverse causation—remain to be tested in prospective studies. However, the biological and psychosocial mechanisms underlying these associations warrant further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The direction of the observed associations cannot be established: healthier movement behaviours may contribute to lower anxiety and depressive symptoms, better mental health may facilitate adherence to recommended behaviours (reverse causation) or unmeasured underlying factors—such as chronic physical health conditions or psychosocial adversity—may confound both exposure and outcome. Bidirectional relationships are biologically plausible and consistent with current evidence in young adults. Second, all movement behaviours and mental health outcomes were assessed using self-reported instruments, which may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. In addition, the HADS is a screening rather than a diagnostic instrument; therefore, our prevalence estimates reflect probable cases of anxiety and depressive symptoms identified by a validated screening cut-off (≥11), rather than clinical diagnoses confirmed by a structured psychiatric interview. Objective measures (eg, accelerometry or polysomnography) would improve precision in future research. Third, participants were recruited from a single public university, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other Latin American student populations. Fourth, differences in survey methods and contextual factors may complicate direct comparisons with other studies.10 12 Finally, participation represented approximately 22% of the eligible undergraduate population (1125 of 5113 enrolled students). Because recruitment relied on voluntary participation, selection bias cannot be excluded. Students who chose to participate may have differed systematically from non-participants with respect to health behaviours, academic engagement or interest in mental health, potentially affecting the observed prevalence of both mental health symptoms and adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines. Several measures were implemented to reduce the likelihood of selection bias. Recruitment was conducted across all faculties, academic programmes, and semesters throughout the 2024–2025 academic year using multiple dissemination channels, including institutional email lists, the university website, social media announcements and on-campus posters and flyers. Participation was free of charge, accessible online and available to all eligible students, thereby minimising potential logistical barriers to participation. In addition, questionnaire responses were anonymised prior to analysis, and participants were informed that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect their academic standing, which may have reduced reluctance to disclose sensitive information. Nevertheless, the possibility of residual selection and non-response bias remains, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Despite these limitations, our findings add to the growing body of literature linking 24-hour movement behaviours with mental health in young people. Converging evidence from Chinese university students, European youth, and now a Latin American sample suggests that promoting an integrated lifestyle characterised by adequate PA, limited sedentary time and sufficient sleep may represent a meaningful target for mental health promotion in higher education settings. Universities and public health programmes may benefit from adopting comprehensive movement-based strategies similar to those advocated in Canadian and WHO guidelines. Given the relative novelty of this line of research in Latin America, longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to confirm these associations and clarify causality. Nonetheless, the present findings reinforce the principle that daily movement patterns across the entire 24-hour cycle are relevant to student mental health.13

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides evidence from a Latin American university student population showing a statistically significant cross-sectional association between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and lower levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms. In fully adjusted models, the association appeared numerically stronger for anxiety symptoms, than for depressive symptoms among students meeting all three guideline components compared with those meeting none. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be made, and alternative explanations, including reverse causation (eg, better mental health facilitating healthier movement behaviours) and residual confounding (eg, underlying physical or psychosocial factors influencing both exposure and outcome), remain plausible. The convergence of associations across diverse international settings is consistent with the hypothesis that balanced daily behaviours—regular physical activity, limited sedentary time and adequate sleep—may be relevant to student mental well-being, although causal inference requires longitudinal and interventional confirmation. Future longitudinal and interventional research is warranted to validate these findings and to inform campus-based mental health strategies grounded in integrated movement behaviours.
