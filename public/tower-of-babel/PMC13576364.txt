Adaptive genomic evolution and WD40-regulated temporal dynamics of anthocyanins support leaf photoplasticity in Parrotia subaequalis
Authors: Jinwen Chen, Qian Zhou, Xi Huang, Lingjun Yan, Huiting Luo, Yuyi Zhang, Huanli Wang, Shijie Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParrotia subaequalis, a Tertiary relict endemic to China, plays a significant role in phylogeny and adaptive evolution as a key species in the early differentiation of angiosperms. It has abundant leaf colors and great potential as an ornamental tree.ResultsThis study assembled the first chromosome-level genome of P. subaequalis (Contig N50 = 2.15 Mb), revealing transposable element proliferation, key paleopolyploid events and dynamic gene family evolution, including the expansion of secondary metabolite transport and synthesis genes (such as WD40, 2OG-FeII_Oxy) and the contraction of gene families related to flower morphogenesis (such as F-box-like, K-box). Through integrative transcriptomics and targeted metabolomics approaches, we further revealed that the color transition of young leaves from red to green was driven by temporal accumulation differences of malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside, whose biosynthesis is progressively down-regulated during leaf development. WGCNA revealed that a subset of WD40 genes (light-signaling, TTG1/HOS15-like, etc.) coexpresses with anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, like 4CLL9, GT1, in anthocyanin-related modules enriched for auxin signaling and hydrolase activity, suggesting a potential link between WD40 expansion and photoprotective plasticity. Relevant regulatory networks were found to complement the species-specific gene pool related to leaf color regulation.ConclusionThis genomic resource of P. subaequalis advanced our understanding of early angiosperm adaptation through neofunctionalized regulatory networks and established a foundation for molecular breeding aimed at enhancing environmental resilience while preserving ornamental traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09377-x.Keywords: Parrotia subaequalis, Anthocyanin biosynthesis, Gene family, Leaf color, Multi-omics analysis

Introduction

East Asia has the highest diversity of Tertiary relict forest trees [1, 2]. Parrotia subaequalis (Hamamelidaceae), a Tertiary relictual tree endemic to China, is classified as “critically endangered” by both the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN) and the Chinese Plant Red Book because of its small and fragmented wild populations restricted to eastern China (Jiangsu, Anhui, and Zhejiang Provinces) [3, 4]. As early as 1935, the first specimen was collected from Yixing (Jiangsu Province, China) [5] and deposited in the Nanjing Botanical Garden Memorial Sun Yat-Sen (also known as Institute of Botany, Jiangsu Province and Chinese Academy of Sciences (JIB)). In 1992, a flowering specimen led to its reclassification as Shaniodendron subaequale based on its distinctive apetalous floral morphology [6]. Subsequent taxonomic revisions, supported by floral traits and molecular phylogenetic evidence, reassigned the species to the genus Parrotia, resulting in its current nomenclature, Parrotia subaequalis [7–9]. It is a small deciduous tree inhabiting evergreen and deciduous broad-leaved mixed forests at altitudes of 400–1100 m [10]. Its leaves are thinly coriaceous and obovate. The flowers emerge before the leaves, arranged in short spicate inflorescences with silvery-white filaments, and have male and bisexual small flowers. The fruit is a nearly spherical, woody capsule containing narrowly fusiform, brown, and lustrous seeds, and its timber is fine-textured, hard, and dense (Fig. 1A) [4].

As a crucial group involved in the early differentiation of angiosperms ~ 67 million years ago, P. subaequalis holds significant ecological and evolutionary importance, representing a valuable lineage for studying plant adaptation and speciation in East Asia [11]. A particularly striking and ecologically relevant feature of P. subaequalis is the transient red coloration of its young leaves, which gradually fades to green as they mature. This phenomenon is thought to be an adaptive trait, potentially offering photoprotection against high light stress during leaf development or acting as a defense mechanism [12]. Despite its ecological significance as a flagship species for biodiversity conservation and its potential ornamental value due to its abundant leaf colors throughout the seasons and unique apetalous floral structure [13], the genomic basis of the adaptability of P. subaequalis and the specific molecular mechanisms regulating its leaf color transformation have not been fully studied. The lack of chromosome‑level reference genomes has hindered in-depth research on its evolutionary history and the genetic networks that control these key traits.

The formation of colorful leaves is closely related to anthocyanins, a class of flavonoid secondary metabolites derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway [14]. Anthocyanin biosynthesis proceeds through a well-characterized enzymatic cascade that chalcone synthase (CHS) catalyzes the first committed step, followed by chalcone isomerase (CHI), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), and flavanone 3’-hydroxylase (F3’H)/flavonoid 3’,5’-hydroxylase (F3’5’H), which determine the B-ring hydroxylation pattern [15–17]. Subsequently, dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR) reduces dihydroflavonols to leucoanthocyanidins, and anthocyanidin synthase (ANS) converts them into unstable anthocyanidins [18]. The diversity of anthocyanin structures arises from subsequent modifications, primarily glycosylation, acylation, and methylation. Glycosylation, catalyzed by UDP-glycosyltransferases (UGTs), enhances anthocyanin solubility and stability, while acylation further stabilizes these pigments and influences their hue [19, 20]. Collectively, these modifications determine the final color, stability, and biological functions of anthocyanins [14]. Beyond their well-known roles as colorants, anthocyanins fulfill diverse ecological functions, including photoprotection, antioxidant activity, pollinator attraction, and seed dispersal [21]. At the transcriptional level, anthocyanin biosynthesis is tightly controlled at the transcriptional level by a highly conserved regulatory complex known as MYB-bHLH-WD40 (MBW). In this tripartite complex, R2R3-MYB transcription factors provide DNA-binding specificity, bHLH factors enhance transcriptional activation, and WD40 repeat proteins, most notably TTG1 in Arabidopsis, act as scaffolds that stabilize the complex [22]. The MBW complex directly activates the expression of multiple structural genes in the anthocyanin pathway, including DFR, ANS, and UGT [23]. In Liquidambar formosana, a close relative of P. subaequalis, the red coloration of young leaves is attributed to high expression of LfMYB5 and LfMYB123, which activate anthocyanin biosynthesis [24]. However, knowledge of how anthocyanin biosynthesis underlies leaf color variation in P. subaequalis remains limited.

Therefore, to elucidate the genomic architecture underlying the adaptive evolution of P. subaequalis and to decipher the molecular basis of its dynamic leaf color change, in this study, we assembled the first chromosome-scale genome of P. subaequalis, revealing paleopolyploidization events and dynamic gene family evolution related to secondary metabolism and flower morphogenesis, providing insights into its genomic evolution. Karyotype analysis further revealed species-specific chromosomal rearrangements linked to divergence. Crucially, comprehensive transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses pinpointed the molecular drivers of the characteristic young leaf color transition. We demonstrated that the fading of the red color is causally linked to the time-dependent downregulation of malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside biosynthesis. Furthermore, we revealed that amplified WD40 gene family members played pivotal roles, in the formation of integrated adaptive networks crucial for anthocyanin synthesis, transport, and their coordination with leaf growth and photoprotective plasticity. These findings provide molecular insights into the adaptive evolution of P. subaequalis and establish the foundation for targeted conservation strategies and ornamental development.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The study materials of P. subaequalis were collected from Yixing National Forest Park (Jiangsu Province, China) under the permission of the collaboration agreement based on the funding (LYKJ[2020]24). The plant material used in the study was formally identified by Yuning Xiong (JIB staff). Several branches from one individual P. subaequalis were carried out cutting propagation. The cutting seedlings were grown in a greenhouse under natural light (25 ± 2℃, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod). Fresh young leaves from three independent cutting seedlings were pooled for genome sequencing. The roots, stems, young leaves, and flower organs were collected for genome sequence annotation at the same growth stage. Leaves with different colors across developmental stages, namely, red young leaves (S1), red margin leaves (S2), and green mature leaves (S3), were collected for transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis, with three biological replicates consisting of three leaves pooled from three different seedlings prepared for each color. All leaves were collected from the same set of cutting seedlings on a sunny day at 10:00 AM, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection and stored at -80 °C.

Genome and transcriptome sequencing

A paired-end genomic DNA library with an insert size of 350 bp was constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. An amplification-free whole genome sequencing library was constructed and then sequenced via a PromethION sequencer. A Hi-C library was generated following a Hi-C protocol [25] and then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing work was performed at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

To improve annotation accuracy, the SMRTbell library and the single-strand circle DNA library were constructed from RNA extracted from different tissues and then sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II and the DNBSEQ-T7RS platforms, respectively. cDNA libraries of total RNA from three leaf color phenotypes were constructed and then sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus System.

Genome size estimation

Fastp (v0.23.4) [26] (-n 0 -f 5 -F 5 -t 5 -T 5 -q 20) was used for quality control of the Illumina raw data to remove the adapters, inaccurate bases at both ends, and reads containing N and of low quality. The clean Illumina short reads were used to estimate the genome size of P. subaequalis: (1) k-mers were counted and plotted as a histogram using Jellyfish (v2.2.10) [27]; (2) GenomeScope (v2.0) [28] and GCE (v1.0.2) [29] were used to calculate genome size.

Genome sequence assembly

Raw ONT reads were filtered with a threshold of a mean_qscore_template less than 7 using Guppy (v6.4.6) [30] during sequencing. The error correction was performed via NextDenovo (v2.3.1) [31] (reads_cutoff: 1k, seed_cutoff: 27k), followed by preliminary genome assembly, which was conducted with the corrected reads using the NextGraph module of the same software. Subsequently, genome polishing was carried out using NextPolish (v1.2.4) [32], incorporating both ONT and Illumina clean reads.

Initial quality control of the raw Hi-C data was conducted using HiC-Pro (v2.8.1) [33], and low-quality reads (quality scores < 20), adapters and short sequences (length < 30 bp) were subsequently filtered using Fastp (v0.12.6) [26]. The clean Hi-C reads were then mapped using Bowtie2 (v2.3.2) [34] (-end-to-end --very-sensitive -L 30) to obtain the unique mapped paired-end reads. After filtering, valid interaction paired reads were identified and retained by HiC-Pro (v2.8.1). LACHESIS [35] was used to anchor the assembled contigs to the chromosomal level, with the parameters CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 100, CLUSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 2.5, CLUSTER NONINFORMATIVE RATIO = 1.4, ORDER MIN N RES IN TRUNK = 60, and ORDER MIN N RES IN SHREDS = 60. Finally, errors in chromatin interaction patterns were manually corrected to increase assembly accuracy.

Three assessment strategies were employed to assess the assembly quality of the P. subaequalis genome sequence: (i) Genome completeness was evaluated utilizing benchmarking universal single-copy ortholog (BUSCO) (v5.1.3) [36] based on the embryophyta_odb10 database; (ii) Merfin (v1.1) [37] was used to construct a 21-Kmer library, and then Merqury (v1.3) [38] was performed to evaluate the genome assembly based on K-mer to calculate the consensus quality value (QV) and error rate. (iii) BWA (v0.7.17) [39] and Minimap2 (v2.26) [40] (-ax map-ont) were used to evaluate genome sequence consistency by aligning Illumina and ONT clean reads, respectively. SAMtools (v1.18) [41] and FreeBayes (v1.3.6) [42] with default parameters were used to calculate the mapping results of the Illumina clean reads. The single-base error rate of the genome sequence was calculated by the corresponding homozygous and heterozygous mutation sites, and the homozygous site was regarded as the error site.

Repeat annotation

GMATA (v2.2) [43] was employed to identify simple sequence repeats (SSRs) across the genome sequence, and the identified SSRs were subsequently softmasked. Next, tandem repeats were detected using TRF (v4.07b) [44]. For the annotation of transposable elements (TEs), RepeatModeler (v1.0.11) with default parameters was employed to predict a de novo repeat library on the hardmasked genome sequence based on the MITE library constructed by MITE-Hunter [45] (-n 20 -P 0.2 -c 3) and the LTR-RT library combining LTR_Finder (v1.07) [46] and LTRharvest (v1.6.5) [47] results constructed by LTR_retriever (v2.9.8) [48]. The obtained library was classified into each type of repeat family using TEclass [49]. RepeatMasker (v1.331) [50] (nolow -no_is -gff -norna -engine abblast -lib lib) was applied to search for known and novel TEs by mapping sequences against the de novo repeat library and Repbase [51] TE library to perform genome-wide repeat annotation. Overlapping TEs belonging to the same repeat class were collated and combined.

Gene annotation

For prediction based on homology, protein sequences from Cercidiphyllum japonicum, Liquidambar formosana, Vitis vinifera, and Tiarella polyphylla were aligned against the P. subaequalis repeat-masked genome sequence using GeMoMa (v1.6.1) [52]. For transcriptome prediction, mRNA-seq reads filtered by Fastp (v0.23.4) were aligned to the P. subaequalis genome sequence using STAR (v2.7.3a) [53], followed by transcript assembly via StringTie (v1.3.4d) [54]. PacBio Isoseq reads were filtered and mapped using the Isoseq3 pipeline [55] and Minimap2 (v2.26) [40] to generate full-length transcripts. Finally, open reading frames were predicted using PASA (v2.3.3) [56]. For de novo prediction, genes were predicted based on the training model derived from the transcriptome-predicted gene models using AUGUSTUS (v3.3.1) [57] with default parameters. A final gene set without miscoded and TE-inserted genes filtered by TransposonPSI (http://transposonpsi.sourceforge.net/) was integrated using the EVidenceModeler (v1.1.1) pipeline [56].

For ncRNA identification, transfer RNAs were identified using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0) [58] based on parameters specific to eukaryotes. MicroRNA, rRNA, small nuclear RNA, and small nucleolar RNA were detected using Infernal (v1.1.2) [59] with the cmscan algorithm to search the Rfam database [60]. Ribosomal RNAs, along with their corresponding subunits, were further annotated using RNAmmer (v1.2) [61].

The SwissProt, NR, KEGG and KOG databases were used for homology searches of EvidenceModeler-integrated protein sequences using BLASTp (v2.7.1) (-evalue 1e-5, -max_target_seqs 1). Additionally, annotations including the putative domains and Gene Ontology (GO) terms were further supplemented with InterProScan (v5.32) [62] to generate comprehensive functional annotations for the predicted genes.

Phylogenetic reconstruction and divergence time estimation

Protein sequences from the relatively closely related species (C. japonicum, L. formosana, V. vinifera, and T. polyphylla) of P. subaequalis and Zea mays, Oryza sativa, and Arabidopsis thaliana were obtained, and only the longest transcript of each gene was preserved. OrthoFinder (v.3.0.1) [63] was used to screen orthologous genes. Single-copy orthologs were aligned with MAFFT (v7.525) [64]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE (v2.3.6) [65]. The estimated divergence time of O. sativa and A. thaliana was obtained from timetree (http://www.timetree.org/) for r8s (v1.81) [66], modifying the scale of the phylogenetic tree to reflect time.

Evolutionary changes in gene families and positive selection analysis

CAFÉ (v5.1.0) [67] was employed to identify gene families that have undergone significant evolutionary changes based on the gene counts of orthogroups from Orthofinder (-a 5 -S blast -T iqtree -M msa). A maximum-likelihood model was applied, with the exclusion of gene families exhibiting a notable copy number variation, specifically those presented with a copy number greater than 100 in only one species. The ultrametric time tree was from r8s (v1.81) and the lambda of each species node was set to 1. A significance threshold of 0.05 was used to determine families exhibiting substantial changes in size across different species with other parameters default. Additionally, the annotations of the orthogroups were conducted based on the orthogroup sequences searching against the Pfam database using HMMER (v3.4) [68].

The longest protein sequences of each species from each family exhibiting substantial changes were aligned using MAFFT (v7.525) with ‘-auto -quiet’ parameters. Codon-based nucleotide alignments were generated from protein alignments using PAL2NAL (v14.0) (https://github.com/liaochenlanruo/PAL2NAL). The branch leading to the P. subaequalis clade was designated as the foreground branch for selection analysis and the others were all background branches. Positive selection was tested using CodeML from the PAML package (v4.10.7) under the branch-site model framework. The null model (H0) was set as model = 0, NSsites = 0, fix_omega = 1, omega = 0.4, and the alternative model (H1) was set as model = 2, NSsites = 0, fix_omega = 0. Likelihood ratio tests were conducted to compare the H1 vs. H0 models. P values were calculated as P = 1 - χ²(df = 1) and adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. Gene families with FDR < 0.05 and ω > 1 were considered under positive selection, and gene families with log2(foreground ω/background ω) < 0 and FDR < 0.05 were considered under intensified purifying, whereas those with log2(foreground ω/background ω) > 0 and FDR < 0.05 were relaxed constraints.

LTR insertion-age analysis around WD40 genes

Intact LTR retrotransposons were annotated using LTR_retriever (v2.9.8) [48] with default parameters, combining predictions from LTR_Finder (v1.07) [46] and LTRharvest (v1.6.5) [47] Insertion ages were calculated from the Kimura distance between 5′ and 3′ LTRs. WD40 genes were extracted from OrthoFinder (v.3.0.1) [63] annotated by Pfam domain. For each WD40 gene, intact LTRs within 50 kb flanking windows were retrieved. Duplicate LTRs shared by multiple WD40 genes were collapsed by unique LTR identifier. Local WD40 amplification clusters were defined as adjacent WD40 genes separated by ≤ 500 kb, and LTRs within 50 kb of each cluster were analyzed separately. Age distributions were compared among genome‑wide LTRs, WD40‑proximal LTRs, and cluster‑associated LTRs using histograms and density‑normalized plots.

Genome synteny analysis and karyotype evolution analysis

Protein-coding sequences within or between the genomes of P. subaequalis and other species were aligned using BLASTp (v2.9.0), applying an E-value threshold of 1e-5. Syntenic blocks were determined from the detected homologous gene pairs without tandem duplicates using WGDI (v0.71) [69]. The assessment of Ks was conducted utilizing the PAML package (v4.10.7) [70]. The formula t = Ks/2r was employed to calculate the time of whole-genome duplication (WGD) events (r = 8.25 × 10− 9) [71]. The genome-wide collinearity visualization among the five species was carried out in accordance with the JCVI (v0.9.14) [72] process (--cscore = 0.9, --minspan = 30). Based on the published process, the karyotype evolution analysis of P. subaequalis was conducted using WGDI (v0.71). We mapped the V. vinifera genome to the P. subaequalis genome using DIAMOND (v0.9.29) [73] to explore the chromosome collinearity, which was intact and independently preserved between two species.

Metabolomic analysis

The P. subaequalis samples for the determination and analysis of anthocyanin-related metabolites were freeze-dried after collection, ground into powder (30 Hz, 1.5 min), and stored at -80 °C until needed. 50 mg powder was weighted and extracted with 0.5 mL methanol/water/hydrochloric acid (500:500:1, V/V/V). Then, the extract was vortexed for 5 min and ultrasonicated for 5 min and centrifuged at 12, 000 g under 4 °C for 3 min. The residue was re-extracted by repeating the above steps again under the same conditions. The supernatants were collected and filtrated through a membrane filter (0.22 μm, Anpel) before analyzed using an UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system. All the samples were analyzed in biological triplicate.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the stats package from R (v4.2.0). Metabolites with detectable concentrations in at least three samples were used for the identification of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) between groups based on |log2FC| ≥ 1 and P value < 0.15 (for the retention of procyanidins). The identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG compound database and mapped to the KEGG pathway database. Pathways containing DAMs were subjected to metabolite set enrichment analysis using a hypergeometric test. The heatmaps representing the abundance of metabolites were drawn via pheatmap in R (v3.6.1).

Gene expression analysis

FASTQC (v0.12.1) [74] was used to assess the quality of the raw transcriptome reads obtained from the samples of different leaf color types (S1, S2, and S3), which included three biological replicates for each type. Quality control procedures were conducted using Fastp (v0.23.4). The clean RNA-seq reads were aligned using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [75]. The calculations for normalized expression level TPM and the mapped read counts were performed by StringTie (v1.3.4d) [54] and featureCounts (v1.6.4) [76], respectively. PCA was conducted to identify the intersample duplication using FactoMineR (v2.4) [77] as implemented in R (v3.6.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with varying leaf colors were identified using DEseq2 (v1.12.4), applying a threshold of |log2FC| ≥ 2 and an FDR of less than 0.05 [78]. GO enrichment analysis was performed by the ClusterProfiler (v3.14.0) [79] in R (v3.6.1). All heatmaps representing gene expression were drawn by pheatmap in R (v3.6.1).

KIPEs-based identification of flavonoid biosynthesis genes

To systematically identify flavonoid biosynthesis-related genes in the P. subaequalis genome, we used KIPEs3 (v3.2.7) [80] on the protein sequences. KIPEs3 was run with its built‑in flavonoid biosynthesis bait sequence dataset and conserved residue/region dataset. Default parameters were used, with the BLAST similarity cutoff 40%, score ratio cutoff 0.3, and global alignment similarity cutoff 0.4.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis

The weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) package [81] in R (v3.6.1) was used to construct the coexpression networks for P. subaequalis. The input for WGCNA consisted of the merged rlog normalized output derived from the expression gene matrix of various leaf color samples, which was generated using DESeq2 (v1.12.4) [78]. Network modules were defined through a dynamic tree-cutting algorithm [82] with default parameters. The module membership (MM) of each gene representing the intramodular connectivity, was estimated to search for the hub genes [83].The identification of anthocyanin biosynthesis (AB) modules was conducted using two parallel methods [84]. First, AB genes in P. subaequalis were enriched in network modules according to the ‘greater’ Fisher’s exact test [85] (P value < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [86] was subsequently employed to identify modules where AB genes served as network hubs (P < 0.05, FDR Q<0.05) based on module genes ranked by MM using the GSEA function in the R package ClusterProfiler (v3.14.0) [79].

Results

Chromosome-level genome assembly and annotation revealing TE-driven genome expansion

P. subaequalis (2n = 2x = 24) is a deciduous tree with distinct morphological features (Fig. 1A). To generate its chromosome-level genome assembly, an integrated sequencing strategy was utilized (Table S1). The genome size of P. subaequalis was estimated to be 951.57 Mb and 1025.88 Mb through two approaches using K-mer analysis [27] (Fig. S1), consistent with previous flow cytometry measurements [87]. The initial assembly was first generated based on ~ 93.34 Gb Nanopore long reads (~ 90.99× genome equivalents) (Fig. S2 and Table S1). Then, the assembled genome sequence corrected by ~ 49.82 Gb Illumina short reads (~ 48.56× genome equivalents, Q30 rate = 93.74%) resulted in a 1152.55 Mb genome sequence with 952 contigs of an N50 size of 2.17 Mb (Tables S1-S2). These contigs were arranged into a final assembly using ~ 58.42 Gb Hi-C data (~ 56.95× genome equivalents) (Fig. S3 and Table S1). Finally, a total of 647 contigs with 1022.69 Mb (anchor rate: 90.59%) were successfully anchored onto 12 chromosomes, ranging in length from 65.99 Mb to 107.50 Mb (Table 1 and S3). The final assembly of P. subaequalis captured 1128.93 Mb with a scaffold N50 of 83.16 Mb (Table 1 and S4).

Assembly summary for the Parrotia subaequalis genome

To evaluate the quality of the assembled genome, we first aligned the Illumina and Nanopore reads, and the mapping ratios were 97.40% and 99.82%, respectively, indicating the integrity of the assembly (Table S5). The error rate and QV calculated based on K-mers were 0.0029% and 25.3529, respectively. Strong intrachromosomal interactions along the diagonal observed in the Hi-C interaction matrix supported the chromosome-scale structure (Fig. S3). Additionally, BUSCO estimation [36] of the assembled genome revealed 97.46% completeness, indicating a nearly complete assembly (Table S6).

Through an integrated pipeline, 34,194 protein-coding genes were annotated in the P. subaequalis genome (Fig. 1B and Table S7). Among these genes, 93.51% (31,976 genes) were successfully anchored to chromosomes, and were unevenly distributed on the chromosomes of P. subaequalis (Fig. 1B). 91.58% (31,316 genes) of the total genes were functionally annotated against different databases (Fig. S4 and Table S8). BUSCO estimation revealed 98.39% gene-space completeness for all protein-coding genes, suggesting that the assembly of gene-coding regions was nearly complete (Table 1 and S6). In the assembled P. subaequalis genome, we identified 3,762 non-coding RNAs in total, including 2,940 small RNAs, 535 transfer RNAs, 178 ribosomal RNAs and other regulatory elements (Fig. 1B and Table S9). We found a total of 842.56 Mb repeat sequences, constituting 74.63% of the assembled P. subaequalis genome (Table S10). This proportion is significantly higher than those observed in the genomes of C. japonicum (59.07%), V. vinifera (66.47%), L. formosana (55.77%), and T. polyphylla (60.10%) [88–91] (P < 0.05, Fisher’s exact test). Among these repeat sequences, TEs were predominant, comprising 71.22% of the genome. Specifically, long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) were the most abundant TEs, covering 664.35 Mb, which is 58.85% of the total genome. Among the LTR-RTs, the Gypsy superfamily was the most prevalent, accounting for 172.49 Mb (15.28% of the genome), followed by the Copia superfamily with 113.72 Mb (10.07% of the genome) (Fig. 1B and Table S10).

Comparative genomics uncovering paleopolyploid events and gene family dynamics

To elucidate the phylogenetic relationship and evolutionary position of P. subaequalis, a comparative genomic analysis of eight species was performed, including one species from the same family Hamamelidaceae (L. formosana), apetalous species from adjacent families (C. japonicum, T. polyphylla), the adjacent petalous species V. vinifera, the model Eudicots plant (A. thaliana) and O. sativa and Z. mays as outgroups (Table S11) [88–91]. A total of 250,698 genes from the above eight species were identified and clustered into 17,427 orthogroups (gene families). Notably, 529 species-specific gene families consisting of 1,886 genes were identified for P. subaequalis and were enriched in hydrolase activity, protein glycosylation and glycolytic processes (Fig. S5). A phylogenetic tree of the aforementioned eight species was constructed using 1,016 single-copy orthologs (Table S12). The results revealed that P. subaequalis is most closely related to L. formosana from the same family and C. japonicum from Cercidiphyllaceae (Fig. 2B). The established divergence time of O. sativa-A. thaliana (160 mya) was used for fossil calibration. Divergence time estimates indicate that P. subaequalis split from the shared C. japonicum-L. formosana lineage at approximately 25.6 mya, followed by the divergence of the common ancestor of C. japonicum-L. formosana at approximately 23.3 mya (Figs. 2B and S6), which is consistent with the previous studies on the phylogenetic relationship between the two species [88]. Gene family evolution analysis identified 2017 expanded families and 1318 contracted families in P. subaequalis, respectively (Fig. 2B). PFAM functional annotation was performed on the orthogroups of genes with significant number changes, which were subsequently ranked (Fig. 2C). Among the significantly expanded gene families, the functions of genes were mainly associated with the synthesis and transport of secondary metabolites (flavonoids, anthocyanins, proanthocyanidins, etc.) [92–94], such as WD40 (PF00400), 2OG-FeII_Oxy (PF03171), UDPGT (PF00201), DIOX_N (PF14226), and adh_short (PF00106). The gene families undergoing positive selection were mainly associated with immune defense, stress response, and the evolution of the reproductive system, while the expanded secondary metabolism-related gene families, such as WD40, mostly underwent accelerated evolution under weak purifying selection (Fig. 2D and Table S13). The WD40 genes were widely and nonuniformly distributed on chromosomes (Fig. S7). To calibrate the timing of WD40 family expansion, we estimated insertion ages of LTR retrotransposons located within 50 kb of WD40 genes. A total of 36 LTRs were identified near WD40 genes, with insertion ages ranging from 0 to 2.79 Ma (median = 0.69 Ma). For the WD40 local amplification clusters, 16 LTRs were found, ranging from 0 to 1.48 Ma (median = 0.76 Ma; Fig. S8). Among the gene families with significant contraction, many genes, such as SRF-TF (PF00319), F-box-like (PF12937) and K-box (PF01486), were found to be involved in regulating physiological activities such as plant morphogenesis and flower organ development [95–97].

Synonymous substitution rates (Ks) and syntenic analysis were employed to investigate the WGD of the P. subaequalis genome and the other four species. A unique Ks peak at 1.05, based on syntenic blocks of P. subaequalis, was identified (Fig. 2E). The Ks peaks of the other four species ranged from 0.91 to 1.32, which corresponded to the species divergence time (Fig. 2E). We found that the five species experienced a shared ancient whole-genome triplication (WGT) event at approximately 55–80 mya and did not undergo WGD events after then (Figs. 2E and S9). Chromosomal collinearity analysis between P. subaequalis and the other four species also revealed a collinearity pattern of 1:1 (Fig. 2F). These findings are in agreement with the results of the Ks analysis and suggest that P. subaequalis has not undergone any independent WGD events during its evolutionary history.

Ancestral karyotype reconstruction elucidates the chromosomal evolution process of P. subaequalis

Using the assembled genome of P. subaequalis as a reference to examine genome collinearity, two V. vinifera chromosomes (Vv08 and Vv10) were found to be largely preserved in the P. subaequalis chromosomes, although in different orders (Fig. S10). Thus, we could infer two relatively ancestral chromosomes (Ps09 and Ps11). However, other chromosomes appeared to have undergone multiple fusion and fission events (Fig. S10). To assess the karyotype evolutionary history of P. subaequalis, a possible ancestral karyotype was reconstructed based on limited genomic resources following a published pipeline [69] (Fig. 3A). According to the ancestral karyotype, V. vinifera underwent at least 5 chromosomal fissions (Cfiss) and 7 chromosomal fusions (Cfus), while T. polyphylla experienced 17 Cfiss and 31 Cfus events (Fig. 3A). Compared with the closely related species, L. formosana and C. japonicum, P. subaequalis presented significantly fewer Cfiss events (n = 4), and a relatively few Cfus events (n = 13), which indicated that P. subaequalis had a conserved genome structure and stable evolutionary traits, reinforcing its ancient characteristics (Fig. 3A).

Reconstructed proto-karyotypes and evolutionary trajectories of P. subaequalis. A The ancestral chromosomes are represented with seven colors. Polyploidization events are shown by red star, along with the numbers of cfus and cfiss. The chromosome structures of all the species are illustrated by the same color bars at the bottom. B Evolutionary trajectory of extant P. subaequalis chromosomes. RTA, reciprocal translocation of chromosome arms; EEJ, end-to-end joining of chromosomes; NCF, nested chromosome fusion

Following the reconstruction of ancestral prochromosomes, we delineated the evolutionary trajectory underlying the chromosomal architecture of P. subaequalis (Fig. 3B). Subsequent to the WGT event, a rather intricate restructuring process involving two reciprocal translocations of chromosome arms (RTAs), seven end-to-end joinings (EEJs), and one nested chromosome fusion (NCF) led to a chromosome number of 12 for P. subaequalis (Fig. 3B). Notably, the forming process of chromosome Ps12 involved a multi-step reorganization process, which first went through two RTAs followed by one single EEJ again. This complex series of structural changes indicates strong selective pressure for stabilizing the karyotype after polyploidization, with the formation of Ps12 possibly acting as a crucial event in speciation.

Malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside as the main anthocyanin underlying leaf red fading

To investigate the metabolite differences contributing to the distinct leaf colors in P. subaequalis across three developmental stages, three colors of leaves were collected: red S1, red margin S2, and green S3 (Fig. 4A), and metabolites were detected. PCA demonstrated strong repeatability within groups and effectively distinguished between different groups of samples (Fig. S11). A total of 58 metabolites were identified from the three color leaves, mainly composed of 51 anthocyanins, also including 2 flavonoids and 5 procyanidins. The anthocyanin metabolites mainly consisted of 13 delphinidins, 12 cyanidins, 8 malvidins, 7 peonidins, 6 pelargonidins and 5 petunidins (Table S14). The total anthocyanin content of S1 was the highest, approximately 1.5 times that of S2 and 22 times higher than that of S3. Notably, malvidins accounted for approximately 97% of the total detected anthocyanins of S1, S2, and S3 (Fig. 4B), which indicated that malvidin derivatives are the primary anthocyanins synthesized in the leaves of P. subaequalis.

Metabolome profiles of three different P. subaequalis color leaves during different developmental stages. A Three types of leaf samples with different colors during different developmental stages. Scale bars, 1 cm. B Contents of the anthocyanins in S1, S2 and S3. C Numbers of DAMs among leaves of different colors during different developmental stages. D Venn diagram depicting the shared and specific DAMs among the three compared combinations. E The relative metabolite contents of S1, S2 and S3

A total of 35 DAMs between different colors of leaves were identified based on a |log2FC|≥1 and P value < 0.15 among the total metabolites (Table S15). As the leaves changed from red to green, a large number of metabolites demonstrated a significant down-regulation trend (Figs. 4C). The KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the DAMs were mainly enriched in anthocyanin biosynthesis (ko00942) and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110; Fig. S12). A total of 16 DAMs exhibited consistent differential accumulation between the three leaf color phenotypes, of which 15 anthocyanin metabolites, primarily consisting of malvidins, peonidins and delphinidins, gradually down-regulated during the process of leaf transition from red to green, and 1 gradually increased (Fig. 4D and Table S15). During the green-driven chromatic transition, stage-specific metabolic alterations between S1 and S2 were primarily characterized by marked downregulation of procyanidin B2, B4, and C1 (Fig. 4E and Table S15). During the red recession phase, metabolomic divergence was predominantly manifested in the progressive accumulation of procyanidin B1/B3 coupled with a concomitant decrease of anthocyanin diglucosides (malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside and delphinidin-3,5-O-diglucoside) (Fig. 4E and Table S15). Notably, among the major DAMs, malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside accounted for about 97% of the total detected anthocyanins in the three types of leaves, with its concentration in S1 being 22 times greater than that in S3 (Fig. 4E).

Collectively, these findings delineated that P. subaequalis greening initiation was mechanistically linked to the metabolic arrest of some procyanidins, whereas complete red dissipation likely resulted from a dynamic equilibrium between procyanidin B1/B3 and anthocyanin diglucosides through coordinated metabolic flux redistribution.

WD40 networks associated with anthocyanin response to strong light

To further explore the potential regulatory factors of young leaf red fading, transcriptome data of P. subaequalis were sequenced concurrently and jointly analyzed with the metabolome data. PCA showed good intergroup differences (Fig. S13). The observation of the dynamic changes in gene expression in leaves of different colors revealed that gene expression was very active in S2. Compared with those in S1, many genes were down-regulated in S2, and 2401 genes were up-regulated in S3 (Fig. 5A), indicating that the S2 leaf type, as a key leaf color turning point, coordinates the physiological transformation of leaves through dual regulatory mechanisms. The functional enrichment of the transcriptome and metabolome indicated that the coenriched pathways were associated with the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and anthocyanins (Fig. S14). WGCNA was performed on the expressed genes in all the samples, and 10 modules (including gray) were finally divided, with the number of genes in each module ranging from 380 to 4893 (Fig. S15). Based on 155 genes associated with AB from the genome functional annotation, whose core anthocyanin and flavonoid synthesis pathway genes was supported by KIPES (hypergeometric test, P = 5.56 × 10− 17), we located each expressed AB gene in the modules and found that AB genes were significantly enriched in the red module (Fig. 5B). Combining the malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside content and 0–1 matrix of each leaf color type, we explored the correlation between modules and phenotypes. The results revealed that the blue, green and red modules were significantly correlated with the malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside content, with the blue and green modules positively correlated, while the red module negatively correlated (Fig. 5C). The gene expression profiles of these three modules exhibited a strong association with distinct leaf color developmental stages, demonstrating significant differential expression patterns across the stages (Fig. 5C and D). We performed GSEA on all the genes in each module and found that the hubgenes in these three modules were mainly related to growth and development regulation and environmental adaptation (Fig. S16). The correlation between gene connectivity and gene significance in the three modules indicated that the hubgenes in the three modules were strongly correlated with phenotype (Fig. S17).

In consideration of the significant expansion of the WD40 gene families, we observed that the three coexpression modules containing multiple WD40 genes were significantly enriched in protein heterodimerization activity, response to auxin, microtubule binding/movement function and hydrolase activity (Fig. 5E and Table S16), which suggested a potential link between the expanded WD40 families and the recruitment of these adaptive processes in P. subaequalis. To explore this link in greater detail, we retained 205 high-confidence WD40 proteins after Pfam domain filtering, of which 194 have WGCNA module assignments and 61 fall into the three anthocyanin-associated modules (Fig. 5F). Multiple WD40 subfamilies occupied distinct positions within coexpression networks to coordinately regulate pigmentation, photoprotection and environmental responses, such as light-signaling WD40s (G22 and G29), TTG1/HOS15-like subfamily (G27) representing the most pigment-proximal candidates, chromatin-related WD40s (G02, G03, G04, G06, G11, G15, G23) providing epigenetic plasticity, and vesicle transport, vacuolar sorting and autophagy-related WD40s (G05, G08, G09, G10, G12, G14, G19). Together, these observations indicate that the expanded WD40 families in P. subaequalis contribute to leaf photoprotective plasticity through a multi-layered regulatory network integrating light perception, MBW-driven biosynthesis, chromatin remodeling and vacuolar trafficking.

By further observing the coexpression networks of WD40s and AB genes, we discovered important hubgenes, 4CLL9 and GT1 coexpressed with multiple WD40s, with quite high MMs and gene significances (|MM| > 0.9 & |GS. content| > 0.9) (Fig. S18 and Table S17). Most genes in the network showed significant differential expression between S1 and S3 (Fig. S18). The expression levels of 4CLL9 and GT1 were positively and negatively correlated with the accumulation patterns of anthocyanins respectively (Fig. S18), suggesting functional divergence between these two genes in anthocyanin synthesis pathway and highlighting the complexity of the multi-layered regulatory network involving WD40s.

Discussion

P. subaequalis is predominantly distributed in gravelly valley bottoms and the xeric margins of mountain ridges, where edaphic conditions are characterized by low soil water content and high frequency of drought stress [98]. Moreover, the habitat distribution pattern of P. subaequalis is fragmented with insular configurations, resulting in limited gene flow and decreased interspecific competitive capacity [5]. Additionally, P. subaequalis features an apetalous inflorescence structure with pendulous stamens and dichogamous reproductive traits, which inherently limits pollination efficiency and the multi-year flowering cycle reduces the frequency of pollination [99, 100]. These factors collectively constitute the primary drivers of P. subaequalis endangerment and allow it to survive for millions of years with conservative adaptive characteristics.

Genome assembly quality and TE-driven genome expansion

This chromosome‑level assembly for P. subaequalis not only facilitates a systematic dissection of genome structure, evolutionary trajectories and adaptive genomic signatures, but also provides insights for preserving genetic diversity and adaptive potential. In this study, an integrative multi-platform sequencing strategy combined with advanced assembly pipelines resulted in the reliability, accuracy, and completeness of the P. subaequalis genome sequence, which was evaluated using a comprehensive approach, encompassing sequence consistency analysis and BUSCO assessments. Through comparative genome analysis of P. subaequalis with other published Saxifragales genomes, we observed that the size of the P. subaequalis genome sequence, measuring 1.13 Gb, is substantially larger than those of closely related species, despite all sharing a common ancient WGT event. This expansion was predominantly driven by the proliferation of TEs, which constitute 71.22% of the genome. This TE composition ratio aligns with the key role of TEs as major contributors to plant genome size variation [101]. Additionally, the P. subaequalis genome also has longer average intron lengths compared to closely related species (Table S18), and the activity of TEs and their insertion site preferences likely explain the correlation between the genome and intron sizes [102, 103], suggesting potential links between TE dynamics and the evolution of gene structure complexity in P. subaequalis.

Paleopolyploidization and dynamic gene family evolution underlying adaptation

WGD, which has historically been recognized as a major driver of genome diversification and long-term evolution, fundamentally reshapes the genomic architecture through DNA replication and chromosomal rearrangements and further enhances the adaptive plasticity of the species through the availability of duplicated genes [104]. These duplicated genes are subsequently shaped by selective pressures and regulatory mechanisms, leading to the contraction or expansion of gene families [105]. In P. subaequalis, this evolutionary trajectory was exemplified by the strategic expansion and contraction of key gene families, which directly underpinned its unique ecological adaptations. Gene family expansion and contraction analysis performed on the eight species revealed that the expanded gene families in P. subaequalis were highly associated with biosynthesis and transport of secondary metabolites, including WD40 (PF00400) and 2OG-FeII_Oxy (PF03171), which are particularly crucial in anthocyanin pathway regulation and abiotic stress adaptation [106, 107]. Notably, several WD40 genes underwent accelerated evolution under weak purifying selection, suggesting neofunctionalization events critical for environmental adaptation, where these copies may be related to adaptive survival, such as UV resistance and drought tolerance [108–110], as we discovered in our preliminary experiments that strong light and UV light caused young leaves to turn red. LTR insertion dating placed the major activity of WD40-proximal elements within the Pleistocene, a period of intense glacial-interglacial cycles, with signals enriched around the Mid-Pleistocene Transition (~ 1.2–0.8 Ma) [111]. This temporal correlation is consistent with the possibility that Pleistocene climate oscillations stimulated TE activity, which could have facilitated the expansion and neofunctionalization of stress-related WD40 families.

Conversely, significant contraction was observed in gene families governing plant morphogenesis and floral development, such as F-box-like (PF12937) and K-box (PF01486) belonging to MADS-box gene family, which may underpin the apetalous phenotype, dichogamous reproduction, and a multi-year flowering cycle [97]. These genomic patterns may suggest a strategic resource allocation trade-off in P. subaequalis: under xeric and nutrient-poor conditions characterized by intense solar radiation and water scarcity, the species prioritizes metabolic pathways that enhance stress tolerance over energetically costly reproductive structures [112]. We acknowledge that the gene ontology and annotation data may contain numerous biases [113]. To better understand the genomic basis and mechanism of the speciation process, more experimental work is still required, even though our work offered putative genomic evidence of P. subaequalis’ ecological adaptability.

WD40 co-expression networks: correlations and limitations

Mechanistically, the change in P. subaequalis young leaf color from red to green was associated with the metabolic arrest of some procyanidins, whereas the complete red dissipation might be caused by the altered metabolic flux ratio between procyanidins B1/B3 and anthocyanin diglucosides, especially malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside. However, differential anthocyanidin profiles revealed that in L. formosana purplish-red young leaves, delphinidin and cyanidin constitute the major pigments responsible for coloration [24]. Therefore, these findings demonstrated that even within closely related species, the dominant anthocyanins underlying the same young leaf color may differ significantly, potentially indicating divergent selection pressures acted on anthocyanin biosynthetic pathways.

Mining the network modules closely related to anthocyanin accumulation through WGCNA revealed that three modules were highly correlated with the synthesis of malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside. WD40, an important component of the key MBW (MYB-bHLH-WD40) complex that regulates anthocyanin synthesis [92, 93], played a significant role in gene family dynamic changes in P. subaequalis. Although direct functional validation of WD40 genes in P. subaequalis is currently constrained by its protected status and lack of transformation systems, their conserved role in anthocyanin regulation is strongly supported by functional studies in other species. For instance, VIGS silencing of the WD40 homolog CaTTG1 in pepper significantly reduced anthocyanin accumulation in hypocotyls [114], and knockdown of SmWD40-56 in eggplant resulted in decreased expression of anthocyanin structural genes [115].

The WD40 gene families emerged as a key evolutionary innovation in P. subaequalis, correlated with the coexpression networks regulated by anthocyanins, including protein heterodimerization activity, auxin response, microtubule-mediated processes, etc. Multiple WD40 subfamilies occupy distinct positions within the anthocyanin-associated coexpression modules, demonstrating that the core value of WD40s in leaf color transition is not a single determinant but a coordinated multi-subfamily network. Light-signaling WD40s act as upstream switches linking light signals to anthocyanin accumulation [116]. The TTG1/HOS15-like subfamily represents the most pigment-proximal candidates [117], paralleling the decrease of malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside and making it a prime MBW complex participant. Chromatin-related WD40s provide epigenetic plasticity [92]. Vesicle transport and autophagy-related WD40s connect anthocyanin synthesis to vacuolar sequestration and cellular turnover [118]. Under comprehensive consideration, we supposed that WD40 expansion and network integration may enhance leaf photoprotective plasticity through three synergistic mechanisms: regulating structural constituent of chromatin, coordinating auxin signaling, and enhancing hydrolase activity [119], which needs more evidence in P. subaequalis. Relevant studies have already mentioned that the COP1 WD40 protein was involved in chromatin remodeling in the optical signaling pathway and regulated the expression of genes related to light morphogenesis through ubiquitination modification [120].

In the network of WD40s, the antagonistic hubs 4CLL9 and GT1 exemplified the regulatory complexity of anthocyanin metabolism (Fig. 6). The strong positive correlation of 4CLL9, a 4-coumarate-CoA ligase-like gene, with anthocyanin accumulation suggested its role in maintaining phenylpropanoid precursor flux [121]. In apple, Md4CL10 expression was 516-fold higher in deeply pigmented varieties and correlated directly with anthocyanin content [122]. The negative correlation of GT1 with anthocyanin accumulation implied a potential feedback inhibitor in P. subaequalis. VIGS silencing of the GT1 homolog RrGT1 in rose led to significantly reduced petal pigmentation and anthocyanin content, while its overexpression in Arabidopsis restored red coloration [123]. These homologous validations strongly support our inferred regulatory roles for WD40, 4CLL9 and GT1 in P. subaequalis, although direct confirmation awaits future functional assays. More importantly, future studies should investigate how these genes influence anthocyanin accumulation and confer tolerance to strong light stress in P. subaequalis.

Dynamic coordination of metabolites and gene regulation during leaf red fading in P. subaequalis

Conclusion

This study assembled the first chromosome-scale genome of the Tertiary relict species Parrotia subaequalis (Contig N50 = 2.15 Mb), elucidating its adaptive evolution through paleopolyploidization events, species-specific chromosomal rearrangements, and dynamic gene family evolution, notably the expansion of secondary metabolism genes (e.g., WD40) and contraction of flower morphogenesis genes (e.g., F-box-like). Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis deciphered the molecular basis of its ornamental leaf color transition, demonstrating that temporal accumulation differences of the malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside may drive young leaf red-to-green shifts. Crucially, WD40 neofunctionalization participated in adaptive coexpression networks coordinating anthocyanin synthesis, transport, and photoprotective plasticity via chromatin remodeling, auxin signaling, and hydrolase activity. This genomic resource advances understanding of early angiosperm adaptation and establishes a foundation for molecular breeding targeting environmental resilience and ornamental trait enhancement.
