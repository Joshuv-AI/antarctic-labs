Formalizing Galaxy Population Evolution: Drift and Mergers as Transport Processes on Manifolds
Tsutomu T. Takeuchi

Abstract
Galaxy evolution is commonly described through the time evolution of observational statistics such as luminosity functions and stellar mass functions. However, these quantities are projections of an underlying multivariate galaxy state space rather than fundamental dynamical variables. We develop a unified framework in which galaxy evolution is formulated as the time evolution of a probability measure on the galaxy manifold. Representing galaxy states by latent variables θ ∈ ℳ \theta\in\mathcal{M} and the population by a density ρ ⁡ ( θ , t ) \rho(\theta,t) , the evolution is governed by a general equation containing continuous transport and nonlocal jump processes. By reinterpreting manifold learning as the pushforward of measures, we distinguish observational, representation, and physical measures, and emphasize that manifold coordinates themselves need not carry direct physical meaning. In this picture, luminosity functions and stellar mass functions arise as projected observables of a single underlying dynamics, and generally do not form closed equations in observational space. The framework contains existing models as limiting cases: reduction to a single mass variable yields continuity-equation models, while additive post-merger states recover the Smoluchowski coagulation equation. We further show that luminosity-function evolution is naturally described within the Schechter family, whose apparent stability is interpreted as an effective consequence of projection. Since observables are projections of measures, inference of galaxy evolution becomes a statistical inverse problem of recovering manifold dynamics from data. This framework shifts the focus from fitting observed statistics directly to inferring the underlying state-space dynamics, thereby bridging manifold learning and physical theory.

Introduction

A fundamental challenge in galaxy formation theory lies in identifying the essential state variables that govern galaxy evolution and, more crucially, the physical laws dictating their trajectories.
Modern surveys characterize galaxies through an extensive suite of observables, ranging from stellar mass and luminosity to chemical abundances and gas fractions.
While these parameters offer a multifaceted view, their high dimensionality often obscures the underlying physical simplicity. Observations reveal galaxies not as a uniform filling of multivariate space, but as structured point clouds with tight mutual dependencies. Such correlations imply that the effective state space is constrained to a lower-dimensional manifold,
\mathcal{M}
.
While representation learning now provides the numerical tools to extract this geometric structure, a deeper theoretical question remains:
what fundamental evolution equation governs the dynamics of galaxy populations upon this inferred manifold?

The objective of this study is to characterize galaxy populations as probability densities on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, formulating their temporal evolution through a stochastic framework that accounts for both continuous internal development and discrete merger events. By doing so, we bridge several disparate theoretical pillars within a unified mathematical architecture.
The first is the
hierarchical paradigm
, where galaxy statistics are fundamentally rooted in the gravitational growth of dark matter halos.
The second is the
kinetic theory of mergers
, which utilizes coagulation equations to explain the emergence of mass and luminosity functions from nonlinear interactions. The third is the
phenomenological continuity approach
, often employed to decouple the observational impacts of star formation, quenching, and assembly.
Our aim is to transcend these individual perspectives by deriving a single, self-consistent evolution equation defined directly on the galaxy manifold.

The foundational premise of galaxy formation theory resides in the gravitational amplification of primordial density fluctuations, which dictates the emergence of dark matter halos.
A seminal early synthesis was provided by
[
6
]
, who integrated primordial instabilities, galaxy clustering, and the mapping between multiplicity and luminosity functions into a singular conceptual framework.
In this classical view, the statistical properties of galaxy populations are treated primarily as a direct mapping of initial conditions, with mass and luminosity functions appearing as subsequent deterministic transformations.
This lineage was further solidified by
[
11
]
and
[
1
]
, whose development of the Press–Schechter formalism and its extensions provided the rigorous basis for describing hierarchical halo growth.
Their work established the theoretical bedrock for merger trees and semi-analytic models, framing galaxy assembly as a structured history derived from the underlying cosmic web.

A representative expansion of this paradigm into the realm of baryonic physics is the semi-analytic formulation by
[
20
]
.
Their work synthesized halo assembly, radiative gas cooling, disk formation, and feedback mechanisms into a comprehensive modeling pipeline. In this architecture, galaxies evolve as baryonic components within hierarchically merging dark matter halos, where gas cools to form stars and galaxies coalesce following the mergers of their host halos.
However, these semi-analytic models were primarily structured as a modular assembly of subgrid prescriptions for disparate physical processes, rather than a singular, self-consistent statistical equation governing the global distribution of the galaxy population.

Parallel to the hierarchical lineage, another theoretical tradition has focused on the nonlinear stage of galaxy evolution itself, proposing that mergers are the primary drivers in shaping mass and luminosity functions.
Early foundations were laid by
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
, who investigated collisions among protogalaxies within nascent clusters. These studies argued that such dynamical processes fundamentally influence not only mass and luminosity functions but also observable properties such as colors, metallicities, and the enrichment of the intracluster medium. They described the evolution of the number density
N(M,t)
through a Smoluchowski-type coagulation equation with gain and loss terms
[
15
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial N(M,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M^{\prime},M-M^{\prime}}N(M^{\prime},t)N(M-M^{\prime},t)\,{\rm d}M^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad-N(M,t)\int\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M,M^{\prime}}N(M^{\prime},t)\,{\rm d}M^{\prime}.

(1.1)

Their analysis demonstrated that sufficiently evolved merger systems naturally generate mass distributions characterized by a low-mass power law and a high-mass exponential cutoff,
N(M)\propto M^{-1}\exp(-M/M_{\ast})
. Assuming a mass–luminosity relation
L\propto M^{\gamma}
, they further showed that luminosity functions close to the Schechter form emerge as a robust consequence of this nonlinear kinetics. In this picture, the morphology of the galaxy distribution is dictated less by primordial initial conditions than by the self-organizing nature of the merger process itself.

[
13
]
advanced this kinetic paradigm by formalizing galaxy mergers within an expanding cosmological background. A critical step in their derivation was the decomposition of the coagulation kernel,
a_{c}(m,m^{\prime},t)=\psi(m,m^{\prime})\,f(t)
, which separates the interaction probability into intrinsic mass-dependent and extrinsic time-dependent components. Under the scaling law
\psi(km,km^{\prime})=k^{\nu}\psi(m,m^{\prime})
, they obtained the self-similar solution:

\displaystyle n(m,\tau)=m_{\ast}(\tau)^{-2}\Phi\!\left(\frac{m}{m_{\ast}(\tau)}\right).

(1.2)

This result provided a profound physical insight: the functional form
\Phi
of the mass distribution is dictated not by primordial initial conditions, but by the scaling symmetries of the merger kernel itself. This kinetic perspective offered an alternative, dynamical origin for the Schechter-type distributions observed in galaxy populations
[
12
]
, framing them as emergent statistical equilibria of the merger process.

Despite their conceptual elegance, these classical coagulation models were characterized by several fundamental limitations. First, the state representation was typically restricted to a single scalar quantity, such as mass or luminosity, thereby ignoring the multi-faceted nature of galaxy evolution. Second, post-merger properties were treated deterministically, lacking a probabilistic description of the diverse multivariate attributes—including morphology, star-formation rates, and chemical abundances—that emerge from complex assembly histories. Third, the projection from internal states to observable distributions was often left implicit, failing to formalize how high-dimensional evolutionary tracks manifest as specific scaling relations. A comprehensive framework capable of integrating these multi-dimensional and stochastic elements would only begin to emerge in subsequent decades.

Simultaneously, the hierarchical paradigm had to be reconciled with the observed pattern of
downsizing
, where massive galaxies appear to complete their star formation earlier than their lower-mass counterparts. While seemingly at odds with bottom-up growth,
[
9
]
demonstrated that this behavior can emerge from the interplay between halo accretion and star-formation efficiency. By coupling halo growth histories,
\dot{M}_{\rm h}=\dot{M}_{\rm acc}(M_{\rm h},t)
, with a mass-dependent efficiency
\epsilon(M_{\rm h},t)
, the star-formation rate can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\ast}(t)=\epsilon(M_{\rm h},t)\dot{M}_{\rm acc}(M_{\rm h},t).

(1.3)

This formulation shows that the observed temporal shift in star formation is not a contradiction of hierarchy, but a direct consequence of the nonlinear coupling between mass accretion and gas-processing efficiency.

Beyond deterministic growth, the complexity of hierarchical assembly was further clarified by
[
8
]
, who scrutinized halo merger rates within the extended Press–Schechter framework. They demonstrated that mergers in a continuous time limit frequently involve multiple progenitors, rendering the conventional binary approximation insufficient. This implies that the assembly history of galaxies is more rigorously described as a
jump process
in state space, where multiple discrete events can simultaneously reshape the population distribution.

Complementary to this discrete view is the tradition of modeling galaxy evolution through population-level continuity equations. A seminal contribution by
[
5
]
formalized the evolution of the stellar mass function
\phi(M_{\ast},t)
by invoking the principle of number conservation. In this framework, star formation acts as a smooth advective flow, shifting galaxies toward higher masses without altering their total count:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm SF}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{\ast}}\left(\phi\dot{M}_{\ast}\right).

(1.4)

Any residual deviation from this flow can then be isolated as the net contribution from non-conservative processes, such as mergers or quenching:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)_{\rm merge}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{\ast}}\left(\phi\dot{M}_{\ast}\right).

(1.5)

The profound conceptual shift here is the reinterpretation of mass-function evolution not as an empirical sequence of fits, but as
flux conservation in state space
.

This continuity-equation approach reached a high degree of phenomenological success in the work of
[
10
]
, who formalized the evolution of mass functions by incorporating mass-dependent and environmental quenching. Their dynamics, expressed as
\partial_{t}\phi+\partial_{M}(\dot{M}\phi)=S-Q
, provided a dynamical explanation for the stability of galaxy distributions, linking the star-forming and passive populations through a single, consistent evolution equation. However, these frameworks remain limited by their reliance on a one-dimensional state variable,
M_{\ast}
. In reality, galaxies of identical mass exhibit vast diversity in star-formation rates, chemical abundances, and morphologies—the very correlations that encode the underlying physics of galaxy formation. Static observational classifications, such as positions on a color–magnitude diagram, fail to capture the disparate timescales of the latent evolution that lead to those states.

The natural progression, therefore, is to lift these continuity-equation approaches into a
higher-dimensional state space
. By defining the evolution directly upon the
effective low-dimensional manifold
extracted from data, we can finally bridge the gap between static observational configurations and the latent dynamical processes that drive them.

To operationalize this view, we introduce the concept of the
galaxy manifold
. Although the observable properties of galaxies are intrinsically multivariate, they do not populate the high-dimensional parameter space stochastically. The existence of scaling relations such as the star-forming main sequence suggests that galaxy populations are constrained to low-dimensional structures. This motivates a description of galaxy states as points
\theta
on an effective manifold
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\theta\in\mathcal{M}.

(1.6)

Here,
\mathcal{M}
represents either a theoretically postulated state space or an objective latent space extracted via manifold learning techniques, such as diffusion maps or autoencoders.

Crucially, manifold learning identifies where galaxies are located, but it does not dictate how they move. The central objective of this work is to define a population density
\rho(\theta,t)
on
\mathcal{M}
and derive its governing dynamics. Galaxy evolution encompasses both continuous trajectories—driven by star formation, gas flows, and chemical enrichment—and discrete transitions, most notably galaxy mergers. The natural evolution of the population is thus described by the following master equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho(\theta,t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\bigl(\rho(\theta,t)\bm{v}(\theta,t)\bigr)+S(\theta,t)-L(\theta,t)+J[\rho](\theta,t),

(1.7)

where
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
represents the velocity field on the manifold,
S
and
L
denote source and loss terms, and
J[\rho]
is a nonlocal jump operator characterizing mergers and assembly events.

Equation (
1.7
) provides a unified synthesis that recovers existing theories as specific limiting cases. In the one-dimensional scalar limit, it reduces to the continuity equations of
[
5
]
and
[
10
]
.
When mergers are treated as mass-additive events, the system recovers classical Smoluchowski kinetics.
Furthermore, the diverse subgrid prescriptions of semi-analytic models can be interpreted as specific physical realizations of the transport and source terms within this geometric framework.

The connection to observational statistics is inherently rigorous in this formulation.
If properties such as luminosity
L(\theta)
are defined as functions on the manifold, the observed luminosity functions emerge as direct projections of the density
\rho
:

\displaystyle\phi(L,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}(L-L(\theta))\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(1.8)

Consequently, disparate phenomena, including downsizing, environmental quenching, and merger-driven mass growth, can all be reinterpreted as projected manifestations of a singular evolution equation defined upon the galaxy manifold.

From this perspective, the disparate historical approaches to galaxy evolution converge on a singular objective: to describe the temporal evolution of galaxy populations. The progression is evident—from primordial fluctuation theories and kinetic coagulation models to semi-analytic pipelines and mass-function continuity equations. Building upon these foundations, the present work seeks to extract their common core, unifying them as a stochastic dynamics defined directly on the galaxy manifold.

The primary contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we represent galaxy states upon the effective low-dimensional geometry of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Second, we formalize galaxy evolution through a master equation that integrates continuous transport with nonlocal jumps. Third, we demonstrate that observational statistics, such as luminosity functions and downsizing, emerge naturally as projected manifestations of this singular underlying dynamics.

Observational studies of galaxy evolution have traditionally proceeded by statistically comparing multivariate observables, including multiwavelength photometric quantities, spectroscopic properties, morphological indicators, and environmental measures.
With the development of large-scale surveys, it has become standard to treat galaxies as high-dimensional data points.
However, these observables are merely indicators that manifest as a result of evolution; they do not directly provide the intrinsic physical state of a galaxy.
Consequently, simply tracking the temporal variation of statistics within the observational space is often insufficient for achieving a dynamical understanding of galaxy evolution.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce a framework in which galaxy evolution is formulated as a stochastic process on a latent state space.
We refer to this latent space as the galaxy manifold

A schematic overview of the framework is shown in Fig.

In the following, we describe the relationship between the observational space and the latent state space, the probabilistic representation of galaxy populations, and their governing dynamics.

We first represent galaxy observables in general as:

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in X\subset\mathbb{R}^{D},

(2.1)

where
\bm{x}
is a high-dimensional vector composed of luminosities, colors, spectral indicators, morphological quantities, and environmental densities.
By contrast, the fundamental physical state of a galaxy is expected to be characterized by a relatively small number of effective degrees of freedom, such as the assembly history of stellar mass and the enrichment of gas. From this perspective, we assume that the state of a galaxy can be described by a low-dimensional latent variable:

\displaystyle\theta\in\mathcal{M},

(2.2)

where
\mathcal{M}
is a low-dimensional manifold satisfying
\dim\mathcal{M}=m\ll D
.
The relationship between the latent state and the observable data is given by a mapping:

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\Phi(\theta).

(2.3)

That is, the observational space
X
is understood as the image of the galaxy manifold
\mathcal{M}
under the map
\Phi
.
We assume that the galaxy manifold
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with a Riemannian metric
g
.
The corresponding volume element is denoted by
{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}=\sqrt{\det g(\theta)}\,{\rm d}\theta^{1}\cdots{\rm d}\theta^{m}.

(2.4)

This structure provides a geometric expression for the idea that a vast diversity of observables is generated from a compact set of underlying physical degrees of freedom.

Next, we describe the galaxy population by a probability density

\rho(\theta,t)
with respect to
{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}
on the manifold.
This density represents the distribution of physical states within the population at time
t
.
Because our access to the latent state is mediated by observations, the observed distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}(\bm{x},t)
is obtained by accounting for selection effects and measurement uncertainties:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{obs}}(\bm{x},t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}W(\bm{x},t)\,p(\bm{x}|\theta,t)\,\rho(\theta,t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(2.5)

where
W(\bm{x},t)
is the selection function and
p(\bm{x}|\theta,t)
is the observational error model.
Thus, observables are understood as projections of the latent-state distribution.
This separation is conceptualized to distinguish structurally between the observation process and the physical state, allowing for a more rigorous treatment of the “true” evolution of the population.

When galaxy evolution is governed by continuous processes, such as smooth star formation and secular gas evolution, the density distribution obeys a conservation-type equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho(\theta,t)}{\partial t}+\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\bigl(\rho(\theta,t)\bm{v}(\theta,t)\bigr)=S(\theta,t)-L(\theta,t),

(2.6)

where
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
is the evolutionary velocity field on the state space.
In this equation, the divergence term represents the transport of the galaxy population along the evolutionary tracks on the manifold. The terms
S
and
L
represent the source and loss terms, respectively, which correspond to the creation of new galaxies (e.g., through birth or the onset of visibility) and their removal from the specific state space.
This formulation demonstrates that the time evolution of galaxy populations can be fundamentally described as a transport process on the manifold, providing a unified mathematical basis for disparate evolutionary phenomena.

Furthermore, if an observable quantity
y=y(\theta)
is given, its distribution
\Psi(y,t)
is expressed by projecting the manifold density through the corresponding mapping:

\displaystyle\phi(y,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(y-y(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(2.7)

In this way, the evolution of observational statistics is obtained as the pushforward of the density evolution from the latent manifold onto the observable axis.
Accordingly, the evolution of luminosity functions, mass functions, and other scaling relations can be understood in a unified manner as the projected consequences of the transport and transformation of the galaxy population within the latent state space
\mathcal{M}
.

3
Manifold Learning as Measure Pushforward

In Section
2
, we established the viewpoint that galaxy populations are described as probability distributions on a latent state space
\mathcal{M}
, where observables manifest as projections of those distributions.
In the present section, we clarify how the low-dimensional representation obtained by applying manifold learning to observational data should be interpreted within this framework.
From the standpoint of this work, the output of manifold learning is not the “true state space” of galaxies itself, but rather a representation obtained by mapping the observational distribution into a lower-dimensional space.
To make this distinction explicit and mathematically rigorous, we formulate manifold learning as the pushforward of measures.
A more detailed measure-theoretic treatment is deferred to
[
19
]
; here, we restrict our discussion to the structural elements required for the present paper.

We begin by representing a galaxy sample as a finite point set in the observational space
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
:

\displaystyle X_{N}=\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}\}\subset X.

(3.1)

The corresponding empirical measure is written as:

\displaystyle\mu_{N}({\rm d}\bm{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})\,{\rm d}\bm{x},

(3.2)

where
\mu_{N}
represents the discrete distribution of the sample in the
D
-dimensional observational space. In the continuum limit, the associated observational distribution is represented by a measure
\mu
. This transition from discrete samples to a continuous measure allows us to treat the galaxy population as a collective entity rather than a collection of independent points.

Manifold learning may then be understood as providing a low-dimensional representation through a coordinate mapping:

\displaystyle\bm{z}=\Psi(\bm{x}),\qquad\Psi:X\to Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},

(3.3)

where
Z
is a representation space of dimension
m
. Under this map, the observational measure
\mu
is carried to a measure
\nu
on
Z
via the pushforward operation:

\displaystyle\nu=\Psi_{\#}\mu.

(3.4)

For finite samples, one similarly obtains the discrete representation
\nu_{N}=\Psi_{\#}\mu_{N}
. Thus, the output of manifold learning is not only the point cloud
\{\bm{z}_{i}\}
in the embedding space, but also, implicitly, the measure
\nu
obtained as the pushforward of the original observational distribution.

When a density representation exists, the pushforward can be explicitly written as:

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{z})=\int_{X}\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(\bm{z}-\Psi(\bm{x})\bigr)\,\mu({\rm d}\bm{x}).

(3.5)

This expression makes it clear that manifold learning is fundamentally an operation that maps the observational distribution into another space, rather than directly constructing the underlying physical state space itself.

A key point in this formulation is that
\Phi
is generally noninvertible. Typically, the representation space has a much lower dimension than the original observational space, such that
\dim Z=m\ll D=\dim X
. Therefore, the representation obtained by manifold learning does not preserve all information present in the observational space; instead, it compresses the data. In this sense,
\nu=\Psi_{\#}\mu
is a low-dimensional summary of the observational distribution
\mu
, rather than a complete encoding of all details within
X
. This information loss is also reflected in the change-of-variables identity:

\displaystyle\int_{Z}f(\bm{z})\,\nu({\rm d}\bm{z})=\int_{X}f(\Psi(\bm{x}))\,\mu({\rm d}\bm{x})

(3.6)

for any measurable function
f
. Consequently, statistics defined on the representation space retain only those aspects of the observational distribution that can be expressed through the mapping
\Phi
. Accordingly, one cannot directly identify the structures found by manifold learning with the intrinsic physical state of galaxies.

From this viewpoint, the description of galaxy populations in the present framework consists of a hierarchical three-layer structure:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lcl}\text{observational measure}&:&\mu(\bm{x}),\\
\text{representation measure}&:&\nu(\bm{z})=\Psi_{\#}\mu,\\
\text{physical measure}&:&\rho(\theta).\end{array}

Here,
\mu
denotes the distribution in the raw observational space,
\nu
is the low-dimensional representation obtained through manifold learning, and
\rho
is the distribution on the theoretically introduced latent state space
\mathcal{M}
. By distinguishing these three layers, we can systematically analyze how observational biases and information compression affect our ability to infer the true physical dynamics of galaxy evolution.

An important point is that the representation measure
\nu
depends inherently on the map
\Phi
. In practice,
\Phi
is constructed through a chosen algorithm and its associated hyperparameters, so different representation measures
\nu
may be obtained even from the same observational data. Thus,
\nu
is only one possible representation of the observational distribution
\mu
, and is not itself a uniquely defined physical object. By contrast, the distribution
\rho(\theta)
on the latent state space is a theoretical object introduced to describe the dynamics of galaxy evolution; it does not depend on any specific representation map. For this reason, the present work formulates galaxy evolution not in terms of the data-driven representation
\nu
, but in terms of
\rho
as the fundamental dynamical variable.

Therefore, the role of manifold learning is not to determine the physical state of galaxies itself, but to organize the observational distribution and represent its structure in low dimensions. The role of theory, by contrast, is to describe how the distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
on the latent state space evolves in time. Keeping these two functions distinct provides a clear separation of roles between the representation extracted from data and the dynamical description of galaxy evolution. On this basis, the following sections formulate the evolution of probability densities on the galaxy manifold through equations containing both continuous transport and nonlocal jump processes.

4
Galaxy Mergers on the Galaxy Manifold: A Smoluchowski-Type Jump Process

From the discussion in the previous sections, galaxy evolution is described as the temporal evolution of a probability density
\rho(\theta,t)
on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Internal evolution, such as star formation, gas inflow, feedback, and chemical enrichment, is represented as continuous transport on the state space. However, galaxy evolution also contains processes that cannot be expressed solely by such continuous deformations. The representative example of this is galaxy mergers. In a merger, two galaxies interact, the original pair disappears, and a new single system appears in a significantly different state. Accordingly, this process should be described not as a local flow on state space, but as a nonlocal jump from a two-body state
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
to a product state
\theta
.

With this in mind, the fundamental equation of galaxy evolution takes the form of continuous transport combined with nonlocal jumps:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho(\theta,t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\bigl(\rho(\theta,t)\bm{v}(\theta,t)\bigr)+S(\theta,t)-L(\theta,t)+J[\rho](\theta,t).

(4.1)

In this master equation, the first term represents the continuous transport on the manifold, while
S
and
L
are source and sink terms corresponding to the creation and removal of objects. The term
J[\rho]
is the jump term describing mergers. In what follows, by making the structure of
J[\rho]
explicit, we position galaxy mergers as a Smoluchowski-type process occurring on the galaxy manifold.

The jump term
J[\rho]
is expressed as the net balance between the gain from mergers supplying new galaxies into state
\theta
, and the loss of galaxies originally in state
\theta
through mergers with others. Its most natural form is:

\displaystyle J[\rho](\theta,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}K(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\to\theta;t)\rho(\theta_{1},t)\rho(\theta_{2},t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}\,1}\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}\,2}

\displaystyle\quad-\rho(\theta,t)\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta^{\prime},t)\kappa(\theta,\theta^{\prime};t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},

(4.2)

where the first term is the gain term, representing the rate at which parent systems in states
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
merge to produce an object appearing near state
\theta
. The second term is the loss term, representing the rate at which galaxies initially in state
\theta
disappear by merging with any partner
\theta^{\prime}
. Formulated in this manner, it becomes clear that mergers are not local deformations but nonlocal transitions between distinct regions of the state space.

Here,
K(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\to\theta;t)
is the production kernel, representing the rate density at which two galaxies merge and produce an object in state
\theta
. By contrast,
\kappa(\theta,\theta^{\prime};t)
appearing in the loss term is the total rate at which a galaxy in state
\theta
is removed through a merger with a galaxy in state
\theta^{\prime}
. The two are related by the following consistency relation:

\displaystyle\kappa(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\to\vartheta;t)\,{\rm d}\vartheta.

(4.3)

This identity indicates that if one integrates over and discards the information about the final state of the product, what remains is the total rate at which the parent pair is removed from the system. This relation demonstrates that the gain and loss terms are not independent quantities, but rather two complementary aspects of the same physical merger process.

To make the physical meaning of the merger kernel
K
clearer, it is useful to decompose it as:

\displaystyle K(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\to\theta;t)=R(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)\,P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t).

(4.4)

In this representation,
R(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)
is the rate at which the parent pair merges per unit time, while
P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)
is the conditional distribution for the product state given that a merger has occurred. This decomposition conceptually separates the merger process into "how frequently it occurs" and "what outcome it produces."

This distinction is essential from the viewpoint of galaxy formation physics. The former quantity,
R
, is governed by dynamical factors such as galaxy number density, relative velocity distributions, dynamical friction, halo environment, and redshift. That is,
R
mainly encodes encounter dynamics and environmental dependencies. By contrast, the latter quantity,
P
, describes the nature of the resulting stellar system: how much gas dissipation or starburst activity occurs, and how the morphology is reorganized. Accordingly,
P
may be regarded as a transition distribution summarizing the internal baryonic physics following the merger. By writing
K=RP
, one can explicitly decouple these distinct physical processes. In practical modeling, this allows
R
to be specified from dynamical considerations while
P
is constructed from subgrid physics or empirical relations.

Furthermore, in realistic galaxy mergers, the product state is not necessarily uniquely determined by the coarse-grained parent states
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
alone. Additional degrees of freedom affect the remnant, such as orbital angular momentum, collision geometry, gas distribution, and unresolved star formation or feedback processes. To represent this inherent non-determinism within the coarse-grained manifold, we introduce a latent variable
u
and write:

\displaystyle\theta=T(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},u;t),

(4.5)

where
T
is a map that yields the product state from the parent states and the latent variable
u
. If
q(u\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)
denotes the conditional distribution of this latent variable, then:

\displaystyle P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}q(u\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)\delta_{\rm D}\Bigl(\theta-T(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},u;t)\Bigr)\,{\rm d}u.

(4.6)

This expression demonstrates that the product distribution
P
may be understood as the pushforward of a probability distribution on the latent variable space
\mathcal{U}
through the map
T
. Simultaneously, it interprets the finite spread of merger remnants not as mere random scatter, but as the coarse-grained consequence of unresolved, yet physically well-defined, degrees of freedom.

A particularly important point is that the state variable
\theta
is multivariate. In the present framework,
\theta
may encompass not only stellar mass
M_{\ast}
, but also gas mass, metallicity, dust mass, star formation rate, color, and morphological indicators. Therefore, galaxy mergers should be described not merely as simple mass addition, but as a complex redistribution of a multivariate state on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

For example, for stellar mass, one may adopt the simplest approximation:

\displaystyle M_{\ast}=M_{\ast,1}+M_{\ast,2}.

(4.7)

However, in reality, mergers compress gas, trigger starbursts, mix metals, and in some cases expel material from the system through outflows. Hence, the post-merger gas mass, metallicity, dust content, and star-forming activity are not determined by the simple sum of the parent values. Rather, these properties change in ways that reflect the specific chemical evolution and dissipative processes that occurred during the merger event. In this sense, chemical evolution is not an external add-on to the present theory, but content that should be naturally incorporated into the transition distribution
P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)
. Within this framework, a merger is interpreted simultaneously as a dynamical process and as a stochastic transition involving complex baryonic physics.

Another important feature of the merger equation is that it clearly distinguishes the galaxy number from conserved physical quantities. Since mergers are fundamentally
2\to 1
reactions, the total number of galaxies is generally not conserved
2
2

2

More generally, many-body mergers may also occur. That these can be effectively reduced to a sequence of two-body mergers is demonstrated in
[
19
]
.
. By contrast, mass or certain additive conserved quantities can be preserved by imposing how they are transferred across the merger. To formalize this point, consider for any weight function
w(\theta)
the integral:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}w(\theta)J[\rho](\theta,t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(4.8)

which gives the merger contribution to the time evolution of the corresponding moment of the distribution
\rho
. If
w
corresponds to a conserved quantity, and if the transition distribution
P
satisfies the following condition:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}w(\theta)P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}=w(\theta_{1})+w(\theta_{2}),

(4.9)

then the merger process preserves that specific moment.
This means that although individual galaxies decrease in number, the total physical quantity is faithfully transferred to the remnant. Thus, mergers are reactions that reduce particle number while simultaneously satisfying conservation laws in an appropriate physical sense.

Conversely, if one chooses
w(\theta)=1
, then the left-hand side of equation (
4.9
) is always unity, leading to:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}J[\rho](\theta,t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}<0.

(4.10)

Hence, the jump term monotonically decreases the total number of galaxies. In this way, the merger equation simultaneously expresses the “nonconservation of counts” and the "preservation of conserved quantities." This structure makes it clear that galaxy mergers are not merely processes of number reduction, but processes of redistribution and restructuring of physical quantities across the population.

Finally, let us verify that the present formulation contains the classical Smoluchowski equation as a special case. If the state variable is restricted to the mass
M
alone, and if the post-merger product is deterministically given by:

\displaystyle P(M\mid M_{1},M_{2})=\delta_{\rm D}\!\bigl(M-(M_{1}+M_{2})\bigr),

(4.11)

then the jump term in equation (
4.2
) reduces to:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\rho(M,t)}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{M}R(M_{1},M-M_{1})\rho(M_{1})\rho(M-M_{1})\,{\rm d}M_{1}-\rho(M)\int R(M,M^{\prime})\rho(M^{\prime})\,{\rm d}M^{\prime}.

(4.12)

This is precisely the classical Smoluchowski coagulation equation. Accordingly, the present formulation should not be viewed as rejecting existing coagulation theory, but as naturally extending it into the context of galaxy evolution with multivariate states and probabilistic remnant distributions.

We therefore arrive at a unified formulation in which galaxy mergers are described as nonlocal jump processes acting on probability measures on the galaxy manifold. The fundamental object of the theory is not the trajectory of each individual galaxy, but the time evolution of the collective distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
. The merger kernel
K
is the central operator governing this evolution, simultaneously encoding merger frequencies and remnant-state distributions. From this perspective, galaxy mergers are understood not as simple mass-addition events, but as restructuring processes of galaxy populations possessing multivariate internal states.

5
Parameter Estimation for Galaxy Evolution on the Galaxy Manifold

Having established the formal transport dynamics and merger kinetics on the galaxy manifold in the preceding sections, we now shift our focus to their observational consequences. While the evolution of the population density
\rho(\theta,t)
provides a complete description of galaxy assembly, it remains a latent representation. In this section, we demonstrate how this high-dimensional dynamics manifests as the empirical scaling relations and distribution functions familiar to observational astronomy. By projecting the manifold density onto specific physical axes, such as stellar mass or luminosity, we recover the classical evolution equations while providing a new geometric basis for phenomena such as downsizing and the stability of the Schechter function.

In the present framework, the fundamental variable of galaxy evolution is not the observable itself, but the probability density
\rho(\theta,t)
on the galaxy manifold
\mathcal{M}
. In actual data analysis, however, one first constructs a low-dimensional representation from galaxy samples in observational space, thereby obtaining a coordinate system for the manifold. Accordingly, we assume in this section that an effective manifold representation of the galaxy population has already been obtained and consider the problem of inferring the dynamical parameters: the velocity field
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
, the source and sink terms
S(\theta,t)
and
L(\theta,t)
, and the merger kernel
K(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\to\theta;t)
. The evolution equations for observables arise only as projections of these manifold dynamics rather than constituting fundamental equations themselves.

What is directly measured observationally is not the latent state
\theta
, but observables such as luminosity, color, mass, and spectral indices. To bridge theory with observations, one must explicitly specify the operation that projects the manifold density into observable space. Let a general observable be defined as
y=y(\theta)
. The distribution of
y
is then given by:

\displaystyle\phi(y,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(y-y(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(5.1)

where
\phi(y,t)
is obtained by pushing forward the density distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
into observational space through the map
y(\theta)
. Equivalently, the observational statistic
\phi(y,t)
is the result of integrating the manifold density along the level sets of the observable
y(\theta)
.

A crucial point is that the projected distribution
\phi(y,t)
generally does not possess closed dynamics by itself. That is, the time evolution of an observational statistic is not determined entirely within observational space; it is governed by the evolution of the underlying distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
on the manifold. The essence of the inference problem is therefore not to model
\phi
directly, but to identify the dynamical parameters on the manifold that generate it.

Let the evolution equation on the manifold be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=T[\rho]+J[\rho],

(5.2)

where
T[\rho]
is a linear operator containing continuous transport and source and sink terms, and
J[\rho]
is the nonlinear jump operator corresponding to mergers. Differentiating equation (
5.1
) with respect to time yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(y,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\partial\rho(\theta,t)}{\partial t}\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(y-y(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(5.3)

Substituting equation (
5.2
) into this expression gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(y,t)}{\partial t}=T_{y}[\rho]+J_{y}[\rho],

(5.4)

where the projected operators are defined as:

\displaystyle T_{y}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}T[\rho](\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(y-y(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(5.5)

\displaystyle J_{y}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}J[\rho](\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(y-y(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(5.6)

Formally, this appears to be an effective evolution equation in observational space. However, these terms cannot, in general, be written as functions of
\phi
alone. In other words, the projected evolution equation is not closed in the space of observables. This non-closure is the decisive difference between conventional approaches that directly model luminosity or mass functions and the present viewpoint, where one infers the underlying manifold dynamics.

This structure is a general consequence independent of the specific observable and applies to various statistics such as color distributions or spectral indices. For instance, considering multiple observables simultaneously,
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, their joint distribution is:

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{y},t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(\bm{y}-\bm{y}(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(5.7)

where
\delta_{\rm D}
denotes the multivariate delta function. Increasing the number of observables corresponds to increasing the information available about the manifold dynamics. Conversely, with only a single observable, identification of the underlying dynamics is intrinsically incomplete due to information loss under projection.

In realistic observations, measurement errors and selection effects are also present. If an observed quantity
y_{\mathrm{obs}}
is obtained through a probabilistic map
p(y_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid y(\theta))
, the observed distribution is:

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}(y_{\rm obs},t)=W_{\mathrm{sel}}(y_{\rm obs},t)\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\,p(y_{\rm obs}\mid y(\theta))\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(5.8)

where
W_{\mathrm{sel}}(y_{\rm obs},t)
is the selection function. Therefore, inference from real data must be formulated as a forward model connecting the manifold dynamical parameters
\{\bm{v},S,L,K\}
, the observational map
y(\theta)
, the conditional error model
p(y_{\rm obs}\mid y(\theta))
, and the selection function
W_{\mathrm{sel}}
.

5.1
Projection of the Manifold Evolution Equation: Luminosity-Function Evolution and Downsizing

The most significant application of the general framework presented in Section
5
is the evolution of the luminosity function.
By introducing the luminosity mapping
L=L(\theta)
, which assigns a specific luminosity value to each latent state on the manifold, the luminosity function
\phi(L,t)
is defined as the pushforward of the density distribution from the galaxy manifold onto the observable
L
:

\displaystyle\phi(L,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(L-L(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(5.9)

Under this formulation, the luminosity function is understood as a specific image obtained by projecting the high-dimensional manifold dynamics onto the observable
L
.
It is a crucial conceptual point that
\phi(L,t)
is not an input of the theory, but rather a dynamical outcome of the evolution occurring on the manifold.

Let us first consider only the contribution from continuous transport.
We write the transport term on the manifold as
T[\rho]=-\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot(\rho v)+S-L
.
The temporal evolution of the luminosity function is given by the time derivative of equation (
5.9
):

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi(L,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\dfrac{\partial\rho(\theta,t)}{\partial t}\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(L-L(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(5.10)

Substituting the transport term and applying integration by parts, we obtain:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho v\cdot\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}L\,\delta_{\rm D}^{\prime}\bigl(L-L(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}+S_{L}-L_{L},

(5.11)

where
S_{L}
and
L_{L}
denote the projected source and sink terms in luminosity space. Now, we introduce the rate of luminosity change along the manifold flow,
\dot{L}(\theta,t)=v(\theta,t)\cdot\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}L(\theta)
, which describes how the manifold velocity field
v(\theta,t)
projects onto the luminosity direction.
By defining the conditional mean at fixed luminosity as
A(L,t)=\mathbb{E}[\dot{L}\mid L(\theta)=L]
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\dot{L}(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(L-L(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}=A(L,t)\phi(L,t).

(5.12)

Therefore, we arrive at the following transport equation:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial L}(A\phi)=S_{L}-L_{L}.

(5.13)

This equation formally resembles a continuity equation in luminosity space.
However, its coefficient
A(L,t)
is an effective quantity obtained by averaging the manifold velocity field
v(\theta,t)
over the luminosity shell
L(\theta)=L
.
Hence, even this apparently closed transport equation is in reality only a projected equation whose true dynamics reside on the manifold.

Next, we consider the contribution from galaxy mergers.
The jump term in luminosity space is defined as

\displaystyle J_{L}[\rho]=\int_{\mathcal{M}}J[\rho](\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}(L-L(\theta))\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(5.14)

Under the approximation that luminosity is approximately additive,
L\simeq L_{1}+L_{2}
, the gain term becomes:

\displaystyle J_{L,+}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}R_{L}(L_{1},L-L_{1})\phi(L_{1})\phi(L-L_{1})\,{\rm d}L_{1}.

(5.15)

This expression coincides with the Smoluchowski-type coagulation equation, in the form discussed by
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
in the context of luminosity-function evolution driven by galaxy mergers.
From the viewpoint of the present paper, however, this is not the fundamental theory.
Rather, it should be understood as an effective theory that emerges after projecting the jump operator
J[\rho]
on the galaxy manifold onto the single observable luminosity, combined with the additional approximation of luminosity additivity.

Accordingly, the evolution of the luminosity function including galaxy mergers is described by:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial(A\phi)}{\partial L}=S_{L}-L_{L}+J_{L}[\rho].

(5.16)

Each term on the right-hand side is an effective quantity obtained by projecting the dynamics on the manifold.
This means that, even if the luminosity-function evolution appears at first sight to be a classical population model, it in fact contains within it high-dimensional dynamics on the manifold, namely the velocity field, the source/sink terms, and the merger kernel.

This equation is also important in separating quantities that can be inferred from observational data from quantities that are defined only on the manifold.
What is directly obtained from observations is
\phi(L,t)
, and what is constrained from it are projected effective quantities such as
A(L,t)
,
S_{L}-L_{L}
, and
J_{L}[\rho]
.
By contrast, the underlying quantities
v(\theta,t)
,
S(\theta,t)
,
L(\theta,t)
, and
K(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\to\theta;t)
cannot be determined uniquely from a single luminosity function alone.
Therefore, the essence of the inference problem is not to fit the luminosity function directly, but to ask to what extent the dynamical parameters on the manifold can be constrained by using multiple observational statistics.

From this viewpoint, the meaning of the Schechter function becomes even clearer when reconsidered within the present framework.
From the ultraviolet to the near-infrared, the observed luminosity function of galaxies is often well approximated by the Schechter form:

\displaystyle\phi(L)=\phi_{*}\left(\frac{L}{L_{*}}\right)^{\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{L}{L_{*}}\right)\frac{{\rm d}L}{L_{*}}

(5.17)

In the present framework, this shape is understood as a structural property of the solutions of the luminosity-function evolution equation:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial(A\phi)}{\partial L}=S_{L}-L_{L}+J_{L}[\rho].

(5.18)

What must be emphasized here again, however, is that this equation is not closed in terms of the luminosity function alone.
The appearance and apparent invariance of the Schechter form are phenomena that emerge in observational space as a result of projecting the dynamics on the manifold, and do not constitute a first-principles description complete within luminosity space itself.

We next ask under what conditions the Schechter family forms an invariant family.
Let the Schechter luminosity function be written as:

\displaystyle\phi(L,t)=\frac{\phi_{*}(t)}{L_{*}(t)}\left(\frac{L}{L_{*}(t)}\right)^{\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{L}{L_{*}(t)}\right),

(5.19)

where
\phi_{*}(t)
is the normalization factor,
L_{*}(t)
is the cutoff luminosity, and
\alpha
is the faint-end slope.
If we also define
Q[\phi](L,t)=S_{L}(L,t)-L_{L}(L,t)+J_{L}[\rho](L,t)
, then the luminosity-function evolution equation can be written as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial\bigl(A(L,t)\phi(L,t)\bigr)}{\partial L}=Q[\phi](L,t).

(5.20)

Introducing
w=L/L_{*}(t)
, one has
\phi(L,t)=[\phi_{*}(t)/L_{*}(t)]w^{\alpha}e^{-w}
.
Taking the time derivative at fixed
L
then gives:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=\phi\left[\frac{\dot{\phi}_{*}}{\phi_{*}}-(1+\alpha)\frac{\dot{L}_{*}}{L_{*}}+w\frac{\dot{L}_{*}}{L_{*}}+\dot{\alpha}\ln w\right].

(5.21)

Furthermore, if the luminosity growth rate can be written as
A(L,t)=\gamma(t)L
, then:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial(A\phi)}{\partial L}=\dfrac{\partial(\gamma L\phi)}{\partial L}=\gamma\phi\bigl[(1+\alpha)-w\bigr].

(5.22)

Therefore, the left-hand side of equation (
5.20
) can be arranged as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial(A\phi)}{\partial L}=\phi\left[\frac{\dot{\phi}_{*}}{\phi_{*}}+(1+\alpha)\left(\gamma-\frac{\dot{L}_{*}}{L_{*}}\right)+w\left(\frac{\dot{L}_{*}}{L_{*}}-\gamma\right)+\dot{\alpha}\ln w\right].

(5.23)

For the Schechter family to be invariant, the right-hand side
Q[\phi]
must also close as a linear combination of
1
,
w
, and
\ln w
. The simplest and most important case is when:

\displaystyle Q[\phi](L,t)=\phi(L,t)\bigl[q_{0}(t)+q_{1}(t)w\bigr].

(5.24)

That is, the projected source/sink/merger terms are at most linear in the dimensionless luminosity
w=L/L_{*}
.
Comparing coefficients in equations (
5.23
) and (
5.24
), one finds that the
\ln w
term is absent on the right-hand side, and therefore
\dot{\alpha}=0
follows.
In other words, under this closed effective dynamics, the faint-end slope
\alpha
remains constant in time.
Comparing the constant and
w
terms further yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{L}_{*}}{L_{*}}=\gamma(t)+q_{1}(t),

(5.25)

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\phi}_{*}}{\phi_{*}}=q_{0}(t)+(1+\alpha)q_{1}(t).

(5.26)

Thus, under the assumption (
5.24
), the Schechter family (
5.19
) is an invariant family of the evolution equation, and the time evolution is reduced to the evolution of the two parameters
\phi_{*}(t)
and
L_{*}(t)
.

This structure is particularly important from the viewpoint of the inference problem.
The reason is that what is directly constrained by real data is generally not the velocity field
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
or the merger kernel
K
on the manifold itself, but rather the projected effective quantities
\gamma(t)
,
q_{0}(t)
, and
q_{1}(t)
.
The quantity
\gamma(t)
is the average luminosity growth rate on the manifold, reflecting continuous evolution through star formation and gas accretion, while
q_{1}(t)
controls the evolution of
L_{*}
, and
q_{0}(t)
represents the effective contribution of the total galaxy number density and source/sink terms.
Accordingly, fitting a Schechter function to observational data does not directly determine the detailed dynamics on the manifold, but instead constrains a small number of effective coefficients that appear as its projection.
In this sense, the evolution of the Schechter parameters is not a sufficient statistic of the manifold dynamics, but a coarse-grained summary of it.

Finally, these results make explicit the loss of information caused by projection.
In equation (
5.18
), the only directly observable quantity is
\phi(L,t)
, while
A(L,t)
,
S_{L}-L_{L}
, and
J_{L}[\rho]
cannot in general be separated uniquely.
Moreover, the quantities that appear in equations (
5.25
) and (
5.26
) are only the projected effective parameters
\gamma(t)
,
q_{0}(t)
, and
q_{1}(t)
.
The detailed velocity field
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
on the manifold, the structure of the source/sink terms, and the form of the merger kernel
K
behind them cannot be uniquely reconstructed from the luminosity function alone.
In other words, different manifold dynamics may produce the same evolution of
\phi_{*}(t)
,
L_{*}(t)
, and
\alpha
.
This nonuniqueness originates from the fact that the projection map
\rho(\theta,t)\to\phi(L,t)
is an intrinsically irreversible transformation that loses information.
Accordingly, the invariance of the Schechter family simultaneously explains the observational stability of luminosity function shapes and demonstrates the theoretical limitation that the underlying physics cannot be uniquely reconstructed from the luminosity function alone.
This strongly suggests the need for inverse problems that make simultaneous use of multiple observational statistics to break these degeneracies.

This nonuniqueness, arising from the effect of information compression under projection, is particularly evident in the concrete example of the luminosity function.
Indeed, observationally, galaxy luminosity functions across a wide wavelength range are well described by the Schechter form:

\displaystyle\phi(L)=\phi_{*}\left(\frac{L}{L_{*}}\right)^{\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{L}{L_{*}}\right)\frac{{\rm d}L}{L_{*}}.

(5.27)

In the framework of the present paper, this characteristic shape is interpreted not as a fundamental input, but as an effective description that emerges from projecting the manifold dynamics onto the single observable, luminosity.
As established above, the evolution of the luminosity function is governed by:

In this sense, the Schechter function should not be regarded as a fundamental law of galaxy evolution, but rather as an effective description that appears when the evolution equation on the manifold becomes self-closing within a particular observational space.

5.2
A Geometric Interpretation of the Downsizing Phenomenon

In the previous subsection, we established that the evolution of observables is described as the projection of dynamics on the galaxy manifold. This projection is generally not closed but can be represented through an effective equation. Specifically, the Schechter form of the luminosity function emerges as an effective description when the manifold evolution equation becomes approximately self-closing for that specific observable. From this viewpoint, another fundamental observational feature of galaxy evolution, namely, downsizing, should also be understood not as an a priori physical law, but as a geometric consequence of projecting manifold dynamics into observational space
3
3

3

The term “downsizing” is used in a wide variety of ways by different authors. Here we adopt it in its most basic sense.
.

Downsizing generally refers to the mass-dependent evolutionary pattern in which more massive galaxies appear to complete their star formation at earlier cosmic times, whereas lower-mass galaxies continue star formation over longer periods
[
3
,
10
, e.g.,]
. Traditionally, this phenomenon has been discussed as reflecting mass-dependent differences in physical processes such as feedback efficiency or gas accretion. In the present framework, however, it is described in a unified way as an intrinsic property of the transport field
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
on the galaxy manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

Evolution on the galaxy manifold is fundamentally given by the autonomous system:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}=\bm{v}(\theta,t),

(5.29)

where
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
is the velocity field on state space, representing the direction and rate at which the physical state of a galaxy changes. If we denote the stellar-mass direction by
M_{*}
, the mass growth rate of an individual galaxy is:

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{*}(\theta,t)=v_{M}(\theta,t).

(5.30)

To connect this manifold-level velocity field to the observed mass dependence, we introduce the mean transport velocity conditioned on mass:

\displaystyle A_{M}(M_{*},t)=\mathbb{E}\left[v_{M}(\theta,t)\mid M_{*}(\theta)=M_{*}\right].

(5.31)

This
A_{M}(M_{*},t)
is an effective quantity representing how rapidly a galaxy population at a given stellar mass
M_{*}
evolves on the manifold. After averaging over the complex histories of individual galaxies, it provides a typical evolutionary “speed” for each mass scale. Geometrically, this corresponds to the mean flow rate of the galaxy population along the manifold toward higher mass or different physical phases.

Within this geometric picture, downsizing is formulated as the mass dependence of this conditional transport velocity:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{*}}A_{M}(M_{*},t)>0.

(5.32)

In other words, the condition that more massive galaxies possess a larger mean evolutionary speed on the manifold is the direct mathematical origin of the observed temporal asymmetry in galaxy evolution. Massive galaxies traverse the manifold at higher velocities, reaching their final evolutionary states more quickly than their lower-mass counterparts.

It is important to emphasize that the “evolutionary speed” discussed here does not necessarily refer to the rate of change in apparent observables like color or morphology, but to the rate of change in the latent state variables. For instance, when projected onto the mass–star-formation-rate (SFR) plane, the dominant motion consists of secular mass growth along the star-forming main sequence, followed by the transition (quenching) to the passive population. Thus, saying that massive galaxies “evolve faster” means they build up their mass on shorter timescales and reach the quenching phase at earlier epochs. This perspective resolves the apparent paradox where massive galaxies appear “static” (red and dead) at the present epoch; their current inactivity is a natural consequence of having moved rapidly across the manifold in the past. Since the color–magnitude diagram involves color as a luminosity ratio, it can be an ambiguous indicator of evolutionary speed due to its complex projection effects; the mass–SFR plane provides a more direct representation for characterizing this transport.

Furthermore, to evaluate the duration of the star-forming phase, we consider the star-forming region
\Theta_{\rm SF}\subset\mathcal{M}
. The typical residence time within this region can be estimated by the kinematic relation:

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm SF}(M_{*})\sim\frac{\Delta\theta_{\rm SF}}{A_{M}(M_{*},t)},

(5.33)

where
\Delta\theta_{\rm SF}
represents the characteristic width or "distance" across the star-forming region on the manifold. Under the downsizing condition defined in equation (
5.32
), it follows that:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{*}}\tau_{\rm SF}(M_{*})<0.

(5.34)

This inequality implies that more massive galaxies traverse the star-forming phase in shorter physical timescales. This provides a clear, quantitative expression of downsizing as a kinematic consequence: the higher the velocity on the manifold, the shorter the stay in a particular evolutionary phase.

What is fundamentally important here is that the observed "mass-dependent evolutionary speed" is directly represented as a structural component of the manifold velocity field
\bm{v}(\theta,t)
. If the velocity component in the mass direction follows a scaling such as:

\displaystyle v_{M}(\theta,t)\propto M_{*}^{\beta},

(5.35)

then galaxies at higher mass scales naturally move faster through the latent state space. Consequently, massive systems exit the region of active star formation rapidly, while lower-mass galaxies remain as star-forming systems for extended periods. Accordingly, downsizing is interpreted not as a phenomenon tied to a specific observable, but as a geometric property of the transport field on the manifold. This interpretation is remarkably robust; because mass, luminosity, color, and star-formation rate are all different projections of the same underlying trajectories, the evolutionary asymmetry appears across various observational planes simultaneously as a manifestation of the manifold dynamics.

This geometric viewpoint is closely related to the influential empirical model proposed by
[
10
]
.
Peng et al.
[
10
]
demonstrated that the probability of star-forming galaxies being quenched is proportional to their star-formation rate:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm quench}\propto{\rm SFR}.

(5.36)

In our manifold representation, star formation is intrinsically tied to the transport velocity in the mass or luminosity direction.
Therefore, this empirical relation suggests that the quenching rate (the loss term) depends on the transport speed itself. In other words, galaxies that move rapidly across the manifold also cross the “boundary” out of the star-forming region more quickly.
In this sense, the empirical findings of Peng et al. can be understood as a special case of the present master equation, specifically where the transport term
T[\rho]
and the loss term
L[\rho]
are dynamically coupled through the velocity field.

Our approach also shares conceptual ground with previous theoretical efforts to treat galaxy population evolution through continuity equations. For instance, Neistein et al. proposed analytic frameworks where the time evolution of the mass function is described by one-dimensional continuity equations
[
9
,
8
]
.
While those models can describe mass function evolution once growth and merger rates are specified, the present framework distinguishes itself by extending the study from a single mass coordinate to the higher-dimensional latent manifold
\mathcal{M}
spanned by the full physical state of galaxies.
By doing so, the evolution of mass functions, luminosity functions, and color distributions are understood in a unified manner as multiple projections of a single transport process.

However, as emphasized in the previous subsection, the essential caveat remains: observables are merely projected quantities.
Downsizing appears as an effective phenomenon resulting from the projection of manifold dynamics, and one cannot uniquely determine the underlying evolution equation from it alone.
For example, the observed evolution of a characteristic mass or luminosity scale could originate from mass-dependent transport, mass-dependent source/sink terms, or the cumulative effect of mergers.
These distinct physical drivers cannot, in general, be distinguished by examining a single projected observable.

Therefore, even in understanding a phenomenon as ubiquitous as downsizing, the essential problem is not merely to describe the time evolution of observational statistics, but to determine how the latent manifold dynamics can be rigorously inferred.
Downsizing serves as both a natural consequence of our framework and a cautionary example of the problems of projection and non-identifiability.
In the following section, we address this challenge by developing the standpoint of an inverse problem, discussing how the individual components of the galaxy manifold evolution equation may be inferred from the collective structure of observational distributions.

6.1
Forward Operators and the Structure of the Inverse Problem

For a latent state
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
on the galaxy manifold, let a general observable be denoted by
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Here, the observational space
\mathcal{Y}
may represent a single quantity such as luminosity, a pair such as mass and color, or a high-dimensional vector collecting multiple statistics. To connect the latent state to the data, we define an observational kernel
\mathcal{K}(y\mid\theta,t)
that incorporates measurement errors, selection effects, and instrumental responses:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(y\mid\theta,t).

(6.1)

The forward map from the theoretical density distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
to the observed distribution
\Psi_{\mathrm{obs}}(y,t)
is then given by the integral:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{obs}}(y,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{K}(y\mid\theta,t)\rho(\theta,t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(6.2)

This expression defines a linear projection operator that maps the manifold density into a function on observational space. We denote this operator as:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathcal{P}_{t}[\rho].

(6.3)

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is generally time-dependent, reflecting the fact that survey conditions, depth, and instrumental characteristics often vary with cosmic time or redshift.

Recalling the evolution equation on the manifold (eq. (
5.2
))

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=T[\rho]+J[\rho],

(6.4)

we differentiate the projection in equation (
6.3
) with respect to time to obtain:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\Psi_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{P}_{t}[T[\rho]]+\mathcal{P}_{t}[J[\rho]]+\left(\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{P}_{t}}{\partial t}\right)[\rho].

(6.5)

The final term,
\left(\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{t}\right)[\rho]
, represents the explicit time dependence of the observational operator itself.
This term is non-zero when selection functions, filter transformations, or completeness corrections vary over the observed epoch. Physically, this implies that the observed evolution of a galaxy population is a
composite process
involving both the intrinsic physical dynamics on the manifold and the temporal evolution of the observation process itself.
Accounting for this distinction is essential when interpreting changes in observational statistics as genuine physical evolution.

Viewed as an inverse problem, the task is significantly more complex than reconstructing a static distribution.
In the present theory, the following set of quantities are simultaneously unknown:

\displaystyle\{\rho_{0}(\theta),\bm{v}(\theta,t),S(\theta,t),L(\theta,t),R(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t),P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t),\mathcal{K}(y\mid\theta,t)\}.

(6.6)

These represent the initial state distribution (
\rho_{0}
), the velocity field generating continuous transport (
\bm{v}
), the source and loss terms (
S,L
), the merger rate and transition kernel (
R,P
), and the observational map (
\mathcal{K}
).
Thus, the problem is one of
simultaneously inferring the dynamical system governing galaxy evolution and the mapping to observables
.

The high dimensionality of this unknown set is the fundamental source of the non-identifiability discussed in previous chapters.
From the time evolution of the projected distribution
\Psi_{\mathrm{obs}}(y,t)
alone, one cannot uniquely disentangle all components of equation (
6.6
).
Consequently, any practical implementation must employ one or more of the following strategies:

1.

Parametric or semi-parametric modeling of the manifold dynamics to reduce the degrees of freedom.

2.

The introduction of multiple, independent auxiliary observables to provide additional constraints.

3.

The use of regularization techniques to select physically plausible solutions among the infinite set of possible candidates.

Formulating this as an inverse problem is therefore not merely a numerical necessity, but a theoretical tool to delineate the limits of how much dynamical structure can be faithfully reconstructed from observational data.

6.2
Parameterization, Regularization, and Identifiability

The most direct implementation for solving the inverse problem is to introduce finite-dimensional parameterizations for the dynamical terms.
For instance, the velocity field can be expanded in a suitable set of basis functions as:

\displaystyle\bm{v}(\theta,t)=\sum_{a=1}^{N_{v}}c_{a}(t)\bm{\psi}_{a}(\theta),

(6.7)

where
\bm{\psi}_{a}(\theta)
represents a basis in the latent state space. Similarly, the net contribution of the source and loss terms may be expressed as:

\displaystyle S(\theta,t)-L(\theta,t)=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{s}}d_{b}(t)\chi_{b}(\theta).

(6.8)

The merger rate, which governs the frequency of discrete interactions, can also be expanded to capture environmental or temporal dependencies:

\displaystyle R(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{R}}r_{m}(t)\Psi_{m}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}).

(6.9)

On the other hand, since the transition distribution
P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)
must satisfy a strict normalization constraint, it is more natural to utilize the latent-variable representation:

\displaystyle P(\theta\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}q(u\mid\theta_{1},\theta_{2};t)\delta_{\rm D}\Bigl(\theta-T(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},u;t)\Bigr){\rm d}u,

(6.10)

and parameterize the mapping
T
or the distribution
q
, rather than expanding
P
directly.
This representation ensures that normalization and physical conservation laws, such as mass conservation, are preserved by construction while maintaining clear physical interpretability.
While such fully parametric approaches stabilize the inference, they also increase model dependence.
A semiparametric alternative, in which for example only
\bm{v}
and
R
are represented by low-dimensional parameters while
\rho(\theta,t)
itself is estimated nonparametrically, presents a compelling option for future implementations.

Inverse problems in galaxy evolution are generally ill-posed and become particularly unstable due to finite-sample fluctuations and selection effects.
It is therefore essential to introduce regularization terms into the objective function.
A typical form is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}+\lambda_{v}\mathcal{R}_{v}+\lambda_{R}\mathcal{R}_{R}+\lambda_{P}\mathcal{R}_{P},

(6.11)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}
quantifies the fit between the forward model and observational data, and the terms
\mathcal{R}_{v},\mathcal{R}_{R},\mathcal{R}_{P}
impose prior constraints.
These constraints typically enforce smoothness, symmetry, or locality, reflecting our physical expectations of the underlying manifold dynamics.

As demonstrated in Section 5, the contributions from transport, sources, and mergers cannot be uniquely disentangled from a single projected observable.
Integrating multiple observables is therefore essential in practical inference. Useful auxiliary statistics include:

1.

Joint distributions of distinct observables, such as mass and color.

2.

Conditional subdistributions segmented by environment or morphology.

3.

Close-pair counts or morphological disturbance indicators, which serve as direct proxies for mergers.

4.

Temporal distribution sequences across multiple redshift bins.

The combined objective function for such joint inference is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{main}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}+\text{regularization},

(6.12)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{main}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}
represent the goodness-of-fit for the primary and auxiliary observational distributions, respectively. By combining multiple projections in this way, dynamical components that are degenerate under a single observable become effectively identifiable.

Importantly, adding auxiliary observables does not necessarily require an expansion of the state space
\mathcal{M}
itself. In our framework, diverse observables such as luminosity, color, and SFR are often different projections of the latent state
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
.
In such cases, auxiliary data provide independent constraints on the same density distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
through different kernels:

\displaystyle\Psi_{a}(y_{a},t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{K}_{a}(y_{a}\mid\theta,t)\rho(\theta,t){\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(6.13)

This effectively provides a “triangulation” of the latent density, helping to resolve the degeneracies inherent in the inverse problem.

However, if an auxiliary observable involves degrees of freedom that the current state variable
\theta
cannot represent, the state space itself must be extended.
This necessity arises when history-dependent quantities, exogenous degrees of freedom like halo mass, or previously unmodeled internal variables are required.
In such cases, it becomes necessary to extend the state space as

\displaystyle\theta\to\tilde{\theta}=(\theta,\eta).

(6.14)

Thus, the role of auxiliary observables is twofold: they act as constraints to resolve degeneracies on a fixed manifold, and they serve as a diagnostic tool to test if the chosen state space is physically adequate.
In this sense, they provide a fundamental theoretical check on whether the description of the galaxy manifold
\mathcal{M}
is physically sufficient for describing the observed evolution.

6.3
Discretization and Connection to Observational Estimators

Furthermore, for practical implementation, it is necessary to discretize the manifold
\mathcal{M}
into a finite set of cells or basis functions.
By introducing representative points
\theta_{i}
, where
i=1,\dots,N
, and writing the discrete density as:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(t)\simeq\rho(\theta_{i},t),

(6.15)

the observational operator can be approximated by a matrix
\mathsf{P}(t)
, such that:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{obs},\alpha}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{P}_{\alpha i}(t)\rho_{i}(t).

(6.16)

Under this discretization, the evolution equation on the manifold takes the form of a system of ordinary differential equations:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=\sum_{j}T_{ij}(t)\rho_{j}+J_{i}(\bm{\rho},t),

(6.17)

where the merger term is expressed as a quadratic form:

\displaystyle J_{i}(\bm{\rho},t)=\sum_{j,k}K_{jk\to i}(t)\rho_{j}\rho_{k}-\rho_{i}\sum_{j}\kappa_{ij}(t)\rho_{j}.

(6.18)

At this stage, the problem of reconstructing galaxy evolution is reduced to a finite-dimensional, nonlinear inverse problem.
This matrix-based representation is particularly well-suited for numerical optimization and allows for the straightforward incorporation of the regularization terms discussed previously.

The general discussion above becomes more concrete when the luminosity function is taken as a primary example.
In this case, the observational space is
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and the observable is the luminosity
L
.
The corresponding forward map is:

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}(L,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{K}(L\mid\theta,t)\rho(\theta,t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}},

(6.19)

where
\mathcal{K}(L\mid\theta,t)
is the observational kernel combining the selection function and the measurement-error model for luminosity.

In actual data analysis, one does not directly obtain the theoretical quantity
\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}(L,t)
.
Instead, one works with an estimator such as a
1/V_{\max}
type estimator, a maximum-likelihood estimator, or a bias-corrected estimator
\hat{\phi}(L,t)

[
17
,
7
, see, e.g.,]
.
In the idealized large-sample limit, one may assume the convergence:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(L,t)\longrightarrow\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}(L,t).

(6.20)

Accordingly, the observed time differences or redshift dependencies of the estimator
\hat{\phi}
can be interpreted as approximate observables of the projected evolution equation.

Moreover, when a Schechter fit is performed, the estimated parameters
\hat{\phi}_{*}(t)
,
\hat{L}_{*}(t)
, and
\hat{\alpha}(t)
provide a low-dimensional summary of the observed luminosity function.
As demonstrated in Section
5
, their temporal evolution is understood as a combination of the projected effective coefficients
q_{0}
,
q_{1}
, and
\gamma
.
It is vital to reiterate, however, that these low-dimensional summaries alone do not allow one to separate the underlying dynamics uniquely.
This situation provides the most concrete example of nonidentifiability within the general inverse-problem framework.

Consequently, the luminosity function should be regarded as one representative example of the general inverse-problem structure developed in this chapter.
This same structure extends directly to mass functions, color distributions, environment-conditioned distributions, and the joint distributions of multiple observables.
Thus, the inverse problem formulated here provides a general implementation principle for the entire evolution equation on the galaxy manifold.
A concrete implementation example, for instance, using mock catalogs generated from semi-analytic models, will be presented in a forthcoming paper.

7
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we have constructed a theoretical framework in which galaxy evolution is formulated as a stochastic process on the galaxy manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
Within this framework, the physical state of a galaxy is represented by a latent variable
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
, and the statistical state of the galaxy population is described by a density distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
on the manifold.
By reinterpreting manifold learning as the pushforward of measures, we have clearly distinguished the observational measure
\mu
, the representation measure
\nu=\Psi_{\#}\mu
, and the physical measure
\rho
.
This positioning allows the theory to treat the time evolution of measures as its primary object.
From this standpoint, the manifold is understood not merely as a physical entity, but as a coordinate system for representing measures, and the essence of galaxy evolution is formulated as the dynamics of the measure distributed upon it.

Through this lens, galaxy evolution is described by the general equation:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho(\theta,t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\bigl(\rho(\theta,t)\bm{v}(\theta,t)\bigr)+S(\theta,t)-L(\theta,t)+J[\rho](\theta,t),

(7.1)

representing a combination of transport and jump processes on the manifold. Here,
\bm{v}
is the velocity field on the manifold,
S
and
L
are source and loss terms, and
J
represents nonlocal jump processes such as galaxy mergers.
This equation naturally encompasses existing theories of galaxy evolution.
For instance, when the state space is reduced to a single mass variable, it recovers continuity-equation models for mass growth by star formation.
In the limit where post-merger states obey an additive rule, it becomes equivalent to the Smoluchowski coagulation equation.
Accordingly, the equation developed in this work provides a unified basis for describing phenomena that have often been treated separately, including mass-function evolution, luminosity-function evolution, galaxy mergers, quenching, and downsizing, as different manifestations of a common dynamical structure.

The relationship to observables is expressed as a projection of the density distribution on the manifold.
Introducing the luminosity mapping
L=L(\theta)
, the luminosity function is given by:

\displaystyle\phi(L,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho(\theta,t)\delta_{\rm D}\bigl(L-L(\theta)\bigr)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(7.2)

As a result, observational statistics such as luminosity functions and mass functions are understood as projected phenomena derived from the evolution equation on the manifold.
In other words, observational statistics are not independent objects; instead, they are unified as quantities generated by observational maps acting on the measure
\rho
.
The explicit example of luminosity-function evolution presented in this work, as illustrated in Figure
2
, serves as a representative case of how observational statistics are generated within this framework.

At the same time, the projection-based viewpoint calls for an important reconsideration of observational interpretation.
Classifications that appear static on observational planes do not necessarily represent the dynamical speed in latent state space directly.
The downsizing phenomenon discussed in this work is a typical example: the fact that massive galaxies appear red and dead at the present epoch is not inconsistent with the fact that, in the past, they evolved more rapidly along evolutionary trajectories and reached the quenched population at earlier times.
Consequently, static configurations in observational diagrams must be distinguished from temporal evolution on the manifold.
This point demonstrates that the appearance of galaxy populations changes depending on the chosen observational projection, such as the color–-magnitude diagram, the stellar mass–star formation rate plane, or morphological classification diagrams.
It implies that a proper understanding of galaxy evolution requires an explicit treatment of the dynamics in latent state space.

Because projection loses information, it is generally impossible to reconstruct the manifold dynamics uniquely from observational statistics alone.
This irreversibility arises naturally once manifold learning is understood as a pushforward of measures. In this work, we formulated this issue explicitly as an inverse problem and connected theoretical quantities with observational estimators through a forward model including an observational kernel:

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}(y,t)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{K}(y\mid\theta,t)\rho(\theta,t)\,{\rm d}V_{\mathcal{M}}.

(7.3)

Accordingly, the central task of galaxy evolution studies shifts from reproducing the time evolution of individual statistics one by one to determining how far the manifold dynamics that generate them simultaneously can be identified.
Here, the representation
\nu
obtained by manifold learning plays an important role as an intermediate representation connecting observations and theory.

The principal contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
First, we formulated galaxy evolution as a stochastic dynamics on the manifold and introduced a unified equation that incorporates both continuous transport and discrete jump processes.
Second, by reinterpreting manifold learning through the pushforward of measures, we clarified the three-layer structure consisting of the observational measure
\mu
, the representation measure
\nu
, and the physical measure
\rho
.
Third, we expressed galaxy mergers as general jump kernels and systematized their relationship to observational statistics using projection operators.

This framework provides a fundamental methodological reorganization of galaxy evolution studies.
While conventional studies have often modeled the time evolution of observational statistics, such as luminosity and mass functions, as separate entities, our formulation understands all such statistics as different projections of a single density distribution
\rho(\theta,t)
on the galaxy manifold.
Accordingly, the evolution of individual statistics is interpreted not as a set of independent phenomena, but as disparate observational images derived from a common underlying dynamics.
This viewpoint implies a significant methodological shift, moving from theories that explain the evolution of statistics to theories that infer the fundamental dynamics on the latent state space.

Several directions are identified for future work.
The first is the challenge of estimating the observational measure
\mu
and its pushforward
\nu
from data, which involves statistically identifying the density
\rho
on the manifold alongside the velocity field
\bm{v}
and the merger kernel
K
.
This can be formulated as a statistical inverse problem that integrates manifold learning with Bayesian inference and machine learning methods.
The second direction involves the physical modeling of the merger kernel
K
, where it will be necessary to derive concrete forms incorporating gravitational interactions, environmental dependencies, gas physics, and chemical enrichment.
The third is the connection with numerical simulations; by interpreting galaxy distributions from
N
-body simulations and semi-analytic models as measures, it becomes possible to infer the effective parameters of the present framework directly.
Furthermore, application to real observations, accounting for time-dependent selection effects and light-cone effects, remains a vital subject for future study.

More broadly, the framework presented here is not restricted to galaxy evolution, but can be generalized as a dynamical statistical theory for multivariate data.
The perspective of the time evolution of measures and their subsequent projections provides a universal structure linking data science and physical theory.
In this sense, the present work serves as both a new formulation of galaxy evolution theory and a step toward a general methodology for constructing physical theories directly from high-dimensional observational data.
