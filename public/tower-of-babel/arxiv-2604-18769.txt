The Isoperimetric Problem in Regular Trees
Marc Troyanov

Abstract
We investigate the inner vertex-isoperimetric problem on the d d -regular tree T d T_{d} . For each integer k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , we determine the exact value of the inner vertex-isoperimetric profile I d ( k ) = min { | ∂ D | ∣ D ⊂ T d finite and connected , | D | = k } . I_{d}(k)=\min\{|\partial D|\mid D\subset T_{d}\text{ finite and connected},\ |D|=k\}. We introduce a boundary invariant, called the boundary branching excess τ ⁡ ( D ) \tau(D) , and show that it provides a simple criterion for optimality. A domain D ⊂ T d D\subset T_{d} is isoperimetrically optimal if and only if τ ⁡ ( D ) ≤ d − 2 \tau(D)\leq d-2 . Finally, we show that every domain in T d T_{d} admits a canonical decomposition as an iterated gluing of full domains , namely domains whose entire boundary consists of leaves. This yields a complete description of all inner vertex-isoperimetric minimizers in T d T_{d} . Keywords: regular tree, isoperimetric profile, discrete isoperimetric inequalities, optimal domains, vertex boundary, Cheeger constant. MSC(2020): 05C05, 05C35, 05C10.



1
Introduction

The present paper addresses the inner vertex-isoperimetric problem on the regular tree
T_{d}
and provides an exact solution together with a complete structural description of optimal domains.
This investigation grew out of material developed during the PhD thesis of Bruno Luiz Santos Correia at EPFL
[
4
]
. The thesis contains initial results and observations on the inner isoperimetric problem on the regular tree, including a computation of the isoperimetric profile. In the present paper, we develop these ideas into a complete and systematically organized treatment.

1.1
Statement of the problem

The
isoperimetric problem
in a locally finite graph
X
addresses the following general question: among all domains
D\subset X
(that is, finite
connected sets of vertices) of fixed cardinality, which ones have the smallest possible boundary? This problem naturally splits into two parts:

(i)

What is the smallest possible size of the boundary
\partial D
of a
domain
D\subset X
of given cardinality?

(ii)

Describe all domains that are
optimal
, that is, those which attain this minimum.

Question (i) corresponds to determining the best possible isoperimetric inequality in
X
, encoded by the
isoperimetric profile

I_{X}(k)=\min\left\{\,|\partial D|\mid D\subset X\text{ finite and connected},\ |D|=k\,\right\}.

By definition, the isoperimetric inequality

|\partial D|\geq I_{X}(|D|)

holds for all domains
D\subset X
, and the isoperimetric profile is the largest function for which this inequality holds for all domains.

Question (ii) then consists in describing the structure of all domains
D

satisfying

|\partial D|=I_{X}(|D|).

In graph theory, one may consider the outer vertex boundary, the inner vertex boundary, or the edge boundary. In this article we focus on the inner vertex
boundary, for which the isoperimetric problem on the regular tree
T_{d}
is non-trivial; by contrast, the outer and edge boundaries lead to essentially
trivial profiles (see Proposition
2.5
).
Despite the extensive literature on isoperimetric inequalities on graphs, we are not aware of any previous work addressing the specific inner vertex-isoperimetric problem on the infinite regular tree considered here.

Remark.
While edge-isoperimetric problems quantify the total amount of interaction
between a domain and its complement, the inner vertex boundary measures the
number of vertices within the domain that are directly exposed to the exterior.
It is the natural notion of boundary in vertex-based models, such as
epidemic spreading, information flow, or failure propagation in networked
systems.

1.2
Outline of the paper

In Section
2
we introduce the basic graph-theoretic notions and notation used throughout the paper, and recall the relevant boundary notions for domains in graphs.

Specializing to the
d
-regular tree
T_{d}
, we introduce a natural decomposition
of the boundary of a domain into leaves and residual boundary vertices.
This leads to the notion of a
full domain
, namely a domain whose entire
boundary consists of leaves.
The section concludes with a short proof of the classical identity relating the
size of a domain in
T_{d}
to the size of its outer boundary:

|\partial^{\prime}D|=(d-2)\,|D|+2.

The exact inner isoperimetric profile of the
d
-regular tree is established in Section
3
. It is given by

I_{d}(k)=\left\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\right\rceil.

Section
4
investigates isoperimetrically optimal domains in
T_{d}
. Although one might initially expect metric balls to be the only optimal domains, B. L. Santos Correia observed in his thesis that the class of optimal domains is in fact considerably richer. In this section, we introduce a fundamental invariant, the
boundary branching excess

\tau(D)
, and show that optimality is characterized by the condition
\tau(D)\leq d-2
. In particular, full domains satisfy
\tau(D)=0
and are therefore optimal.

Section
5
is devoted to several equivalent characterizations
of full domains, and in Section
6
we study a natural gluing
operation along boundary vertices for general domains and analyze the behavior
of the invariant
\tau
under this operation.

Section
7
, which forms one of the central parts of the paper, develops a detailed combinatorial description of arbitrary domains in

T_{d}
.
We introduce the
stem
of a domain, a finite tree that encodes how full components are attached along the residual boundary, establish its basic structural properties, and show how the invariant
\tau(D)
can be read directly from its combinatorics. See in particular §
7.3

We further prove that any domain in
T_{d}
can be reconstructed, up to isomorphism, from its stem together with a collection of full domains and suitable gluing data. This leads to a classification of all domains in the regular tree, and in particular of all isoperimetrically optimal domains, summarized in Theorem
7.13
.

1.3
Related work

Isoperimetric inequalities, and more generally geometric inequalities, have a long history dating back to the nineteenth century, and there is a vast literature which we do not attempt to survey here. A natural point of entry is the work of C. Pittet
[
11
]
, which introduces the notion of isoperimetric profile from the perspective of Riemannian geometry and highlights the connections between continuous and discrete methods.

Isoperimetric inequalities have also been extensively studied in geometric group theory.
A fundamental result in this direction is that of Coulhon and Saloff-Coste
[
6
]
, who estimate the isoperimetric profile of a finitely generated group in terms of its growth function.
See also
[
5
]
for some recent refined estimates.

Discrete isoperimetric problems also play a central role in combinatorics, expansion theory, percolation, random walk, and other probabilistic aspects of graph theory. General background can be found in
[
17
,
18
]
.

The exact solution to the isoperimetric problem, including the exact isoperimetric profile and an explicit description of some (or all) minimal sets, is only known in a few cases. A classical example is that of the Hamming cube, whose vertex isoperimetric problem was solved by Harper
[
13
,
14
]
; see also Leader
[
16
]
for a pedagogical presentation. We further refer to Harper’s monograph
[
15
]
for a broad overview of combinatorial isoperimetric problems and the compression methods often used to study them.

More closely related to the present work are recent studies of isoperimetric properties of finite trees, in particular the outer or edge isoperimetric profiles of complete
d
-ary trees, that is, balls of finite radius in the regular tree
T_{d}
. Relevant results and bounds can be found in
[
1
,
3
,
9
,
19
]
.

We also note that our notion of a
full domain
is related to, but slightly different from, the notion of a
full subtree
in the algorithms and data structures literature. In that setting, “full” typically refers to the number of children of each node in a rooted tree, whereas our notion is intrinsic to the underlying non-rooted tree and does not depend on a choice of root. On the other hand, our notion coincides with what is called a
complete subtree
in the representation theory of groups acting on trees (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).

In this work, our focus is on the inner vertex boundary of domains in the infinite
d
-regular tree
T_{d}
. We obtain an exact formula for the corresponding isoperimetric profile together with a complete description of all optimal domains as assemblies of full components. To the best of our knowledge, this yields the first explicit and constructive classification of all inner vertex-isoperimetric minimizers in the regular tree
T_{d}
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Basic notions

Throughout this paper we work with simple undirected graphs. We use standard graph-theoretic terminology and notation, as in

[
2
,
12
]
. Let
X
be a graph with vertex set
V(X)
and edge set
E(X)
.
For a vertex
x\in V(X)
, we denote by
N_{X}(x)
its set of neighbors, and by

\deg_{X}(x)=|N_{X}(x)|

its degree in
X
.
A nonempty subset
D\subset V(X)
will always be identified with the induced subgraph on
D
; that is, we do not distinguish notationally between
D
as a set of vertices and the corresponding induced subgraph. In particular, we write
|D|
for the number of vertices of
D
.

A
domain
in
X
is a finite connected subset
D\subset V(X)
.
If
|D|=2
, we call
D
a
two-vertex domain
.
If
D
is a domain in
X
and
x\in D
, the
degree of
x
in
D
is defined by

\deg_{D}(x)=|N_{X}(x)\cap D|.

A subset
S\subset V(X)
is
independent
if no edge of
X
connects two vertices of
S
.

The
Euler characteristic
of a finite graph
X
is defined by

\chi(X)=|V(X)|-|E(X)|.

For a connected finite graph,
\chi(X)=1
if and only if
X
is a finite tree, and for a forest
\chi(X)
equals the number of connected components.

The
inner vertex boundary
of a domain
D\subset X
is defined by

\partial D=\bigl\{\,x\in D\mid\exists\,y\notin D\text{ such that }\{x,y\}\in E(X)\,\bigr\}=\bigl\{\,x\in D\mid\deg_{D}(x)<\deg_{X}(x)\,\bigr\}.

Similarly, the
outer vertex boundary
of
D
is defined as

\partial^{\prime}D=\bigl\{\,y\notin D\mid\exists\,x\in D\text{ such that }\{x,y\}\in E(X)\,\bigr\},

and the
edge boundary
of
D
is

\partial_{\mathrm{edge}}D=\bigl\{\,\{u,v\}\in E(X)\mid u\in D,\ v\notin D\,\bigr\}.

We record two simple but useful facts on domains in a tree.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
D
be a domain in a tree
T
. Then every vertex
x\in\partial^{\prime}D
has exactly one neighbor in
D
.
Moreover, the outer boundary
\partial^{\prime}D
is an independent set.

Proof.

By definition, a vertex
x\in\partial^{\prime}D
has at least one neighbor in
D
.
If it had two distinct neighbors
y,z
in
D
, then the unique path in
D

joining
y
to
z
, together with the edges
\{x,y\}
and
\{z,x\}
would form a cycle in
T
, contradicting the fact that

T
is a tree. Hence
x
has exactly one neighbor in
D
.

Now suppose that two vertices
x,y\in\partial^{\prime}D
are adjacent. Let
u,v\in D
be their respective neighbors in
D
.
Then the unique path in
T
joining
u
to
v
lies in
D
, and together with the edges
\{x,u\}
,
\{x,y\}
and
\{y,v\}
forms a cycle in
T
, again a contradiction. Therefore
\partial^{\prime}D
is an independent set.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Every finite tree
G
of maximum degree at most
d
embeds as a domain in
T_{d}
.

We include a brief proof for completeness.

Proof.

Choose a root
x_{0}\in G
and consider the rooted tree structure given by the distance from
x_{0}
. We construct the embedding inductively.
Map
x_{0}
to an arbitrary vertex
u_{0}\in T_{d}
. Suppose inductively that all vertices at distance at most
k
from
x_{0}
have been embedded in
T_{d}
and pick a vertex
x\in G
at level
k
. Its children in
G
are adjacent only to
x
and lie at level
k+1
.
Since
\deg_{G}(x)\leq d
and at most one neighbor of its image in
T_{d}
has already been used (towards its parent),
there remain enough free neighbors to embed all its children.
Since
G
is finite, the construction ends after finitely many steps.
∎

2.2
Domains in a regular tree: leaves and residual boundary

From now on, we work in the
d
-regular (or homogeneous) tree
T_{d}
, where
d\geq 2
.
Recall that
T_{d}
is the connected, acyclic graph in which every vertex has degree
d
.
It is infinite and uniquely determined up to graph isomorphism.
For general background on trees, we refer to
[
12
, Section 1.5]
or
[
20
]
.

A domain in
T_{d}
is simply a finite subtree of
T_{d}
. Let
D\subset T_{d}
be a domain with at least two vertices.
We define its
leaf boundary
and
residual boundary
by

L(D)=\{x\in\partial D\mid\deg_{D}(x)=1\},\qquad R(D)=\{x\in\partial D\mid 2\leq\deg_{D}(x)\leq d-1\}.

Vertices in
L(D)
are called
leaves
, and
R(D)
forms the
residual boundary
. These two sets form a partition of the boundary:

\partial D=L(D)\,\cup\,R(D),\quad L(D)\,\cap\,R(D)=\emptyset.

The following elementary lemma on finite trees is well known and we state it without proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
D\subset T_{d}
be a domain with
|D|\geq 2
. Then
D
contains at least two leaves.
Moreover, a vertex
x\in D
is a leaf of
D
if and only if
D\setminus\{x\}
is connected.

Definition 2.4
.

A domain
D\subset T_{d}
with
|D|\geq 2
is said to have a
full leaf boundary
, or simply to be a
full domain
,
if every boundary vertex is a leaf, that is,
\partial D=L(D)
; equivalently
R(D)=\emptyset
.

Full domains will play a central role in the description of optimal domains developed later.
We end this section by stating a basic property of the outer boundary of a domain in
T_{d}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The boundary of an arbitrary domain
D\subset T_{d}
satisfies

|\partial^{\prime}D|=(d-2)\,|D|+2=\sum_{x\in\partial D}(d-\deg_{D}(x)).

(2.1)

Although this identity is probably well known to experts and appears in
[
5
]
, we include a short proof, since the argument provides a useful template for several constructions that follow in the present paper.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
|D|
. If
|D|=1
, then
\partial^{\prime}D
consists of the
d
neighbors of the unique vertex of
D
, and the formula holds. Assume
|D|\geq 2
, and choose a leaf
x\in\partial D
.
Then
x
has one neighbor inside
D
and
d-1
neighbors outside.
Let
D^{-}=D\setminus\{x\}
.
Removing
x
deletes its
d-1
neighbors outside of
D
from
\partial^{\prime}D
, while
x
itself becomes a vertex of
\partial^{\prime}D^{-}
.

|\partial^{\prime}D^{-}|=|\partial^{\prime}D|-(d-1)+1=|\partial^{\prime}D|-(d-2).

On the other hand, by Lemma
2.3
,
D^{-}
is connected, so we have from the induction hypothesis:

|\partial^{\prime}D^{-}|=(d-2)\,|D^{-}|+2=(d-2)(|D|-1)+2.

We thus conclude that

|\partial^{\prime}D|=|\partial^{\prime}D^{-}|+(d-2)=(d-2)(|D|-1)+2+(d-2)=(d-2)|D|+2,

proving the first equality in (
2.1
).
For the second equality, observe that for each
x\in\partial D
, exactly
d-\deg_{D}(x)
of its
d
neighbors lie outside
D
.
Summing over
x\in\partial D
gives

|\partial^{\prime}D|=\sum_{x\in\partial D}(d-\deg_{D}(x)).

∎

Remark 2.6
.

(i) In a regular tree, the outer vertex boundary
\partial^{\prime}D
and the edge boundary
\partial_{\mathrm{edge}}D
are naturally in bijection. In particular, (
2.1
) remains valid with
|\partial_{\mathrm{edge}}D|
replacing
|\partial^{\prime}D|
.

(ii) In the literature on finite trees, the term
interior vertex
often refers to a vertex that is not a leaf.
In the present paper, since we work with domains
D
in a tree, an interior vertex would more naturally be defined as a vertex all of whose neighbors belong to
D
. To avoid any ambiguity, we altogether avoid the terminology of interior vertex here.

3
The isoperimetric profile of the regular tree

In this section, we compute the isoperimetric profile
I_{d}
of the
d
-regular tree
T_{d}
. Recall that this is the function
I_{d}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by

I_{d}(k)=\min\bigl\{|\partial D|\,\big|\,D\subset T_{d}\text{ is finite, connected, and }|D|=k\bigr\},

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Observe that
I_{d}(k)\leq k
since
\partial D\subset D
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
d\geq 2
, the isoperimetric profile of
T_{d}
is given by
I_{d}(1)=1
, and

I_{d}(k)=\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil,

(3.1)

for
k\geq 2
.

Here
\lceil x\rceil
is the ceiling function, that is
m=\lceil x\rceil
is the unique integer such that
m-1<x\leq m
.

The formula (
3.1
) already appears in
[
4
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we include here a self-contained proof, which relies on the following three lemmas.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every finite connected
D\subset T_{d}
with
|D|\geq 2
we have

|\partial D|\ \geq\frac{(d-2)|D|+2}{d-1}.

Proof.

Each boundary vertex has at most
(d-1)
neighbors outside
D
, so

|\partial^{\prime}D|\leq(d-1)|\partial D|.

Combining this with Proposition
2.5
, which states that
|\partial^{\prime}D|=(d-2)|D|+2
, yields the claim.
∎

The next two lemmas are purely arithmetical:

Lemma 3.3
.

For
k\geq 2
we have

k=\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\leq k\leq d.

Proof.

A direct computation gives

\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}=k-\frac{k-2}{d-1},

which lies in the interval
(k-1,k]
precisely when
0\leq k-2<d-1
, i.e. when
2\leq k\leq d
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
k\geq d+1
, and set

s=\Bigl\lceil\frac{k-2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil\qquad\text{and}\qquad q=(d-1)s-(k-2).

Then
s\geq 1
and
0\leq q<d-1
.

Note that this lemma expresses the Euclidean division of
2-k
by
d-1
, with remainder
q
and quotient
-s
.

Proof.

By definition of
s
, we have

s-1<\frac{k-2}{d-1}\leq s.

Since
k\geq d+1
, it follows that
\frac{k-2}{d-1}\geq 1
, hence
s\geq 1
. Multiplying the above inequalities by
d-1
yields

(d-1)(s-1)<k-2\leq(d-1)s,

which can be rewritten as

0\leq q=(d-1)s-(k-2)<d-1.

∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Proof.

The result is trivial for
k=1
. The case
d=2
is also trivial since in that case both sides of (
3.1
) are clearly equal to
2
(when
k\geq 2
). For the rest of the proof we thus assume
d\geq 3
and
k\geq 2
.

Lemma
3.2
gives us

I_{d}(k)\ \geq\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1},

and since the left-hand side is an integer, we have in fact

I_{d}(k)\ \geq\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil.

To prove equality, we need to produce for each
k\geq 2
, an explicit domain
D\subset T_{d}
with
|D|=k
and

|\partial D|=\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil.

(3.2)

We divide the construction in the following three cases:

(i)

2\leq k\leq d
,

(ii)

k\geq d+1
and
k\equiv 2\mod(d-1)
.

(iii)

k\geq d+1
and
k\not\equiv 2\mod(d-1)
.

Case (i): If
2\leq k\leq d
, any connected set
D
of cardinality
k
satisfies

\partial D=D
. Using Lemma
3.3
, we then have

|\partial D|=k=\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil.

Case (ii): We now assume
k\geq d+1
(
\geq 3
) and
k\equiv 2\mod(d-1)
, i.e. there exists an integer
s\geq 0
such that
k-2=(d-1)s
.
If
s=0
, then
k=2
and this case has been considered in the previous step. We therefore assume
s\geq 1

and we consider the domain
D\subset T_{d}
defined as follows:

D=A_{s}\cup\partial^{\prime}A_{s},

where
A_{s}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}
is a path in
T_{d}
(i.e.
x_{i}
is adjacent to
x_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq s-1
) and
\partial^{\prime}A_{s}
is its outer boundary in
T_{d}
. A direct inspection shows that

\partial D=\partial^{\prime}A_{s},\quad|\partial D|=|\partial^{\prime}A_{s}|=(d-2)s+2,

and

D=A_{s}\cup\partial^{\prime}A_{s},\quad k:=|D|=s+(d-2)s+2=(d-1)s+2.

A straightforward computation yields

\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}=(d-2)s+2,

which is an integer, so

|\partial D|=(d-2)s+2=\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}=\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil.

Case (iii): We finally assume
k\geq d+1
and
k\not\equiv 2\mod(d-1)
. By Lemma
3.4
we can write

k-2=(d-1)s-q,

(3.3)

with integers
s\geq 1
and
1\leq q<d-1
.
In that case we consider the domain
D
obtained by a modification of the previous construction. Again, let

A_{s}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}
be a path in
T_{d}
and define now:

D=A_{s}\cup\partial^{\prime}A_{s}\setminus Q,

where

Q
is a set of
q
external neighbors of
x_{s}
(see Figure
1
). We then have

|D|=(d-1)s+2-q=:k.

and

|\partial D|=|\partial^{\prime}A_{s}|-q+1=(d-2)s+3-q,

since we have removed
q
leaves compared to the previous construction, but now there is an additional boundary vertex
x_{s}\in\partial D
.

Using
k=(d-1)s+2-q
and
|\partial D|=(d-2)s-q+3
, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-2)\bigl((d-1)s+2-q\bigr)+2}{d-1}=(d-2)s+\frac{2(d-1)-(d-2)q}{d-1}

\displaystyle=|\partial D|-1+\frac{q}{d-1},

and since
1\leq q<d-1
, we have
0<\frac{q}{d-1}<1
, therefore

|\partial D|-1<\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}<|\partial D|;

equivalently

\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil=|\partial D|.

In all cases we have constructed a domain
D
with
|D|=k
and
|\partial D|=\bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\bigr\rceil
; this completes the proof.
∎

On the Cheeger constant

The
inner Cheeger constant
of an infinite graph
X
is defined as

h_{\mathrm{in}}(X)=\inf\left\{\frac{|\partial D|}{|D|}\;\middle|\;D\subset X\text{ finite, connected, and non-empty}\right\}=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\;\frac{I_{X}(k)}{k}.

For the regular tree
X=T_{d}
, we obtain the following value:

h_{\mathrm{in}}(T_{d})=\frac{d-2}{d-1}.

Indeed, applying Theorem
3.1
, we obtain

\frac{I_{T_{d}}(k)}{k}=\frac{1}{k}\Bigl\lceil\frac{(d-2)k+2}{d-1}\Bigr\rceil.

Therefore

\frac{d-2}{d-1}\leq\frac{I_{T_{d}}(k)}{k}<\frac{d-2}{d-1}+\frac{2}{(d-1)k}+\frac{1}{k}.

It follows that

\frac{d-2}{d-1}\leq h_{\mathrm{in}}(T_{d})=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{I_{T_{d}}(k)}{k}\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{I_{T_{d}}(k)}{k}=\frac{d-2}{d-1},

which proves the claim.

Using the outer and edge boundaries, we similarly define the
outer
and
edge
Cheeger constants
h_{\mathrm{out}}(X)
and
h_{\mathrm{edge}}(X)
(the latter is also referred to as the
expansion constant
of the graph). Applying Proposition
2.5
, we see that

h_{\mathrm{edge}}(T_{d})=h_{\mathrm{out}}(T_{d})=d-2.

This value is classical, it appears e.g. as Exercise 6.1 in
[
17
]
.
We are not aware of an explicit reference for the above value of
h_{\mathrm{in}}(T_{d})
in the literature.

4
Optimal domains

A domain
D\subset T_{d}
is said to be
isoperimetrically optimal
, or simply an
optimal domain
,
if it has minimal boundary among all domains of the same size, that is,

|\partial D|=I_{d}(|D|).

As a first example, consider the ball
B(x,r)\subset T_{d}
of radius
r\geq 2
centered at some vertex
x
.

A straightforward induction shows that

|\partial B(x,r)|=d(d-1)^{r-1},\qquad|B(x,r)|=1+d\,\frac{(d-1)^{r}-1}{d-2}.

One readily checks that

|\partial B(x,r)|=\frac{(d-2)|B(x,r)|+2}{d-1}=I_{d}\left(|B(x,r)|\right),

therefore the ball
B(x,r)
is an optimal domain; but, as we shall see now, the class of optimal domains is much richer than the family of balls. To characterize them, we introduce the following invariant:

Definition 4.1
.

The
boundary branching excess
of a domain
D\subset T_{d}
of cardinality at least 2 is defined as

\tau(D)=\sum_{x\in\partial D}\bigl(\deg_{D}(x)-1\bigr).

(4.1)

Observe that in fact

\tau(D)=\sum_{x\in R(D)}\bigl(\deg_{D}(x)-1\bigr),

(4.2)

since
\deg_{D}(x)=1
for any
x\in L(D)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
D\subset T_{d}
be a finite domain with
|D|\geq 2
. Then

\tau(D)=(d-1)\,|\partial D|-(d-2)\,|D|-2.

(4.3)

Furthermore

|R(D)|\leq\tau(D)\leq|D|-2.

(4.4)

Moreover, the equality
\tau(D)=|D|-2
holds if and only if
\partial D=D
.

Observe that in (
4.3
), for domains of fixed cardinality
k\geq 2
the boundary size
|\partial D|
is minimized exactly when
\tau(D)
is minimized. In other words, isoperimetric optimality is equivalent to minimality of
\tau(D)
among domains of the same size.

Proof.

Every boundary vertex
x\in\partial D
has
d-\deg_{D}(x)

neighbors outside
D
, so

|\partial^{\prime}D|=\sum_{x\in\partial D}(d-\deg_{D}(x))=\sum_{x\in\partial D}\bigl[(d-1)-(\deg_{D}(x)-1)\bigr]=(d-1)\,|\partial D|-\tau(D),

hence (
4.3
) follows from Proposition
2.5
.
The inequality
|R(D)|\leq\tau(D)
follows immediately from (
4.2
) since each term in the sum is
\geq 1
. To prove the inequality
\tau(D)\leq|D|-2
, we use that
\chi(D)=1
, hence

\sum_{x\in D}\deg_{D}(x)=2|E(D)|=2(|D|-1);

therefore

\tau(D)=\sum_{x\in\partial D}(\deg_{D}(x)-1)\leq\sum_{x\in D}\bigl(\deg_{D}(x)-1\bigr)=2(|D|-1)-|D|=|D|-2,

with equality if and only if
\partial D=D
.
∎

Using the boundary branching excess, we state a convenient optimality criterion:

Theorem 4.3
.

A domain
D\subset T_{d}
with
|D|\geq 2
is isoperimetrically optimal, i.e.
|\partial D|=I_{d}(|D|)
, if and only if

\tau(D)\leq d-2.

(4.5)

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, the domain
D
is optimal if and only if

|\partial D|=\lceil A\rceil,\quad\text{where}\quad A:=\frac{(d-2)|D|+2}{d-1}.

By Lemma
4.2
, we have

|\partial D|=A+\frac{\tau(D)}{d-1}.

Since
\tau(D)\geq 0
, we have
|\partial D|\geq A
, and hence

|\partial D|=\lceil A\rceil
if and only if

0\leq\frac{\tau(D)}{d-1}<1.

As
\tau(D)
is a non-negative integer, this is equivalent to

\tau(D)\leq d-2
, which completes the proof.
∎

Our next result provides some simple examples of optimal domains:

Corollary 4.4
.

(a)

Every domain of size
k\leq d
is optimal.

(b)

Every full domain is optimal.

(c)

If
|R(D)|=1
, then
D
is optimal.

(d)

If
D
is optimal, then
|R(D)|\leq d-2
.

Proof.

(a) That every domain of size
k\leq d
is optimal has been established in step (i) of the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
It also follows from the previous Theorem together with Lemma
4.2
.

(b) For a full domain
D
, we have
R(D)=\emptyset
and hence
\tau(D)=0

by (
4.2
). Therefore
D
is optimal.

(c) If
R(D)=\{x\}
, then
\tau(D)=\deg_{D}(x)-1\leq d-2
.

(d) Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), we see if
D
is an optimal domain, then
|R(D)|\leq\tau(D)\leq d-2
.
∎

We now describe a more elaborate example.

Consider the domain

and

hence

and

. With

and

confirming that
D
is indeed optimal.

\tau(D)=3+3=6\not\leq 5-2
. This shows that

5
On full domains

Recall that a domain
D\subset T_{d}
is full if every boundary vertex is a leaf. Full domains are isoperimetrically optimal and play a central role in the sequel. In this section we give several equivalent characterizations in the case
|D|\geq 3
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
D\subset T_{d}
be a domain with
|D|\geq 3
and
d\geq 2
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

D
is full, i.e.
\partial D=L(D)
;

(b)

R(D)=\emptyset
;

(c)

\tau(D)=0
;

(d)

|\partial D|=\dfrac{(d-2)\,|D|+2}{d-1}
;

(e)

D
is optimal and
|D|\equiv 2\pmod{d-1}
;

(f)

D\setminus\partial D
is connected;

(g)

there exists a domain
D_{1}\subset D
such that

D=D_{1}\cup\partial^{\prime}D_{1}
.

Remark 5.2
.

The hypothesis
|D|\geq 3
cannot be removed.
When
|D|=2
, the domain
D
is always full, so conditions (a)–(f) hold trivially, but condition (g) fails.
Note also that the theorem is trivially true when
d=2
. In fact every domain in
T_{2}
is full.

Proof.

The equivalences (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) follow directly from the definitions of
L(D)
, and
\tau(D)
; and
(c)
\Leftrightarrow
(d) follows from the identity (
4.3
), which says that

|\partial D|=\frac{(d-2)|D|+2}{d-1}+\frac{\tau(D)}{d-1}.

(5.1)

The following implications remain to be proved:

(d)\Rightarrow(e)\Rightarrow(c),\qquad(a)\Leftrightarrow(f),\qquad(a)\Leftrightarrow(g).

(d)
\Rightarrow
(e).
If (d) holds then, by Theorem
3.1
, we have
|\partial D|=I_{d}(|D|)
, so
D
is optimal.
Moreover since
(d-2)\equiv-1\pmod{d-1}
, we have

-|D|+2\equiv(d-2)|D|+2\equiv(d-1)|\partial D|\equiv 0\pmod{d-1}.

Hence
|D|\equiv 2\pmod{d-1}
.

(e)
\Rightarrow
(c).
Using (
4.3
) or (
5.1
), and computing modulo
(d-1)
, we have

\tau(D)\equiv-(d-2)|D|-2\equiv|D|-2\pmod{d-1}.

Thus the hypothesis
|D|\equiv 2\pmod{d-1}
implies
\tau(D)\equiv 0\pmod{d-1}
. On the other hand, since
D
is optimal, Theorem
4.3
gives
0\leq\tau(D)\leq d-2
. It follows that
\tau(D)=0
.

(a)
\Leftrightarrow
(f).
By Lemma
2.3
, removing any leaf of a domain does not disconnect the remainder.
Applying this repeatedly shows that
D\setminus\partial D
is connected if and only if all boundary vertices are leaves.

(g)
\Rightarrow
(a).
Assume that
D=D_{1}\cup\partial^{\prime}D_{1}
for some domain
D_{1}
.
Then
\partial D=\partial^{\prime}D_{1}
, and by Lemma
2.1
,
each vertex
x\in\partial D
has exactly one neighbor in
D
.
Thus every boundary vertex is a leaf, i.e.
\partial D=L(D)
.

Finally we prove (a)
\Rightarrow
(g). Assume
\partial D=L(D)
and set

D^{\mathrm{o}}=D\setminus\partial D=D\setminus L(D).

Since
|D|\geq 3
the set
D^{\mathrm{o}}
is non-empty and by (f) it is connected. We now prove that
\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}=\partial D
.

We first show that
\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}\subset\partial D
. Assume by contradiction that there exists a vertex
z\in\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}\setminus\partial D
.
Then
z\notin D
, and since
z\in\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}
, it is adjacent to some vertex
y\in D^{\mathrm{o}}
.
As
D^{\mathrm{o}}\subset D
, this implies that
y\in D
has a neighbor
z\notin D
, hence
y\in\partial D
. However, by definition
D^{\mathrm{o}}=D\setminus\partial D
,
so
y\notin\partial D
, a contradiction. Therefore, no such vertex
z
exists, and we conclude that
\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}\subset\partial D
.

To prove the converse inclusion
\partial D\subset\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}
, choose a vertex
x\in\partial D
.
Since
x
is a leaf of
D
, it has exactly one neighbor
y\in D
; two leaves cannot be adjacent, so
y\notin L(D)
and therefore
y\in D^{\mathrm{o}}
. Thus
x\in\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}
.
This shows that
\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}}=\partial D
.
Since
D^{\mathrm{o}}=D\setminus\partial D
, we obtain

D=D^{\mathrm{o}}\cup\partial D=D^{\mathrm{o}}\cup\partial^{\prime}D^{\mathrm{o}},

so that (g) holds with
D_{1}=D^{\mathrm{o}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

As an immediate consequence of (e), we have the following

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
D
be a domain in
T_{d}
such that
|D|\equiv 2\pmod{d-1}
. Then
D
is optimal if and only if
D
is full.

6
Gluing domains along a vertex

We now introduce a basic operation for assembling larger domains from smaller ones.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
be domains in
T_{d}
, each of cardinality at least
2
, with
2\leq m\leq d
, and let

p\in T_{d}
be a vertex such that
p\in\partial D_{i}
for all
i
.
We say that the domain
D
is obtained by
gluing

D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
at the vertex
p
if

(i)

D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}D_{i}
,

(ii)

\partial D_{i}\cap\partial D_{j}=\{p\}
for all
i\neq j
.

Geometrically, we attach
m
subtrees along the single common vertex
p
. From the definition it follows immediately that

\deg_{D}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg_{D_{i}}(p),

(6.1)

thus
p\in\partial D
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg_{D_{i}}(p)<d.

Example 6.2
.

If
m=2
and
D_{2}
is a two-vertex domain, then gluing
D_{2}
to another domain
D_{1}
amounts to attaching a leaf to
D_{1}
at a vertex
p\in\partial D_{1}
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
D
be obtained by gluing the domains
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
at
p
. Then

\tau(D)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})+(m-1),&\text{if }p\in\partial D,\\[8.5359pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})-(d-m),&\text{if }p\notin\partial D.\end{cases}

In particular, gluing increases the total branching excess
\tau
by exactly
m-1
if
p\in\partial D
, while
\tau
remains unchanged or decreases if
p\not\in\partial D
.

Proof.

For each
i
and any vertex
x\neq p
lying in
D_{i}
we have
\deg_{D}(x)=\deg_{D_{i}}(x)
. As for the point
p
, from (
6.1
) we have

\deg_{D}(p)-1=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg_{D_{i}}(p)\right)-1=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\deg_{D_{i}}(p)-1)+(m-1).

(6.2)

We now separately consider the cases where
p
belongs to the boundary of
D
or not.

If
p\in\partial D
, then using (
6.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\tau(D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\partial D\setminus\{p\}}\bigl(\deg_{D}(x)-1\bigr)+\bigl(\deg_{D}(p)-1\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{x\in\partial D_{i}\setminus\{p\}}\bigl(\deg_{D_{i}}(x)-1\bigr)\right)+\bigl(\deg_{D}(p)-1\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{x\in\partial D_{i}}\bigl(\deg_{D_{i}}(x)-1\bigr)\right)+(m-1)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})+(m-1).

If
p\notin\partial D
, the argument is similar, except that the contribution
\deg_{D}(p)-1
must
not
be counted in the computation of
\tau(D)
. Since in this case
\deg_{D}(p)=d
, we obtain

\tau(D)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})+(m-1)-(d-1)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})-(d-m).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Combining Theorem
4.3
with Proposition
6.3
we obtain the following optimality criterion.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
D
be obtained by gluing the domains
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
at a vertex
p
.
Then
D
is isoperimetrically optimal if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

If
p\in\partial D
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})+(m-1)\leq d-2.

(ii)

If
p\notin\partial D
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})-(d-m)\leq d-2.

In particular, if all subdomains
D_{i}
are full, then
\tau(D)=m-1
when
p\in\partial D
, and
\tau(D)=0
otherwise.
Indeed, if
p\notin\partial D
, then
\deg_{D}(p)=d
. Furthermore, each domain
D_{i}
is glued at
p
along a distinct edge, i.e. each
D_{i}
contributes exactly one neighbor of
p
. Therefore

m=\deg_{D}(p)=d.

Since
\tau(D_{i})=0
for all
i
, Proposition
6.3
gives

\tau(D)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(D_{i})-(d-m)=0.

In both cases,
D
is optimal (and full in the latter case).
This also follows from Corollary
4.4
, since
R(D)
is either empty or equal to
\{p\}
, and hence has cardinality at most
1
.

7
The combinatorial structure of domains in
T_{d}

In this section, we describe a canonical decomposition of arbitrary domains in the regular tree
T_{d}
(of cardinality at least
2
)
into full components glued along the residual boundary. We introduce the notion of
stem
, a finite tree encoding this decomposition. This framework leads to an alternative criterion for isoperimetric optimality.

7.1
Canonical decomposition

We first observe that full domains are indecomposable with respect to gluing along boundary vertices:

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
D\subset T_{d}
be a full domain. Then
D
cannot be written as a nontrivial gluing of domains along a boundary vertex.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive. Assume that
D
is obtained by gluing domains
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
at some boundary vertex
p\in\partial D
, with
m\geq 2
.
Then

\deg_{D}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg_{D_{i}}(p)\geq m\geq 2,

so
p
is not a leaf of
D
. Hence
\partial D\neq L(D)
, and therefore
D
is not full.
∎

Let
D\subset T_{d}
be a non-full domain and denote by
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
the connected components of
D\setminus R(D)
.
Note that
m\geq 2
else
D
would be full, since in this case we would have
R(D)=\emptyset
. For each
i
we define

F_{i}=B_{i}\cup\bigl(R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}\bigr)\subset D;

(7.1)

that is,
F_{i}
consists of the component
B_{i}
together with all vertices of
R(D)
that are adjacent to
B_{i}
.
The number
m
will be referred to as the
number of full components of
the domain
D
. We will see in Remark
7.9
below that
m\leq\tau(D)+1
. In particular, if
D
is optimal, then
m\leq d-1
.

Our next theorem states that the
F_{i}
’s are full domains and
D
is obtained by gluing these full domains along some vertices in
R(D)
:

Theorem 7.2
.

With the above notations, each
F_{i}
is a full domain.
Moreover, if we set

R_{0}=\{x\in R(D)\mid\deg_{R(D)}(x)=\deg_{D}(x)\},

then we have

(i)

R_{0}=D\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
.

(ii)

D=R(D)\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}\right)=R_{0}\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}\right)
.

Our proof will use the following Lemma about the neighbor sets of a vertex in
B_{i}
:

Lemma 7.3
.

For any
x\in B_{i}
we have
N_{D}(x)=N_{F_{i}}(x)
. In particular
\deg_{F_{i}}(x)=\deg_{D}(x)
.

Proof.

The inclusion
N_{F_{i}}(x)\subset N_{D}(x)
is clear.
To prove the reverse inclusion, we show that every vertex
z\in N_{D}(x)

belongs to
F_{i}
. We distinguish two cases.

Suppose first that
z\in R(D)
. Then
z\notin B_{i}
, but
z
is adjacent to

x\in B_{i}
. Hence
z\in\partial^{\prime}B_{i}
, and therefore

z\in R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}\subset F_{i},

by definition of
F_{i}
.

Suppose now that
z\notin R(D)
. Then
z\in D\setminus R(D)
, and since
B_{i}
is the connected component of
D\setminus R(D)
containing
x
, it follows that
z\in B_{i}\subset F_{i}
. In particular,
z\in F_{i}
.

This proves that
N_{D}(x)\subset N_{F_{i}}(x)
, and hence
N_{D}(x)=N_{F_{i}}(x)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Properties (i)–(ii) follow directly from the definitions, so it remains to prove that each
F_{i}
is a full domain.
Since
B_{i}
is connected and every vertex of
R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}
is adjacent to
B_{i}
, the set
F_{i}
is connected. We therefore only need to show that every boundary vertex
x\in\partial F_{i}
is a leaf of
F_{i}
. Two cases arise.

If
x\in R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}
, then by Lemma
2.1
,
x
has exactly one neighbor in
B_{i}
and no neighbor in
\partial^{\prime}B_{i}
. Hence
x
has exactly one neighbor in
F_{i}=B_{i}\cup\bigl(R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}\bigr)
, that is
x
is a leaf of
F_{i}
.

If
x\in B_{i}
, then by Lemma
7.3
we have
\deg_{F_{i}}(x)=\deg_{D}(x)
. Since
x\in\partial F_{i}
, we have

\deg_{F_{i}}(x)\leq d-1
, hence also
\deg_{D}(x)\leq d-1
and therefore
x\in\partial D
, but since
x\notin R(D)
, we conclude that
x\in L(D)
. Hence
\deg_{D}(x)=1
and thus also
\deg_{F_{i}}(x)=1
, i.e.
x
is a leaf of
F_{i}
.
∎

We will show in Section
7.3
below how the boundary branching excess
\tau(D)
can be expressed in terms of the combinatorial data associated with the above decomposition. This leads to a convenient criterion for isoperimetric optimality; see Lemma
7.7
and Theorem
7.13
.

7.2
The stem of a domain

In this subsection, we associate to any domain
D\subset T_{d}
a quotient graph, called the
stem
, which encodes the combinatorial pattern according to which the full components of
D
are attached along the residual boundary
R(D)
.
Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
be the connected components of
D\setminus R(D)
, and recall that

F_{i}=B_{i}\cup\bigl(R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}\bigr)

is a full domain for any
i=1,\dots,m
and
D
is obtained by gluing these domains along vertices in
R(D)
.

Definition 7.4
.

The
stem
of the domain
D
is the graph
D^{*}
obtained by contracting each connected component
B_{i}
of
D\setminus R(D)
to a single vertex, that we denote by
b_{i}
, while leaving all vertices of
R(D)
distinct. More precisely, the vertex set of
D^{*}
is defined as the partitioned set

V(D^{*})=R(D)\,\sqcup\,\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\},

(7.2)

and the edges in
D^{*}
are defined as follows:

\circ

if
x,y\in R(D)
are adjacent in
D
, then they are adjacent in
D^{*}
;

\circ

if
x\in R(D)
and
y\in B_{i}
are adjacent in
D
, then
x
is adjacent to

b_{i}
in
D^{*}
;

\circ

there are no edges between distinct vertices
b_{i}
and
b_{j}
if
i\neq j
.

Note that
D^{*}
is not a subgraph of
T_{d}
but rather an abstract graph, in fact a quotient of
D
. Furthermore, the partition (
7.2
) is regarded as part of the structure of the stem. By construction, the stem records the adjacency relations between residual boundary vertices and the full components of
D
, while forgetting the internal structure of each full component. We now collect some basic properties of this graph.

Proposition 7.5
.

The stem
D^{*}
of a domain
D\subset T_{d}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

D^{*}
is a finite tree.

(ii)

For every vertex
x\in R(D)
, we have

2\leq\deg_{D^{*}}(x)=\deg_{D}(x)\leq d-1.

(iii)

For each contracted vertex
b_{i}
, we have

\deg_{D^{*}}(b_{i})=|R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}|=\bigl|\{x\in R(D)\mid x\text{ has a neighbor in }B_{i}\}\bigr|.

Observe that, by (ii), no leaf of
D^{*}
belongs to
R(D)
.

Proof.

(i)
Since
D
is a tree and each
B_{i}
is connected, contracting the
subgraphs
B_{i}
preserves connectedness and does not create cycles. Hence
D^{*}

is a finite tree.

(ii)
Let
x\in R(D)
. Each edge incident to
x
in
D
either joins
x
to
another vertex of
R(D)
or to a vertex in some component
B_{i}
.
In the latter case, Lemma
2.1
applied to
B_{i}
implies
that
x
has a unique neighbor in
B_{i}
.

Edges between vertices of
R(D)
are unchanged by the contraction, while an edge
joining
x
to
B_{i}
becomes an edge joining
x
to
B_{i}
in
D^{*}
.
Therefore
\deg_{D^{*}}(x)=\deg_{D}(x)
, and the bounds follow from the definition of
the residual boundary.

(iii)
An edge incident to
b_{i}
in
D^{*}
corresponds precisely to an edge
of
D
joining a vertex of
B_{i}
to a vertex of
R(D)
.
Such vertices of
R(D)
are exactly those in
R(D)\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i}
, which proves
the claim.
∎

7.3
Abstract stem diagrams

In order to investigate certain numerical relations arising in the stem of a domain, it is convenient to adopt an abstract viewpoint.
To this end, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 7.6
.

A
stem diagram
is an abstract graph
G^{*}
whose vertex set is partitioned into two subsets

V(G^{*})=R\sqcup B.

Vertices in
R
are called
red
, while those in
B
are called
blue
. We impose the following conditions:

(i)

G^{*}
is a finite tree;

(ii)

B
is an independent set (i.e., there are no blue–blue edges);

(iii)

all leaves of
G^{*}
belong to
B
(that is, every leaf is blue, although a blue vertex may have degree greater than
1
).

A stem diagram is said to be
trivial
if
R=\emptyset
. In that case,
G^{*}
consists of a single blue vertex.

We write
r:=|R|
for the number of red vertices,
m:=|B|
for the number of blue vertices, and
l:=|E(R)|
for the number of red–red edges. We also define

\tau^{*}=\tau^{*}(G^{*}):=\sum_{x\in R}\bigl(\deg_{G^{*}}(x)-1\bigr).

For a non-trivial stem diagram, the following basic inequalities hold:

m\geq 2,\qquad 1\leq r\leq\tau^{*},\qquad\text{and}\qquad r-l\geq 1.

(7.3)

Indeed, since
G^{*}
is a tree, it has at least two leaves; by definition all leaves
are blue, and therefore
m\geq 2
.
Moreover, non-triviality means that
R\neq\emptyset
, hence
r\geq 1
.
Since no red vertex is a leaf, we have
\deg_{G^{*}}(x)\geq 2
for every
x\in R
, and thus

\tau^{*}=\sum_{x\in R}(\deg_{G^{*}}(x)-1)\geq|R|=r.

Finally, the induced subgraph on
R
is a forest, so
r-l=\chi(R)\geq 1
.

Lemma 7.7
.

With the above notation, we have

m+l=\tau^{*}+1.

Proof.

Let
n=|E(G^{*})|
denote the total number of edges of
G^{*}
. Write
l
for the number of edges with both endpoints in
R
, and
k
for the number of edges with one end in
R
and the other end in
B
.
Since
B
is an independent set, we have

n=l+k.

Each red–red edge contributes
2
to the sum of degrees

\sum_{x\in R}\deg_{G^{*}}(x)
, while each red–blue edge contributes
1
.
Hence

\sum_{x\in R}\deg_{G^{*}}(x)=2l+k=(l+k)+l=n+l.

It follows that

\tau^{*}=\sum_{x\in R}\bigl(\deg_{G^{*}}(x)-1\bigr)=(n+l)-r.

Since
G^{*}
is a tree, we have
n=|G^{*}|-1=(r+m)-1
, and therefore

\tau^{*}=(r+m-1)+l-r=m+l-1.

∎

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
G^{*}
be a non-trivial stem diagram. With the notation introduced above, we have

\tau^{*}+2-r\leq m\leq\tau^{*}+1,

and

\tau^{*}=|G^{*}|-1-\chi(R).

In particular
\tau^{*}\leq|G^{*}|-2
, with equality if and only if the subgraph
R\subset G^{*}
is connected.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.7
, we have
m=\tau^{*}+1-l
, which immediately gives
m\leq\tau^{*}+1
.
Since the red induced subgraph is a forest, its Euler characteristic satisfies
\chi(R)=r-l\geq 1
, and therefore

m=\tau^{*}+1-l=\tau^{*}+1+\chi(R)-r\geq\tau^{*}+2-r.

Moreover, using again
\chi(R)=r-l
, we compute

\tau^{*}=m+l-1=m+(r-\chi(R))-1=(r+m)-1-\chi(R)=|G^{*}|-1-\chi(R).

Since
\chi(R)\geq 1
, this implies
\tau^{*}\leq|G^{*}|-2
, with equality if and only if
\chi(R)=1
, that is, if and only if
R
is connected.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

Applying the previous corollary to an arbitrary domain
D\subset T_{d}
, we obtain

\tau(D)=\tau^{*}(D^{*})\leq|D^{*}|-2,

which improves the upper bound in (
4.4
).
Furthermore, the number
m
of full components of
D
satisfies

m\leq\tau(D)+1.

In particular, if
D
is optimal, then
m\leq d-1
.

Examples of stem diagrams

We display below all isomorphism classes of stem diagrams with
\tau^{*}\leq 3
(note that each such stem is a finite tree with maximal degree at most
\tau^{*}+1
).

The situation becomes significantly richer when
\tau^{*}\geq 4
. As an illustration, we describe a large family of stem diagrams with a prescribed value of
\tau^{*}\geq 1
.

1.

Start with an arbitrary finite tree
T
having
\tau^{*}+2
vertices.

2.

Turn
T
into a stem diagram
G^{*}=R\,\sqcup B
by coloring all leaves blue and all other vertices red.
Since the red subgraph
R\subset G^{*}
is connected, Corollary
7.8
implies
that the resulting stem diagram has branching excess exactly
\tau^{*}
.

3.

We now generate further examples with the same value of
\tau^{*}
by the following
local modification.
Choose a red–red edge
\{x,y\}
and insert a new blue vertex
z
in between.
The edge
\{x,y\}
is then replaced by two red–blue edges
\{x,z\}
and
\{y,z\}
.
This operation increases the number of blue vertices by one and decreases the number
of red–red edges by one.
By Lemma
7.7
, the value of
\tau^{*}
remains unchanged.

4.

Repeating step 3 produces additional stem diagrams with the same prescribed value of
\tau^{*}
.

This construction produces a large, albeit very special, family of stem diagrams with prescribed branching excess
\tau^{*}
.
Already the choice of the initial tree on
n=\tau^{*}+2
vertices
grows exponentially with
\tau^{*}
(see

]
and

for precise asymptotics).
This shows that even within this restricted construction,
the combinatorial structure of stem diagrams rapidly becomes intricate for large
\tau^{*}
.

7.4
Reconstructing a domain from a stem diagram

In Sections 7.1–7.3, we proceeded in an
analytic
way: we
decomposed a domain
D\subset T_{d}
into its stem
D^{*}
and its full
components
F_{i}
. We now proceed
synthetically
, showing that
this procedure can be inverted. More precisely, we prove that suitable
abstract data—consisting of a stem diagram, a collection of full
domains, and compatible gluing maps—determine a domain of
T_{d}

uniquely up to isomorphism, yielding a complete combinatorial classification of all domains in
T_{d}
.

Definition 7.10
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 2
. An admissible
reconstruction datum
consists of a triple
(G^{*},(F_{i}),(g_{i}))
, where

(i)

G^{*}=R\sqcup\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}
is an abstract stem diagram (Definition
7.6
), that is, a finite tree whose vertex set is partitioned as

V(G^{*})=R\sqcup\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\},

such that the vertices
b_{i}
are pairwise non-adjacent and, for every vertex
x\in R
, one has

2\leq\deg_{G^{*}}(x)\leq d-1,

(ii)

a collection of full domains
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
in
T_{d}
satisfying

|\partial F_{i}|\geq\deg_{G^{*}}(b_{i});

(iii)

and for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, an injective map

g_{i}:N_{G^{*}}(b_{i})\to\partial F_{i},

where
N_{G^{*}}(b_{i})\subset R
denotes the set of neighbors of
b_{i}
in
G^{*}
.

Remark 7.11
.

As a concrete instance, the domain in Figure 2 (§4) corresponds to the admissible datum where
G^{*}
is the stem shown at the end of §7. 2, the full
components
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}
are balls of radius
1
in
T_{5}
, and the maps
g_{i}
are the natural identifications along the unique
leaf of each
F_{i}
adjacent to
p
. The construction below then recovers
D
up to isomorphism.

Construction of a graph
G
.

Given admissible gluing data
(G^{*},(F_{i}),(g_{i}))
, we construct a graph
G
as follows. Consider first the disjoint union

X=R\sqcup\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{i},

and for each
i
and each
x\in N_{G^{*}}(b_{i})
, identify the vertex
x\in R
with the vertex
g_{i}(x)\in\partial F_{i}
.
This defines a quotient graph that we denote by
G=X/\sim
. We denote by
\omega:X\rightarrow G
the canonical projection.
Now, for each
i=\{1,\dots,m\}
, we set

F_{i}^{\prime}:=F_{i}\setminus g_{i}\!\bigl(N_{G^{*}}(b_{i})\bigr)\subset X\quad\text{and}\quad B_{i}:=\omega(F_{i}^{\prime})\subset G,

and we define a map

\pi:G\rightarrow G^{*},\qquad\pi(x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if }x\in R,\\
b_{i}&\text{ if }x\in B_{i}.\end{cases}

(7.4)

We collect some basic properties of this construction in the following

Lemma 7.12
.

The graph
G
is a finite tree and the degree of each vertex in
G
is at most
d
.
Moreover:

(a)

The map
\omega
is injective on
R
and on each
F_{i}^{\prime}
.

(b)

The vertex set of
G
decomposes as the disjoint union
V(G)=\omega(R)\;\sqcup\;B_{1}\;\sqcup\;\cdots\;\sqcup\;B_{m}.

(c)

The map
\pi
restricts to the identity on
\omega(R)
and collapses each
B_{i}
to the vertex
b_{i}
.

(d)

The sets
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
are precisely the connected components of
G\setminus\omega(R)
.

Proof.

Points (a)–(c) follow directly from the construction of
G
.

To prove (d), observe first that each
F_{i}^{\prime}
is connected. Indeed, since each

F_{i}
is full, every vertex of
\partial F_{i}
is a leaf of
F_{i}
, and by
Lemma
2.3
, removing finitely many leaves from a tree does not disconnect it. Hence each
F_{i}^{\prime}
is connected, and therefore
its image
B_{i}=\omega(F_{i}^{\prime})
is connected.
It now follows from (b) that
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
are precisely the connected components of
G\setminus\omega(R)
.

We now prove that
G
is a finite tree. Finiteness follows from (b), since
R
and each
F_{i}^{\prime}
are finite.

G
is connected.

By (a),
\omega
restricts to a graph isomorphism between
R
and
\omega(R)
.
Since
R
is connected (as a subtree of the tree
G^{*}
), it follows that
\omega(R)
is connected.
Since each
B_{i}
meets
\omega(R)
along at least one vertex,
G
is also connected.

G
is acyclic.
Indeed, if
G
contained a non-trivial cycle, its image under
\pi
would give a non-trivial closed walk in
G^{*}
. Since
\pi
collapses only the connected subgraphs
B_{i}
,
this would produce a cycle in
G^{*}
, contradicting that
G^{*}
is a tree.

We finally prove that the degree of any vertex
x
in
G
is at most
d
. By (b), we know that
x
either belongs to
\omega(R)
or to some
B_{i}
. If
x\in B_{i}
, there exists a vertex
z\in F_{i}^{\prime}
such that
\omega(z)=x
, thus

\deg_{G}(x)=\deg_{F_{i}}(z)\leq d

since
F_{i}
is by definition a domain in
T_{d}
.
On the other hand, if
x\in\omega(R)
, each neighbor
b_{i}
in
G^{*}
corresponds to exactly one edge in
G
, since
x
is identified with a unique boundary leaf of
F_{i}
, which has a unique neighbor inside
F_{i}
.
Thus

\deg_{G}(x)=\deg_{G^{*}}(\pi(x))\leq d-1.

Hence
\max_{x\in G}\deg_{G}(x)\leq d
.

∎

We now state the reconstruction Theorem:

Theorem 7.13
.

There exists a graph isomorphism
\phi:G\to D
from
G
onto a domain
D\subset T_{d}
.
It satisfies the following properties

(i)

The
\phi
-image of
\omega(R)
coincides with the residual boundary of
D
.

(ii)

the full components of
D
are the images of the graphs
F_{i}
;

(iii)

The map
\phi
induces an isomorphism
\phi^{*}
between
G^{*}
and the stem
D^{*}
of
D
.

Moreover,

\tau(D)=\tau^{*}(G^{*})=m+l-1,

where
l=|E(R)|
is the number of edges in the subgraph
R\subset G^{*}
. In particular,
D
is optimal if and only if

m+l\leq d-1.

Conversely, every domain
D\subset T_{d}
arises, up to isomorphism, from admissible gluing data
(G^{*},(F_{i}),(g_{i}))
by this construction.

Proof.

The existence of
\phi
follows from Lemmas
2.2
and
7.12
.
For simplicity, we henceforth regard
R
as a subset of both
G
and
D
via
the maps
\omega
and
\phi
. This identification is justified since

\omega
is injective on
R
by Lemma
7.12
(a)
, and
\phi
is a graph
isomorphism; in particular, both maps preserve the combinatorial
structure of
R
.
We prove (i)–(iii) :

(i)
Let
x\in R
. By Definition
7.10
(i),

2\leq\deg_{G^{*}}(x)\leq d-1.

Since
\pi
restricts to the identity on
R
, we have

\deg_{G}(x)=\deg_{G^{*}}(x).

After embedding
G
into
T_{d}
, the vertex
x
therefore has degree between
2
and
d-1
inside
D
.
Because
T_{d}
is
d
–regular,
x
has at least one neighbor in
T_{d}\setminus D
, and since its degree in
D
is at least
2
, it is not a leaf. Thus
x
belongs to the residual boundary of
D
.

Conversely, if
y\in B_{i}
, then
y
lies in the image of
F_{i}^{\prime}
. Its degree inside
D
coincides with its degree in the full domain
F_{i}
. Since interior vertices of a full domain are not in the residual boundary, we conclude that the residual boundary of
D
is precisely
R
.

(ii) Since
F_{i}=B_{i}\cup(R\cap\partial^{\prime}B_{i})
inside
G
, its image under
\phi
is

\phi(F_{i})=\phi(B_{i})\cup\bigl(\phi(R)\cap\partial^{\prime}\phi(B_{i})\bigr).

These are the full components of
D
by definition (
7.1
).

(iii)
Collapsing each full component of
D
corresponds exactly to the map
\pi
,
so the stem
D^{*}
of
D
is isomorphic to
G^{*}
.

Using Lemma
7.7
and
D^{*}\simeq G^{*}
, we have now

\tau(D)=\tau^{*}(D^{*})=\tau^{*}(G^{*})=m+l-1.

The optimality criterion follows then from Theorem
4.3
since the condition

\tau(D)\leq d-2
is equivalent to
m+l=\tau(D)+1\leq d-1
.

In the converse direction, given a domain
D\subset T_{d}
, its stem
D^{*}
,
its full components, and the natural identification maps along the residual boundary
provide admissible gluing data.
Applying the above construction produces a graph isomorphic to
D
.
∎

where
\rho:D\to D^{*}
is the canonical projection:

,
