Cross-cultural determinants of AI acceptance in medical education among medical students in China and Pakistan
Authors: Xin Xu, Xin Li, Abdulelah Ahmed Alghamdi, Zahoor Elahi, Shazia Rehman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly introduced into medical education, little is known about the psychological mechanisms through which social influences shape AI acceptance across different cultural contexts. This study addresses this gap by examining whether perceived usefulness and attitude mediate the relationship between subjective norms and AI acceptance, sequentially, among medical students in China and Pakistan.MethodsBetween March and April 2025, a cross-sectional survey was administered to medical students enrolled in accredited universities across two culturally distinct regions. A stratified sampling approach was employed to ensure representativeness across academic years. Data from 1,232 students (Pakistani, n = 551, and Chinese, n = 681) were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM) and multi-group analysis. The hypothesized model assessed the direct and indirect effects of subjective norms on AI acceptance, with perceived usefulness and attitude as mediators. Measurement invariance was tested to validate cross-cultural comparisons.ResultsIn the Chinese sample, subjective norms had a significant direct effect on AI acceptance (β = 0.231, p < 0.01), while this effect was not significant in the Pakistani sample (β = 0.113, p = 0.125). In both samples, subjective norms significantly influenced perceived usefulness and attitude, which, in turn, mediated the relationship between these variables and AI acceptance. The sequential mediation path was significant in both samples. Multi-group analysis confirmed full measurement and structural invariance, supporting the model’s cross-cultural validity.ConclusionThe findings underscore the importance of culturally responsive strategies when promoting AI acceptance in medical education. While the sequential mediation of perceived usefulness and attitude appears robust across contexts, the influence of social norms on behavioral intention to use AI varies across cultural and institutional environments, underscoring the need for tailored engagement and implementation approaches.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09675-w.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become an increasingly influential component of medical education, reshaping how knowledge is delivered, assessed, and applied in training environments [1, 2]. AI-based tools, such as intelligent tutoring systems, clinical simulations, and large language models, are widely promoted for their potential to personalize learning, enhance diagnostic reasoning, and support clinical skill development [3–5]. In the context of medical education, AI inclusion refers to integrating AI-driven educational tools and decision-support technologies into learning environments to support knowledge acquisition, clinical reasoning, and skill development. Such tools may include AI-supported tutoring systems, automated feedback systems, virtual patients, and generative AI platforms that assist with problem-solving and clinical case analysis [6–9]. While these technologies have the potential to improve learning efficiency and clinical competence, their educational value ultimately depends on whether students perceive them as beneficial and are willing to incorporate them into their learning processes [6, 10, 11]. Despite this rapid technological advancement and institutional interest, AI acceptance among medical students remains uneven across educational settings and cultural contexts. Prior research has identified multiple barriers to effective uptake, including limited training, variability in digital readiness, ethical concerns, and unequal institutional support, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [12–14]. Although AI technologies offer considerable educational benefits, the literature also highlights potential challenges and risks associated with their use in academic environments. Studies suggest that AI can enhance personalized learning, improve access to information, and support diagnostic reasoning among medical students [6, 15]. Beyond immediate educational benefits, concerns have emerged regarding the long-term implications of AI reliance within medical training. Excessive dependence on AI-generated recommendations may reduce opportunities for independent clinical reasoning and critical evaluation, skills that remain fundamental to safe medical practice [16–18]. Scholars have also raised concerns that routine reliance on AI-assisted problem solving could weaken diagnostic judgment, diminish reflective learning processes, and encourage passive knowledge acquisition. In addition, the widespread availability of generative AI systems has intensified debates regarding academic integrity, authorship, and the ethical use of AI-generated content within higher education. These concerns are particularly relevant in medical education, where future healthcare professionals are expected to develop strong analytical capabilities, professional autonomy, and accountability in clinical decision-making. Consequently, understanding the factors that influence students' acceptance of AI requires balancing its perceived educational value against concerns regarding responsible and sustainable use.

Importantly, students’ engagement with AI in medical education is not driven by technological availability alone but is shaped by psychological perceptions and social environments [19]. In this study, AI acceptance refers to students’ behavioral intention to use AI-enabled learning technologies during their academic training. Acceptance, therefore, reflects the extent to which medical students are prepared to integrate AI tools into their study practices, clinical simulations, or problem-solving activities during their medical education [20, 21]. Differences in social expectations, institutional norms, and prior exposure to educational technologies contribute to substantial variation in students’ willingness to use AI-based tools, especially across cultures [19]. This disconnect between technological promise and user acceptance highlights a critical gap in current research: while AI continues to advance rapidly, the social and cognitive mechanisms underlying its acceptance among medical students remain insufficiently understood. Addressing this gap requires a theoretically grounded examination of AI acceptance that integrates social influence, individual perceptions, and cultural context.

To explain how students develop intentions to use emerging educational technologies, prior research has frequently drawn on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), defined as the degree to which a student believes that using a technology will enhance academic performance—and [22]. TAM has demonstrated strong explanatory power across multiple domains, including business, healthcare, and education [23–25]. In medical education, where technologies such as AI-based simulations, virtual patients, and clinical decision support systems are increasingly embedded in training, these cognitive evaluations remain central to understanding AI acceptance decisions. However, the AI acceptance in medical education is not determined solely by individual beliefs. To account for the influence of social context, TAM2 extends the original model by incorporating subjective norms, defined as perceived social expectations from important referent groups [26]. TAM2 proposes that social influence affects AI acceptance both directly and indirectly through perceived usefulness, particularly in structured or high-stakes environments [27]. This extension is especially relevant in medical education, where faculty guidance, peer norms, and professional expectations strongly shape students’ learning behaviors and technology-related decisions [28]. As medical students evaluate whether AI tools can enhance academic performance or clinical competence, their perceptions are likely shaped by both personal experience and the attitudes of instructors and peers [1]. By integrating cognitive appraisals with social influence, TAM2 provides a theoretically appropriate framework for examining AI acceptance in medical education. Its emphasis on multi-pathway mechanisms makes it particularly well-suited to exploring how social norms translate into acceptance through perceived usefulness and attitudinal evaluation in culturally embedded educational settings.

Theoretical framework and hypothesis development

The role of subjective norms in shaping technology use

Subjective norms refer to individuals’ perceptions of social pressure from significant others to engage in a particular behavior [21] and represent a core social influence construct in extended technology acceptance frameworks such as TAM2 and UTAUT [26]. In educational contexts, subjective norms reflect perceived expectations from peers, instructors, and institutional culture that can encourage or discourage engagement with emerging technologies, including AI-based learning tools [29]. Such social influences are often more pronounced in collectivist or hierarchical educational settings, where conformity to group norms and deference to authority figures play a central role in shaping behavior [30–32]. Recent empirical evidence supports the relevance of subjective norms in AI-acceptance within educational settings. Studies have shown that perceived endorsement by peers and instructors is positively associated with students’ intentions to use AI tools such as ChatGPT, particularly in university environments where such technologies are newly introduced or embedded within institutional expectations [33, 34]. At the same time, findings across contexts remain mixed. Some research suggests that subjective norms may exert weaker or non-significant effects in settings characterized by greater learner autonomy or individualistic cultural orientations, highlighting the potential role of contextual and cultural variation in shaping normative influence [35].

The positioning of subjective norms as the starting point of the proposed framework is grounded in TAM2 and related social influence theories. In TAM2, subjective norms are conceptualized as external social influences that affect technology-related decision-making both directly and indirectly through cognitive evaluations, particularly perceived usefulness [26]. Similarly, the Theory of Planned Behavior and UTAUT identify normative influence as an antecedent of behavioral intention, reflecting the role of perceived expectations from important referent groups in shaping individual judgments and actions [21, 36]. In educational environments, students often evaluate new technologies within broader social and institutional contexts, suggesting that encouragement from instructors, peers, and academic institutions may influence not only acceptance intentions but also perceptions of the usefulness and desirability of AI technologies. Consistent with these theoretical perspectives, the present study positions subjective norms as an exogenous construct that initiates the cognitive and affective processes underlying AI acceptance. Building on this literature, the present study examines subjective norms as a direct antecedent of AI acceptance among undergraduate medical students in two culturally distinct contexts. Perceived usefulness as a cognitive mediator

Within the TAM2 framework, beyond its direct influence, perceived usefulness plays an important mediating role by translating social influence into AI acceptance intentions, particularly in professional and educational settings characterized by technological innovation, such as AI integration in medical education [37]. Empirical research consistently supports this mediating mechanism. Prior studies have shown that subjective norms positively influence perceived usefulness, which, in turn, predicts the intention to use AI-based systems in healthcare and educational contexts. For example, Li and Wang (2024) [38] demonstrated that perceived usefulness mediated the association between social influence and adoption of AI diagnostic tools among healthcare users, while Ezeudoka and Fan (2024) [39] reported a similar mediating effect in pharmacy education settings involving AI-enabled technologies. Evidence from educational contexts further suggests that perceived usefulness often serves as a more stable predictor of AI acceptance than social norms alone, highlighting its role as a key cognitive pathway in adoption processes [40, 41]. Drawing on this theoretical and empirical foundation, the present study proposes that perceived usefulness functions as a mediator through which subjective norms shape medical students’ acceptance of AI technologies. Attitude toward using AI as an affective mediator

Attitude is a central construct in behavioral decision-making theories, including the Theory of Reasoned Action, and is defined in technology acceptance research as an individual’s overall evaluative judgment toward using a particular system [22, 36]. Within TAM-based frameworks, attitude represents the affective and motivational response to technology use and serves as a mediator through which cognitive beliefs influence behavioral intention, particularly in discretionary adoption contexts [42]. In AI acceptance research, willingness to use AI technologies generally refers to an individual’s behavioral intention or readiness to use AI-based systems in learning or professional activities, reflecting the degree to which users are inclined to accept and integrate emerging technologies into their routines [21, 43, 44]. In medical education, where cognitive demands and emotional pressure often characterize learning environments, students’ attitudes toward AI-based tools may play a critical role in shaping acceptance [45]. Prior research suggests that when students perceive AI technologies as useful, engaging, and aligned with their learning needs, they are more likely to develop favorable attitudes that facilitate AI acceptance [34, 41, 46]. Importantly, attitude is frequently shaped by perceived usefulness, such that positive cognitive evaluations of AI tools foster more favorable emotional responses, which in turn strengthen intentions to accept AI [26, 47]. In this respect, attitude can function as an affective pathway linking social influence and AI acceptance. Based on this rationale, the present study conceptualizes attitude toward AI use as an independent mediator between subjective norms and AI acceptance, acknowledging that affective receptiveness to innovation may vary across educational and cultural contexts. Contextual and cultural influences on AI acceptance

The acceptance of AI in educational settings is shaped not only by technological features but also by the cultural and institutional contexts in which learners operate. Prior research has consistently shown that technology acceptance processes vary across societies, particularly with respect to social influence, perceived usefulness, and behavioral intention [48, 49]. In collectivist and high power-distance educational cultures, social expectations and conformity to authority figures tend to exert stronger normative pressure, making subjective norms a more salient determinant of AI acceptance in individualistic contexts [50]. Beyond cultural orientation, institutional trust also plays an important role in shaping students’ responses to AI-based educational tools. In settings where confidence in educational institutions and technological systems is high, learners may be more willing to accept AI technologies even without extensive personal experience [51]. Conversely, limited infrastructure or skepticism toward automated systems can weaken perceived usefulness and reduce AI acceptance intentions, irrespective of potential functional benefits. Empirical evidence further indicates that the mechanisms proposed by TAM are not culturally invariant. Studies have demonstrated that collectivist orientations strengthen the influence of subjective norms on behavioral intention and that trust and conformity are particularly influential in hierarchical educational environments common in many Asian societies [52, 53]. Despite these insights, comparative research examining whether the psychological pathways linking social influence, cognitive evaluation, and affective response operate similarly across cultures remains limited, especially in AI-integrated medical education. Addressing this gap, the present study adopts a cross-cultural comparative design to examine the robustness of TAM2-based mediation mechanisms across two culturally distinct student populations.

Study gaps and contribution

Despite the extensive application of the TAM in educational research, important gaps remain in understanding AI acceptance within undergraduate medical education. First, although subjective norms are widely recognized as a critical social influence factor in extended TAM frameworks, limited empirical work has examined the sequential psychological mechanisms through which subjective norms translate into AI acceptance via both cognitive (perceived usefulness) and affective (attitude) pathways. Most prior studies have examined these constructs independently or tested direct associations, rather than evaluating an integrated mediation framework that captures how social influence shapes beliefs, attitudes, and ultimately behavioral acceptance within AI-enhanced health professions education. Second, evidence on AI acceptance in medical education remains largely context-specific. The predominance of single-country studies limits understanding of whether TAM-based psychological mechanisms operate consistently across cultural contexts. Given that social norms and perceptions of technology may vary across sociocultural contexts, cross-cultural validation of mediation pathways is necessary to establish the robustness and generalizability of AI acceptance models.

China and Pakistan represent two rapidly developing medical education systems with distinct educational ecosystems. Chinese medical universities have experienced substantial digital transformation, supported by strong national investment in artificial intelligence, smart classrooms, and technology-enhanced learning initiatives [54–56]. AI applications in medical imaging, diagnostics, and simulation-based training are increasingly integrated into curricula [57, 58]. In contrast, Pakistani medical education operates within a more resource-limited environment, where AI integration is emerging but remains less systematically embedded across institutions [59–61]. Differences in technological infrastructure, institutional policy support, and sociocultural norms regarding innovation and authority structures may shape how social influence affects perceptions and acceptance of technology across contexts [62, 63].

In addition to structural differences, cultural characteristics may further influence the role of subjective norms in AI acceptance. Both China and Pakistan are commonly described as collectivist societies in which group expectations, peer influence, and authority figures often play an important role in shaping individual decision-making [21, 63, 64]. In such contexts, students may place greater weight on the opinions of instructors, peers, and institutional authorities when evaluating new educational technologies. However, variations in technological exposure and institutional support across these contexts may lead to differences in the strength of social influence on medical students' acceptance of AIs.

Together, these contextual and theoretical gaps underscore the need for an integrated and culturally comparative framework that captures the social, cognitive, and affective processes underlying AI acceptance in medical education. Consequently, the present study makes four key contributions. First, it proposes and empirically tests a sequential mediation model that links subjective norms to AI acceptance via perceived usefulness and attitude. Second, it applies this framework specifically within undergraduate medical education, a rapidly evolving yet under-examined context of AI integration. Third, it adopts a cross-cultural comparative design involving medical students from China and Pakistan, thereby extending TAM research beyond single-context investigations. Finally, it employs multi-group structural equation modeling to assess measurement invariance and structural equivalence, providing a rigorous evaluation of the cultural robustness of the proposed framework. Methods

Study design, participants, and sampling

This study employed a correlational predictive design grounded in the TAM to examine the relationships among subjective norms, perceived usefulness, attitude, and AI acceptance. Data were collected using a cross-sectional survey administered to undergraduate medical students in Islamabad (Pakistan) and Changsha (China), between March and April 2025. A stratified sampling approach was used to obtain proportionate representation across all five academic years of the undergraduate medical curriculum (Years 1–5). Stratification by year of study was implemented across the sampled programs to enhance representativeness within each city-based sample, reduce potential sampling bias, and facilitate meaningful comparisons of medical training stages between students from Islamabad and Changsha.

Sample size estimation followed structural equation modeling and multi-group analysis guidelines, which recommend at least 150 participants per group for adequate power [65–67]. The final sample comprised 1,232 students (Pakistan = 551; China = 681), exceeding recommended thresholds and providing robust power for hypothesis testing, measurement invariance, and cross-national comparisons across the two national contexts. Data were screened for quality and suitability. Responses showing incomplete submission, straight-lining, or completion times under two minutes were excluded. Cases with more than 10% missing data were removed, while those with less than 10% missing within a construct were handled using mean imputation. Assumptions of normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity were assessed, and extreme or anomalous values were addressed prior to inferential analysis.

Eligibility criteria

Participants included undergraduate students enrolled in full-time medical degree programs (MBBS) at accredited medical colleges and universities in Islamabad, Pakistan, and Changsha, China. Students from all five academic years (Year 1 to Year 5) were eligible to participate.

Inclusion criteria

Currently enrolled in a recognized undergraduate medical program, studying in any academic year (Year 1 to Year 5), and able and willing to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Postgraduate medical trainees (e.g., interns, residents), students enrolled in non-medical or allied health programs, and international students, to ensure cultural and linguistic consistency within each country sample.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using an online, self-administered questionnaire distributed through official institutional channels, including university mailing lists, learning management systems, and academic messaging platforms (WeChat in China; WhatsApp and email in Pakistan). Survey links included a study introduction and informed consent statement, and participants provided electronic consent before proceeding. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with no incentives offered. To prevent duplicate responses, submissions were limited to one per device or per institutional email address, depending on the platform. Participants could withdraw before submission. All responses were stored in encrypted form and accessible only to the research team.

To ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence, the survey instrument was adapted for use in both Pakistan and China. In Pakistan, where English is the language of medical instruction, the questionnaire was administered in English without modification. In China, the instrument was translated into Mandarin using a forward–backward translation procedure. A bilingual expert conducted the initial translation, followed by an independent back-translation. A bilingual expert panel resolved discrepancies. The finalized version was pilot-tested with 35 medical students via snowball sampling, confirming clarity, comprehension, and cultural relevance, and requiring no major revisions.

Measures

General demographics

Age, gender, year of study, and type of institution (public or private) were reported. Additionally, participants were asked whether they had prior exposure to AI-based educational or clinical tools, and how frequently they used technology for academic purposes.

Subjective norms

Subjective norms were assessed using the Subjective Norms Scale (3 items), adapted from TAM2 and UTAUT, which captures perceived social pressure from faculty members, clinical mentors, and peers to use AI-based tools [21, 26]. Items reflected encouragement and expectations regarding AI use in medical education (e.g., “Faculty members, clinical mentors, and peers who influence my education think that I should use AI tools”). Responses ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). This scale has demonstrated reliability and validity in prior educational and healthcare technology studies [27, 68, 69].

Perceived usefulness

Perceived usefulness was measured using Davis’ Perceived Usefulness Scale [22], a widely used six-item instrument in TAM and TAM2 research that assesses the extent to which AI tools enhance learning efficiency, productivity, and academic performance. Items were adapted to the medical education context (e.g., “Using AI tools improves my academic performance”). Responses were recorded on a 7-point agreement scale, and item scores were averaged, with higher values indicating greater perceived usefulness. Previous studies report high internal consistency for this scale (Cronbach’s α > 0.90) in educational and healthcare settings [70, 71].

Attitude towards AI use

Attitude toward AI use was assessed using Davis’ Attitude Toward Use Scale, a six-item semantic differential measure derived from TAM [22], capturing students’ affective evaluations of AI use in medical education. Participants responded to the prompt “Using AI tools in my medical education would be…” using bipolar adjective pairs (e.g., bad–good, foolish–wise, unpleasant–pleasant) on a 7-point scale. Higher scores indicated a more favorable attitude. This measure has demonstrated strong reliability and construct validity across technology adoption studies [70, 72–74].

Behavioral intention

Behavioral intention to use AI tools was assessed using Davis’ Behavioral Intention Scale [22], adapted for AI-enabled educational and clinical learning technologies. Items assessed students’ intentions to use and continue using AI-based educational and clinical learning systems, referring to technologies such as intelligent tutoring platforms, virtual patient simulations, clinical decision-support systems, and generative AI tools used to assist knowledge acquisition, clinical reasoning, and academic problem-solving (e.g., “I intend to use AI tools in my medical education”). Responses were rated on a 7-point agreement scale, with higher scores indicating stronger intention. This scale has shown high internal consistency in prior TAM2-based research (α ≥ 0.88) [26, 71, 75]. The questionnaire is attached in Appendix A.

Confounding variables

Given the behavioral and cross-cultural nature of this study, year of study, gender, and prior exposure to AI tools were included as control variables. Prior exposure to AI tools refers to students’ previous interaction with AI-enabled educational or clinical learning systems commonly used in medical training. These systems may include AI-assisted tutoring platforms, virtual patient simulations, clinical decision support systems, automated feedback tools, and generative AI applications for academic support. In medical education, such technologies are increasingly used to support personalized learning, diagnostic reasoning, and clinical problem-solving [6, 76]. Because prior experience with these systems may shape students’ perceptions of usefulness and their willingness to use AI technologies, prior exposure was included as a control variable in the analysis.

Ethical protocols

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the relevant institutional review boards in both participating countries. In Pakistan, approval was granted by the Ethics Review Committee of the Foundation University Medical College (Approval number: RT/4091). In China, ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of Changsha Medical University (ref. # A-24/5661). All participants were provided with a detailed informed consent statement at the beginning of the online survey. The consent form outlined the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw at any time before submission, and assurances of anonymity and confidentiality. Only those who provided digital consent were permitted to proceed with the questionnaire. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). All collected data were stored securely in encrypted formats, accessible only to the research team, and used exclusively for academic research purposes.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS (v28) and AMOS (v24). Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables and study constructs, and preliminary diagnostics were performed to assess normality, linearity, and multicollinearity. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, and construct validity was assessed through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Model fit was evaluated using commonly recommended indices, including the chi-square statistic, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), following established structural equation modeling guidelines [77]. Composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) were calculated to assess convergent validity. Bivariate associations among key variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Mediation effects were tested using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (95%), and indirect effects were considered significant when the confidence intervals did not include zero. Subjective norms were specified as the independent variable, AI acceptance (intention to use AI) as the dependent variable, perceived usefulness as the first mediator, and attitude towards AI use as the second mediator, consistent with the hypothesized sequential mediation model of AI acceptance. All hypothesis tests used a significance level of p < 0.05, and standardized path coefficients were reported. In addition, multi-group structural equation modeling was conducted to examine whether the proposed model functioned equivalently across Pakistani and Chinese samples. Measurement and structural invariance were tested using a sequence of nested models with increasing constraints. Invariance decisions were based on changes in model fit indices across models, consistent with recommended criteria for cross-group SEM comparisons [77]. This approach enabled meaningful comparison of the hypothesized relationships across cultural contexts.

Results

Common method bias

Common method bias (CMB) was assessed using both procedural and statistical approaches. Procedurally, anonymity and confidentiality were ensured, and scale design minimized priming effects, consistent with recommended practices for reducing method bias [78]. Statistically, Harman’s single-factor test and a CFA marker-variable approach were applied. According to established criteria, the absence of a dominant single factor and negligible shared variance attributable to the marker construct indicate that CMB is unlikely to pose a serious threat [78, 79]. The results indicated that CMB was not a significant concern and did not threaten the validity of the observed relationships.

General demographics

The final sample comprised 1,232 medical students, including 551 (44.72%) from Pakistan and 681 (55.28%) from China (Table 1). Overall, the gender distribution was comparable across the two groups, with no statistically significant difference observed. Male participants constituted 52.3% of the Pakistani group and 50.1% of the Chinese group. In contrast, a statistically significant difference emerged in participants’ ages, with Chinese students (M = 22.0, SD = 1.78) being slightly older than their Pakistani counterparts (M = 21.0, SD = 1.91). However, when comparing years of study, no significant differences were found between groups, suggesting a relatively balanced academic stage across both populations. Institutional affiliation, however, differed significantly by country. A larger proportion of Chinese students were enrolled in public universities (71.95%) compared to Pakistani students (51.72%), a statistically significant difference. In addition, a substantial gap was observed in prior exposure to AI-based educational or clinical tools. A significantly higher percentage of Chinese students (86.2%) reported prior exposure to AI compared to Pakistani students (53.9%). This difference may reflect varying degrees of institutional integration of AI technologies across the two national contexts. Finally, the frequency of technology use for academic purposes also differed significantly between groups. Chinese students reported more frequent engagement with educational technologies, with 22.9% indicating very frequent use compared to only 8.5% among Pakistani students. Model fit analysis

Discussion

The present study explored how subjective norms, perceived usefulness, and attitudes toward AI influence medical students’ intention to use AI technologies in two urban academic settings—Islamabad (Pakistan) and Changsha (China). By integrating theoretical insights from the TAM and TAM2, the study proposed a sequential mediation model to capture both the cognitive and affective pathways through which social influence translates into behavioral intention. Importantly, the research extends the existing technology acceptance literature by incorporating multi-group structural equation modeling to compare these mechanisms across city-based samples in two national contexts. Overall, the findings reveal both shared patterns and context-specific differences, offering new insight into how AI acceptance unfolds among medical students in urban educational environments within emerging economies.

The results for Hypothesis 1 revealed a clear divergence between the two city-based samples: subjective norms were directly associated with AI acceptance among medical students in Changsha but not among those in Islamabad. This pattern suggests that while students in both contexts may perceive social encouragement to use AI tools, the extent to which such normative pressure translates into behavioral intention depends on broader institutional and cultural characteristics of the educational environment. In the Chinese medical education system, where universities have undergone rapid digital transformation and educational practices are often shaped by hierarchical academic structures, social expectations from instructors, institutions, and professional authorities can strongly influence students’ learning behaviors [63, 64, 80]. Within such environments, faculty members' or academic institutions' endorsement of AI tools may therefore translate more readily into behavioral intention. This interpretation is consistent with prior research indicating that technology-related decisions in Chinese educational contexts are strongly influenced by collective norms, institutional guidance, and trust in authoritative knowledge sources [63, 81]. In contrast, the absence of a significant direct association in the Pakistani sample suggests that social influence alone may be insufficient to motivate AI acceptance within this academic context. Pakistan’s medical education system is characterized by greater variability in technological infrastructure and less systematic integration of AI-based learning tools across institutions [59, 82]. In such settings, students may rely more heavily on personal assessments of the practical value and academic relevance of AI technologies before translating social expectations into behavioral intentions. Previous studies in Pakistan similarly indicate that learners often prioritize instrumental usefulness and institutional support when deciding whether to adopt emerging educational technologies [83, 84]. Consequently, normative pressure may operate more indirectly in this context, exerting its influence through cognitive evaluations, such as perceived usefulness, or through favorable attitudes toward AI tools. Taken together, these findings suggest that the behavioral impact of subjective norms is shaped by both cultural orientation and the maturity of the educational system. In environments where AI technologies are visibly integrated into institutional practices and endorsed by academic authorities, normative influence may directly encourage acceptance of AI. In contrast, in contexts where technological integration is still developing, social influence may operate primarily through mediated psychological pathways rather than direct behavioral pressure.

The findings for Hypothesis 2 indicated that perceived usefulness functioned as a significant mediator linking subjective norms and AI acceptance in both cultural contexts. Notably, in the Pakistani sample, the indirect association via perceived usefulness was significant even though the direct association between subjective norms and acceptance was not. This pattern suggests that normative cues may influence students’ evaluations of the practical utility of AI tools, and that perceived usefulness represents the primary pathway through which social encouragement translates into AI acceptance in this academic environment. In Pakistan’s medical education system, where the integration of AI-based learning tools remains relatively uneven and institutional exposure to such technologies varies across universities, students may rely more strongly on their personal assessment of whether AI tools can meaningfully support their academic performance or clinical training before deciding to adopt them [21, 82]. In the Chinese sample, perceived usefulness also served as a robust mediator, alongside a significant direct association between subjective norms and acceptance, indicating that both indirect (via perceived usefulness) and direct normative pathways were evident. This pattern aligns with the Chinese higher education environment, where rapid digital transformation, strong institutional promotion of educational technologies, and structured learning systems often reinforce both social expectations and perceived functional benefits of technology use [63, 80, 85]. In such settings, students may simultaneously respond to normative encouragement from instructors and institutions while recognizing the practical advantages of AI-supported learning tools. These findings are consistent with TAM-based research that emphasizes perceived usefulness as a central cognitive belief in AI acceptance and a key mechanism by which social influence translates into behavioral intention, particularly for emerging technologies such as AI [46, 86]. Cross-cultural scholarship further suggests that while the immediacy of normative pressure may vary across cultural contexts, evaluations of usefulness tend to remain a stable determinant of AI acceptance across educational systems [87, 88]. Taken together, the results indicate that perceived usefulness operates as a culturally robust mediating mechanism, while the relative strength of direct social influence may depend on how educational institutions structure technology integration and guide students’ learning practices.

Findings related to Hypothesis 3 indicated that attitude toward AI use served as a significant mediator between subjective norms and AI acceptance in both the Islamabad (Pakistan) and Changsha (China) samples. This pattern suggests that perceived social encouragement is associated with more favorable affective evaluations of AI, which in turn relate to stronger intentions to use such technologies among students in these two urban academic settings. Importantly, this mediating pathway was evident even in the Islamabad sample, where subjective norms were not directly associated with acceptance, highlighting the role of attitude as an indirect mechanism through which normative cues translate into behavioral intention within this academic environment. This pattern is consistent with prior TAM-based and AI acceptance research, indicating that attitude represents a stable affective pathway linking social influence to AI-related intentions in educational contexts [86, 89, 90]. Although the mediating role of attitude was observed in both samples, it was more pronounced in the Changsha context. This may reflect features of the Chinese higher education system, where rapid technological modernization and institutional endorsement of digital learning tools have normalized the use of advanced educational technologies [88, 91]. In such environments, students may be more likely to internalize socially endorsed innovations and develop favorable attitudes toward AI-supported learning. By contrast, in Pakistan’s medical education system—where AI integration remains comparatively less systematic and exposure to AI-based educational technologies varies across institutions—students may interpret social cues more cautiously and rely more heavily on their own evaluations of the academic value of AI tools. These contextual differences may explain why attitudinal mediation was present in both settings but comparatively stronger in the Chinese sample. Overall, these findings align with cross-cultural evidence suggesting that attitudes function as robust mediators across diverse technological and educational contexts, including AI-based learning environments [92]. Consequently, the results underscore the importance of fostering positive affective orientations toward AI in medical education. When students perceive AI tools as supportive of their academic and clinical learning, favorable attitudes can serve as a key psychological bridge linking social encouragement with AI acceptance.

Findings related to Hypothesis 4 indicated a significant sequential mediation pathway linking subjective norms to AI acceptance through perceived usefulness and attitude in both city-based samples, although the magnitude of this pathway differed across contexts. In the Chinese sample, the sequential indirect association was stronger, suggesting that social expectations were more closely associated with perceptions of usefulness, which, in turn, were associated with more favorable attitudes and higher intentions to accept AI. This pattern aligns with characteristics of the Chinese higher education system, in which universities have undergone rapid digital transformation and advanced learning technologies are often promoted through institutional policies and faculty endorsement [80, 85]. In such environments, students may be more likely to interpret social encouragement from instructors and peers as signals that AI tools are academically valuable and institutionally supported. As a result, normative influence may more readily translate into cognitive evaluations of usefulness and positive affective responses toward AI-supported learning. These findings are consistent with prior research suggesting that, in East Asian and higher-power-distance educational contexts, social influence can be more readily internalized by students’ perceptions and attitudes toward emerging technologies [93, 94]. In contrast, although the same sequential pattern was present in the Pakistani sample, the indirect associations were comparatively weaker. This difference may reflect characteristics of the Pakistani medical education environment, where the integration of AI-based learning tools remains comparatively less uniform and institutional exposure to such technologies varies across universities. In such contexts, students may require clearer evidence of the practical academic benefits of AI tools before social encouragement translates into strong perceptions of usefulness and favorable attitudes. Consequently, while social influence still contributes to AI acceptance, its effects may operate more gradually through students’ evaluations of how AI technologies support their learning and clinical training. Cross-cultural research similarly indicates that cognitive and affective mediation mechanisms commonly underlie AI acceptance, but the strength of these pathways can vary with the technological maturity and institutional integration of digital learning tools within educational systems [95, 96]. Taken together, the present findings suggest that subjective norms are most strongly associated with AI acceptance when social endorsement is accompanied by visible academic utility and positive user experiences. From an applied perspective, this implies that strategies to promote AI acceptance in medical education should combine institutional encouragement with efforts to demonstrate the practical educational value of AI tools and to foster positive user experiences, particularly in contexts where technological integration is still evolving.

Furthermore, multi-group analysis supported structural invariance, indicating that key constructs were conceptualized similarly across Pakistani and Chinese samples and that cross-group differences reflect substantive contextual variation rather than measurement bias. Social influence was more salient in the Chinese sample, whereas in Pakistan, its association with AI acceptance appeared primarily indirect and required cognitive or affective reinforcement. This difference may reflect characteristics of the two academic environments: Chinese medical universities have increasingly integrated digital technologies and AI-supported learning within structured institutional frameworks, thereby strengthening the influence of social endorsement from instructors and institutions. In contrast, in Pakistan’s medical education system, where the integration of AI-based learning tools remains comparatively less systematic and technological exposure varies across institutions, students may rely more heavily on personal evaluations of usefulness and attitudes before accepting and using such technologies. Overall, the findings suggest that while the AI acceptance framework is broadly applicable, the relative importance of its components varies across cultural and institutional contexts, underscoring the need for context-sensitive implementation strategies.

While the present findings support the importance of promoting AI acceptance in medical education, they should not be interpreted as advocating unrestricted reliance on AI technologies. Although AI-based tools can enhance access to information, support clinical learning, and improve educational efficiency, excessive dependence on AI may also create unintended challenges for medical training. Scholars have cautioned that overreliance on AI-generated recommendations could reduce opportunities for independent critical thinking, reflective learning, and the development of diagnostic reasoning skills that remain essential for safe clinical practice. Furthermore, routine reliance on AI-assisted decision-making may weaken learners’ confidence in exercising clinical judgment and professional autonomy, particularly when AI outputs are accepted without sufficient evaluation or verification. Concerns have also been raised regarding academic integrity and the responsible use of generative AI in educational settings. Consequently, efforts to increase AI acceptance should be accompanied by educational strategies that promote critical evaluation, ethical awareness, and responsible human oversight of AI-supported learning and decision-making processes.

Study implications

This study contributes to technology acceptance theory by extending TAM and TAM2 by identifying a sequential cognitive–affective pathway linking subjective norms, perceived usefulness, attitude, and AI acceptance across two cultural contexts. The observed cross-group differences in direct and indirect effects underscore the importance of multi-group modeling and caution against assuming structural uniformity when applying technology acceptance frameworks across transnational contexts. Practically, the findings suggest that AI acceptance strategies in medical education should be context-sensitive and operationally grounded. In China, where subjective norms were directly associated with AI acceptance, institutions may strengthen AI acceptance by embedding explicit AI endorsement in curricula, assessments, and clinical training, and by leveraging faculty champions and senior clinicians to model AI use. In Pakistan, where normative influence operated primarily through perceived usefulness and attitude, efforts should prioritize experiential exposure, such as piloting AI-based case simulations, integrating AI tools into problem-based learning, and offering structured workshops that demonstrate practical educational value. Across both contexts, the consistent mediating role of attitude highlights the importance of fostering positive affective orientations toward AI through gradual curricular integration, supervised hands-on use, and transparent discussion of ethical and professional implications. Overall, the results emphasize that effective AI implementation requires culturally adaptive strategies that combine social endorsement with meaningful learning experiences, rather than uniform approaches to AI acceptance.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite its methodological rigor and cross-cultural scope, this study has several limitations that should be considered. Methodologically, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reliance on self-reported survey data may introduce social desirability or CMB, despite procedural and statistical controls. Future studies using longitudinal, experimental, or mixed-method designs could provide stronger evidence regarding temporal dynamics and behavioral mechanisms. Contextually, the sample was drawn from medical students in urban universities in Islamabad and Changsha, which may limit generalizability to students in rural settings or institutions with different levels of technological infrastructure. Although structural invariance was supported, within-country institutional and cultural heterogeneity was not examined and warrants further investigation. Conceptually, the study focused on a selected set of TAM2 constructs and did not include factors such as perceived ease of use, trust in AI, ethical concerns, or actual usage experience. Additionally, variability in students’ prior exposure to AI applications was not fully captured. Future research could incorporate these factors or stratify participants by AI experience to provide a more comprehensive understanding of AI acceptance in medical education.

Conclusion

This study advances understanding of AI acceptance in medical education by demonstrating how social influence, perceived usefulness, and attitude jointly shape students’ intentions within a unified, sequential framework. By adopting a cross-cultural design and confirming structural invariance across city-based samples from Islamabad (Pakistan) and Changsha (China), the study provides novel evidence that core mechanisms of AI acceptance are broadly stable. At the same time, the strength and configuration of specific pathways vary across local sociocultural and institutional contexts. These findings extend TAM2 by highlighting the contextual dependence of subjective norms and the importance of culturally sensitive approaches to AI implementation within specific urban academic environments. From both theoretical and practical perspectives, the results underscore that effective integration of AI in medical education requires strategies aligned with local social contexts, institutional readiness, and learners’ evaluative processes, rather than AI acceptance models or assumptions of national-level generalizability.
