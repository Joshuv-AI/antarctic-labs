Cerebral perfusion features linking subclinical cardiac and aortic dysfunction to vascular brain injury
Authors: Ye Zhang, Timothy M. Hughes, Wenxin Zhang, Lidia Glodzik, Sudipto Dolui, Thiago Oscar Goulart, Daniel A. Nation, Amy R. Mahar, Amani M. Norling, Lewis A. Lipsitz, Murray A. Mittleman, Yuan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSubclinical cardiovascular remodeling may impair cerebral hemodynamics and contribute to dementia, but the perfusion features linking subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction to cerebrovascular injury remain unclear.METHODSWe studied 1748 UK Biobank participants with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI, and structural brain MRI. Six cardiac MRI biomarkers were analyzed individually and as a composite score. Cerebral perfusion was assessed by cerebral blood flow (CBF) and arterial transit time (ATT).RESULTSGreater subclinical cardiac and aortic dysfunction was associated with lower CBF and prolonged ATT. Participants with low CBF and prolonged ATT had the greatest white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden, equivalent to 5.5 years of age‐related accumulation. CBF and ATT jointly mediated 53% of the association between subclinical cardiac and aortic dysfunction and WMH burden.DISCUSSIONImpaired cerebral perfusion, captured by lower perfusion volume and delayed perfusion timing, may be a mechanism linking subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction to brain vascular injury.

BACKGROUND

Vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) are increasingly recognized as central mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of late‐life dementias, with vascular injury representing not only a primary contributor to “pure” vascular dementia but also an important, yet often overlooked, contributor to Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 , 2 The brain is a highly perfused organ with substantial demands for oxygen and nutrients delivered via the blood but limited energy reserves, making optimal cerebral blood flow (CBF) essential for maintaining brain health. 3 Cerebral hypoperfusion, reflecting a combination of diminished vascular integrity, cortical atrophy, and decreased neuronal activity, has been implicated in stroke and dementia. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 With rapid advances in non‐invasive perfusion imaging, detecting early abnormalities in cerebral perfusion may facilitate the early detection and prevention of vascular brain injury and its sequelae. 8 , 9 Yet, the specific cerebral perfusion features linking cardiovascular alterations, especially during the subclinical phase, to brain injuries are not well characterized.

Approximately 20% of cardiac output is allocated to the brain. 10 Clinically, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as coronary heart disease and heart failure, commonly co‐occur with cognitive decline and AD and related dementias (ADRD), and have been linked to cerebrovascular injury. 1 , 2 Beyond overt clinical stages, alterations in cardiac structure and function often begin decades earlier, marking the earliest stages of cardiac remodeling. 11 Subclinical alterations in cardiovascular morphology may impair cerebral hemodynamics by delivering inadequate blood flow and disrupting pulsatile energy transmission from the aorta to the brain. 12 , 13 It remains largely unclear which specific subclinical cardiovascular features, as assessed by high‐resolution cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are most closely linked to impaired cerebral perfusion and whether they collectively exert a cumulative effect on cerebral perfusion in older adults without CVD.

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI enables non‐invasive quantification of CBF. 9 Multi‐post‑labeling‑delay ASL allows estimation of arterial transit time (ATT), which complements CBF. 14 Prolonged ATT reflects delayed arterial blood delivery and has been associated with vascular risk factors and poorer cognitive performance. 15 Lower CBF has been linked to hypoxia‐related ischemic insults and vascular brain injury, 16 , 17 , 18 including white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), a well‐established marker of cerebral small vessel disease and a key contributor to stroke and dementia. 19 However, whether joint assessment of CBF and ATT improves the detection of early cerebral perfusion deficits, and their associations with subclinical brain injury such as WMHs, in generally healthy community‑dwelling adults remains unclear. It is also unknown to what extent CBF and ATT link subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction to WMH burden.

These gaps are largely due to the scarcity of studies integrating cardiac MRI, perfusion MRI, and brain structural MRI in healthy older adults. To address these questions, we leveraged advanced cardiac and brain MRI data from the UK Biobank to examine the relationships among subclinical cardiac MRI biomarkers, two complementary cerebral perfusion features (CBF for perfusion volume and ATT for perfusion timing), and WMH burden in community‐dwelling older adults.

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study of > 500,000 participants aged between 40 and 69 from across the UK, conducted between 2006 and 2010. Approximately 100,000 participants have been invited to undergo a multimodal imaging scan, including cardiac MRI, since 2015. 20 A subset of 1799 participants had both cardiac MRI (measured in 2015) and brain MRI (measured in 2021). The mean interval between the two imaging visits was 3.24 ± 1.52 years (median: 2.72 years, 25th‐75th percentiles: 2.09–4.20 years). Participants with dementia (n = 2), heart failure (n = 5), stroke (n = 19), and myocardial infarction (n = 25) at the cardiac MRI visit were excluded, leaving a total of 1748 participants without dementia and cardiovascular disease eligible for the current study (study overview shown in Figure S1 in supporting information). The UK Biobank study has been approved by the North West Multi‐Centre Research Ethics Committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Cardiac MRI acquisition and cardiac measures

Cardiac MRI was performed using a 1.5 Tesla scanner (MAGNETOM Aera, Syngo Platform VD13A, Siemens Healthcare) with a standardized acquisition protocol developed for the UK Biobank. Cine imaging was performed using a balanced steady‐state free‐precession sequence with retrospective electrocardiographic gating and breath holding. Acquisitions included long‐axis cines in two‐, three‐, and four‐chamber views, and a complete short‐axis stack from the atrioventricular ring to the left ventricular (LV) apex covering both ventricles. 21 The imaging parameters were: repetition time/echo time 2.6/1.1 ms, flip angle 80°, parallel imaging (generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisitions [GRAPPA] factor 2), voxel size 1.8 × 1.8 × 8 mm3 (slice thickness 6 mm for long‐axis views), field of view 360 × 270 mm2, matrix 208 × 180, temporal resolution 32 to 40 ms with 50 cardiac phases per cycle. Image quality and automated manual segmentation pipelines have been completed across two core laboratories. 21 , 22

We selected six cardiac MRI biomarkers to capture distinct domains of cardiovascular morphology: cardiac index, ascending and descending aortic maximal area, LV mass, LV wall thickness, and LV global longitudinal strain (correlation among the six cardiac biomarkers are shown in Figure S2 in supporting information). Cardiac index and LV global longitudinal strain reflect systolic function, 10 , 23 aortic areas reflect aortic size and stiffness, 13 and LV mass and wall thickness reflect hypertrophy and concentric remodeling. 24 We used LV global longitudinal strain instead of ejection fraction and aortic maximal area instead of aortic distensibility based on their greater sensitivity and reproducibility for early aortic and myocardial remodeling. 22 , 23 , 25 , 26 We calculated a composite cardiac MRI score by summing standardized values from four domains: (1) inverse cardiac index, (2) inverse LV longitudinal strain, (3) aortic area score, and (4) LV hypertrophy score. The resulting composite score was then standardized to ensure that each domain contributed equally, and the scale was comparable across participants; higher scores indicate greater subclinical cardiac burden. Based on score quantiles, participants were categorized into three groups reflecting increasing cardiovascular burden: low burden (quantiles 1 and 2), moderate burden (quantile 3), and high burden (quantile 4). Details of biomarker measurement, rationale for selection, and the composite scoring procedure are provided in the Supplementary Methods in supporting information.

Brain MRI acquisition and measures of cerebral perfusion and subclinical brain injury

Brain MRI scans were performed at three dedicated imaging centers using identically configured Siemens Skyra 3T scanners with 32‐channel head coils. ASL MRI data were acquired using a multi‐post labeling delay pseudo‐continuous ASL (pcASL) sequence with a two‐shot segmented 3D gradient and spin echo (GRASE) readout (voxel size: 3.4 × 3.4 × 4.5 mm3; matrix: 64 × 48 × 32; scan duration = 2 minutes). The protocol included a labeling duration of 1800 ms and five post labeling delays (400, 800, 1200, 1600, and 2000 ms), enabling voxel‐wise estimation of cerebral blood flow (mL/100g/min) and arterial transit time (seconds; details of ASL MRI acquisition are described in Supplementary Methods). Brain imaging data were processed through a standardized and fully automated pipeline based on FSL software, incorporating machine learning–based quality control—the UK Biobank generated “imaging‐derived phenotypes (IDPs),” including structural and functional brain metrics.

For the present analysis, we included two cerebral perfusion features (i.e., CBF for perfusion volume and ATT for perfusion timing) at both the whole‐brain level and across four major cortical regions (frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes) to capture potential regional variation in perfusion dynamics. Subclinical brain injury was defined as WMH burden derived automatically from T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR; plus T1) images using Brain Intensity Abnormality Classification Algorithm (BIANCA), as implemented in the UK Biobank automated processing pipelines. 27 To account for inter‐individual differences in head size, WMH volumes were normalized by intracranial volume (ICV) and expressed as percentages. We examined total WMHs, periventricular WMHs (defined as the volume of lesion voxels within 10 mm of a ventricle mask), and deep WMHs (the volume of lesion voxels that are > 10 mm from a ventricle mask) in the current analysis. Given the skewed distribution of WMH volumes, all models used log‑transformed WMH (%ICV) as the outcome.

Assessment of other variables

Demographic information was collected at the cardiac MRI visit. Ethnicity and body mass index (BMI) were assessed with standardized protocols. Two blood pressure measurements were obtained on the left upper arm after at least 1 minute of rest in a seated position. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated using the standard formula: MAP = diastolic blood pressure + 1/3 × (systolic blood pressure − diastolic blood pressure), and the average of the two readings was used for analysis. Diabetes was ascertained using the UK Biobank first occurrences data, derived from four sources: primary care records, hospital inpatient data, International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision codes from death registries, and self‐reported diagnoses. Hypertension was defined by meeting any of the following criteria: (1) a resting systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mmHg; (2) current use of antihypertensive medication; or (3) a recorded diagnosis of hypertension from the UK Biobank first occurrences data. As ASL MRI was conducted as part of the UK Biobank COVID‐19 re‐imaging study, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) infection status was determined based on any of the following sources: home‐based lateral‐flow antibody testing, polymerase chain reaction antigen test results, general practitioner records, or public health records. 28

Statistical analysis

We used multivariable linear regression models to assess associations between cardiac MRI biomarkers and cerebral perfusion metrics, including whole‐brain and regional CBF and ATT. We also assessed the relationship between the composite cardiac MRI score and brain perfusion using similarly adjusted linear models. All models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status, smoking habits, MAP, and history of diabetes.

We then fitted multivariable linear regression models to investigate the associations between cerebral perfusion features and WMH burden. To examine WMH burden across combinations of CBF and ATT, we categorized participants into four perfusion profiles based on tertiles of mean CBF and ATT: (1) normal perfusion (middle/high‑tertile CBF and low/middle‑tertile ATT), (2) low CBF (lowest‑tertile CBF and low/middle‑tertile ATT), (3) prolonged ATT (middle/high‑tertile CBF and highest tertile ATT), and (4) impaired perfusion (lowest tertile CBF and highest tertile ATT). WMH burden was modeled as the outcome with perfusion profile as the main exposure, adjusting for the same covariates. Because the current sample was collected during the COVID‐19 pandemic, to limit the potential influence of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, 29 we sought to replicate the associations between perfusion metrics and WMH burden in an independent sample from the UK Biobank (N = 3227) with ASL MRI and T2‑FLAIR available but no cardiac MRI. In this replication sample, we applied the same regression models and covariate adjustment as in the main analyses (demographic information shown in Table S1 in supporting information).

To explore whether cerebral perfusion statistically mediates the association between cardiovascular health (as captured by the composite cardiac MRI score) and WMH burden, we performed mediation analyses treating whole‑brain mean CBF and mean ATT as joint mediators. Mediation analyses were conducted using the mma package in R, which estimates the total, direct, and indirect effects and allows comparison of the relativeindirect effects of multiple mediators. 30 These analyses decompose statistical associations; causal interpretation would require additional assumptions (e.g., absence of unmeasured confounding) that cannot be verified in the present observational data. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. An FDR‐adjusted p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multiple imputation was performed using the MICE package in R to handle missing data (i.e., smoking, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and BMI), with results pooled across 20 imputed datasets using Rubin's rules. 31

To assess robustness of the primary findings, we conducted four sensitivity analyses examining the associations between cardiac MRI biomarkers and whole‐brain perfusion metrics: (1) excluding participants with incident major events occurring between the cardiac MRI and brain MRI visits, including myocardial infarction (n = 5), stroke (n = 7), heart failure (n = 2), and dementia (n = 0); (2) additionally adjusting for antihypertensive medication use, lipid‐lowering medication use, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) status and excluding eight participants with prevalent CKD; (3) further adjusting for mean carotid intima‐media thickness (cIMT) and arterial stiffness index (ASI) to assess the potential confounding effect of subclinical carotid atherosclerosis and systemic arterial stiffness; and (4) further adjusting for total WMH volume to evaluate whether associations between cardiac MRI biomarkers and perfusion metrics were independent of white matter disease. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Core Team).

Cardiac MRI biomarkers and brain perfusion

Table 2 and Figure 1 show the multivariable‐adjusted associations between cardiac MRI biomarkers and cerebral perfusion features. Six cardiovascular measures were collectively linked to a total of 0.34 mL/100g/minute lower whole‐brain CBF, an association estimate comparable to 1 year of age‐related decline in global CBF. 32 A higher composite cardiac MRI score (indicates greater subclinical cardiac burden) was associated with both lower whole‐brain CBF (per SD increase: −0.11 [95% confidence interval (CI): −0.16 to −0.05]) and longer whole‐brain ATT (per SD increase: 0.08 [95% CI: 0.02 to 0.14]). Among individual cardiac MRI measures, lower cardiac index, higher ascending aortic area, and higher LV wall thickness were significantly associated with lower CBF. For instance, per SD higher cardiac index was associated with 0.06 mL/100g/minute greater whole‐brain mean CBF (95% CI: 0.01 to 0.10). In contrast, larger ascending and descending aortic areas were associated with longer whole‐brain mean ATT (per SD increase: 0.10 [95% CI: 0.04 to 0.15], and 0.14 [95% CI: 0.08 to 0.20] seconds longer in ATT, respectively). These associations were consistent across four cortical lobes. Additionally, compared to individuals in the low cardiac MRI burden group, those in the high cardiac MRI burden group had markedly lower CBF in the whole brain (52.2 vs. 48.7 ml/100 g/minute) and across the cortical lobes (Figure 2). Consistently, ATT was longer in the highest versus lowest burden groups in the whole brain and frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes (differences ≈ 0.04–0.05 seconds; p < 0.05), whereas occipital ATT did not differ significantly between tertiles (p = 0.176). Stratified analyses by hypertension status (Table S2 in supporting information) revealed that the associations between cardiac MRI biomarkers and perfusion metrics did not differ significantly between hypertensive and non‐hypertensive participants, although the magnitude of the associations tended to be larger among those with hypertension. Stratified analyses by sex (Table S3 in supporting information) showed that inverse associations of LV wall thickness and the composite cardiac MRI score, and the positive association of LV longitudinal strain, with whole‑brain mean CBF were more evident in men than in women; however, p for interaction by sex did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction.

Brain perfusion and WMH burden

Figure 3 shows the multivariable‐adjusted association of whole‑brain mean CBF and mean ATT with total WMH burden. Lower CBF and longer ATT were each associated with higher total WMH burden, and the combination of low CBF and prolonged ATT was linked to the greatest WMH burden. Figure 4 further quantifies these associations. In models including each perfusion metric separately, both low whole‑brain mean CBF and prolonged mean ATT were significantly associated with greater total and periventricular WMH volumes, but not with deep WMHs. When CBF and ATT were combined into four perfusion groups, compared to participants with normal perfusion, those in the impaired perfusion group had the highest total WMH burden, averaging 0.27% of intracranial volume, corresponding to ≈ 5.5 years of age‐related WMH accumulation based on the age coefficient estimated in the current analytic sample. This contrast was primarily driven by periventricular WMH (p < 0.001), whereas the association with deep WMH volume did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.074). In the independent replication sample (Figures S3–S5 in supporting information), we observed a similar pattern of association between perfusion metrics and WMH burden across the four perfusion groups (all p < 0.005), supporting the robustness of these findings in a seperate UK Biobank sample.

Sensitivity analyses showed results broadly consistent with the primary analyses. Estimates for the associations between cardiac MRI biomarkers and whole‐brain perfusion metrics were similar after excluding participants with incident myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or dementia between imaging visits (Figure S6 in supporting information). The associations remained largely unchanged after additional adjustment for antihypertensive medication use, lipid‐lowering medication use, and CKD status (Figure S7 in supporting information). Further adjustment for subclinical carotid atherosclerosis and arterial stiffness measures did not change the overall pattern of associations; however, the association between higher LV mass and longer whole‐brain ATT became more pronounced compared to the primary model (β = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.16, p = 0.029, PFDR = 0.051; Figure S8 in supporting information). Additional adjustment for total WMH volume yielded similar results (Figure S9 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

In this large, community‐based study integrating advanced cardiac and brain MRI among healthy adults without cardiovascular disease and dementia, we found that subclinical cardiac and aortic alterations on cardiac MRI, individually and as a higher composite score, were associated with impaired cerebral perfusion, captured by lower perfusion volume and delayed perfusion timing across the whole brain and four cortical lobes. Lower CBF and prolonged ATT were each associated with greater total and periventricular WMH burden, and their combination identified participants with the highest WMH burden. Joint mediation analysis further suggests that CBF and ATT together statistically explained more than half of the association between the composite cardiac MRI score and WMH burden, particularly for periventricular WMHs. These findings suggest that impaired cerebral perfusion may represent a potential mechanistic pathway linking subclinical cardiac and aortic alterations to vascular brain injury.

Among individual cardiac MRI biomarkers, lower cardiac index, greater LV wall thickness, and larger descending aortic area were consistently associated with reduced CBF, aligning with prior studies showing that reduced cardiac output and structural cardiac remodeling relate to lower cerebral perfusion. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 The associations between lower cardiac index and reduced regional CBF were strongest in the temporal and frontal lobes, concordant with a previous report and the notion that these territories may be particularly vulnerable because of limited collateral circulation. 34 , 37 LV wall thickness, showed robust associations with lower CBF across multiple regions, whereas the associations with LV mass were less consistent. This suggests that wall thickness may be a particularly relevant marker of concentric remodeling and pressure overload in relation to cerebral perfusion. 38 , 39 We also extended prior studies of aortic stiffness by using ascending and descending aortic maximum areas as direct anatomical measures of aortic remodeling. Whereas pulse wave velocity is a functional marker of arterial stiffness, 33 our findings indicate that larger aortic area, consistent with reduced aortic elasticity, is associated with both lower CBF and prolonged ATT. These findings suggest that direct measurements of aortic size, captured via cardiac MRI, may serve as a sensitive and clinically relevant marker of aortic dysfunction with relevance for brain perfusion. Beyond individual markers, the composite cardiac MRI score captured the cumulative burden of subclinical cardiac dysfunction and was strongly related to impaired cerebral perfusion, underscoring the importance of considering the global cardiovascular profile when studying heart–brain connections.

By jointly considering CBF (perfusion volume) and ATT (perfusion timing), we showed that an integrated perfusion profile more effectively identified individuals with high WMH burden than either metric alone. To our knowledge, the joint consideration of CBF and ATT in relation to WMH burden in the context of subclinical cardiac and aortic remodeling has not been previously examined. While lower CBF is widely implicated in white matter pathology, prior findings have been inconsistent, often limited by small samples, methodological variability, or use of techniques that did not adequately account for delayed arterial transit. 9 , 16 , 17 , 18 Our results indicate that jointly considering these two complementary features of cerebral perfusion strengthens their association with WMHs, particularly in periventricular regions that are anatomically predisposed to hypoperfusion due to their end‐artery supply and limited collateralization. 14 , 40

Mediation analyses further suggested that CBF and ATT jointly accounted for more than half of the association between subclinical cardiac burden and WMHs, especially periventricular WMHs. Although these analyses are statistical and do not prove causality, they are consistent with a mechanistic model in which adverse cardiovascular remodeling augments pulsatile energy transmission, compromises cerebral oxygen delivery, and impairs autoregulatory capacity. 41 , 42 Over time, sustained perfusion inefficiency may promote downstream microvascular damage and neurodegenerative changes, including mitochondrial dysfunction, dendritic spine loss, and neuroinflammation, 40 , 43 ultimately manifesting as WMH accumulation. Consistent with prior findings on ASL CBF, 44 we observed that impaired cerebral perfusion was more significantly linked to WMH burden in periventricular areas. The periventricular white matter, supplied by long penetrating arterioles that serve as end arteries with limited collateral flow, is especially vulnerable to hypoperfusion given the small caliber of these vessels and their distance from major arterial sources. 40 , 45 This anatomical configuration makes periventricular areas especially vulnerable to ischemic injury when systemic cardiovascular function is compromised. Our findings support cerebral hemodynamics as a potential target for early vascular prevention strategies aimed at preserving brain vascular health in the context of cardiovascular aging. Beyond the heart–brain axis, the co‐occurrence of impaired cerebral perfusion and WMH burden in the context of subclinical cardiovascular remodeling may reflect a broader pattern of systemic microvascular vulnerability. Consistent with this notion, neuroimaging markers of small vessel disease have been shown to predict acute kidney injury after contrast exposure, 46 and to act as indicators of brain frailty that attenuate functional recovery after acute stroke intervention, 47 independent of chronological age. These converging observations suggest that cerebral perfusion impairment and WMHs may serve not only as markers of brain‐specific vascular injury, but as imaging surrogates of a broader susceptibility to hemodynamic stress across organs—reinforcing the value of cardiac MRI‐based phenotyping as part of a systemic vascular risk assessment.

Our study has several limitations. First, UK Biobank participants are healthier and more socioeconomically advantaged than the general population and are predominantly of European ancestry, which may lead to an underestimated burden of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular pathology and limit generalizability to higher risk or more diverse populations. Second, while we used WMHs as a marker of brain vascular injury, future studies incorporating multimodal neuroimaging for detecting other markers, such as lacunes and microbleeds, could provide a more comprehensive assessment of cerebrovascular damage. Third, despite the advantages of multi‐delay pCASL, ASL remains susceptible to noise and motion, which may introduce variability in regional perfusion estimates. 9 Fourth, despite additional adjustment for mean cIMT and ASI as proxies for subclinical carotid atherosclerosis and systemic arterial stiffness, direct measures of intracranial arterial stenosis were unavailable; therefore, residual confounding, such as unmeasured cerebrovascular pathology, is possible. Future longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive cardiovascular and cerebrovascular phenotyping will be important to further delineate the relative contributions of cardiac, aortic, and cerebrovascular pathology to altered cerebral perfusion and clarify the temporal relationship. Fifth, because CBF, ATT, and WMHs were assessed at the same brain MRI visit and the mediation analysis was based on observational data, temporal and causal directionality cannot be established. Although cardiac MRI preceded brain MRI in relatively healthy participants free of major conditions, reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future studies with repeated cardiac and brain MRI are needed to establish the temporal ordering of heart–brain associations. Finally, because the ASL MRI data were acquired as part of the UK Biobank COVID‐19 Re‐imaging Study, the observed association may have been affected by SARS‐CoV‐2 infection or pandemic‐related selection. We therefore adjusted for confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection status in the primary models. Replication analyses in a seperate UK Biobank sample whose imaging data were acquired outside the COVID‐19 re‐imaging protocol provide evidence of reproducibility within UK Biobank.

CONCLUSION

Subclinical alterations in cardiac and aortic morphology and function on cardiac MRI are associated with impaired cerebral perfusion and greater WMH burden in community‐dwelling older adults without overt CVD or dementia. The combination of perfusion volume (lower CBF) and perfusion timing (prolonged ATT) identified individuals with the highest WMH burden, and two perfusion features jointly mediated a substantial proportion of the association between composite cardiac MRI burden and subclinical vascular brain injury. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the heart–brain axis and highlight cerebral perfusion as a promising target for early identification and prevention of vascular brain injury and subsequent stroke and dementia.
