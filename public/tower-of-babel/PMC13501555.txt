Moral distress among nurses: an umbrella review of its prevalence, severity, causes, and consequences
Authors: Mansour Arad, Masoud Amini, Zakariya Fattahi, Jamal Seidi, Loghman Khaninezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMoral distress is a major ethical and psychological challenge in nursing practice that adversely affects nurses’ well-being, patient care quality, and healthcare organizations. This umbrella review aimed to synthesize and critically appraise evidence from existing systematic reviews regarding the prevalence, severity, causes, and consequences of moral distress primarily among nurses across diverse healthcare settings worldwide, with particular attention to regional and contextual variations.MethodsThis umbrella review was conducted according to the JBI methodology for umbrella reviews and reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar from inception until 10 March 2025. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (with or without meta-analysis) published in English were included if they focused on moral distress among nurses or reported extractable nurse-relevant findings within mixed healthcare professional populations. Methodological quality was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses. Due to considerable heterogeneity in populations, settings, and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis was undertaken.FindingsOf 435 records identified, 19 systematic reviews published between 2009 and 2025 met the inclusion criteria. Reported moral distress scores varied across settings and instruments. Higher levels were consistently reported in intensive care, coronary care, and oncology units. Key contributing factors include younger age, female gender, poor teamwork, inadequate resources, and perceived futile or non-beneficial care. Major consequences comprise burnout, depression, reduced quality of nursing care, medication errors, and intention to leave the profession. Significant global and contextual variations were observed.ConclusionMoral distress remains a complex, multifaceted phenomenon influenced by individual, interpersonal, organizational, and systemic factors. Organizational and educational interventions may help mitigate its impact; however, further high-quality research is needed to strengthen the evidence base and inform effective strategies. This umbrella review synthesizes current evidence and highlights important implications for nursing practice, management, education, and future research.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Moral distress (MD) occurs when an individual makes an ethical decision but is unable to act on it due to external constraints. This concept was first defined by Wilkinson as “psychological imbalance and negative feelings resulting from the inability to act on an ethical decision” [1, 2]. Early descriptions emphasized the psychological discomfort experienced when individuals are unable to act in accordance with their ethical judgment. Jameton later highlighted the role of institutional constraints, defining MD as knowing the ethically appropriate action but being prevented from acting accordingly [3].

MD has been widely reported among healthcare providers, including doctors, nurses, occupational therapists, and social workers. These professionals frequently face situations in which their personal ethical values conflict with organizational or legal barriers, leading to significant stress [4]. Studies have shown that MD can have severe consequences, including frustration, guilt, anger, burnout, and a decrease in the quality of patient care [5–7]. For example, Epstein et al. [8] stated that MD could lead to higher turnover rates and decreased job satisfaction among healthcare providers, ultimately weakening patient trust in the healthcare system.

Moreover, during crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the ethical pressures on healthcare providers have increased unprecedentedly, emphasizing the need for evidence synthesis to design appropriate intervention strategies [9, 10]. Cultural and organizational differences in global health systems also necessitate the comparative review of findings from previous studies to develop managerial strategies suited to local contexts [11].

With the rapid growth of research in this field, findings have become difficult to interpret because reported levels of moral distress vary substantially across countries and clinical settings, associations with demographic factors such as age and experience are inconsistent, and studies have used diverse measurement instruments [12]. In such conditions, umbrella reviews emerge as a credible method for synthesizing and integrating the results of existing studies. This type of study, by combining data from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses, provides a comprehensive view of the risk factors, outcomes, and interventions related to MD, while uncovering research gaps and methodological inconsistencies [13].

Despite the growing number of primary studies and systematic reviews, important synthesis-level gaps remain. Existing reviews have typically focused on specific clinical settings, countries, or selected outcomes, resulting in fragmented evidence. In addition, differences in scope, methodology, and reported conclusions make it difficult for policymakers and nursing leaders to draw clear inferences. To date, no comprehensive umbrella review has integrated evidence on the prevalence, severity, associated factors, and consequences of moral distress among nurses across settings and regions. The primary aim of this umbrella review was to synthesize evidence regarding the prevalence, severity, causes, and consequences of moral distress primarily among nurses across diverse healthcare settings.

Materials and methods

This umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for umbrella reviews [14] and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15].

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to March 10, 2025, to identify relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses. No restrictions were applied regarding publication date during the search process. To ensure broad retrieval, the search strategy was initially inclusive of all study designs; however, during screening, only systematic reviews and meta-analyses were considered eligible, consistent with the methodological framework of umbrella reviews. The search strategy included backward and forward citation searches of included studies to capture additional relevant literature. Search terms were developed to target moral distress among healthcare professionals and included the following keyword combinations: (“moral distress” OR “ethical distress”) AND (“healthcare providers” OR “nurses” OR “health professionals”) AND (“systematic review” OR “meta-analysis” OR “review”). The detailed search strategy is presented in Table 1. To maximize sensitivity, broader professional search terms (e.g., healthcare providers, health professionals) were included during database searching; however, final inclusion was restricted to reviews relevant to nurses according to predefined eligibility criteria.

Study selection

Retrieved articles were imported into EndNote version 20 for reference management and duplicate removal. Two independent reviewers (LKH and ZF) screened titles and abstracts to assess eligibility based on predefined criteria. Full-text reviews were then conducted by the same reviewers to confirm inclusion. Discrepancies during screening or full-text review were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (SHR) acting as an external arbitrator when consensus could not be reached. No language restrictions were applied during the database search. However, due to feasibility constraints, only full-text systematic reviews published in English were included at the eligibility stage.

The eligibility criteria were structured according to an umbrella review framework:

Population: Nurses working in clinical or educational settings. Reviews involving mixed healthcare professional populations were considered eligible only when nurse-specific findings were reported separately or when nurses constituted a substantial subgroup of the included population (defined as representing the majority professional group or being a clearly identifiable subgroup relevant to the review objectives). Reviews in which nurse-specific data could not be extracted or meaningfully interpreted were excluded.

Phenomenon of Interest: Moral distress, including its prevalence, severity, causes, and consequences.

Context: All healthcare and nursing education settings across geographical regions.

Outcomes: Measures or synthesized findings related to levels, determinants, and impacts of moral distress.

Review Type: Systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Exclusion criteria included:

Non-systematic reviews or studies lacking a clearly defined methodology.

Study designs other than systematic reviews (e.g., cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, cohort studies, or clinical trials).

Reviews not involving nurses, or mixed-population reviews without extractable nurse-specific data, were excluded.

This umbrella review focused on moral distress among nurses. Reviews examining mixed healthcare professional populations were eligible only when nurse-specific findings were reported separately or when nurses represented a substantial subgroup relevant to the review objective. The study selection process was conducted according to predefined eligibility criteria and is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of each included review was critically assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses [16]. This checklist does not generate an overall score but instead provides a comprehensive evaluation based on deficiencies identified in key areas.

To facilitate the critical appraisal, an Excel spreadsheet was created, where each checklist item was assigned to a separate column. Two independent reviewers (LKH and ZF) conducted the assessments. In case of any discrepancies, a third person outside the study (SHR) was consulted to resolve disagreements. Most included reviews fulfilled key methodological appraisal domains, including clearly defined research questions, appropriate inclusion criteria, and comprehensive search strategies. Data synthesis

Given the broad scope of this umbrella review and the anticipated diversity in populations, healthcare settings, measurement instruments, and reported outcomes, a narrative synthesis was planned as the primary method of evidence integration. Quantitative pooling across reviews was not considered appropriate due to expected conceptual and methodological heterogeneity. Heterogeneity statistics reported in this manuscript (e.g., I² values ranging from 98.4% to 100%) were obtained from individual meta-analyses within the included reviews and reflect variability across their respective primary studies, outcomes, and settings. No pooled heterogeneity estimate was calculated for the present umbrella review.

Data from the included reviews were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form, including information on prevalence, associated factors, and reported outcomes of moral distress. Extracted findings were compared and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer when necessary.

Due to heterogeneity in study populations, settings, and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis was conducted. Findings were organized inductively by grouping recurring patterns across studies into four main domains: settings and populations, prevalence and severity, causes and triggers, and consequences of moral distress. These domains emerged from repeated comparison of extracted data rather than being based on a predefined theoretical framework. During narrative synthesis, findings from mixed-population reviews were interpreted cautiously to avoid overgeneralization beyond nursing contexts. Greater emphasis was placed on findings directly related to nurses or nursing practice when synthesizing conclusions.

Results

Literature search

Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram, illustrating the selection process and screening of systematic reviews, as well as the rationale for exclusions. A total of 435 articles were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, 378 records remained. Following title and abstract screening, 343 records were excluded, and 35 full-text articles were retrieved for further assessment. Two researchers independently reviewed these articles and identified 19 studies that met the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies in selection were resolved through discussion. Ultimately, this umbrella review included 19 studies for data extraction (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

All articles included in this umbrella review were published in English between 2009 and 2025. These studies appeared across 14 different journals, with Nursing Ethics being the most frequently represented, contributing five articles. The majority of the studies were published in 2023. Among the 19 included reviews, six were meta-analyses, seven were narrative systematic reviews, and six were qualitative reviews or qualitative meta-syntheses, reflecting a diversity of methodological approaches. The primary studies encompassed a wide range of nursing populations, including nurses working in intensive care units (ICUs), emergency departments, oncology wards, palliative care settings, and nursing students. These studies focused on various geographical contexts, including both high-income and developing countries. The regions represented included Iran, the United States, Canada, European countries (such as Italy and Sweden), and Asian countries (such as China and Japan), although representation from African regions was limited. The databases searched included PubMed (used in 85.71% of the studies), Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, and regional databases such as SID and MagIran. The search periods spanned from 1984 to 2024, indicating comprehensive coverage of both historical and contemporary literature. Methodological quality was assessed using tools such as JBI (3 studies), CASP (3 studies), MMAT, and STROBE, revealing that most studies were of high quality, with clearly defined research questions, appropriate inclusion criteria, and robust search strategies. In total, 404 articles were included in these 19 systematic reviews, reflecting a broad scope of primary research on MD among nurses. The number of articles per review ranged from 4 to 86. Quality assessment results

The methodological quality of the 19 included reviews was assessed using the JBI checklist. Overall, most reviews adequately fulfilled several core methodological criteria assessed by the JBI checklist, although limitations were identified in areas such as publication bias assessment and reporting of independent data extraction. All reviews clearly defined their research questions, inclusion criteria, and search strategies (100%). Most applied appropriate critical appraisal methods (94.7%). However, some limitations were noted: independent data extraction was clearly reported in 63.2% of reviews, and methods to minimize extraction errors in 52.6%. Assessment of publication bias was limited, reported in only 36.8% of reviews. In addition, some studies provided recommendations that were not fully supported by the strength of the evidence. Overall, while the evidence base is methodologically sound, these limitations were considered when interpreting the findings.

Outcomes

This umbrella review synthesizes findings from 19 systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining MD among nurses across diverse settings, including ICUs, emergency departments, oncology, palliative care, mental health, and nursing education.

1. Prevalence and severity of MD

MD varies in intensity and frequency across healthcare settings, influenced by measurement tools, professional roles, and geographical context. Studies consistently report it as a significant issue, particularly prevalent in high-stakes environments such as ICUs.

ICU settings: One mixed-population meta-analysis involving ICU healthcare workers, including nurses and other professionals, reported moderate levels of moral distress (Mean = 27.79, 95% CI: 7.40–64.18) and high frequency (Mean = 46.83, 95% CI: 8.34–262.87), with significant heterogeneity (I² = 100%, τ² = 4.252, p = 0). High-income countries such as Canada (Mean = 91.99) and the USA (Mean = 52.54) reported elevated distress scores, suggesting that resource availability does not necessarily alleviate distress [17]. Giannetta et al. [18] confirmed moderate distress using Corley’s Scale, attributing variability to triggers like futile care and poor communication. Salari et al. 2. Causes and Triggers of MD

MD arises from a complex interplay of personal, relational, organizational, and systemic factors, with several recurring triggers across settings.

Personal factors: Beheshtaeen et al. [27] identified age, gender (female), marital status (single), and limited work experience as contributing factors—both pre- and during COVID-19. Giannetta et al. [18] found that younger age, less experience, and female gender were associated with higher distress among ICU nurses.Relational factors: Poor teamwork and weak physician collaboration emerged as consistent triggers. Atashzadeh-Shoorideh et al. [28] highlighted lack of support and collaboration in ICUs. Beheshtaeen et al. and Giannetta et al. [18, 27] reported poor teamwork as a widespread cause. Rittenmeyer and Huffman [29] discussed hierarchical imbalances between nurses and physicians. Heng and Shorey [25] noted that unprofessional behavior by colleagues contributed to distress among students.Organizational/Systemic factors: Resource shortages, staff deficits, and inadequate equipment were frequently cited. Haghbeen et al. [28] noted these issues were exacerbated by shifting protocols during the pandemic. Joseph et al. [30] emphasized workload pressures and the absence of ethical support in developing countries. Maffoni et al. [31] discussed futile treatments and perceived powerlessness in palliative care. Miller et al. [32] noted challenges related to leadership and organizational distrust. Salari et al. [19] cited ineffective care, prolonged suffering, and weak administrative support. Patient-related factors: Frequent triggers included end-of-life decisions, futile treatments, and perceived patient suffering. Boulton and Farquharson [23] highlighted unnecessary interventions in emergency settings. Eche et al. [22] emphasized the emotional toll of end-of-life care in oncology. Prentice et al. [30] reported overuse of technology and staff powerlessness in NICUs and PICUs.

3. Impacts of MD

The included reviews suggest that moral distress may be associated with personal, professional, and organizational consequences, affecting both provider well-being and patient care.

Personal impacts: Emotional and psychological effects are widespread. Atashzadeh-Shoorideh et al. and Beheshtaeen et al. [27, 33] noted depression, anxiety, and emotional exhaustion among ICU nurses. Joseph et al. [34] reported emotional fatigue, sleep disturbances, and thoughts of withdrawal in low-resource settings. Lamoureux et al. [24] linked mental health nurses’ distress to cynicism and burnout. Heng and Shorey [25] described anxiety, guilt, and burnout risk among students. [19] reported reduced efficiency and increased healthcare costs.Impacts on patient care: MD was associated with perceived reductions in quality of care. Eche et al. [22] reported adverse patient outcomes in oncology. Joseph et al. and Salari et al. [19, 34] linked distress to decreased care quality and medication errors. Heng and Shorey [25] reported desensitization and reduced empathy among students.

[32] emphasized adverse effects on nurse leaders and organizational culture. Salari et al. [19] reported reduced efficiency and increased healthcare costs.

Impacts on patient care: MD was associated with perceived reductions in quality of care. Eche et al. [22] reported adverse patient outcomes in oncology. Joseph et al. and Salari et al. [19, 34] linked distress to decreased care quality and medication errors. Heng and Shorey [25] reported desensitization and reduced empathy among students.

Discussion

This umbrella review aimed to synthesize and critically appraise evidence from existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses regarding the prevalence, severity, causes, and consequences of moral distress primarily among nurses across diverse healthcare settings. MD among nurses, particularly in high-stress units such as ICU, emergency, and oncology, appears to be more frequently reported in settings characterized by complex decision-making and ethical conflicts. It is argued that this difference stems from the inherent nature of these units, which expose nurses to high-risk situations where their moral values conflict with operational demands. In developing countries, resource shortages and inadequate support structures, particularly in CCU units, exacerbate this phenomenon [36]. This is attributed to systemic inequalities in resource allocation and the absence of standardized protocols for managing ethical conflicts. Conversely, general nursing demonstrates lower MD levels due to its less intensive nature, as nurses in these settings are less frequently confronted with life-critical decisions that challenge their values.

In ICUs and emergency departments, rapid decision-making under conditions of ethical uncertainty (such as whether to continue or withdraw life-sustaining treatments), coupled with shortages of staff, medical equipment, and time, forces nurses to prioritize actions that may conflict with their moral beliefs. It is argued that these pressures arise not only from material resource constraints but also from the lack of a supportive organizational culture that enables nurses to actively participate in decision-making. In oncology units, MD is particularly severe due to frequent encounters with patients in end-of-life stages and the inability to fully alleviate their suffering, as reported by Aljabery et al. [37]. This heightened distress may reflect the emotional conflict nurses experience between their professional duty to preserve life and the acceptance of patients’ inevitable death. In emergency departments, although MD is often short-term, its intensity is high due to significant psychological pressure and frequent urgent decisions, with 20–50% of nurses intending to leave their jobs [38]. This high turnover intention may result from the cumulative effect of ethical stressors that deprive nurses of opportunities for psychological processing and recovery. Nursing students also experience distinct forms of MD due to lack of experience and feelings of inadequacy when confronted with complex ethical situations, aligning with previous studies [39]. It is argued that this group is more vulnerable due to insufficient cognitive and emotional readiness to manage ethical conflicts.

Factors influencing MD include individual, interpersonal, organizational, and patient-related elements. Younger nurses and women are more susceptible to MD due to higher sensitivity to ethical conflicts and less experience [40]. This may be explained by psychological or social differences that lead women and less-experienced individuals to engage in deeper self-reflection when faced with ethical dilemmas. Poor teamwork and limited involvement of nurses in clinical decision-making may be associated with feelings of powerlessness, particularly in environments with rigid hierarchies [41]. It is argued that this issue is rooted in organizational cultures that perceive nurses as executors of physicians’ orders rather than decision-making partners. Resource shortages, heavy workloads, and inflexible policies place nurses in situations where they must perform actions contrary to their moral values, such as continuing futile treatments [42]. These findings may reflect the influence of economic and organizational pressures that prioritize operational efficiency over ethical values. Encounters with dying patients or ineffective treatments further amplify feelings of guilt and failure, especially in oncology units. These factors align with the findings of Rice et al. [43], which associate MD with reduced care quality, increased medication errors, and adverse effects on nurses’ mental health. It is argued that these outcomes could be related to unresolved ethical conflicts experienced in high-stress work environments. Recent evidence also supports the negative consequences of moral distress in high-acuity settings. An integrative review by Ananzeh and Miller [44] focusing on critical care nurses identified three major domains of impact: reduced well-being and job satisfaction, compromised patient care, and increased turnover intention and burnout. These findings are consistent with the results of the present umbrella review and reinforce the substantial personal, professional, and organizational burden of moral distress among nurses, particularly in critical care environments.

The consequences of MD are multidimensional, manifesting at individual, organizational, and clinical levels [44]. At the individual level, MD has been associated with depression, burnout, and decreased job satisfaction, potentially due to chronic stress from the inability to act in accordance with moral values. At the organizational level, the increasing turnover rate poses a threat to workforce sustainability in healthcare systems [38]. This is particularly concerning as nurse shortages can create a cycle of higher workloads and greater MD. At the clinical level, MD may negatively influence patient care quality and has been associated with increased reports of medication errors [45]. It is argued that these errors may result from distraction or diminished focus among nurses under ethical stress.

The limitations of this umbrella review arise from the high heterogeneity among the included systematic reviews, preventing a meta-analysis and limiting the ability to generate precise, comparable estimates of MD prevalence and severity due to variations in measurement tools and definitions. Geographic bias is also a concern, as a significant proportion of studies either lacked specific regional focus or were categorized as global, potentially overlooking regional differences beyond a few well-studied countries. The restriction to English-language full-text reviews may have introduced language bias and may have excluded relevant evidence published in other languages. The reliance on qualitative data limits the quantitative rigor of the findings, and the use of diverse quality assessment tools complicates methodological comparisons. Furthermore, the limited evaluation of intervention strategies restricts actionable insights, while the relatively low representation of studies examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic may underestimate its broader effects on MD.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on intervention-based studies to better clarify the development, persistence, and outcomes of moral distress among nurses. More standardized approaches to measuring and reporting MD are needed to improve comparability across studies and settings. Additional research in underrepresented regions and healthcare environments is also recommended. Furthermore, future reviews should more clearly distinguish nurse-specific findings from mixed healthcare professional populations and evaluate the effectiveness of organizational, educational, and psychological strategies aimed at reducing MD.

Conclusions

This umbrella review indicates that moral distress is a common and important challenge among nurses across diverse healthcare settings, particularly in high-acuity and resource-constrained environments. Available evidence suggests associations with burnout, turnover intention, and reduced quality of care; however, interpretations should be made cautiously due to heterogeneity in study populations, measures, and review methods. Further high-quality longitudinal and interventional research is needed to clarify causal pathways and identify effective mitigation strategies.
