First Passage Times for Variable-Order Time-Fractional Diffusion
Wancheng Li, Daniel S. Han

Abstract
We derive the asymptotic first passage time (FPT) distribution for space-dependent variable-order time-fractional diffusion, where the fractional exponent α ⁡ ( x ) \alpha(x) varies with position. For any sufficiently smooth α ⁡ ( x ) \alpha(x) on a finite domain with absorbing and reflecting boundaries, we show that the survival probability decays as Ψ ⁡ ( t ) ∼ C ​ t − α ∗ / ( ln ⁡ t ) ν \Psi(t)\sim C\,t^{-\alpha_{*}}/(\ln t)^{\nu} , where α ∗ \alpha_{*} is the minimum value of the fractional exponent and ν \nu is determined by the location and shape of the minimum. For a constant fractional exponent ν = 0 \nu=0 and this provides a theoretical prediction that can identify spatially heterogeneous anomalous transport in experiments. We validate the theory against exact Laplace-space solutions and Monte Carlo simulations for linear and nonlinear profiles of α ⁡ ( x ) \alpha(x) .

In this Letter, we formulate the FPT problem for the variable-order time-fractional diffusion equation for any general fractional exponent,
\alpha(x)

We find that the FPT distribution scales asymptotically as a power law dependent on the minimum value of

We illustrate the general result with two examples,

First passage times
—
We consider the FPT distribution of the random walk whose governing equation is (
1
) for general
\alpha(x)
given the initial condition

p(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0}),

(6)

for
x\in(0,L]
.
The FPT distribution, or the survival probability, is calculated via
Redner 2001

\Psi(t)=\int_{0}^{L}p(x,t)\,dx,

(7)

and the FPT PDF is obtained from
\psi(t)=-d\Psi(t)/dt
,
where
p(x,t)
is the PDF of the random walk governed by (
1
) given initial condition (
6
).
After taking the Laplace transform of (
1
), the density
\hat{p}(x,s)
is governed by (
5
) with the absorbing boundary condition

\hat{p}(0,s)=0,

(8)

and the reflecting boundary condition

\partial_{x}\!\Big(D_{\alpha(x)}s^{1-\alpha(x)}\hat{p}(x,s)\Big)\Big|_{x=L}=0.

(9)

If we define

F(x,s):=D_{\alpha(x)}s^{1-\alpha(x)}\hat{p}(x,s),

(10)

then
\hat{p}(x,s)=s^{\alpha(x)-1}F(x,s)/D_{\alpha(x)}
, and (
5
) yields

-F^{\prime\prime}(x,s)+q(x,s)F(x,s)=\delta(x-x_{0}),

(11)

with

q(x,s)={s^{\alpha(x)}}/{D_{\alpha(x)}}={2}(\tau s)^{\alpha(x)}/{a^{2}},

(12)

and boundary conditions
F(0,s)=0,
and
F^{\prime}(L,s)=0
.
To solve (
11
) asymptotically as
s\rightarrow 0
, we can write (
11
) as

-\lim_{{s\to 0}}F^{\prime\prime}(x,s)=\delta(x-x_{0}).

(13)

since
q(x,s)\to 0
uniformly and
F(x,s)
is bounded as
s\to 0
.
The solution of (
13
) is
F(x,0)=\min\{x,x_{0}\}
where
x,x_{0}\in[0,L]
.
Now substituting (
10
) into (
7
), we obtain

\hat{\Psi}(s)=\frac{2\tau}{a^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}(\tau s)^{\alpha(x)-1}F(x,s){d}x.

(14)

Since by a comparison argument

0\leq F(x,0)-F(x,s)\leq L^{2}\|q(x,s)\|_{\infty}F(x,0),

(15)

where
\|q(x,s)\|_{\infty}:=\displaystyle\sup_{x}q(x,s)
, and
\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\|q(x,s)\|_{\infty}=0
, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{s\to 0}&\int_{0}^{L}(\tau s)^{\alpha(x)-1}F(x,s)\,{d}x=\\
&\left(1+O(\|q(x,s)\|_{\infty})\right)\int_{0}^{L}(\tau s)^{\alpha(x)-1}F(x,0)\,{d}x.\end{split}

(16)

Hence (
7
) in Laplace space becomes

\hat{\Psi}(s)\sim\frac{2\tau}{a^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}(\tau s)^{\alpha(x)-1}F(x,0)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(17)

as
s\to 0
.
As long as
\alpha(x)
is twice differentiable, we can use Laplace’s method to approximate (
17
), such that

\hat{\Psi}(s)\sim\frac{2}{sa^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}e^{\alpha(x)\ln(\tau s)}F(x,0)\,{d}x.

(18)

In what follows, we consider various cases for the fractional exponent,
\alpha(x)
, and provide asymptotic expressions for the first passage time distribution.

Unique interior minimum
—
If
\alpha(x)
has a unique interior minimum
\alpha_{*}=\alpha(x_{*})
at
x_{*}\in(0,L)
, then using

\alpha(x)=\alpha_{*}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})(x-x_{*})^{2}+O\big((x-x_{*})^{3}\big),

(19)

(
18
) becomes

\begin{split}\hat{\Psi}(s)&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha_{*}-1}}{D_{\alpha_{*}}}F(x_{*},0)\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})}{2}|\ln(\tau s)|(x-x_{*})^{2}}dx\\
&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha_{*}-1}}{D_{\alpha_{*}}}F(x_{*},0)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})\,|\ln(\tau s)|}},\end{split}

(20)

where we have used change of variable
u=\sqrt{|\ln(\tau s)|}\,(x-x_{*})
to evaluate the integral about
x_{*}
.
Here we have assumed that
\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})\neq 0
.
Applying the Tauberian theorem
Feller 1991
to (
20
) yields

\Psi(t)\sim\frac{Ct^{-\alpha_{*}}}{\sqrt{\ln\left(t/\tau\right)}},

(21)

where
C=\frac{F(x_{*},0)}{D_{\alpha_{*}}\Gamma(1-\alpha_{*})}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})}}
.

Several equal isolated minima
— If
\alpha(x)
has
m
isolated interior minima at
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in(0,L)
, all sharing the same minimum value
\alpha_{*}
, then using

\alpha(x)=\alpha_{*}+\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})(x-x_{j})^{2}+O\left((x-x_{j})^{3}\right),

(22)

around each
x_{j}
and
\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})\neq 0
, (
18
) becomes

\begin{split}\hat{\Psi}(s)&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha_{*}-1}}{D_{\alpha_{*}}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}F(x_{j},0)\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})|\ln(\tau s)|(x-x_{j})^{2}}\,dx\\
&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha_{*}-1}}{D_{\alpha_{*}}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|\ln(\tau s)|}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{F(x_{j},0)}{\sqrt{\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})}},\end{split}

(23)

where we have applied the same Gaussian localization as in (
20
) within disjoint neighbourhoods of each
x_{j}
and summed the leading contributions.
Applying the Tauberian theorem to (
23
) yields

\Psi(t)\sim\frac{C\,t^{-\alpha_{*}}}{\sqrt{\ln\bigl(t/\tau\bigr)}},

(24)

where
C=\frac{1}{D_{\alpha_{*}}\Gamma(1-\alpha_{*})}\sum_{j=1}^{m}F(x_{j},0)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})}}
.
Equation (
24
) has the same time scaling as (
21
) and only differs by a multiplicative factor due to the presence of multiple minima.
If the fractional exponent
\alpha(x)
contains multiple minima with different values, only the lowest survives in the asymptotic limit.

Higher order minimum
—
More generally, if the first nonzero derivative of
\alpha
at
x_{*}
is of even order

\alpha^{\prime}(x_{*})=\cdots=\alpha^{(k-1)}(x_{*})=0
and
\alpha^{(k)}(x_{*})>0
, then using

\alpha(x)=\alpha_{*}+\frac{\alpha^{(k)}(x_{*})}{k!}(x-x_{*})^{k}+O\big((x-x_{*})^{k+1}\big),

(25)

(
18
) becomes

\begin{split}\hat{\Psi}(s)&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha_{*}-1}}{D_{\alpha_{*}}}F(x_{*},0)\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{(k)}(x_{*})}{k!}|\ln(\tau s)|(x-x_{*})^{k}}\,dx\\
&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha_{*}-1}}{D_{\alpha_{*}}}F(x_{*},0)\,\frac{2}{k}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\!\left(\frac{k!}{\alpha^{(k)}(x_{*})|\ln(\tau s)|}\right)^{\!1/k},\end{split}

(26)

where we have used the change of variable
u=\big(\alpha^{(k)}(x_{*})|\ln(\tau s)|/k!\big)^{1/k}(x-x_{*})
to evaluate the integral about
x_{*}
.
Applying the Tauberian theorem to (
26
) yields

\Psi(t)\sim\frac{C\,t^{-\alpha_{*}}}{\big(\ln(t/\tau)\big)^{1/k}},

(27)

where
C=\frac{F(x_{*},0)}{D_{\alpha_{*}}k\Gamma(1-\alpha_{*})}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\!\left(\frac{k!}{\alpha^{(k)}(x_{*})}\right)^{\!1/k}
.

Absorbing boundary minimum
—
We now consider when
\alpha(x)
has a unique minimum
\alpha_{*}
on the absorbing boundary at

\alpha(x)=\alpha(0)+\alpha^{\prime}(0)x+O(x^{2}),

(28)

where
\alpha^{\prime}(0)>0

From (

F(x,0)=x

\begin{split}\hat{\Psi}(s)&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha(0)-1}}{D_{\alpha(0)}}\int_{0}^{L}x\,e^{-|\ln(\tau s)|\alpha^{\prime}(0)x}\,dx\\
&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha(0)-1}}{D_{\alpha(0)}\alpha^{\prime}(0)^{2}|\ln(\tau s)|^{2}},\end{split}

(29)

where we have used integration by parts to evaluate the one-sided Laplace integral about

.
Applying the Tauberian theorem to (

\Psi(t)\sim\frac{Ct^{-\alpha(0)}}{\left(\ln\left(t/\tau\right)\right)^{2}}\,,

(30)

where
C=\frac{1}{D_{\alpha(0)}\alpha^{\prime}(0)^{2}\Gamma(1-\alpha(0))}
.
If the minimum at
x=0

\alpha^{(j)}(0)=0

\alpha^{(k)}(0)\neq 0

.

Reflecting boundary minimum
—
Similarly, we consider when
\alpha(x)
has a unique minimum
\alpha_{*}=\alpha(L)
on the reflecting boundary at

, such that

\alpha(x)=\alpha(L)+\alpha^{\prime}(L)(x-L)+O\left((x-L)^{2}\right),

where
\alpha^{\prime}(L)<0

Again from (

, and (

\begin{split}\hat{\Psi}(s)&\sim\frac{1}{D_{\alpha(L)}}s^{\alpha(L)-1}x_{0}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-|\ln(\tau s)||\alpha^{\prime}(L)|(L-x)}\,dx\\
&\sim\frac{s^{\alpha(L)-1}x_{0}}{D_{\alpha(L)}|\alpha^{\prime}(L)||\ln(\tau s)|},\end{split}

(32)

where we have used integration by parts to evaluate the one-sided Laplace integral about
x=L
.
Applying the Tauberian theorem to (
32
) yields

\Psi(t)\sim\frac{C\,t^{-\alpha(L)}}{\ln\left(t/\tau\right)},

(33)

where
C=\frac{x_{0}}{D_{\alpha(L)}|\alpha^{\prime}(L)|\Gamma(1-\alpha(L))}
. If the minimum at
x=L
is of higher order, with
\alpha^{(j)}(L)=0
for

and
\alpha^{(k)}(L)\neq 0
, a calculation similar to (

) shows that the logarithmic term in (

.

The survival probabilities when the fractional exponent has a single interior minimum (

Physically, this is intuitive when considering the underlying random walks

As we consider long time asymptotics, the strongest trap dominates in contribution to the first passage time statistics.
Interestingly, the logarithmic correction

is sensitive to the shape of

The different scenarios are

This theoretical result means that FPT distributions for variable-order time-fractional diffusion are distinguishable depending on the shape of the fractional exponent

So if experiments are suspected to exhibit variable-order fractional diffusion, parameterisation of the logarithmic correction term via fitting the FPT distributions should allow experiments to test whether variable-order fractional diffusion occurs.

To make explicit the difference between the variable-order and constant-order cases, we provide the FPT distribution of the fractional diffusion equation (

Summary
—
We have derived the asymptotic FPT distribution for variable-order time-fractional diffusion equation (
1
) for general
\alpha(x)
.
The survival probability universally decays as
\Psi(t)\sim C\,t^{-\alpha_{*}}/(\ln t)^{\nu}
, where the power law is set by the global minimum
\alpha_{*}
and the logarithmic exponent
\nu
classifies the local geometry of
\alpha(x)
at that minimum.
This logarithmic correction is a qualitative departure from the pure power-law decay (
36
) of constant-order fractional diffusion, and provides a direct experimental diagnostic.
Measuring
\nu
from FPT data can distinguish between constant and spatially varying anomalous transport, and constrain the functional form of
\alpha(x)
.
The asymptotic results were confirmed for linear and Gaussian-well profiles using numerical inversion of exact Laplace-space solutions and Monte Carlo simulations.
