Fascial plane blocks in pediatric patients
Authors: Cooper Quiroz, Mackenzie Jacoby, Alparslan Turan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric perioperative pain management remains challenging, and opioid-related adverse effects have driven interest in opioid-sparing strategies. Thus, regional anesthesia techniques are an increasingly important component of multimodal analgesia and may contribute to improved pain control and reduced opioid-related systemic effects. The rapid advancement of ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia has broadened the use of pediatric fascial plane blocks. These approaches offer technically straightforward, safe, and effective analgesia across a range of thoracic and abdominal procedures. However, these developments have not translated uniformly into clinical adoption. The practice of pediatric regional anesthesiology is variable and institution dependent. The lack of standardized recommendations raises safety concerns as the current evidence is limited by heterogeneity in study design and procedural variability. Age-related differences in anatomy, physiology, and pharmacokinetics limit extrapolation from adult data and present challenges in clinical application. Although complication rates are low, the risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity and conservative dosing practices are important considerations. This narrative review discusses the mechanisms, indications, and clinical applications of fascial plane blocks in pediatric patients and highlights key factors for their safe and effective implementation.Keywords: Fascial plane blocks, Pediatric anesthesia, Regional anesthesia, Postoperative pain, Opioid-sparing analgesia

Introduction

Pain management is a principal component in the perioperative care of children. Uncontrolled or untreated pain in the pediatric population is associated with increased stress responses, delayed recovery, prolonged hospitalization, and the potential development of long-term pain sensitization and negative behavioral outcomes [1, 2]. Postoperative pain in children remains a persistent challenge with important implications for both short- and long-term outcomes. Opioids are a cornerstone of perioperative pain management in pediatric patients but are a significant contributor to complications. Their use is a well-documented cause of adverse effects, including respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, ileus, pruritus, and the potential for dependence, all of which may adversely affect recovery and rehabilitation and have driven increasing interest in opioid-sparing strategies [3].

Regional anesthesia techniques are increasingly incorporated into multimodal analgesic plans in pediatric patients, offering effective analgesia while potentially reducing opioid consumption and opioid-related adverse effects [4–8]. Interest in regional anesthesia in children has also been driven by concerns regarding the potential neurocognitive effects of general anesthetic exposure in early development, further supporting efforts to minimize systemic anesthetic and opioid requirements. Neuraxial techniques, including caudal and epidural anesthesia, have traditionally served as the foundation of pediatric regional analgesia and remain highly effective with numerous benefits. While complications in neuraxial anesthesia are rare, concerns remain over needle-related nerve injury and LAST. Duration of spinal anesthesia is limited to procedures less than 60–100 min even with adjuncts such as clonidine. With continuous neuraxial techniques, despite dosing restrictions, the serum concentration of local anesthetic increases with the duration of the infusion. These concerns have prompted growing interest in alternative regional techniques that may offer effective analgesia with a more favorable safety and side-effect profile [9].

Advances in ultrasound technology have significantly improved the safety, precision, and accessibility of regional anesthesia, facilitating the expansion of ultrasound-guided techniques in pediatric practice [8, 9]. In this context, fascial plane blocks (FPBs) have emerged as attractive alternatives to traditional neuraxial approaches. In adult practice, the emergence of ultrasound-guided FPBs has transformed perioperative analgesia, providing effective alternatives to techniques such as thoracic epidural and paravertebral blocks, which may be limited by technical difficulty and risk of complications. The transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block, first described by Rafi in 2001, represents an early fascial plane technique and was adopted into pediatric anesthesia practice within the following decade [10, 11]. Early pediatric-focused studies and reviews emerged in the early 2010 s, demonstrating the feasibility and analgesic potential of these approaches in children [10].

However, the rapid expansion of ultrasound-guided FPBs has outpaced the development of high-quality pediatric-specific evidence. Extrapolation of adult data is constrained by variability in pain assessment, anatomy, and pharmacokinetics across age groups, while existing studies are often small and methodologically heterogeneous, limiting data synthesis and standardized recommendations [5, 12–15]. Consequently, there is a paucity of evidence for FPBs in pediatric patients compared to adults, with few high-quality randomized trials and substantial variability in technique, local anesthetic selection, and dosing. As their popularity grows, a focused pediatric-specific appraisal is warranted to address key knowledge gaps. This review evaluates the current evidence and provides a clinically oriented framework for the application of FPBs, particularly in cardiothoracic and abdominal analgesia.

What defines safe maximum doses in pediatric fascial plane blocks?

High-quality evidence guiding dosing strategies for pediatric fascial plane blocks is limited. These blocks rely on the spread of local anesthetic within the targeted plane and are therefore dependent on numerous factors, including size of that plane in varying age groups, injectate volume, concentration, total dose, choice of local anesthetic, and injection site. Age-dependent anatomic and physiologic variability in children and variable injectate spread in adult studies limit extrapolation to pediatric populations [16].

Complication rates in pediatric regional anesthesia are comparable to adults, and severe events are rare; the estimated incidence of LAST is 5–8 per 100,000 cases [17]. However, children, particularly neonates and infants, are at increased risk due to physiologic and anatomic differences. Immature hepatic metabolism reduces local anesthetic clearance. Serum clearance is approximately 33% of adult rates at 1 month and 66% at 6 months of age, not reaching full maturation until approximately 8 years. Looser connective tissue in neonates may further contribute to less predictable systemic absorption and within fascial planes [18]. A larger volume of distribution reduces peak plasma concentrations after a single bolus but increases the risk of accumulation with repeated doses or continuous infusions. Lower plasma protein levels, particularly in infants under 1 year, increase the free (active) fraction of drug. Additionally, higher cardiac output accelerates systemic absorption, resulting in higher peak plasma concentrations and increased susceptibility to cardiac toxicity, especially in children younger than 2 years. Ropivacaine has a smaller volume of distribution than bupivacaine in adults, and likely also in children, which may increase the window of safety and favor its use in pediatric regional anesthesia [5, 19].

Evidence-based dosing of local anesthetics in children is inconclusive, with limited high-quality data and inconsistent guidance across sources. The 2018 ASRA-ESRA joint advisory aimed to reduce this variability and improve safety. For single-shot ultrasound-guided fascial plane blocks (e.g., rectus sheath, transversus abdominis plane), recommended doses of bupivacaine or ropivacaine are 0.25–0.75 mg/kg (Evidence B1). For continuous fascial plane catheters, 0.2% ropivacaine or bupivacaine at 0.1–0.3 mg/kg/h is recommended (Evidence B3), with careful consideration of accumulation risk and age-dependent metabolism. Notably, recommended doses for peripheral nerve blocks are higher (0.5–1.5 mg/kg; Evidence B2), potentially reflecting more rapid absorption and higher peak plasma concentrations in fascial plane blocks. This underscores the importance of identifying the minimal effective dose and optimizing the balance between volume and concentration in this population [5].

A narrative review of pediatric truncal catheters (predominantly paravertebral, with some TAP blocks) demonstrated wide variation in both bolus and infusion dosing [20]. Median doses were 2.5 mg/kg bolus dose and 0.5 mg/kg/h continuous infusion for ropivacaine and 1.25 mg/kg and 0.33 mg/kg/h for bupivacaine, often exceeding guideline-based recommendations. The authors suggest that extrapolation and lumping of paravertebral data with TAP data is warranted due to similar elimination half-lives at the two sites [21]. They sought to identify/summarize safe dosages, not compare block sites. Although clinical toxicity was rare, pharmacokinetic studies identified multiple cases with serum concentrations above toxic thresholds, highlighting a narrow therapeutic margin.

Similarly, studies of erector spinae plane blocks (ESPB) demonstrate heterogeneity in volume (0.3–0.5 mL/kg), concentration, and choice of local anesthetic, with insufficient evidence to define optimal practice. These inconsistencies are compounded by limited understanding of local anesthetic spread and mechanism of action in pediatric fascial planes, which are largely extrapolated from adult and cadaveric data [15].

Another PRAN study further suggested that institutional practice is the strongest predictor of local anesthetic dosing, suggesting that dosing patterns are driven more by local culture than standardized evidence [22]. Notably, this analysis excluded children under 1 month of age and most fascial plane blocks (except transversus abdominis plane and rectus sheath blocks) due to small population size, underscoring the limited evidence base and challenges in standardizing dosing in this population.

In conclusion, Pediatric fascial plane blocks are safe and increasingly utilized; however, dosing remains variable and often institution dependent. Standardization is limited by gaps in pediatric-specific evidence, underscoring the need for improved pharmacokinetic data and well-designed clinical studies (Table 1).

Methods

A focused narrative review of the literature was performed to evaluate the role of fascial plane blocks in pediatric perioperative analgesia. Electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar were searched for relevant studies published from inception through April 2026. Search terms included combinations of “pediatric,” “regional anesthesia,” “fascial plane block,” “truncal block,” “analgesia,” and specific block names (e.g., transversus abdominis plane block, erector spinae plane block, quadratus lumborum block). Articles were limited to English-language publications. Both randomized controlled trials and observational studies were considered, along with relevant review articles, consensus statements, and society guidelines. Studies were selected based on relevance to clinical application, safety, and analgesic efficacy of commonly utilized pediatric fascial plane techniques. Additional key references were identified through manual review of bibliographies and author expertise.

Erector spinae plane block

The erector spinae plane block (ESPB) provides analgesia primarily through blockade of the dorsal rami of the ipsilateral thoracic spinal nerves, with variable extension to the ventral rami. Local anesthetic deposited deep to the erector spinae muscle is thought to spread cranio-caudally along the fascial plane, with potential anterior diffusion into the paravertebral, prevertebral, and, less consistently, epidural spaces. Cranio-caudal spread within the fascial plane results in multilevel dermatomal coverage, which is influenced by the volume of injectate [23]. The potential anterior spread may facilitate involvement of the ventral rami, however, the extent and consistency of such spread remain incompletely understood, with studies demonstrating variable and sometimes limited anterior distribution based on anatomical or positional changes during the block [16, 24]. As a result, the relative contribution of these structures to the clinical effects of ESPB continues to be an area of ongoing investigation. Sympathetic blockade is generally less pronounced with ESPBs, with little to no resultant hypotension in most cases [25, 26]. The relatively superficial erector spinae plane is located in a compressible compartment. Thus, ESPBs carry a decreased risk of neurologically devastating neuraxial hematoma [27].

Indications

In pediatric patients, ESPBs are broadly used for thoracic and abdominal analgesia. Lumbar and sacral erector spinae plane blocks have also been described for analgesia of the lower extremities and pelvis; however, their efficacy remains under investigation [28–30]. Indications for ESPB in pediatric patients include procedures associated with moderate to severe thoracic and abdominal pain, such as laparotomy, thoracotomy, rib fractures, and median sternotomy [25, 31, 32].

Evidence and clinical outcomes

The current evidence base consists predominantly of case reports and small case series demonstrating feasibility and analgesic efficacy in individual patients, as well as larger retrospective observational studies suggesting reduced opioid requirements when ESPB is incorporated into multimodal analgesic pathways [6, 31, 32].

Although the ESPB has gained considerable attention in pediatric anesthesia due to its versatility, relative technical simplicity, and favorable safety profile, the current evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous [25, 33]. Available data suggest that ESPB may provide effective perioperative analgesia and reduce opioid requirements across a range of pediatric procedures, including cardiac via sternotomy, thoracic (thoracoscopic lung lesion resection, diaphragmatic hernia repair, VATS), abdominal (appendectomies, inguinal hernia repair), spinal (posterior spinal fusion for scoliosis), pelvic (hypospadias repair), and lower extremity surgeries, and may serve as an effective alternative to neuraxial or paravertebral techniques [7, 15, 34–39]. Factors such as local anesthetic selection, dosing, and the use of single injection versus continuous techniques may influence fascial spread and thereby affect dermatomal coverage, block consistency, and duration of analgesia, with larger volumes generally associated with more extensive cranio-caudal spread but unpredictable anterior distribution. Other factors such as patient positioning during the procedure have shown to affect anterior spread in adult patients as well [16, 24]. Some comparative studies have been performed in adult patients showing reduced opioid usage in ESPB patients over those who received quadratus lumborum blocks in some abdominal surgeries (i.e., colorectal procedures), while in other abdominal procedures (i.e., total abdominal hysterectomy) each block showed overall reduction in opioid usage, but no clear difference between the two blocks [40, 41]. Further well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to define the optimal approach and establish standardized guidelines for ESPB in the pediatric population.

Considerations

Although ESPBs have a favorable safety profile, block-specific complications have occurred. These include unintended spread of local anesthetic into the paravertebral or epidural space with resultant sympathectomy and hypotension [42], as well as the general risks associated with any regional anesthetic technique (such as bleeding, infection, and inadvertent nerve damage). Minor complications include catheter-related issues (e.g., displacement or occlusion) as well as unintentional motor blockade [33, 43]. More serious complications are uncommon, but bradycardia and LAST have been reported [44–46].

Serratus anterior plane block

The serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) primarily targets the lateral branches of the thoracic intercostal nerves (T2–T9) with inconsistent spread to the long thoracic nerve, thoracodorsal nerve, and intercostobrachial nerve [47]. The lateral branches of the intercostal nerves emerge from the midaxillary line, traverse the deep and superficial serratus fascial planes, and divide into anterior and posterior branches supplying the anterolateral chest wall. Although cadaveric studies support spread to the long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerve, clinical significance of this spread remains variable [23, 47].

In the 2021 ASRA-ESRA Delphi nomenclature study, there was a strong consensus for distinguishing deep and superficial SAPB as distinct from neighboring procedures including pectoserratus plane block and rhomboid intercostal plane block [48]. However, in the meta-analyses identified for this narrative review, the two were generally not analyzed as distinct entities and instead the results were grouped in most instances. While these studies largely support SAPBs as safe and effective alternatives to ESPBs and paravertebral blocks, further high-quality clinical studies are needed to delineate differences in efficacy and outcomes between deep and superficial SAPB approaches in the pediatric population [49–54].

Indications

SAPB is described for perioperative analgesia to the anterior and lateral chest wall. In addition to its use in thoracic (e.g., Nuss procedure, pectus bar removal, thoracotomy, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery) and breast surgery, indications include pain associated with rib fractures, costochondral cartilage harvesting, chest tube placement or removal, as well as minimally invasive thoracic or cardiac procedures [49–51, 55].

Evidence and clinical outcomes

The current pediatric evidence for the SAPB consists primarily of small randomized controlled trials and observational cohort studies, supplemented by case series reports. It remains unclear whether SAPB provides analgesia equivalent to paravertebral, epidural, or ESPB techniques for thoracic procedures; however, its technical simplicity and favorable safety profile may make it an attractive option in appropriate clinical contexts. SAPB offers practical advantages, as it can be performed in the supine or slight lateral position without the need for patient repositioning—unlike erector spinae plane or neuraxial techniques, which often require lateral or sitting positioning [52]. SAPB provides analgesia primarily to the somatic innervation of the anterolateral thoracic wall and may therefore be useful as part of a multimodal analgesic strategy. However, in procedures associated with more complex pain mechanisms, such as thoracotomy, which involve substantial visceral and neuropathic components, SAPB alone may provide incomplete analgesia and is generally insufficient as a sole technique, necessitating supplementation with additional regional or systemic analgesic modalities [53, 54].

Considerations

SAPB is associated with minimal hemodynamic effects, as it does not produce significant sympathetic blockade. Rare complications such as transient upper extremity weakness or motor blockade have also been reported [56]. Analgesia is limited to the antero-lateral chest wall and does not reliably provide medial or posterior coverage [49]. Accordingly, SAPB may be particularly advantageous when targeted analgesia is desired, such as antero-lateral thoracotomy, chest tube placement, and rib fractures.

Interpectoral and pectoserratus plane blocks

The interpectoral plane block (IPB, previously also referred to as PECS 1) and a combination of interpectoral and pectoserratus plane blocks (IPB + PPB, previously also referred to as PECS 2) provide anterior thoracic wall analgesia [57]. The IPB targets the lateral and medial pectoral nerves, which innervate the pectoralis muscles, but does not reliably affect cutaneous sensory nerves [57]. The addition of PPB anesthetizes the lateral branches of the intercostal nerves (T2–T6), providing cutaneous sensory analgesia of the anterolateral chest wall. Similar to the superficial SAPB (SAPB), the PPB may spread to the long thoracic, thoracodorsal, and intercostobrachial nerves [58]. In the 2021 ASRA–ESRA Delphi nomenclature study, there was a weak consensus to continue distinguishing PPB as a distinct block from the superficial SAPB [48].

Indications

The IPB was first described by Blanco in 2011 for breast surgery in adults [57]. Due to its effect on the lateral and medial pectoral nerves, the IPB is most appropriately utilized for procedures involving the upper anterior chest wall and pectoral muscles with smaller incisions, such as pacemaker or port-a-cath insertion [59, 60]. The addition of PPB more reliably provides cutaneous sensory coverage of the anterior chest wall. Thus, the combination of IPB + PPB is more commonly indicated for pediatric procedures with larger incisions, more extensive axillary dissection, or increased anticipated postoperative pain, such as Nuss procedure and sternotomy [61, 62].

Evidence and clinical outcomes

In this narrative review of the literature, the IPB and PPB appear less frequently discussed in pediatric populations compared with SAPBs. The current pediatric evidence for the IPB and PPB is limited to case reports and small case series, supplemented with one small randomized-controlled trial. Most studies compare the success of IPB and PPB against no block in reduction of pain scores and opioid usage, showing success in sternotomy, thoracotomy, and pacemaker procedures [53, 59, 61]. There are little to no comparative studies comparing the success of IPB and PPB against other blocks in pediatric patients. There is some data comparing IPB + PPB versus ESPB in adult patients showing similar reductions in pain scores and opioid usage in mastectomy and breast-conserving surgery, while superior pain control in ESPB patients undergoing mitral valve repair via mini-thoracotomy incision [63–65]. Further investigation, especially in pediatric patients, is warranted.

Technique/approach

A high-frequency linear probe is placed medial to the coracoid process in the parasagittal plane until the first and second ribs are visualized. The probe is then moved laterally to identify the third and fourth ribs along with the major and minor pectoral muscles. Local anesthetic is injected between the pectoralis major and minor muscles (IPB) and, for addition of PPB, between the pectoralis minor and serratus muscle or rib (Fig. 4). For dosing, see Table 1.

Considerations

The interpectoral and pectoserratus plane blocks have an increased risk of pneumothorax given relative proximity to the thoracic cavity. Doing this block in plane and seeing the needle throughout the procedure will heavily reduce this risk. The thoracoacromial artery is also present in this area. The use of color Doppler technology can be an important tool used to help identify this artery, but vigilant aspirations must be done to also decrease the risk of LAST, especially in smaller children, as the artery may be hard to visualize.

Parasternal intercostal plane block

The parasternal intercostal plane block (PIPB) is an ultrasound-guided fascial plane block designed to provide analgesia to the anterior chest wall by targeting the anterior branches of the intercostal nerves [66]. The block is typically performed adjacent to the sternum within the parasternal intercostal fascial plane and has emerged as a useful regional anesthetic technique for procedures involving median sternotomy, anterior thoracic incisions, and chest wall surgery.

Indications

Indications for the parasternal intercostal plane block in pediatric practice include cardiac surgery via median sternotomy, sternal fracture, midline mediastinal and chest tubes, and other procedures involving midline thoracic incisions [67–69]. Given its targeted coverage of the parasternal region, this block may be particularly useful as an adjunct to other thoracic wall fascial plane blocks when broader chest wall analgesia is required.

Both deep and superficial approaches to the PIPB provide anteromedial analgesia at levels T2–T6, inconsistently reaching T1–T7 [66]. However, superficial PIPB may be associated with less reliable spread due to a tighter fascial plane. Thus, in cases in which greater spread of local anesthetic is necessary for analgesia (for example, the presence of a midline chest tube), a deep PIP may be preferable.

Evidence and clinical outcomes

Literature for pediatric PIPB is limited to retrospective cohort studies, small randomized control studies, and case series. Available studies in pediatric cardiac and mediastinal surgery demonstrate improved postoperative analgesia, reduced opioid requirements, and lower pain scores following median sternotomy [67, 69]. Emerging literature also supports similar efficacy in anterior chest wall dermatomal coverage with both superficial and deep parasternal approaches in cardiac surgery [70]. In scenarios where the transversus thoracis plane or internal thoracic artery may already be threatened, or where avoidance of this plane is prudent, superficial PIPB may be preferred [71].

Technique/approach

Placement of the probe in the parasagittal position in between ribs 3–4 or 4–5 lateral to the sternum will help to identify the important structures: the pectoralis major, intercostal, and transverse thoracis muscles, the sternum, pleura, and the internal thoracic artery (also known as the internal mammary artery) which travels parallel to the sternum between the internal intercostal muscles and transverse thoracis muscle. Considerations

Local anesthetic is deposited in-plane between the internal intercostal muscle and the transversus thoracis muscle adjacent to the sternum, resulting in analgesia over the parasternal region. Special caution is required in this region due to the proximity of the internal thoracic artery [72]. Identification of this vessel using color Doppler ultrasound prior to needle advancement and injection is recommended to reduce the risk of inadvertent vascular puncture or intravascular injection. Although generally considered safe in experienced hands, potential complications include pleural puncture with pneumothorax and, given the proximity to the pericardium and internal thoracic vessels, rare but serious vascular or cardiac injury [73]. As always, aspiration prior to injection can decrease the risk of intravascular injection and LAST.

Quadratus lumborum block

The quadratus lumborum block (QLB) is a common fascial plane block in which local anesthetic is deposited adjacent to the quadratus lumborum muscle, with the goal of achieving somatic and potentially visceral analgesia. The proposed mechanism involves the spread of local anesthetic along the thoracolumbar fascia, which may facilitate cranio-caudal extension toward the paravertebral space. This spread may result in blockade of the ventral rami of the thoracolumbar spinal nerves and, in some cases, involvement of the sympathetic trunk, thereby contributing to both somatic and visceral analgesia. However, the extent of paravertebral and sympathetic spread remains variable and has not been consistently demonstrated across anatomical and imaging studies [74–76]. Furthermore, the mechanism of action may differ depending on the specific QLB approach (Fig. 6), with anterior techniques more likely to achieve paravertebral extension, whereas lateral and posterior approaches may produce more limited or primarily somatic analgesic effects [48, 77]. Overall, the precise mechanism of QLB remains incompletely understood and is likely influenced by injection site, volume, and technique.

Indications

The QLB is a versatile technique commonly utilized for procedures involving the lower abdomen, including appendectomy and inguinal hernia repair, where it provides analgesia for the lower thoracoabdominal dermatomes. It has also been applied in selected cases for surgeries such as orchiopexy, hydrocele repair, and hip procedures, reflecting its potential for broader dermatomal coverage depending on the approach and spread of local anesthetic [78–80]. Importantly, the various approaches to quadratus lumborum block (QLB) are associated with distinct patterns of injectate spread and resulting analgesic distribution, which may inform selection of technique depending on the surgical procedure. Lateral and posterior approaches generally result in more limited or predominantly somatic abdominal wall analgesia, whereas anterior approaches are more likely to facilitate spread toward the paravertebral space and may provide broader dermatomal coverage, including potential visceral analgesia. As such, the choice of QLB approach should be tailored to the surgical site and expected pain distribution, particularly in abdominal procedures.

Evidence and clinical outcomes

In pediatric patients, the QLB has gained popularity as an alternative to neuraxial techniques for abdominal surgery due to its effectiveness in analgesia and broader dermatomal coverage [78, 80]. There is more pediatric literature for the QLB efficacy consisting of retrospective cohort studies, case series, and prospective data. In pediatric patients undergoing unilateral inguinal hernia repair or orchiopexy, quadratus lumborum blocks have demonstrated longer-lasting and more effective postoperative analgesia compared with transversus abdominis plane blocks [4]. In adult patients, QLB had shown superior pain control and decreased opioid consumption in abdominal surgery [81].

Technique/approach

The QLB is performed with the patient in the lateral decubitus or supine position with a slight tilt, depending on surgical access and practitioner preference. A high-frequency linear probe is often sufficient in infants and small children, whereas a lower-frequency curvilinear transducer may be required in larger patients. The transducer is placed in the transverse plane superior to the anterior superior iliac spine and moved posteriorly to identify the abdominal wall muscles (external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis) and the quadratus lumborum muscle adjacent to the transverse process.

For the lateral approach, local anesthetic is deposited at the anterolateral border of the quadratus lumborum muscle. In the posterior technique, injection occurs posterior to the quadratus lumborum muscle, whereas the anterior approach involves advancing the needle through the quadratus lumborum muscle to deposit local anesthetic between the quadratus lumborum and psoas major muscles (Fig. 6).

Considerations

The QLB is generally considered safe under ultrasound guidance; however, potential complications include inadvertent intraperitoneal or intravascular injection, local anesthetic systemic toxicity, and unintended spread into the paravertebral or epidural space, which may result in sympathetic blockade and hypotension [80]. However, spread toward the lumbar plexus has been reported, particularly with anterior QLB, and may result in transient lower-extremity weakness, including quadriceps weakness, which should be considered when early ambulation is desired [82]. Block failure may also occur if local anesthetic is deposited within the incorrect fascial plane.

Transversus abdominis plane block

The transversus abdominis plane blocks (TAPB) are ultrasound-guided fascial plane blocks that provide analgesia to the anterolateral abdominal wall through blockade of the ventral rami of the lower thoracic and upper lumbar spinal nerves. Local anesthetic is deposited between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles, targeting the nerves as they traverse this plane. When incorporated into multimodal analgesic regimens, TAPBs have been associated with improved postoperative pain control and reduced opioid consumption, particularly in procedures involving lower abdominal incisions [83–85].

Variations of the TAPB—including lateral, subcostal, and oblique-subcostal approaches—may be utilized depending on the surgical site and desired dermatomal coverage. A combination of these TAP blocks can also be used to block the targeted dermatomes.

Indications

TAPBs are commonly utilized for a variety of lower abdominal surgical procedures, including appendectomy, inguinal hernia repair, orchiopexy, urologic procedures, colorectal surgery, and laparoscopic abdominal interventions [86, 87].

Evidence and clinical outcomes

Current pediatric evidence for TAP blocks includes randomized controlled trials, retrospective cohort studies, meta-analyses, and case series across a wide range of lower abdominal procedures. Published studies consistently demonstrate reductions in postoperative opioid consumption and improved pain scores compared with systemic analgesia alone [86, 87] Comparative investigations have shown TAP blocks to provide effective analgesia for appendectomy, hernia repair, urologic, and laparoscopic procedures against caudal neuraxial procedures [83]. As noted previously, in pediatric patients undergoing unilateral inguinal hernia repair or orchiopexy, TAP blocks are less effective for postoperative analgesia compared with QLB [4].

Overall, TAP blocks are well established in pediatric regional anesthesia and possess one of the strongest evidence bases among pediatric fascial plane blocks, but like most of the blocks mentioned in this paper, data remain limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneity.

Technique/approach

A lateral TAPB is performed with the patient in the supine position. A high-frequency linear probe is placed in a transverse orientation along the midaxillary line to identify the three layers of the abdominal wall musculature: the external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis muscles. Using an in-plane approach, the needle is advanced into the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles. Local anesthetic is then injected to achieve separation of the plane and appropriate spread of the local anesthetic. A lateral TAPB covers T10–12 dermatomes.

A subcostal TAPB is performed similarly to the lateral TAPB, but the probe is placed transversely along the most caudal rib (as shown in Fig. 7). The same musculature is identified on ultrasound and again; local is deposited using an in-plane approach into the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles. A subcostal TAPB will provide pain relief for levels T6–T9.

The oblique subcostal TAPB involves advancing the probe and needle along the subcostal margin in an oblique trajectory, allowing for more extensive longitudinal spread of local anesthetic along the transversus abdominis plane. This approach may provide broader coverage of the upper and mid-abdomen and is often utilized for procedures involving supraumbilical or large abdominal incisions, covering T6–T9 with some coverage extending to T10 [88].

Of note, the 2021 ASRA-ESRA Delphi consensus study recommended that posterior TAPB and lateral QLB be considered synonymous, with lateral QLB designated as the preferred terminology (Fig. 6).

Considerations

Potential complications of the TAPB include inadvertent intraperitoneal injection, bowel injury, vascular puncture, and local anesthetic systemic toxicity. Intraperitoneal injury is rare but has been reported despite ultrasound guidance [10]. Accurate identification of the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles is essential to ensure block efficacy and reduce the risk of complications. Continuous ultrasound visualization of the needle tip, along with careful aspiration prior to injection, helps minimize the risk of unintended intravascular or intraperitoneal administration.

Rectus sheath block

The rectus sheath block (RSB) provides analgesia to the anterior abdominal wall by targeting the terminal branches of the thoracoabdominal nerves within the rectus sheath. Local anesthetic is deposited in the potential space between the posterior aspect of the rectus abdominis muscle and the posterior rectus sheath, resulting in blockade of the anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves corresponding to the T6–T11 dermatomes. This technique has been shown to provide effective postoperative analgesia and may contribute to a reduction in emergence delirium in pediatric patients [88, 89].

Indications

The RSB is utilized in pediatric patients undergoing procedures involving midline abdominal incisions, such as exploratory laparotomy. It has shown efficacy in single-port appendectomy procedures [90]. It is particularly well suited for surgeries in which somatic pain is confined to the anterior abdominal wall, as it provides analgesia via blockade of the terminal branches of the intercostal nerves within the rectus sheath. In addition to single-injection techniques, continuous catheter-based approaches have been described in case reports mostly in adult patients, allowing for prolonged postoperative analgesia and potentially reducing opioid requirements in patients undergoing more extensive or painful procedures [91]. These techniques may be particularly beneficial when extended duration of analgesia is desired or when neuraxial techniques are contraindicated.

Evidence and clinical outcomes

Current pediatric evidence for RSB includes small retrospective studies, and case reports mostly focused on midline abdominal surgeries. Existing literature supports improved perioperative analgesia, reduced opioid consumption, and lower postoperative pain scores following umbilical hernia repair and other midline abdominal procedures [84, 90].

Comparative studies have demonstrated superior analgesia compared with local wound infiltration in selected pediatric populations [90]. In adult patients, studies have shown that in patients undergoing major abdominal surgery more likely benefit from TAP blocks rather than RSB, likely due to lack of visceral coverage in RSB [92].

Technique/approach

The RSB is performed with the patient in the supine position. A high-frequency linear ultrasound transducer is placed transversely over the anterior abdominal wall near the midline to identify the linea alba and the paired rectus abdominis muscles. The transducer is then moved laterally to better delineate the lateral border of the rectus muscle and the posterior rectus sheath. Using an in-plane approach, the needle is advanced into the fascial plane between the posterior aspect of the rectus muscle and the posterior rectus sheath. Local anesthetic is then injected to achieve separation of this plane and appropriate spread along the sheath (Fig. 8). For dosing, see Table 1.

Schematic of relevant anatomy with needle placement and probe placement for rectus sheath block. The linea alba may be identified midline with the probe in transverse orientation. The anterior transversus abdominis becomes visible with lateral movement of the probe

Considerations

Care must be taken to avoid inadvertent peritoneal puncture and injury to underlying intra-abdominal structures. Continuous ultrasound visualization of the needle tip is essential to ensure accurate placement within the intended fascial plane. Additionally, the inferior epigastric vessels may course within or adjacent to the rectus sheath and should be identified prior to needle advancement as large hematomas have been identified in adults undergoing RSBs [93]. Careful sonographic assessment, along with frequent aspirations before injection, helps minimize the risk of unintended intravascular administration.

Collectively, these techniques represent an increasingly important component of pediatric anesthetic practice. Pediatric regional anesthesiology is rapidly expanding and evolving through new techniques, technology, and research, yielding insights into safe clinical practice and systematic implementation. Although pediatric regional anesthesia is generally safe with a low complication rate, concerns about safety and variability in acceptance persist. Adoption of pediatric regional anesthesia varies widely due to differences in provider comfort, institutional resources, and global healthcare disparities. Safe implementation requires the availability of skilled and available providers, and regional anesthesia is increasingly incorporated into educational requirements by the American Board of Anesthesiology. In addition to exploring potential benefits such as reduced opioid requirements, psychological stress, behavioral consequences, hospital costs, and length of hospital stay, there are ongoing efforts to discern the neurodevelopmental impact of regional anesthesia.
