A solvable model of noisy coupled oscillators with fully random interactions Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Harukuni Ikeda

Abstract
We introduce a solvable spherical model of coupled oscillators with fully random interactions and distributed natural frequencies. Using the dynamical mean-field theory, we derive self-consistent equations for the steady-state response and correlation functions. We show that any finite width of the natural-frequency distribution suppresses the finite-temperature spin-glass transition, because the resulting low-frequency singularity of the correlation function is incompatible with the spherical constraint. At zero temperature, however, a spin-glass phase persists for arbitrary frequency dispersion. This residual zero-temperature glassiness is likely a special feature of the spherical dynamics and would be destroyed by local nonlinearities. The model thus provides a solvable oscillator framework for studying how nonequilibrium perturbations suppress finite-temperature glassy freezing.

Introduction

Synchronization is a ubiquitous collective phenomenon observed in a wide
variety of nonequilibrium systems, ranging from biological and chemical
oscillators to engineered dynamical networks. A standard theoretical
framework for studying synchronization is the Kuramoto model, which
describes interacting phase oscillators with distributed natural
frequencies
19

accessibility, the Kuramoto model and its variants have played a central
role in the study of collective dynamics in many-body
systems

;

;

An important extension of the Kuramoto model is to introduce disorder
into the

When the couplings contain both positive and negative components,

behavior in addition to synchronization. Randomly coupled oscillator
systems have therefore attracted considerable interest as nonequilibrium
analogues of disordered mean-field systems in equilibrium statistical
mechanics. Exact analytical results, however, are available only for
special choices of the interaction matrix, such as low-rank
couplings

;

;

;

whereas models with fully random interactions have been studied mainly
by numerical or perturbative

;

;
20
. Recent works
suggest that a robust glass phase may be absent in
the thermodynamic limit

basis for this conclusion remains incomplete because these models are
not analytically solvable.

A useful perspective on this problem comes from earlier studies of

limit destroy the finite-temperature spin-glass

mechanism may also operate in disordered oscillator systems, where
nonequilibrium effects arise not from asymmetric couplings but from

In this paper, we introduce a solvable mean-field model of randomly
coupled oscillators inspired by the previous works of the spherical

with fixed unit modulus,
\left|z_{i}\right|^{2}=1

As a first step, we show that the spherical formulation reproduces the
basic synchronization transition in the ferromagnetic case. Although the
spherical model is simpler than the original phase model, it still

We then turn to fully random Gaussian couplings. Using the dynamical

zero-temperature glassiness is likely a special feature of the spherical

II
Model

In this section, we introduce the model studied in this work.
We begin with the standard Kuramoto model and then formulate
its spherical counterpart, which replaces the local unit-modulus
constraints by a global spherical constraint. This modification
preserves the basic oscillator structure while rendering the model
analytically tractable.

II.1
Kuramoto model

The Kuramoto model is defined by the equation of motion
19
;
32
;
1

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}=\Omega_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}),

(1)

where
\Omega_{i}
denotes the natural frequency and
J_{ij}
is a
symmetric interaction matrix. For later convenience, we rewrite the
dynamics in terms of the complex amplitude

\displaystyle z_{i}=e^{i\theta_{i}}.

(2)

In this representation, the equation of motion can be written
as
33
;
22

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{i}=-\mu_{i}z_{i}+i\Omega_{i}z_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}z_{j},

(3)

where
\mu_{i}
is a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the constraint

\left|z_{i}\right|^{2}=1
. The difficulty is that
\mu_{i}
depends implicitly on
the instantaneous state, which makes the dynamics nonlinear and
precludes a simple analytical treatment.

II.2
Spherical model

To obtain an analytically solvable model, we relax the local constraints

\left|z_{i}\right|^{2}=1
and instead impose the global spherical constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|z_{i}\right|^{2}=N.

(4)

The equation of motion then becomes

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{i}=-\mu z_{i}+i\Omega_{i}z_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}z_{j}+\xi_{i},

(5)

where
\mu
is the Lagrange multiplier enforcing
Eq. (
4
). We also include a complex Gaussian white
noise with zero mean and covariance
2

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}^{*}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2T\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\qquad\left\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=0.

(6)

This spherical formulation retains the competition among coupling,
frequency disorder, and noise, while greatly simplifying the analysis.
As we show below, it reproduces the standard synchronization transition
in the ferromagnetic case and remains analytically tractable even in the
presence of fully random interactions.

The Lagrange multiplier
\mu
is determined from the spherical
constraint
9
;
8
. Taking the time
derivative of Eq. (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|z_{i}\right|^{2}=-2\mu N+2\sum_{ij}J_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}^{*}+2TN=0,

(7)

which yields

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}J_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}^{*}+T.

(8)

This expression shows in particular that
\mu
is real.

III
Benchmark case: ferromagnetic interaction

Before turning to fully random interactions, we first consider the
ferromagnetic case as a benchmark. In this case, the spherical model
reproduces the standard synchronization transition and thus provides a
useful reference point for the disordered model studies below.

III.1
Settings

We consider the uniform interaction matrix

\displaystyle J_{ij}=\frac{K}{N}.

(9)

The equation of motion then becomes

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{i}(t)=-\mu z_{i}(t)+i\Omega_{i}z_{i}(t)+KZ(t)+\xi_{i},

(10)

where

\displaystyle Z(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}(t)

(11)

is the complex order parameter. The state with
{Z=0}
corresponds to
an incoherent state, whereas
{\left|Z\right|>0}
signals synchronization.

III.2
Steady state

We now analyze the model in the steady state, in which
\mu
and
Z

are time independent.
Since Eq. (
10
) is linear,
it can be solved straightforwardly by Fourier transformation:

\displaystyle z_{i}(\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega)\frac{KZ}{\mu-i\Omega_{i}}+\frac{\xi_{i}(\omega)}{\mu+i(\omega-\Omega_{i})}.

(12)

Here and below, Fourier-transformed quantities are written as functions
of
\omega
, with the convention

\displaystyle f(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{-i\omega t}f(t).

(13)

Assuming that
\xi_{i}
and
\Omega_{i}
are uncorrelated,
and averaging over
i=1,\cdots,N
, we obtain

\displaystyle Z=\frac{KZ}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\mu-i\Omega_{i}}=KZ\int d\Omega g(\Omega)\frac{1}{\mu-i\Omega},

(14)

where

\displaystyle g(\Omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\Omega-\Omega_{i})

(15)

is the distribution of natural frequencies.
The Lagrange multiplier (
8
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mu=K\left|Z\right|^{2}+T.

(16)

The order parameter is determined self-consistently from
Eqs. (
14
) and (
16
).

Unless otherwise stated, we assume in the following that the natural
frequencies are drawn from the Cauchy distribution

\displaystyle g(\Omega)=\frac{\Delta}{\pi}\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+\Delta^{2}}.

(17)

In this case, Eq. (
14
) becomes

\displaystyle Z=\frac{KZ}{\mu+\Delta}.

(18)

This result also follows directly from the analytic structure of the
Cauchy distribution. Because
g(\Omega)
has a simple pole at

\Omega=i\Delta
in the upper half-plane, the integral in
Eq. (
14
) is given by the residue at that pole, namely
by evaluating the non-singular part of the integrand at

\Omega=i\Delta

23
. Combining Eqs. (
16
)
and (
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|Z\right|=\begin{cases}0&T>T_{c},\\
\sqrt{\frac{K-T-\Delta}{K}}&T\leq Tc,\end{cases}

(19)

with the transition temperature

\displaystyle T_{c}=K-\Delta.

(20)

The resulting phase diagram is shown in Fig.
1
. The
spherical model reproduces the standard mean-field synchronization
transition in the presence of both frequency dispersion and thermal
noise, in qualitative agreement with previous results for noisy Kuramoto
models
19
;
29
;
31
;
1
. The
same result can also be obtained by directly analyzing the relaxation
dynamics of the ferromagnetic model, see Appendix.
A
.

Phase diagram of the
spherical model with ferromagnetic interactions. The filled and unfilled
regions denote the synchronized and incoherent phases, respectively.

IV
Random interactions

IV.1
Settings

We now consider random interactions. The coupling matrix
J_{ij}
is
symmetric,
J_{ij}=J_{ji}
, and is drawn from a Gaussian distribution
with zero mean and covariance

\displaystyle\left\langle J_{ij}J_{lk}\right\rangle=\frac{J^{2}}{N}\left(\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\right).

(21)

Since the model is fully connected, its dynamics can be treated by a
standard cavity construction, or equivalently by a dynamical mean-field
theory for the single-site response and correlation functions

12
;
13
;
4
.

IV.2
Cavity equations

We now derive the corresponding effective single-site process. The key
step is to add an extra variable
z_{0}(t)
to the original
N
-site
system and examine its coupling to the other degrees of freedom. In the
thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, the enlarged
(N+1)
-site system is
statistically equivalent to the original one, which closes the cavity
construction.

For
i=1,\dots,N
, the equation of motion becomes

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{i}(t)=-\mu z_{i}(t)+i\Omega_{i}z_{i}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}z_{j}(t)+\xi_{i}(t)+\delta\xi_{i}(t),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\delta\xi_{i}(t)=J_{i0}z_{0}(t)

(23)

represents the perturbation induced by the additional site
z_{0}
. To
linear order in this perturbation, one obtains

\displaystyle z_{i}(t)=z_{i\backslash 0}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,R_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})\,\delta\xi_{j}(t^{\prime}),

(24)

up to corrections of order
1/N
. Here
z_{i\backslash 0}(t)
denotes
the dynamical variable in the cavity system without site
0
, and

\displaystyle R_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta z_{i}(t)}{\delta\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})}

(25)

is the response function.

The equation of motion for the added site
reads

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{0}(t)=-\mu z_{0}(t)+i\Omega_{0}z_{0}(t)+\xi_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{0i}z_{i}(t),

(26)

where
J_{0i}
obeys the same statistics as the original couplings:

J_{0i}=J_{i0}
,
\left\langle J_{0i}\right\rangle=0
, and

\left\langle J_{0i}J_{0j}\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}J^{2}/N
. Substituting the linear-response
expression for
z_{i}(t)
into the last term of
Eq. (
26
), we obtain
4

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{0i}z_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{0i}z_{i\backslash 0}(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\sum_{ij}J_{0i}R_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})J_{j0}z_{0}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{0i}z_{i\backslash 0}(t)+J^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,R(t,t^{\prime})z_{0}(t^{\prime}),

(27)

where

\displaystyle R(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{ii}(t,t^{\prime}).

(28)

To justify the second term in Eq. (
27
), note that

R_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})
is evaluated in the cavity system without site
0
and is
therefore independent of
J_{0i}
and
J_{j0}
to leading order in

1/N
. In the thermodynamic limit, this quantity is self-averaging.
One may
then average over the couplings to obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{ij}J_{0i}R_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})J_{j0}\right\rangle=\frac{J^{2}}{N}\sum_{i}R_{ii}(t,t^{\prime})=J^{2}R(t,t^{\prime}),

(29)

which justifies the replacement in
Eq. (
27
)
4
. We thus
arrive at the effective single-site process

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mu z_{0}(t)+i\Omega_{0}z_{0}(t)+J^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,R(t,t^{\prime})z_{0}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\xi_{0}(t)+\eta_{0}(t),

(30)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{0i}z_{i\backslash 0}(t)

(31)

is a sum of many weak random contributions and therefore becomes an
effective Gaussian noise with covariance

\displaystyle\left\langle\eta_{0}(t)\eta_{0}^{*}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=J^{2}C(t,t^{\prime}),

\displaystyle C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle z_{i\backslash 0}(t)z_{i\backslash 0}^{*}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(32)

The structure of the effective stochastic process derived above is
essentially the same as that obtained previously for the spherical SK
model and related mean-field disordered
systems
7
;
6
;
4
;
12
,
although in the present case it is extended to complex variables with
distributed natural frequencies.

IV.3
Steady-state solution

We now solve the effective single-site process (
30
) in
Fourier space, as in the ferromagnetic case. For a fixed value of

\Omega_{0}
, we denote the corresponding solution by
z_{0}(t|\Omega_{0})
.
Upon averaging over
\Omega_{0}
with weight
g(\Omega_{0})
, the added site
becomes statistically equivalent to a typical site in the original
system in the thermodynamic limit. Assuming time-translation
invariance in the steady state,
R(t,t^{\prime})=R(t-t^{\prime})
, we obtain

\displaystyle z_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})=R_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})\left[\xi_{0}(\omega)+\eta_{0}(\omega)\right],

(33)

where we have defined the response for
z_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})
as follows:

\displaystyle R_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})=\frac{\partial z_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})}{\partial\xi_{0}(\omega)}=\frac{1}{i\omega-i\Omega_{0}+\mu-J^{2}R(\omega)}.

(34)

Averaging over
\Omega_{0}
, we then obtain the self-consistency equation

\displaystyle R(\omega)=\int d\Omega_{0}\,g(\Omega_{0})R_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0}).

(35)

This equation determines the response function
R(\omega)
. Similarly,
taking the disorder and noise average of the squared modulus of
Eq. (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})=\left|R_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})\right|^{2}\left[2T+J^{2}C(\omega)\right],

(36)

where
C_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})
is the Fourier transform of the single-site
correlation function for fixed
\Omega_{0}
. Averaging over
\Omega_{0}

yields

\displaystyle C(\omega)=\left(2T+J^{2}C(\omega)\right)\int d\Omega_{0}\,g(\Omega_{0})\left|R_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})\right|^{2}.

(37)

The integral in Eq. (
37
) can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{0}\,g(\Omega_{0})\left|R_{0}(\omega|\Omega_{0})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int d\Omega_{0}\,g(\Omega_{0})\frac{1}{s(\omega)+s^{*}(\omega)}\left(\frac{1}{s(\omega)-i\Omega_{0}}+\frac{1}{s^{*}(\omega)+i\Omega_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(\omega)+R^{*}(\omega)}{s(\omega)+s^{*}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\mu-J^{2}\alpha(\omega)},

(38)

where
\alpha(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\,R(\omega)
and

\displaystyle s(\omega)=i\omega+\mu-J^{2}R(\omega).

(39)

Substituting this result into Eq. (
37
), we find

\displaystyle C(\omega)=\frac{2T\alpha(\omega)}{\mu-2J^{2}\alpha(\omega)}.

(40)

Finally, the Lagrange multiplier
\mu
is fixed by the spherical
constraint,

\displaystyle 1=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\omega\,C(\omega)=\frac{T}{\pi}\int d\omega\,\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\mu-2J^{2}\alpha(\omega)}.

(41)

IV.4
Ergodicity-breaking condition and transition point

In the nonergodic phase, the correlation function does not decay to zero
at long times:

\displaystyle C_{\infty}\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}C(t)>0.

(42)

Equivalently, its Fourier transform contains a delta-function
contribution at zero frequency,

\displaystyle C(\omega)=\delta C(\omega)+2\pi\delta(\omega)C_{\infty},

(43)

where
\delta C(\omega)
denotes the regular part. Substituting this
decomposition into Eq. (
37
) and collecting the terms
proportional to
C_{\infty}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=J^{2}\int d\Omega_{0}\,g(\Omega_{0})\left|R_{0}(0|\Omega_{0})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{J^{2}\alpha(0)}{\mu-J^{2}\alpha(0)},

(44)

which implies

\displaystyle\mu=2J^{2}\alpha(0).

(45)

Substituting this relation into the spherical
constraint (
41
), we obtain the transition temperature

\displaystyle T_{c}=2\pi J^{2}\left(\int d\omega\,\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\alpha(0)-\alpha(\omega)}\right)^{-1}.

(46)

For
T<T_{c}
, the delta-function contribution at
\omega=0
must be
treated explicitly in the spherical constraint:

\displaystyle 1=C_{\infty}+\frac{T}{\pi}\int d\omega\,\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\mu-2J^{2}\alpha(\omega)}.

(47)

Using Eq. (
45
) and (
46
), we then find

\displaystyle C_{\infty}=1-\frac{T}{T_{c}}.

(48)

IV.5
Cauchy distribution

We now specialize to the Cauchy distribution,

\displaystyle g(\Omega)=\frac{\Delta}{\pi}\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+\Delta^{2}},

(49)

for which the self-consistency equation for the response function can be
solved analytically.
Starting from Eq. (
35
), and proceeding as in the
ferromagnetic case, the integral over
\Omega
can be evaluated by
contour integration for the Cauchy distribution, yielding

\displaystyle R(\omega)=\frac{1}{i\omega+\Delta+\mu-J^{2}R(\omega)},

(50)

leading to

\displaystyle R(\omega)=\frac{i\omega+\Delta+\mu-\sqrt{(i\omega+\Delta+\mu)^{2}-4J^{2}}}{2J^{2}}.

(51)

Its real part,
\alpha(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\,R(\omega)
, is given by

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)=\frac{1}{2J^{2}}\left[\Delta+\mu-\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{a(\omega)^{2}+b(\omega)^{2}}+a(\omega)}{2}}\right],

(52)

where

\displaystyle a(\omega)=(\Delta+\mu)^{2}-\omega^{2}-4J^{2},\qquad b(\omega)=2\omega(\Delta+\mu).

(53)

For finite
\Delta
, the small-
\omega
behavior is

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)=\alpha(0)-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left[(\Delta+\mu)^{2}-4J^{2}\right]^{3/2}}+O(\omega^{3}).

(54)

The condition for ergodicity breaking, Eq. (
45
),
determines the critical value of the Lagrange multiplier as

\displaystyle\mu_{c}=-\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+4J^{2}}.

(55)

We can then examine whether a finite-temperature transition exists. For

\Delta=0
, the equations reduce to those of the spherical
Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
6
, and one recovers
the standard result

\displaystyle T_{c}=J.

(56)

For any finite
\Delta
, however, the situation changes qualitatively.
At the putative transition point, Eqs. (
54
) and
(
55
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\alpha(0)-\alpha(\omega)}\sim\frac{\alpha(0)\Delta^{3}}{\omega^{2}}\qquad(\omega\to 0).

(57)

The integral in Eq. (
46
) therefore diverges in the
infrared, so that the transition temperature is driven to zero:

\displaystyle T_{c}=0\qquad(\Delta>0).

(58)

Thus, within the present spherical dynamics, any finite width of the
natural-frequency distribution destroys the finite-temperature
spin-glass transition.

This analytic conclusion is reflected directly in the long-time behavior
of the correlation function. To illustrate this point,
Fig.
2
shows the time correlation function obtained
from

\displaystyle C(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,C(\omega)\cos(\omega t).

(59)

For
\Delta=0
, the correlation function approaches a nonzero plateau
below
T_{c}
and decays to zero above it. For
\Delta>0
, by contrast,

C(t)
decays to zero at long times for all
T
, consistent with the
absence of a finite-temperature glass phase.

\displaystyle\Lambda\equiv\lim_{\omega\to 0}C(\omega).

(60)

Since
\Lambda
is proportional to the time-integrated correlation
function, it provides a natural measure of the characteristic

controlled by a single relaxation time scale,

\displaystyle C(t)\sim f(t/\tau),

(61)

with an integrable scaling function
f
, then one has

\Lambda\propto\tau
.

As shown in Fig.

diverges at the

\Delta>0
it remains finite for all

and diverges only in the
limit
T\to 0
. A more detailed analysis, presented in
Appendix

, shows that

\displaystyle\Lambda\sim T^{-1}\Delta^{-3}

(62)

for small
T
and small
\Delta
. This scaling is confirmed by the data

(b). The above result therefore
shows that the relaxation becomes increasingly slow as
\Delta\to 0
.
For fixed
T>0
, one expects

\displaystyle\tau\sim\Delta^{-3}\qquad(\Delta\to 0).

(63)

This expectation is supported by Fig.

(a), which
shows that the relaxation becomes progressively slower as

decreases, and more directly by Fig.

(b), where the
late-time correlation functions collapse when time is rescaled by

.

IV.6
General symmetric distribution

We now show that the suppression of the finite-temperature glass
transition is not specific to the Cauchy distribution, but holds more
generally for symmetric distributions of natural frequencies

g(\Omega)=g(-\Omega)
. To this end, we analyze the low-frequency
behavior of the self-consistency equation in a distribution-independent
form.

Introducing

\displaystyle F(s)=\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\frac{1}{s-i\Omega},

(64)

the self-consistency equation (
35
) can be written as

\displaystyle R(\omega)=F(s(\omega)),

(65)

where

\displaystyle s(\omega)=i\omega+\mu-J^{2}R(\omega).

(66)

At zero frequency, this reduces to

\displaystyle R(0)=F(s(0)),

(67)

or equivalently

\displaystyle R(0)=\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\frac{1}{\mu-i\Omega-J^{2}R(0)}.

(68)

To determine the transition temperature, it is sufficient to examine the
small-
\omega
behavior of
\alpha(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\,R(\omega)
.
Differentiating Eq. (
65
) with respect to
\omega
, we
find

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(\omega)=F^{\prime}(s(\omega))\,s^{\prime}(\omega)=F^{\prime}(s(\omega))\left(i-J^{2}R^{\prime}(\omega)\right),

(69)

which gives

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(0)=i\,\frac{F^{\prime}(s(0))}{1+J^{2}F^{\prime}(s(0))}.

(70)

Differentiating once more, we obtain

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(s(0))}{\left(1+J^{2}F^{\prime}(s(0))\right)^{3}}.

(71)

Provided this expression is finite, the real part of the response admits
the expansion

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\,R(\omega)=R(0)+\frac{R^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}\omega^{2}+\cdots.

(72)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\alpha(0)-\alpha(\omega)}\propto\omega^{-2}\qquad(\omega\to 0),

(73)

so the integral in Eq. (
46
) diverges and therefore

\displaystyle T_{c}=0.

(74)

Thus, the finite-temperature glass transition is absent whenever the
low-frequency expansion (
72
) is valid.

It remains to examine the exceptional case in which this expansion
breaks down. According to Eq. (71), such a breakdown can occur only if
the denominator vanishes, namely,

\displaystyle 1=J^{2}\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\left(\frac{1}{\mu-i\Omega-J^{2}R(0)}\right)^{2}.

(75)

By contrast, the numerator
F^{\prime\prime}(s(0))
remains finite under mild
assumptions; see Appendix
C
. We now show that the
condition above cannot be satisfied for any nontrivial symmetric
distribution in the glass phase.

Using the integral form of the triangle inequality together with
Eq. (
44
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\left(\frac{1}{\mu-i\Omega-J^{2}R(0)}\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\left|\frac{1}{\mu-i\Omega-J^{2}R(0)}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{J^{2}}.

(76)

Hence, Eq. (
75
) can hold only if the above inequality
is saturated. This requires the phase of

\left(\mu-i\Omega-J^{2}R(0)\right)^{-2}
to be independent of
\Omega
on
the support of
g(\Omega)
, which is possible only in the monodisperse
case
g(\Omega)=\delta(\Omega)
. We therefore conclude that the
finite-temperature spin-glass transition survives only in the singular
monodisperse limit. For any other symmetric distribution of natural
frequencies, no finite-temperature glass phase exists.

For asymmetric frequency distributions, the low-frequency expansion of

\alpha(\omega)
generally allows a linear term, so that the singularity
is weakened from
\omega^{-2}
to
\omega^{-1}
. Although this
divergence is weaker than in the symmetric case, it is still infrared
divergent. This suggests that the finite-temperature transition remains
absent also in the asymmetric case, although a separate analysis is
required.

V
Summary and discussions

In this work, we introduced a solvable mean-field model of globally
coupled oscillators with quenched random interactions under a spherical
constraint. For uniform ferromagnetic couplings, the model reproduces
the standard synchronization transition. For fully random symmetric
couplings, we derived closed self-consistent equations for the response
and correlation functions by means of a cavity construction, or
equivalently, a dynamical mean-field theory.

Our main result is that a finite-temperature spin-glass transition
occurs only in the singular monodisperse limit in which all natural
frequencies are identical. In that limit, the model reduces to the
spherical Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model and reproduces its standard
transition. Once the frequency distribution has a finite width,
however, the finite-temperature transition is suppressed. The physical
reason is that frequency dispersion generates a low-frequency
singularity in the correlation function that is incompatible with the
spherical constraint. At zero temperature, by contrast, the present
spherical dynamics still admits a frozen glassy phase even for finite
frequency dispersion.

The present results place randomly coupled oscillator models in close
relation to earlier nonequilibrium spherical spin-glass models. In
particular, Crisanti and Sompolinsky showed that perturbations away from
the equilibrium limit suppress the finite-temperature spin-glass phase
while leaving a frozen state at zero
temperature
7
. The present model has a closely related
structure, but the nonequilibrium ingredient is introduced through
distributed natural frequencies rather than through asymmetric
couplings.

The zero-temperature frozen phase should nevertheless be interpreted
with considerable care. In the present spherical dynamics, it survives
even for arbitrarily large frequency dispersion
\Delta
and arbitrarily
weak coupling
J
. Such behavior is physically implausible for a generic
system of randomly coupled oscillators and strongly suggests that the
residual frozen phase is an artifact of the quasi-linear spherical
approximation rather than a robust property of the original nonlinear
phase dynamics
7
. In a genuinely nonlinear extension,
the oscillator dynamics can feed back on itself and generate additional
self-induced fluctuations even in the absence of external thermal
noise. If these fluctuations carry a continuous low-frequency spectrum,
they may destabilize the frozen state found here. A related lesson has
recently been discussed in nonequilibrium hyperuniform systems, where a
state present in the linear theory is lost once nonlinearities generate
an additional effective large-scale noise
contribution
21
. Although the present problem is
different, the same general mechanism may be relevant here:
nonlinearities can qualitatively change the fate of phases that appear
stable within a linearized or spherical description.

It also remains unclear to what extent the spherical approximation can
capture phenomena discussed in previous studies of Kuramoto models with
random interactions, including volcano transitions, chaotic dynamics,
and algebraic decay of the ferromagnetic order
parameter
11
;
27
;
20
. Clarifying these
issues remains an important direction for future work.

Appendix A
Dynamics of the ferromagnetic model

Here we investigate the relaxation dynamics of the ferromagnetic model
by directly solving the equation of motion (
10
),
starting from the homogeneous initial condition
z_{i}(0)=1
for

i=1,\cdots,N
. In Eq. (
10
), the
i
dependence of

z_{i}
appears only through
\Omega_{i}
, so that

\displaystyle z_{i}(t)=z(t,\Omega_{i}).

(77)

In the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, the order parameter can
therefore be written as

\displaystyle Z(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}(t)\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\,z(t,\Omega),

(78)

where

\displaystyle g(\Omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\Omega-\Omega_{i})

(79)

denotes the distribution of natural frequencies.

For the Cauchy distribution, the integral in
Eq. (
78
) can be evaluated explicitly by contour
integration in the complex
\Omega
plane, following the standard
treatment of Lorentzian frequency distributions in coupled-oscillator
models
23
. If
z(t,\Omega)
, viewed as a function of
complex
\Omega
, is analytic in the upper half-plane and sufficiently
well behaved at infinity, the contribution from the large semicircle
vanishes. Since
g(\Omega)
has a single pole at
\Omega=i\Delta
in
the upper half-plane, the residue theorem yields

Z(t)=\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\,z(t,\Omega)=z(t,i\Delta).

(80)

Evaluating the equation of motion at
\Omega=i\Delta
, we obtain

\dot{Z}(t)=\left(-\mu-\Delta+K\right)Z(t).

(81)

Using the spherical constraint,
\mu(t)=K|Z(t)|^{2}+T
, we finally arrive
at

\dot{Z}(t)=(K-T-\Delta)Z(t)-K|Z(t)|^{2}Z(t).

(82)

In the steady state,
\dot{Z}=0
, and therefore

\displaystyle\left|Z\right|=\begin{cases}0,&T>K-\Delta,\\[3.0pt]
\sqrt{\frac{K-T-\Delta}{K}},&T\leq K-\Delta,\end{cases}

(83)

which is consistent with the result presented in the main text.

Appendix C

Here we show that

Since

\displaystyle F(s)=\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\frac{1}{s-i\Omega},

(89)

its

\displaystyle F^{(n)}(s)=(-1)^{n}n!\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\frac{1}{(s-i\Omega)^{n+1}}.

(90)

In the glass phase, using
\mu=2J^{2}\alpha(0)

\displaystyle s(0)=\mu-J^{2}R(0)=J^{2}\alpha(0),

\displaystyle\left|F^{(n)}(s(0))\right|

\displaystyle=n!\left|\int d\Omega\,g(\Omega)\frac{1}{(s(0)-i\Omega)^{n+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n!}{\left|J^{2}\alpha(0)\right|^{\,n+1}},

\displaystyle\left|s(0)-i\Omega\right|=\sqrt{(J^{2}\alpha(0))^{2}+\Omega^{2}}\geq\left|J^{2}\alpha(0)\right|.

(93)

Thus,

\alpha(0)>0
