Differential associations of muscle and fat body composition profiles with inflammatory cytokines in preschool children: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wenya Cai, Qingshan Yan, Yong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBody composition in early childhood is closely linked to growth, metabolic function, and immune development, yet the specific relationships between muscle‑ and fat‑related body composition indicators and inflammatory cytokine profiles in preschool children remain inadequately characterized. In this cross‑sectional study, 171 preschool children aged 4 ~ 6 years in Guangzhou, China, were recruited by multistage cluster random sampling. Body composition parameters were assessed using a multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer. Plasma levels of IL‑2, IL‑4, IL‑6, IL‑10, TNF‑α, and IFN‑γ were measured by flow cytometry. Sex differences in body composition were examined, and the associations between body composition indices and cytokine concentrations were analyzed using Spearman correlation and principal component analysis (PCA). Among the 171 children (57.9% boys), 15 of 24 body composition indicators were significantly higher in boys than in girls (P < 0.05). When cytokine levels were compared across BMI categories, no significant differences were observed for any of the six cytokines (all P > 0.05). Spearman analysis showed that 12 body composition indices were inversely correlated with IL‑10 (r = − 0.177 to − 0.235, all P < 0.05). PCA identified four composite factors with distinct cytokine associations: the basic body composition factor was inversely associated with IL-10 (r = − 0.167, P < 0.05), the body fat proportion factor was positively associated with IL-10 (r = 0.151, P < 0.05), the muscle mass factor was positively correlated with IL-6 (r = 0.165, P < 0.05), and the central obesity factor was positively correlated with IL-4 (r = 0.206, P < 0.05). In preschool children, distinct muscle‑ and fat‑related body composition profiles are differentially associated with circulating IL‑10, IL‑6, and IL‑4 levels, independent of BMI categories. These findings indicate correlations between early-life muscle and fat body composition profiles and low-grade inflammatory markers, providing potential evidence for the early prevention of metabolic disorders related to obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56579-2.Keywords: Body composition, Inflammatory cytokines, Preschool children, Obesity, GrowthSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Health care, Immunology, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Preschool age represents a critical window for growth and development, during which changes in body composition can profoundly affect current health and the risk of future disease. Body composition refers to the relative proportions of water, protein, fat, minerals, and other constituents that make up the human body. It is widely used as an indicator of nutritional and health status and is closely linked to metabolic function and immune system maturation in children1. Body composition is dynamic and can vary in response to geographic environment, nutritional intake, climate, physical activity, disease status, and genetic background2. With rapid lifestyle changes and population aging, abnormalities in body composition—particularly obesity—have become major public health challenges. These abnormalities are strongly associated with a range of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and osteoporosis3,4.

Recent research has underscored the complex interplay between adipose tissue and systemic inflammation. Adipose tissue functions as an active endocrine organ, secreting inflammatory cytokines that modulate immune responses5,6. Chronic low‑grade inflammation is implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous chronic diseases, with inflammatory cytokines playing central roles in these processes7. In obese states, expanded adipose tissue is often associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, characterized by elevated proinflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, CRP) and reduced anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, adiponectin)8–10. This inflammatory imbalance may disrupt metabolic homeostasis and immune development11. Beyond adiposity, skeletal muscle is increasingly recognized as a secretory organ releasing myokines, which also participate in inflammatory regulation12. The age-related decline in muscle mass in adults is known to exacerbate inflammation13; however, the relationship between muscle mass and inflammatory markers in early childhood remains underexplored. Most existing studies focus on obesity-related inflammation using Body Mass Index (BMI) as a proxy, which fails to distinguish between fat and lean mass. Consequently, the specific contributions of muscle versus fat compartments to the inflammatory profile in healthy preschool children are not well understood.

We hypothesized that muscle and fat indicators are differentially associated with specific pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. This study aimed to comprehensively assess body composition using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and investigate its correlation with a panel of serum cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) in preschool children, providing novel insights into the metabolic-immune crosstalk in early life.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted in a preschool in Guangzhou, China, between September and November 2020. The preschool was purposively selected based on its willingness to participate and its representativeness of typical urban preschool settings in the region. A stratified random sampling method was employed, with age group (corresponding to junior, middle, and senior classes for children aged 4, 5, and 6 years, respectively) serving as the stratification variable. Within each age stratum, three classes were randomly selected. All eligible children in the selected classes were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 4 and 6 years; (2) absence of acute infection symptoms in the past 2 weeks (no fever, cough, or diarrhea); (3) no vaccination in the past month; and (4) provision of written informed consent by parents or legal guardians. Exclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosed metabolic diseases (e.g., diabetes, thyroid dysfunction); (2) chronic inflammatory diseases (e.g., juvenile idiopathic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease); (3) use of medications affecting metabolism or immunity in the past 3 months (e.g., glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants); (4) severe dysfunction of major organs (heart, brain, kidney, or liver); (5) malignant tumors or physical disabilities; or (6) acute infection at the time of examination.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Board of Guangdong Women and Children Hospital (No. 202001123). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The participants were fully informed of the study procedures and purposes, and written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians prior to enrollment.

Body composition measurement

Body composition was assessed using a multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 770, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), a method validated for pediatric populations14. Measurements were performed by trained personnel. Participants stood barefoot on the platform, holding the hand electrodes with arms extended, and remained stationary for approximately 90 s. A total of 24 body composition parameters were obtained by direct measurement or calculation, including height, weight, body mass index (BMI), protein, minerals, neck circumference, chest circumference, abdominal circumference, hip circumference, waist–hip ratio (WHR), total body water (TBW), basal metabolic rate (BMR), body fat mass (BFM), soft lean mass (SLM), fat‑free mass (FFM), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), percent body fat (PBF), visceral fat area (VFA), body cell mass (BCM), bone mineral content (BMC), fat‑free mass index (FFMI), fat mass index (FMI), skeletal muscle index (SMI), and the TBW/FFM ratio. Quality control measures included asking about recent diet and fluid intake before measurement, and remeasuring for children with poor cooperation after appropriate rest.

Assessment of blood inflammatory cytokine levels

Fasting venous blood samples were collected into EDTA-anticoagulated tubes between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m. to minimize diurnal variation. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2000–4000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and stored at − 20 °C until batch analysis. All samples were analyzed within 3 months of collection to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Concentrations of six cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) were measured using a flow cytometry-based Cytometric Bead Array platform with the Six Cytokine Detection Kit (Changsha Microm Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols15. The limits of detection were 2.5 pg/mL for IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, and 5.0 pg/mL for IFN-γ. All assays were performed within validated linear ranges: 2.5–2500 pg/mL for IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, and 5–2500 pg/mL for IFN-γ. Quality control measures included: (1) generation of standard curves and inclusion of two quality control samples (high and low concentrations) in each assay batch; (2) intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) < 15% and inter-assay CV < 15%; and (3) linear regression correlation coefficients for all calibration curves ≥ 0.95. This cytokine panel was selected based on three considerations: (1) these cytokines represent the major Th1 (IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF-α) and Th2 (IL-4, IL-6, IL-10) axes that are central to immune regulation; (2) IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α are well-established mediators in obesity-related low-grade inflammation; and (3) the Th1/Th2 balance undergoes dynamic maturation during early childhood, making this panel particularly relevant for understanding immunometabolic interactions in preschool populations.

Statistical analyses

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Differences in cytokine levels across BMI categories were analyzed using One-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate associations between body composition indicators and cytokine levels. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the 24 body composition variables and identify latent factors. R software (version 4.4.1) was used for statistical analysis. P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics and body composition

A total of 171 preschool children aged 4 ~ 6 years were included in the analysis, comprising 99 boys (57.89%) and 72 girls (42.11%). The mean age was 4.74 ± 0.68 years, mean height was 109.70 ± 5.79 cm, mean weight was 18.67 ± 3.49 kg, and mean BMI was 15.43 ± 1.73 kg/m2 (Table 1). Significant sex differences were observed in several body composition parameters. Boys had higher weight, BMI, protein, abdominal circumference, hip circumference, BMR, SLM, FFM, SMM, FFMI, SMI, BCM, TBW, and TBW/FFM than girls (all P < 0.05). The sex comparisons in body composition were not adjusted for age. Although mean age did not differ significantly between boys and girls, some observed differences may reflect developmental variation rather than true sex effects. Future studies should use age-standardized indices (e.g., fat-free mass index) to better characterize sex differences.

The levels of inflammatory factors

All 171 children had detectable levels of the six inflammatory cytokines (detection rate 100%). The median concentrations of IL‑2, IL‑4, IL‑6, IL‑10, TNF‑α, and IFN‑γ were 0.48, 0.51, 2.12, 1.02, 0.63, and 0.94 pg/mL, respectively, and the corresponding geometric means were 0.42, 0.46, 2.06, 0.95, 0.58, and 0.85 pg/mL (Table 2). When children were categorized by BMI status (underweight, normal weight, overweight/obese), nonparametric tests showed no statistically significant differences in the levels of any inflammatory cytokines between underweight or overweight/obese children and those with normal BMI (all P > 0.05).

The correlation between body composition and inflammatory factors

Spearman correlation analysis revealed multiple significant associations between body composition parameters and IL-10 levels (Fig. 1). We observed that protein (r = − 0.214), minerals (r = − 0.205), abdominal circumference (r = − 0.235), BMR (r = − 0.212), TBW (r = − 0.210), SLM (r = − 0.211), FFM (r = − 0.214), SMM (r = − 0.217), BCM (r = − 0.214), and BMC (r = − 0.177) showed significantly inversely correlated with IL-10 expression (all P < 0.05). In contrast, height (r = 0.231) and weight (r = 0.156) demonstrated significant positive correlations with IL-10 levels (P < 0.05). Additionally, abdominal circumference was inversely correlated with IL-6 (r = − 0.167, P < 0.05), whereas no significant correlations were observed between body composition parameters and IL-2, IL-4, TNF-α, or IFN-γ in the individual indicator analysis.

Principal component analysis

The suitability of the data for PCA was confirmed by the KMO measure of sampling adequacy (MSA = 0.5) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < 0.01), indicating adequate intercorrelation among variables. PCA with varimax rotation identified four principal components that collectively explained a substantial proportion of the total variance in body composition (Table 3). Correlation analysis between PCA-derived factor scores and cytokine levels revealed distinct patterns (Table 4). The basic body composition factor was significantly inversely correlated with IL-10 (r = − 0.167, P < 0.05). Conversely, the body fat proportion factor demonstrated a significant positive correlation with IL-10 (r = 0.151, P < 0.05). The muscle mass factor was significantly positively correlated with IL-6 (r = 0.165, P < 0.05), and the central obesity factor showed a significant positive correlation with IL-4 (r = 0.206, P < 0.05).

Discussions

This study comprehensively examined the associations between body composition and inflammatory cytokine profiles in preschool children aged 4 ~ 6 years. Our findings reveal significant sex-specific differences in body composition and demonstrate that muscle and fat compartments are differentially associated with inflammatory profiles, specifically IL-10, IL-6, and IL-4, independent of BMI categories.

Our observation that boys possessed significantly higher values for weight, BMI, protein, BMR, SLM, FFM, and SMM compared with girls is consistent with findings from cross-sectional studies of Chinese adolescents16 and retrospective analyses examining sex differences in body composition across the lifespan17,18. The differential distribution patterns of muscle and adipose tissue between sexes, with females tending toward gluteofemoral fat accumulation and males toward abdominal and visceral fat deposition, likely reflect both genetic programming and hormonal influences19,20. Epigenetic mechanisms, including genomic imprinting and X-chromosome inactivation, may also contribute to these sex-specific patterns19.

We found no statistically significant differences in inflammatory cytokine levels across BMI categories. While numerous studies have demonstrated elevated TNF-α, IL-6, and high-sensitivity CRP in obese children and adolescents compared with healthy-weight peers21,22, our findings align with studies reporting no significant differences in inflammatory markers between obese and normal-weight young children23. Our study did not find a specific association between BMI and inflammatory cytokines, which may indicate that obesity-related inflammation may not be fully established in early childhood or may be influenced by factors other than obesity, such as diet and lifestyle24. These findings underscore the importance of examining body composition components individually rather than relying solely on BMI classification.

A central finding of this study is the complex relationship between body composition parameters and IL-10, a potent anti-inflammatory cytokine critical for immune homeostasis. We observed that multiple indicators reflecting lean mass and metabolically active tissue—including protein, minerals, BMR, TBW, SLM, FFM, SMM, BCM, and BMC—were inversely correlated with IL-10 levels. This pattern was corroborated by PCA, which demonstrated an inverse association between the basic body composition factor and IL-10. The observed associations between IL-10 and body composition parameters were complex and bidirectional: IL-10 was inversely correlated with multiple lean mass indicators (protein, minerals, BMR, TBW, SLM, FFM, SMM, BCM, BMC) but positively correlated with height, weight, and the body fat proportion factor derived from PCA. This pattern is biologically difficult to interpret and may reflect several possibilities: (1) large physiological fluctuations in IL-10 levels during early childhood, such that single-timepoint measurements may not reliably capture individual characteristics; (1) influence of unmeasured factors (e.g., recent subclinical infections, dietary patterns) on IL-10 levels; or (1) residual confounding due to intercorrelations among body composition parameters. Although some of the associations reached statistical significance, the effect sizes were small (|r| < 0.3) and the directions were inconsistent, which limited their clinical and biological significance. We caution against over-interpreting these findings. The biological significance of these associations is unclear and may reflect random variation or residual confounding factors rather than true biological relationships. Further exploration in prospective studies is needed to clarify these matters. In addition, height and weight gain may also be affected by a variety of factors, including nutritional status and growth hormone levels25.

Our results also revealed that muscle mass was significantly positively correlated with the IL-6 level, whereas abdominal circumference was negatively correlated with the IL-6 level, suggesting that both muscle content and abdominal fat accumulation may be related to the inflammatory response in preschool children. IL-6, a proinflammatory factor, is crucial for obesity-related inflammatory cytokines. It is secreted by fat cells and is associated with the distribution and percentage of fat tissue to participate in a variety of inflammatory and metabolic disorders11,26. This interpretation is supported by studies demonstrating that inhibition of IL-6 activity promotes subcutaneous fat accumulation, suggesting that IL-6 participates in fat metabolism regulation27. In addition, studies have shown that there is an association between abdominal fat and inflammatory markers, but this relationship varies among different fat types (such as subcutaneous fat and visceral fat), with a stronger association between subcutaneous fat and inflammatory markers28.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of associations between body composition and inflammatory cytokines; reverse causation (e.g., inflammatory status influencing body composition accumulation) or bidirectional associations cannot be excluded. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to elucidate temporal relationships and underlying mechanisms. Second, we did not systematically measure potential confounding variables, including recent infection history (exclusion criteria applied, but incubation periods may have been missed), dietary patterns (particularly anti- or pro-inflammatory dietary components), physical activity levels, sleep quality, and environmental exposures (e.g., secondhand smoke, air pollution). Residual confounding may explain some observed associations. Third, cytokines were measured from single fasting blood samples, which have large intraindividual day-to-day variation that may dilute true associations. Additionally, the bioelectrical impedance analysis for young children is susceptible to the influence of water content. Although we adopted a standardized protocol, measurement errors for body composition indicators may still exist. Fourth, the relatively modest sample size recruited from a single geographic region may limit statistical power and generalizability; multiple comparisons may increase false positive risk, particularly for weak correlations (|r|<0.3) which should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, KMO = 0.5 indicates marginal suitability for factor analysis, and PCA results should be considered exploratory rather than definitive biological constructs. Sixth, sex comparisons in body composition were not adjusted for age, and some observed differences may reflect developmental variation rather than true sex effects. Finally, it is crucial to emphasize that circulating cytokine levels in preschool children primarily reflect physiological immune development rather than the low-grade pathological inflammation described in adults; thus, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence for clinical intervention.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study demonstrates that muscle- and fat-related body composition profiles exhibit differential associations with circulating IL-10, IL-6, and IL-4 levels in preschool children, independent of BMI classification. These associations have an inferential role, but due to the cross-sectional design, the small effect size (|r| < 0.3), and the lack of correction for multiple comparisons, it is impossible to infer causality. The clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain. From a clinical perspective, comprehensive body composition assessment—rather than reliance on BMI alone—may provide more nuanced insights into early metabolic and immune health. Future longitudinal studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate these associations and elucidate the mechanistic pathways linking body composition to inflammatory regulation in early life.
