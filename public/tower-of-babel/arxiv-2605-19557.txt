Density-Ratio Losses for Post-Hoc Learning to Defer
Alexander Soen, Ragnar Thobaben, Joakim Jaldén, Richard Nock

Abstract
We study post-hoc Learning to Defer (L2D) through the lens of ideal distributions: divergence-regularized reweightings of the data distribution under which a model attains low loss. We define deferral via the density-ratio between a model’s and an expert’s ideals. Using the reduction from density-ratio estimation to class-probability estimation, we derive the DR CPE losses for post-hoc L2D scorers. Deferral decisions are then made by thresholding the scorer, allowing deferral rates to be adjusted without retraining. For KL-based ideal distributions, our deferral rules recovers Chow’s rule under the original distribution and a connection to an expert-tilted Bayes posterior—which incorporates the expert’s performance—depending on if the ideal distributions are joint or marginal distributions. Experimentally, our approach is competitive compared to common baselines and more robust across dataset settings. More broadly, our results cast post-hoc L2D as density-ratio learning between ideal distributions, bridging Chow-style rules, expert comparison, and elucidating connections to related learning settings including anomaly detection.

Introduction

Machine learning (ML) models are now embedded in many tasks we encounter every day. As these systems are deployed in increasingly high-stakes domains, including healthcare
(
Esteva et al. 2017
;
Kadampur and Al Riyaee 2020
;
Zoabi et al. 2021
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Singhal et al. 2025
)
and finance
(
Burrell 2016
;
Yousefi et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2023
)
, it is crucial that they make predictions only when their outputs are reliable.
In such high-stakes decision-making systems, ideally, a synthesis of both ML prediction and human expertise can be used
(
Topol 2019
)
: ML models can defer to experts whenever their own predictions are deemed unreliable, and the burden on human experts can be reduced by leveraging ML models.
Dual to the problem of predicting responsibly, the rise of huge ML models and edge deployment has created strong demand for systems that improve efficiency by routing only difficult inputs to a larger model while handling easier cases with a smaller model
(
Viola and Jones 2001
;
Mamou et al. 2022
;
Varshney and Baral 2022
;
Khalili et al. 2022
;
Dohan et al. 2022
;
Gupta et al. 2024
;
Narasimhan et al. 2025
)
.
Learning to defer (L2D) proposes a solution to these problems by modifying the standard supervised learning setting to allow (smaller) models to defer their predictions to an upstream (potentially human) expert
(
Madras et al. 2018
)
.

To allow models to abstain from predictions, learning to reject (L2R) was initially proposed
(
Chow 1957
;
Chow 1970
;
Cortes et al. 2016
)
, where rejection is learned without access to the downstream expert used at inference.
A useful categorization of these approaches are by how abstention is represented.
In score-based approaches, abstention is derived from the predictor’s own scores, encompassing classical reject-option rules, convex-surrogate formulations, and confidence-threshold methods
(
Chow 1957
;
Chow 1970
;
Herbei and Wegkamp 2006
;
Bartlett and Wegkamp 2008
;
Yuan and Wegkamp 2010
;
Cao et al. 2022
;
El-Yaniv et al. 2010
;
Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017
;
Geifman and El-Yaniv 2019
;
Ni et al. 2019
)
. In predictor-rejector approaches, a separate rejector is learned
(
Cortes et al. 2016
;
Mao et al. 2024a
)
. Finally, reduction-based approaches cast rejection as a related supervised learning problem
(
Charoenphakdee et al. 2021
;
Ramaswamy et al. 2018
)
.
L2D extends this picture by incorporating an expert into training
(
Madras et al. 2018
)
. Here too, one can distinguish score-based formulations, where deferral is encoded through shared scores or defer classes
(
Mozannar and Sontag 2020
;
Verma and Nalisnick 2022
;
Mao et al. 2024b
)
, from predictor-rejector formulations with a separate deferral function
(
Okati et al. 2021
;
Mozannar et al. 2023
;
Mao et al. 2023
)
. A second, orthogonal distinction is between end-to-end methods and post-hoc (two-stage) methods.

The post-hoc regime is especially important since retraining modern pretrained models can be prohibitively expensive. We focus on post-hoc L2D
(
Kag et al. 2022
;
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
(also called two-stage L2D
(
Mao et al. 2023
)
), where the base model is fixed, and only the deferral rule is learned. In this setting, simple score-based heuristics can fail when downstream experts are specialized or under distribution shift
(
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
, while many existing methods still amount to estimating pointwise expert-comparison targets, which aim to predict when the expert will outperform the model
(
Kag et al. 2022
;
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
.
A desirable property in this regime is for deferral models to be adaptable to different rates of deferral without full retraining.

Recently, a distributional perspective on post-hoc L2R was proposed
(
Soen et al. 2024
;
Soen 2026
)
.
Its central object is an
ideal distribution
: a divergence-regularized reweighting of the data distribution under which a fixed predictor attains low loss.
Rejection is then defined by thresholding a density-ratio, using either a marginal construction over
\mathcal{X}

(
Soen et al. 2024
)
or a joint construction over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
that is later marginalized to
\mathcal{X}

(
Soen 2026
)
.
Extending this framework to post-hoc L2D is nontrivial: the resulting rules are essentially plug-in and depend on the Bayes class-posterior, while a direct translation would additionally require pointwise access to the expert at test time.

In this paper, we make the following contributions (

Settings

We consider a space of observations
\mathcal{X}
and labels
\mathcal{Y}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}[L]=\{1,2,\ldots,L\}
, with a data distribution
\textup{{P}}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
.
The learning task we consider is to learn classifiers
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
w.r.t.
the data distribution
P
.
Instead of directly learning a classifier
f
, we will consider scorers
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
which are related via
f(x)=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}h_{y}(x)
.
To measure performance, we consider a loss function
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}^{L}
to define the expected risk
{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}[\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))]
.
We shorthand a decomposition of the data distribution by
\textup{{P}}(x,y)=\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}(x)\upeta^{\star}_{y}(x)
, where
\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X})
is the marginal over observations and
\upeta^{\star}(x)\in\triangle(\mathcal{Y})
corresponds to the Bayes class-posterior function,
i.e
.
,
\upeta^{\star}_{y}(x)=\textup{{P}}[\mathsf{Y}=y\mid\mathsf{X}=x]
.

Learning to Defer

To move from the standard multi-class classification paradigm to learning to defer (L2D), alongside the typical optimization of classification model
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
, we introduce an
expert
model
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
. Using the expert, in addition to the learning a classifier
h
, we can also jointly learn a rejector
r\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
via an objective
(
Mozannar and Sontag 2020
)
:

\mathcal{L}(h,r)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}\sim\textup{{P}}}\left[(1-r(\mathsf{X}))\cdot\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))+r(\mathsf{X})\cdot\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))\right]+c\cdot{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathsf{X}\sim\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}[r(\mathsf{X})=1],

(1)

In
Eq.

1
, we explicitly separate the expert’s performance
\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))
and the additional cost of calling the expert
c\geq 0
.
If one removes the loss term corresponding to the expert
\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))
, the objective simplifies to the learning to reject (L2R) objective where the performance of how deferred observations are dealt with are not modelled
(
Chow 1970
)
.

The L2D objective balances two tasks. First, it wants to decrease the loss of
h
by smartly deferring observations
x
to the expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
for inputs it will predict poorly for. However, we do not want the rejector
r
to defer too much, and thus
Eq.

1
balances the rate of deferral in its second term.
Once one has a pair of classifier and rejector
(h,r)
, one can derive a classifier which can defer to an expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
through a combination

\bar{h}(x)=\llbracket r(x)=0\rrbracket\cdot h(x)+\llbracket r(x)=1\rrbracket\cdot h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x)
.

While
Eq.

1
is posed as a joint optimization over
(h,r)
, we can also imbue a fixed classifier
h
with a deferral mechanism, yielding the standard notion of perfect deferral in the post-hoc L2D setting.

Lemma 2.1

(Chow’s Rule
(
Chow 1970
)
)
.

For fixed classifier
h
, the Bayes optimal deferral rule
r^{\star}
of
Eq.

1
is given by

r^{\star}(x;c,\textup{{P}})=\left\llbracket{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\upeta^{\star}(x)}\left[\ell(x,\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x))-\ell(x,\mathsf{Y},h(x))\right]\leq c\right\rrbracket,

(2)

where
\llbracket\textup{{pred}}\rrbracket
are Iverson brackets which evaluate to
1
when the predicate
pred
is true and is equal
0
when the predicate is false
(
Knuth 1992
)
.

Lemma

2.1
presents an intuitive mechanism for deciding which examples to defer to an expert: if the loss of
h
is expected to be much higher (w.r.t.
threshold value
c
) than the expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
, the one should defer.
In the sequel, we will consider the setting of post-hoc L2D.

Divergence-Based Deferral Rules

Eq.

1
presents a loss-based objective for learning rejectors.
Instead of directly learning mechanisms for deferral as a binary function
r
, one can first aim to learn
ideal distributions
and then define decision corresponding to their density-ratios
(
Soen et al. 2024
;
Soen 2026
)
.

Ideal Distributions

An ideal distribution is intuitively a distribution in which a classifier-loss pair
(h,\ell)
performs best at within a specified (regularized) distance away from the true probability measure
P
, where the distance is defined by a divergence. In the following definition, we define two types of ideal distribution which differ in whether we are optimizing within a marginal or joint space.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a fixed classifier-loss pair
(h,\ell)
, divergence
D
, and
\gamma>0
:

• An
(\textup{\rm D},\gamma)
-marginal ideal distribution
\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm m}}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X})
of
(h,\ell)
is given by

\displaystyle\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm m}}\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{\textup{{Q}}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X})}\;\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mathsf{X}\sim\textup{{Q}}}\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\upeta^{\star}(\mathsf{X})}\left[\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))\right]+\gamma\cdot\textup{\rm D}(\textup{{Q}}\Mid\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}).

(4)

• An
(\textup{\rm D},\gamma)
-joint ideal distribution
\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm j}}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X})
of
(h,\ell)
is given by

\displaystyle\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm j}}(x;\gamma)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{\textup{{Q}}}_{\textup{\rm j}}(x,y)\;\textup{{d}}y,\qquad\bar{\textup{{Q}}}_{\textup{\rm j}}\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bar{\textup{{Q}}}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\;\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})\sim\bar{\textup{{Q}}}}\left[\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))\right]+\gamma\cdot\textup{\rm D}(\bar{\textup{{Q}}}\Mid\textup{{P}}).

(5)

Fitting our intuition, the ideal distributions defined in
Definition

3.1
are found by explicitly minimizing the loss
\ell
of the classifier
h
.
The regularization weights
\gamma
control how far the ideal distributions are from the ground truth distribution
P
.
We note that the definition abuses notation slightly, where the divergence
D
corresponds to both a divergence on the marginal space and also on the joint space.

Generalized Variational Inference (GVI) and Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO):

It has been previously noted in
Soen et al. 2024
that
Eqs.

4
and
5
resemble the optimization problems associated with GVI
(
Zellner 1988
;
Knoblauch et al. 2022
)
and DRO
(
Scarf et al. 1957
;
Duchi et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. Indeed,
\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}
corresponds to solutions to a GVI problem and is analogous to an adversarial distribution in DRO.

As the ideal distribution in
Definition

3.1
corresponds to the distributions in which a classifier best performs at, we propose a deferral rule for L2D by comparing the ideal distributions of our classifier
h
versus the ideal distribution of our expert classifier
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
.
More precisely, we propose considering the density-ratio between ideal distributions.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\textup{\rm i}\in\{\textup{\rm m},\textup{\rm j}\}
.
Given
(\textup{\rm D},\gamma)
-ideal distributions
\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}
and
\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}
of
(h,\ell)
and
(h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})},\ell)
, respectively, the
D
-density-ratio deferral rule is given by

r_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\tau,\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\llbracket\uprho_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\leq\tau\rrbracket,\quad\textrm{where}\;\uprho_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma)}{\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x;\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})}.

(6)

An advantage of making deferral decision through density ratios is that the functional form of
r_{\textup{\rm i}}
becomes convenient once we make our choice of divergence
D
(
i.e
.
, by considering
\varphi
-divergences).

Hypothesis Testing:

The deferral decision of
Definition

3.2
has a direct connection to hypothesis testing.
Thresholding density-ratios corresponds exactly to optimal hypothesis testing in the Neyman-Pearson sense
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
;
Lehmann and Romano 2005
)
,
i.e
.
, it is a likelihood ratio test. More concretely,
r_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\tau,\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})
corresponds to the hypothesis test

H_{0}\colon x\sim\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\cdot;\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\quad\textrm{and}\quad H_{1}\colon x\sim\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\cdot;\gamma).

(7)

The rule fails to reject the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, and hence defers to the expert, whenever the likelihood ratio
\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}/\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}
is sufficiently small,
i.e
.
, whenever
\uprho_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\leq\tau
. Intuitively, this means that deferral occurs precisely when the observation
x
is better explained by the expert’s ideal distribution than by the classifier’s ideal distribution. Equivalently, the test asks which of the two classifiers performs better at
x
: if
x
is more likely under the expert’s ideal distribution, then the expert’s predictions are better supported there, whereas if
x
is more likely under the classifier’s ideal distribution, then the evidence favors keeping the prediction with
h
rather than deferring to
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
.

Defining deferral decision via the density-ratios of ideal distributions was originally utilized for L2R
(
Soen et al. 2024
)
.
In this setting, instead of considering a pair of classifiers
h
,
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
, L2R only considers a single classifier
h
.
As a result, there is only a single ideal distribution, and the distribution we compare it against is just the data measure
P
.
Although not stated, the hypothesis testing interpretation corresponding to
Eq.

7
(replacing
\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
with
P
) also holds for the L2R setting.

KL-Divergence

In prior work, it was shown that a canonical choice of divergence to choose for constructing ideal distributions is the KL-divergence
(
Soen et al. 2024
;
Soen 2026
)
.
As a result, in this work, we will also focus on the class of ideal distributions generated by this choice.
We first recall that the KL-divergence between probability measures
\textup{{P}}\ll\textup{{Q}}
is given by
(
Amari and Nagaoka 2000
)
:
\textup{\rm KL}(\textup{{P}}\Mid\textup{{Q}})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}{{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mathsf{Z}\sim\textup{{P}}}\left[\log\left({\textup{{d}}\textup{{P}}}/{\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}}(\mathsf{Z})\right)\right]
.

Utilizing the KL-divergence, both of the corresponding marginal and joint ideal distributions can be shown to be (exponential) multiplicative re-weightings of the true data distribution
P
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Given a fixed classifier-loss pair
(h,\ell)
, the
(\textup{\rm KL},\gamma)
-ideal distributions are multiplicative re-weightings
\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma)=w_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma)\cdot\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}(x)
, with weights given by

w_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{Z_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma}}\tilde{w}_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma),\quad\tilde{w}_{\textup{\rm i}}(x;\gamma)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\upeta^{\star}(x)}\left[\ell(x,\mathsf{Y},h(x))\right]}{\gamma}\right)&\textrm{if }\;\textup{\rm i}=\textup{\rm m}\\
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\upeta^{\star}(x)}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(x,\mathsf{Y},h(x))}{\gamma}\right)\right]&\textrm{if }\;\textup{\rm i}=\textup{\rm j},\end{cases}

(8)

where
Z_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma}
are normalizing constants of the distributions,
i.e
.
,
Z_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{X}\sim\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}\left[\tilde{w}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\mathsf{X};\gamma)\right]
.

Corollary

3.3
follows directly from a more general result that holds for
\varphi
-divergences (a.k.a.
, Csiszár
f
-divergences), as shown in
Appendix

C
.
In this more general case, the ideal distribution generated by
\varphi
-divergences are also multiplicative re-weightings of
P
. However, only the KL-divergence’s
normalization
are determined by a multiplicative constant
1/Z_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma}
. This latter property will be key in our construction of loss functions in
Section

4
.

From the functional form presented in
Corollary

3.3
, it becomes apparent why taking the threshold of density-ratios suits the functional form of KL-divergence (and
\varphi
-divergence) ideal distributions: dependence on the marginal data measure
\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}(x)
will cancel out in the density-ratio. As a result, for optimal ideal distributions, the rejection decision follows from a ratio of weights defined in
Corollary

3.3
.
Furthermore, in this case, the role of the regularizer
\gamma
becomes clear:
\gamma
corresponds to the
temperature
of the multiplicative reweighting.

The choice of utilizing the KL-divergence for ideal distributions is known to be canonical as it can be shown that in the L2R setting, rejection via the density-ratio mechanism and marginal ideal distributions recovers Chow’s rule
(
Soen et al. 2024
, Theorem 4.2)
. Specific to the L2R setting, it was also shown that rejectors corresponding to joint ideal distributions are more lenient than their marginal ideal distribution counterparts
(
Soen 2026
, Lemma 1)
.

The shift from working with one ideal distribution to a pair of ideal distributions in L2D changes the relationship between marginal and joint ideal distributions that was previously established in L2R. The connection to Chow’s rule for the marginal ideal distribution is retained; however, the analogous connection between the joint and marginal ideal distributions no longer holds for deferral mechanisms of the form in
Definition

3.2
. Instead, the joint deferral mechanism can be related to Chow’s rule under a data distribution with a
tilted
Bayes class-posterior.

Definition 3.4
.

For a distribution
P
, we denote its
(\ell,\gamma)
-expert-tilted distribution as

\tilde{\textup{{P}}}_{\gamma}(x,y)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}(x)\cdot{\tilde{\upeta}^{\star}}_{y}(x;\gamma),\quad{\tilde{\upeta}^{\star}}_{y}(x;\gamma)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}t(y\mid x;\gamma)\cdot\upeta^{\star}_{y}(x),

(9)

where

t(y\mid x;\gamma)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{\exp\left(-{\ell(x,y,h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x))}/{\gamma}\right)}{{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\upeta^{\star}(x)}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-{\ell(x,\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x))}/{\gamma}\right)\right]}.

(10)

The informed reader may identify the transformation of the Bayes class-posterior as the exponential tilting of a distribution
(
Escher 1932
)
or as the Boltzmann-Gibbs transformation
(
Moral 2004
)
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For any cost
c
, there exists a
\tau>0
such that the KL-marginal density-ratio deferral rule satisfies
r_{\textup{\rm m}}(x;\tau,\gamma,\gamma)=r^{\star}(x;c,\textup{{P}})
.
Furthermore, there exists a
\tau>0
such that the KL-joint density-ratio deferral rule satisfies
r_{\textup{\rm j}}(x;\tau,\gamma,\gamma)\leq r^{\star}(x;c,\tilde{\textup{{P}}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\gamma})
.

The second part of
Theorem

3.5
shows that the joint density-ratio deferral rule is pointwise more conservative than Chow’s rule under the expert-tilted distribution
\tilde{\textup{{P}}}_{\gamma}
. In particular, whenever the joint rule defers at an input
x
, Chow’s rule under
\tilde{\textup{{P}}}_{\gamma}
also defers at
x
. Equivalently, the rejection region of the joint rule is contained in the rejection region induced by Chow’s rule under the tilted measure.

Beyond its theoretical interest, the second part of
Theorem

3.5
also provides a useful practical interpretation of deferral. In many applications, the Bayes class-posterior
\upeta^{\star}
is not, on its own, the most relevant object for deciding whether to predict or defer. Indeed,
\upeta^{\star}
only captures the (aleatoric) label uncertainty under the data distribution, whereas the deferral decision should also reflect the downstream loss and the expert’s comparative advantage. The tilted posterior
\tilde{\upeta}^{\star}(x;\gamma)
incorporates this additional information by reweighting labels according to the expert loss
y\mapsto\ell(x,y,h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x))
. Consequently, applying a Chow-type rule under
\tilde{\textup{{P}}}_{\gamma}
means deferring not simply when the labels are ambiguous, but when they are ambiguous in a way that favors the expert. This can be preferable in practice, since deferral is only useful in-so-far that the expert handles the prediction task better than the deferring model. From this perspective, Chow’s rule under the tilted posterior ranks examples not only by uncertainty, but by uncertainty that is especially relevant to the expert.

4
Density-Ratio Estimation Losses from Ideal Distributions

So far we have seen that ideal distributions, together with thresholding their associated density-ratios, can yield analytical and even optimal deferral rules. However, directly applying the closed-form expressions in,
e.g
.
,
Corollary

3.3
, raises two fundamental difficulties. First, both the marginal and joint ideal distributions, and hence the resulting density-ratio, depend on the Bayes class-posterior
\upeta^{\star}
. Using these expressions as plug-in deferral rules would therefore require access to the Bayes-optimal predictor in advance, which defeats the purpose of learning a good classifier
h
in the first place. Second, the deferral rule also depends on the expert ideal distribution
\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}
, which in turn requires pointwise evaluation of the expert model
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x)
at decision time. If the expert has already been queried on
x
, then one may as well use the expert’s prediction directly rather than first using it only to decide whether to defer.

These two issues prevent us from directly utilizing the closed-form analytical solutions for L2D. However, instead of trying to use a plug-in estimator to evaluate ideal distribution density-ratios, we propose a method which aims to estimate the density-ratio
\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}/\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}
. To do so, we rely on links between
Density-Ratio Estimation
(DRE)
(
Sugiyama et al. 2012
)
and
Class-Probability Estimation
(CPE)
(
Buja et al. 2005
;
Reid and Williamson 2011
)
, where we reduce the problem of estimating the density-ratio to a binary classification problem. Uniquely, the binary classification problem will be to distinguish between two reweightings of the data distribution
P
determined by the performance of the model
h
and expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
.

DRE via CPE

Before specifying the CPE optimization problem for learning the density-ratio between ideal distributions, we revise how general DRE can be expressed as a CPE problem.
Suppose that we have a strictly proper composite loss
\uplambda\colon\mathbb{R}\times\{\pm 1\}\to\mathbb{R}
with invertible link function
\Phi\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}

(
Reid and Williamson 2010
)
.
Recall that to estimate the density-ratio
\textup{{d}}\mu/\textup{{d}}\nu
between probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, we can first find a (binary) scoring function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that minimize the following CPE objective
(
Sugiyama et al. 2008
;
Menon and Ong 2016
;
Nock et al. 2016a
)
:

\mathop{\arg\min}_{s\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\quad\pi\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})]+(1-\pi)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}[(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})],

(11)

where
\pi\in(0,1)
and denote the partial losses
\uplambda_{+1}(v)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\uplambda(v,+1)
and
\uplambda_{-1}(v)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\uplambda(v,-1)
.

The optimization of
Eq.

11
is equivalent to a binary classification task on a dataset generated by class-conditionals defined by
\mu
and
\nu
—
i.e
.
,
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{X}=x\mid\mathsf{R}=+1)=\mu(x)
—and base label rate
\pi=\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{R}=+1)
. The label rate
\pi
is a free parameter which the user can set, where a typical choice is to set
\pi
based on the ratio of empirical data used to optimize
Eq.

11
.
Key to this interpretation of the optimization problem is that we obtain class-probability estimates via the inverse link function
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{R}=+1\mid\mathsf{X}=x)=\Phi^{-1}\circ s
.
As a result, the scorer
s
can then be converted to a density-ratio estimator between
\mu
and
\nu
via Bayes’ rule
(
Bickel et al. 2009
;
Menon and Ong 2016
)

\hat{\uprho}(x;s)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1-\pi}{\pi}\cdot\frac{(\Phi^{-1}\circ s)(x)}{1-(\Phi^{-1}\circ s)(x)}\approx\frac{\textup{{d}}\mu}{\textup{{d}}\nu}.

(12)

If our pair of loss function and link
(\uplambda,\Phi)
is indeed strictly proper composite, then the minimizer of
Eq.

11
will result in a scorer
s^{\star}
corresponding to the Bayes optimal class-posterior. As a result, the density-ratio learned will be exactly
\textup{{d}}\mu/\textup{{d}}\nu
.

Typically, DRE via CPE introduces the invertible link function to transform a scorer
s
into an approximate density-ratio. However, as we are interested in deferral, we only need to calculate the threshold of the approximated density-ratio. It turns out that the thresholding of density-ratios of the form
Eq.

12
can be simplified to a thresholding of the scorer.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
s\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a scorer learned via the minimization of
Eq.

11
with loss
\uplambda
and invertible link
\Phi
. Then for
\hat{\uprho}
given by
Eq.

12
, we have for
\tau>0
that

\left\llbracket\hat{\uprho}(x)\leq\tau\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket s(x)\leq\Phi\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{1-\pi+\pi\tau}\right)\right\rrbracket.

(13)

Although it appears that the deferral decision is still dependent on the link function
\Phi
on the RHS of
Lemma

4.1
, in practice one does not typically calculate an exact thresholding value.
Instead, the scorer
s
can be evaluated over a validation dataset and the threshold value can be specified w.r.t.
a target deferral percentage—a common approach for deferring via with scoring functions or confidence estimates
(
Pugnana and Ruggieri 2023a
;
Pugnana and Ruggieri 2023b
)
.
One should note that even the “original” formulation of the L2D problem in
Eq.

1
requires a calibration of the cost
c
—we remind that the deferral regularized
c\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}[r(\mathsf{X})=1]
can be interpreted as a Lagrange multiplier relaxation of a hard deferral rate constraint
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}[r(\mathsf{X})=1]\leq\texttt{budget}

(
Franc et al. 2023
)
.
In the worst case, this may require training multiple deferral mechanisms
r
from scratch for each
c
in a cross validation scheme.
As a result,
Lemma

4.1
allows us to make deferral decision based on density-ratios by thresholding in the scorer’s output space, rather than explicitly working in the space of density-ratio values.

The Ideal DR CPE Loss

With the general setup of DRE via CPE, we now define a pair of density-ratio loss functions which correspond to the optimization problem of learning the density-ratio of ideal distributions. In particular, we exploit the fact that the introduced DRE framework of
Eq.

11
allows for density-ratio estimates for any
\pi\in(0,1)
.

Definition 4.2
.

Given a DR loss
\uplambda
, the corresponding
(\textup{\rm i}\in\{\textup{\rm m},\textup{\rm j}\})
-ideal DR CPE loss is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm i}}(s;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}\left[\tilde{w}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\mathsf{X};\gamma)\cdot(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})+\tilde{w}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\mathsf{X};\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\cdot(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})\right].

(14)

The
i
-ideal DR CPE loss in
Definition

4.2
is simply derived by setting
\mu=\textup{{Q}}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\cdot;\gamma)
,
\nu=\textup{{Q}}^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i}}(\cdot;\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})
, and
\pi=Z_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma}/(Z_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma}+Z^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{\textup{\rm i},\gamma})
.
The latter choice of setting the marginal label probabilities
\pi
conveniently eliminates the need to estimate the normalizers of the ideal distributions.

Although
Definition

4.2
defines a pair of loss functions—one for the marginal ideal distributions, one for the joint ideal distributions—practically only the joint variant can be utilized. This is due to the dependence of
{\mathbb{E}}_{\upeta^{\star}(x)}
inside the exponential function in the weights
\tilde{w}_{\textup{\rm m}}(x;\gamma)=\exp\left(-{{\mathbb{E}}_{\upeta^{\star}(x)}\left[\ell(x,\mathsf{Y},h(x))\right]}/{\gamma}\right)
. Although one could attempt to empirically estimate the weights (and the corresponding loss
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm m}}
), the resulting estimator would be biased. Furthermore, the estimated loss would typically have a high variance as for most high dimensional input spaces
\mathcal{X}
, only a single class label
y
is available for each observation
x
.

Despite the marginal variant of the DR CPE loss being difficult to estimate, the joint DR CPE loss
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}(s;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})
has a convenient form:

\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})\sim\textup{{P}}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))}{\gamma}\right)\cdot(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})+\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))}{\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}}\right)\cdot(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})\right].

(15)

Indeed, as the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}_{\upeta^{\star}(x)}
is on the outside of the exponential function, the objective
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}
can be calculated as an expectation of a random variable w.r.t.
the joint data measure
P
. From an estimation perspective, one can simply switch the expectation in
Eq.

15
to yield an unbiased estimator.

A natural question is how are these two objectives related.
As the marginal ideal distributions (and thus the corresponding loss function) relate to the typical notion of optimal deferral (via
Theorem

3.5
), the objective we would like to minimize is the marginal DR CPE loss
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm m}}
.
Although
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm m}}
is not easy to estimate, through Jensen’s inequality, one can directly show that the joint DR CPE loss
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}
directly upper bounds the marginal DR CPE loss
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm m}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
\ell
is non-negative. Then
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm m}}(s;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}(s;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})
.

From
Lemma

4.3
, one can expect that minimizing the joint variant DR CPE loss will reduce the marginal DR CPE loss to some degree. However, despite this, there will be a non-trivial gap between the two quantities. The following result shows that the gap between these loss functions are intrinsically dependent on the variance of performance for the model
h
and the expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
.

Theorem 4.4

(Informal)
.

Fix
\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}>0
. Suppose
\ell
is bounded and
\uplambda_{+1},\uplambda_{-1}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}(s;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})-\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm m}}(s;\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}[(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X}){\mathbb{V}}_{\textup{{P}}}[\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))\mid\mathsf{X}]]}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}}[(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X}){\mathbb{V}}_{\textup{{P}}}[\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))\mid\mathsf{X}]]}{(\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})})^{2}}\right),

where
\Theta
depends on the loss bound and
\gamma,\gamma^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
. A precise statement is given in
Theorem

E.2
.

Theorem

4.4
shows that the gap between loss functions is small when the losses of
h
and
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
vary little over the label distribution
\upeta^{\star}(x)
.
There are two distinct circumstances when this gap vanishes. First, the gap is small when for a fixed
x
, the loss is near constant. For the typical loss functions, such as cross-entropy, this occurs when the model predicts randomly. Second, the gap is small when the Bayes optimal class-posterior
\upeta^{\star}(x)
is concentrated on a single label. As such, whenever there is no label uncertainty (
i.e
.
, no aleatoric uncertainty
(
Hüllermeier and Waegeman 2021
)
), the two objectives are equivalent.

We remind that although generally, the two loss functions will disagree, the Bayes optimal scorer of the nicer to estimate joint variant does correspond to a version of Chow’s rule, but on an expert-tilted distribution (
Theorem

3.5
). So, the learned scorer, irrespective of the gap to its marginal counterpart, corresponds to meaningful deferral decisions.

Expert-Comparison Estimation:

Previous work in the literature suggests learning deferral rules by regressing pointwise targets that compare model and expert performance
(
Kag et al. 2022
;
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
.
In particular, one seeks to estimate a pointwise expert-comparison target that approximates how Chow’s rule makes deferral decisions.
For example, one considers the zero-one loss function to design a least-squares regression task on the random variable

\mathsf{Y}^{({\rm d})}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket-\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket.

(16)

These target labels
\mathsf{Y}^{({\rm d})}
simply aim to identify whenever the expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
would be correct but the model
h
would not.
Interestingly, a direct connection can be made to the joint ideal DR CPE loss.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
\ell
is the zero-one loss function and
\uplambda
is the squared loss function. Let
\Sigma(x)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]+{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]
.
Then, for
\textup{{P}}_{\rm x}
-almost every
x
with
\Sigma(x)>0
, the unique pointwise minimizer
s^{\star}_{0}(x)
of the limiting risk satisfies, for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

\llbracket s^{\star}_{0}(x)\leq\tau\rrbracket=\left\llbracket{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}^{({\rm d})}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\leq\tau\,\Sigma(x)\right\rrbracket.

(17)

An interesting interpretation of
Proposition

4.5
is that, in the low temperature limit
\gamma\to 0
, the optimal deferral mechanism induced by the joint ideal loss with squared loss is equivalent to the optimal deferral mechanism learned by regressing on
\mathsf{Y}^{({\rm d})}
. The only difference is that the threshold is no longer constant, but instead rescaled by the input-dependent factor
\Sigma(x)
. Thus, the rule preserves the same expert-comparison signal used in prior work, while additionally favoring deferral in regions where the total correctness probability of the model and expert is larger.
This can be desirable, since deferral is discouraged when neither the base model
h
nor the expert
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
is likely to be correct.

Anomaly Detection (AD):

One definition of anomalous observations are examples which are not concentrated
(
Ripley 2007
;
Chandola et al. 2009
)
,
i.e
.
,
examples that are sampled from areas of low density w.r.t.

P
.
To identify such examples, one approach in AD seeks to solve an objective similar to DRE
Eq.

11
. More concretely, they seek to learn a scorer
s
via optimizing
(
Steinwart et al. 2005
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm anomaly}(s)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}[(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})]+\int(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(x)\,\textup{{d}}\mu(x),

(18)

where
\mu
corresponds to the reference measure of
P
. An observation
x
can then be classified as anomalous via the scorer
s(x)
,
i.e
.
, via a threshold
\llbracket s(x)\leq\alpha_{q}\rrbracket
with
\alpha_{q}
chosen to correspond to the
q
th quantile of the scorer
s
.
A calibrated variant of the AD task can also be considered, where instead of just wanting to identify anomalous observations, we want the learned scorer
s
(via a link function) to corresponds to the cumulative distribution function of
P

(
Menon and Williamson 2018
)
.

This form of AD mirrors that of learning deferral rules from ideal distributions—the only change is
what is considered a typical versus anomalous distribution.
Here, the typical distribution is considered as the data distribution itself
P
and anything which deviates from it is considered anomalous,
i.e
.
, the reference measure
\mu
. Instead, in L2D both the typical and anomalous distributions are reweightings of the data distribution.
From an optimization perspective, our deferral task is easier. In
Eq.

18
, the integral term is difficult to estimate (for general
\mu
)
(
Menon and Williamson 2018
)
.

5
Experiments

In the following section, we evaluate our ideal DR CPE method learning deferral decisions. To do so, we consider a variant of the experimental model cascade setup presented in
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
on corrupted CIFAR-100
(
Krizhevsky 2009
)
and MedMNIST
(
Yang et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2023
)
datasets.
We consider label noise, long-tail, and specialization variants of these datasets: label noise random flips the labels of the first 10 classes; long-tail subsamples 500 examples from the first 25 classes and 50 examples from the rest; and specialization subsamples examples at a rate of 20% for non-specialist class labels.
We consider a three-way partition of the dataset: a corrupted training set for
h
,
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
; a clean training set for
r
; and a test set for evaluation.
When a validation split is available, we use it to train the deferral mechanism; otherwise, we use a partition of the original training set for this purpose.
The model architectures are ResNet models of various sizes
\{8,14,32,56\}
(
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
typically larger) and (when applicable) deferral approaches utilize a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron with features constructed from probability outputs from
h

(
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
.
Further details and experiments with different corruption levels are presented in
Appendices

G
and
H
.

For comparison, we consider 3 types of baselines. First we consider a
confidence-based
approach (
Conf
), which simply defers based on thresholding
h
’s perceived confidence (no deferral training)
(
Ramaswamy et al. 2018
)
. Second, we consider a
two-stage
surrogate loss method (
TwoStage-Exp
)
(
Mao et al. 2023
)
. Finally, we consider two
expert-comparison estimations

(
Kag et al. 2022
;
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
: one which regresses on
\mathsf{Y}^{(d)}
as per
Eq.

16
(
Estimate-Diff01
); and one which uses the relative confidence between
h
and
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
by estimating
h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}
’s maximum predicted probability (
Estimate-MaxProb
).
For
DR CPE
, we set
\uplambda
to be the squared loss and the temperature to
\gamma=0.5
; we consider
\ell
to be either the generalized cross-entropy (GCE)
(
Zhang and Sabuncu 2018
)
or a probabilistic version of the zero-one loss which consists of the
L_{1}
distance between the one-hot label and the predicted probabilities (Prob01).

Fig.

summarizes our experiments, where we evaluate the accuracy-deferral trade-offs (upper bounds are better). The approaches perform similarly under clean data. Over corrupted settings,
DR CPE
performs best to second best. When compared to the

and the
two-stage
approaches,
DR CPE
is consistently better. Both of these baselines perform extremely badly on the DermaMNIST specialist setting. The exception is that the
two-stage
approach performs well clean PathMNIST and corrupted PathMNIST for smaller deferral rates (less than

); however, for higher deferral rates it performs significantly worse than

(GCE).

.

performs significantly worse than

with GCE.

has strong performance, beating

for the CIFAR-100 long-tail and DermaMNIST specialist settings, but suffers from a larger variance in the latter.
However, the approach critically fails in the PathMNIST specialist setting—which could be attributed to its tendency to overfit
(
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
.

with Prob01 also has a loss in performance (and a larger variance) in this setting despite outperforming its GCE counterpart in other settings.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a novel method for learning deferral mechanisms based on ideal distributions and the link between Density Ratio Estimation (DRE) and Class-Probability Estimation (CPE). Practically, we find that the DR CPE loss function we propose can yield good accuracy-deferral trade-offs while also being robust in its performance over a variety of dataset and corruption combinations.
So far, the narrative has been for L2D with a pair of models. Of course one could easily chain deferral mechanism, as per model cascades
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
)
; however, ideally we would want to directly deal with multi-expert deferral, where there may be multiple base models and/or experts
(
Verma et al. 2023
;
Mao et al. 2023
;
Mao et al. 2024b
;
Mao et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2026
)
. An obvious connection here could be to exploit the connection between multi-distributional DRE and CPE in the multi-class setting
(
Yu et al. 2021
;
Nock et al. 2016b
)
.
Another important direction is to modify our ideal distribution framework to allow additional constraints for,
e.g
.
, fairness or limiting expert calls in training
(
Narasimhan et al. 2024
;
Charusaie and Samadi 2024
;
DeSalvo et al. 2025
)
.

Appendix A
Limitations

Although the approach of the paper has many connections, a theoretical limitation is that it does not have consistency guarantees explored in other L2D approaches which utilize surrogate loss functions
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
. However, we suspect that some finite sample bounds could be established similarly to the expert-comparison estimation approaches
[
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
, Lemma 4.2]
(as a result of the connections we establish between these approaches,
e.g
.
,
Proposition

4.5
). Practically, although we are not required to commit to a “deferral cost”
c
or threshold
\tau
at training time, the temperature
\tau
can have a non-trivial effect on performance (experiments are detailed in the
Appendix

H
).

Appendix B
Broader Impact

L2D inherently has impact in various downstream tasks,
e.g
.
, in health.
This can cause potential issues in scenarios of
overly conservative deferral
,
i.e
.
, the overall system does not defer to a human expert when the base model is unreliable.
As such, in practice, one should ensure that the deferral rates
\tau
selected are validated to ensure that unreliable base model predictions are not utilized at a high rate.
On the flip side, a well-calibrated L2D model has the potential of reducing the burden on a human expert.
Other impacts our and other L2D approaches could have is on the fairness and privacy of the overarching deferring system (when compared to just the base model).
Although, L2D has been previously motivated to improve fairness
[
Madras et al. 2018
]
, our approach does not have any explicit constraints on any fairness metrics. Furthermore, deferral could impact the privacy of users,
e.g
.
, in a loan prediction system, patterns of deferral could leak certain demographic information.
These aspects should be considered when utilizing our method.

Appendix C
Ideal Distribution with
\varphi
-Divergences

In the following section, we present a generalization of the optimal ideal distributions considered in
Corollary

3.3
.
Instead of considering the KL-divergence, we will pick the family of
\varphi
-divergences (a.k.a.
, Csiszár
f
-divergences) as our choice of dissimilarity
D
in
Definition

3.1
.
We first recall the definition of an
\varphi
-divergence.

Definition C.1

(
\varphi
-Divergence)
.

Given a convex lower semi-continuous generator
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,\infty]
that satisfies
\varphi(1)=0
, the corresponding
\varphi
-divergence between non-negative measures
\textup{{P}}\ll\textup{{Q}}
is defined by

\textup{\rm D}_{\varphi}(\textup{{P}}\Mid\textup{{Q}})=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\varphi\left(\frac{\textup{{d}}\textup{{P}}}{\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}}(z)\right)\textup{{d}}\textup{{Q}}(z).

(19)

We will be looking at the generic problem of solving:

\displaystyle\min_{\uprho\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\uprho(\mathsf{Z})L(\mathsf{Z})\right]+\gamma\cdot\textup{\rm D}_{\varphi}(\uprho\cdot\textup{{P}}\Mid\textup{{P}}),

(20)

\displaystyle s.t.\hbox{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}[\uprho(\mathsf{Z})]=1.

where
L\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma>0
.

Eq.

20
accounts for both marginal and joint variants of the optimization problems in
Definition

3.1
.
The only difference is that instead of optimizing for a distribution, we optimize for the density-ratio directly (similar to
Dvijotham et al. 2020
). The corresponding ideal distribution can be recovered via
\textup{{Q}}(z)=\uprho(z)\textup{{P}}(z)
,
i.e
.
,
\uprho
is the reweighting.
Solutions to such an equation under different assumptions on
\varphi
are well-known and have been explored in various literatures
[
Knoblauch et al. 2022
,
Picard-Weibel and Guedj 2022
,
Soen et al. 2024
,
Roulet et al. 2025
]
.
In what follows, we present a novel (to the best of our knowledge) solution to
Eq.

20
. This will immediately give the proof of
Corollary

3.3
via an instantiation of
\textup{\rm D}_{\varphi}=\textup{\rm KL}
.
The approach we utilize is heavily inspired by
Picard-Weibel and Guedj 2022
.

C.1
The Result

We first present the result and the necessary definitions required to state it.

First, we will only consider a subset of possible generators
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
. Formally, we consider the set

\Phi\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\left\{\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]\mid\varphi\textrm{ is proper convex, lower semi-continuous},\varphi(1)=0,\varphi(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\subset\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(21)

\Phi
defines the class of usual
\varphi
-generators with an additional constraint that
\varphi(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\subset\mathbb{R}
. This ensures that
\varphi
is subdifferentiable on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and that the right derivative
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
is well-defined in
[-\infty,+\infty)
.

Definition C.2

(Right Derivative,
Rockafellar 1970
)
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
be any function. Let
x
be a point where
\varphi
is finite. Then given that the following limit exists, the right derivative is defined as,

\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(x)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\varphi(x+h)-\varphi(x)}{h}.

(22)

We also consider the standard convex conjugate.

Definition C.3

(Convex Conjugate,
Rockafellar 1970
)
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
be any function. The convex-conjugate (Fenchel conjugate) is defined as,

\varphi^{*}(p)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{xp-\varphi(x)\right\}.

(23)

We also consider a refinement of the generator class
\Phi
:

\Phi_{\uparrow}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\left\{\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]\mid\varphi\in\Phi,\varphi(\mathbb{R}_{<0})=\{+\infty\}\right\}.

(24)

Intuitively,
\Phi_{\uparrow}
is just the class of generators where negative inputs are set to
\infty
. Indeed, we can define a transform from any generator
\varphi\in\Phi
to
\Phi_{\uparrow}
via

\varphi_{\uparrow}(x)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\begin{cases}\varphi(x)&\text{if }x\geq 0\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(25)

It immediately follows that as
\varphi
only takes the Radon-Nikodym derivative of non-negative measures in
\varphi
-divergences, the resulting divergence is invariant from the choice of generator
\varphi
or
\varphi_{\uparrow}
,
i.e
.
,
\textup{\rm D}_{\varphi}=\textup{\rm D}_{\varphi_{\uparrow}}
. Despite this, in what follows, we will see that the properties of
\varphi_{\uparrow}
are much easier to work with. For instance, the convex-conjugate of
\varphi_{\uparrow}
conveniently becomes

\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=\sup_{x\geq 0}\{xp-\varphi(x)\}.

(26)

With these definitions, we can now state our result.

Theorem C.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\Phi
. Suppose that there exists a constant
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[(\gamma\varphi_{\uparrow})^{*}(b-L(\mathsf{Z}))\right]<+\infty,

(27)

the right derivative
(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}
exists
P
-almost surely at
\frac{b-L(\mathsf{Z})}{\gamma}
,
the quantity

{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\left|(\gamma\varphi_{\uparrow})^{*}(b-L(\mathsf{Z}))-\max\left\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}\left(\frac{b-L(\mathsf{Z})}{\gamma}\right)\right\}\cdot(b-L(\mathsf{Z}))\right|\right]<+\infty

(28)

is finite, and

{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\max\left\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}\left(\frac{b-L(z)}{\gamma}\right)\right\}\right]=1

(29)

Then optimal ratio
\uprho^{\star}
of
Eq.

20
is given by

\uprho^{\star}(z)=\max\left\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}\left(\frac{b-L(z)}{\gamma}\right)\right\}.

(30)

It follows that
Corollary

3.3
follows immediately from
Theorem

C.4
by consider the generator of the KL-divergence.

C.2
Helper Lemmas

To prove
Theorem

C.4
, we first prove a number of helper lemmas.

We first consider a simplification one can yield for the convex conjugate of
\varphi_{\uparrow}
by examining its right derivative.

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper convex, lower semi-continuous function such that
\varphi(0)<+\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)\in[-\infty,+\infty)
.

\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=\begin{cases}-\varphi(0)&\text{if }p\leq\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)\\
\varphi^{*}(p)&\text{if }p>\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0).\end{cases}

(31)

Proof.

Fix
p\in\mathbb{R}
and define
g_{p}(x)=xp-\varphi(x)
. Then
g_{p}
is concave and

\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=\sup_{x\geq 0}g_{p}(x).

Suppose first that
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)=-\infty
. We claim that then
\varphi(x)=+\infty
for all
x<0
. Indeed, if there existed
x_{0}<0
with
\varphi(x_{0})<+\infty
, then for any
y>0
convexity would imply

\frac{\varphi(y)-\varphi(0)}{y}\geq\frac{\varphi(0)-\varphi(x_{0})}{-x_{0}}>-\infty,

and sending
y\downarrow 0
would yield
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)>-\infty
(note,
Definition

C.2
), a contradiction. Hence, in this case
\varphi(x)=+\infty
for all
x<0
, and thus
\varphi=\varphi_{\uparrow}
, so
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=\varphi^{*}(p)
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}
.

We now proceed further with two cases with
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)>-\infty
.

①Suppose that
p\leq\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
. For any
x>0
, convexity implies that

\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)}{x}\geq\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)\geq p.

Rearranging gives
g_{p}(x)=xp-\varphi(x)\leq-\varphi(0)=g_{p}(0)
, and so
x=0
is a maximizer of
g_{p}
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Therefore,
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=-\varphi(0)
.

②Now suppose that
p>\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
. By the definition of the right derivative, there exists
\bar{x}>0
such that

\frac{\varphi(\bar{x})-\varphi(0)}{\bar{x}}<p,

which implies
g_{p}(\bar{x})>-\varphi(0)=g_{p}(0)
.
Hence,
x=0
is not a maximizer over
\mathbb{R}
.

For
x<0
and any
y>0
, convexity gives

\frac{\varphi(0)-\varphi(x)}{-x}\leq\frac{\varphi(y)-\varphi(0)}{y}.

Sending
y\downarrow 0
yields
\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(0)+\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)x
,
and thus

g_{p}(x)=xp-\varphi(x)\leq-\varphi(0)+(p-\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0))x<-\varphi(0).

Since
g_{p}(\bar{x})>-\varphi(0)
for some
\bar{x}>0
, no negative
x
can maximize
g_{p}
over
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}g_{p}(x)=\sup_{x\geq 0}g_{p}(x),

which yields
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=\varphi^{*}(p)
.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper convex, lower semi-continuous function such that
\varphi(0)<+\infty
.
Then, at every
p\in\mathbb{R}
where the right derivatives are defined,

(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=\max\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\}.

(32)

Proof.

We utilize
Lemma

C.5
and consider a few cases. First consider when
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)=-\infty
. From the proof of
Lemma

C.5
, this implies that
\varphi(x)=+\infty
for all
x<0
. Hence,
\varphi=\varphi_{\uparrow}
. Therefore,

\varphi^{*}(p)=\sup_{x\geq 0}\{xp-\varphi(x)\}.

Since each affine function
p\mapsto xp-\varphi(x)
has non-negative slope
x\geq 0
, the supremum
\varphi^{*}
is non-decreasing. Thus, at every point where
(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}
is defined, we have
(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\geq 0
, and therefore

(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=\max\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\}.

This proves the claim in the case
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)=-\infty
.

We further proceed with the assumption that
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)>-\infty
.

We note that
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)\in\partial\varphi(0)
. Indeed, for
x>0
, convexity implies

\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)}{x}\geq\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0),

and hence
\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(0)+\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)x
. For
x<0
and any
y>0
, convexity gives

\frac{\varphi(0)-\varphi(x)}{-x}\leq\frac{\varphi(y)-\varphi(0)}{y}.

Sending
y\downarrow 0
yields
\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(0)+\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)x
.
Thus,
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)\in\partial\varphi(0)
, and Fenchel-Young duality gives
0\in\partial\varphi^{*}(\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0))
.
Hence,
\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
is a minimizer of the convex function
\varphi^{*}
, and therefore

(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\leq 0\quad\text{for all }p<\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0),\qquad(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }p\geq\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0),

at every point where the right derivative is defined.

We now further proceed via considering three cases.

①Suppose first that
p<\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
.
Then
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}
is constant on the interval
[p,\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)]
, and hence
(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=0
.
Therefore,
(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=0=\max\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\}
.

②Now suppose that
p>\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
.
Then
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(q)=\varphi^{*}(q)
for all
q
in a right-neighborhood of
p
, and therefore
(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)
.
Since
p>\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
, we also have
(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\geq 0
.
Thus,
(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=\max\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\}
.

③Finally, suppose that
p=\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)
. For any
h>0
, we have
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p+h)=\varphi^{*}(p+h)
, while
Lemma

C.5
gives
\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)=-\varphi(0)
.
Since the argument above shows
p=\varphi^{\prime}_{+}(0)\in\partial\varphi(0)
, Fenchel-Young duality yields
\varphi^{*}(p)=-\varphi(0)=\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)
.
Therefore,

(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p+h)-\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*}(p)}{h}=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\varphi^{*}(p+h)-\varphi^{*}(p)}{h}=(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p).

Since
p
minimizes
\varphi^{*}
, we have
(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\geq 0
, and hence

(\varphi_{\uparrow}^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)=\max\{0,(\varphi^{*})^{\prime}_{+}(p)\}
.
∎

around
z=0
with an explicit Lagrange remainder. Given the maps sufficient smoothness, we have

\displaystyle\exp(-z/\gamma)

\displaystyle=\exp(0)-\frac{\exp(0)}{\gamma}z+\frac{\exp(-(\theta z)/\gamma)}{2\gamma^{2}}z^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{z}{\gamma}+\frac{z^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\exp(-(\theta z)/\gamma),

where
\theta\in[0,1]
.

Re-arranging, setting
z=\mathsf{Z}
, and using the positivity of
z^{2}/(2\gamma^{2})
, we get the bound

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathsf{Z}}{\gamma}+\frac{\mathsf{Z}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{M}{\gamma}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\mathsf{Z}}{\gamma}\right)-1\leq-\frac{\mathsf{Z}}{\gamma}+\frac{\mathsf{Z}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{m}{\gamma}\right).

Finally, taking an expectation and noting that
\mathsf{Z}
is a centered random variable yields the desired result.
∎

Appendix F
Connections to Expert-Comparison Estimation

The joint ideal DR CPE losses connect to various expert-comparison estimation approaches. In the following, we prove
Proposition

4.5
and also consider a few different instantiations of
\ell
and
\uplambda
which yields other interesting expert-comparison estimation approaches.

Lemma F.1
.

Let
c\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&c>0,\\
1&c=0.\end{cases}

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=1.

Proof.

If
c>0
, then
c/\gamma\to\infty
as
\gamma\to 0^{+}
, and therefore

-c/\gamma\to-\infty
. Since
\exp(x)\to 0
as
x\to-\infty
, we obtain

\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\gamma}\right)=0.

If instead
c=0
, then for every
\gamma>0
we have

\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\gamma}\right)=\exp(0)=1,

and hence

\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\gamma}\right)=1.

Finally, as
\gamma\to\infty
we have
c/\gamma\to 0
, so by continuity of
the exponential function,

\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\gamma}\right)=\exp(0)=1.

∎

Corollary F.2
.

Suppose that
\ell
the zero-one loss function. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}(s;\gamma,\gamma)={\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket\cdot(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})+\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket\cdot(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})\right],

Proof.

As
\ell
is the zero-one loss, for every

we have

\ell(x,y,h(x))=1-\llbracket f(x)=y\rrbracket,\qquad\ell(x,y,h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x))=1-\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(x)=y\rrbracket.

Hence, by the preceding

F.1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket.

Therefore, from
Eq.

15
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}(s;\gamma,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h(\mathsf{X}))}{\gamma}\right)(\uplambda{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 48.00009pt\left.+\exp\left(-\frac{\ell(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y},h^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X}))}{\gamma}\right)(\uplambda{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket\cdot(\uplambda_{+1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})+\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket\cdot(\uplambda_{-1}\circ s)(\mathsf{X})\right],

F.1
Proof of
Proposition

4.5

Proof of
Proposition

4.5
.

For the squared loss, the partial losses are
[
Menon and Ong 2016
]

\uplambda_{+1}(v)=(v-1)^{2},\qquad\uplambda_{-1}(v)=(v+1)^{2}.

Therefore, from
Corollary

F.2

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{L}_{\textup{\rm j}}(s;\gamma,\gamma)={\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\left[\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket(s(\mathsf{X})-1)^{2}+\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket(s(\mathsf{X})+1)^{2}\right].

To identify the optimal scorer of the limiting objective, we condition on
\mathsf{X}=x
and consider the pointwise conditional risk

\displaystyle R_{x}(v)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket(v-1)^{2}+\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket(v+1)^{2}\mid\mathsf{X}=x\right]

\displaystyle=p_{h}(x)(v-1)^{2}+p_{e}(x)(v+1)^{2},

where

p_{h}(x)={\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x],\qquad p_{e}(x)={\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x].

Equivalently, with

\Delta(x)={\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}^{({\rm d})}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]=p_{h}(x)-p_{e}(x),\qquad\Sigma(x)=p_{h}(x)+p_{e}(x),

we can write

R_{x}(v)=\Sigma(x)v^{2}-2\Delta(x)v+\Sigma(x).

Therefore, whenever
\Sigma(x)>0
, the unique minimizer satisfies

\frac{\partial R_{x}(v)}{\partial v}=2\Sigma(x)v-2\Delta(x)=0,

and hence

s_{0}^{\star}(x)=\frac{\Delta(x)}{\Sigma(x)}.

If
\Sigma(x)=0
, then
R_{x}(v)=0
for all
v
, so any value of
v
is
optimal. Finally, for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and any
x
with

\Sigma(x)>0
, we have

s_{0}^{\star}(x)\leq\tau\iff\Delta(x)\leq\tau\,\Sigma(x),

as claimed.
∎

F.2
Ratio Expert Comparison

One interesting variant of
Proposition

4.5
is when we change the exact DRE loss
\uplambda
to other well-known DRE loss functions.

Proposition F.3
.

Suppose
\ell
is the zero-one loss function and
\uplambda
is the LSIF
[
Kanamori et al. 2009
]
loss function.
Then the unique pointwise minimizer

of the limiting risk satisfies, for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

\llbracket s^{\star}_{r}(x)\leq\tau\rrbracket=\left\llbracket{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\leq\tau{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\right\rrbracket.

(35)

Proof.

For the LSIF loss function, the partial losses are
Menon and Ong 2016
,

\uplambda_{+1}(v)=-v\qquad\uplambda_{-1}(v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}.

Mirroring the proof of
Proposition

4.5
, we have the pointwise condition risk

R_{x}(v)=-p_{h}(x)v+\frac{1}{2}p_{e}(x)v^{2}.

Therefore, taking the minimizer, we have that

\frac{\partial R_{x}(v)}{\partial v}=-p_{h}(x)+p_{e}(x)v=0,

and hence,

s^{\star}_{r}(x)=\frac{p_{h}(x)}{p_{e}(x)}.

The result follows immediately.
∎

Proposition F.4
.

Suppose
\ell
is the zero-one loss function and
\uplambda
is the KLIEP
[
Sugiyama et al. 2008
]

Then the unique pointwise minimizer
s^{\star}_{r}(x)
of the limiting risk satisfies, for any

\llbracket s^{\star}_{r}(x)\leq\tau\rrbracket=\left\llbracket{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\leq\tau{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\right\rrbracket.

For the KLIEP loss function, the partial losses are

Mirroring the proof of

Therefore, taking the minimizer, we have that

\frac{\partial R_{x}(v)}{\partial v}=-\frac{1}{v}p_{h}(x)+p_{e}(x)=0,

The result follows immediately.

It is particularly interesting that two of (arguably) the most well-known DRE losses
[
Sugiyama et al. 2008
,
Kanamori et al. 2009
]
yield the same optimal deferral mechanism, as per

F.4
and
F.3
.
This is an interesting variant of the usual “difference” which is optimized for via
Eq.

16
. The optimal scorer is also very intuitive: we want to reject whenever our base model’s (conditional) accuracy is worse than what the expert would have (up to discount scaling
\tau
).

The logistic loss also yields a similar optimal deferral mechanism.

Proposition F.5
.

Suppose
\ell
is the zero-one loss function and
\uplambda
is the logistic loss function.
Then the unique pointwise minimizer
s^{\star}_{r}(x)
of the limiting risk satisfies, for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

\llbracket s^{\star}_{r}(x)\leq\tau\rrbracket=\left\llbracket{\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\leq\exp(\tau){\mathbb{P}}_{\textup{{P}}}[f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}=x]\right\rrbracket.

(37)

Proof.

For the logistic loss function, the partial losses are
Menon and Ong 2016
,

\uplambda_{+1}(v)=\log(1+\exp(-v))\qquad\uplambda_{-1}(v)=\log(1+\exp(v))

Mirroring the proof of
Proposition

4.5
, we have the pointwise condition risk

R_{x}(v)=p_{h}(x)\log(1+\exp(-v))+p_{e}(x)\log(1+\exp(v)).

Therefore, taking the minimizer, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{x}(v)}{\partial v}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\exp(-v)}{1+\exp(-v)}p_{h}(x)+\frac{\exp(v)}{1+\exp(v)}p_{e}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{1+\exp(v)}p_{h}(x)+\frac{\exp(v)}{1+\exp(v)}p_{e}(x)

\displaystyle=0.

Thus we have

-p_{h}(x)+\exp(v)p_{e}(x)=0

and hence,

s^{\star}_{r}(x)=\log\left(\frac{p_{h}(x)}{p_{e}(x)}\right).

The result follows immediately.
∎

The optimal deferral mechanisms are essentially the same ratio thresholds reparameterized when comparing
Propositions

F.4
and
F.3
versus

. Despite this, as in practice we are thresholding the scorers (as per

4.1
), KLIEP and LSIF may be more preferable: a small change in the scorer for the logistic loss (

F.5
) can cause a large shift in the decision w.r.t.
the accuracy of base versus expert.

We go into more detail regarding the baselines we consider.

Confidence-Based Deferral

Perhaps the simplest approach to deferral is to utilize the base model
h

At inference time, one can simply threshold this confidence (

\textrm{Conf}(x)=\max_{y}h_{y}(x),

(38)

that is,
\llbracket\textrm{Conf}(x)\leq\tau\rrbracket
for some threshold
\tau\in[0,1]
.

Expert-Comparison Estimation

These approaches are based on simple prediction targets (typically trained via regression,
i.e
.
, squared loss functions)
[
Kag et al. 2022
,
Jitkrittum et al. 2023
]
. As a result, we end up with a model
g
that approximates a constructed random variable. Deferral can then be established by thresholding
g
,
i.e
.
,
r(x)=\llbracket d(g(x))\leq\tau\rrbracket
for some
\tau
which we can tune and
d
is a decision function.

•

Difference in zero-one accuracy
(
Estimate-Diff01
):
This baseline considers the difference in zero-one accuracy. This is exactly the target discussed in
Eq.

16
. For completeness, we repeat the target random variable that is defined:

\mathsf{Y}^{({\rm d})}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\llbracket f(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket-\llbracket f^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Y}\rrbracket.

Thus, negative values correspond to examples on which the expert is correct and the base model is not.
The decision function in this case is just the identity
d(z)=z
.

•

(

)
This baseline regresses the expert’s maximum predicted class probability:

\mathsf{P}^{({\rm m})}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\upeta^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{y}(\mathsf{X}).

(39)

At test time, the learned score is compared against the base model’s maximum confidence,

d(g(x))=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\upeta_{y}(x)-g(x),

(40)

where we remind that
\upeta
is the base model and
g(x)
approximates
\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\upeta^{(\textup{\rm{e}})}_{y}(\mathsf{X})
via
Eq.

.
Thus, deferral is favored when the expert appears more confident than the base model.

We adapt

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{two-stage}}(s;c)={\mathbb{E}}_{\textup{{P}}}\Bigg[

Typically, one defers from thresholding
s
to the base model’s confidence,
i.e
.
,
\llbracket s(x)>\max_{y}h_{y}(x)\rrbracket
. We consider a modification by thresholding over additional values to create a deferral curve
\llbracket s(x)>\max_{y}h_{y}(x)+\tau\rrbracket
for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Usually, one would train various deferral models over different
c
, and then build a deferral curve from each
c
via thresholding at
\tau=0

\tau
s. Instead, we search over the hyperparmeters
c
and show the best deferral curve by ranging over
\tau
at inference time.
