Dosimetric and Clinical Impact of Bone Marrow-sparing Radiation Therapy for Anal Cancer: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jasmine Chen, Elizabeth Forde, Michelle Leech, Shao Hui Huang, Olive Wong, Rouhi Fazelzad, Ali Hosni, John Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis systematic review investigated the technical considerations, dosimetric feasibility, and clinical impact on hematologic toxicity (HT) associated with bone marrow (BM)-sparing radiation therapy in anal cancer.Methods and MaterialsOriginal studies reporting associations between BM dosimetry and HT were identified from 6 databases and independently screened by 2 reviewers and reported according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses 2020 standards. Study quality and risk of bias were appraised using Joanna Briggs Institute tools and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach. Dosimetry analysis included active BM (ABM) structural definition, optimization strategies, and achievable dose reduction. Clinical efficacy was assessed using response measurement tools, toxicity predictors, and reported acute and late HT.ResultsThirty-five studies met the inclusion criteria. The outer pelvic bone marrow (PBM) was the most common BM surrogate, followed by the ilium, lumbar-sacrum, and lower pelvis. Contouring variations of the lumbar-sacrum were noted in 15 studies, primarily in the L3 to L5 vertebrae and inferior sacrum/coccyx. ABM was defined in 14 studies using various imaging modalities, including fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography, computed tomography, fluorothymidine positron emission tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging scans. Dosimetric findings suggested that selective sparing of PBM or its subunits reduced PBM V10 to V20 by 0% to 47%, and ABM V3 to V45 by 2% to 14%. Clinically derived predictors highlighted the importance of limiting low-to-intermediate dose exposure to the whole pelvis and lumbar-sacrum. Dose-response modeling accounted for variation in assessment tools and study endpoints. A wide range of acute HT rates was reported, with comparable outcomes among patients treated with and without BM-sparing intensity-modulated radiation therapy.ConclusionsMultimodality imaging facilitates the structure delineation of PBM and its substructures. Dose reduction to these structures can be achieved by using various planning strategies and treatment techniques. Predictive model development requires a comprehensive analysis of response measures and clinical confounders. Given the multifactorial and heterogeneous nature of HT, further investigation is required to determine whether BM dose sparing translates into clinically meaningful HT reduction.

Introduction

Definitive chemoradiation with whole-pelvic radiation therapy and concurrent 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and mitomycin-C (MMC) is the standard organ-preservation approach for anal squamous cell carcinoma (ASCC), supported by 6 phase 3 randomized trials.1 This approach achieves a >70% 5-year overall survival and colostomy-free survival2,3; however, it is associated with significant normal tissue toxicity, particularly to skin, gastrointestinal tract, and bone marrow (BM).3,4

BM is conventionally classified by appearance and hematopoietic activity into red marrow (active BM, ABM) and yellow marrow (inactive or fatty).5,6 ABM is primarily located around the trabecular bones of vertebrae, pelvis, femoral heads, humeral heads, ribs, sternum, scapula, and skull (6). The pelvis and proximal femora contain approximately 34% to 44% of the body’s active BM,7, 8, 9 and are sensitive to radiation. Even low-to-moderate radiation doses can acutely decrease lymphocytes and immature blood cell counts, while doses >30 Gy in conventional fractionation may cause permanent loss of BM cellularity.6 Concurrent chemotherapy further compromises BM tolerance. MMC is highly myelotoxic and suppresses platelet production with a delayed onset and long recovery, while 5-FU rapidly depletes granulocyte progenitor cells.6,10,11 Patients treated with this chemotherapy combination showed increased risks of acute myelosuppression compared to those receiving 5-FU alone, capecitabine, or cisplatin.12

Severe myelosuppression can lead to blood cell disorders, infection, bleeding, treatment interruptions or dosage reduction, and the need for blood transfusion.1 Because unplanned treatment breaks have been associated with worse colostomy-free survival in patients with ASCC,4 it is conceivable that reducing hematologic toxicity (HT) could improve patient outcomes. Although rare, late BM toxicity can be life-threatening and may hinder future therapy options in the event of a locoregional or distant failure.13 Therefore, cumulative myelosuppression is a critical dose-limiting toxicity for ASCC treatments, particularly when pelvic targets typically receive 27 to 45 Gy.

HT appears to be dose-dependent, as shown in a systematic review of pelvic nodal irradiation across multiple cancer types.14 The review recommended incorporating BM sparing into radiation therapy (RT) planning as a parallel organ of avoidance to reduce HT. However, BM-sparing planning has not been routinely adopted for ASCC treatments, and advanced imaging for BM delineation is not universally available. Effective implementation of this technique requires a comprehensive understanding of current BM-sparing planning strategies and judicious integration of available clinical evidence.

This review aimed to evaluate the optimal planning strategies and dosimetric feasibility of BM-sparing planning in ASCC treatments. This includes describing imaging and delineation methods for BM and ABM, comparing dose-volume constraints and planning strategies, and analyzing dose-volume data. The secondary aim was to assess the clinical impact of BM-sparing planning on HT by identifying commonly used response assessment tools, summarizing significant dosimetric predictors and confounders of HT, and comparing the incidence of acute and late HT in patients treated with and without BM-sparing techniques.

Methods and Materials

This review followed Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) 2020 guidelines15 and adhered to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses 2020 reporting standards.5

Search strategy and information sources

A comprehensive search was conducted from inception to July 2024 in Medline ALL, Embase, Cochrane Central, and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (all from the OvidSP platform), as well as Scopus (Elsevier) and Web of Science (Clarivate), with no language restrictions. Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary terms and text words, tailored to each database search syntax. Where applicable, searches were limited to human studies and excluded books, conferences, dissertations, and preprints. Full database search details are provided in Appendix E1. Reference lists of the selected full-text articles and relevant reviews were hand-searched to identify additional studies.

Identifying eligible studies

No prior reviews have comprehensively addressed BM-sparing strategies across diverse study designs. Because case series are often undervalued compared to cohort studies when conducting systematic reviews,16 we adopted a more inclusive approach during abstract screening, accepting case series reporting general toxicities. This strategy ensured the inclusion of studies that might otherwise have been excluded but contained relevant secondary dosimetric analyses eligible for our review. Additionally, to comprehensively assess all available BM-sparing strategies and their impact, this systematic review included both interventional clinical trials evaluating real-world outcomes and computational modeling studies that investigate planning approaches and simulated dose-toxicity prediction models.

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review using Covidence.17 Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Original studies of any design reporting on BM-sparing RT planning and correlated HT were eligible. The population included adults with nonmetastatic (M0) ASCC. Reports of multiple pelvic tumor histopathologies were eligible if data pertaining to ASCC could be extracted. Various types of intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) techniques, such as volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and tomotherapy (rotational IMRT), were included, while 2-dimensional/3-dimensional (3D) non-IMRT techniques were excluded unless used in comparative cohort studies against IMRT techniques. Both photon- and proton-based delivery systems were acceptable. Studies were excluded if they lacked a clear and detailed description of planning parameters, such as BM segmentation (delineation/contouring on a planning computed tomography scan) and dosimetry. Inclusion criteria accommodated alternative chemotherapy regimens (eg, capecitabine, cisplatin, and cetuximab). Studies were required to report dose-response relationships for myelosuppression or HT, with no restrictions on clinical endpoints and assessment timelines. Only the most updated reports reflecting clinical trial progress were selected.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data using customized Excel spreadsheets. Extracted planning data included imaging selection, structural definitions, optimization constraints, and BM dose reduction. Given the multifactorial nature of treatment-induced HT, the extracted data were categorized into (1) tumor (size and N-categories) and treatment factors (chemotherapy cycles and radiation dose levels); (2) clinical endpoints (metrics and timeline); (3) mathematical/biological models; and (4) reported confounders. Relevant information was obtained from text, tables, figures, and supplementary materials. Risk of bias was assessed using JBI critical appraisal tools,15 with agreement measured using Cohen’s kappa statistic.18 Certainty of evidence was rated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.19

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and narrative synthesis were based on the tabulated findings. Both significant and nonsignificant dosimetric results were documented and compared where available. Meta-analysis was not performed owing to the heterogeneity of study designs among the included studies.

Definition and segmentation of BM

The outer table of pelvic bones was consistently used as a BM surrogate on planning computed tomography scans for all 35 studies. Three studies (9%) also investigated the feasibility of using the inner medullary cavity.28,35,46 The earliest BM-sparing record was reported by Milano et al20 in 2005, involving the avoidance of bilateral iliac crests during IMRT optimization. Menkarios et al21 later provided a clear demarcation of the ilium (ILI) from the top of the iliac crest to the superior aspect of the acetabulum. Mell et al22 subsequently established a benchmark segmentation strategy by defining the outer pelvic bone as the pelvic BM (PBM) and dividing it into the ILI, lumbosacral spine (LSS), and lower pelvis (LP). They defined the LSS as extending from the superior border of the L5 vertebra to the coccyx, without extending below the superior edge of the femoral heads. The LP comprised the pubes, ischia, acetabula, and proximal femora, extending from the superior border of the femoral heads to the inferior border of the ischial tuberosities. Among the included literature, PBM was evaluated in 27 studies (77%), followed by the subunits of ILI (66%), LSS (46%), and LP (43%).

In clinical practice, commonly used optimization constraints include limiting ILI V30 to V50 (specifically, V30 < 50%, V40 < 35%, and V50 < 5%) based on the RT Oncology Group 0529 guidelines.4,12,31,33,36,41,45,53 Similarly, PBMs are often adapted from cervical cancer studies, and limiting PBM V10 < 90% and V20 < 75%.26,35,39,42 While ABM structures shared these same volumetric constraints as their corresponding outer bone counterparts, they were assigned a higher optimization priority.26,41,48 Additionally, a few studies apply BM constraints adapted from institutional-specific protocols, such as PBM V10 < 65%, ILI V10 < 35% to 45%, V20 < 25% to 30%, and ABM-LSS V40 < 41%.21,31,48 As shown in Table 220,21,23,26,29,30,32,33,35,42,44,46,50,51 applying region-specific constraints can significantly reduce PBM V10 to V30 by 3% to 9%,26 PBM mean dose (Dmean) by 2 Gy, ABM-PBM V5 to V45 by 2% to 14%, and ABM-LSS Dmean by 10 Gy.41 While ABM can be effectively spared at all dose ranges.12,26,41 PBM sparing was limited to low-to-intermediate doses. No dosimetric difference was observed between selective sparing of outer versus inner bone tables based on differing trabecular bone densities.46

BM-sparing capabilities were compared across delivery systems, including linear accelerator-based RT (LINAC), helical tomotherapy, and PT. Yeung et al30 reported that tomotherapy reduced ILI V10 to V20 by 5% to 11% compared with LINAC. Specifically, the median (range) values for tomotherapy versus LINAC were 67 versus 79 (63-75 vs 47-98) Gy for V10 and 56 versus 61 (51-60 vs 22-88) Gy for V20. However, this superior ILI-sparing effect was not observed in a separate tomotherapy study by Joseph et al.31 In contrast, PT has been shown to provide a more substantial reduction in PBM dose compared with LINAC. Pooled median (range) values were 48 versus 89 (37-72 vs 84-96) Gy for V10, 43 versus 73 (39-54 vs 71-75) Gy for V20, and 18 versus 31 (17-23 vs 28-31) Gy for Dmean. These values corresponded to relative percentage reductions of 21% to 44% for V10, 17% to 47% for V20, and 18% to 52% for Dmean.29,33,42,44,50,51 Nevertheless, it should be noted that in certain clinical scenarios, posterior beam arrangements may unintentionally increase LSS V20 to V35.29

Multiple RT planning studies, both comparative and noncomparative, suggested that BM-sparing planning does not compromise target coverage.12,20,21,23,26,29,30,33,4042,48 The use of tomotherapy and PT further enhances target dose homogeneity. Evaluation of surrounding organs at risk reveals distinct sparing characteristics among modalities. IMRT offers comparable or slightly improved sparing.12,20,21,23,26,35,40,48 However, Yeung et al30 reported less favorable coverage of the bladder, femoral heads, peritoneum, and skin with tomotherapy.30 PT effectively reduces low-dose exposure to the small bowel,29,33,42,50 though individual variation exists.33

High-grade toxicity (G3+) was more frequently reported than moderate-grade (G2+) (64% vs 21%). White blood cell counts, absolute neutrophil counts, red blood cell counts, hemoglobin concentration, and platelet counts were more commonly analyzed than lymphocytes. Three studies (11%) used the count differences or ratios to account for baseline variability and temporal changes.32,45,47 Most studies analyzed acute myelosuppressive responses, typically measured by the lowest blood counts (nadir) or the worst hematologic episodes occurring either throughout the entire course of treatment up to 90 days postchemoradiation or within the first 3 weeks of treatment, before the second chemotherapy cycle. Functional imaging studies correlated acute responses with doses accumulated during weeks 1 to 3 of treatment,12,45,47 end of treatment, with late responses reported up to 1 year postradiation.12 None of the included studies examined the molecular response associated with BM hypocellularity.

Radiation dose-response predictors of HT

Thirteen studies developed PBM dose-toxicity models.22,24,27,28,32,34,36, 37, 38, 39, 40,46,49 Seven (54%) found significant associations between low-to-intermediate dose exposure (PBM V5-20) and increased acute G3+ toxicity, with a particularly strong influence on leukopenia prevention (P < .05).22,24,27,28,32,36,40 A Dmean <21 to 27 Gy was associated with a 0% to 10% G3+ toxicity risk.24,46 Meanwhile, the normal tissue complication probability models estimated a TD50 at 30 to 32 Gy with slopes m = 0.11−0.35 and a strong volume effect (n = 1).24,27,28,32 High-dose exposure (>30 Gy) showed borderline or no correlation in two-thirds of studies22,24,32,36, 37, 38,40,49; Based on 7 studies,12,36,37,43,45,47,48 ABM demonstrated a broader range of dose-toxicity prediction across all doses. Functional imaging confirmed ABM radiosensitivity, with an approximately 50% uptake reduction following 4 to 6 Gy pelvic irradiation,12 the most prominent in LSS.45 However, ABM dosimetry did not consistently outperform PBM for toxicity prediction. Rose et al36 and Cheng et al28 found PBM provided stronger predictive performance than ABM or inactive BM, while Franco et al37 and Freese et al43 supported ABM’s superior role. When analyzing the prediction pattern of regional ABM subunits, ABM-LSS aligned with its corresponding outer bone; other ABM subunits did not (Figs. 3A, B). This inconsistency limits the overall predictive reliability and interchangeability of outer bone structures in place of ABM.

Clinical evidence of HT Following BM-sparing and non-BM-sparing IMRT

Of the 35 included studies, 23 reported grades 3 to 4 HT outcomes. Ten of these studies evaluated clinical cohorts treated with BM-sparing IMRT4,12,20,22,25,26,31,36,39,48 and reported HT rates ranging from 25% to 71%, which appeared comparable with the 14% to 86% observed in 13 studies treated without BM-sparing IMRT.24,27,28,32,38,40,43,46,47,49, 50, 51, 52 The wide variability in HT across both groups likely reflects underlying heterogeneity in tumor size, nodal status, and disease stage; study-level covariates are detailed in Appendix E7.

Table 54,12,20,22,25,26,31,36,39,48 provides a detailed overview of the 10 institutions treating a combined 331 patients with online BM-sparing IMRT. All protocols were LINAC-based, except 1 utilizing tomotherapy. The clinical evidence for the benefit of BM-sparing IMRT remains mixed. Based on qualitative conclusions within the reviewed text, 71% (n = 5) of the studies demonstrated significant dosimetric improvement, while 50% (n = 4) reported satisfactory HT mitigation. Overall, toxicity mitigation was more pronounced in ABM-sparing trials than ILI-sparing trials. In an intra-institutional, mixed disease cohort, Liang et al26 observed a >15% reduction in G3+ toxicity rates in the ABM-sparing group relative to the nonsparing group. While direct comparative data for patients with ASCC are lacking, the G3+ HT rates for patients with ASCC reported by Liang et al26 and Arcadipane et al48 fall on the lower end of the historical ranges. Discussion

This systematic review addressed the need for improved understanding of BM-structure definitions and dose-volume-outcome relationships because no explicit BM radiation tolerance doses are defined in the foundational literature, such as the Quantitative Analyses of Normal Tissue Effects in Clinic.54

Our review identified considerable variability in ABM definitions used in BM-sparing IMRT planning. We confirmed that BM-sparing planning can reduce doses to PBM, ABM, and bone subunits. While modifying optimization strategies yields modest dosimetric gain (<10% in PBM and <15% in ABM) compared with changes in treatment delivery modalities, these strategies remain the most practical and cost-effective option for many centers within current resource constraints. However, our review does not provide definitive evidence that BM-sparing IMRT reduces HT. This uncertainty is reflected in both institutional reports from BM-sparing cohorts and the overlapping ranges of HT observed across studies, regardless of whether BM-sparing techniques were implemented. Several recurring themes emerged from the included studies and are discussed below.

Optimization challenges: interplay between target, organ-at-risk, and BM-sparing objectives

Safe implementation of BM-sparing planning requires careful patient selection and prioritization of PTV coverage over organ-at-risk sparing. Because locoregional recurrence remains the predominant pattern of treatment failure,55 maintaining adequate PTV coverage is critical. Tumor location strongly influences the feasibility of BM sparing. For instance, LP-sparing is generally not feasible in perianal tumors because of the high (94%) risk of inguinal nodal involvement.56 Similarly, LSS-sparing may be compromised in cases involving perirectal and presacral nodal disease. Therefore, most studies prioritized target coverage and soft tissue organs at risk over BM constraints. This trade-off drives the need to identify the most clinically relevant BM or ABM subregions to reduce optimization burden. Among the included studies, selective sparing strategies were predominantly implemented in LINAC-based settings, with ILI-sparing emerging as the earliest and most widely adopted approach, owing to its relative anatomic separation from the primary target volume. More recently, interest has shifted toward functional imaging for ABM identification. First introduced by Liang et al,26 this approach has expanded to another 9 studies in less than a decade.

Toward standardizing disease-specific BM definitions and constraints

Standardizing BM definitions is essential for meaningful comparison across dosimetric studies. Early IMRT studies that constrained only the iliac crest demonstrated increased integral dose to the iliac BM, underscoring the importance of accurate ABM delineation for both dosimetric and clinical outcomes. In this review, contouring variability was mostly evident in the LSS, with some studies extending superiorly to L3 to L4 or including the entire sacrococcygeal bone. Given that ASCC is an uncommon disease, these broader contours were often observed in mixed disease cohorts. However, as nodal spread in ASCC rarely extends above the L5 level,56 such superior extensions may be unnecessary. Additionally, the initial LSS contour introduced by Mell et al22 recommended excluding the lower sacrococcygeal bone, likely to minimize optimization conflicts with target coverage. Conversely, our review found that over 60% of studies (particularly more recent ones) contour the entire sacrococcygeal bone for simplicity. For reporting consistency, we recommend defining the LSS as L5 plus the full sacrococcygeal bone, with careful management of overlapping targets during planning. For the LP, we suggest ending contours at the lesser trochanter while considering the total treated pelvic volume when evaluating lymphocyte-related effects.

In the current review, the wide range of reported mean ABM volumes likely reflects both interpatient variability and methodological inconsistencies across functional imaging studies. Technical factors such as scanning time, imaging registration, normalization, and segmentation errors can hinder accurate marrow delineation. Because most studies in this review relied on staging PET scans, ABM may be overestimated because of tumor shine‑through in this highly FDG‑avid disease. Standardized imaging protocols and refined segmentation techniques are needed for reliable ABM quantification in future prospective studies.

Key predictive factors in HT

Consistent with findings in other pelvic malignancies (cervical, rectal, bladder, and prostate),14,24,28,37 our review supports PBM as a parallel organ with a strong low-dose volume effect.14,24,28,32,37,39 PBM, and notably the LSS, showed stronger toxicity prediction than ILI and LP. The strong predictive value of LSS appears more specific to anal cancer, likely owing to its high ABM concentration45,47 and the dose heterogeneity imposed by clinical stage-related changes in this region.41,51 This heterogeneity results in prominent dose and toxicity variation, increasing the likelihood of statistically significant findings.

A PBM threshold dose of 30 Gy has been consistently supported by the literature. Rose et al36 demonstrate that a PBM equivalent uniform dose of 31 Gy strongly correlates with neutropenia nadirs. To mitigate G3+ HT, Lee et al39 recommended sparing at least 750 cm3 of absolute PBM volume, while McGuire et al12 found that irradiated volumes of 25 Gy to 35 Gy highly correlated with chronic decline in BM function. These findings align with historical data showing that radiation of 30 Gy or higher induces irreversible morphologic and physiological marrow changes.6,57 Beyond hematological suppression, exceeding this threshold compromises skeletal integrity; specifically, a sacral dose >30 Gy—particularly to the bilateral sacral alae—has been significantly associated with pelvic insufficiency fractures.58

Our review found ABM dosimetry is a weaker or less consistent toxicity predictor than outer bone contours, despite the observed correlation between ABM volumes—defined by SUV reduction on FDG or FLT-PET scans—and complete blood.12,39,45,47 Possible causes for this discrepancy include interpatient variation, differences in study designs, or an unforeseen interplay between active and inactive BM in a highly cytotoxic chemo-RT environment. ABM concentration and distribution vary by sex, body composition, and physiological conditions.59 For example, females typically have higher concentrations but lower overall absolute volumes of pelvic ABM than males.60 Certain physiological conditions, such as those observed in smokers, long-distance runners, and obese individuals, can induce red marrow reconversion to accommodate tissue hypoxia.61 These patients may experience greater physiological compromise despite a lower dosimetrically calculated percentage of ABM exposure. Thus, population-based constraints are insufficient for accurate toxicity prediction.

Variable tracer uptake and differing study designs can confound ABM quantification and toxicity correlation. Different blood cells exhibit varying levels of glucose uptake throughout their life cycles.62 Murata et al62 found levels of FDG uptake correlated significantly with white blood cell count and absolute neutrophil counts, but not with lymphocytes, red blood cells, or platelets. This difference is likely because of the high metabolic activity of granulocyte progenitors and the abundance of short-lived neutrophils (constituting 49%-65% of hematopoietic cells in BM). Conversely, McGuire et al12 reported FLT-PET scan SUV value changes associated with all blood cell types. These inconsistencies underscore the need for a more comprehensive and standardized approach to ABM dose-toxicity collection and prediction.

In predictive modeling, hematologic variables have been analyzed as both continuous and categorical measures. While G3+ HT is clinically meaningful and requires medical intervention, the real‑world incidence can be masked by the proactive use of supportive care to prevent its onset; incorporating chemotherapy dose reductions or prolonged RT delays—as done by Bazan et al24,27 and Rose et al36—may better capture clinically relevant events. Furthermore, correlating cumulative radiation doses with the timing of blood collection, specifically prior to the second MMC cycle, represents an effective methodological control for temporal and therapeutic confounders. Multiple included studies performed multivariable analysis to control for confounding variables, often narrowing stratification metrics or isolating specific subgroups [eg, HIV infection/immunosuppression24,28,38 or excluding T1 tumor].4,47,49 Synthesizing the multivariable data across these studies reveals that, alongside dosimetric factors, the most significant risk factors for HT—in descending order of reporting frequency—were MMC use, node-positive disease, low body mass index, and female sex.

Incorporating disease-specific goals is imperative to optimize BM-sparing planning for patients receiving ASCC treatments. As illustrated in Fig. 3B, the distinct toxicity prediction patterns observed across ABM, PBM, and bone subunits suggest that their respective dose-volume constraints may not be interchangeable during planning. Additionally, our review highlighted the difference in dosimetry-toxicity correlations between anal and cervical cancers. While cervical cancer literature supports delineating low-density trabecular bone for BM sparing (in the absence of functional imaging),63 studies in anal cancer by Cheng et al28 and Lohynska et al46 found that using the inner bone cavity offered no dosimetric advantages and yielded inferior toxicity prediction to PBM.

BM sparing or lymphocyte sparing

Lymphopenia is increasingly recognized as a key prognostic factor yet remains underreported. Our review noted that severe lymphopenia occurs frequently across both proton and IMRT cohorts. Radiation-induced lymphopenia is highly complex, stemming from irradiation to large blood vessels, lymph nodes, and lymphoid tissues, even with doses as low as 2 to 4 Gy. 12,64 Lymphocytes are primarily distributed across lymph nodes (65%), the spleen (22%), Peyer’s patches (8.5%), and circulatory blood (2.6%).65 The distribution underscores the difficulty of avoiding lymphocyte depletion during standard ASCC fractionation, given the large treatment nodal volumes and the proximity of the lymphocyte-rich gut-associated lymphoid tissue in the distal ileum.

RT technical factors, such as prolonged beam-on time, low-dose rate, and fractionation patterns, further suppress lymphocyte counts and delay recovery. Paradoxically, while proton therapy reduces integral dose and BM exposure, its limited benefit in mitigating HT may be related to a distinct cytotoxic cell-killing pattern and extended beam-on time.64,66 Proton therapy exhibits a denser ionization pathway than photons; accordingly, both in vitro and in vivo studies show higher rates of cellular necrosis, unique DNA repair mechanisms, and possibly greater lymphocyte depletion, particularly during pelvic RT. Furthermore, its prolonged beam-on time may be less favorable for circulating lymphocytes.64 Crucially, the recovery of radiation-induced lymphopenia is mostly dependent on the physiological capability of the BM function.67 Consequently, alongside BM dosimetry, the irradiated nodal volume, integral doses to total blood volume and Gut-Associated Lymphoid Tissue likely interact to drive HT and overall patient outcomes.

Limitations

This review is subject to several limitations, including variability of data sources, reporting bias, and challenges inherent in establishing causal relationships. Because anal cancer is an uncommon disease, several studies contain small sample sizes, collected data over extended periods, or pooled data from multiple institutions. Although this review focused exclusively on anal cancer, some studies involved mixed cancer cohorts, which may have different objectives regarding segmentation and imaging techniques. Additionally, several studies originated from the same institutions; the potential for overlapping patient data may skew findings, a possibility that could not be validated. Dosimetric comparisons between institutions are limited by differences in treatment modalities and reliance on limited dose-volume parameters; drawing broad conclusions from limited metrics can therefore be misleading. Furthermore, the effects of radiation on BM cannot be isolated in the combined therapy setting, and a subset of patients may receive alternative chemotherapy to prevent or manage increasing toxicity risks. Lastly, this review did not assess the formal validity and weighting of study contributions because of the limited number of studies available to derive the findings.

Conclusion

BM is highly sensitive to cytotoxic effects of chemoradiation, making acute myelosuppression a major challenge in ASCC treatment. This review confirmed that BM-sparing planning reduces doses to PBM, ABM, and specific bone subregions, with the greatest reductions observed in proton therapy and within the LSS. We emphasized the importance of limiting low-to-intermediate dose exposure to PBM and the LSS, with suggested constraints of V10 <85% and V20 <65% to mitigate severe HT, with accumulating evidence supporting prioritizing LSS over PBM and PBM over ABM or the inner medullary cavity. Minimizing irradiation volumes receiving ≥30 Gy may further preserve long-term BM integrity.

Given the multifactorial nature of HT, there is a need to develop and validate ASCC-specific predictive models incorporating patient factors and comprehensive HT metrics. Monitoring differential leukocyte counts is essential for assessing neutropenia and lymphopenia, the most clinically significant toxicities during treatment. While current evidence focuses on acute effects, chronic BM injury and recovery dynamics warrant further longitudinal study.

Although this review did not establish a definitive clinical benefit of BM-sparing IMRT for HT reduction, the radiobiological rationale remains strong. In line with the ALARA principle, protecting the radiosensitive hematopoietic microenvironment is critical. We therefore support the routine integration of BM-sparing strategies in clinical practice and research, alongside systematic evaluation of their therapeutic impact.
