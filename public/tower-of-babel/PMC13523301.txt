Knowledge gaps in pressure injury, medical device–related pressure injury, and skin tear among intensive care unit nurses
Authors: Öznur Erbay Dalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntensive care unit (ICU) patients are at high risk for pressure injuries (PIs), medical device-related pressure injuries (MDRPIs), and skin tears (STs). Although nurses play a key role in preventing all three conditions, existing research has mainly focused on PIs, with limited evidence addressing nurses’ combined knowledge related to MDRPIs and STs. In particular, shared deficiencies across these conditions remain unclear. This study aimed to assess ICU nurses’ knowledge of PIs, MDRPIs, and STs together to identify overlapping knowledge gaps that may affect skin integrity management in ICU settings.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted with 283 ICU nurses. Data were collected using three validated tools: the Pressure Ulcer Knowledge Assessment Tool 2.0 (PUKAT 2.0), the Medical Device–Related Pressure Injury Knowledge Assessment Tool (MDRPI-KAT), and the Skin Tear Knowledge Assessment Instrument (OASES), enabling construct-specific measurement across related conditions.ResultsKnowledge levels varied across domains, with mean total scores of 14.44 ± 3.43 (PUKAT 2.0), 8.73 ± 1.83 (MDRPI-KAT), and 10.17 ± 2.44 (OASES). Across all three tools, prevention consistently emerged as the lowest-scoring domain. Higher knowledge scores were observed among nurses with greater professional experience, postgraduate education, and prior care experience related to STs.ConclusionsThe findings provide a comprehensive overview of ICU nurses’ knowledge regarding PIs, MDRPIs, and STs, highlighting variability across domains and consistently lower scores in prevention-related areas. These results underscore the need for targeted educational strategies to strengthen knowledge across key aspects of skin integrity management in intensive care settings. However, findings should be interpreted with caution due to potential sampling bias related to social media-based recruitment and the absence of multivariable analysis.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Nurses, Critical care nurses, Pressure injuries, Medical device-related pressure injuries, Skin tears, Knowledge

Background

Maintaining skin integrity is a critical component of quality care in the intensive care unit (ICU). Patients in these settings are highly vulnerable to skin injury due to immobility, invasive procedures, and complex medical conditions [1, 2]. Pressure injuries (PIs), medical device related pressure injuries (MDRPIs), and skin tears (STs) represent the most common and clinically significant threats to skin integrity in critically ill patients [3–5].

PIs remain highly prevalent in ICUs and are associated with prolonged hospitalization and increased risk of adverse outcomes, including mortality [6]. Their development is influenced by multiple interrelated factors, including sustained pressure and shear forces, immobility, impaired perfusion, medication use, nutritional deficits, excessive skin moisture, and prolonged ICU length of stay [7]. MDRPIs arise from prolonged pressure or friction exerted by medical devices, including endotracheal tubes, catheters, and masks, and constitute a substantial proportion of hospital acquired pressure injuries, particularly among mechanically ventilated patients and those exposed to multiple devices [8–10]. Similarly, STs, although often underrecognized, are frequently observed in ICU populations, especially among older adults and patients with fragile or dry skin, and are commonly related to mechanical trauma, friction, and reduced tissue tolerance [5, 11]. Importantly, despite differences in clinical presentation, PIs, MDRPIs, and STs share overlapping risk factors and prevention mechanisms. Sustained pressure, friction or shear forces, immobility, medical device use, and compromised skin integrity contribute to the development of all three conditions [6, 8, 10]. Consequently, effective prevention depends largely on nurses’ knowledge, vigilance, and timely implementation of prevention-focused interventions rather than on general care quality alone.

Despite advances in clinical guidelines and prevention strategies, these skin injuries remain widespread in ICU settings. Prevention efforts are often challenged by insufficient training, heavy workloads, and gaps in interdisciplinary collaboration [12, 13]. While PIs have long been the focus of prevention programs and nursing education, the distinct characteristics and increasing clinical relevance of MDRPIs and STs have only recently begun to receive greater attention in the literature [5, 13, 14]. Their limited emphasis in institutional protocols and educational curricula may contribute to persistent knowledge gaps, particularly in prevention focused domains that are essential for early risk identification and mitigation [15, 16].

Nurses serve as the frontline defense in safeguarding skin integrity through continuous bedside presence, systematic skin assessment, appropriate medical device management, and the implementation of evidence-based prevention measures [17]. However, inadequate or fragmented knowledge across different types of skin injuries may hinder these efforts, resulting in delayed recognition and missed prevention opportunities in complex ICU environments [18, 19]. Although PIs, MDRPIs, and STs frequently coexist in clinical practice and present overlapping prevention-related challenges, existing research has predominantly examined nurses’ knowledge of PIs in isolation, with considerably fewer studies addressing MDRPIs or STs, and almost none assessing all three conditions simultaneously using validated tools. Accordingly, this study aimed to provide a combined assessment of ICU nurses’ knowledge and prevention gaps related to PIs, MDRPIs, and STs, and to explore factors associated with their knowledge levels.

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional design using an online survey platform, which was appropriate for assessing nurses’ knowledge levels and associated factors at a single point in time. The reporting process was guided by the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) [20]. Due to the open recruitment design, certain CHERRIES-recommended metrics (e.g., view rate, participation rate, and completion rate) could not be determined.

Sample, setting and recruitment

Data were collected through a web-based survey distributed to ICU nurses working in hospital-based intensive care units across Türkiye. Following ethical approval, the researcher contacted the administrator of “Nurse Turkey,” a widely followed social media platform focused on nursing-related information and professional support. The survey link was disseminated through the platform’s social media channels, including Instagram posts and WhatsApp groups commonly used by ICU nurses for communication and professional exchange. As recruitment was conducted via social media, participation may have been more likely among younger nurses or those who are more professionally engaged and digitally active. Participation was voluntary, and a convenience sampling approach was employed.

Eligibility criteria included current employment in a hospital-based ICU and a minimum of one year of ICU work experience. Information regarding study eligibility and informed consent was provided on the first page of the online survey. In addition, ICU work experience was assessed using the Nurse Information Form to confirm eligibility. All respondents met the inclusion criteria, and no exclusions were required based on these criteria.

Data collection procedures and instruments

Following ethical approval, data were collected using an online survey administered via the Google Forms platform. The survey link was distributed through the Nurse Turkey social media platform to reach eligible ICU nurses. The principal investigator had no direct interaction with participants throughout the data collection process, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with data confidentiality and privacy standards.

Informed consent was obtained electronically on the first page of the survey, which provided information regarding the study purpose, eligibility criteria, instruments used, estimated completion time, and data protection procedures. Only participants who provided consent were allowed to proceed to the survey. To enhance data completeness and methodological rigor, all survey items were configured as mandatory, thereby preventing incomplete submissions.

The survey remained open for 35 days between October 01 and November 04, 2024. No incentives, reminders, or additional recruitment strategies were used in order to minimize potential participation bias. Data were collected using the following instruments: (a) Nurse Information Form (NIF), (b) Pressure Ulcer Knowledge Assessment Tool Updated Version (PUKAT 2.0), (c) Medical Device Related Pressure Injury Knowledge Assessment Tool (MDRPI-KAT), and (d) Skin Tear Knowledge Assessment Instrument (OASES).

Nurse Information Form (NIF)

The Nurse Information Form collected demographic and professional variables that were considered potentially associated with knowledge scores, including age, gender, educational level, years of ICU experience, type of ICU, prior training related to skin integrity, and previous clinical experience with PIs, MDRPIs, and STs [15–19].

Pressure Ulcer Knowledge Assessment Tool Updated Version (PUKAT 2.0)

The PUKAT 2.0 was developed to assess nurses’ knowledge of PI care and prevention based on current evidence-based guidelines [21]. It includes 25 multiple-choice items covering six domains: etiology, classification and observation, risk assessment, nutrition, prevention, and specific patient groups. Each correct answer is scored as 1, while incorrect or unanswered items are scored as 0; the total score ranges from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. The instrument does not define standardized cut-off values for categorizing knowledge levels. The original version demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties (Intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.69), while the Turkish version showed high test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.83) and acceptable internal consistency (KR-20 = 0.72) [21, 22]. In the present study, internal consistency was found to be 0.78.

Medical Device-Related Pressure Injury Knowledge Assessment Tool (MDRPI-KAT)

The MDRPI-KAT, developed by Dalli & Girgin (2023), was designed to measure nurses’ knowledge of MDRPIs based on current guidelines and expert consensus [23]. The instrument consists of 16 multiple-choice items across six domains: characterization and etiology, classification and observation, selection and suitability of devices, skin assessment, prevention, and specific patient groups. Each correct answer is scored as 1, incorrect or unanswered responses as 0, with a total score range of 0–16; higher scores reflect greater knowledge. No established thresholds are provided by the developers to classify knowledge levels as adequate or inadequate. The tool demonstrated strong psychometric properties in its original validation study (ICC = 0.82; KR-20 = 0.77) [23]. In the present study, internal consistency was found to be 0.81.

Skin Tear Knowledge Assessment Instrument (OASES)

The OASES was developed by Van Tiggelen et al. (2021) to assess nurses’ knowledge of STs prevention and management based on current evidence-based guidelines [24]. It consists of 20 multiple-choice items organized into six domains: etiology, classification and observation, risk assessment, prevention, treatment, and specific patient groups. Each correct response is scored as 1, while incorrect or unanswered responses are scored as 0; total scores range from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. The instrument does not include predefined cut-off points for determining categorical knowledge levels. In the original study, the tool demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties (ICC = 0.83; CVI = 0.90–1.00) [24]. The Turkish version showed strong content validity (S-CVI = 0.97), excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.90), and acceptable internal consistency (KR-20 = 0.78) [25]. In this study, internal consistency reliability was estimated at 0.75.

Following the modified Bloom’s cut-off points commonly used in knowledge-test studies, percentage scores were interpreted descriptively as good (75%–100%), moderate (50%–74%), and poor (< 50%) [26, 27]. These categories were used for descriptive interpretation only and do not represent validated or instrument-specific competency thresholds.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed on SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) for Windows 29.0 package. Normality was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values, with values between − 2 and + 2 considered acceptable for a normal distribution, along with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test [28]. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage) were used to summarize participants’ sociodemographic and other characteristics.

Nonparametric tests, including the Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and Spearman correlation analysis, were used due to the distributional characteristics of the knowledge scores and the discrete nature of summed scores derived from binary responses. These tests were applied to examine associations between total and domain scores of PUKAT 2.0, MDRPI-KAT, and OASES and participants’ sociodemographic, professional, organizational, and educational characteristics. Effect sizes were calculated as r for Mann–Whitney U tests and η² for Kruskal–Wallis tests to assess the magnitude of observed differences. Knowledge test responses were coded as binary variables (1 = correct, 0 = incorrect). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Approximately one quarter reported having an ICU nursing certification. Nurses were mainly employed in public or university hospitals, most commonly working in general, anesthesiology, and medical ICUs, and the majority were employed in closed ICU organizations. The most frequently reported nurse-to-patient ratio was 1:2.

Patterns of awareness, training, and clinical experience differed markedly across injury types. Awareness and exposure were highest for PIs, followed by MDRPIs, and were lowest for STs. Nearly all nurses reported having cared for patients with PIs, whereas exposure to MDRPIs was lower and exposure to STs was substantially less frequent. Institutional training was the most commonly reported source of education across all injury types; however, perceived adequacy of training was limited. Monitoring and documentation practices showed a similar gradient, with PIs recorded almost universally, while MDRPIs and STs were documented considerably less often. Nurses’ monthly estimates also reflected this distribution, with PIs perceived as commonly encountered and STs as relatively infrequent. The Braden Scale was the most frequently used tool for PI risk assessment.

Knowledge levels were evaluated based on the proportion of correct responses relative to the total possible scores. Variability was observed across knowledge domains, with prevention consistently representing the lowest-performing domain across PIs, MDRPIs, and STs, whereas domains related to risk assessment, device suitability, and specific patient groups demonstrated relatively higher correct response rates.

Comparison of knowledge scores by ICU nurses’ characteristics

Subgroup comparisons are presented in Table 3. Only a limited number of nurse characteristics were significantly associated with knowledge scores. For PUKAT 2.0, nurses with more than five years of ICU experience had higher total scores than those with 1–5 years of experience (p = 0.024, r = 0.13). For the MDRPI-KAT, total scores differed significantly according to education level (p = 0.044, η² = 0.09), with postgraduate nurses having the highest mean scores. For the OASES, total scores were significantly associated with both education level (p = 0.007, η² = 0.12) and prior experience caring for patients with STs (p = 0.039, r = 0.12), with higher mean scores observed among postgraduate nurses and among those with prior ST care experience. No significant associations were identified for the other sociodemographic, professional, organizational, or workplace variables.

Discussion

This study comprehensively evaluated ICU nurses’ knowledge of PIs, MDRPIs, and STs using validated tools, showing varying levels of knowledge and persistent gaps, particularly in prevention domains. By examining all three conditions simultaneously, the study offers a broader perspective on shared challenges in skin integrity care that have rarely been addressed in previous research. Importantly, the consistent pattern of relatively lower scores in prevention-related domains across all three conditions suggests that knowledge gaps may not solely reflect individual deficits; however, this interpretation remains speculative, as system-level factors were not directly assessed in the present study.

Across PIs, ICU nurses demonstrated relatively stronger knowledge in domains related to risk assessment and classification, whereas prevention and nutrition were comparatively weaker. Similar domain-level patterns have been reported in previous studies. Guerrero et al. (2023) identified low performance in prevention, nutrition, and risk assessment domains despite relatively better scores in etiology and classification [29], and Klaas and Serebro (2024) similarly reported lower performance in prevention-related items [17]. These findings are clinically important, as prevention is considered the cornerstone of PI management and a substantial proportion of PIs are regarded as preventable [30]. Inadequate nutritional knowledge is also concerning given the established role of poor nutritional status in impaired tissue tolerance and delayed wound healing [31]. Beyond individual knowledge, these patterns may reflect broader contextual or organizational factors in the ICU, such as workload and time constraints that limit the consistent application of preventive practices; however, such interpretations should be approached with caution, as these factors were not directly examined in this study. In the Turkish healthcare context, nurses’ involvement in nutrition-related decision-making and structured interdisciplinary prevention teams is often limited, which may further constrain the routine application of nutrition-focused risk mitigation. Educational interventions, particularly simulation-based and guideline-oriented programs, have been associated with improvements in nurses’ knowledge and prevention-related behaviors, and with reductions in PI incidence in previous studies [32–34]. Moreover, interdisciplinary collaboration involving nurses, dietitians, and physicians has been associated with improved prevention outcomes [35, 36], supporting the importance of both educational and system-level strategies.

For MDRPIs, ICU nurses demonstrated varying levels of knowledge, consistent with prior studies reporting comparable correct response rates [37–41]. Knowledge related to device selection and suitability was relatively stronger, whereas prevention-related knowledge appeared comparatively lower [37–39]. MDRPIs differ from conventional PIs in their pathophysiology and anatomical distribution, as they are caused by pressure or friction from medical devices and frequently involve less visible or mucosal sites [9, 10]. This clinical profile can hinder early recognition and make prevention more dependent on structured routines (e.g., scheduled device repositioning, fit checks, and skin surveillance around device contact points). Patients exposed to medical devices have been shown to have a higher risk of PI development [42], and MDRPIs account for a substantial proportion of hospital-acquired PIs in acute care settings [8, 43]. These findings suggest that targeted MDRPI-focused education and integration of device-specific protocols into routine care may be associated with improvements in knowledge and prevention practices [44].

For STs, ICU nurses demonstrated variable levels of knowledge overall, consistent with earlier studies reporting similar score ranges [16, 45, 46]. Knowledge was relatively stronger in domains related to specific patient groups, while prevention-related knowledge appeared comparatively lower [16, 45]. STs remain underrecognized in clinical practice despite their high incidence and association with adverse outcomes [14, 47, 48]. Although international studies similarly report gaps in ST-related knowledge, this disparity may be more pronounced in settings where ST-specific guidelines and educational opportunities are limited; however, such contextual factors were not directly evaluated in this study. In practice, ST prevention requires early risk recognition and consistent application of skin protection strategies, which can be difficult to sustain without unit-level protocols. Supporting this, McTigue et al. (2009) reported that targeted educational programs were associated with significant improvements in nurses’ knowledge across multiple domains [49].

Overall, the convergence of findings across PIs, MDRPIs, and STs highlights shared gaps in prevention-related knowledge and underscores the need for comprehensive interventions that address both individual knowledge and the clinical systems that support preventive care. Although several subgroup differences reached statistical significance, the associated effect sizes were consistently small, indicating limited practical or clinical relevance. These findings may also be interpreted through knowledge-to-practice and behavioral frameworks, which emphasize that knowledge alone is insufficient to drive clinical practice change, and that effective implementation depends on multiple interacting factors, including individual, organizational, and contextual influences. Rather than focusing solely on knowledge deficits, future efforts may benefit from combining guideline-based education with unit-level implementation strategies (e.g., protocol integration, interdisciplinary pathways, and routine auditing/feedback) to to support improvements in preventive practices related to skin integrity.

Implications for nursing practice and further research

The findings suggest the need to strengthen ICU nurses’ knowledge and clinical competencies regarding PIs, MDRPIs, and STs, particularly in prevention-related domains. The consistently low prevention scores underscore the importance of revising nursing curricula and institutional protocols to place greater emphasis on prevention-focused strategies, particularly for underrecognized injuries such as STs.

Prevention-focused educational approaches should move beyond didactic training and incorporate practical, application-oriented methods. These may include simulation-based training scenarios addressing common ICU-related skin integrity risks, device-specific prevention checklists to support routine assessment and repositioning, and case-based workshops focusing on early identification and prevention of MDRPIs and STs. Integrating structured skin assessment tools and standardized documentation templates into daily workflows may further support consistent prevention practices across injury types. In the Turkish healthcare context, limited interdisciplinary collaboration and the lack of structured, standardized training programs may contribute to gaps in prevention. Strengthening collaboration between nurses, physicians, wound care specialists, and other relevant disciplines, alongside the development of institution-specific prevention protocols, could support the translation of knowledge into practice and may contribute to improved patient safety. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of targeted prevention-focused educational interventions, such as simulation programs and checklist-based approaches, and examine their potential impact on both nurses’ knowledge retention and clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between nurses’ knowledge levels and their clinical behaviors; therefore, observed associations cannot be interpreted as indicating directionality or effect. In addition, because the study was primarily descriptive, it was not designed to examine complex correlational or mediating pathways among demographic, professional, and workplace variables, which may have limited the identification of more nuanced relationships influencing knowledge levels. Second, knowledge was assessed using self-administered multiple-choice questionnaires, which may introduce response bias. Nurses may overestimate their knowledge due to social desirability or self-perception, and self-reported responses may not fully reflect actual clinical performance or the consistent application of prevention practices in real-world ICU settings. Third, data were collected at a single time point, providing only a snapshot of nurses’ knowledge. Given that knowledge evolves with ongoing education, clinical exposure, and institutional changes, the findings may not capture temporal variations or learning trajectories. Fourth, the use of an open, social media–based recruitment strategy without a predefined sampling frame prevented calculation of a response rate and may have introduced selection bias. These methodological constraints substantially limit the external validity of the study and restrict the generalizability of the findings to the broader ICU nursing population. Participants may have been more digitally engaged, younger, or more professionally motivated or interested in the topic than the broader ICU nursing population, potentially limiting representativeness and generalizability. Fifth, as the instruments do not provide validated competency cut-off scores, knowledge levels were interpreted descriptively; accordingly, the use of modified Bloom’s cut-off points should be understood as a pragmatic approach for reporting and comparison rather than as an indicator of adequate versus inadequate competency. Although the instruments have established validity and reliability, additional psychometric analyses were not conducted in this sample, as the primary aim was not to re-evaluate their measurement properties. Sixth, statistical analyses were primarily bivariate; therefore, potential confounding could not be fully controlled and independent predictors of knowledge could not be definitively identified. In addition, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed; therefore, given the number of statistical tests conducted, there is an increased risk of Type I error inflation, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Seventh, because all survey items were mandatory, some participants may have provided guesses for certain knowledge items, which may have inflated scores and introduced a potential threat to the internal validity of the findings. Eighth, although organizational and workplace characteristics were collected, their integration into the analysis remained limited within the scope of the design and analytic approach, which may have contributed to a greater emphasis on individual-level knowledge patterns. Ninth, although prevention mechanisms overlap across PIs, MDRPIs, and STs, knowledge was assessed separately using condition-specific validated tools; therefore, an integrated or composite measure of shared prevention knowledge could not be examined. Finally, as the study was conducted among ICU nurses in Türkiye, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other countries with different healthcare systems, educational frameworks, staffing models, or clinical protocols. Future studies employing longitudinal or interventional designs and incorporating objective measures of clinical performance, as well as multivariable analytic approaches, are warranted to better assess how nurses’ knowledge translates into prevention-related practice and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

By integrating the assessment of nurses’ knowledge on PIs, MDRPIs, and STs within a single analytical framework, this study contributes to a more holistic understanding of skin integrity management in intensive care settings. Rather than addressing these conditions in isolation, the integrated approach highlights shared prevention-related challenges and underscores the importance of unified strategies for skin integrity care. The findings of this study can provide a practical evidence base to support the refinement of nursing care protocols, inform the focus of in-service training programs, and contribute to the ongoing updating of clinical guidelines aimed at improving skin integrity management and patient safety in intensive care settings. Collectively, this integrated perspective supports the alignment of education, clinical practice, and quality improvement efforts toward prevention-focused and patient-centered intensive care.
