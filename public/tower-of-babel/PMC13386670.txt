Walking the talk for dementia: building social networks and collaborations
Authors: Walter D Dawson, Jenn Reed, Fernando Aguzzoli-Peres, Kate Irving, Sherril B Gelmon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDementia is one of the greatest global health challenges requiring multimodal efforts to address its impact on people and societies. Walking the Talk for Dementia (WTD) is a unique global initiative designed to challenge the stigma that surrounds dementia, foster intergenerational dialogue, build a community, and inspire a renewed sense of purpose among participants. A social network analysis (SNA) was conducted to provide greater detail and contextualization of the connections made during WTD and insights into the collaborations that arose from this immersive event which may influence dementia policy, care, and services.MethodsA robust, comprehensive, and minimally intrusive evaluation was created in 2024 to capture the results, outcomes, and impacts of WTD 2024. This mixed methods research examined the experiences of the WTD 2024 participants, 79 of whom consented to participate in this research. Pre- and post-surveys collected quantitative and qualitative data (94% and 87% response rates), and were augmented by 95 written, audio, or video reflections. To further investigate the social network of the larger WTD community, a follow-up survey was sent six months after WTD 2024 to the 2024 participants plus the additional 40 participants from WTD 2023. Quantitative survey data were analyzed with descriptive and inferential statistics; qualitative data were analyzed using content analysis to identify core aspects and common themes of the experience. A social network analysis (SNA) was used to visualize and quantify connections between participants of WTD.ResultsResults from the SNA analyses illustrate the development of multiple new connections and collaborations among WTD participants. The results also point to how connections and collaborations among participants that existed prior to WTD 2024 may have changed as a result of WTD.ConclusionsConnections appear to have increased post-WTD. A community developed among WTD participants, who are a multi-disciplinary, global group of professionals and people with lived experience of dementia. This analysis provides evidence of the benefits of social connections for individuals living with dementia and their care partners with other dementia advocates. These findings are important to future advancement of dementia awareness, advocacy, and policy change.Keywords: Dementia, Implementation science, Social network analysis, Lived experience, Health policy, Collaborations, Community, Lived experience

Contributions to the literature

• Advances an understanding of the different roles played within the WTD social network by expertise.

• Provides a better understanding of variation in collaborations across geography of the global dementia network.

• Social network analysis offers a better understanding of the contribution of people with lived experience to dementia networks.

• Offers insight into how an intensive, weeklong experience can seed robust ongoing interpersonal networks.

Background

Dementia is one of the foremost global health challenges with an estimated prevalence of 57.4 million worldwide increasing to 152.8 million by the year 2050 [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and many other entities have identified dementia as a top public health priority due to the multidimensional individual and societal impacts of dementia across communities, cultures, and geographies [2–4]. Given the scale and impact of dementia worldwide, there is an urgent need to foster global collaborations that can improve outcomes for people living with dementia, as well as care and support [5]. These collaborations should take a multidisciplinary, multinational approach, drawing on multiple perspectives, backgrounds, and geographies to support collective efforts to address dementia. These collaborations must also engage people with lived experience of dementia, including people living with dementia as well as their care partners, as this inclusive approach can reduce stigma surrounding dementia, improve outcomes, and advance a person-centered strategy for policy and practice [6, 7]. These types of efforts are increasing yet remain an underutilized approach to more inclusive dementia care, advocacy, and research.

Walking the Talk for Dementia (WTD) is a unique global initiative conceptually grounded in the principles of meaningful engagement, co-creation, and collaboration [8, 9], which are recognized as beneficial in dementia care and advocacy [10, 11]. This multi-day event integrates a four-day, 40 km walk along the Camino de Santiago in Northern Spain with a two-day symposium and multiple less structured events, creating an immersive space to reflect on the physical, behavioral, and societal dimensions of living with dementia. WTD prioritizes diverse, multicultural, and multistakeholder engagement, and elevates the voices of people living with dementia, care partners, artists, and advocates while promoting inclusive and sustainable global solutions for brain health. This approach stimulates the integration of professional and lived experiences, fostering a collaborative platform to address stigma, increase awareness, and build a community [12].

Understanding the scope and scale of the connections and collaborations that develop through participation in WTD is essential for assessing the potential of WTD to bring about change in dementia care and support, research, and policy arrangements. Expanded and more deeply connected networks can help facilitate coordinated advocacy for policy changes as well as clearer channels for the exchange of ideas for novel collaboration [13] within dementia care and support, research, and policy arrangements. A comprehensive mixed methods study was developed and conducted (see Gelmon et al., 2026) [8] to map and track over time the connections and collaborations established as a result of WTD participation. This manuscript presents the results of a social network analysis (SNA) which was conducted to provide greater detail and contextualization of the connections made during WTD and insight into the collaborations that arose which may influence dementia policy, care, and services. By using data from WTD in 2024 along with participant data from 2023, the analysis sought to assess aggregate community that may have developed across both years of this event. This understanding of how social networks impact dementia can support better dementia care within health systems.

Literature review

Several theories informed the design of this research on WTD. These draw on well-established theoretical frameworks including implementation science and social network analysis.

Consolidated framework for implementation research framework

One of the most widely used models within implementation science, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), informs this work [14]. CFIR was developed to better understand the influence of contextual factors on intervention implementation within complex and interacting environmental systems [14]. CFIR includes five domains: intervention characteristics, the outer setting, the inner setting, participant characteristics, and the implementation process [14–16]. The model helps to understand the complexity of WTD: the weeklong event (intervention), the world of dementia services, advocacy, and policy (outer setting), the WTD community (inner setting), participant roles and experiences (characteristics), and the specific elements of WTD planning and execution (implementation process). The CFIR model is used to predict or explain the barriers to and facilitators of implementation effectiveness [17].

The Camino de Santiago

The Camino de Santiago is a historic, popular pilgrimage route that concludes in northern Spain [18]. The impact of walking the Camino is spiritual and has transformative aftereffects [18], and many people walk the Camino for “authentic encounters with themselves and others” (p. 10) [19]. WTD convenes people living with dementia, care partners, artists, advocates, innovators, clinicians, policy influencers, and researchers in a multidisciplinary, intergenerational, and cross-cultural gathering. These identities are all important players within the wider dementia continuum, and some of them are less common at dementia-related conferences, thus they are highlighted to illustrate the unique dynamics of the WTD community. The experience of walking part of the Camino enables a space for knowledge exchange, community building, and reflection outside of professional, academic, and/or daily life.

Social movements

Given the aims of WTD, the social movements literature is applicable to understanding this work. Social movements seek to bring about changes in society. There are multiple historical examples of social movements that have resulted in societal and policy change, including several focused on health [20]. Examples include addressing environmental conditions and health and disability rights. Social movements build relationships among people, mobilize those individuals, and ultimately organize participants to bring about changes to society [21]. Understanding the depth and breadth of those relationships is also important for fostering successful social movements. Many WTD participants view it as an emerging social movement.

Social network analysis

Social network analysis is used to understand relationships within social networks using quantitative analysis and graphical visualizations [22]. Social network analysis evolved from the fields of mathematics, sociology, psychology, and psychiatry [23–25]. Early studies using social network analysis ranged from examining role-taking within families to the effects of urbanization on networks [24]. Within health services research, social network analysis has been used to understand the transmission of HIV, the diffusion of innovations amongst physicians, and the connections among social services organizations [23].

Within the field of dementia research, social network analysis has been used to study the networks of people living with dementia, dementia care partners, healthcare workers, and organizations providing services to people living with dementia [26–28]. Studies examining different types of roles within dementia and dementia care are unusual. A systematic review of social network analyses in long-term care of people living with dementia found one study linking the social networks of residents and staff; all other analyses focused exclusively on one population [29]. In spite of these single population studies, an analysis of social media posts on Twitter by dementia caregivers found that caregivers re-tweeted posts from a wide variety of users, including media outlets, clinicians, community organizations, and lay people [30]. Social networks of care partners of people living with dementia and of American Alzheimer Disease Centers tend to have high density [26, 31], that is, each person or center within a network is connected to nearly every other person or center within the network. On the other hand, the social networks of organizations providing services to people living with dementia tend to have low density [27, 32]. Understanding the characteristics of social networks is important because it provides information on the potential performance of the network, such as the ability to diffuse information or engage in collective action [33].

While social network analyses related to dementia focused on just one or two types of node (care partners, organizations providing services, etc.) within a single country, the true picture of dementia care and services is much more complex: people living with dementia receive care from informal care partners, paid caregivers, and healthcare providers; academics conduct research with both healthcare systems and dyads of people living with dementia and their care partners; and service organizations use the skills of advocates and communicators to perform their work. Connections and collaborations among dementia-interested parties are inherent within dementia care, and WTD provided a unique opportunity to examine a global social network with participants with varying roles with regard to dementia.

Methods

The aim of this study was to document existing and planned collaborations among WTD participants from 2023 to 2024. A robust, comprehensive evaluation was designed in 2024 to capture the results, outcomes, and impacts of WTD 2024. This mixed methods research used a convergent parallel design [34] to examine the experiences of the 80 WTD 2024 participants, 79 of whom consented to participate in this research. Table 1 presents demographic characteristics of the participants. A survey administered one week after WTD asked participants about the WTD social network by inviting them to identify the strength of their connections with other participants whose names they recognized – a personal connection, plans to collaborate, current collaboration(s), and completed collaboration(s). These levels of connection amongst WTD participants are not mutually exclusive, rather participants selected the level they felt best represented the connection. Participants also responded to qualitative questions about steps taken related to collaborations and influencing policy or practice. Pre- and post-surveys collected quantitative and qualitative data (94% and 87% response rates), and were augmented by 95 written, audio, or video reflections. These reflections were accepted up to two weeks post-WTD.

To further investigate the social network of the larger WTD community, a follow-up survey was sent six months after WTD 2024 to the 2024 participants plus the additional 40 participants from WTD 2023. A question about social networks nearly identical to that asked in the previous surveys was included (“I have previously collaborated with this person”), as were qualitative questions about steps taken related to collaborations and influencing policy or practice. This follow-up survey also included a question that included a list of specific collaborative activities, with response choices reflecting scope of contacts (few/many) and breadth of connections (small number/many). Twenty-three participants from WTD 2023, 35 participants from WTD 2024, and seven participants from both years responded, resulting in a response rate for the follow-up survey of 55%.

Written consent to participate in this study was acquired from all research participants prior to the start of this research. The potential study population included the 80 participants who participated in the full week of WTD activities. Individuals who only attended the symposium at the end of the week were excluded from the study population. All participants were furnished with a Human Research Protections Program-approved research information and consent form, either via email in advance of WTD or in print form on arrival, and were asked to sign the form. The research team co-leaders were available on site and by email to answer any questions participants had about the consent process. In addition, 40 participants from WTD 2023 who did not attend in 2024 were invited by email to participate in the study and were sent a research information/consent form in English or Spanish; 28 consented to participate. The survey instruments are available upon request.

Quantitative survey data were analyzed without adjustment using R [35]; qualitative data were analyzed using content analysis to identify core aspects and common themes of the experience. Some degree of interpretation was needed when creating themes and for determining their relationship with the research questions [36]. Coding of reflections and survey responses was conducted by pairs of research team members, utilizing Microsoft Excel for the initial coding and also for the refining of themes. This coding used a set of previously identified indicators in an overall conceptual matrix created to guide the WTD 2024 research [8]. A member of the research team initially coded the relevant statements for themes, followed by a second coder to establish inter-coder reliability [37]. Any discrepancies in coding were reviewed by the full research team and consensus was sought on the definition of a code or several codes. Coding discussions were not framed around accuracy but rather to check ideas and explore assumptions or interpretations of the data. Quotes included in this manuscript were selected to illustrate broader themes and are presented without attribution to individuals; they are identified with the participant identification number, role, and data source (pre-survey, reflection, post-survey, or follow-up survey).

Social network analysis was used to visualize and quantify connections between participants of WTD. Nodes within the social networks represent individual WTD participants; edges represent the collaborations participants reported having with other participants. In the figures presented subsequently, nodes are color-coded by their role or region, and edges are color-coded by the type of collaboration reported. Role and region were used as identifiers throughout the larger WTD study given the emphasis WTD places on an inclusive community that reflects multiple roles related to dementia and a global focus. To reduce the risk of potential identification of participants, two different figure layouts were used to visualize changes in the network by role and region (Fruchterman-Reingold and circular, respectively); each layout presents the same network but in differing formats.

For each time point, we report network density and centrality measures for WTD participants. Density is “the number of connections that are actually made [between nodes] as opposed to all conceivable connections” (p. 4) [22]. Degree centrality describes the number of edges a node is linked with and betweenness centrality measures the extent to which a node serves as a bridge on the shortest route between two other nodes [22]. The network is directed, in that a connection between nodes may be unidirectional (one participant reported being connected to another, but not vice versa) or bidirectional (participants mutually reported connections to each other). Directionality is not shown within the figures due to the large number of edges between nodes. Social network analysis and creation of social network figures were performed using R [35] packages “igraph” [38] and “ggraph” [39].

The study protocols were reviewed and approved by the Portland State University Human Research Protections Program (IRB #626); this approval included standard procedures for anonymity/confidentiality as relevant; use of participant identification numbers to blind names; protection of personal information; storage of data in password-protected files; destruction of recordings after transcription; and secure maintenance of all study data for required time periods. Additional detail regarding the methods is available in Gelmon et al. (2026) [8].

Results

Results from the analyses illustrate the development of multiple new connections and collaborations among WTD participants. The results also point to how connections and collaborations among participants that existed prior to WTD 2024 may have changed as a result of participation.

Connections before WTD 2024

The network of existing connections prior to WTD 2024 is shown in Fig. 1. This network has a low density (0.0784), indicating that a small proportion of all possible connections between nodes (participants) are present in this network. Centrality measures by role are displayed in Table 2. Degree centrality ranged from 1 to 119 (M = 11.5, SD = 15.2) indicating that participants had an average of just 11.5 direct connections to other participants before WTD 2024. Participants with the role of Academic had the highest degree centrality, which can be seen in Fig. 1 by those nodes (participants) having the greatest number of edges emanating from them. Betweenness centrality ranged from 0 to 3201 (M = 59.0, SD = 374.7). Participants with roles of People Living with Dementia and Advocate had the highest betweenness centrality, which can be seen as Fig. 1 by those nodes serving as bridges between otherwise unconnected nodes. These roles were the most likely to serve as the connection between participants that were not directly connected. Care Partners had the lowest degree centrality and betweenness centrality, meaning these roles were both least directly connected to other participants and least likely to serve as a connection between otherwise unconnected participants.

WTD offers the opportunity to develop new connections and collaborations across geography, disciplines, cultures, and lived experience, which in turn can enhance the individual as well as the collective perspective on dementia. Multiple examples of new connections and collaborations were identified by WTD participants, which span dementia care, research, education, and advocacy as well as new organizational collaborations.

New global collaborations on approaches to dementia care were one of the examples. One clinician stated:

I have already made specific contacts with WTD participants to continue working within a network. … I have offered to connect [country] professionals with WTD participants. (58, Clinician, Post-survey)

Some of the new collaborations are between individuals and organizations, rather than between individuals. An example of new collaborations with organizations is: “I intend to establish stronger partnerships with the international organisations and individuals I met during the Walk, exploring opportunities for joint projects or knowledge-sharing initiatives” (28, Advocate, Post-survey).

New collaborations in public education are important for changing the global understanding of dementia and reducing stigma. One participant noted: “I am continuing to learn and collaborate with different cultures to co-build our [curriculum] in the hopes that soon we will reach many thousands more with the message of how to radically transform the experience of dementia” (12, Communicator, Post-survey).

Conscious networking and strategic engagement also occurred during WTD, and spanned multiple areas of dementia engagement including research, advocacy, or care. The unique format and nature of the WTD allowed for intense and in-depth connections, and innovative thinking in participants’ approaches to dementia work. According to one academic:

Several post-WTD collaborations are planned with other participants. Some of these projects will be with new collaborators and new projects. Some are with previous collaborators but new projects/ideas, and some are with current collaborators but based on our conversations at WTD that will take us in new, more focused directions in our work to support care partners. (75, Academic, Post-survey).

These quotes provide insight into the connections and collaborations developed during WTD. Some will need more time to develop into tangible outcomes for people living with dementia and care partners, while others are already well underway.

Connections across the entire WTD community

WTD leaders view all participants as a single network, connected by their involvement in WTD regardless of year. Therefore, WTD 2023 participants were invited to respond to the WTD post-survey conducted as a six-month follow-up. It was anticipated that some connections were already in place across the two cohorts given involvements in other networks such as the Global Brain Health Institute (GBHI), Alzheimer’s Disease International (ADI), International Society to Advance Alzheimer’s Research and Treatment (ISTAART), and other dementia-specific networks.

Six months after WTD, with the addition of WTD 2023 participants, the network had a density of 0.168. While this network is less dense than the previous network (see Fig. 3), this is likely due to the addition of the 2023 cohort participants and not due to a decrease in connectedness amongst 2024 participants. The somewhat triangular shape of this final network is a result of the 2023 cohort, showing that connections between 2023 and 2024 participants are not equally dispersed. Degree centrality ranged from 4 to 173 (M = 39.9, SD = 30.9). Artists had the highest degree centrality with more edges connecting them to other participants than participants in other roles. Betweenness centrality ranged from 0 to 1428 (M = 49.8, SD = 154.9), Academics had the highest betweenness centrality, indicating they served as bridges connecting other participants who were not directly connected. Care Partners had the lowest degree of centrality and betweenness centrality, indicating that they had both the fewest direct connections to other participants and were least often serving as bridges connecting other participants. Since WTD, I have become even more committed to an ongoing collaboration with [name]. Collaborations related to research were reported by several participants, including research proposals, grant applications, conference submissions, and manuscripts. … collaboration is essential if the work is to achieve its goals. The bonds built at WTD bring us closer together and make it much easier for different groups working in the same area to cooperate and share information and data, making the research more relevant for everyone. We have already been able to plan other lines of collaboration with colleagues, thinking about the target audiences we serve. (11, Clinician, Follow-up survey).

I have maintained connections with several WTD participants, engaging in discussions on dementia care. Through these networks, I have collaborated on research initiatives, knowledge exchange, and advocacy efforts. I also remain in contact with some WTD participants exploring potential joint projects and opportunities to promote inclusive and culturally relevant practices in dementia care. (83, Artist, Follow-up survey).

Global reach of WTD

The global reach of WTD is one of the most salient characteristics of the identity of this event. Figure 4 visualizes the changes in global connections across the years of WTD, evident from the increasing density of connections from before WTD 2024 (density = 0.0784) to after WTD 2024 (density = 0.358). After the WTD 2023 cohort was included, network density decreased somewhat to 0.168, suggesting that the 2023 participants are not as well-connected to 2024 participants as the 2024 participants are to each other (Table 6, Appendix 1).

Changes in global connections. a: Pre 2024. b: Post 2024. c: Inclusion of WTD 2024 and WTD 2023

Degree centrality across all five regions was relatively low and in a narrow band (M = 7.14–17.23, SD = 5.27–31.36) before WTD 2024, with Latin America the highest degree centrality and Asia/Oceania the lowest. After WTD 2024, all regions had increased degree centrality (M = 49.69–72.33, SD = 18.30-31.45), with North America the highest degree centrality and Europe/UK the lowest. When WTD 2023 participants were added, overall degree centrality decreased somewhat (M = 27.4-46.84, SD = 15.72–38.07), likely due in part to lower response rates. Latin America had the highest degree centrality and Africa the lowest in the final survey. The increase in degree centrality between the first and second time points reflects an increase in connections before and after WTD 2024, with participants from North America becoming the most connected after WTD 2024. The apparent decrease in degree centrality at the third time point (WTD 2023 & WTD 2024) is an artifact of the increase in participants when WTD 2023 was added and does not indicate a loss of connections. Figure 5 displays changes in degree centrality across all three surveys.

Changes in mean degree centrality by region

Betweenness centrality across four of the five regions was relatively low and in a narrow band (M = 4.80-33.67, SD = 10.40-64.21) before WTD 2024, with Latin America having markedly higher betweenness centrality (M = 249.58, SD = 886.92) due to one highly connected participant. After WTD 2024, all regions had similar betweenness centrality (M = 18.91–52.64, SD = 15.46–87.95), with North America having somewhat higher betweenness centrality than Latin America. In the follow-up survey, Latin America regained the highest betweenness centrality (M = 105.86, SD = 322.73), while the remaining regions remained relatively stable (M = 6.94–49.01, SD = 14.93-119.49). Before WTD 2024, participants from Latin America were most likely to serve as a bridge between any two other participants. After WTD 2024, the network became more integrated, allowing for participants from all regions to have relatively similar likelihoods of serving as a bridge. The integration of WTD 2023 participants in the third survey changed this dynamic somewhat, with Latin America becoming the region with the highest likelihood of serving as a bridge, followed by Europe/UK. Participants often spoke about how they are attempting to influence dementia policy in their home countries, including through connections made with other WTD attendees. One clinician stated:

Since WTD, I have been collaborating with colleagues in my country to improve the regulations for the care of people with dementia. I’m participating in the organization of a national event, which will include participants from other countries where this issue is already more advanced and working better for the population… Some of these guest speakers also took part in the WTD. (11, Clinician, Follow-up survey).

Another participant described a policy-focused collaboration:

I am working with another WTD 2024 participant to influence policy at our state level with respect to dementia services. We are seeking funding for a specific initiative to support people living with dementia and their care partners. (66, Academic, Follow-up survey).

Other kinds of connections

WTD participants were also asked to indicate the frequency and scope of nine other kinds of connections across the WTD community. Table 5 shows there are significant differences in the kinds of connections, the frequency (few/several), and the scope (small/many WTD participants). One would expect to see collaborations for academic activities (presentations, academic manuscripts, research proposals) arising from other professional dementia-related gatherings. Across all roles, these were most often reported as a few connections with a small number of participants. Similarly, one might expect some professional gatherings to promote mentoring; again, a few connections with a small number of participants were the most common response.

What may be unique about the WTD community are the other kinds of connections. Nearly half the respondents indicated they had a few contacts with a small number of participants for advice for personal navigation of dementia supports, services, and care. Most respondents indicated at least some if not many private communications via various mechanisms. The continuing active use of WhatsApp group chats for both WTD cohorts is reflected in the results, with both a few and many contacts. Many people have also connected with others in the WTD community via social media.

Discussion

The findings from this study build on previous work on social movements and the analysis of dementia-focused networks [14, 20]. While the findings align with existing literature, this study provides new evidence on the way that collaborations develop and sustain after an immersive event like WTD.

The use of both social network analysis and qualitative analysis demonstrates how connections and collaborations existed prior to WTD and increased during WTD. This reinforces findings on interdisciplinary professional collaborations within healthcare and other fields that can result in enhanced knowledge transfer [40], as well as the potential for social network analysis to identify factors of a network that may contribute to knowledge transfer and improved implementation efforts [41].This is particularly important for early career professionals who had the opportunity to connect with more senior academic researchers and clinicians, as well as a unique opportunity to connect with people living with dementia and their care partners [42]. The value and potential impact of this knowledge transfer is likely high and can help ensure more dementia-informed care, services, and research.

The connections of people living with dementia and care partners also changed as a result of WTD. These groups have been involved with previous efforts to raise awareness, improve dementia services, and advocacy [43]. However, globally these efforts remain limited. The findings provide further evidence that people living with dementia and their care partners can mobilize to advance global changes in dementia policy. The mobilization of care partners and people living with dementia can also promote stigma reduction and person-centered, culturally responsive models of dementia care, which all have been shown to be effective for improving care and support [44–46].

Many regional or country-specific connections existed prior to WTD, especially in a domain such as dementia research where networks are often small and close-knit. For instance, The Multi-Partner Consortium to Expand Dementia Research in Latin America (or ReDLat) brings together dementia researchers across the Latin American and Caribbean region [47]. While sharing an interest in brain health and dementia, these connections also differ from those developed through the intense nature of WTD. The connections that developed across geographies during WTD demonstrate the potential to meaningfully connect cultures and professions, creating an opportunity for bi-directional learning between participants from low/middle income countries and high-income countries [48].

In parallel to WTD, multiple other networks within the global brain health and dementia continuum exist and are well-established, which likely facilitated many pre-existing connections and collaborations [49–51]. Some of these include the Atlantic Fellows, the Global Brain Health Institute’s (GBHI) training program [52], Alzheimer’s Disease International (ADI), and the professional membership group International Society to Advance Alzheimer’s Research and Treatment (ISTAART). WTD builds and expands on these other brain health and dementia-focused initiatives. WTD is unique in the intensive and immersive nature of the event, and how it centers people with lived experience of dementia. WTD complements these global initiatives by creating further opportunities for people with dementia and care partners to directly engage in policy change and advocacy.

This analysis benefitted from grounding in CFIR, which clarified some of the complexity of WTD in terms of the elements of the “intervention” (the weeklong event), consideration of the “outer setting” (dementia services, advocacy, and policy), the “inner setting” (the WTD community), “characteristics” (participant roles and experiences), and the “implementation process” (WTD planning and execution).

Due to the nature of WTD, the community that developed is one of the most salient findings of this research. This social network analysis shows a substantial shift in connections among participants pre and post WTD. This sense of community has implications for the wellbeing of WTD participants, as a feeling of connectedness that transcends the WTD experience across time and regions of the world. This community is facilitated by social media use among participants (WhatsApp, Instagram, LinkedIn) to remain in regular contact with one another and share updates about their experiences and ongoing work to support dementia care, research, and policy [53]. This is particularly important for participants who are living with dementia and their care partners, and may help mitigate the isolation and loneliness that often accompanies the dementia journey [54]. This community connectedness may also have implications for increased and continued collaboration among participants, especially in terms of awareness raising and advocacy for better dementia policies. Indeed, the density of the WTD network after WTD 2024 is in the “Goldilocks” range that promotes diffusion of information and collective action [33].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. The sample of participants is not representative of all people who are interested in dementia globally, and there is self-selection among individuals who apply to participate in WTD. A process of intentional curation of WTD participants by the organization’s leadership seeks to ensure a globally diverse selection of participants.

A lower response rate on the follow-up survey is another limitation of this study, which was likely due to the burden of reviewing 119 names. While this was eased somewhat by presenting the names in four groups of 30, some respondents commented on fatigue as well as difficulty remembering names – an important consideration given that some of the respondents were people living with dementia. Many commented on how cumbersome the survey was. A possible way to overcome this limitation in future would be to add pictures next to each name; this was not feasible for this survey due to resource constraints.

While this study measured the WTD social network at three points in time, changes in the network over time may not be attributable purely to WTD. Some of the changes may be due to collaborations developed outside of WTD, such as through clinical research networks, conferences, or social media. Additionally, the relative magnitude of collaborations was not measured within the surveys. As a result, relatively brief collaborations received the same weight within the analysis as long-lasting and complex collaborations.

Another limitation is the choice to assign each participant one primary role. Many people played two or more roles simultaneously – for example, an academic who is also a clinician and a care partner for a family member living with dementia. The choice was made to analyze by primary role to make the analyses less complex, with the recognition that many participants spoke to their multiple roles in the qualitative answers to the surveys and in the personal reflections.

There is also a limitation on only reporting geography by where participants were living currently. This may have introduced some bias for those participants from low- and middle-income countries who were currently living in high income countries. This decision was made given that responses would in general reflect their current practice. Again, participants could speak to their own context (such as country of origin) in the qualitative answers to the surveys and in the personal reflections.

Conclusions

Understanding the scope and scale of the connections and collaborations that develop through participation in WTD is essential for assessing the potential of WTD to bring about change in dementia advocacy, care, and research. This SNA demonstrates that a community was developed as a result of WTD among a multi-disciplinary, global group of professionals and people with lived experience of dementia. Importantly, this analysis provides evidence for the benefits of social connections for individuals living with dementia and their care partners and for organizations. These connections can be sustained through social media use.

Some possible next steps for additional research include developing a method to make tracking a network of nearly 200 people after three WTDs possible. Another option might be tracking changes over time of the evolution of social networks that developed from WTD such as by using a time series analysis, including analyses looking at connections within and across roles and regions.

Social networks such as the WTD community are important to further advancement of dementia awareness, advocacy, and policy change in the years ahead.
