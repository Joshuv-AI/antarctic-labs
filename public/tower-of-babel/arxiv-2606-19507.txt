The t t -Split Two-Periodic Aztec Diamond Model
Meredith Shea

Abstract
In this work we consider an Aztec diamond model split into two unequal regions which are asymptotically fixed in size. Each region is weighted with a distinct two-periodic weighting. We refer to this model as the t t -split two-periodic Aztec diamond, to signify its difference from the previous work title Split Two-Periodic Aztec Diamond , where the model was split into two equal regions. We derive an integral expression for the correlation kernel of the model and give a partial description of the scaling limit behavior, along with a conjecture for the remainder. We refer to the larger and smaller sides of the model as the dominant and non-dominant sides, and to the location of the weight change as the interface . The dominant side exhibits a limit shape that depends only on its own weighting and is identical to that of the two-periodic Aztec diamond, while the non-dominant side appears to have a novel limit shape that depends on both weightings and the location of the interface. Lastly, we consider the complete limit shape in the case where the dominant side two-periodic parameter goes to 0.

Introduction

1.1
The Aztec Diamond and Dimer Models

The
Aztec diamond
of size
n
is a subset of the square grid on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which includes all squares whose centers,
(x,y)
, satisfy the inequality
|x|+|y|\leq n
. For more than a quarter of a century mathematicians have studied
domino tilings
of the Aztec diamond, a problem first introduced in
[
10
]
. A domino tiling of the Aztec diamond, or any subset of the square grid, is a placement of dominoes
1
1

1

Here a
domino
is a
2\times 1
rectangle.
that covers the entire region and no dominoes overlap.

One can interpret domino tilings of the Aztec diamond as a stochastic process by assuming a domino tiling is chosen at random from the set of all possible tilings. The simplest case, which we call the uniform model, assumes that all domino tilings are equally likely. The scaling limit of this model, in which the size of the Aztec diamond,
n
, tends to infinity, was studied by Jockusch, Propp and Shor in
[
11
]
. They proved that the scaling limit of the model exhibits a deterministic region and that boundary between the deterministic and non-deterministic regions forms a circle. This has become known as the
Arctic Circle Theorem
.

:
Simulations of the uniform Aztec diamond of sizes

https://lpetrov.cc/domino/
, created by Leonid Petrov.

Domino tilings are a subset of a class of models known as
dimer models
.
2
2

2

For a general primer on dimers see
[
15
]
.
A dimer model is defined on a graph and the possible configurations are known as
dimer coverings
. A dimer covering is a subsets of the edges that match every vertex to exactly one neighbor. In graph theory, dimer coverings are also referred to as
perfect matchings
of the graph. The ideas behind dimer models were initially introduced by Kasteleyn
[
13
]
and Temperley and Fisher
[
17
]
. Associated to the dimer model is a natural Gibbs measure which can be interpreted in the following manner: to each edge of the graph we assign a weight (typically a positive, real number) and the probability of selecting any dimer covering is proportional to the product of the edge weights. The uniform tiling model is equivalent to all edge weights being equal. Kenyon, Okounkov, and Sheffield showed that there are three types of Gibbs measures that can appear in dimer model with doubly periodic edge weights
[
14
]
. These
macroscopic regions
are classified as: frozen, rough, and smooth.
3
3

3

In the literature these regions are also referred to as solid, liquid, and gas, respectively.
In the frozen region dimers are deterministic. In the rough region dimer correlations decay polynomially with distance, while in the smooth region dimer correlations decay exponentially with distance. Not all dimer models exhibit smooth regions, however most exhibit rough regions.

A key aspect towards understanding correlations of the Aztec diamond is the fact that the model is a
determinantal point process
. One approach to computing correlations is via
Kasteleyn theory
. The
inverse Kasteleyn matrix
for the Aztec diamond with two-periodic weights was originally computed by Chhita and Young in
[
8
]
. Further asymptotics of this model have been studied in
[
7
,
5
,
1
]
among others. The two-periodic Aztec diamond is, notably, the simplest Aztec diamond model which exhibits all three macroscopic regions.

Over the past decade there have been many advancement towards understanding general weights on the Aztec diamond. The correlation kernel for general periodic weightings was derived in the recent work of Berggren and Borodin
[
2
]
. This analysis included asymptotics of the model. Additionally in
[
4
]
, Boutillier and de Tilière compute the inverse Kasteleyn for Fock’s weights on the Aztec diamond. This is the first result towards understanding non-periodic models, as Fock’s dimer model is only quasi-periodic.

This work is a follow up to
[
16
]
where an Aztec diamond dimer model with a non-periodic weighting scheme was analyzed. In particular, the
split
two-periodic Aztec diamond divides the Aztec diamond into two equal regions and each region is assigned a different two-periodic weighting. The line where the change of weight occurs is referred to as the
interface
of the model. In
[
16
]
a correlation kernel for the model was derived by extending methods defined in
[
3
]

In the leading order, the local asymptotics of the split two-periodic model are determined only by the two-periodic weighting on the side of the interface where the coordinate lies. This means that the boundary between the macroscopic regions can be determine by computing the boundary in the separate two-periodic cases and then using the interface to ‘‘glue’’ them together.
4
4

4

In general, the two boundary curves will not meet up along the interface, however there will possibly be segments where a smooth region meets with a rough region at the interface.

1.2
Definition of the Model

We will define the model in the dimer setting, so to start we will detail the coordinate convention for the vertices and edges of the Aztec diamond graph of size
n
. The vertices have a natural bipartite structure
\mathtt{V}_{n}^{\text{Az}}=\mathtt{W}^{\text{Az}}_{n}\sqcup\mathtt{B}^{\text{Az}}_{n}
where

\mathtt{W}^{\text{Az}}_{n}=\Big\{(2j+1,2k):0\leq j\leq n-1\text{ and }0\leq k\leq n\Big\}

(1.1)

and

\mathtt{B}^{\text{Az}}_{n}=\Big\{(2j,2k+1):0\leq j\leq n\text{ and }0\leq k\leq n-1\Big\}.

(1.2)

The edges are given by

\mathtt{E}^{\text{Az}}_{n}=\Big\{((2j+1,2k),(2j+1\pm 1,2k+1)):0\leq j\leq n-1,0\leq k\leq n-1\Big\}\\
\cup\Big\{((2j+1,2k),(2j+1\pm 1,2k-1)):0\leq j\leq n-1,1\leq k\leq n\Big\}.

(1.3)

Note that this coordinate convention expresses the rotated convention of the Aztec diamond depicted in Figure
2
. Since our weights are two-periodic, we will assume
n=2N
. The
t
-split two-periodic Aztec diamond has an interface at the vertical line
x=4tN
. We will restrict to cases where
tN
is an integer, so that interface falls between the weight repetitions. Let
b=(b_{x},b_{y})\in\mathtt{B}^{\text{Az}}_{n}
and
w=(w_{x},w_{y})\in\mathtt{W}^{\text{Az}}_{n}
. We say that
b
and
w
are neighbors, and write
b\sim w
, if there is an edge between the two vertices. We will use the notation
\text{wt}(b,w)
to denote the weight of the edge connecting the vertices
b
and
w
. Note that
\text{wt}(b,w)=\text{wt}(w,b)
. The weight function for the
t
-split two-periodic Aztec diamond is

\text{wt}(b,w)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{2}&\text{if }w_{x}\equiv 1\bmod{4},\,w_{y}\equiv 0\bmod{4},\,w-b=(\pm 1,-1),\text{ and }w_{x}<4tN,\\
\alpha^{-2}&\text{if }w_{x}\equiv 1\bmod{4},\,w_{y}\equiv 2\bmod{4},\,w-b=(\pm 1,-1),\text{ and }w_{x}<4tN,\\
\beta^{2}&\text{if }w_{x}\equiv 1\bmod{4},\,w_{y}\equiv 0\bmod{4},\,w-b=(\pm 1,-1),\text{ and }w_{x}>4tN,\\
\beta^{-2}&\text{if }w_{x}\equiv 1\bmod{4},\,w_{y}\equiv 2\bmod{4},\,w-b=(\pm 1,-1),\text{ and }w_{x}>4tN,\\
1&\text{if }w_{x}\equiv 1\bmod{4},\text{ and }w-b=(\pm 1,1),\\
1&\text{if }w_{x}\equiv 3\bmod{4},\text{ and }w-b=(\pm 1,\pm 1),\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1.4)

where
0<\alpha,\beta<1
are constants that we refer to as the
two-periodic parameters
. If
\alpha=\beta
, this model recovers the usual two-periodic Aztec diamond. If
\alpha=\beta=1
we recover the uniform Aztec diamond; however we will generally assume that the constants are not equal to 1. The case of
t=1/2
was studied in
[
16
]
. In the present work we will primarily focus on the case
t<1/2
.

\mathtt{E}^{\text{Az-Alt}}_{n}=\Big\{\left((2j+1,k),(2j+1\pm 1,k)\right):0\leq j<n\text{ and }-n\leq k\leq-1\Big\}\\
\cup\Big\{\left((2j+1,k),(2j+1\pm 1,k+1)\right):0\leq j<n\text{ and }-n-1\leq k\leq-2\Big\}

2.1
t
-Split Correlation Kernel

The goal of this section is to state the correlation kernel for the
t
-split two-periodic Aztec diamond under the assumption that
t<1/2
.

Remark 1
.

Because of the interface, there is not a symmetry argument between the two discrete models,
t<1/2
and
t>1/2
, when the weighting scheme are swapped. In
[
16
]
, there is a description between the asymptotic symmetry of the models. We conjecture the same is true for this generalize model, however at this time it has not been carefully checked.

Before we are able to state the correlation kernel, we must first define some necessary preliminary functions. Most of the notation here is unchanged from
[
16
]
. Let

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z^{-1})^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\varepsilon^{2}z^{-1}\\
\varepsilon^{-2}&1\end{pmatrix}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{-1}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}^{2}.

(2.1)

The origins of this matrix and how it relates to the model can be found in
[
3
, Theorem 5.2]
. Additionally, for the
t
-split two periodic Aztec diamond we define

\varphi_{m^{\prime}\to m,N}(z)=\begin{cases}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{m-m^{\prime}}&m^{\prime}<m<tN,\\
\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{tN-m^{\prime}}\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{m-tN}&m^{\prime}<tN<m,\\
\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{m-m^{\prime}}&tN<m^{\prime}<m.\end{cases}

(2.2)

The eigenvalues of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)
are

r_{\varepsilon,1}(z)=\frac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}\left((z+1)^{2}+2z(\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{-2})+2(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-1})\sqrt{z^{3}+(\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{-2})z^{2}+z}\right)

(2.3)

and

r_{\varepsilon,2}(z)=\frac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}\left((z+1)^{2}+2z(\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{-2})-2(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-1})\sqrt{z^{3}+(\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{-2})z^{2}+z}\right).

(2.4)

We chose the branch cuts for the above to be
(-\infty,-\varepsilon^{-2}]\cup[-\varepsilon^{2},0]
and for
z>0
we choose the positive square root. We also adopt the notation

F_{\varepsilon,1}(z)=E_{\varepsilon}(z)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}E_{\varepsilon}(z)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z(\varepsilon^{2}-1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}&-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(z+1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}\\
-\frac{z(z+1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}&\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z(\varepsilon^{2}-1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}\end{pmatrix}

(2.5)

and

F_{\varepsilon,2}(z)=E_{\varepsilon}(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}E_{\varepsilon}(z)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z(\varepsilon^{2}-1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}&\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(z+1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}\\
\frac{z(z+1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}&\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z(\varepsilon^{2}-1)}{2\sqrt{z(z+\varepsilon^{2})(1+\varepsilon^{2}z)}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.6)

which uses the same branch cuts as above. Lastly we define the function,

g_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\frac{2z(1+\alpha^{2}\beta^{2})+\beta^{2}(z^{2}+1)+\alpha^{2}(z^{2}+1)}{4\sqrt{(z+\alpha^{2})(1+\alpha^{2}z)(z+\beta^{2})(1+\beta^{2}z)}}.

(2.7)

Note that
g_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=g_{\beta,\alpha}(z)
, and
g_{\alpha,\alpha}(z)=1/2
. Assuming
\beta>\alpha
, we define
g_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
to have the branch cuts
[-\alpha^{-2},-\beta^{-2}]\cup[-\beta^{2},-\alpha^{2}]
. Additionally we define

c(z)=\frac{2}{1+2g_{\alpha,\beta}(z)}

(2.8)

for brevity. We are now prepared to state the correlation kernel. Note that the correlation kernel is presented in the form of a
2\times 2
matrix.

Theorem 1
.

Let
-N\leq\xi,\xi^{\prime}\leq-1
and
0<m,m^{\prime}<N
. The correlation kernel
t
-split two-periodic Aztec diamond of size
2N
with
t<1/2
can be written as a piecewise expression depending on whether
m^{\prime}\leq tN
or
m^{\prime}>tN
. For
0<m^{\prime}\leq tN
the kernel is:

\left[\mathbb{K}_{N}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\right]_{i,j=0,1}=-\frac{\mathds{1}_{m>m^{\prime}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}z^{\xi^{\prime}-\xi}\varphi_{m^{\prime}\to m,N}(z)\\
+\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\alpha,1}(w)^{tN-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,1}(w)\\
\times\Phi_{\beta}(w)^{(1/2-t)N}\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{(t-1/2)N}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{-tN}\varphi_{0\to m,N}(z)\\
+\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\alpha,2}(w)^{tN-m^{\prime}}c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,1}(w)\\
\Phi_{\beta}(w)^{(1/2-t)N}\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{(t-1/2)N}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{-tN}\varphi_{0\to m,N}(z).

(2.9)

For
tN<m^{\prime}<N
the kernel is:

\left[\mathbb{K}_{N}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\right]_{i,j=0,1}=-\frac{\mathds{1}_{m>m^{\prime}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}z^{\xi^{\prime}-\xi}\varphi_{m^{\prime}\to m,N}(z)\\
+\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\beta,1}(w)^{N/2-m^{\prime}}F_{\beta,1}(w)\\
\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{(t-1/2)N}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{-tN}\varphi_{0\to m,N}(z)\\
+\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\beta,1}(w)^{(2t-1/2)N-m^{\prime}}c(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,1}(w)F_{\beta,2}(w)\\
\times\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{(t-1/2)N}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{-tN}\varphi_{0\to m,N}(z).

(2.10)

In both cases,
\gamma_{1}
is a simple, positively oriented contour surrounding
1
but not
0
, while
\gamma_{0,1}
is a simple, positively oriented contour surrounding both
0
and
1
with
\gamma_{1}
in its interior.

The above kernel recovers the split two-periodic kernel when
t=1/2
and it recovers the two-periodic kernel when
\alpha=\beta
. The proof of this theorem is presented in Section
3
with some technical details deferred to Section
5
.

Remark 2
.

It is useful to relate the above expression to the correlation kernel of the two-periodic Aztec diamond. Let
\mathbb{K}_{TP,N}^{\varepsilon}
denote the kernel for the two-periodic Aztec diamond with weight parameter
\varepsilon
. If
m,m^{\prime}>tN
, the correlation for the
t
-split two-periodic model can be expressed as

\left[\mathbb{K}_{N}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\right]_{i,j=0,1}=\left[\mathbb{K}_{TP,N}^{\beta}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\right]_{i,j=0,1}\\
+\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\beta,1}(w)^{(2t-1/2)N-m^{\prime}}c(w)\\
\times F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,1}(w)F_{\beta,2}(w)\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{m-N/2},

(2.11)

so to analyze the asymptotics we only need to focus on the last integral. This is not the case for
m,m^{\prime}<tN
, so the asymptotics prove to be a more laborsome task.

2.2
Asymptotics

Let
(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[-1,0]
denote the
asymptotic coordinate
of
(4m,2\xi+i)
, where
m=xN+\mu
,
\xi=yN+\sigma
and
\mu,\sigma=o(N)
. Given an asymptotic coordinate, it is natural to ask which macroscopic region–smooth, rough, or frozen–the point is located in or whether it lies on some boundary between regions. This analysis constitutes the first level of understanding with respect to the limit shape of the model.

Duits and Kuijlaars neatly relate the macroscopic boundary of the two-periodic Aztec diamond to a saddle function defined on a Riemann surface in
[
9
]
. Consider the two-periodic Aztec diamond with parameter
\alpha
. The related Riemann surface
\mathcal{R}
consists of two copies of
\mathbb{C}\backslash\left((-\infty,-\alpha^{-2}]\cup[-\alpha^{2},0]\right)
glued together along the branch cuts
(-\infty,-\alpha^{-2}]
and
[-\alpha^{2},0]
in the usual crosswise manner. This surface has genus 1 unless
\alpha=1
, in which case it has genus 0. The corresponding saddle function is then

\Psi_{\alpha}(z;x,y)=(1+y)\log z-\log(z-1)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\log\mathbf{r_{\alpha}}(z),

(2.12)

where
\mathbf{r_{\alpha}}(z)=r_{\alpha,1}(z)
on the first sheet of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathbf{r_{\alpha}}(z)=r_{\alpha,2}(z)
on the second sheet of
\mathcal{R}
. Recall that the functions
r_{\alpha,i}(z)
are defined back in (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). The differential of
\Psi_{\alpha}
is single-valued and meromorphic,

\Psi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(z)\,dz=\left(\frac{1+y}{z}-\frac{1}{z}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\frac{\mathbf{r_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(z)}{\mathbf{r_{\alpha}}(z)}\right)\,dz.

(2.13)

Counting multiplicities, the above differential has four zeros. The zeros of this differential are referred to as the saddle points of
\Psi_{\alpha}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
denote the union of the intervals
[-\alpha^{-2},-\alpha^{2}]
on both sheets of
\mathcal{R}
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{2}
denote the union of the intervals
[0,\infty)
on both sheets of
\mathcal{R}
.
7
7

7

This is the same notation used in
[
9
]
.
The union
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}
is referred to as the
real part
of
\mathcal{R}
. It was shown in
[
9
]
that for any fixed
(x,y)
, there are always at least two distinct saddle points on
\mathcal{C}_{1}
. The location of the remaining saddle points completely determines the macroscopic region of the point
(x,y)
.

Definition 1

(Macroscopic regions of the two-periodic Aztec diamond)
.

Fix
(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[-1,0]
.

(i)

If there are two simple saddle points on
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, then
(x,y)
is in the
frozen region
.

(ii)

If there are two simple saddles points not in the real part of
\mathcal{R}
, then
(x,y)
is in the
rough region
.

(iii)

If there are two simple saddles on
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, then
(x,y)
is in the
smooth region
.

The boundary of regions occurs when saddle points coalesce:

(iv)

If there is a double saddle point on
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, then
(x,y)
is on the
frozen-rough boundary
of the model.

(v)

If there is a double or triple saddle point on
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, then
(x,y)
is on the
rough-smooth boundary
of the model.

A similar definition was given in
[
16
]
for the split two-periodic Aztec diamond. Since the correlation kernel for the split two-periodic model has a piecewise definition, it is natural to describe the macroscopic boundary via a piecewise saddle function:

\psi_{\alpha,\beta,i}(z;x,y)=\begin{cases}(1+y)\log z-\log(z-1)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\log r_{\alpha,i}(z)&x\leq 1/2,\\
(1+y)\log z-\log(z-1)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\log r_{\beta,i}(z)&x>1/2,\end{cases}

(2.14)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the two-periodic parameters to the left and right of the interface, respectively. That is, the saddle function on either side of the interface coincides with the saddle function of the corresponding two-periodic model. Note that the saddle function above is described sheet by sheet, rather than on a single Riemann surface. This is because there is currently no single Riemann surface that is consistent across the interface. For fixed
(x,y)
, one can realize the same Riemann surface as the two-periodic case and use Definition
1
to describe the regions of the model. For fixed
z
, the saddle function in (
2.14
) is continuous, but not necessarily differentiable, as we vary
(x,y)
across the interface. In particular, certain points along the interface may lie on the boundary between macroscopic regions.

Since the
t
-split correlation kernel is also piecewise, we describe the macroscopic boundary separately on either side of the interface. Below we state what is known and conjectured regarding the asymptotics of the model in each case.

Proposition 1

(Macroscopic boundary to the right of the interface)
.

The macroscopic boundary of the
t
-split two-periodic Aztec diamond to the right of the interface is governed by the saddle functions

\psi_{\beta,i}(z;x,y)=(1+y)\log z-\log(z-1)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\log r_{\beta,i}(z),

(2.15)

for
i=1,2
. In other words, the macroscopic boundary to the right of the interface is identical to that of the two-periodic model with parameter
\beta
.

The proof of this result is found in Section
4
. As stated in Remark
2
, the correlation kernel for two coordinates to the right of the interface can be written as the two-periodic correlation kernel plus an additional double contour integral,

\left[I_{\beta,N}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\right]_{i,j=0,1}\\
=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\beta,1}(w)^{(2t-1/2)N-m^{\prime}}c(w)\\
\times F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,1}(w)F_{\beta,2}(w)\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{m-N/2}.

(2.16)

To prove Proposition
1
, it suffices to show that
I_{\beta,N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
for any points strictly to the right of the interface. This proof, in fact, reveals an even strong statement about the contour integral
I_{\beta,N}
:

Corollary 1
.

For
m,m^{\prime}>tN
, there exists
c>0
such that
I_{\beta,N}=O(e^{-cN})
.

This is notably different than the results obtained for the split two-periodic model in
[
16
]
, where we saw that the second order asymptotic term in the split two-periodic model was, in certain regions of the model, different in order from the two-periodic model. The above corollary shows that the right side of the
t
-split model agree in both the first and second order with the two-periodic model.

The story to the left of the interface is more complicated. First, we restrict the kernel presented in (
2.9
) to the case where
m<tN
. Making some analytic simplifications yields

Lemma 1
.

For
m,\,m^{\prime}<tN
, the correlation kernel of the
t
-split model can be written as

\left[\mathbb{K}_{N}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\right]_{i,j=0,1}=\frac{\mathds{1}_{m\leq m^{\prime}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}z^{\xi^{\prime}-\xi}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{m-m^{\prime}}\\
-\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}+N}(z-1)^{N}}{z^{\xi+N}(w-1)^{N}}r_{\alpha,2}(w)^{tN-m^{\prime}}r_{\beta,2}(w)^{(1/2-t)N}\\
\times c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,2}(w)\Phi_{\beta}(z)^{(t-1/2)N}\Phi_{\alpha}(z)^{m-tN}.

(2.17)

The proof of the above lemma is postponed to Section
5
. The form of this kernel motivates the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1

(Macroscopic boundary to the left of the interface)
.

The macroscopic boundary of the
t
-split two-periodic Aztec diamond to the left of the interface is governed by the saddle function

\varphi_{\alpha,\beta}(z;x,y)=(1+y)\log z-\log(z-1)+(t-x)\log r_{\alpha,2}(z)+(1/2-t)\log r_{\beta,2}(z),

(2.18)

in the sense that the macroscopic boundary corresponds to the coalescing of critical points, in the same way as in the two-periodic model.

The use of
r_{\alpha,2}(z)
and
r_{\beta,2}(z)
follows from the convention chosen for the square root and logarithm branch cuts; an alternative choice may alter the second indices. Proving the above conjecture involves understanding, to an extent, the location and movement of the critical points of
\varphi_{\alpha,\beta}
. From this information, one can then use the method of steepest descent to approximate the kernel for large
N
. The critical points of
\varphi_{\alpha,\beta}(z;x,y)
can not be computed explicitly for general
(x,y)
and is the main source of tediousness in proving the stated claim. For explicit parameters we are able to understand the critical points of
\varphi_{\alpha,\beta}
numerically. Numerical results allow us to produce sample limit shapes such as the one presented in Figure
5
.

Based on the form of the function in Conjecture
1
, it is clear that the limit shape to the left of the interface now depends on the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
t
. For
0<\alpha<1
, numerical evidence suggest that the parameters
\beta
and
t
determine whether or not there is a smooth region to the left of the interface. While the location of the cusp depends on
\alpha
, we conjecture that the existence of the cusp only depends on
\beta
and

. We state this precisely in the following conjecture,

Conjecture 2
.

Let
0<\beta,\,\alpha<1
. Then a smooth region exists to the left of the interface if and only if

\frac{\beta^{2}}{1+\beta^{2}}<t.

Given a two-periodic model with parameter
\beta=p/q
such that
0<\beta<1
, the left most
cusp
of the rough-smooth boundary occurs at the horizontal position
p^{2}/(p^{2}+q^{2})\in(0,1/2)
. Therefore, the statement in the conjecture is simply saying that the cusp for the two-periodic model with parameter
\beta
would occur before the interface line. Note that we do not have a similar condition on left side parameter
\alpha
. Figure

gives an example where the left side cusp would happen after the interface in the two-periodic model; however, since

3
Derivation of the Correlation Kernel

To derive the correlation kernel, we wish to use Theorem 3.1 from
[
3
]
; however it cannot be immediately applied to this case because
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)
has a singularity on the unit circle. In Section 5.2 of
[
3
]
the authors present a method for overcoming this issue by introducing a bias into the weights. We write the biased matrices as

\Phi_{\varepsilon,a}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-a^{2}z^{-1})^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\varepsilon^{2}z^{-1}a^{-1}\\
\varepsilon^{-2}a^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\varepsilon^{2}z^{-1}a\\
\varepsilon^{-2}a&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{-1}a^{-1}\\
a^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{-1}a\\
a&1\end{pmatrix}

(3.1)

where
0<a<1
. Sending
a\to 1
gives
\Phi_{\varepsilon,a}(z)\to\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)
. Now we can apply Theorem 3.1 from
[
3
]
to the biased
t
-split two-periodic model. We begin by explicitly defining the Wiener-Hopf factorization of the matrix

\varphi_{N,a}(z)=\Phi_{\alpha,a}(z)^{tN}\Phi_{\beta,a}(z)^{(1-t)N}.

(3.2)

We say that

\varphi_{N,a}(z)=\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(z)\widetilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}(z)

(3.3)

is a Wiener-Hopf type factorization provided:

1.

\tilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}^{\pm 1}
is analytic for
|z|<1
and continuous for
|z|\leq 1
,

2.

\tilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}^{\pm 1}
is analytic for
|z|>1
and continuous for
|z|\geq 1
,

3.

and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}\to I_{2}z^{-N}
as
z\to\infty
.

The explicit form of the matrices
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\pm,N,a}(z)
is complicated for general
a
. However, the factorization is continuous in
a
and the matrices are simple to write when
a=1
. See
[
3
, Section 4]
for more details on computing the factorization in the
2\times 2
matrix case. From
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
we obtain the kernel,

\Big[\mathbb{K}_{a,N}(4m^{\prime},2\xi^{\prime}+j;4m,2\xi+i)\Big]_{i,j=0}^{1}=-\frac{\mathbb{I}_{m>m^{\prime}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0,1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}z^{\xi^{\prime}-\xi}\varphi_{m\to m^{\prime},N,a}(z)\\
+\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oint_{\gamma_{a}}\mathrm{d}w\oint_{\gamma_{0,1,a}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z(z-w)}\frac{w^{\xi^{\prime}}}{z^{\xi}}\varphi_{m^{\prime}\to N,N,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}(w)^{-1}\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(z)^{-1}\varphi_{0\to m,N,a}(z)

(3.4)

where
\gamma_{a}
is a simple, positively oriented contour that contains the points
a^{2}
and
1
and does not contain the points
0
or
a^{-2}
and
\gamma_{0,1,a}
is a simple, positively oriented contour containing the points
0
,
1
,
a^{2}
, and
a^{-2}
and surrounds the contour
\gamma_{a}
. The matrices
\varphi_{m^{\prime}\to m,N,a}
are defined as in (
2.2
), with each constituent matrix replaced by its biased counterpart.

We would like to take the limit
a\to 1
, however the
w
integral contains singularities inside and outside of the curve
\gamma_{a}
that converge to the same point. Our hope is to massage the integrand so that all the singularities lie inside of the contour, thus allowing us to take the required limit. We present a proof of this in cases:
0<m^{\prime}<tN
and
tN<m^{\prime}<N
. In both cases the proof involves the infinite repetition of matrix identities, eventually resulting in elimination of the poles.

Remark 3
.

This proof is different than the proof presented in
[
16
]
and presents an alternative, simpler, method to proving the correlation kernel for that model.

3.1
Case 1: Left of the Interface

We will first present the proof for
0<m^{\prime}\leq tN
. Under this restriction we write,

\varphi_{m^{\prime}\to N,N,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}(w)^{-1}=\Phi_{\alpha,a}(w)^{tN-m^{\prime}}\Phi_{\beta,a}(w)^{(1-t)N}\widetilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}(w)^{-1}.

(3.5)

Now apply (
3.3
) to get,

\Phi_{\alpha,a}(w)^{tN-m^{\prime}}\Phi_{\beta,a}(w)^{(1-t)N}\widetilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}(w)^{-1}=\Phi_{\alpha,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w).

(3.6)

Insert the eigen-decomposition of
\Phi_{\alpha,a}
into the above expression yields,

\Phi_{\alpha,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w)=r_{\alpha,1,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,1,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w)+r_{\alpha,2,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,2,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w).

(3.7)

The explicit definitions of
r_{\alpha,j,a}
and
F_{\alpha,j,a}
for
j=1,2
are given in Appendix
A
. The functions
F_{\alpha,j,a}
are analytic away from the negative real axis. The function
r_{\alpha,1,a}
and
r_{\alpha,1,a}
are both analytic and non-zero away from the negative real axis, except
r_{\alpha,1,a}
has a pole of order 2 at
a^{2}
and
r_{\alpha,2,a}
has a zero of order 2 at
a^{-2}
.

Substituting the term

r_{\alpha,1,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,1,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w)

(3.8)

back into (
3.4
) gives no poles at
a^{-2}
, and so the limit
a\to 1
may be taken. The contribution in (
3.4
) from the term

r_{\alpha,2,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,2,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w)

(3.9)

still has poles both inside and outside of the contour. Applying (
3.3
) once more, we may write this as,

r_{\alpha,2,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,2,a}(w)\widetilde{\varphi}_{-,N,a}(w)=r_{\alpha,2,a}(w)^{-m^{\prime}}F_{\alpha,2,a}(w)\Phi_{\alpha,a}(w)^{tN}\Phi_{\beta,a}(w)^{(1-t)N}\widetilde{\varphi}_{+,N,a}(w)^{-1}.

(3.10)

Recognize that, by orthogonality,

F_{\alpha,2,a}(w)\Phi_{\alpha,a}(w)^{tN}=r_{\alpha,2,a}(w)^{tN}F_{\alpha,2,a}(w).

(3.11)

Thus we obtain,

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)-F_{\alpha,2}(w).

\displaystyle-F_{\alpha,2}(w)+c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,1}(w)

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)-c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)

(5.7)

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)-c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)

(5.8)

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)(F_{\beta,1}(w)-I)

(5.9)

\displaystyle=-c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,2}(w)

(5.10)

Plugging the above into equation (
5.5
) gives the statement presented in the lemma.
∎

Next we would like to prove the two technical lemmas necessary for proving the explicit statement of the kernel. We start with Lemma
2
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We can explicitly compute the eigenvalues of the matrix
F_{\alpha,2,a}(w)F_{\beta,1,a}(w)
. One eigenvalue is 0 while the other is,

\lambda(w)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})(w+1)^{2}+w(\alpha^{2}-1)(\beta^{2}-1)(a+a^{-1})^{2}}{8\alpha\beta\sqrt{(w^{2}+x_{\alpha}w+1)(w^{2}+x_{\beta}w+1)}}.

(5.11)

We must show that
|\lambda(w)|\leq\rho<1
for all
w\in\gamma_{a}
. Before we begin to bound the components of
\lambda(w)
, one should recognize that we are able to shrink the contour
\gamma_{a}
down to a circle of radius
r
around
a^{2}
, where
r
can be made arbitrarily small. This is because the components of the contour integral in equation (
3.4
) have no poles away from the branch cuts besides at
a^{2}
and
a^{-2}
.

Observe that
\lambda(w)
is analytic in a neighborhood of
w=a^{2}
. This is because the factor
w^{2}+x_{\varepsilon}w+1
, for
\varepsilon=\alpha,\beta
, is positive and non-zero at
w=a^{2}
. Moreover, one can compute that

\displaystyle(w^{2}+x_{\alpha}w+1)(w^{2}+x_{\beta}w+1)\Big|_{w=a^{2}}

\displaystyle=(a^{4}+x_{\alpha}a^{2}+1)(a^{4}+x_{\beta}a^{2}+1)

(5.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}(a^{2}+1)^{4}(\alpha+\alpha^{-1})^{2}(\beta+\beta^{-1})^{2}.

(5.13)

Plugging the above into
\lambda
yields,

\displaystyle\lambda(a^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})(a^{2}+1)^{2}+a^{2}(\alpha^{2}-1)(\beta^{2}-1)(a+a^{-1})^{2}}{2\alpha\beta(a^{2}+1)^{2}(\alpha+\alpha^{-1})(\beta+\beta^{-1})}

(5.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})(a^{2}+1)^{2}+(\alpha^{2}-1)(\beta^{2}-1)(a^{2}+1)^{2}}{2(a^{2}+1)^{2}(\alpha^{2}+1)(\beta^{2}+1)}

(5.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{(\alpha^{2}+1)(\beta^{2}+1)(a^{2}+1)^{2}}{2(a^{2}+1)^{2}(\alpha^{2}+1)(\beta^{2}+1)}=0.

(5.16)

Since
\lambda(a^{2})=0
,
\lambda(w)
is analytic in a neighborhood of
a^{2}
, and we can shrink the contour
\gamma_{a}
down to an arbitrarily small circle of radius
r
, this implies we may choose
r
small enough to guarantee that
\lambda(w)\leq\rho<1
.
∎

Next we will prove Lemma
3
. The above lemma was proven for any
a\in(0,1]
, however in the following lemma we restrict to

for simplicity as it occurs after we take the limit
a\to 1
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Lemma
2
is true for any
a\in(0,1]
, thus it is enough to justify that the sum in Lemma
3
converges. We write,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)\right)^{k}=\left(I-F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)\right)^{-1}-I.

(5.17)

To prove the above lemma, we only need to check that

\left(I-F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)\right)^{-1}=I+c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w).

(5.18)

The above fact can be proven using the following relation,

\displaystyle c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)(I-F_{\beta,2}(w))F_{\alpha,2}(w)

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)-c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,2}(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)

\displaystyle=c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)-F_{\alpha,2}(w).

Now we can check

\left(I-F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)\right)\left(I+c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)\right)\\
=I+c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)-F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)-c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)\\
=I+c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)-F_{\alpha,2}(w)F_{\beta,1}(w)-(c(w)F_{\alpha,2}(w)-F_{\alpha,2}(w))F_{\beta,1}(w)=I.

∎
