Menstrual blood–derived stromal cells exhibit superior protection against lung injury in myocardial Infarction–Induced heart failure in rats compared to bone marrow–derived stromal cells
Authors: Mahmood Manshori, Zahra Saffarian, Nahid Aboutaleb, Hannaneh Golshahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLung injury is a frequent complication following heart failure (HF) due to systemic inflammation, hypoxia, and reduced cardiac function. This study compared the therapeutic effects of menstrual blood–derived stromal cells (MenSCs) and bone marrow–derived stromal cells (BMSCs) on HF-induced lung injury in rats.MethodsTwenty male Wistar rats underwent permanent ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery to induce myocardial infarction (MI). They were randomly assigned to receive intravenous injections of MenSCs (2 × 106 cells), BMSCs (2 × 106 cells), or PBS 7 days post-MI. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography, and lung tissues were analyzed 28 days post-cell administration. Histopathology (H&E and Masson's trichrome staining) evaluated pulmonary structural changes and fibrosis, TUNEL assay assessed apoptosis, and immunohistochemistry measured NF-κB and iNOS expression.ResultsResults showed that MenSC treatment significantly improved left ventricular ejection fraction compared with MI and BMSC groups (both P < 0.0001). MenSCs markedly reduced pulmonary injury scores and apoptotic cell numbers compared with both untreated and BMSC-treated rats (P < 0.0001). Both cell types reduced pulmonary fibrosis compared to MI (P < 0.0001). Both MenSCs and BMSCs similarly reduced NF-κB expression, whereas only MenSCs effectively decreased iNOS expression (P < 0.0001). Human mitochondrial antibody staining confirmed successful homing of both cell types to injured lung tissue.Conclusionsystemic administration of MenSCs effectively ameliorated HF-induced lung injury through anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anti-fibrotic mechanisms. Compared with BMSCs, MenSCs demonstrated superior therapeutic potential, highlighting their promise as a non-invasive and immunologically favorable cell source for regenerative therapy in remote organ injury following MI.

Introduction

Despite significant advances in myocardial infarction (MI) treatment over the past two decades, MI remains a leading cause of heart failure (HF) worldwide [1]. MI most commonly results from coronary artery obstruction, which reduces blood flow and induces oxygen deprivation in the ischemic myocardium [2].

Reduced coronary perfusion induces oxidative stress, impairs myocardial calcium handling, promotes arrhythmias and cardiac remodeling through hypertrophic signaling and cell death, including apoptosis and necrosis [3]. These alterations ultimately contribute to the development of ischemic heart failure (IHF), which is associated with structural and functional changes in multiple organs, including the kidney, liver, and lungs. In patients with post-MI HF, pulmonary complications such as congestion, interstitial edema, and inflammatory lung injury are frequently observed and contribute significantly to morbidity and impaired gas exchange which can lead to pulmonary vascular remodeling, and respiratory muscle weakness [[4], [5], [6]]. Clinical evidence indicates that pulmonary congestion and left ventricular dysfunction occur in a substantial proportion of patients following MI, underscoring the close functional interplay between the heart and lungs [7,8]. Although overt pulmonary fibrosis is relatively uncommon after MI, early pulmonary alterations including endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory cell infiltration, and oxidative stress are well documented and may precede structural remodeling [[9], [10], [11]]. Indeed, IHF also promotes infiltration of inflammatory cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, which elevates levels of inflammatory mediators, such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), which is strongly implicated in lung dysfunction [12]. These changes are primarily driven by elevated pulmonary venous pressure, systemic inflammation, and hypoxia. Increasing evidence also supports the concept of a heart–lung axis, whereby cardiac injury triggers secondary inflammatory and molecular responses in the lung, contributing to progressive pulmonary dysfunction [13].

Impaired myocardial contraction exacerbates pulmonary injury by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production under hypoxic conditions [14]. Elevated ROS activates the immune system and triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), via NF-κB signaling, leading to structural and functional lung abnormalities and promoting apoptosis of pulmonary parenchymal and endothelial cells [15]. Moreover, MI-induced inflammation activates inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which contributes to lung injury [16].

Bone marrow–derived stromal cells (BMSCs) are effective in tissue regeneration through angiogenesis promotion, immunomodulation, and cellular recruitment [17]. Despite their broad therapeutic applications, BMSC-based therapies face limitations, including invasive harvesting procedures and restricted proliferative capacity [18]. Menstrual blood–derived stromal cells (MenSCs) have emerged as a promising alternative due to their easy accessibility, high proliferation rate, and absence of ethical concerns [19]. MenSCs can differentiate into all three germ lineages and be expanded for at least 20 passages without chromosomal abnormalities [20].

MenSCs also exhibit immunomodulatory properties by suppressing pro-inflammatory factors such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interacting with lymphocytes, which collectively support their safety in exogenous transplantation [21,22]. Several bioactive factors, including hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), have been identified in MenSC-conditioned medium (CM) and may contribute to tissue regeneration [[23], [24], [25]].

Heart failure is a multisystem disorder that impairs blood supply and affects multiple organs. Although the primary pathology originates in the heart, circulatory dysfunction can induce secondary damage in vital organs such as the brain, lungs, and kidneys [26]. The lungs are particularly susceptible to hypoxia and the accumulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, which contribute to lung injury and dysfunction following MI. We previously demonstrated that MenSCs and BMSCs can improve liver and kidney function after MI, with MenSCs showing superior therapeutic potential by reducing cell death and exerting anti-inflammatory effects [27].

In the present study, we investigated, for the first time, the protective effects of systemic administration of MenSCs compared with BMSCs on lung function in a rat model of MI.

Materials and methods

Ethics

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1399.542). All animals experimental were carried out in accordance with National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals [28] and accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines [29], and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

Menstrual blood and bone marrow samples were obtained from healthy volunteers after informed consent, following evaluation through medical history and physical examination. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Avicenna Research Institute (IRCT20180619040147N5).

Preparation and culture of MSCs (MenSCs and BMSCs)

All procedures were conducted under sterile conditions in a Class B cleanroom using a certified laminar-flow hood. Menstrual blood samples were collected into 15 mL conical tubes containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and 2.5 μg/mL Fungizone (Gibco, Scotland, UK) to prevent clotting and maintain single-cell suspensions.

Samples were seeded into 25 cm2 culture flasks containing Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F12 Ham (DMEM/F12) (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% human platelet lysate (hPL) and penicillin (100 U/mL)/streptomycin (100 μg/mL) (Inoclon, Iran). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To enrich adherent stromal progenitor cells, cultures were left undisturbed for 4–5 days, with daily checks to ensure no contamination occurred. Non-adherent cells were removed, and adherent cells formed spindle-shaped colonies. The medium was refreshed every 2–3 days until cells reached 70–80% confluence, typically within 12–14 days.

Cells were then detached using 0.25% TrypLE™ (Gibco, UK) for 3–5 min, collected by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in fresh medium supplemented with hPL. Cells were sub cultured at a 1:2 ratio, and only low-passage cells (P2–P3) were used in experiments to maintain their phenotype and functionality.

Before transfer to the master cell bank, cells underwent quality control testing in the pre-master cell bank, including a viability assay using Trypan Blue. Following approval, cells were transferred to the master and working cell banks. For final quality assessment, cryopreserved cells were thawed and expanded to passage 2 before use in animal experiments.

Bone marrow aspirates (5–10 mL) were obtained from the iliac crests of five healthy donors (age 25–35 years) at the Bone Marrow Transplantation Center, Shariati Hospital, Tehran University of Medical Sciences. BMSCs were isolated using a combination of density gradient centrifugation and plastic adherence as previously described [30]. All experiments were performed using cells at passages 2–4.

Characterization of cultured MSCs by flow cytometry

Based on our previous study [27] the morphology of cultured cells was examined using phase-contrast microscopy, confirming a spindle-shaped appearance typical of stromal cells. For phenotypic characterization, flow cytometry was employed to evaluate the expression of MSC-associated surface markers (CD73, CD90, CD44), the embryonic stem cell marker OCT-4, and the hematopoietic marker CD45.

Briefly, 1 × 105 cells in 100 μL PBS containing 2% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) were incubated with PE-conjugated antibodies against CD73 (561014; BD Pharmingen), CD44 (550989; BD Pharmingen), and CD45 (560975; BD Pharmingen) at 4 °C for 40 min in the dark. For intracellular OCT-4 detection, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% saponin, incubated with rabbit anti-human OCT-4 antibody (b19857, Abcam) for 40 min, followed by FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit Ig (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. An isotype IgG control (555748; BD Pharmingen) was included to ensure specificity. After washing and fixation with 1% formaldehyde, samples were analyzed using a Partec flow cytometer (Partec GmbH, Germany).

Myocardial infarction induction and stem cell administration

Twenty male Wistar rats, weighing 300–350 g and aged 12–15 weeks, were obtained from the Animal Care and Breeding Center at the animal laboratory of Iran University of Medical Sciences. Animals were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group): Sham, MI + PBS (200 μL), MI + MenSCs (2 × 106 cells in 200 μL MenSC-conditioned medium), and MI + BMSCs (2 × 106 cells in 200 μL BMSC-conditioned medium).

Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 75 mg/kg ketamine (10%; Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands), 5 mg/kg xylazine (2%; Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany) and 5 mg/kg acepromazine (1% Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands). Prior to surgery, 10 mg/kg enrofloxacin 5% (Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany) and 0.5 mg/kg tramadol 100 mg/2 mL (Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands) were administered to prevent infection and pain. Animals were intubated and mechanically ventilated using a Drager Fabius Plus system (Lübeck, Germany) at 60 cycles/min, tidal volume of 1 mL, and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 2 cm H2O, with 1% isoflurane for anesthesia maintenance.

MI was induced via permanent ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery, 2 mm below the left auricle, using 6-0 Prolene sutures (KeyhanTeb, Eshtehard, Iran) through an open left thoracotomy. Successful MI induction was confirmed by blanching of the anterior wall distal to the ligation, dyskinetic motion, and ST-segment elevation on electrocardiogram (ECG). Sham-operated animals underwent the same procedure without LAD ligation. The chest wall and skin were closed using absorbable sutures. Postoperatively, enrofloxacin (10 mg/kg) and tramadol (0.5 mg/kg) were administered for three consecutive days.

Stem cells were administered systemically via the tail vein one week after MI induction. Echocardiography was performed at 7 and 35 days after MI by a blinded cardiologist. Left ventricular function and dimensions were assessed according to Schiller guidelines [31]. At least three consecutive cardiac cycles were analyzed to measure posterior wall thickness, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, and left ventricular end-systolic diameter. Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) was calculated using EchoPAC software. Animals showing EF outside the expected post-MI range or any complications were excluded.

Histological examination

Thirty-five days after MI induction, animals were euthanized in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals using an intraperitoneal injection of 200 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital (Panpharma, France) [32]. Death was confirmed by the absence of pulse, respiration, corneal reflex, response to toe pinch, and lack of heart and respiratory sounds. Samples were collected from lungs and hearts, washed with PBS, and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Formalin-fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome. The extent of lung and heart injury was assessed under a light microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan) by a blinded veterinary pathologist.

Parameters evaluated included exudate accumulation, hyperemia/congestion, inflammation, intra-alveolar hemorrhage or debris, and cellular hyperplasia. Each parameter was scored on a scale from 0 (uninjured) to 3 (most severely injured). In each sample, five random high-power fields (HPFs) were analyzed, and the scores were averaged.

Evaluation of NF-κB, iNOS, and anti-human mitochondria expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC)

To assess NF-κB and iNOS expression and track the administered stem cells in lung tissues, IHC was performed. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and treated with 3% H2O2 in methanol for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidases. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave.

Sections were then incubated with 10% goat serum for 40 min at 37 °C to block non-specific binding. Primary antibodies, including NF-κB (ab16502, Abcam, UK, 1:100), iNOS (sc-651, Santa Cruz, USA, 1:200), and anti-human mitochondria (MAB1273, Merck, Germany, 1:150), were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with Tris-buffered saline (TBS), sections were incubated with goat polyclonal horseradish peroxidase (HRP -conjugated secondary antibody (Biorbyt, UK) for 30 min at room temperature. Positive cells were visualized using 100 μL of DAB substrate (Sigma, USA). Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin (Sigma, USA), followed by dehydration in graded alcohols, clearing in xylene, and mounting. Sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan) connected to a digital camera (Olympus DP71, Japan).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Apoptotic cell death in lung tissues was evaluated using a TUNEL assay kit (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene (Sigma, USA) and rehydrated in PBS. Sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and treated with 3% H2O2 in the dark. Subsequently, 50 μL of TUNEL reaction mixture was applied, followed by diaminobenzidine (DAB) development to visualize positive cells. TUNEL-positive cells were counted in five random high-power fields per section, across five sections per tissue. The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated using the following formula.(The number of apoptotic cells/apoptotic cells + non-apoptotic cells) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of cultured MSCs: MenSCs and BMSCs

Flow cytometry analysis revealed that both MenSCs and BMSCs expressed MSC markers CD105 and CD29, and were negative for the hematopoietic marker CD45. In contrast, only MenSCs were positive for the embryonic marker OCT-4 [27]. These results confirm the MSC identity of both cell types and highlight the pluripotency potential of MenSCs.

MenSCs administration improved cardiac function and preserve structure

Echocardiographic assessment performed 35 days after MI induction demonstrated a significant reduction in left ventricular EF in the MI group compared with the sham group (P < 0.0001). Administration of both MenSCs and BMSCs significantly improved EF compared with the MI group (P < 0.0001 and P = 0.0021, respectively). Notably, MenSC treatment resulted in greater improvement in EF than BMSC treatment (P < 0.0001), highlighting the superior cardioprotective effect of MenSCs (Fig. 1A and B). Also, histopathological evaluation revealed that MenSCs administration led to the most pronounced preservation of cardiomyocytes in the infarcted area. MenSCs administration improved MI-induced pulmonary injuries

Histopathological evaluation demonstrated that systemic administration of MenSCs provided greater protection against lung injury following MI compared with BMSCs (Fig. 2AI–VII).

In sham-operated rats, the lungs exhibited normal histological architecture. In contrast, the untreated MI group exhibited severe structural abnormalities, including thickening of inter-alveolar septa, interstitial edema, vascular congestion, intra-alveolar hemorrhage, marked infiltration of inflammatory cells within and around bronchial walls, perivascular regions, and inter-alveolar septa, as well as partial atelectasis. Additionally, medial hypertrophy of small and medium-sized pulmonary arteries, indicative of smooth muscle cell proliferation, was noted.

In rats treated with BMSCs, consolidated lung areas were frequently observed, and systemic administration of BMSCs did not significantly reduce inflammatory cell infiltration. Extravasation of red blood cells into alveolar spaces and inter-alveolar septa, as well as persistent medial hypertrophy in small pulmonary arteries, were evident.

Conversely, systemic administration of MenSCs markedly reduced congestion, edema, interstitial inflammation, intra-alveolar hemorrhage, and epithelial structural damage. Many lung regions appeared intact or only minimally affected. Histopathological scoring demonstrated that systemic administration of MenSCs significantly attenuated MI-induced lung injury, as reflected by lower lung injury scores (P < 0.0001 vs. MI and P < 0.0001 vs. BMSCs). In contrast, BMSCs did not produce a significant reduction compared with the MI group (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the MI group exhibited pronounced NF-κB expression, predominantly in the nuclei of alveolar and small airway epithelial cells. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant increase in NF-κB-positive cells in the MI group compared with the sham group (P < 0.0001). Treatment with stem cells reduced NF-κB expression in compared with the MI group (both P < 0.0001).

Similarly, iNOS expression was markedly elevated in lung cells in the MI group. Administration of MenSCs significantly decreased the percentage of iNOS-positive cells compared with the MI group (P < 0.0001). In contrast, BMSC treatment did not produce a statistically significant reduction in iNOS expression (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5A–E). Discussion

Excessive oxidative stress, overexpression of proinflammatory cytokines, and hemodynamic alternation contribute to secondary lung injury, which worsens following MI [15, 33]. Clinical studies have shown that pulmonary congestion and lung dysfunction may occur in a substantial proportion of patients following MI, with reports indicating that pulmonary congestion or left ventricular dysfunction can be present in approximately 13–32% of cases [34]. In severe conditions such as cardiogenic shock, pulmonary complications including acute lung injury and respiratory failure are frequent and significantly impact patient outcomes [35]. Although the therapeutic potential of MenSCs and BMSCs following MI has been widely reported [36,37], their capacity to prevent remote organ injury, such as lung dysfunction, has received limited attention.

Previous studies have shown that MenSCs inhibit TGF-β, enhance antioxidant capacity in fibrosis models, and can alleviate pulmonary fibrosis by delivering miRNA Let-7 to alveolar epithelial cells, thereby preserving standard alveolar structure [38,39]. These findings align with ours, indicating that MenSCs, by modulating chronic inflammatory and hypoxic signaling, can inhibit fibrosis in MI-induced remote lung injury. MenSC therapy more effectively prevented pulmonary fibrosis by reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and collagen deposition in the lung compared to BMSCs [[40], [41], [42]].

Ischemia and hypoxia following MI promote excessive production of inflammatory mediators in the circulation, triggering systemic inflammatory responses that lead to endothelial injury [43,44].

In our study, MI induced a significant increase in NF-κB and iNOS expression in lung tissue, whereas MenSC administration was more effective than BMSCs in reducing their expression. Lee et al. demonstrated that alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis is strongly associated with hypoxia-induced upregulation of NF-κB and iNOS [45]. Consistently, MenSC treatment significantly reduced apoptotic cell death, likely due to suppression of these inflammatory signaling pathways. In contrast, BMSC treatment did not significantly alter apoptosis levels compared to the MI group. We observed that MenSC-treated rats showed a marked reduction in NF-κB and iNOS expression, which are known mediators of inflammation-induced oxidative and nitrosative stress. Thakur et al. reported that blocking NF-κB in the lung suppressed fibrosis by reducing pro-inflammatory mediator production, inhibiting extracellular matrix damage, and preventing myofibroblast differentiation [46]. Therefore, it is reasonable to suggest that suppression of iNOS via effective inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway by MenSCs may represent a primary mechanism for attenuating lung fibrosis compared to BMSCs.

TUNEL-positive cells were significantly reduced in the MenSC group compared with the MI group, indicating superior protection against cell death. Since apoptosis is a key mechanism in ischemic and inflammatory conditions, this reduction in apoptotic cells likely reflects decreased inflammatory activity. Given that inflammation readily induces apoptosis, MenSCs may inhibit cell death by suppressing NF-κB and iNOS activation. Xiang et al. demonstrated that MenSCs increase the survival of lung epithelial cells and inhibit apoptosis in injured lungs [47], findings consistent with ours. Similarly, MenSCs exhibit anti-apoptotic effects in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models [42].

Mechanistically, MenSCs can reduce collagen fiber deposition and downregulate Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and pro-apoptotic genes, such as Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), while upregulating anti-apoptotic (Bcl-2) and anti-fibrotic genes, including HGF and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) [48]. Both MenSCs and BMSCs were detected in injured lung and heart tissues four weeks after intravenous injection, confirming homing to damaged sites and allowing continuous delivery of reparative signals. Due to high pulmonary blood flow and the production of inflammatory cytokines by injured tissues, intravenously injected MSCs are predominantly trapped in pulmonary capillaries, suggesting that IV injection may be as effective as direct intratracheal administration [49]. While both cell types reached the injured lung, MenSCs demonstrated greater efficacy across multiple parameters, including fibrosis, apoptosis, histological outcomes, and modulation of inflammatory markers.

Conclusion

MI initiates a cascade of deleterious events that lead to lung injury via apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling. Lung injury may result from cardiomyocyte degeneration, abnormal immune signaling from injured cardiomyocytes, and hypoxia. Our study demonstrated that systemic administration of MenSCs effectively ameliorated lung injury in MI rats by reducing cell death, modulating inflammatory responses, and preventing fibrosis progression. Compared to BMSCs, intravenous injection of MenSCs exhibited superior protective effects under identical experimental conditions.

Considering our findings, along with the significant advantages of MenSCs over BMSCs—including easier isolation, low immunogenicity, and non-invasive harvesting—MenSCs represent a promising and suitable cell source for therapeutic and regenerative strategies targeting remote organ injury following MI. However, further studies with larger sample sizes and detailed mechanistic evaluations are warranted to fully elucidate the precise effects of MenSCs on lung function after MI.
