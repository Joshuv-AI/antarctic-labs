Comments on the journal article entitled “Translation and pilot testing of the ReSPECT form into Danish” published in Resuscitation Plus May 2026
Authors: Charlotte Hardy
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

As a UK Internal Medicine Trainee leading a Quality Improvement Project on Emergency Care Planning documentation in Intensive Care Units using Recommended Summary Plan for Emergency Care and Treatment (ReSPECT) forms, I read with interest the article, Translation and pilot-testing of the ReSPECT form into Danish.1 The authors make a valuable contribution towards improving advance care planning within the Danish healthcare system.

A strength of the study is the diverse inclusion of settings in the pilot testing of the Danish ReSPECT form. By involving physicians, and patients, from a range of healthcare environments, including intensive care, palliative care and general practice, the study reflects the range of contexts across which ReSPECT forms may be used, improving the relevance of the findings.

The positive feedback from this study regarding ReSPECT forms is important and the learning points should be extrapolated for UK physicians. Clinicians appreciate the structured framework of ReSPECT forms to broach complex conversations around emergency care. 6 of 15 patients (40%) had not previously discussed their wishes about their care. Engaging in shared decision making promotes patient autonomy.2

Several aspects of the study would benefit from further discussion.

The article discusses ReSPECT forms in preference to dualistic DNACPR discussions but does not explore other advance care planning frameworks, such as ‘Treatment Escalation Plans’, utilised in the UK. It would be interesting to understand whether the Danish healthcare system has comparable pathways for documenting ceilings of treatment/escalation decisions, and how the ReSPECT process can support this in Denmark.

An advantage of ReSPECT forms within the UK is their standardisation across healthcare systems, enabling clinicians nationally to become familiar with a single, unified document.3 Discussion whether similar national standardisation is anticipated in Denmark would add useful context.

Most patients (53%) were recruited from intensive care units (ICU), whereas only a small number (6%) were recruited from palliative care. Periods of acute critical illness are not the optimal time for completion of ReSPECT discussions,4 with feelings of sudden anxiety and panic influencing decisions. Recruiting participants for this study largely directly from ICU does not represent optimum advanced care planning.

One stated strength of the study was that participating physicians had “at least ten years of experience”. In the UK, ReSPECT discussions are completed across the multidisciplinary team, including by Advance Care Practitioners, Clinical Nurse Specialists, and resident doctors. It would be useful to know whether experience differed according to seniority or professional background amongst clinicians involved in the Danish pilot. We should empower a diverse range of healthcare professionals to undertake these conversations to maximise the strengths of the process and increase uptake.5

Finally, although training (a lecture) was mentioned within the study, the article did not draw conclusions regarding the effectiveness of the training programme itself or identify areas for improvement.

Overall, this study represents an important insight towards the implementation of ReSPECT within Denmark and provides guidance into the usage of structured emergency care planning discussions.
