Two Decades of Therapeutic Trials in Supratentorial Intracerebral Haemorrhage: Lessons from Failure and Success
Authors: Xuan Wu, Shiling Chen, Zhouping Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) remains associated with high mortality and long-term disability worldwide. Since the publication of the Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Haemorrhage (STICH) in 2005, numerous randomized controlled trials have evaluated surgical, pharmacological, and physiological interventions, yet most have not demonstrated consistent improvements in functional outcomes.MethodsWe critically reviewed major randomized controlled trials and influential studies of acute sICH treatment published over the past two decades, with particular attention to surgical strategies, physiological and pharmacological interventions, treatment timing, patient selection, and sex-related biological heterogeneity.ResultsMost conventional surgical and single-target medical interventions yielded neutral or modest results. Recent trials suggest that carefully selected surgery, early intervention, and bundled protocolized care may provide greater benefit. However, optimal treatment timing and sex-specific biological differences remain insufficiently investigated.ConclusionsFurther progress in acute sICH treatment will require time-sensitive, mechanism-informed, anatomically tailored, and sex-aware multimodal treatment strategies rather than isolated interventions.Keywords: blood pressure management, haematoma expansion, haematoma evacuation, multimodal treatment, neuroprotection, spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage

Introduction

A dark cloud called spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage (sICH) has long hung over the heads of people worldwide. sICH is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for 28.8% of all strokes (Refs. 1, 2). Despite advances in prevention and acute care, 30-day mortality remains high at 30%–40%, with many survivors experiencing severe neurological deficits. Unlike the significant therapeutic advances achieved in ischaemic stroke with reperfusion therapies, the treatment of sICH faces greater challenges, with numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) showing neutral effects on functional outcomes (Ref. 3–10). The Surgical Treatment for Ischemic Heart Failure (STICH) trial, the first large-scale RCT published in 2005, and subsequent trials have explored surgical and medical interventions, such as haematoma evacuation and intensive blood pressure (BP) management, with limited efficacy (Refs. 4, 6–9).

More recently, selected trials have reported benefits. The Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Haemorrhage (ENRICH) trial, focusing on early minimally invasive surgery for lobar haemorrhage, showed positive outcomes in lobar sICH but no benefit in deep sICH (Ref. 11). Similarly, the Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trial (INTERACT)-3 trial, which implemented a bundled care strategy targeting BP, glucose and coagulation management, demonstrated modest functional improvement, though maintaining protocol targets was challenging in routine practice (Ref. 7, 12, 13). The INTERACT4 trial introduced early prehospital BP reduction, showing promise in sICH, highlighting the importance of timing and accurate stroke classification (Ref. 14).

The neutral results from previous RCTs and challenges with complex bundled interventions point to a key therapeutic gap. Unlike ischaemic stroke, which is approached from the concept of the ischaemic penumbra, sICH involves dynamic, multifactorial processes like haematoma expansion, toxicity, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and blood–brain barrier disruption, for which no single target has yet been identified (Refs. 2, 15–18).

This review synthesizes therapeutic strategies in sICH, emphasizing that haematoma evacuation alone is insufficient for recovery. Effective treatment likely requires timely intervention to prevent secondary injury, combining haematoma evacuation with neuroprotection and physiological optimization. Non-surgical management also appears more effective when early, targeted, and system-level care is applied rather than relying on single therapies. This review outlines lessons from past trials and highlights future directions in research and multimodal treatment for sICH.

Search strategy and selection criteria

To identify high-quality RCTs related to the treatment of intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), we conducted a search in PubMed using the following strategy: (intracerebral haemorrhage [Title] OR cerebral haemorrhage [Title]) AND (treatment [Title/Abstract] OR therapy [Title/Abstract] OR intervention [Title/Abstract]), and applied PubMed’s ‘Randomized Controlled Trial’ filter, resulting in a total of 241 identified articles. To further categorize the studies, we applied the additional keywords ‘surgery’ and ‘rehabilitation’, thereby identifying 38 surgery-related studies and 11 rehabilitation-related studies. The remaining potential pharmacological studies (n = 92) underwent further manual screening to exclude studies on subarachnoid haemorrhage and other non-ICH conditions.

To assess the impact of gender and reproductive factors on ICH, we conducted an additional search for (‘intracerebral hemorrhage’ [Title/Abstract] OR ‘cerebral hemorrhage’ [Title/Abstract]) AND (gender differences [Title/Abstract] OR gender [Title/Abstract] OR women [Title/Abstract]), limiting the search to studies published within the past five years.

Ultimately, 12 surgical studies, 2 rehabilitation studies, 12 pharmacological studies and 5 studies related to gender or reproduction were included.

Surgical treatment

Surgical evacuation has long been considered a rational strategy for sICH, aiming to relieve mass effect, reduce haematoma toxicity and mitigate secondary brain injury (Refs. 2, 18). Over the past two decades, surgical techniques have evolved from conventional open craniotomy toward minimally invasive, image-guided and tissue-sparing approaches (Refs. 19, 20). Despite these conceptual advances, consistent functional benefit has remained difficult to demonstrate in randomized trials (Refs. 21, 22). A summary of key surgical RCTs is provided in Table 1.

Early single-centre studies of endoscopic evacuation emerged in the late 1980s, suggesting that lobar sICH may benefit from early endoscopic haematoma evaluation (Ref. 23). This finding laid the groundwork for subsequent RCTs, but they were limited by small sample sizes (Ref. 23). The first large multicentre evaluation was the International Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Haemorrhage (STICH) trial, which compared early surgery (primarily craniotomy) with conservative therapies across a broad sICH population and showed no overall improvement in functional outcome (Ref. 24). Although STICH established the feasibility of surgical randomization in ICH, its pragmatic design, heterogeneous inclusion criteria and substantial inter-centre variability likely diluted potential benefit (Ref. 24). Subgroup analysis suggested that the closer the location of the ICH to the cortex, the more likely the patient will benefit from surgery (Ref. 24). Thus, STICH II subsequently restricted enrolment to patients with lobar sICH, thereby improving population homogeneity in order to obtain statistical significance (Ref. 8). However, despite refined selection, the STICH II study also failed to demonstrate a significant functional advantage of early craniotomy over conservative management in lobar sICH, suggesting that patient selection alone could not overcome the tissue damage caused by the surgical procedure associated with craniotomy (Ref. 8).

Subsequent research shifted its focus to minimally invasive strategies aimed at significantly reducing hematoma volume while minimizing surgical trauma (Refs. 6, 25). Stereotactic aspiration combined with urokinase was explored in the Stereotactic Treatment of Intracerebral Hematoma by Means of a Plasminogen Activator (SICHPA) trial, which demonstrated modest haematoma reduction but no improvement in mortality or functional outcome (Ref. 26). These early catheter-based approaches nonetheless established procedural feasibility and laid the foundation for the Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Recombinant Tissuetype Plasminogen Activator for Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation (MISTIE) program. The phase II MISTIE study confirmed safety and technical reproducibility. MISTIE III prespecifying a residual haematoma target (≤15 mL), which introduced a key concept influencing the efficacy of haematoma in minimally invasive surgery (Refs. 6, 27). Although the overall primary endpoint was neutral, secondary and per-protocol analyses showed that patients achieving the evacuation target experienced better functional outcomes, identifying residual clot burden as a key mediator of surgical efficacy (Ref. 25).

More recently, modern minimally invasive strategies have emphasized early intervention, standardized operative corridors and tissue preservation. The Intraoperative Stereotactic Computed Tomography-Guided Endoscopic Surgery (ICES) trial demonstrated the safety and feasibility of CT-guided endoscopic evacuation. Then the The Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Haemorrhage (ENRICH) trial provided the first large-scale randomized evidence suggesting functional benefit from early minimally invasive parafascicular surgery, particularly in lobar haemorrhage, while no benefit was observed in deep sICH (Ref. 28). These findings highlight the importance of anatomical selection and reinforce the concept that surgical benefit is location dependent (Ref. 11). The subsequent Minimally Invasive Surgery vs Medical Management Alone for Intracerebral Hemorrhage (MIND) study failed to demonstrate improved long-term functional outcomes, possibly due to the predominance of deep haemorrhages, delayed intervention (ICH<72 h), and insufficient attention to perihaematomal neuroprotection.

Unlike surgical haematoma evacuation strategy, which inevitably cause tissue damage, the Swiss trial of decompressive craniectomy versus best medical treatment of spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral haemorrhage (SWITCH) trial evaluated decompressive craniectomy without targeted hematoma evacuation, which can alleviate the increased intracranial pressure caused by sICH (Ref. 29). SWITCH demonstrated no improvement in functional outcomes compared with best medical therapy, underscoring the limited efficacy of decompression alone and suggesting that reduction of intracranial pressure without effective clot removal is insufficient to alter the biological course of sICH (Ref. 29). Together with previous trials, these results indicate that surgery must directly address haematoma burden rather than provide non-specific decompression.

Despite decades of investigation, most surgical trials have yielded neutral or modest results. After reviewing these study results, we have found that haematoma evacuation surgeries require a careful balance between the benefits of removing the haematoma and the tissue damage caused by the surgical procedure. The additional trauma caused by the surgical procedure is likely to offset any benefits gained from haematoma removal. This explains why craniotomy offers no benefit whatsoever to patients with sICH, as the surgical trauma is too great compared with minimally invasive surgery; it also explains the differences resulting from the location of the haematoma: for patients with deep sICH who require haematoma evacuation, the damage caused during the procedure is far greater than that experienced by patients with lobar sICH.

Moreover, maximizing haematoma removal is critical to patient prognosis, and endoscopic minimally invasive surgery offers significant advantages in this regard. Unlike stereotactic minimally invasive aspiration, which is less effective for irregular haematomas, endoscopic surgery ensures nearly complete removal of the haematoma (Ref. 6).

In addition, prompt and effective removal of the haematoma is crucial to prevent any secondary damage that can be caused by it; if it is not removed in time, the patient is unlikely to benefit from surgery. However, in many trials, surgery occurred relatively late: STICH required operation within 24 h of randomization rather than onset, MISTIE III allowed intervention within 72 h, and STICH II and ICES within 48 h. Only ENRICH implemented surgery within 24 h of symptom onset (Refs. 6, 8, 11, 24, 28, 30). Delayed intervention may miss the opportunity to interrupt evolving secondary injury cascades, whereas excessively early surgery risks rebleeding if haematoma has not stabilized (Refs. 31, 32). We propose a potential strategy – ‘stability-gated early evacuation’ – where surgery is performed early but only after physiological parameters like BP and glucose are rigorously controlled, which were highlighted by mediation analyses from the INTERACT3 trial (Ref. 13). This ‘stability-gated early evacuation’ may create a safer window for earlier and more effective haematoma evacuation. However, this strategy has yet to be directly tested in RCTs.

Finally, perioperative physiological management has often been inconsistent. Hypertension, hyperglycaemia, fever and coagulopathy exacerbate secondary injury, yet these factors were frequently uncontrolled or non-protocolized in surgical trials. Recent bundled care strategies demonstrate that systemic optimization may be essential for maximizing the benefit of focal interventions, suggesting that surgery alone is unlikely to succeed without coordinated physiological management.

In conclusion, current evidence indicates that haematoma evacuation surgery alone does not automatically translate into neurological recovery. Although removal of the clot may reduce primary mechanical injury, meaningful functional improvement likely requires simultaneous minimizing surgical tissue trauma while mitigating secondary pathological processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, oedema and metabolic dysfunction. Future progress will depend on earlier and adequately targeted evacuation, reliable achievement of low residual hematoma volumes, tissue-sparing operative techniques and integration with standardized perioperative physiological optimization. Advances in neuroimaging, tractography and molecular profiling may further enable precision selection of patients most likely to benefit from surgical intervention.

Non-surgical treatment

Blood pressure reduction treatment

Elevated BP is common in the acute phase of sICH and is strongly associated with haematoma expansion and poor clinical outcomes (Refs. 33–35). Accordingly, early BP reduction has been widely investigated as a cornerstone of non-surgical management. Timely BP control may limit ongoing bleeding, reduce intracranial pressure, and mitigate secondary injury.

The the Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trial (INTERACT) program provided the earliest large-scale randomized evidence supporting the safety and feasibility of intensive BP lowering. In INTERACT1, patients presenting within 6 h of symptom onset were randomized to intensive treatment (target systolic BP <140 mmHg within 1 h) versus guideline-recommended management (<180 mmHg), primarily using intravenous antihypertensives, demonstrating short-term safety and feasibility (Ref. 33). Building on this, INTERACT2 randomized 2,839 patients within 6 h to the same targets. BP reduction was achieved using intravenous agents such as urapidil, nicardipine or labetalol, followed by oral therapy after stabilization (Ref. 4). The achieved systolic BP difference between groups was modest (~10–15 mmHg), and reduction was gradual and sustained. Although mortality was unchanged, INTERACT2 demonstrated a modest but statistically significant improvement in functional outcome on ordinal modified Rankin scale analysis, suggesting that early, moderate and well-tolerated BP lowering may confer functional benefit without compromising cerebral perfusion (Ref. 4).

In contrast, the Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage (ATACH)-II trial tested a more aggressive strategy (Ref. 9). Patients within 4.5 h of onset were randomized to an intensive target of 110–139 mmHg versus a standard target of 140–179 mmHg, with BP control achieved predominantly through continuous intravenous nicardipine infusion (Ref. 9). Despite more rapid and pronounced BP reduction, ATACH-II showed no functional benefit and a higher incidence of renal adverse events (Ref. 9). These results suggest that excessively aggressive and rapid BP lowering may increase systemic complications without improving neurological outcomes.

Together, INTERACT2 and ATACH-II illustrate a narrow therapeutic window for BP management in sICH: moderate lowering towards approximately 140 mmHg appears safe and potentially beneficial, whereas more intensive targets may be harmful. Meta-analyses indicate that intensive BP reduction consistently reduces haematoma expansion but does not reliably translate into improved long-term functional outcomes, highlighting the importance of treatment intensity and implementation.

Beyond single-target strategies, INTERACT3 evaluated a goal-directed, bundled care approach integrating BP control with standardized management of hyperglycaemia, fever and anticoagulation reversal (Ref. 7). This multicomponent protocol significantly improved functional outcomes at 6 months, suggesting that BP management may be most effective when embedded within coordinated physiological optimization rather than delivered in isolation (Ref. 7).

Extending intervention even earlier, INTERACT4 initiated BP reduction in the prehospital setting (Ref. 14). Patients with suspected stroke and systolic BP ≥150 mmHg received ambulance-based intravenous urapidil targeting 130–140 mmHg within 2 h of onset (Ref. 14). While no overall benefit was observed, subgroup analyses suggested potential improvement in sICH but not ischaemic stroke, emphasizing the importance of ultra-early treatment and accurate stroke classification (Ref. 14). These findings highlight the importance of timing and accurate stroke classification for optimizing treatment efficacy.

Drug treatment of ICH

sICH is associated with high mortality and long-term disability. Pharmacological strategies for sICH have primarily focused on two therapeutic domains: (i) limiting hematoma expansion and (ii) attenuating secondary brain injury (Ref. 18). The latter involves multiple pathological processes, including neuroinflammation, immune-cell dysregulation, oxidative stress, and blood–brain barrier disruption (Refs. 19–20).

Agents targeting haematoma expansion

Early pharmacological strategies focused on limiting ongoing bleeding and haematoma growth given the strong association between haematoma expansion and poor outcome (Refs. 33–35). Recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa) was among the first agents evaluated in large randomized trials (Ref. 36). Although rFVIIa significantly reduced haematoma expansion when administered within a narrow hyperacute window, it failed to improve survival or functional outcome and raised concerns regarding thromboembolic complications (Ref. 37). These findings demonstrated that modifying haematoma growth alone is insufficient to translate into meaningful functional recovery.

Similarly, antifibrinolytic therapy with tranexamic acid was investigated in large phase III trials (Ref. 38). Although tranexamic acid modestly reduced early hematoma expansion in some patients, no clear improvement in long-term functional outcome was observed (Ref. 38). Together, these studies suggest that pharmacological suppression of haematoma expansion, particularly when administered beyond very early time windows, yields limited clinical benefit when applied as a standalone intervention.

The failure of anti-expansion strategies highlights several constraints: the extremely narrow therapeutic window required to meaningfully alter bleeding dynamics, the inability of pharmacological agents to reverse established mass effect or hematoma toxicity and the likelihood that downstream secondary injury mechanisms continue to drive disability even when early expansion is attenuated.

Agents targeting secondary brain injury

A second major class of investigational therapies for sICH has focused on limiting secondary brain injury, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, iron-mediated toxicity and blood–brain barrier disruption.

Iron chelation with deferoxamine (DFO) has been investigated based on strong preclinical evidence implicating haemoglobin degradation products in perihaematomal injury. Early-phase trials demonstrated safety and feasibility but limited efficacy signals. In the phase 2 i-DEF trial, DFO did not improve the primary functional outcome at 90 days in the overall population (Refs. 39–41). However, post hoc analyses suggested haematoma volume as a potential effect modifier: Patients with moderate haematoma volumes (approximately 10–30 mL) showed improved 180-day functional outcomes, whereas those with small or large haematomas derived no benefit (Refs. 42, 43). These findings indicate that DFO may provide selective benefit in biologically permissive subgroups and highlight the importance of mechanism-informed patient selection and timing (Ref. 44). Therefore, the Fifth INTEnsive pReventing Secondary Injury in Acute Cerebral Haemorrhage Trial Within ACT-GLOBAL (INTERACT5) aims to evaluate the efficacy of deferoxamine/colchicine in cases of sICH exceeding 10 mL in volume, and is currently recruiting patients.

Other anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory approaches have yielded significant positive results. Small proof-of-concept studies of fingolimod showed reductions in perihaematomal oedema and short-term neurological improvement, but larger clinical trials are needed (Ref. 45). Minocycline demonstrated safety without functional benefit (Ref. 46). The CHAIN trial, evaluating a multimodal neuroprotective strategy, also failed to improve long-term outcomes (Ref. 47).

Several factors likely explain the limited success of these therapies. Persistent clot burden and mass effect may overwhelm neuroprotective effects in the absence of haematoma removal, while blood–brain barrier disruption restricts drug delivery. Moreover, substantial inter-individual heterogeneity in haematoma characteristics and inflammatory responses may dilute treatment effects.

Although many agents modify intermediate phenotypes such as haematoma expansion or oedema, these biological effects rarely translate into functional recovery. Collectively, these findings suggest that pharmacological therapy alone is insufficient and that future strategies will likely require integration with timely haematoma evacuation and standardized physiological management within multimodal, stage-specific treatment paradigms.

Rehabilitation treatment

Survivors of sICH frequently experience persistent motor, language, cognitive and neuropsychiatric impairments that contribute substantially to long-term disability and reduced quality of life. Accordingly, contemporary guidelines emphasize early initiation of rehabilitation in clinically stable patients, multidisciplinary team-based care, coordinated discharge planning and continuity across the care pathway as core components of high-quality ICH management (Ref. 48).

A central question in ICH rehabilitation is not whether rehabilitation is beneficial, but when and how intensively it should be delivered given concerns regarding neurological instability, blood pressure lability and risk of early deterioration in the hyperacute phase. A multicentre RCT conducted in China reported that commencing structured rehabilitation within 48 h of ICH onset improved survival and functional outcomes at 6 months compared with usual care, supporting the potential value of early, protocolized rehabilitation in selected hospitalized patients (Ref. 49).

However, ‘earlier’ should not be conflated with ‘more aggressive’. Evidence across stroke rehabilitation has increasingly highlighted that very early, high-dose mobilization may not uniformly benefit all patients and requires careful patient selection and dose titration; in ICH, this principle is particularly salient given the evolving secondary injury cascade and systemic instability that may limit tolerance to intensive early activity (Ref. 48). Thus, the emerging message is that early rehabilitation is best conceptualized as a stability-gated, stepwise program, beginning with early assessment and low-risk task engagement, followed by progressive intensification as physiological and neurological parameters stabilize, rather than a uniform ‘very early mobilization for all’ paradigm (Ref. 48).

Sex differences and reproductive considerations in sICH

Emerging evidence supports the notion that biological sex and reproductive status may influence the epidemiology, clinical presentation and outcomes of sICH (Refs. 50, 51). Large population-based cohorts consistently report sex-related differences in ICH incidence and mortality: Men experience higher incidence at younger ages, whereas women, particularly postmenopausal, show increasing risk later in life, suggesting a potential influence of hormonal milieu on cerebrovascular vulnerability. Epidemiological analyses indicate that male sex is associated with worse functional outcomes and higher case fatality rates in some cohorts, partly mediated by factors such as deeper haematoma location and more severe presentations (Ref. 52).

The role of sex hormones, especially oestrogen, as modulators of vascular integrity and neuroinflammation has been proposed, supported by experimental models showing oestrogen-mediated neuroprotection after haemorrhagic injury although clinical translation remains limited (Ref. 53). Importantly, major RCTs and basic research in sICH have not systematically stratified by sex or reproductive status, leaving unresolved whether treatment responses, including blood pressure reduction, surgical evacuation or neuroprotective interventions, differ meaningfully between men and women.

Women of reproductive age and pregnancy-associated ICH represent distinct subpopulations with unique pathophysiological triggers (e.g. preeclampsia, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction) and therapeutic considerations, such as antihypertensive safety and anaesthetic risk, yet evidence to guide tailored acute management is scarce (Ref. 54). Furthermore, postmenopausal hormonal changes may influence cerebral vessel structure and inflammatory responses, highlighting a biologically plausible but clinically underexplored modifier of sICH outcome.

These observations underscore a critical knowledge gap: Sex and reproductive status are potential effect modifiers in sICH pathophysiology and treatment response but have not been prospectively incorporated into trial design or stratified outcome analyses. Future trials should prospectively stratify randomization and statistical analysis plans by sex and collect data on reproductive status to refine precision treatment paradigms in sICH.

Discussion

Over the past two decades, therapeutic progress in sICH has lagged behind advances in ischaemic stroke treatment. Despite extensive international efforts, randomized trials across surgical and non-surgical domains have largely failed to deliver consistent or transformative improvements in long-term outcomes. As summarized in Figure 1 (drawn using Biorender, https://www.biorender.com/), both surgical evacuation strategies, from open craniotomy in STICH and STICH II to minimally invasive approaches in MISTIE III and ENRICH, and intensive BP reduction have produced, at best, modest and context-dependent benefits.

A consistent lesson has emerged: Single-modality interventions are insufficient. Surgery alone rarely improves outcomes without careful attention to timing, patient selection and adequate haematoma clearance, while isolated physiological targets such as BP control provide limited functional gains. These neutral results likely reflect the multifactorial and dynamically evolving pathophysiology of sICH, in which haematoma toxicity, inflammation, metabolic dysfunction and systemic stress interact to drive injury. Targeting a single pathway is therefore unlikely to meaningfully alter prognosis without integration into coordinated care. In this context, the INTERACT3 trial is instructive, demonstrating that a bundled, protocolized physiological management strategy can achieve modest but significant functional benefit, supporting system-level optimization over escalation of individual therapies.

Progress has also been hindered by limited mechanistic and translational research. Unlike ischaemic stroke, where the penumbra concept guided decades of innovation, ICH lacks a unifying biological framework. Key questions remain regarding optimal haematoma volume thresholds, surgical timing, BP targets and windows for immunomodulation.

Encouragingly, recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have provided new insights into the pathophysiology of sICH. Studies utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics have identified critical cellular and molecular responses to haemorrhagic injury. (Refs. 55–61) These findings, along with integrated multi-omics analyses, have revealed how metabolic dysfunction and inflammation drive secondary injury in perihaematomal tissues, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for future clinical trials (Refs. 57, 62). For instance, Zhou et al. used scRNA-seq, identified a pro-inflammatory C5aR1+ microglial subtype that exacerbates neuroinflammation and promotes the polarization of neurotoxic astrocytes via the LGALS9–CD44 pathway and through the CCL3/CCL5–CCR1 axis, thereby exacerbating blood–brain barrier disruption and cerebral oedema following sICH. This finding may provide guidance for the development of precision therapies targeting the C5a–C5aR1 pathway like avacopan, rather than broadly inhibiting neuroinflammation using drugs such as minocycline (Ref. 61).

All in all, we call upon clinicians and researchers alike to join forces in the pursuit of meaningful breakthroughs for the treatment of ICH despite the setbacks encountered in past efforts. Through collaboration and innovation, we can strive to uncover the potential for improving outcomes in this challenging field.
