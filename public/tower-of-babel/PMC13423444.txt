Clinical Outcomes and Recurrence Prevention in Laparoscopic Wells Rectopexy for Rectal Prolapse through Mesh Trimming and Fixation Techniques
Authors: Takashi Ueno, Keita Ishido, Tomotaka Kuraya, Tomoko Mizota, Kunishige Okamura, Yoshihiro Murakami, Yuji Sasamura, Satoshi Hirano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: Laparoscopic Wells rectopexy (LWR) is a standard procedure for rectal prolapse; however, recurrence and mesh-related complications remain clinical challenges, particularly in elderly patients with small pelvic anatomy. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of a modified LWR technique featuring intraoperative mesh tailoring and a hybrid fixation method. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 29 patients who underwent modified LWR between October 2014 and June 2025. Technical modifications included intraoperative trimming of the polypropylene mesh to fit each patient's pelvic dimensions and a hybrid fixation technique using both sacral tacks and continuous barbed sutures. Patient demographics, operative outcomes, and recurrence rates were evaluated. Results: The study cohort consisted of 29 patients (median age, 78 years; median body mass index, 20.3 kg/m2). All procedures were completed laparoscopically, with a median operative time of 171 min and minimal blood loss (median, 0 mL). The mesh was tailored to a median size of 12 × 8 cm, and fixation was performed using a median of eight tacks combined with barbed sutures. The median postoperative hospital stay was 4 days. One patient (3.4%) experienced a minor complication (Clavien–Dindo Grade II). During a median follow-up of 190 days, recurrence occurred in only one patient (3.4%) at 30 months postoperatively. Conclusions: Our modified LWR technique, featuring individualized mesh tailoring and robust hybrid fixation, is safe and effective for high-risk, elderly Asian patients with small pelvic frames. Keywords: rectal prolapse, laparoscopic Wells rectopexy, mesh tailoring, barbed suture, recurrence

Introduction

Rectal prolapse is a pelvic floor disorder that predominantly affects older women, and its incidence is increasing with the aging global population[1,2]. Surgical intervention remains the only curative treatment and is broadly categorized into perineal and abdominal procedures. Although perineal procedures are less invasive, they are associated with higher recurrence rates compared with abdominal approaches[1]. Recently, laparoscopic rectopexy (LRP) has become widely accepted as a standard treatment option owing to its minimally invasive nature and favorable curative outcomes[3,4]. Several LRP techniques have been described, including suture rectopexy, posterior mesh rectopexy (the Wells procedure), and ventral mesh rectopexy (VMR). While suture rectopexy eliminates the need of artificial materials, meta-analyses have demonstrated a significantly higher recurrence rate compared with mesh rectopexy[2,5]. Conversely, VMR has been reported to better preserve autonomic nerve and reduce postoperative constipation; however it requires advanced surgical skills[2,6].

Among these techniques, laparoscopic Wells rectopexy (LWR) remains widely performed in many institutions owing to its reliable rectal fixation and standardized approach[2,7]. Our institution has also adopted LWR as the standard posterior fixation method for rectal prolapse. While this procedure offers high curative potential, several clinical concerns remain. For example, there are risks of mesh-related complications such as erosion into the rectum or infection, along with postoperative defecation disorders including symptomatic constipation[2,8,9]. Furthermore, maintaining a low recurrence rate, which typically ranges from 0%–7% in the literature, continues to be a primary focus[2,10]. Controlling recurrence and complications is particularly critical for maintaining the quality of life in older patients.

Regarding the mechanisms of recurrence, mesh dimensions and fixation security are likely critical factors. Previous reports have highlighted the general benefits of mesh fixation and the potential risks of recurrence associated with “mesh folding” or redundant material[3,8]. However, few studies have examined detailed technical modifications within the context of LWR, particularly regarding standardization of intraoperative mesh trimming dimensions and hybrid fixation techniques. The use of standardized mesh products designed for Western populations in patients with smaller pelvic anatomy may result in excessive material, preventing flat integration against the presacral space and potentially increasing the recurrence risk. Furthermore, although the utility of barbed sutures for stable fixation has been discussed in pelvic floor reconstructive procedures such as sacrocolpopexy[11], their specific application and contribution to recurrence prevention in LWR have received limited attention.

To address these issues, our institution has standardized a specific surgical approach: intraoperative trimming of the mesh to an optimal size tailored to each patient's pelvic morphology, combined with a hybrid fixation method using both sacral tacks and continuous barbed sutures. This study presents the short- and mid-term clinical outcomes of these modifications and examines their impact on improving the safety and durability of LWR.

Methods

Patients

Between October 2014 and June 2025, 31 patients underwent laparoscopic surgery for rectal prolapse at our institution. After excluding two patients treated with suture rectopexy without mesh, 29 patients who underwent LWR were retrospectively analyzed. In this study, these patients were considered high-risk surgical candidates, defined as elderly patients (typically aged 75 years or older) with clinical frailty or multiple comorbidities. We evaluated patient demographics, operative factors (operative time, blood loss, mesh size, and number of tacks), and postoperative outcomes. Clinical data, including complications and recurrence, were collected. The median follow-up period was 190 days (range, 5–2,096 days). This study was approved by the Asahikawa City Hospital Institutional Review Board, which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective design.

Surgical technique

Positioning and dissection

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia in the lithotomy position using a standard five-port configuration. Posterior dissection was carried out along the presacral plane while preserving the hypogastric nerves (Figure 1A). Lateral dissection extended to the pelvic floor with preservation of the lateral ligaments. Adequate rectal mobilization was confirmed before fixation.

Surgical technique for laparoscopic Wells rectopexy (LWR).

(A) Posterior dissection is performed along the presacral plane while preserving the hypogastric nerves. (B) The mesh is tailored intraoperatively to fit the patient’s specific pelvic dimensions. (C) Initial sacral fixation is performed at the sacral promontory using a tacking device. (D) The mesh, proper rectal fascia, and pelvic peritoneum are secured together and retroperitonealized using continuous barbed sutures.

Mesh tailoring

In all cases, we used a 15 × 10 cm polypropylene mesh (Versatex™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) as the base material. The mesh was tailored extracorporeally after precise intraoperative measurements (Figure 1B). Anatomical landmarks for trimming included the sacral promontory (cranial), the levator ani muscles (caudal), and the lateral margins of the peritoneal incision (lateral) to ensure the mesh remained fully retroperitonealized while allowing secure fixation to the presacral fascia. This customization accommodates the smaller pelvic cavities commonly observed in older Asian women, ensuring flat integration and preventing kinking or dead space.

Fixation and peritoneal closure

The mesh was first anchored to the longitudinal ligament of the sacral promontory using a tacking device (Figure 1C). Tacks were placed at approximately 2–3 cm intervals to prevent mesh displacement. To ensure safety, the anatomy of the presacral veins and hypogastric nerves was confirmed on preoperative CT, and tacks were placed under direct visualization to avoid vascular or nerve injury. Subsequently, the mesh, proper rectal fascia, and pelvic peritoneum were secured together using two 15-cm, 3-0 barbed sutures (V-Loc™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) (Figure 1D). This technique ensures secure fixation with uniform tension distribution while simultaneously retroperitonealizing the mesh.

Perioperative Management

Our institutional protocol prioritizes early recovery (ERAS), including mobilization within 4 hours postoperatively and starting oral intake on the evening of surgery.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study cohort consisted of 29 patients (4 men, 25 women). The median age was 78 years, with a high proportion of older patients (range: 31–91) (Table 1). Notably, the cohort included several high-risk patients with comorbidities such as dementia and psychiatric disorders. The median body mass index was 20.3 kg/m2, indicating a lean population. Five patients (17.2%) had a history of prior perineal surgery.

Operative outcomes

All procedures were completed laparoscopically, with a median operative time of 171 min. Intraoperative blood loss was minimal (median, 0 ml) (Table 2). Meshes were tailored to a median size of 12 × 8 cm to fit the narrow pelvic cavity. Fixation was consistently performed using a median of eight tacks and continuous barbed sutures (V-Loc™).

Postoperative Outcomes and Recurrence Analysis

The median postoperative hospital stay was 4 days (range: 1–10) (Table 3). Clavien–Dindo Grade ≥ II complications occurred in one patient (3.4%), who experienced temporary diarrhea. Recurrence was observed in one patient (3.4%) at 30 months postoperatively. This patient, who had initially presented with complete rectal prolapse, experienced a mucosal and full-thickness recurrence. The patient underwent multiple mucosal ligation (Gant–Miwa procedure) for the recurrence. This procedure was chosen because the presence of the previous mesh and associated adhesions made a second abdominal approach technically challenging and potentially higher risk.

Discussion

In this study, our modified LWR technique achieved a low recurrence rate of 3.4%, which is favorable compared to reported rates of 0% to 10% for abdominal approaches[2,10]. Considering that perineal procedures often demonstrate recurrence rates exceeding 20%[1,4], our findings support LWR as a highly curative treatment option.

Our modified LWR technique is positioned as an optimized approach for patients with narrow pelvic anatomy, such as elderly Asian women. Compared to established procedures (e.g., standard Wells or ventral mesh rectopexy) that often use standardized mesh sizes, our technique prioritizes individualized tailoring to prevent mesh “kinking,” which has been identified as a potential risk factor for recurrence in previous literature[3]. Furthermore, the hybrid fixation method offers a unique balance: tacks provide secure anchoring to the presacral fascia, while barbed sutures allow for uniform rectal fixation and simultaneous peritoneal closure.

The primary focus of this study was recurrence prevention through anatomical optimization. First, we emphasized intraoperative mesh trimming. Standard mesh sizes are often excessive for small-statured patients, who were common in our cohort (median height, 146 cm). An oversized mesh may cause “kinking” or folding in the presacral space. Such folds prevent tight integration between the mesh and the rectum, potentially creating a “sliding” effect that leads to recurrence[3,8]. By trimming the mesh to a size optimized for each patient, we ensured a flat and secure interface, which is crucial for long-term stability.

Second, the fixation strength was maximized using a hybrid method. While tacks provide robust initial anchoring to the sacrum, the rectal side is secured with continuous barbed sutures. This distributes tension evenly across the rectal wall. We hypothesize that mesh folding and the “cheese-cutter effect” may contribute to recurrence, and our technique is designed to mitigate these potential issues[11,12]. This stable fixation ensures that the rectum remains suspended even as the patient resumes normal activity. In our single recurrence case, a detailed review of the surgical video revealed no obvious technical issues regarding dissection, mesh size determination, fixation, or peritoneal closure. Instead, the review suggested that the recurrence might be attributed to severe tissue friability or pelvic floor attenuation rather than technical failure. This underscores that while our technique provides strong mechanical support, biological factors remain a challenge in very elderly patients.

Furthermore, our data revealed that LWR is safe even for patients with cognitive or psychiatric comorbidities. Regarding the choice of surgical approach, abdominal procedures are increasingly considered even for high-risk patients if they are fit for general anesthesia. This is primarily owing to their significantly lower recurrence rates compared to perineal approaches[1,2]. When comparing laparoscopic and open abdominal surgery, previous studies have shown no significant differences in recurrence rates or functional outcomes[2,4]; however, the laparoscopic approach offers clear advantages in terms of shorter hospital stays, reduced postoperative inflammatory response, and lower complication rates[2,13]. Consistent with these findings, our results showed minimal blood loss and short hospital stays. These outcomes demonstrate that our tailored LWR approach minimizes surgical stress and allows for rapid recovery, even in vulnerable populations.

A rare but noteworthy concern associated with long-term mesh placement is the potential for serious complications. Although extremely uncommon, cases of rectal cancer arising near the site of mesh fixation have been reported[8,14]. Furthermore, long-term placement of synthetic materials carries inherent risks such as mesh erosion into the intestinal lumen[15] and the worsening of postoperative defecatory dysfunction[16]. While no such complications were observed in our study, the use of tailored, minimal-volume mesh and long-term surveillance remain essential. Considering these potential long-term risks, the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) guidelines emphasize that the choice of surgical procedure should be individualized[4]. Therefore, the use of mesh should be determined through thorough informed consent, ensuring that patients fully understand both the clinical benefits and the potential for long-term complications.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single-arm study with a small sample size, making it hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The favorable outcomes may reflect surgeon experience or patient selection. Second, we lack preoperative and postoperative functional scores (e.g., Wexner score), limiting our ability to objectively assess functional outcomes. Third, our cohort consisted primarily of lean, elderly Asian women (median BMI 20.3, height 146 cm); thus, the results may not be directly generalizable to Western populations with higher BMIs or different pelvic anatomy. Finally, the median follow-up was relatively short (190 days), and further long-term prospective studies are required to evaluate the statistical correlation between mesh dimensions and clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, we report our institutional experience with LWR for rectal prolapse. By tailoring the mesh size through intraoperative trimming and employing a robust hybrid fixation technique using sacral tacks and continuous barbed sutures, we achieved favorable outcomes with minimal complications. This modified approach ensures flat mesh integration and prevents mechanical failure, making it a safe and effective treatment strategy particularly suitable for older Asian patients with smaller pelvic frames.
