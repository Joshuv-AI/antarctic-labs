A study on the efficiency of supercritical CO2 fracturing in hot dry rocks using coupled finite-discrete element method
Authors: Zeinab Aliabadian, Mansour Sharafisafa, Mojtaba Bahaaddini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite widespread use, reservoir stimulation by water hydraulic fracturing has drawbacks, including difficulties in areas with water shortages. To address challenges associated with hydraulic fracturing, supercritical carbon dioxide (ScCO2) fracturing has been proposed as a suitable alternative to address water-use issues. The main characteristic of ScCO2 is its low viscosity at supercritical conditions, which makes it particularly useful as a working fluid for stimulation and fracture generation. This study utilizes a 2D fully coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) model, developed using the combined finite-discrete element method (FDEM), as the tool for fluid injection in hot dry rock (HDR). Two different models are considered: the continuum model and fracture models with discrete fracture networks (DFN). First, the employed method is verified against some available theoretical and experimental data to validate its capabilities in this regard. Once verified, models are analyzed and three influencing and controlling factors are examined: in-situ stresses, fluid viscosity, and fluid flow rate. The results show that the performance of ScCO2 injection is highly dependent on the magnitude and difference of ground stresses. It is revealed that ScCO2 performs better in anisotropic stress fields, whereas its efficiency in isotropic stress fields is lower. It is shown that changes in ScCO2 viscosity have negligible effects on fracture development patterns and breakdown pressure. In addition, fluid flow rate plays a crucial role in both breakdown pressure and the ultimate fracture pattern. The higher the flow rate, the more fluid-driven fractures are generated, the higher the breakdown pressure, and the shorter the time to reach it. Results indicate that a higher fluid flow rate is not recommended for ScCO2 injection, as this change is less efficient at improving fluid flow. In fractured reservoirs, 12 interaction types between ScCO2 and pre-existing fractures are identified; some are newly identified, such as branching at fracture tips and rebranching, making this fluid more efficient than other working fluids. Also, comparisons are made between ScCO2 and water injection. The results indicate that ScCO2 generates many more fractures, longer fracture lengths, lower breakdown pressures, and longer post-breakdown pressure times. The longer post-breakdown pressure means more stable fluid flow in the fractures, an extended fluid-immersed zone, and thus a larger stimulated area.Keywords: Supercritical carbon dioxide; hot dry rock, Finite-discrete element method, Discrete fracture networkSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

As one of the promising resources for renewable energy, geothermal energy is attracting more attention to overcome the limitations associated with the fuel energy market. Geothermal energy resources are capable of making a considerable contribution to the sustainable energy market and assist in reducing carbon emissions. Among the geothermal energy resources, hot, dry rocks (HDR) are a great source of energy that can be found in some specific areas and depths. HDR are dense rocks and are buried at a depth of about 3 to 10 km and have an extraordinary potential to supply heat and energy because of their abundant capacity and high temperature1–3. Their temperature can surpass 150 °C and are characterized by some features such as being safe, low carbon, clean, and efficient. Moreover, they have low permeability content and a very low amount of water or steam4. However, the power generation efficiency of an HDR geothermal resource is very low because of the limited heat exchange extent between the injecting fluid and hot rock due to the tight rock texture and considerably low permeability of the rock mass with a significantly reduced rate of heat exchange between the two mediums5,6. In order to harness the maximum capacity of these energy resources, methods to artificially stimulate these reservoirs are in great demand. This goal is achieved by high-pressure fluid injection to the HRD to generate artificial fractures to improve the conductivity of the fluid, resulting in easy extraction of heat energy from the complex fracture network via cold injecting fluid and recovery of the hot fluid, which is heated by the hot rock exposure7,8. This kind of system is called an enhanced geothermal system (EGS). Fluid injection to create artificial fractures is called hydraulic fracturing which is a common way in HDR stimulation to improve the fluid flow. Nevertheless, it remains a big challenge to enhance the heat extraction rate due to the very low connection between the injection and production walls, loss of fluid injection, and challenges such as early thermal breakthrough or short circuit of heat9,10. Furthermore, some other crucial factors play a vital role in the efficiency of fluid injection in an HDR such as ground stress conditions, distribution of natural pre-existing fractures such as discrete fracture networks (DFN), aperture of fractures, type of working fluid, etc., which impact on the hydraulic connectivity between the injecting and producing walls.

Water based hydraulic fracturing has shown to impose some limitations which makes this method unsuitable for some fundamental reasons. Gradual change from conventional resources to unconventional resources has led to appear disadvantages of hydraulic fracturing. The development of unconventional reservoirs often needs high water consumption which makes it hard applicable in areas having water shortages. Furthermore, hydraulic fracturing easily causes clay minerals expansion, reducing the permeability of matrix. Moreover, considerable fracture productivity deterioration can be observed due to numerous residues in the fractures as a result of incomplete flowback. In addition, environmental pollution is inevitable since a huge volume of fracture fluid flowback with large turbidity and high volume of chemical oxygen is hard to deal with11–14. To mitigate the mentioned disadvantages, waterless or low water fracture technologies such as liquid nitrogen, hydrocarbon fracture stimulation, high energy gas stimulation, and self-popping fracture stimulation have been proposed15. However, these technologies bring some serious issues such as being flammable or explosive, ultra low temperature causing huge damage to steel injection pipes, short fracture length in high-energy gas stimulation causing preventing propped fractures, non-ideal transformation of phase state in fracturing fluid, blockage of matrix pore due to the leaking fracturing fluid, etc.16. These drawbacks have led to the emergence of novel methods, such as supercritical CO2 (ScCO2) fracturing.

Carbon dioxide (CO2) as an inert gas is a nontoxic and non-flammable and is noncorrosive in its pure state, making it a suitable candidate to overcome the aforementioned problems17. From the phase state perspective of CO2 in a fracture, the fracturing of CO2 can be particularly separated into a liquid state of CO2 fracturing and ScCO2 fracturing18. ScCO2 has a dual characteristic of both gas and liquid with low viscosity and surface tension, plus large density and coefficient of diffusion19, meaning that ScCO2 fracturing can gain an improved fracture stimulation impact than liquid CO2 fracturing20. ScCO2 brings some extraordinary advantages in minimizing reservoir damage and dependency on water access21. Among these advantages, CO2 has a high energy-enhancing impact, quick and full flow back after stimulation, and flow back fluid is easily treated. Moreover, a higher coefficient of diffusion and low viscosity properties are conducive to the ScCO2 flow and are capable of reducing pressure loss, making it suitable for generating complex fracture networks. Furthermore, large filtration of ScCO2 working fluid causes lower fracture initiation pressure compared to hydraulic fracturing, resulting in less fracture difficulty. To add more, ScCO2 is capable of dissolving in crude oil to decrease viscosity, replacing adsorbed methane on the surface of rocks, or reacting with hydrogen to create methane, enhancing recovery of oil and gas in the reservoir, realizing CO2 permanent storage, and mitigating the greenhouse effects22–25. Thus, ScCO2 stimulation and fracturing are more promising in developing unconventional reservoirs compared with water-less stimulation methods. To sum up, ScCo2 has been proven to have the following extraordinary benefits as a medium for heat transfer in hot reservoirs. First, ScCO2 has a high enough diffusion coefficient, which makes its flow rate reach one to six times of water. Second, its low dynamic viscosity and lesser energy loss while flowing cause easier penetration into the rock matrix than water. This working fluid is a suitable candidate for utilization in tight reservoirs with low coefficients of permeability. Third, the thermal expansion coefficient is bigger than water. Subject to the similar injecting temperature, the change in volume is larger than water. Furthermore, it can cause a stronger effect of thermosiphon, which may decrease the loss of energy effectively while pumping stages. Many HDRs are acidic and, therefore, are less reactive while containing CO2. As a result, unprovable consequences such as blockage and scaling are less likely to happen within the flow channels. Fifth, problems such as wastewater resources or limited access to water sources do not exist, and this can indirectly gain CO2 storage in geological strata. Theoretically, ScCO2 is much more suitable as a heat-transfer fluid in EGS than water26–30.

The use of ScCO₂ as a working fluid in Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) has attracted significant attention due to its favorable thermophysical properties, including low viscosity, high expansivity, and enhanced heat extraction efficiency compared with conventional water-based systems. Early conceptual work by Brown31 proposed replacing water with scCO₂ in hot dry rock geothermal reservoirs, while Pruess32 demonstrated the dual benefits of geothermal energy recovery and geological CO₂ sequestration. Subsequent reviews have highlighted the potential of ScCO₂-EGS technology and identified key challenges associated with reservoir stimulation, heat transfer, and long-term system performance33,34. The injection of cold ScCO₂ into hot fractured rock induces complex thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) interactions, including thermal contraction, pore-pressure changes, stress redistribution, and fracture propagation. Experimental and numerical investigations have shown that thermal stresses can significantly influence fracture initiation and propagation in hot dry rock reservoirs, particularly during hydraulic and scCO₂ fracturing operations35. Consequently, coupled THM modelling has become essential for evaluating reservoir behavior, wellbore stability, and permeability evolution under CO₂ injection conditions. Recent developments in fully coupled THM damage formulations have enabled the simulation of fracture growth, elastoplastic deformation, and progressive rock degradation in fractured geothermal reservoirs36–38. In particular, the THM elastoplastic damage framework proposed by Reppas et al.39 provides a robust methodology for capturing damage evolution associated with thermal loading and fluid-pressure variations, while their wellbore-scale analyses demonstrate the critical role of CO₂-induced cooling and pressure changes on wellbore integrity. These advances underscore the importance of incorporating coupled THM processes, damage evolution, and fracture mechanics into numerical models to accurately predict reservoir stimulation, fracture development, and wellbore stability in ScCO₂-based geothermal systems.

Among the factors influencing the efficiency of heat extraction from HDRs is pre-existing fractures, which play a crucial role in the development of hydraulic fractures (HF) and their connectivity. The distribution of such fractures determines the HF development pattern and can impact the efficiency of fluid injection considerably. The effect of natural pre-existing fractures on the performance of fluid injection and fracturing have been the subject of many studies. Wang et al.40 studied hydraulic fracture development in HDRs containing natural fractures through simulations and concluded that the natural fracture develops continuously along the intertwined fractures, resulting in formation of multiple secondary fluid routes and widening the scale of fluid channels. Sun et al.41 investigated the effect of in-situ stresses and geometry of fracture networks on heat transfer in damaged EGS. Their results showed that system of natural fractures critical stress condition may further develop the flow channels and promote the anomality of process of heat transfer. In another study by Wang et al.42, the evolution of damage in HDRs with natural fracture systems stimulated by fluid injection was studied. They figured out that HDRs natural fractures impact significantly the pattern of fracture networks after the hydraulic fracturing process.

Across the techniques used to investigate the HF development behaviour, numerical modelling has been widely employed because of their extraordinary strengths in modelling complicated mechanisms. A number of numerical techniques can be used to evaluate the mechanism of the development of the HFs under different situations and reservoir conditions. For example, the finite element method (FEM) is the most popular and utilized simulation technique. However, the FEM faces a basic problem while dealing with a discontinuity, and complicated zoning is usually needed, decreasing the modelling efficiency43. In order to comply with a discontinuity, a cohesive zone model (CZM) is an alternate to be programmed in the FEM to prevent remeshing; albeit the crack propagation path requires to be predetermined which limits the feasibility of the technique44. The CZM was then further developed to an unidentified crack path to get control of this challenge by simulating cohesive interface meshes among all the finite elements45. The extended finite element method (XFEM) has proven its extraordinary abilities in modelling discontinuities assuming a FEM simulation with no need for remeshing, causing increase the effectiveness of this technique compared to the traditional FEM. This simulation strategy has also been utilized to simulate the HF propagation in rocks46–49. Also, a finite volume method (FVM)-based phase-field model was used in50 to study CO2 fracture development in poroelastic media, showing effectiveness when dealing with a limited number of fractures. However, analyzing complicated discontinuity networks and 3D non-planar cracks by XFEM is still difficult and of little precision51. Another way is the displacement discontinuity method (DDM) which meshes the area of interest by discontinuity surface to allow for fracture propagation52. However, the DDM encounters basic problems when inhomogeneities and plastic deformation governs the behaviour of a rock mass. The discrete element method (DEM) is a robust and strong modelling technique proposed by53, which is able to simulate the propagation of cracks by employing bonded particles. Furthermore, DEM is capable of modeling fluid diffusion in discontinuities and the complicated mutual response between HFs and fluid flow in a jointed rock mass54,55. However, the computation capability of the DEM declines with the growth in the number of meshes. Therefore, the utilization of the DEM in large-scale daimonions is limited. There exist many other numerical methods; each carries some specific advantages and weaknesses while facing complicated discontinuity network development in rock masses. Thus, a robust yet powerful numerical tool needs to be selected with care considering the rock mass properties and complex interaction with the preexisting discontinuities. Each numerical method has capabilities and limitations which make them a suitable or improper tool for a particular problem. The strength of each numerical tool can be found in literature when they are employed to solve a specifics problem. The methods can be divided into two mainstreams; continuum and discontinuum methods. The strengths of the continuum methods are briefly; boundary element method: only the boundary of the domains meshed, representation of simplified data by reduced number of dimensions in space, and capable of modelling infinite domain problem. Displacement discontinuity method: introducing a single boundary condition and reducing calculation times. Finite element method: variation of property is allowed by model discretization and capable of fracture analysis in a fine scale. Finite volume method: simple, conservative and memory efficient. However, these methods impose some limitations such as complexity of the simulations when dealing with layers, edges or boundaries, inability to deal with heterogenous rocks, hydraulic fracture propagation path needs to be predefined, and introducing remeshing, cracks are only allowed to propagate through the element’s edges, and requirements for interpolation to generate compatible meshes by node splitting. On the other hand, some of the advantages of the discontinuum tools are: commutation independency for the existing or newly formed fractures, no requirement for coordination convergence and equations for displacement discontinuity, ability to preserve the features of natural fractures, capable of simulating a large number of fractures quickly, able to introduce more additional parameters impacting on fracture extension, no requirement for fracture path pre-defining, and no need for remeshing. These methods also suffer from some limitations including very large computation time to discrete elements, simplified matrix, limitation on introducing new fractures, limitation to develop to 3D space. However, the limitations are overshadowed by the strong capabilities56–59. These limitations inspire development of a method fully capable of modelling many fracture networks under various loading conditions.

The aforementioned numerical tools all impose some deficiencies and exhibit obvious disadvantages when modelling complex fracture networks in terms of fracture initiation and development in rocks with complicated geological features. This challenge becomes even mor pronounced when these ground conditions are located in deep hot places with a fluid flow regime. The combined finite-discrete element method (FDEM) has been developed to overcome the deficiencies of the previous numerical tools. The FDEM primary proposed by Munjiza60 combines the strengths of the FEM for solving challenges of solid deformation and the DEM for modelling the interfaces between deforming grains. Moreover, the FDEM can model fluid flow by forming flow channels at the contacts between adjacent finite elements and these flow routes join the virtual cavities attached to the nodes of the primary finite elements. Concurrently, the FDEM simulates the crack propagation through the fracturing of embedded crack elements. The FDEM has been extensively utilized to simulate crack development in rock masses61–64. The FDEM has also exhibited extraordinary abilities in simulating fluid flow through the rock grains by modelling hydromechanical (HM) or thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) couplings65,66.

This work aims to explore the efficiency of ScCO2 injection into HDR containing DFNs by employing the combined finite-discrete element method. Fully coupled thermos-hydro-mechanical (THM) coupling is used within the framework of FDEM for a realistic simulation. First the THM login in the current FDEM algorithm is explained and related equations are described. Then the FDEM is verified against existing experimental and analytical results to validate its robustness and capabilities in modeling heat and fracture development in brittle materials. Once verified, the FDEM models will be built to implement the DFNs with various characteristics. The influence of fluid flow rate, in-situ stresses, density of the DFNs, and properties of the DFNs will be assessed to explore the fluid drive fracture patterns, fluid pressure distribution, and temperature gradient.

Coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) model in FDEM

In the FDEM, a simulation steps begin with an intact domain or a mix of discrete intact bodies. By stepping into the simulation, these areas (bodies) experience elastic deformation, observe translate, exhibit interaction and rotation, and undergo failure when some specific failure criteria are met, therefore forming new distinct domains or areas. The newly generated domains can then experience larger displacement and deformation, and observe more interaction, and failure. The method employed the FEM to evaluate the rock deformation and investigates the failure criterion for failure and the DEM to identify newly generated interfaces and carries out the deformation stages of the discrete domains. The FDEM benefits the continuity of the FEM and the discontinuum algorithm of the DEM. The FDEM is not only able to simulate the damage of brittle rocks before fracturing but also is capable of modelling the complicated interaction of the newly formed discontinuities. Moreover, in a HM coupling, in every hydraulic time frame, the following major mathematical steps are sequentially evaluated; identification of the volume of the cavities and their saturation degree, and also fluid flow openings and channels; employing of Darcy’s law and parallel-plate flow algorithm at every channel; employing the continuity mathematics at every cavity; and then calculation of the fluid pressure field. In the THM algorithm implemented in the FDEM, THM simulation employs the same simulation strategy used for the mechanical and hydraulic computations while forming new thermal-related variables (such as thermal energy) to the characteristic points and cavities and generating new functions to identify some heat transfer stages. This work takes advantages of the 2D FDEM in Irazu V. 5.1.0 for the fluid injection modelling67. Irazu has etraordinary abilities for a comprehensive THM coupling, which equips us to accurately simulate the HF propagation in complicated subsurface situations68. The coupled process has been successfully implemented into the FDEM to study fracture propagation under various loading schemes, for example works by Tatone and Grasselli69, Yan et al.70, and Wang et al.64.

For heat transfer in rocks, thermal energy is transferred between the points of every triangular finite element. For the thermal conduction in fluids, thermal energy transformation is carried out between the virtual cavities filled with a fluid and is connected by flow cavities. As per the thermal advection, thermal energy is moved between the virtual cavities with the fluid through the bulk displacement of the fluid within the flow channels. Regarding the convective thermal transfer, thermal energy is moved between the fluid-filled cavities and the element end points of the triangular finite elements. For the contact thermal transfer, thermal energy is transferred through the fractured crack elements between the nodes of the interface pairs of finite element edges. The heat transfer stages explained here are depicted in the content of the computational algorithm of the Irazu using the illustration in Fig. 1.

The thermal transfer formulation in the contact of the finite element is according to the topology of (broken) cohesive elements, that are pre-implemented and will be activated once the failure criterion is achieved. Thus, it is only valid for the fracture small slip cases. In other words, only contacts of the edge-to-edge geometry among finite elements which are primarily jointed are inspected (Fig. 2a). The logic of coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) is according to a two way, explicit coupling technique achieved from changes among the three solvers whilst forward marching over time (Fig. 2b). In a modelling, the step for hydraulic is followed by the step of thermal calculation, which is then followed by enough steps by the mechanical logic to make sure quasi-static situation. Calculations of thermal and hydraulic steps are normally synchronised (meaning the smallest size of the two critical timestep is chosen for the two solvers) whilst the mechanical solver employs its size of timestep (very smaller). The coupled TM is according to a two way explicit coupling technique gained by changing between the thermal solver and mechanical solvers whereas forward marching over time in solid (Fig. 2c). In the coupling between TM, the temperature field of the solid leads to the contraction or expansion of the finite element and thermal stresses might normally appear in the calculation (depends on boundary conditions and or deformation of inhomogeneous thermal).

In a coupled mechanical-thermal (M-T), the breakage of one or more cohesive elements (crack element) about a node/cavity position imposes the following influences (Fig. 2a): (1). the flow channel number participate to the cavity expansion, (2). splitting to group of original nodes (according to the newly formed topology of the cohesive elements) and the regrouping nodes to generate new groups, (3). pairing the number of finite element and flow channel within which transferring convective thermal occurs to increases, and (4). Increasing the pairs of number of finite element within which transferring contact thermal occurs. The coupling of thermohydraulic (TH) is according to a twoway explicit coupling technique, achieved by periodic change between the thermal and hydraulic solvers whilst marching forward over time (Fig. 2b).

Modeling strategy

The main objective of this study is to investigate the fluid-driven fracture (FDF) propagation in deep HDRs with and without preexisting natural fractures under the influence of ScCO2 injection as a working fluid. The first step is to model the ScCO2 injection in an HDR model without any pre-existing fracture network to evaluate the performance of such fluid injection in hot rocks. The influence of in-situ ground stresses, working fluid injection flow rate, and fluid viscosity is studied, and fracture development patterns and fluid pressure histories are extracted and discussed. Next, the influence of the discrete fracture network (DFN) on the ScCO2 fracturing is studied. Considered DFN density is 0.1 m/m2, with a standard deviation (m/m2) of 1e− 5. Besides, the influence of ground in-situ stresses, working fluid flow rate, ScCO2 viscosity, in both continuum, and fractured reservoirs with DFNs aperture are investigated in detail. The models are 2D rectangle domains with a dimension of 10 m×10 m and a borehole with a 20 cm diameter. The models have about 34,500 triangle elements. The first stage of the simulations is to run the thermo-mechanical (TM) FEM logic to establish the in-situ stresses and rock temperature to a specific value of 200 °C. Once these two inputs are fully established and reached the equilibrium, the thermos-hydro-mechanical (THM) FDEM is activated and ScCO2 is applied to the borehole nodes to start the fluid injection. Results and Discussions

ScCO2 injection in continuum reservoirs

This section is devoted to studying the influence of in-situ stresses on the ScCO2 fracturing in HDR models without DFN. Six in-situ stress arrangements are considered; σH-σh = 30 MPa-30 MPa, 50 MPa-50 MPa, 70 MPa-70 MPa, 30 MPa-20 MPa, 40 MPa-30 MPa, 70 MPa-40 MPa. All models are under the flow rate of 5 kg/s. Figure 7 illustrates the zoomed failure pattern and fracture development under the mentioned ground stresses. Under the low isotropic stress state of 30 MPa, many fluid drive fractures develop in all directions by an analogous maximum length of 2.2 m. Moreover, fracture branching occurs in all directions and creates new flow channels. Increasing the ground in-situ stresses to isotropic 50 MPa state shows shorter fractures (maximum fracture length of 1.7 m), but the density of the fractures increases around the injection borehole. Under the isotopic state of 70 MPa ground stresses, the maximum length of the fractures becomes much shorter than the two other cases and extends to about 1.1 m. Although the higher ground stress levels lead to shorter final FDFs, they cause excessive fracturing near the borehole. The number of fractures increases significantly by increasing ground stress levels due to the greater resistance of the rock to fracturing against the ScCo2 injection. Therefore, the injected working fluid stops further flow and thus causes extensive fracturing. Under the anisotropic stress state of 30 MPa-20 MPa, long fluid drive cracks propagate in the direction of maximum principal stress, with some fractures deviating from this direction. ScCO2 penetrates the fractures easily and helps the ground stress generate long fractures in a specific direction. Increasing the ground stresses to higher amounts of 40 MPa-20 MPa leads to more directional fractures parallel to σH and longer fractures. However, the increased ground stresses do not always favor the long fractures, as observed for the ground with 70 MPa-40 MPa stresses. Under such a high anisotropic stress state, the fractures are inhibited to short lengths, but the accumulation of fractures becomes more excessive around the borehole due to the high directional stress orientation and higher accumulation of ScCO2 in the newly formed fractures.

The graphs clearly show that the breakdown pressure increases significantly under higher isotropic stress states, and more fluid energy is required to break the borehole and initiate fractures. This trend is also true for the anisotropic stress states. When the difference between σH and σh increases, higher fluid energy is needed to break the rock and generate FDFs. It is also observed that the breakdown pressure for isotropic stress states is much higher than that of anisotropic ones because the large difference between the ground stresses helps higher stress concentration, lowering the need for fluid energy.

The existing studies focus on the viscosity of water as the working fluid, and outcomes indicate the strong influence of change in fluid viscosity on the hydraulic fracture pattern. This phenomenon is mainly because the viscosity of ScCO2 is much lower compared to other working fluids, such as water and is in a supercritical state under critical pressure and temperature, and a change in viscosity does not change fracture propagation pattern. Nevertheless, the changes are not pronounced in this case, and it can be said that the viscosity change in a ScCO2 injection does not considerably influence the fracturing pattern.

Another influencing factor on fracturing under fluid injection is the rate of fluid injection, which has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in a successful injection. This controlling factor has been studied experimentally and numerically by some researchers, including experiments conducted by Zhuang et al66. and Cheng et al67. and simulations by Liang et al68.. These existing studies prove the dominating role of fluid flow rate on the pattern and breakdown pressure of hydraulic fracturing. However, the current studies focus on water as a working fluid, which is fundamentally different from a supercritical fluid. Therefore, in this section, we aim to explore the influence of the ScCO2 flow rate on FDF patterns.

Figure 11 depicts the fracture pattern under a flow rate of 10 kg/s. This figure should be compared with Fig. 7 (cases with 5 kg/s flow rate) for better understanding. Under the isotropic stress fields, the number of fractures has increased considerably in all directions because the high injection rate increases the number of fractures initiated from the borehole, facilitating fluid penetration into the primary fractures and expanding them. However, the length of the FDFs does not experience significant perceptible compared to Fig. 7. This negligible change is due to the rapid increase of the fluid pressure around the borehole, which causes excessive fracturing, thus making the fluid circulate hard into the fracture and avoiding them to propagate longer. In this situation, fluid leak-off occurs frequently, and the efficiency of fluid fracturing decreases, which increases the cost of fluid injection. However, in the state of anisotropic stress, the story is different. Subject to anisotropic stress fields (regardless of the magnitude difference), the length and number of the fluid-drive fractures increase meaningfully. It is also observed that the effect of the high anisotropic stress differences becomes weaker and the fractures tend to propagate in the direction of σh compared to Fig. 7 where 5 kg/s injection rate directs the fractures parallel to the direction of σH. It is also observed under the higher flow rate and larger in-situ stresses difference (70 MPa-40 MPa), the severity of the fractures around the borehole reduces, resulting in less leak-off and better fluid diffuse. This observation demonstrates that ScCO2 working fluid injection in anisotropic stress fields works better and more efficiently than in isotropic stress fields. The performance of a ScCO2 in anisotropic stress fields facilitates the generation of more and favorably directed new fluid channels which helps smoother flow circulation, better heat exchange between the hot dry rocks and cold fluid, and more effective fracture connection. Therefore, ground in-situ stress measurements should be carefully assessed before any fluid injection operation design.

Figure 12 shows the failure modes of the fracturing patterns shown in Fig. 11. Red colors indicate tensile failure while blue colors represent the mixed mode (shear-tensile fracturing). When the stress difference (the contrast between the maximum and minimum in-situ principal stresses) is too large, shear failure becomes the dominant mechanism over tensile failure in hydraulic fracturing. This shift occurs because a high differential stress creates a geological environment in which the rock’s effective stress state favors slip along planes of weakness. The increased stress difference can cause two primary effects: activation of natural fractures, and large stress contrasts promoting shear slip along existing natural fractures or bedding planes rather than opening new tensile fractures against the minimum principal stress. This is particularly important in naturally fractured reservoirs. Direct shear fracturing of the rock matrix: even in intact rock, a sufficiently high stress difference can cause the rock matrix to fail in shear directly. Experimental studies show that when the differential stress exceeds a certain threshold (relative to confining pressure), hydraulic fluid diffusion reduces the effective confining pressure until the rock fails by shear fracture instead of a pure tensile crack. While hydraulic fracturing is traditionally thought to create tensile fractures perpendicular to the minimum stress, this understanding is based on ideal, isotropic conditions. In reality, particularly in tectonically active regions or deep reservoirs, the high stress anisotropy means that shear failure is not just possible, but often more likely to occur, making it a critical consideration for predicting fracture complexity and reservoir stimulation effectiveness.

First is the breakdown pressure difference between the injection rates. Overall, the breakdown pressure increases by about 4 MPa for each case. The second important finding is the breakdown time. Under the higher injection flow rate of 10 kg/s, the breakdown pressure and, thus fracture initiation time reduces significantly. For example, in the case of 70 MPa-70 MPa, the breakdown pressure time decreased from 5.19 s to 3.16 s, which implies that less fluid is required for fracture initiation. This advantage coexists with the disadvantages of high flow rate in isotropic stress fields, therefore, all scenarios need to be taken into account when designing fluid injection operation by a supercritical fluid.

ScCO2 injection in fractured reservoirs

Having understood the behavior of continuum reservors subject to ScCO2 working fluid injection, this section is devoted to studying the performance of injection of such working fluid in fractured reservors with DFNs. The dimensions, properties, ground stresses, and controlling factors are identical to the previous section. DFN properties are listed in Table 2 and the following functions are enabled in the FDEM simulation for realistic simulations: flow through intact DFN, thermal conduction through fluid inside fractures, thermal advection, impermeable matrix and permeable fractures, and convective heat transfer allowing for thermal exchange between fluid in fracture and solid. Moreover, fluid pressure change is enabled in response to the mechanical deformation of fractures. These assumptions would produce simulations involving fluid flow in a hot rock as precisely as possible.

Before we begin analyzing the fractured models, identifying the interaction between ScCO2 working fluid and DFN is worth identifying. Figure 14 presents the identified interaction types in a model with the DFN sets. At the center of Fig. 14 is a model’s ultimate fracturing pattern, and the zoom-in images are captured from different corners of the central image, shown by arrows. Twelve interaction types were identified by closely viewing the fracture propagation within the model. The first interaction type is a direct connection, which is fluid flow through one fracture and initiating an FDF from its tip and developing to an adjacent tip. The second is rebranching which is due to high fluid pressure dispersion into a new fracture and leading to the development of new fractures. Third, is offset crossing because of rapid fluid flow through an existing long fracture and initiating new fractures from the surface of the fracture. Low viscous fluid initiates some new fractures before reaching the tips of the long fracture. The fourth is due to the interaction of the cold, low viscous fluid with the hot rock, causing rapid heat exchange and generating many short thermal shrinkage fractures. The fifth one shows the quick crossing of a fluid fracture from an existing one without activating it. The sixth type arrests the FDF and stops further propagation when this new fracture meets an existing fracture. The seventh type is a hybrid one, combining activation of a pre-existing fracture and making new offset fractures. The eighth one is also a combined one, made up of both crossing and activating. The ninth one represents dilation, fluid dispersion into a pre-existing fracture without making new fractures from its surface. The tenth type is an interesting, notable one; the high-pressure low viscous ScCO2 branches the FDF into two from the tip of a pre-existing fracture. The eleventh type illustrates a complex one, comprising dilating the pre-existing fracture, making offset fractures, and generating FDF surface branching. The twelfth interaction type shows high fluid dispersion into the pre-existing fracture, which makes combined interaction made up of dilation, offset crossing, and activation. These interaction types will help to better analyze the FDF development in fractured models in the following section.

Figure 15 shows the fracture propagation pattern in the fractured models under different ground in-situ stresses. Under the isotropic stress field, the extent of the FDFs is considerably influenced by the magnitude of the stresses. Under the low 30 MPa isotropic stress field, FDFs propagate in all directions, hit the DFNs, and interact with the existing fractures to make new fluid channels by generating the abovementioned interaction types. Since the ground stress state is relatively low, ScCO2 can flow easily inside the DFNs and dilate them, causing large DFN openings and better fluid dispersion. This creates a better environment for generating new FDFs and thus longer fluid immersion and longer FDFs. When the magnitude of the isotopic stresses increases, not only does the extent of high fluid pressure and FDFs decrease significantly, but the interaction types also change. By stress increase, offset and activation types decrease, arresting increases notably, and thermal shrinkage cracks show remarkable growth. This behavior occurs because the large ground stresses keep the DFNs close and do not allow for easy fluid flow inside the DFNs, resulting in difficult fluid dispersion inside the pre-existing fractures. Therefore, continuous fluid injection and interaction with the HDR in the close vicinity of the borehole cause excessive thermal cracking and lower the efficiency of fluid injection. The FDF behavior shows notable differences when the ground stresses are anisotropic. Under such ground stresses, the extent of the FDFs is much larger than that of isotropic ones. Under any anisotropic stresses, the fractures’ extent propagates toward the models’ boundaries, regardless of the magnitude of σH and σh. When the horizontal stress increases, the FDFs tend to propagate in the direction of σH and are reluctant to develop in other directions. Furthermore, the interaction types vary by change in the difference between σH and σh. Under the low σH−σh difference, the DFNs can open and dilate easily, facilitating the fluid dispersion into them and, thus, frequent dilation, offset crossing, and activation to occur. This observation is somehow true for anisotropic stress fields; however, the higher the σH-σh difference becomes, the lower the breakdown pressure. This might be attributed to the effect of the DFNs, which facilitate fluid flow in certain directions by increasing the difference between the ground stresses.

Discussion

The previous results showed the performance of ScCO2in HDR under different scenarios. It is worth comparing the results of a supercritical fluid injection with water as a working fluid. To begin with, a model is built to model the lab tests carried out by Yang et al73. that ScCO2 injection was done within a borehole inside a rock specimen. In their test, a granite specimen with dimensions of 50 mm by 50 mm by 85 mm was fabricated. Further details including sample fabrication, setup of the tests, injecting fluid, in-situ stress setting, adjusting the temperature, etc. may be read in their study. The properties assigned to the material in this study are taken as a density of 2595 kg/m³, uniaxial compressive strength of 240 MPa, and uniaxial tensile strength of 11.71 MPa. However, these parameters are insufficient for FDEM modeling; some other input parameters must be estimated based on the available empirical and experimental equations. The estimated values are E = 60 GPa, ν = 0.26, cohesion = 25 MPa, Mode I fracture energy 1300 µN/mm, and Mode II fracture energy 13,000 µN/mm. Other input parameters are the same as those listed in Table 2. Many endeavors have been made for an accurate estimation of the parameter according to many trial and error runs, and the provided simulation inputs illustrate the true granite sample with a large matching range.

The following are the input data from the experiments. The reported experimental temperature was the ScCO₂ fracturing temperature, 40 °C, and the hydraulic fracturing (water) temperature, room temperature (≈ 20–25 °C). Since the critical temperature of CO₂ is 31.1 °C, the selected temperature ensured that the injected CO₂ remained supercritical throughout the experiment. The shale specimens were loaded in a true-triaxial loading system. The study investigated different horizontal stress regimes to evaluate fracture propagation. The applied principal stresses were: case, vertical stress (σv), maximum horizontal stress (σH), and minimum horizontal stress (σh) were equal at 20 MPa, 20 MPa, and 20 MPa. Moderate stress differences of 20 MPa, 25 MPa, and 15 MPa. Large stress differences of 20 MPa, 30 MPa, and 10 MPa. These boundary conditions were used to examine the effect of horizontal stress contrast on fracture geometry and fracture-network complexity. The injection was performed using an ISCO syringe pump at a constant flow rate (displacement-controlled injection). The reported injection rate was 0.5 mL/s, equivalent to 30 mL/min or 1.8 L/h. The pump continuously injected fluid until fracture initiation (breakdown) occurred. These conditions are frequently used as benchmark laboratory conditions for comparing water fracturing and ScCO₂ fracturing in shale, particularly for studying fracture initiation pressure, acoustic emission activity, and fracture-network morphology.

Mesh and time-step sensitivity analyses demonstrated numerical convergence of the FDEM model. An average element size of approximately one-tenth of the borehole diameter and a time step corresponding to 20–50% of the critical CFL limit were sufficient to accurately capture breakdown pressure, fracture propagation, and fracture-network complexity during supercritical CO₂ fracturing of hot dry rock. Time-step sensitivity analyses were performed using time steps ranging from s to s. The breakdown pressure and fracture length differed by less than 2% and 4%, respectively, between the two smallest time steps. Therefore, a time step of s was adopted for all simulations.

The thermophysical properties of ScCO₂ are highly dependent on both pressure and temperature, particularly near its critical point (31.1 °C and 7.38 MPa). As pressure increases at constant temperature, the density, viscosity, and bulk modulus of ScCO₂ generally increase, while its compressibility decreases as the fluid becomes more liquid-like. Conversely, increasing temperature at constant pressure typically reduces density and the bulk modulus, but slightly increases viscosity and thermal expansion. Near the critical point, even small changes in pressure or temperature can cause substantial variations in density and compressibility, making ScCO₂ highly responsive to reservoir conditions. In geothermal and hot dry rock (HDR) reservoirs, where temperatures commonly range from 100 to 250 °C and pressures from 20 to 40 MPa, ScCO₂ exhibits densities of approximately 400–900 kg/m³ and viscosities of only 0.04–0.12 mPa·s. These characteristics provide ScCO₂ with excellent penetration into microfractures and natural discontinuities, promote rapid pressure diffusion, and enhance fracture complexity compared with conventional water-based fracturing fluids. Consequently, accurate numerical simulations of ScCO₂ fracturing should account for pressure- and temperature-dependent fluid properties using appropriate equations of state rather than assuming constant fluid parameters.

In hydraulic fracturing, thermal parameters influence fracturing performance through thermally induced tensile stresses, but their role differs fundamentally between water and supercritical CO₂ (ScCO₂) due to differences in fluid viscosity, compressibility, and heat transfer efficiency. For water fracturing, thermal parameters play a secondary, assisting role: the injected water’s lower temperature relative to the hot reservoir creates a moderate thermal gradient, inducing tensile thermal stresses that generate a narrow zone of micro-cracks primarily near the wellbore wall, which enhances near-wellbore permeability and aids fracture initiation, but because water’s high viscosity and low compressibility allow it to efficiently transmit hydraulic pressure deep into the fracture, the primary fracture propagation remains dominated by mechanical pressurization rather than thermal effects, with the thermal stress contribution being significant only for reducing breakdown pressure modestly and creating minor secondary cracks. In contrast, for ScCO₂ fracturing, thermal parameters become a primary and synergistic fracturing mechanism: ScCO₂’s extremely low viscosity (similar to a gas) and high diffusivity allow it to penetrate instantly into microscopic pores and existing micro-cracks throughout the rock matrix, while its relatively high specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity enable rapid heat extraction from the rock upon injection, creating a large temperature difference (ΔT) between the cold ScCO₂ and the hot reservoir; this large ΔT generates intense, uniformly distributed tensile thermal stresses across a wide volume of rock, significantly lowering the rock’s effective tensile strength and reducing breakdown pressure by up to 50–60% compared to water, while also promoting the initiation of multiple fractures in various directions rather than a single planar fracture, ultimately creating a complex, multi-branched, highly tortuous fracture network with greater stimulated reservoir volume and higher fracture conductivity, especially in high-temperature reservoirs where the thermal shock effect is most pronounced.

ScCO2 is superior to water for hydraulic fracturing primarily because it solves water’s three major problems: formation damage, water blocking, and high environmental impact. While water has higher viscosity for proppant transport, ScCO2’s extremely low viscosity (0.03–0.10 mPa·s) enables it to infiltrate microcracks and natural fractures, creating complex fracture networks that dramatically increase stimulated reservoir volume while reducing breakdown pressure by over 50%, lowering pumping energy and seismic risk. Unlike water, ScCO2 is non-polar and does not cause clay swelling or fines migration, preserving natural permeability in clay-rich shales. It also eliminates water blocking because it vaporizes upon pressure release, allowing immediate hydrocarbon flow without the weeks of cleanup required with other methods. Furthermore, ScCO2 actively enhances production by dissolving organic matter and reducing oil viscosity (enhanced oil recovery), with no freshwater or toxic additives required. The only drawback- poor proppant transport due to low viscosity-is being solved with thickened ScCO2 formulations. Thus, water merely fractures and damages, whereas ScCO2 fractures, cleans, and enhances flow with a dramatically lower environmental footprint.

Conclusion

This study focuses on the performance of supercritical carbon dioxide (ScCO2) as the working fluid for stimulation of hot dry rocks (HRD) analyzed via a fully coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) scheme implemented into the 2D combined finite-discrete element method (FDEM). Two scenarios are considered: continuum reservoirs without pre-existing fractures and reservoirs with discrete fracture networks (DFN). First, a series of verification examples are presented to ensure the method’s capability in modeling fluid flow in a solid medium. Next, a comprehensive search in the literature lists rock, fluid, and DFN properties. For each type of reservoir, changes in three controlling factors- ground in-situ stresses, fluid viscosity, and fluid flow rate- are examined in relation to the performance of the ScCO2. Furthermore, the difference between water and ScCO2 injection is discussed. The following main conclusions are drawn:

Based on a close observation of ScCO2 dispersion in fracture reservoirs, twelve interaction types between the fluid and DFN are identified. Some can be formed in water injection, but some specific ones are identified only with ScCO2 injection, such as crack tip branching and rebranching, and are not observed in water injection.

To conclude, this study demonstrates the excellent performance of ScCO2 as the working fluid in deep hot dry rocks and is promising in successful reservoir stimulations. However, this study can be regarded as a starter and further studies are needed to fully characterise this kind of fluids and address challenges.

For ScCO₂ fluid fracturing in HDR geothermal reservoirs, several geological, geomechanical, thermal, and operational conditions can limit the effectiveness of fracture initiation, propagation, and reservoir stimulation. These limitations should be carefully considered during reservoir selection and project design. These limitations are explained briefly. When the difference between the maximum and minimum principal stresses is large ( fractures tend to propagate as a single planar fracture rather than a complex network, which results in reduced fracture branching, smaller stimulated reservoir volume (SRV), and limited activation of natural fractures. Very deep HDR reservoirs may experience confining stresses exceeding 70–100 MPa, with consequences including higher breakdown pressure requirements, closure of natural fractures, reduced permeability enhancement, and increased pumping requirements. Highly homogeneous rock masses are another problematic issue. ScCO₂ performs best when pre-existing discontinuities exist. In very homogeneous granites, few natural weaknesses are available; fracture propagation becomes relatively planar, and the benefits of ScCO₂-induced branching are reduced. Excessive heterogeneity can also be problematic, leading to unpredictable fracture paths, localized stress concentrations, and non-uniform stimulation. Another problem arises in the absence of natural fractures. ScCO₂’s low viscosity promotes penetration into existing microcracks. If natural fractures are scarce, limited fracture complexity develops, and fracturing behavior approaches conventional hydraulic fracturing. When large connected fractures already exist, significant fluid leak-off, poor pressure buildup, and reduced fracture extension are consequences. Reservoir temperature is another factor contributing to the problem. Extremely high temperatures (> 250–300 °C) reduce CO₂ density, alter thermophysical properties, and decrease fluid storage capacity. Consequences: are lower fracturing efficiency, more difficult pressure control, and potential thermal degradation of well components. Formation permeability also contributes. While extremely low permeability is generally desirable for stimulation, it can lead to poor pressure transmission and delayed fracture initiation. Excessively high permeability can cause rapid pressure dissipation and limit fracture growth. Therefore, all limitations and strengths should be considered simultaneously for a successful fluid injection.
