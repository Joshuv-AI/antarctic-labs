ABSTRACT
This project explored the feasibility of using fiber-optic sensing technologies for condition monitoring
of offshore synthetic mooring lines used in marine energy systems. The work focused on evaluating
optical fiber materials, interrogation methods, and rope-embedment approaches capable of enabling
distributed strain or elongation measurements along mooring lines. A literature review was conducted
to assess prior work on polymer and glass optical fibers, optical time-domain reflectometry (OTDR),
fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs), and embedded sensing strategies for synthetic ropes.

Experimental activities included evaluation of multiple OTDR systems and compatibility testing with
large-core polymer optical fiber. Significant procurement challenges were encountered because the
selected CYTOP-based polymer optical fiber had to be sourced from Japan due to limited domestic
availability. In parallel, preliminary distributed strain measurements were performed using a Luna
glass-fiber sensing system bonded to a Kevlar line to assess strain-transfer behavior and rope-
embedment concepts. Additional hand-braiding trials were conducted for both core and helical
embedment approaches.

The results showed that embedded fiber-optic monitoring remains technically promising; however,
major challenges remain related to OTDR compatibility with polymer optical fibers, connector
preparation, strain-transfer reliability, fiber abrasion, and fabrication repeatability. Preliminary strain
measurements demonstrated that localized strain variations within rope structures could be detected,
although hand-braided embedment methods did not provide sufficiently uniform or durable
mechanical coupling for reliable distributed sensing. While the project did not fully validate a
deployable monitoring system, it identified key technical barriers and established several promising
directions for future development of embedded fiber-optic sensing systems for synthetic mooring
applications.

1. METHODOLOGY

1.1. Background
As marine energy (ME) technologies mature, maintaining reliability of mooring lines is a crucial
consideration to the economic and technical viability of the technology. Synthetic fiber mooring lines
are emerging as a preferred alternative to traditional chain and wire due to their lower cost, easier
handling, and superior axial compliance. These properties make synthetic ropes particularly well-suited
to floating platforms such as wave energy converters and floating offshore wind turbines, where
compliant moorings can accommodate platform motion while maintaining station keeping. Despite
these advantages, the inability to monitor the condition of synthetic mooring lines in real time remains
a fundamental challenge, limiting their broader adoption in offshore environments.

Traditional monitoring solutions, such as load cells or tension sensors, are typically installed at the
topside or fairlead locations and provide limited insight into the distributed condition of the mooring
line itself. These techniques cannot detect internal wear, elongation from creep, or damage caused by
external events such as entanglement or marine growth. In response, researchers have proposed
embedding fiber optic cables directly within synthetic ropes to serve as internal strain sensors. When
paired with optical time-domain reflectometry (OTDR) or related interrogation techniques, these
embedded sensors can detect local displacements or strain accumulation along the entire length of the
mooring line, enabling real-time structural health monitoring without altering the rope’s mechanical
function.

This sensing approach has several potential advantages for offshore deployment. Fiber optic cables
are immune to electromagnetic interference, lightweight, and can be designed to survive in harsh
environments. Recent developments in polymer-based optical fibers have demonstrated high strain
tolerance far exceeding 10%, improving their suitability for embedded, long-term strain sensing
applications. These methods show promise for shielding the fiber from excessive stress while ensuring
it experiences deformation proportional to rope elongation.

Despite this progress, several open questions remain before embedded fiber optic sensors can be
considered a viable monitoring solution for commercial ME deployments. Most prior studies have
been limited to a small number of polymer fiber formulations and controlled laboratory conditions.
There is little published data on how these sensors behave under realistic loading conditions typical
of offshore mooring systems—particularly in the presence of time-dependent effects such as creep
and hysteresis. Furthermore, the performance trade-offs between polymer and silica-based fiber optics
and between different embedment strategies have not been fully characterized.

The goal of this project is to address these gaps by:

 • Experimentally evaluating the performance of different fiber optic materials (both polymer
 and glass) subjected to strain loads applicable in a marine environment.
 • Formulate and investigate various embedment strategies and whether they are suitable for
 mooring lines for ME
 • Identifying practical limitations associated with interrogation hardware, fiber handling, and
 strain transfer.

 8

This research aims to consolidate existing research and take the next logical step forward to help assess
whether embedded fiber optic sensors can reliably monitor elongation, creep, and damage in synthetic
mooring lines for ME, and whether this potential avenue of condition monitoring merits future
research to ultimately assist future deployments for ME systems.

1.2. Literature Review
Prior research on fiber optic sensing for synthetic mooring lines has focused on three related
questions: whether optical fibers can survive the large strains experienced by synthetic ropes, whether
strain can be transferred reliably from the rope to the fiber, and whether optical interrogation methods
can resolve distributed or localized changes along the mooring line.
Robertson and Ludden [1] introduced one of the earliest demonstrations of an embedded distributed
optical fiber sensing approach in synthetic fiber ropes by integrating an optical fiber along a helical
path inside a parallel‑lay rope structure, ensuring the fiber deformed with rope tension instead of
breaking prematurely. The authors report results from bench-scale tensile tests from cyclically loading
the rope and measuring the distributed strain response of the fiber via OTDR techniques to detect
both global and local strain variations. The work finds the helical embedment method significantly
increases the strain compatibility between rope and optical fiber, with the distributed measurement
approach offered real‑time spatial mapping of strain along the rope.

Rebel et al. [2] provides a comprehensive review of non-destructive monitoring approaches for
polyester fiber mooring ropes at the time (published in 2000), comparing techniques such as
vibrational spectroscopy, magnetic resonance, conductive internal elements, and especially embedded
distributed fiber optic sensors employing multiple fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs). The review found the
mismatch between typical fiber rope failure strains (~10–15 %) and the much lower strain capacity of
silica-optic sensors (~1 %) poses a major integration challenge. Expanding on the work from
Robertson and Ludden [1], they emphasize that embedding the FBG fiber along a helical path within
the rope structure can mitigate this mismatch and preserve sensor survival under large elongation. The
authors found fiber optic sensing to show promise as a future technology, though several engineering
barriers remain, notably designing rope architectures that accommodate sensors without sacrificing
mechanical integrity and developing robust interrogation systems suited for offshore deployment.

Smith and Williams [3] investigated embedding polymeric (plastic) optical fibers (POFs) directly within
synthetic‑fiber mooring ropes to measure large axial strains in situ using Optical Time‑Domain
Reflectometry (OTDR). In their laboratory tests, they integrated POFs made from perfluorinated
carbon (PFC) or polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) into small polyester rope elements, including
braiding the fiber within a sub‑rope to ensure it experienced the same tensile deformation as the host
rope. They embedded a reflective interface along the fiber, allowing discrete gauge‑length strain
measurements along the rope. Test cycles involved repeated tensile loading to strains of around 3 %
(and up to about 8–10 %), with correlation between applied strain and OTDR‑measured strain
recorded over multiple cycles. The POFs exhibited near one‑to‑one strain tracking, maintained
performance under repeated loading, and (particularly the PFC fiber) strained to the point of being
able to withstand elongations of ropes in marine settings.

Liehr et al. [4] presents a comprehensive evaluation of POFs for distributed strain sensing using
OTDR and optical frequency domain reflectometry (OFDR) techniques. In their experimental work,
they conducted bench‑scale tensile tests, straining fiber sections up to ~40% elongation, while

 9

monitoring changes in OTDR backscatter amplitude and trace length, as well as using OFDR-based
cross‑correlation of characteristic fiber signatures to resolve spatial strain distribution. The authors
also characterized external disturbances (temperature changes, bending, humidity), showing
manageable sensitivity and correction possibilities.

Gordelier et al. [5] evaluates the suitability of PMMA polymer optical fibers for in-situ strain sensing
in synthetic mooring lines using optical time-domain reflectometry (OTDR). The authors conducted
controlled laboratory tensile tests on bare PMMA fibers to map their mechanical behavior across
different strain rates, identifying a proportional limit around 1.16% strain and a yield point near 5.41%
strain under the lowest test speed. They also subjected samples to fatigue cycling up to 1.25% strain
and found no measurable shifts in mechanical thresholds, though creep became significant beyond
1.5% strain, limiting the use of PMMA for marine applications. The OTDR measurements
demonstrated the system could reliably resolve strains at or above ~4%. The authors also discussed
integration challenges, such as dealing with creep and fiber strain capacity limitations, and highlighted
criteria necessary for practical offshore application, including setting safe operating strain envelopes
and managing environmental effects.

Chung et al. [6] and Nguyen et al. [7] evaluate related testing for a mooring line system integrating
optical fibers into syntehtic polymer mooring rope segments to measure tension and creep. The
laboratory experiments (in Chung et al.) and wave experiments (in Nguyen et al.) tested several rope
diameters using stepwise tension loading up to 100 ton-force. The results show reliable strain-to-
wavelength response in laboratory testing, though strain readings varied significantly by rope section.

Grangeat et al. [8] reinforces previous findings by testing plastic optical fibers (POFs) for monitoring
mooring lines in floating wind turbine applications, focusing on long-term reliability of OTDR-based
elongation measurement. Their results show that POFs can sustain up to 70% strain with negligible
loss in light transmission under both tensile and torsional stress, and that water immersion has minimal
effect on optical attenuation regardless of exposure length. Elongation sensing was validated over
fiber lengths up to 100 m using both transmission and reflection OTDR modalities, confirming its
viability for large-scale deployment.

Across the reviewed literature, embedded fiber optic sensing has demonstrated strong potential for
monitoring the condition of synthetic mooring lines in marine environments. Early work established
the feasibility of integrating polymer optical fibers (POFs) into ropes using helical or core embedment
techniques, enabling distributed strain measurement through OTDR or fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs).
Several studies confirmed that POFs can tolerate large strains, in some cases up to 70%, making them
mechanically compatible with the elongation demands of synthetic mooring lines. More recent
experimental efforts have validated the accuracy of optical strain and tension sensing under dynamic
and irregular loading conditions, including wave basin and field deployments, and have identified key
design considerations such as strain transfer efficiency, section-specific calibration, and fiber creep
behavior. Studies also highlighted limitations, including the need for creep-aware signal interpretation,
sensitivity to local rope geometry, greater investigation into behavior when embedded in the mooring
rope, and trade-offs between polymer and glass-based fibers. Collectively, this body of research
supports the viability of fiber optic sensors for real-time, embedded condition monitoring in marine
energy applications, but also underscores the need for further testing of fiber materials and
embedment strategies under realistic long-term loading scenarios to enable reliable predictive
maintenance in offshore environments.

1.3. Test Plan

The testing plan was designed to assess whether embedded fiber optic sensing is a practical condition-
monitoring approach for synthetic mooring lines used in marine energy (ME) applications. The
planned work focused on three core questions: whether selected fiber optic materials could survive
the strain and cyclic loading expected in synthetic mooring ropes, whether the fibers could measure
strain, creep, and dynamic response with sufficient accuracy, and whether the fibers could be
embedded into rope structures in a way that preserved both sensing function and rope integrity.

The first step was to review and select candidate fiber optic materials. The review focused on modern
polymer optical fibers, including perfluorinated polymer fibers, because of their improved attenuation
characteristics and higher strain tolerance compared with conventional silica fibers. Silica-based fibers
were also considered as a reference because of their compatibility with established commercial
interrogation systems.

The second step was to develop rope embedment approaches. The project aimed to repeat and adapt
methods reported in the literature and used by Sandia’s technical embroidery laboratory to integrate
fiber optic cables into synthetic mooring-line specimens. Candidate approaches included placing the
fiber near the rope core and wrapping the fiber helically within the rope structure to improve strain
compatibility while shielding the fiber from direct environmental exposure and excessive local loads.

The third step was tensile testing. Embedded specimens were to be tested under static, creep, and
cyclic loading representative of synthetic mooring-line behavior in offshore applications. These tests
were intended to determine whether the embedded fibers could survive relevant loading levels and
provide repeatable measurements of strain, elongation, creep, and dynamic response.

The fourth step was environmental exposure followed by repeated tensile testing. Specimens were to
be exposed to marine-relevant conditions, including salt water, sand, and temperature variation, using
available environmental and salt-chamber resources. The tensile tests would then be repeated to
evaluate whether environmental exposure degraded fiber survivability, strain-transfer behavior, or
measurement accuracy.

The final step was reporting and outreach. Results were to be documented through a technical report
and conference dissemination, with follow-on discussions planned with mooring-line and fiber optic
manufacturers to evaluate practical manufacturing pathways and future deployment potential.

2. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION AND PRELIMINARY RESULTS

2.1. Condition Monitoring System Definition

In this study, a mooring-line condition monitoring system is defined as an integrated sensing approach
that can detect changes in the mechanical condition of a synthetic mooring line during service. The
primary quantities of interest are line elongation, distributed strain, creep accumulation, and localized
changes that may indicate damage, abrasion, entanglement, or partial failure.

For embedded fiber optic sensing, the monitoring system consists of four coupled components: the
optical fiber, the rope embedment strategy, the optical interrogation system, and the data
interpretation method. The fiber must survive the strain levels expected in synthetic mooring ropes,
the embedment method must transfer rope deformation to the fiber without causing excessive
abrasion or slippage, the interrogator must resolve measurable changes along the fiber, and the
interpretation method must relate the optical response to useful engineering quantities such as
elongation, creep rate, or damage location.

Therefore, the feasibility of this monitoring approach depends on the combined performance of the
rope, fiber, and interrogation hardware rather than on any single component alone. This project
evaluates that coupled system by assessing fiber material options, detection methods, rope embedment
concepts, and preliminary strain measurements under laboratory conditions.

2.2. Optical Strain Measurement and Detection Methodology
Distributed strain measurements in mooring lines can be accomplished using optical-pulse time-of-
flight measurements in optical fibers integrated into the mooring lines. An optical time-domain
reflectometer (OTDR) launches short-duration laser pulses into an optical fiber and then analyzes the
reflected signal that are subsequently detected. Breaks, splices, and connectors located along the fiber
produce these reflections. The OTDR uses time-of-flight calculations to report the locations of the
reflection events. If the OTDR produces very short laser pulses (10s of picoseconds) and has a short-
deadtime detector, it can measure event locations to within a few millimeters. Changes in the spacing
between reflection events can then be used to infer local elongation and strain over defined gauge
lengths [3].

Previous strain measurements using a high-performance OTDR and a highly elastic optical fiber
demonstrated that cyclical strains as large as 10% could be accurately measured in the optical fiber [3].
The optical fiber was a cyclic transparent optical polymer (CYTOP). The strain gauge length was
determined by the spacing of type ST optical fiber connectors joining segments of the fiber. For
marine applications, this strain measurement method would not be practical because connectors are
large in comparison to optical fiber and it is difficult to make them water-tight for extended periods.

The use of fiber optic Bragg gratings (FBGs) to replace the use of connectors to demarcate gauge
lengths in the fiber was an initial consideration. The principal disadvantage of FBGs is the difficulty
in creating them in plastic optical fibers. Generating a fiber Bragg grating (FBG) in a CYTOP (cyclic
transparent optical polymer) optical fiber would require a high-power UV-emitting pulsed-output laser
and a complicated optical arrangement for a phase-mask imaging technique. Fortunately, there are a
few less complicated and less expensive methods for creating reflections in CYTOP optical fibers.
One method would be to create small refractive index changes in very short segments (≪ 1 mm) along

 12

the fiber. The refractive index change would be sufficient to produce a Fresnel-type reflection, where
a portion of the optical signal is reflected due to the abrupt change in refractive index between adjacent
materials. What follows is a brief description of a technique that could produce these refractive index
changes.

2.2.1. Laser fluence and processing time required to create a refractive index
 change in a perfluorinated plastic optical fiber (CYTOP)
Modifying the refractive index in CYTOP is particularly challenging when compared to optical fiber
core materials such as polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) and polystyrene (PS) because CYTOP is
intrinsically resistant to photochemical and photothermal damage. Photothermal modifications to the
fiber are difficult because CYTOP is exceptionally transparent in the visible, near-infrared, and near-
ultraviolet wavelength regions. CYTOP begins to absorb strongly in the deep UV region (<300 nm),
however, and this absorption provides a way to create photo-induced modifications in the fiber’s core
and cladding.

For this reason, a continuous-wave (CW) UV laser diode was considered as a potential light source
for inducing localized refractive-index changes in the fiber. For lasers with >300 nm, photochemical
and photothermal modifications are slight unless the power densities are very high. Lasers with <300
nm are more effective for photochemical changes.

2.2.2. Creating Fresnel reflections in Fontex (CYTOP) optical fibers

There are three alternatives to using a CW UV Laser Diode to create localized Fresnel reflections in
CYTOP optical fibers. The alternatives, ranked in order of increasing complexity, are:

 • Air gap interface – Cleave the fiber using a razor blade and butt the cleaved ends together.
 • Optical cement interface – Cleave the fiber using a razor blade and rejoin the cleaved ends
 using optical cement.
 • Fiber densification using heat – Heat a short segment of fiber to a temperature below the
 glass transition temperature and put the fiber under tension to reduce the density in the heated
 segment.

Alternative 1: Air Gap Interface

This method is the most likely to produce a reflection that would be detected by the OTDR. The air
gap interface would be created by cleaving the fiber with a sharp razor blade at a precise 90° angle and
then butting the ends together to make physical contact. The thin layer of air (n = 1.000) at the

 16

interface would create a reflection due to the changes in refractive index, i.e., n = 1.335 → 1.000 →
1.335. A simple calculation of the intensity of this Fresnel-type reflection suggests that the air-gap
interface would produce a reflection of about 2-3% of the incident laser pulse. A reflection of this
magnitude would be detectable by the OTDR.

Alternative 2: Fiber Ends Joined using Optical Cement

This method is similar to Alternative 1 approach. After cleaving, the fiber ends would be joined using
an optical cement such as ELJIN EJ-500 (n = 1.57). The thin layer of cement would create the Fresnel
reflection due to the index change, n = 1.335 → 1.57 → 1.335. The higher index optical cement would
produce a reflection of about 1%. The advantage of this method is that the smaller reflection would
correspond to a smaller insertion loss.

Alternative 3: Fiber Densification using Heat

The feasibility of this method is speculative and would likely require significant trial and error. It would
involve applying heat to a short segment of the fiber ( ̴ 5 𝑚𝑚) and precisely control the temperature
of the fiber to a value that is high enough to soften the fiber and elongate it by a small amount
(~1 𝑚𝑚). The elongation necessary to achieve a decrease in reflective itself is difficult to predict.
However, the advantage of this method, if it were to be successful, would be that it is very similar to
the laser diode method; a stable Fresnel reflection would be created without having to splice the optical
fiber.

Alternatives 1 and 2 would require that the cleaved ends of the optical fiber be joined using a
mechanical splice like a Corning Fibrlok optical fiber splice.

2.3. Rope Embedment
The two embedment methods listed below were reviewed and tested to evaluate the feasibility of
integrating optical fiber cables into synthetic mooring ropes for offshore strain monitoring
applications:

 • The “12-braid strand core embedment” method placed the cable along the centerline of the
 rope and braided twelve strands tightly around it to create a conventional-looking mooring
 line with an internal sensing core, similar to the approach used in previous work by Smith et
 al. [3].
 • The “dual braid helical embedment” method used a smaller three-strand rope as the core
 structure. The fiber was helically wrapped around this core, and a 12-strand braid was applied
 over the assembly to form a layered construction. The helical wrapping technique was inspired
 by the methodology developed by Robertson and Ludden [1] and Rebel et al. [2].

Both methods aimed to protect the fiber, ensure sufficient mechanical coupling for strain transfer,
and preserve the rope’s structural integrity.

During hand-braiding trials, both approaches encountered significant difficulties. In both methods,
achieving tight and uniform braiding by hand was challenging, which led to poor mechanical coupling
between the optical fiber and the surrounding rope structure. The fiber frequently slipped within the
braid under moderate tension, indicating unreliable strain transfer. Even when braiding as tightly as

 17

possible to prevent slippage, excessive abrasion was observed at locations where the cable contacted
the rope fibers, particularly in the dual braid helical embedment method. In this configuration, the
outer braid and inner core moved semi-independently and induced shear on the embedded cable.
These issues highlighted the difficulty of achieving sufficient mechanical integration between the
optical fiber and rope structure using manual fabrication techniques.

However, given the problems were related to the tension and handling of the rope rather than any
inherent problem with the materials, we believe that the embedment methods are valid and worth
further investigation using controlled rope-manufacturing equipment instead of by hand. These ideas
are explored in Section 2.4 below.

2.4. Fiber Optic Strain Testing
While awaiting delivery of the CYTOP optical fiber, preliminary strain-transfer testing was performed
using a commercial glass-fiber distributed sensing system from Luna Innovations. These experiments
focused on evaluating the embedment methodology, strain-transfer behavior, and distributed sensing
response within braided rope structures rather than achieving the large strain ranges expected from
CYTOP fibers. Unlike CYTOP polymer optical fibers, standard glass optical fibers have limited strain
capacity, but they provide a convenient platform for validating the general sensing and embedment
approach.

The HD6S High-Definition Fiber Optic Strain Sensor from Luna Innovations is a distributed
strain sensing fiber that provides nearly continuous, sub-millimeter scale resolution strain
measurements along its length. When used with Luna’s ODiSI interrogators, the HD6S can deliver
over a thousand distinct strain data points per meter, making it ideal for detailed strain mapping in
structural health monitoring, composite testing, or embedding in smart materials. The sensor is
packaged as a low-profile, flexible polyimide-coated fiber (nominal diameter ~155 µm) capable of
operating from –40 °C to 300 °C, and is immune to electromagnetic interference. Luna specifies that
it uses “standard unaltered optical fiber,” although the datasheet does not explicitly specify the core
and cladding diameters. Visual inspection suggests that the fiber dimensions are approximately 9 µm
/125 µm.

A 3.2 mm diameter braided Kevlar line was used to test the strain measurement technique for a 2 m
test length (Figs. 3 and 4). The Luna fiber was glued using M-Bond 200 at discrete points along the
length of the Kevlar line every 8.3 cm for 24 glued points. This was accomplished using a similar
strategy to the “dual braid helical embedment” described in section 2.3 without an over braid. The
Luna fiber was helically wrapped around the Kevlar line at three wraps per meter, while keeping the
wrapping consistent throughout the length of the rope.

To simulate tensile loading and progressive stretching of the rope, increasing weights were applied
incrementally by adding 10.5-lb weight plates while strain measurements were continuously recorded.
Loading conditions ranged from 10.5 lb to 42 lb total applied weight (Fig. 5). The Luna live data
stream is shown in Figs. 6 and 7. Each of these areas of discrete strain was averaged into a single
representative point, and strain snapshots corresponding to each loading condition are shown in Figs.
8-11.

 18

Due to the 24 points of adhesion, the strain data became semi-isolated at each discrete point, resulting
in slightly different strain at each point along the length of the Kevlar rope, as can be seen in these
figures. The semi-isolated strain at each discreate point can be seen as plateau-like regions in the strain
profile. Each plateau corresponds approximately to the 8.3 cm spacing between adjacent adhesion
points on the Kevlar rope.

Although the overall strain magnitude generally increased with increasing applied load, the strain
distribution along the rope remained highly nonuniform. The repeated peaks and valleys observed
along the rope length suggest that the measured strain was strongly influenced by the discrete adhesion
points, helical wrapping geometry, and local fiber–rope coupling conditions. These results
demonstrate that the distributed sensing system was capable of detecting localized strain transfer
within the rope structure; however, the bonding and wrapping methodology did not provide uniform
strain transfer along the rope length. Improved embedment methods and more controlled rope-
manufacturing techniques will likely be required to achieve reliable distributed strain measurements in
synthetic mooring ropes.

3. INSTRUMENTATION EVALUATION AND LESSONS LEARNED
This section summarizes the experimental evaluation and testing activities performed during the
project, including OTDR instrumentation testing, polymer optical fiber evaluation, connector
preparation, rope embedment investigations, and distributed strain data acquisition. Multiple OTDR
systems and fiber configurations were assessed to evaluate their compatibility with large-core polymer
optical fibers and their suitability for distributed strain sensing in synthetic mooring ropes. The testing
process also identified several practical limitations related to fiber handling, connector preparation,
signal coupling, and mechanical integration of the sensing fiber within rope structures.

3.1. Fontex50 Polymer Optical Fiber
As mentioned previously, the plastic optical fiber used in this project was the Fontex50 fiber (see
Appendix A.1), which was selected because it was expected to accommodate significantly larger strains
than conventional glass optical fiber. The Fontex50 is a graded-index multimode polymer optical fiber
manufactured by AGC with an overall fiber diameter of approximately 490 µm and a core diameter
of approximately 55 µm.
The large-core polymer optical fiber was selected to support high-strain distributed sensing
applications associated with synthetic mooring ropes, where strain levels may exceed the practical
operating limits of conventional glass optical fibers. The larger fiber diameter and polymer
construction were also expected to improve mechanical flexibility and survivability during rope
embedment and handling.
Significant effort was required to procure the polymer optical fiber used in this project. None of the
US-based companies appeared to carry this product any longer. After discussions with multiple
suppliers, AGC, one of the primary manufacturers of CYTOP-based optical fiber, was identified as
the source for the Fontex50 fiber. Procurement of the fiber experienced significant delays due to
overseas sourcing, and securing export approval from Japan. These delays postponed compatibility
testing between the polymer optical fiber and the OTDR systems evaluated during the project. As a
result, several instrumentation compatibility issues were not identified until after the fiber was received
and experimental testing began.

3.2. Evaluation of the Thunderbolt OTDR
The initial OTDR evaluated for detecting reflection events in the polymer optical fiber was the
Thunderbolt OTDR Model TB1315 (Serial No. 100524655). This OTDR was selected because it was
capable of resolving reflection events within approximately 1 m, which was necessary to maintain a
reasonably short laboratory testing area.

The Thunderbolt OTDR was optimized for single-mode optical fiber operating at 1310 nm and 1550
nm wavelengths. However, the Fontex50 plastic optical fiber procured for this project was a large-
core multimode polymer optical fiber with an approximate 55 µm core diameter, compared to the
approximately 9 µm core diameter used in standard single-mode telecommunications fiber. This
difference in fiber geometry and operating wavelength created significant coupling and compatibility
challenges between the OTDR and the plastic optical fiber.

Initial testing involved connecting the Fontex50 fiber directly to the OTDR. Under this configuration,
the OTDR did not produce any meaningful reflection signal from the plastic optical fiber. After
consultation with FIS, the supplier of the Thunderbolt OTDR, it was determined that a launch cable
was required to properly isolate the initial connector reflection and improve signal interpretation. A

 24

100 m 9/125 µm launch cable was then acquired and connected between the OTDR and the fiber
under test, with the primary reflection event expected near the 100 m location, as shown in Fig. 12.

The Fontex50 plastic optical fiber was subsequently connected to the end of the launch cable in an
attempt to obtain detectable reflection signals from the polymer fiber. Although the OTDR registered
a weak signal near approximately 180 m, as shown in Figure 13, the response was small and insufficient
for reliable detection of localized reflection events within the Fontex50 fiber.

Based on these results, it was concluded that the single-mode 1310/1550 nm Thunderbolt OTDR was
not well suited for interrogation of the large-core polymer optical fiber used in this project. A decision
was therefore made to pursue testing with an OTDR compatible with 850 nm multimode operation.

3.4. Evaluation of the OTDR OFM20
Following the unsuccessful evaluation of the Thunderbolt and FIS MM ATLAS Mini-OTDR systems,
additional efforts were made to identify an OTDR compatible with large-core polymer optical fibers
while remaining within the project budget. A used 2005 Opto Electronics OFM20 OTDR was
identified through an independent seller in Albuquerque, NM, as shown in Fig. 15.
The OFM20 OTDR was specifically selected as earlier reports and studies suggested that the system
performed well with large-diameter plastic optical fibers and was capable of detecting reflection events
with millimeter-scale spatial resolution. Although the unit was significantly older than the previously
evaluated OTDR systems, it was considered promising due to its compatibility with polymer optical
fiber systems rather than conventional telecommunications-oriented single-mode fiber systems.
Computer interfacing with the OTDR was achieved using a third-party National Instruments GPIB-
to-USB adapter connected to a modern computer system (Fig. 16). The National Instruments
Measurement & Automation Explorer (NI MAX) software enabled direct terminal communication
with the OFM20 over the GPIB interface, allowing command transmission, instrument control, and
retrieval of OTDR measurement data.

3.5. Rope Embedment

Given the tension-control and abrasion issues associated with manual braiding discussed in Section
2.3, several potential modifications were identified to improve future rope-embedment approaches
for fiber optic sensing applications. Future development efforts would strongly benefit from
collaboration with rope manufacturers experienced in controlled rope fabrication and machine
braiding processes.

 • Mechanical coupling could be achieved by applying intermittent bonding agents or stitch
 points during braiding to physically lock the cable to adjacent strands without fully rigidifying
 the rope.
 • The addition of protective buffer layers (e.g., compressible sleeves, flexible jackets)—could
 mitigate abrasion and promote smoother strain transfer by decoupling small relative
 movements. Similar concepts were considered in the Parafil rope investigations described by
 Rebel et al. [2].
 • Pre-tensioning the cable during braiding may improve alignment and reduce slack that
 contributes to slippage and inconsistent strain transfer.

All of these approaches would be better implemented using machine braiding or semi-automated
processes, which offer tighter control over braid uniformity, pitch, tension, and strain transfer.

The embedment investigations conducted during this project also identified several broader
considerations for future rope-integrated sensing systems:

 • Embedding sensing elements into rope cores or around rope structures requires precise,
 repeatable braid tensioning and strand placement, which are not achievable through manual
 techniques.
 • Rope designs intended for embedded sensing must be tailored for mechanical compatibility
 with the embedded element, whether by creating dedicated fiber channels, co-braiding the
 fiber with structural yarns, or integrating tack points during manufacturing
 • Existing rope architectures do not account for internal differential motion between layers,
 which can damage fragile sensing elements if not properly buffered. Future rope designs for
 sensing applications should be co-developed with sensor integration in mind to ensure
 durability, measurement accuracy, and structural performance in marine environments.

4. KEY FINDINGS AND CONCLUSIONS

This project evaluated the feasibility of using embedded optical fibers and OTDR-based interrogation
methods for distributed strain sensing in synthetic mooring lines for marine energy applications. The
work investigated fiber-optic material selection, optical reflection-generation methods, rope
embedment strategies, and OTDR instrumentation compatibility.
The literature review conducted during this project showed that embedded fiber-optic sensing remains
a promising approach for distributed condition monitoring of synthetic mooring lines. Prior studies
demonstrated that polymer optical fibers can tolerate significantly larger strains than conventional
silica fibers and that helical or core embedment strategies can improve strain compatibility between
the fiber and rope structure. However, the reviewed literature also highlighted several unresolved
challenges and areas requiring further investigation, including strain-transfer consistency, creep
behavior, interrogation-system compatibility, and long-term durability in marine environments. These
findings helped guide the experimental focus of this project toward evaluating polymer optical fibers,
OTDR compatibility, and practical rope-embedment approaches.
Based on a simplified feasibility estimate, UV-laser-induced refractive-index modification in CYTOP
optical fiber may require impractically large fluence levels and processing times to generate detectable
Fresnel reflections. Simpler alternatives, such as air-gap interfaces and optical-cement interfaces, were
therefore identified as more practical methods for generating detectable reflection events within
polymer optical fibers.
Significant procurement challenges were also encountered during the project. The Fontex50 CYTOP-
based polymer optical fiber was not readily available from domestic suppliers and ultimately had to be
sourced directly from Japan through AGC. Delays associated with overseas sourcing, export approval,
and limited product availability postponed experimental testing and OTDR compatibility evaluations
until later stages of the project timeline. As a result, substantial project effort was devoted to
establishing reliable interrogation and handling procedures for the polymer optical fiber before
meaningful sensing measurements could be performed.
Testing performed during this project demonstrated that compatibility between OTDR
instrumentation and large-core polymer optical fibers is also a major consideration for distributed
sensing applications. The Thunderbolt OTDR and FIS MM ATLAS Mini-OTDR showed limited
compatibility with the Fontex50 polymer optical fiber and were unable to reliably detect localized
reflection events with sufficient signal quality. Among the evaluated systems, the Opto Electronics
OFM20 OTDR demonstrated the most promising compatibility with the polymer optical fiber. After
careful connector preparation and polishing, the OFM20 successfully detected reflection signals from
the Fontex50 fiber, and measurable signal shifts were observed during manual fiber straining.
The project also demonstrated that polymer optical fiber handling and connector preparation are
critical to successful operation. Due to the malleability of the Fontex50 fiber, small amounts of
deformation or connector misalignment could significantly degrade optical coupling and reflection
detection performance.
Preliminary rope embedment investigations and distributed strain measurements using the Luna
sensing system demonstrated that localized strain transfer within rope structures could be detected.
However, the measured strain distribution remained highly nonuniform due to inconsistent
mechanical coupling between the optical fiber and the rope structure. The results showed that manual
braiding techniques are insufficient for producing reliable and repeatable embedded sensing

 31

configurations. Controlled machine braiding, improved embedment architectures, and rope designs
specifically developed for sensor integration will likely be required for future applications.
The original test plan included five primary tasks: 1) evaluation of candidate optical fiber materials, 2)
development of rope embedment approaches, 3) tensile and strain-transfer testing, 4) environmental
exposure testing, and 5) reporting and outreach. Tasks 1, 2, and portions of 3 were successfully
completed through evaluation of polymer and glass optical fibers, development of preliminary
embedment approaches, OTDR compatibility testing, and distributed strain measurements using the
Luna sensing system. However, several planned activities were only partially completed or not
completed within the project timeline. In particular, extensive tensile testing of embedded CYTOP
fibers, long-duration cyclic and creep testing, and environmental exposure testing under marine
conditions were not completed due to procurement delays, instrumentation compatibility challenges,
and the significant effort required to establish reliable OTDR interrogation of the polymer optical
fiber.
Overall, the project demonstrated that CYTOP-based polymer optical fibers remain promising
candidates for high-strain distributed sensing applications in synthetic mooring systems. Although
several instrumentation, fiber-handling, and embedment challenges remain, the work established
important lessons regarding OTDR compatibility, optical reflection generation, connector
preparation, and rope integration techniques that can guide future development of embedded fiber-
optic sensing systems for marine environments.
