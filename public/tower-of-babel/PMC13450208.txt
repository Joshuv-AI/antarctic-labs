Creation and validation of the 8-Item Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, version 1.0) in youth and adults
Authors: David Javier-Aliaga, Dámaris Quinteros-Zúñiga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlthough several instruments have been developed to assess emotional eating, no measure has yet been identified that evaluates this construct based on the eight basic emotions. Assessing emotional eating is particularly important because it represents a relevant factor associated with overweight and obesity. In this context, the aim of the present study was to develop and validate the Brief Emotional Eating Scale based on the Eight Basic Emotions (BEES-8, version 1.0) in a sample of Peruvian youth and adults.MethodsA cross-sectional psychometric study was conducted. The non-probabilistic sample consisted of 481 participants of both sexes, with a mean age of 31.72 years (SD = 12.08), recruited from Metropolitan Lima, Peru. The Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, v1.0) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-2) were administered.ResultsA second-order confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated an adequate model fit (CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.962, SRMR = 0.029, RMSEA = 0.076). Furthermore, BEES-8 (v1.0) scores showed a positive and statistically significant correlation with generalized anxiety (r = 0.449, p < 0.001), providing evidence of validity based on relationships with other variables. Finally, the scale demonstrated adequate overall reliability (ω = 0.919).ConclusionThe BEES-8 (v1.0) demonstrated adequate evidence of validity and reliability for assessing emotional eating based on the eight basic emotions. It constitutes a potentially useful tool for research and for the study of eating behaviors associated with emotional states, as well as variables related to overweight, obesity, and emotional regulation.

Introduction

Emotional eating has a pooled prevalence of 44.9% (95% CI: .29–.62) among populations with overweight or obesity, according to a recent meta-analysis that included 18 studies [1]. This high prevalence has been linked to multiple adverse outcomes. In the metabolic domain, emotional eating is positively associated with higher body mass index (BMI), overweight, and obesity [2]. Furthermore, individuals with emotional eating tendencies have been found to consume greater amounts of energy-dense foods, fast food, ultra-processed products, and sugar-sweetened beverages, contributing to poorer diet quality and an increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases [2,3]. From a psychological perspective, emotional eating is consistently associated with depressive symptoms, anxiety, stress, and greater emotional dysregulation, creating a vicious cycle in which emotional distress reinforces maladaptive eating behaviors [2,3]. In terms of eating-related behaviors, emotional eating has been associated with overeating and binge eating, suggesting that it may serve as a marker of more severe eating-related psychopathology [3]. Taken together, the available evidence indicates that emotional eating is not only a highly prevalent phenomenon but also a variable associated with obesity and mood-related disorders, highlighting the need to address it in both clinical and public health interventions.

In this context, several instruments have been developed to assess emotional eating. For example, one of the earliest and most widely used measures is the Emotional Eating Scale (EES), developed by Arnow et al. [4], which consists of 25 items and assesses the urge to eat in response to negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and anger/frustration [4]. Subsequently, Garaulet et al. [5] developed the Emotional Eater Questionnaire (EEQ), a 10-item instrument primarily designed for individuals with obesity, identifying dimensions such as disinhibition, food type, and guilt [5]. Later, Sultson et al. [6] proposed the Positive-Negative Emotional Eating Scale (PNEES), comprising 19 items that distinguish between positive and negative emotional eating [6]. Likewise, Meule et al. [7] developed the Salzburg Emotional Eating Scale (SEES), a 20-item instrument that incorporates both positive and negative emotions and differentiates between increased and decreased food intake according to emotional states [7]. Along the same line, Paleo Garnica [8] developed the Emotional Intake Scale (EIE), whose final version consisted of 42 items distributed across dimensions related to positive emotions, negative emotions, weight concerns, and post-intake thoughts [8]. Furthermore, Cassioli et al. [9] validated the Florence Emotional Eating Drive (FEED), a 25-item measure that simultaneously assesses the intensity and frequency of emotions associated with the desire to eat [9]. Finally, Hooper et al. [10] developed the Comprehensive Emotional Eating Scale (CEES), a 40-item instrument comprising four dimensions related to both positive and negative overeating and undereating behaviors [10].

Although these instruments have substantially contributed to the assessment of emotional eating, no instrument has yet been identified that measures this construct specifically according to Plutchik's model of the eight basic emotions [11]. This represents an important conceptual and practical gap, as existing instruments assess emotional eating without being grounded in a theoretical framework that integrates a specific set of emotions, such as Plutchik's model adopted in the present study [11]. Consequently, they do not allow emotional eating to be examined within this theoretical framework or facilitate the identification of distinct emotional profiles associated with eating behavior. In this context, a brief instrument based on Plutchik's model may provide a more theory-driven assessment of emotional eating, support the identification of specific emotional patterns, and contribute to the development of more targeted preventive and therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, considering that existing instruments comprise between 10 and 42 items, the development of a brief eight-item scale represents a more efficient alternative for large-scale epidemiological studies, routine clinical screening, and other healthcare settings where assessment time is limited, by reducing respondent burden without compromising the theoretical coverage of the construct.

Plutchik's theory of basic emotions [11] proposes that emotions serve essential adaptive functions for survival and adaptation to the environment. The author identified eight basic emotions—fear, anger, sadness, joy, surprise, anticipation, trust, and disgust (operationalized as rejection in the present study)—which represent fundamental emotional responses in humans. According to this model, each emotion serves a specific adaptive function and can be experienced at different levels of intensity [11]. Although disgust is commonly translated into Spanish as asco [12], it was operationalized as rejection in the present study, considering its interpersonal manifestation, because this perspective encompasses experiences of disapproval, exclusion, or interpersonal rejection rather than sensory disgust reactions. This decision is supported by evidence indicating that emotions serve adaptive and motivational functions that influence both individual behavior and the regulation of social interactions [13,14]. Specifically, disgust extends beyond the avoidance of physical contaminants and may encompass moral and social dimensions associated with responses of rejection or avoidance toward people, behaviors, or interactions perceived as aversive [15,16]. This adaptation was intended to enhance the conceptual clarity and contextual relevance of the instrument for assessing emotional eating in the target population.

The present study proposes that emotional eating may be influenced by any of these eight basic emotions, extending beyond the traditional distinction between positive and negative emotions. This approach seeks to broaden the theoretical understanding of emotional eating and provide an alternative framework for its psychometric assessment. Furthermore, it is important to note that the underlying mechanism through which emotions influence eating behavior lies in individuals' tendency to use food as a strategy to regulate their emotional states [17]. Thus, individuals may turn to food to relieve the emotional distress associated with fear, anger, sadness, surprise, anticipation, or rejection, or to maintain and prolong positive emotional experiences related to joy and trust, regardless of physiological signals of hunger and satiety. Consequently, eating behavior may become less guided by biological needs and increasingly influenced by emotional states, thereby promoting the development of emotional eating patterns. Over time, this type of behavior may contribute to excessive food consumption, weight gain, and the development or maintenance of obesity, particularly when food is repeatedly used as a strategy for emotion regulation [[17], [18], [19], [20]].

The Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, version 1.0) proposed in this study is grounded in Plutchik's theory of basic emotions [11], which identifies eight fundamental emotions. Consistent with this theoretical framework, eight items were developed, each designed to represent one of Plutchik's basic emotions. Furthermore, the items were organized into two distinct dimensions: eating driven by survival-related emotions (fear, anger, sadness, and joy) and eating driven by coexistence-related emotions (surprise, anticipation, trust, and rejection). This organization represents a conceptual proposal derived from Plutchik's evolutionary perspective on emotions [11] and from the contributions of Izard [13] and Panksepp [14], who emphasized the adaptive role of emotions in both individual survival and the regulation of social relationships. Within the framework of the present study [11,13,14,20], emotional eating is defined as a dysfunctional eating behavior influenced by dysregulated basic emotions— fear, anger, sadness, joy, surprise, anticipation, trust, and rejection—that occurs independently of physiological signals of hunger or satiety. In addition, eating driven by survival-related emotions (Dimension 1) is conceptualized as the tendency to modify eating behavior in response to emotions primarily associated with adaptation, protection, and individual preservation in the face of environmental demands or threats (fear, anger, sadness, and joy). In contrast, eating driven by coexistence-related emotions (Dimension 2) is defined as the tendency to modify eating behavior in response to emotions linked to exploration, social interaction, and the regulation of relationships with the environment and other people (surprise, anticipation, trust, and rejection).

Considering the contextual framework outlined above, the aim of the present study was to develop and validate the Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, v1.0) in a sample of youth and adults. Additionally, the following specific objectives were established: to evaluate the instrument's construct validity, reliability, and convergent validity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a quantitative, observational, cross-sectional, instrumental (psychometric validation) study situated within health/clinical psychology research [21]. The sample consisted of 481 participants residing in Metropolitan Lima, Peru, who were selected through non-probability purposive sampling [22]. Eligibility criteria included being of Peruvian nationality, aged between 18 and 59 years, and voluntarily agreeing to participate by providing informed consent through an online form. Data were collected virtually using Google Forms during the month of May 2026.

Instruments

Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, version 1.0)

The Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, v1.0) consists of eight items. It is organized into two dimensions. Dimension 1, entitled Eating Driven by Survival-Related Emotions, comprises the emotions fear, anger, sadness, and joy, which are assessed by items 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. Dimension 2, entitled Eating Driven by Coexistence-Related Emotions, comprises the emotions surprise, anticipation, trust, and rejection, which are assessed by items 5, 6, 7, and 8, respectively. Furthermore, for each basic emotion, its continuum of emotional intensity was considered, incorporating both low- and high-intensity manifestations. For example, fear ranges from feeling scared (low intensity) to panic (high intensity). Responses are recorded using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from never (1) to always (5). The total score is obtained by summing the responses across all items, yielding a possible range from 8 to 40 points, with higher scores indicating greater emotional eating. Any use of this scale in scientific publications, research projects, or other forms of dissemination requires prior written permission from the author of the instrument (davidjavieraliaga@gmail.com). The psychometric evidence for the BEES-8 (v1.0) demonstrated adequate validity and reliability. A second-order confirmatory factor analysis showed an acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.962, SRMR = 0.029, RMSEA = 0.076). In addition, BEES-8 scores were positively and significantly correlated with generalized anxiety (r = 0.449, p < 0.001), providing evidence of validity based on relations to other variables. Finally, the scale demonstrated excellent overall reliability (ω = 0.919), as well as adequate internal consistency for the dimensions of Eating Driven by Survival-Related Emotions (ω = 0.827) and Eating Driven by Coexistence-Related Emotions (ω = 0.909). The instrument is presented in Appendix A (Spanish version) and Appendix B (English version).

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2)

The GAD-2 is a brief version of the GAD-7, originally developed by Spitzer et al. [23]. The instrument is designed to assess anxiety symptoms experienced during the previous two weeks. It consists of two items: (1) feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge, and (2) not being able to stop or control worrying. Response options are rated on a four-point scale: not at all, several days, more than half the days, and nearly every day, scored from 1 to 4, respectively. Total scores range from 2 to 8, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety symptoms. The GAD-2 was validated in the Peruvian context by Andrei Franco-Jiménez and Núñez-Magallanes [24], demonstrating a unidimensional structure and adequate internal consistency (ω = 0.80).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31, R version 4.1.2 (RStudio environment), and Jamovi version 2.3.28. Descriptive statistics included frequencies, means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis. The psychometric analyses comprised reliability assessment using McDonald's omega coefficient, construct validity evaluation through second-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and validity based on relations to other variables through Pearson correlation and simple linear regression analyses. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05, with 95% confidence intervals reported when applicable.

Discussion

The present study aimed to develop and validate the Brief Emotional Eating Scale (BEES-8, version 1.0), a measure grounded in the theory of basic emotions. The discussion is structured around the main sources of psychometric evidence obtained in this study, including validity based on internal structure, reliability, convergent validity, and validity based on relations to other variables.

Regarding the internal structure of the BEES-8 (v1.0), the second-order confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated an adequate model fit (CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.962, SRMR = 0.029, and RMSEA = 0.076). These findings provide empirical support for the proposed hierarchical structure, indicating that the two specific dimensions assessed—Eating Driven by Survival-Related Emotions and Eating Driven by Coexistence-Related Emotions—are associated with a higher-order general factor referred to as emotional eating. To the best of our knowledge, no previous psychometric study on emotional eating has evaluated internal structure validity using a second-order confirmatory factor analysis. Nevertheless, the multidimensional structure identified in the present study is consistent with prior evidence. For example, the Positive-Negative Emotional Eating Scale (PNEES) identified a structure composed of positive emotional eating and negative emotional eating, demonstrating that both dimensions represent distinct yet related components of the construct [6]. Similarly, the Salzburg Emotional Eating Scale (SEES) confirmed a four-factor structure corresponding to happiness, sadness, anger, and anxiety, and its replication across independent samples provided additional evidence of structural stability and validity [7]. Unlike the structures proposed by the PNEES and the SEES, which organize dimensions according to emotional valence or specific emotions, respectively, the present scale proposes a theoretically grounded classification that distinguishes between emotions linked to survival and emotions associated with social coexistence [11,13,14]. This approach offers a complementary perspective for understanding emotional eating by considering not only the affective nature of emotions but also their adaptive function within human experience. In this regard, the findings support the conceptualization of emotional eating as a multidimensional construct organized into two specific dimensions: survival-related emotions and coexistence-related emotions. Furthermore, beyond supporting the interpretation of these specific dimensions, the results provide psychometric evidence for the use of an overall scale score, an aspect that has received limited attention in previous emotional eating measures.

Regarding reliability and convergent validity evidence, the findings support the consistency and precision of the scores derived from the BEES-8 (v1.0). The excellent omega coefficient observed for the general factor suggests that the scale provides a reliable overall measure of emotional eating, offering empirical support for the interpretation and use of a total score. Likewise, the specific dimensions demonstrated satisfactory levels of internal consistency. In particular, the Eating Driven by Survival-Related Emotions dimension exhibited adequate homogeneity among its items, supporting the consistency of responses associated with this domain. Similarly, the Eating Driven by Coexistence-Related Emotions dimension showed high internal coherence, suggesting that its items consistently contribute to the measurement of the proposed construct. Compared with previously developed instruments such as the Florence Emotional Eating Drive (FEED) and the Emotional Intake Scale (EIE), which reported reliability evidence for a global emotional eating score without explicitly evaluating a general factor through a second-order confirmatory factor model [8,9], as well as instruments such as the Emotional Eating Scale (EES), the Emotional Eater Questionnaire (EEQ), and the Salzburg Emotional Eating Scale (SEES), which primarily focused on the reliability of specific dimensions or subscales [4,5,7], the BEES-8 (v1.0) explicitly examined the reliability of both the specific dimensions and the general factor derived from a second-order confirmatory factor model. Consequently, the findings provide psychometric support for both the interpretation of the specific dimensions and the use of an overall scale score, thereby extending the evidence base available for the assessment of emotional eating.

The composite reliability and average variance extracted values obtained for the BEES-8 (v1.0) exceeded the recommended criteria, providing favorable evidence of convergent validity. These findings suggest that the indicators adequately represent the underlying factors and that a substantial proportion of item variance is explained by their respective constructs. It is noteworthy that, unlike several previously developed emotional eating instruments, which primarily reported internal consistency coefficients based on Cronbach's alpha, the present study additionally incorporated composite reliability and convergent validity indicators, allowing for a more comprehensive psychometric evaluation of measurement quality. This approach is consistent with current recommendations in psychometric research, which emphasize the importance of assessing multiple sources of reliability and validity evidence beyond traditional internal consistency estimates. Overall, the evidence obtained supports the reliability of both the specific dimensions and the general emotional eating factor. Furthermore, the satisfactory average variance extracted values provide additional support for convergent validity, reinforcing the usefulness of the BEES-8 (v1.0) for assessing emotional eating from a multidimensional and hierarchical perspective.

Regarding evidence of validity based on relations to other variables, a positive, moderate, and statistically significant association was found between emotional eating as assessed by the BEES-8 (v1.0) and generalized anxiety. These findings support the relevance of emotional eating as a construct closely linked to psychological well-being and provide additional validity evidence for the BEES-8 (v1.0). Furthermore, the analyses indicated that emotional eating scores were significantly associated with anxiety levels, accounting for 20.1% of the variance in anxiety. These results are consistent with previous research documenting a close relationship between emotional eating and various indicators of psychological distress, particularly anxiety [3,18,25]. In this regard, it has been suggested that individuals experiencing higher levels of anxiety may be more likely to use food as a strategy for emotion regulation, thereby increasing the likelihood of eating in response to negative emotional experiences rather than physiological hunger cues. Therefore, the magnitude and direction of the associations observed support the validity of the BEES-8 (v1.0), demonstrating that its scores are related in a theoretically coherent manner to a construct closely linked to emotional eating. These findings provide additional evidence that the instrument adequately assesses the phenomenon it was designed to measure and are consistent with theoretical frameworks that associate emotional eating with anxiety symptoms.

Strengths of the study

One of the main strengths of the present study is the development of an instrument grounded in Plutchik's theory of basic emotions, which enabled the conceptualization of emotional eating from an innovative perspective based on the adaptive functions of emotions. Unlike other instruments that organize their dimensions according to specific emotions or emotional valence, the BEES-8 (v1.0) proposes a structure that distinguishes between survival-related emotions and coexistence-related emotions, offering a novel theoretical framework for understanding emotional eating.

Another important strength is the comprehensive psychometric evaluation conducted in this study, which included evidence of validity based on internal structure, relations to other variables, reliability, and convergent validity. In particular, the use of a second-order confirmatory factor model provided empirical support for the existence of a general emotional eating factor, thereby supporting both the interpretation of the specific dimensions and the use of an overall scale score. Thus, the BEES-8 (v1.0) contributes evidence not only regarding the quality of its specific dimensions but also regarding the coherence and usefulness of a global measure of emotional eating, an aspect that has received limited attention in previous emotional eating instruments.

Furthermore, the incorporation of currently recommended psychometric indicators, such as McDonald's omega, composite reliability, and average variance extracted, allowed for a more robust evaluation of measurement quality, strengthening the empirical evidence supporting the use of the BEES-8 (v1.0) among youth and adults.

Finally, another notable strength of the scale is its brevity. With only eight items, the BEES-8 (v1.0) represents a rapid and time-efficient assessment tool, facilitating its use in clinical, community, and research settings. Despite its brevity, the instrument demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, indicating that emotional eating can be assessed efficiently without compromising measurement quality. The brevity of the BEES-8 (v1.0) is particularly relevant considering that most available instruments for assessing emotional eating are substantially longer, including the Emotional Eating Scale (EES; 25 items), the Positive-Negative Emotional Eating Scale (PNEES; 19 items), the Salzburg Emotional Eating Scale (SEES; 20 items), the Florence Emotional Eating Drive (FEED; 25 items), the Emotional Intake Scale (EIE; 42 items), and the Comprehensive Emotional Eating Scale (CEES; 40 items) [4,[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Even when compared with relatively brief instruments such as the Emotional Eater Questionnaire (EEQ; 10 items), the BEES-8 (v1.0) provides a more concise assessment while maintaining adequate psychometric quality [5]. Moreover, unlike previous scales, the BEES-8 (v1.0) allows the assessment of all eight basic emotions proposed by Plutchik using a single representative item for each emotion, thereby providing a parsimonious and theoretically grounded alternative for the assessment of emotional eating.

Public health implications

From a public health perspective, the availability of a brief, valid, and reliable instrument such as the BEES-8 (v1.0) may contribute to the early identification of eating patterns potentially associated with physical and mental health problems. This relevance is particularly important given that emotional eating has been linked to overweight, obesity, eating disorders, and various indicators of psychological distress, including anxiety and depression. In this context, the timely identification of eating behaviors influenced by emotional states may facilitate the design and implementation of preventive strategies aimed at promoting healthy eating habits and improving overall well-being. Given the established associations between emotional eating and adverse health outcomes, the BEES-8 (v1.0) may represent a useful tool for the screening and monitoring of this behavior among youth and adult populations.

Furthermore, the brevity of the instrument facilitates its incorporation into epidemiological studies, health promotion programs, and preventive initiatives conducted in community, educational, and healthcare settings. In particular, the possibility of assessing both an overall emotional eating score and specific dimensions related to survival-related and coexistence-related emotions provides more detailed information regarding the emotional factors that may influence eating behaviors.

Finally, the identification of individuals with higher levels of emotional eating may inform the development of multidisciplinary interventions integrating psychological, nutritional, and educational components, thereby contributing to the promotion of healthier eating habits and overall well-being. In this regard, the BEES-8 (v1.0) may serve as a valuable tool for future research and public health programs aimed at preventing chronic diet-related diseases and promoting healthy lifestyles.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample consisted exclusively of youth and adults residing in Metropolitan Lima, Peru, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other regions of the country, cultural contexts, countries, or age groups. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the assessment of the temporal stability of the BEES-8 (v1.0) and does not allow causal inferences regarding the observed relationships between emotional eating and other variables. In addition, data were collected through self-report measures, and therefore the findings may have been influenced by social desirability bias, recall errors, or subjective response tendencies. Finally, the use of a non-probability sampling strategy limits the representativeness of the sample.

Despite these limitations, the findings provide favorable psychometric evidence for the BEES-8 (v1.0). Future research should examine measurement invariance across sex, age groups, and cultural contexts to determine whether the scale operates equivalently across different populations. In addition, it would be valuable to assess temporal stability through test–retest studies and to expand validity evidence by examining relationships with other relevant variables, such as stress, depression, psychological well-being, body mass index, eating disorders, and dietary habits. Finally, future studies could evaluate the proposed factorial structure in both clinical and non-clinical samples, as well as explore the usefulness of the BEES-8 (v1.0) for the early identification of emotional eating patterns associated with risks to physical and mental health.

Conclusion

The BEES-8 (version 1.0) demonstrated adequate evidence of validity and reliability for assessing emotional eating among Peruvian young people and adults. The findings supported a second-order confirmatory factor structure composed of two dimensions—Eating Driven by Survival-Related Emotions and Eating Driven by Coexistence-Related Emotions—integrated within a general emotional eating factor. Furthermore, favorable evidence was obtained regarding validity based on internal structure, relations to other variables, reliability, and convergent validity. In addition, due to its brevity, theoretical foundation grounded in Plutchik's theory of basic emotions, and adequate psychometric properties, the BEES-8 (v1.0) may be useful for research, epidemiological studies, and health promotion programs aimed at understanding and monitoring the influence of emotional states on eating behaviors, particularly in the context of public health concerns such as overweight and obesity. Overall, the BEES-8 (v1.0) provides a brief, valid, and reliable measure of emotional eating based on the eight basic emotions. Its use may contribute to the advancement of research in public health, psychology, and nutrition by facilitating a better understanding of eating behavior patterns associated with emotional states. Furthermore, this instrument may prove useful for the design, implementation, and evaluation of interventions targeting eating behaviors and related variables, including overweight, obesity, and emotion regulation.
