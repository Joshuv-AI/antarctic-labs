Summary
 This report presents the first direct numerical simulations (DNS) of transient mixed
convection in an idealized downcomer-like channel (Challenge Problem 1, Phase II). Using the
GPU-accelerated NekRS solver, we modeled a sudden decay in driving pressure, mimicking
loss-of-flow events, and tracked the resulting evolution of Reynolds number, boundary-layer
structure, turbulence statistics, and heat-transfer metrics. Key findings include the systematic
thickening and eventual asymmetry of velocity and thermal boundary layers under buoyant
deceleration; minimal “memory” lag in Reynolds shear stress and TKE profiles when sampled
at matching Re, yet clear shifts of peak locations toward the cooled wall; overshoots in transient
eddy-viscosity and eddy-diffusivity (and corresponding sub-unity turbulent Prandtl numbers) on
the cooled side; and a pronounced transient Nusselt-number enhancement driven by wall-
temperature inertia and residual eddy mixing. These effects combined to offer a temporary
cooling margin above steady-state predictions during reactor LOF transients. Future work will
extend this work to a more complex “Case II” geometry (90° turn + lower plenum) and generate
multi-Re/Pr datasets for data-driven turbulence closures.

1 Introduction
 Nuclear energy continues to play a central role in the global energy landscape, with over 400
nuclear power reactors currently in operation across more than 30 countries [1]. The majority of
these installations are pressurized water reactors (PWRs), reflecting their widespread adoption
due to proven reliability and mature technology. Looking forward, advancements in reactor
design and materials science indicate that Generation-IV (Gen-IV) nuclear reactors hold the
potential to deliver enhanced thermal efficiency, improved safety characteristics, and broader
applicability across diverse energy systems [2, 3].
 Generation IV International Forum has selected 6 reactor technologies for research and
development. The designs include Gas-cooled Fast Reactor (GFR), Lead-cooled Fast Reactor
(LFR), Molten Salt Reactor (MSR), Super Critical Water-cooled Reactor (SCWR), Sodium-
cooled Fast Reactor (SFR), and Very-High-Temperature Reactor (VHTR) [2]. Many of these
designs employ unconventional coolant fluids with complex thermophysical properties, posing
unique challenges to thermal-hydraulic modeling. To support their development, the U.S.
Department of Energy's NEAMS Program Integrated Research Project (IRP) has prioritized
these systems under Challenge Problem 1 (CP1) [4]. Challenge Problem 1 in Phase II of the IRP
focuses on Flexible Modeling of Heat Transfer, targeting the formulation of turbulence models
and heat transfer correlations applicable to a broad range of flow regimes and transient operating
conditions encountered in advanced reactors.
 A critical aspect of Gen-IV safety analysis is understanding transient heat transfer
phenomena, particularly those involving transitions between forced and natural convection
during events such as loss-of-flow (LOF) scenarios [5]. Several advanced reactor designs,
including those developed by Kairos Power, TerraPower, and others may exhibit complex flow
behavior under such conditions, including potential flow reversal in downcomer regions. These
and other transient phenomena are often inadequately captured by conventional modeling
approaches, highlighting the need for high-fidelity numerical simulations to inform the
development of reduced-order and system-level models.
 To address these challenges, this work utilizes Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS)
performed with NekRS, a GPU-accelerated spectral element solver, to investigate transient
mixed convection in geometries representative of Gen-IV reactor designs. These simulations aim
to provide fundamental insight into turbulence dynamics, enable accurate statistical averaging
methods, and support the future development of surrogate models based on emerging machine
learning techniques.
 The presented report describes data collection capabilities and preliminary DNS results of
challenge problem 1. To meet the need of advanced turbulence and heat transfer modeling, the
first and second order statistics for momentum and energy fields are collected during transient.
In addition to this, macroscopic parameters dynamics such as Reynolds, Nusselt and Richardson
numbers were numerically collected and analyzed.
Multiscale Challenge Problem Definition
 Challenge Problem 1 aims to establish a foundational dataset and modeling framework for
multiscale heat transfer and turbulence modeling in transient, mixed convection regimes. The
focus is on capturing the complex interplay between buoyancy and inertia in various flow
scenarios where low Reynolds number and flow reversal conditions arise, particularly in
downcomer geometries relevant to advanced reactor designs.

 The goal is to support the development of flexible turbulence closures and heat transfer
correlations that remain valid across varying flow regimes and scales. These efforts address the
broader needs of the nuclear community, with specific relevance to reactor systems developed
by Kairos Power. The data presented in this report focuses on producing numerical datasets for
closures in thermal hydraulics analysis at system and Reynolds Averaged Navier Stokes

2 Numerical Methodology and Problem Description
 The focus of the CP1 research team is on investigating transient thermal-hydraulic behavior
in a simplified downcomer geometry representative of advanced reactor designs. A typical flow
path of nuclear reactor downcomer is shown in Figure 2-1. CP1 research team narrowed down
problem scope to two geometries of interest:
 • Conventual downcomer geometry which will be referred as Case I
 • Modified downcomer geometry with an extended outlet which will be referred as
 Case II and discussed in the end of this section
 In this preliminary study, we focus on Case I: a parallel-plate channel is employed as an
idealized configuration to capture the essential physics of vertical flow passages found in reactor
downcomers, while minimizing geometric complexity. The flow domain is modeled with
periodic boundary conditions in the streamwise and spanwise directions to represent a fully
developed region and to reduce computational cost. From energy conservation perspective, the
configuration adopts a differentially heated wall condition, in which one wall is uniformly heated
while the opposite wall is uniformly cooled. This configuration enables the study of buoyancy-
induced effects and mixed convection relevant to reactor transients.
 The transient scenario of interest involves a rapid deceleration of the flow, initiated by a
sudden reduction in the driving pressure gradient. This mimics operational events such as pump
trip or coast down, which are characteristic of loss-of-forced-flow (LOF) transients in Gen-IV

 2

systems. During such events, the convective transport of heat and momentum undergoes
significant changes, transitioning from forced to mixed or even natural convection regimes.
 By focusing on this transient flow scenario, the study aims to characterize the evolution of
velocity fields, turbulence statistics, temperature distributions, and associated heat transfer
mechanisms. The resulting high-fidelity data will inform the development of generalized
turbulence closures, benchmark simplified models, and ultimately support the safe and efficient
deployment of Generation-IV reactors.

Planned Work
 In addition to the baseline parallel-plate configuration (Case I), we plan to investigate a
modified downcomer geometry (Case II) featuring a 90° inward turn and an extended outlet
leading into a lower plenum. This configuration is intended to reveal potential stagnation zones
and flow non-uniformities as fluid transitions from the downward channel into the plenum. A
detailed schematic and dimensional specifications for Case II are provided in Figure 2-3, Table

 For Case II, we will assess the required turbulence intensity and select the most appropriate
inlet treatment. By default, turbulence can be generated and sustained using recycling boundary
conditions [5], a technique proven to reliably trigger and maintain turbulent flow in downcomer
configurations. If preliminary tests indicate that this approach is unnecessary, we will employ
standard inlet velocity boundary conditions.

equations are formulated in a non-dimensional form to facilitate numerical stability, scalability,
and generalization across flow regimes.

 The computational domain is discretized into 8,640 hexahedral spectral elements using
GMSH, an open-source 3D finite element mesh generator [11]. Detailed mesh parameters are

element, resulting in approximately 4.4 million grid points in total.

Here, these parameters were used as target resolutions.

resolved due to computational cost considerations. However, since the focus of the study is on
the deceleration transient and subsequent transition from forced to mixed convection, the mesh
is designed to ensure adequate resolution as the flow slows down. As the Reynolds number
decreases during the transient, the effective resolution improves. Figure 2-5 represents the

 Figure 3-4 compares the wall-normal, space-averaged profiles of streamwise velocity and
temperature at 5 reference snapshots. In each panel, solid curves denote the evolving transient
solution, and dashed curves show the corresponding statistically steady profiles at the same
Reynolds number. As the flow decays, buoyant forces act most strongly on the heated side,
causing that portion of the fluid to slow more rapidly. This unequal deceleration shifts the peak
of the velocity profile toward the cooled wall (𝑦 = +0.25). The resulting imbalance in shear
reduces near-wall heat transfer on the heated side, which in turn skews the temperature profile:
the zero-crossing and bulk temperature distribution move toward the cooled wall. Together, these
effects illustrate how buoyancy-driven asymmetry in momentum leads to non-symmetric
thermal behavior during the forced-to-mixed convection transient. The same behavior was seen
in IRP Phase I research for time independent mixed convection channel flows [5, 13, 14].

generally lower than that, especially for near cooled wall region.

 As shown in Figure 3-2, the instantaneous wall-to-wall temperature difference during the
transient is consistently lower than in equivalent steady-state cases, reflecting the walls slower
thermal adjustment. Figure 3-11 demonstrates that the Nusselt number, being defined from these
same wall temperatures, remains elevated throughout most of the Reynolds-number decay. This
occurs because the surfaces “remember” their earlier, thinner thermal boundary layers,
producing a temporarily higher apparent convective coefficient. Meanwhile, the inertia of
residual turbulent eddies (still energized by the prior high-Re conditions) continues to promote
mixing near the walls even as the bulk flow slows.

 The combined effect of wall-temperature lag and eddy inertia thus drives a pronounced
overshoot in Nu during the forced-to-mixed convection transient. From a reactor-safety
perspective, this transient enhancement of heat transfer provides important additional cooling
margin during loss-of-flow events, buying valuable time before heat-transfer rates settle to their
lower, steady-state values.

4 Conclusions and Future Work
 This report has presented the first DNS results for Challenge Problem 1, documenting the
transient evolution of forced-to-mixed convection in a canonical downcomer‐like channel. Our
direct numerical simulations using the spectral element codes Nek5000 and NekRS, captured the
decay of bulk Reynolds number, the associated thickening of thermal and velocity boundary
layers, and the emergence of buoyancy‐driven asymmetries in both momentum and heat
transport. Key findings include:
• Boundary‐layer dynamics: As Re decreases, both velocity and thermal boundary layers
 thicken, and small‐scale eddies dissipate — first symmetrically, then skewed toward the
 cooled wall by buoyant deceleration.
• Turbulence statistics: Wall‐normal profiles of Reynolds shear stress and TKE closely follow
 their steady‐state counterparts (minimal “memory” lag), yet the peaks shift asymmetrically
 toward the cooled side, signaling unequal eddy decay rates.
• Eddy diffusivities and 𝑃𝑟𝑡 : Transient eddy‐viscosity and eddy‐diffusivity profiles overshoot
 steady‐state values on the cooled wall (remnant high‐Re eddies), while remaining closer to
 equilibrium on the heated wall. These combine to produce a turbulent Prandtl number below
 unity near cooled wall and converging to unity near heated wall
• Transient heat‐transfer enhancement: The lag in wall‐temperature adjustment combined with
 the persistence of energetic eddies produces a temporary overshoot in heat transfer, offering
 a short‐term cooling margin above steady‐state predictions.

 19

 Looking ahead, last year of this project will extend these baseline results to the modified
downcomer geometry (Case II), incorporating 90° turns and a lower plenum to probe flow
reversal and stagnation effects. Planned efforts include generating multi‐Re, multi‐Pr datasets to
feed physics‐informed machine‐learning closures. These steps will solidify the foundational
datasets and turbulence correlations needed for multiscale thermal‐hydraulics analysis in Gen‐
IV reactor safety and design.
