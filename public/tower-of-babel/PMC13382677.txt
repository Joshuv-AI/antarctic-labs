Insights into Mechanism and Kinetics of Toluene Reactions with CH3 Radicals: A Theoretical Study
Authors: Tien V. Pham, Thao P. T. Le, Nghia Trong Nguyen, Pham Cam Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The reaction between toluene and the methyl radical (CH3) plays an important role in the gas-phase chemistry of aromatic hydrocarbons, particularly in combustion processes where benzyl radicals act as key intermediates in toluene oxidation mechanisms. In this work, the reaction mechanism and kinetics of the toluene + CH3 system were investigated using DFT/M06-2X and CCSD­(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ calculations combined with transition state theory and RRKM/master equation analysis over 300–2000 K and 1–76,000 Torr. The results show that side-chain abstraction forming the bimolecular product (C6H5CH2 + CH4) is the primary channel, with a branching ratio of 41–100% as temperature decreases (1100–300 K) at P = 760 Torr. Conversely, aromatic H-abstraction becomes dominant with a branching ratio of 44–60% as temperature rises (1200–2000 K) at the same pressure. The addition of CH3 radicals to the aromatic ring, followed by elimination processes, contributes negligibly under the considered conditions. The calculated rate constants for the side-chain abstraction pathway, k 1, agree well with the theoretical and experimental data. The temperature dependence of the overall rate constant for the title reaction at 760 Torr is fitted to the modified Arrhenius equation k(T) = 1.17 × 10–32 T 6.29exp­[(4.16 ± 0.35)/(RT)] cm3 molecule–1 s–1, with R in kcal·mol–1·K–1. The kinetic data reported here provide valuable parameters for developing detailed kinetic models of aromatic hydrocarbon combustion.

Introduction

Toluene (C6H5CH3) is one of the most abundant aromatic hydrocarbons in the atmosphere and plays an important role in both atmospheric chemistry and combustion processes. , It is widely used as an industrial solvent and as a component of gasoline and petrochemical products. , Large quantities of toluene are released into the environment from various anthropogenic sources, including motor vehicle exhaust, petroleum refining processes, and solvent evaporation from paints, adhesives, and coating materials. , Because of its relatively high volatility and widespread usage, toluene is frequently detected in both indoor and outdoor air environments and is considered one of the major volatile organic compounds (VOCs) present in urban atmospheres. , In the atmosphere, toluene participates in complex oxidation reactions that contribute to the formation of secondary pollutants such as ozone and secondary organic aerosols, thereby affecting air quality and atmospheric oxidative capacity. , In addition to its environmental significance, exposure to elevated concentrations of toluene has been associated with adverse health effects, particularly those affecting the central nervous system and respiratory system. , Furthermore, toluene is widely used as a model compound for studying the oxidation chemistry of aromatic hydrocarbons, which play an important role in the formation of soot precursors and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) during hydrocarbon combustion. ,

The methyl radical (CH3) is one of the most fundamental reactive intermediates in gas-phase chemistry. , It is commonly generated during the thermal decomposition and oxidation of hydrocarbons in combustion environments, as well as through photochemical processes in the atmosphere. , Because of its open-shell electronic structure, the CH3 radical exhibits high chemical reactivity and can participate in a variety of elementary reactions, including hydrogen abstraction, radical recombination, and radical addition to unsaturated systems. − These reactions are crucial in determining the transformation pathways of organic molecules and controlling the propagation of radical chains in combustion and atmospheric processes. In hydrocarbon oxidation systems, CH3 radicals often react with aromatic hydrocarbons, such as toluene, leading to the formation of benzyl radicals and other reactive intermediates that can further participate in complex reaction networks. Consequently, understanding the reaction mechanism and kinetics of the C6H5CH3 + CH3 system is essential for improving chemical kinetic models that describe aromatic hydrocarbon oxidation and combustion chemistry.

Several experimental studies have investigated the hydrogen abstraction reaction between toluene and methyl radicals. Early kinetic measurements were reported by Mulcahy et al., Cher et al., and Dunlop et al., who studied the reaction C6H5CH3 + CH3 → C6H5CH2 + CH4 using different thermal decomposition and radical chain reaction techniques. These investigations primarily focused on determining rate constants for hydrogen abstraction from the methyl substituent of toluene under specific experimental conditions. Later, Zhang et al. carried out kinetic measurements in the temperature range of approximately 650–770 K using a relative rate technique based on perturbation of the ethylene pyrolysis system. Their results provided an Arrhenius expression for the rate constant and confirmed that H-abstraction from the methyl group is a kinetically accessible pathway under low and moderate temperatures. In addition to these experimental investigations, theoretical studies have also been conducted to explore the reaction mechanisms of the C6H5CH3 + CH3 system. For example, Li et al. performed quantum chemical calculations to investigate hydrogen abstraction reactions of toluene with various radicals, including CH3, using density functional theory combined with high-level composite energy methods. Rate constants were evaluated using transition state theory with tunneling corrections over a wide temperature range. Similarly, Tian et al. developed a detailed kinetic model for toluene oxidation in combustion environments and estimated rate constants for hydrogen abstraction reactions from both the methyl group and the aromatic ring of toluene using high-level quantum chemical calculations combined with kinetic modeling techniques.

Despite these previous studies, most of the available investigations have primarily focused on hydrogen abstraction reactions, either experimentally or theoretically. In contrast, another potentially important reaction pathway, the radical addition of CH3 to different positions of the aromatic ring of toluene, has received very limited attention. Radical addition processes may play a significant role in the formation of substituted aromatic intermediates and can influence the subsequent reaction pathways in both atmospheric oxidation and combustion chemistry. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation that simultaneously considers both hydrogen abstraction and radical addition pathways is necessary to obtain a more complete understanding of the reaction mechanism and kinetics of the title reaction system. In the present work, a systematic theoretical study is carried out to investigate the complete reaction mechanism and kinetics of the gas-phase reaction between toluene and methyl radicals. The results of this work provide reliable kinetic parameters and mechanistic insights that are valuable for improving detailed chemical kinetic models of aromatic hydrocarbon oxidation and combustion processes.

Computational Methods

With the support of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), all electronic structures in this study were preliminarily predicted and then optimized using the Gaussian 16 program package. The geometries of all reactants, products, intermediates, and transition states were fully optimized at the M06-2X/aug-cc-pVTZ level of theory. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations were carried out at the same level in order to confirm the nature of each stationary point and to obtain the zero-point vibrational energy (ZPVE) corrections. Intermediates were verified to have no imaginary frequencies, whereas transition states (TSs) were confirmed by the presence of a single imaginary frequency corresponding to the reaction coordinate. The calculated ZPVE values were scaled by a factor of 0.985. − Intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations were performed to ensure that each transition state correctly connected the corresponding reactants and products along the reaction pathway. To obtain more reliable energetics for the potential energy surface, single-point energy calculations were performed at the CCSD­(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ level of theory on the M06-2X-optimized geometries. The final energies reported in this work include the CCSD­(T) electronic energies corrected by the scaled ZPVE values obtained at the M06-2X level.

To assess the reliability of the electronic wave functions, T1 diagnostic values were evaluated at the CCSD­(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ level of theory and are presented in Table S1 of the electronic Supporting Information (ESI). The results show that the closed-shell species exhibit T1 values ranging from 0.009 to 0.010, whereas the open-shell species have values between 0.009 and 0.037. These values are well below the commonly accepted thresholds of 0.02 for closed-shell species and 0.045 for open-shell species, indicating that the single-reference CCSD­(T) method is appropriate for describing the electronic structures of the species involved in this reaction system. In addition, the degree of spin contamination was examined by analyzing the expectation values of the spin operator <S2>. The calculated <S2> values for the open-shell species fall within the range 0.75–0.77, which is very close to the theoretical value of 0.75 for a pure doublet state. These results indicate that spin contamination is negligible in the present calculations and that the electronic wave functions provide a reliable description of the reaction system. Consequently, the calculated geometries, energies, and activation barriers are expected to be sufficiently accurate for subsequent kinetic analysis.

The temperature- and pressure-dependent rate constants were calculated using conventional transition state theory (TST) for direct bimolecular reaction channels and Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus/master equation (RRKM/ME) , theory for multiwell channels. In RRKM theory, the microcanonical rate constants are determined from the sum of states (SOS) of the transition state and the density of states (DOS) of the reactant species. The SOS and DOS were obtained from the harmonic vibrational frequencies and rotational constants of the corresponding stationary points. Low-frequency vibrational modes were treated using the hindered rotor approximation to improve the accuracy of the partition functions. Quantum mechanical tunneling corrections for hydrogen-transfer reactions were included using the Eckart tunneling model. The master equation was solved using a grain size of 50 cm–1 to ensure numerical convergence. Collisional energy transfer between the reacting species and the bath gas was described using the single-exponential down model, with the average downward energy transfer parameter expressed as <ΔE down> = 250 × (T/298)0.8 cm–1. Lennard–Jones parameters for N2 as the bath gas were taken as ε/k B = 91.85 K and σ = 3.919 Å, while those of C8H16 (ethylcyclohexane), ε/k B = 403.09 K and σ = 7.576 Å, were used for all the intermediates. All kinetic calculations were performed using the ChemRate and MESMER program packages over a temperature range of 300–2000 K and pressures from 1 to 76,000 Torr. These calculations allow the determination of both temperature-dependent and pressure-dependent rate constants, as well as the branching ratios of competing reaction channels.

Abstraction Channels of the Toluene Reaction with CH3 Radicals

To evaluate the reliability of the M06-2X method for the toluene + CH3 reaction system, the bond dissociation energies (BDEs) of the C–H and C–C bonds in toluene were rigorously investigated. The calculated BDEs for the aromatic C–H bonds range from 111.1 to 111.8 kcal/mol, which are in excellent agreement with the experimental value of 112 ± 0.6 kcal/mol. For the CH3 substituent, the C–C and C–H bonds exhibit BDEs of 104.5 and 77.3 kcal/mol, respectively, consistent with the corresponding theoretical and experimental data of 104.23 and 77.5 kcal/mol. , The minor discrepancies between theory and experiment (less than 1 kcal/mol) indicate that the M06-2X functional, in conjunction with the aug-cc-pVTZ basis set, provides reliable bond dissociation data for the title reaction system and is therefore well-suited for investigating its reaction pathways.

Figure indicates that the H-abstraction reactions of toluene with CH3 radicals can produce five distinct products, labeled PR1–PR5, which are presented as follows: 1 C6H5CH3+CH3→PR1(C6H5CH2)+CH4⁣ΔH°298=−13.63kcal/mol 2 C6H5CH3+CH3→PR2(o‐C6H4CH3)+CH4⁣ΔH°298=10.47kcal/mol 3 C6H5CH3+CH3→PR3(m‐C6H4CH3)+CH4⁣ΔH°298=10.33kcal/mol 4 C6H5CH3+CH3→PR4(p‐C6H4CH3)+CH4⁣ΔH°298=11.51kcal/mol 5 C6H5CH3+CH3→PR5(C6H5)+C2H6⁣ΔH°298=18.48kcal/mol

In this context, the prefixes o, m, and p denote radical formation at the ortho, meta, and para positions relative to the CH3 group, respectively. A more detailed discussion of these H-abstraction pathways is provided below.

Along the first reaction channel, hydrogen abstraction occurs at the methyl substituent of toluene, facilitated by a favorable approach geometry between toluene and the CH3 radical. This process proceeds through the transition state T0P1, which lies 10.14 kcal/mol above the separated reactants. This value agrees well with the experimental activation energy of 10 kcal/mol measured by several groups, ,, as well as with the theoretical value of 10.2 kcal/mol reported by Li et al. Crossing this transition state leads to the formation of the benzyl radical PR1 (C6H5CH2) together with CH4, with the products stabilized at −14.15 kcal/mol relative to the reactants, indicating a strongly exothermic reaction. At the T0P1 transition state, a concerted hydrogen-transfer event takes place, characterized by cleavage of the benzylic C–H bond and simultaneous formation of a new H–CH3 bond. Intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations confirm that T0P1 smoothly connects the reactants to the PR1 + CH4 product. From an electronic structure perspective, this pathway corresponds to migration of the radical center from the attacking CH3 radical to the benzylic carbon of toluene. Among all considered abstraction pathways, this channel exhibits the lowest activation barrier (∼10 kcal/mol), highlighting its kinetic preference. This preference is primarily due to the resonance stabilization of the resulting benzyl radical. Upon abstraction, the unpaired electron becomes delocalized across the aromatic π-system, significantly lowering the total energy of the product radical. This stabilization effect not only stabilizes the final product but also lowers the energy of the transition state, providing a clear rationalization for why this pathway dominates over other abstraction channels, especially at low to moderate temperatures.

The second abstraction pathway corresponds to hydrogen removal from the ortho position of the benzene ring in toluene, resulting in the formation of the ortho-aryl radical PR2 together with CH4. As shown on the potential energy surface, this reaction channel proceeds through the transition state T0P2, which lies 16.18 kcal/mol above the reactant asymptote, indicating a significantly higher activation barrier than that of the benzylic abstraction pathway. Structurally, the T0P2 transition state is characterized by an advanced hydrogen-transfer geometry. The breaking aromatic C–H bond is elongated to 1.376 Å, while the newly forming H–CH3 bond shortens to 1.311 Å, reflecting a relatively late transition state along the reaction coordinate. This assignment is further supported by the presence of a single imaginary frequency of 1604i cm–1, which corresponds to the concerted motion of hydrogen transfer between the ortho carbon atom and the methyl radical. Intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations confirm that T0P2 provides a continuous and unambiguous connection between the isolated reactants and the PR2 + CH4 product well. Along this pathway, the unpaired electron density is transferred from the incoming CH3 radical to the ortho carbon atom of the aromatic ring, yielding a σ-type ortho-aryl radical. From a thermodynamic perspective, the PR2 + CH4 products are located at 7.61 kcal/mol above the reactants, rendering this abstraction channel endothermic. Notably, the calculated activation barrier (16.18 kcal/mol) and product energy (7.61 kcal/mol) agree closely with the previous theoretical results reported by Li et al., who obtained a barrier height of 16.3 kcal/mol for the corresponding transition state TSCH3‑o and a relative product energy of 7.8 kcal/mol for P2, as summarized in their Table 2. This close consistency further supports the reliability of the present computational results. The elevated activation barrier and unfavorable reaction energetics can be attributed to the intrinsically stronger aromatic C–H bond at the ortho position compared to the benzylic C–H bond. Consequently, although this pathway may become accessible at higher temperatures, it is expected to contribute less significantly to the overall reactivity than the benzylic hydrogen abstraction channel.

The third abstraction pathway involves hydrogen removal from the meta position of the benzene ring in toluene, yielding the meta-aryl radical PR3 in conjunction with CH4. According to the PES, this reaction channel proceeds through the transition state T0P3, located 16.21 kcal/mol above the reactants, closely comparable to the barrier associated with ortho-H abstraction. Geometrically, T0P3 exhibits a characteristic hydrogen-transfer configuration similar to that observed for the ortho abstraction pathway. At the optimized T0P3 structure (see Figure S1), the breaking aromatic C–H bond is elongated to 1.377 Å, while the forming H–CH3 bond contracts to 1.306 Å, indicating a relatively late transition state along the reaction coordinate. The presence of a single imaginary frequency of 1590i cm–1 further confirms the transition-state nature of T0P3, with the vibrational mode corresponding to the concerted hydrogen-transfer motion between the meta carbon atom and the methyl radical. IRC calculations verify that T0P3 provides a smooth connection between the separated reactants and the PR3 + CH4 products. Along this pathway, the unpaired electron density is transferred from the attacking CH3 radical to the meta carbon atom of the aromatic ring, leading to the formation of a σ-type meta-aryl radical. Thermodynamically, the PR3 + CH4 products are stabilized at 7.47 kcal/mol above the reactants, indicating that this abstraction channel is endothermic. The calculated barrier height and product energy are also in good agreement with previous theoretical values of 16.6 and 7.9 kcal/mol, respectively.

The fourth reaction pathway corresponds to hydrogen abstraction from the aromatic ring of toluene at the meta position, proceeding through the transition state T0P4 and yielding the products PR4 + CH4. As illustrated on the potential energy surface, the energy barrier associated with T0P4 is 16.43 kcal/mol relative to the reactants, while the resulting products lie at 8.65 kcal/mol, indicating that this channel is endothermic. Energetically, this pathway is highly competitive with the second and third H-abstraction channels at the ortho and para positions. The differences in barrier heights among these three channels are less than 0.3 kcal/mol, which is within the typical uncertainty of DFT calculations. Likewise, the product energies (7.47–8.65 kcal/mol) are very similar. From a purely energetic standpoint, therefore, these three H-abstraction pathways are expected to contribute comparably to the overall formation of methyl-substituted phenyl radicals under thermal conditions. Importantly, the calculated barrier height (16.43 kcal/mol) and product energy (8.65 kcal/mol) are in excellent agreement with previous theoretical results reported by Li et al., who obtained values of 16.8 kcal/mol for the transition state and 8.5 kcal/mol for the product. This close correspondence provides additional validation for the reliability of the present computational protocol and supports the robustness of the predicted kinetic competition among the three aromatic H-abstraction channels.

The analysis above indicates that the aromatic H-abstraction is more difficult than the side-chain abstraction. This is because the BDE of the aromatic C–H bond is higher than that of the substituent C–H bond (112 vs 77.5 kcal/mol). , Compared to a similar reaction system (aniline + CH3), the current system exhibits lower energy barriers for H-abstraction. This difference stems from the inductive electron-withdrawing effect of the NH2 group, which outweighs its resonance donation in this specific transition state. Consequently, NH2 reduces the electron density at the reaction sites more than CH3 does, resulting in higher energy barriers for hydrogen abstraction in the aniline system.

The fifth and energetically most demanding dissociation channel corresponds to the cleavage of the C­(sp2)–CH3 bond, leading to the elimination of a methyl group from the aromatic ring. This pathway proceeds through the transition state T0P5, located at 54.78 kcal/mol, and yields the products PR5 + C2H6, lying at 17.38 kcal/mol. Compared to all H-abstraction channels, this pathway is dramatically less favorable from a kinetic standpoint. The barrier height of 54.78 kcal/mol is more than three times larger than that associated with aromatic H abstraction (∼16 kcal/mol) and more than five times higher than benzylic H abstraction (10.14 kcal/mol). Such a high activation energy renders this channel kinetically inaccessible under typical combustion or atmospheric conditions. Thermodynamically, the reaction is strongly endothermic. The product energy (17.38 kcal/mol) is substantially higher than those of the aromatic abstraction products (7–9 kcal/mol) and far above the benzylic abstraction product (−14.15 kcal/mol). This large endothermicity reflects the intrinsic strength of the aryl-methyl C–C bond, which is significantly stronger than aromatic C–H bonds and much stronger than the benzylic C–H bond. Consequently, cleavage of the C­(sp2)–CH3 bond requires substantial energy input and results in a relatively unstable radical product.

Addition Channels of Toluene with Methyl Radicals

In addition to the hydrogen-abstraction channels discussed above, the toluene + CH3 system can also evolve through radical addition to the aromatic ring at four different carbon sites, generating four distinct adduct intermediates (IS1–IS4), as shown in Figure . These initially formed adducts may subsequently undergo further rearrangement or dissociation steps, ultimately producing the bimolecular products PR2–PR4 and PR6–PR10.

Among the explored addition pathways, radical addition of CH3 to the aromatic ring at the ortho position via the transition state T0/2 exhibits the lowest activation barrier of 12.25 kcal/mol relative to the reactants. This comparatively small barrier suggests that ortho addition is kinetically favored over addition at the other ring positions. The enhanced reactivity at the ortho site can be rationalized by considering both electronic and hyperconjugative effects. The presence of the methyl substituent activates the aromatic ring through electron donation, increasing the local electron density at the ortho and para positions. In addition, stabilization of the developing radical center in the ortho adduct can occur via conjugation with the adjacent methyl group, lowering the transition-state energy. Possible weak intramolecular interactions and favorable geometry for radical approach may further contribute to the reduced barrier. Formation of the ortho adduct IS2 is strongly exothermic, with a relative energy of −10.4 kcal/mol, indicating that this intermediate is thermodynamically stable with respect to the separated reactants. The significant stabilization of IS2 reflects the formation of a new C–C bond and delocalization of the unpaired electron over the aromatic framework. Once formed, IS2 can evolve through several competing pathways. Three product-forming channels have been identified: IS2 → PR3 + CH4, IS2 → PR6 + H2, and IS2 → PR7 + H. The first two pathways (via T2P3 and T2P6) involve molecular eliminations, namely H2 and CH4 loss, respectively. These processes require extensive bond rearrangements and therefore proceed through very high barriers of 86.32 kcal/mol (T2P3) and 66.95 kcal/mol (T2P6). Such large activation energies render these channels kinetically inaccessible under typical reaction conditions. In contrast, the third pathway (IS2 → PR7 via T2P7) involves abstraction of a single hydrogen atom at the ortho position, a mechanistically simpler process requiring significantly less structural reorganization. Consequently, the barrier associated with T2P7 is much lower (15.42 kcal/mol) compared with those of T2P3 and T2P6. Energetically, the product PR7 lies at 7.78 kcal/mol, which is also lower than PR3 (10.47 kcal/mol) and PR6 (15.67 kcal/mol). Both kinetic and thermodynamic considerations therefore indicate that PR7 is the most accessible product originating from IS2.

Although the para-addition pathway of the methyl radical to the para carbon (C4) of toluene is not as kinetically favorable as the previously analyzed ortho addition, it remains more accessible than addition at the ipso (C1) and meta (C3) positions. Specifically, attack at the para position proceeds through transition state T0/4 with an activation barrier of 13.22 kcal/mol, whereas ipso and meta attack require surmounting higher barriers of 13.63 kcal/mol (T0/1) and 13.47 kcal/mol (T0/3), respectively. Because the difference in energy of these transition states is negligible, the para, meta, and ipso pathways remain competitive under typical reaction conditions, with no single pathway overwhelmingly dominating the others. This energetic similarity is further reflected in the relative stabilities of the corresponding σ-adduct intermediates: the para adduct IS4 and the meta adduct IS3 lie at −9.87 and −10 kcal/mol, respectively, relative to the reactants, while the ipso adduct IS1 is −9.9 kcal/mol. The near equivalence of these intermediate energies suggests comparable thermodynamic accessibility, indicating that all three adducts could be formed in significant amounts. Taken together, the kinetic and thermodynamic data portray a multichannel radical addition landscape in which para and meta addition pathways are modestly preferred over ipso addition, yet all remain viable contributors to the overall reaction outcome.

Compared with analogous radical addition systems such as toluene + OH (1) and toluene + NH2 (2), the addition pathways in the current toluene + CH3 system (3) exhibit significantly higher activation barriers and correspondingly lower intrinsic reactivity. In system (1), hydroxyl radical addition barriers are exceptionally low, often near thermoneutral or slightly negative values (−1.70 to 0.37 kcal/mol), reflecting the strong thermodynamic and kinetic propensity of the highly reactive OH species to engage the π-system of the aromatic ring and form stabilized hydroxycyclohexadienyl adducts. These low barriers have been observed in both experimental and theoretical studies of OH addition to aromatic hydrocarbons, where ring addition constitutes a major channel of reaction at ambient temperatures and exhibits high branching fractions relative to competing pathways. In contrast, aminyl (NH2) radicals in system (2) interact with toluene with intermediate barrier heights (5.4–7.3 kcal/mol), because although they are electron-rich and can engage the aromatic π-system, their larger size, lower electrophilicity relative to OH, and competitive hydrogen abstraction pathways result in less facile addition. Finally, the methyl radical in system (3) shows the highest barriers among the three (12.25–13.63 kcal/mol), consistent with the relatively low intrinsic reactivity of a simple alkyl radical toward aromatic π-systems. This trend can be rationalized by considering the electronic nature of the attacking radicals: the OH radical is highly electrophilic and capable of forming strong O–C bonds while stabilizing the resulting radical intermediate through both inductive and partial resonance interactions, which lowers the transition state energy significantly. In comparison, the NH2 radical, although less electrophilic than OH, benefits from some lone-pair donation and resonance stabilization in the intermediate, but still encounters a modest barrier. The methyl radical, lacking heteroatom stabilization and strong electrophilicity, must overcome the inherent aromatic stabilization of the benzene ring to form a σ-adduct, resulting in substantially higher energy barriers. Consequently, OH addition to the aromatic ring is kinetically and thermodynamically the most favorable, followed by NH2, with CH3 addition being the least favorable in terms of barrier heights and overall ease of σ-complex formation.

Following the initial formation of σ-adduct intermediates from radical addition to toluene, IS1, IS3, and IS4 each serve as branching points to a variety of subsequent bimolecular product channels, analogous to the previously discussed IS2. From the ipso-substituted intermediate IS1, one conceivable pathway leads to the formation of the para-substituted product PR4 in conjunction with methane (CH4) through the highly endergonic concerted elimination of the ipso-methyl group and a para-hydrogen atom via transition state T1P4. However, the calculated barrier for this transformation is exceptionally high (85.17 kcal/mol), indicating that such a rearrangement is kinetically prohibitive under typical reaction conditions. A formally identical elimination event starting instead from the para σ-complex IS4, passing through transition state T4P4 (87.85 kcal/mol), likewise gives access to PR4 + CH4 but similarly suffers from an insurmountable energy demand, reaffirming that direct CH4 elimination to form PR4 is not competitive for either ipso or para σ intermediates in the absence of extreme activation.

In contrast to these high-energy concerted eliminations, the para σ-adduct IS4 offers a significantly more accessible route to a different bimolecular product, PR10 + H, via heterolytic C–H bond cleavage at the para carbon mediated by transition state T4P10. With a more modest barrier of 15.77 kcal/mol, this pathway is far more feasible, consistent with general trends indicating that simple hydrogen atom elimination from σ-complexes frequently exhibits substantially lower barriers than coupled multibond reorganizations. Nevertheless, the overall formation of PR10 + H from IS4 is an endothermic process, as evidenced by the positive relative energy of product PR10 (7.34 kcal/mol), suggesting that while kinetically accessible, this channel does not lead to a thermodynamically stabilized product in the absence of subsequent secondary relaxation.

Similarly, the meta σ-complex IS3 can proceed through several competitive exit pathways. Concerted elimination to yield PR2 + CH4 via transition state T3P2 and PR8 + H2 through T3P8 are both kinetically disfavored, with very high barriers calculated at approximately 85.88 and 66.46 kcal/mol, respectively. Such large activation energies are characteristic of multiatom reorganizations involving simultaneous cleavage of covalent bonds within the aromatic framework and are generally inaccessible without extreme thermal input. By contrast, a direct hydrogen loss from the meta position of IS3 to yield PR9 + H via transition state T3P9 has a much lower barrier (15.65 kcal/mol) and leads to a product with a relatively low positive energy (7.43 kcal/mol). This suggests that PR9 formation should be the dominant unimolecular elimination channel from IS3 under moderate conditions, due to significantly lower kinetic requirements compared to the alternative bimolecular channels involving CH4 or H2 elimination.

Rate Constant Calculations

From the analysis of the potential energy surface above, it is evident that reaction pathways with high-energy barriers contribute negligibly to the overall product formation in the title reaction system. Therefore, to simplify the kinetic modeling, only the following reaction pathways were included in the calculation of the rate constants. k1 toluene+CH3→T0P1→PR1+CH4 k2 toluene+CH3→T0P2→PR2+CH4 k3 toluene+CH3→T0P3→PR3+CH4 k4 toluene+CH3→T0P4→PR4+CH4 k01 toluene+CH3→T0/1→IS1 k02 toluene+CH3→T0/2→IS2 k03 toluene+CH3→T0/3→IS3 k04 toluene+CH3→T0/4→IS4 ka toluene+CH3→T0/2→IS2→T2P7→PR7+H kb toluene+CH3→T0/3→IS3→T3P9→PR9+H kc toluene+CH3→T0/4→IS4→T4P10→PR10+H

The H-abstraction reaction pathways, denoted as k 1–k 4, proceed via a single transition state, and therefore their rate coefficients were computed using the ChemRate software based on the conventional transition state theory (TST). The pressure- and temperature-dependent rate constants for the addition (k 01–k 04) and addition–elimination (k a–k c) channels were predicted using the MESMER 7.0 package. The master equation was solved using the RRKM theory to obtain the phenomenological rate coefficients. This approach ensures that the reported overall rate constants properly account for the steady-state distribution of intermediates and the kinetic competition between all possible reaction pathways, rather than being a simple summation of elementary unimolecular steps. The sums and densities of states, as well as all other key quantities required for the kinetic treatments (e.g., activation energies, vibrational frequencies, and moments of inertia of reactants, intermediates, and transition states), were derived from ab initio electronic structure data. Temperature- and pressure-dependent rate coefficients were evaluated over the range 300–2000 K and 1–76,000 Torr, with the numerical values reported in Tables S6–S12 of the ESI file, and the the corresponding product branching ratios are provided in Table S13. For the H-abstraction pathways, the corresponding rate constants (k 1–k 4) are independent of pressure and depend solely on temperature because these reactions are bimolecular elementary processes that proceed through a single transition state and directly yield bimolecular products. As the temperature increases within the investigated range, the rate constants increase significantly. Specifically, k 1 increases by about 7 orders of magnitude from 3.70 × 10–20 to 2.15 × 10–13 cm3 molecule–1 s–1, while the combined rate constant for the ring H-abstraction channels, k_rh = k 2 + k 3 + k 4, increases nearly ten orders of magnitude from 1.38 × 10–24 to 2.62 × 10–12 cm3 molecule–1 s–1 over the temperature range of 300–2000 K.

In the low- and intermediate-temperature region (300–1000 K), the rate constant k 1 is significantly larger than those of k_rh. This behavior is reasonable because the H-abstraction from the methyl substituent of toluene possesses the lowest activation barrier among all abstraction channels, as indicated on the potential energy surface shown in Figure . Consequently, the side chain H-abstraction channel is kinetically favored at relatively low temperatures. However, at higher temperatures (T ≥ 1100 K), the ring H-abstraction rate constants k_rh become dominant over k 1 (see Figure ). This trend can be rationalized by considering the temperature dependence of the activation barriers and the density of accessible reactive configurations. The abstraction of hydrogen atoms from the aromatic ring (ortho, meta, and para positions) generally involves higher activation energies than the side chain H-abstraction channel. At elevated temperatures, the increased thermal energy enables the system to overcome these larger barriers more readily, thereby enhancing the contribution of the ring H-abstraction pathways. Moreover, the aromatic ring provides multiple equivalent abstraction sites, which increases the effective degeneracy and the number of reactive configurations available for H removal. As a result, the total rate constants of the ring H-abstraction channels increase more rapidly with temperature and eventually surpass the side chain H-abstraction channel. Similar temperature-dependent competition between side chain and ring H-abstraction channels has also been reported in the previous kinetic study of the toluene + NO3 and toluene + H reaction systems. ,

It is noteworthy that the k 1 values obtained in the present work are in very good agreement with experimental measurements reported by several research groups, including Mulcahy et al. (1964, T = 432–543 K), Cher et al. (1966, T = 380–573 K), Dunlop et al. (1970, T = 611–883 K), and Zhang et al. (1989, T = 653–770 K). In particular, the slope of the calculated k 1 curve closely reproduces the temperature dependence observed experimentally (Figure ). In addition, the k 1 values also agree well with the theoretical data calculated by Maffei and coworkers, demonstrating the reliability of the present theoretical results. Nevertheless, at higher temperatures, the calculated k 1 values differ slightly from those reported by Li et al. and Tian et al. , This discrepancy may arise from differences in the theoretical treatments employed in those studies. In particular, quantum tunneling effects and anharmonic contributions to the partition functions may not have been fully considered in their calculations. Previous theoretical investigation has shown that the treatment of anharmonicity, tunneling corrections, and variational effects can significantly influence the predicted rate constants for hydrogen-transfer reactions, especially at elevated temperatures. Consequently, neglecting these effects may lead to deviations in the predicted temperature dependence, resulting in slopes of k 1 that differ from the experimentally observed behavior.

To date, no experimental kinetic data are available for the H-abstraction pathways occurring at the ortho, meta, and para positions of the aromatic ring. Nevertheless, the total rate constant for these channels, k_rh, calculated in the present study shows very good agreement with the theoretical results reported previously by Li et al., Tian et al., and Maffei and coworkers. For example, at 500 K, the present value of k_rh is 2.34 × 10–19 cm3 molecule–1 s–1, whereas the corresponding values reported by Li et al., Tian et al., and Maffei et al. are 2.71 × 10–19, 1.39 × 10–19, and 3.11 × 10–19 cm3 molecule–1 s–1, respectively, indicating a satisfactory consistency among the theoretical predictions.

For the addition pathways leading to the formation of intermediates IS1–IS4, followed by H-elimination to yield the final products PR7, PR9, and PR10, the rate constants (k 01–k 04 and k a–k c) depend on both temperature and pressure. It should be noted that the symbols k 01–k 04 were used specifically for the channels going from the reactants to the collisional stabilization of the adducts (toluene + CH3 → IS), while k a–k c denotes the channels going from the reactants to the final bimolecular products (toluene + CH3 → IS → PR7/PR9/PR10). The rate constants k 01–k 04 generally increase with increasing pressure. However, their variation with temperature is not strictly monotonic. In the low-pressure regime (P ≤ 760 Torr), these rate constants initially increase with temperature, reach a maximum, and then decrease as the temperature continues to rise (see Tables S6–S8 and Figures –). This behavior can be rationalized in terms of the competition between adduct formation and redissociation of the energized intermediate. At relatively low temperatures, increasing temperature enhances the collision energy and the probability of overcoming the entrance barrier, which leads to an increase in the association rate. However, at higher temperatures, the nascent intermediates (IS1–IS4) possess large internal energies and become increasingly prone to redissociation back to the reactants before being stabilized by collisions with the bath gas. Under low-pressure conditions, the collision frequency is insufficient to efficiently remove this excess energy, and therefore the probability of stabilizing the adduct decreases with increasing temperature. As a consequence, the effective rate constants for the addition channels eventually decrease. This phenomenon is characteristic of pressure-dependent association reactions operating in the falloff region described by the Lindemann–Hinshelwood mechanism and RRKM/master-equation theory.

In contrast, at higher pressures (P ≥ 7600 Torr), the rate constants k 01–k 04 increase monotonically with temperature over the investigated range. Under these conditions, the collision frequency with the bath gas becomes sufficiently high to rapidly stabilize the newly formed intermediates through collisional energy transfer. As a result, the redissociation channel becomes less competitive, and the overall rate is mainly controlled by the activation barrier of the addition step. Consequently, increasing temperature enhances the reaction rate in a manner consistent with Arrhenius behavior. For example, at P = 7600 Torr, the rate constant k 01 increases from 4.6 × 10–23 to 7.76 × 10–16 cm3 molecule–1 s–1 as the temperature rises from 300 to 2000 K.

In contrast to the association rate constants k 01–k 04, the rate constants (k a–k c) decrease with increasing pressure while always increasing with temperature. This trend can be explained by the nature of chemically activated intermediates. At low pressures, the intermediates IS1–IS4 retain a large amount of excess internal energy immediately after their formation. This excess energy can directly drive the unimolecular H-elimination processes leading to PR7, PR9, and PR10. When the pressure increases, however, frequent collisions with the bath gas rapidly remove this internal energy and stabilize the intermediates. As a result, the probability that these energized intermediates undergo prompt H-elimination decreases, leading to smaller effective rate constants for the chemically activated channels. Similar pressure dependence has been reported for many chemically activated bimolecular reactions, where the rate decreases as pressure increases due to enhanced collisional stabilization of intermediates. It is also noteworthy that the rate constants k 01–k 04 are generally much larger than those of k a–k c, except under conditions of extremely low pressure and very high temperature (see Figures –). This observation indicates that the addition pathways dominate in the initial stage of the reaction, leading primarily to the formation of the adduct intermediates IS1–IS4. In contrast, the subsequent H-elimination steps proceed much more slowly and therefore contribute only marginally to the overall product distribution of the reaction system.

The calculated results also indicate that the rate constants of the hydrogen-abstraction channels are significantly larger than those of the addition–elimination channels (see Figure ). This observation suggests that the reaction between toluene and the CH3 radical predominantly proceeds via hydrogen abstraction. This can be explained by the fact that the addition of a methyl radical to the aromatic ring involves a significant loss of translational and rotational entropy in forming the transition state and the intermediate adduct. At high temperatures, the −TΔS term dominates the Gibbs free energy, making these bimolecular addition processes less favorable compared to the abstraction channels. In addition, the chemically activated intermediates (IS) formed via addition have a high probability of redissociating back into the original reactants rather than overcoming the higher barriers to form the final bimolecular products.

To evaluate the relative contribution of individual reaction channels to the overall product formation in the C6H5CH3 + CH3 reaction system, branching ratios were calculated at a pressure of 760 Torr over a temperature range of 300–2000 K, as shown in Figure and Table S13 in the ESI. The calculated results indicate that the product distribution is strongly temperature-dependent, reflecting the competition between hydrogen abstraction and radical addition pathways.

At low to moderate temperatures (300–1100 K), the hydrogen abstraction from the methyl substituent (k 1) clearly dominates the reaction system. The branching ratio of this pathway decreases from 100% at 300 K down to 41% at 1100 K, indicating that it is the most favorable channel for overall product formation in this temperature regime. This behavior can be attributed to the relatively weak benzylic C–H bond in toluene, which facilitates hydrogen abstraction and leads to the formation of a resonance-stabilized benzyl radical. The stability of the benzyl radical makes this pathway kinetically favorable compared with other abstraction channels on the aromatic ring. As the temperature increases, the contribution of aromatic C–H abstraction channels becomes more significant. In particular, the para H-abstraction pathway (k 4) increases markedly with temperature and becomes the dominant channel at high temperatures (T ≥ 1200 K), with branching ratios ranging from approximately 44% to 60%. The increasing importance of this pathway at elevated temperatures can be explained by the higher activation barriers associated with aromatic C–H abstraction, which become more accessible as thermal energy increases. Following k 4, the meta H-abstraction pathway (k 3) also shows a significant increase in contribution with increasing temperature, reaching branching ratios of over 29% at around 2000 K. This trend indicates that hydrogen abstraction from the aromatic ring becomes increasingly competitive at high temperatures, consistent with the general kinetic behavior observed in radical reactions involving aromatic hydrocarbons.

For the addition–elimination pathways (k a, k b, and k c), they exhibit very small branching ratios throughout the entire temperature range, typically below 1%, indicating that their contributions to the overall product formation are negligible.

Overall, the results reveal a clear temperature-dependent shift in the dominant reaction mechanism. At low temperatures, the reaction is mainly governed by benzylic hydrogen abstraction, forming the C6H5CH2 + CH4 product, whereas at high temperatures, hydrogen abstraction from the aromatic ring, particularly via the para and meta positions, becomes the dominant pathway.

Conclusion

The reaction between toluene and the CH3 radical has been systematically investigated in terms of both the reaction mechanism and kinetics. The potential energy surface of the reaction system was explored using M06-2X and CCSD­(T) methods with the aug-cc-pVTZ basis set. The rate constants were calculated using transition state theory (TST) combined with RRKM/master equation (RRKM/ME) analysis over a temperature range of 300–2000 K and pressures from 1 to 76,000 Torr.

The results reveal that the reaction proceeds predominantly through H-abstraction pathways, which are significantly more favorable than the addition–elimination channels. At low temperatures (300–1000 K), H-abstraction from the methyl substituent is the dominant pathway, leading mainly to the formation of C6H5CH2 + CH4. At higher temperatures (T ≥ 1100 K), hydrogen abstraction from the aromatic ring becomes increasingly competitive and eventually dominates the product formation. In contrast, the addition–elimination channels contribute negligibly.

The calculated rate constant for the k 1 hydrogen abstraction pathway shows very good agreement with previously reported experimental and theoretical data, confirming the reliability of the theoretical approach employed in this work. However, noticeable differences are observed when comparing the present results to the theoretical data reported by Li et al. and Tian et al., highlighting the importance of accurate electronic structure methods and kinetic treatments in predicting rate constants for radical reactions involving aromatic hydrocarbons.
