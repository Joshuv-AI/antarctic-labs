Abstract
We consider several examples of nonautonomous systems of difference equations coming from semi-classical orthogonal polynomials via recurrence coefficients and ladder operators, with respect to various generalisations of Laguerre and Meixner weights. We identify these as discrete Painlevé equations and establish their types in the Sakai classification scheme in terms of the associated rational surfaces. In particular, we find examples which come from different weights and share a common surface type D 5 ( 1 ) D_{5}^{(1)} but are inequivalent in two ways. First, their dynamics are generated by non-conjugate elements of W ^ ​ ( A 3 ( 1 ) ) \widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)}) . Second, some of the examples have associated surfaces being non-generic in the sense of having nodal curves. The symmetries of these examples form subgroups of the generic symmetry group, which we compute. In particular, we find ( W ⁡ ( A 1 ( 1 ) ) × W ⁡ ( A 1 ( 1 ) ) ) ⋊ ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ (W(A_{1}^{(1)})\times W(A_{1}^{(1)}))\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} . These examples give further weight to the argument that any correspondence between different weights and the Sakai classification should make use of the refined version of the discrete Painlevé equivalence problem, which takes into account not just surface type, but also the group elements generating the dynamics as well as parameter constraints, e.g. those corresponding to nodal curves.



On the discrete Painlevé equivalence problem, non-conjugate translations and nodal curves

1
Introduction

Second-order systems of differential or difference equations satisfied by various quantities related to semi-classical orthogonal polynomials often turn out to be differential or discrete Painlevé equations.
The earliest example is perhaps that of a second-order difference equation shown by Shohat
[
Sho39
]
to be satisfied by the coefficients in the three-term recurrence for polynomials
P_{n}(x)
orthogonal with respect to the weight
w(x;t)=e^{tx^{2}-x^{4}}
.
This turns out to be a special case of a discrete Painlevé equation, which was first dubbed a discrete Painlevé-I equation when it was found, also in relation to orthogonal polynomials, in
[
FIK91
]
, due to it admitting a continuum limit to the first Painlevé equation
\text{P}_{\mathrm{I}}
.
Since then, numerous cases of polynomials orthogonal with respect to semi-classical weights have been shown to be related to Painlevé equations, differential or discrete, via systems of differential or difference equations satisfied by recurrence coefficients or other associated quantities (see e.g. the monograph
[
VA18
]
and references therein).

However, the systems may not immediately take the standard form of a Painlevé equation, but rather are written in coordinates coming from the orthogonal polynomials side - they are Painlevé equations in disguise.
The problem of unmasking such an example, i.e. finding a birational change of variables to some standard form of a Painlevé equation, is known as a “Painlevé equivalence problem”
[
Cla19
]
.
Sometimes, solving it is possible by inspection of the equations themselves, but at other times it is far from obvious.
In
[
DFS20
]
, an algorithmic procedure was presented for recognising a discrete Painlevé equation written in non-standard coordinates, determining its type in the Sakai classification scheme
[
Sak01
]
, and transforming it to a standard form of a discrete Painlevé equation of the same type, e.g. as appearing in
[
KNY17
]
(see
[
DFS22
]
for the differential case and
[
DFLS24
]
for considerations of Hamiltonian structures).

The Sakai classification scheme for discrete Painlevé equations is based on a classification of certain rational surfaces.
Sakai defined a generalised Halphen surface as a smooth projective rational surface over
\mathbb{C}
with an effective anticanonical divisor
D=\sum_{i}m_{i}D_{i}
such that the intersection numbers
D_{i}\bullet D=0
for all
i
(such an anticanonical divisor
D
is said to be of canonical type).
A generalised Halphen surface is either a Halphen surface of index one (a kind of rational elliptic surface), or has unique effective anticanonical divisor, and in this paper we refer to this latter type as
Sakai surfaces
, which were classified in
[
Sak01
]
.
Symmetries of Sakai surfaces give rise to discrete Painlevé equations.

Part of the data specifying the type of a discrete Painlevé equation is a pair of affine Dynkin diagrams associated to the
surface type

\mathcal{R}
and
symmetry type

\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
.
The surface type
\mathcal{R}
describes the unique effective anticanonical divisor, in a way similar to the Kodaira classification of singular fibres of elliptic surfaces.
The symmetry type
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
is that of the affine Weyl group in terms of which the symmetries of the surface are described, and is determined by
\mathcal{R}
.

When there are multiple instances of discrete Painlevé equations arising from some common situation where there is a classification present, it is natural to ask how this matches with the Sakai scheme.
This is the case for discrete Painlevé equations appearing in relation to recurrence coefficients of semiclassical orthogonal polynomials and gap probabilities in related probabilistic models, and one may ask how different weights on the orthogonal polynomials side match up with different types of discrete Painlevé equations.
Among the discrete systems coming from these contexts that have been identified as discrete Painlevé equations and placed in the Sakai classification are examples of surface/symmetry type
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\perp})
being

(A_{1}^{(1)},E_{7}^{(1)})

[
DK20
]
,

(D_{4}^{(1)},D_{4}^{(1)})

[
DFS20
,
Bor03
,
BB03
]
,

(D_{5}^{(1)},A_{3}^{(1)})

[
BB03
,
HDC20
]
,

(D_{6}^{(1)},(A_{1}+A_{1})^{(1)})

[
LDFZ25
]
,

(E_{6}^{(1)},A_{2}^{(1)})

[
Sho39
,
DFS22
,
TD24
]
,
(A_{5}^{(1)},(A_{2}+A_{1})^{(1)})

[
HDC25
]
.

However, the pair
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\perp})
does not uniquely determine a discrete Painlevé equation, even up to equivalence via birational changes of variables, and there can be infinitely many inequivalent discrete Painlevé equations of the same surface type, corresponding to non-conjugate (quasi-)translation elements of the symmetry group corresponding to
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
.
Further, while each surface type determines a unique symmetry type, this refers to symmetries of families of surfaces in the
generic
case.
When parameters take special values, the symmetries compatible with these form a subgroup of that from the generic case, which may not appear explicitly in the Sakai list.
The existence of such ‘exotic’ symmetry types arising from non-generic Sakai surfaces has been known about for quite some time, at least as far back as
[
Tak03
]
, in which the extended affine Weyl group
\widetilde{W}((A_{1}+A_{3})^{(1)})
was identified as a subgroup of the generic
\widetilde{W}(D_{5}^{(1)})
symmetry for surfaces of type
A_{3}^{(1)}
.
For more examples of exotic symmetry types being identified in discrete Painlevé equations, see
[
AHJN16
,
Shi25
,
DSSW25
]
.
Such non-generic symmetry types can arise when the surface has nodal curves (here meaning
(-2)
-curves disjoint from the anticanonical divisor, see
Definition

3.1
) or when there are constraints on parameters in the surface and associated equations related to projective reduction
[
KNT11
]
.
Combinations of the two are also possible, see
Remark

3.4
in the main text.

The point of this paper is to demonstrate that any correspondence between different weights and different types of discrete Painlevé equations should be
finer
than the type
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\perp})
.
When a discrete Painlevé equation appearing in the context of orthogonal polynomials (or another of the many areas of mathematics and physics in which they play a role), to specify the equation up to equivalence via birational changes of variables one should provide a tuple
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\perp},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{S},[w])
, where:

•

\mathcal{R}
is the surface type,

•

\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
is the generic symmetry type, which is determined by
\mathcal{R}
,

•

\mathcal{C}
is the specification of any parameter constraints, which when written in terms of root variables defines a subset of the set of isomorphism classes of surfaces of type
\mathcal{R}
,

•

\mathcal{S}
is the symmetry type, which corresponds to a subgroup (which may be proper if
\mathcal{C}
is nontrivial) of the generic one corresponding to
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
,

•

[w]
is the conjugacy class of the symmetry associated to the equation.

We demonstrate the necessity to specify not just the pair
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\perp})
through several examples coming from orthogonal polynomials with respect to different weights.
The examples we consider all share the same
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
, but have different
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{S}
and
[w]
.
In
[
LDFZ25
]
, two of us, together with Li and Zhang, pointed out the necessity to specify
[w]
when considering a discrete Painlevé identification problem, and in this paper we demonstrate also the necessity to specify
\mathcal{C}
and the
\mathcal{S}
it determines, and the implications of this for the notion of symmetry types for discrete Painlevé equations.

1.1
Discrete systems from various generalisations of Meixner and Laguerre orthogonal polynomials

The way in which the examples we consider arise from orthogonal polynomials is via three-term recurrence relations for the polynomials, or the related ladder operator construction.
The former is classical, see e.g.
[
Sze75
,
Chi78
,
VA18
]
, and the latter was developed by Chen and Ismail
[
CI97
]
and is by now well-known, see
[
Ism05
, Ch. 3]
.
We remark that discrete Painlevé equations can also be derived from orthogonal polynomials via isomonodromy deformations of linear differential equations satisfied by the polynomials and associated Riemann-Hilbert problems as in, e.g.,
[
FIK91
,
BB03
]
.

In each case we consider below, there are two quantities, related to the recurrence coefficients and/or ladder operators of the relevant orthogonal polynomials, which satisfy a system of difference equations with respect to the index
n
of the polynomials, and a system of differential equations with respect to a parameter in the weight.

The examples we will consider are as follows. We will not reproduce the derivations of the systems and instead defer these to the references given below.

1.1.1
Laguerre weight on a finite interval (L)

This weight was considered in
[
LC17
]
and is given by

(1.1)

w^{\operatorname{L}}(x;\alpha)=x^{\alpha}\exp(-x),\quad x\in[0,s],\quad s\geq 0,\alpha>0.

This leads to a system of difference equations with respect to
n
and a system of differential equations with respect to
s
for functions
f=f_{n}(s)
,
g=g_{n}(s)
coming from the ladder operators for the polynomials orthogonal with respect to the weight (
1.1
).
Explicitly the discrete system is given by

(L)

\left\{\bar{f}f=\frac{(g-n)(g-\alpha-n)}{g^{2}},\qquad\underaccent{\bar}{g}+g=\frac{(1-\alpha+s-2n)f+\alpha+2n+1}{(f-1)^{2}},\right.

where
\bar{f}=f_{n+1}
,
\underaccent{\bar}{g}=g_{n-1}
.
Similar notation will be used to indicate discrete evolution with
n
throughout the paper.

1.1.2
Perturbed Laguerre weight on the nonnegative real line (pL)

This weight was considered in
[
MC20
]
, and also earlier in
[
FO10
]
, and is given by

(1.2)

w^{\operatorname{pL}}(x;s,\alpha,\gamma)=x^{\alpha}\exp(-x)|x-s|^{\gamma}(A+B\theta(x-s)),\quad x\in[0,\infty),\quad\begin{gathered}s\geq 0,\,\alpha>0,\,\gamma>0,\\
A\geq 0,\,A+B\geq 0.\end{gathered}

This leads to a system of difference equations with respect to
n
and a system of differential equations with respect to
s
for functions
f=f_{n}(s)
,
g=g_{n}(s)
coming from the ladder operators for the polynomials orthogonal with respect to the weight (
1.2
), in which
\alpha
and
\gamma
are regarded as constant parameters.
Explicitly, the discrete system is given by

(pL)

\left\{\bar{f}f=\frac{(g-n)(g-\alpha-n)}{g(g+\gamma)},\quad\underaccent{\bar}{g}+g=\frac{-\gamma f^{2}+(1-\alpha+s-2n+\gamma)f+\alpha+2n+1}{(f-1)^{2}},\right.

and reduces to the system (
L
) when
\gamma=0
.

1.1.3
Semi-classical Meixner weight on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(M)

This weight was considered in
[
BFVA11
]
and is given by

(1.3)

w^{\operatorname{M}}(k;c,\gamma)=\frac{(\gamma)_{k}c^{k}}{(k!)^{2}},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\quad c>0,\,\gamma>0.

This leads to a system of difference equations with respect to
n
and a system of differential equations with respect to
c
for functions
x=x_{n}(c)
,
y=y_{n}(c)
related to the coefficients
a_{n}^{2}
,
b_{n}
from the three-term recurrence satisfied by the polynomials orthonormal with respect to the weight (
1.3
).
Explicitly the discrete system is

(M)

\left\{(\bar{x}+y)(x+y)=\frac{(\gamma-1)}{c^{2}}y(y-c)^{2},\quad(x+\underaccent{\bar}{y})(x+y)=\frac{x(x+c)^{2}}{x-\tfrac{cn}{\gamma-1}}.\right.

1.1.4
Generalised semi-classical Meixner weight on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(gM)

The weight was considered in
[
SVA12
,
FVA11
]
and is given by

(1.4)

w^{\operatorname{gM}}(k;c,\beta,\gamma)=\frac{(\gamma)_{k}c^{k}}{(\beta)_{k}k!},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\quad c>0,\,\beta>0,\,\gamma>0.

This leads to a system of difference equations with respect to
n
and a system of differential equations with respect to
c
for functions
x=x_{n}(c)
,
y=y_{n}(c)
related to the coefficients
a_{n}^{2}
,
b_{n}
from the three-term recurrence satisfied by the polynomials orthonormal with respect to the weight (
1.4
).
Explicitly the discrete system is

(gM)

\left\{(\bar{x}+y)(x+y)=\frac{(\gamma-1)}{c^{2}}y(y-c)\left(y-c\frac{\gamma-\beta}{\gamma-1}\right),\quad(x+\underaccent{\bar}{y})(x+y)=\frac{x(x+c)}{x-\tfrac{cn}{\gamma-1}}\left(x+c\frac{\gamma-\beta}{\gamma-1}\right),\right.

and reduces to the system (
M
) when
\beta=1
.

1.1.5
Known relations to discrete Painlevé equations

Regarding connections between the above weights and differential and discrete Painlevé equations, we make the following remarks.

For (
L
), the discrete system is a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
, and the associated differential system with respect to
s
can be transformed to a special case of one of the standard Hamiltonian forms of the fifth Painlevé equation
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
, which shares that surface type.
This was established in
[
HDC20
]
, using the approach of
[
DFS20
,
DFS22
]
.

For (
pL
), we remark that the
\gamma=0
case of the weight
w^{\operatorname{pL}}
in equation (
1.2
) was studied by Basor and Chen in
[
BC09
]
, and they derived a particular case of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
, which coincides with that associated with (
L
).
In the general case, the ladder operator construction was performed by Forrester and Ormerod
[
FO10
]
, who also compared this with the approach involving a Riemann-Hilbert problem for the orthogonal polynomials along the lines of
[
FIK91
]
.
They connected the differential system with respect to
s
with
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
, which was also done in
[
MC20
]
(in a way consistent with the results of
[
BC09
]
when
\gamma=0
), but the discrete system itself was not identified as a discrete Painlevé equation.
We remark that the discrete system studied by Zhu, Chen and Zhang in
[
ZCZ25
]
is very similar but comes from a different weight, and was identified with a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
. This example is the same one with which we identify (
pL
) below, but the recurrence considered in
[
ZCZ25
]
comes with a different initial condition.

For (
M
), the weight
w^{\operatorname{M}}
in equation (
1.3
) is a special case of one introduced by Ronveaux
[
Ron86
]
, and when
\gamma=1
, the weight
w^{\operatorname{M}}
reduces to the classical Charlier weight
\frac{c^{k}}{k!}
.
In this case, the discrete system breaks down and is not required to compute the recurrence coefficients, which are given by
a_{n}^{2}=cn
,
b_{n}=n+c
.
We remark that the
N=2
case of the generalised Charlier weight
\frac{c^{k}}{(k!)^{N}}
on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
defined in
[
HHR00
]
was shown to be related to a particular case of a discrete Painlevé equation known as
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{II}}
in
[
VAF03
]
, which also shares the surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
.

For (
gM
), the connection to the fifth Painlevé equation was found in
[
FVA11
]
.
The discrete system was derived in
[
SVA12
]
, and was identified as a limiting case of a discrete Painlevé equation quoted from
[
BR04
]
and
[
VA07
]
.
This limiting procedure involves introducing a parameter
\varepsilon
into the variables and coefficients in an example of a discrete Painlevé equation, then taking a formal limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
to derive the system (
gM
).
Therefore neither the type of the discrete system (
gM
) in the Sakai scheme, nor its possible relation to a standard example of a discrete Painlevé equation via birational transformation, were established.
The weight
w^{\operatorname{gM}}
in equation (
1.4
) can be considered on either the lattice
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
or the shifted lattice
\mathbb{N}+1-\beta
, and these can be combined to give a weight on the bilattice
\mathbb{N}\cup\left(\mathbb{N}+1-\beta\right)
.
These three situations lead to the same discrete and differential systems but with different initial conditions, as shown in
[
SVA12
]
and explained in
[
FVA11
]
.
An alternative derivation of the relation between the discrete and differential systems and
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
via Hankel determinant representations and the
\sigma
-form of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
was given in
[
Cla13
]
.

1.2
Summary of results

The systems of difference equations (
L
), (
pL
), (
M
), and (
gM
) correspond to discrete Painlevé equations which all share a surface type
\mathcal{R}=D_{5}^{(1)}
, and the systems of differential equations are all related by birational transformations to instances of the fifth Painlevé equation
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
in its standard Hamiltonian form, which lives on the same type of surface.
However, the examples of discrete Painlevé equations are distinguished by the conjugacy classes of their associated symmetries as well as their symmetry groups, which is related to the presence of nodal curves on the surfaces, as we will elaborate on in
Section

3
.

The generic symmetry type of a Sakai surface of type
D_{5}^{(1)}
is
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}=A_{3}^{(1)}
.
That is, for a generic surface of type
D_{5}^{(1)}
, the symmetry group (in the sense of Cremona isometries of the surface and their Cremona action as made precise in
[
Sak01
,
AGS25
]
) is
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)}):=W(A_{3}^{(1)})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(A_{3}^{(1)})
,
where
W(A_{3}^{(1)})
is the affine Weyl group of type
A_{3}^{(1)}
and
\operatorname{Aut}(A_{3}^{(1)})
is the group of automorphisms of the corresponding (extended) Dynkin diagram.
Discrete Painlevé equations are generated by translation elements (or more generally elements of infinite order) of this group.

There are two examples of discrete Painlevé equations of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
which are relevant in this paper.

The first is that appearing in
[
KNY17
, Sec. 8.1.17]
, which we refer to as the KNY example:

(
\operatorname{KNY}
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{q}&=1-q-\frac{a_{0}}{p+t}-\frac{a_{2}}{p},\\
\underaccent{\bar}{p}&=-p-t+\frac{a_{1}}{q}+\frac{a_{3}}{q-1},\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} \bar{a}_{0}&=a_{0}+1,&&\bar{a}_{1}=a_{1}-1,\\
\bar{a}_{2}&=a_{2}+1,&&\bar{a}_{3}=a_{3}-1,\end{aligned}\right.

where we have adopted the usual convention of writing the non-autonomous nature of a discrete Painlevé equation in terms of evolution of parameters
a_{i}
, which here are normalised so that
a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=1
.
The associated symmetry is a translation, which we denote by

T_{\operatorname{KNY}}\in\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
,
associated with a weight of squared length
1
of the underlying finite
A_{3}
type root system.

The second is one that appeared in
[
Sak01
, Sec. 7]
(though in different coordinates) labelled as
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{IV}}
, which we refer to as the Sakai example:

(
\operatorname{Sak}
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{q}&=\frac{p+t}{t}\left(1-\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}}{a_{0}+(p+t)q}\right),\\
\bar{p}&=\frac{1}{1-\bar{q}}\left(a_{2}+\frac{(a_{0}+a_{1})p}{t+p-t\bar{q}}\right),\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} \bar{a}_{0}&=a_{0}+1,&&\bar{a}_{1}=a_{1},\\
\bar{a}_{2}&=a_{2},&&\bar{a}_{3}=a_{3}-1.\end{aligned}\right.

The associated symmetry is a translation, which we denote by

T_{\operatorname{Sak}}\in\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
,
which is associated to a weight of squared length
\tfrac{3}{4}
, so is not conjugate to
T_{\operatorname{KNY}}
.

Our results establishing the types of the systems of difference equations from the various weights above as discrete Painlevé equations are summarised as follows.

Theorem A
.

The system of difference equations (
pL
) is a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
with

(1)

symmetry group element being a translation conjugate to
T_{\operatorname{KNY}}
;

(2)

surfaces being generic, in that root variables are free when parametrised by parameters from the weight;

(3)

the full symmetry group
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})=W(A_{3}^{(1)})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(A_{3}^{(1)})
.

The system (
pL
) is related to the KNY example (

KNY

) by a birational change of variables up to appropriate parameter identification.

Theorem B
.

The system of difference equations (
L
) is a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
with

(1)

symmetry group element being a translation conjugate to
T_{\operatorname{KNY}}
;

(2)

parameter constraint
a_{1}+a_{2}=0
on root variables, which corresponds to the existence of a nodal curve;

(3)

symmetry subgroup
W(({A_{1}}+\underset{|\alpha|^{2}=4}{A_{1}})^{(1)})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\subset\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
, where the generator of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
acts by a particular automorphism of the pair of two Dynkin diagrams of type
A_{1}^{(1)}
.

The system (
L
) is related to the
a_{1}+a_{2}=0
case of the KNY example (

KNY

) by a birational change of variables up to appropriate parameter identification.

Theorem C
.

The system of difference equations (

) is a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
with

(1)

surfaces being generic, in that root variables are free when parametrised by parameters from the weight;

(2)

symmetry group element being a translation conjugate to
T_{\operatorname{Sak}}
;

(3)

the full symmetry group
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})=W(A_{3}^{(1)})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(A_{3}^{(1)})
.

The system (
gM
) is related to the Sakai example (

.

The system of difference equations (

) is a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
with

(1)

symmetry group element being a translation conjugate to
T_{\operatorname{Sak}}
;

(2)

parameter constraint
a_{2}=1
on root variables, which corresponds to the existence of a nodal curve;

(3)

symmetry subgroup
W(({A_{1}}+\underset{|\alpha|^{2}=4}{A_{1}})^{(1)})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\subset\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
, where the generator of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

2
Reference model of Sakai surfaces of type
D_{5}^{(1)}

In this Section, we recall the necessary geometric and algebraic data for the realisation of the
D_{5}^{(1)}
surface family and its associated discrete Painlevé equations that we will use as our reference model, with which to identify the examples from orthogonal polynomials outlined in
Section

1.1
.
This realisation is the one provided in Section 8.2.18 of the survey paper
[
KNY17
]
of Kajiwara, Noumi and Yamada, most of the details of which can also be found in
[
HDC20
]
, but we include it here to make the present paper self-contained.
For a formal account of details of the general theory of discrete Painlevé equations we refer to
[
AGS25
]
as well as the original paper of Sakai
[
Sak01
]
.

To differentiate between the different instances of surfaces of type
D_{5}^{(1)}
we encounter in this paper, we provide the following.

Notation 2.1
.

For the Kajiwara-Noumi-Yamada (KNY) model of
D_{5}^{(1)}
surfaces we use the following notation: coordinates
(q,p)
;
parameters
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
(the root variables); continuous independent variable
t
(extra parameter);
centres of blowups
b_{i}
; exceptional divisors
E_{i}
.

2.1
Point configuration and surfaces

We begin with the sequence of blowups performed in the KNY realisation of Sakai surfaces of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
.
Take
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with coordinates
(q,p)
, and compactify this to
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
with the atlas

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}=\mathbb{C}^{2}_{(q,p)}\cup\mathbb{C}^{2}_{(Q,p)}\cup\mathbb{C}^{2}_{(q,P)}\cup\mathbb{C}^{2}_{(Q,P)},\quad Q=1/q,P=1/p,

where here we use subscripts to indicate coordinates.
This provides a compactification of
\mathbb{C}^{2}_{(q,p)}
, in that we have glued in two copies of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
corresponding to
q=\infty
and
p=\infty
, given respectively by
Q=0
and
P=0
.
Before we specify the points to be blown up to arrive at the surfaces, we specify our notation for charts.

Notation 2.2
.

For each blowup, we introduce two coordinate charts according to the following convention:
after blowing up a point
b_{i}
in a surface
Y
, we denote the resulting surface by
\operatorname{Bl}_{b_{i}}Y
, the projection (blowup morphism) by
\pi_{i}:\operatorname{Bl}_{b_{i}}Y\rightarrow Y
, and the exceptional divisor of the blowup of
b_{i}
by
E_{i}=\pi^{-1}(b_{i})\subset\operatorname{Bl}_{b_{i}}Y
,
E_{i}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
If
b_{i}
is given in some local coordinates
(x,y)
for an open subset of
Y
by

b_{i}:(x,y)=(x_{*},y_{*})
,
then the exceptional divisor
E_{i}
is covered by two charts on
\operatorname{Bl}_{b_{i}}Y
with coordinates
(u_{i},v_{i})
and
(U_{i},V_{i})
, related to those of
Y
under the blowdown morphism according to

(2.2)

\begin{aligned} x&=u_{i}+x_{*},&y&=u_{i}v_{i}+y_{*},\\
u_{i}&=x-x_{*},&v_{i}&=\frac{y-y_{*}}{x-x_{*}},\end{aligned}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{aligned} x&=U_{i}V_{i}+x_{*},&y&=V_{i}+y_{*},\\
U_{i}&=\frac{x-x_{*}}{y-y_{*}},&V_{i}&=y-y_{*}.\end{aligned}

In particular, in the coordinates
(u_{i},v_{i})
the exceptional divisor
E_{i}
is given by the local equation
u_{i}=0
, with the part of
E_{i}
visible in this chart parametrised by
v_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Similarly, in the coordinates
(U_{i},V_{i})
,
E_{i}
is given by
V_{i}=0
, with the part of
E_{i}
visible in this chart parametrised by
U_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 2.3
.

There is some variation in the literature in the definitions of charts introduced in the blowup procedure, despite the same symbols
(u_{i},v_{i})
and
(U_{i},V_{i})
being used.
Here we adopt the same convention as
[
DFS20
,
DFS22
,
DFLS24
]
, which is different from, e.g.,
[
FS23A
,
FS23B
]
.

The points to be blown up depend on parameters (root variables)
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
, subject to
a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=1
, as well as
t\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
(called the ‘extra parameter’ in
[
Sak01
]
) which will play the role of independent variable of the Hamiltonian form of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
which will be given below.

We initially blow up the four points
b_{1},b_{3},b_{5},b_{7}
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, then perform four subsequent blowups of points which lie on the exceptional divisors of the first four.
We give the locations of these points in coordinates explicitly in Figure
1
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}b_{1}:(q,p)=(\infty,-t)&\leftarrow\quad b_{2}:(u_{1},v_{1})=\left(Q,\frac{p+t}{Q}\right)=(0,-a_{0}),\\
b_{3}:(q,p)=(\infty,0)&\leftarrow\quad b_{4}:(u_{3},v_{3})=\left(Q,\frac{p}{Q}\right)=(0,-a_{2}),\\
b_{5}:(q,p)=(0,\infty)&\leftarrow\quad b_{6}:(U_{5},V_{5})=\left(\frac{q}{P},P\right)=(a_{1},0),\\
b_{7}:(q,p)=(1,\infty)&\leftarrow\quad b_{8}:(U_{8},V_{8})=\left(\frac{q-1}{P},P\right)=(a_{3},0),\end{array}

Figure 1:
Point locations for the KNY realisation of
D_{5}^{(1)}
surfaces
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}

Denote the surface obtained by this sequence of eight blowups by

\begin{gathered}X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}=\operatorname{Bl}_{b_{1}\cdots b_{8}}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}),\\
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathscr{A}=\left\{(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{4}~~|~~a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=1\right\},\quad t\in\mathscr{T}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.\end{gathered}

Here we have introduced the parameter space
\mathscr{A}
consisting of the root variables, see
[
AGS25
, Sec. 3.6]
, as well as the extra parameter
t
which takes values in
\mathscr{T}
. This acts as the independent variable in
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
, and
\mathscr{T}
is the complement in
\mathbb{C}
of the fixed singularities of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
.

The surfaces
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
for
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathscr{A}
,
t\in\mathscr{T}
, form a family of Sakai surfaces of type
D_{5}^{(1)}
parametrised by root variables, which we will write as

\mathcal{X}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}.

We denote the composition of the projection morphisms of the eight blowups by

\pi_{\boldsymbol{a},t}:X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}.

The Picard group of the surface
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
, which we think of as the group of classes of divisors on
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
up to linear equivalence, can be written as

(2.3)

\Pic(X_{\boldsymbol{a},t})=\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}\left\{\mathcal{H}_{q},\mathcal{H}_{p},\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{8}\right\},

where
\mathcal{H}_{q}
and
\mathcal{H}_{p}
are classes of (pullbacks under
\pi_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
of) fibres of the projections from
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
onto the two
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-factors, and
\mathcal{E}_{i}=[E_{i}]
corresponds to the class of the exceptional divisor of the blowup of
b_{i}
, or more precisely its pullback under any subsequent blowups.

For any
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathscr{A}
,
t\in\mathscr{T}
, the surface
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
is a Sakai surface, i.e. a generalised Halphen surface with unique effective anticanonical divisor, see
[
AGS25
, Def. 3.21 & Def. 3.22]
.
Here this divisor is given by
D=-\operatorname{div}\omega_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
, where
\omega_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
is the rational 2-form on
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
given by the pullback under
\pi_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
of the 2-form on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
given in the four charts in
Equation

2.1
by

dq\wedge dp=-\frac{dQ\wedge dp}{Q^{2}}=-\frac{dq\wedge dP}{P^{2}}=\frac{dQ\wedge dP}{Q^{2}P^{2}}
.
In
\Pic(X_{\boldsymbol{a},t})
, the linear equivalence class of
D
gives the anticanonical divisor class

-\mathcal{K}_{X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}}=2\mathcal{H}_{q}+2\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{1}-\dots-\mathcal{E}_{8}
.

Remark 2.4
.

If, instead of
a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=1
, the parameters were subject to
a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=0
, there would be a pencil of biquadratic curves on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
passing through the eight points
b_{1},\dots,b_{8}
and we would get
\dim|-\mathcal{K}_{X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}}|=1
and have a Halphen surface of index 1, i.e. a particular kind of rational elliptic surface, with elliptic fibration coming from the linear system
|-\ell\mathcal{K}_{X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}}|
, with
\ell=1
.

2.2
Root data and Cremona isometries

We now introduce bases of simple roots for the surface and symmetry root lattices coming from this realisation of Sakai surfaces of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
, which we use in the algebraic description of symmetries and discrete Painlevé equations below.
The geometric realisation of the surfaces in the family
\mathcal{X}
is interpreted as carrying the data of the sequence of blowups and their enumeration. This gives an identification of all the
\Pic(X_{\boldsymbol{a},t})
into a single lattice, see
[
AGS25
, Def. 3.74]
, which we denote by

\Pic(\mathcal{X})=\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}\left\{\mathcal{H}_{q},\mathcal{H}_{p},\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{8}\right\},

abusing notation in using the same symbols as for
\Pic(\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{a},t})
in
Equation

2.3
, including for the anticanonical class

-\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{X}}:=2\mathcal{H}_{q}+2\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{1}-\mathcal{E}_{2}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8}\in\Pic(\mathcal{X}),

and the symmetric, bilinear product given by the intersection form, defined by extension of

\mathcal{H}_{q}\bullet\mathcal{H}_{p}=1,\quad\mathcal{H}_{q}\bullet\mathcal{H}_{q}=\mathcal{H}_{p}\bullet\mathcal{H}_{p}=\mathcal{H}_{q}\bullet\mathcal{E}_{i}=\mathcal{H}_{p}\bullet\mathcal{E}_{i}=0,\quad\mathcal{E}_{i}\bullet\mathcal{E}_{j}=-\delta_{ij},\quad\text{ for }~i,j=1,\dots,8.

Remark 2.5
.

Strictly speaking, we are abusing notation here in more than one way, since
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
is not the Picard group of the family
\mathcal{X}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}
, but rather an identification of Picard groups of all of its fibres.

2.2.1
Surface and symmetry root lattices

The surface root basis is formed of elements
\delta_{i}\in\Pic(\mathcal{X})
corresponding to classes of the components
D_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,5
, of the unique effective anticanonical divisor

D=D_{0}+D_{1}+2D_{2}+2D_{3}+D_{4}+D_{5},

for each
\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
in the family.
These are indicated in blue on
Figure

2
.
The elements
\delta_{i}\in\Pic(\mathcal{X})
corresponding to classes of the components
D_{i}
are given in
Figure

3
and play the role of simple roots for the affine root system of type
D_{5}^{(1)}
, with null root corresponding to the anticanonical class:

\delta=\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}+2\delta_{3}+\delta_{4}+\delta_{5}=-\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{X}}.

These span the surface root lattice
Q(\mathcal{R})
,
\mathcal{R}=D_{5}^{(1)}
contained in the orthogonal complement of
\delta
:

(2.4)

Q=Q(D_{5}^{(1)})=\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}\left\{\delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{5}\right\}\subset\delta^{\perp}\subset\Pic(\mathcal{X}).

2.2.3
Translations

The group
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
contains a normal subgroup of translations, which is contained in a smaller extension of
W(A_{3}^{(1)})
by only certain Dynkin diagram automorphisms, which we describe now.
The subgroup of these Dynkin diagram automorphisms in this case is

(2.11)

\Sigma=\langle\rho:=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{4},\qquad\rho=(\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}).

which acts on
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
according to

(2.12)

\rho=(\mathcal{H}_{q}\mathcal{H}_{p})(\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathcal{E}_{5}\mathcal{E}_{3}\mathcal{E}_{7})(\mathcal{E}_{2}\mathcal{E}_{6}\mathcal{E}_{4}\mathcal{E}_{8}).

The extended affine Weyl group of type
A_{3}^{(1)}
is

(2.13)

\widetilde{W}(A_{3}^{(1)}):=W(A_{3}^{(1)})\rtimes\Sigma\cong W(A_{3})\ltimes T_{\accentset{\circ}{P}},

where
W(A_{3})=\langle w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\rangle\subset W(A_{3}^{(1)})
is the finite Weyl group of type
A_{3}
, and
T_{\accentset{\circ}{P}}
is a subgroup of translations corresponding to the weight lattice of the underlying finite root system.
The root lattice of the underlying finite root system is
\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\perp}:=\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{2}\subset Q^{\perp}
, and the weight lattice is

(2.14)

\accentset{\circ}{P}:=\left\{h\in\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Q}~|~h\bullet\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }\alpha\in\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\perp}\right\}=\mathbb{Z}h_{1}+\mathbb{Z}h_{2}+\mathbb{Z}h_{3}\supset\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\perp},

where the fundamental weights
h_{i}
are

h_{1}=\frac{3}{4}\alpha_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{3},\quad h_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{3},\quad h_{3}=\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{2}+\frac{3}{4}\alpha_{3},

so
\alpha_{i}\bullet h_{j}=-\delta_{i,j}
.
The associated translations act on
Q^{\perp}
by

(2.15)

T_{h}(\alpha)=\alpha-(\alpha\bullet h)\delta,\quad\alpha\in Q^{\perp},h\in\accentset{\circ}{P}.

Further,
T_{h}T_{h^{\prime}}=T_{h+h^{\prime}}
for any
h,h^{\prime}\in\accentset{\circ}{P}
, and
T_{\accentset{\circ}{P}}=\langle T_{h_{1}},T_{h_{2}},T_{h_{3}}\rangle
.
On
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
, the actions of the translations associated to fundamental weights are given by the following compositions of simple reflections and
\rho
as in (
2.12
):

(2.16)

T_{h_{1}}=\rho^{3}w_{2}w_{3}w_{0},\quad T_{h_{2}}=\rho^{2}w_{0}w_{3}w_{1}w_{0},\quad T_{h_{3}}=\rho w_{2}w_{1}w_{0}.

Note that the action of
T_{\accentset{\circ}{P}}
on the whole of
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
, not just
Q^{\perp}
, is not given in general by the formula (
2.15
), but rather via the expressions in
Equation

2.16
using the action of
\rho
on
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
given in
Equation

2.12
.

Remark 2.6
.

Conjugacy classes of translations in
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
correspond to orbits of weights modulo
\mathbb{Z}\delta
, and
T_{w(h)}=wT_{h}w^{-1}
for
h\in\accentset{\circ}{P}
,
w\in W(A_{3})
.
In particular, this gives a necessary condition for conjugacy of translations: if two weights
h,h^{\prime}\in\accentset{\circ}{P}
are such that
h\bullet h\neq h^{\prime}\bullet h^{\prime}
, then
T_{h}
and
T_{h^{\prime}}
are not conjugate in
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
.

2.3
Cremona action and discrete Painlevé equations

The action of
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})=W(A_{3}^{(1)})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(A_{3}^{(1)})
on
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
in
Section

2.2
is realised by an action of
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
on the family
\mathcal{X}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}
, called the Cremona action
[
Sak01
]
.
In particular, for each
w\in\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
, there is an action on parameters
\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T},(\boldsymbol{a},t)\mapsto(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}},\tilde{t})
and isomorphisms
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}\to X_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}},\tilde{t}}
, which induces the action of
w
on
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
by pushforward or pullback depending on convention (see
[
AGS25
, Sec. 3.6.3]
for details).
In the following lemma we give this action, in the form of the actions of generators of
W(A_{3}^{(1)})
and
\Aut(A_{3}^{(1)})
on parameters as
\boldsymbol{a}\mapsto\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
, as well as the birational map
\mathbb{C}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2},(q,p)\mapsto(\tilde{q},\tilde{p})
which gives the isomorphism
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}\to X_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}},\tilde{t}}
when extended to
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
and lifted under the blowups.

Lemma 2.7
.

The Cremona action of
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
on the family
\mathcal{X}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}
is given as follows.
The simple reflections
w_{i}\in W(A_{3}^{(1)})
act according to

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle:\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{matrix}-a_{0}&a_{0}+a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{0}+a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}\displaystyle q+\frac{a_{0}}{p+t}\\
p\end{matrix}\right)\!,

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle:\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}+a_{1}&-a_{1}\\
a_{1}+a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
\displaystyle p-\frac{a_{1}}{q}\end{matrix}\right)\!,

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle:\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}+a_{2}\\
-a_{2}&a_{2}+a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}\displaystyle q+\frac{a_{2}}{p}\\
p\end{matrix}\right)\!,

\displaystyle w_{3}

\displaystyle:\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}+a_{3}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}+a_{3}&-a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
\displaystyle p-\frac{a_{3}}{q-1}\end{matrix}\right)\!.

The actions of the generators
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
of
\Aut(A_{3}^{(1)})
are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle:\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{matrix}a_{3}&a_{2}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}\end{matrix}\ ;\ -t\ ;\begin{matrix}\displaystyle-\frac{p}{t}\\
qt\end{matrix}\right)\!,

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle:\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{matrix}a_{2}&a_{1}\\
a_{0}&a_{3}\end{matrix}\ ;\ -t\ ;\begin{matrix}q\\
p+t\end{matrix}\right)\!.

Via the Cremona action, elements of infinite order in
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
, e.g. translations, define discrete Painlevé equations of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
.
There are two reference examples which will be relevant in this paper, which we present next.

2.3.1
The KNY example of a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}

The first reference example is that given in
[
KNY17
, Section 8.1.17]
, which comes from the Cremona action of the translation

(2.17)

T_{\operatorname{KNY}}:=T_{h_{1}-h_{2}+h_{3}}=T_{h_{1}}T_{h_{2}}^{-1}T_{h_{3}}=\rho^{2}w_{1}w_{3}w_{2}w_{0},

whose associated weight
h_{\operatorname{KNY}}:=h_{1}-h_{2}+h_{3}
has squared length
-(h_{\operatorname{KNY}}\bullet h_{\operatorname{KNY}})=1
.
We reproduce the expression of this in coordinates as
(q,p)\mapsto(\bar{q},\bar{p})
, with parameter evolution
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\mapsto(\bar{a}_{0},\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2},\bar{a}_{3})=\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
, as follows:

(2.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{q}&=1-q-\frac{a_{0}}{p+t}-\frac{a_{2}}{p},\\
\underaccent{\bar}{p}&=-p-t+\frac{a_{1}}{q}+\frac{a_{3}}{q-1},\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} \bar{a}_{0}&=a_{0}+1,&&\bar{a}_{1}=a_{1}-1,\\
\bar{a}_{2}&=a_{2}+1,&&\bar{a}_{3}=a_{3}-1.\end{aligned}\right.

Regarding this as a (family of) birational map(s)

\varphi^{\operatorname{KNY}}_{\boldsymbol{a},t}:\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\quad(q,p)\mapsto(\bar{q},\bar{p}),

lifting under the blowups to the surfaces gives the isomorphism(s)

(2.19)

\tilde{\varphi}^{\operatorname{KNY}}_{\boldsymbol{a},t}:X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}\rightarrow X_{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}},t},

which act on
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
via pushforward according to

(2.20)

T_{\operatorname{KNY}}:\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}_{q}&\mapsto 5\mathcal{H}_{q}+2\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{1}-\mathcal{E}_{2}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-2\mathcal{E}_{5}-2\mathcal{E}_{6}-2\mathcal{E}_{7}-2\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\ \mathcal{H}_{p}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{1}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{2}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{3}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{2}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{4}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{1}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{5}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{6}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}-\mathcal{E}_{7},\\
\mathcal{E}_{7}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}-\mathcal{E}_{6},\\
\mathcal{E}_{8}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}-\mathcal{E}_{5},\end{aligned}\right.

the restriction of which to
Q^{\perp}
gives

(2.21)

T_{\operatorname{KNY}}:\left\{\begin{aligned} \alpha_{0}&\mapsto\alpha_{0}-\delta,\\
\alpha_{1}&\mapsto\alpha_{1}+\delta,\\
\alpha_{2}&\mapsto\alpha_{2}-\delta,\\
\alpha_{3}&\mapsto\alpha_{3}+\delta.\end{aligned}\right.

2.3.2
The Sakai example of a discrete Painlevé equation of surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}

The second reference example corresponds to that labeled
\operatorname{d}
-
\text{P}_{\mathrm{IV}}
in
[
Sak01
, Section 7]
, which comes from the Cremona action of the translation

(2.22)

T_{\operatorname{Sak}}:=T_{h_{3}}=\rho w_{2}w_{1}w_{0},

whose associated weight
h_{\operatorname{Sak}}:=h_{3}
has squared length
-(h_{\operatorname{Sak}}\bullet h_{\operatorname{Sak}})=\frac{3}{4}
.
So
T_{\operatorname{Sak}}
and
T_{\operatorname{KNY}}
are not conjugate in
\widehat{W}(A_{3}^{(1)})
, see
Remark

2.6
.

We reproduce the expression of this discrete Painlevé equation, recycling the notation for the action on variables
(q,p)\mapsto(\bar{q},\bar{p})
, and parameter evolution
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\mapsto(\bar{a}_{0},\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2},\bar{a}_{3})=\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
, as follows:

(2.23)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{q}&=\frac{p+t}{t}\left(1-\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}}{a_{0}+(p+t)q}\right),\\
\bar{p}&=\frac{1}{1-\bar{q}}\left(a_{2}+\frac{(a_{0}+a_{1})p}{t+p-t\bar{q}}\right),\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} \bar{a}_{0}&=a_{0}+1,&&\bar{a}_{1}=a_{1},\\

Regarding this as a (family of) birational map(s)

\varphi^{\operatorname{Sak}}_{\boldsymbol{a},t}:\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\quad(q,p)\mapsto(\bar{q},\bar{p}),

this gives the isomorphism(s)

(2.24)

\varphi^{\operatorname{Sak}}_{\boldsymbol{a},t}:X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}\rightarrow X_{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}},t},

which acts on
\Pic(\mathcal{X})
via pushforward of
\varphi^{\operatorname{Sak}}
according to

(2.25)

T_{\operatorname{Sak}}:\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}_{q}&\mapsto 3\mathcal{H}_{q}+2\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-2\mathcal{E}_{7}-2\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\ \mathcal{H}_{p}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{1}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{6}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{2}&\mapsto 2\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{3}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{5}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{3}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\
\mathcal{E}_{4}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}-\mathcal{E}_{7},\\
\mathcal{E}_{5}&\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{q}+\mathcal{H}_{p}-\mathcal{E}_{4}-\mathcal{E}_{7}-\mathcal{E}_{8},\\

\mathcal{E}_{7}&\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{1},\\
\mathcal{E}_{8}&\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{2},\end{aligned}\right.

the restriction of which to

gives

(2.26)

T_{\operatorname{Sak}}:\left\{\begin{aligned} \alpha_{0}&\mapsto\alpha_{0}-\delta,\\
\alpha_{1}&\mapsto\alpha_{1},\\
\alpha_{2}&\mapsto\alpha_{2},\\
\alpha_{3}&\mapsto\alpha_{3}+\delta.\end{aligned}\right.

2.3.3
Hamiltonian form of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}

The family of surfaces
\mathcal{X}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}
also provides the initial value spaces for the fifth Painlevé equation
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
, in the sense of Okamoto
[
Oka79
]
.
For the standard form of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
as a scalar second-order ODE we take the one in
[
Oka87
]
,

(2.27)

\frac{d^{2}w}{dt^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{2w}+\frac{1}{w-1}\right)\left(\frac{dw}{dt}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{t}\frac{dw}{dt}+\frac{(w-1)^{2}}{t^{2}}\left(\alpha w+\frac{\beta}{w}\right)+\frac{\gamma}{t}w+\delta\frac{w(w+1)}{w-1},

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{C}
are parameters, and we assume
\delta\neq 0
since otherwise
Equation

2.27
reduces to the third Painlevé equation
\text{P}_{\mathrm{III}}
.
We consider the form of
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
as a non-autonomous Hamiltonian system as given in
[
KNY17
]
, which is polynomial just as that provided by Okamoto for
\text{P}_{\mathrm{V}}
in
[
Oka80
]
(see also
[
Oka87
,
Mal23
]
- for the relations between various Hamiltonian forms of Painlevé equations on the level of geometry see
[
DFLS24
]
).
This is given by

(2.28)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{dq}{dt}&=\frac{\partial\operatorname{H}}{\partial p}=\frac{1}{t}\Big(q(q-1)(2p+t)-a_{1}(q-1)-a_{3}q\Big),\\
\frac{dp}{dt}&=-\frac{\partial\operatorname{H}}{\partial q}=\frac{1}{t}\Big(p(p+t)(1-2q)+(a_{1}+a_{3})p-a_{2}t\Big),\end{aligned}\right.

where the Hamiltonian is

(2.29)

\operatorname{H}(q,p;t)=\frac{1}{t}\Big(q(q-1)p(p+t)-(a_{1}+a_{3})qp+a_{1}p+a_{2}tq\Big).

The system (
2.28
) reduces to the standard Painlevé equation (
2.27
) for the variable

w(t)=1-\frac{1}{q(t)}
with the parameter values

(2.30)

\alpha=\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{2},\quad\beta=-\frac{a_{3}^{2}}{2},\quad\gamma=a_{0}-a_{2},\quad\delta=-\frac{1}{2}.

For given
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in\mathscr{A}
, the Okamoto space of initial conditions at
t\in\mathscr{T}
for system (
2.28
) is obtained by removing the components of the unique effective anticanonical divisor from
X_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
,
i.e. the surface as in Figure
2
, with blue curves removed.
The Cremona action in
Lemma

2.7
provides Bäcklund transformations for the system (
2.28
).

3
The identification procedure

We now recall the procedure, proposed in
[
DFS20
]
, for identifying discrete Painlevé equations written in non-standard form.
The kind of discrete systems the procedure is applicable to are systems of two first-order difference equations for
(x_{n},y_{n})
, say

(3.1)

x_{n+1}=f_{n}(x_{n},y_{n}),\quad y_{n+1}=g_{n}(x_{n},y_{n}),

such that if we regard
(x_{n},y_{n})
and
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
as coordinates for two copies of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, the discrete system (
3.1
) defines a birational map.
Further, we will be considering non-autonomous discrete systems containing parameters, i.e. the functions
f_{n},g_{n}
in
Equation

3.1
are rational in
x_{n},y_{n}
with coefficients being functions of
n
as well as some complex parameters.

In the case of systems coming from orthogonal polynomials there is often a differential system satisfied by the recurrence coefficients with respect to some parameter in the weight, say
s\in\mathscr{S}
.
Here
\mathscr{S}\subset\mathbb{C}
is the domain of analyticity of this differential system, playing the same role as the independent variable space
\mathscr{T}
of the Painlevé equation.

We consider
Equation

3.1
as defining a family of birational maps

(3.2)

\psi_{\boldsymbol{b},s}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad(x,y)\mapsto(\bar{x},\bar{y}),

where
(x,y)=(x_{n},y_{n})
and
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
.
We take
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathscr{B}
, where
\mathscr{B}
is the space of parameters in the equation (
3.1
), which is a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{r}
for some
r
, along with the parameter evolution
\boldsymbol{b}\mapsto\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}
induced by
n\mapsto n+1
.

Suppose such a system is indeed a discrete Painlevé equation in disguise, related by a birational change of variables to a standard form coming from the Cremona action on a family of Sakai surfaces parametrised by root variables.
If the discrete Painlevé equation in standard form corresponds, in the same way as the examples in
Sections

2.3.1
and
2.3.2
, to a family of birational maps

(3.3)

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a},t}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad(q,p)\mapsto(\bar{q},\bar{p}),

with evolution of root variables
\boldsymbol{a}\mapsto\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}
, then the change of variables is a family
\upsilon_{\boldsymbol{b},s}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
of birational maps and an identification of parameters
\rho:\mathscr{B}\times\mathscr{S}\to\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{T}
that matches
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a},t}
and
\psi_{\boldsymbol{b},s}
.
That is, the following diagrams commute:

(3.4)

3.1
Parameter constraints and obstructions to symmetries

The parameter space
\mathscr{B}
may not exhaust all possible values of root variables, and thus not admit the whole Cremona action.
Such examples occur in several contexts, where Sakai surfaces with constraints on root variables either appear naturally or are engineered.
Below we will describe two such situations which will be relevant in this paper.

3.1.1
Projective reduction conditions

In
[
Sak01
]
, discrete Painlevé equations were said to arise from translation elements of the corresponding extended affine Weyl group.
While elements of infinite order more generally are now widely regarded as also defining discrete Painlevé equations (and in fact some well-studied early examples are associated to non-translation elements
[
DSSW25
]
), one benefit of working with translations is that the evolution of parameters (root variables) under these is such that the discrete dynamical systems can be neatly written as non-autonomous difference equations of additive,
q
-difference, or elliptic difference type.

When considering an element of infinite order that is not a translation (which will necessarily be a ‘quasi-translation’), the evolution of root variables is not purely translational and to write the system down as an equation with
n
-dependent coefficients requires periodic terms.
There is a procedure
[
KNT11
]
called projective reduction, in which parameters are constrained to a subset on which a non-translation element acts translationally, so the corresponding discrete Painlevé equation can be written as a non-autonomous difference equation free from periodic
n
-dependence of coefficients.
We will refer to such parameter constraints as
projective reduction conditions
.
See
[
Shi25
]
for algebraic details of quasi-translations in affine Weyl groups that appear in the context of discrete Painlevé equations.

3.1.2
Parameter constraints from nodal curves

A second type of parameter constraint that arises naturally is that corresponding to surfaces that include nodal curves.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Sakai surface with effective anticanonical divisor
D
.
A
nodal curve
is a smooth rational curve of self-intersection
-2
disjoint from the irreducible components of
D
.
The set of classes of nodal curves is
\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}}\subset\Pic(X)
.

Note that the genus formula immediately implies that if a smooth rational curve of self-intersection
-2
on a Sakai surface is not among the components of the anticanonical divisor then it must be disjoint from them.

We now explain parameter constraints corresponding to nodal curves.
The root variables, which are used as parameters for families of Sakai surfaces as above, are defined in terms of a kind of period map, which is a function
\chi
on the symmetry root lattice
Q^{\perp}
defined with respect to a choice of 2-form which is free of zeroes on
X
, and whose divisor of poles gives
D
(see
[
AGS25
, Sec. 3.6.2]
and
[
Sak01
, Sec. 5]
for details).
For additive surface types, like the case of
\mathcal{R}=D_{5}^{(1)}
we consider in this paper, the period map takes values in
\mathbb{C}
and the root variables are
\chi(\alpha_{i})=a_{i}
.
The existence of nodal curves implies constrained root variables due to the following.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
Sak01
]
, Prop. 22.)
.

Let
X
be a Sakai surface, of surface type
\mathcal{R}
and symmetry type
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
.
Denote by
W^{\operatorname{nod}}\subset W(\mathcal{R}^{\perp})
the subgroup generated by reflections with respect to the elements of
\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}}
.
Then
\alpha\in W^{\operatorname{nod}}\cdot\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}}\iff\chi(\alpha)=0
, where
\chi
is the period map on
X
.

In particular, the surface
X
having a single nodal curve,
\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}}=\{\alpha\}
, is equivalent to
\chi(\alpha)=0
.
Nodal curves present obstructions to symmetries of Sakai surfaces, and the symmetries compatible with the existence of some set of nodal curves will form a subgroup of the generic one for the corresponding surface type.
This can be understood both in terms of the parameter constraints that arise as in
Proposition

3.2
and on the level of Cremona isometries (see
[
Sak01
]
for details).
We quote the part of the relevant result which pertains to the surface type
\mathcal{R}=D_{5}^{(1)}
.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
Sak01
]
, Th. 26.)
.

Let
X
be a Sakai surface, of surface type
\mathcal{R}\neq A_{6}^{(1)}
,
A_{7}^{(1)}
,
A_{7}^{(1)^{\prime}}
,
A_{8}^{(1)}
,
D_{7}^{(1)}
,
D_{8}^{(1)}
, and corresponding symmetry type
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
.
Then the group of Cremona isometries of
X
is the setwise stabiliser

\operatorname{Cr}\left(X\right)\cong\left(W(\mathcal{R}^{\perp})\rtimes\Aut(\mathcal{R}^{\perp})\right)_{\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}}}=\left\{w\in W(\mathcal{R}^{\perp})\rtimes\Aut(\mathcal{R}^{\perp})~|~w(\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}})=\Delta^{\operatorname{nod}}\right\}.

Remark 3.4
.

Note that projective reduction conditions and parameter constraints corresponding to nodal curves are not mutually exclusive.
For example, there is an elliptic discrete Painlevé equation constructed from the Q4 equation in the Adler-Bobenko-Suris list
[
ABS03
]
(also known as Adler’s discrete Krichever-Novikov equation
[
Adl98
]
) by reduction
[
JGTR06
]
and deautonomisation
[
RCG09
]
, whose symmetry group
W(F_{4}^{(1)})
was established in
[
AHJN16
]
.
The associated surface is non-generic, with parameter constraints that correspond to four nodal curves appearing and that also ensure the evolution of root variables of the non-translational symmetry corresponding to the elliptic discrete Painlevé equation in question is translational.
Another example of the combination of nodal curves and projective reduction conditions in a single example is that of the
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{XXXIV}}
equation
[
CJ99
]
(equivalent to a special case of that known as
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{II}}
).
This is associated with a half-translation on a
D_{5}^{(1)}
surface with a nodal curve and a projective reduction condition, see
[
DSSW25
]
.

5
Discussion

The main point of this paper was to demonstrate that, when specifying examples of discrete Painlevé equations that arise in the context of semi-classical orthogonal polynomials,
one should specify not just the surface type
\mathcal{R}
and generic symmetry type
\mathcal{R}^{\perp}
, but also the conjugacy class
[w]
of the element that generates the dynamics and the true symmetry type
\mathcal{S}
.

Not only do we believe that this should be taken into consideration in any attempt to match different weights with the Sakai classification scheme, but it seems that the kinds of non-generic discrete Painlevé equations we have studied, corresponding to surfaces with nodal curves, appear frequently in similar contexts.
For example, the instances of the discrete Painlevé equations labelled by
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{IV}}
and
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{V}}
appearing in
[
BB03
]
are also non-generic, and their parameter constraints correspond to nodal curves.
Further, the instance of
\text{dP}_{\mathrm{IV}}
that appears there corresponds to a single nodal curve on a
D_{5}^{(1)}
surface, which is exactly the same situation that we saw here. Our description of the translation element and symmetry group for the non-generalised Meixner case in
Section

4.4
is applicable to the system derived in
[
BB03
, Th. 9.3 (b)]
.
It would be interesting to determine if there is some fundamental significance of nodal curves in such contexts.

Further, the nodal curves we have been concerned with in this paper are those fixed by the discrete Painlevé dynamics under consideration.
Another way that nodal curves appear is as rational curves on which continuous Painlevé flows become linearisable as Riccati equations.
Discrete Painlevé equations that do not fix such a nodal curve will act as Bäcklund transformations generating hierarchies of hypergeometric-type special solutions, and indeed it is often these solutions that are of interest to express recurrence coefficients of semi-classical orthogonal polynomials.

Finally, we remark that one could pose a further refined version of the Painlevé identification problem, that asks not just for the equation but for a specific solution of interest.
Often a particular solution of a discrete Painlevé equation is the one relevant to the orthogonal polynomials, and to specify such a solution one should also include with the data
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\perp},\mathcal{S},[w])
an initial condition or other characterising behaviour.
In many cases coming from semi-classical orthogonal polynomials, this is a seed solution for a hierarchy of hypergeometric-type special solutions of the relevant Painlevé equation.
