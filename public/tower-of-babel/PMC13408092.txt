Cell polarity control by an unconventional G-protein complex in bacteria
Authors: Céline Dinet, Corinne Sebban-Kreuzer, Deborah Byrne-Kodjabachian, Sébastien Lhospice, Julien Herrou, Fabien Durbesson, Renaud Vincentelli, Vincent Calvez, James Sturgis, Alphée Michelot, Tâm Mignot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Myxococcus xanthus, a predatory soil bacterium, cell polarity and motility are regulated by MglA (a small GTPase) and its regulators, MglB (a potential GAP) and RomRX (a previously proposed GEF), but their precise roles remain unclear. Using fluorescent nucleotides suitable for fluorescence anisotropy measurements, we show that RomRX does not function as a GEF but instead acts as an effector binding MglAGTP. We further find, using an enzymatic model, that MglB exhibits both GAP and GEF properties depending on the local MglAGDP concentration. Additionally, RomRX, specifically RomR, effectively dissociates the MglA-MglB complex by sequestering MglB. This low-affinity interaction permits GAP activity while still partitioning the proteins in vivo, as shown by a biomimetic oil-emulsion droplet assay in a cell-sized system. A minimal mathematical model incorporating these interactions and previous findings recapitulates how dynamic interactions between these three players form an invertible polarity axis in the M. xanthus cell.Subject terms: Enzymes, Bacteria, Kinetics, Molecular modelling

Introduction

Cell polarity, the asymmetric organization of cellular components, is a fundamental characteristic of many living organisms. It is essential for effective cell motility and a myriad of other directional functions, such as epithelial transport, neuronal signaling, and body axis specification in embryos1,2. A polarized cell establishes a distinct axis with a specific front, known as the polarity site, enabling it to maintain functionally distinct domains. This ability relies on intricate interactions between various protein complexes, including small GTPases, polarity proteins, and cytoskeletal components, that regulate multiple signaling pathways. In most eukaryotic cells, small GTPases of the Ras superfamily act as molecular switches in many signaling pathways and play a central role in cell polarization processes. For example, the Rho-family GTPases, including Cdc42 (one of the most highly conserved GTPases) and its relatives (Rac in animals, Rop in plants), are crucial for controlling polarization. Polarization signals act through Cdc42 regulators to accumulate Cdc42-GTP at the future cell front. Cdc42-GTP then organizes the cytoskeleton via various effectors, shaping the polarized morphology of the cell3,4. Understanding the molecular details of polarity is essential for understanding various diseases, as disruptions in polarity mechanisms are often linked to cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and immune system deficiencies5,6.

The discovery that bacterial cells are also polarized dates back to the first electron micrograph showing flagella at the cell poles of bacteria. Since then, efforts to study polarity-based mechanisms on model organisms including B. subtilis, C. crescentus, and E. coli have offered fundamental insights into the mechanisms underlying the spatiotemporal organization of bacterial cells7–13. Similar to eukaryotes, bacterial polarity can be stably maintained over time, dictating the assembly and activity of specific cellular organelles such as flagella, pili and stalks. Alternatively, polarity can be a dynamic process involving the active diffusion and accumulation of polarized protein to precise locations as seen in the front-rear polarity of moving Myxococcus xanthus or Pseudomonas aeruginosa cells14–16.

M. xanthus, a Gram-negative bacterium belonging to the phylum Myxococcota17, glides on solid surfaces along its long axis with well-defined front-rear polarity. Directed cell motility is critical for its complex life cycle, which includes multicellular development and cooperative predation14,18,19. M. xanthus cells move using two genetically distinct motility systems. The gliding (A, adventurous) motility system enables single-cell movement at the colony edges. At the molecular level, A-motility is powered by the Agl-Glt motor, which propels individual cells by adhering to specific points along the cell surface known as bacterial focal adhesions20,21. M. xanthus cells can also move in groups using a twitching-like (S, social) motility that involves type IV pili, which extend and retract to pull groups of cells together, facilitating collective movement across surfaces22. Remarkably, M. xanthus cells control their movement by reversing direction, achieved through the redirection of their motility apparatus to the opposite cell pole14,23. These events termed “reversals” correspond at the cellular level, to a switching of the leading and lagging cell poles.

The establishment and maintenance of polarity reversals in M. xanthus involves a finely tuned interplay between signaling proteins and regulatory complexes, with the small Ras-like GTPase MglA playing a central role. Similar to other well-characterized eukaryotic small Ras-like GTPases, MglA binds to GTP (MglAGTP) and localizes at the leading pole where it interacts with effector proteins to activate each of the motility systems spatially, SgmX for the S-motility system22,24,25, GltJ, AglZ and MreB for the A-motility system26,27. Inactivation of MglA occurs upon GTP hydrolysis because the GDP-bound state (MglAGDP) cannot interact with effector proteins18,28. The importance of this GTP hydrolysis step is underscored by GTP-locked mutants (MglA-Q82A/L) that remain locked in pendular movements provoked by constitutive motility engines29. Thus, in the cell, the MglA GTP/GDP cycle is tightly regulated by MglA interacting partners, notably the MglB protein and the complex formed by the RomR and RomX proteins (RomRX). Together, these four proteins form the core regulatory components of the polarity control network28,30–32. However, the exact design of the regulatory interactions by which these proteins establish the polarity axis remains a topic of debate and is the subject of this article.

The current model suggests that the functions of MglB and RomRX are spatially separated, with MglB acting at the lagging cell pole and the RomRX complex at the leading cell pole14,33,34. This is believed to generate a polarity axis via the following mechanism:

(i) At the lagging cell pole, MglB, a so-called roadblock domain protein is recruited by another roadblock domain protein MglC35. MglB interacts with MglA and activates GTP hydrolysis activity allosterically (see below, MglB is hence defined as a GTPase-Activating Protein (GAP) for MglA). In vitro, MglB is sufficient to activate hydrolysis, but in the cell this process is further assisted by a cognate activator, RomY36. In vivo, the action of MglB precludes accumulation of MglA at the lagging pole.

(ii) Conversely at the leading pole, RomRX is proposed to exert a dual function: first, promoting the insertion of GTP over GDP in MglA and thus acting as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF), and second, acting as a primary determinant for the polar targeting of MglAGTP33.

This model is attractive and inspired by other polarity regulations, for example in yeast3. There are however a number of reported inconsistencies that question its validity:

- First, the mechanism of MglA polar localization is not well understood. While it is clear that an interaction between RomRX and MglAGTP is required for localization at the leading cell pole, these proteins only colocalize during a short window of time after a reversal. Once the cell starts moving in the new direction, the RomRX complex relocalizes gradually at the lagging cell pole but MglAGTP remains bound to the leading pole14,33,37. This clearly suggests that other factors maintain MglA at the leading cell pole once its localization is established. There is also the conundrum that antagonistic GAP and GEF systems co-localize at the lagging pole once polarity is established, which could only be explained by additional layers of regulations.

- Second, the GEF activity of RomRX remains questionable because conventional GEFs generally operate by first forming a complex with the GDP-bound GTPase, leading to nucleotide dissociation, and then allowing GTP-binding to displace the GEF38,39. In the RomRX system, RomR binds to RomX, which in turn interacts with MglAGTP. This ternary complex has a higher affinity for GTP, as it cannot form in the presence of MglAGDP33. However, GEFs generally bind to both the GTP and GDP-bound states of their substrates with equal affinity, favoring the detachment of nucleotides irrespective of their specificity, the directionality of the exchange being dependent on the relative concentrations of the nucleotides38–40. Therefore, the strict interaction between RomX and MglAGTP is inconsistent with a GEF activity and requires a re-examination of the function of RomX and RomR in vitro.

- Third, although MglB activates GTP hydrolysis in vivo, as shown by expression of the MglAQ82L mutant, a GTPase-defective variant locked in the GTP-bound states that phenocopies an mglB mutant28, its function as a canonical GAP remains questionable. First, MglB lacks the active site residues (glutamine and arginine) that are typically found in GAPs which indicates that MglB does not engage directly in hydrolysis but orients/and or stabilizes the catalytic machinery18. In vitro, MglB binds to MglA in a 2:1 stoichiometry, a distinct configuration for GTPase/GAP complexes. This binding positions a critical arginine of MglA (Arg53) into the GTP binding site, remodeling it into a catalytic site29,41. While this explains how MglB promotes GTP hydrolysis, there are also a number of unexpected additional regulations that arise from the asymmetry of the MglB dimer: i) the binding of additional regulators on one of the MglB monomers, i.e., the GltJ ZnR domain26, and perhaps RomY, which activates the GAP activity36. Adding complexity, the asymmetric binding of MglA on the MglB protomers confers a dual GAP/GEF activity to MglB: one protomer (MglB2) remodels the catalytic site, primarily acting as a GAP, while the other protomer (MglB1) facilitates nucleotide exchange through an allosteric mechanism29,42. Here, the C-terminal helix of MglB1 prevents dissociation from the GDP-bound conformation of MglA and favors GTP insertion allosterically, promoting nucleotide exchange29,42. This interaction is very specific to an interaction with MglA, because MglB can also act as a GAP for SofG, another small GTPase in M. xanthus, but in this case it dissociates from SofGGTP43. In vivo, the MglB Ct-helix is critical for the unipolar localization of MglB because in its absence, MglA, RomR and MglB all co-localize at both cell poles29. The molecular mechanism for this localization pattern is not clear but it shows that regulating the interaction between MglA and MglB and modulating the dual MglB function is essential. Critically, the Ct-helix prevents the MglA-MglB complex from dissociating, which is the main basis of the current polarity model. The available in vivo data must therefore be explained by in-depth quantitative analyses of the interactions between MglA and its regulators MglB and RomRX.

In this work, we develop a fluorescence anisotropy assay to study these interactions. By combining these tools with an enzymatic assay that measures GTP hydrolysis, we establish an enzymatic model of the MglB-dependent MglAGTP hydrolysis pathway. The model reveals the need for an additional partitioning system, which we identify as the RomRX complex. A mathematical model integrating this and previous data demonstrates how these interactions could establish cell polarity.

Results

Analysis of MglA nucleotide-binding specificities by fluorescence anisotropy

Previous work analyzed MglA nucleotide exchange in vitro using non-hydrolysable mant-GTP analogs (m-GMPPNP or m-GppNHp) or radioactive nucleotides with low sensitivity28,29,42,44. The affinity of GTP for MglA could not be measured because no detectable signal was produced. Similarly, measurements of GDP affinity were unreliable due to weak and unstable signals in nucleotide exchange assays. Nucleotide binding to MglA was detected in the presence of MglA-interacting partners; however, the binding was unstable, as the fluorescence curve showed a decrease at steady state, hindering accurate measurement of nucleotide exchange dynamics29,42,44. We hypothesized that more sensitive fluorescent GTP/GDP derivatives, structurally compatible with MglA, could be used as alternative tools to study the dynamics of MglA nucleotide exchange both in the absence and presence of regulatory proteins. Fluorescent nucleotide analogs conjugated to bright dyes are available in various chemistries, with fluorophores covalently attached to the base, ribose, or phosphate group of the nucleotide45. Structural analysis of MglA bound to GTP suggested that modification of the GTP ribose would not interfere with its interaction with MglA. Based on this insight, we selected the 2’/3’-O-(2-Aminoethyl-carbamoyl)-Guanosine-5’-tri/diphosphate GTP/GDP analogs, labeled with ATTO-488 which we referred to as GTP488 or GDP488 throughout this study. In these molecules, the ATTO-488 dye is attached to the nucleotide via a long and flexible linker, allowing various orientations relative to MglA and positioning the dye away from the nucleotide-binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b).

The low molecular weight of these fluorescent nucleotides (MW < 1.5 kDa) compared to MglA (MW ≈ 22 kDa) and the long fluorescence lifetime of ATTO-488 (τfl = 4.1 ns) allow real time monitoring of their binding to MglA using fluorescence anisotropy46. Fluorescence anisotropy measurements are not sensitive to photobleaching, allowing signals to be recorded over long periods of time. Purified MglA is found to have a GDP in its active site pocket29. Addition of GTP488 or GDP488 to MglA (MglAGDP) leads to an increase of fluorescence anisotropy signals to plateau values corresponding to steady-state binding of the fluorescent analogues, given that MglA does not hydrolyze GTP on its own (or very slowly, see below and refs. 28,29,42) (Fig. 1a). At initial time points, the rate limiting step of the reaction is the dissociation of GDP from MglA, which is similar in both conditions (koffGDP). The higher plateau value in the presence of GDP488 indicates a higher affinity of GDP488 compared to GTP488. Because MglA does not exist in a nucleotide-free state, it is only possible to determine the relative affinities of GTP488 or GDP488 for MglA. To this aim, we first incubated MglAGDP with GTP488 or GDP488, allowing the reaction to reach equilibrium. We then added an excess of the corresponding unlabeled nucleotide (100 µM) and monitored the decrease in fluorescence anisotropy over time (Fig. 1b, c). The corresponding decay reaction is a measure of the dissociation rate (koff) of the fluorescent nucleotide. Fitting the data to exponential binding equations (see methods) allowed us to measure koffGTP488 = 2.8 × 10−3 ± 5.6 × 10−4 s−1 and koffGDP488 = 6.0 × 10−4 ± 8.7 × 10−5 s−1, confirming the higher affinity of GDP488 compared to GTP488 for MglA. These measurements validate our methodology and highlight its sensitivity, as we could record binding at GTP488 concentrations as low as 10 nM (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In contrast, nucleotide exchange using mant-GTP could not be recorded at 100 nM29,42,44.

RomRX does not function as a Guanine nucleotide Exchange Factor (GEF) in vitro

Next, we investigated the effect of RomRX in this experimental system. The addition of RomX led to higher anisotropy values at steady-state in the presence of GTP488, but not in the presence of GDP488. This result indicates an increase in the molecular weight of MglAGTP488, specifically due to its binding to RomX. In contrast, RomR did not interact directly with either nucleotide-bound form of MglA (Fig. 2a, b). When RomR and RomX were added together with MglAGTP488, a ternary complex formed, as evidenced by an additional increase in the fluorescence anisotropy signal (Fig. 2a). The binding mode of the RomRX complex is thus clearly different from that expected for a GEF and more similar to the behavior of an MglA effector. To directly test this hypothesis, we compared RomX binding to that of a known MglA effector, the C-terminal Tetratricopeptide Repeat triad of the SgmX Type-IV pilus activator (SgmX-Ct)22. Similar to RomX, SgmX-Ct binds only to MglAGTP488 and not to MglAGDP488 (Fig. 2 c, d). Quantitative analysis revealed that MglAGTP488 binds RomX with a dissociation constant (KD) of 1.7 ± 0.3 µM, which decreased slightly to KD = 1.0 ± 0.4 µM upon addition of RomR (Fig. 2e, f). In comparison, MglAGTP488 binds SgmX-Ct with a slightly lower dissociation constant KD = 0.6 ± 0.3 µM, but within the same order of magnitude (Fig. 2g). Thus, MglAGTP488 binds RomX and SgmX-Ct through similar modes, further supporting the notion that RomRX functions as an MglA effector rather than a GEF.

Small G-protein effectors often stabilize the GTP-bound state of their cognate G-protein, which might explain why RomRX was previously proposed to facilitate nucleotide exchange33. If this hypothesis was correct, a similar effect would be expected with SgmX-Ct. To test this possibility, we conducted chase experiments by preloading MglA with either GDP488 or GTP488, followed by the addition of excess unlabeled GTP to monitor the rates of nucleotide release. We found no effect of RomX, RomR or the RomRX complex on the rate of GDP488 to GTP exchange (Fig. 2h). However, we observed that RomX binding stabilizes the binding of GTP488 to MglA (Fig. 2i). In the presence of RomX, we measured a koffGTP488 = 8.4 × 10−4 ± 3.3 × 10−4 s−1, compared to 2.8 × 10−3 ± 5.6 × 10−4 s−1 in its absence (Fig. 2i and Table 1). This stabilization effect is even more pronounced in the presence of RomRX (koffGTP488 = 2.4 × 10−4 ± 5.5 × 10−5 s−1). Most importantly, we observed that SgmX-Ct also stabilized MglAGTP488 and to similar levels (koffGTP488 = 2.7 × 10−4 ± 2.4 × 10−5 s−1) (Supplementary Fig. 2b and Table 1). Thus, we conclude that RomX and the RomRX complex are bona fide MglA effectors like SgmX-Ct and not an MglA GEF as originally proposed.

MglB is an atypical MglA GTPase-activating protein

We next used the anisotropy assay to explore the interaction between MglA and MglB. The addition of GTP488 to a mixture of MglAGDP and MglB resulted in higher anisotropy levels compared to the absence of MglB, demonstrating that an MglAGTP488-MglB complex is formed (Fig. 3a and supplementary Fig. 2c and d). However, after reaching a maximal value, the anisotropy signal gradually decreased with slow kinetics. This decrease likely corresponds to the hydrolysis of the GTP488 nucleotide bound to MglA into GDP488, driven by the GAP activity of MglB. This is supported by the fact that the decrease was not observed when an MglA mutant (MglAQ82L), which is resistant to hydrolysis28, was co-incubated with MglB (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). The persistently high anisotropy signal also suggests that MglAGDP488 remains attached to MglB. To prove this, we incubated MglAGDP with MglB and added GDP488. A sharp increase in the anisotropy signal was observed, confirming that MglB interacts with MglAGDP488 (Fig. 3a and supplementary Fig. 2c). Over time, the signals observed with MglAGTP488 and MglAGDP488 converged, further validating that GTP hydrolysis was induced by MglB (Fig. 3a inset). Consistent with the findings of Baranwal et al.29, our results show that MglB behaves as a non-typical GAP. It activates GTP hydrolysis but does not dissociate from MglAGDP. This atypical behavior was further confirmed using an MglB variant lacking the Ct-helix (MglBΔCt), which bound MglAGTP488 but not MglAGDP488 (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g).

MglB has also been proposed to facilitate GTP exchange upon binding to MglA29,42. To further test this hypothesis, we measured the GTP/GDP koff by performing chase experiments with unlabeled nucleotides in the presence of MglB or MglBΔCt (Fig. 3b, c). We found that MglA koff for GTP is decreased in the presence of MglB ( ~ 3 folds, Table 1) and becomes similar to the koff for GDP, in contrast to the GTP and GDP koff values measured for the MglA free form (Table 1). To further measure the binding affinities (KD), we used isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) using GTPγS (a non-hydrolysable form of GTP) and GDP in the presence of MglB. We indeed observed the formation of an MglAGTPγS-MglB complex by ITC. However, despite repeating this experiment several times, we consistently obtained a molar ratio close to 1 rather than the expected value of 2 dictated by the 2:1 stoichiometry of the MglA-MglB complex. One possible explanation is that a fraction of the MglA protein precipitates in the presence of GTPγS, as we have observed in other instances27. Consequently, this experiment does not allow determination of an accurate dissociation constant, but only an upper bound KD = 310 ± 180 nM, which is nonetheless lower than the KD measured for the MglAGDP/MglB interaction (KD = 751 ± 111 nM, molar ratio ~ 2), confirming that MglB favors formation of the GTP-bound form under equilibrium conditions (Fig. 3d, e). Furthermore, the Ct-helix of MglB stabilizes the MglAGTP-MglB complex, favoring GTP binding (koffGTP488 = 5.8 × 10−4 ± 9.4 × 10−5 s−1) (Fig. 3b and Table 1). This stabilization is further evidenced in the interaction between MglBΔCt and MglAGTPγS which showed a lower KD of 150 ± 100 nM (Fig. 3f). In this case, the observed molar ratio was close to 2, and thus an accurate KD can be determined. Although the reason why the molar ratio is affected only when MglA is mixed with full-length MglB in the presence of GTPγS remains unclear, the ITC results converge with the anisotropy measurements in demonstrating that interaction with MglB stabilizes GTP binding to MglA.

An enzymatic model describing MglA-MglB interaction

The results presented above highlight the function of RomRX as an MglA effector and confirm that MglB does not dissociate from MglAGDP, which deeply challenges the prevailing model. Formally, MglB cannot be classified as a GAP39,41 despite activating hydrolysis, as it remains bound to MglAGDP with high affinity and can also facilitate GTP insertion.

To better understand how these antagonistic activities set the MglAGTP hydrolysis cycle, we aimed to build an enzymatic model. To achieve this, we performed additional kinetic experiments to determine the GTPase activity of MglA. We monitored the release of inorganic phosphate (Pi) using an established enzyme-coupled assay47 at various concentrations of GTP and MglB (Supplementary Fig. 3). To further investigate the function of the MglB Ct-helix, we also performed a similar kinetic study using MglBΔCt as an activator (Supplementary Fig. 4a–e).

To obtain an initial description of the kinetics, we fitted the GTP hydrolysis rates to a Michaelis–Menten equation. As shown in Fig. 4a, b, this empirical fit captured the dependence of hydrolysis velocity on GTP concentration across different MglB conditions and yielded apparent kinetic parameters (Table 2). However, because GDP remains stably bound to MglA and all components are present at comparable micromolar concentrations, the assumptions of classical Michaelis-Menten or Briggs-Haldane kinetics are not satisfied. Thus, while useful as a first approximation, this analysis cannot be considered mechanistic and prompted us to develop a more comprehensive kinetic model (see Supplementary Methods-Enzymology Model).

Kinetic parameters determined by non-linear fitting to the Michaelis Menten equation of the kinetic data on GTP hydrolysis rate

To better model the data, and reduce the number of independent parameters, we built a model in which separate exchange (GDP/GTP exchange was described using two rate constants and a branching ratio, to account for a slow equilibration process) and hydrolysis rates are assumed for the MglB associated and MglB free forms of MglA (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Methods-Enzymology Model for the differential equations). This approach leads to a relatively complex model containing 20 parameters. Because such a model cannot be resolved analytically, we searched for a numerical solution. The kinetic model was thus integrated numerically using estimated parameters from initial experimental conditions to predict initial rates of phosphate release. The parameters were then adjusted to minimize the discrepancy between the predicted and observed rates of phosphate release, quantified by the Chi2 statistic.

As detailed in the Supplementary Methods-Enzymology Model, the kinetic model is poorly constrained, as many parameters have similar effects on the predicted hydrolysis rates. To explore the range of possible solutions, we generated multiple fits from independent starting points; the fit shown in Fig. 4d, e corresponds to the best solution (chi2 = 2.14), with deviations greater than 1 SD observed for the control, in the absence of MglB at low GTP concentration and for two experimental points measured at high concentration of both MglBΔCt and GTP. Despite this variability, all acceptable solutions converged on a set of recurrent features that help us better understand the system. These features are illustrated in Fig. 4f where the distributions of parameters are plotted as boxplots on a logarithmic scale to account for widely differing ranges.

First, it is not surprising that many of the parameters describing GDP/GTP exchange are not well constrained, as our experiments did not explore the conditions under which they are important for the observed hydrolysis rate.

Nevertheless, estimation of the parameters suggest that MglB is able to act both as GAP and a GEF:

(i) The GDP dissociation rate from free MglA is low and needs to be close to the observed value for a good fit, near 2.10−3 s−1. In contrast, the GDP dissociation rates from the MglA-MglB complexes (MglBWT and MglBΔCt) must be higher. This is particularly evident for MglBΔCt, where the rate is constrained and close to 10−2 per second.

(ii) The branching ratio (BR) for nucleotide reassociation to MglA consistently favors GDP (~ 8-fold) over GTP. Association with MglB or MglBΔCt decreases this BR, consistent with a shift toward GTP rebinding.

(iii) GTP dissociation rates are similar in the MglA, MglB and MglBΔCt systems but they are not robustly predicted.

Analyzing MglA-MglB binding, we first observe that the association rate constants are unsurprisingly all fast (around 25 s−1) relative to the rate constants tested by the experiments and consequently poorly constrained and all similar. More interestingly the dissociation constants show some clear trends, and allow us to understand the observed effects of MglB on hydrolysis rates. The affinity of MglB for the GTP form of MglA is relatively high, near 0.8 µM for the wild type protein and 0.05 µM for the C-terminal deletion mutant. The affinities of MglB forms for the GDP form of MglA are lower, near 8 µM and 80 µM for the wild-type and C-terminal deletion mutant respectively. This last value is rather tightly constrained by the model. These values are consistent with the dissociation constants determined by ITC. These effects allow us to understand the effects of MglB on the observed hydrolysis rate, where the affinity for the GTP form pulls the equilibrium and causes the rapid rise in hydrolysis at lower MglB concentrations, due to GAP activity, while the lower affinity for the GDP form of MglA results in a slower rise at high MglB concentration due to the GEF activity (Fig. 4d, e). This is particularly clear for the enzymatic results with the MglBΔCt mutant where the two characteristic concentrations are more clearly separated, and better probed by the experimental conditions.

Finally, the hydrolysis rate is affected by MglB binding, showing a consistent and reproducible rise in the predicted hydrolysis rate constant with MglB binding (in either form), thus indicating a GAP activity of MglB.

Taken together these results show that MglB has both GAP and GEF activity with rather different affinities associated with these activities (particularly in the case of the C-terminal deletion mutant). GAP activity is associated with a sub-micromolar dissociation constant while GEF activity requires higher MglB concentrations. Further, the observed hydrolysis rates are dependent both on the GDP dissociation rate and the hydrolysis rate. Finally, the most important differences between the wild-type and ΔCt mutant of MglB are the increased affinity for MglAGTP and the reduced affinity for MglAGDP, consistent with Baranwal et al.29 and with our fluorescence anisotropy results.

RomR acts on MglB to inhibit the formation of the MglA-MglB complex

In vivo, MglB is required to exclude MglA from the lagging pole, a function previously assumed to be linked to its GTPase activity. However, the observation that MglA, whether bound to GTP or GDP, forms a strong complex with MglB raises the question of the mechanism of dissociation. Additional regulators could be involved and release MglAGDP or MglAGTP depending on the GAP or GEF activity of MglB. To explore this, we first tested whether effectors such as SgmX and RomX could compete with formation of the MglA-MglB complex (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 5). We observed that SgmX-Ct and RomX only induced a modest decrease in anisotropy at high concentrations (up to 30 µM), but the effect was minor suggesting that effector competition may not be sufficient to dissociate MglA-MglB effectively.

Given that RomR localizes to the lagging pole and has been reported to interact directly with MglB (although this interaction is debated and has recently been challenged by the same group32,35), we tested whether the addition of RomR affects the MglAGTP-MglB complex. The addition of 30 µM RomR led to an abrupt decrease in the fluorescence anisotropy signal (Fig. 5b), indicating that RomR triggers the dissociation of the complex. Similarly, RomR also disrupted the MglAGDP-MglB complex when added at the same concentration (Fig. 5c). Since RomR does not interact with MglA, it likely competes with MglA for binding to MglB, thereby dissociating the complex and reducing the availability of MglB for interaction with MglA. This suggests a regulatory role for RomR in modulating the MglA-MglB interaction, which may contribute to the exclusion of MglA from the lagging pole.

There is conflicting data regarding a potential direct interaction between RomR and MglB, as some pull-down experiments detect this interaction while others do not32,35. We revisited this potential direct interaction using ITC and found a low affinity interaction with a KD of 31 ± 15 µM (Fig. 5d). Previous studies33 have shown that RomR does not affect the GTPase activity of MglA when added at equal concentration to MglB. This could be explained by the weak affinity between RomR and MglB. When we conducted GTPase assays in the presence of varying concentrations of MglB and a high concentration of RomR (20 µM), we observed a moderate but consistent 1.4-fold reduction in the reaction rate (Fig. 5e). This weak effect reveals that RomR is mostly permissive for GAP activity when present at concentrations that are near the KD (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 4f).

In vitro reconstitution of MglA exclusion by the RomR-MglB complex in a cell-like configuration

The results above suggest that RomR modulates the interaction between MglA and MglB, thereby controlling the turnover rate of the MglA-MglB complex in vivo. However, due to the low affinity between MglB and RomR, this effect is only expected if there is sufficient accumulation of RomR to compete with the formation of the high affinity MglA-MglB complex. Such accumulation is possible in vivo, as most of the RomR pool relocalizes with RomX to the lagging pole (Fig. 6a, b).

To test this possibility in conditions that mimic the cellular context closely, we formed individual spherical droplets with a diameter of 10 to 20 µm, that reproduce the concave shape of the bacterial poles, although the radii of curvature are larger and constant around the droplets (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 6a). The lipids stabilizing the droplet interface were selected to be similar in composition to the inner membrane of M. xanthus48. We fluorescently labeled MglA, MglB, and RomR to track their localization. MglA was labeled directly (Supplementary Fig. 6b) or via fluorescently labeled nucleotides bound to it, which served as proxy for MglA localization (Fig. 7b, c and Supplementary Fig. 6c). As shown by anisotropy measurements, these fluorescent nucleotides remain stably bound to MglA for at least 20 min, supporting the validity of this approach for the duration of these experiments. In the droplets, MglA (in both its GTP- or GDP- bound states) and RomR displayed diffuse localization at all tested concentrations (Fig. 7b–d and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c and 7a). In contrast, the distribution of MglB at the lipid/water interface was found to be concentration dependent. At lower concentrations (up to 2 µM), MglB formed large domains or clusters likely due to self-association or cooperative interactions (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 7b), whereas at higher concentrations (10 µM), it exhibited a more uniform distribution across the interface possibly due to saturation effects (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Control experiments in the absence of lipids showed no detectable MglB binding (Supplementary Fig. 8b), consistent with previous work indicating weak binding of MglB to liposomes49. The characteristic localization of MglB in the droplets provided a good opportunity to characterize protein-protein interactions in this cell-like configuration using two-color imaging.

When red-labeled MglB was mixed with green-labeled MglAGTP488 or MglAGDP488, the localization of MglB at the lipid-water interface remained unchanged. However, MglA was recruited to the interface alongside MglB both at low and high concentrations (Fig. 7f, g and Supplementary Fig. 8c). This observation demonstrates that MglB and MglA interact in the droplet assay and that this interaction does not interfere with MglB lipid-binding. Similarly, mixing red-labeled MglB (10 µM) with RomRGFP (25 µM) resulted in the recruitment of RomR-GFP to the droplet interface, indicating that the MglB-RomR interaction is detectable within this system (Fig. 7h). Importantly, when red-labeled MglB was mixed with unlabeled RomR and MglAGTP488, diffuse localization of MglA was obtained (Fig. 7i). Modeling M. xanthus polarity

We next asked whether these defined interactions, together with the large existing body of literature, could support to a consistent polarity model. We developed a mathematical model combining the previously published models by Guzzo et al.37 and Carreira et al.15, adapted to include our results. This model incorporates the following interactions:

i) At the leading pole, the RomRX complex functions as an MglA effector, recruiting MglAGTP. The interaction between MglA and RomRX is transient and RomRX relocalizes to the lagging pole within minutes after the reversal. However, this interaction is critical because the polar localization of MglA is strongly affected in absence of RomR and RomX33. Thus, we assume that the polar targeting of MglA is a two-step process where RomR allows MglAGTP to associate with its polar effectors (SgmX the activator of Type-IV pili and GltJ the activator of the Agl-Glt complex22,24–27) (Fig. 8a). This interaction to the polar effectors is strengthened as MglA adopts a third conformation insensitive to the action of MglB, further reinforcing polar localization48. Last, the high concentration of MglAGTP prevents the formation of the MglB-MglC complex and thus excludes MglB from the leading cell pole35.

We opted for a minimal set of equations to keep the mathematical model tractable (Fig. 8b). Accordingly, the model was restricted to four protein concentrations, despite the large number of molecular entities and reaction complexes involved.

This minimal model was derived based on two principles of simplification:

First: it is assumed that RomR, MglB, and MglAGTP preferentially reside at the cell poles rather than in the bulk, which is supported by live fluorescence images of each protein localization15,33,35,36. As such, RomR and MglB were assumed to be localized only at the poles, with respective amounts R−1 and R1, and R1= Rtot - R−1, and B−1 and B1, and B1= Btot - B−1. The MglA components were split into three compartments: at the two poles (under the form MglAGTP), and in the bulk (under the form MglAGDP). The main features of the model are based on the experimental results presented in this study and from previously published works:

(i) RomR attaches to the poles at a constant rate kR and is additionally recruited by MglB at rate kRB. It detaches at a constant rate dR and can also be released following its interaction with MglAGTP at the pole, at rate dRA.

(ii) MglB binds spontaneously to the poles at a constant rate kB. Cooperative MglB attachment was also assumed, with a rate kBB, based on our observation of MglB micro-domains in lipids droplets (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 7b and 8a) and consistent with Guzzo et al.37, MglB detaches from the poles at a constant rate dB with a saturation threshold Bsat following Guzzo et al.37.

(iii) MglAGTP is recruited at the pole by RomR, with constant KAR. It detaches at constant rate dA, and also through its interaction with MglB combined with RomR, during which it is transformed into MglAGDP, at rate dABR.

(iv) We assume that MglAGDP is converted into MglAGTP in the bulk (via some unknown mechanism). The latter becomes available for recruitment at the poles.

Second: the dynamics of some components such as MglC, RomX and RomY were not explicitly described:

- Since MglC mediates the recruitment of MglB via RomR at the pole35. We introduced a dependency on RomR for polar localization but did not describe the MglC-RomRX complex, which would only induce some delay in the process.

- Since RomX is always seen to interact with RomR (Fig. 6a, b), the effect of RomX is implicitly contained in RomR concentrations.

- The effect of RomY is included through activation of the GAP activity of the MglB-RomRX complex.

Numerical integration of the model leads to a polarized steady state in which RomR, MglB and MglAGTP segregate asymmetrically between the two poles (Fig. 8c). We checked that polarity does not require any pre-existing asymmetry between the two cell poles, as it emerges from an instability near the symmetrical configuration (Supplementary Fig. 9). Polarity emerges spontaneously because the interactions between the proteins reinforce small fluctuations through nonlinear feedback loops. Critically, this arises from the competition between the two poles for shared molecular pools and from the dual role of RomR in recruiting MglAGTP while promoting the dissociation of the MglA-MglB complex at the lagging pole. Therefore, polarity emerges as an intrinsic property of the interaction network rather than a consequence of finely tuned parameters.

We next tested if the model captures mutant configurations that are consistent with published localization patterns. For this we removed MglA, RomR or MglB and tested the consequence on the remaining protein localization. In the absence of MglA, RomR and MglB become bipolar asymmetric (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In contrast, deletion of RomR leads to diffuse localization of MglA in the bulk and a bipolar asymmetric distribution of MglB (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Finally, deletion of MglB results in bipolar accumulation of RomR as well as accumulation of MglAGTP at both poles in a symmetrical configuration (Supplementary Fig. 10c). These results are consistent with all localization patterns experimentally reported by Carreira et al.15.

Together, these simulations indicate that the experimentally identified biochemical properties of the RomRX-MglA-MglB system are sufficient to generate and maintain front-rear polarity in M. xanthus. The model supports a revised polarity framework in which RomRX acts as partitioning factor rather than a GEF, coordinating MglA localization through a combination of effector binding, MglB sequestration and pole-to-pole competition.

Discussion

The polarity axis formed by MglA and MglB has long been considered to be formed via an exclusion mechanism linked to the activation of GTP hydrolysis at the lagging cell pole. This mechanism was proposed based on strong evidence: (i) in vitro studies on MglA and MglB homologs purified from T. thermophilus (MglBtt) showed that MglBtt only binds to MglAGTPtt and (ii), in vivo, an MglAQ82A/L that cannot hydrolyze GTP, even in the presence of MglB, localizes symmetrically in M. xanthus cells27. It was surprising that when Baranwal et al.29 elucidated the structure of the M. xanthus MglA-MglB complex to find that MglB remains associated to MglAGDP after hydrolysis, which is due to the presence of a Ct-helix, a motif that is not present in MglBtt. These findings raised profound questions about how cell polarity is established via MglA and MglB and prompted this study.

To this aim, we re-examined how MglA interacts with MglB and how this affects the MglA hydrolysis cycle. Our findings demonstrate that the MglA system is not controlled by a canonical GAP/GEF system as originally proposed, but rather involves a composite system formed by MglB and the RomRX complex. The function of RomRX is dual: (i) At the leading cell pole, RomRX is a polar effector that recruits MglAGTP and transmits it to downstream motility effectors. (ii) Following dissociation, it relocalizes to the lagging cell pole, where it promotes the dissociation of the MglB-MglA complex and thus preventing accumulation of MglAGTP. Remarkably, the low affinity interaction between RomR and MglB ensures that GAP activity still occurs even at high RomR local concentrations.

Consequently, the common view that the Mgl system is a prokaryotic version of a classical small G-protein system, regulated by spatially localized GAP (MglB) and GEF (RomRX), is obsolete. Considering all available data, a view emerges that distinct signaling hubs, one formed by MglA (which is insensitive to the action of MglB48), the other formed by MglB, segregate at opposite cell poles.

At the lagging pole, the activity of MglB is dictated by its affinity for MglAGTP (sub-micromolar) and MglAGDP (micro-molar). These affinities bias the system toward either GAP or GEF activities depending on the local concentrations of MglB and MglAGTP/GDP, as well as the presence of RomR, which acts as a dissociation factor. There is evidence that other protein regulators also bias MglB activity. Direct regulation is suggested by the notable asymmetry of the complex formed between MglA and the MglB dimer, the Ct-helix of the proximal MglB1 protomer being required to maintain contact with MglA in its GDP- and GTP-bound states42, while MglB2 providing an important additional binding interface for regulators36. In fact, such binding has been proposed to explain the direct stimulation of MglB GAP activity by the GltJ ZnR domain, leading to dissociation of the Agl-Glt complex near the lagging cell pole26. At the lagging pole, a similar activation might occur via RomY, ensuring that GAP activity dominates between reversals. However, RomY could also act indirectly, for example by titrating MglB and orienting its activity by lowering its free concentration. So how is polarity switched to provoke reversals? It is known that the Frz chemosensory-like system provokes this switch via two response regulators FrzX and FrzZ34,49. When they become phosphorylated by the FrzE kinase, FrzX~P and FrzZ~P respectively localize to the lagging and leading poles. We speculate that Frz signaling biases the activity of MglB-RomRX from GAP to GEF activity to recruit MglA to the lagging cell pole. This would provoke a domino effect, destabilizing the MglC complex as previously proposed35 and thus leading to its reassembly at the opposite pole together with MglB. FrzX~P is essential to provoke the switch and it localizes at the lagging pole in an MglB-dependent manner37, making it an ideal candidate to provoke the activity change. At the opposite pole, FrzZ~P could destabilize the MglAGTP effector complex and thus facilitate its release and relocalization at the lagging pole. This mechanism remains speculative as direct interactions between the Frz regulators and the MglA-MglB system still need to be demonstrated. Nevertheless, this work uncovers highlights interfaces for modulating the MglA-MglB complex, suggesting multiple potential layers of regulation.

Lastly, given that MglA-MglB systems are broadly distributed, the conclusions of this study extend beyond Myxococcus and possibly predict the functioning of other systems. Structural homologs of MglA and MglB have recently been identified in Archaea, including members of the Asgard superphylum50. Notably, analysis of two archaeal pairs from Candidatus Thorarchaeota and Candidatus Nitrosomicus reveals distinct scenarios concerning potential GAP/GEF functions based on the presence of a potential C-terminal helices (Ct-helix). In both systems, Alphafold3 models predict that a dimer formed by the MglB homologs interacts with their respective GTPase, similar to the interaction formed between MglA and MglB (Supplementary Fig. 11). Interestingly, binding of the Ct-helix formed by one of the RB to the cognate G-protein is predicted for C. Nitrosomicus but not for C. Thorarchaeota (Supplementary Fig. 11). Based on this study, it would be especially interesting to test whether these MglA-MglB pairs have only deep evolutionary roots or also exhibit the same enzymatic properties demonstrated by this study.

In eukaryotic cells, the bi-functionality of Roadblock domain-containing proteins and their structurally related counterparts, the Longins, is also apparent. These proteins also frequently form dimeric platforms that directly interact with small GTPases51–53. They are typically incorporated into complex molecular scaffolds, where they primarily function as guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) but occasionally act as GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) through allosteric regulation or with the help of additional regulatory components41,52,54,55. A prominent example is the Ragulator complex in the mTOR pathway, where multiple Roadblock domain-containing proteins form a scaffold for Rag GTPases, creating a multi-layered regulatory hub critical for mTOR signaling.

In conclusion, given the diversity of its interactions, the MglB system may also be regarded as a modular signaling unit, akin to the Ragulator complex. Generally, these observations suggest that studying the MglA-MglB system could uncover universal principles underlying these widespread regulatory modules.

Fractions containing the protein were pooled, concentrated, dialyzed overnight in Buffer D (Buffer A without imidazole) and stored at −80 °C in Buffer D. Protein purity was analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentrations were quantified using NanoDrop™ (Thermo Scientific™). The following extinction coefficients, calculated based on the amino acid sequence of the constructed proteins using ProtParam from ExPASy, were used: 16,515 M−1·cm−1 for MglA-His6 and MglAQ82L-His6, 2,980 M−1·cm−1 for MglB-His6 and MglBΔCt-His6, 11,920 M−1·cm−1 for SgmX-Cter-His6, and 8,480 M−1·cm−1 for RomX-His6.

Expression and purification of RomR-His6 and RomRGFP-His6

Synthetic coding sequences of RomR and RomRGFP were obtained from Twist Bioscience, cloned in a modified pET28 vector for expression of C-terminal hexa-histidine tagged proteins. Constructs were transformed into the LEMO21(DE3) E. coli strain. Cells were grown overnight in LB medium at 37 °C and transferred into NZY auto-induction LB medium (NZYtech) for 24 h at 18°C for overexpression. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and pellets were resuspended in Buffer E (50 mM tris-HCl, pH = 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM imidazole, pH = 8.0) supplemented with cOmplete™ EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and 20 µg/ml DnaseI. Cells were lysed with a French press at 1kbar and lysates were centrifuged at ≈ 36,200 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were loaded on a 2 ml HisTrap beads column (GE Healthcare) functionalized with Nickel and equilibrated with a Buffer E. After 10 min of incubation, the resin was washed with 5-column volume of Buffer E and 5-column volume of Buffer F (Buffer A containing 75 mM Imidazole). The protein was then eluted with Buffer G (Buffer A containing 200 mM Imidazole). Fractions containing the protein were pooled, concentrated, dialyzed overnight in Buffer H (Buffer A without imidazole) for ITC and MST experiments or dialyzed in Buffer D for all other experiments and stored at −80 °C. Protein purity was analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentrations were quantified using NanoDrop™ (Thermo Scientific™). The following extinction coefficients were used: 6990 mol−1·cm−1 for RomR-His6 and; 26025 mol−1·cm−1 for RomRGFP-His6.

Labeling of MglA and MglB

Purified MglAGDP and MglB were labeled overnight at 4 °C under gentle agitation in the presence of a 5-fold excess of Alexa Fluor™ 488 C5-maleimide (ThermoFisher scientific) or Janelia Fluor® 646 Maleimide (TOCRIS) in Buffer E supplemented with 2 mM TCEP. Unbound fluorophore was removed by passing the reaction mixture through a pre-equilibrated protein desalting column (Zeba Spin Desalting columns from ThermoFisher scientific). Labeled proteins were analyzed on SDS-PAGE gel and imaged using ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad) to quantify the fraction of labeling. Labeled proteins were stored at −80 °C.

Fluorescence Anisotropy experiments

Fluorescent nucleotides analogs

Fluorescent nucleotides used in this study were purchased from Jena Bioscience (https://www.jenabioscience.com/), specifically EDA-GTP-ATTO-488 (ref. NU-820-488) and EDA-GDP-ATTO-488 (ref. NU-840-488). When needed, fluorescent nucleotide analogs were diluted in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.546.

Fluorescence anisotropy

The binding of fluorescent nucleotides analogs to proteins was monitored by measuring changes in anisotropy using excitation and emission wavelengths of 504 nm and 521 nm, respectively. These experiments were performed using a Safas Xenius XC spectrofluorometer (Safas Monaco) controlled by SP2000 software, version 7.8.13.046.

Nucleotides exchange assays

For kinetic experiments, MglAGDP, either alone or in combination with MglB, MglBΔCt, RomR, RomX, RomRX or SgmX-Ct, was mixed in a volume of 150 µl in Buffer D (supplemented with 2 mM TCEP) at the indicated concentrations. First MglAGDP was mixed with the protein of interest, and nucleotide exchange was initiated by adding 0.1 µM of the fluorescent nucleotide analog. The increase in fluorescence anisotropy was recorded over time until equilibrium was reached. The main reaction controlling the rate in this condition is the dissociation of GDP from MglA (koffGDP). Protein competition experiment

MglAGDP (2 µM) was incubated with MglB (4 µM). The reaction was initiated by adding GTP488 or GDP488 (0.1 µM) and fluorescence anisotropy was monitored until steady state. Subsequently, RomR, RomX, RomRX or SgmX-Ct was added at the indicated concentrations and measurements were continuously taken.

GTPase assay

Rates of GTP hydrolysis were measured using the EnzChek Phosphate Assay Kit (E-6646, Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the method originally described by Webb47. Briefly, in the presence of phosphate Pi, the substrate 2-amino-6-mercapto-7-methylpurine riboside (MESG) is converted enzymatically by purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) to ribose 1-phosphate and 2-amino-6-mercapto-7-methylpurine. The assay was performed using a constant concentration of MglAGDP (2 μM) and varying concentration of MglB, MglBΔCt or RomR-RomX complex in a total reaction volume of 200 μl, and a range of GTP concentration as indicated. A master mix was prepared in buffer D containing 1x reaction buffer, 2 mM TCEP, 0.2 mM MESG and PNP (1 U/ml). The proteins were then added, and the reaction was started by the addition of MglA. Phosphate release was recorded by the change in absorbance at 360 nm using 96-well flat-bottom plates (Greiner Bio-One) and a SPARK multimode microplate reader (Tecan) every 30 seconds for at least 30 min. Experiments were repeated a minimum of three times.

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was employed to determine the thermodynamic parameters and binding constants of the binding interactions between a) MglAGTPγS or GDP and MglB; b) MglAGTPγS and MglBΔCt and c) RomR and MglB.

Case a-b: Experiments were conducted using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. Each titration consisted of 19 injections, starting with an initial injection of 0.4 μl followed by 18 injections of 2 μl each. Injections were carried out over 4 seconds with 150-second intervals between injections. The experiments were carried out in Buffer E Supplemented with 5 mM dithiothréitol (DTT). Purified MglAGDP “macromolecule” was placed in the sample cell, while the ligand MglB or MglBΔCt was loaded into the syringe. Both the cell and the syringe contained 100 μM of GTPγS or GDP. Several control experiments were performed to account for heat changes including: titration of MglB (with GTPγS) into buffer containing GTPγS, titration of buffer containing GTPγS into the same buffer, and injection of GTPγS-containing buffer into MglA (with GTPγS). The ITC data for each experiment was modeled using a one-site binding model provided in the MicroCal PEAQ- ITC analysis software with fitted offset control.

Case c: Experiments were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (Malvern, UK) at 12.5 °C. Each titration consisted of 13 injections, with the first injection of 0.4 μl and the subsequent 12 injections of 3 μl each. Injections lasted for 4 seconds with 150-second intervals between each injection. The experiments were conducted in Buffer H supplemented with 5 mM DTT. RomR was placed in the sample cell, while MglB was loaded into the syringe. A second series of MglB injections with the same parameters was performed, and the resulting ITC data files were concatenated for analysis as a single data file. Control experiments involved titrating the ITC buffer into RomR alone to account for heat changes due to dilution. This fitted offset (a constant control heat) was subtracted from the experimental data to accurately calculate the dissociation constant. Data analysis was performed using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis software.

Fluorescence microscopy

For each experiment, 1 ml of a CYE-grown culture with an OD of 0.5–1 was centrifuged 5 min at ≈ 1000 × g and resuspended to an OD of 2 in TPM CaCl2 buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 8 mM MgSO4, 1 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM CaCl2). Then 2 µl of the cell suspension was placed on a glass slide, covered with a 1.5% agar pad, and incubated for 20 min at 32 °C before imaging. Time-lapse experiments were performed using an automated inverted epifluorescence Ti2E microscope with Perfect Focus (Nikon), a 100x oil immersion objective (NA 1.4), and a back-illuminated Kinetix Scientific CMOS (sCMOS) camera (Teledyne). Image acquisition was performed using NIS-Elements software (Nikon). All fluorescence images were acquired with minimal exposure time to reduce photobleaching and phototoxicity. For time-lapse movies, images were captured every 2 sec over a 15-minute period. Image processing and analysis were performed using Image J (Fiji). Processing was limited to linear adjustments of brightness and contrast applied uniformly across entire images. No nonlinear adjustments, filtering, or thresholding were applied.

Water-in-oil lipid stabilized emulsion

The lipids used for this experiment included L-α-phosphatidylethanolamine (E. coli PE), L-α-phosphatidylglycerol (Egg, Chicken) (sodium salt) (EGG PG), L-α-phosphatidylserine (Brain, Porcine) (sodium salt) (Brain PS), 1’,3’-bis[1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho]-glycerol (sodium salt) (cardiolipin 18:1) and L-α-lysophosphatidylcholine (Egg, Chicken) (EGG LysoPC), 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (ammonium salt)(PE*). All lipids were purchased from Merck.

A lipid solution was prepared in a glass vial by mixing 76 mol% E. coli PE (or 70 mol% E. coli PE + 6 mol% PE* for fluorescent lipids), 4.9 mol% EGG PG, 9.3 mol% cardiolipin 18:1, 6.5 mol% PS and 3 mol% Egg Lyso PC in chloroform. The mixture was dried overnight in a vacuum desiccator and then rehydrated in Buffer E to a final concentration of 2 mg/ml ( ≈ 0.83 mM). The resulting suspension was sonicated using a tip sonicator with refrigeration, followed by centrifugation at ≈ 112 × g for 10 min to remove titanium particles. The suspensions were stored at 4 °C under nitrogen and used within a week.

To prepare water-in-oil emulsions, the lipid suspension was diluted to a final concentration of 3 µM with the proteins of interest in Buffer E. The oil phase was prepared by dissolving 1 mg/ml cithrol (CRODA) surfactant in Squalene oil (Merck). To form the emulsion, the water phase was added to the oil phase at a typical volume ratio of 1:20 in a microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was gently mixed by pipetting up and down, avoiding vigorous agitation to maintain emulsion stability. Brief vortex mixing at low speed was used for additional mixing. The successful formation of the droplets was indicated by the uniform cloudiness of the sample. The emulsion was placed in a glass microscope chamber, with droplets positioned between a microscope coverslip and glass slide spaced with double-sided adhesive tape (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Droplets and fluorescent proteins were imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 microscope equipped with a 100x/1.4NA Oil Ph3 Plan-Apochromat objective and a Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash 4.0LT camera. Images were acquired with Zen 2.3 blue edition. Image processing and analysis were performed using Image J (Fiji). Processing was limited to linear adjustments of brightness and contrast applied uniformly across entire images. No nonlinear adjustments, filtering, or thresholding were applied.
