Real-Rootedness of the Poincaré polynomials of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} : an AI-assisted proof
Gergely Bérczi and Young-Hoon Kiem

Abstract
. We prove the real-rootedness for the Poincaré polynomial P n ​ ( t ) = ∑ i = 0 n − 3 dim H 2 ​ i ​ ( ℳ ¯ 0 , n , ℚ ) ​ t i P_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}\dim H^{2i}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n};\mathbb{Q})t^{i} of the Deligne–Mumford moduli space ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} of stable n n -pointed rational curves, proving a conjecture of Aluffi–Chen–Marcolli. The proof starts from the Keel–Manin–Getzler recurrence for these polynomials, but its main new idea is the introduction of a bivariate deformation F m ​ ( y , t ) F_{m}(y,t) of the Poincaré polynomial. This deformation is the heart of the argument: it reveals a hidden interlacing structure which is not visible from the one-variable recurrence alone. For fixed t < 0 t<0 , the zero set of F m F_{m} in the y y -direction is governed by a Sturm–Rolle argument on the interval 0 < y < 1 − t 0<y<1-t . The original polynomial P n ​ ( t ) P_{n}(t) is recovered by the slice y = 1 y=1 , and the ordered crossings of the moving roots through this slice give both real-rootedness and strict interlacing. As a consequence, the Betti numbers of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} form an ultra-log-concave sequence. We further show the real-rootedness and ultra-log-concavity for the Poincaré polynomial of the Fulton-MacPherson space ℙ 1 ​ [ n ] \mathbb{P}^{1}[n] of n n ordered points in (degenerations of) the complex projective line ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} . The proof of the real-rootedness theorem for the Poincaré polynomials of ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} was obtained through an iterative AI-assisted workflow with Co-Mathematician, an agentic frontier-model system developed by Google DeepMind [ ZvGZ+26 ] . The human role was to pose the problem, evaluate the mathematical plausibility of successive attempts, identify and request repairs of gaps, compare the evolving argument with the literature, and assemble the final human-verifiable proof. Our additional human contribution was to observe that a similar, residual deformation strategy also applies to the Fulton–MacPherson spaces ℙ 1 ​ [ n ] \mathbb{P}^{1}[n] , which yields the corresponding real-rootedness theorem.



1.
Introduction

The Deligne–Mumford moduli spaces are central geometric objects of
modern algebraic geometry. Deligne and Mumford introduced stable curves and their
moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
in their 1969 paper
[
DM69
]
. The pointed compactifications

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
, including the genus-zero spaces considered in the present paper, were constructed
in the Grothendieck–Knudsen theory of stable pointed curves

[
Knu83a
,
Knu83b
]
. The space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is a smooth projective variety of
dimension
n-3
compactifying configurations of
n
ordered points on the complex projective line

\mathbb{P}^{1}
modulo projective equivalence.
The importance of these moduli spaces comes from their rich geometry which has led to several complementary discoveries.
First, the boundary, forgetful, and clutching morphisms relating the spaces

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
organize them into the basic geometric framework for stable
curve theory. In particular, the so-called clutching morphisms encode the fundamental
operation of gluing pointed curves along marked points, an operation which gives
the collection of moduli spaces its operadic, and in higher genus modular
operadic, structure
[
GK98
]
. In genus zero, Keel
computed the Chow ring and intersection theory of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
, proving in
particular the recursive structure of its Chow groups and Betti numbers

[
Kee92
]
. Kapranov then related
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
to explicit birational and
combinatorial models, including iterated blow-ups, Veronese curves and Chow
quotients of Grassmannians
[
Kap93
]
. In a different
direction, Witten’s conjectural description of intersection numbers on moduli
spaces of curves through two-dimensional gravity, and Kontsevich’s proof via the
matrix Airy function, made
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
a central bridge between algebraic
geometry, integrable systems and mathematical physics

[
Wit91
,
Kon92
]
. Kontsevich–Manin’s formulation of
Gromov–Witten classes and quantum cohomology then placed stable curves and
stable maps at the foundation of modern enumerative geometry

[
KM94
]
. More recently, the cohomology of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+1}
has
been understood as a Chow ring attached to the braid matroid and the minimal
building set, connecting these moduli spaces to matroid Chow rings and
combinatorial Hodge theory
[
EFM+25
,
AHK18
,
FMSV24
]
.

In this paper we study Betti numbers of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
, encoded by the Poincaré polynomial (in half-degree
convention)

(1)

P_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}b_{n,i}t^{i},\qquad b_{n,i}=\dim H^{2i}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n};\mathbb{Q}).

The main result is a fundamental new structural property of the
topology of these moduli spaces: for every
n\geq 3
, the Poincaré polynomial

P_{n}(t)
has only real roots.
The proof was found with the assistance of Co-Mathematician, a frontier agentic LLM-based
system for mathematical research introduced in
[
ZvGZ+26
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
n\geq 3
, the polynomial
P_{n}(t)
has only real roots. More
precisely, for
n\geq 4
it has
n-3
simple roots, all lying in

(-\infty,0)
.

We also obtain strict interlacing.

Theorem 1.2

(Strict interlacing)
.

Let
n\geq 4
, and let

\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{n-3}<0

be the roots of
P_{n}(t)
. If

\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}<\cdots<\sigma_{n-2}<0

are the roots of
P_{n+1}(t)
, then

\sigma_{1}<\tau_{1}<\sigma_{2}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\sigma_{n-3}<\tau_{n-3}<\sigma_{n-2}<0.

Note that odd cohomology vanishes, and
P_{n}
is palindromic by Poincaré duality. Hard
Lefschetz gives unimodality of the coefficients. Since the coefficients are
nonnegative, the roots are necessarily negative; Newton’s inequalities then give
log-concavity, indeed ultra-log-concavity, of the Betti number sequence.

Corollary 1.3

(Log-concavity of the Betti numbers)
.

For every
n\geq 3
, the Betti numbers of
\overline{M}_{0,n}
form an ultra-log-concave sequence:

\left(\frac{b_{n,i}}{\binom{n-3}{i}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{b_{n,i-1}}{\binom{n-3}{i-1}}\frac{b_{n,i+1}}{\binom{n-3}{i+1}}\qquad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq n-4.

A closely related space to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is the Fulton–MacPherson space
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
which compactifies the configuration space of
n
ordered distinct points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}

without
projective equivalence. It is a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
with vanishing odd cohomology and its even Betti sequence is unimodal and palindromic. By the same technique as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we further prove the real-rootedness (Theorem
5.1
) and the ultra-log-concavity (Corollary
5.2
) for the Poincaré polynomial of
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
for
n\geq 1
.

We next recall the precise origin of the problem. Aluffi–Chen–Marcolli
formulated the real-rootedness conjecture for the polynomial representing the
Grothendieck class of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
; in the Poincaré specialization this is exactly
the assertion that
P_{n}(t)
has only real zeros
[
ACM25
, Conjecture 1]
.
They proved asymptotic ultra-log-concavity and
\gamma
-positivity as evidence
for the conjecture.
Nascimento improved the asymptotic ultra-log-concavity bounds

[
Nas25
]
. Aluffi–Marcolli–Nascimento gave explicit formulas for
the Grothendieck class and Betti numbers in terms of Stirling and Bernoulli
numbers, and verified related log-concavity phenomena in large finite ranges

[
AMN25
]
. Choi–Kiem–Lee first gave closed formulas for the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-characters on
cohomology, then developed recursive algorithms for these representations and
proposed strong equivariant log-concavity refinements of the Betti number
log-concavity problem; their later work introduced characteristic polynomials of
representations and further asymptotic log-concavity results

[
CKL23
,
CKL24
,
CKL25
]
. Choi–Kiem later proved
that the distribution of the Betti numbers of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is asymptotically Gaussian and
obtained a stronger asymptotic ultra-log-concavity
[
CK26
]
.

The recursion used in this paper is the standard recursion for the Betti numbers
of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
:

(2)

P_{1}(t)=P_{2}(t)=P_{3}(t)=1,

and, for
n>3
,

(3)

P_{n}(t)=(1+t)P_{n-1}(t)+t\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{j-1}P_{j}(t)P_{n+1-j}(t).

This recurrence follows from Keel’s
recursive construction and Chow ring computation
[
Kee92
]
; it is
equivalent to generating function identities appearing in the work of Manin and
Getzler
[
Man95
,
Get95
]
.
We use (
3
) as our starting point.

The proof is not a direct manipulation of the coefficients of
P_{n}
. Its
central idea is to lift the one-variable problem to a two-variable problem. We
introduce polynomials
F_{m}(y,t)
satisfying

(4)

F_{1}(y,t)=1,\qquad F_{m+1}(y,t)=(my-m+1)F_{m}(y,t)+y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}F_{m}(y,t).

The deformation is designed so that the slice
y=1
recovers the original
Poincaré polynomial:

F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t).

The derivative in the new direction recovers the convolution term in the
recurrence:

\partial_{y}F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t)+S_{m+2}(t),

where

S_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{j-1}P_{j}(t)P_{n+1-j}(t).

Thus the auxiliary variable
y
is not decorative: it turns the nonlinear
convolution in (
3
) into the slope of a moving zero curve.

Geometrically, for fixed
t<0
the positive roots of
F_{m}(y,t)
lie in the
interval
0<y<1-t
and are simple. As
t
moves from
0^{-}
to
-\infty
, these
roots move in ordered, noncolliding branches. Near
t=0^{-}
all positive
branches lie below the horizontal line
y=1
, while for
t\ll 0
they all lie
above it. Hence each branch crosses
y=1
. The degree of
P_{m+1}
leaves room
for exactly one crossing per branch, and those crossings are precisely the roots
of
P_{m+1}
. The ordering of the branches then gives the sign alternation
needed for interlacing. No monotonicity of the branches is assumed; the degree
count is what rules out multiple crossings.

The deformation
F_{m}(y,t)
is not a routine technical device:
it is the key structural ingredient of the proof, converting the original
recursion into a one-parameter family in which the move of real zeros can be
controlled. Its discovery is a clear indication that the
AI-assisted process reached beyond local symbolic manipulation and identified
a genuinely new idea behind the theorem.

2.
The recurrence and the Co-Math deformation

Recall that the polynomials
P_{n}(t)
can be recursively computed by (
2
) and (
3
).
For example, we have

P_{4}(t)=1+t,\ \ P_{5}(t)=1+5t+t^{2},\ \ \ P_{6}(t)=1+16t+16t^{2}+t^{3}.

By (
1
), it is obvious that
P_{n}(t)
is monic of degree
n-3
and satisfies
P_{n}(0)=1
.
Put

(5)

S_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{j-1}P_{j}(t)P_{n+1-j}(t)

for
n\geq 4
, with the convention that an empty sum is zero.

The exponential generating function form of (
3
) will be used
to identify the deformation. Define

(6)

U(x,t)=\sum_{n\geq 3}P_{n}(t)\frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+(1+t)\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+(1+5t+t^{2})\frac{x^{4}}{4!}+\cdots.

Lemma 2.1
.

The recurrence (
3
) is equivalent to

(7)

\partial_{x}U-x=(1+t)U+tU\partial_{x}U.

Moreover, (
7
) has a unique formal power-series solution in

x^{2}\mathbb{Q}[t][[x]]
.

Proof.

The coefficient of
x^{n-2}/(n-2)!
in
\partial_{x}U
is
P_{n}(t)
, and the
coefficient of the same monomial in
U
is
P_{n-1}(t)
. The coefficient
of
x^{n-2}/(n-2)!
in
U\partial_{x}U
is

\sum_{j=3}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{j-1}P_{j}(t)P_{n+1-j}(t)=S_{n}(t).

The term
-x
removes the initial
n=3
coefficient. Thus the coefficient
identity for
n\geq 4
is exactly (
3
). Conversely, the same
coefficient extraction recovers the recurrence. Since the coefficient of

x^{n-1}
in (
7
) is solved recursively from lower
coefficients, the formal solution is unique.
∎

Now we define the bivariate deformation.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
F_{1}(y,t)=1
, and for
m\geq 1
set

(8)

F_{m+1}(y,t)=(my-m+1)F_{m}(y,t)+y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}F_{m}(y,t).

See §
6
for geometric intuition behind this definition.

For example, we have

F_{2}=y,\qquad F_{3}=3y^{2}+(t-2)y,\qquad F_{4}=15y^{3}+10(t-2)y^{2}+(t-2)(t-3)y.

Let

(9)

\Phi(x,y,t)=\sum_{m\geq 1}F_{m}(y,t)\frac{x^{m}}{m!}=x+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}y+\frac{x^{3}}{3!}\left(3y^{2}+(t-2)y\right)+\cdots.

Coefficient extraction from (
8
) gives the following linear PDE.

Lemma 2.3
.

The series
\Phi
uniquely satisfies

(10)

\bigl(1-(y-1)x\bigr)\partial_{x}\Phi-y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}\Phi=1+\Phi,\qquad\Phi(0,y,t)=0.

Proof.

Since

\partial_{x}\Phi=1+\sum_{m\geq 1}F_{m+1}(y,t)\frac{x^{m}}{m!},

substituting (
8
) gives

\partial_{x}\Phi=1+(y-1)x\partial_{x}\Phi+\Phi+y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}\Phi,

which is (
10
). Conversely, (
10
) implies (
8
) and thus follows the uniqueness.
∎

The next proposition is the algebraic bridge between the deformation and
the original recurrence.

Proposition 2.4

(Slice and slope identities)
.

For all
m\geq 1
,

(11)

F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t).

For all
m\geq 2
,

(12)

\partial_{y}F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t)+S_{m+2}(t).

Proof.

Let
\varphi(x,t)=\Phi(x,1,t)
. We first identify
\varphi
. For fixed
t\neq 0,1
, we solve
(
10
) by characteristics. The characteristic equations are

(13)

\frac{dx}{ds}=1-(y-1)x,\qquad\frac{dy}{ds}=-y(y+t-1),\qquad\frac{dt}{ds}=0.

Start with the initial condition
x(0)=0
. By (
9
), we have
\Phi(0)=0
and

\frac{d\Phi}{ds}=\frac{dx}{ds}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}+\frac{dy}{ds}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y}=1+\Phi

by (
10
). Thus letting
u=e^{s}
,

\Phi=e^{s}-1=u-1.

Let the integral curve
(x(s),y(s))
reach
y=1
at
u=z
so that

\varphi=\Phi|_{y=1}=z-1.

By Calculus, we can solve the differential equation for
y
with
y|_{u=z}=1
to find

\frac{y}{y+t-1}=\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{z}{u}\right)^{t-1}.

Substituting this expression into the linear equation

\frac{dx}{du}+\frac{y-1}{u}x=\frac{1}{u},\qquad x|_{u=1}=0,

and integrating from
u=1
to
u=z
after multiplying

\exp\left(\int\frac{y-1}{u}du\right)=\frac{z}{u}\left(t-\left(\frac{z}{u}\right)^{t-1}\right)

gives the local parametrization near
z=1
as

(14)

\varphi=z-1,\qquad x=\frac{t^{2}(z-1)-z^{t}+1}{t(t-1)}.

Here
z^{t}
is expanded as
\exp(t\log z)
near
z=1
. The computation is an
identity in
\mathbb{Q}(t)[[z-1]]
; after coefficient comparison, the resulting
identities extend to all
t
because both sides are polynomial in
t
.

Set
V=\varphi-x=z-1-x
. From (
14
),

\frac{dx}{dz}=\frac{t-z^{t-1}}{t-1},\qquad\frac{dV}{dx}=\frac{z^{t-1}-1}{t-z^{t-1}}.

A direct substitution shows that
V
satisfies

\frac{dV}{dx}-x=(1+t)V+tV\frac{dV}{dx},\qquad V\in x^{2}\mathbb{Q}[t][[x]].

By Lemma
2.1
,
V=U
. Therefore

(15)

\varphi(x,t)=x+U(x,t).

Comparing coefficients in (
15
) gives (
11
). The
identity was proved for generic
t
, hence holds as a polynomial identity
in
t
.

It remains to identify the
y
-slope. Evaluate (
10
) at

y=1
:

\partial_{x}\varphi=1+\varphi+t\partial_{y}\Phi(x,1,t).

Using
\varphi=x+U
and (
7
), we obtain

t\partial_{y}\Phi(x,1,t)=\partial_{x}U-x-U=t\bigl(U+U\partial_{x}U\bigr).

Cancelling
t
in the integral domain
\mathbb{Q}[t][[x]]
gives, as a formal
power-series identity,

(16)

\partial_{y}\Phi(x,1,t)=U+U\partial_{x}U.

The coefficient of
x^{m}/m!
in
U
is
P_{m+1}(t)
for
m\geq 2
, and the
coefficient of
x^{m}/m!
in
U\partial_{x}U
is
S_{m+2}(t)
. This proves
(
12
).
∎

By (
14
) and (
15
), we find that

\varphi(x,t)=x+\sum_{m\geq 2}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}P_{m+1}(t),\quad x=\frac{t^{2}\varphi-(1+\varphi)^{t}+1}{t(t-1)}

which is precisely Getzler’s formula in
[
Get95
, p228]
.

3.
A Sturm theorem for the deformation

We now study
F_{m}(y,t)
as a polynomial in
y
for fixed
t<0
. Put

a=1-t>1.

Then
y(y+t-1)=y(y-a)=-y(a-y)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
m\geq 1
,
F_{m}(y,t)
has degree
m-1
in
y
and leading coefficient

(2m-3)!!
for
m\geq 2
; for
m=1
the leading coefficient is
1
. For

m\geq 2
and
t<0
,
y=0
is a simple root of
F_{m}(y,t)
.

Proof.

The degree and leading coefficient follow immediately from the recurrence:
if the leading term of
F_{m}
is
c_{m}y^{m-1}
, then the leading term of

F_{m+1}
is

\bigl(m+(m-1)\bigr)c_{m}y^{m}=(2m-1)c_{m}y^{m}.

Thus
c_{m}=(2m-3)!!
for
m\geq 2
.

For the root at zero,
F_{2}(y,t)=y
. If
F_{m}(y,t)=d_{m}(t)y+O(y^{2})
, then
(
8
) gives

F_{m+1}(y,t)=d_{m}(t)(t-m)y+O(y^{2}).

Since
t<0
, the factor
t-m
is nonzero. Hence the root at
0
is simple
for every
m\geq 2
.
∎

Proposition 3.2

(Fixed-
t
root structure)
.

Fix
t<0
and
m\geq 2
. The polynomial
F_{m}(y,t)
has the simple root

y=0
and exactly
m-2
further simple roots in the interval
(0,1-t)
.
Consequently all roots of
F_{m}(y,t)
are real and lie in
[0,1-t)
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
m
. The case
m=2
is
F_{2}=y
. Assume the
claim for
F_{m}
. Let
a=1-t
and define the positive weight

(17)

w_{m}(y)=y^{(m-1)/a}(a-y)^{(1-mt)/a},\qquad 0<y<a.

A direct logarithmic differentiation gives

\frac{w_{m}^{\prime}(y)}{w_{m}(y)}=-\frac{my-m+1}{y(a-y)}.

Using
F_{m+1}=(my-m+1)F_{m}-y(a-y)F_{m}^{\prime}
, we obtain

(18)

\frac{d}{dy}\bigl(w_{m}(y)F_{m}(y,t)\bigr)=-\frac{w_{m}(y)}{y(a-y)}F_{m+1}(y,t).

By the induction hypothesis,
F_{m}
has roots

0<r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{m-2}<a

in the open interval, in addition to the root at zero. The function

w_{m}F_{m}
vanishes at
0
, at each
r_{i}
, and at
a
; the endpoint
vanishing follows from the positive exponents in (
17
). Rolle’s
theorem applied on the intervals

(0,r_{1}),(r_{1},r_{2}),\ldots,(r_{m-2},a)

gives at least
m-1
distinct zeros of
F_{m+1}
in
(0,a)
. By Lemma

3.1
,
F_{m+1}
has degree
m
and also has the simple root

0
. Therefore these are all its roots, and all are simple.
∎

For later use we need the behavior of the positive roots as
t
varies.
By Proposition
3.2
, the positive roots are simple for every

t<0
, hence the implicit function theorem gives local real-analytic root
branches. Since the roots are globally ordered and never collide, these local
branches glue uniquely to continuous, indeed real-analytic, functions

(19)

0<r_{1}^{(m)}(t)<r_{2}^{(m)}(t)<\cdots<r_{m-2}^{(m)}(t)<1-t,\qquad t<0.

No monotonicity of the branches is used anywhere below; the proof only uses
continuity, endpoint position, and the degree count.

Lemma 3.3

(Behavior near
t=0
)
.

For fixed
m\geq 2
, all positive roots
r_{i}^{(m)}(t)
lie below
1
for
all sufficiently small negative
t
.

Proof.

Suppose not. Then there are
t_{k}\to 0^{-}
and positive roots

r_{i_{k}}^{(m)}(t_{k})
with
r_{i_{k}}^{(m)}(t_{k})\geq 1
. Since the roots lie in

(0,1-t_{k})
, a subsequence converges to a limit
r\in[1,1]
, hence to

r=1
. Passing to the limit in
F_{m}(r_{i_{k}}^{(m)}(t_{k}),t_{k})=0
gives

F_{m}(1,0)=0
. But by Proposition
2.4
,

F_{m}(1,0)=P_{m+1}(0)=1
, a contradiction.
∎

To analyze the other end, scale
y
by
t
. Define

(20)

H_{m,t}(x)=t^{-(m-1)}F_{m}(tx,t).

Substituting the recurrence for
F_{m}
gives

H_{m+1,t}(x)=\left(mx-\frac{m-1}{t}\right)H_{m,t}(x)+x\left(x+1-\frac{1}{t}\right)H^{\prime}_{m,t}(x).

Hence, as
t\to-\infty
, these polynomials converge coefficientwise to
polynomials
G_{m}(x)
determined by

(21)

G_{1}(x)=1,\qquad G_{m+1}(x)=mxG_{m}(x)+x(x+1)G_{m}^{\prime}(x).

Lemma 3.4
.

For
m\geq 2
,
G_{m}(x)
has a simple root at
x=0
and exactly
m-2

further simple roots in
(-1,0)
.

Proof.

The proof is the limiting analogue of Proposition
3.2
. The
case
m=2
is
G_{2}=x
. Assume the result for
G_{m}
. On
(-1,0)
set

v_{m}(x)=(1+x)^{m}
. Then

(22)

\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(v_{m}(x)G_{m}(x)\bigr)=\frac{v_{m}(x)}{x(1+x)}G_{m+1}(x).

The function
v_{m}G_{m}
vanishes at
-1
, at the
m-2
roots of
G_{m}
in

(-1,0)
, and at
0
. Rolle’s theorem gives
m-1
distinct roots of

G_{m+1}
in
(-1,0)
. The recurrence gives degree
m
and a simple root
at
0
, so these roots account for the full degree and are simple.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(Behavior as
t\to-\infty
)
.

For fixed
m\geq 2
, every positive root
r_{i}^{(m)}(t)
tends to
+\infty

as
t\to-\infty
. In particular, all positive roots lie above
1
for all
sufficiently negative
t
.

Proof.

The polynomials
H_{m,t}
converge coefficientwise to
G_{m}
. By Lemma

3.4
, the nonzero roots of
G_{m}
are simple and lie in

(-1,0)
. Standard continuity of simple roots under coefficientwise
convergence gives convergence of the nonzero roots of
H_{m,t}
to these
roots. If
r_{i}^{(m)}(t)
is a positive root of
F_{m}(y,t)
, then

r_{i}^{(m)}(t)/t
is a negative root of
H_{m,t}
. Hence

\frac{r_{i}^{(m)}(t)}{t}\longrightarrow\alpha_{i}

for some
\alpha_{i}\in(-1,0)
. Since
t\to-\infty
, this implies

r_{i}^{(m)}(t)\to+\infty
.
∎

4.
Proof of real-rootedness and interlacing

We now prove the main theorem. Fix
n\geq 4
and put

m=n-1,\qquad d=n-3=m-2.

Besides the trivial root
y=0
, the polynomial
F_{m}(y,t)
has

d=m-2
positive root branches

0<r_{1}(t)<\cdots<r_{d}(t)<1-t.

Only these positive branches are relevant for the crossing argument, since the
trivial branch
y=0
never intersects the slice
y=1
.
By Lemma
3.3
, all
r_{i}(t)
are below
1
when
t
is close
to
0^{-}
. By Lemma
3.5
, all
r_{i}(t)
are above
1
for

t\ll 0
. Continuity of the ordered roots therefore implies that each branch
crosses the horizontal line
y=1
at least once. This step uses no monotonicity
of the branch in
t
. By Proposition
2.4
, these crossings are
exactly the zeros of

F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t)=P_{n}(t).

By (
1
),
P_{n}
has degree
d
. Since there are

d
branches and each branch crosses at least once, there can be exactly
one crossing on each branch and no other zeros. Hence
P_{n}
has
d=n-3

distinct roots, all lying in
(-\infty,0)
. This proves Theorem

1.1
.

It remains to record the interlacing, which follows from the same geometry.
Let
\tau_{i}
be the unique crossing time of the branch
r_{i}
, so that

r_{i}(\tau_{i})=1,\qquad P_{n}(\tau_{i})=0.

The branch ordering implies

(23)

\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{d}<0.

Indeed, at time
\tau_{i}
the branch
r_{j}
with
j>i
lies above
1
, and
since it is below
1
near
0^{-}
and crosses only once, its crossing time
must be larger than
\tau_{i}
.

At
t=\tau_{i}
, factor
F_{m}
as a polynomial in
y
:

(24)

F_{m}(y,\tau_{i})=c_{m}\,y\prod_{k=1}^{d}\bigl(y-r_{k}(\tau_{i})\bigr),\qquad c_{m}=(2m-3)!!>0.

Since
r_{i}(\tau_{i})=1
, the lower branches satisfy
r_{k}(\tau_{i})<1
for

k<i
, while the upper branches satisfy
r_{k}(\tau_{i})>1
for
k>i
. Thus

(25)

\operatorname{sgn}\,\partial_{y}F_{m}(1,\tau_{i})=(-1)^{d-i}.

Using the slope identity (
12
) with
m=n-1
gives

\partial_{y}F_{n-1}(1,t)=P_{n}(t)+S_{n+1}(t).

At a zero
\tau_{i}
of
P_{n}
, this becomes

(26)

S_{n+1}(\tau_{i})=\partial_{y}F_{n-1}(1,\tau_{i}),

so
S_{n+1}(\tau_{i})
has the sign in (
25
). The recurrence for

P_{n+1}
gives

(27)

P_{n+1}(\tau_{i})=\tau_{i}S_{n+1}(\tau_{i}).

Since
\tau_{i}<0
, the signs of
P_{n+1}(\tau_{i})
alternate as
i
varies.
Therefore
P_{n+1}
has a root in each interval
(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})
.

There is also a root on each side. At the right end,

P_{n+1}(\tau_{d})<0
by (
25
)–(
27
), while

P_{n+1}(0)=1
, so there is a root in
(\tau_{d},0)
. At the left end,

P_{n+1}
is monic of degree
d+1
, hence

\operatorname{sgn}P_{n+1}(t)=(-1)^{d+1}\qquad(t\ll 0),

whereas
P_{n+1}(\tau_{1})
has sign
(-1)^{d}
. Hence there is a root in

(-\infty,\tau_{1})
. We have found
d+1=\deg P_{n+1}
distinct real roots,
with exactly one in each interval. This proves Theorem

1.2
.

Corollary 4.1
.

For every
n\geq 3
, the Betti number sequence

b_{n,0},b_{n,1},\ldots,b_{n,n-3}

of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is ultra-log-concave:

\left(\frac{b_{n,i}}{\binom{n-3}{i}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{b_{n,i-1}}{\binom{n-3}{i-1}}\frac{b_{n,i+1}}{\binom{n-3}{i+1}}\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-4).

Proof.

The coefficients of
P_{n}
are nonnegative, either by their cohomological
meaning or directly from the recurrence. The ultra-log-concavity follows directly from the real-rootedness together with
Newton’s inequalities.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Higher ultra-log-concavities)
.

A sequence of positive integers

a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}

is called
r
-
ultra-log-concave
(r-
ULC
for short) if

\left(\frac{a_{i}}{\binom{n}{i}^{r}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{a_{i-1}}{\binom{n}{i-1}^{r}}\frac{a_{i+1}}{\binom{n}{i+1}^{r}}\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-1).

It is obvious that
r
-ULC implies
(r-1)
-ULC for
r\geq 1
and
0
-ULC is the ordinary log-concavity.
Based on numerical computations, we expect that the Betti sequence
\{b_{n,i}\,|\,0\leq i\leq n-3\}
is in fact
2
-ULC.
Furthermore, it was proved in
[
CK26
, Corollary 3.4]
that the Betti sequence is
asymptotically

3
-ULC but not
4
-ULC.
As the roots of a polynomial determine the coefficients, these higher ULCs indicate that the locations of zeros of
P_{n}(t)
may have certain patterns which warrant further investigations.

Remark 4.3

(Refined log-concavities)
.

The log-concavity in Corollary
4.1
can be refined by considering the action of the symmetric group
S_{n}
on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
by permuting the marked points. For each
0\leq i\leq n-3
, we can decompose the
S_{n}
-module
H^{2i}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n})
into a direct sum of irreducible representations and ask whether the multiplicities
\mathrm{mult}_{\lambda}H^{2i}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n})
of an irreducible representation
\lambda
of
S_{n}
form a log-concave sequence or not (cf.
[
CKL24
, Conjecture 1.6]
).

Of particular interest is the invariant part

H^{2k}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n})^{S_{n}}=H^{2k}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}/S_{n})

which is the cohomology of the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}/S_{n}
of stable curves of genus 0 with
n

unordered
marked points. It was proved in
[
CKL24
, Corollary 6.10 and Corollary 6.11]
that the Betti sequence of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}/S_{n}
is asymptotically log-concave but not ultra-log-concave. In particular, the Poincaré polynomial of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}/S_{n}
is not real-rooted. This indicates that a new approach is required for refined log-concavities.

5.
Fulton–MacPherson compactification of the configuration space

A closely related space to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is the Fulton–MacPherson compactification
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
(
FM space
for short) of the configuration space

(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}-\Delta,\qquad\Delta=\bigcup_{i\neq j}\Delta_{ij}

of
n
ordered distinct points in the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}
where
\Delta_{ij}
denotes the locus where
i
th and
j
th points coincide. See
[
FM94
]
and
[
Man99
, §4.3]
.
The FM space
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
is the wonderful compactification of the configuration space which is obtained by a sequence of blow-ups from
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
and is isomorphic to the moduli space of stable maps to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree 1:

\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]\cong\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}(\mathbb{P}^{1},1).

There is a canonical choice of an ample line bundle and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is the geometric invariant theory quotient of
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
by the action of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
.

By construction,
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
is a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
with vanishing odd cohomology and its Betti sequence

\tilde{b}_{n,i}=\dim H^{2i}(\mathbb{P}^{1}[n])\qquad(0\leq i\leq n)

is unimodal and palindromic.

By the same technique we used for
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 5.1
.

The Poincaré polynomial
\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)=\sum_{i}\tilde{b}_{n,i}t^{i}
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
has only negative real roots for
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Since the proof parallels that of Theorem
1.1
, we only provide an outline here. Let

\varphi(x,t)=\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}P_{m+1}(t),\quad\psi(x,t)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\widetilde{P}_{n}(t).

Then by
[
Man99
, IV. (4.24)]
, we have

\psi=(1+\varphi)^{t+1}

which is equivalent to the formula (cf.
[
CK26
, §4]
)

(28)

\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}t^{\min\{n-j,2\}}P_{j+1}(t)P_{n+1-j}(t)

with the convention
P_{1}=P_{2}=1
. Geometrically, (
28
) is a direct consequence of
the wall crossings of moduli spaces of
\delta
-stable maps to the quotient stack
[\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathbb{C}^{*}]
(cf.
[
CKL23
, §6.2]
).

The deformations
\widetilde{F}_{n}(y,t)
and
\Psi(x,y,t)
of
\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)
and
\psi(x,t)
respectively are defined by

\Psi(x,y,t)=(1+\Phi(x,y,t))^{t+1}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\widetilde{F}_{n}(y,t)

using the deformation (
9
) of
\varphi
.

The coefficients
\widetilde{F}_{n}(y,t)
are all divisible by
t+1
. Indeed,

(1+\Phi)^{t+1}-1=\sum_{k\geq 1}\binom{t+1}{k}\Phi^{k},

and each binomial coefficient
\binom{t+1}{k}=\frac{(t+1)t(t-1)\cdots(t-k+2)}{k!}
contains the factor
t+1
.
Thus the zero locus of
\widetilde{F}_{n}
contains the extraneous vertical
component
t=-1
. We remove this common component and work with the residual
deformation

\widehat{F}_{n}(y,t):=\frac{\widetilde{F}_{n}(y,t)}{t+1}.

Equivalently,

\widehat{\Psi}(x,y,t):=\frac{(1+\Phi(x,y,t))^{t+1}-1}{t+1}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\widehat{F}_{n}(y,t)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

On the slice
y=1
, this gives

\widehat{F}_{n}(1,t)=\widehat{P}_{n}(t),\qquad\widehat{P}_{n}(t):=\frac{\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)}{t+1}.

Thus it suffices to prove that
\widehat{P}_{n}(t)
has only negative real
roots; the removed factor
t+1
then contributes the additional root

t=-1
of
\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)
.

We next record the recurrence satisfied by the residual deformation. Since
\Psi=(1+\Phi)^{t+1}

and
\Phi
satisfies

\bigl(1-(y-1)x\bigr)\partial_{x}\Phi-y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}\Phi=1+\Phi,

we get

\bigl(1-(y-1)x\bigr)\partial_{x}\Psi-y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}\Psi=(t+1)\Psi.

Comparing coefficients and dividing by
t+1
, the polynomials

\widehat{F}_{n}
satisfy

(29)

\widehat{F}_{n+1}(y,t)=(ny-n+t+1)\widehat{F}_{n}(y,t)+y(y+t-1)\partial_{y}\widehat{F}_{n}(y,t),\qquad\widehat{F}_{1}(y,t)=1.

Fix
t<0
, and put
a=1-t
. Then (
29
)
becomes

\widehat{F}_{n+1}=(ny-n+2-a)\widehat{F}_{n}-y(a-y)\partial_{y}\widehat{F}_{n}.

For
0<y<a
, define

w_{n}(y)=y^{\alpha_{n}}(a-y)^{\beta_{n}},\qquad\alpha_{n}=\frac{n+a-2}{a},\qquad\beta_{n}=\frac{(n-1)a-n+2}{a}.

Both exponents are positive. A direct calculation gives the Sturm identity

(30)

\frac{d}{dy}\bigl(w_{n}(y)\widehat{F}_{n}(y,t)\bigr)=-\frac{w_{n}(y)}{y(a-y)}\widehat{F}_{n+1}(y,t),

exactly as in the proof of the Sturm theorem for
F_{m}(y,t)
, Rolle’s theorem
applied to (
30
) shows by induction that, for every

t<0
, the polynomial
\widehat{F}_{n}(\,\cdot\,,t)
has
n-1
simple roots,
all lying in the interval
0<y<1-t
.
We denote them by

0<r_{1}(t)<\cdots<r_{n-1}(t)<1-t.

Since the roots are simple, these ordered roots depend continuously on
t<0
. The recurrence also shows inductively that

\deg_{y}\widehat{F}_{n}=n-1
and that the leading coefficient is

(2n-3)!!>0
for
n\geq 2
.

It remains to compare the branches
r_{i}(t)
with the slice
y=1
. First,
as
t\to 0^{-}
, no branch can stay at or above
y=1
. Otherwise, after
passing to a subsequence, one would obtain a limiting zero of

\widehat{F}_{n}(y,0)
at
y=1
. But

\widehat{F}_{n}(1,0)=\widehat{P}_{n}(0)=1,

a contradiction. Hence all branches lie below
y=1
for
t<0
sufficiently
close to
0
.

Second, as
t\to-\infty
, all branches go to
+\infty
. This follows by
the same scaling argument used earlier. Namely, define

H_{n,t}(x)=t^{-(n-1)}\widehat{F}_{n}(tx,t).

Then
H_{n,t}
converges coefficientwise to the polynomial
K_{n}(x)
defined
by

K_{1}(x)=1,\qquad K_{n+1}(x)=(nx+1)K_{n}(x)+x(x+1)K_{n}^{\prime}(x).

For
n\geq 2
, the same Sturm–Rolle argument on
(-1,0)
shows that

K_{n}
has
n-1
simple roots in
[-1,0)
, one at
-1
and the
remaining
n-2
in
(-1,0)
. Hence the roots of
H_{n,t}
converge to negative limits, and
therefore the corresponding positive roots
r_{i}(t)
of

\widehat{F}_{n}(y,t)
tend to
+\infty
as
t\to-\infty
.

Thus each continuous branch
r_{i}(t)
lies below
y=1
near
t=0^{-}
and
above
y=1
for
t\ll 0
. Therefore each branch crosses the slice
y=1

at least once. These crossing times are exactly the zeros of

\widehat{F}_{n}(1,t)=\widehat{P}_{n}(t).

Moreover, at a fixed
t<0
, the roots of

\widehat{F}_{n}(\,\cdot\,,t)
are simple and distinct, so two different
branches cannot cross
y=1
at the same time.

Consequently
\widehat{P}_{n}(t)
has at least
n-1
distinct negative real
roots. Since
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
is smooth projective of dimension
n
,

\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)
has degree
n
and leading coefficient
1
. Therefore
\widehat{P}_{n}(t)=\frac{\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)}{t+1}

has degree
n-1
. The
n-1
negative roots found above are all its roots.
Finally, the removed factor
t+1
contributes the additional negative root

t=-1
. Hence
\widetilde{P}_{n}(t)
has only negative real roots.
∎

By Newton’s inequalities again, we have the following.

Corollary 5.2
.

The Betti sequence
\{\tilde{b}_{n,i}\}_{0\leq i\leq n}
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}[n]
is ultra-log-concave.

By
[
CK26
, Corollary 4.2]
, the Betti sequence is asymptotically 3-ULC.

Geometric intuition for the deformation
F_{m}(y,t)

The key structural idea of the proof was to replace the one-variable nonlinear
recurrence for the Poincaré polynomials
P_{n}(t)
by a two-variable
deformation
F_{m}(y,t)
whose geometry is much more rigid. Writing
m=n-1
, we
introduced polynomials
F_{m}(y,t)
satisfying

F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t).

Thus the original Poincaré polynomial is recovered as the slice of the
deformation along the hyperplane
y=1
, see Figure
1
.

The point of this construction is that the variable
y
is not merely an
auxiliary parameter: it is chosen so that the recurrence in
m
becomes a
first-order differential recurrence in the
y
-direction,

F_{m+1}(y,t)=(my-m+1)F_{m}(y,t)+y(y+t-1)\,\partial_{y}F_{m}(y,t).

From the point of view of analysis, this is a transport-type evolution in the

y
-variable. The coefficient
y(y+t-1)
is the decisive feature that anchors a Sturm problem on the interval
0<y<1-t
. For

t<0
, writing
a=1-t>1
, one has

y(y+t-1)=y(y-a)=-\,y(a-y),

so the derivative term vanishes exactly at the two boundary points

y=0,\qquad y=a=1-t.

This singles out the interval
0\leq y\leq 1-t
as the natural geometric
domain for the zero set of
F_{m}(\,\cdot\,,t)
.
The conceptual gain is that, for fixed
t<0
, the recurrence may be
rewritten in Sturm form. After multiplication by a suitable positive weight

w_{m}(y)
, one obtains an identity of the shape

\frac{d}{dy}\bigl(w_{m}(y)F_{m}(y,t)\bigr)=-\frac{w_{m}(y)}{y(1-t-y)}\,F_{m+1}(y,t).

Hence the zeros of
F_{m+1}(\,\cdot\,,t)
are the critical points of
w_{m}F_{m}
. Rolle’s theorem then forces the roots of

F_{m+1}
to appear between the roots of
F_{m}
, with the endpoints

y=0
and
y=1-t
providing the extra control needed to close the
induction. In this way the deformation turns the original problem into a
Sturm-chain argument in the auxiliary variable
y
.

7.
Overview of the Co-Math proof development

The proof above emerged from an iterative Co-Math process rather than from
a single polished answer. The progression is instructive because it shows
both the power and the risks of agentic mathematical collaboration.

The first attempts already found the main architecture: reduce the problem
to interlacing, introduce a bivariate deformation, study its roots by a
Rolle-theoretic argument, and interpret the roots of
P_{n}
as crossings of
positive
y
-root branches through the line
y=1
. At that stage, however,
several crucial details were still heuristic. The derivation of the

F_{m}
-recurrence was not fully justified, the branch-crossing argument
assumed monotonicity that had not been proved, and the endpoint
behavior as
t\to 0^{-}
and
t\to-\infty
was only partially controlled.

The next round strengthened the algebra. The generating function
(
7
), the PDE (
10
), and the slice identity

F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t)
were made explicit. The fixed-
t
root structure was
converted into the weighted derivative identity (
18
), so
Rolle’s theorem could be applied without hidden assumptions. Computation
was useful in this stage, but only as a guardrail: it checked indexing,
small cases, and the identities involving
F_{m}(1,t)
and

\partial_{y}F_{m}(1,t)
. It did not prove the analytic branch argument.

The final step replaced the remaining experimental and computation-based claims by
proof. A possible topological zigzag problem for root branches was eliminated by
a degree count: each branch must cross
y=1
at least once, while

P_{n}(t)=F_{n-1}(1,t)
has exactly as many possible zeros as there are
branches. The behavior at
t\to-\infty
was justified by the scaled limit

H_{m,t}\to G_{m}
and the independent Sturm analysis of
G_{m}
on
(-1,0)
.
This turned the geometric picture into a rigorous crossing theorem and then
into strict interlacing.

Our workflow aligns closely with the design principles described in the
AI co-mathematician paper of Zheng et al.
[
ZvGZ+26
]
: the system
supported iterative refinement, numerical exploration, proof development,
and production of native mathematical artifacts. It also illustrates the
paper’s cautions. Early drafts were written in confident LaTeX even when
some lemmas were not yet proved; the bibliography changed across attempts,
and key provenance information, such as the Keel–Manin–Getzler origin of
the recurrence and the relevant work of Choi–Kiem–Lee, was not preserved
consistently.

A stronger agentic workflow should maintain a persistent gap registry, a
computation log indicating exactly what was checked and the exact ranges
verified, and a reference manifest distinguishing sources used in the proof
from background literature. In this problem, the most productive and interesting behavior was
not one-shot theorem proving but the ability to keep a promising architecture
alive, accept finite computation as provisional evidence, and gradually replace
it with exact lemmas. The point is not that computation was used as proof, but
that it was used as a temporary scaffold for proof discovery. For instance,
the identities

F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t),\qquad\partial_{y}F_{m}(1,t)=P_{m+1}(t)+S_{m+2}(t),

together with the observed real-rootedness and branch-crossing behavior of

F_{m}(y,t)
, were first supported by finite symbolic and numerical checks.
Those checks were strong enough for the system to preserve the bivariate
deformation as the central proof architecture, even while several steps
remained unproved. Later iterations then discharged the gaps: the identities
were derived from the generating-function PDE, the root structure was proved
by a Sturm–Rolle argument, and the endpoint behavior of the moving branches
was proved. This illustrates a genuine
strength of the Co-Mathematician workflow: it can reason provisionally from
well-documented computational evidence without confusing that evidence with
the final proof.
