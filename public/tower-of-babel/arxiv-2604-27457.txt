Demonstration of Exponential Quantum Speedup with Constant-Depth Compiled Circuits for Simon’s Problem
Phattharaporn Singkanipa, Victor Kasatkin, Daniel A. Lidar

Abstract
We demonstrate exponential quantum speedup for a restricted-Hamming-weight version of Simon’s problem on present-day superconducting quantum processors by introducing a hardware-aware compilation strategy that compiles the quantum part of each Simon query circuit to constant depth. The resulting compiled circuits have O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) depth and linear connectivity, map directly onto common device layouts, and avoid additional routing and SWAP overhead. Implemented on IBM’s 156 156 -qubit Boston and 120 120 -qubit Miami processors, the resulting circuits achieve sufficiently high fidelity to exhibit algorithmic quantum speedup without error suppression. Using the number-of-queries-to-solution metric, we observe exponential speedup over the classical lower bound across the full Hamming-weight range studied on Boston and across low-to-intermediate Hamming weights on Miami; at higher Hamming weights on Miami, we still observe polynomial speedup. The same construction also reaches a regime where the original Simon problem is recovered for the problem sizes studied. These results show that careful hardware-aware compilation can make exponential quantum speedup experimentally accessible for a canonical hidden-subgroup problem in the NISQ regime.

In the original formulation of Simon’s problem,
one is given a function
f_{b}\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n}

promised to be either 1-to-1 or 2-to-1,
such that
f_{b}(x)=f_{b}(y)
if and only if
x=y
or
x=y\oplus b
,
where
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is a hidden bitstring.
The task is to determine which case holds and,
in the 2-to-1 case, to identify
b
.
Here we focus on the 2-to-1 case and restrict
b
to have Hamming weight at most
w\leq n
,
yielding the restricted-HW version of Simon’s problem
\mathsf{w}_{{w}}\textnormal{Simon-}{n}
.
The original Simon’s problem is recovered by setting
w=n
.

We analyze performance using an oracle-query metric, the number-of-queries-to-solution (NTS)
Singkanipa et al. 2025
,
and show that this setting enables a clear quantum scaling advantage even on contemporary superconducting qubit architectures.
The same compiled-circuit construction also allows the original Simon’s problem to be implemented up to a certain problem size,
so the demonstrated exponential speedup is not limited to the restricted-Hamming-weight setting in those cases.

Singkanipa et al. 2025

whose rules are briefly summarized in

In

The speedup results are presented in

II
Quantum speedup in a guessing game

II.1
Rules of the game

We adopt the single-player guessing game introduced in Ref.
Singkanipa et al. 2025
. A function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying the 2-to-1 condition is drawn uniformly at random and is not revealed to the player, who instead has oracle access to evaluate
f(x)
for any input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. The player may perform any number of oracle queries, along with arbitrary classical computation, before submitting a guess for the hidden bitstring
b
. The correctness of the guess is then verified, after which a new function is drawn and the game proceeds to the next round. The objective of the player is to maximize the number of correct guesses while minimizing the total number of oracle queries.

The performance of each player is quantified by the average score per oracle query,
\mathrm{NTS}^{-1}
, with a higher value indicating better performance, where

\mathrm{NTS}=\frac{\langle Q\rangle}{\langle P\rangle}.

(1)

Here, NTS denotes the
number-of-oracle queries-to-solution
,
\langle\bullet\rangle
denotes the expectation value averaged over many rounds of the game,
Q
is the number of oracle queries per round, and
P
is the score obtained in a round. To discourage uninformed guessing without consulting the oracle, incorrect guesses are penalized by setting
P=1
for a correct guess and
P=-p_{r}/(1-p_{r})
for an incorrect guess, where
S
denotes the allowed set of hidden strings and
p_{r}=1/|S|
is the probability that a uniformly random guess is correct. In the restricted-HW setting considered below,
|S|=N_{w}
.

Three players compete in this game: (i) a classical player, whose oracle access consists of submitting a query
x
and receiving
f(x)
in return; (ii) a noiseless quantum player, whose oracle is the unitary
\mathcal{O}_{f}
acting as
\mathcal{O}_{f}\ket{x}\ket{a}=\ket{x}\ket{a\oplus f(x)}
;
and (iii) a NISQ player, who has indirect access to a noisy quantum processing unit (QPU) on which circuits containing
\mathcal{O}_{f}
are executed after compilation.

II.2
Classical algorithm

To generalize to
\mathsf{w}_{{w}}\textnormal{Simon-}{n}
, we introduce
N_{w}
as the cardinality of the set
S
of possible values of
b\neq 0^{n}
, restricted to those satisfying
\HW(b)\leq w
, with
w\leq n
:

N_{w}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{w}\binom{n}{j},

(2)

which simplifies to
N_{n}\equiv 2^{n}-1
in the original Simon’s problem.

Theorem 1

.

For the trivial case

(3)

Following this theorem, we define

\mathrm{NTS}_{C}^{\mathrm{lb}}\equiv\begin{cases}0,&N_{w}=1,\\[5.69054pt]
k_{\min}-\dfrac{k_{\min}(k_{\min}-1)(k_{\min}-2)}{6N_{w}},&N_{w}\geq 2.\end{cases}

(5)

In this work we compare the scaling of the
\mathrm{NTS}_{Q}
with

\mathrm{NTS}_{C}^{\mathrm{lb}}

\mathrm{NTS}_{C}

(1) we do not know the exact optimal value of

(for the best possible classical player), and

For fixed

Consequently,

Without restricting

II.5
Quantum speedup quantification

We define an

for the
\mathsf{w}_{{w}}\textnormal{Simon-}{n}
problem as better scaling with
N_{w}

of the function
\mathrm{NTS}_{Q}
[

7

than of the function
\mathrm{NTS}_{C}
for the best possible classical strategy:

\mathrm{NTS}_{Q}=o(\min_{\pi}\mathrm{NTS}_{C}(\pi))
, where the minimum is taken
over all classical strategies
\pi
.
As follows from
1

the sufficient condition for such speedup is scaling with
N_{w}
better than
\mathrm{NTS}_{C}^{\mathrm{lb}}

defined in
Eq.

5
.
Since we do not know how exactly

scales with
N_{w}

we use that sufficient condition in our analysis.
We determine the scaling by fitting two two-parameter models.
Both models satisfy the constraint
\mathrm{NTS}(N_{w}=1)=0

since when

First, consider a polylogarithmic (polylog) model in

:

\mathrm{NTS}_{\text{polylog}}=a(\log_{2}{N_{w}})^{\alpha}.

(8)

Second, consider a polynomial (poly) model in
N_{w}
:

\mathrm{NTS}_{\text{poly}}=b(N_{w}^{\beta}-1).

(9)

Since the classical lower bound scales as
N_{w}^{1/2}
and therefore follows the poly model,
an
exponential

when
\mathrm{NTS}_{Q}
follows the polylog model.
If, instead,
\mathrm{NTS}_{Q}
follows the poly model with
\beta_{Q}<\beta_{C}
for a fixed

a

speedup is observed.
We refer to
\alpha
and
\beta
as the scaling exponents.
The fitting parameters
a
and
b
do not matter for scaling purposes.
We explain how we determine which model is the better fit in

To quantitatively assess which model better describes
\mathrm{NTS}_{Q}
, we employ two complementary fit diagnostics:
the coefficient of determination
R^{2}
and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
Akaike 1974

The

1

V
Conclusions

Demonstrating a quantum speedup that scales favorably with problem size is central to establishing the practical utility of quantum computers.
Simon’s problem,
an early instance of the Abelian hidden subgroup problem and a conceptual precursor to Shor’s factoring algorithm,
provides a natural setting for this pursuit:
it requires an exponential number of oracle queries classically
yet only linear number of oracle queries on a noiseless quantum computer.
Thus, in the oracle-query model, Simon’s problem exhibits a theoretical quantum speedup when the resources required to implement the oracle are not accounted for.

A restricted-HW version of Simon’s problem,
in which the hidden bitstring is restricted by
\HW(b)\leq w\leq n
,
was studied in Ref.
Singkanipa et al. 2025
,
where it was demonstrated that exponential quantum speedup is achievable
on NISQ devices provided
w
does not scale with
n
.
For fixed
w
, the classical lower bound scales as
n^{w/2}
;
in the unrestricted case
w=n
,
the classical query complexity is instead
\Omega(2^{n/2})
.

In this work, we revisited this problem with a new circuit compilation scheme
that achieves constant circuit depth.
We performed Simon’s algorithm experiments on the IBM Quantum platform
and demonstrated that the
156
-qubit Boston device exhibits exponential quantum speedup
over the Hamming-weight range for which we could reliably distinguish
between poly and polylog scaling,
while the
120
-qubit Miami device similarly exhibits exponential speedup
at low-to-intermediate Hamming weights and polynomial speedup at higher Hamming weights.
Moreover, for a certain range of problem sizes
n
,
this speedup extends to the original Simon’s problem with
w=n

and is therefore not limited to the small-
w
regime.
The observed performance is consistent with the reduced depth
and routing overhead of the new
compiled-circuit construction,
although comparisons with
Singkanipa et al. 2025

should be interpreted with some care because
the present experiments were performed on newer hardware.

These results significantly extend experimental demonstrations
of algorithmic quantum speedup in the oracle model.
In particular, relative to our previous work
Singkanipa et al. 2025
, the main advances are
the broader Hamming-weight range over which exponential speedup is observed,
the observation of polynomial speedup on Miami when exponential scaling is no longer favored,
and the extension of the experiments to a regime where the original Simon problem
\mathsf{w}_{{n}}\textnormal{Simon-}{n}
is recovered for the problem sizes studied.
Over the problem sizes and Hamming-weight ranges studied here, we therefore observe either exponential or polynomial quantum speedup. More generally, our work adds to the experimental evidence from previous demonstrations
Pokharel and Lidar 2023
;
Singkanipa et al. 2025
that algorithmic quantum speedup for structured, nontrivial oracle problems is becoming experimentally accessible on present-day superconducting hardware. Our constant-depth compilation scheme for Simon query circuits is also of independent interest and suggests that further hardware-aware depth reductions may be possible in other quantum algorithms, including beyond the oracle setting.
