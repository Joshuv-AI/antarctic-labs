ABSTRACT
The Clinical Implementation Pilots (CIPs) within Singapore’s National Precision Medicine (NPM) programme are designed to integrate genomic testing into clinical pathways for conditions including hereditary cancers, familial hypercholesterolemia, breast cancer, primary glomerular disease, and pharmacogenomics. These multidisciplinary pilots emphasize collaboration to enhance cost-effectiveness and improve patient outcomes, thereby establishing a sustainable foundation for future scalability in clinical genomics and ultimately fostering a healthier population through personalized healthcare solutions.

Precision Health around the World

In precision health, lifestyle, environmental and other health data is supplemented by genetic and molecular profiles to enable more informed clinical decision-making. Beyond a “one-size-fits-all” approach, precision health can lead to improved diagnosis enhanced disease prediction, tailored therapies to optimize outcomes, minimized adverse treatment effects, and even identification of novel therapeutic targets where existing treatments are unavailable or inadequate1,2. For these advancements to become reality, genomics must be incorporated into clinical pathways.

However, several factors limit the broad adoption of these technologies. These challenges include accessibility, which can be physical (e.g. infrastructure, trained workforce) or financial (e.g. health financing), the paucity of validated clinical pathways effectively implemented into healthcare systems, and the need for policy safeguards such as protection against genetic discrimination2–6. While genomics has already demonstrated cost-effectiveness in numerous clinical contexts such as cascade testing for familial hypercholesterolemia (FH)7, healthcare system constraints should be considered during implementation to ensure efficient resource allocation at a systemic level. Addressing these challenges systematically is critical to successfully integrating precision health into existing healthcare systems.

Globally, several initiatives have attempted to harness the benefits of precision medicine, such as Japan’s Tohoku Medical Megabank project8, Genomics England in the UK9, All of Us programme in the US10, and the China Kadoorie Biobank11. However, Asian ancestry remains significantly underrepresented in large-scale genomic datasets6,12. This underrepresentation can hinder population and medical genetics research, leading, for example, to an increased risk of misdiagnosis or mistreatment due to the mistaken identification of pathogenic variants13,14. Furthermore, it exacerbates global health inequities.

Singapore’s National Precision Medicine (NPM) programme aims to bridge this gap. Now halfway through its three-phase plan, the programme has already produced the world’s most extensive database of individuals living in Southeast Asia12,15. This was possible due to the diversity of Singapore’s population which comprises three major ethnic groups: Chinese (>70%), Malay (13%), Indian (9%), alongside other communities. The NPM programme was carefully planned and designed so that learnings from its genomic data are readily embedded into clinical workflows right from the outset.

This paper describes the development and execution of the Clinical Implementation Pilots (CIP) within the NPM programme, which aim to study how genetic testing with the potential to impact healthcare might be incorporated in a cost-effective, scalable and sustainable manner into the Singapore healthcare system.

Singapore’s healthcare system: balancing excellence and emerging challenges

Singapore is globally recognized for its efficient and high quality healthcare system. Compared to other high-income countries, Singapore’s healthcare costs are low whilst achieving amongst the highest average life expectancy16,17. Healthcare spending in Singapore makes up approximately 5% of the country’s GDP, in stark contrast to the United States where it constitutes around 17%18. The Ministry of Health (MOH) governs Singapore’s healthcare system, overseeing both public healthcare and statutory boards, such as the Health Promotion Board (HPB), Health Sciences Authority (HSA), and the Agency for Integrated Care (AIC)16,19–21.

The system is structured in three tiers—primary, secondary, and tertiary care as illustrated in Fig. 122–25. Singapore’s healthcare philosophy emphasizes a balance between individual responsibility and social equity. While there is no entirely free healthcare in Singapore26, universal health coverage is achieved through multiple layers of financial protection. Citizens and permanent residents receive significant subsidies for care at public healthcare institutions27–29. Additional health financing is provided through the “3Ms” (MediSave, MediShield Life, and MediFund)16,22,30. Medisave functions as a mandatory savings scheme from payroll deductions16,31, MediShield life ensures universal lifelong coverage, regardless of age or pre-existing conditions16,26,32,33 and MediFund acts as an ultimate safety net for Singaporean citizens who cannot afford their out-of-pocket expenses after subsidies16,34.Fig. 1Singapore’s Healthcare system.Key actors of Singapore’s Healthcare system, showcasing those components that are either privately or government owned or delivered20,21,24,25. The public sector is organized into three 3 clusters—National University Health System, National Healthcare Group, and SingHealth—offering a range of services from the Polyclinics to general hospitals22,35. Polyclinics offer affordable basic health services, preventative care and chronic disease management by general practitioners (GPs), nurses, and allied health professionals while referring specialised treatments to acute hospitals23. However, 80% of primary care is delivered in private clinics16,22–24. While the public sector forms the backbone of Singapore’s healthcare system, for those who wish to use private hospitals or care in private wards in public hospitals, private health insurance can also be purchased.

Despite its strengths, Singapore’s healthcare system is under pressure from an aging population, increasing prevalence of chronic disease burden, workforce constraints, rising healthcare costs, and the integration of emerging technologies. Singapore’s population of approximately 4.2 million Singaporeans and permanent residents is aging rapidly. In 2000, 1 in 14 Singaporeans were aged 65 or older; by 2015, this increased to 1 in 8 and is projected to reach 1 in 4 by 203036,37.

Responding to these pressures, Singapore is shifting from a reactive, curative care model to a proactive, preventive approach38. One major initiative is the “Healthier SG” campaign, an initiative launched by the MOH in 2022 to rebalance the healthcare system39. The programme encourages residents to enrol with a primary care doctor to create personalized health plans, engage in regular health screenings for early detection, and adopt preventive measures39. By September 2025, Healthier SG had been implemented nationally, targeting individuals aged 40 and above. Early reports show strong public engagement with significant enrolment in the program. Initial efforts are focused on preventive health and care management, including vaccinations and screenings. The Healthier SG Chronic Tier introduced in February 2024, further supports chronic condition management by offering subsidized rates for common medications at participating GP clinics40. By promoting community engagement and leveraging digital health solutions, Healthier SG aims to reduce healthcare costs while improving citizen’s quality of life39.

Singapore’s National Precision Medicine Programme

Singapore’s National Precision Medicine programme (NPM) was established to test the hypothesis that the incorporation of genomics into healthcare—together with lifestyle, environmental and other health data—could improve health outcomes and reduce healthcare costs in Singapore. To achieve this, the programme was designed as a three-phase initiative: Phase I (Proof of Concept), Phase II (Proof of Value), and Phase III (Proof of Scale). The strategy, focus, and objectives for each phase, as well as early insights into the Singapore genome, have been detailed by Wong et al. (2023)12.

Aligned with the Healthier SG initiative, the CIPs within the NPM programme emphasizes prevention, early detection and early treatment. This approach extends partnerships beyond hospitals to include primary care providers. The clinical implementation strategy under the NPM programme prioritizes cost-effectiveness across the care continuum, ensuring proper financial coverage while reducing unnecessary healthcare service use41. Laying the groundwork for clinical implementation pilots

Phase I laid the foundation for the design principles that guided the CIPs in Phase II. A Request for Proposals (RFP) was issued to identify clinical pathways—defined in this context as care pathways that are developed or adapted from international guidelines and subsequently piloted, evaluated, and refined based on real-world implementation evidence within Singapore’s healthcare system—where genomic tests could improve diagnosis, management, or treatment of specific patient cohorts or community populations. Within the NPM program, these pathways are led by clinician champions, who drive the implementation and evaluation of the clinical implementation pilots. A use case is considered ‘validated’ once it has demonstrated analytical and clinical validity, favourable budget impact, and the cost-effectiveness of the entire pathway, including subsequent treatment. Furthermore, the evaluation process assesses organizational readiness to ensure successful integration into routine care. This includes securing equitable access to testing and establishing health financing models that cover not only the cost of the genetic test itself but also the long-term clinical care required following a diagnosis.

Three studies were commissioned under Phase I for promising applications (11): (1) Pediatric Genetic Diseases, (2) Hereditary Cancer (HC), and (3) Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH). Among these, the use of whole exome sequencing (WES) as first-line testing for pediatric patients with global developmental delay and multiple congenital abnormalities was found to be cost-saving42. Economic models for hereditary cancer and FH suggested cost-effectiveness compared to the existing pathway, with further testing and evaluation recommended for these use cases.

Findings from Phase I studies were synthesised into generalisable design principles informing Phase II CIP development. While these principles were applied consistently across all proposals, Phase I applications did not receive preferential weighting in the Phase II selection process. As a result, HC and FH were subsequently selected as CIPs to be further tested and evaluated while the use case for paediatric genetic disease was deemed ready for implementation pending establishment of protocols for first-line WGS testing. On the latter, it is worth noting that in the Singapore context, direct referrals of paediatric patients to genetic services have been long established within the local paediatric healthcare system. A key limitation to this existing practice involves subjecting the referred paediatric patients through a step-by-step test progression from basic, complex, discretionary, single/multi-gene investigations to clinical genomic sequencing. Within this context, the RFP study has provided clear evidence suggesting the use of WGS as first-line testing for paediatric patients with global developmental delay and multiple congenital anomalies.

Through this exercise, it demonstrated the feasibility in the Singapore context of aligning clinicians on a clinical pathway and generating economic models to inform healthcare and policy. It also highlighted gaps in data availability and accessibility, which would require improvement for broader adoption.

Design of Phase II Clinical Implementation Pilot proposals

Insights from Phase I informed the design of the call for Clinical Implementation Pilots (CIPs) in Phase II. Grant proposals were required to be co-led by teams of clinicians and health economists, and were expected to identify clinical innovations involving genetic or genomic testing. Each proposal needed to use the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study Design) and was required to include a preliminary assessment of health economic implications suggesting the potential that the use case would be cost-effective in the local healthcare system, compared to the current standard of care. The preliminary economic assessment was to identify key factors likely to exert a large influence on cost or cost effectiveness; this was intended to inform the design of the CIP (Fig. 2). Description of the CIP Grant Proposal Design.

Grant proposals, co-led by teams of clinicians and health economists, were expected to identify a potential clinical innovation that involved the use of a genetic/genomic test. Using a preliminary economic model, key determinants around effectiveness and/or implementation were to be identified.

The ultimate aim of Phase II was to address uncertainties and limitations in implementing genomic testing clinical pathways, ensuring financial and operational sustainability and optimizing healthcare experiences for both providers and patients.

Finalizing the Phase II CIP proposals: competitive and collaborative steps

Finalizing the CIP proposals included two steps: competition and collaboration which took place over roughly 10 months (Fig. 3). In the competitive step, the process was open to all public healthcare institutions in Singapore, and was initiated with a virtual roadshow attended by about 100 participants.Fig. 3Clinical Implementation Pilot Grant Call: a multi-step approach.The Clinical Implementation Pilot Grant Call consisted of a complex multi-step approach, that was divided into a Competitive and Collaborative Phase. Shortly after, the CIP grant call was launched and 13 applications from a broad spectrum of disease areas were received. Each application was scored by 4 independent reviewers who represented a variety of stakeholders in the ecosystem, including representatives of the and its affiliated agencies, clinical researchers, and international health economists. This was followed by a thorough review process to eliminate any biases that might have resulted from the reviewer’s rankings. For example, it was verified that no reviewers consistently gave very high/low scores on proposals and whether there were wide discrepant scores between reviewers. Additionally, two international health economists were appointed to provide scoring on the health economics model for each application to evaluate whether incorporating these scores into the overall score would alter the ranking. This quality control step showed that there was no significant change in the ranking of proposals. The next step involved a virtual briefing session, necessitated by the COVID-19 pandemic, with an 18-member review panel. This was followed by an additional review panel that included 15 reviewers from the MOH, healthcare clusters, and A*STAR. A Delphi-like model was used for scoring and finalizing the top five CIPs, which covered a broad spectrum of disease areas. Reviewers then scored the 10 proposals and finalized the 5 top-scoring CIP proposals. This marked the end of the Competitive Phase.

The subsequent collaborative step involved joint meetings of shortlisted CIP teams from multiple disciplines and therapeutic areas, with the goal of maximising efficiency and achieving economies of scale in addressing shared challenges. The Collaborative phase opened with a 1-day workshop attended by all five shortlisted CIP teams to identify team leads and opportunities for cross-collaboration that can benefit more than one CIP. The aim was to form workgroups/workstreams to develop broad “shared services” ideas into proposals, and to draft 6 “shared services” supplementary proposals. Another 1-day workshop with all five shortlisted CIPs was organized, this time also comprising the “shared services” workgroup/workstream leads to jointly decide on the outcomes of the shared services supplementary proposals. At the end of this process, five CIPs and six supplementary shared services projects were awarded. As an ongoing effort to promote cross CIP collaboration, a scientific committee and a health economics workgroup were established and include representatives from all five CIPs, health economists, and the PRECISE leads, with two of the CIP leads serving as co-chairs. Regular bimonthly meetings have been held in collaboration with the Chief Medical Office at PRECISE to discuss strategy for precision medicine implementation in Singapore; review progress, share findings, address any issues, and reallocate resources for optimisation within the CIPs. These meetings aimed to develop resources for Phase III and beyond, ensuring that CIP data and findings benefited the ecosystem and provided a platform for ongoing knowledge exchange.

In parallel, a health economics workgroup was formed, also consisting of representatives from all five CIPs, health economists, and PRECISE leads. In this case, the co-chairs were two health economists who served as co-principal investigators for the CIPs. The workgroup’s purpose was to identify common activities, consolidate findings, standardize data collection and analysis efforts, and produce joint publications.

Each pilot use case was rolled out in the local healthcare system, where it involved genetic testing as part of the clinical pathway to guide patient care. The use cases were used as pathfinder conditions to identify and overcome barriers to implementation; and to identify ways to optimise the clinical pathways, minimising the demands on resources that were currently constrained within the healthcare system (e.g., but not limited to: budget, utilisation of hospital services (i.e., appointments, admissions) and healthcare manpower).

Notably, the Healthier SG programme provides a vital platform through which genomic information can be integrated with clinical and lifestyle data to strengthen risk stratification and earlier case-finding beyond conventional age-based screening. Leveraging this national framework, the CIPs demonstrate how targeted testing (e.g., for hereditary cancer and familial hypercholesterolemia), risk-informed screening (e.g., polygenic risk scores for breast cancer), and pre-emptive pharmacogenomics can identify individuals at higher risk or likely to benefit from earlier intervention. By embedding these genomic insights into Healthier SG’s preventive-care model, clinicians can deliver personalised health plans in primary care, ensuring timely specialist referrals and earlier interventions that reduce downstream complications and costs.

Key accomplishments of Phase I and Phase II

To date, the NPM programme has generated invaluable insights and resources, benefitting not only Singapore but also the broader Southeast Asian region. Phase I provided insight into genetic variations in disease risk, helped develop tailored disease screening panels for Asians and addressed how genetic factors affect individuals’ response to medications12,15,48,49.

Across five CIPs, more than 6000 patients and their family members have participated in genetic testing as part of a clinical pathway aimed at more effective care. Moreover, the CIPs initiative has garnered significant industry interest, both locally and globally, resulting in several promising partnerships. For example, Lifestrands, a Singapore-based start-up, develops genetic tests and services tailored to local needs53, while Nalagenetics focuses on a mobile application that seamlessly returns pharmacogenomics results to patients54. By providing healthcare professionals with data-driven tools for personalized treatment decisions, Nalagenetics aims to enhance patient safety and therapeutic outcomes in Singapore54. Further demonstrating their impact, Nalagenetics has engaged the Biomedical and Genome Science Institute (BGSi), led by BB Binomika of Indonesia’s Ministry of Health, leveraging AWS to deploy pharmacogenomic (PGx) reports via a Clinical Decision Support platform55. On a global scale, the CIP initiative has attracted partnerships with Illumina56 and Alamya Health (both US-based) to develop improved algorithms for genomic analysis and bring clinical-grade long-read sequencing services to Singapore and the region, respectively.

Lastly, CIP success is and will be evaluated using a predefined implementation and impact framework encompassing several key domains. These include reach and uptake (e.g., the proportion of eligible patients tested and completion of downstream actions such as referrals, surveillance, or cascade testing); timeliness and efficiency (e.g., turnaround time from patient identification to result disclosure and subsequent clinical action, as well as impacts on clinicians’ workload); clinical impact (e.g., the proportion of actionable results leading to guideline-concordant interventions and indications of earlier detection or avoided adverse outcomes); and health system value (e.g., cost per patient tested or per actionable finding, overall budget impact, and equity of access across care settings).

Notably, insights generated from the CIPs have directly informed policy decision-making. The FH pilot provided the local evidence base for a national-scale delivery model (i.e., Genomic Assessment Centre, GAC). Introduced by Singapore’s MOH in June 2025, this mainstreamed service for FH management and cascade testing leverages primary care physicians for screening and referral to FH genetic testing, with subsidized testing and treatment57. Similarly, subsidies for HBOC genetic testing and relevant downstream interventions will be introduced from December 2026, ensuring equitable access58.

Scaling preventive care: Phase III

Transitioning to Phase III will shift the focus to scaling genomic clinical implementation at the population level, building on findings from Phase II and other initiatives. In Phase II, healthcare delivery for CIPs was primarily developed by specialists within tertiary care settings, thereby engaging only a small fraction of the total potential patient population, as individuals outside of these specialized settings were not readily included. Therefore, Phase III, a large-scale research initiative, will generate robust, population-specific evidence in a cost effective manner when using genomic information is scaled to population levels59. The aim is to recruit up to 450,000 participants and will work with individuals receiving medical treatments and clinical services, including specific disease conditions60. The NPM with its hospital partners and MOH is working systematically to assess clinical utility, population health impact, system readiness, and patient perspectives of more disease areas so as to guide these innovations through the appropriate pathways for clinical adoption59. This requires identifying key barriers to widespread implementation and developing strategies that enhance effective deployment while considering the constraints of the Singapore healthcare system, such as financing, manpower, and digital health - policies that were considered during the design of the NPM to ensure equitable access but need further refinement. Strategies to identify and address these key barriers include the adoption of iterative improvement cycles in NPM Phase III (pilot → adapt → scale). This approach is intended to refine clinical pathways through short-cycle testing (e.g., Plan–Do–Study–Act–type iterations) prior to national scale-up. In parallel, closer coordination with the MOH will be pursued to identify policy-relevant issues at an earlier stage and to contribute to policy development through the evidence base generated from clinical implementation studies.

There is a critical need to shift from “reactive curative care” to “proactive preventive care,” which necessitates greater collaboration with primary care providers. As part of this effort, PRECISE plans to co-develop clinical pathways for integration within primary care settings, such as polyclinics and private GP clinics, under the Healthier SG programme39,61. However, since ~80% of primary care is private, these providers may not utilize shared infrastructure and processes, such as drug procurement and IT systems, or connect to national digital healthcare platforms. Leveraging digital health solutions, AI, and automation can enable scalable preventive care by supporting EMR-based case-finding, streamlined digital consent and follow-up, and primary-care decision support for test ordering and results interpretation; however, these approaches must be implemented with attention to interoperability across heterogeneous primary care IT systems, alert fatigue and workflow burden, data quality and algorithmic bias, and governance requirements for transparency, auditability, privacy, and clinical accountability. Examples of how Phase III may co-develop clinical pathways with primary care providers include the co-design of pathways centred on “primary care–ready” deliverables (beyond traditional clinical guidelines), embedding these pathways within Healthier SG preventive care workflows, integrating them into primary care IT and electronic medical record (EMR) systems with appropriate clinical decision support, and providing implementation support to facilitate feasible and sustainable adoption in routine practice.

Additionally, a key realization from these efforts was the need for a centralized, privacy-compliant data-sharing platform to act as a clearinghouse for clinically observed variants, facilitating real-time visibility of variant classifications, phenotypic contexts, and evidence supporting reclassification. While offering clear benefits, including the prevention of duplicated efforts, harmonization of interpretations, and accelerated case resolution nationally, the costs associated with data storage and sharing must be carefully considered. This will be studied in Phase III, considering centralised procurement to capture economies of scale, tiered storage and lifecycle management (hot vs cold, retention policies), reduce the “central footprint” (store variant-level knowledge). Further development requires a more efficient mechanism to support multiple data-sharing, research collaboration, and ethics agreements between institutions to ensure scalability.

Finally, while the current moratorium on genetic testing and insurance for precision medicine in Singapore provides a foundation to encourage individuals to undergo clinical genetic tests62, concerns about insurability remain. While the MOH continues to refine its current moratorium, it is also in the process of developing legislation to formalise it60,62,63. This is expected to extend coverage to employment in addition to insurance. Further work is needed to ensure equitable access to testing, care, and treatments that can help mitigate the negative impact of genetic factors on health. Transitioning to Phase III will enable the implementation of proactive, equitable healthcare solutions at the population level by addressing key challenges and fostering collaboration across the healthcare system. Upon completion of Phase III, three key outcomes are anticipated: a world-class database reflecting Asia’s diversity; a healthcare system better equipped to deliver cost-effective genomic care; and a thriving biomedical ecosystem60.

Conclusion

Through its National Precision Medicine programme, Singapore is building the largest multi-ethnic Asian genetic database and advancing clinical innovation. The findings from Phase II Clinical Implementation Pilots provide strong evidence for integrating genomics into routine care, setting the stage for Phase III’s goals of scaling implementation across the population. By addressing barriers and fostering collaboration, the NPM programme aims to create a sustainable, equitable, and healthier Singapore.
