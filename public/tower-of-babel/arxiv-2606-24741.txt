All mixed identities are singular in groups with no algebraicity
Paolo Marimon, Michael Pinsker

Abstract
We show that if a group G G admits an action with no algebraicity then all of its mixed identities are singular. Previously, such groups were only known to be lawless by a theorem of Abért. Our result confirms, in particular, a conjecture of Bodirsky, Schneider, and Thom for a large class of oligomorphic permutation groups. It thereby not only subsumes numerous results from the literature in a simple uniform theorem, but also settles the question for prominent groups for which the conjecture was an open problem, such as the automorphism group of ( ℚ , < ) (\mathbb{Q};<) . Outside the oligomorphic context, it moreover applies to much-investigated groups, e.g. to Thompson’s groups F , T F,T , and V V , to Grigorchuk’s group, and to the homeomorphism groups of any manifold of dimension ≥ 1 \geq 1 . More generally, we prove that all mixed identities of a group G G are singular as long as G G has an action satisfying certain geometric conditions. This additionally covers the infinite-dimensional general and projective linear groups, recovering e.g. results of Bradford, Schneider, and Thom.

Introduction

One of the very core problems of algebra is understanding the solutions to equations over algebraic structures; the powerful tools of Galois theory, for example, are among the most fundamental and prominent achievements in this direction in the case of fields. Over a group
G
, equations can be written in the form
w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})=1
, where
w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
is a word with constants from
G
and variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}
.
On one hand, we wish to understand when
w
is solvable in
G
, that is, when we can find
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\in G
such that
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})=1
. On the other hand, we wonder
when
w
is a
mixed identity
of
G
(called a
law
if it has no constants): that is, for all
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\in G
,
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})=1
. There is an obvious tension between solving equations and finding mixed identities: if
w
is a mixed identity of
G
, then for any
\gamma\in G\setminus\{1\}
,
\gamma w
is not solvable in
G
.

A word is
singular
if, when “forgetting” its constants (by replacing them by
1
) and reducing, one obtains the identity: for example, this is the case for the word
x_{1}^{-1}\gamma x_{1}
, where
\gamma\in G
. We call non-singular words
regular
. Clearly, in the case of laws (without constants) only regular words are of interest. Mixed identities, on the other hand, do occur commonly with singular words even in infinite groups, but seem to be rare with regular words. This observation is underpinned by the fact that while there are singular words over groups
G
which have no solution in any overgroup
H\supseteq G

[
33
,
\lx@sectionsign
1]
, it is a major open problem whether this phenomenon might occur also for regular words
[
33

: given a regular word with constants from a group

, can we, in analogy to fields, always find a solution to it in an adequate extension?
The one-variable case of this question is the famous Kervaire-Laudenbach Conjecture, answered positively for finite groups
[
24
]
and for hyperlinear groups
[
45
]
.

In order to address the problem of equation solvability, it is of obvious importance to isolate general conditions under which regular word maps (Definition
2.1
) have large, ideally surjective, image; dually,
this calls for versatile techniques and

assumptions that allow us to derive the absence of mixed identities in a group. Mixed identities have been studied intensively over the past 50 years, starting with the work of Soviet mathematicians
[
4
,

,
27
]
, and with substantial progress in the last decade
[

,
9
,

29
,
9

Recently, Bodirsky, Schneider, and Thom

individuated a natural richness condition for a group under which they conjecture that all mixed identities are singular: a permutation group
G\curvearrowright\Omega

in its diagonal action

[

, all countable highly transitive groups, the automorphism group of the rational numbers with their order

, general linear groups of countably infinite vector spaces over finite fields
[

, and the automorphism groups of homogeneous structures in a finite relational language
[
36

. These groups are of special interest to model theory since they appear as automorphism groups of countable
\omega
-categorical structures. We know from
[
37
]
that oligomorphic permutation groups are lawless (i.e., with no non-trivial law), and in various important contexts we have general

under which word maps without constants are surjective

Let

Despite the abundance of examples, there are substantial challenges to Conjecture

In the present paper, we find one single, simple, and surprisingly general richness condition on a group action that implies that all mixed identities of the group are indeed singular, and prove this by methods that are uniform in the sense that they do not depend on an analysis of the possible behaviour of constants (which at this level of generality would be utopian). Theorem A

Preliminaries

2.1
Mixed identities

Definition 2.1
.

A
word with constants

w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
for the group
G
and with

variables is an element

\gamma_{n}\ x_{\iota_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\ \gamma_{n-1}\ x_{\iota_{n-1}}^{\epsilon_{n-1}}\ \dots\ \gamma_{1}\ x_{\iota_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\ \gamma_{0}

(1)

of
G*F_{r}
,
the free product of

and the free group
F_{r}
with

(which we also refer to as

), where
\iota_{1},\dots,\iota_{n}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, and
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
, and where
\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
are elements of

. For

, we say that a
\gamma_{j}
is a

if
\iota_{j-1}=\iota_{j}
and
\epsilon_{j-1}=-\epsilon_{j}
, i.e. the same variable appears in

to its left and to its right, and it is inverted precisely once. We
will assume throughout that

is

, by which we mean that critical constants do not belong to the centre

; obviously, every word is equivalent in

to a reduced word.

The word

determines the

, which is obtained by sending

to the image of

under the homomorphism

fixing

to

for each

. The word

is called a

. We say that

.

The

is the unique homomorphism

which sends each element of

and fixes

. As is common in the literature, we say that

Group actions

Throughout this paper,

Notation 2.3
.

For

,

, the pointwise stabiliser of

, on tuples. We extend this notation to tuples

Definition 2.4
.

For

, we endow

In Theorem

Definition 2.5
.

Let

.

Notation 2.6
.

For

,

. For a set

Definition 2.7
.

Let

. We define the operation of

as follows: for

Note that algebraic closure is a

.

We say that

It is well-known that if

Remark 2.9

If we defined the algebraic closure of infinite sets in the same way as we defined it on finite sets, we would obtain what is sometimes referred to as group-theoretic (or Galois) algebraic closure. The two notions might differ:

2.3
Pregeometries and algebraic closure

The context of the most general formulation of our result (Theorem
3.8
) will be so that algebraic closure satisfies similar axioms to those of linear span in a vector space. For this reason, we need to introduce some standard terminology from matroid theory
[
43
]
and model theory
[
17
]
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\Omega
be a set. A
closure operator
is a function
\cl:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
satisfying the following axioms:

(reflexivity)

A\subseteq\cl(A)
;

(transitivity)

\cl(\cl(A))=\cl(A)
;

(monotonicity)

if
A\subseteq B
, then
\cl(A)\subseteq\cl(B)
.

We say that a closure operator is
algebraic
if it is of finite character:

(finite character)

\cl(A)
is the union of all
\cl(C)
where
C
is a finite subset of
A
.

We say that an algebraic closure operator forms a
pregeometry
if it satisfies the following analogue of Steinitz’s exchange lemma:

(exchange)

if
a\in\cl(A\cup\{b\})\setminus\cl(A)
, then
b\in\cl(A\cup\{a\})
.

We say that
A\subseteq\Omega
is
closed
if
\cl(A)=A
.

Note that for
G\curvearrowright\Omega
, the operator
\acl
satisfies reflexivity, transitivity, and monotonicity; and we have defined it to have finite character. Furthermore, as long as some element of
\Omega
has an infinite orbit (which will be the case for all groups we consider),
\acl
is non-trivial in the sense that
\acl(\emptyset)\neq\Omega
.

In general, exchange may fail to be satisfied by
\acl
, and hence it does not always form a pregeometry. However,
is easy to see that
\acl
does satisfy exchange e.g. in the following cases: whenever
G\curvearrowright\Omega
has no algebraicity; and when
G
is
\mathrm{GL}(\kappa,\mathbb{F}_{q})
or
\mathrm{PGL}(\kappa,\mathbb{F}_{q})
, in its action on the corresponding
\kappa
-dimensional vector/projective space where
\kappa
is an infinite cardinal. In a pregeometry we can define notions of independent sets, basis, and dimension in the obvious way (see
[
49
, Appendix C]
for the claims below). For the reader not familiar with these notions in this abstract setting, it might be useful to keep the above examples in mind. We remark that in the case of no algebraicity, the notion of dimension degenerates to that of cardinality.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
(\Omega,\cl)
form a pregeometry. We call
A\subseteq\Omega
an
independent set
if for each
b\in A
we have
b\not\in\cl(A\setminus\{b\})
. We say that
A\subseteq\Omega
is a
generating set
if
\Omega=\cl(A)
. A
basis
is an independent generating set. It is easy to prove that every pregeometry has a basis and all bases in a pregeometry have the same cardinality. The
dimension
of
\Omega
is the cardinality of any/all of its bases.

The above notions can then be extended to arbitrary subsets of a pregeometry in the standard way. For this it will be helpful to define restrictions and relativisations.

Definition 2.12
.

For a pregeometry
(\Omega,\cl)
and

, we can define two new pregeometries:

•

the

(A,\cl^{A})
, where for

,
\cl^{A}(B)=\cl(B)\cap A
; and

•

the

, where for
B\subseteq\Omega
,
\cl_{A}(B)=\cl(B\cup A)
.

For
A\subseteq\Omega
, we write
\dim(A)
for the dimension of
(A,\cl^{A})
and
\dim(\Omega/A)
for the dimension of
(\Omega,\cl_{A})
. We call the latter the

of

in
\Omega
.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\Omega
be a set. Given a

\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}

from

over

\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}
to tuples and elements of

Fact 2.14
.

Let
(\Omega,\cl)
be a pregeometry. Let
C\subseteq\Omega
be closed, and, let

be the closure in
(\Omega,\cl)
of a basis for
(\Omega,\cl_{C})
. Then,
D\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}C
.

Proof.

Fix a basis
B
for
(\Omega,\cl_{C})
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists
x\in(D\cap C)\setminus\cl(\emptyset)
. There is a minimal

such that

. We must have

; for any

we then have have that

Definition 2.15
.

We say that a pregeometry
(\Omega,\cl)
is
modular
if for all closed
A,B\subseteq\Omega
,

\dim(A\cup B)+\dim(A\cap B)=\dim(A)+\dim(B)\;.

The operation above is cardinal addition since the dimension of a set can be infinite.

Fact 2.16

(Proposition 1.2.12 in
[
17
]
)
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

•

\cl
is modular;

•

for any closed
A,B\subseteq\Omega
we have
A\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{A\cap B}B
;

•

whenever
A,B\subseteq\Omega
and

, then there are
a\in\cl(A)
and

such that

.

Examples 2.17
.

The following are examples of modular pregeometries:

•

any pregeometry where

is
degenerate
: for each

we have

•

the pregeometry

where

is a vector space and

is linear span;

•

the pregeometry

where

is a projective space and

An example of a non-modular pregeometry is

Fact 2.19
.

Let
(\Omega,\cl)
be a modular pregeometry. Then, the intersection of finitely many closed sets of finite codimension has finite codimension.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show the fact for the intersection of two closed sets
A,B
of finite codimension. We know that

\dim(\Omega/A\cap B)=\dim(A/A\cap B)+\dim(\Omega/A)

[
49
, Lemma C.1.8]
(note these may be infinite cardinals). Since
A
has finite codimension,
\dim(\Omega/A)
is finite. By modularity, Fact
2.16
implies that
A
and
B
are independent over

; this easily implies
\dim(A/B)=\dim(A/A\cap B)

[
49
, Lemma C.1.10]
. Since
B
has finite codimension in
\Omega
,
\dim(A/B)
is finite. Hence,
\dim(\Omega/A\cap B)
is finite, as desired.
∎

The following notion, which notably makes sense also when

The Proof

3.1
Rough strategy and notation setup

Throughout this section we work with a reduced word
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
as in (
1
).

Given such word, assuming regularity we will want to find group elements

g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\in G
such that
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})\neq 1
. Since
G\curvearrowright\Omega
is a permutation group, this amounts to identifying some
a_{0}\in\Omega

3.2
The strategy in more detail, and the actual proof

The core idea of our proof is that for a reduced word
w
and initial condition
B
, we inductively build a sequence
\overline{a}
for
w
whose elements of different freeness rank are sufficiently independent from each other. If
w
is regular, this then yields
a_{0}\neq a_{n+1}
since their freeness ranks differ. Hence,
w
does not yield a mixed identity, and this is witnessed by group elements belonging to the basic open sets as given by the initial condition.

Naively, one would be tempted to establish this independence condition at every step, without looking forward in the future trajectory of the sequence: just pick the next element of the sequence to be independent from all previous elements of different freeness rank.
The main issue that arises is that sometimes later elements in our sequence may be entirely determined by previous elements. This is due to the presence of critical constants. To see this, consider the context of Figure
1
, and suppose that we have already built the sequence
(a_{0},\dots,a_{3},a_{3}^{\prime},a_{4}^{\prime})
. It may be that
\gamma_{3}
fixes
a_{3}^{\prime}
and so
a_{4}=a_{3}
and
a_{5}^{\prime}=\gamma_{4}\gamma_{2}a_{2}^{\prime}
. By our desired independence condition, we want
a_{5}^{\prime}
to still be independent from
a_{0}
. So, for this to be the case, when we constructed
a_{2}^{\prime}
, we must have already ensured that
\gamma_{4}\gamma_{2}a_{2}^{\prime}
is independent from
a_{0}
. This in itself can already be ensured by Neumann’s lemma. When more than one critical constant occurs, we need to deal with additional issues which require the higher Neumann property. For example, consider the case where we have built the sequence
(a_{0},\dots,a_{7})
, again in the context of Figure
1
. By construction,
(a_{1},\dots,a_{5})\equiv(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{5}^{\prime})
. We could have that
\gamma_{5}a_{5}=a_{6}\in\acl(a_{2},a_{5})
(we cannot ensure independence amongst elements of the same freeness rank since some singular words might be mixed identities). But then, this would mean that
a_{7}^{\prime}\in\acl(\gamma_{6}a_{5}^{\prime},\gamma_{6}a_{2})
. So in order to ensure that
a_{7}^{\prime}\neq a_{0}
, we need to also ensure that the algebraic closure of translates of elements of a given rank is still independent from elements of other ranks. The definition of higher Neumann is designed to ensure the satisfaction of independence conditions of this kind. In order to formally state the sophisticated independence conditions we need in our induction (which we shall call having a rank-clean sequence), below we define the notions of paths, foreseeable future, and foreseeable closures.

F(a_{\ell+1},\ell+1)\;=\;\mathfrak{P}(w,\ell+1)\cdot a_{\ell+1}\;=\;\mathfrak{P}(w,\ell+1)\cdot\gamma_{\ell}a_{\ell}^{\prime}\;\subseteq\;\mathfrak{P}(w,\ell)\cdot a^{\prime}_{\ell}\;=\;F(a_{\ell}^{\prime},\ell)\;\subseteq\;U^{\star}\;.

In this case, the first two equalities are by definition; the containment thereafter follows from the fact that any product of the form
\alpha\gamma_{\ell}
for
\alpha\in\mathfrak{P}(w,\ell+1)
will be contained in
\mathfrak{P}(w,\ell)
; the next equality is by definition; and the final containment was just proven above.

∎

Claim 1.3:

(a_{0},a_{1},a_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{\ell},a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1})
is a rank-clean sequence for
u
.

Proof of Claim 1.3.

Let
(P,Q)
be any partition of the freeness ranks occurring in the sequence. We may assume without loss of generality that
P
contains
\rk(a_{\ell}^{\prime})=x_{1}\cdot\rk(a_{\ell})=\rk(a_{\ell+1})
. Let
S
be as in Claim 1.2. Since by inductive hypothesis the sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{\ell-1},a_{\ell-1}^{\prime},a_{\ell})
is rank-clean,

FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S,\;\rk(x)\in P\})\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{B}FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S,\;\rk(x)\in Q\})\;.

However, since
\rk(a_{\ell}^{\prime})=\rk(a_{\ell+1})\in P
,

FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S,\;\rk(x)\in Q\})=FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S\cup\{a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1}\},\;\rk(x)\in Q\})\;,

and by Claim 1.2

FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S,\;\rk(x)\in P\})=FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S\cup\{a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1}\},\;\rk(x)\in P\})\;.

Hence, we can deduce that

FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S\cup\{a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1}\},\;\rk(x)\in P\})\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{B}FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S\cup\{a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1}\},\;\rk(x)\in Q\})\;,

as required by the definition of rank-cleanness. For the same reason we also have that for
R\in\{P,Q\}
,

FC(\{x\;|\;x\in S\cup\{a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1}\},\;\rk(x)\in R\})\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B\;.

This concludes the argument for Claim 1.3.
∎

We have thus finished the proof of our induction for Case 1.

We now consider
Case 2
. Since
(\Omega,\acl)
forms a pregeometry, we know that for
a,b\in\Omega
,
a\in\acl(b)
if and only if
b\in\acl(a)
. Thus, from the hypothesis of Case 2, we can deduce that

a_{\ell}\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B\cup\{a_{j}\;|\;j<l,\;\iota_{j}=1\}\;.

We will then be able to use higher Neumann to extend the sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{\ell-1},a_{\ell-1}^{\prime},a_{\ell})
to a rank-free sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{\ell},a_{\ell}^{\prime},a_{\ell+1})
for
u
.

Firstly, set
a_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}:=\delta a_{\ell}
for an arbitrary witness
\delta
of

(\bar{b}_{1},a_{m_{1}},\dots,a_{m_{s}})\equiv(\bar{b}_{1}^{\prime},a^{\prime}_{m_{1}},\dots,a^{\prime}_{m_{s}})\;.

(16)

Now, by the above independence condition, we have that
a_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}(\overline{b}^{\prime}_{1},a^{\prime}_{m_{1}},\dots,a^{\prime}_{m_{s}})
. In particular, by higher Neumann, we can find

a_{\ell}^{\prime}\equiv_{(\overline{b}^{\prime}_{1},a^{\prime}_{m_{1}},\dots,a^{\prime}_{m_{s}})}a_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}

(17)

such that

F(a_{\ell}^{\prime},\ell)\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B\cup FC(\{a_{j}^{\epsilon}\;|\;a_{j}^{\epsilon}\in(a_{0},\dots,a_{\ell})\})\;.

(18)

From (
17
),

(\bar{b}_{1},a_{m_{1}},\dots,a_{m_{s}},a_{\ell})\equiv(\bar{b}_{1}^{\prime},a^{\prime}_{m_{1}},\dots,a^{\prime}_{m_{s}},a^{\prime}_{\ell})\;.

Then, from (
18
) and Lemma
2.21
we can prove all conditions showing that
(a_{0},\dots,a_{\ell+1})
is rank-clean for
u
and with initial condition
B
.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
G\curvearrowright\Omega
. Let
(\Omega,\acl)
form an infinite-dimensional higher Neumann modular pregeometry. Let
w
be a
regular word with constants. Then, the set

\{(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})\in G^{r}\;|\;w(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})=1\}

(19)

is nowhere dense. In particular, all mixed identities of
G
are singular.

Proof.

We shall write
\mathfrak{S}
for the set in (
19
). Let

\mathcal{U}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}G_{(\overline{b}_{i},\overline{b^{\prime}_{i}})}\;.

be an arbitrary non-empty basic open set of
G_{r}
. We set
B:=((\overline{b}_{1},\overline{b}^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(\overline{b}_{r},\overline{b}_{r}^{\prime}))
. We need to prove that we can find
\overline{g}:=(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})\in\mathcal{U}
and a neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
\overline{g}
such that
\mathcal{V}\cap\mathfrak{S}=\emptyset
. By Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
, there is a rank-clean sequence for
w
with initial condition
B
. Since
w
is regular,
\rk(a_{n+1})\neq\rk(a_{0})
, and so by rank-cleanness,
a_{n+1}\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}a_{0}
, and in particular
a_{n+1}\neq a_{0}
.
Take
(g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{r}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{U}
yielding
w(g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{r}^{\prime})(a_{0})=a_{n+1}
and let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
be the open neighbourhood of
G^{r}
of tuples that agree with
(g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{r}^{\prime})
on
B\cup\bigcup_{i\leq n+1}a_{i}
. By construction,
\mathcal{V}\cap\mathfrak{S}=\emptyset
, concluding
the proof.
∎

4
Higher Neumann modular pregeometries: superspaciousness

In this section, we show that groups
G\curvearrowright\Omega
with no algebraicity and the general linear and projective groups of infinite dimension form infinite dimensional higher Neumann modular pregeometries. More generally, we isolate the property of superspaciousness which can easily be verified in these cases and which implies higher Neumann.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G\curvearrowright\Omega
. Suppose that
(\Omega,\acl)
forms a pregeometry. We say that
G\curvearrowright\Omega
is
superspacious
if for any finite-dimensional set
B\subseteq\Omega
and
a\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B
, the
G_{B}
-orbit
X
of
a
intersects every closed subset
Y\subseteq\Omega
of finite codimension.

Fact 4.2
.

The following are examples of superspacious group actions
G\curvearrowright\Omega
such that
(\Omega,\acl)
are modular pregeometries:

1.

any
G\curvearrowright\Omega
where
\acl
is locally finite and
(\Omega,\acl)
is an infinite-dimensional degenerate pregeometry. This includes all group actions with no algebraicity;

2.

any
G\curvearrowright\Omega
such that
(\Omega,\acl)
forms an infinite-dimensional modular pregeometry and which is
homogeneous
in the following sense: for any closed subset
B\subseteq\Omega
and
a,a^{\prime}\in\Omega\setminus B
we have
a\equiv_{B}a^{\prime}
. This includes
\GL(\kappa,K)
and
\PGL(\kappa,K)
when
\kappa
is an infinite cardinal and
K
is a field in their action on their corresponding vector space and projective space.

Proof.

We begin by proving (1). Firstly, as noted earlier, if
\acl
is a degenerate pregeometry, then it is modular.
Take
B\subseteq\Omega
finite-dimensional,
a\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B
, and
X
be the
G_{B}
-orbit of
a
. By Neumann’s Lemma and local finiteness of
\acl
,

\dim(X/B)
is infinite. Let
Y
be a closed set of finite codimension. Then there exists a finite
F\subseteq\Omega
such that
\acl(Y\cup F)=\Omega
. Since
\dim(X)
is infinite, there is some
a\in X
not contained in
\acl(F)
. Since
\acl
is degenerate,
a\in\acl(Y\cup F)
now implies
a\in Y
, so
Y\cap X\neq\emptyset
as desired.

For (2):
given a closed finite dimensional set
B\subseteq\Omega
and
a\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B
, by homogeneity we have that the
G_{B}
-orbit
X
of
a
is all of
\Omega\setminus B
, and so clearly
X
intersects every subset of
\Omega
of finite codimension.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G\curvearrowright\Omega
. Suppose that
(\Omega,\acl)
forms a modular pregeometry and that
G\curvearrowright\Omega
is superspacious. Then,
(\Omega,\acl)
is higher Neumann.

Proof.

Let
B,C\in[\Omega]^{<\omega}
and
a\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}B
be as in the definition of higher Neumann and let
X
denote the
G_{B}
-orbit of
a
. Extend a basis for
\acl(C)
to a basis for
\Omega
and let
D
be the closure of the elements in this basis which are not in
\acl(C)
. By construction,
D
has finite codimension, and by Fact
2.14
we have
D\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}C
. By modularity, the intersection of finitely many sets of finite codimension has finite codimension (Fact
2.19
). Hence,

E:=\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Sigma}\gamma^{-1}D

has finite codimension. By superspaciousness,
E\cap X\neq\emptyset
. Then, picking any
a^{\prime}\in E\cap X
,

\Sigma\cdot a^{\prime}\subseteq D\;,

and since
D
is algebraically closed,

\acl(\Sigma\cdot a^{\prime})\subseteq D\;.

Thus,

\Sigma\cdot a^{\prime}\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}C\;,

as desired.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
G
be any of the following groups:

•

a group
G\curvearrowright\Omega
such that
\acl
is locally finite and
(\Omega,\acl)
is an infinite-dimensional degenerate pregeometry.
Any action
G\curvearrowright\Omega
with no algebraicity is of this kind;

•

\GL(\kappa,K)
or
\PGL(\kappa,K)
where
\kappa
is an infinite cardinal and
K
is a field.

Let
w
be a
regular word with constants. Then, the set

\{(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})\in G^{r}\;|\;w(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})=1\}

is nowhere dense.
In particular, all mixed identities of
G
are singular.

Proof.

The corollary is just a consequence of putting together Fact
4.2
, Lemma
4.3
, and Theorem
3.8
.
∎
