Comparison of robot-assisted surgical and transcatheter closure of secundum atrial septal defect: A single-center retrospective study
Authors: İsa Can, Ersin Kadiroğulları, Zinar Apaydın, Timuçin Aksu, Zihni Mert Duman, Barış Timur, Batuhan Yazıcı, Musa Kaya, Ali Rıza Demir, Taner İyigün
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial septal defect (ASD) closure can be performed using either transcatheter or robot-assisted surgical approaches. This study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes, procedural characteristics, and complications associated with these two techniques in a large patient cohort.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 635 patients who underwent ASD closure via either robot-assisted surgery (Group I, n=290) or transcatheter closure (Group II, n=345). Preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative data, including procedural times, complications, and follow-up outcomes, were evaluated. In addition, patients treated after 2019 were analyzed as a subgroup and categorized as Group Is (robot-assisted surgery, n=101) and Group IIs (transcatheter closure, n=105).ResultsPatients in Group I had significantly longer procedural times and hospital stays than those in Group II (p<0.001). Postoperative tricuspid regurgitation was more frequent in the transcatheter group (p=0.003). Device migration occurred in 3.2% of transcatheter cases. Overall neurological events occurred in 0.7% of Group I patients and 2.6% of Group II patients (p=0.06). No significant difference in postoperative arrhythmia rates was observed between the groups. In the subgroup analysis of patients treated after 2019, the mean hospital stay was 2.36±0.5 days in Group Is and 2.21±0.6 days in Group IIs (p=0.07). Ventilation time was 3.6±1.4 hours versus 3.1±1.9 hours (p=0.05), and intensive care unit stay was 7.8±2.9 hours versus 7.2±3.0 hours (p=0.20), respectively.ConclusionBoth transcatheter and robot-assisted surgical ASD closure are safe and effective when patients are appropriately selected. Robot-assisted surgery offers the additional advantage of concomitant tricuspid valve annuloplasty and demonstrates improved outcomes with increasing surgical experience. Larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to further validate these findings.

Secundum-type atrial septal defect (ASD) is one of the most common congenital heart diseases encountered in adults. If left untreated, it can lead to serious complications, including embolic stroke and right heart failure. ASD closure can be performed either through transcatheter device implantation or surgical intervention.[1] The transcatheter approach is generally preferred because of its minimally invasive nature and shorter hospital stay. However, surgical intervention may be required in cases involving large defects or inadequate septal rims.[2] Robot-assisted surgical techniques offer the benefits of minimally invasive surgery while enabling the simultaneous management of associated cardiac pathologies.[3] Owing to these advantages, robotic surgery has emerged as an alternative to conventional surgical approaches for the treatment of ASD.[4] Nevertheless, comparative data regarding the long-term outcomes and complication profiles of these two treatment modalities remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes, procedural complications, and long-term follow-up results of robot-assisted surgical versus transcatheter closure of secundum-type ASD in a single-center cohort.

Methods

This study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of University of Health Sciences Türkiye, Mehmet Akif Ersoy Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery Training and Research Hospital (IRB no: 2024.01-07; date: April 2, 2024) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 653 patients who underwent secundum ASD closure at University of Health Sciences Türkiye, Mehmet Akif Ersoy Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery Training and Research Hospital between January 2011 and March 2024 were retrospectively screened. After the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 635 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were categorized into two groups according to the treatment modality: robotic surgery (Group I) and transcatheter closure (Group II).

A similar study was previously conducted at our institution in 2019.[4] Patients who underwent secundum ASD closure after that study were analyzed separately in a subgroup analysis. This subgroup comprised 206 patients, including 101 who underwent robotic surgery and 105 who underwent transcatheter closure. These patients were designated as Group Is (robotic surgery) and Group IIs (transcatheter closure), respectively (Figure 1).

Procedure-specific eligibility criteria were applied to each treatment modality. In the transcatheter group, patients were excluded if follow-up data were unavailable or if they had a history of prior cardiac surgery. In the robotic surgery group, patients were considered unsuitable if they required concomitant cardiac surgical procedures or had undergone previous cardiac surgery. Patients with non-secundum ASDs were excluded from both groups. Missing data were handled using a complete-case analysis approach. In both the transcatheter and robotic cohorts, patients with incomplete baseline, intraoperative, or postoperative data required for the predefined study endpoints were excluded from the analysis. Overall, 13 patients in the transcatheter group and 5 patients in the robotic group were excluded because of missing data. The final study cohorts consisted exclusively of patients with complete datasets, and no data imputation methods were used.

According to preoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) findings, tricuspid regurgitation (TR) was considered absent when reported as trace, mild, or mild-to-moderate and present when reported as moderate, moderate-to-severe, or severe. In Group I, patients with preoperative TR underwent concomitant tricuspid valve annuloplasty (TVP).

All surgical and interventional procedures were performed by physicians with at least five years of experience in robotic or transcatheter ASD closure. Demographic and clinical data were obtained from hospital archives and electronic medical records. TTE was performed after the procedure, at discharge, and during follow-up in all patients.

Right heart catheterization and pulmonary vascular resistance measurements were not routinely performed in all patients. Invasive hemodynamic assessment was reserved for selected cases with suspected advanced pulmonary hypertension, borderline operability, or discrepancies between clinical findings and transthoracic echocardiographic parameters. In most patients, treatment decisions were based on a comprehensive transthoracic echocardiographic evaluation, including the Qp/Qs ratio, defect size, right heart chamber dimensions, and estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure, in accordance with current guideline recommendations.

Perioperative and postoperative outcomes were evaluated in terms of cardiopulmonary complications, conversion to open surgery, device migration or embolization, mortality, and reintervention. Mortality was defined as any death occurring within 30 days of the procedure or during follow-up. Postprocedural residual shunts measuring <2 mm were classified as residual shunting.

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF) was defined as a newly diagnosed arrhythmia occurring after the procedure and persisting for more than 24 hours. Reintervention was defined as any repeat procedure performed more than 24 hours after the initial intervention because of a significant residual shunt or device migration. Neurological complications were defined as newly developed transient ischemic attacks or strokes occurring after the procedure.

Procedure-related complications in the robotic surgery group included conversion to mini-thoracotomy or sternotomy. In the transcatheter group, device migration resulting in procedure termination or conversion to surgery was considered a procedure-related complication.

Transcatheter ASD Closure Procedure

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. The size and location of the ASD were assessed using a Vivid 7 ultrasound system (GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway). Following systemic heparinization, the left atrium was accessed through the femoral vein using a multipurpose catheter and guidewire. An appropriately sized septal occluder device (AMPLATZER Septal Occluder; St. Jude Medical [now Abbott], St. Paul, MN) was advanced into the left atrium. The left and right atrial discs were then sequentially deployed to achieve optimal device positioning. Residual shunting was assessed using TEE, after which the procedure was completed. Hemostasis was achieved at the femoral access site. All patients received acetylsalicylic acid (100 mg/day) and clopidogrel (75 mg/day), which were initiated before the procedure and continued for 6 months thereafter.

Robot-assisted Surgical ASD Closure Procedure

Robot-assisted surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia using the Da Vinci SI system (Intuitive Surgical, Inc., Sunnyvale, CA). Venous cannulation was achieved percutaneously through the right internal jugular vein and right femoral vein. Arterial cannulation was established via the right femoral artery to initiate cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Following docking of the robotic system, the right hemithorax was entered, and the pericardium was opened to expose the heart. The aorta was cross-clamped using a Chitwood clamp inserted through the second intercostal space. Cardiac arrest was achieved with cardioplegia. The superior and inferior vena cavae were subsequently occluded. After right atriotomy, the morphology of the ASD was evaluated, and the defect was closed either primarily with sutures or using a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch. Concomitant TVP was performed in patients with moderate-to-severe TR. The right atrium was then closed. TEE was used to assess both ASD closure and tricuspid valve repair. After discontinuation of CPB, the pericardium was closed, and chest drains were placed in the right thoracic cavity and mediastinum. The skin incisions were subsequently closed, marking the completion of the procedure. Postoperatively, all patients received acetylsalicylic acid (100 mg/day) for 12 months.

Patient Follow-up

All patients were monitored postoperatively in either the cardiovascular surgery intensive care unit (ICU) or the cardiology ICU. After achieving hemodynamic stability, patients were transferred to the general ward. Patients who underwent TVP received warfarin therapy for 3 months.

Within the first 24 hours after the procedure, TTE was performed to assess device position, residual shunting, and potential complications. In patients who received larger occlusion devices, a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was also obtained. Follow-up evaluations were scheduled at 1 week, 6 months, and 1 year after the procedure and subsequently every 1-2 years. These evaluations included physical examination, ECG, and TTE assessment. In the transcatheter group, aspirin therapy and endocarditis prophylaxis were discontinued at the 6-month follow-up visit in patients without residual shunting.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the chi-square test. For continuous variables, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 635 patients were included in the analysis. The proportion of female patients was 57.9% in Group I and 70.7% in Group II (p<0.001). The mean age was significantly higher in Group II (39.6±12.6 years) than in Group I (32.9±12.9 years) (p<0.001). The mean defect diameter was significantly larger in Group I (24.7±6.8 mm) than in Group II (16.0±5.4 mm) (p<0.001). Preoperative TR was present in 37.9% of patients in Group I and 27.0% of those in Group II (p=0.003). Preoperative AF was more common in Group II (p=0.027), whereas chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was more prevalent in Group I (p=0.025) (Table 1).

Procedure duration was significantly longer in Group I (165.3±40.9 minutes) than in Group II (81.9±26.2 minutes) (p<0.001). TVP was performed in 103 patients in Group I. Overall complications were significantly more frequent in Group II (p=0.003). Device migration and procedural failure were observed in 3.2% of patients who underwent transcatheter closure. Procedure-related complications occurred in 2.8% of patients in Group I and 3.2% of patients in Group II, with no significant difference in procedural conversion rates between the groups (p=0.75) (Table 2).

The lengths of ICU stay and total hospital stay were significantly longer in Group I than in Group II (p<0.001). In-hospital mortality occurred in one patient in Group I. No residual shunts were detected in either group at 12 months after the procedure. Postoperative TR was significantly more frequent in Group II (12.5%) than in Group I (5.5%) (p=0.003) (Table 3).

In the subgroup analysis of patients treated after 2019, procedure duration remained significantly longer in Group Is than in Group IIs. No significant differences were observed between the groups in hospital length of stay, ventilation time, or ICU stay. Postoperative TR rates were also comparable between the groups (Table 4).

New-onset arrhythmia occurred in 4.1% of patients in Group I and 7.0% of patients in Group II, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p=0.13). Pulmonary complications were more frequent in Group I (p=0.002). No significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to neurological events or femoral access-site complications (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we compared the clinical outcomes of robot-assisted surgical and transcatheter closure of secundum ASDs in a large single-center cohort. The main findings were that transcatheter closure was associated with significantly shorter procedure times and hospital stays, whereas robotic surgery enabled concomitant tricuspid valve intervention and was associated with lower rates of postoperative TR. Although overall complication rates were higher in the transcatheter group, procedure-related complication rates were comparable between the two approaches. Importantly, the subgroup analysis demonstrated that, with increasing institutional experience after 2019, differences in postoperative recovery parameters between the two techniques became less pronounced. These findings underscore the importance of appropriate patient selection and highlight the influence of the institutional learning curve on outcomes following robotic ASD closure.

The risk of stroke and thromboembolic events may increase to as high as 4% in patients with untreated ASD.[5] In our study, the preoperative stroke rates were consistent with those reported in the literature. During postoperative follow-up, neurological complications occurred in 0.7% of patients in the robotic surgery group and 2.6% of patients in the transcatheter group. The requirement for CPB, together with longer operative times and the complexity of postoperative management, contributes to prolonged hospitalization following surgical procedures. Consistent with this observation, hospital stay was significantly longer among patients who underwent robotic surgery than among those treated using the transcatheter approach. This finding is in agreement with previous studies conducted at our institution.[6] In a large comparative series by Krumsdorf et al.,[7] patients who underwent transcatheter closure had significantly shorter hospital stays than those who underwent surgical treatment (p<0.001). Our findings support these observations. The longer procedural and hospitalization times associated with robotic surgery have been attributed primarily to the frequent need for concomitant procedures addressing associated cardiac pathologies. However, increasing surgical team experience has led to reductions in procedure duration and, consequently, shorter hospital stays. In our subgroup analysis of patients treated after 2019, hospital stay was reduced, and no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups. These findings suggest that robotic ASD closure may be a feasible alternative to transcatheter closure in experienced cardiac surgery centers.

In patients with secundum ASD, TR is often functional and related to chronic right ventricular volume overload. Previous studies have shown that TR, even when advanced, may improve after ASD closure as a result of reverse right heart remodeling.[8] Therefore, concomitant tricuspid valve intervention is not universally required.

Treatment decisions in our study were made following a comprehensive evaluation by a multidisciplinary heart team, taking into account TR severity, right heart dimensions, symptom status, and the overall clinical context. In selected patients, transcatheter closure was considered an appropriate treatment option based on this integrated assessment. In a study by Xiao et al.,[9] postoperative TR was significantly more frequent among patients treated using the transcatheter approach. Consistent with these findings, postoperative TR was more commonly observed in the transcatheter group in our study. However, subgroup analyses showed no statistically significant difference in postoperative TR between the groups. This observation may be explained by the recent tendency to preferentially treat patients with preoperative TR using robotic surgery, whereas the transcatheter approach is less commonly selected in this patient population. These findings emphasize the importance of appropriate patient selection in achieving favorable long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the ability to perform concomitant tricuspid valve repair during robotic surgery highlights the procedural versatility of this approach, enabling the simultaneous treatment of multiple cardiac pathologies.

Technical considerations play a critical role in determining the optimal method for ASD closure. Previous studies have reported that transcatheter closure can be performed successfully, with high success rates, in secundum ASDs measuring <38 mm and having adequate septal rims. In contrast, surgical repair is generally preferred when the defect is located adjacent to the atrioventricular valves, vena cavae, or coronary sinus.[10] Consistent with the reported success rates of 96-98% in the literature, the success rate of transcatheter closure in our cohort was 96.8%.[10, 11] Despite its advantages, the transcatheter approach has several limitations. Large defect size, inadequate septal rims, or suboptimal device positioning may result in procedural failure and serious complications requiring urgent intervention, including device embolization, cardiac rupture, or cardiac perforation.[12] In our cohort, transcatheter closure failed in 11 patients, and 3 of these patients required urgent surgical intervention because of device migration. Similarly, Du et al.[10] reported a 4.3% failure rate for transcatheter ASD closure, most commonly associated with large defects and rim deficiency. The failure rate observed in our study was comparable at 3.2%.

Long-term follow-up revealed mortality in only one surgical patient, who experienced sudden cardiac arrest postoperatively without intraoperative complications. Although robotic surgery is less invasive than conventional surgical approaches, factors such as CPB, general anesthesia, and wound-related complications should be considered as potential contributors to mortality risk.[10] Previous studies have reported higher early mortality associated with surgical procedures; however, most late deaths have not been directly attributed to ASD closure.[13]

Postoperative complications were rare in both groups but differed in nature. Surgical closure was most commonly associated with postoperative bleeding and arrhythmias,[14] whereas femoral access-site complications, arrhythmias, and device embolization were more frequently observed after transcatheter closure.[15] In our study, device embolization occurred in 1.4% of patients. Femoral complications occurred at similar rates in both groups, with no significant difference. Furthermore, consistent with the findings of Siddiqui et al.[16] there was no significant difference in clinically relevant arrhythmias requiring treatment between the groups. All cases of AF were successfully managed within 24 hours using electrolyte correction and medical therapy. Notably, postoperative pulmonary complications were more frequent in Group I, which may be explained by the higher prevalence of COPD in this cohort. Patients with COPD have reduced pulmonary reserve, which predisposes them to delayed extubation and atelectasis. Therefore, preoperative COPD should be considered an important risk factor for postoperative pulmonary complications, which may prolong mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and overall hospitalization.

In a four-year prospective study by Hote et al.,[17] the mean aortic cross-clamp time was 68.4 minutes, CPB time was 121.6 minutes, and total procedure duration was 178.4 minutes in 52 patients. The same study reported an ICU stay of 22.1 hours and a hospital stay of 3.6 days.[17] In our study, among 290 patients undergoing robotic surgery, the mean aortic cross-clamp time was 45.3 minutes, CPB time was 90.7 minutes, and total procedure time was 165.3 minutes. ICU stay averaged 10.4 hours, and hospital stay was 3.05 days, which are shorter than previously reported values and reflect improved outcomes with increasing institutional experience.

There is a paucity of comparative studies evaluating robotic and transcatheter ASD closure; therefore, our study contributes additional evidence in this field and includes one of the larger patient cohorts reported to date. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. This study was a single-center, retrospective, non-randomized investigation, which limits generalizability. Additionally, outcomes such as return to normal daily activities, cosmetic satisfaction, and procedural cost were not assessed. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating these parameters are warranted.

In conclusion, appropriate patient selection directly influences treatment success and complication rates. Transcatheter closure achieves high success rates in anatomically suitable patients, whereas robotic surgery represents a safe and effective alternative, particularly in cases with concomitant cardiac pathologies such as TR. With increasing surgical experience, marked improvements in procedural time and hospital stay have been observed in robotic surgery. This large single-center experience adds to the existing literature and underscores the need for future multicenter, prospective studies.
