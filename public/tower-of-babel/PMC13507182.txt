Hybrid RSM–SF-AHP–fuzzy MARCOS approach for multi-response optimization of WAAM-fabricated Ni–SS bimetallics in face milling
Authors: S. P. Sundar Singh Sivam, Stalin Kesavan, A. Johnson Santhosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study develops Wire Arc Additively Manufactured (WAAM) Nickel–Stainless Steel bimetallic samples with silicon enhancement and investigates their face-milling machinability using a hybrid multi-criteria optimization framework. Seventeen machining trials were designed and conducted using a Response Surface Methodology (RSM)-based Central Composite Design (CCD) to evaluate the effects of cutting speed (8000–9500 rpm), feed rate (0.1–0.25 mm/tooth), depth of cut (0.5–1.5 mm), and tool flute count (two and four flutes) on surface roughness (Ra), material removal rate (MRR), power consumption (Pc), and cutting force (CF). A three-stage hybrid methodology was implemented, in which RSM modelled machinability behaviour and identified significant process parameters, Spherical Fuzzy AHP (SF-AHP) assigned criteria weights under uncertainty, and Fuzzy MARCOS ranked the machining alternatives. Run 1 (9500 rpm, 0.1 mm/tooth, 1.5 mm, four flutes) was identified as the optimal setting, exhibiting lower surface roughness (~ 0.6 μm) and higher MRR (~ 35.6 mm³/min), whereas Run 6 (8750 rpm, 0.175 mm/tooth, 1 mm, two flutes) showed comparatively poorer performance with higher Ra (~ 0.8 μm), reduced MRR (~ 34 mm³/min), and less favorable power consumption and cutting force, indicating weaker overall machinability. Cutting speed and depth of cut strongly influenced Ra and MRR, while feed rate and flute configuration primarily affected Pc and CF. Validation through confirmation experiments, criteria-weight verification, and ranking consistency demonstrated strong agreement between predicted and measured performance. Although limited to a specific silicon-enhanced Ni–SS bimetallic system and finite experimental range, the proposed framework provides a practical and robust strategy for multi-response machinability optimization of WAAM components, offering industrial benefits such as improved surface finish, reduced power consumption, enhanced productivity, and better machining stability.

Introduction

The growing demand for high-performance materials in aerospace, automotive, marine, and energy-related industries has intensified the search for manufacturing routes capable of producing structurally reliable components with high material efficiency and reduced production cost1. In this context, wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) has emerged as a promising metal additive manufacturing technology due to its high deposition rate, low material wastage, and suitability for fabricating large-scale near-net-shape components2. Compared with powder-based additive manufacturing processes, WAAM offers significant advantages in terms of productivity, scalability, and economic feasibility, particularly for medium- to large-sized metallic parts3. Among the materials considered in the present study, nickel alloy and stainless steel (SS) are widely used parent materials in advanced engineering applications. Nickel alloys are extensively employed in aerospace engines, turbines, marine systems, and energy equipment because of their excellent high-temperature strength, oxidation resistance, and corrosion resistance, although they are associated with high cost and machining difficulty. Stainless steel is broadly used in structural, automotive, marine, and process industries owing to its good mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, formability, and comparatively lower cost; however, it is susceptible to work hardening and offers relatively lower high-temperature capability than nickel alloys. Owing to these machining characteristics, both materials are of considerable relevance for the present investigation.

A substantial proportion of existing WAAM research has focused on process development, heat transfer behavior, deposition stability, microstructural evolution, and bead geometry control4,5. Several studies have examined the influence of heat input, arc characteristics, and deposition strategy on the structural quality of WAAM-fabricated parts6. Other investigations have reported that deposition path design, process monitoring, and bead overlapping play an important role in improving dimensional consistency and reducing process-related defects7. Additional work has explored hybrid or modified WAAM approaches to enhance structural integrity, reduce distortion, and improve mechanical performance8. These contributions have significantly advanced the understanding of WAAM as a fabrication route. However, they have largely concentrated on deposition-stage optimization rather than the post-processing behavior of the fabricated components.

Post-processing is a critical stage in the industrial implementation of WAAM because near-net-shape deposition alone is rarely sufficient to achieve the dimensional precision, surface integrity, and functional tolerances required in engineering applications9. Machining is therefore often necessary to produce the final geometry and surface condition of WAAM-fabricated parts10. In practice, machinability directly affects tool wear, surface roughness, force generation, specific energy demand, and manufacturing efficiency11. Consequently, a material system that performs well during deposition may still encounter practical limitations if its post-processing machinability is poor. Despite this important industrial consideration, the machining behavior of WAAM-fabricated materials remains less thoroughly studied than their deposition characteristics.

This limitation becomes more significant when bimetallic materials are considered. Bimetallic structures are attractive because they enable the integration of complementary properties from two dissimilar alloys within a single component12. Such materials can be designed to combine corrosion resistance, high-temperature capability, toughness, strength, and wear resistance according to service requirements13. Nickel-based alloys and stainless steels are particularly relevant in this regard because both are widely used in harsh operating environments. Nickel-based alloys are valued for their superior thermal stability, oxidation resistance, and mechanical performance at elevated temperatures14. Stainless steels are widely applied because of their corrosion resistance, structural reliability, and broad industrial usability15. A Nickel–Stainless Steel bimetallic system therefore offers strong potential for multi-functional applications where both durability and environmental resistance are required.

However, the same material combination that provides attractive service properties may also create difficulties during machining. Nickel-based alloys are often associated with work hardening, high cutting resistance, and elevated thermal load during machining16. Stainless steels can also present machining challenges due to strain hardening, adhesion, and variability in chip formation under unfavorable cutting conditions17. When these two materials are integrated into a bimetallic structure, the resulting interface and compositional gradient may generate local variations in hardness, thermal conductivity, microstructure, and deformation behavior18. As a result, the cutting tool may experience changing contact conditions as it moves across different regions of the material. This can affect force generation, power consumption, material removal stability, and final surface quality. Therefore, the machinability of WAAM-fabricated Nickel–Stainless Steel bimetallics cannot be assumed from the behavior of the constituent metals alone and requires direct investigation.

The role of silicon addition further increases the complexity of this material system. Silicon is known to influence solidification behavior, microstructural evolution, and phase characteristics in metallic alloys19. In bimetallic WAAM structures, such compositional modification may alter local hardness distribution, interfacial response, and resistance to material removal during machining20. These effects are especially relevant in face milling, where repeated interrupted cutting and multi-point tool engagement can amplify the influence of heterogeneous material behavior21. Even though silicon-containing Ni–SS bimetallic systems may offer useful performance benefits, their face-milling response remains insufficiently understood. This knowledge gap restricts the ability to select suitable cutting parameters for reliable and efficient finishing operations.

Current literature also reveals an imbalance in research emphasis. Existing studies on WAAM materials predominantly address fabrication quality, metallurgical characterization, and mechanical behavior22. While these are essential aspects, fewer studies have focused on how WAAM-fabricated materials perform during secondary machining operations23. This shortage is even more evident for bimetallic WAAM materials, where material heterogeneity can substantially influence machinability24. Most available studies also concentrate on single-material deposits, meaning that the machining behavior of dissimilar WAAM bimetallics remains comparatively underexplored25. Accordingly, there is a clear need for studies that move beyond deposition optimization and directly examine the machining performance of complex WAAM-fabricated bimetallic systems.

Another important limitation in the literature concerns the analytical framework used to evaluate machining performance. Machinability is inherently multi-response in nature. In most machining environments, surface roughness, material removal rate, cutting force, and power consumption do not improve simultaneously under the same cutting condition26. A parameter combination that increases productivity may adversely affect surface quality or energy demand. Likewise, a condition that minimizes force may not maximize removal rate. This creates a multi-criteria decision problem in which trade-offs must be managed carefully. Conventional one-factor or single-response approaches are not sufficient for selecting an overall best machining condition when several performance measures must be considered together27. Therefore, a more robust methodology is required to analyze the interaction between cutting parameters and to support rational decision-making among competing responses.

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is well established as a useful statistical tool for modeling and analyzing manufacturing processes involving multiple interacting variables28. It enables the development of predictive equations, quantification of factor significance, and evaluation of nonlinear effects and interaction behavior29. In machining studies, RSM is particularly valuable because it provides a structured experimental design framework while also enabling response prediction within the selected factor space30. However, predictive modeling alone does not fully resolve the optimization problem when several responses must be balanced simultaneously. A statistical model may identify influential parameters and response trends, but it does not automatically determine the most suitable experimental alternative when the criteria are conflicting.

For this reason, fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making tools are increasingly relevant in advanced manufacturing analysis31. Fuzzy approaches are useful when decision problems involve uncertainty, ambiguity, and subjective weighting of performance criteria32. In practical machining optimization, it is often necessary to assign relative importance to responses such as surface roughness, productivity, cutting force, and power requirement33. Such weighting is difficult to perform using purely deterministic methods when expert judgment and uncertainty are involved. Spherical Fuzzy AHP (SF-AHP) is particularly suitable in this context because it extends classical pairwise comparison by providing greater flexibility in representing uncertainty and hesitation in human decision-making34. As a result, SF-AHP offers a stronger basis for criteria weighting in complex engineering optimization problems where multiple responses must be evaluated simultaneously35.

Despite these methodological advances, the integration of predictive statistical modeling with uncertainty-aware fuzzy weighting remains limited in the machinability analysis of WAAM-fabricated bimetallic materials36. Many studies apply statistical tools without incorporating a rigorous multi-criteria decision framework37. Other studies employ ranking approaches without sufficiently modeling the nonlinear response behavior of the machining process38. This separation reduces the effectiveness of the analysis, particularly in heterogeneous materials where both process-response prediction and multi-criteria prioritization are important. For WAAM-fabricated Ni–SS bimetallic systems with silicon addition, such an integrated framework is especially necessary because machining performance depends not only on cutting conditions but also on material heterogeneity and the balance among competing response variables.

Based on the foregoing discussion, three key research gaps can be identified. First, although WAAM has been extensively studied for single-metal deposits, the face-milling machinability of WAAM-fabricated Nickel–Stainless Steel bimetallic materials remains insufficiently investigated39. Second, the influence of silicon addition and bimetallic heterogeneity on key machining responses, including surface roughness, material removal rate, cutting force, and power consumption, has not been adequately clarified40. Third, previous studies have rarely combined response modeling and fuzzy multi-criteria weighting within a unified methodology for optimizing the machinability of such materials41. These limitations are important because insufficient understanding of machining behavior can reduce process reliability, increase finishing cost, and constrain the broader industrial adoption of WAAM-fabricated bimetallic components.

This study introduces a novel sequential framework integrating Response Surface Methodology (RSM), Spherical Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (SF-AHP), and Fuzzy MARCOS for the multi-response machinability optimization of silicon-enhanced WAAM-fabricated Ni–SS bimetallics. The integration of RSM, SF-AHP, and Fuzzy MARCOS was selected based on the specific needs of this multi-response machining problem. RSM offers statistical modeling of nonlinear response behavior and identifies significant effects of cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and flute number on responses such as surface roughness (Ra), material removal rate (MRR), power consumption (Pc), and cutting force (CF). However, RSM alone does not assign relative importance to conflicting responses. Therefore, SF-AHP was used to determine criteria weights under uncertainty, accounting for membership, non-membership, and hesitation in expert judgment. Fuzzy MARCOS was then applied to rank machining alternatives using utility degrees relative to ideal and anti-ideal solutions. Compared to fuzzy TOPSIS, which relies on geometric distance from ideal solutions, and VIKOR, which focuses on compromise ranking, Fuzzy MARCOS provides a more direct utility-based ranking structure. This hybrid framework is particularly suitable for the WAAM Ni–SS bimetallic machining problem, as it combines predictive modeling, uncertainty-aware criteria prioritization, and ranking under conflicting responses, thus advancing multi-response optimization in advanced manufacturing.

The robotic arm ensured uniform travel speed, accurate layer placement, and consistent heat distribution across the build surface. The system was synchronized with the control computer and encoder-based servo stage to achieve high positional accuracy and repeatability. This integration enabled precise alternating deposition of 316LSi and ERNiCrMo-3 layers while maintaining stable arc dynamics and minimizing thermal distortion. Figure 1(h) Fabricated Bimetallic Sample, The final WAAM-fabricated bimetallic structure exhibited dense multilayer stacking with uniform bead morphology and strong metallurgical bonding between nickel and stainless steel layers. Hybrid approach

The proposed hybrid approach integrates Response Surface Methodology (RSM), Spherical Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (SF-AHP), and Fuzzy MARCOS to achieve a comprehensive and reliable optimization of the machining process. RSM is used to capture the nonlinear relationships between machining parameters and machinability responses accurately, while SF-AHP addresses the uncertainty and hesitation in expert judgment to determine the relative importance of quality, productivity, and energy-related criteria. These weighted criteria are then applied within the Fuzzy MARCOS framework to rank machining alternatives by considering both beneficial and non-beneficial responses. The adoption of this integrated approach is driven by the limitations of single-method techniques in addressing the multi-response nature of machining problems. By sequentially linking prediction, criteria weighting, and decision-making, the proposed methodology offers a holistic optimization strategy that enhances process understanding, improves decision accuracy, and identifies machining conditions that simultaneously improve surface quality, reduce power consumption, and increase tool and process efficiency in WAAM-fabricated bimetallic components. The selection of SF-AHP and Fuzzy MARCOS is based on the multi-response nature of the problem. The relative importance of Ra, MRR, Pc, and CF involves expert judgment and uncertainty; hence, SF-AHP is employed due to its ability to represent membership, non-membership, and hesitation in the weighting process, offering more flexibility than traditional crisp weighting methods. Fuzzy MARCOS was chosen for alternative ranking because it evaluates each machining condition relative to ideal and anti-ideal reference solutions, providing a utility-based ranking under conflicting criteria. Although methods like TOPSIS and VIKOR are effective for multi-criteria decision-making, TOPSIS focuses on geometric distance from ideal solutions, while VIKOR emphasizes compromise ranking. In contrast, the SF-AHP–Fuzzy MARCOS combination provides a structured framework that separately addresses uncertain criteria weighting and robust alternative ranking, making it particularly suitable for optimizing the machinability of WAAM-fabricated Ni–SS bimetallic materials.

Experimental design

Figure 3(a) illustrates the application of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) in modelling the machinability behavior of WAAM-fabricated bimetallic samples by examining the influence of process parameters—cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and flute count—on key performance responses, including surface roughness (Ra), material removal rate (MRR), power consumption (Pc), and cutting force (CF). The procedure begins with the selection of process parameters, followed by the determination of the central composite design with an appropriate α value, identification of response variables, and execution of virtual experiments. Model validation is carried out through ANOVA, assessing adequacy via statistical indicators such as the F-test, lack-of-fit test, adjusted and predicted R² values, and signal-to-noise ratio (S/N > 4). Once validated, regression equations are developed, and predicted values are compared to experimental results. Two-dimensional and three-dimensional surface plots are then generated to visualize parameter interactions and response trends, facilitating optimization and experimental verification. The outputs from this stage feed into subsequent phases: RSM quantifies the effects of machining parameters on Ra, MRR, Pc, and CF; SF-AHP assigns relative weights to these responses based on their importance under uncertainty; and Fuzzy MARCOS uses these weighted criteria to rank machining alternatives and identify the optimal parameter combination. This sequential integration ensures that the final decision is backed by both rigorous statistical modelling and uncertainty-aware multi-criteria decision-making. The 17-run RSM-based experimental design was selected to strike a balance between statistical modelling capacity and experimental feasibility. Given the significant fabrication, machining, and measurement effort required for WAAM-fabricated bimetallic samples, a compact design was adopted to minimize experimental burden while still allowing the evaluation of dominant effects of cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and flute number on Ra, MRR, Pc, and CF. The design is sufficient for identifying main effects, curvature behavior, and selected two-factor interactions within the parameter range studied. The adequacy of the fitted models was verified through residual diagnostics, actual-versus-predicted plots, ANOVA, non-significant lack-of-fit results, high coefficient of determination values, and adequate precision values. However, this design does not fully capture all higher-order or three-factor interactions. Future research may use expanded factorial or sequential RSM designs to explore these effects more comprehensively.

Spherical fuzzy AHP –criteria weights

Spherical Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (SF-AHP) was selected for this study due to its ability to effectively handle uncertainty, hesitation, and subjective expert judgment in the weighting of machining responses. In machining optimization, experts must balance conflicting criteria such as surface roughness, material removal rate, power consumption, and cutting force, which often involve linguistic vagueness and ambiguity. While Type-1 Fuzzy AHP can represent such vagueness, it lacks the capacity to explicitly model hesitation. Interval-Valued Fuzzy AHP captures uncertainty through lower and upper bounds, but does not distinguish between support, opposition, and hesitation as flexibly as needed. Intuitionistic Fuzzy AHP accounts for membership and non-membership degrees but does not provide the level of flexibility required in this context. In contrast, SF-AHP extends this representation by allowing membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees to be expressed independently within a spherical fuzzy framework, offering greater adaptability. This flexibility is particularly useful when expert judgments are influenced by the uncertain behavior of machining processes, heterogeneous material responses, and trade-offs between quality, productivity, energy demand, and cutting stability. Consequently, SF-AHP was adopted to provide more realistic, uncertainty-aware criteria weights for the WAAM Ni–SS bimetallic machining optimization.

Figure 3(b) illustrates the SF-AHP process, where factors and their respective levels are determined, and initial comparison matrices are constructed. Geometric means are employed to calculate fuzzy weights for each comparison, and the consistency ratio (C.R.) is checked for validity (C.R. < 0.1). After calculating the fuzzy weights, they are integrated into a spherical fuzzy comparison matrix, providing a robust decision-making framework. This approach ensures that appropriate weight coefficients are assigned to various machining criteria based on their relative importance, thereby offering an accurate representation of process requirements in the optimization model.

Fuzzy MARCOS - ranking alternatives

Multi-response machinability optimization involves inherent conflicts among the selected responses. In this study, Ra, Pc, and CF were considered cost-type criteria, while MRR was considered a benefit-type criterion. A machining condition that increases MRR may also increase Pc and CF due to higher chip load and tool engagement. Similarly, conditions that reduce Ra may not always provide the highest productivity. Therefore, the optimal solution cannot be selected based on a single response. The proposed hybrid framework resolves these conflicts by combining response prediction, uncertainty-based criteria weighting, and fuzzy utility-based ranking. RSM identifies the effect of machining parameters on each response, SF-AHP assigns relative weights to the responses, and Fuzzy MARCOS ranks the alternatives by comparing their weighted performance with ideal and anti-ideal solutions. Thus, the final optimum represents a compromise solution that balances surface quality, productivity, energy demand, and cutting stability rather than optimizing one response at the expense of others.

Figure 3 (c) Fuzzy MARCOS (Multi-criteria Analysis and Ranking of Alternatives): The final stage evaluates alternative machining parameters with respect to the process requirements. The aggregated decision and normalized matrix are calculated to establish the fuzzy weighted comparability sequence and Si values. This approach employs fuzzy exponentially weighted comparability to rank alternatives and assess the sensitivity of rankings based on varying inputs. The final evaluation generates fuzzy scores, providing a clear ranking of the machining alternatives, which helps to identify the optimal combination of parameters based on the fuzzy analysis. This hybrid methodology integrates RSM for response modeling, Spherical Fuzzy AHP for assigning weights, and Fuzzy MARCOS for ranking alternatives, creating a comprehensive decision-making tool for optimizing machining parameters in WAAM-fabricated bimetallic materials.

Mechanical property and hardness

Figure 4a presents the as-fabricated Ni-alloy/stainless steel bimetallic structure containing silicon as a minor alloying addition. The multilayer deposition exhibits uniform bead geometry and consistent layer stacking along the build direction. No visible macroscopic defects such as delamination, interlayer cracking, or excessive porosity are observed, indicating stable process parameters and adequate metallurgical bonding between the dissimilar alloys. The continuous interface suggests effective dilution control and thermal compatibility between the Ni-based alloy and stainless steel substrate. The addition of silicon is expected to enhance melt pool fluidity and reduce solidification cracking susceptibility by promoting improved wetting behavior and controlled micro segregation. The absence of abrupt structural discontinuities implies successful compositional gradient formation across the interface, which is essential for mitigating thermal residual stresses in bimetallic systems.

The Vickers hardness profile measured at fourteen locations along the deposition height is shown in Fig. 4b. The hardness values range from approximately 202 HV to 220 HV, with an average value of ~ 211 HV. The relatively narrow variation (~ 18 HV) demonstrates a homogeneous microstructural evolution throughout the deposited region. The slight increase in hardness at specific locations (≈ 220 HV) can be attributed to localized grain refinement and possible solid-solution strengthening induced by silicon addition. Silicon is known to enhance lattice distortion and restrict dislocation mobility, contributing to moderate strengthening without promoting excessive brittleness. Conversely, regions exhibiting lower hardness (~ 202 HV) likely correspond to dilution zones dominated by austenitic stainless-steel phases. The absence of sharp hardness fluctuations across the interface indicates minimal formation of brittle intermetallic compounds. Such mechanical uniformity is particularly beneficial in multi-material systems, as abrupt hardness gradients often lead to stress concentration and premature failure. Therefore, the obtained hardness profile confirms effective metallurgical compatibility and controlled thermal cycling during fabrication.

The engineering stress–strain response (Fig. 4c) reveals a typical ductile deformation behavior characterized by a smooth elastic–plastic transition, pronounced strain hardening, an ultimate tensile strength of approximately 450 MPa, and total elongation of 32–35%. The substantial plasticity indicates effective load transfer across the metallurgical interface and confirms that the austenitic matrix remains structurally dominant throughout deformation. The absence of premature fracture or abrupt stress drops prior to failure suggests robust interfacial bonding between the Ni-alloy and stainless steel regions, demonstrating successful metallurgical integration without the formation of mechanically weak interlayers. The synergistic combination of moderate strength and high ductility is primarily governed by solid-solution strengthening from Ni and Si, together with microstructural refinement induced by rapid solidification during deposition. Silicon contributes to lattice distortion–based strengthening while suppressing brittle phase formation, thereby preserving ductility and promoting stable strain hardening.

Complementary hardness measurements (200–220 HV) further position the material within a favorable machinability regime. This moderate and uniform hardness distribution minimizes abrasive tool wear and prevents differential tool loading across the bimetallic interface, thereby reducing vibration and chatter during milling operations. The high ductility facilitates stable and continuous chip formation, although chip-breaking strategies may be necessary to control chip morphology. Additionally, silicon enhances surface integrity by mitigating built-up edge formation and improving solidification homogeneity. Collectively, the mechanical and hardness characteristics indicate that the fabricated bimetallic system achieves an optimal balance between structural performance and post-processing machinability, making it suitable for advanced manufacturing applications.

Microstructure

Microstructure: (a) Bimetallic Structure, (b) Top Layer, (c) Mid Layer, (d) Bottom Layer.

The Ni–SS interface plays a pivotal role in determining the machining response of the WAAM-fabricated bimetallic material. During face milling, the cutting edge does not engage with a homogeneous workpiece but instead interacts with Ni-rich regions, SS-rich regions, and transition zones created by compositional dilution and metallurgical bonding. These regions can differ in properties such as hardness, thermal conductivity, microstructure, and plastic deformation behavior, leading to varying contact conditions across the interface. These changes influence several aspects of the machining process, including force generation, power consumption, chip formation, material removal stability, and surface quality. The Ni-rich region may increase cutting resistance and heat generation, while the SS-rich region may contribute to adhesion and strain hardening. The combined effect of these mechanisms at the bimetallic interface can cause localized instability, particularly under interrupted multi-tooth engagement during face milling. Therefore, the observed variations in surface roughness (Ra), material removal rate (MRR), power consumption (Pc), and cutting force (CF) should be viewed not only as effects of cutting parameters but also as consequences of the heterogeneous tool–workpiece interaction across the Ni–SS interface. Figure 5 presents the macro- and microstructural characteristics of the fabricated Ni-alloy/stainless steel bimetallic structure along the build direction. The macroscopic view (Fig. 5a) shows a well-defined multilayer architecture with consistent bead geometry, free from visible macrocracks, delamination, or distortion, suggesting stable melt pool behavior, controlled dilution, and effective thermal management during deposition. Microstructural examination reveals a gradient evolution influenced by thermal conditions: the bottom region (Fig. 5d) exhibits coarser, well-developed dendritic structures from substrate-driven directional solidification and thermal cycling; the mid-layer (Fig. 5c) displays a mixed cellular–dendritic morphology with interconnected grain boundary networks, typical of epitaxial growth under moderated cooling and thermal accumulation; while the top layer (Fig. 5b) shows finer, more homogeneous equiaxed grains due to enhanced convective heat dissipation and rapid solidification near the free surface. Uniformly dispersed secondary phases, particularly in the upper region, suggest effective silicon distribution, promoting solid-solution strengthening while suppressing coarse intermetallic formation. The absence of microcracks, voids, or segregation across all regions confirms strong metallurgical bonding and successful integration between the Ni-alloy and stainless steel constituents. The gradual transition from coarse dendritic structures at the bottom to refined equiaxed grains at the top correlates with the measured hardness profile, where finer grains correspond to slightly higher hardness and coarser regions to marginally lower hardness, though the overall variation is minimal. This microstructural stability, achieved through controlled thermal gradients and silicon-assisted phase regulation, supports the balanced mechanical performance, structural integrity, and suitability of the bimetallic system for load-bearing and high-temperature applications.

The experimental design utilizes a Response Surface Methodology (RSM)-based 17-run scheme to investigate the machinability of WAAM Nickel–Stainless Steel bimetallic samples, considering four factors: cutting speed (8000–9500 rpm), feed rate (0.10–0.25 mm/tooth), depth of cut (0.5–1.5 mm), and tool flute count (two or four flutes), as detailed in Table 1. This design records four responses—surface roughness (Ra), material removal rate (MRR), power consumption (Pc), and cutting force (CF)—providing a comprehensive dataset that captures low, medium, and high levels of the numerical factors and both tool configurations. The design enables second-order modeling of main effects and interactions, while minimizing the number of machining trials. These selected responses are crucial for a holistic evaluation of machinability: Ra reflects surface quality and finishing performance, MRR indicates productivity and material removal efficiency, Pc reveals energy demand and machining sustainability, and CF represents mechanical loading, machining stability, and potential tool–workpiece interaction. Together, Ra, MRR, Pc, and CF provide a balanced assessment of quality, productivity, energy consumption, and process stability in the face milling of WAAM-fabricated Ni–SS bimetallic materials.

Pareto front analysis

A Pareto front analysis was conducted using the experimental responses surface roughness (Ra), material removal rate (MRR), power consumption (Pc), and cutting force (CF) to evaluate multi-objective trade-offs and to verify whether the selected optimal condition lies on or near the Pareto frontier. In this analysis, Ra, Pc, and CF were treated as cost-type objectives to be minimized, while MRR was treated as a benefit-type objective to be maximized. Based on dominance criteria, an experimental run is considered Pareto-optimal if no other run simultaneously improves all objectives without deterioration in at least one response. The comparison between the best condition (Run 1: Ra = 0.6 μm, MRR = 35.6 mm³/min, Pc = 320 W, CF = 120 N) and the worst condition (Run 6: Ra = 0.8 μm, MRR = 34 mm³/min, Pc = 318 W, CF = 119 N) shows that Run 1 dominates Run 6 in terms of surface quality and productivity, while maintaining comparable energy and force requirements. As a result, Run 1 lies on the Pareto frontier, whereas Run 6 is Pareto-dominated and excluded from the optimal solution set. Scientifically, this outcome reflects the ability of the optimal cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and flute configuration to achieve a balanced compromise between surface integrity, material removal efficiency, and mechanical–thermal loading. The Pareto analysis therefore confirms that the selected optimal run represents a non-dominated, physically meaningful trade-off solution, reinforcing the validity of the RSM optimization and its consistency with the multi-criteria decision-making results, and demonstrating that the chosen operating condition is both statistically and practically optimal within the investigated parameter space.

Conclusion

This study optimized the machinability of WAAM-fabricated Nickel–Stainless Steel bimetallic samples with silicon enhancement using a hybrid RSM–Spherical Fuzzy AHP–Fuzzy MARCOS framework. Mechanical characterization confirmed uniform hardness values of 202–220 HV, strong metallurgical bonding, and ductile tensile properties, with an ultimate tensile strength of approximately 450 MPa and elongation of 32–35%, indicating good structural integrity and favorable machinability. Response Surface Methodology identified cutting speed and depth of cut as important factors influencing surface roughness and material removal rate, while feed rate and flute configuration significantly affected power consumption and cutting force. The optimal machining condition of 9500 rpm cutting speed, 0.1 mm/tooth feed rate, 1.5 mm depth of cut, and a four-flute tool produced low surface roughness, high productivity, and stable energy-force characteristics. Statistical validation through ANOVA, residual diagnostics, actual–predicted comparison, and confirmation experiments verified the adequacy and predictive capability of the developed models. The agreement between RSM optimization and fuzzy MCDM ranking confirmed the robustness of the proposed hybrid framework. From an industrial perspective, the optimized condition can support improved surface integrity, enhanced productivity, reduced power consumption, and better cutting stability in the post-processing of WAAM-fabricated multi-material components. These benefits are relevant to aerospace, marine, energy, automotive, and tooling industries, where Ni–SS bimetallic structures may be used for components requiring corrosion resistance, thermal stability, mechanical strength, and dimensional accuracy. However, the study is limited to the selected Ni–SS bimetallic material with silicon content, the investigated face-milling parameter range, and a 17-run RSM-based design, which may not fully capture all higher-order interactions. Direct tool wear progression, chip morphology, cutting temperature, and interface-specific machining behavior were also not examined in detail. Future work should include expanded experimental designs, tool wear and chip morphology analysis, SEM/EDS evaluation of worn tools, interface-level force measurement, thermal analysis, and sustainability indicators such as energy per unit material removed and carbon footprint.
