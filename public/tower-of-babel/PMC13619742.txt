Genomic Investigation of Population Structure in a Secretive, Semi‐Fossorial Snake (Colubridae: Tropidoclonion lineatum) Across the Great Plains

Taylor Probst, Cameron D Siler, Drew R Davis

Abstract

With advances in genomic technologies, it has become increasingly feasible to generate large numbers of loci to address population genetic and phylogeographic questions. Nevertheless, many widespread and relatively abundant species remain unsampled using molecular approaches. The lined snake (
Tropidoclonion lineatum
), a semi‐fossorial snake distributed across the Great Plains, is one such example. Despite the historical recognition of multiple subspecies based on noted variation in morphological characters, no formal molecular investigation of population structure has been conducted to date due to a paucity of vouchered genetic samples. Here, we present the first genomic assessment of range‐wide population structure in 
T. lineatum
 using genome‐wide single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) generated via triple‐enzyme restriction site‐associated DNA (3RAD) sequencing. Our results indicate largely continuous gene flow across the Great Plains, questioning the validity of the recognized subspecies. In contrast, populations at the northern range edge in South Dakota exhibit relative genetic homogeneity and modest differentiation from more southern populations, potentially reflecting recent isolation or increased genetic drift in peripheral habitats. Given the species' endangered status in South Dakota, these findings highlight the conservation importance of range‐edge populations while underscoring the need for denser, range‐wide sampling—particularly in putatively disjunct regions—to fully characterize genetic diversity in 
T. lineatum
.
Here we provide the first examination of population structure and genetic variation within a secretive semi‐fossorial snake (
Tropidoclonion lineatum
), widely distributed across the Great Plains. In our evaluation, we assess the validity of the four historically described subspecies and identify a new potentially isolated population. Our findings reveal widespread gene flow, with conservation implications for the state‐endangered isolated population.

Introduction

Fossoriality, a life history strategy defined by subterranean burrowing (Cyriac and Kodandaramaiah ), has evolved multiple times within squamate reptiles (lizards and snakes; Anelli et al. ; Bergmann and Morinaga ; Brandley et al. ; Greer ) and is thought to be the ancestral condition of snakes (Da Silva et al. ; Longrich et al. ; Martill et al. ). Among fossorial snakes, there exists a spectrum of specialization, ranging from highly derived forms such as the scolecophidians, characterized by microphthalmy, increased cranial ossification, and miniaturization (Da Silva et al. ; Kley ; Miralles et al. ), to lineages lacking obvious morphological modifications, such as Diadophis and Storeria (Willson and Dorcas ). The secretive nature of fossorial snakes complicates their detection (Durso et al. ; Matthias et al. ), thereby hindering studies of their natural history, population abundance, and evolution (Miralles et al. ; Turner ; Willson and Dorcas ). As a result, biases exist in herpetofaunal survey data and museum collections, resulting in many common and widespread fossorial snake species lacking basic assessments of population and phylogeographic structure, despite growing evidence that many fossorial squamates harbor unrecognized cryptic diversity (Fontanella et al. ; Graboski et al. ; Parham and Papenfuss ; Thomas and Hedges ). The lined snake (
Tropidoclonion lineatum
), a small, fossorial snake in a monotypic genus that is distributed across much of the Great Plains in the United States, is one such example (Stebbins ).

Lined snakes spend most of the time underground (Fogell ; Schultz and Caven ) and are proficient burrowers in loose, moist soils, where they forage for earthworms and other small invertebrates (Stebbins ). Although 
T. lineatum
 occurs primarily in grasslands, open woods, and flood plains below 1500 m (Fogell ; Stebbins ), records exist of individuals found above 2000 m, including one from Torrance County, New Mexico, at approximately 2810 m elevation (Hathcock et al. ). The species is distributed across much of the central United States from south‐central Texas to the northern limits of its range in southwestern Minnesota and South Dakota, where it is considered a species of special concern and state endangered, respectively (Minnesota Department of Natural Resources ; South Dakota Game, Fish, and Parks ). Despite its broad geographic range, relatively few tissue samples from 
T. lineatum
 are available in museum collections to our knowledge (e.g., no tissue samples are available in public institutions from Colorado populations), and the species has only been included in a small number of higher‐level molecular phylogenetic analyses (Alfaro and Arnold ; Deepak et al. ; McVay and Carstens ; Zaher et al. ). Consequently, intraspecific structure and population‐level genetic variation within the species remain poorly understood.

Despite a lack of molecular sampling, five subspecies of 
Tropidoclonion lineatum
 have been described based primarily on minor differences in caudal and ventral scale counts: T. l. lineatum (Hallowell ; Ramsey ), T. l. iowae (Call ), T. l. mertensi (Smith ), T. l. texanum (Ramsey ), and T. l. annectens (Ramsey ). Shortly after its description, T. l. iowae was synonymized with T. l. lineatum (Smith and Smith ; Stejneger ) due to a lack of diagnostic character differences. The remaining subspecies are not widely recognized in published literature (Nicholson ; Stebbins ), as the ranges proposed by Ramsey () and Smith and Smith () overlap extensively with broad areas of intergradation among the subspecies (Figure ). Yet, many reptiles and amphibians distributed across the Great Plains exhibit substantial population divergence and genetic structure (Finger et al. ; Fontanella et al. ; Waraniak et al. ), therefore similar patterns across the broad distribution of 
T. lineatum
 may be expected. Thus, the taxonomic validity of subspecies of 
T. lineatum
 warrants further investigation through modern molecular methods.

In addition to subspecies designations as hypotheses of geographic differentiation, populations at the periphery of a species' range may exhibit substantial genetic divergence as differential selective regimes reduce effective gene flow from core populations while promoting local adaptation (Angert et al. ; Bontrager et al. ; Sexton et al. ). The recent discovery of a seemingly disjunct lined snake population along the James River in South Dakota may represent one such population (Davis and Farkas ). Within South Dakota, the lined snake was thought to occur only in habitats along the Big Sioux River (Backlund ; Ballinger et al. ; Kiesow ), until recent surveys located a population along the James River in Hutchinson County (Davis and Farkas ; Kiesow and Davis ). This population along the James River occupies an area of only ca. 4000 ha and appears to be isolated from populations of lined snakes along the Big Sioux River, as no other lined snakes have been detected further south along the James River in Yankton County, nor have they been detected along the Vermillion River to the east. The lack of verifiable records between the James and Big Sioux rivers, combined with the limited dispersal ability of these small, fossorial snakes may suggest that this James River population is an isolated, potentially unique lineage of lined snakes.

In this study, we employ genome‐wide single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data generated using triple‐enzyme restriction site‐associated DNA (3RAD) sequencing to carry out a preliminary assessment of range‐wide population structure in the lined snake, estimate effective population size of the James River population, and assess gene flow with the James River population to the core population. Our results provide the first examination of population genomic structure in Tropidoclonion and we discuss implications of the findings for taxonomy and conservation status of this enigmatic, widespread, fossorial snake.

Materials and Methods

We obtained 36 tissue samples (liver, tail clips, and blood) for 
T. lineatum
, representing seven of the 12 states in which the species is found (Appendix ). Unfortunately, we were unable to locate any lined snake tissue samples from Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, Arkansas, or Colorado. Total genomic DNA was extracted in the Core Genomics Lab at the Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History (University of Oklahoma) using Qiagen DNAeasy Blood & Tissue Kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Extracts were quantified using a Qubit, and aliquots were sent to the Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF, Oklahoma City, OK) Sequencing Facility for genomic library preparation and genotyping using 3RAD (3‐enzyme Restriction site‐Associated DNA sequencing) sequencing methodologies (Bayona‐Vásquez et al. ). Samples were digested with NheI‐HF, EcoRI‐HF, and XbaI enzymes, size‐selected for approximately 150 bp, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus.

De‐multiplexing, adapter trimming, and read filtering were performed in iPyrad v0.9.105 (Eaton and Overcast ). Filtering followed the default iPyrad parameters, with zero mismatches allowed in the barcodes, applying a strict adapter filter (2), with reads requiring base calls to exceed a minimum quality score of 20. Individuals for which fewer than 250,000 raw reads were recovered due to sequencing failure were removed from subsequent analyses because insufficient sequencing coverage prevented the recovery of a sufficient number of meaningful loci during clustering and assembly. Reads were then assembled into de novo loci, with the clustering threshold set to 0.90 as determined to be appropriate for population genetics (Eaton and Overcast ). Although inappropriate clustering thresholds can bias genotype inference by splitting orthologs at high thresholds and clustering paralogs at low thresholds (Ilut et al. ; Mastretta‐Yanes et al. ), genetic diversity and differentiation are generally robust to moderate variation in this parameter (Gargiulo et al. ). To reduce these risks, sequencing depth was checked to ensure that depth was > 10 for a cluster, and that the hetero_est was between 0.001–0.05 (Mastretta‐Yanes et al. ), with the error rate ~0.001 to reflect the average error rate of Illumina reads (Stoler and Nekrutenko ) (Appendix ). For assembled loci to be included in the final assembly, we used default parameters specifying that the loci needed to be shared across a minimum of four samples with the max number of Ns (ambiguous/unknown base calls) not exceeding a proportion of 0.05.

To examine genetic structure and cluster assignment, we first carried out Bayesian model‐based clustering using STRUCTURE v2.3.4 (Pritchard et al. ) through iPyrad's API, testing values of K from 1 to 7 under an admixture model with correlated allele frequencies. We specified mincov = 0.9 (retaining SNPs shared across at least 90% of samples), minmap = 0.5 (requires 50% coverage in specified groups), and ran 10 replicates per K value with 100,000 MCMC iterations, discarding the first 50,000 as burn‐in. To visualize individual ancestry proportions, we generated bar plots of STRUCTURE results. The second method we used for examining genetic clusters was principal components analysis (PCA) through ipyrad's built‐in analysis tools. Like our STRUCTURE runs, we specified mincov = 0.9, minmap = 0.5, using k‐means clustering with k set to 2 clusters (reflecting the optimal numbers of clusters found in STRUCTURE analysis) for 1000 iterations. Results were then visualized through scatterplots.

To explore phylogenetic relationships within our dataset, we used three phylogeny‐based approaches: phylogenetic networks, maximum likelihood (ML) inference, and a multi‐species coalescent (MSC) inference. Because a single bifurcating tree cannot fully capture the genealogical relationships within a population (Leaché et al. ), phylogenetic networks are useful for visualizing reticulate patterns of evolution and identifying admixed samples between populations. (Blair and Ané ). Phylogenetic networks were constructed from the concatenated SNP dataset excluding non‐variable sites using the SplitsTree App v6.7.6 (Huson and Bryant ) with the NeighborNet algorithm (Bryant and Moulton ) and uncorrected P‐distances.

Several limitations can complicate phylogenetic inference from RADseq SNP datasets, including allelic dropout (Arnold et al. ), acquisition bias (Leaché et al. ; Leaché and Oaks ), and concatenation of loci (Edwards et al. ; Leaché and Oaks ). In particular, allelic dropout can have a significant impact on outgroup selection, as the number of orthologous loci shared with the ingroup decreases as phylogenetic distance increases (Arnold et al. ; Gautier et al. ). While an optimal outgroup would be 
Regina grahamii
 or another Thamnophiine snake (Alfaro and Arnold ), no such taxa were included within our dataset. As an alternative, we attempted to root the phylogeny using two species of Philippine snakes sequenced on the same plate, 
Cyclocorus lineatus
 and 
Calamaria gervaisii
. However, even under lenient filtering thresholds, too few orthologous loci were recovered, and the phylogenetic distances between the outgroups and the ingroup were too great (Huelsenbeck et al. ; Swofford et al. ) to permit confident rooting of the ingroup. Accordingly, we chose to visualize relationships using unrooted trees. In the absence of a root, evolutionary polarity cannot be assessed and reciprocal monophyly cannot be established in a strict rooted sense; nevertheless, unrooted trees still permit evaluation of whether particular groups of samples form well‐supported, distinct genetic clusters that would be consistent with monophyly under at least one possible rooting (Wilkinson et al. ).

We generated a Maximum Likelihood (ML) tree using RAxML v8.2.12 (Stamatakis ) under the GTR+Γ (GTRGAMMA) substitution model. To reduce the effect of missing data, we filtered the SNP matrix to retain only biallelic sites shared across 85% of samples through VCFtools v0.1.17 (Danecek et al. ), specifying ‐‐min‐alleles 2, ‐‐max‐alleles 2, and ‐‐max‐missing 0.85. We then performed 100 rapid bootstrap replicates, followed by 10 independent ML searches from random starting trees using rapid hill‐climbing. To visualize relationships among individuals using an MSC approach, we constructed an MSC tree with Tetrad v0.9.14 as implemented in iPyrad (Eaton and Overcast ), specifying nquartets = 1e6 to analyze all possible quartets, with 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess support. Tetrad uses the SVDquartets algorithm (Chifman and Kubatko ), which estimates quartet relationships from SNP data across all possible four‐taxon subsets and then assembles these quartet trees into a species tree.

To assess signatures of isolation by distance (Wright ), we used the ape v5.8.1 package (Paradis and Schliep ) in R (R Core Team ) to calculate raw (uncorrected) pairwise genetic distances between individuals from the filtered 85%, biallelic SNP dataset. These were then plotted against pairwise geographic distances as calculated using the geosphere v1.6.5 package (Hijmans et al. ). A Mantel test was used to assess correlation between genetic and geographic distances as implemented in the R package vegan v2.7.3 (Oksanen et al. ).

To estimate the contemporary effective population size (N
e) for the James River population in Hutchinson County, South Dakota, we used the linkage disequilibrium (LD) method implemented in NeEstimator v2.1 (Do et al. ) on the filtered 85%, biallelic SNP dataset (excluding sample TNHC 111755 due to high proportions of missing data). Because physical linkage between SNPs of close genomic proximity can bias N
e downwards (Waples et al. ), we performed an additional analysis of the 85%, biallelic SNP dataset, manually filtering to retain the first SNP of every locus (retaining 10,293 SNPs). We assumed random mating and examined minor allele frequency thresholds of P
crit = 0.05, 0.02, and 0.01 (unfiltered) to assess sensitivity to the inclusion of rare alleles (Waples and Do ). Confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using both the parametric and jackknife methods across loci, and final interpretations were based on the P
crit = 0.02 threshold. To compare heterozygosity of the James River population (n = 15) to the core population, we calculated observed heterozygosity using the HIERFSTAT v0.5‐11 package (Goudet ) on both the filtered 85%, biallelic SNP dataset and single‐SNP‐per‐locus dataset (as used for calculating N
e). To avoid confounding genetic signals from other samples at the range edge, our core population was restricted to samples within Kansas, Oklahoma, and Nebraska (n = 11). To determine the level of gene flow and identify potential population isolation in the James River population, we used the Python package scikit‐allel v1.3.13 (Miles et al. ) to calculate F‐statistics, implementing the Weir and Cockerham () estimator similarly on both datasets as specified above for N
e and H
o.

Results

Our sequencing efforts resulted in the recovery of 132,499 loci and 684,738 single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from 36 lined snake samples following filtering. Unfortunately, four samples failed to meet quality requirements and were thus removed from the dataset for downstream analyses (Appendix ). Of the 23,264 SNPs retained after filtering for STRUCTURE and PCA, 8483 were unlinked and used to infer population structure. STRUCTURE analyses identified an optimal clustering of K = 2 according to the Evanno ∆K method (Evanno et al. ). STRUCTURE analysis revealed weak population structure along a latitudinal gradient (Figures and ). Individuals from northern populations primarily cluster with genetic group 1, whereas southern individuals show a higher proportion of genetic assignment to group 2. The gradual change in allele frequency across the range suggests weak genetic differentiation rather than sharply defined population boundaries. Furthermore, this latitudinal genetic cline is particularly evident along PC1, which explains 11.6% of the total variation (Figure ). Northern samples have negative PC1 scores (left side of the plot), while samples further south progressively shift toward positive PC1 scores (right side), reflecting gradual genetic differentiation along the latitudinal gradient.

Filtering to retain biallelic SNPs shared across 85% of samples resulted in 58,091 SNPs in our final matrix. Phylogenetic network analysis of 
T. lineatum
 supports the latitudinal genetic cline inferred from our clustering analyses (Figure ), as most samples cluster to geographically proximate localities with intermediate individuals positioned in between. For example, samples from Nebraska (TNHC 110040–110042, FHSM 17470) are positioned between those of South Dakota (North, T. l. lineatum) and Kansas (South, T. l. annectens). One exception is sample TNHC 111755 (Hutchinson County, SD), which is recovered as a cluster to an Oklahoma sample. Given the high proportion of missing data in this individual (80.67% of the final concatenated loci dataset), this placement may be artifactual despite filtering thresholds. Consistent with this interpretation, TNHC 111755 also shows anomalous clustering in STRUCTURE, PCA, and ML, and MSC analyses.

Similar topologies are recovered in both maximum likelihood (ML) and multispecies coalescent (MSC) unrooted trees (Figure ); however, support for specific clusters differs between methods. Under ML, T. l. lineatum forms a well‐supported cluster (excluding TNHC 111755), whereas this grouping is not supported under MSC and is incompatible with monophyly under any rooting (Figure ). Conversely, T. l. annectens forms a well‐supported cluster in the MSC tree but is not recovered in ML. However, in all three methods (phylogenetic networks, ML, and MSC) T. l. mertensi is recovered consistently as a distinct, well‐supported cluster. In addition, specimens from the James River population of South Dakota group together in a distinct cluster with high support across all topologies (Figure ). Tests of isolation by distance reveal a strong positive correlation between pairwise genetic and geographic distance (R = 0.863, p = 0.001; Figure ).

The estimated N
e for the James River population was found to be 163.7 for the 85%, biallelic dataset not accounting for linkage disequilibrium, consistent across all minor allele frequency thresholds, with a parametric CI of 162.8–164.6. The jackknife CI however was much more variable, ranging from 22 to infinity, a common problem when working with small sample sizes (Mamoozadeh et al. ). Filtering the dataset to a single SNP per locus resulted in a larger estimate of N
e (426.5; parametric CI of 368.1–506.6, jackknife CI of 61 to infinity). Given the high uncertainty as evident by the jackknife confidence intervals, and that only 15 individuals were sampled, these results should be interpreted with caution. The observed heterozygosity (H
o) of the James River population and the core population were very similar, with the James River population (H
o = 0.077; 0.100 using one SNP per locus) having only slightly reduced H
o relative to the core (H
o = 0.087; 0.116 using one SNP per locus). The F
ST value between the James River population and all other samples was 0.097 (0.103 using one SNP per locus), indicating moderate differentiation very recent population isolation (Hartl and Clark ).

Discussion

Our combined analyses indicate that population genetic structure within 
T. lineatum
 may be better characterized as clinal rather than partitioned into multiple discrete units (Figures , , , ). Although sampling across the southern end of the range is sparse relative to the northern edge, the continuity of the distribution (Figure ), linear isolation by distance, and lack of genetic structure are consistent with weak genetic differentiation and substantial connectivity. Signatures of range wide genetic connectivity across central North America have been detected in other widespread species of amphibians and reptiles, such as North American ground snakes (genus Sonora; Cox and Chippindale ), North American narrow‐mouthed toads (genus Gastrophryne; Streicher et al. ), and prairie rattlesnakes (
Crotalus viridis
; Smith et al. ). Such patterns may reflect the relatively continuous landscapes of the North American Great Plains, which lack more significant topographic barriers and may facilitate dispersal and gene flow across large spatial scales (Cox and Chippindale ).

How subspecies are defined, their value in taxonomy, and their acceptance and use across fields of study have been heavily debated in the literature (Burbrink et al. ; Guedes et al. ). Nevertheless, the subspecies of 
T. lineatum
 described by Ramsey () and Smith () appear to follow the historical precedent of using variation across a suite of phenotypic traits—often clinal, non‐discrete, or partially overlapping—as a proxy for evolutionary divergence and to identify geographically structured populations within a species (Meikle ; Phillimore et al. ; Mayr ). During the period in which Ramsey and Smith described these subspecies (the 1950s–1960s), the use of the subspecies rank (as defined by the number of annual subspecies descriptions) reached its peak (Uetz and Stylianou ), followed by a substantial decline from the latter half of the 20th century into the 21st century. Because modern population genetic and phylogenetic methods were not available at the time, these subspecific designations were necessarily based primarily on relatively limited morphological characters and may have reflected misconstrued patterns of intraspecific variation. As such, our phylogenetic analyses bring the validity of at least two of the four subspecies into question, T. l. lineatum and T. l. annectens (Figure ), with support for both groups depending on the method of phylogenetic inference. Topological discordance can arise from violations of model assumptions, underlying evolutionary processes, and gene flow (Edwards et al. ; Leaché et al. ; Mirarab et al. ). For example, our concatenated ML analyses assume a single underlying topology across loci and therefore do not account for gene tree heterogeneity arising from processes such as incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) (Edwards et al. ; Leaché and Oaks ; Liu et al. ). In contrast, the MSC explicitly models gene tree discordance expected under ILS by estimating the species tree from a distribution of gene trees (Chifman and Kubatko ; Degnan and Rosenberg ; Jiang et al. ; Mirarab et al. ). Alternatively, discordance and weak support for relationships is an expected outcome of gene flow between populations (Leaché et al. , ), in this case specifically between the subspecies of T. l. annectens and T. l. lineatum as supported by our phylogenetic network (Figure ). Thus, mixed phylogenetic signals may reflect substantial genetic connectivity across the range. While T. l. mertensi is consistently recovered as a well‐supported cluster across all phylogenetic methods, substantial sampling gaps in western Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas limit our ability to draw strong inferences regarding its taxonomic status. Furthermore, T. l. texanum is represented by a single sample, further constraining interpretation. Because of these limitations, increased range‐wide sampling is needed in future studies, particularly targeting under sampled regions, with appropriate outgroup taxa incorporated for rooting phylogenetic analyses (i.e., 
Regina grahamii
 or other Thamnophiini species).

Given molecular evidence weakens support for the interpretation of the four recognized subspecies as independent, geographically structured populations, it seems pertinent to re‐examine the morphological characters used for their delimitation. In his 1953 treatment of Tropidoclonion, Ramsey's recognition of three distinct subspecies (T. l. annectens, T. l. lineatum, and T. l. texanum) and Smith's subsequent 1965 description of 
T. lineatum mertensi
 from New Mexico were based primarily on differences in ventral and caudal scalation, all of which show some overlap between subspecies (Table ). The number of ventral scales differs from the other subspecies in T. l. annectens and T. l. mertensi, being higher in the former and lower in the latter across both sexes (Table ). Furthermore, males of T. l. texanum had significantly lower caudal counts when compared to every other subspecies. However, Ramsey () noted that 26 specimens appeared to be intergrades between T. l. annectens and T. l. lineatum, and 37 specimens were intergrades between T. l. annectens and T. l. texanum with males of intermediate caudal counts, in regions where their ranges intersect (Figure ). Furthermore, Ramsey () recovered subtle north–south geographic gradients in multiple characters, including the ventral, caudal, infralabial, and supralabial scale counts, dorsal scale row number, and body size. Although there may be true regional variation in these meristic and mensural characters, the existence of intermediate forms and molecular signals for continuous gene flow across the range suggest these characters may be clinal rather than discrete. Given the concordant patterns in both genetic and morphological data, together with the species' largely continuous distribution, we recommend future studies re‐examine the taxonomic validity of the four subspecies of 
T. lineatum
.

Although the validity of the recognized subspecies is uncertain, individuals from the South Dakota James River may represent a population that has become geographically isolated relatively recently (Figure ). Our limited sampling across the species' distribution prevents a comprehensive comparison of genetic diversity and variation; however, we observed comparable, although lower, levels of heterozygosity H
o in the James River population relative to core populations. These results are somewhat surprising, given the general assumption that genetically divergent peripheral populations exhibit decreased genetic diversity (Brown et al. ; Lesica and Allendorf ; Vucetich and Waite ). One possible explanation for the moderately low F
st and comparable H
o to the core population is recent isolation following colonization, with fluctuating environmental conditions at the range edge possibly enhancing genetic drift and differentiation (Lammi et al. ; Sexton et al. ; Slatkin and Excoffier ). While this hypothesis remains to be tested in other small fossorial snakes, the James River population may represent a particularly promising system to investigate these dynamics.

There is growing recognition of the importance of conservation efforts for populations at the range edge (Channell ; Lesica and Allendorf ; Peterman et al. ), and the James River population may represent one such conservation priority. Its potential recent isolation, putatively small effective population size, and state endangered status in South Dakota (South Dakota Game, Fish, and Parks ) underscore its conservation significance. We recommend continued conservation efforts focused on habitat protection and long‐term monitoring of the James River population. Additionally, two seemingly disjunct populations, one in the Denver, Colorado region and another centered around St. Louis, Missouri and extending into Illinois (Figure ), are not represented in our dataset (Ramsey ; Smith and Smith ; Stebbins ). Dense, range‐wide sampling, particularly incorporating these relict populations, will be necessary to fully characterize genetic diversity and population structure in 
T. lineatum
 and to inform future conservation management decisions.
