Abstract
Critical-mineral supply chains can remain exposed to a country even when direct imports from
that country are limited, and disruptions can alter sourcing through pathways that bilateral trade
statistics do not capture. This paper develops three analytical capabilities within the Supply-
chain Exposure Network Tracking and Risk Assessment (SENTRA) framework to measure these
dependencies, detect emerging structural changes, and evaluate supply reallocation under disrup-
tion. Building on the temporal attributed multi-relational graph developed in Part I, the first
method attributes a destination country’s supply to a nominated country of origin through multi-
step trade and production pathways and decomposes exposure into four channels: direct trade,
transshipment, downstream value-chain exposure, and upstream input exposure. The second uses
five graph-derived signals and past-only reference distributions to identify unusual changes in trade
routes and country-level network structure. The third places a constrained reallocation optimization
downstream of the forecast to estimate how production and trade could adjust following a specified
disruption, subject to material-balance, capacity, ramp-rate, and supplier-switching constraints.
The methods are evaluated on the global aluminum supply chain. The exposure analysis shows
that bilateral trade can substantially understate dependence when material reaches a destination
through third countries or later stages of the value chain. For Chinese-origin flat-rolled aluminum,
direct U.S. exposure was 1.8% in 2025 while downstream exposure was approximately 7.9%. The
anomaly methodology identifies changes in routing, supplier and customer structure, and re-export
activity while distinguishing unusual changes from large but stable channels. And in a retrospective
disruption analysis of Indonesia’s 2014 bauxite export ban, the model, using the pre-disruption
network, identifies Australia and India as the principal replacement suppliers to China; predicted
and observed supplier-share changes across the modeled suppliers correlate at +0.96.
These three methods extend SENTRA from graph construction and forecasting to measurement,
monitoring, and conditional disruption analysis on a common supply-chain representation. The
exposure and anomaly results are descriptive and observational, while the disruption model es-
timates conditional reallocation under a specified shock and model assumptions rather than the
causal effect of the historical policy.

Keywords: supply-chain networks; origin attribution; transshipment detection; supply-chain mon-
itoring; disruption scenario analysis; convex optimization; uncertainty quantification; critical min-
erals.

1 Introduction
Bilateral trade statistics identify the country that shipped a product, but they do not provide
a complete account of what occurs within that country. They do not show how much of the
product is produced or consumed domestically, how imported materials are transformed, or how
those materials become embedded in the country’s exports. These missing relationships are nec-
essary to assess supply chain exposure beyond immediate trading partners. SENTRA treats un-
observed domestic production, final demand, and product input dependencies as latent quantities
and estimates them from available trade, production, and value-chain observations, subject to the
accounting requirement that supply equal use (T. Barlock et al. 2026). The resulting tempo-
ral attributed multi-relational graph connects countries through trade relationships and products
through within-country value-chain relationships. Indirect dependencies then become graph paths
that can be traced, quantified, and monitored. A decline in imports from one country may represent
reduced underlying dependence, a shift through an intermediary country, or the persistence of the
same dependence through another production stage. These outcomes have different implications
for supply security but cannot be distinguished from bilateral trade relationships alone.
Part I established the computational foundation of SENTRA through temporal attributed multi-
relational graph construction, latent-quantity estimation, graph-derived exposure measures, and
graph forecasting (T. Barlock et al. 2026). Part II builds on that foundation by developing methods
for supply chain assessment. It addresses three questions: Where do a country’s materials and
inputs originate when we trace them through intermediate trade and production stages? Is the
network changing in a way that warrants analyst attention? How could production and trade adjust
if a supplier, trade relationship, or production were disrupted? These questions require separate
methods. Exposure analysis measures an existing dependency, anomaly detection identifies unusual
changes in the network, and scenario analysis estimates adjustment under a specified disruption.
The first method, Origin Exposure, attributes a destination country’s supply to a nominated country
of origin through successive trade and value-chain relationships. The method separates exposure
into direct trade, transshipment, conversion into later-stage products, and conversion from earlier-
stage inputs. This decomposition distinguishes a decline in bilateral imports from a decline in the
underlying dependence on material originating in that country.
The second method, Structural Anomaly Detection, screens the graph for five types of change:
shipment surges, shifts from direct to indirect routing, pass-through activity, and emerging hubs
and turnover, the last two are assessed at node level. Each signal is evaluated against the entity’s
prior observations using a past-only reference distribution. The resulting alerts identify countries,
products, and trade relationships for analyst review. They flag unusual trade patterns but do not,
by themselves, establish circumvention, intent, illegality, or a causal response to a policy measure.
The third method, Disruption Scenario Analysis, estimates how supply could be reallocated after
a specified loss of production or trade access. The method places a constrained optimization
model downstream of the graph forecast. The forecast defines the undisrupted baseline, while
the optimization reallocates production and trade subject to material balance, available capacity,
production adjustment rates, supplier-switching constraints, and scenario-specific restrictions.
These methods are implemented within the SENTRA platform and accessed through a common

 2

analyst-facing interface. Analysts can examine production stages, trade relationships, direct and
indirect exposure, concentration, structural alerts, forecasts, and disruption scenarios within the
same supply chain representation. The interface allows an analyst to move from an aggregate
exposure estimate or alert, to countries, products, and graph paths that produced it.
The three methods were applied to the global aluminum supply chain from January 2010 through
June 2026. Direct U.S. exposure to Chinese-origin flat-rolled aluminum fell to 1.8% in 2025, while
indirect exposure remained approximately 7.9%. The underlying dependence had shifted across
trade and value-chain channels rather than declined by the same amount as direct exposure.
Indonesian-origin bauxite exposure in China followed a different pattern. Exposure fell to near
zero following Indonesia’s 2014 export ban and returned after the restrictions were relaxed. It later
declined again and has remained near zero since 2024. The remaining 0.4% arrives in China as
alumina or aluminum rather than as bauxite. In this case, the underlying origin exposure largely
ended rather than moving to another channel.
The anomaly analysis identifies shifts from direct trade to routing through third countries and dis-
tinguishes these changes from periods in which direct and indirect trade increase together. The re-
sulting alerts identify changes that warrant analyst review but do not determine why those changes
occurred. The scenario analysis examines how China’s bauxite sourcing could adjust following the
loss of Indonesian supply in 2014. Using the pre-disruption network, SENTRA identifies Australia
and India as the principal replacement suppliers, closely matching the observed changes in China’s
sourcing. The results show how the model can estimate reallocation among represented suppliers
while accounting for cost, capacity, production adjustment, and supplier-switching constraints.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the SENTRA platform
and computational architecture. Section 3 describes data and evaluation design. Section 4 develops
Origin Exposure and applies it to changes in direct and indirect dependence. Section 5 presents the
structural anomaly detection methodology and results. Section 6 develops the disruption scenario
model and evaluates it using Indonesia’s 2014 bauxite export restriction. Section 7 discusses the
limitations of the current methods and priorities for further development.

2 The SENTRA Platform
SENTRA is a modular computational framework implemented through an access-controlled an-
alytical platform. The framework defines the data model, graph representation, and analytical
methods, while the platform integrates data pipelines, model outputs, visualizations, and analyst
tools. This separation allows methods and interface components to evolve independently while
using a common supply chain representation.
Part I established SENTRA’s computational architecture and temporal attributed multi-relational
graph (T. Barlock et al. 2026). Part II extends the analytical capabilities built on this foundation
and makes their outputs available through the platform.

2.1 Part II capabilities
SENTRA is organized into three connected components: Data Integration, Network Intelligence,
and Decision Support (T. Barlock et al. 2026). Part II adds Origin Exposure and Structural
Anomaly Detection to Network Intelligence and Disruption Scenario Analysis to Decision Support.
Origin Exposure traces material from a nominated country of origin through trade and value-
chain relationships. Structural Anomaly Detection identifies unusual changes in trade routes and
country-product relationships. Disruption Scenario Analysis estimates how production and trade
could adjust following a specified loss of supply, production, or trade access. All three methods
operate on the graph established in Part I and can therefore be added or revised without creating
separate supply chain representations.

2.2 Platform implementation
The platform integrates supply chain data, graph-derived measures, model outputs, and visual-
izations developed across Parts I and II. Authorized analysts can examine conditions by material,
country, product, production stage, and month.
Analytical views cover facilities and production, concentration, sourcing exposure, trade relation-
ships, contextual trade information, structural anomalies, forecasts, and disruption scenarios. Their
organization and naming reflect the current implementation and may change as requirements and
capabilities develop.
The Command Center is the current landing page. For a selected material, destination, product,
and month, it summarizes production stages, sourcing pathways, concentration, chokepoints, and
supporting information. Analysts can navigate from this summary to more detailed analyses in the
respective sections in the platform.
Analysts can inspect the data and relationships underlying each result. Exposure estimates can be
disaggregated by supplier, origin, product, production stage, and exposure channel. A concentration
measure can be traced to the countries and import shares on which it is based. An anomaly alert
can be compared with the direct and indirect trade series that generated it. Scenario results identify

 4

replacement suppliers and changes in sourcing.
The platform also includes a plain-language query function supported by large language models.
The function retrieves, synthesizes, and presents information from SENTRA’s constructed graph,
data, and analytical outputs, helping analysts work with results that would otherwise be difficult
to process and interpret. Responses are restricted to data and information available within the
platform and do not draw on internet sources or the language model’s general knowledge.

Figure 1: Current Command Center view of U.S. aluminum supply exposure in June 2026. The
view links production stages and multi-step sourcing pathways with concentration, chokepoints,
exposure channels, and supporting information. Analysts can trace individual relationships and
navigate to more detailed analyses. The interface shown reflects the current implementation and
may change as SENTRA develops.

2.3 Model execution and integration
The SENTRA platform currently uses a hybrid execution structure. Some graph queries and
analytical calculations run within the platform, while computationally intensive models are executed
separately and their outputs are loaded for analysis and visualization.
Graph forecasts are generated by models trained and executed at Argonne computing resources at
the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF). Disruption scenario analyses are also executed
externally, after which the resulting supply-reallocation estimates are loaded into the corresponding
analytical views.

 5

2.4 Current scope and development priorities
The current platform covers aluminum, lithium, cobalt, copper, and nickel. Across these supply
chains, it represents 121 production stages and 239 economies. Each monthly graph contains
approximately 204,000 trade and value-chain relationships and is updated as new data are processed
and validated. The Part II methods are evaluated on the global aluminum supply chain from
January 2010 through June 2026.
Development priorities include automated data ingestion, quality control, model execution, result
validation, and publication. Planned capabilities will also allow analysts to incorporate their own
data into defined workflows and specify disruption scenarios directly through the platform.
Future versions will connect the currently separate material supply chains through shared pro-
duction stages, material inputs, downstream products, and end uses. This integration will support
analysis of exposure, structural change, and disruption propagation across interacting supply chains,
as well as transfer learning for materials with sparse or limited data.

3.2 Aluminum data instance
Aluminum supply chain used in this analysis contains 21 products including bauxite and scrap, alu-
mina and refining products, primary aluminum, cast forms, semi-fabricated products, and selected
end uses. The end-use representation includes construction, industrial equipment, metal products,
and vehicles. The product relationships form the process map used to connect upstream inputs
with downstream production stages.
The country dimension contains 239 countries and territories identified by M49 codes. The analysis
covers 198 consecutive monthly graphs from January 2010 through June 2026. All results use
aluminum data generated on August 15, 2026.
A July 2026 graph was available during preparation of the analysis but was excluded because
reporting was incomplete. The July graph contained 22,338 trade relationships, compared with
44,727 in June, and approximately $121 billion in recorded trade, compared with $142 billion in
June. June 2026 is therefore the final month used in this analysis.

3.3 Evaluation design
The three methods are evaluated using historical cases selected to represent different supply chain
questions and network responses. Origin Exposure uses Chinese-origin flat-rolled aluminum reach-
ing the United States, Russian-origin primary aluminum reaching the United States, and Indonesian-
origin bauxite reaching China. The cases provide variation in whether declining direct trade is
accompanied by persistent indirect exposure or by a broader decline in origin-specific dependence.
Structural Anomaly Detection is evaluated against historical changes in trade relationships and
routing patterns. Each observation is evaluated using information available before the month
being assessed. Reference distributions are constructed from preceding observations, preserving
the temporal ordering of the monitoring problem.
Disruption Scenario Analysis is evaluated using historical disruptions for which subsequent sourc-
ing changes can be observed. The primary case examines China’s sourcing adjustment following
Indonesia’s 2014 bauxite export restriction. Pre-event information restrictions are applied when
evaluating the model’s ability to identify replacement supply. Any scenario specification or param-
eter informed by observations after the event is identified separately in Section 6.
The evaluation design preserves the information set available at the time of each historical assess-
ment. Future observations are excluded from anomaly reference distributions and from pre-event

 8

scenario inputs. This temporal separation prevents realized post-event network changes from being
used to generate the result against which those same changes are subsequently evaluated.

4 SENTRA Exposure Measures
4.1 Two families of exposure measures
The SENTRA graph supports two complementary measures of supply chain exposure. Sourcing
Exposure, developed in Part I, evaluates a country’s exposure through its direct suppliers by trac-
ing the sources that contribute to each supplier’s available supply (T. Barlock et al. 2026). Origin
Exposure starts from a nominated country of origin and traces its contribution to a destination
country’s supply across direct trade, transshipment, and value-chain pathways. Sourcing Expo-
sure characterizes exposure associated with existing supplier relationships, while Origin Exposure
identifies how material from a specified origin reaches the destination through the broader supply
chain.

which gives the fraction of X’s supply of p obtained directly from X ′ . Sourcing Exposure de-

using imported inputs. These contributions define sourcing transshipment, local value-chain, and

Sourcing transshipment measures the share supplied by X ′ that X ′ had itself imported and therefore
characterizes the direct supplier’s dependence on foreign supply. The local and foreign value-chain
components distinguish whether the inputs used in production by X ′ were sourced domestically or
imported. These measures characterize the supply structure associated with the nominated direct
supplier.

Origin Exposure: Origin Exposure starts from an origin country o and traces its material
through the supply chain graph to a destination. The measure attributes exposure to the nominated
origin across direct trade, transshipment, and conversion through subsequent production stages.
Four channels are defined: direct trade, origin transshipment, downstream value-chain exposure,
and upstream value-chain exposure. The downstream value-chain channel is further separated
according to whether conversion occurs in the origin country or in another country.
Attributing what a destination consumes back to a source across multiple steps is the problem
the global value chain literature addresses by decomposing gross trade into value added by origin
(Hummels, Ishii, and Yi 2001; Koopman, Wang, and Wei 2014). Origin Exposure differs in three
ways that matter for monitoring. It runs on a monthly bilateral graph of individual products rather
than on annual country-by-sector input-output tables, so it resolves the month a route changes and
the product it changes in. It conditions on one nominated origin rather than decomposing every

 10

The Indonesian bauxite case shows a different pattern. Direct Indonesian-origin bauxite falls from
16.8% of China’s aluminum-chain imports in 2013 to zero within a quarter, subsequently recovers
to 4.8% by 2021, and later returns to zero. Total exposure closely follows the direct channel, falling
initially to 0.004% and later to 0.32%. Unlike the two US cases, the decline in direct exposure is
not accompanied by a substantial increase in indirect exposure. In 2025, the remaining exposure
consists of 0.13% through alumina refined in Indonesia from Indonesian bauxite and 0.20% through

These examples show that changes in direct trade do not necessarily track changes in total origin
exposure. In the Chinese and Russian cases, large declines in direct US imports coexist with
continued exposure through transshipment and value-chain pathways. In the Indonesian case,
direct and total exposure move much more closely together. Origin Exposure distinguishes these
outcomes by tracing whether material attributed to the origin continues to reach the destination
through indirect pathways.
These patterns are observational. They describe the evolution of supply-chain exposure over time
but do not identify whether particular policy interventions or other events caused the observed
changes or structural breaks. For example, the structural changes in Indonesian bauxite exposure
coincide with periods of restriction and relaxation of Indonesia’s bauxite export policy, but the
current SENTRA methodology does not quantify the causal effect of those policy changes. Estab-
lishing causality is outside the scope of the analysis presented here but is part of the planned future
development of SENTRA.

5 Structural Anomaly Detection
The exposure measures of Section 4 characterize the state of the supply chain network. Monitoring
requires identifying changes in that state. This section develops five detection signals computed
directly on Gt , each evaluated against a reference distribution estimated using only preceding ob-
servations. The resulting p-values are combined within two families and screened using a false
discovery rate procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).
Two design choices apply throughout. First, the reference distribution used to evaluate an entity
at month t is constructed using observations preceding t, so the observation being evaluated does
not enter its own reference distribution. Second, the signals are structural, not learned. They
are computed directly from the graph and therefore do not require a trained model. The section
defines the five signals, their temporal reference distributions, tail calibration, signal combination
and multiplicity procedure, and evaluates the resulting flags.

5.1 Units of analysis
Two units carry the signals and are kept separate throughout. A route is a directed country pair for
a given product, while a node is a country–product pair (p, k). Three anomaly signals are defined
at the route level and two at the node level. This distinction is important for error control: a node
and its incident routes are not independent testing units, and pooling them would count the same
event several times (Section 5.5). Throughout this section, local notation carries the superscript
det (e.g. adet ) to distinguish it from similarly named quantities in Sections 4 and 6. Table A-3
summarizes these symbols.

evaluated. A threshold udet is set at the pooled 95th percentile, and exceedances above it are fit by
a generalized Pareto distribution, the limiting form of the distribution of exceedances over a high
threshold (Balkema and Haan 1974; Pickands 1975). For a statistic above udet ,

Fisher’s method (Fisher 1925) is retained within the two node-level signals in Section 5.2, where

volume and intensity for pass-through activity. In both cases, the combination is evaluated only
after the gating conditions defined in that section are satisfied, so both constituent measurements
carry information. We do not use Fisher’s method to combine the full set of anomaly signals, where
signals are potentially dependent and some may be inactive for a given entity and month.

established here.

provide simultaneous control across entities in a given month. Cross-entity FDR control would
require an additional multiple-testing procedure, which we do not impose here. Reported flag
counts should therefore be interpreted under this within-entity definition of multiplicity.

5.6 What the construction does not reach
The detector is edge-relative and its baseline is trailing, so it identifies the onset of a change rather

however large it is. Section 4.9 measures exactly such a channel on the direct routes between the
same pair of countries. The two capabilities answer different questions. Attribution speaks to a
dependence that exists; detection speaks to one that has just appeared. A monitoring system built
on the second alone would not have reported the first.

6 Disruption Scenario Analysis
This section describes a methodology for predicting how supply networks may adjust in the case
of a specified change in a part of the supply chain such as a disruption to trade flows between
certain countries. The exposure measures in Section 4 characterize existing dependencies, while
the signals in Section 5 identify changes in those dependencies; neither describes how the network
would reallocate supply under a disruption. The forecasting model developed in part I is also
not designed for this purpose. It extrapolates observed network dynamics but does not impose
the accounting and capacity constraints needed to redistribute lost supply, particularly when the
disruption lies outside the conditions represented in the training data.
We therefore place a structured optimization model downstream of the forecast. The forecast
provides the baseline supply network, while the optimization determines how flows can reallocate
under a specified disruption. The broad separation of prediction from constrained decision-making
has precedent in predict-then-optimize methods (Elmachtoub and Grigas 2022) and large-scale
operational systems (Sinha et al. 2026). Here we develop this idea specifically for supply-chain
disruption analysis: the optimization reallocates lost supply across countries and products while
enforcing trade capacity, production limits, substitution behavior, and the physical input–output
relationships of the value chain. The resulting framework therefore combines a learned baseline
with a supply-chain-specific model of feasible adjustment.

Willingness: The friction Rσ encodes how far an importer will move its sourcing away from its
established pattern. Estimating this lane by lane from historical co-movement is not identifiable
here: a flexible per-pair fit cannot recover a nonlinear elasticity from a few dozen noisy monthly
returns, and co-movement confounds genuine substitution with common demand shocks that move
two suppliers together. The identified, standard object is the Armington elasticity of substitution σp
(Armington 1969), the constant-elasticity-of-substitution parameter governing source substitution.
The literature provides stronger support for the relative ordering across product types than for any
specific elasticity value. Homogeneous commodities generally exhibit greater substitutability than
differentiated manufactures (Rauch 1999; Broda and Weinstein 2006), while estimated magnitudes
vary substantially with product aggregation and estimation method (Feenstra et al. 2018; Caliendo
and Parro 2015). We therefore assign σp by product tier according to this ordering and vary its
magnitude across the ensemble; the values and ranges are given in Section D.5.

elasticity says how easily an importer substitutes; a set of source-preference weights says whom it

6.4 Base-year calibration and the no-shock property
A scenario model must satisfy one elementary requirement: if nothing is disrupted it must return the
observed baseline. With placeholder costs it does not. Set production costs to a common constant
and lane costs to freight alone, and nothing in the objective rewards the observed allocation.
The undisrupted program simply re-optimises. On the aluminum chain it collapses United States
primary sourcing onto the nearest large supplier at 98% against an observed 46%, and every scenario
then reports a large apparent impact before any shock is applied.

Entering inventory: We do not choose Lss by hand. A short disruption-free burn-in of B
pre-shock months is solved first, in which baseline demand and baseline flows apply and no shock
is present, subject to Equation (31), the capacity bounds and a stationarity condition closing the
window into a cycle. The chain holds whatever stock is cost-optimal against the baseline demand
path, trading the holding cost against the value of smoothing production and imports. Solving the
burn-in separately is deliberate: because it never sees the disruption it cannot pre-build inventory
in anticipation, so Lss is a genuine pre-shock steady state, not a shock-aware buffer.

6.5 From a point to a distribution
A point solution is sufficient for describing a historical case, where the realized outcome is known
and can be compared directly with the model output. Forward scenarios require a different treat-
ment: the baseline is itself a forecast, the quantitative realization of the disruption is uncertain, and
the structural parameters are not known exactly. We therefore use a hierarchical uncertainty frame-
work, detailed in Section D.2, that propagates these sources of uncertainty through the scenario
model and produces an ensemble of feasible supply-chain responses rather than a single allocation.
The results reported in Section 6.6 are drawn from this hierarchy, with Equation (37) providing
the conditional point solution for each realization.

Four sources of uncertainty: The scenario analysis distinguishes four sources of uncertainty:
the baseline, structural parameters, scenario realization, and model behavior. Baseline uncertainty
arises from the multi-month forecast rollout and increases with lead time. Structural uncertainty
reflects uncertainty in the parameters of the optimization. Scenario uncertainty describes how a
specified disruption is realized, including its magnitude, coverage, timing, and leakage. Behavioral
uncertainty allows realized flows to depart from the allocation selected by the optimization.
For scenario uncertainty, we distinguish between the occurrence of a disruption and its realization
conditional on occurring. The framework does not assign a probability to whether a particular
disruption will occur. Instead, the analyst specifies the scenario class as a conditioning event, and
we randomize only its quantitative realization. This separates event frequency, which is outside
the scope of the model, from conditional consequences, which the model evaluates, following the

 35

standard risk decomposition of Kaplan and Garrick (1981). Section D.2 gives the resulting hierarchy
and identifies which quantities are sampled, derived, and fixed.

The solve as the mode of a Gibbs measure: The friction term in Equation (39) is
the negative log-density of a Gaussian distribution over each trade lane centered on its baseline
value, while the remaining terms in the objective are linear. Writing J for the objective, the
optimization can therefore be interpreted as the mode of a measure proportional to exp(−J/ϵ) over
the feasible set. This measure is log-concave on the feasible polytope; the derivation is relegated to
the Section D.1.
The parameter ϵ acts as a temperature, controlling how tightly the distribution concentrates around
the optimum. As ϵ → 0, the distribution concentrates at the minimizer of J; at larger values, allo-
cations with higher objective values receive non-negligible probability. The mode itself is unchanged
for any ϵ > 0, since maximizing exp(−J/ϵ) is equivalent to minimizing J. This probabilistic inter-
pretation therefore leaves the point solution of Section 6.2 unchanged.
It also gives two components of the model a probabilistic interpretation. The friction term de-
fines a prior centered on the forecast rather than serving only as a penalty, with σp controlling
its dispersion. The temperature ϵ, in turn, provides a mechanism for behavioral uncertainty by
allowing allocations away from the optimizer’s solution. The reported solves are the ϵ → 0 limit,
so behavioral uncertainty does not contribute to the intervals shown.

The hierarchy is used for forward uncertainty propagation: The hierarchy is used
generatively: its distributions are sampled in sequence, and none is conditioned on observed flows.
The flow distribution pϵ (z | ·) could, in principle, also be used for inference. If observed flows were
substituted for z and this distribution were viewed as a function of θ, it would define a likelihood
through which the structural parameters could be updated by Bayes’ rule. We do not perform that
update here.
Instead, the structural parameters retain the distributions specified from the ranges in Section 6.3,
and forward uncertainty is obtained by propagating draws from those distributions together with
draws of the scenario realization. The resulting ensemble is therefore a forward uncertainty distri-
bution, not a posterior predictive distribution.
Historical events are used only for validation. In the cases considered in Section 6.6, the ensemble
is compared with the observed outcome after the model has been run; those observations do not
update the parameter distributions. The comparison therefore evaluates empirical coverage without
conditioning the model on the validation events. The uncertainty analysis is supported by two forms
of evidence: the input distributions are grounded in documented sources, and the resulting intervals
are evaluated against observed historical outcomes rather than only by the direction of the point
estimates.

6.6 Case study: the 2014 Indonesian bauxite ban
In January 2014 Indonesia banned exports of unprocessed ore, cutting off China’s largest source
of bauxite. The case is a useful test of the program because the substitutes are upstream miners
expanding within their own capacity and not downstream fabricators, and because it contains a
response the method provably cannot anticipate, which makes the boundary of the approach visible
and not rhetorical.
We evaluate two specifications: The pre-event structural run takes the measure as announced and
uses only the 2013 network. The ex post informed run adds two quantities read from the outturn:
the ban proved 82.4% effective and not total, and Malaysia’s new output is entered at the level the
U.S. Geological Survey reports (U.S. Geological Survey 2024). Both quantities postdate January
2014, so the second run is not a forecast an analyst could have made. The comparison separates
what the pre-event network determines from what requires outside information about policy leakage
and new capacity. Both are run as ensembles of 300 draws over the structural parameters and the
scenario realisation, so each bar carries an ensemble range.

Uncertainty: The uncertainty bands propagate uncertainty in the structural parameters and in
the scenario realization through the optimization. They do not include uncertainty in the forecast
baseline path or in the behavioral temperature introduced in Section 6.5, both of which would add
further sources of variation. The bands should therefore be interpreted as conditional uncertainty
under the specified baseline and behavioral response, rather than as calibrated predictive intervals.
In this case, the nominal 80% interval contains the observed outcome for four of the nine suppliers,
and the nominal 90% interval for five. These bands propagate only two of the four uncertainty
sources identified in Section 6.5; they therefore do not represent full predictive uncertainty. Hence,
the resulting coverage below the nominal level is consistent with this omission and should not, by
itself, be interpreted as evidence of miscalibration.

6.7 Scope and limitation of the scenario model
The scenario model reallocates supply within the capacities available to it; it does not predict
new investment or capacity expansion. The Malaysian entrant above illustrates this distinction.
Once new capacity is specified as part of a scenario, the model can incorporate it into subsequent
reallocations, but it cannot infer from the base year that such capacity will emerge.
The model also assumes that disruptions and capacity changes over the scenario horizon are known
in advance. For shocks that occur unexpectedly, the optimization can instead be repeated in a
rolling horizon as new information becomes available. Price responses are not modeled, and the
framework therefore does not address shocks that operate solely through prices.
Finally, the resulting scenarios should not be interpreted as identified causal counterfactuals. They
describe how the supply-chain network adjusts under a specified set of disruptions, capacities, and

 39

accounting constraints. Their credibility rests on reproducing the observed baseline in the absence
of a disruption and on validation against historical events with known outcomes, as discussed in
Section 7.1.

7 Discussion and Future Research
7.1 Current limitations
The capabilities developed in this paper produce estimates based on modeling assumptions and
should not be interpreted as ground truth. These limitations reflect the current estimation and
calibration methods, not the underlying computational framework.

• The exposure attribution of Section 4 assumes proportional mixing, so that a country’s exports
  are taken to draw on its available supply in proportion to the origin composition of that sup-
  ply. The resulting quantity is therefore an estimate of exposure, not a determination of the
  provenance of any individual shipment. The trade data record value by country pair, product,
  and month but do not track individual consignments, so the proportional-mixing assumption
  cannot be verified directly from the available data. The reported exposure should therefore be
  interpreted as an expectation under this assumption. Shipment-level or firm-level information,
  where available, could provide more direct evidence on the routing and provenance of traded
  material and reduce reliance on proportional mixing.

• The exposure measure requires the country of origin to be specified in advance. It quantifies a
  dependence that an analyst has identified as being of interest; it does not itself identify which
  dependencies warrant attention. Methods for ranking or screening candidate origins within the
  same attribution framework are an area of future research.

• The detection signals of Section 5 are standardized relative to each entity’s own recent history,
  so a route whose history is quiet registers a given movement as more extreme than an equivalent
  movement on a route that is large and variable. Reported results therefore require a volume
  threshold, and a standardization that accounts for route size would be preferable to a floor
  applied after the fact.

• The detection methodology identifies the onset of a change and not its persistence, because the
  reference distribution is estimated from an entity’s own recent observations and adjusts to a
  sustained new level. A channel that is large and slowly growing is therefore not identified, which
  is the case for the downstream value chain, which Section 4.9 measures as the largest of the
  four. A detector for persistent channels requires a different reference, either a cross-sectional
  comparison against comparable routes or a counterfactual derived from the forecasting model
  of Part I.

• The reported false discovery rate is controlled within each entity’s own observation history, not
  across entities at a given time point, which would require a hierarchical procedure. The error
  rate claimed is therefore conditional on the entity, and a null-permutation study remains the
  direct test of that claim.

• The reallocation methodology of Section 6 redistributes existing productive capacity and can-
  not anticipate capacity commissioned in response to a disruption, as Section 6.6 demonstrates.
  Such capacity can be supplied to the program as external information, and is then represented
  correctly, so the limitation concerns anticipation, not representation.

 41

• The ensemble ranges of Section 6.5 propagate assumed uncertainty in the structural parameters
  and the scenario specification but not in the baseline forecast path or in behavioral departure
  from the optimum. They should therefore be interpreted as conditional uncertainty under the
  specified baseline and behavioral response, not as calibrated predictive intervals. Their empirical
  coverage in Section 6.6 falls short of nominal.

• The exposure and anomaly results are descriptive and observational and do not identify causal
  effects of the policy measures or events with which the observed changes coincide. The disruption
  analysis is a conditional structural counterfactual: it estimates reallocation under an imposed
  shock and the model’s constraints and assumptions, not the causal effect of the historical policy.

7.2 Future research directions
This paper identifies five areas of future work:

Causal inference: Future work will incorporate causal inference methods into SENTRA. The
causal inference methods will be able to quantify changes attributed to a policy measure. Identify-
ing policy effects requires an explicit counterfactual for how the network would have evolved in the
absence of the intervention. Section 6 constructs structural counterfactuals whose credibility rests
on accounting constraints and validation against observed outcomes, not on a causal identification
strategy. Future work will examine how SENTRA’s exposure measures and structural counterfac-
tuals can be combined with appropriate causal inference designs to estimate the effects of tariffs,
sanctions, export restrictions, and other policy disruptions.

Structural anomaly detection: Evaluating the calibration of the resulting alerts requires a
null-permutation study on time-permuted data. The current standardization also requires further
treatment of route size and reference-distribution stability. Extending detection from the onset of
structural changes to persistent channels requires a different reference, such as a cross-sectional
comparison against comparable routes or a counterfactual derived from the forecasting model de-
veloped in Part I.

Disruption scenario analysis: The structural parameters are currently assigned from the
applied economics literature and evaluated through sensitivity analysis rather than estimated from
historical disruptions. A library of historical disruption events would permit these parameters to
be estimated jointly with a model discrepancy term and evaluated retrospectively under a forward
validation protocol. Such evaluation is required before the resulting uncertainty intervals can be
described as calibrated. Substituting observed market price series for the fitted price system would
also ground price levels that the current calibration determines only up to a gauge.

Firm- and facility-level data: Ongoing work is expanding the integration of firm- and
facility-level data into SENTRA. The current framework relies on latent supply estimates where
observed production, processing, or trade information is incomplete. Greater use of firm- and

 42

facility-level data can reduce reliance on latent quantities where direct observations are available
and add information on ownership, production capacity, processing activity, and other character-
istics that are not observable from country-level trade flows alone. These data can improve the
representation of individual supply-chain nodes and the attribution of exposure through them.

Platform integration and deployment: Further development will focus on tighter inte-
gration between SENTRA’s analytical models and the user-facing platform. The current imple-
mentation uses a hybrid architecture in which some analytical outputs are generated outside the
platform and subsequently integrated into the application. Future development will deploy the
analytical models directly within the platform, allowing exposure, anomaly detection, forecasting,
and scenario analysis to operate through a common computational backend and to update as new
data become available.
Future extensions of SENTRA will also expand the framework to multi-material supply chain
modeling through hierarchical transfer learning and cross-supply-chain reasoning, in which products
from one supply chain enter others as inputs and the exposure, detection, and scenario analyses
developed here are extended across interconnected material systems.

C.2 Why there are four channels
Fix an origin, a destination and a reference product. Total Origin Exposure is a single number:
the value of origin material that reaches the destination and is related to that product. The four
channels split that number, and the split is not a taxonomy chosen for convenience. It follows from
the graph carrying two kinds of relationship and no others.
A path from the origin to the destination is built from trade relationships, which move one product
between countries, and value-chain relationships, which convert one product into another inside a
country. Those are the two step types Equation (10) admits, and they are Equations (2) and (3).
Measured against the reference product, a path can differ in two ways only: whether it changed
country while staying the same product, and whether it changed product. Crossing the two gives
four cases, which are the four channels of Table 4-1.
Direct and origin transshipment move in the country dimension. In both the material is the
reference product when it leaves and when it arrives, and they differ in whether it passed through a
third country on the way. Downstream and upstream value chain move in the product dimension.
In both the material changed form, and they differ in which side of the reference product it changed
on. A path has no other way of using the two relations, which is why the list closes at four rather
than at some larger number.

C.3 The iteration and its numerical guard
The linear system of Section 4.5 is solved by the iteration ρ(n+1) = A(p) ρ(n) + ap /Sp , with the
pin reimposed after each sweep. Sweep n contributes the paths of length n from o. A tolerance
of 10−13 is reached within tens of sweeps. The implementation also clips the iterate to [0, 1],
which is a numerical guard and not part of the construction: started at ρ(0) = 0 the iteration is
monotone increasing, because A(p) and ap are non-negative, and the constant vector one satisfies
A(p) 1 + ap /Sp ≤ 1 by the same computation as in Section 4.3 with s ≤ 1. The iterates are therefore
bounded above by one and the clip does not bind whenever the condition below holds.

where Φ is the phased production floor of Equation (34) and R the chain roots.

Five dependencies are modelling decisions, not bookkeeping: The baseline enters
four times, which is why the model is sensitive to it and why sampling it is not optional: as the
centre and the scale of the friction, through the reconciliation constant in the balance, through the
capacity basis, and through the disruption bound, which is expressed relative to baseline exports.
The freight intensity enters only through the calibrated prices on lanes carrying baseline volume,
and directly on lanes without, so a prospective new supplier still pays full distance. The scenario
realisation splits: its tariff component acts in the objective on top of the calibrated cost, while its
disruption and quota components act as constraints. That is why a policy shock still bites after
calibration, the calibration changing the baseline the shock bites against and not the shock itself.
The burn-in does not see the scenario or the belief realisation, so it cannot pre-build inventory in
anticipation, and the resulting steady state is shared by the two solves of the same draw. And
the calibration is inside the loop: it is fitted from the baseline, so a different baseline draw yields
a different price system. Holding it fixed while sampling the baseline would both understate the
width and break the no-shock property draw by draw.

D.4 Computation
The program is small and sparse, so the outer sampling is affordable and embarrassingly parallel:
each draw requires one Laplacian refit and two solves. Three details belong in the design, not in the
discovery. The reported quantity is the paired difference, so the shocked and undisrupted solves,
and the burn-in beneath them, must use the same draw; the common variation induced by the

 64

baseline draw is large and irrelevant to the counterfactual. The reported solves use the quadratic
form of the friction. A piecewise-linear implementation can introduce flat regions with non-unique
minimizers, which has little effect on a point estimate but can create artificial multimodality under
Monte Carlo sampling. And where the full measure Equation (49) is wanted instead of parameter
propagation alone, it is log-concave on a polytope, so the equalities can be eliminated by a null-
space parametrisation and the remainder sampled by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo on the resulting
constrained support (Brubaker, Salzmann, and Urtasun 2012).

The elasticity tiers follow the standard Armington ordering (Armington 1969): a homogeneous

The ramp quantile deserves one caveat. The p95 is taken over a mostly unstressed period, so
a producer that never needed to ramp fast shows low growth and the quantile understates what
it could do under pressure. The bound is therefore conservative by construction, and it carries
the same demonstrated-against-potential limitation as the capacity basis of Equation (40). The
quantile is set at the 95th percentile. The 99th is not usable here: production is solved rather than
measured in the cache, so its upper tail mixes genuine expansion with recovery from outage and
with estimation noise.
