Reinforcement learning-driven adaptive game therapy for cognitive impairment patients with improved vision transformer based detection model
Authors: Youseef Alotaibi, Surendran Rajendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment is the growing challenge that requires early diagnosis and personalized management of neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s disease. Neuroimaging modalities like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) provide valuable structural and functional insights into brain changes associated with cognitive decline. However, existing deep learning (DL) based diagnostic models have the challenges in non-consideration of long-range spatial dependencies and contextual information across brain slices that lead to suboptimal classification accuracy.MethodsTo overcome the limitations, this research introduces the framework that combines an Improved Vision Transformer (Im-ViT) with the Residual Simple Recurrent Unit (ResNet-SRU) based Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) to capture spatial and temporal dependencies in neuroimaging data. Preprocessing using Multiscale Gaussian Filter (MGF) enhances feature clarity by removing multiscale noise. In addition, the system integrates the Visual Working Memory (VWM)-based game therapy, where difficulty levels dynamically adapt using the proposed Iterative Hiking-based Reinforcement Learning (ItHRL) approach.ResultsThe analysis of the proposed model based on various assessment measures like Accuracy, Recall, Precision, F-Score, Specificity, and Mean Squared Error (MSE) acquired the values of 99.62%, 99.33%, 98.97%, 99.56%, 99.62% and 0.018 respectively.ConclusionsThe proposed model with combined detection and game therapy approach yield higher classification accuracy, faster convergence and patient engagement.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is the condition where an individual experiences difficulties with mental abilities like memory, attention, language, problem-solving and decision-making [1]. These deficits lead to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) that affect daily functioning, and the severe condition leads to dementia or Alzheimer’s disease [2]. It drastically impacts the independence and quality of life of the affected patient. Cognitive impairment is not a specific disease; rather it is a neurological change associated with aging, brain injury, neurodegenerative diseases, or psychiatric conditions [3]. Early detection of cognitive impairment is critical because it enables timely intervention that slows the progression of neurodegenerative disorders and improves the management of reversible causes like vitamin deficiencies or depression [4]. Traditionally, cognitive impairment has been assessed through standardized neuropsychological tests, clinical interviews, and questionnaires like Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Clock Drawing Test [5, 6]. Still, the assessment methods require trained personnel and are time-consuming that makes them less practical for large-scale screenings or rural settings [7, 8].

Deep learning (DL) methods of cognitive impairment detection overcome the limitations of traditional cognitive impairment detection techniques [9, 10]. Unlike manual assessments that are time-consuming and subjective, DL models automatically learn complex patterns from large volumes of multimodal data like neuroimaging scans like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Positron Emission Tomography (PET), voice recordings, electronic health records, and behavioral data [11, 12]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Transformers, and hybrid architectures provides the enhanced outcome using the feature extraction, spatial–temporal modeling, and classification [13, 14]. The DL models are less affected by demographic biases like age, education, or language that are trained on large datasets [15, 16]. Besides, DL frameworks provide continuous, real-time assessments through mobile or wearable devices that enable remote screening and early intervention [17, 18]. Using the automated complex diagnostic tasks and enabling large-scale deployment, DL significantly enhances the accessibility, objectivity and accuracy of cognitive impairment detection [19].

Game therapy is the serious game and cognitive training games are considered as the valuable therapeutic tool for individuals with cognitive impairment. Game therapy makes the patients engage in mentally stimulating tasks that promote neuroplasticity [20, 21]. It stimulates the brain's ability to reorganize and form new neural connections. The traditional therapy is monotonous and passive. The game-based interventions require being interactive, adaptive and are enjoyable to increase patient motivation and adherence [22, 23]. The games must be targeting specific cognitive functions based on attention, memory, reasoning, and executive function and hence provide personalized and goal-oriented training [24, 25]. Furthermore, games integrated with adaptive difficulty adjustment mechanisms and neurofeedback are challenges to the user’s cognitive capacity in real-time that ensure neither under-stimulation nor frustration [26, 27]. Elderly individuals or patients with early dementia can use the game therapy to slow cognitive decline, improve mood, reduce anxiety and enhance social interaction. The game therapy with theDL continuously monitors cognitive performance by providing valuable insights into the progression or improvement of the condition [28, 29]. Thus, game therapy serves as a non-invasive approach and is utilized in real-world data for ongoing cognitive assessment and makes it a vital component in modern neurocognitive rehabilitation strategies [30]. Therefore, this research introduces the DL based cognitive impairment detection and game therapy approach for enhancing the detection accuracy and diagnoses patients. Related works

Patil & Kukreja [31] designed an advanced technique for early detection of cognitive skill impairment using a hybrid Deep Reinforced Cognitive Analytics Model (DRCAM). The methodology combined a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for spatial feature extraction with a Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent that dynamically optimized decision policies based on evolving cognitive scores [32]. The model was trained in neuropsychological test data and sensor-based inputs to mimic real-time decision-making during cognitive evaluations. The designed DRCAM achieved high accuracy in early impairment detection and demonstrated adaptability to temporal changes in cognitive behavior. However, the challenge involved balancing the complexity of RL with real-time clinical deployment due to computational overhead.

Rykov et al. [33] aimed to predict cognitive scores in mild cognitive impairment patients using wearable-derived digital physiological features and machine learning (ML). The designed model conducted clinical trial by collecting continuous physiological signals and fed them into Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Gradient Boosting models. The designed approach successfully predicted cognitive decline markers and correlated them with clinical cognitive scores and demonstrated non-invasive early detection potential. However, challenges included individual variability in physiological signals and limited interpretability of the black-box ML models.

Ching et al. [34] aimed to improve Alzheimer’s disease classification using transfer learning with the EfficientNet-B0 CNN model. MRI images were preprocessed and fed into the pre-trained EfficientNet-B0 and then fine-tuned for Alzheimer’s disease classification. The model was trained to distinguish between various classes of disease. The study achieved competitive accuracy and reduced training time and demonstrated the effectiveness of transfer learning in medical imaging. The challenge was the risk of domain mismatch, because the pre-trained network was originally developed for natural images and possibly limiting feature generalization.

Jayaprakash and Kanimozhiselvi [35] aimed at the early detection of autism spectrum disorders (ASD) using Multinomial Logistic Regression (MLR) model. Behavioral and diagnostic features from early developmental assessments were used as inputs to the MLR classifier to categorize subjects into multiple ASD risk levels. The model provided simple and interpretable results with moderate performance in classifying early symptoms. Still, the linear nature of MLR limits the ability of the model to capture non-linear relationships present in behavioral data and makes the model more challenging for higher classification accuracy.

Chen et al. [36] designed automated Alzheimer’s disease classification framework by integrating Soft Non-Maximum Suppression with an Improved ResNet50 model. The designed model utilized feature extraction based on ResNet50 and Soft Non-Maximum Suppression for effective region proposal filtering to reduce redundancy. The designed approach led to high classification accuracy and improved focus on informative brain regions by suppressing less relevant activations [37]. The advantages included enhanced precision in region selection and reduced false positives in imaging interpretation. The main challenge associated with the model was the computational load due to DL depth and post-processing that may hinder real-time applications in clinical settings.

Ma et al. [38] designed the transformer-based model that utilized the multi-head self-attention with structured health records and imaging features that enables the model to learn complex relationships across heterogeneous inputs. The designed method utilized the feature embedding, positional encoding and attention-based interpretability mechanisms make the model to highlight clinically relevant biomarkers. The designed model achieved high predictive accuracy and improved computational efficiency. Still, the challenges like high computational requirements, dependency on large annotated datasets and potential overfitting limits the performance.

Liu et al. [39] designed the transformer-based multi-channel model for detecting K-complex events in electroencephalogram (EEG) signals. The designed method considered multi-channel EEG inputs with the transformer architecture to capture both temporal dynamics and inter-channel correlations that enabling precise identification of K-complex patterns across different brain regions [40]. The designed model achieved superior detection accuracy and consistency using the temporal modeling, multi-channel feature consideration and applicability for early cognitive impairment monitoring. Still, challenges like computational complexity, the requirement for high-quality annotated EEG datasets and limitations in generalizing across diverse patient populations and recording conditions degrades the performance [41]. Problem statement

The development of DL makes the cognitive impairment detection more efficient and existing methods of cognitive impairment detection still face limitations in accurately classifying impairment stages and adapting to patient-specific cognitive changes. Traditional CNNs methods of cognitive impairment detection failed to capture long-range spatial dependencies in neuroimaging data. The recurrent models like Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) or Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs) are capable of modeling sequential data and are computationally intensive and prone to over-fitting on limited medical datasets. Besides, reinforcement learning-based cognitive therapies suffer from sparse rewards and unstable learning policies that limit the adaptive game-based interventions. In addition, lack sensitivity to finer structural changes in the brain and generalization among heterogeneous patient data. To address the challenges, this research integrates the Multiscale Gaussian Filter (MGF) for enhanced preprocessing, Im-ViT to effectively capture spatial dependencies and temporal relations with low computational cost. Furthermore, VWM game therapy with ItHRL for dynamic difficulty adjustment enables continuous and personalized cognitive intervention. The proposed end-to-end framework enhances both detection accuracy and therapeutic adaptability to overcome the key bottlenecks in existing systems.

Proposed methodology

The proposed game therapy approach for the cognitive impairment patient acquires input data acquisition from neuroimaging dataset Alzheimers Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and detects cognitive impairment as shown in Fig. 1. To enhance the feature quality of the image, MGF is devised in the pre-processing phase. Then, cognitive impairment detection is employed using Im-ViT model, which effectively captures spatial dependencies within imaging patches. To enhance the model, ResNet-SRU based MLP is integrated for capturing the temporal dependencies across sequential image slices for improving detection accuracy. Then, the game therapy using VWM interface is provided to the cognitive impaired patient to enhance the user’s cognitive state. The next level adjustment of the game’s difficulty is dynamically optimized using the proposed ItHRL, which mimics human hiking behavior to navigate complex policy spaces for ensuring personalized and adaptive cognitive stimulation.Fig. 1Proposed workflow

Game therapy

For the cognitive impaired patient, game therapy is provided, wherein the next level adjustment is devised using the ItHRL technique. In the proposed game therapy based on VWM is given. The VWM game engages patients through tasks that require memorizing and recalling visual stimuli arranged on a hexagonal grid to enhance memory retention and spatial cognition. To assess and enhance VWM in individuals with cognitive impairment, the interactive game structured into two sequential stages like memorization and recall.

In the memorization stage, the player is presented with a 6 × 6 hexagonal grid on the screen for a fixed duration of 2 s. During this interval, a subset of hexagons is highlighted in yellow to indicate the target positions that the user needs to memorize. The remaining hexagons remain white that serve as non-target distractors. After the memorization interval, the grid resets, and all hexagons revert to white. In the recall stage, the player must click on the hexagons that were previously highlighted as targets. Each click is evaluated in real-time, wherein correct selections are marked green, while incorrect ones turn red. The game score (p) is computed as the ratio of correctly recalled targets to the total number of targets:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=\frac{q_c}{q_n}$$\end{document}where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_c$$\end{document} symbolizes the number of correctly recalled targets and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_n$$\end{document} notates the total number of targets in that round. A perfect score of p=1 signifies complete success (a win), while any score p<1 is considered a loss for adaptive feedback purposes. Traditionally, the number of items to memorize (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_n$$\end{document}) was considered the sole determinant of task difficulty. However, in VWM tasks, difficulty is more nuanced due to visual complexity. Spatial grouping, distribution, and component segmentation affect the memorability of the model. Hence, continuous difficulty metric d,based on three key visual-spatial features like number of targets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_n$$\end{document}, number of connected components \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_a$$\end{document} and distribution score b. The continuous difficulty score is calculated using a normalized linear combination:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b=\eta_1q_n+\eta_2q_a+\eta_3b$$\end{document}where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta_1,\eta_2, and\ \eta_3$$\end{document} symbolizes the tunable weights. To calculate the distribution metric b, a distance matrix B of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_n\times q_n$$\end{document}, where each element \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{r,s}$$\end{document} represents the shortest-path distance between the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{th}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{th}$$\end{document} targets on the hexagonal grid. Because the hexagons are arranged in a two-dimensional plane, the shortest path is calculated using hexagonal adjacency.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B=[b_{rs}],where\ b_{rs}=0,\forall r$$\end{document}

The final distribution score is computed as the average pair-wise distance between targets:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b=\frac{2}{q_n\left(q_n-1\right)}\sum_{r=1}^{q_n}\sum_{s=r+1}^{q_n}b_{rs}$$\end{document}

The distribution score value increases when targets are spread farther apart on the grid, signifying higher cognitive load due to a lack of chunking or grouping. To prevent the repetitiveness inherent in fixed-level task databases with 100 predefined tasks, the designed system dynamically generates new memory tasks on the fly. Each generated task is embedded with a difficulty vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_n,q_a,b$$\end{document}, and its scalar difficulty score is computed using Eq. (14). The tasks are stored in a Difficulty Lookup Database (DDB) to avoid duplications and help track difficulty progression over time. The computed difficulty score d is used in the ItHRL framework to dynamically adjust task difficulty based on the player's performance history. A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent continuously updates the task parameters to keep the player's accuracy within a desired range (70%–90%). When the player consistently wins, the agent raises \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_n,q_a,b$$\end{document}; when the player struggles, it selects easier configurations from the DDB. The closed-loop system ensures personalized cognitive training, progressively challenging the user while maintaining engagement and therapeutic value.

Design of ItHRL for dynamic task adjustment

The ItHRL is efficient in real-time adjustment of game difficulty to match individual cognitive capabilities for enhancing engagement and preventing frustration or boredom. The RL with optimal reward functions maintains players in the optimal challenge zone of 85% performance, which is ideal for maximizing flow and motivation. The ItH algorithm enhances action selection in the RL policy by performing efficient local and global search over the continuous difficulty space for improving learning speed and convergence. Unlike coarse rule-based adjustments, ItHRL uses a continuous difficulty metric for finer granularity in tuning for precise skill matching. The use of player simulation agents in training minimizes the need for costly human trials and enables robust policy development under diverse gameplay conditions. Environment

To enable training without relying on constant human input, the environment simulates virtual player agents of varying skill levels with low, medium, and high proficiency by modeling reasonable performance trends. The simulations act as stand-ins for human participants that ensures robust pre-training of the RL policy.

PPO based reinforcement learning

The framework uses PPO is a modern policy gradient method that improves training stability. PPO with clipped surrogate objectives that prevent destabilizing updates to the policy. Compared to Actor-Critic, PPO offers a better trade-off between sample efficiency and performance consistency that makes it suitable for human-in-the-loop scenarios.

DDB

A massive Difficulty Database (DDB) is pre-computed and consists of tuples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{t_x,b_x\right\}$$\end{document} where each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_x$$\end{document} is a unique VWM layout and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_x$$\end{document} is its difficulty value, calculated using the continuous metric. It ensures the agent’s continuous action is grounded in a discrete, real-world task pool.

ItH optimization algorithm

The proposed ItH algorithm is utilized torefine action selection, which is integrated into the agent’s decision-making process. The ItH algorithm selects the optimal action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h^*$$\end{document} from these candidates by maximizing the expected reward:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h^*=\arg\max{_{hx\in \left\{h_x^\prime,h_x^b"\right\}}}E[R\left(g_n,h_x\right)]$$\end{document}

The proposed ItH algorithm allows the RL agent to adaptively fine-tune difficulty selection in dynamic and human-sensitive environments.

Mathematical modeling

The mathematical modeling of the proposed ItH algorithm is designed with the integration of Iterative chaotic mapping within the conventional hiking optimization algorithm. The algorithm considers the score difference from target as its fitness function in choosing the optimal best action in adjusting the difficulty level. In the ItH algorithm, each hiker is initialized randomly in the solution space:

21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{r,u}=\delta_1^v+\gamma_v\left(\delta_2^v-\delta_1^v\right),\gamma_v\sim U\left(0,1\right)$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_1^v$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_2^v$$\end{document} signifies the lower and upper bounds of the search space in dimension, current position of hiker r at time u is signified as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{r,u}$$\end{document}, uniform random number for dimension v in initialization is notated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma_v$$\end{document}. It distributes hiker positions uniformly across the search space.

Tobler’s hiking function

Tobler’s hiking function estimates walking speed on a slope. In ItH algorithm, Tobler’s hiking function is employed to control the speed of exploration based on the steepness calculated. When the slope is mild, the initial velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{r,u}$$\end{document} is high and diverse the search area by making larger jumps in the solution space. Else, when the slope is steep, the velocity is low and makes the algorithm performexploitation. The initial velocity estimated through the Tobler’s hiking function is interpreted through:

23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{r,u}=6e^{-3.5\left|Q_{r,u}+0.05\right|}$$\end{document}

Velocity update

The reinforcement-inspired behavioral modeling is considered in the velocity update phase.

24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{r,u}=V_{r,u-1}+\xi_{r,u-1}\left(\lambda_b-\right)\overline{\omega}_{r,u}\cdot\lambda_{r,u}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_b$$\end{document} refers to the position of the best-performing hiker. The difference between best and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{th}$$\end{document} hiker is the directional vector pointing toward the optimal area of the fitness space. The sweep factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{\omega}_{r,u}\in[1,3]$$\end{document} adds a control mechanism for how tightly the hiker should follow the best solution, and factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi_{r,u}$$\end{document} introduces stochasticity or randomness to prevent premature convergence.

Position update

The updated velocity is finally used to compute the next position of the hiker in the solution space. The new position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{r,u+1}$$\end{document} represents a new candidate level for the VWM game. The actual adjustment in game difficulty is determined by how far and in which direction the hiker moves. The large steps signify aggressive difficulty shifts, while small steps suggest fine-tuning. The update mechanism is employed in the reinforcement loop of dynamic difficulty adjustment.

25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{r,u+1}=\lambda_{r,u}+V_{r,u}$$\end{document}

Here, to avoid local optima, a chaotic map based on iteration is employed by introducing the randomness in the algorithm:

26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{r,u+1}=\sin\left(z\frac{\pi}{\lambda_{r,u}}\right)$$\end{document}

Here, the control parameter is symbolized as z, and the solution accomplished by the chaotic iterative mapping is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{r,u+1}$$\end{document}. The new position update by the ItH algorithm is interpreted through:

27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\lambda_{r,u+1}&=0.5\lfloor\lambda_{r,u+1}\rfloor_{Hiking}\\&+0.5\lfloor\lambda_{r,u+1}\rfloor_{IterativeChaotic}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Conclusion

The proposed cognitive impairment detection and game therapy approach achieved enhanced cognitive impairment classification using the Im-ViT with the temporal modeling of ResNet-SRU based MLP that result in improved detection of cognitive impairment from neuroimaging data. Besides, the integration of MGF significantly assists in improving the detection accuracy by enhancing the image quality. Moreover, the ItHRL-enhanced VWM game therapy provided an adaptive and personalized cognitive reinforcement mechanism. The proposed model with combined detection and game therapy approach yielded higher classification accuracy, faster convergence and patient engagement. Future work will be designed with the integration of multimodal data, real-time cognitive feedback and larger longitudinal datasets to generalize across diverse populations and refine therapy personalization further.
