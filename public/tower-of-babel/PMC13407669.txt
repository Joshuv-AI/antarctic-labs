Is Patience a Virtue When Withdrawing Antiseizure Medications After Epilepsy Surgery? Maybe Not
Authors: Claude Steriade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Timing for Starting Antiseizure Medication Withdrawal After Epilepsy Surgery in Adults. Ferreira-Atuesta C, De Tisi J, McEvoy AW, Miserocchi A, Khoury J, Yardi R, Vegh D, Butler JT, Lee HJ, Ives-Deliperi V, Yao Y, Wang FP, Zhang X, Shakhatreh L, Siriratnam P, Neal A, Sen A, Tristram M, Liem B, Varghese EN, Biney WB, Gray WP, Correia Rodrigues C, Peralta AR, Rainha Campos A, Goncalves-Ferreira AJ, Pimentel J, Všianský V, Arias JF, Farazdaghi M, Terziev R, Kadali KR, Koenig K, Haeberlin M, Otte WM, Rugg-Gunn F, Terman SW, Braun KPJ, Imbach LL, Asadi-Pooya AA, Gonzalez-Salazar W, Pail M, Bentes CC, Hamandi K, O'Brien TJ, Perucca P, Yao C, Burman RJ, Jehi LE, Duncan JS, Sander JW, Koepp M, Galovic M. Neurology 2026; 106(4): e214613.Background and ObjectivesMore than half of the people undergoing epilepsy surgery become seizure-free and may consider withdrawing antiseizure medications (ASMs). Withdrawal practices vary, and the optimal timing remains unclear. We aim to compare seizure relapse risk among individuals initiating ASM withdrawal at different time points after epilepsy surgery.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter observational cohort study of adults who underwent resective epilepsy surgery between 1990 and 2016 at 12 tertiary centers. Participants were seizure-free before medication withdrawal and had at least 1 year of follow up. Seizure relapse risk was compared among those initiating withdrawal 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 years postoperatively versus later. We used propensity score matching for each comparison to adjust for treatment selection bias.ResultsOf the 964 people included (51% female; median age at surgery 34 years [interquartile range 26-44]), 446 (46%) began ASM withdrawal in the first year after surgery, 255 (26%) in the second, 110 (11%) in the third, 58 (6%) in the fourth, 29 (3%) in the fifth, and 66 (7%) after the fifth year. After matching, those starting withdrawal in the first (hazard ratio [HR] 1.4; P = .003) or second (HR 1.18; P < .001) year had a higher risk of relapse than those who withdrew later. Starting withdrawal in the third (HR 1.7; P = .12), fourth (HR 1.3; P = .45), or fifth (HR 0.17; P = .82) year after surgery showed no increase in risk compared with later withdrawal. Long-term outcomes, such as seizure freedom and being entirely off ASMs at the final follow up, were not substantially associated with withdrawal timing.DiscussionInitiating ASM withdrawal within the first 2 postoperative years was linked to a higher initial risk of seizure relapse compared with later withdrawal, although long-term outcomes were similar regardless of withdrawal timing. Waiting more than 2 years did not confer additional benefit in reducing seizure risk. Deciding whether and when to withdraw ASMs is a shared process involving individuals, caregivers, and clinicians, balancing preferences, risk of injury, social factors (eg, driving, work, and supervision), and clinical judgment. Transparent information on risks and benefits is essential. Our findings offer real-world evidence that may inform future evidence-based withdrawal protocols and follow-up strategies.

Commentary

Antiseizure medication (ASM) burden is a core driver of poor quality of life in epilepsy, 1 likely due to cumulative side effects and their daily consequences on function. 2 Long-term ASM polypharmacy can also have consequences on systemic health, including bone and cardiovascular consequences with certain classes of ASMs. 3 Deprescribing is generally encouraged whenever possible in other medical settings, but the risks of seizure relapse in a previously seizure-free heighten the stakes. The desire to wean ASMs is often central to presurgical discussions, but a specific plan on timing of ASM withdrawal after epilepsy surgery is often deferred to postoperative discussions, and is highly variable across individual patient profiles and centers. 4 Data-driven counseling can help presurgical expectations and postsurgical shared decision making. 4 Patients report more clarity when presented with numerical data with ASM withdrawal risk in the context of seizure-free period on ASMs only 5 —it is likely that a data-informed approach also benefits patients and their physicians in the context of surgery-induced seizure freedom. In this recent large scale endeavor, Ferreira-Atuesta et al provide sound data to share with patients during high stakes decisions in the postsurgical period, when epilepsy surgery has been successful and the risk of a breakthrough seizure after hard-earned seizure control can feel like a daunting risk to take on. 6

A multicenter registry of ASM withdrawal after epilepsy surgery of adults across 12 international cohorts was leveraged in this observational study. Geographic representation across many continents is a significant strength of this study, conferring meaningful generalizability of the findings. Nine hundred and sixty-four adults who underwent resective surgery and achieved seizure freedom (excluding the 30-day postoperative period) before beginning ASM withdrawal were included. The overall cohort was skewed toward lesional focal epilepsy (only 8% of the overall cohort was nonlesional), and the majority underwent lobectomy (60%), followed by lesionectomy (38%), some incomplete (9%), and in a minority, hemispherectomy (2%). Any seizure relapse after ASM withdrawal was documented, and the last year of follow up was examined for determination of seizure freedom, ASM status (complete withdrawal), and ASM-free remission. Propensity score matching was then applied to cohorts (withdrawal within first, second, third, fourth, and fifth years) to ensure similar characteristics were present between groups, based on readily available clinical factors associated with seizure relapse after postsurgical ASM withdrawal. All groups were similar after this propensity score matching. Withdrawal in the first or second year was associated with a higher initial seizure relapse risk (hazards ratio (HR) 1.4 and 1.18, respectively, HR 2.2 cumulatively) compared to later withdrawal, with third, fourth, and fifth year withdrawals carrying similar risks of seizure relapse. Importantly, the long-term outcome (defined according to the last year of follow up) did not significantly differ between early and late ASM withdrawals, to the exception of a mildly lower odds of seizure freedom in the group withdrawal in the second postoperative year (relative risk (RR) 0.18, 95% confidence interval 0.90-0.99).

How do these findings impact our clinical practice? To answer this question, one must decide which outcome is the one that patients and their physicians care the most about—immediate seizure relapse risk (which was affected by timing of withdrawal), or long-term outcome (unaffected by timing of withdrawal)? Since the last year of follow-up data was overall similar regardless of ASM withdrawal timing, one conclusion might be to do it as early as the patient feels comfortable. In fact the authors argue that perhaps early relapse risk can be diagnostically informative. But seizure recurrence likely occurs due to different mechanisms depending on its timing—typically, early seizure recurrence typically stems from incomplete resection of the epileptogenic zone, while late seizure recurrence may be due to de novo epileptogenesis, as evidenced by different patterns of recurrence 7 and alteration of brain transcriptome neuroinflammatory and brain healing or remodeling pathways. 8 Since ASM withdrawal was only initiated in seizure-free patients postepilepsy surgery, one can assume that the epileptogenic zone was likely completely resected. It is possible that early withdrawal patients who experienced a short-term seizure relapse after ASM withdrawal had an incomplete resection, in which the residual epileptogenic zone is responsive to ASMs.

Since the long-term outcomes are similar regardless of timing of ASM withdrawal, one may ask, is there anything to gain from waiting to withdraw ASMs? Depending on presurgical seizure frequency, a certain period of time would need to elapse to establish a clear benefit from surgery, but once enough time has elapsed, there may be benefits to starting earlier weans. Nonseizure outcomes were not examined in this study, but one may question whether rehabilitation after epilepsy surgery can be hampered by ASM burden, and whether functional and cognitive outcomes may be better with earlier withdrawal of ASM. Future studies may aim to answer these additional questions.

The discrepancy between early relapse risk and long-term outcomes begs the question—What happened to the patients who relapsed? Did they simply come back under control with re-initiation of ASMs? Most epileptologists have experienced the infrequent but distressing risk of drug-resistant epilepsy in a patient who had experienced prolonged seizure remission on ASMs then undergoes an unsuccessful ASM wean. In one study of long-term outcomes after ASM withdrawal in seizure-free patients, the probability of remission after the first relapse was 76% at 12 months. 9 In this surgical cohort, one may conclude that early relapse does not similarly jeopardize long-term outcomes. It is possible that medication adherence in a cohort that has pursued epilepsy surgery is higher and that continued breakthrough seizures may be less likely to occur in patients eager to maintain the seizure benefit from epilepsy surgery and restart their prior ASM regimen. Currently, this data seems to tell us that it is ok to take an earlier risk, and that one doesn’t risk letting the horse out of the barn by allowing an initial seizure relapse. It is unclear whether this observational data with propensity score matching will be replaced by prospective interventional studies in the future, but for now, this data is likely good enough to inform counseling in the clinic, and probably, take some pressure off that decision.
