Measuring Perceived Goal Concordant Care Among Patients With Advanced Liver Diseases and Their Caregivers
Authors: Manisha Verma, Victor Navarro, Bryce B. Reeve, Li Lin, Simona Jakab, Marie Bakitas, Annette Totten, Michael Volk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:High-quality care in advanced chronic liver disease requires alignment between patients' goals and preferences and the care delivered. However, no validated instruments exist to measure perceived goal-concordant care (GCC), defined as patients' and caregivers' assessment of the extent to which received care aligns with their preferences.METHODS:We developed a novel dyadic instrument with input from clinicians, researchers, patients with advanced chronic liver disease, and their caregivers. The measure includes 2 domains: (i) goals-of-care conversations (GoCC), a 7-item scale assessing the patients' and caregivers' perception of the extent to which clinicians engaged them in discussion of prognosis, and (ii) care concordant with preferences (CCPs), a 4-item scale measuring the perceived alignment of care with preferences. Patients and caregivers were enrolled as dyads in the Palliative Care for Liver Diseases (PAL-LIVER) trial. Baseline data were used for psychometric evaluation. Factor structure was examined using confirmatory factor analysis and reliability using the Cronbach coefficient. Construct validity was tested by associations with patient satisfaction, quality of life, and caregiver burden.RESULTS:Eight hundred thirty patients and 490 caregivers completed the GCC instrument. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated acceptable fit for GoCC (patient: comparative fit index [CFI] = 0.95, Tucker-Lewis index [TLI] = 0.92, root mean square error of approximation [RMSEA] = 0.12; caregiver: CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.07) and CCP after item refinement (patient: CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.06; caregiver: CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.18). Internal consistency was good (GoCC α = 0.79–0.84; CCP α = 0.82–0.85). CCP scores demonstrated moderate positive correlations with patient satisfaction (r = 0.49; caregiver r = 0.35) and quality of life (r = 0.31; r = 0.25), and small inverse correlations with caregiver burden (r = −0.17; r = −0.22).DISCUSSION:This novel instrument provides initial evidence supporting validity and reliability for measuring perceived GCC in chronic liver disease patients and their caregivers.

INTRODUCTION

Delivering care that aligns with patients' care goals and preferences is a hallmark of high-quality, patient centered care (1,2). Yet, it remains unclear whether clinical care consistently reflects these priorities or whether clinicians effectively facilitate perceived goal-concordant care (GCC). Importantly, GCC encompasses both the process of engaging patients in goals-of-care conversations (GoCC) and the subsequent incorporation of those goals into clinical decision-making (3,4). Despite this conceptual linkage, previous studies have typically measured these components separately, and approaches that capture both components in an integrated manner remain limited (5).

Researchers have traditionally conceptualized GCC as the degree of alignment between stated patient goals and treatments received. Objective assessment of alignment, however, has been limited. Previous approaches have relied on single item measures, or implicit review of medical records comparing formal documentation of goals and independent adjudication of subsequent clinical care received, an approach that is methodologically complex, resource intensive, and often infeasible in real-world clinical settings (6,7).

Clinical practice guidance recommends that GoCC for patients with advanced chronic liver diseases (CLDs) be iterative and revisited at key sentinel events, such as hospitalization, and after determination of liver transplant eligibility or ineligibility (8). However, despite these recommendations, no validated patient-reported instruments exist to measure whether patients perceive GoCC as meaningful or whether the care they receive aligns with their values. This gap limits assessment of current communication practices and impedes rigorous testing of interventions aimed at improving GCC in CLD care.

Measurement efforts are further constrained by methodological challenges (9). Retrospective surveys are vulnerable to recall bias and do not capture perceptions of GoCC processes or care alignment across the course of serious illness (10,11). Postbereavement caregiver surveys may be influenced by limited awareness of patients' goals and preferences, coupled with their own emotional distress, grief, and selection bias (12). Similarly, attempts to compare advance directives documented within electronic medical records with care delivered are hindered by inconsistent and incomplete documentation (13). Prospective matching of stated preferences with subsequent care received is methodologically demanding and often fails to capture patient's evolving priorities, the nuance of clinical decision-making or the degree to which patients trust that their values will guide current or future treatments (14).

To address these gaps, we developed a novel instrument to measure perceived GCC, that assesses both engagement in GoCC and the perceived alignment of care with patients' priorities from the perspectives of patients and caregivers concurrently. This instrument was embedded within the Palliative Care for Liver Diseases (PAL LIVER) trial (NCT03540771), a prospective, cluster-randomized trial comparing specialist-led consultative palliative care (PC) with PC delivered by hepatologists trained in PC for patients with CLD (15,16).

The objective of this study was to describe the development of this instrument and to evaluate its psychometric properties in patients with CLD and their caregivers. Using baseline data, we assessed construct validity by testing prespecified hypotheses regarding the expected direction of associations, i.e., higher perceived GCC scores would be associated with better patient-reported quality of life, greater satisfaction with care, and lower caregiver burden (17). Longitudinal analyses examining responsiveness to intervention and change in perceived GCC over time will be reported separately.

Conceptual framework and instrument development

The concept of perceived GCC has evolved from a narrow focus on legal documentation and discrete end-of-life decisions (e.g., code status) to a broader proactive approach that emphasizes ongoing communication, shared understanding of prognosis and treatment options, and alignment of care with patient priorities throughout the course of serious illness (2,18). Within this framework, alignment is not solely a documented outcome but also a patient perceived experience shaped by quality of communication, trust in clinicians, and longitudinal clinical decision making, particularly while living with the disease (19).

Existing instruments such as the Prognosis and Treatment Preferences Questionnaire assess stated preferences but do not evaluate communication processes or perceived alignment of care delivery (20). Our literature review identified 2 core dimensions of patient-perceived goal-concordant care: (i) communication processes that elicit and clarify patient goals, values, and treatment preferences, and (ii) the extent to which care received is perceived to align with those goals and preferences. These dimensions informed item generation and the development of the 2 subscales. Hence, we conceptualized perceived GCC as comprising 2 distinct but interdependent domains: (i) engagement in GoCC and (ii) care concordant with preferences (CCP) (Figure 1). The first domain reflects the processes, i.e., communication about prognosis and preference elicitation, whereas the second captures patients' perception of whether current care, and anticipated future care, reflects those preferences.

Conceptual framework.

The instrument was developed using a multistep, stakeholder-engaged approach incorporating input from patients and caregivers to ensure relevance, comprehensiveness, and clarity, consistent with Consensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments guidance (21) and best practices in patient-reported measure development (22–25). An initial item pool was generated by a multidisciplinary team of hepatologists, PC clinicians, and health services researchers, informed by literature review, conceptual mapping, and input from patients and caregivers (serving as research advisors). Item domains were grounded in previous work on serious illness communication, shared decision-making, and goal-concordant care (26–28).

The preliminary questionnaire was pilot tested with 3 stakeholder groups (n = 6–9 per group), including primary care patients, hepatology patients, and caregivers. Participants provided structured written feedback on clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness, followed by semi-structured virtual group discussions. The team reviewed feedback and revised through consensus. Based on stakeholder input, a caregiver version was developed to reflect the dyadic study design, and both versions were retested to confirm clarity and content validity. Response formats were selected to align with construct measurement: GoCC items use a 3-point ordinal scale (No, Yes, somewhat, Yes, definitely) to capture gradations of engagement while minimizing respondent burden; whereas CCP items use 0–10 numeric rating scales to allow finer discrimination in perceived alignment and enhance sensitivity to longitudinal change. One CCP item was removed during psychometric evaluation (see Results section), resulting in a 4-item final CCP scale.

The near-final dyadic instrument underwent additional review by members of the PC learning network (expert palliative care researchers who were conducting large scale PC trials) to ensure alignment with established PC principles (29,30). Although a formal Delphi process was not used, iterative structured feedback and multidisciplinary consensus methods were used to strengthen content validity.

METHODS

Design and population

This study used data from adult patients with CLD, and their caregivers enrolled across 19 academic and community-based hepatology practices in the PAL-LIVER trial between January 2019 and September 2024. This study was registered on clinicaltrials.gov with approval obtained from all appropriate institutional review boards. Further details of the PAL-LIVER protocol and primary analysis have been published (17). All participants (patients and caregivers) were consented before any study procedures. The study enrolled dyads and patients alone. Patients and caregivers completed the GCC instrument at baseline, 3 months, and at 6, 9, and 12 months. Nonrandomized baseline data for participants enrolled in this trial were used for this psychometric analysis.

Patients enrolled in this trial were adults with decompensated cirrhosis with at least 1 complication of cirrhosis (i.e., presence of ascites, variceal bleed or hepatic encephalopathy) within the past 6 months, or patients with hepatocellular cancer. Eligible caregivers were individuals older than 18 years of age who could read and understand English and were providing direct care for at least 10 hours per week. Caregivers were identified by patients and were consented separately.

Additional data collected

Baseline demographics and medical history were obtained from participants after consent. Caregivers self-reported their demographics, relationship to patients, employment status, and duration of caregiving. To assess construct validity, we examined associations with following measures, which were collected as a part of the parent trial:

Patient satisfaction with care was assessed using the Family Satisfaction with Cancer Care-Patient scale (FAMCARE-P 13), a brief validated 13-item instrument evaluating experiences with outpatient PC. It captures 4 key domains including information sharing (e.g., prognosis discussion), availability of care (e.g., answering questions), physical care (e.g., symptom assessment, referral to specialists), and psychosocial support (e.g., inclusion of family in decision making) (31,32). The scale has a unidimensional structure with high reliability (33). Higher scores reflect better patient experience (range 1–65).

Patient-reported quality of life was measured using the functional assessment of cancer therapy-hepatobiliary (FACT-Hep), a 45-item instrument assessing overall Quality of Life in patients with advanced liver disease (34). Higher scores reflect better quality of life (range 0–180) (35).

Caregiver Burden was assessed using the Zarit Burden interview-12 (ZBI-12), a widely used validated 12-item instrument with strong internal consistency and construct validity in advanced illness populations (36). Higher scores reflect higher burden (range 0–48).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics and classical psychometric methods were used to evaluate item and scale performance. Item-level analyses included response distributions, interitem correlation matrices, item-total correlations, and changes in the Cronbach alpha if an item were deleted. These quantitative metrics, together with assessment of item clarity, relevance, and conceptual alignment, informed decisions regarding item retention.

We then conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the hypothesized 2-domain structure (GoCC and CCP), evaluating patient-reported and caregiver-reported versions separately. Given the ordinal response scales, models were estimated using polychoric correlations with a weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted estimator, which is robust to non-normality. Model fit was assessed using standard indices: comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) ≥0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) <0.08, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) <0.08, indicating acceptable fit.

For the patient-reported CCP scale, item 5 (worry) was reverse scored because of inverse labeling of response options in the survey.

After confirmation of unidimensionality within each domain, scale scores were calculated as the mean of nonmissing item responses. Higher scores indicated greater engagement in GoC conversations and greater perceived alignment of care with patient preferences, respectively. Internal consistency and reliability was assessed using the Cronbach alpha (unstandardized estimates), with values ≥0.70 considered acceptable.

Construct validity was evaluated using prespecified hypotheses regarding the direction of associations between GCC scores and related constructs. Correlations were examined with patient satisfaction (FAMCARE-P 13), quality of life (FACT-Hep), and caregiver burden (Zarit Burden Interview-12). We hypothesized that higher scores would be positively associated with satisfaction and quality of life and inversely associated with caregiver burden because care that reflects patients' values and preferences may contribute to improved patient and caregiver experiences. Correlation magnitudes were interpreted using conventional thresholds (0.10–0.29 small, 0.30–0.49 moderate, ≥0.50 large) (37,38). Participants were included in the analyses if they completed at least 1 of the 2 scales. For scale-specific analyses, participants were required to complete at least 50% of the items on a given scale to allow score calculation. Application of this criterion resulted in a smaller sample size for analyses involving the GoCC scale.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

A total of 935 patients and 559 caregivers were enrolled in the PAL-LIVER study (Tables 1 and 2). The sample for this analysis was restricted to 830 patients and 490 caregivers who completed at least 1 of the GCC scales at baseline. Mean age of patients was 63 (SD = 10) years, 72% men, 79% White, with 30% Medicare and 15% Medicaid beneficiaries, 38% with alcohol-related liver disease, 24% Chronic hepatitis C, and 27% with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Twenty-eight percent had hepatocellular cancer at the time of enrollment. Mean age of caregivers was 58 (SD 14) years, 77% women, 44% retired, and 53% spouses. Mean (SD) duration of caregiving was 5.4 (8.69) years.

Factor structure, reliability, and score distribution

Goals-of-Care Conversations (GoCC) scale

The distribution of average scores across the patient-reported and caregiver-reported versions of the GoCC scale is shown in the upper half of Figure 2. The distribution shows high ratings near the score of 5, which reflects a strong endorsement of the yes, somewhat rating, with a relatively even balance of respondents above and below that rating (see Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/CTG/B580). For the patient version, the floor and ceiling effects (as defined by the min score 0 and max score of 10) were 8.3% and 3.8%, respectively. For the caregiver version, the floor and ceiling effects were 15.7% and 3.6%, respectively. The correlation between the patient's and caregiver's ratings for GoCC scale is modest (r = 0.30).

Score distributions of the goals of care and care concordant with preferences scales.

CFA supported a unidimensional structure for the 7-item GoCC scale. Model fit indices were acceptable across most indices for the patient-reported version (CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.92, SRMR = 0.04, RMSEA = 0.12), and all indices for the caregiver-reported version (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.03, RMSEA = 0.07). Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.55 to 0.82 (patients) and 0.70 to 0.82 (caregivers). Internal consistency and reliability was acceptable, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.79 for patients and 0.84 for caregivers.

Care concordant with preferences scale

The distribution of average scores across the patient and caregiver versions of the instrument is shown in the lower half of Figure 2. The distribution is right-skewed, indicating generally high perceived alignment of care with preferences for both patients and caregivers (see Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/CTG/B580). For the patient version, the floor and ceiling effects are 1.3% and 31.4%, respectively. For the caregiver version, the floor and ceiling effects are 1.3% and 23.8%, respectively. Patient–caregiver ratings were moderately correlated (r = 0.51).

An initial unidimensional CFA including all 5 items demonstrated suboptimal performance for item 5 (How worried are you that your medical care will not match what is important to you if you get sicker?), with low standardized loadings (0.23 for patients; 0.51 for caregivers) compared with the remaining items (0.56–0.88 for patients; 0.71–0.87 for caregivers). Based on these findings, item 5 was removed.

The revised 4-item CCP scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach alpha = 0.82 for patients; 0.85 for caregivers). One-factor model fit indices were acceptable across all statistics for the patient-reported version (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.02, RMSEA = 0.06) and were acceptable for almost all indices for the caregiver-reported version (CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.91, SRMR = 0.03, RMSEA = 0.18).

For both patient-reported and caregiver-reported versions, GoCC scores demonstrated small positive correlations with satisfaction with care (FAMCARE-P 13; P < 0.01), but no association with quality of life (FACT-Hep) or caregiver burden (Zarit Burden interview-12).

In contrast, the CCP scale demonstrated associations consistent with a priori hypotheses. The patient-reported CCP scale showed moderate positive correlations with satisfaction (r = 0.49) and quality of life (r = 0.31), and a weak inverse correlation with caregiver burden (all P < 0.01). The caregiver-reported CCP scale demonstrated similar patterns of association.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we present initial evidence supporting the initial validity and reliability of a novel, pragmatic instrument to assess perceived GCC from the perspectives of both patients with advanced liver disease and their caregivers. Both unidimensional scales demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, supporting their use as concise (7-item GoCC and 4-item CCP) measures of perceived GCC that remain feasible for clinical and research settings. Importantly, patient-reported and caregiver-reported scores were moderately correlated, suggesting shared but not identical perceptions of care experiences. This underscores the importance of capturing both perspectives in serious illness care, particularly in CLD where caregivers are frequently involved in decision-making and care coordination.

Unlike existing metrics that rely primarily on clinician documentation or retrospective chart review, this instrument captures the lived experience of communication and care alignment as perceived by patients and caregivers. This distinction is especially relevant in transplant hepatology, where clinicians often initiate or facilitate discussions regarding prognosis, treatment tradeoffs, and goals of care, particularly when liver transplantation is not an option. In such contexts, documentation of GoCC may not reflect whether patients and caregivers feel their values are understood or meaningfully incorporated into clinical decision-making. By directly measuring perceived GCC, this tool provides a practical approach to evaluating both communication processes and perceived outcomes of care, with potential utility for improving value-aligned care delivery.

CFA provided mixed evidence for model fit. Although CFI, TLI, and SRMR values generally supported the proposed 1-factor structures for both the GoCC and CCP scales, RMSEA values exceeded conventional thresholds for acceptable fit in the patient GoCC and caregiver CCP models. These findings suggest potential model misfit; however, interpretation is complicated by the small number of items in each scale (4-item GoCC; 7-item CCP), which results in low degrees of freedom where RMSEA is known to be less stable and may overstate lack of fit (39,40). Taken together, the CFA results provide preliminary support for the proposed single-factor structure, but further validation in larger and independent samples is recommended.

Construct validity evidence differed between the 2 subscales. The CCP scale demonstrated associations with quality of life, satisfaction with care, and caregiver burden that were consistent with our prespecified hypotheses, providing support for its validity as a measure of perceived alignment of care with patient goals and preferences. In contrast, the GoCC scale was associated only with satisfaction with care and showed no meaningful association with quality of life or caregiver burden. This differential pattern of associations may reflect important conceptual and measurement distinctions between the 2 constructs. The GoCC scale captures communication processes related to discussions of goals, values, and treatment preferences, whereas the CCP scale reflects patients' perceptions that the care they receive is aligned with those preferences. As a more proximal construct, GoCC may be less directly related to downstream outcomes such as quality of life and caregiver burden than perceived alignment of care, which more directly reflects treatment experiences and decisions. In addition, communication processes may vary in depth and effectiveness, and their impact on patient-reported and caregiver-reported outcomes may depend on whether discussions translate into meaningful changes in care (41). These findings support the clinical relevance of the GoCC scale while suggesting that its construct validity evidence is preliminary and warrants further evaluation. In fact, our findings arguably support the idea that the 2 scales are measuring related but distinct aspects of GCC rather than being redundant measures of the same construct.

The removal of the worry item from the CCP scale was data-driven and resulted in improved psychometric performance. Notably, the weaker factor loading and inconsistent contribution of worry item to model fit suggest that it may represent a distinct construct, such as anticipatory distress or illness-related anxiety, rather than perceived care concordance. However, it must be noted that the item on the patient version had response options ordered inconsistently with other items in the scale, which may have caused some confusion among respondents. Future work, with a more consistent response scale, should reevaluate the contribution of the worry item to the CCP domain or as a separate domain because it may hold independent clinical relevance.

Clinical implications

Our findings have several important implications for hepatology practice. First, the CCP scale offers a feasible approach to measuring whether patients perceive their care as aligned with their goals and preferences, an outcome increasingly recognized as a core component of high-quality serious illness care yet rarely assessed in routine practice. In hepatology, where disease trajectories are often unpredictable and clinical decisions frequently involve tradeoffs between life-prolonging therapies and quality of life, understanding perceived alignment between care and patient values is particularly critical.

Second, the instrument may support quality improvement efforts within hepatology programs. Although many institutions have implemented initiatives to increase GoCC, evaluation is often limited to documentation rates or process metrics (42). A patient and caregiver-reported measure enables a shift toward outcome-oriented assessment, allowing programs to determine whether communication initiatives translate into care experiences that patients and families actually perceive as value-concordant.

Finally, the brevity and pragmatic design of the scale enhance its suitability for clinical research. Because integrated hepatology-PC models continue to expand, there is a growing need for validated patient-reported outcome measures that capture meaningful aspects of communication quality and decision-making. This instrument may therefore serve as a useful end point in clinical trials and implementation studies evaluating interventions designed to improve serious illness communication, shared decision-making, and supportive care in advanced liver disease.

Contribution to the field

The significance of our findings is multifold. First, to our knowledge, this is the first instrument assessing 2 key aspects of perceived GCC from patients with CLD and their caregivers simultaneously (17). Existing metrics heavily rely on clinician documentation or retrospective chart reviews, which do not capture the lived experiences or the quality of GoC discussions from patient and caregiver perspectives (43). Notably, few patient and caregiver items were intentionally tailored to reflect the distinct roles and experiences of each respondent group. Consequently, the measures capture complementary rather than identical perspectives. Second, by focusing on patient and caregiver perspectives, this tool addresses an important measurement gap and provides a scalable framework for evaluating serious illness communication and care alignment. Although developed in a cohort of patients with CLD, the instrument was designed to be applicable across serious illnesses. The inclusion of participants from both veterans affairs (VAs) and non-VA academic centers enhances external validity. Nevertheless, further validation in other populations, including cancer, heart failure, and kidney disease, will be important to establish generalizability. Given that most participants were not at the end of life, future studies should also evaluate the instrument among patients with more limited prognosis, where discordance between patients' values and care received may be more pronounced.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, psychometric testing was conducted in a baseline cohort of patients with CLD and their caregivers, which may limit generalizability to other hepatology populations and stages of illness. Future studies should evaluate performance in broader cohorts, including patients earlier in the disease trajectory and those undergoing evaluation for liver transplantation.

Second, although the present study provides initial evidence supporting the internal consistency and construct validity of the GCC measures, additional psychometric evaluation is needed. Future studies should assess test-retest reliability, responsiveness to change, and other aspects of construct validity to further establish the measurement properties and clinical utility of these scales across diverse patient populations and settings.

Third, a potential limitation of the CCP scale is the clustering of responses at the upper end of the scale, with a substantial proportion of respondents selecting the highest response category. This pattern may reflect genuinely high perceptions of CCP in the study population, but it also raises the possibility of a ceiling effect. Ceiling effects may limit the ability of the scale to detect improvement over time and could attenuate associations with other measures. Further studies are needed to evaluate the responsiveness of the CCP scale and its performance in populations with a broader range of experiences with goal-concordant care.

Finally, as with all self-reported measures, responses may be subject to recall and response bias. In addition, the instrument reflects patient and caregiver perceptions, which may differ from clinician-reported assessments or documentation of care plans. However, this is also a key strength, as GCC is inherently defined by the degree to which care aligns with patient values; therefore, capturing patient and caregiver perspectives is essential. Integrating this measure with clinician and documentation-based metrics in future studies may help identify sources of discordance and opportunities for improvement.

This patient and caregiver-reported instrument provides initial evidence for a brief, pragmatic measure of perceived GCC in CLD. By capturing care experiences of communication and care alignment that may not be reflected in clinical documentation, it offers a patient-centered perspective on care quality. With further validation, it may support quality improvement and integrated hepatology-supportive care models focused on aligning care with patient and family priorities.
