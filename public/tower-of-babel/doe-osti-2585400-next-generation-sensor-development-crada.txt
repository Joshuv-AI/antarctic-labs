B. Project Scope
This was a collaborative effort between Lawrence Livermore National Security, LLC (LLNS), as
manager and operator of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and The Federal
Reserve Bank of San Francisco ("FRBSF" or "Participant"), to research and develop advanced
processing techniques to measure the fitness and authenticity of U.S. currency.

The FRBSF previously partnered with LLNL to evaluate end-of-life predictions, review sensor
requirements, develop communications protocols, and perform thermal analysis. These Strategic
Partnership Projects (SPP) included SPP No. L20960 - Federal Reserve Currency Technology
Office Studies, SPP No. L15900 - Development of a Common Detector Interface (CDI 2.0)
Specification and Hardware Simulators, and SPP No. L15610 - Limited Design Review of a
Second-Generation E-Material Authentication Sensor (EMAS2).

C. Technical Accomplishments
The major technical accomplishments during the project are as follows:
 • LLNL developed machine learning-based techniques and algorithms to detect the fitness
 of currency.
 • LLNL successfully designed and fabricated a unique, first of its kind hyperspectral
 camera system.
 • LLNL evaluated the performance and feasibility of the developed hyperspectral camera
 system as it pertains to FRB applications.

Machine Learning-based Fitness Detection
LLNL developed a machine learning-based algorithm to classify notes as soiled or unsoiled. The
algorithm is comprised of four stages:

CRADA TC02281 Page 4

 1. Given an input image of a note, the algorithm simulates a spatially equivalent unsoiled
 output image. This step of the algorithm is completed by a sub-algorithm known as the
 “scrubber” (Figure 1).
 2. The algorithm calculates the difference between the input image and the simulated output
 image.
 3. A threshold is applied to the difference to determine if the note in the input image should
 be classified as soiled or unsoiled.

 Figure 1: Illustration of the scrubber algorithm.

The difference between the input and output images can be quantified with different methods.
Initially, a difference map is created by taking a pixel-for-pixel difference between the two
images (Figure 2).

 Figure 2: Generation of a difference map from the input and simulated output images.

Initially, the mean value of the difference map was used to quantify the level of soiling, and a
threshold was set to create a distinction between soiled and unsoiled notes. Figure 3 shows the
full fitness detection algorithm for four notes – one unsoiled and three of various soil levels and
quality.

CRADA TC02281 Page 5

 Figure 3: Mean difference used to classify soiled and unsoiled notes.

During initial algorithm testing, it was found that mean difference was not adequate in
quantifying various levels of soiling. To better position the algorithm to classify various levels of
fitness and soiling, the structural similarity index measure (SSIM) was used to quantify the
difference between the input and output images of the scrubber. An illustration of the benefits of
more accurately quantifying differences between two images using SSIM versus more basic
methods, such as mean square error (MSE), can be seen in Figure 4.

Figure 4: Illustration of the benefits of SSIM in quantifying the "quality" of difference between two images as compared to MSE.

The algorithm was trained and tested using data collected from an LLNL-developed grayscale
camera system integrated on test transport system at the FRB Dallas branch. Due to the amount
of data needed to train the algorithm and the limited availability of notes that have been labeled

CRADA TC02281 Page 6

and separated into various fitness levels, LLNL focused on testing the feasibility of the algorithm
using two classes of notes: circulated and uncirculated. The algorithm shows strong performance
in detecting the differences between circulated and uncirculated notes. Furthermore, LLNL was
able to show that less than 1000 training notes were needed to train the algorithm to reach
desired performance – a significant achievement as there were concerns on the feasibility of the
algorithm with respect to required training. Additionally, the performance of the algorithm on
uncirculated and circulated notes indicates that the algorithm will be well fit to classify notes of
various fitness and/or soil levels. Figure 5 shows the performance of the algorithm as it pertains
to the number of notes it has been trained on, with a bigger difference between the uncirculated
and circulated lines showing greater performance. The algorithm was able to classify
uncirculated and circulated notes for the various denominations and series (Figure 6) with strong
performance.

  Figure 5:SSIM value of circulated versus uncirculated notes versus the number of notes that the algorithm is trained with.

 Figure 6: SSIM values of circulated and uncirculated notes for different denominations and series.

Hyperspectral Camera System
LLNL designed and fabricated a protype hyperspectral camera system to test the feasibility and
viability of such a camera system for FRB applications. The overall outcome of this effort was a
protype sensor that is unique in technical capabilities and application. The system was designed
(Figure 7 and Figure 8) to meet the requirements (Figure 9) agreed upon by LLNL and CTO, and
to serve as both a bench-top prototype for testing and evaluation as well as a system that could be
integrated onto a Cash Office system. While the initial plan was to have the camera system
fabricated after 18-24 months of the project start, significant delays due to the COVID-19
pandemic and optics vendor issues resulted in a total project delay of 24 months.

 Figure 7: Design of the hyperspectral camera.

 Figure 8: Camera system block diagram.

 Figure 9: Hyperspectral camera system requirements target and as-designed.

The fabricated camera system meets the desired performance and is able to produce
hyperspectral image cubes (Figure 10) from collected data. The prototype sensor was integrated
onto an LLNL-developed Transport Simulator for testing and data collection in an FRB-like
environment.

 Figure 10: Illustration of image cube data extraction code (left) and an example image cube (right).

Initially, camera system testing was intended to occur at a Cash Office facility. However, due to
delays and the COVID-19 pandemic, the testing was conducted at LLNL using borrowed
currency from FRB. Data was collected over various currency denominations and image cubes
were constructed. The image cubes were then analyzed to understand the viability of a
hyperspectral sensor for the characterization of note fitness. Figure 11 demonstrates the ability of
the sensor to measure differences in fit and unfit currency.

Summary of Results and Lessons Learned
LLNL was successful in evaluating advanced techniques that apply to the measurement of
currency fitness and authenticity. During the project, LLNL successfully:
 • Developed, trained, and evaluated a machine learning-based algorithm for classifying
 currency fitness.
 • Designed and documented a hyperspectral camera system for FRB applications.
 • Fabricated a prototype camera system.
 • Evaluated the performance of the camera system.
 • Evaluated the feasibility of the camera system, including how a camera system could be
 integrated into existing systems with regards to required lighting, thermal dissipation,
 size, weight, power, and data interfaces.

D. Specific Benefits

Benefits to DOE
The project has provided several benefits to the DOE community:
 • Development and patented algorithm with broad applications for image authentication
 and classification.
 • Development of and experience designing, fabricating, integrating, and operating a
 unique hyperspectral camera system with capabilities for new application areas
 previously prohibitive to hyperspectral systems.

Benefits to Industry
No direct benefit, too low of a TRL. It is a first or second step towards a viable system (next
generation of camera system) which is when a next gen system is desired. Reduced from R&D to
engineering challenges. Moved from R&D engineering.

D. Expected Economic Impact

The goal of this partnership was to develop sensing techniques to be incorporated into the
banknote sorting machines that extend the capabilities of the Federal Reserve System to
efficiently confirm U.S. currency authenticity and fit for commerce and distribution by financial
institutions to circulate within the worldwide general public.

 • Requirements of existing and desired future systems.
 • Preliminary data for algorithm development and camera system benchmarking.
 • Test facilities, systems, and currency for data collection.
 • Advise and consultation on system integration and concept of operations.
