Pomalidomide significantly enhances the antitumor immune efficacy of dendritic cell-derived exosomes from multiple myeloma patients
Authors: Hu Zhang, Yifan Wang, Xueqing Wang, Hailing Duan, Duonan Yu, Xiaochuan Kuang, Lin He, Wenyi Zhang, Jie Chen, Jingying Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the effect and mechanism of pomalidomide on the immune activity of dendritic cell-derived exosomes (DEXs) from patients with multiple myeloma (MM).MethodsMoDCs were induced from monocytes obtained from patients with MM and HDs with or without pomalidomide treatment. DEXs were then isolated from culture supernatants via ultracentrifugation and characterized by TEM and NTA. Surface markers (CD63, CD81, CD80, CD86, and HLA-DR) were analyzed using nanoflow cytometry. Functional assays included coculturing DEXs with autologous PBMCs to activate T cells, followed by sorting CD8+ T cells and assessing their pro-apoptotic effect on U266 cells via flow cytometry. Mechanistic insights were explored through miRNA sequencing of DEXs and transcriptome sequencing of T cells.ResultsPomalidomide significantly increased the proportion of DEXs derived from patients with multiple myeloma (MM) and increased the expression levels and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of surface activation markers, including CD80, CD86 and HLA-DR. Functional assays demonstrated that DEXs treated with pomalidomide promoted the generation of CD8+ T cells and markedly increased both early and total apoptosis rates in U266 cells. Mechanistically, miRNAomic analysis indicated that pomalidomide potentially facilitates T-cell activation by modulating signaling pathways such as the MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathways. Transcriptome profiling further verified that this treatment triggered substantial metabolic reprogramming in T cells and significantly activated pathways associated with T-cell activation.ConclusionsThis study provides a new strategy for enhancing the activity of DEXs derived from patients with MM, with the potential to develop DEXs as an antitumor vaccine for MM.Keywords: Pomalidomide, Dendritic cell-derived exosomes, Multiple myeloma, Antitumor immune efficacy, T cell

Background

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells in the bone marrow. Its incidence ranks second among hematological malignancies [1]. In recent years, the application of proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory agents, and monoclonal antibodies has significantly improved treatment outcomes and survival in MM patients [2]. However, although significant improvements in treatment response rates and progression-free survival are achieved when these novel agents are used, the majority of patients eventually experience disease relapse [3]. One of the most important factors is immune deficiency/dysfunction [4–6]. Enhancing autologous anti-tumor immunity through immunotherapeutic strategies represents a fundamental approach to prevent disease relapse and even achieve a cure, which thus emerged as a key research focus in the field of MM.

Dendritic cells (DCs), as the most potent professional antigen-presenting cells, play a pivotal role in initiating specific immune responses. In multiple myeloma, however, DCs become immunosuppressive and dysfunctional, characterized by a reduced number and activity of DCs [7]. A previous study by our group revealed that pomalidomide could significantly increase DC activity/maturation in patients with MM [8]. Nevertheless, DC-based immunotherapy still faces several limitations that need to be addressed. The survival time of DCs in vitro is short (approximately 7 days), and their storage is difficult [9]. Peripheral blood is frequently collected from patients and standardization of the preparation process is lacking [10]. In addition, the cost is high (up to 380,000 RMB per treatment), and laboratories must comply with stringent GMP standards. Therefore, overcoming these limitations of DC-based immunotherapy remains an urgent and important challenge.

DC-derived exosomes (DEXs) are extracellular vesicles secreted by DCs with diameters between 30 and 150 nm that carry some of the same genetic information, signaling molecules and biological functions as those of DCs [11] and are capable of transmitting antigenic and costimulatory signals to activate T cells, a feature that makes DEXs an alternative to DCs to a certain extent for stimulating T-cell activation [12, 13]. Compared with DCs, DEXs are highly stable, can be stored at −80 °C for 6 months [9], are easy to standardize [14], can reduce costs, can improve patient tolerance and compliance, and are expected to become cell-free vaccines with antigen presentation and immune cell activation functions. However, the function of DEXs should be based on the normal or high activity of DEXs. Based on the results of a previous study showing that pomalidomide increases the activity/maturity of DCs in patients with MM, we propose that pomalidomide can also increase the activity of DEXs in patients with MM and produce highly activated DEXs that can exert significant immune response effects.

Pomalidomide, a third-generation immunomodulatory drug (IMiD), exhibits not only potent antitumour efficacy but also significant immunomodulatory properties [15]. It can activate natural killer cells and T lymphocytes while simultaneously suppressing regulatory T cells [16, 17]. Pomalidomide enhances the activity/maturation of DCs derived from mice, healthy humans and patients with MM. However, the modulatory effect of pomalidomide on MM patient-derived DEXs is unknown; therefore, this study, for the first time, investigated the use of pomalidomide as an immune adjuvant to increase the activity of DEXs from patients with MM. In addition, we will evaluate the efficacy of DEXs as a cell-free vaccine against myeloma. This study provides supportive data for the potential application of DEX as a novel cell-free vaccine for MM therapy.

Methods

Donors

In this study, 11 patients with MM and 10 healthy donors (HDs) with ages ranging from 50 to 80 years were included. All the donors had white blood cell counts in peripheral blood ≥ 3.5 × 10^9/L and monocyte counts in peripheral blood ≥ 0.10 × 10^9/L. Patients with congenital or acquired immunodeficiency disorders were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. All the donors signed informed consent forms. The selection of healthy donor samples for omics analysis was primarily based on practical and ethical considerations regarding sample requirements. DEX miRNA sequencing necessitated the collection of a large volume of peripheral blood (200 mL) from a single donor to extract DEXs, thereby obtaining sufficient and high-quality nucleic acids. For patients with MM who were undergoing treatment with generally compromised physical conditions, the additional collection of such a large amount of blood beyond routine diagnostic blood draws poses significant safety risks and ethical concerns. In contrast, healthy donors can safely tolerate this procedure. Therefore, to ensure the feasibility and safety of obtaining adequate materials for deep sequencing analysis, we used samples from healthy donors in mechanomics studies.

Reagents and equipment

Pomalidomide was obtained from Sigma (United States). Human lymphocyte separation medium (Ficoll) was obtained from GE Lifesciences, United States. Human AB serum was obtained from GEMINI (United States). IL-4 and GM-CSF were obtained from Novoprotein (China). Penicillin‒streptomycin was obtained from HyClone (United States). Anti-human CD83, CD40, CD86, and CD80 antibodies, and Pacific Blue anti-human HLA-DR antibody were obtained from BioLegend (United States). Anti-human CD63 and CD81 antibodies were obtained from BD (United States). An inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) was used. An ST16 centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) was used. An Optima XE-90 Ultracentrifuge and Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman, United States) were used.

Generating DEXs in vitro

Peripheral blood (20 ml) from each MM donor was centrifuged using density gradient centrifugation to isolate PBMCs. The PBMC layer was collected and resuspended in 1× PBS. The washing step was repeated twice, and the supernatant was discarded. The PBMCs were evenly divided into two groups. Each group was cultured in basic medium supplemented with RPMI 1640, 2 mmol/L L-glutamine, 5% human serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin at 37℃ with 5% CO₂. After 24 hours, the adherent cells were retained, while the nonadherent cells were immediately cryopreserved. Adherent cells were cultured for 7 days in DC medium supplemented with RPMI 1640, 2 mmol/L glutamine, 5% human serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin, 800 U/mL GM-CSF, and 500 U/mL IL-4. Concurrently, the A group received 10 μM pomalidomide as the experimental group, while the other group received an equivalent volume of drug solvent (DMSO) as the control group. On Day 5, both groups were supplemented with the MART10-03 peptide, which is an antigen peptide of myeloma cells with the specific amino acid sequence p-Glu-Leu-Ala-Gly-Ile-Gly-Ile-Leu-Thr-Val-OH and cultured for an additional 2 days. On Day 8, the supernatant was collected for DEX extraction.

Separation and extraction of DEXs by ultracentrifugation

The supernatant from the DC cultures on Day 8 in Sect. 2.3 was collected into centrifuge tubes. It was then centrifuged three times at 300×g for 10 minutes, 1500×g for 10 minutes and 10,000×g for 30 minutes at 4℃. The centrifuge tubes were moved to a tube rack on an ice box. The centrifuged supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter and collected in a prewashed ultracentrifuge tube with 1× PBS. Strict balancing was performed using filtered 1× PBS with an error of <0.01 g. After balancing, ultracentrifugation was conducted at 120,000×g and 4℃ for 70 minutes. The obtained precipitate was resuspended in filtered 1× PBS. Ultracentrifugation was repeated at 120,000×g and 4℃ for 60 minutes. The supernatant was discarded. The resulting precipitate (i.e., DEXs) was resuspended in filtered 1× PBS to a volume between 1/1000 and 1/200 of the initial sample volume.

Characterization of exosomes and detection of surface markers

TEM for exosome morphology Analysis

Ten microliters of the ultracentrifuged exosome suspension was placed onto a 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grid. After 1 minute of adsorption, 10 μL of 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 6.8) staining solution was added for negative staining for 120 seconds. Excess phosphotungstic acid solution was removed by filter paper. The copper grid was then placed in a clean environment and air-dried naturally. After sample drying, its morphology was observed under a transmission electron microscope at a magnification of 50,000–100,000×g with an acceleration voltage of 100 kV.

Detection of exosome particle size and concentration by NTA

Exosome samples were rapidly thawed in a water bath at 25 °C and then handled on ice. The samples were serially diluted (usually 1:100–1:1000) with 0.22 μm-filtered 1× PBS. They were further filtered through a 0.1 μm filter to remove aggregates. A nanoparticle analyzer was used for detection. For each sample, 5 videos (60 seconds each) were collected with an injection volume of 1 mL and a pump speed of 30 AU. The resulting data were processed using NTA software for distribution histogram analysis.

Nanoflow cytometric detection of exosomal surface markers

Nanoflow cytometry was used to detect exosomal surface identification markers (CD63 and CD81) and activity-associated markers (CD80, CD86, and HLA-DR). A total of 100 μL of the exosome suspension was removed. It was coincubated with anti-CD63, anti-CD81, anti-CD80, anti-CD86 and anti-HLA-DR antibodies. The samples were incubated at 4℃ for 25–30 minutes in the dark. After incubation, the mixture was washed once with 1× PBS. Unbound antibodies were removed by centrifugation at 120,000×g for 70 minutes. The exosomes were then resuspended in 300 μL of PBS. Stained exosomes were detected using a nanoflow cytometer. Data analysis was performed with FlowJo 10.8.1 software. Gating was set to exclude particles with a diameter less than 50 nm. T-cell-mediated killing of the MM cell line U266 after DEX stimulation

Two hundred milliliters of blood from healthy donors was collected and isolated to obtain PBMCs. Some PBMCs were used to produce DEXs, while the remaining PBMCs were used as T cell resources. PBMCs, which are used as the source of T cells, were coincubated with DEXs in T-cell culture medium for 7 days. After T-cell activation was induced, the mixed cells were collected. CD8+ T cells were isolated from the mixed cells by flow sorting. They were cocultured with U266 cells to evaluate the efficacy of T-cell killing. Detection of T-cell killing activity: A portion of the CD8+ T cells was mixed with target cells (myeloma cells line U266) at an effector-to-target ratio of 7:1. The mixture was added to 48-well plates with 3 replicate wells. After being incubated for 1.5 hours in a cell incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, the cells were collected. The apoptosis rate of target cells was detected by flow cytometry using Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining. Another portion of CD8+ T cells was mixed with target cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 7:1 to prepare a 240 μL cell suspension. The suspension was added to a confocal dish. Continuous observation was performed under a confocal microscope for 2 hours. Videos of T cells killing tumor cells were captured.

miRNAomics of DEXs

MoDCs were produced from 200 ml of blood collected from healthy donors (n = 10). Exosomes were isolated from moDC culture supernatants from 200 ml of blood from healthy donors, and DEXs were used for total RNA extraction. High-quality RNA samples were used to construct small RNA libraries with Illumina TruSeq kits and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000/2500 platform (1 × 50 bp)（LC-BIO Co., Ltd (HangZhou, China). After low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed, the clean reads were analyzed with ACGT101-miR to detect the miRNAs. Differentially expressed miRNAs (|log₂FC|>1, p < 0.05) were identified and visualized by PCA and volcano plots. Target genes were predicted using TargetScan and miRanda, and their associations were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

RNA-seq of T cells

CD8+ T cells were isolated by flow cytometry from PBMCs stimulated with DEXs from 200 ml of blood collected from healthy donors (n = 3). Total RNA was extracted, and its quality was verified before library construction. PolyA-enriched mRNA was fragmented, reverse-transcribed into strand‑specific cDNA, and used to prepare sequencing libraries. Paired‑end sequencing (PE150) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. After quality control with fastp, the clean reads were aligned to the GRCh38 genome using HISAT2. Transcript assembly and FPKM quantification were conducted with StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (|log₂FC|>1, p < 0.05) were identified by edgeR and subsequently subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using DAVID.

Identification of DEXs

DEXs were evaluated for morphology, particle size, concentration and identification-associated markers. The morphology of the exosomes was observed by transmission electron microscopy. The exosomes are cup shaped or disc shaped and are small vesicles with an obvious phospholipid bilayer (Fig. 2A). The particle size and concentration of the exosomes were analyzed by NTA. The particle size of the exosomes ranged mainly from 50 nm to 150 nm, with a peak particle size occurring at 100 nm. The particle concentration of the exosomes was 3.78 × 10^6 particles/ml (Fig. 2B). In addition, we detected the expression of the exosome markers CD63 and CD81 by nanoflow analysis. The exosomes highly expressed CD81 and CD63, which confirmed the successful isolation of the exosomes (Fig. 2C–D).

Pomalidomide enhances the antitumor immune response effect of DEXs

DEXs treated with pomalidomide were used to further stimulate T-cell activation and ultimately improve the efficacy of immune killing against myeloma cells. First, during the process of DC generation, a multiple myeloma-specific antigen, MART-1 peptide, was loaded into DCs for the preparation of tumor antigen-presenting exosomes. Subsequently, the exosomes were cocultured with autologous PBMCs to stimulate T-cell activation and proliferation. Finally, the antitumor immune killing efficacy of the T cells was evaluated. The mechanism of pomalidomide on DEXs based on miRNAomics

In this study, we evaluated miRNAomics to investigate the mechanism of pomalidomide on DEXs. DEXs from 10 healthy donors were included and analyzed. DEXs from each donor were divided into two groups: those treated with pomalidomide and those not treated with pomalidomide. First, miRNAomic sequencing was conducted on DEXs. MiRNAs with a base length of 18–26 nt were retained (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, miRNA species were identified and analyzed (Fig. 8B). Through biological analysis, a total of 3,987 types of miRNAs were detected in the 20 exosome samples. Seventeen significantly differentially expressed miRNAs were identified between the control group and the pomalidomide group, among which 13 miRNAs were significantly upregulated and 4 miRNAs were significantly downregulated (Fig. 8C, Table 1). Moreover, the expression levels of these 17 miRNAs were analyzed for each exosome sample (Fig. 8D). List of differentially expressed genes

In the KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 8F), we identified 21 pathways that were significantly associated with the direct or indirect regulation of T-cell activation (all p < 0.05). Among these pathways, seven key signaling pathways with a Rich Factor > 0.5 and a p value < 0.01 were selected. The calcium signaling pathway activates transcription factors such as NFAT to promote the expression of cytokines such as IL-2, thereby promoting T-cell activation. The PI3K-Akt pathway enhances T-cell survival and proliferation by inhibiting the expression of proapoptotic proteins (e.g., BAD) and promoting the expression of metabolism-associated proteins (e.g., mTOR). It also contributes to T-cell metabolic reprogramming, supporting the activation and functional regulation of T cells. The MAPK pathway regulates cell proliferation and inflammatory factor production and is involved in T-cell differentiation and effector function regulation. The actin cytoskeleton regulatory pathway mediates immune synapse formation to promote signal transduction. The mTOR signaling pathway integrates nutrients and energy signals to support protein synthesis and metabolic demands in the process of T-cell activation. The TGF-beta signaling pathway may promote the differentiation of specific T-cell subsets through noncanonical pathways, while the Wnt pathway promotes T-cell survival and clonal expansion by regulating T-cell homeostasis-associated genes (e.g., c-Myc). The MAPK, PI3K-Akt and calcium signaling pathways, characterized by the greatest enrichment of factors and gene counts, were identified as the core target pathways. These findings elucidate the mechanism through which DEXs from the pomalidomide group promote T-cell activation. Specifically, the significantly downregulated miRNAs relieve the inhibition of positive regulators, thereby activating the MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. Conversely, the significantly upregulated miRNAs inhibit negative regulatory factors, thus enhancing the calcium signaling pathway. In conclusion, these significantly differentially expressed miRNAs coordinately regulate pathways involved in T-cell proliferation, inflammatory responses and metabolic reprogramming, ultimately promoting T-cell activation.

T-cell RNA-seq reveals the molecular mechanism underlying the effects of DEXs on T-cell function

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the effects of DEXs on T-cell function, we conducted an RNA-Seq transcriptomic analysis of T cells that were stimulated with DEXs and treated with or without pomalidomide. The results revealed 31,688 genes that were differentially expressed between the pomalidomide group and the control group. Among these genes, 271 genes were significantly different, including 142 upregulated genes and 129 downregulated genes (Fig. 9A). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that metabolic-associated pathways, including phenylalanine metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, and phenylalanine, were the most significantly enriched pathways. The significant enrichment of these pathways indicates that DEXs treated with pomalidomide induce canonical metabolic reprogramming in effector T cells, specifically a shift from oxidative phosphorylation toward glycolysis to rapidly generate energy and biosynthetic precursor synthesis, thereby supporting effector T-cell activation and effector functions.

The red bar represents upregulated genes (142), while the blue bar represents downregulated genes (129). (B) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (bubble chart)

Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that multiple pathways associated with cellular signal transduction, particularly the cAMP signaling pathway, apelin signaling pathway and dopaminergic synapses, were activated. These findings indicate that DEXs treated with pomalidomide enhance downstream signaling of the T-cell receptor, second messenger regulation and neuroimmune regulation, thereby strengthening the intracellular signaling network essential for T-cell activation. This provides a molecular explanation for the observed CD8+ T-cell proliferation and enhanced killing function, which were observed in the previous part (Part 2).

Notably, several differentially expressed genes were enriched in infection‑immunity pathways, such as pertussis, legionellosis, and amoebiasis, which represent classic antipathogen immune‑response models. This enrichment further supported the observation that T cells stimulated by pomalidomide-treated DEXs were polarized toward a highly activated effector phenotype, indicating overall enhanced immune responsiveness (Fig. 9B).

In conclusion, the results of the KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DEXs treated with pomalidomide strongly affected key pathways related to CD8+ T-cell metabolism, signal transduction, the stress response and immune function, providing important insights into the mechanism of T-cell immune regulation by DEXs.

Integrated analysis of exosomal miRnaomics and T-cell transcriptomes

To elucidate the molecular mechanism through which pomalidomide-treated DEXs regulate T-cell function, an integrated analysis combining exosomal miRNAomics and T-cell transcriptomics was conducted. First, miRNAs significantly upregulated in pomalidomide-treated DEXs were extracted from the miRNA sequencing data. Target gene predictions were performed using the TargetScan and miRanda databases to construct a predicted target gene set. The predicted target gene set was subsequently cross-referenced with the set of genes whose expression was significantly downregulated in T cells following DEX stimulation, after which the overlapping genes were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 10). The results indicated that the intersecting genes were significantly enriched in key pathways, such as the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, PD-L1 expression and the PD-1 checkpoint pathway (p < 0.05). The JAK-STAT pathway is critical for T-cell signal transduction and functional homeostasis. Under pathological conditions such as the tumor microenvironment, inhibitory cytokines such as IL-6 can persistently induce abnormal and excessive activation of the JAK-STAT pathway, leading to T-cell exhaustion and diminished antitumor immune responses. The enrichment of this pathway indicates that the upregulated miRNAs carried by pomalidomide-treated DEXs may downregulate the aberrant activation of inhibitory cytokine-mediated pathways by downregulating key molecules within this pathway. Therefore, this relieves the persistent negative regulation of T cells and ultimately reverses T-cell exhaustion while promoting T-cell activation and functional restoration. Moreover, PD-1/PD-L1 are the core immune checkpoints that mediate T-cell exhaustion. Their interaction transmits potent inhibitory signals to T cells, inducing proliferation blockade and loss of effector function, ultimately leading to an immunosuppressive state. The enrichment of this pathway further indicated that the upregulated miRNAs in DEXs may target core inhibitory molecules within the pathway, thereby weakening inhibitory signaling by downregulating their expression. This mechanism helps T cells resist exhaustion and maintain their sustained antitumor efficacy. Through integrated analysis, this study provides evidence for the mechanism by which the miRNAs of pomalidomide-treated DEXs modulate T-cell functional activation and improve the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

Immunotherapy has started a new era of precision cancer treatment. Although DC-based immunotherapy has become a highly concerning immunotherapy option for anticancer treatment in recent decades, its clinical application has been constrained by several limitations. In recent years, the use of exosomes as biologically derived nanocarriers has garnered significant attention as a novel delivery approach. More importantly, DEXs can partially replace the functions of DCs [18]. Owing to their unique membrane structure, they not only avoid high degradation risks in vivo but also ensure excellent biocompatibility and safety [19, 20]. Additionally, DEXs are advantageous because they can be easily stored in vitro for a long time, are highly stable, can be easily standardized, can reduce costs, and can enhance patient tolerance and compliance. These characteristics compensate for the limitations of DC-based therapies. Preclinical studies have shown that compared with DC vaccines, DEXs demonstrate superior antitumor efficacy because of their stronger delivery capacity, immunogenicity and anti-immunosuppressive effect [21, 22].

However, their efficacy in enhancing T-cell immunity and clinical outcomes remains limited. This may be attributed to the relatively low activity and inadequate antigen-loading capacity of DEXs. Multiple studies have shown that the promotion of DC maturation enhances antigen loading and exosome production, thereby effectively stimulating T-cell activity [23, 24]. Therefore, this study focuses on enhancing the activity of DEXs from patients with MM to exert significant antitumor immune effects.

Previous studies by our group have indicated that pomalidomide enhances DC activity/maturation in patients with MM. We hypothesize that pomalidomide may also increase the activity of DEXs from patients with MM. No associated studies have been reported to date. Thus, the first part of this study investigated the regulatory effects of pomalidomide on DEXs from patients with MM. Pomalidomide significantly increased the expression levels of MHC-II molecules (HLA-DR) and costimulatory molecules (CD80 and CD86) on DEXs. These findings indicate that pomalidomide enhances the immune function of DEXs by promoting antigen presentation and costimulation signals. This finding is consistent with the previous results of our group that pomalidomide enhances the activity/maturation of DC. The second part of this study further confirmed that pomalidomide enhances the antitumor immune response stimulated by DEXs from patients with MM. In vitro functional experiments indicated that pomalidomide-treated DEXs increased the number of CD8+ T cells and enhanced the specific cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells against the MM cell Line U266, suggesting that pomalidomide may improve the immune activity of DEXs to exert antitumor immune effects.

Among exosomal nucleic acids, miRNAs are the main type of nucleic acid. Their molecular structure protected by membrane vesicles avoids degradation by RNases, thereby maintaining full biological activity during intercellular transport. They are selectively sorted and loaded into exosomes. They subsequently reach target cells through body fluid circulation and enter target cells via endocytosis, ultimately exerting regulatory effects on recipient cells [25–28]. Exosomal miRNAs play key roles in immune modulation and antitumor immunity. In immune modulation, exosomal miRNAs promote T-cell activation, proliferation and differentiation by regulating T-cell receptor signaling pathways and costimulatory molecule expression [29]. For example, exosomal miRNAs such as miR-155 and miR-146a-5p have been shown to regulate T-cell differentiation and function [30, 31]. Mirza et al. confirmed that exosomal miR-146a enhances DC maturation and antigen presentation capacity, thereby strengthening the T-cell immune response [32]. In antitumor immunity, exosomal miRNAs play significant regulatory roles. On the one hand, certain exosomal miRNAs influence tumor biological behaviors. Zhou et al. revealed that exosomal miR-21 promotes tumor-associated fibroblast activation and progression by targeting PTEN [33]. On the other hand, exosomal miRNAs also indirectly exert antitumor effects by regulating immune and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment. For instance, exosomal miR-130b-3p can inhibit the proliferation of non-small cell lung cancer cells and induce apoptosis [34]. In conclusion, exosomal miRNAs serve as key regulators of immune and tumor microenvironments and represent potential therapeutic targets for cancer immunotherapy. In this study, miRNAomics analysis revealed 17 significantly altered miRNAs in pomalidomide-treated DEXs. Their target genes were enriched in seven signaling pathways closely related to T‑cell activation, including the calcium, PI3K‑Akt, and MAPK pathways. These findings suggest that pomalidomide may remodel the exosomal miRNA profile to concurrently modulate multiple T‑cell activation pathways, thereby promoting immune synapse formation, metabolic reprogramming, and cytokine release and ultimately enhancing T‑cell function. In addition, transcriptomic analysis of T cells revealed that DEXs treated with pomalidomide broadly modulate key pathways associated with T-cell function. The mechanisms involve primarily the induction of metabolic reprogramming in effector T cells (e.g., shifting toward glycolysis) and the activation of critical intracellular signaling networks such as the cAMP and apelin pathways. Therefore, the activation status and immune response capacity of CD8+ T cells can be systematically enhanced.

However, the following aspects of this study remain to be further explored. First, the small sample size may have led to individual differences in the results. Second, the in vivo efficacy and safety of DEXs as vaccines require additional validation. Future research should focus on identifying the specific regulatory targets of exosomal miRNAs in T-cell activation, which can provide a foundation for the development of miRNA-based immune targeting strategies.

In conclusion, this study establishes a new strategy to increase the activity of DEXs derived from patients with MM, which has the potential to provide a new idea for developing DEXs as antitumor vaccines for MM.
