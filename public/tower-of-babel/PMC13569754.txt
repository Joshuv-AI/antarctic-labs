Destroyed Lungs and Surgical Management
Authors: Lihui Yu, Jingyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Destroyed lung is a radiographic and clinical end-stage condition of chronic pulmonary infection, characterized by irreversible parenchymal destruction and major functional impairment. Medical treatment may control active infection, but established structural damage is usually irreversible. Pneumonectomy remains the conventional surgical option for selected patients with unilateral destroyed lung, although it is associated with considerable perioperative risk. Lung transplantation may be considered only in highly selected patients with end-stage disease, bilateral involvement, severe functional compromise, or failure of conventional resection-based strategies. However, it should not be regarded as a routine substitute for pneumonectomy. This narrative review summarizes the major etiologies, clinical manifestations, and surgical approaches to destroyed lung, with particular attention to the selective role, indications, technical challenges, and limitations of lung transplantation in this setting.

Introduction

Destroyed lung is an end-stage structural and functional condition characterized by irreversible parenchymal destruction, major functional impairment, chronic infection-related architectural distortion, and radiographic abnormalities. It is most commonly associated with severe chronic respiratory infection, with tuberculosis representing the leading cause [1]. Other etiologies include bronchiectasis, pulmonary aspergillosis, and multiple lung abscesses [2]. Clinical outcomes are often poor, and recurrent infection, hemoptysis, spontaneous pneumothorax, and respiratory failure may complicate the disease course [3]. Pneumonectomy remains the standard surgical treatment for selected patients because removal of the nonfunctional lung can reduce shunt physiology and improve oxygenation [4]. However, this procedure is technically demanding and carries substantial perioperative risk [5]. Therefore, careful patient selection and strict perioperative management are essential.

A narrative literature review was performed using PubMed through January 2026. Search terms included ‘destroyed lung’, ‘tuberculosis-destroyed lung’, ‘pneumonectomy’, ‘bronchiectasis’, and ‘lung transplantation’. Priority was given to English-language studies addressing surgical outcomes, perioperative management, and transplantation strategies. Given the rarity and heterogeneity of destroyed lung, the available evidence was synthesized narratively rather than through formal meta-analysis. This narrative review summarizes the etiologies, clinical characteristics, and surgical management of destroyed lung, with particular attention to the selective role of lung transplantation. In addition, four illustrative cases from two centers are presented to highlight the technical complexity, perioperative risks, and limitations of transplantation-based strategies in this population.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis remains a major global health problem and is the most common cause of destroyed lung [6]. After pulmonary infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, persistent inflammation and immune-mediated injury may lead to caseous necrosis, cavitation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and progressive architectural destruction of the lung [7, 8]. Several factors appear to increase the risk of destroyed lung in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. In a retrospective study of 341 patients, age older than 46 years was associated with a higher risk of destruction, whereas normal or high body mass index appeared protective [9]. Malnutrition was also associated with increased risk [9]. These findings suggest that nutritional optimization may be relevant during long-term management, although the available evidence remains observational.

Bronchiectasis and Cystic Fibrosis

Bronchiectasis is defined by irreversible bronchial dilatation accompanied by chronic airway infection and inflammation. In a retrospective pediatric series, bronchiectasis accounted for 72.2% of destroyed lung cases [10], suggesting that it is an important cause in children. The causes of bronchiectasis are heterogeneous and include chronic infection as well as congenital disorders such as cystic fibrosis [11], which is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene [12]. CFTR dysfunction impairs chloride and bicarbonate transport, lowers airway surface pH, and promotes mucus stasis and recurrent respiratory infection [13]. Over time, repeated infection, consolidation, and collapse may culminate in irreversible parenchymal destruction [14].

Clinical Features

Destroyed lung usually presents radiographically with marked volume loss, collapse, consolidation, and severe unilateral structural distortion. Left-sided involvement is reported more frequently, possibly because the left main bronchus is longer and narrower [15]. Loss of function in the affected lung may be accompanied by compensatory overexpansion of the contralateral lung and mediastinal shift toward the diseased side, resulting in substantial thoracic asymmetry [16]. Imaging may also show narrowing of the pulmonary vessels and systemic-to-pulmonary collateralization, including bronchial artery enlargement and shunt formation [17]. These hemodynamic changes may increase the risk of recurrent infection and hemoptysis [18]. In addition, destroyed lung may be complicated by secondary pulmonary hypertension, while reported risk factors include older age, elevated D-dimer levels, and hypercapnia [19].

Surgical Management of Destroyed Lung

Tuberculosis is the most common cause of destroyed lung [1]. Although anti-infective therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for active infection, surgery is required in selected patients with massive hemoptysis, persistent sepsis, or nonfunctional lung [4], 20. Compared with medical therapy alone, resection can remove the infectious focus, reduce shunt physiology, and improve oxygenation [21–24]. Nevertheless, pneumonectomy for destroyed lung is technically challenging because severe pleural adhesions, calcified hilar lymph nodes, and vascular entrapment are common [25–27]. Major complications include bronchopleural fistula, empyema, hemothorax, and adjacent organ injury.

Open Pneumonectomy

Before the development of minimally invasive thoracic surgery, posterolateral thoracotomy was the standard approach. Open surgery provides excellent hilar exposure and allows direct manual dissection in patients with dense adhesions [28]. Infection control remains paramount throughout pneumonectomy management for destroyed lungs. Infection control remains central to perioperative management. For example, preoperative drainage and irrigation may be required in patients with empyema before definitive resection [22]. In patients with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, perioperative anti-tuberculosis therapy remains indispensable because surgery complements rather than replaces medical treatment [29]. Although open pneumonectomy is associated with substantial morbidity, it remains an important option in anatomically complex cases [30–32].

Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery Pneumonectomy

With advances in thoracoscopic techniques and increasing surgical experience, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has been applied in selected cases of destroyed lung. A recent retrospective study suggested that VATS is feasible in bronchiectasis-associated destroyed lung in carefully selected patients [33]. Potential advantages include less surgical trauma, faster postoperative recovery, and fewer complications in appropriate candidates [34, 35]. However, dense pleural adhesions, severe hilar calcification, and marked thoracic contraction may still necessitate conversion to thoracotomy [36, 37]. Accordingly, VATS should be considered a selective approach rather than a universal strategy for destroyed lung.

Role of Lung Transplantation

Lung transplantation is an established treatment for several forms of end-stage lung disease, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and pulmonary hypertension [38, 39]. In destroyed lung, however, transplantation is uncommon and should not be presented as a routine alternative to pneumonectomy. Its role is complementary and limited to highly selected patients, particularly those with bilateral disease, severe functional impairment, complex thoracic deformity, or failure of resection-based strategies. Bilateral lung transplantation has been reported in patients with destroyed lung and asymmetric chest deformity [16]. Single-lung transplantation combined with contralateral pneumonectomy has also been described in special circumstances, especially when unilateral infection or severe asymmetry precludes standard bilateral implantation [40]. Because post-transplant immunosuppression increases susceptibility to infection, balancing infection control against rejection prevention is especially important in this population.

Transplant Eligibility Considerations in Destroyed Lung

The following considerations should be interpreted cautiously. They are extrapolated largely from general lung transplantation criteria and limited destroyed-lung reports rather than from destroyed-lung-specific consensus statements [41]. Therefore, they should be regarded as a practical framework for multidisciplinary discussion rather than formal indications.

Situations in Which Transplant Assessment May be Reasonable

Transplant assessment may be reasonable in highly selected patients with end-stage destroyed lung when conventional medical treatment and resection-based strategies are insufficient or unsafe. Relevant situations include diffuse or bilateral destroyed lung with severe functional compromise; unilateral disease that is not safely manageable by pneumonectomy because of severe pleural fibrosis, mediastinal distortion, previous thoracic surgery, or poor contralateral reserve; recurrent infection, hemoptysis, or secretion burden that remains clinically significant despite optimized therapy; or progression after previous resection. In these settings, the purpose of transplantation is not simply to correct gas-exchange failure, but to address the combined effects of structural destruction, infection burden, pulmonary vascular changes, and cardiopulmonary impairment.

In infectious destroyed lung, the decision must also consider whether diseased native lung tissue would remain after transplantation. Retention of a chronically infected native lung may increase the risk of post-transplant contamination under immunosuppression. Conversely, adding pneumonectomy to transplantation may increase bleeding, operative duration, extracorporeal support requirements, and early postoperative risk. These competing hazards should be weighed individually.

Factors Arguing Against Transplantation or Requiring Caution

Severe pleural fibrosis, prior thoracic drainage, recurrent pleural infection, and marked mediastinal distortion should not be described as absolute contraindications by themselves; rather, they represent factors that increase operative complexity and perioperative risk. Premature transplantation should be avoided in patients whose infection remains medically controllable, whose pulmonary destruction is localized and amenable to conventional resection, or whose overall condition makes the balance of transplant benefit and perioperative risk unfavorable.

Current eligibility considerations for lung transplantation in destroyed lung remain insufficiently validated. Their use may lead to premature transplantation if other therapeutic options, such as localized resection, infection control, bronchial artery embolization, drainage, or symptomatic management, are not adequately considered. Further clinical research is needed to define destroyed-lung phenotypes, validate selection criteria, and determine which patients are most likely to benefit from transplantation-based strategies.

These cases are illustrative rather than representative, and they should not be interpreted as evidence of broad efficacy. Patient 1

A 49-year-old woman with bronchiectasis presented with recurrent cough, sputum production, and hemoptysis. She had previously undergone interventional embolization with temporary symptom relief, but massive hemoptysis recurred 3 months before referral. Clinical and imaging findings supported a diagnosis of left-sided destroyed lung; pericardial effusion and elevated pulmonary arterial pressure were also detected.

After evaluation, VATS left pneumonectomy with pericardial reconstruction was performed. The operation was technically difficult because the left hemithorax was obliterated and dense adhesions involved the pleura, hilum, and pericardium. The operative time was 3.5 h, with 1200-mL blood loss and 925-mL packed red blood cell transfusion. Postoperative complications included surgical site infection, thoracic infection, and pleural effusion, which were managed with drainage, thoracic lavage and dressing changes, antibiotics, nutritional support, and wound debridement and closure. Two months after pneumonectomy, cough and sputum production had subsided, the incision was dry and clean, and the patient was discharged for local follow-up. Patient 2

A 63-year-old man with old tuberculosis and right-sided destroyed lung was referred for progressive dyspnea and long-term oxygen requirement. His functional status had declined to near bedbound status. On 25% inspired oxygen, oxygen saturation was 94.8%, PaO2 was 69.9 mmHg, and PaCO2 was 51.9 mmHg. The 6-minute walk distance was 209 m, with a nadir oxygen saturation of 83%. Because right-sided transplantation was considered technically high-risk, left unilateral lung transplantation with ECMO support was selected.

During transplantation, dense adhesions and rightward mediastinal shift were encountered, requiring conversion of ECMO support to VVA mode. The left donor lung cold ischemia time was 4.5 h. Intraoperative blood loss was 1500 mL, and transfusion volume was 1575 mL. The patient was initially weaned from ECMO on postoperative day 1 and extubated on postoperative day 2. However, sputum culture subsequently grew Acinetobacter baumannii, and the patient developed impaired consciousness, severe desaturation, and suspected septic shock. Because secretion clearance from the retained right lung was poor and infection did not respond adequately to medical treatment, right pneumonectomy was performed 11 days after left lung transplantation.

The subsequent right lung resection was complicated by severe pleural adhesions and cardiac displacement. Blood loss was approximately 1200 mL, and 1700 mL transfusion was required. Postoperatively, the patient developed hypercapnia and multidrug-resistant bacterial infection. These were managed with bronchoscopic suctioning, intermittent non-invasive ventilation, antibiotics, nutritional support, and anti-rejection medications. Approximately 2 months after right lung resection, inflammatory markers normalized, respiratory symptoms improved, and the patient was discharged. Patient 3

A 60-year-old woman with bronchiectasis had a long history of cough, sputum production, and hemoptysis. She had undergone right lower lobectomy 25 years earlier, followed by recurrent clinical deterioration managed mainly with conservative treatment. Two months before admission, she was assessed for lung transplantation. Given right-sided destroyed lung, prior surgery, and extensive thoracic adhesions, a staged strategy was selected to reduce operative risk: right pneumonectomy followed by transplantation after donor availability.

During the first-stage right pneumonectomy, extensive adhesions, absence of a clear dissectible plane, and partial rib fusion made exposure difficult. After resection, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. Twelve days later, a compatible donor lung became available, and left lung transplantation was performed with ECMO support. Mild adhesions, left bronchial dilation, pulmonary emphysema, and an enlarged pulmonary artery were noted. The patient returned to the ICU on peripheral VV-ECMO support. Approximately 2 months postoperatively, oxygen saturation on room air was 99%, and the patient was discharged for local follow-up.

Patient 4

A 51-year-old man with bronchiectasis had undergone right complete pneumonectomy in 2013 for right-sided destroyed lung. He later developed progressive chest tightness, wheezing, severe pulmonary infection, left distal lung destruction, pulmonary hypertension, and cardiomegaly. On admission, he was nearly bedridden and required non-invasive ventilatory support. Minimal activity caused marked wheezing, and oxygen saturation could fall to 80% on room air. With 50% inspired oxygen, oxygen saturation was 94–98%, PaCO2 was 45.0 mmHg, and PaO2 was 89.8 mmHg. The 6-minute walk distance was only 2 m, and he could not be separated from mechanical ventilation. After 4 months of medical management and waiting, a complex transplantation-based procedure was undertaken.

The operation consisted of left whole-lung transplantation, donor right lower lobe transplantation, left pneumonectomy, and pericardial window procedure under thoracoscopic assistance. Extensive adhesions, severe thoracic asymmetry, rightward cardiac displacement, pulmonary hypertension, and an undersized donor lung increased operative complexity. Blood loss was approximately 3640 mL, and about 2800 mL of blood products were transfused. The patient had intra-operative hemodynamic instability, required high-dose vasoactive support, and was transferred to the ICU on VAV-ECMO. Postoperative care included repeated bronchoscopy, ventilator-assisted ventilation, anti-infection therapy, anti-rejection treatment, inhaled nitric oxide, and nutritional support.

On postoperative day 7, thrombosis developed from the proximal left external iliac artery to the femoral artery, requiring thrombectomy, femoral artery angioplasty, and left lower extremity arteriography. Acute kidney injury with decreased urine output subsequently required continuous renal replacement therapy. The patient died of circulatory failure 27 days after lung transplantation.

Taken together, these cases illustrate why the surgical pathway for destroyed lung should be individualized. Patient 1 demonstrates that conventional pneumonectomy remains appropriate for selected localized unilateral disease. Patients 2 and 3 show that transplantation-based strategies can be technically feasible in selected end-stage situations but may require staged source control and prolonged perioperative support. Patient 4 emphasizes that technical feasibility alone does not ensure clinical success in patients with severe cardiopulmonary compromise and high operative risk.

Transplant Strategy Selection in Destroyed Lung

Based on the limited literature and the illustrative cases above, strategy selection should begin by determining whether conventional resection can safely remove the nonfunctional or infected lung. When transplantation is required for infectious destroyed lung, bilateral lung transplantation is generally preferable when technically feasible because it avoids retention of a chronically infected native lung. Single-lung transplantation combined with contralateral pneumonectomy should be regarded as an exceptional or salvage strategy, reserved for highly selected patients in whom bilateral implantation is technically unsafe or anatomically impractical. The choice depends on infection burden, thoracic anatomy, hemodynamic status, donor quality, and center experience.

Infection Control

When the native diseased lung remains a major infectious reservoir, such as in multidrug-resistant bacterial infection, non-tuberculous mycobacterial disease, or fungal colonization, routine transplantation alone may carry a high risk of post-transplant contamination under immunosuppression. In such cases, single-lung transplantation combined with contralateral pneumonectomy may offer better source control. However, this strategy also increases operative trauma and should therefore be reserved for carefully selected patients.

Thoracic Anatomy

Long-standing destroyed lung may cause pleural fibrosis, dense hilar adhesions, mediastinal shift, and marked chest asymmetry [42]. In this setting, bilateral implantation may become technically hazardous. Some surgeons may therefore favor implantation on the less distorted side, with resection of the retained native lung performed simultaneously or in a staged fashion when necessary. This approach prioritizes operative safety, although it does not always achieve complete immediate eradication of diseased tissue.

Hemodynamic Considerations

Bilateral transplantation preserves a larger vascular bed and may provide better long-term hemodynamic recovery. In contrast, single-lung transplantation may be advantageous when one pulmonary artery is severely diseased or when intra-operative right heart instability makes bilateral implantation unsafe. Thus, pulmonary artery pressure, vascular quality, and real-time intra-operative tolerance all influence the final decision.

Resource and Operative Risk

Single-lung transplantation with contralateral pneumonectomy may be associated with greater bleeding risk, longer operative duration, and more difficult perioperative management when extensive collateral circulation is present [43]. Bilateral transplantation may provide better long-term pulmonary reserve when donor quality and recipient stability are acceptable[44]. However, when donor quality is marginal or pulmonary hypertension is severe, the risk of intra-operative circulatory instability during bilateral transplantation may increase [45]. Overall, treatment selection must remain individualized [46–48].

Technical Challenges of Lung Transplantation in Destroyed Lung

For patients with diffuse or bilateral disease who are not candidates for conventional resection, transplantation may be the only definitive option. The main technical issues involve recipient preparation, anastomotic reconstruction, and postoperative management.

Recipient Preparation: ECMO Strategy and Management of Collateral Bleeding

Chronic inflammation in destroyed lung often produces dense hilar scarring and abundant systemic collateral vessels [49]. As a result, difficult dissection and severe bleeding are major intra-operative concerns. In patients with severe pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular dysfunction, or anticipated extreme dissection difficulty, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) may be established early to maintain hemodynamic stability [50]. In less unstable patients, a standby ECMO strategy may also be reasonable. Once support is available, early vascular control and multimodal hemostatic measures are important to limit blood loss.

Anastomotic Reconstruction

Severe mediastinal shift and hilar distortion can complicate vascular and atrial anastomoses [51]. In particular, fibrotic retraction may leave a short pulmonary artery, and inflammatory destruction may result in an inadequate left atrial cuff, making standard implantation difficult. In such circumstances, reconstruction using donor vascular tissue or donor atrial tissue may be required to achieve a tension-free and patent anastomosis [52, 53]. Excessive traction should be avoided because it increases the risk of vascular or atrial injury. When the mediastinum is severely scarred, the safest possible field for anastomosis should be selected.

Postoperative Management

Postoperative management should focus on prevention of primary graft dysfunction (PGD), control of infection, and assessment of any retained diseased native lung tissue. PGD is a major early complication after lung transplantation [54, 55]. Prolonged operative time, large transfusion volume, and ischemia–reperfusion injury may further increase this risk in patients with destroyed lung. Strategies such as restrictive fluid administration, protective ventilation, appropriate ECMO support, and careful optimization of graft perfusion are therefore important. If diseased native lung tissue is retained, ventilation–perfusion mismatch, intra-pulmonary shunt, bleeding, and persistent infection remain important postoperative concerns. Early imaging and individualized respiratory management are required in these cases.

Discussion

This review summarizes the major etiologies, clinical characteristics, and surgical options for destroyed lung. Destroyed lung is an end-stage structural and functional consequence of chronic pulmonary infection, most commonly associated with tuberculosis, although bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis may also contribute in selected populations. For patients with localized unilateral destroyed lung and adequate cardiopulmonary reserve, pneumonectomy remains the principal surgical treatment. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery may be feasible in selected patients, but dense pleural adhesions, hilar distortion, calcified lymph nodes, and thoracic contraction often limit its applicability. Therefore, VATS should be considered a selective approach rather than a routine alternative to open pneumonectomy.

The role of lung transplantation in destroyed lung should be interpreted with caution. Transplantation should not be regarded as a routine substitute for pneumonectomy. Instead, it may have a selective and complementary role in patients with end-stage disease, bilateral involvement, severe functional compromise, or failure of conventional resection-based strategies. This distinction is important because patients with destroyed lung often have chronic infection, pleural fibrosis, mediastinal shift, pulmonary hypertension, and poor cardiopulmonary reserve, all of which may increase perioperative risk.

The current evidence base remains limited. Most available studies are retrospective reports, small case series, or case reports rather than prospective comparative studies. In addition, the etiologies, anatomical patterns, infection burden, and cardiopulmonary reserve of affected patients are heterogeneous. These limitations make it difficult to compare outcomes across studies or to establish universal transplant criteria for destroyed lung. Therefore, transplant indications should be framed as individualized, center-based clinical judgments rather than formal consensus standards.

The choice of transplant strategy requires particular caution in infectious destroyed lung. When transplantation is necessary, bilateral lung transplantation is generally preferable when technically feasible because it avoids retention of a chronically infected native lung. By contrast, single-lung transplantation combined with contralateral pneumonectomy should be regarded as an exceptional or salvage strategy rather than a standard option. Although this approach may be considered in highly selected patients with severe thoracic asymmetry or prohibitive anatomical risk for bilateral implantation, it also increases operative complexity and may expose patients to bleeding, infection, prolonged extracorporeal support, and early postoperative deterioration.

The four illustrative cases in this article support this cautious interpretation. Patient 1 shows that conventional pneumonectomy remains appropriate for selected patients with localized unilateral destroyed lung. Patients 2 and 3 demonstrate that transplantation-based strategies may be considered in complex end-stage situations when conventional resection alone is insufficient or technically difficult. However, these cases should not be interpreted as evidence that such approaches are broadly applicable. Patient 2 also highlights the risk of post-transplant infection and the need for subsequent removal of the retained diseased lung. Patient 4 is particularly important because it demonstrates that technical feasibility does not necessarily lead to clinical success. In the presence of severe cardiopulmonary compromise, pulmonary hypertension, thoracic distortion, major bleeding, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, vascular complications, and acute kidney injury, complex transplantation may still carry substantial early mortality risk.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This article is a narrative review rather than a systematic review, and the literature search was not designed to provide pooled outcome estimates. The available destroyed-lung literature is dominated by retrospective studies, small case series, and case reports. The four clinical cases are illustrative and cannot establish comparative effectiveness. In addition, several transplant-related considerations are necessarily extrapolated from general lung transplantation experience because destroyed-lung-specific consensus criteria and prospective outcome data are lacking. These limitations restrict the strength of the conclusions and support a cautious, individualized interpretation.

Overall, management of destroyed lung requires individualized integration of infection control, thoracic anatomy, cardiopulmonary reserve, and operative risk. Future studies should emphasize clearer phenotype definitions, standardized reporting of perioperative variables, complications, survival, and long-term functional outcomes. Multicenter data are needed before more definitive recommendations can be made. Pneumonectomy remains the principal resection-based strategy for selected unilateral disease, whereas lung transplantation has a limited, selective, and complementary role in end-stage or unresectable destroyed lung.
