Effective hyperuniformity in time-integrated stochastic Turing patterns
Anirban Mukherjee 1, Hong-Yan Shih 1,2

Abstract
Demographic noise generates stochastic Turing patterns even when reaction-diffusion systems are deterministically stable. We show analytically and verify numerically in the Levin-Segel model that temporal integration of configurations reveals emergent large-scale organization. The intensive number variance in a window of size R ≫ 1 R\gg 1 approaches a finite reaction-kinetic floor as 1 / R 1/R , over a spatial range growing by orders of magnitude near the Turing instability. This yields an effectively hyperuniform, fine-tuning-free regime previously unidentified in non-conserved multispecies stochastic systems.



Introduction.
—
Living systems often display striking spatial patterns—from stripes and
spots in animal pigmentation to vegetation bands in drylands—raising a
fundamental question: how does robust macroscopic organization emerge at
scales far larger than those of individual
constituents

;
Cross and Hohenberg 1993
;
Camazine et al. 2003
;
Rietkerk et al. 2021
? Turing’s landmark theory showed that such patterns can
emerge spontaneously from a homogeneous state through the coupling of a
slowly diffusing activator to a faster-diffusing inhibitor, a central
paradigm of reaction–diffusion pattern formation

;
Kondo and Miura 2010
;
Rietkerk and van de Koppel 2008
. Central to this picture, particularly in
developmental biology

, are

morphogens

;
Gurdon and Bourillot 2001
;
Wolpert 1996
,
signaling molecules whose local concentration acts as a positional cue for
how cells differentiate and organize. In most such systems noise is
intrinsic and unavoidable

;
Lande et al. 2003
: stochastic
reaction–diffusion models capture this discreteness through individual
reaction and diffusion events, generating finite-wavelength patterns even
below the deterministic Turing threshold by selectively amplifying weakly
damped modes at an intermediate wave number
McKane and Newman 2004
;
Lugo and McKane 2008
;
McKane and Newman 2005
;
Butler and Goldenfeld 2009
;
Butler and Goldenfeld 2011
;
Biancalani et al. 2010
;
Asslani et al. 2012
, a mechanism invoked for patterning in synthetic bacterial
populations
Karig et al. 2018
, cyanobacterial
filaments
Di Patti et al. 2018
, and Arabidopsis
trichomes
Di Patti et al. 2023
(for a review, see
Ref.
Di Patti et al. 2026
).

The prevailing treatment assumes that biological entities read the local
morphogen concentration at a given instant
Turing 1952
;
Wolpert 1969
;
Pagès and Kerridge 2000
;
Ashe and Briscoe 2006
, as do activator-inhibitor models
of ecological pattern formation
Murray 2003
;
Rietkerk and van de Koppel 2008
;
Borgogno et al. 2009
. Yet cells and organisms often do not act on instantaneous
snapshots alone; in several patterning systems they accumulate signals and
make fate decisions reflecting a history of morphogen

;
Harfe et al. 2004
;
Dessaud et al. 2007
;
Dessaud et al. 2008
;
Sagner and Briscoe 2017
and
stressor
Ogle et al. 2015
;
Johnstone et al. 2016
activity rather than—or in
addition to—the instantaneous concentration. This raises a question: can
large-scale spatial organization emerge from accumulated fluctuations,
beyond the instantaneous patterns at intermediate Turing scales?

A particularly stringent form of large-scale spatial organization is
hyperuniformity, in which long-wavelength density fluctuations are
anomalously suppressed: the number variance grows more slowly than the
observation volume, or equivalently the structure factor vanishes in the
long-wavelength limit,
S(\mathbf{q})\rightarrow 0
as
|\mathbf{q}|\to 0

Torquato and Stillinger 2003
;
Torquato 2018
. Beyond crystals and
quasicrystals, hyperuniformity also occurs in disordered systems that
appear irregular locally but highly uniform at large scales, and has been
identified in biological and ecological systems where such uniformity is
often linked to function, including the avian retinal photoreceptor
mosaic
Jiao et al. 2014
, looped leaf-vein networks
Liu et al. 2024
,
dryland vegetation
Ge 2023
, swimming algae
Huang et al. 2021
,
immune receptors
Mayer et al. 2015
, and epithelial tissue
layers
Tan 2024
;
Siegert et al. 2026
. Deterministic pattern-forming
equations, including the Swift–Hohenberg and Cahn–Hilliard
equations
Ma and Torquato 2017
;
Zheng et al. 2024
and generic Gray–Scott
models
Ballestero et al. 2025
, can also generate hyperuniform states. In
equilibrium systems, hyperuniformity arises naturally in crystals,
quasicrystals, and certain disordered ground states with long-range
interactions or constrained correlations, whereas in stochastic
nonequilibrium systems known routes to hyperuniformity are more restrictive
and often rely on conservation laws: particle-number conservation can
suppress long-wavelength density fluctuations
Hexner and Levine 2015
;
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
, while conservation of the center of
mass
Hexner and Levine 2017
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
or higher moments of the mass
distribution
Maire and Chaix 2025
enforces stronger forms of
hyperuniformity. Although hyperuniform density fluctuations were recently
reported in a nonconserved stochastic population model through
self-organized criticality
Agranov et al. 2025
, whether noisy
reaction–diffusion systems can generically produce hyperuniform-like order
without conservation laws or imposed constraints remains unresolved.

Here we show that time integration can reveal effective hyperuniform
organization in stochastic reaction–diffusion systems exhibiting
finite-wavelength Turing patterns. Time integration acts as a dynamical
filter for persistent fluctuations, so that the zero-frequency dynamic
structure factor at small wavenumber and the long-time intensive subsystem
fluctuations at large scales are controlled by a noise floor pinned by
reaction kinetics. In the stochastic Turing regime, this reaction-induced
floor is subdominant over an intermediate range of length scales above the
Turing scale, taking over only beyond a crossover length that diverges as
the Turing instability is approached. The resulting suppression of
long-wavelength fluctuations reflects an incomplete but increasingly
extended spatiotemporal compensation: local clustering promotes growth but
also enhances inhibition, generating anticorrelated fluctuations whose spatial
sum leaves only a finite reaction-controlled residual. Near the instability,
the weak damping of the Turing modes sharpens the pattern and extends the
oscillatory correlations, making this compensating response long-ranged and
long-lived enough to let the hyperuniform-like regime dominate the floor
across a broad range of scales. Thus, although particle number is not
conserved, reactions impose an emergent constraint on accumulated-density
fluctuations, providing a nonconserved route to effective hyperuniformity.
Our stochastic simulations confirm the theory and show that temporal
accumulation sharpens spatial structures nearly invisible in instantaneous
snapshots, suggesting effective hyperuniformity as a diagnostic for hidden
order in stochastic pattern-forming systems.

Time-integrated configurations.—
We consider a generic
two-species stochastic reaction-diffusion process on a discrete

d
-dimensional hypercubic lattice with periodic boundary conditions.
Each site
\mathbf{r}
has internal volume
V
and hosts integer populations

N^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}(t)
, with
X=P
(activator) or
Q
(inhibitor).
Using the van Kampen system-size expansion

Lugo and McKane 2008

N_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}\simeq V\rho_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}+\sqrt{V}\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}

The deterministic part

obeys the mean-field

\partial_{t}\rho_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}=f_{X}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathbf{r}}\right)+\sum_{Y}\mathcal{D}_{XY}\nabla^{2}\rho_{\mathbf{r}}^{(Y)},

(1)

where

is the local reaction kinetics and
\mathcal{D}
is the
diffusion matrix coupling species
X
and
Y
. The fluctuating part

\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}
obeys the linear Langevin equation

\partial_{t}\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}=\sum_{Y}\mathcal{R}_{XY}\,\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(Y)}+\sum_{Y}\mathcal{D}_{XY}\nabla^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(Y)}+\eta_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}(t),

(2)

where
\mathcal{R}
is the reaction matrix, obtained by linearizing

f_{X}
about the homogeneous steady state, and is the same matrix that
enters the standard deterministic Turing stability analysis. The
noise
\eta_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}(t)
is Gaussian and white,

\langle\eta_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}(t)\,\eta_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}^{(Y)}(t^{\prime})\rangle=B^{(XY)}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\,\delta_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and
encodes the demographic noise from the underlying
reactions
Sup
. As
V\to\infty
at fixed time,
\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}

converges to the Gaussian process governed by
Eq. (
2
)
Kurtz 1970
;
Kurtz 1971
, together
with Eq. (
1
) constituting the linear noise
approximation (LNA).

Deterministic Turing pattern formation requires the underlying
reaction-diffusion system to be unstable
Turing 1952
;
Cross and Greenside 2009

for at least one wave vector
\mathbf{q}^{*}
, via the condition

\det\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{*}}=0,

(3)

where the stability matrix is given by

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}}=\left(\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{D}\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}\right)
;
this requires the inhibitor-to-activator diffusivity ratio to exceed
a threshold set by
\mathcal{R}
.
Crucially, the Turing mechanism also requires the
well-mixed system itself to be stable,

\det\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}=0}=\det\mathcal{R}>0
, so any Turing
instability necessarily occurs at a finite wave vector

\left|\mathbf{q}^{*}\right|>0
, leaving the
\mathbf{q}\to 0
limit governed entirely by
stable dynamics at the deterministic level.
The system remains stable and does not form any Turing pattern if the
stability matrix has a positive determinant,
\det\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}}>0
,
and a negative trace,
\operatorname{tr}\,\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}}<0
,
for all wave vectors
\mathbf{q}
.

Stochastic Turing patterns can develop

;
Butler and Goldenfeld 2011

in an otherwise stable deterministic reaction–diffusion system when
demographic fluctuations, arising from the intrinsic stochasticity of
the dynamics and amplified by the slowly relaxing eigenmodes of the
stability matrix

, produce a peak for a certain
wave vector
\mathbf{q}^{*}
at
zero frequency
in the dynamic structure factor of
\xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}(t)
.
Unlike deterministic Turing patterns, stochastic Turing patterns are not
static in steady state: the
statistics
(mean densities, dynamic
power spectra, and equal-time structure factors) are stationary, but
individual configurations
N_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}(t)
fluctuate indefinitely,
mixing fast reaction noise with slow diffusion-mediated spatial
organization, even producing giant fluctuations

.

Our main objects of interest are the time-integrated demographic fluctuations,

\Xi^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\xi^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}(t),

(4)

and their structure factor in steady state,

S^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}(T)=\frac{\left\langle\tilde{\Xi}_{\mathbf{q}}^{(X)}(T)\,\tilde{\Xi}_{-\mathbf{q}}^{(X)}(T)\right\rangle}{L^{d}T},

(5)

where
\tilde{\Xi}_{\mathbf{q}}^{(X)}(T)
is the spatial Fourier transform
of
\Xi^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}(T)
. In the limit
T\to\infty
, the structure
factor equals the zero-frequency component of the dynamic power
spectrum of species
X
,

\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}\equiv\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}S^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}(T)=\left\langle\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{q}}^{(X)}(0)\tilde{\xi}_{-\mathbf{q}}^{(X)}(0)\right\rangle,

(6)

where
\tilde{\xi}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}(\omega)
denotes the spatio-temporal
Fourier transform of
\xi^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}(t)
.
The convergence timescale of
S^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}(T)
to

\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}
is model-dependent and physically
meaningful for natural systems, as it quantifies the integration
time required to resolve the large-scale spatial organization of
time-integrated configurations.

Large-scale structure.—
The large-scale structure is encoded in the small-wavevector behavior as

\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}\simeq\mathcal{F}^{(X)}+\mathcal{G}^{(X)}\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{4}).

(7)

\mathcal{F}^{(X)}=\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{0}}>0
measures fluctuations of the total space-time-integrated population of species
X
and is determined solely by the reaction kinetics, independent of diffusion
Sup
.
Because the
\mathbf{q}=0
mode remains linearly stable at the
deterministic level (see above;
\det\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}=0}=\det\mathcal{R}>0
),
\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
has no deterministic
counterpart. It vanishes identically when the noise term

\eta^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}
is switched off, rather than persisting as an
unavoidable floor of the structure factor.
Relatedly, in contrast to conserved stochastic systems,
\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
is not
constrained to vanish by conservation laws or
fluctuation-dissipation relations
Mukherjee and Pradhan 2023
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
. Furthermore,

\mathcal{G}^{(X)}>0

(8)

is the condition
Butler and Goldenfeld 2011
for stochastic Turing pattern
formation — achievable even when the inhibitor-to-activator
diffusivity ratio lies below the deterministic threshold — causing

\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}
to attain a global maximum at a finite
wavevector
\mathbf{q}^{*}
and exhibit the small-wavevector scaling

\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}-\mathcal{F}^{(X)}\sim\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}

for
\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\ll\left|\mathbf{q}^{*}\right|
. It is also known that stochastic Turing patterns exhibit a fat-tail spectrum governed by
\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}\sim\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{-2}
for
\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\gg\left|\mathbf{q}^{*}\right|

Butler and Goldenfeld 2011
.
Beyond the leading quadratic term in
Eq. (
7
), higher-order
corrections proportional to
|\mathbf{q}|^{4},|\mathbf{q}|^{6},\ldots
are also present.
Although their net effect is positive at small
|\mathbf{q}|
, a negative
higher-order contribution emerges near
|\mathbf{q}^{*}|
, causing the spectrum
to decrease beyond the Turing peak.

Subsystem fluctuations.
—
We quantify large-scale spatial organization of time-integrated
configurations through the population fluctuation within a spherical
window of radius
R
centered at
\mathbf{r}_{0}
,

\Xi^{(X)}(R;T)=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\Xi^{(X)}_{\mathbf{r}}(T)\,w(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0};R),

(9)

where
w(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0};R)=1
if
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega(\mathbf{r}_{0};R)\equiv\{\mathbf{r}:|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}|\leq R\}

and
0
otherwise, and
|\Omega(R)|=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}w(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0};R)
denotes
the number of lattice sites inside the window.
In homogeneous steady state, the intensive population fluctuation
within that window at an arbitrary integration time
T
is independent
of
\mathbf{r}_{0}
and can be written as

{\rm var}^{(X)}(R;T)=\frac{1}{v_{1}(R)\,T}\left\langle\left[\Xi^{(X)}(R;T)\right]^{2}\right\rangle,

(10)

where
v_{1}(R)=\pi^{d/2}R^{d}/\Gamma(d/2+1)
is the continuum
hyperspherical window volume, equal to
|\Omega(R)|
for
R\gg 1
.
In the large-
T
limit,
\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}{\rm var}^{(X)}(R;T)
,
can be expressed in terms of the structure factor as
Sup

\displaystyle\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\,\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{q}}\;\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}}(R).

(11)

The sum rule.
—In the large-
R
limit, the window function
can be written as
Sup

\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}}(R)\xrightarrow{R\gg 1}\frac{2^{d}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2+1)}{\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{d}}\,J_{d/2}^{2}(\left|\mathbf{q}\right|R)\geq 0,

(12)

where
J_{d/2}
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order
d/2
.
In the thermodynamic limit,
\lim_{R\to\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}}(R)=(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}(\mathbf{q})
, so Eq. (
11
) gives

\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{L\to\infty}\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)=\overline{S}^{(X)}_{\mathbf{0}}=\mathcal{F}^{(X)},

(13)

and the corresponding sum rule on the correlation function of
time-integrated configurations,

\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\,\overline{C}_{\mathbf{r}}^{(XX)}=\mathcal{F}^{(X)},

(14)

where
\overline{C}_{\mathbf{r}}^{(XX)}=\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}\left\langle\Xi_{\mathbf{0}}^{(X)}(T)\,\Xi_{\mathbf{r}}^{(X)}(T)\right\rangle
.
The large-subsystem fluctuation is thus determined entirely by local
reaction kinetics, independent of diffusion and spatial organization
— a nonequilibrium counterpart of the grand-canonical
fluctuation-compressibility relation, in which the role of the
chemical reservoir is played by the reactions.

Effective hyperuniformity.
—The sum rule requires

\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)\to\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
from above as

R\to\infty
. Since the stochastic Turing peak forces

\smash[t]{\overline{\rm var}}^{(X)}(R^{*})>\mathcal{F}^{(X)}

at
R^{*}\sim 1/|\mathbf{q}^{*}|
, the variance must decay for
R>R^{*}

to satisfy Eq. (
13
) — this decay is an
exact and inevitable consequence of the sum rule. The rate of decay
is controlled by the small-
|\mathbf{q}|
structure factor,

\overline{S}_{\mathbf{q}}^{(X)}-\mathcal{F}^{(X)}=\mathcal{G}^{(X)}\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{4})

(cf. Eq. (
7
)), signaling a
subleading class-I hyperuniform
correction
Torquato and Stillinger 2003
;
Torquato 2018

\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)\approx\mathcal{F}^{(X)}+\frac{\mathcal{G}^{(X)}_{\rm eff}}{R},

(15)

where
\mathcal{G}^{(X)}_{\rm eff}
includes the higher-order
contributions from Eq. (
7
).
For
R^{*}\ll R\ll R_{c}^{(X)}
, where

R_{c}^{(X)}=\mathcal{G}^{(X)}_{\rm eff}/\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
,
the subsystem variance effectively decays as

\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)\approx\frac{\mathcal{G}^{(X)}_{\rm eff}}{R}.

(16)

As the system approaches the deterministic Turing instability
— equivalently, as the inhibitor-to-activator diffusivity ratio
approaches its deterministic threshold from below —

\det\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{*}}\rightarrow 0
,
the eigenmode at
\mathbf{q}^{*}
becomes the slowest decaying and
demographic noise is maximally amplified, causing the spectral
peak to diverge as

\overline{S}_{\mathbf{q}^{*}}^{(X)}\sim\frac{1}{\left[\det\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{*}}\right]^{2}}.

(17)

Via Eq. (
11

\mathcal{G}_{\rm eff}^{(X)}
, and consequently
R_{c}^{(X)}
increases, since
\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
remains fixed by the reactions. In this regime,
R^{*}
and
R_{c}^{(X)}

become well separated, extending the window over which

\overline{\rm var}^{(X)}(R)
exhibits a
1/R
decay across
several orders of magnitude in
R
. The system is therefore
effectively hyperuniform within this window.

Summary and conclusions.—
We have shown that, near the deterministic Turing instability,
time-integrated stochastic Turing patterns exhibit effective
hyperuniformity over a spatial-window larger than the Turing length scale

R^{*}
: the population variance per unit volume decays as
1/R

within this window, while instantaneous configurations remain
Poisson-like beyond
R^{*}
. Temporal integration can therefore reveal
spatial patterns otherwise hidden in instantaneous snapshots: in the
stochastic Levin-Segel model, the predator (inhibitor) at moderate
diffusivity ratios shows no discernible Turing length scale in any
snapshot — it emerges only through temporal integration. This
effective hyperuniformity is an inevitable consequence of stochastic
Turing pattern formation, not a fine-tuned coincidence. The
large-subsystem fluctuation per unit volume is pinned to a reaction noise floor by a
sum rule on the correlation function — a nonequilibrium
counterpart of the grand-canonical fluctuation-compressibility
relation — while the growing peak at
R^{*}
near the instability
drives the crossover length
R_{c}^{(X)}
to increase monotonically,
widening the hyperuniform scaling regime as patterns sharpen.

Our work quantitatively demonstrates the emergence of a large-scale
hyperuniform-scaling regime in a stochastic multi-species
reaction-diffusion system driven by demographic noise alone, with no
conservation law required, in contrast to most earlier systems with
some conserved quantity where hyperuniform spatial organization has
been found
Hexner and Levine 2017
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
. The key insight
is that this organization is hidden in instantaneous configurations
and becomes visible only through temporal integration, which we
believe opens a route to discovering such hidden structure in
biological and ecological systems.

Population fluctuation of time-integrated configurations in spherical windows

The time-integrated population is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{-1}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mu-\nu p/d&-\mu+\nu e/p\\
\mu p/d-\nu e/d&-\mu p/d+\nu e^{2}/p^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(S37)

SIII.2
Reaction noise matrix

The components of the noise strengths, given by
Eq. (
S7
), can be written using
Table
1
as

\displaystyle B^{(PP)}

\displaystyle=b\bar{\rho}^{(P)}+e(\bar{\rho}^{(P)})^{2}+p\bar{\rho}^{(P)}\bar{\rho}^{(Q)},

(S38)

\displaystyle B^{(PQ)}

\displaystyle=B^{(QP)}=-\,p\bar{\rho}^{(P)}\bar{\rho}^{(Q)},

\displaystyle B^{(QQ)}

\displaystyle=p\bar{\rho}^{(P)}\bar{\rho}^{(Q)}+d(\bar{\rho}^{(Q)})^{2},

where only the third reaction
P+Q\to 2Q
contributes to the
off-diagonal
B^{(PQ)}
, since it is the only reaction that changes
both species. Substituting the steady-state densities from
Eq. (
S34
), we write the complete noise-strength matrix as

B=\frac{b^{2}dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{pmatrix}.

(S39)

SIII.3
Computation of
\mathcal{F}^{(X)}

Substituting Eqs. (
S36
) and
(
S39
) into Eq. (
S13a
), we obtain the following expressions of
\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
.

Prey (
X=P
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(P)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{R}^{-1})^{(PX^{\prime})}(\mathcal{R}^{-1})^{(PX^{\prime\prime})}\,B^{(X^{\prime}X^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\frac{b^{2}dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\bigl[(-1)^{2}\cdot 2+2(-1)(+1)(-1)+(+1)^{2}\cdot 2\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{6dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}.

(S40)

Predator (
X=Q
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(Q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\frac{b^{2}dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\left[\left(-\frac{p}{d}\right)^{2}\cdot 2+2\!\left(-\frac{p}{d}\right)\!\left(\frac{e}{p}\right)(-1)+\left(\frac{e}{p}\right)^{2}\!\cdot 2\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\left[\frac{p^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{e}{d}+\frac{e^{2}}{p^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(d^{2}e^{2}+dep^{2}+p^{4})}{d(p^{2}-de)^{2}}.

(S41)

SIII.4
Computation of
\mathcal{G}^{(X)}

Substituting Eqs. (
S36
), (
S37
) and (
S39
) into Eq. (
S13b
), we obtain the following expressions of
\mathcal{G}^{(X)}
.

Prey (
X=P
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(P)}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{R}^{-1})^{(PX^{\prime})}(\mathcal{R}^{-1}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{R}^{-1})^{(PX^{\prime\prime})}B^{(X^{\prime}X^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{b}\cdot\frac{b^{2}dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{b^{2}}\Bigl[(-1)\bigl((\mu-\nu p/d)\cdot 2+(-\mu+\nu e/p)\cdot(-1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+(+1)\bigl((\mu-\nu p/d)\cdot(-1)+(-\mu+\nu e/p)\cdot 2\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{6p\,[\nu(p^{2}+de)-2\mu dp]}{b(p^{2}-de)^{2}}.

(S42)

Predator (
X=Q
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(Q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{b}\cdot\frac{b^{2}dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{b^{2}}\Bigl[\!\left(-\tfrac{p}{d}\right)\!\bigl((\mu p/d-\nu e/d)\cdot 2+(-\mu p/d+\nu e^{2}/p^{2})\cdot(-1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{e}{p}\bigl((\mu p/d-\nu e/d)\cdot(-1)+(-\mu p/d+\nu e^{2}/p^{2})\cdot 2\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,[\,2\nu e(p^{4}+dep^{2}+d^{2}e^{2})-3\mu p^{3}(p^{2}+de)\,]}{bdp(p^{2}-de)^{2}}.

(S43)

SIII.5
Final spectra at zero frequency and small wave vector limit

Assembling
\mathcal{F}^{(X)}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(X)}
in Eq. (
S12
),

S_{q}^{(X)}(0)\simeq\mathcal{F}^{(X)}+\mathcal{G}^{(X)}q^{2}
gives

\displaystyle S_{q}^{(P)}(0)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{6dp^{2}}{(p^{2}-de)^{2}}+\frac{6p\,[\nu(p^{2}+de)-2\mu dp]}{b(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\,q^{2},

(S44)

\displaystyle S_{q}^{(Q)}(0)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2(d^{2}e^{2}+dep^{2}+p^{4})}{d(p^{2}-de)^{2}}+\frac{2\,[\,2\nu e(p^{4}+dep^{2}+d^{2}e^{2})-3\mu p^{3}(p^{2}+de)\,]}{bdp(p^{2}-de)^{2}}\,q^{2}.
