Executive Summary

In this project, robust heat-ﬂux measurement systems were developed. The heat-ﬂux sensors utilize
thermoelectric effects to directly transduce the heat-ﬂux inputs to analog electrical voltage signals.
They were constructed from dedicated materials that can withstand temperatures of at least 1000°C
and maintain adequate performance at these conditions for prolonged periods of time. The proposed
approaches took into account numerous considerations, including system cost, sensor head
resilience, sensor footprint, data accuracy, response time, and maintenance requirements. Through
modern thermoelectric materials design, methodical materials selection and rigorous testing in
materials characterization labs and medium-scale ﬁre research facilities, we have demonstrated
functioning laboratory prototypes, upon which one could base industrial heat-ﬂux sensing platforms
capable of operating in the challenging high-temperature, corrosive environments of the boilers of
coal-ﬁred power plants.

A distributed sensor array for heat-ﬂux measurements throughout the furnace water-wall, the
superheater area and the economizer coils can provide critical data for the power plant control
systems to increase efficiency, improve safety and reduce down times. For example, the combined
heat-ﬂux sensor/control systems can contribute to the optimization of burner and boiler operations
under ﬂexible loads, the optimization of heat-exchange conditions and overall reduction of heat rate
and emissions, the prediction of imminent overheating conditions, and the optimization of the soot-
blowing protocols.

Milestones achieved during the project include:

The scientiﬁc concept

Heat-ﬂux sensing at extreme conditions places multiple constraints on the materials that can be
selected for the transducer of the sensor, and once these are chosen, on the materials for the
packaging of the sensor. In addition to each individual material needing to withstand the operation
conditions (e.g. remain solid, retain its oxidation state), materials that are in physical contact should
not intermix, and their joints should be mechanically robust (e.g. tolerate the stress due to thermal
expansion mismatch).

There are two approaches documented in the thermoelectrics literature for heat ﬂux-to-electric
voltage transduction. A conventional Seebeck effect (CSE) transducer produces an electric ﬁeld
parallel to the heat ﬂux. A transverse Seebeck effect (TSE) transducer produces, additionally, an
electric ﬁeld perpendicular to the heat ﬂux. The CSE and the TSE can be employed in the design of
heat ﬂux sensors, yielding devices with very different internal structure and performance
parameters. These are summarized in Fig. 1.

For extreme environments the simpler architecture of the TSE sensor (Fig. 1(B.2)) provides signiﬁcant
advantages in terms of robustness and reliability. The transducer consists of a single material, and
the electrical contacts can be placed on the colder side of the transducer. Consequently, issues
such as inter-diffusion and joint failures, common concerns with the multi-segment wire in the CSE-
based sensor when exposed to high temperatures, are not a concern in the TSE sensor. This project
focused on demonstrating the feasibility of using TSE sensors at high temperatures. The research
involved identifying transducer materials for TSE sensors, designing the package for the sensor,
fabricating and calibrating the sensor, and demonstrating the sensor meets application-relevant

requirements. Each of these steps was thoroughly investigated, experimentally and using computer
simulations as appropriate, as the prior art on this topic is not extensive. Dedicated facilities and
protocols were developed to enable this research.

Relevance to UNIVERSITY COAL RESEARCH priorities

With the proposed new, compact, solid-state heat-ﬂux sensors, the industry’s real-time heat-ﬂux
sensing capabilities will be extended to a challenging regime of extreme environments. These
developments will allow the placement of more heat-ﬂux sensors in more critical locations, for
longer periods of time. The ability to monitor in real-time heat-ﬂuxes in various locations across the
coal-ﬁred power plant undergoing ﬂexible operation will offer new opportunities to understand the
mechanisms by which operational parameters affect the power generation efficiency. Furthermore,
adverse effects of overheating, uneven burner operation and deposition of soot and slag can be
detected and remediated before irreversible damage to components requires shutdowns for repairs.

Project activities

Design of transverse Seebeck effect (TSE)-based sensors

Materials that exhibit the TSE are anisotropic in terms of the components of the Seebeck coefficient
tensor. That is, the measurement of the Seebeck coefficient along different principal crystallographic
axes results in different values. In order to build heat ﬂux sensors suitable for operation in power plant
environments, the transducer materials were selected based on the following criteria: high
anisotropy in the Seebeck coefficient tensor (based on available literature data), low thermal
conductivity, low cost, material availability through commercial suppliers, high melting point, no
phase transitions, low reactivity. The materials selection process narrowed down the transducers
materials to Antimony (Sb) and Rhenium (Re). Antimony is the element with the second highest
Seebeck anisotropy after Bismuth with a melting point of 630 C. Rhenium is the element with
Seebeck anisotropy with the highest melting point. Thus, antimony was chosen for initial proof-of-
concept investigations being less expensive and more responsive. Rhenium was chosen for the
sensors tested for resilience and ruggedness at high temperatures. Both elements can oxidize and
sublimate in air at high-temperatures, therefore, encapsulation of the transducer is part of the sensor
design. Table 1 summarizes the relevant properties of Re and Sb in the context of this project.

Figs. 2 and 3 show the Seebeck literature data that guided our materials selection. The room
temperature values of 𝑆𝑆11 and 𝑆𝑆33 in antimony are 44 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇/𝐾𝐾 and 21 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇/𝐾𝐾, respectively, while in
rhenium they are only −6 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇/𝐾𝐾 and −4.5 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇/𝐾𝐾. This data indicates that the transverse Seebeck
effect at room temperature is an order of magnitude more intense in Sb compared to Re. However,
as shown in Fig. 3, the value of |𝑆𝑆11 − 𝑆𝑆33 | increases in Re as the environment becomes hotter. This
means that the response of the sensor made of rhenium will be stronger at elevated temperatures,
ideal for the target application. We also note that at ~550 K in rhenium 𝑆𝑆11 = 𝑆𝑆33 . At this temperature
the Seebeck coefficient anisotropy vanishes, and the heat ﬂux sensor is “blind”.

Design of the sensor package and Fabrication

Fig. 4 shows the design of the sensors built for this project. The functional components are the two
transducer rectangular prisms from the chosen material (Sb or Re) and electrical wires. For Sb, W
wires were attached to the prisms with silver paint. For Re, W-26Re wires were welded to the prisms
using an electrical-pulse welder. For diagnostic purposes, thermocouples were embedded in the
sensor near prism–wire junctions. The prisms were cemented to each other and to the ceramic
housing. A thin cement layer (~0.1mm) and a thin paint coat covered the heat-facing (i.e. front)
surface of the transducers and limited their exposure to the ambient chemistry. The back surface of
the sensor was placed against a water-/air-cooled heat sink and secured in place using an Inconel
cover frame. A completed and mounted sensor is shown in the photograph in Fig. 4. The signal from
the sensor is collected via the color-coded wiring (on the right side of the image) using a
nanovoltmeter. The other cables seen in the image are the thermocouples.

 Fig 4: (left) Expanded view schematic of the transverse Seebeck effect sensors. (right) Photograph of
 sensor mounted on heat sink.

Computational studies were employed to optimize the geometry of the components of the sensor.
The target of the optimization was to obtain one-dimensional heat ﬂow through the transducers, i.e.
minimize the lateral heat ﬂow (and thus lateral temperature gradients). The optimization parameters
were the thickness and material of the front-side heat spreader, and the material of the ceramic
housing. Fig. 5 shows simulation results of an unoptimized and an optimized conﬁguration. The
optimization succeeded in creating a temperature gradient in the prisms that is almost exclusively
along the 𝑧𝑧̂ – axis. This work fulﬁlled the milestone “One-dimensional Heat Flow”.

Milestone “High Temperature Resilience Test” was accomplished using a Re heat ﬂux sensor. The
sensor was baked under high-purity argon at 1000°C for 10hrs. The health of the sensor could not be
ascertained during the high-temperature processing because the temperature in the
characterization setup cannot be increased beyond 550°C. However, the sensor survived the process
and, after cooling, it was reinstalled in the characterization setup and testing indicated that its
properties remained the same. In other words, the response of the sensor to incident heat ﬂux was
unchanged upon its exposure to high temperatures (Fig. 6b). Inspection of the sensor after the high-
temperature heat treatment revealed minor issues with the black paint and the thermocouple wires.
As shown in Fig. 6c, cracks appeared in certain locations in the black paint. Some greening
developed in the thermocouple wires, as is known for chromium-containing K-type thermocouples.
Both the paint and the thermocouples are auxiliary components that are not essential to the function
of the heat ﬂux sensor. Both can be replaced as needed to suit the application in mind. Speciﬁcally,

when installed in the power plant, the surface of the sensor will be repeatedly covered with soot,
which will supersede the black paint as a radiation absorber. For the post-heat treatment calibration
in Fig. 6b, the chipped black paint was removed and new paint was reapplied, in order to recreate the
conditions of the pre-heat treatment calibration.

Milestone “Calibrated Heat-Flux Measurement System – Demonstration of the precision and
reliability of the measurement system at extreme conditions” was also demonstrated with the data
shown in Fig. 6. Four sweeps of the temperature of the sensor from room temperature to 500 C were
performed, and during each sweep the sensor responsivity was recorded at 30, 100, 200, 300, 400,
and 500 C. The data remained consistent between sweeps. The shaded area in Fig. 6a represents the
theoretical values with ±1𝜎𝜎 of measurement uncertainty. During the ﬁrst temperature sweep,
components of the sensor were still drying and setting (i.e. the cement), explaining the slight
difference between this sweep and the others. Baking the sensor at 500 C towards the end of the ﬁrst
sweep was the necessary step to prevent further evolution of the structure. Post-baking, the high-

temperature sensor demonstrated precision and reliability in measuring heat ﬂux at temperatures up
to 500°C.

Novel infrastructure and protocols

Two important pieces of infrastructure were built at the university to perform the research. These are
the laser scan sensor characterization facility, and the high-temperature sensor calibration facility.
These facilities were also made available to the community. To date, they were used to evaluate the
performance of sensor by Varmo [a startup from Karlsruhe, Germany], in addition to the sensors
created by this project.

In the laser scan sensor characterization facility, a 808-nm CW laser (optical power = 156 mW,
beam diameter = 2 mm) is used to stimulate the heat ﬂux sensor with a localized heat probe that
produced temperature gradients both normal and parallel to the front surface of the sensor. The
location of the beam incident on the front face of the sensor is controlled using a translation stage.
The laser beam is scanned at normal incidence across a square area near the center of the heat ﬂux
sensor, stopping at N discrete locations. The sensor’s steady-state voltage was recorded as a
function of beam position. Fig. 7 shows an example of a data set obtained using the laser scan sensor
characterization facility. The four peaks of alternating positive and negative voltage values originate
from the junctions between the prisms and the wires. They indicate the presence of the conventional
Seebeck effect in the sensor due to the mismatch of the Seebeck coefficient across materials in the
electrical circuit. This effect averages out due to the intentional symmetry in the design of the sensor.
At the center of the plot, a residual voltage of ~1 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇 is due to the transverse Seebeck effect, and it is
representative of the signal generated by the sensor under regular operation conditions (i.e. uniform
incident heat). Thus, the laser scan facility is utilized to elucidate the internal structure and the
operation mechanism of the heat ﬂux sensor in question.

 Fig. 7: Data from a laser scan experiment on a Re-based heat ﬂux sensor. The approximate positions of the
 transducers and the wires is sketched on top of the colored contour map.

In the high-temperature sensor calibration facility, calibration curves are generated for the sensor
under investigation at a ﬁxed temperature selected between 30 C and 500 C. This is made possible
by the facility's unique design, which allows independent control of both the temperature of the
sensor and the incident heat ﬂux. This capability contrasts with other facilities, where the incident
heat ﬂux fully or partially determines the sensor's temperature. The importance of this feature is
clearly demonstrated by the data in Fig. 6a, where the strong dependence of the sensor responsivity
on the temperature is demonstrated.

 Fig. 8: Photographs of the high-temperature sensor calibration facility built for this project at the
 University of Maryland.

In the high-temperature sensor calibration facility, a sensor rack where the prototype sensor and
reference sensor are securely mounted is positioned in front of a tunable heat source (IR lamp in Fig.
8). The prototype sensor is the sensor being tested and calibrated. The reference sensor is a
(commercial) sensor with NIST-traceable calibration. The main goal of the setup is to calibrate the
prototype sensor using the reference sensor. The sensor rack slides between two end positions.
Before any measurement is done, an alignment procedure is followed which guarantees that the end
positions of the rack bring either of the sensors to the exact same position in front of the heat source.
The reference sensor is typically calibrated at 300 K, and during the experiments it is water-cooled to
this temperature. The prototype sensor is placed in a cylindrical compartment (Fig. 9) whose
temperature is maintained at a desired value by a PID-controlled band heater. Each data point in the
calibration curve is obtained by setting the heat source to a certain power, setting the prototype
sensor to a certain temperature, and sequentially positioning the sensors in front of the heat source
for the length of time necessary to achieve steady state. The voltage output of the sensors is read at
each setting. The change in reference sensor voltage is indicative of the heat ﬂux value (i.e.
calibration curve x-axis value). The change in prototype sensor voltage is indicative of the sensor
responsivity (i.e. calibration curve y-axis value). The sensor responsivity is obtained from the slope of
the calibration curve obtained at a single setting of the prototype sensor temperature. Special

corrective procedures were implemented to account for the temperature difference between the two
sensors which affects their thermal emission rate as well as the heat ﬂux their compartment
generates.

 Fig. 9: Schematic of the prototype sensor compartment in the high-temperature sensor calibration setup.
 The band heater is used to modulate the sensor temperature (30-500 C). The sapphire window, present
 also in front of the reference sensor, ensures for reproducibility purposes that the only form of heating
 reaching the sensors’ front surface is radiative heating.

Not shown in Fig. 8 is the data acquisition system. This consists of a Keithley 2812 Nanovoltmeter
that records the output signal from the sensors, and Pico USB_TC-08 Thermocouple Data Logger that
records the temperature from the various thermocouples in the system. These DAQ systems are
integrated and synchronized via a laptop running a Labview program. Additionally, the heat source
and the band heater are regulated manually via analog and digital panels, respectively, on their power
units. Using this setup, we completed the sensor calibration campaigns, and also accomplished
milestone “Autonomous Sensor Operation – Demonstration of self-contained measurement system
using one of the sensor heads”. This involved an experiment in which the system operated
autonomously for 2hr, recording and logging data. The only user intervention during this period was
