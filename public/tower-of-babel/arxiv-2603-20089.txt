A new comparison principle for discrete Volterra equations with an application to convex sweeping processes with infinite delays Journal: Set-Valued Var. Anal.
Thierno Mamadou Baldé, Vuk Milisic, Steffen Plunder

Abstract
Comparison principles for Volterra equations play a role analogous to maximum principles in PDEs: they provide positivity and stability information on the solution and allow one to control the output of bounded inputs. In the continuous setting, such results often rely on Laplace-transform or spectral methods (see e.g. Gripenberg et al. ( 7 , Ch. 4. & 7.) ). However, these tools are not uniform in the discretization step h h hence fail in discrete or semi-discrete approximations. The present note introduces a resolvent-free argument yielding uniform L ∞ ​ ( 0 , T ) L^{\infty}(0,T) -bounds for non-negative kernels. Compactness is a key ingredient in order to show existence of sweeping processes. While in the classical framework it is well established, adding an infinite distribution of delays complicates greatly the obtaining of such a result. In a first step we show a general energy decay estimate, which is then used to establish compactness. The argument is carried out in the discrete setting and that necessitates the introduction of the new comparison principle. In the classical sweeping process the previous position of the particle lies on the boundary of the constraint set, staying O ⁡ ( h ) O(h) close to the next projection point ( h h is the discretization step). Our delay model projects the particle’s averaged (by a unit measure kernel) past positions to the constraint set. Numerical simulations show that the projected point can lie at O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) distance from the convex set’s boundary.

Introduction

Volterra equations arise naturally in the modeling of systems with memory, where the present state depends
on the past history through a convolution kernel. In this work, we provide tools for analyzing a novel type of Volterra
equations which is motivated by a problem from modelling of multi-cellular systems with adhesive memory and non-overlap
conditions (see
13
for the classical differential inclusion setting).

Cells with adhesive memory have been modelled extensively using Volterra-type equations where the memory
represents the forces of adhesion to past positions;
see e.g.
21
;
22
;
12
;
14
;
18
;
15
;
16

The system modelling the quasi-steady state of cells at each point in time reads

\displaystyle z(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t-s)z(s)\,ds=f(t),\quad t>0,

where
z(t)
represents the position of the cell at time
t
,
\varrho
is a
nonnegative kernel describing the adhesive memory, and
f(t)
is an external force acting on the cell.

(left) Adhesive memory cell dynamics. (right) Dynamics of a constraint set moving in circles. The projection distance (red arrow) is not infinitesimal, which is an inherent property of the system and not a time-stepping artifact.

When multiple cells are considered, one might want to impose non-overlapping constraints between cells, effectively constraining the state to a feasible set
C(t)
at each time
t
(see Figure
1
, right, for an example with a moving circular constraint).
This leads to a new Volterra-type sweeping process: find
z(t)\in C(t)
for
t>0
, such that

z(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t-s)z(s)\,ds-f(t)\in-N_{C(t)}(z(t)),\quad t>0,

where
C(t)
can be for instance the
interior convex approximation of the non-overlapping constraint
13

and simplified to a time-dependent convex set and
N_{C(t)}(z(t))
is the normal cone to
C(t)
at
z(t)
.

As the movement of the set
C(t)
might be much faster than the natural dynamics of the unconstrained
system, we experience here a challenging geometric setup. This becomes visible when the dynamics are
recast as a projective system, such as

\displaystyle z(t)=P_{C(t)}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t-s)z(s)\,ds+f(t)\Big),\quad t>0.

Here the projection distance depends crucially on the kernel and on the past trajectory
z(t)
.
This shows the fundamental difference with classical sweeping processes. Indeed, for Moreau’s model,
the projection distance is infinitesimal due to the presence of a time-derivative term.
Dealing with non-infinitesimal projection distances (as illustrated in Figure
1
(right)) requires new bounds on the solution
z(t)
in order to
establish stability and compactness. Often stability results
are based on some kind of Gronwall’s lemma (for ODEs for instance) or more generally on comparison principles.
For Volterra equations these are not straightforward to obtain, this is due to the non-local nature of the convolution operator
and from the physical perspective, the memory effect implies that the solution at time
t
depends on the entire past history,
and can exhibit oscillations or growth that are not present in local differential equations.
Comparison principles for such equations play a
role analogous to maximum principles in parabolic PDEs: they ensure that bounded or positive data produce
bounded or positive solutions, and they provide essential
a priori
bounds for qualitative and numerical analysis.

In the continuous setting, stability results can often be obtained thanks to
the concept of a
resolvent
associated with the Volterra kernel. This is a special function
r
that solves:

r(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t-s)r(s)\,ds=\varrho(t),\qquad t>0.

Using the Laplace transform provides a spectral
decomposition of
r
as a sum of exponentials and a remainder term that
is often integrable or decays in a sufficient manner
(
7
, Ch. 4 & 7)
.
However, in many applications—especially when the kernel is not exponentially decaying or when it depends on
the discretization parameter—such a spectral decomposition is not directly available. Moreover,
even if Wiener’s lemma can be invoked to provide a similar asymptotic description of the discrete resolvent,
the corresponding representation is not uniform with respect to the discretization step
h

and therefore does not provide uniform
a priori
bounds for the discrete solutions.

Classical convergence and stability analyses of discrete Volterra equations,
such as those of McKee and Jones
9
, Bakke and Jackiewicz
1
,
and Lubich
10
;
11
, rely on spectral or Laplace-transform techniques that
require exponentially decaying kernels or j whose Laplace transform is well-behaved.
These approaches provide powerful tools
for these kernels but fail to yield uniform bounds when the memory kernel is not explicit
or lacks analyticity. The present work develops a comparison principle that
circumvents the use of any spectral decomposition and applies equally to continuous and
discrete settings. The key novelty lies in the construction of an
initial layer

corrector compensating errors introduced by the tails of the leading order part of the super-solution.
Applying the Volterra operator to the complete ansatz (leading order plus initial layer corrector) is then able to
dominate the right-hand side of the Volterra equation,
whose solution we aim to control, thanks to a comparison principle specific to Volterra equations
7
;
14
.
We underline that the comparison principle found in
(
14
, Theorem 2.7)
does not hold in the case of a
non-exponential kernel that is constant in time.

Moreau’s sweeping process (classical formulation)
is the baseline model: a first-order differential inclusion

-\dot{X}(t)\in N_{C(t)}(X(t))
introduced by J.-J. Moreau
20
.
Delay and history-dependent perturbations have been studied since:
existence results for sweeping processes with delays and history operators were
established in works by Castaing & Monteiro Marques
3
and
Edmond
5
.
Most results for delayed sweeping processes keep a time-derivative
term (i.e. explicit
\dot{X}
or its fractional analogue) in the formulation;
the analysis relies on this differential feature to obtain compactness and pass-to-the-limit.
Fractional-time derivative sweeping processes (Caputo-type) have been developed recently;
see e.g. Zeng
24
and Bouach
2
,

and—importantly—compactness properties coming from fractional regularizing effects.
By contrast, replacing the time-derivative by a general Volterra (integro-differential / history)
operator leads to different analytic difficulties: compactness is generally lost unless very
strong hypotheses are imposed.
Recent papers prove well-posedness for Volterra-type integro-differential
sweeping processes using tailored a priori estimates;
see Vilches
23
, Godoy et al.
6
, and Haddad
8
.
State-dependent (moving set depends on
x
) and prox-regular (non-convex) extensions have been
developed for both delay and fractional setups; proofs typically combine prox-regular geometry
with fixed-point or reparametrization techniques.
Numerical and applied analyses (contact, frictionless impact, plasticity)
motivate the integro-differential and fractional variants.

Our work contributes to this recent line of research by establishing a new comparison principle
for discrete integral Volterra equations with non-exponential non-negative kernels.
To be more precise, our problem does not involve a time-derivative term,
rather it is replaced by a memory operator involving an infinite distribution of delays.
This operator has been introduced in
21
to account
for a microscopic friction mechanism mediated by transient elastic linkages to past positions.
Biological considerations motivate this model, as cells adhere to their past positions
through transient bonds that form and break over time.
In order to show existence of sweeping processes relying on such principles,
we use minimizing movements à la De Giorgi. The key step is to obtain
uniform
H^{1}
-bounds on the discrete solutions, which then provides
compactness by Ascoli-Arzelà’s theorem. The new comparison principle
allows one to obtain such uniform bounds in the discrete setting.
This is crucial for passing to the limit and establishing existence of solutions.
To our knowledge, there is no such result in the literature yet,
even for the case of the simplest sweeping process where the moving convex set
is a sphere.

2.1
Comparison principles for Volterra equations: continuous and discrete settings

In a first step, and for the sake of clarity, we show the
theoretical construction leading to the comparison principle
in the continuous setting.

Theorem 2.1
(Comparison principle for continuous Volterra equations)

Assume that

solves:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(z(t)-z(t-a))\varrho(a)da=f(t),&t>0\\
&z(t)={z}_{p}(t),&t<0,\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

under Hypothesis
2.1

z\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
for any finite

This result is made possible by introducing an initial layer corrector.
For the sake of clarity, technical aspects are further detailed in section
3.1

We discretize the time interval

Theorem 2.2
(Uniform discrete comparison principle)

Consider the discrete problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &Z^{n}-h\sum_{j\geq 0}Z^{n-j}R_{j}=f^{n},&n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}\\

(3)

2.2
Energy estimates for delayed sweeping processes

We formulate our sweeping process as the projection of the averaged

X^{n}:=P_{C^{n}}(\overline{X}^{n}),\quad\overline{X}^{n}:=\frac{h\sum_{j\geq 1}X^{n-j}R_{j}}{h\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}},\quad n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}.

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(z(t)-z(t-a))\varrho(a)da=f(t),&t>0\\

By standard arguments:

Theorem 3.1

Under assumptions

An initial layer

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\tilde{p}+\partial_{a}\tilde{p}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\partial_{a}\varrho({\tilde{a}})\tilde{p}({\tilde{a}},t)d{\tilde{a}},&a>0,\;t>0\\
&\tilde{p}(0,t)=0&a=0,\;t>0\\
&\tilde{p}(a,0)=-\int_{0}^{a}u_{I}(s)ds&a>0,\;t=0\end{aligned}\right.

Theorem 3.2

Assume that
\partial_{a}\varrho(a)\leq 0
and that there exists a constant
\zeta>0
s.t.
-\partial_{a}\varrho/\varrho<\zeta
, then

w\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}_{+}})
and
w\geq 0
for a.e.
t\geq 0
.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
3.2
)

We apply the key idea of
(
18
, Lemma 5.1)
and write:

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathit{t}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varrho(a)|\tilde{p}(a,t)|da

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\partial_{a}\varrho(a)|\tilde{p}(a,t)|da\leq-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\partial_{a}\varrho(a)|\tilde{p}(a,t)|da,

(14)

providing that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varrho(a)|\tilde{p}(a,t)|da

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varrho(a)|\tilde{p}(a,0)|da=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varrho(a)\left|\int_{0}^{a}u_{I}\right|da\lesssim\|\varrho(1+a)^{2}\|_{1}.

(15)

Then writing that

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=w(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{w}(s)\,ds=w(0)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\partial_{a}\varrho(a)u(a,s)\,da\,ds

\displaystyle=w(0)-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\partial_{a}\varrho(a)p(a,t)\,da,

this provides that:

\displaystyle\left|w(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{0}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{-\partial_{a}\varrho}{\varrho}\varrho(a)|p(a,t)|da\leq\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{0}}+\zeta\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varrho(a)|p(a,t)|da

(16)

\displaystyle\leq C+\zeta\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|\tilde{p}(a,t)|\varrho(a)da+\zeta\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left|\int_{0}^{a}u_{I}(s)ds\right|\varrho(a)da

(17)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\|\varrho\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},(1+a)^{2})}\right).

(18)

The initial layer corrector
w
is thus bounded in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}_{+}})
. Moreover it is non-negative since the source term in (
8
) is non-negative and the kernel is also non-negative.
Indeed, the resolvent associated with (
8
) is positive:
as we assumed that
0\leq-\partial_{a}\varrho(a)/\varrho(a)\leq\zeta
,
which gives immediately that

\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(a)da=1-\int_{t}^{\infty}\varrho(a)da\leq 1-\frac{\varrho(0)\exp(-\zeta t)}{\zeta\mu_{0}}<1.

We are in the position to apply
(
7
, Proposition 9.8.1)
, which ensures the positivity of the resolvent. Next, one has the
representation formula for the solution
w
of (
8
):
w(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}(a-t)\varrho(a)da+\int_{0}^{t}r(t-a)\int_{a}^{\infty}(s-a)\varrho(s)dsda\geq 0
.

3.2
A new comparison principle

Theorem 3.3

Assume that
z\in L^{1}_{{{\mathrm{loc}}}}({\mathbb{R}_{+}})
solves:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(z(t)-z(t-a))\varrho(a)da=f(t),&t>0\\
&z(t)={z}_{p}(t),&t<0,\end{aligned}\right.

(19)

under Assumptions
2.1
.
then
z\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
for any finite
T
.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
3.3
(stated in Section
2
as Theorem
2.1
))

Our aim is now to construct a super-solution of our problem, for this purpose we start as in the proof of
(
14
, Theorem 1.1)
and we define:

U_{f}(t):=C+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu_{1}}\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{t}{\left\|{f}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(s,T)}ds&t>0\\
t{\left\|{f}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(20)

and we complete this profile by adding an initial layer,
defining
S_{f}(t):=U(t)+\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1}}w(t)
, where we denote

\|f\|_{\infty}:=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}
.
We compute

\displaystyle S_{f}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}S_{f}(t-a)\varrho(a)\,da

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(U_{f}(t)-U_{f}(t-a)\right)\varrho(a)\,da+\int_{t}^{\infty}U_{f}(t-a)\varrho(a)\,da

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1}}\int_{t}^{\infty}(a-t)\varrho(a)\,da,

(21)

by the same arguments as in the proof of
(
14
, Theorem 1.1)
, one writes:

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}+\int_{t}^{\infty}\right)\left(U(t)-U(t-a)\right)\varrho(a)\,da

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu_{1}}\biggl\{\int_{0}^{t}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T)}a\varrho(a)\,da

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{\infty}\bigl(t\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T)}+(a-t)\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\bigr)\varrho(a)\,da\biggr\}\geq|f(t)|.

it remains to take care of the non-positive tail
\int_{t}^{\infty}U_{f}(t-a)\varrho(a)da
added in order to obtain the latter inequality. one concludes that

\displaystyle S_{f}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}S_{f}(t-a)\varrho(a)\,da

\displaystyle\geq|f(t)|+\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(C+\frac{{\left\|{f}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}}{\mu_{1}}(t-a)\right)\varrho(a)\,da

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1}}\int_{t}^{\infty}(a-t)\varrho(a)\,da\geq|f(t)|

(22)

the major improvement is here the compensation that our initial layer corrector
w
provides: the linear part of
U
’s tail is negative and exactly
matching the positive contribution from
w
.
As in the proof of Theorem
3.2
, the resolvent being positive one can apply
(
7
, Lemma 9.8.2 p. 257)
in order to conclude.
Namely

\displaystyle|z(t)|-\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle|z(t-a)|\varrho(a)da

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|f(t)|}{\mu_{0}}+\int_{t}^{\infty}\left|{z}_{p}(t-a)\right|\varrho(a)\,da=:\tilde{f}

\displaystyle\leq S_{\tilde{f}}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}S_{\tilde{f}}(t-a)\varrho(a)\,da

which implies that
|z(t)|\leq S_{\tilde{f}}(t)
, as soon as
|z(0)|\leq S(0)
, the latter condition is fulfilled by tuning the value of
C
in the definition of
U
in (
20
).

Remark 3

The condition
-\partial_{a}\varrho(a)/\varrho(a)\leq\zeta
allows to consider polynomial decrease of the kernel at infinity. The method of recovering the tails introduced in
14

in Lemma 2.4 fails for the latter kernels. If one considers only
U
as a super-solution, then applying the integral operator implies:

\displaystyle U(t)-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}U(t-a)\varrho(a)da\geq|f(t)|+C\int_{t}^{\infty}\varrho(a)da+\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1}}\int_{t}^{\infty}(t-a)\varrho(a)da

\displaystyle=|f(t)|+\int_{t}^{\infty}\varrho(a)da\left(C+\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1}}\frac{\int_{t}^{\infty}(t-a)\varrho(a)da}{\int_{t}^{\infty}\varrho(a)da}\right)

\displaystyle=:|f(t)|+\int_{t}^{\infty}\varrho(a)da\left(C-\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1}}A[\varrho](t)\right)

Here one could ask that
A[\varrho](t)
is uniformly bounded in time, as in
(
14
, Lemma 2.4)
. However, there are simple counterexamples for this, such as
\varrho(a)=(1+a)^{-4}
for which
A[\rho]
is not uniformly bounded:

A[\varrho](t)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}a\varrho(a+t)da}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\varrho(a+t)da}\sim\frac{t}{2}.

The motivation of our approach is that there is no need anymore
to compute the bound on this latter quantity since the negative term of the right hand side in the comparison principle is compensated by the contribution of the initial layer corrector.

Another argument should be used in the case of a compactly supported kernel. Indeed in this case there exists a time
t_{0}
great enough s.t.

\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(a)da=1
, which prevents from using
(
7
, Lemma 9.8.2)
.

4
Numerical counterpart

In order to apply the previous ideas to the discrete setting, we first need to
introduce a discrete framework that approximates the solutions of (
7
).
We use piecewise constant approximations. Indeed, for any locally integrable function
g
defined on
{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
, we set:

g^{n}:=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}g(t)dt
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Using the rectangular rule, the discrete counterpart of (
7
) reads: find
(Z^{n})_{n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}}
solving

\left\{\begin{aligned} &Z^{n}-h\sum_{j\geq 0}Z^{n-j}R_{j}=f^{n},&n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}\\
&Z^{n}=Z_{p}^{n},&n<0\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

where the discrete kernel
(R_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined as in Section
4.1
below. The sequence
(R_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
discretizes the kernel
\varrho
:

R_{j}:=\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}\varrho(a)da
.

Lemma 1

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.3
, the discrete kernel
(R_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a non-increasing sequence satisfying

-\frac{R_{j}-R_{j-1}}{hR_{j}}\leq\zeta^{\prime}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and all
h\leq h_{0}
,
where
\zeta^{\prime}:=2\zeta
and
h_{0}:=1/(2\zeta)
.

Proof

Writing the difference:
R_{j+1}-R_{j}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}(\varrho(a+h)-\varrho(a))da
,
then using the monotonicity of
\varrho
provides that
R_{j+1}\leq R_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Next, using the hypothesis
\partial_{a}\varrho(s)\geq-\zeta\varrho(s)
and
the monotonicity of
\varrho
(which gives
\varrho(s)\leq\varrho(a)
for
s\geq a
):

\displaystyle R_{j+1}-R_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}(\varrho(a+h)-\varrho(a))da=\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}\int_{a}^{a+h}\partial_{a}\varrho(s)\,ds\,da

(24)

\displaystyle\geq-\zeta\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}\int_{a}^{a+h}\varrho(s)\,ds\,da\geq-\zeta\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}h\,\varrho(a)\,da=-\zeta hR_{j}

(25)

Rearranging:
(R_{j}-R_{j+1})/(hR_{j+1})\leq\zeta/(1-\zeta h)\leq 2\zeta
for
\zeta h\leq 1/2
.
Setting
j\to j-1
provides the desired result (with
\zeta
replaced by
\zeta^{\prime}:=2\zeta
).

4.1
The initial layer

We solve:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &W^{n}-h\sum_{j\geq 0}W^{n-j}R_{j}=0&n\geq 0\\
&W^{n}=-nh&n<0.\end{aligned}\right.

(26)

The discrete elongation variable can be defined as in the continuous setting:
U^{n}_{j}:=W^{n}-W^{n-j}
, and the analog of (
10
) becomes

U^{n+1}_{j+1}=U^{n}_{j}+\delta W^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},\quad\delta W^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}:=W^{n+1}-W^{n},\quad j\geq 0,\quad n\geq 0.

(27)

Expressing (
26
) with the elongation variable leads to the conservation principle
h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n}_{j}R_{j}=0
for all
n\geq 0
.

Lemma 2

The discrete finite differences satisfy:

\delta W^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}=-h\sum_{j\geq 1}{(R_{j}-R_{j-1})}U^{n+1}_{j},\quad n\geq 0

Proof

Summing (
27
) against
hR_{j}
for
j\geq 0

provides:

h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}U^{n+1}_{j+1}=h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}U^{n}_{j}+\delta W^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}
,
then adding and subtracting
h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}U^{n+1}_{j}
, one obtains:

h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}U^{n+1}_{j}+h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}(U^{n+1}_{j+1}-U^{n+1}_{j})=h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}U^{n}_{j}+\delta W^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}.

(28)

Performing an integration by parts transforms the second term on the left hand side into:

h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}(U^{n+1}_{j+1}-U^{n+1}_{j})=-h\sum_{j\geq 1}{(R_{j}-R_{j-1})}U^{n+1}_{j}

and the conservation principle cancels the first terms on both sides of (
28
).

Corollary 1

The elongation variable satisfies a closed equation:

U^{n+1}_{k+1}=U^{n}_{k}-h\sum_{j\geq 1}{(R_{j}-R_{j-1})}U^{n+1}_{j},\quad k\geq 0,\quad n\geq 0

(29)

which is the discrete version of (
10
).

Proposition 2

Assuming that there exists
\zeta^{\prime}>0
s.t. for all
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

-\frac{R_{j}-R_{j-1}}{hR_{j}}\leq\zeta^{\prime}

The discrete initial layer
(W^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
solving (
26
) is a bounded non-negative sequence (and the bound is uniform with respect to
h
).

Proof
(Proof of Proposition
2
)

We set
P^{n}_{j}:=h\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}U^{\ell}_{j}
, and we notice again that, as
\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}U^{\ell}_{j}\equiv 0
,

\sum_{j\geq 0}P^{n}_{j}R_{j}\equiv 0
. Next using (
29
), one obtains that:

P^{n+1}_{k+1}-hU^{0}_{k+1}=P^{n}_{k}-h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})(P^{n+1}_{j}-hU^{0}_{j}),\quad k\geq 0

which is the discrete equivalent of (
12
).
Now we want to avoid constant source terms in the left hand side of
the previous expression so that we define
\tilde{P}_{j}^{n}:=P^{n}_{j}-h\sum_{k\in\{0,\dots,j\}}U^{0}_{k}

which transforms the previous equality into:

\tilde{P}_{k+1}^{n+1}=\tilde{P}_{k}^{n}-h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})\left(\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{j}+h\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}U^{0}_{\ell}\right)

But
\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}U^{0}_{\ell}=-\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}U^{0}_{j}-R_{0}U^{0}_{0}=0
, leading to

\tilde{P}_{k+1}^{n+1}=\tilde{P}_{k}^{n}-h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{j},\quad\forall k\geq 0,\;\forall n\geq 0.

Applying the absolute value to the previous expression and integrating against
hR_{k}
yields:

I^{n}:=h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{k+1}|\leq h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}_{k}^{n}|-h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})|\tilde{P}_{j}^{n}|=:J^{n}

But

I^{n}=h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{k}|-h\sum_{k\geq 1}(R_{k}-R_{k-1})|\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{k}|
,
and as
I^{n}\leq J^{n}
, this simplifies into:

h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{k}|\leq h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}^{n}_{k}|

and by induction this provides:

h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{k}|\leq h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}|\tilde{P}^{0}_{k}|\leq h\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{k}\left|h\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}U^{0}_{\ell}\right|\leq C(\mu_{2,h},\mu_{1,h},\mu_{0})

Returning to the definition of
(W^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, one has

\delta W^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}=-h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})U^{n+1}_{j}

leading to

W^{n+1}-W^{0}=-\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j-1})(P^{n+1}_{j}-hU^{0}_{j})

and then

\displaystyle|W^{n+1}|\leq

\displaystyle|W^{0}|-h\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{R_{j}-R_{j-1}}{hR_{j}}R_{j}\left(|P^{n+1}_{j}|+h|U_{j}^{0}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq|W^{0}|+\zeta^{\prime}\left\{h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}|P^{n+1}_{j}|+h^{2}\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}|U_{j}^{0}|\right\}

\displaystyle\leq|W^{0}|+h\zeta^{\prime}\left\{\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}|\tilde{P}^{n+1}_{j}|+h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\left|U_{k}^{0}\right|+c_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq|W^{0}|+h\zeta^{\prime}\left\{2h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\left|U_{k}^{0}\right|+c_{1}\right\}\leq C(\mu_{0,h},\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h})

Next we prove that
W^{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 0
by induction.
First
W^{0}=\mu_{1,h}/(1-hR_{0})\geq 0
for
h

small enough (and the bound is uniform by Lebesgue’s Theorem for
h<h_{0}
).
By definition of
W
,

W^{n}=-nh\geq 0
for
n<0
.
Assume now that
W^{k}\geq 0
for all
k\leq n
, then using (
26
),

(1-hR_{0})W^{n+1}=h\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}W^{n+1-j}\geq 0
,
which concludes the proof.

4.2
Discrete resolvent and comparison principle

First we discretise (
7
). To do so we recall that the meshsize is
h>0
and the discrete kernel reads
(R_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
as in the previous section.

By using successive convolutions of
(R_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

(Q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
resolvent that solves

Q_{n}-h\sum_{j=0}^{n}Q_{n-j}R_{j}=R_{n},\quad\forall n\geq 0.

The discrete convolution of two sequences
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(V_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined as:

(U\star V)_{n}:=h\sum_{j=0}^{n}U_{n-j}V_{j},\quad\forall n\geq 0.

The solution of (

Z=f+f\star Q
.

Theorem 4.1

Assume that
-(R_{j}-R_{j-1})/(hR_{j})\leq\zeta^{\prime}
for all
h>0
and all
j\geq 1
,
and assumptions
2.1
, then the solution of (
23
)

(Z^{n})_{n\in\{0,\dots,N\}}
is bounded uniformly with respect to
h
.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
4.1

(stated in Section
2
as Theorem
2.2
))

First notice that thanks to the first hypothesis of the claim,

h\sum_{j=0}^{n}R_{j}=1-h\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}R_{j}\leq 1-(1+\zeta^{\prime}h)^{-n+1}R_{0}/\zeta^{\prime}<1
.
Then let’s define
Q^{m}:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}R^{k\star}
, in the sense of sequences in
\ell^{1}
such that

(R\star R)_{n}=h\sum_{j=0}^{n}R_{n-j}R_{j}
.
One has that
Q=\lim_{m\to\infty}Q^{m}
remains non-negative and finite for any
n\geq 0
.
Indeed

|Q^{m}_{n}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}(h\sum_{j=0}^{n}R_{j})^{k}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}(1-\delta(n))^{k}\leq\delta(n)^{-1}<\infty
.
The solution
Z
can then be expressed as
Z=f+f\star Q

and as the verification is purely algebraic it follows the same steps as in
(
7
, Theorem 2.3.5 p. 44)
.
In the same spirit, one can extend
(
7
, Lemma 9.8.3)
and show that if
S\geq S\star R+f

in the sense of convolution of sequences, then
S^{n}\geq Z^{n}
for every
n\geq 0
,
where
Z
is the sequence solving the equation
Z=R\star Z+f
.

The purpose is now to construct
S
. We set

S^{n}_{f}:=\frac{|f|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1,h}}W^{n}+V^{n},

where

V^{n}:=C+\frac{1}{\mu_{1,h}}\begin{cases}h\sum_{j=0}^{n}\max_{k\in\{j,\dots,N\}}|f^{k}|&\text{if }n\geq 0,\\
hn|f|_{\infty}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

then applying the integral operator gives:

\displaystyle I^{n}

\displaystyle:=S^{n}_{f}-h\sum_{j=0}^{n}S^{n-j}_{f}R_{j}

\displaystyle\geq h\sum_{j=1}^{n}(V^{n}-V^{n-j})R_{j}+h\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}(V^{n}-V^{n-j})R_{j}+h\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}V^{n-j}R_{j}

\displaystyle+h\frac{|f|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}h(j-n)R_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{h}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h\!\sum_{k=n-j}^{n}\max_{\ell\in\{k,\dots,N\}}|f^{\ell}|R_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}h\max_{\ell\in\{k,\dots,N\}}|f^{\ell}|R_{j}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}(V^{0}-V^{n-j})R_{j}

\displaystyle+h\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}V^{n-j}R_{j}+h\frac{|f|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}h(j-n)R_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{h}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}hj|f^{n}|R_{j}+\frac{h}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}hn|f^{n}|R_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{|f|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}h({j-n})R_{j}+h\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}V^{n-j}R_{j}

\displaystyle+h\frac{|f|_{\infty}}{\mu_{1,h}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}h(j-n)R_{j}

\displaystyle\geq|f^{n}|

The first line in the last inequality is then greater than
|f^{n}|
(the three summations with respect to

reduce to 1)
and the second line cancels since the initial layer corrector’s right hand side compensates exactly the
tail of
U
that was added and subtracted in the first lines above. One then compares:

\left|Z^{n}\right|-h\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left|Z^{n-j}\right|R_{j}\leq|\tilde{f}^{n}|\leq S_{\tilde{f}}-h\sum_{j=0}^{n}S^{n-j}_{\tilde{f}}R_{j}

where
\tilde{f}^{n}:=f^{n}+h\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}|Z^{n-j}|R_{j}
, which implies that

as soon as

.

5
Application to delayed sweeping processes

We now apply the comparison principle developed in the previous sections to study delayed sweeping processes.
Recall Hypothesis
2.2
on the family of closed convex sets.

In this section we solve the following discrete problem: for any
n\geq 0
,

X^{n}:=P_{C^{n}}(\overline{X}^{n}),\quad\overline{X}^{n}:=\frac{h\sum_{j\geq 1}X^{n-j}R_{j}}{h\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}}.

(30)

where
P_{C^{n}}
is the projection onto the convex set
C^{n}
defined above.

This problem can be reformulated as the following minimization problem: for any
n\geq 0
,

X^{n}:=\argmin_{W\in C^{n}}{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(W),\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(W):=\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}(W-X^{n-j})^{2}R_{j}.

(31)

Proposition 3
(Minimization principle)

The two minimizing problems (
30
) and (
31
) are equivalent.

Proof

By definition of the projection onto a convex set: given
\overline{X}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
there exists a unique
X^{n}\in C^{n}
s.t.

X^{n}=\argmin_{W\in C^{n}}|W-\overline{X}^{n}|^{2}

Now we start from the energy functional
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
and we compute:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(W)

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(W-\overline{X}^{n}+\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n-j}\right)^{2}R_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(W-\overline{X}^{n}\right)^{2}R_{j}+h\sum_{j\geq 1}(W-\overline{X}^{n})(\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n-j})R_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}(\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n-j})^{2}R_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1-hR_{0})|W-\overline{X}^{n}|^{2}+R((X^{n-j})_{j\geq 1})

where the remainder term
R
does not depend on
W
. This concludes the proof.

5.1
Energy estimates

Proposition 4

Assume that the sequence
(R_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing and that the convex sets satisfy
Hypothesis
2.2
.
Then the discrete sweeping sequence
(X^{n})_{n\geq 0}
defined by (
31
) satisfies the energy estimate:

{\mathcal{E}}_{N}(X^{N})+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}D_{n}\leq e^{2T}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(X^{0})+e^{2T}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e_{n},

where
D_{n}=\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j+1})|X^{n}-X^{n-j}|^{2}\geq 0
is the dissipation term and

e_{n}:=e(C^{n},C^{n+1})^{2}/h
.

Proof
(Proof of Proposition
4
, stated in Section
2
as Proposition
1
)

The minimization principle (
31
) gives

{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n+1})\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(P_{C^{n+1}}(X^{n})).

Adding and subtracting
X^{n}
inside the energy functional leads to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-hR_{0}}{2}|X^{n}-P_{C^{n+1}}(X^{n})|^{2}

\displaystyle+h\sum_{j\geq 1}(X^{n}-P_{C^{n+1}}(X^{n}),\,X^{n+1-j}-X^{n})R_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}|X^{n+1-j}-X^{n}|^{2}R_{j}

We denote
e_{n}:=e(C^{n},C^{n+1})^{2}/h
, and write accordingly

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h}{2}e_{n}+h\sum_{j\geq 1}e(C^{n},C^{n+1})|X^{n+1-j}-X^{n}|R_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}|X^{n+1-j}-X^{n}|^{2}R_{j}.

Using Young’s inequality on the second term on the right-hand side gives

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq(1+2h)e_{n}+(1+2h){\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n}).

Then using the definition of the energy functional and rearranging terms leads to

{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n})={\mathcal{E}}_{n}(X^{n})-\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j+1}-R_{j})|X^{n}-X^{n-j}|^{2}=:{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(X^{n})-D_{n},

where
D_{n}=\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j+1})|X^{n}-X^{n-j}|^{2}\geq 0
is the dissipation term.
Putting everything together, we obtain

{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n+1})+(1+2h)D_{n}\leq(1+2h){\mathcal{E}}_{n}(X^{n})+(1+2h)e_{n}.

Iterating this inequality from
n=0
to
n=N-1
gives

{\mathcal{E}}_{N}(X^{N})+(1+2h)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}D_{n}\leq(1+2h)^{N}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(X^{0})+(1+2h)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(1+2h)^{N-1-n}e_{n}.

Since
(1+2h)^{N}\leq e^{2T}
and
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(1+2h)^{N-1-n}e_{n}\leq e^{2T}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e_{n}
, the proof is complete.

Remark 4

The energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{N}(X^{N})
completes the dissipation term at step
n=N
since one writes:

\displaystyle h\sum_{p=0}^{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j+1})\left|X^{p}-X^{p-j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathcal{E}}_{N}(X^{N})+2h\sum_{p=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j}-R_{j+1})\left|X^{p}-X^{p-j}\right|^{2}

Corollary 2

Under the previous hypotheses,

\sum_{p=0}^{N}h\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})\left|X^{p}-X^{p-j}\right|\right)^{2}\leq C<\infty

uniformly with respect to the discretization step
h
.

Proof

Since the sequence
(R_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is non-increasing and of mass 1 (indeed
h\sum_{j\geq 0}R_{j}=1
), one has that

\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j-1}-R_{j}=R_{0}\leq(h{\mathrm{Lip}}_{\varrho}/2+\varrho(0))/\mu_{0}<\infty

where we have used the Lipschitz hypothesis in Assumptions
2.1
.
Moreover, using the discrete ratio bound
(R_{j-1}-R_{j})\leq\zeta^{\prime}hR_{j}
for
j\geq 1
,
one has

h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})|X^{p}-X^{p-j}|^{2}\leq\zeta^{\prime}h^{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}|X^{p}-X^{p-j}|^{2}=2\zeta^{\prime}{\mathcal{E}}_{p}(X^{p}).

Then using Cauchy-Schwarz,
one writes:

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{N}

\displaystyle h\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})|X^{p}-X^{p-j}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=0}^{N}h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})|X^{p}-X^{p-j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq R_{0}\sum_{p=0}^{N}h\sum_{j\geq 1}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})|X^{p}-X^{p-j}|^{2}\leq 2\zeta^{\prime}R_{0}\sum_{p=0}^{N}{\mathcal{E}}_{p}(X^{p})<\infty

where the last bound follows from Proposition
4
.

Corollary 3

Under the previous hypotheses, for
h
small enough, one has the uniform bound:

\left|\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n}\right|\leq C

Proof

Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, one writes:

\displaystyle\left|\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{h\sum_{j\geq 1}(X^{n-j}-X^{n})R_{j}}{1-hR_{0}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h\sum_{j\geq 1}(X^{n-j}-X^{n})^{2}R_{j}}{1-hR_{0}}\times\frac{h\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}}{1-hR_{0}}\leq\frac{2{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(X^{n})}{1-hR_{0}}<\infty

where we have used the energy estimate provided by Proposition
4
.

Remark 5

We notice that contrarily to the standard sweeping process without delay,
the distance between the projected point
X^{n}
and the unconstrained point
\overline{X}^{n}

remains only bounded whereas in the standard case it is
h
close.
This greatly complicates the
analysis and does not allow to obtain straightforward Lipschitz or
BV
estimates in time as in the classical cases
19
.

5.2
The case of a moving circular set

In this section we prove Theorem
2.4
. We consider the case where the convex sets are moving circles:

C(t):=\overline{B}(c(t),R)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\phi_{t}(x):=|x-c(t)|^{2}-R^{2}\leq 0\}

(32)

with
c\in H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
We discretize the sets as
C^{n}:=C(nh)
for
n=0,\dots,N
with
N=\lfloor T/h\rfloor
.
The corresponding discrete sweeping process is defined by (
31
). Under these assumptions,
Hypothesis
2.2
is satisfied since

d_{H}(C^{n},C^{n-1})\leq|c(nh)-c((n-1)h)|

and
c
being in
H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})
implies that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}d_{H}(C^{n},C^{n-1})^{2}/h\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}|c(nh)-c((n-1)h)|^{2}/h\leq|c|_{H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})}^{2}<\infty.

We denote by
\varphi_{n}(X):=|X-c^{n}|^{2}-R^{2}
where
c^{n}:=c(nh)
. These constraints
are qualified in the sense of
4
since
\nabla\varphi_{n}(X)=2(X-c^{n})\neq 0

for all
X
such that
\varphi_{n}(X)=0
. This provides the necessary condition
in order to write Euler Lagrange equations (in their Kuhn and Tucker version)
associated to the minimization problem (
31
), they read:

h\sum_{j\geq 0}(X^{n}-X^{n-j})R_{j}+\lambda^{n}\nabla\varphi_{n}(X^{n})=0,\quad\forall n\geq 0.

(33)

Since
\mu_{0}=1
, the Euler-Lagrange equation identifies
\lambda^{n}\nabla\varphi_{n}(X^{n})=\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n}
.
We denote the
projection displacement increment

\delta P^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}:=\lambda^{n+1}\nabla\varphi_{n+1}(X^{n+1})-\lambda^{n}\nabla\varphi_{n}(X^{n})

and recall the
step

\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}:=X^{n+1}-X^{n}
.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
2.4
)

We recall the definition of
\varphi_{n}(X):=|X-c^{n}|^{2}-R^{2}
where
c^{n}:=c(nh)
.
From the Euler-Lagrange equation (
33
), setting the elongation variable as

U^{n}_{j}:=X^{n}-X^{n-j}
for
j\geq 1
, it satisfies by definition

U^{n+1}_{j+1}=U^{n}_{j}+X^{n+1}-X^{n}

which summed against
R_{j}
gives

h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n+1}_{j+1}R_{j}=h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n}_{j}R_{j}+\mu_{0}(X^{n+1}-X^{n})=h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n}_{j}R_{j}+X^{n+1}-X^{n},

(34)

since
\mu_{0}=h\sum_{j}R_{j}=1
.
Adding and subtracting
U^{n+1}_{j}
in the left hand side gives

\displaystyle h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n+1}_{j+1}R_{j}

\displaystyle=h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n+1}_{j}R_{j}+h\sum_{j\geq 0}(U^{n+1}_{j+1}-U^{n+1}_{j})R_{j}

\displaystyle=h\sum_{j\geq 1}U^{n}_{j}R_{j}+(X^{n+1}-X^{n}).

By a change of indices one recovers that

\sum_{j\geq 0}(U^{n+1}_{j+1}-U^{n+1}_{j})R_{j}=\sum_{j\geq 1}U^{n+1}_{j}(R_{j-1}-R_{j})=:d^{n+1}.

We use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to estimate
d^{n+1}
:

\displaystyle(d^{n+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h\sum_{j\geq 1}|U^{n+1}_{j}|^{2}R_{j}\cdot h\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\frac{R_{j-1}-R_{j}}{h}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{R_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}(X^{n+1})C_{R}=:C_{R}^{\prime}

where we have used the definition of the energy functional
{\mathcal{E}}_{n+1}
and hypothesis (
6
).
Here we underline that although
d^{n+1}
seems to be related to the dissipation term of the energy estimates from Proposition
4
,
the index shift prevents from using Corollary
2
to obtain a uniform bound on
d^{n+1}
,
and we were forced to rely on the alternative above.

The Euler-Lagrange equation (
33
),
can be rephrased as

h\sum_{j\geq 0}U^{n}_{j}R_{j}+\lambda^{n}G^{n}=0

and provides using the equality:

-\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}+d^{n+1}=-\lambda^{n}G^{n}+(X^{n+1}-X^{n})

where we defined
G^{n}:=\nabla\varphi_{n}(X^{n})=2(X^{n}-c^{n})
.
Testing the previous equality by
X^{n+1}-X^{n}
gives:

|X^{n+1}-X^{n}|^{2}+\left(\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},X^{n+1}-X^{n}\right)=h\left(d^{n+1},X^{n+1}-X^{n}\right).

The second term on the left-hand side is the projection-step product

\langle\delta P^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rangle,

for which we need a lower bound (see Remark
6
and Figure
4
).
We decompose it as:

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\left(\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},X^{n+1}-X^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},G^{n+1}-G^{n}\right)

\displaystyle+2\left(\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},c^{n+1}-c^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda^{n+1}+\lambda^{n}\right)|G^{n+1}-G^{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{n+1}-\lambda^{n})((G^{n+1})^{2}-(G^{n})^{2})

\displaystyle+2\left(\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},c^{n+1}-c^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda^{n+1}+\lambda^{n}\right)|G^{n+1}-G^{n}|^{2}+2(\lambda^{n+1}-\lambda^{n})(\varphi_{n+1}(X^{n+1})-\varphi_{n}(X^{n}))

\displaystyle+2\left(\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},c^{n+1}-c^{n}\right)

Notice first that since
(\lambda^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-negative, the first term is non-negative and we can neglect it in the following estimates.
We focus now on the second term.

•

Assume that the constraint is active at step
n
and
n+1
, i.e.
\lambda^{n}>0
and
\lambda^{n+1}>0
.
Then
\varphi_{n}(X^{n})=0
and
\varphi_{n+1}(X^{n+1})=0
and the second term vanishes.

•

Assume that the constraint is inactive at step
n
and
n+1
, i.e.
\lambda^{n}=0
and
\lambda^{n+1}=0
.
Then the second term vanishes as well.

•

Assume that the constraint is active at step
n
and inactive at step
n+1
, i.e.

\lambda^{n}>0
,
\varphi_{n}(X^{n})=0
and
\lambda^{n+1}=0
,
\varphi_{n+1}(X^{n+1})<0
,
the second term reduces to
-\lambda^{n}\varphi_{n+1}(X^{n+1})\geq 0
.

•

Assume that the constraint is inactive at step
n
and active at step
n+1
, i.e.

\lambda^{n}=0
,
\varphi_{n}(X^{n})<0
and
\lambda^{n+1}>0
,
\varphi_{n+1}(X^{n+1})=0
.
the second term reduces to
\lambda^{n+1}(-\varphi_{n}(X^{n}))\geq 0
.

In all cases, the second term is non-negative. To sum up, one has

\displaystyle(X^{n+1}-X^{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\langle\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n},c^{n+1}-c^{n}\rangle+h\langle d^{n+1},X^{n+1}-X^{n}\rangle

Using then that
\lambda^{n+1}G^{n+1}-\lambda^{n}G^{n}=-(X^{n+1}-X^{n})+h\sum_{j\geq 0}(X^{n+1-j}-X^{n-j})R_{j}

from (
33
),
denoting
\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}=X^{n+1}-X^{n}
, we have using Young’s inequality:

\displaystyle|\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},c^{n+1}-c^{n}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(h\sum_{j\geq 0}\delta{X}^{n-j+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}R_{j},c^{n+1}-c^{n}\right)+h\left(d^{n+1},\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}||c^{n+1}-c^{n}|+h\left|\sum_{j\geq 0}\delta{X}^{n-j+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}R_{j}\right||c^{n+1}-c^{n}|

\displaystyle+h|d^{n+1}||\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{1}|\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{-1}|\delta c^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{2}\left|h\sum_{j\geq 0}\delta{X}^{n-j+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}R_{j}\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{-1}|c^{n+1}-c^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle+h^{2}\alpha_{3}^{-1}|d^{n+1}|^{2}+\alpha_{3}|\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|^{2}

Leading to the final estimate:

\displaystyle|\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\alpha_{1}^{-1}+\alpha_{2}^{-1})/(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3})|c^{n+1}-c^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{2}/(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3})\left|h\sum_{j\geq 0}\delta{X}^{n-j+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}R_{j}\right|^{2}+h^{2}\alpha_{3}^{-1}C_{R}^{\prime}/(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3})

One chooses
\alpha_{2}=1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3}
and dividing the previous expression by
h
gives:

\displaystyle y^{n}:={}

\displaystyle\frac{|\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}|^{2}}{h}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}^{-1}+(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3})^{-1}}{1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3}}\frac{|c^{n+1}-c^{n}|^{2}}{h}+\frac{1}{h}\left|h\sum_{j\geq 0}\delta{X}^{n-j+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}R_{j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{3}^{-1}h}{1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3}}C_{R}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq c\omega_{n}+h\sum_{j\geq 0}y^{n-j}R_{j}

where
\omega_{n}:=(c^{n+1}-c^{n})^{2}/h+h
and we used Jensen’s inequality to
estimate
\left|h\sum_{j\geq 0}\delta{X}^{n-j+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}R_{j}\right|^{2}
.
Define now
z^{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}y^{k}
, then summing the previous inequality gives:

z^{n}\leq c\sum_{k=0}^{n}\omega_{k}+h\sum_{j=0}^{n}R_{j}z^{n-j}

We detail here how to obtain the last sum: for
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{n}\sum_{j\geq 0}y^{p-j}R_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{p}y^{p-j}R_{j}=\sum_{0\leq j\leq p\leq n}y^{p-j}R_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-j}y^{k}R_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}z^{n-j}R_{j}

since we assumed that the data is well prepared, for all
j\geq 1

X^{-j}=X^{-1}\in C^{0}
implying that
y^{k}=0
for
k<0
.

Using then Theorem
2.2
leads to the desired estimate since

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\omega_{k}\leq C
. Indeed,
since
\partial_{t}c\in L^{2}(0,T)
, its equivalent
on the discrete level provides
\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}}(\delta c^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}})^{2}/h<\infty
. This ends the proof.

Remark 6

The key step of the proof relies on bounding the projection-step product

\langle\delta P^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rangle
.
Without time dependency, this term would be non-negative by monotonicity of the normal cone.
Here, however,
X^{n+1}
and
X^{n}
do not necessarily belong to the same set, and the decomposition above
yields a positive part and a component depending on the center trajectories.
This heavily depends on the geometry of the moving set, and extending
these results to general convex sets — for instance as a function of the excess
e(C^{n},C^{n+1})
— remains an open problem.
Numerical simulations (Figure
4
) suggest that
-\langle\delta P^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rangle/h^{2}
remains bounded as
h\to 0
, which is consistent with the convergence of the scheme.

5.3
Convergence towards the delayed sweeping process

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
2.3
)

Step 1 – Compactness of
\{X_{h}\}_{h}
.

From the uniform
H^{1}
bound, we obtain (Kondrachov’s selection theorem) that there exists

X\in H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})
and a subsequence such that

X_{h}\to X
in
C^{0}(0,T)
.
Moreover, the piecewise-constant interpolant
\tilde{X}_{h}
also converges to
X
in
L^{p}(0,T)
.

Step 2 – Convergence of the discrete averages.

By Proposition
5
,
the discrete convolution defining
\overline{X}_{h}
converges to the continuous convolution
\overline{X}

in
L^{p}
for any
1\leq p<\infty
. The continuous limit convolution decomposes accordingly as

\overline{X}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}X(t-s)\varrho(s)\,ds+X_{p}\int_{t}^{\infty}\varrho(s)\,ds.

Step 3 – Convergence in the discrete inclusion.

At the discrete level, for each
n\geq 0
, we have the projection characterization
X^{n}=P_{C^{n}}(\overline{X}^{n})
is equivalent to the normal cone inclusion

\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n}\in N_{C^{n}}(X^{n})
or, in duality form,

\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n},\xi\rangle\leq\left|\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n}\right|d_{C^{n}}(\xi+\overline{X}^{n}-X^{n}).

Define the piecewise-constant sets
C_{h}(t):=C^{n}
for
t\in(nh,(n+1)h]
.
By the
H^{1}(0,T)
assumption on the centers of balls
c(t)
,
we may assume
C_{h}(t)\to C(t)
in the Hausdorff sense for a.e.
t
.
Moreover by Corollary
3
, we have that
X^{n}-\overline{X}^{n}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Up to a subsequence, we may fix
t\in(0,T)
such that

\overline{X}_{h}(t)\to\overline{X}(t)
,
\tilde{X}_{h}(t)\to X(t)
,
and
C_{h}(t)\to C(t)
.
Then for such
t
,

\langle\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t),\xi\rangle\leq\left|\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t)\right|d_{C_{h}(t)}(\xi+\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t)).

Because one has that

\displaystyle\left|d_{C_{h}(t)}(\xi+\right.

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t))\left.-d_{C(t)}(\xi+\overline{X}(t)-X(t))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|(\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t))-(\overline{X}(t)-X(t))\right|+d_{H}(C_{h}(t),C(t)),

and that
d_{C_{h}(t)}(\xi+\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t))
is uniformly bounded for every fixed
\xi

thanks to the boundedness of
\overline{X}_{h}(t)-\tilde{X}_{h}(t)
(see Corollary
3
),
we obtain, by successive triangle inequalities and passing to the limit
h\to 0
,

\langle\overline{X}(t)-X(t),\xi\rangle\leq\left|\overline{X}(t)-X(t)\right|d_{C(t)}(\xi+\overline{X}(t)-X(t)).

Since this holds for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which is equivalent to the normal cone inclusion

\overline{X}(t)-X(t)\in N_{C(t)}\big(X(t)\big)
.

Numerical implementation and examples

We now describe the numerical algorithm used to compute the discrete sweeping sequence.
We consider kernels of the form

with
\varepsilon>0

and feasible sets

C(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:g(x-c(t))\geq 0\}
,
where
c(t)
is a given trajectory and
g
is a smooth concave function with
g>0
.
An interactive simulation platform is available at
https://steffenpl.github.io/delayed-sweeping
(source code:

).

6.1
Time-stepping scheme

For
n=0,1,\ldots,N
with
N=\lfloor T/h\rfloor
:

1)

Compute the discrete kernel weights

R_{j}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}\varrho(a)\,da
,
truncated at
j=J_{\max}
where
R_{J_{\max}}<\texttt{tol}
.
For the exponential kernel,
R_{j}=\frac{1}{h}(1-e^{-\varepsilon h})e^{-\varepsilon jh}
.

2)

Compute the weighted average of past positions

\overline{X}^{n}=\frac{h\sum_{j=1}^{J_{\max}}R_{j}\,X^{n-j}}{h\sum_{j=1}^{J_{\max}}R_{j}},

where
X^{n-j}=X_{p}((n-j)h)
whenever
n-j<0
.

3)

Compute the projection
X^{n}=P_{C^{n}}(\overline{X}^{n})
as described below.

6.2
Projection onto the constraint set

When
g_{n}(\overline{X}^{n})\geq 0
, the point is feasible and
X^{n}=\overline{X}^{n}
.
Otherwise we must compute
P_{C^{n}}(\overline{X}^{n})
, the closest point on
\{g_{n}=0\}
.
All gradients are approximated by central finite differences with step
\delta=10^{-6}
.

Step 1: Newton to boundary.

Starting from
p_{0}=\overline{X}^{n}
, we iterate

p_{k+1}=p_{k}-\frac{g_{n}(p_{k})}{|\nabla g_{n}(p_{k})|^{2}}\,\nabla g_{n}(p_{k})

(35)

until
|g_{n}(p_{k})|<\texttt{tol}
. This produces a point
p^{(0)}
on the boundary,
but not necessarily the closest one to
\overline{X}^{n}
.

Step 2: Optimality refinement.

At the metric projection
x^{*}
, the displacement
x^{*}-\overline{X}^{n}
must be
parallel to
\nabla g_{n}(x^{*})
. Starting from
p^{(0)}
, for
\ell=0,1,\ldots

we compute the tangential component of the displacement

d^{(\ell)}=p^{(\ell)}-\overline{X}^{n}
:

d_{\tau}^{(\ell)}=d^{(\ell)}-\frac{d^{(\ell)}\cdot\nabla g_{n}(p^{(\ell)})}{|\nabla g_{n}(p^{(\ell)})|^{2}}\,\nabla g_{n}(p^{(\ell)}).

(36)

If
|d_{\tau}^{(\ell)}|<\texttt{tol}
, we accept
X^{n}=p^{(\ell)}
.
Otherwise, we remove the tangential component and re-project onto the boundary:

\hat{p}^{(\ell)}=p^{(\ell)}-\alpha\,d_{\tau}^{(\ell)},\qquad p^{(\ell+1)}=\text{Newton-to-boundary}\bigl(\hat{p}^{(\ell)}\bigr),

(37)

where
\alpha\in(0,1]
is chosen by backtracking to ensure

|p^{(\ell+1)}-\overline{X}^{n}|<|p^{(\ell)}-\overline{X}^{n}|
.
In practice,
\texttt{tol}=10^{-8}
and at most
50
outer iterations suffice.

6.3
Numerical examples

All numerical experiments use the discrete time-stepping scheme (
31
) with the exponential kernel
\varrho(a)=\varepsilon e^{-\varepsilon a}
.

Circular constraint (Figures
2
–
4
).

The constraint is a disk
C^{n}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|X-c^{n}|\leq R\}
whose center follows a Lissajous curve:

c(t)=\bigl(2\sin(t),\;2\sin(2t)\bigr),\quad t\in[0,9].

Parameters:
R=0.5
,
\varepsilon=0.75
,
h=0.005
, with zero past condition
X_{p}(t)=(0,0)
for
t<0
.
This past is compatible with the initial constraint since
X_{p}(0)=(0,0)\in C^{0}
.

Stadium constraint (Figures

–

).

The constraint is a rotating stadium (capsule) defined by

,

\alpha(t)=4t
.
Parameters:

,

,

,

6.4
Convergence and projection-step product

For both examples, we study convergence as
h\to 0
with step sizes
h\in\{2^{-2},2^{-3},\ldots,2^{-8}\}
and reference solution
h_{\mathrm{ref}}=2^{-10}
.
The
L^{2}
error is computed as

\|z^{h}-z^{\mathrm{ref}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\approx h\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|z^{h}(hk)-z^{\mathrm{ref}}(hk)\|^{2}
.
With compatible past (
X_{p}\equiv 0\in C^{0}
), we observe second-order convergence for both constraint types (Figure
3
).
With incompatible past (
X_{p}\equiv(2,0)\notin C^{0}
), the convergence degenerates to first order.

As discussed in Remark
6
, the projection-step product
\langle\delta P^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rangle
plays a central role in the energy estimates.
Figure
4
shows that the rescaled quantity
-\langle\delta P^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},\delta X^{n+{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rangle/h^{2}
remains bounded as
h\to 0
, for both the circular and the stadium constraint.
This is consistent with the convergence of the scheme and supports the bound used in the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

7
Conclusion and perspectives

This work provides a novel type of comparison principle and applies this principle to the analysis
of a discrete sweeping process with memory. Our comparison principle is flexible enough to yield
compactness and convergence results for the discrete sweeping process. But the non-infinitesimal
nature of the projections imposes strong geometric constraints. Further work is needed to extend
our results to general convex sets and for applications in mathematical biology eventually also
for prox-regular constraints.

Our focus in this work was to be able to start from a delayed constrained gradient flow
17

and show convergence of the discrete sweeping process to its continuous inclusion.
An alternative route to more general results is to consider Yoshida approximations
and fixed point techniques directly in the continuous setting. This is an ongoing work.
The original model introducing the specific delayed gradient flow was motivated by
the modeling of cell migration in structured environments
21
,
and contained a parameter
\varepsilon
representing the inverse of the bond’s stiffness
and the characteristic lifetime of the bonds. For the sake of clarity we did not include this
parameter in the present work. The limit
\varepsilon\to 0
is another perspective of interest since in this case the delayed sweeping process converges to the classical Moreau process.
These aspects will be handled in a forthcoming publication.

On the numerical side, natural extensions include the multicellular case,
a rigorous numerical analysis of the observed convergence rates and
the role of incompatible initial past conditions, and the use of continuation methods to
accelerate the projection step.
