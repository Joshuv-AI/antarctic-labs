Planetary health education in Africa: Building on longstanding One Health frameworks
Authors: Phaedra Henley, Maria Albin Qambayot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent discussions of Planetary Health education in Africa highlight the need for interdisciplinary approaches to address climate change, environmental degradation, and complex health challenges. While this emphasis is timely and important, it is equally important to recognize that many of the principles underpinning Planetary Health have long been operationalized across the continent through One Health frameworks, multisectoral governance systems, and interdisciplinary educational initiatives. This commentary argues that Planetary Health and One Health are highly complementary approaches that share a commitment to systems thinking, cross-sector collaboration, and addressing interconnected threats to human, animal, and environmental health. Drawing on examples from regional One Health networks and national experiences such as Rwanda, we highlight Africa's longstanding contributions to integrated health governance and education. We suggest that future Planetary Health initiatives in Africa should build upon existing One Health foundations, ensuring that African institutions are recognized not only as implementation sites but also as intellectual leaders in advancing integrated approaches to health.

Filho et al.'s recent article, “Climate change, planetary health and education in Africa,” offers a timely and important contribution to discussions on climate change, higher education, and health systems across the African continent [1]. Published at a moment of intensifying ecological disruption, the paper rightly emphasizes the need for interdisciplinary educational approaches that prepare students and institutions to respond to interconnected environmental and health challenges. Building from this context, it is important to consider how Planetary Health discourse supports ongoing practices in Africa.

The rise of Planetary Health marks an important shift toward recognizing that human wellbeing is inseparable from the Earth's life-sustaining systems. However, discussions of Planetary Health education in Africa would benefit from deeper engagement with the continent's longstanding leadership in integrated health approaches, particularly through One Health frameworks and multisectoral governance systems. In many African contexts, the interconnectedness of environmental degradation, food systems, zoonotic diseases, climate variability, and human health has long shaped policy, practice, and institutional responses. As African universities are increasingly called upon to prepare graduates to address complex environmental and health challenges through interdisciplinary and systems-based approaches, it is important to recognize how frameworks such as One Health provide both an operational and conceptual foundation for advancing Planetary Health education across the continent.

Increasingly, One Health and Planetary Health are presented as distinct but related approaches. One Health is often framed around human–animal–environment interactions, while Planetary Health emphasizes the health consequences of ecological disruption and the destabilization of Earth systems, such as climate change [2], [3]. Despite these differences in emphasis, the two frameworks share substantial common ground. Both emphasize the interconnected relationships among ecosystems, governance systems, agriculture, climate, biodiversity, and human wellbeing, and both call for interdisciplinary responses to complex challenges including emerging infectious diseases, environmental degradation, food insecurity, antimicrobial resistance, and climate-related threats. In many African contexts, where livelihoods, ecosystems, and health systems are closely intertwined, these challenges are difficult to separate operationally. As a result, discussions of Planetary Health in Africa should engage with the continent's longstanding experience in integrated and systems-oriented approaches to health, including through One Health governance and practice, and recognize the contributions of One Health and climate-health approaches to advancing integrated educational and policy responses. These existing One Health infrastructures have shaped the development of curricula, faculty expertise, institutional partnerships, and interdisciplinary educational programs across African universities and continue to offer valuable foundations for future growth.

Prior to the 2015 Rockefeller Foundation-Lancet Commission on Planetary Health [3], One Health frameworks were already shaping veterinary-public health collaboration, disease surveillance, university training programs, and national policy development across Africa. Since 2008, regional networks such as One Health Central and East Africa (OHCEA), the Southern African Centre for Infectious Disease Surveillance (SACIDS), and Afrique One supported multidisciplinary training, research, and outreach across multiple countries, demonstrating that integrated approaches to health were already being institutionalized through African-led collaborations. The Africa One Health University Network (AFROHUN), which evolved from OHCEA, further formalized regional One Health education and workforce development across African universities [4].

Rwanda provides one example of how One Health approaches have been operationalized through national governance structures, university training programs, and coordinated responses to outbreaks such as COVID-19 and Marburg [5], [6]. The country adopted a national One Health strategic framework in 2014, establishing coordination across ministries responsible for health, agriculture, wildlife, and environment [7]. In 2024, the Ministry of Health also formalized a Climate and Health Technical Working Group, which includes many leaders working across the One Health space [8]. Across Rwanda, universities have also integrated climate change, biodiversity management, and One Health across formal degree programs and curricula [9]. These examples reflect a broader reality: in many African contexts, interconnected health and environmental challenges have long been governance realities rather than abstract theoretical debates.

This history matters because the institutionalization of global health frameworks is never neutral. Terminology shapes funding priorities, academic legitimacy, publication ecosystems, conference agendas, and institutional visibility. It influences which institutions are viewed as innovators, which regions are framed as implementation sites, and whose intellectual contributions become foundational within emerging global discourse. Therefore, it is critical to assess the way in which the Planetary Health discourse interacts with existing African frameworks.

This is not an argument that One Health and Planetary Health should be viewed as competing frameworks. The two overlap substantially in both ambition and practice. Rather, the concern is that discussions of Planetary Health can unintentionally reproduce longstanding asymmetries in global health knowledge production, positioning African institutions as recipients of emerging frameworks rather than as longstanding contributors to integrated approaches to health. Such framing risks obscuring the extensive body of African scholarship, policy innovation, and institutional leadership that predates the rise of Planetary Health as a distinct field.

The expansion of regional Planetary Health hubs, including across Africa, illustrates both the opportunities and tensions within this moment. Such initiatives may foster important collaboration and visibility. At the same time, they raise important questions: what does it mean to “bring” Planetary Health to regions that have already spent decades building One Health governance systems, university networks, and multisectoral ecological health frameworks? Whose histories are acknowledged within these evolving narratives, and whose are rendered secondary? These questions speak to broader debates about epistemic recognition within global health: who defines the agenda, whose terminology becomes dominant, which institutions are assumed to hold conceptual authority, and whose expertise is viewed as foundational rather than supplementary.

One Health has provided operational mechanisms for integrating human, animal, and environmental health through multisectoral governance, surveillance systems, and workforce development [10]. Rather than positioning Planetary Health as a replacement for existing approaches, there is value in recognizing its complementarities with fields such as One Health, climate and health, and biodiversity conservation, among others. If Planetary Health initiatives are implemented without sufficient engagement with existing African institutions and networks, they risk creating parallel structures instead of strengthening and learning from those already in place. Such an approach is more equitable because it values and builds upon established knowledge systems and institutional experience.

Filho et al. open an important conversation regarding the future of interdisciplinary ecological health education in Africa. African institutions have participated in these discussions and have often been at the forefront of developing contextually relevant solutions through One Health platforms. The next step is ensuring that this future is built not from assumptions of absence or late entry, but from recognition of the extensive institutional, intellectual, and governance foundations that already exist across the continent. As interdisciplinary ecological health frameworks continue to evolve, there is an opportunity to move beyond disciplinary branding and toward more reciprocal forms of knowledge production, ones that recognize African institutions not only as sites for implementation, but as longstanding intellectual and institutional leaders in integrated health thinking. Such an approach would encourage genuine co-creation of knowledge, strengthen locally grounded educational models, and ensure that future global frameworks are informed by the diverse experiences, priorities, and innovations emerging from the African continent.

This recognition is particularly important given the complex challenges that Planetary Health seeks to address. Climate change, biodiversity loss, food systems, human health, animal health, environmental sustainability, and social equity are deeply interconnected and require systems thinking that moves beyond linear cause-and-effect relationships [11]. Preparing the next generation of health professionals therefore requires educational models that foster collaboration across fields, sectors, and institutions, enabling learners to understand feedback loops, trade-offs, and unintended consequences within complex socio-ecological systems. Planetary Health education should therefore cultivate the capacity to work across boundaries, integrate diverse forms of knowledge, and build partnerships among scientists, health professionals, environmental practitioners, policymakers, and communities. In doing so, it can build upon the strengths of established approaches such as One Health and related traditions while preparing learners to address the interconnected challenges facing people, animals, ecosystems, and the planetary systems upon which all health depends. The challenge is therefore not whether Planetary Health should expand across African universities, but how that expansion can build upon, learn from, and strengthen the extensive One Health foundations that already exist across the continent.
