Admission test and academic outcomes in Italian dental education: a retrospective evaluation at the University of Turin
Authors: Giuseppe Migliaretti, DRgroup, Paolo Giacomo Arduino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdmission to dental programs in Italy has traditionally been regulated by a national entrance examination. However, a recent reform replaced this system with a merit-based assessment during a “filter semester.” This study aimed to evaluate whether admission test scores predict academic success in the Dentistry programme at the University of Turin and to compare outcomes between students admitted through the standard process and those admitted via legal appeals.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 431 students enrolled between 2009 and 2018. In this context, CFUs are fully equivalent to ECTS (European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System) credits. Performance indicators included the accumulation of Crediti Formativi Universitari (CFUs; university credits equivalent to ECTS credits) during the first and second years, degree completion, and on-time graduation. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, expressed as area under the curve (AUC), and logistic regression were used to assess the predictive power of entrance test scores.ResultsHigher admission test scores were associated with a greater likelihood of completing coursework and graduating on time. The test showed almost good predictive ability, particularly for first-year performance (AUC = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.65–0.75), total CFUs expected in Years I and II (AUC = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.66–0.81), and degree attainment (AUC = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.67–0.75). A score of 40 (out of 90) was identified as the optimal cut-off for classifying “low” (< 40) and “good” (≥ 40) scores. Students below this threshold had an increased risk of not achieving the expected CFUs in Year I [OR = 5.04; 95% CI: 3.18–7.98], not completing all expected CFUs in Years I and II within the established timeframe [OR = 19.9; 95% CI: 2.72–146.0], and not graduating within six years [OR = 2.27; 95% CI: 1.30–3.96].ConclusionThe admission test showed almost good predictive value for academic success in dentistry. Its elimination may have implications that warrant further evaluation, particularly in relation to attrition rates and resource allocation. These findings provide evidence to inform the ongoing debate on university admissions in Italy.Keywords: Admission, Test, Dental student, Academic outcomes, Academic performance

Introduction

In recent years, the growing demand for advanced academic qualifications has posed substantial financial and operational challenges. In particular, it has become necessary to expand the number of lecture halls, laboratory facilities, institutional infrastructures, technical staff, and teaching personnel simultaneously to accommodate increasing student cohorts without compromising pedagogical standards [1]. Moreover, the persistently high rate of youth unemployment has raised critical concerns regarding both the quantity and quality of graduates in relation to labour market needs, thereby underscoring the importance of rigorous and selective admission processes.

To enrol in an Italian dental school, students must pass a competitive national entrance examination (in place since 1983) after obtaining a high school leaving certificate. The national admission test for medical and dental programmes in Italy is a standardised multiple-choice examination administered annually at the national level. At the time covered by this study, the test consisted of questions designed to assess a range of cognitive abilities and knowledge domains, including logical reasoning, biology, chemistry, physics, mathematics, and general knowledge. The structure of the test reflects an emphasis on both subject-specific knowledge and problem-solving skills, with the aim of identifying candidates with the academic competencies required to successfully undertake a rigorous university curriculum in the health professions.

Compared with dental schools in other EU member states, the number of dental students per school is low—an average of 20 students per year per school, with a range of 10 to 60 [2]. This competitive selection process, designed to limit access to a fixed number of students, aims to identify candidates with the greatest potential for academic success. The usefulness of university admission tests, especially for degree courses in medicine, has been widely discussed in recent years [3–5].

Scores obtained in Italian medical university entrance examinations have recently demonstrated predictive validity for subsequent academic performance during the first year of medical school [6, 7]. As such, the test serves a dual purpose: it benefits both prospective students and academic institutions. Specifically, it discourages candidates who fail to meet the threshold from pursuing medical studies, thereby redirecting them toward alternative educational pathways and ultimately reducing unnecessary expenditure of time and financial resources. By implementing this selective mechanism, universities are better positioned to enrol a smaller yet more motivated cohort of students with a higher likelihood of academic achievement. This, in turn, facilitates a more rational and effective allocation of infrastructural assets and human capital. Nonetheless, the discriminatory power of the admission examination could be further enhanced by replicating the evaluation at later stages—namely at the conclusion of the fourth and sixth academic years—to explore potential correlations between entrance test performance and the development of clinical competencies.

However, very recent educational reforms (May 2025) have abolished the traditional admission test for Italian medical and dental students, replacing it with a so-called “filter semester” during which all enrolled students are evaluated based on their performance in core scientific subjects such as chemistry, physics, and biology. At the end of this initial phase, only the highest-performing students are allowed to continue in the program, effectively introducing a new model of selection based on academic merit during the first months of study. This shift raises important questions about the predictive validity of the former entrance examination system and its effectiveness in selecting students capable of excelling in the academic environment.

The aim of this study is to assess the relationship between admission test scores and subsequent academic performance in the Dentistry program at the University of Turin. Beyond this primary objective, the study seeks to contribute to the broader debate on the role and predictive validity of cognitive admission tools in health professions education. While a growing body of literature has examined these issues in medical education, evidence in dental education remains limited, particularly in the Italian context. By analysing multiple cohorts over a ten-year period, this study provides robust longitudinal evidence on the extent to which admission test scores can predict early academic progression and degree attainment. In addition, the inclusion of students admitted through alternative pathways (i.e., legal appeals) offers a unique opportunity to indirectly assess the effectiveness of the selection system. These findings aim to inform not only the ongoing national reform of university admissions in Italy, but also the international discussion on how to design fair and effective selection systems capable of identifying students most likely to succeed in demanding health professions programmes.

Materials and methods

According to Italian regulations, ethics committee approval is not required for retrospective analyses of anonymised data that do not involve clinical interventions, disease conditions, pharmaceutical treatments, or sensitive personal information.

To meet the requirements of the Helsinki Declaration, all analyses were performed utilizing a dataset devoid of sensitive information, provided directly by the Dental School of Turin, Italy.

Setting

This was an observational retrospective cohort study aimed at assessing the association between admission test scores (Table 1) and subsequent academic performance among students enrolled in the Dentistry and Dental Prosthetics programme at the University of Turin between 2009 and 2018.

The initial dataset included students enrolled in the Dentistry and Dental Prosthetics programme during the study period. Students were included if they had an available admission test score and academic outcome data. Students transferring from or to other degree programmes or universities, as well as students with documented career interruptions or suspensions, were excluded to reduce potential confounding related to non-standard academic pathways. Follow-up started at the time of enrolment and continued until March 31, 2025. Students were classified according to the outcomes available at the end of follow-up. No imputation of missing data was performed; analyses were conducted using available data only.

In the academic year 2014/2015, a considerable number of students were admitted without having passed the national admission test because of legal appeals to the Regional Administrative Court (TAR). These students were retained in the analytical cohort because they represented a relevant subgroup for assessing the association between admission test performance and subsequent academic outcomes. However, their inclusion may introduce selection bias and is therefore addressed as a limitation in the Discussion.

Primary endpoint

In the Italian university system, the term “Crediti Formativi Universitari” (CFUs) represent the workload required to achieve specific learning outcomes. One CFU corresponds to 25 h of student effort, including lectures, self-study, and assessments. CFUs are fully equivalent to ECTS (European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System) credits.

In alignment with the study objectives, the achievement of the expected CFUs during the first year was defined as the primary endpoint, as it represents an early and standardised indicator of academic progression. Secondary endpoints included second-year CFU completion, total CFU completion during the first two years, degree attainment, and graduation within six years from enrolment. Statistical analysis

The prognostic ability of the admission test score to predict the predefined performance indicators was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the curve (AUC), with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), was used to quantify the discriminative ability of the admission test score for each binary academic outcome. ROC analysis was selected because it allows evaluation of how well a continuous predictor discriminates between students who did and did not achieve each outcome. According to the available literature, an AUC > 0.7 is generally considered indicative of moderate predictive ability [8].

The optimal cut-off point was identified from the ROC analysis by considering the balance between sensitivity and specificity. Based on this approach, a cut-off score of 40 out of 90 (confirmed by Youden Index) was selected to classify students into two groups: “low score” (< 40) and “good score” (≥ 40). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and positive likelihood ratio were calculated with corresponding 95% CI.

The association between admission test score category (low/good score) and performance indicators (CFU, Degree) was then evaluated using logistic regression models. Results are expressed as crude odds ratios (ORs) and ORs adjusted for sex and enrolment cohort, with corresponding 95% CI. Logistic regression was used because the outcomes were defined as binary indicators measured at predefined time points.

The ROC analysis identified a score of 40 out of a maximum of 90 as the cut-off providing the best balance between sensitivity and specificity in this dataset. Students were therefore classified as having a “low score” (< 40) or a “good score” (≥ 40). This cut-off should be interpreted as data-driven and specific to the present cohort (Table 3).

Students were then classified as those who achieved a “Good Score” [(n = 318): admitted with a test score ≥ 40] or “Low Score” [(n = 113): admitted with a score < 40)].

Based on this classification, it was possible to highlight (Table 3) how a score of 40 or above (“Good Score”) on the Test is a good indicator to identify particularly those who will regularly achieve all the CFUs expected in the 1 st year (Se = 0.73 95% CI 0.67—0.77 and Sp = 0. 65 95% CI 0.56—0.74), those who will graduate (Se = 0.91 95% CI 0.87—0.94) and those who will graduate within 6 years (Se = 0.88 95% CI 0.84—0.91).

In summary, the results show that students who achieved these Performance Indicators are more likely to be those who had a “Good Score” on the test. The Specificity value also deserve to be highlighted (see Table) for the achievement of the CFU in Year I, which highlights that those who do NOT achieve the expected CFU are more likely those who had a Low Score on the Admission Test.

Similarly, those who do NOT achieve the expected CFUs in Year II and the Total expected CFUs in the first two years of the course are more likely students who had a Low Score on the Test (Sp = 0.85 CI 0.77—0.90 and Sp = 0.98 95% CI 0.95—0.99, respectively).

The results showed an increased risk of not achieving the predefined performance indicators among students with a low admission test score. This was particularly evident for failure to obtain the expected CFUs in Year I [OR = 5.04; 95% CI: 3.18–7.98; p < 0.001], failure to obtain all expected CFUs in Years I and II within the established deadline [OR = 19.9; 95% CI: 2.72–146.0; p = 0.003], and failure to graduate within six years [OR = 2.27; 95% CI: 1.30–3.96; p = 0.004]. The associations remained statistically significant after adjustment for sex and enrolment cohort.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that performance on the Italian dental school admission test is significantly associated with students’ academic success, particularly in the early stages of the curriculum and in overall degree attainment. A threshold score of 40 out of 90 emerged as a data-driven and exploratory cut-off within this cohort, useful for illustrating differences in academic outcomes. Overall, the AUC values indicate almost good rather than strong predictive ability, suggesting that the admission test has measurable discriminative value but should not be interpreted as a perfect predictor of academic success. Additional exploratory resampling analyses (bootstrap approach, not shown) yielded consistent estimates, supporting the robustness of the observed associations. These results are consistent with previous studies conducted in medical education, which have demonstrated that cognitive admission tests have moderate predictive ability to detect early academic performance and progression through the curriculum [9]. However, the moderate AUC values observed in this study suggest that admission test scores should be considered as only one component within a broader, multifactorial selection framework.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings relate to the concept of predictive validity, referring to the ability of an assessment tool to forecast future performance outcomes. In health professions education, admission tests are generally intended to assess cognitive abilities and prior academic preparation that may support progression through demanding curricula. However, predictive validity is inherently context-dependent and may vary according to the structure of the test, the educational system, and the outcomes considered [10].

Non-cognitive factors, such as motivation, resilience, and interpersonal skills, may also contribute substantially to academic success and are not captured by cognitive admission tests.

Compared to the existing literature, this study offers several relevant contributions [11]. First, it focuses on dental education, an area in which evidence remains relatively scarce compared to medicine. Second, it is based on multiple cohorts observed over an extended period, thereby providing more robust and generalisable evidence than studies based on single cohorts. Third, the inclusion of students admitted through legal appeals represents a unique feature of the Italian system and offers an indirect comparison between selected and non-selected students.

The results also reinforce the broader theoretical assumption that cognitive abilities and prior academic preparation, as assessed through standardised testing, play a relevant role in early academic success in health professions education.

However, consistent with the literature, the predictive power of such tests is not absolute and tends to be almost good, particularly when considering complex educational outcomes. Systematic reviews in health professions education have highlighted that selection methods typically explain only a limited proportion of variance in academic performance and should therefore be interpreted within a broader framework of multiple predictors [12, 13].

From a policy perspective, these findings are particularly relevant considering the recent reform of university admissions in Italy, which replaced the traditional entrance test with a “filter semester” model. While this new system aims to increase accessibility and promote merit-based progression, the present results suggest that removing an initial selection mechanism may increase the proportion of students at risk of delayed progression or academic failure, with potential implications for both students and institutions.

At the same time, the generalisability of these findings should be considered with caution. Admission systems vary substantially across countries in terms of structure, content, and underlying educational philosophy. Therefore, while the specific cut-off identified in this study is context-dependent, the broader finding—that cognitive admission tests have a measurable predictive value—may be relevant to other educational settings. Nevertheless, the single-institution design represents a limitation, and further multi-centre or international studies would be needed to confirm these results in different contexts.

Another limitation relates to the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of information on non-cognitive variables, such as motivation, socio-economic background, or psychological traits, which may also influence academic success. Future research should aim to integrate these dimensions to provide a more comprehensive understanding of student performance.

It should be noted that the identified cut-off of 40 was derived for exploratory and methodological purposes within this dataset and should not be interpreted as a prescriptive threshold for admission or evaluation.

Other potentially relevant confounding factors, such as socio-economic background, motivation, resilience, and psychological traits, were not included in the models because this information was not systematically available for the whole cohort. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations.

Regarding the inclusion of students admitted via legal appeal introduces potential selection bias we would like to clarify that the objective was to evaluate the predictive power of the test score (and in this respect, they contribute with their "low" score) and to identify a possible cutoff below which they are less likely to have good academic performance (and they fall squarely within the category of students with test scores below the cutoff). For this reason, in our opinion, it was not necessary to classify the students as "regular/appealing" but rather to see how a low-admissions test score predicts poor academic performance. This subgroup may represent a systematically different population compared to regularly admitted students, potentially influencing the observed associations and limiting the internal validity of the findings.

Finally, it should be noted that the predictive validity observed in this study mainly concerns early academic indicators and overall degree completion. Further investigations are needed to explore the relationship between admission criteria and long-term outcomes, including clinical competence and professional development.

Overall, this study contributes to the ongoing international discussion on admission systems in health professions education by providing empirical evidence from a relatively underexplored context. It supports the view that structured admission processes can play a meaningful role in aligning student selection with academic success, while also highlighting the need for balanced approaches that consider both fairness and effectiveness.

Conclusions

The Italian dental school admission test appears to have measurable predictive value for academic progression and degree attainment in this cohort. These findings suggest that structured admission procedures may help identify students with a higher probability of succeeding in demanding educational pathways. Moreover, the findings support the notion that a structured admission process plays an important role in aligning students’ capabilities with the rigours of dental education. As Italy transitions to a new model of selection via a “Filter Semester,” the current data provide a valuable benchmark for evaluating the effectiveness of this reform. In particular, given that the literature offers little evidence in this area, especially for the Dentistry degree course, our study aims to provide a benchmark of the academic performance of students selected before the reform that can be used in the future to evaluate the academic performance of students selected under the new ‘Filter Semester’.

Future research should compare outcomes between students admitted through the traditional test and those selected under the new system, with particular attention to long-term academic success, clinical competencies, and professional development. Ultimately, reforms in admission policy should balance accessibility, fairness, predictive effectiveness, and the maintenance of academic and professional standards. The present findings provide empirical evidence from one Italian dental school and may serve as a benchmark for future evaluations of admission reforms.
