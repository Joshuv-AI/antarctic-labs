Comparison of phylogenetic metrics of transmission between symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis in individuals who were incarcerated in Brazil in 2008–24: a retrospective genomic epidemiology study
Authors: Kesia Esther da Silva, Paulo César Pereira dos Santos, Daniel Henrique Tsuha, Katharine S Walter, Eunice Atsuko Totumi Cunha, Caroline Colijn, Ted Cohen, Roberto Dias de Oliveira, José Victor Bortolotto Bampi, Mariana G Croda, Crhistinne Cavalheiro Maymone Gonçalves, Luiz Henrique Ferraz Demarchi, Julio Croda, Jason R Andrews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundTuberculosis control efforts have traditionally targeted symptomatic individuals; however, the role of asymptomatic cases in sustaining transmission is increasingly recognised. We aimed to quantify the contribution of asymptomatic tuberculosis to recent transmission using genomic and epidemiological data from a high-transmission setting.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective genomic epidemiology study of Mycobacterium tuberculosis isolates collected in Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil, between Aug 25, 2008, and March 19, 2024. Available isolates underwent whole-genome sequencing. Demographic, clinical, incarceration history, and laboratory metadata were obtained from surveillance records. From Jan 1, 2017, to March 19, 2024, active case finding was conducted in the state’s three largest prisons (all male-only facilities), during which sputum samples were collected from individuals irrespective of symptoms and tested using GeneXpert and culture. Comparisons of transmission between individuals with and without symptoms were restricted to individuals who were incarcerated and were identified through active case finding and for whom high-quality, M tuberculosis lineage 4 genomes were available. Metrics of recent transmission included phylogenetic clustering, time-scaled haplotype density (THD), local branching index (LBI), and transmission probabilities inferred using Bayesian Reconstruction and Evolutionary Analysis of Transmission Histories.Findings4448 tuberculosis cases were notified in Mato Grosso do Sul in 2008–24. After excluding cases for which M tuberculosis isolates were not available or had low sequencing quality, who had contaminated cultures or mixed infection, or who were infected with non-lineage 4 M tuberculosis, we included 2362 lineage 4 M tuberculosis isolates with high-quality genome sequences. 1849 (78·3%) of 2362 isolates were part of a genomic cluster. Among 2362 individuals with tuberculosis, 1137 (48·1%) were incarcerated at diagnosis. Of these individuals, 505 were identified through active case finding in three male-only prisons. The median age was 30 years (IQR 25–37); 304 (60·2%) had mixed ethnicity, 90 (17·8%) were White, 56 (11·1%) were Black, 13 (2·6%) were Indigenous, and six (1·2%) were Asian. 277 (54·9%) had symptomatic disease and 228 (45·1%) had asymptomatic tuberculosis. There were no significant differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals in phylogenetic clustering (213 [76·9%] of 277 vs 195 [85·5%] of 228; p=0·37), THD (median 0·39 [IQR 0·06–0·62] vs 0·50 [0·09–0·65]; p=0·12), or LBI (0·00863 [0·00810–0·00988] vs 0·00871 [0·00829–0·01020]; p=0·088). Bayesian transmission trees showed no significant difference in the number of secondary infections inferred from symptomatic compared with asymptomatic individuals (p=0·56). These findings were consistent across genomic clusters and robust to model assumptions.InterpretationWe identified no differences in transmission between individuals who were symptomatic and those who were asymptomatic using multiple genomic measures. In this high-transmission setting, where systematic screening is implemented, our findings indicate that asymptomatic tuberculosis substantially contributes to tuberculosis transmission at the population level. These results suggest that symptom-based case detection alone is likely to be insufficient to interrupt transmission and highlight the importance of expanded screening strategies in high-risk populations.

Introduction

Tuberculosis remains the leading cause of mortality from an infectious disease worldwide.1 One of the main obstacles to reducing the burden of tuberculosis is the ongoing generation of new infections, primarily through transmission from individuals with undiagnosed disease.2 Approximately half of the individuals with tuberculosis at any given time are asymptomatic, and asymptomatic individuals are increasingly recognised to play an important role in transmission.2,3

However, the relative contribution of individuals with asymptomatic and symptomatic infection to Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission at the population level remains unclear. Studies have reported no significant differences in infection risk among close contacts of individuals with symptomatic versus asymptomatic tuberculosis.3,4 However, although informative, these studies have important limitations. Because these studies are cross-sectional and rely on a binary measure of M tuberculosis infection that reflects cumulative lifetime exposure, they cannot determine whether M tuberculosis infections in contacts resulted from transmission from the putative index case or from other exposures, including potential shared exposures. Furthermore, not all exposures carry an equal risk of progression to tuberculosis disease; therefore, understanding the contribution of asymptomatic tuberculosis to incident disease would provide more direct evidence regarding its role in sustaining the epidemic.

To better characterise the contribution of asymptomatic individuals to tuberculosis transmission and resultant disease, we analysed genomic evidence of M tuberculosis transmission in high-burden prison settings where transmission is intense and active case finding is conducted through systematic, symptom-independent screening.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective genomic epidemiology study using tuberculosis surveillance data collected in the two largest cities in the state of Mato Grosso do Sul, central-west Brazil, between Aug 25, 2008, and March 19, 2024 (appendix 1 pp 2–3). Routine surveillance data included demographic and clinical metadata reported to the Brazilian National Notifiable Disease Information System (SINAN), where sex was recorded as self-reported at the time of notification. In addition, systematic screening was implemented in the three largest state prisons by our research group between Jan 1, 2017, and March 19, 2024. These prisons were male-only facilities selected as sentinel sites based on their size and representation of the state’s incarcerated population. Within these facilities, all individuals who were incarcerated were screened irrespective of symptoms. Screening procedures included symptom assessment using a standardised questionnaire (appendix 1 pp 21–23), chest radiography, and sputum testing using Xpert MTB/RIF, with culture performed for Xpert-positive samples (appendix 1 p 3).

In this genomic analysis, we included culture-positive isolates available at the state reference laboratory that were successfully sequenced. Sequences were required to meet predefined quality criteria, including adequate genome coverage and absence of contamination or mixed infection (appendix 1 pp 3–4). Only lineage 4 isolates (>99% of all isolates) with complete epidemiological metadata relevant to the analyses were included in the final dataset.

This study was approved by the National Commission for Ethics in Research (3.483.377, CAAE 37237814.4.0000.5160), the Federal University of Mato Grosso do Sul Research Ethics Committee, and Stanford University. Written informed consent was obtained from participants enrolled in active case finding before participation. The ethics committees also approved the use of retrospective surveillance (SINAN) and laboratory data, including linked clinical and epidemiologic variables, and waived the need for individual informed consent. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines; a completed checklist is provided in appendix 1 (pp 24–28).

Procedures

Tuberculosis was defined by a positive sputum test using Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra or culture. Individuals were classified as having symptomatic (greater than or equal to one symptom, regardless of duration) or asymptomatic tuberculosis based on responses to a standardised symptom questionnaires (appendix 1 pp 21–23) administered by trained nurses during screening. Tuberculosis-related symptoms were cough, fever, appetite loss, night sweats, chest pain, difficulty breathing, and weight loss.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analyses

Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (2 × 150 bp). We filtered reads, aligned them to H37Rv, and analysed genomic variation using a variant identification pipeline publicly available on GitHub (appendix 1 pp 3–4). Lineages were inferred using TBProfiler version 4.2.0, and drug resistance was predicted in silico using the same software.5

Phylogenetic reconstruction

Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE version 2.2.0. Pairwise genetic distance differences between samples were calculated using the R package ape.6 Genomic clusters were defined using a threshold of 12 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; appendix 1 pp 4–5).7 To assess the robustness of clustering definitions, we conducted sensitivity analyses using a more stringent 6-SNP threshold, indicative of more recent or direct transmission.

Phylogenetic metrics of transmission

We used the time-scaled haplotypic density (THD) method to evaluate the influence of clinical symptoms and incarceration status (categorised as current incarceration, previous incarceration, or no history of incarceration) on the epidemic success of M tuberculosis isolates. THD computes an independent success index for each isolate;8 higher values indicate a greater density of closely related strains, consistent with increased transmission. THD estimates were calculated using a timescale of 20 years and an average per-nucleotide substitution rate of 5·8 × 10−8.9 We used generalised linear models to examine the association among symptoms, incarceration status, and THD success index. We then estimated the local branching index (LBI)10 for each tip in the maximum-likelihood phylogeny using the R package TreeImbalance.11 LBI is a quantitative measure of pathogen fitness derived from phylogenetic tree structure and is based on the total branch length surrounding each node, with contributions discounted exponentially with increasing distance from the node. Higher LBI values indicate more rapid expansion and are associated with increased fitness.12

We included metadata and sequencing data from all participants to construct phylogenetic trees and perform analyses of clustering, transmission metrics, and transmission tree inference. However, comparisons between symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis were restricted to individuals who were incarcerated, as very few individuals diagnosed in the community had asymptomatic tuberculosis (active case finding is not routinely performed). Restricting analyses to individuals who were incarcerated and were identified through symptom-independent, active case finding reduces biases introduced by symptom-based diagnosis.

Transmission inference

To investigate transmission patterns in the ten largest genomic clusters, we grouped M tuberculosis isolates using a 12-SNP threshold. For each cluster, we constructed time-calibrated phylogenetic trees using BEAST version 2.7.6,13 incorporating sample collection dates to calibrate tree tips. Although phylogenies capture evolutionary relatedness among sampled isolates, they do not directly represent transmission events, particularly in the presence of incomplete sampling and within-host diversity. To infer likely transmission events, including those involving unsampled hosts, we used the Bayesian Reconstruction and Evolutionary Analysis of Transmission Histories (BREATH) model.14 BREATH jointly estimates phylogenetic and transmission trees within a single Bayesian framework, enabling reconstruction of who-infected-whom relationships while accounting for unobserved cases and infection timing. The model infers transmission links by integrating genetic distances, sampling times, and epidemiological priors on transmission and sampling rates. To assess the effect of assumptions regarding transmission timing, we conducted sensitivity analyses under two transmission-rate priors. In the long scenario, the mean transmission interval was 2·0 years, representing a longer interval between infection and case detection (slower case detection relative to transmission). In the medium scenario, the mean transmission interval was 1·5 years, representing shorter intervals between infection and case detection. Analyses were configured in BEAUti using the BREATH tree prior and an HKY+Γ4+I substitution model. After sensitivity analysis (appendix 1 p 5), sampling hazard parameters were set to: shape=10, rate=6·5, and C=0·75. Transmission hazard parameters were set to: shape=10, rate=8·5, and C=2. A strict molecular clock was applied, bounded between 4·1 × 10−8 and 1·33 × 10−7 substitutions per site per year, with a log-normal prior on the substitution rate (mean=−17, SD=2), consistent with previous estimates for M tuberculosis lineage 4.15

To quantify the contribution of asymptomatic individuals to transmission, we used who-acquired-infection-from--whom matrices derived from the posterior distributions of BREATH-inferred transmission trees. These matrices summarise the posterior probability of transmission between each pair of sampled individuals and were used to calculate the probability-weighted number of secondary infections attributable to each case. Although this approach accounts for unobserved cases, we restricted estimation of secondary infections to observed cases (sequenced isolates) due to greater confidence in inferred transmission events.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was inferred M tuberculosis transmission between individuals based on genomic and phylogenetic reconstruction. Secondary outcomes were genomic clustering proportions, THD, LBI, and estimated number of secondary infections, each compared by symptom status.

Statistical analysis

No formal sample size calculation was performed, as all available culture-positive isolates meeting sequencing and quality criteria during the study period were included. Differences in the proportion of isolates belonging to genomic clusters were assessed using the χ2 test.

Distributions of THD, LBI, and probability-weighted secondary infections were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To account for missing covariate data, we performed multiple imputation using the mice package in R (version 4.3.2), as detailed in appendix 1 (p 6). Regression models for genomic clustering, THD, LBI, and secondary infections were estimated across imputed datasets, and pooled effect estimates were derived using Rubin’s rules. Results are reported as odds ratios with 95% CIs for clustering and β coefficients with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes. All multivariable models were adjusted for incarceration status, smoking status, alcohol use, drug use, sampling date, and M tuberculosis lineage. These covariates were selected based on prior evidence linking them to tuberculosis transmission dynamics and symptom presentation.16–19 All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2), and a p value <0·05 was considered statistically significant.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Between January, 2008, and December, 2024, a total of 4448 culture-positive, laboratory-confirmed tuberculosis cases were notified in Mato Grosso do Sul, according to the Brazilian National Notifiable Disease Information System (SINAN). Of those cases, 2959 (66·5%) isolates were available for whole-genome sequencing (figure 1). The remaining cases were not sequenced, primarily because of unavailability of viable stored culture material, loss of isolates during storage, or insufficient DNA quantity or quality for sequencing. After excluding 165 genomes with low sequencing coverage, 2794 (94·4%) high-quality isolates remained. An additional 91 isolates were excluded due to contamination or evidence of mixed infection, leaving 2703 isolates for analysis. 2696 (99·7%) of the isolates belonged to lineage 4. Given the predominance of this lineage, analyses were restricted to lineage 4 isolates. After excluding samples with missing metadata, 2362 lineage 4 isolates were included in the final analysis, representing 53·1% of all laboratory-confirmed tuberculosis cases notified in Mato Grosso do Sul during the study period (appendix 1 pp 9, 15). The study population was predominantly male (1957 [82·9%] of 2362), with 315 (13·3%) female participants and 90 (3·8%) participants for whom sex was not reported. The median age was 33 years (IQR 26–43). Most participants self-identified as mixed ethnicity (1134 [48·0%] of 2362), followed by White (608 [25·7%]), Black (178 [7·5%]), Indigenous (134 [5·7%]), and Asian (22 [0·9%]); ethnicity was not reported for 286 (12·1%) participants. 1137 (48·1%) of 2362 isolates were from individuals incarcerated at the time of tuberculosis notification, 116 (4·9%) were from individuals in the community with a history of incarceration, and 1016 (43·0%) were from individuals with no incarceration history; incarceration status was unknown for 93 (4·0%) individuals (appendix 2). Individuals with no incarceration history were classified as community-diagnosed tuberculosis cases.

Phylogenetic analysis of 2362 M tuberculosis lineage 4 isolates revealed a diverse population structure comprising multiple sublineages (figure 2). The most prevalent sublineage was 4.1.2.1, accounting for 628 (26·6%) of 2362 isolates, followed by 4.3.3 (520 [22·0%]), 4.4.1.1 (462 [19·6%]), 4.3.4.2 (234 [9·9%]), and 4.4.1.2 (188 [8·0%]). The distribution of sublineages remained consistent over time, except in 2008, when the small number of samples limited interpretation (appendix 1 p 10). This pattern indicates co-circulation of multiple sublineages with evidence of local clonal expansion, particularly among dominant groups. 2247 (95·1%) of 2362 isolates were genotypically predicted to be drug sensitive. Isoniazid monoresistance was identified in 61 (2·6%) isolates, rifampicin monoresistance in 71 (3·0%), and multidrug resistance in 16 (0·7%). An additional 21 (0·9%) isolates showed other resistance patterns.

Isolates from individuals who were incarcerated and those who were not incarcerated were interspersed throughout the phylogenetic tree and did not form distinct clades, suggesting recent and ongoing transmission between prison and community settings. M tuberculosis diversity was largely driven by circulation of closely related strains. Cluster analysis using a 12-SNP threshold revealed that 1849 (78·3%) of 2362 isolates belonged to one of 198 genomic clusters. Clustering was more frequent among individuals who were incarcerated (938 [83·8%] of 1120) and those with a history of incarceration (88 [75·9%] of 116) than among individuals in the community (722 [71·1%] of 1016) (χ2 test, p<0·0001 for overall group comparison).

Comparisons between symptomatic and asymptomatic tuberculosis were restricted to individuals who were currently incarcerated and who were identified through active case finding. From Jan 1, 2017, to March 19, 2024, 505 isolates were obtained through active case finding from individuals with available symptom data; 277 (54·9%) of these isolates were from symptomatic individuals, and 228 (45·1%) were from asymptomatic individuals (table). No significant difference in age was observed between asymptomatic and symptomatic individuals. A greater proportion of asymptomatic individuals than of symptomatic individuals had fewer than 8 years of schooling. Chest radiographic observations were similar between groups, with most participants showing atypical findings. Bacterial load, measured by Xpert MTB/RIF, differed significantly between groups. Symptomatic individuals had high or medium bacterial loads more frequently than asymptomatic individuals, whereas asymptomatic individuals more often had low or very low loads. Previous incarceration was not associated with symptom status. Behavioural risk factors, including alcohol use, smoking, and drug use, were more prevalent among asymptomatic individuals than symptomatic individuals. Isoniazid resistance was also more frequent in asymptomatic individuals than symptomatic individuals. Among symptomatic individuals, the most commonly reported symptom was cough, followed by chest pain, fever, weight loss, difficulty breathing, night sweats, and loss of appetite.

408 (80·8%) of 505 isolates from individuals identified through active case finding belonged to 98 genomic clusters using a 12-SNP threshold. Clustering proportions did not differ significantly between symptomatic (56 clusters; 213 [76·9%] of 277) and asymptomatic (42 clusters; 195 [85·5%] of 228) individuals (p=0·37). In logistic regression models adjusted for incarceration status, smoking, alcohol use, drug use, sampling date, and strain lineage, no significant difference in clustering was observed between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals (p=0·75). To assess the robustness of these findings, we repeated the analysis using a more stringent 6-SNP threshold. Results were consistent with those obtained using the 12-SNP threshold, with similar clustering proportions in symptomatic (134 [48·4%] of 277) and asymptomatic (146 [64·0%] of 228) individuals (p=0·47; appendix 1 p 16).

We evaluated the epidemic success of M tuberculosis isolates using two phylogenetic metrics: THD and LBI (figure 3). There was no significant difference in THD between isolates from asymptomatic (median 0·50 [IQR 0·09–0·65]) and symptomatic individuals (0·39 [0·06–0·62]; p=0·12). LBI estimates were slightly higher among asymptomatic individuals (median 0·00871 [IQR 0·00829–0·01020]) than among symptomatic individuals (0·00863 [0·00810–0·00988]), but this difference was not statistically significant (p=0·088). To identify predictors of epidemic success and increased fitness, we fit generalised linear models for both THD and LBI, adjusting for relevant covariates. Incarceration status was a significant predictor of higher epidemic success across both metrics. For THD, isolates from individuals who were currently incarcerated had the highest values (β=0·31; p<0·0001), followed by those from individuals who were previously incarcerated (β=0·15; p=0·048), compared with individuals with no incarceration history. Drug use was also associated with higher THD values (β=0·17; p<0·0001). A similar pattern was observed for LBI, with isolates from individuals who were currently incarcerated showing significantly higher estimates (β=0·00020; p<0·0001), whereas no significant association was observed for individuals who were previously incarcerated (β=−0·00007; p=0·45). Symptom status was not associated with either THD (β=0·02; p=0·75) or LBI (β=0·00007; p=0·18).

We examined transmission patterns across the ten largest genomic clusters using posterior distributions of transmission trees inferred with the BREATH model.14 This approach enabled probabilistic reconstruction of transmission relationships while accounting for unsampled hosts. To assess robustness, we compared the distributions of total transmission probabilities for symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals under two plausible scenarios that differed in the assumed timing of transmission relative to case detection. Estimated numbers of secondary infections were similar across the two scenarios (appendix 1 pp 11, 17). Based on these results, we selected the medium scenario (mean transmission interval=1·5) for downstream analyses, as it reflects a more biologically and epidemiologically realistic assumption for M tuberculosis in high-burden settings. Summary statistics describing key transmission tree features are show in appendix 1 (p 18).

Among symptomatic individuals, the median number of estimated secondary infections was 0·37 (IQR 0·15–0·73; range 0–2·29), and among asymptomatic individuals, the median was 0·44 (IQR 0·17–0·69; range 0–1·78). Across all clusters, the median number of onward infections did not differ between groups (Wilcoxon p=0·56; figure 4). When analyses were restricted to individuals who were incarcerated, we found no significant difference in the estimated median number of secondary infections between symptomatic (0·21 [IQR 0·04–0·40]) and asymptomatic individuals (0·30 [0·06–0·47]; Wilcoxon p=0·27). To illustrate transmission patterns, we visualised the inferred network for cluster 6 (the largest genomic cluster in the dataset), highlighting the direction and probability of transmission events by symptom status (figure 5). Similar network visualisations for three additional large clusters are provided in appendix 1 (pp 12–14).

Discussion

This study integrated genomic, epidemiological, and clinical data to examine the role of asymptomatic cases in tuberculosis transmission, leveraging statewide genomic surveillance, including active case finding among individuals who are incarcerated. Using multiple analytical frameworks—genomic clustering, phylogenetic metrics of epidemic success, and probabilistic transmission trees—we observed no significant difference in transmission measures between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals in a high-incidence setting. These findings add to growing evidence that individuals with asymptomatic tuberculosis can contribute substantially to transmission at levels similar to symptomatic individuals. Although no significant differences were identified, we observed higher estimates for transmission metrics and higher proportions of behavioural risk factors among asymptomatic individuals, suggesting potential differences in disease dynamics that warrant further investigation.

Studies published in the past 5 years indicate that at a given timepoint, approximately half of the individuals with pulmonary tuberculosis are asymptomatic.2 Modelling studies suggest that these individuals could contribute substantially to transmission20 and that symptom-based case finding alone might be insufficient to achieve meaningful reductions in tuberculosis incidence.21 However, few empirical data quantifying infectiousness by symptom status are available. Studies using tuberculin skin tests or interferon-gamma release assays have reported similar risks of M tuberculosis infection among close contacts of symptomatic and asymptomatic cases.3,4 However, these approaches are limited because the outcome—M tuberculosis infection—reflects cumulative lifetime exposure and is typically measured cross-sectionally. Furthermore, studies indicate that a substantial proportion of M tuberculosis transmission occurs outside close-contact networks.22 A genomic epidemiology study from Valencia, Spain, estimated that fix of 14 individuals with tuberculosis transmitted M tuberculosis before symptom onset.23 Our findings extend this work by providing evidence from a large genomic surveillance study with an active case-finding component, thereby reducing symptom-related diagnostic bias.

These findings highlight the importance of identifying individuals with asymptomatic tuberculosis and expanding case-finding strategies beyond symptom-based approaches. Incorporating radiographic screening and molecular diagnostics is essential for earlier detection and interruption of transmission. Although this approach is particularly crucial in populations who are incarcerated, among whom transmission is amplified, it also applies to other high-risk groups, such as individuals with alcohol or drug use, who might have higher susceptibility to tuberculosis and reduced likelihood of symptom reporting. Systematic, symptom-independent screening in these settings is essential to reduce missed diagnoses and ongoing transmission.

Our findings underscore the need to strengthen tuberculosis control efforts in prisons. Isolates from individuals who were incarcerated were more frequently found in genomic transmission clusters, providing phylogenetic evidence that recent transmission is more common in prisons than in surrounding communities. M tuberculosis strains from prisons and the community were closely related, and most clusters included both individuals who were incarcerated and individuals who were not incarcerated. Incarceration history appeared as a strong predictor of M tuberculosis epidemic success. Both THD and LBI metrics indicated higher transmission potential among individuals who were currently or had been previously incarcerated, independent of symptom status or bacterial lineage. These results highlight the interconnection between prison and community transmission and indicate that mitigating tuberculosis transmission within prisons is a crucial public health priority. Our findings are consistent with those of earlier genomic studies in high-incarceration settings, which showed frequent transmission between individuals who were and those who were not incarcerated.9,24–26 A previous study from Mato Grosso do Sul found that more than 70% of community tuberculosis cases were genetically linked to clusters that included individuals who were incarcerated, further supporting prisons as amplification points for tuberculosis spread.16 Our findings extend this work by incorporating clinical and symptom status data and showing that transmission potential is not limited to symptomatic individuals.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. Although all available cultures were sequenced, the analysed genomes represent only a subset of notified tuberculosis cases in Mato Grosso do Sul during the study period. Sequencing coverage varied by year, reflecting changes in culture-based diagnostic practices and surveillance intensity. Consequently, some circulating genotypes might not have been captured, potentially underestimating recent transmission. Because this study included only individuals with culture-positive samples, differences in culture sensitivity between individuals who reported symptoms and those who did not could have introduced bias. Lower bacterial loads among individuals who were asymptomatic might have reduced culture positivity, leading to under-representation of this group. Furthermore, although this study includes one of the largest genomic datasets from a population of individuals who were incarcerated, the subset of individuals identified through active case finding with complete symptom data could still limit statistical power to detect modest differences between groups. Symptom data were available only for individuals identified through active screening, and findings reflect transmission dynamics in a high-incidence setting, which might limit generalisability.

Transmission rates of M tuberculosis are much higher in prisons than in the general population, and the relative contribution of symptomatic and asymptomatic infections might differ in lower-transmission settings. Phylogenetic metrics of transmission could also be influenced by unmeasured contextual factors that vary by symptom status, such as mixing patterns within prisons, duration of incarceration, or frequency of facility transfers. As individual-level data on these factors were unavailable, their effects could not be assessed, and our results should be interpreted in light of these limitations. In addition, multiple hypothesis-driven comparisons were conducted, and findings approaching significance should be interpreted cautiously. Symptom reporting among individuals who are incarcerated, who are mostly young men with a high prevalence of tobacco use, might not reflect patterns in the general population.

Finally, symptom status was assessed at a single timepoint, despite evidence that symptoms evolve over the course of tuberculosis. Individuals who are symptomatic at diagnosis probably had a prolonged asymptomatic phase, making it difficult to understand the extent of transmission occurring during the symptomatic versus asymptomatic period. Moreover, transmission inference is sensitive to assumptions regarding timing, sampling, and within-host diversity. Although the BREATH model incorporates unsampled hosts and epidemiological timing and sensitivity analyses on key model parameters were conducted, the contribution of asymptomatic individuals could still be underestimated, particularly in the presence of undiagnosed or unreported cases.

This study provides genomic epidemiological evidence that asymptomatic individuals play a substantial role in M tuberculosis transmission. By integrating clinical data with whole-genome sequencing and transmission modelling, we show that transmission potential is not determined by symptom status alone. These findings underscore the limitations of symptom-based screening and highlight the need for broader diagnostic strategies capable of detecting tuberculosis earlier in the disease course. Systematic screening in high-risk populations, irrespective of symptoms, will be essential to interrupt transmission and accelerate progress towards tuberculosis elimination.
