Less than what? Response to alcohol consumption recommendations in the 2025–2030 dietary guidelines for Americans
Authors: MacKenzie R. Peltier, Geetanjali Chander, Hendrée E. Jones, Grace Chang, Sherry A. McKee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe United States Health and Human Services (HHS) and the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) issued new alcohol guidelines in January 2026. The prior per occasion drinking limits of no more than 2 drinks per day for men and no more than 1 drink per day for women were replaced with the ambiguous advice to ‘consume less for better health’. Alcohol use remains one of the leading causes of preventable disease and death, contributing annually to 3 million deaths worldwide. Women have demonstrated recent increases in drinking and evidence exacerbated health risks associated with drinking alcohol. The removal of sex-specific drinking guidelines has significant health consequences, particularly for women. This Personal View addresses recent conflicting reports regarding the relationship between alcohol and health; summarizes the current state of the knowledge regarding alcohol and health; addresses women as a special population; and provides recommendations to improve public health.

While an estimated 35 million Americans ushered in 2026 with the promises of a “Dry January,” others were left confused as the United States (US) Health and Human Services, and U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) rewrote the longstanding US Dietary Guidelines for alcohol use. Since 1980, the USDA has promoted clear drinking limits and for decades it has advised Americans to limit alcohol use to no more than two drinks per day for men and one drink per day for women (with a standard drink containing 14 grams [g] of pure alcohol), on days when alcohol is consumed. The guidance emphasized moderation on drinking occasions rather than a strict daily or cumulative weekly limit and was intended to frame alcohol intake in terms of individual drinking events, not to establish a risk-free threshold for total weekly or lifetime consumption. However, that guidance changed in the first week of 2026. This new guidance arrives on the heels of three highly publicized, national reports released within the past year, detailing divergent alcohol consumption recommendations for Americans. In early 2025, a Surgeon General's Advisory highlighted the causal relationship between alcohol and various forms of cancers (i.e., oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, esophagus, liver, breast [in women], and colorectal cancers), and marked an important step in making Americans more aware of the dangers of alcohol use.3 The Advisory affirmed the message that many physicians and other health professionals have been communicating to Americans in recent years, that alcohol is directly related to significant increases in cancer. The Surgeon General's Advisory called for changes to the labeling on alcohol products to include a warning about the risk of cancer associated with alcohol consumption, and to “reassess the recommended limit for alcohol consumption to account for the latest evidence on alcohol consumption and cancer risk”.3

Shortly thereafter, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) released their own Review of Evidence on Alcohol and Health report,4 that drew frankly discordant results to the Surgeon General's Advisory. The report asserted a case for moderate drinking, stating that “moderate drinkers” have a lower all-cause mortality and that there may be health benefits, including cardiovascular benefits, to low-volume alcohol consumption.4 However, the methodology utilized in the NASEM literature synthesis has been called into question, as they excluded studies of individuals who formerly consumed alcohol,5,6 placed more weight on observational studies,5,6 and only included one U.S.-based study.7 Similarly, the American Heart Association released a report that recycled the previously refuted messaging that “light drinking” (i.e., <2 drinks per day for a man and <1 drink per day for a women) may improve one's cardiovascular health.8 This messaging directly contradicts countless recent studies, scientific and medical leaders, and even the group's own 2023 statement that “no safe level of alcohol use is acceptable.”9

Media reports have raised concerns that financial interests and lobbying by the alcohol industry may be influencing U.S. health messaging on alcohol, including claims that the Alcohol Intake and Health Study was withdrawn after cautioning against even one drink per day.10 These concerns have been further amplified by the release of a Congressional oversight report on the study process and its role in shaping dietary guidance,11 identifying how politicized this process had become. Regardless of such influences, the recent lack of specific alcohol-related guidance within the USDA guidelines directly contradicts the efforts to improve the overall health of Americans and departs from the previous 2020–2025 guidelines,2 which stated:“Emerging evidence suggests that even drinking within the recommended limits may increase the overall risk of death from various causes, such as from several types of cancer and some forms of cardiovascular disease. Alcohol has been found to increase risk for cancer, and for some types of cancer, the risk increases even at low levels of alcohol consumption (less than 1 drink in a day). Caution, therefore, is recommended (page 49).”2

Part of the rationale for the current change to the alcohol guidance is based on the assertion that there was “never really good data” supporting the prior drinking limits. The fact that the underlying evidence is ‘imperfect’ misrepresents how public health guidelines are developed, as recommendations routinely rely on converging observational and modeled data. Even with wide confidence intervals in studies examining alcohol and health, the direction of evidence is consistent, showing that health risks (e.g., mortality, cancer, liver disease) increase progressively with higher alcohol intake, with no clear threshold of zero risk if an individual consumes alcohol. The prior drinking limits were never intended to be precise biological cutoffs but were pragmatic risk-reduction benchmarks grounded in the best available synthesis of evidence.

An additional challenge in providing guidance is that the relationship between alcohol and health varies substantially by health outcome. Meta-analyses and large cohort studies often reach different conclusions depending on the endpoints examined (e.g., all-cause mortality versus cause-specific disease). For example, a recent meta-analysis found no evidence of reduced all-cause mortality (i.e., protective effects) among “occasional” (<1.3 g ethanol/day) or “low-volume” (1.3–24.0 g/day) drinkers.12 In contrast, even consumption of less than one drink per day has been associated with increased cancer risk, approximately 16.5% in women and 10% in men.3 Consequently, integrating these findings into a single threshold or recommendation is inherently complex.

The removal of clear recommendations to guide Americans to minimize alcohol-related health risks comes at a time when many Americans remain unaware of the detrimental consequences of drinking. While rates of drinking have declined in recent years, 14.5 million Americans continue to engage in heavy drinking within the past month, exceeding even the previous 2020–2025 guidelines.13 Approximately, 70% of Americans report weekly drinking3 and in a recent Gallup poll (2024), 43% of Americans “do not think moderate drinking makes any difference” to a person's health.14

Alcohol use remains one of the leading causes of preventable disease and death in the U.S. and needs to be highlighted as a modifiable risk factor to improve public health. The need for education and guidance is urgent as the World Health Organization (WHO) has causally linked alcohol consumption to over 200 health conditions (based on ICD-10 codes),15 contributing to 178,000 deaths annually in the U.S. and 3 million deaths worldwide.13,15 In addition to the cancers already mentioned, alcohol is causally linked to neurologic disease, including ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes, as well as increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases spanning cardiac arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease and hypertension. Individuals who drink alcohol are more likely to contract pulmonary diseases, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome and pneumonia. Alcohol-related liver disease (e.g., steatosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, alcohol-associated hepatitis, liver cancer) accounts for approximately 45% of all liver disease deaths worldwide. Moreover, drinking alcohol negatively impacts the gastrointestinal system and oral cavity, contributing to gut leakiness, microbial dysbiosis, as well as increasing the risk of inflammation of the pancreas in terms of both acute and chronic pancreatitis. Furthermore, alcohol use is related to musculoskeletal problems, including increases in risk of myopathy wasting and bone disease (e.g., impaired fracture repair, reduced bone density).13

Alcohol use also complicates the management of other chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, HIV) and is linked to suicides, poisonings (including drug overdoses), and injuries (including traffic-related injuries) and may exacerbate mental health conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety).13 In recent years, there has been a 47% increase in emergency department (ED) visits associated with alcohol use; or 7.1% of all ED visits nationwide.16 The public health cost of alcohol in the U.S. is estimated to be $249 billion per year in increased health costs and lost productivity.17

Such negative health consequences are not limited to only heavy drinking or alcohol use disorder. A recent meta-analysis found that consumption of approximately one drink per day was associated with an increased risk of cirrhosis in women, but not men.18 While the focus of the research on alcohol-related mortality and morbidity has been largely biased towards heavy drinking and alcohol use disorder, research examining the health impacts of lower levels of consumption has started to illustrate that there is no safe amount, consistent with recommendations from the WHO.15

It is also important to highlight that the current USDA guidelines have removed the sex-specific guidelines for drinking limits, which has significant consequences for the health of women in particular. While prevalence rates of drinking have been historically greater in men compared to women, this gap has been closing. In fact, amoung young adults, women are now engaging in heavy episodic drinking (i.e., binge drinking) at greater rates than their male counterparts.19 In addition, it is well established that there are sex differences in how alcohol is metabolized. Women will reach ∼66% higher blood alcohol concentrations then men, even if they are the same age and body weight, and had the same amount of alcohol. Increases in drinking in combination with higher blood alcohol concentrations have contributed to women experiencing greater health risks from drinking. In recent years, alcohol use has increased ED visits in women by 70% versus 58% in men,16 hospitalizations in women by 69% versus 43% in men,20 and rates of alcohol-related mortality in women by 35% versus 27% in men.21

Women have a higher risk of developing alcohol-related liver injury,22,23 including cirrhosis,24 hepatitis,25 and inflammation23 compared to men. The odds of death from alcohol-related hepatitis have recently increased in women by 270%, with young adult women having the greatest risk (40× the rate of excess death).25 Women are at greater risk of certain alcohol-related cancers than men,26 and there is a clear linear relationship between increased alcohol consumption and incidence of breast cancer in women (light drinkers have 4% increased risk; moderate drinkers have 23% increased risk; and heavy drinkers have 60% increased risk).27 Women are more vulnerable to alcohol-related stroke and cardiovascular conditions than men, including coronary heart disease,28 cardiomyopathy,29 hypertension,30 and atrial fibrillation.31 Women are also more susceptible to the neurotoxic effects of alcohol, resulting in greater neurodegeneration.32, 33, 34 Women also evidence greater associations between alcohol and mental health conditions, and have had larger increases in rates of alcohol-related suicide when compared to men.35

However, it is important to note that studies examining the relationship between alcohol and health control for confounding modifiers (e.g., smoking status, obesity, genetic variation, diet, physical activity, other health conditions). Although co-occurring risk factors (i.e., smoking) may amplify alcohol-related harms moreso among men at the population level (as smoking is more prevalent among men), this does not negate evidence of heightened biological susceptibility among women. Studies that adjust for these confounders consistently demonstrate that women experience greater alcohol-related harm at lower levels of consumption, and after shorter durations of use—which is referred to as a risk-severity paradox.36 Thus, men may demonstrate greater population-level burden of alcohol-related health effects (i.e., all-cause mortality), and women may demonstrate greater per-unit biological risk when looking a specific health outcomes.12,21,36

Women are also at risk for various sex-specific health conditions as a result of alcohol use. For example, heavy drinking is related to menstrual irregularity and changes in hormone levels.37 These changes include increased levels of free estradiol and testosterone,38 which have been related to heightened incidence of cancers (e.g., breast) and overall poor reproductive health, such as infertility. Currently 14% of pregnant women have consumed alcohol during pregnancy,39 which increases the risk of spontaneous abortion,40 perinatal mortality,41 and fetal alcohol spectrum disorders.42 Maternal alcohol use during pregnancy continues to be the leading preventable cause of birth defects and intellectual and neurodevelopmental disabilities among offspring in the U.S.42 Alcohol consumption is also involved in approximately one half of cases of physical and sexual assaults.43

Accordingly Americans, especially women, need to be provided with clear guidelines regarding alcohol consumption. In the absence of clear federal guidance, state and local public health departments can play a critical role by issuing evidence-based recommendations, implementing risk-reduction policies, and educating communities about alcohol-related harms. Aligning with recommendations from the Surgeon General's Advisory, it is prudent that changes to the warning label on alcohol products be updated to reflect the current state of knowledge, including the past 37 years of research on this topic. For example, Canada is actively considering federal alcohol labeling standards that include warnings about cancer risk and standard drink information. Public health organizations and policymakers have advocated for mandatory labels, and national survey data indicate that a substantial majority of Canadians (approximately 80%) support the inclusion of health warnings on alcohol containers.49 In the Canadian province of Yukon, a real-world implementation study tested large, evidence-based alcohol warning labels (i.e., cancer risk, national drinking guidelines, standard drink information) on alcohol products in the capital city of Whitehorse. Implementation was halted prematurely due to industry pressure, but results from the brief study period showed that the labels significantly reduced alcohol sales and self-reported consumption by ∼10% in the post-intervention period, demonstrating that clear, visible warnings can influence drinking behavior at the population level.50 Other countries (e.g., Ireland, South Korea) have laws requiring a warning about the cancer risk. While alcohol remains a leading modifiable health risk,3 research has yet to identify a ‘safe level’ of alcohol use. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that decreases in alcohol use may reduce some otherwise elevated health risks, without eliminating them.51 However, Americans are largely unaware of significant risks of alcohol consumption, even at “low” levels. The new guidance, or lack thereof, related to alcohol use leaves Americans, especially women who are more vulnerable to health consequences than men, at a significant disadvantage to understanding the relationship between alcohol use and health. Americans are left to manage this ambiguity to define what is “too much” alcohol. The 2025–2030 U.S. Dietary Guidelines fall short of optimally advancing public health of Americans and more specific guidance on alcohol consumption, which leverages current evidence related to health risks, would meaningfully advance the health of Americans.
