Safety and effectiveness of retrograde intrarenal surgery versus percutaneous nephrolithotomy in older patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis by the EAU-YAU urolithiasis and EAU endourology sections
Authors: Łukasz Nowak, Wojciech Krajewski, Klaudia Żywot, Begoña Ballesta Martinez, Amelia Pietropaolo, Wojciech Tomczak, Jan Łaszkiewicz, Matthias Boeykens, Thomas Tailly, Theodoros Spinos, Alberto Olivero, Agata Góral, Adam Chełmoński, Bartosz Małkiewicz, Bhaskar Somani, Tomasz Szydełko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe comparative efficacy and safety of retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) and percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) have been extensively evaluated in the general adult population; however, evidence specifically focused on older patients remains limited. Given the underrepresentation of older individuals in comparative studies and the lack of age-specific meta-analytic data, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of RIRS versus PCNL in the geriatric population.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Comparative studies evaluating outcomes of RIRS and PCNL in older patients were identified through comprehensive searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library up to January 2026. Primary outcomes included overall, minor, and major complications, as well as stone-free rate (SFR). Subgroup analyses were performed according to the age thresholds used to define the geriatric population (≥ 60 and ≥ 65 years).ResultsEight studies including 849 older patients (419 RIRS, 430 PCNL) were analysed. PCNL was associated with a significantly higher final SFR (OR 0.63, 95% CI: 0.43–0.93; p = 0.02). RIRS was associated with lower odds of overall complications (OR 0.54, 95% CI: 0.37–0.80; p = 0.002), minor complications (OR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.98; p = 0.04), major complications (OR 0.30, 95% CI: 0.12–0.74; p = 0.008), and blood transfusion (OR 0.14, 95% CI: 0.04–0.49; p = 0.002). No significant differences were observed in postoperative fever or sepsis. Operative time did not differ significantly, whereas hospital stay was significantly shorter following RIRS (MD − 2.0 days, 95% CI: −2.6 to − 1.3; p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn older adults, PCNL was associated with higher stone clearance rates, whereas RIRS showed a more favourable safety profile and shorter hospitalisation. Treatment selection should balance efficacy against perioperative morbidity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07812-0.

Background

The progressive ageing of the global population has led to an increasing prevalence of urolithiasis among older patients, many of whom require active surgical management [1–3]. Older individuals frequently present with a higher prevalence of comorbidities and reduced physiological reserve, factors that may significantly influence perioperative risk and clinical outcomes [4]. Although active stone removal by endourological procedures was demonstrated to be feasible in the geriatric population, age-related factors necessitate careful consideration when selecting the optimal treatment modality [5].

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) represent the most commonly utilised surgical approaches for large renal stone management. Multiple studies have shown that PCNL can be effectively performed in older adults, albeit with a tendency towards higher rates of minor complications and bleeding-related events [6, 7]. Furthermore, age-based analyses have demonstrated that retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) can be performed safely in patients aged ≥ 65 years, achieving stone-free rates (SFRs) comparable to those observed in younger individuals [8]. Moreover, in recent years RIRS has evolved considerably owing to advances in flexible ureteroscopic technology and the introduction of suction-assisted ureteral access sheaths (UAS), thereby expanding its role in the management of increasingly complex renal stone disease [9].

In the general adult population, RIRS and PCNL have been extensively compared. High-level evidence has demonstrated that PCNL is associated with higher SFR and a lower need for secondary interventions, whereas RIRS offers shorter hospital stay and a less invasive perioperative profile, with comparable rates of major complications [10]. More recent meta-analyses, suggest that technological refinements may further narrow the efficacy gap between RIRS and PCNL while maintaining a favourable safety profile for RIRS [11].

Despite this growing body of comparative evidence, older patients remain underrepresented in most studies, and age-specific analyses are scarce. Definitions of “older patients” vary widely, and outcomes are rarely reported separately for this population. Consequently, the comparative efficacy and safety of RIRS versus PCNL in older patients remain insufficiently characterised.

In light of these limitations, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesise the available evidence comparing RIRS and PCNL specifically in the geriatric population. The aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of RIRS versus PCNL in older patients, thereby addressing an important gap in the current literature and supporting evidence-based decision-making in this growing patient population.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420261301254).

Search strategy

Two review authors (Ł.N. and B.B.M.) independently performed a literature search in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases. This search encompassed the respective databases’ inception timeframe up to January 2026. The detailed search terms were provided in Supplementary Table 1. To ensure the inclusion of all relevant studies, the bibliographies of the included articles were reviewed, and backward citation searches were performed. No limitations regarding the publication date or language were imposed.

Eligibility criteria

The PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design) was used to define the eligibility criteria and to formulate the clinical research question.

Population

Older patients with renal or upper urinary tract stone disease. In the present systematic review and meta-analysis, older patients were defined using age thresholds commonly applied in the existing literature, including cut-offs of ≥ 60 or ≥ 65 years. In addition, studies employing a more stringent definition of older individuals with a cut-off of ≥ 70 years were also considered eligible. The use of multiple age thresholds reflects prevailing practice in clinical research and allows the inclusion of a broader and methodologically consistent body of evidence, while acknowledging the absence of a single universally accepted definition of geriatric status [13].

Intervention

RIRS. No restrictions were imposed regarding specific technical aspects of the procedure, including the type or diameter of the flexible ureteroscope, the use and type of ureteral access sheath, the lithotripsy energy source, or pre-stenting strategy. Studies employing any contemporary RIRS technique were considered eligible for inclusion.

Comparator

PCNL. No restrictions were imposed regarding specific technical aspects of the procedure, including patient positioning, puncture guidance modality, tract dilatation technique, tract size, lithotripsy energy source, or exit strategy. Studies employing any contemporary PCNL technique were considered eligible for inclusion.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were complication rates and final SFR. Complications were analysed as overall complications, minor complications (Clavien-Dindo I-II), and major complications (Clavien-Dindo III-V). Final SFR was defined as stone-free status assessed at postoperative follow-up performed between 4 and 12 weeks after surgery. Although the definition of SFR varied across the studies, final SFR generally referred to the complete absence of residual stones or the presence of clinically insignificant residual fragments, most commonly defined as fragments measuring < 4 mm. Studies were included if final SFR was assessed using postoperative imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), ultrasonography (USG), and/or plain radiography of the kidneys, ureters, and bladder (KUB).

Secondary outcomes were: immediate SFR, postoperative fever rate, sepsis rate, blood transfusion rate, operative time (OT), hospitalisation time (HT). Immediate SFR was defined as stone-free status assessed in the early postoperative period, as reported by individual studies, with evaluation time points ranging from 1 to 7 days after surgery.

Studies were eligible for inclusion only if they reported at least one predefined outcome of interest.

Study design

Prospective and retrospective comparative studies. Reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, commentaries, authors’ replies, meeting abstracts of unpublished studies, and case reports were excluded.

Data extraction

The data extraction process was conducted independently by two authors (Ł.N. and B.B.M.). Following initial screening of the search strategy results by titles and abstracts, full text screening and study selection was carried out using standardized item forms. Disagreements or discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third author who was not involved in the initial screening process (W.K.).

Extracted study-level variables included first author, year of publication, country, study design, enrolment period, definition of geriatric status, and sample size for each treatment group. Patient- and stone-related characteristics were also collected, including mean or median age, sex distribution, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, stone size, stone location, laterality, the presence of multiple stones, and auxiliary procedures rate (defined as any additional endourological intervention, including extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy [ESWL], RIRS, or PCNL, required after the index procedure to achieve stone-free status or to treat clinically significant residual fragments). Procedural details and outcome definitions were extracted, including definitions of SFR, timing and imaging modality used for SFR assessment, as well as technical characteristics of RIRS and PCNL procedures.

Risk of bias (RoB) assessment

The RoB of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (Ł.N. and B.B.M.). For non-randomized studies of interventions, the RoB in the estimation of intervention effects was evaluated using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool, and the results were summarised using traffic-light plots and overall risk-of-bias judgements [14]. In parallel, the methodological quality of the included articles was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates study design and reporting quality across the domains of cohort selection, comparability, and outcome assessment [15, 16]. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third co-investigator (W.K.).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For dichotomous outcomes, results were presented as the proportion of events relative to the total number of patients in each treatment arm, along with pooled effect estimates calculated as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes were analysed using mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. In studies reporting continuous outcomes as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or ranges, these values were converted to means and standard deviations using established statistical methods described by Luo et al. [17]. Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I² statistic. Heterogeneity was considered statistically significant when the Cochran’s Q test yielded a p-value < 0.10 and/or when the I² value exceeded 50%. In the absence of significant heterogeneity (Q test p ≥ 0.10 and I² ≤ 50%), a fixed-effects model was applied. Conversely, when substantial heterogeneity was detected (Q test p < 0.10 and/or I² > 50%), a random-effects model was used to account for between-study variability. Publication bias was assessed via visual inspection of contour-enhanced funnel plots. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach. Subgroup analyses for primary outcomes were planned according to predefined variables, including age thresholds used to define the older adults, and stone size. In the presence of substantial heterogeneity, meta-regression analysis was planned.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The detailed study selection process was summarised in the Fig. 1. Of 1057 records identified through database searching, 8 articles ultimately met the eligibility criteria and were included in the present systematic review and meta-analysis [18–25].

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection

The baseline characteristics of the included studies were presented in Table 1. Across the included studies, a total of 849 older patients were analysed, of whom 419 (49.4%) and 430 (50.6%) underwent RIRS and PCNL, respectively. The majority of the included papers were retrospective single-centre studies [18–21, 23–25], with one prospective single-centre study [22]. Four studies were conducted in Turkey [18, 20, 22, 24] and four originated from China [19 , 21, 23, 25]. Definitions of geriatric status were evenly distributed, with four studies usingan age cut-offof 60 years [19, 21, 22, 25] and four studies using an age cut-off of 65 years [18, 20, 23, 24]. Eligible articles primarily focused on patients with renal stones [18, 20–24], while selected studies also included upper ureteral stones with infection or complex upper urinary tract calculi [19, 25].

The overall RoB assessed using the ROBINS-I tool was presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Most studies were judged to have a high RoB, primarily due to their retrospective design and potential confounding related to patient selection and baseline imbalances between treatment groups. No study was classified as having a critical risk of bias. Methodological quality assessed using the NOS ranged from 6 to 8 points, indicating moderate to high methodological quality across the included studies (Supplementary Table 2).

Patient demographics, stone and procedural characteristics

Baseline demographic, clinical, and stone-related characteristics of the included studies were presented in Table 2. The mean (or median) age ranged from approximately 65 to 75 years. Mean stone diameter ranged from approximately 15 mm to over 30 mm. None of the selected articles reported a statistically significant difference in stone size between the RIRS and PCNL groups at baseline. Most cohorts included renal calculi, with stones located predominantly in the renal pelvis or lower calyx. Two studies [18, 19] reported data exclusively on single stones, whereas in the remaining studies the proportion of patients with multiple stones reached up to 58% in the RIRS group and 60% in the PCNL group. The type of auxiliary procedures varied between studies and was generally poorly reported. Where available, auxiliary interventions included repeat RIRS, PCNL, and extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), although their frequency was typically low.

Definitions of SFR, imaging modalities employed for SFR assessment, and procedural characteristics of the selected articles were summarised in Table 3. All PCNL procedures were performed in the prone position, most commonly under fluoroscopic or combined ultrasonographic and fluoroscopic guidance. Tract dilatation techniques varied and included balloon, fascial, and Amplatz dilators, with sheath diameters ranging from 14 to 30 Fr. A nephrostomy tube was routinely placed in the majority of studies, often combined with a postoperative double-J (DJ) stent. RIRS was performed predominantly using 7.5 Fr flexible ureteroscopes, with routine use of UAS in most studies. Selected studies reported pre-stenting prior to RIRS, with passive dilatation performed for 1–2 weeks before re-intervention [21, 24, 25]. In the study by Chen et al., patients presenting with urinary tract infection underwent initial decompression with either double-J (DJ) stent placement or percutaneous nephrostomy, followed by definitive RIRS or PCNL approximately two weeks later, after infection control had been achieved. Postoperative DJ stenting was consistently applied across the included studies, typically for a duration of 2–4 weeks.

Meta-analysis

Primary outcomes

Overall complications

Seven studies provided data on overall complications [18–24]. The pooled analysis showed that RIRS was associated with significantly lower overall complication rates compared with PCNL (13.1% vs. 22.4%; OR 0.54, 95% CI: 0.37–0.80; p = 0.002) (Fig. 2). Significant heterogeneity was not found (I² = 0%, p = 0.82). Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that the pooled effect remained statistically significant after omission of any individual study (Supplementary Table 3 A). Contour-enhanced funnel plot demonstrated mild asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

Forest plot for overall complication rates. CI – confidence interval, OR – odds ratio, PCNL – percutaneous nephrolithotomy, RIRS – retrograde intrarenal surgery

Minor complications (Clavien-Dindo I-II)

Seven studies provided data on minor complications [18–24]. The pooled analysis showed that RIRS was associated with significantly lower minor complication rates compared with PCNL (11.6% vs. 17.5%; OR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.98; p = 0.04) (Fig. 3). Significant heterogeneity was not observed (I² = 0%, p = 0.91). In leave-one out analysis, statistical significance was lost when omitting studies by Chen et al., Hu et al., Wu et al., or Yarimoglu et al., nevertheless, the direction of effect remained in favour of RIRS, indicating a persistent trend despite loss of statistical significance (Supplementary Table 3 A). The contour-enhanced funnel plot demonstrated slight asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Major complications (Clavien-Dindo III-V)

Eight studies reported data on major complications; however, in two studies [18, 23] no events were observed in either treatment group and were therefore not estimable in the pooled analysis. The meta-analysis of the remaining six studies showed that compared to PCNL, RIRS was associated with significantly lower major complication rates (1.9% vs. 6.3%; OR 0.30, 95% CI: 0.12–0.74; p = 0.008) (Fig. 4). Significant heterogeneity was not observed (I² = 0%, p = 0.88). In the leave-one-out analysis, omission of Yarimoglu et al. resulted in loss of statistical significance; however, the effect estimate indicated a persistent trend in favour of RIRS (Supplementary Table 3 A). The contour-enhanced funnel plot did not demonstrate clear asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

Final SFR

Eight studies provided data on final SFR. The pooled analysis showed that PCNL was associated with significantly higher final SFR compared with RIRS (87.8% vs. 81.9%; OR 0.63, 95% CI: 0.43–0.93; p = 0.02) (Fig. 5). Significant heterogeneity was not observed (I² = 26.3%, p = 0.21). In the leave-one-out analysis, omission of Hu et al., Wu et al., or Yarimoglu et al. resulted in loss of statistical significance, indicating that the overall effect was partially influenced by these studies (Supplementary Table 3 A). The contour-enhanced funnel plot for did not demonstrate clear asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 2D).

Eight studies reported data on postoperative sepsis; however, in five of these studies [18, 20, 22, 23, 25] no events occurred in either treatment group and were therefore not estimable in the pooled analysis. Four studies provided data on immediate SFR [18, 21, 22, 25]. A non-significant trend favouring PCNL was observed (60.1% vs. 76.2%; OR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.17–1.01; p = 0.053) (Fig. 9). Substantial heterogeneity was detected (I² = 62.8%, p = 0.04), and a random-effects model was applied. In leave-one-out analysis, exclusion of Ozgor et al. eliminated heterogeneity and resulted in statistical significance of pooled effect (Supplementary Table 3 A). Subgroup analysis for primary outcomes

Predefined subgroup analyses for primary outcomes were performed according to the age cut-off used to define the geriatric population (≥ 60 vs. ≥ 65 years) (Fig. 12). For overall complications, RIRS was associated with significantly lower odds compared with PCNL in both subgroups, demonstrating consistent findings across age definitions.

Subgroup analysis of primary outcomes according to age threshold (≥ 60 vs. ≥ 65 years). Odds ratio < 1 indicates lower odds of complications and lower odds of SFR in the RIRS group compared with the PCNL group. CI – confidence interval, OR – odds ratio

For minor complications, no statistically significant difference was observed in either subgroup. Regarding major complications, a statistically significant difference favouring RIRS was observed only in the ≥ 65 years subgroup (OR 0.14, 95% CI: 0.02–0.81; p = 0.03), whereas no significant difference was detected in the ≥ 60 years subgroup.

For final SFR, no significant difference was found in the ≥ 60 years subgroup, while PCNL demonstrated significantly higher odds of achieving stone-free status in the ≥ 65 years subgroup (OR 0.52, 95% CI: 0.29–0.92; p = 0.02).

A predefined subgroup analysis according to stone size could not be reliably performed, as most included studies reported comparable baseline stone diameters between treatment groups and did not provide sufficiently stratified data to enable meaningful quantitative comparison. Owing to the low degree of heterogeneity observed across the included studies for primary outcomes, meta-regression analysis was not performed.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis specifically comparing RIRS and PCNL exclusively in a geriatric population, addressing a clinically relevant gap in the current literature. Appropriate selection of surgical modality seems to be particularly relevant in older patients, who frequently present with a higher burden of comorbidities and reduced physiological reserve, increasing their vulnerability to perioperative stress and complications. Age-related alterations in cardiovascular, renal, and pulmonary function may limit tolerance to prolonged anaesthesia, blood loss, and systemic inflammatory response [5]. Studies evaluating endourological interventions in older adults have consistently demonstrated a high prevalence of elevated ASA scores and multimorbidity, factors that may independently influence postoperative outcomes [4]. Consequently, treatment selection in older patients must carefully balance procedural efficacy against perioperative morbidity and functional recovery.

In the present meta-analysis comparing RIRS and PCNL in older patients, PCNL was associated with a higher final SFR, whereas RIRS was linked to lower odds of overall, minor, and major complications as well as blood transfusion. No significant differences were observed in postoperative fever or sepsis rates. Although OT did not differ between techniques, RIRS was associated with a shorter HT. These findings highlight a clinically relevant trade-off between efficacy and invasiveness, suggesting that treatment selection in the geriatric population should balance the higher clearance potential of PCNL against the lower perioperative morbidity observed with RIRS.

Previous meta-analyses conducted in the general adult population have consistently demonstrated that PCNL achieves higher SFR compared with RIRS, whereas RIRS is associated with shorter hospitalisation and a more favourable perioperative safety profile [10, 26]. These patterns align with our results in older patients. Importantly, while the direction of effect mirrors that observed in the general population, the balance between efficacy and safety may be even more clinically relevant in older adults, given their increased susceptibility to perioperative complications.

In the present analysis, PCNL achieved a higher final SFR compared with RIRS (87.8% vs. 81.9%), with most included studies defining final SFR as complete clearance or residual fragments < 4 mm assessed by CT. Although PCNL provides superior single-session clearance, staged RIRS has been reported to achieve comparable overall effectiveness with an acceptable safety profile, particularly in complex stones [27]. Importantly, data on auxiliary procedures were inconsistently reported in our included studies; however, Akman et al. described a secondary RIRS rate of approximately 18%, highlighting the potential need for re-intervention after initial endoscopic management [18]. When interpreting these findings in older patients, it should be acknowledged that each additional anaesthetic exposure carries inherent perioperative risk, especially in individuals with reduced physiological reserve [28]. Therefore, decisions regarding repeat procedures should be carefully individualised and guided by the clinical relevance of residual fragments rather than radiographic findings alone, as not all residual stones warrant intervention [29].

In the present meta-analysis, most included studies enrolled patients with mean stone diameters ranging between 15 and 25 mm, a size range that represents a clinical threshold at which the choice between PCNL and RIRS remains debated. Importantly, contemporary practice increasingly recognises that treatment selection between RIRS and PCNL should not rely solely on the maximum linear stone diameter, which represents a one-dimensional measure that may inadequately reflect the true stone burden, particularly in non-spherical or complex calculi. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by the EAU Urolithiasis Guidelines Panel and the YAU Urolithiasis Working Group demonstrated that multidimensional measures of stone burden, including stone volume, predict stone-free status with significantly higher accuracy than single linear measurements [30]. Consistent with these findings, the French Urolithiasis Committee (CLAFU) has recently emphasised stone volume as a more reliable predictor of SFR after ureteroscopy, particularly for stones exceeding 20 mm [31]. Furthermore, dedicated analyses such as those by Yazici et al. within the RIRSearch group have suggested that stone volume may outperform linear stone size in predicting both efficacy and safety outcomes of RIRS [32]. Notably, none of the studies included in our meta-analysis reported stone volume either as a baseline parameter or as a determinant of treatment allocation. This represents a relevant limitation, as the comparability of efficacy outcomes between RIRS and PCNL in the geriatric population may have been influenced by unmeasured differences in true stone burden. Future studies in older adults should therefore incorporate volumetric stone assessment to allow more accurate patient stratification and more individualised modality selection.

The safety profile of endourological procedures is of particular importance in older individuals. In older patients, even minor complications may result in functional decline or delayed recovery. Major complications following PCNL may include significant haemorrhage, need for transfusion or angioembolisation, and visceral injury, all of which may have amplified clinical consequences in geriatric individuals. In the present meta-analysis, we confirmed that patients undergoing PCNL had significantly higher odds of blood transfusion, compared with those treated with RIRS. Older patients frequently present with additional bleeding risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and the use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, which may further increase the detrimental impact of haemorrhagic complications [33]. These findings are clinically relevant when selecting the appropriate treatment modality, as potential complications may significantly prolong the HT. In our meta-analysis, length of hospital stay was significantly shorter in the RIRS group compared with PCNL (MD − 2.0 days). This finding is clinically relevant in geriatric patients, as prolonged hospitalisation in older adults may be associated with an increased risk of hospital-acquired complications, including nosocomial infections and functional decline [34].

Recent technological developments are currently redefining the contemporary landscape of endourological stone management. In particular, the introduction of flexible and navigable suction ureteral access sheaths (FANS) has expanded the applicability of RIRS to larger and more complex stones while improving intrarenal pressure control and active fragment evacuation [11, 35, 36]. Recent high-level evidence supports this evolution. A multicentre randomised non-inferiority trial by Zeng et al. demonstrated that flexible ureteroscopy (f-URS) with FANS was non-inferior to mini-PCNL for 2–3 cm renal stones in terms of immediate SFR, while offering a significantly lower transfusion rate, shorter hospitalisation, and greater improvement in quality of life [37]. Consistent with these findings, a large propensity score-matched analysis of 1372 patients comparing suction mini-PCNL with FANS f-URS confirmed favourable, modality-specific safety and efficacy profiles for both contemporary suction-based approaches [38]. In parallel, miniaturisation of PCNL instrumentation and supine positioning has been associated with reduced bleeding risk and perioperative morbidity in selected cohorts, features particularly relevant to older patients [39]. Of note, the majority of PCNL procedures in our included studies were performed using miniaturised tracts (14–20 Fr), with only one study using standard 30 Fr access; however, all procedures were performed in the prone position, and none of the included studies incorporated suction-enabled devices (neither FANS-assisted RIRS nor suction mini-PCNL). The application of these novel approaches in geriatric patients could plausibly alter the balance between safety and efficacy reported in the available literature. To the best of our knowledge, however, no comparative study to date has specifically evaluated suction-based devices in geriatric cohorts or reported outcomes stratified by age subgroups within larger mixed-population analyses. Further studies incorporating these devices (FANS-assisted RIRS and suction mini-PCNL) in older adults are therefore warranted.

It is also important to acknowledge that the included studies applied different age thresholds to define the geriatric population (≥ 60 or ≥ 65 years). Although this reflects current variability in clinical research [13], it may influence outcome interpretation. In our subgroup analysis, certain findings (i.e., major complications) reached statistical significance only in patients aged ≥ 65 years, suggesting that treatment-related risk may differ across age strata. These observations underline the importance of consistent age stratification in future studies to better characterise procedure-specific risk profiles within distinct geriatric subgroups.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the overall number of included studies and patients was relatively small, reflecting the limited availability of comparative data specifically focused on geriatric populations. The restricted sample size was also reflected in the sensitivity analyses, where omission of individual studies in certain outcomes led to loss of statistical significance, although the overall direction of effect remained consistent. Notably, no single methodological or clinical factor clearly explained this influence, suggesting that the observed sensitivity was primarily related to the limited number of contributing studies rather than to a specific source of bias. Second, the majority of included studies were retrospective in design, which inherently introduces risks of selection bias, confounding, and incomplete outcome reporting. In the absence of randomisation, it cannot be entirely excluded that treatment allocation between RIRS and PCNL was influenced by non-standardised factors such as surgeon preference or perceived patient frailty, potentially favouring PCNL in patients with greater stone burden and RIRS in those with higher perioperative risk. Only one of the included studies applied propensity score matching, and the ROBINS-I assessment correspondingly classified most studies as having a high risk of bias, primarily driven by confounding and participant selection. These limitations should be considered when interpreting our pooled estimates and underscore the need for prospective, ideally randomised, comparative studies in geriatric populations. Third, high heterogeneity was observed for some outcomes, such as OT and HT. The heterogeneity in OT was likely driven primarily by inter-centre differences in procedural techniques and perioperative protocols. In particular, PCNL procedures varied with respect to tract dilatation methods, sheath diameter, puncture guidance, and exit strategy, whereas RIRS protocols differed in the use and size of ureteral access sheaths, pre-stenting practices, and postoperative stenting duration. By contrast, heterogeneity in HT may have additionally reflected differences in institutional discharge criteria and postoperative care pathways. Therefore, these secondary outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by centre-specific practice patterns rather than treatment modality alone. Fourth, SFR assessment was heterogeneous across the included studies, with variability in imaging modalities (CT, USG, and/or KUB) and timing of evaluation, which may have influenced the pooled estimates of stone clearance given the differing sensitivity of these modalities for detecting residual fragments. Fifth, all included studies originated from only two countries, Turkey and China. Although no definitive explanation for this regional concentration can be established, the limited representation of Western populations and other geographic regions restricts the generalisability of our findings. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to broader populations. Finally, the available evidence may not fully reflect contemporary endourological practice, as none of the included studies incorporated suction-enabled technologies, such as FANS or suction-assisted PCNL systems. Emerging evidence suggests that these innovations may improve SFR with RIRS without compromising its safety profile, potentially narrowing the efficacy gap between the two modalities reported in our pooled analysis.

Conclusions

In older patients, final SFR was higher in the PCNL group than in the RIRS group; however, complication and blood transfusion rates were correspondingly higher following PCNL. These findings highlight a clinically relevant trade-off between efficacy and safety in older individuals, in whom physiological reserve and comorbidity burden must be carefully considered. Treatment selection should therefore be individualised, balancing the likelihood of complete stone clearance against the potential impact of procedure-related complications. Further prospective, multicentre studies focusing specifically on geriatric populations and incorporating contemporary techniques are warranted.
