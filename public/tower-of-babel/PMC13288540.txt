Fluoride varnish application and the temporal evolution of supragingival microbiota in children with differential caries risk
Authors: Fanghong Liu, Haiyan Xue, Yiling Jiang, Shaoyong Chen, Yishan Zhang, Jinmei Yu, Yu Qin, Xiaohua Dong, Yangshan Ou, Rongmin Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFluoride varnish is used to prevent early childhood caries, but its longitudinal ecological impact on the supragingival microbiome beyond enamel remineralization remains poorly understood, especially long-term.ObjectiveTo characterize 24‑month supragingival plaque microbiome evolution in young children under biannual fluoride varnish, stratified by caries risk, and fluoride’s ecological effect on microbial structure, interactions, and metabolism.DesignA total of 48 children were categorized into low‑, moderate‑, and high‑caries‑risk groups using the modified caries-risk assessment tool for preschool children (PSC-MCAT). All received fluoride varnish every six months. Supragingival plaque collected at five timepoints over 24 months. 16S rRNA sequencing assessed diversity, key taxa, co-occurrence networks, and PICRUSt2 metabolic pathways.ResultsBaseline alpha‑ and beta‑diversity differed significantly across risk groups (p<0.05). After sustained fluoride, intergroup differences diminished. High‑risk group retained caries‑associated genera (e.g. Leptotrichia, Prevotella, Veillonella) with suppressed abundance. Fluoride increased network complexity and negative correlations in moderate‑/high‑risk groups. Glycolysis, TCA cycle, and starch/sucrose metabolism were inhibited in the high‑risk group post‑intervention.ConclusionsRegular fluoride varnish reduces ecological disparities across risk groups, suppresses cariogenic taxa, and alters microbial interactions and carbohydrate metabolism, promoting homeostasis. High‑risk children may require more frequent interventions.

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is a prevalent chronic oral disease affecting children under 6 years old, characterized by its high prevalence, rapid progression and severe impact on the well-being of young children, posing a significant global public health challenge [1]. The impact of ECC is multidimensional, leading to impaired physiological development, restricted language function, psychological distress and an increased risk of systemic infections [2]. The disease is associated with frequent sugar consumption in an environment of acid-producing bacteria and enamel developmental defects [3]. The ‘ecological plaque hypothesis’ proposes that disease is not caused solely by specific pathogens, but rather by environmentally driven microbial dysbiosis. Pathobionts, present at low levels under healthy conditions, gain a competitive edge when environmental shifts occur [4]. While species such as Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus are well-documented for their cariogenic potential due to polysaccharide production and high acidogenicity [5,6], a broader range of bacteria, including Veillonella, Actinomyces and Prevotella, are also implicated [7,8]. From the perspective of the ecological plaque hypothesis, frequent exposure to fermentable dietary carbohydrates creates an environmental pressure that disrupts the homeostatic balance of the resident microbiota. This disruption favors the proliferation and metabolic activity of acidogenic (acid-producing) and aciduric (acid-tolerant) bacteria [9]. The resulting sustained production of organic acids overwhelms the buffering capacity of saliva, leading to a net loss of minerals from the tooth enamel and the eventual formation of a carious lesion. Therefore, preventing caries is not merely about eliminating a single pathogen but about maintaining or restoring a healthy, balanced oral ecosystem. Caries-preventive strategies primarily aim to re-establish a healthy and resilient supragingival microbiome.

For decades, fluoride has been the cornerstone of public health strategies for caries prevention. Historically, fluoride varnish prevents and reverses dental caries primarily by promoting enamel remineralization through accelerated calcium and phosphate deposition (creating acid-resistant enamel) and by inhibiting demineralization through adsorption onto crystal surfaces [10]. While these effects are undisputed, the direct impact of fluoride on the supragingival microbiome as a complex ecosystem is less understood. Research on the antimicrobial properties of fluoride has long been constrained by certain limitations. For many years, studies have predominantly focused on its inhibitory effects on a narrow range of cultivable cariogenic bacteria in vitro, or relied on short-term clinical observations and animal models [11,12]. This has left a significant gap in our understanding of how sustained, long-term fluoride intervention modulates the intricate web of microbial interactions, community structure and metabolic functions in vivo.

Although the clinical efficacy of fluoride is well-established, its precise mechanism of action on the supragingival microbiome remains an area of active research. Recent studies suggest a selective inhibitory effect on cariogenic bacteria [13,14], and some have shown an increase in health-associated genera following fluoride varnish application [11,15]. Nevertheless, the existing literature has several limitations. Many studies are of short duration (≤12 weeks), which is likely insufficient to observe the full microbial succession cycle. Moreover, research has often focused on single bacterial species rather than the complex interaction network, while other studies have found minimal impact on the microbiome, attributing fluoride's benefits primarily to its physicochemical effects on the tooth surface [12,16–19]. There remains a significant gap in the systematic investigation of the regulatory mechanisms of microorganisms by fluoride.

This study aimed to address a critical knowledge gap through a 24-month longitudinal investigation of the supragingival plaque microbiome in young children stratified by caries risk. Using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, we characterized the dynamic evolution of the microbial community following a standard biannual topical fluoride intervention. By tracking changes in microbial diversity, community structure, key taxa, interaction networks and predicted metabolic potential, this research pursued three main objectives: to explore the temporal changes in the oral micro-ecosystem under regular fluoride varnish application; to analyze the mechanisms underlying shifts in microbial composition, structure and function; and to examine the association between these microbial dynamics and caries risk status over time. Ultimately, this work seeks to provide an ecologically grounded scientific basis for optimizing fluoride-based caries prevention strategies and clarifying its mechanism of action, thereby supporting more effective interventions in pediatric populations.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study was registered with the National Clinical Trials Center (No: ChiCTR2500 00103295). Written informed consent was obtained from all guardians of the participants.

Study population and experimental design

The study subjects were drawn from a cohort of 464 five-year-old children from five kindergartens in Xixiangtang District, Nanning, China, who had completed a 24-month follow-up survey. The inclusion criteria for the subjects were as follows: (1) age 3 years old; (2) local residency >6 months; (3) no antibiotics/probiotics/other oral treatments within one month before survey; (4) no intraoral appliances and (5) informed and willing guardians. The exclusion criteria were as follows:(1) Presence of severe systemic diseases; (2) inability to cooperate effectively with the investigation and (3) allergy to fluoride.

The participants were stratified into three distinct groups based on their caries risk level, which was determined using the PSC-MCAT (modified caries-risk assessment tool for preschool children) assessment [20]. The PSC-MCAT tool collects relevant information through questionnaires and oral examinations, covering 12 key factors. The total score ranges from 0 to 14 (cumulative sum of individual item scores), with a higher score indicating a greater risk of dental caries. The grouping criteria were as follows: 0–3 = low risk (L), 4–6 = moderate risk (M) and 7–14 = high risk (H). Subsequently, 48 children were randomly and stratifically sampled from the entire cohort for longitudinal microbial analysis. This resulted in the formation of three experimental groups, each consisting of 16 children: a low-risk (L) group, a moderate risk (M) group and a high risk (H) group.

Oral examination

The clinical oral examinations were conducted following the standards outlined in the ‘Basic Methods of Oral Health Survey’ by the World Health Organization (WHO) [13] and the guidelines detailed in ‘Oral Epidemiology’ [14]. Two trained dentists performed the examinations using disposable mouth mirrors equipped with intraoral lights and Community Periodontal Index (CPI) probes. The examination included assessments of caries, enamel hypoplasia and the Visible Plaque Index (VPI). Prior to the study, both dentists underwent rigorous training in both theoretical knowledge and practical skills to ensure consistent examination and diagnosis, achieving a mean Kappa value of >0.85 for internal reliability.

Longitudinal intervention and sampling

All participants adhered to a standardized clinical protocol involving professional fluoride varnish application every six months over a total period of two years. Prior to fluoride varnish application, the oral cavity was cleaned by means such as rinsing and brushing. Fluoride varnish was applied to the deciduous teeth of the subjects following baseline and each follow-up sample collection. The procedure involved cleaning the tooth surfaces, isolating with cotton rolls, drying with cotton balls and then applying 0.5 ml of Duraphat® fluoride varnish (Colgate, Germany) to the tooth surfaces using a dedicated brush according to the product instructions. The selected material had a fluoride ion concentration of 22.6 mg/ml. To maximize fluoride adhesion, participants were advised to avoid drinking water and rinsing for 30 min after the treatment. They were also instructed not to consume solid foods for four hours and to refrain from brushing their teeth that evening. These measures ensured optimal fluoride absorption and efficacy. Supragingival plaque samples were collected from all participants at five distinct time points to enable longitudinal analysis of microbial community dynamics: at baseline (0 months, prior to the first intervention), and the 6th, 12th, 18th and 24th follow-up examinations. All sampling was performed before fluoride varnish application.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Standardized plaque collection

To ensure consistency and minimize variability, all clinicians involved in sample collection were trained according to the standardized protocols outlined in the Human Microbiome Project (HMP) Procedures Manual. On the morning of each sampling appointment, parents were instructed to have their children refrain from brushing their teeth to preserve the natural state of the oral biofilm. Sample collection was conducted between 9:00 am and 11:00 am. Prior to sampling, each child rinsed their mouth with clean water to remove loose food debris. The sampling sites were isolated using sterile cotton rolls, and excess saliva was gently removed with sterile cotton pellets. Supragingival plaque was then meticulously collected using sterile cotton swabs from the buccal surfaces of the maxillary and mandibular primary molars and the cervical one-third of the labial surfaces (teeth 55, 54, 64, 65, 75, 74, 84 and 85).

Sample preservation and DNA isolation

Immediately after collection, the head of each cotton swab was aseptically cut and placed into a sterile 2 ml microcentrifuge tube containing a preservation buffer. The tubes were labeled and stored in an insulated container with ice and dry ice for transport. All samples were transferred to a laboratory −80 °C freezer for long-term storage within two hours of collection. For DNA extraction, each plaque sample was thawed on ice. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the microbial community using the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The integrity of the extracted DNA was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while the DNA concentration and purity were quantified using a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

PCR amplification

The extracted genomic DNA served as the template for amplifying the V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene. PCR was performed using barcoded universal primers: 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806 R (5′- GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [21]. The 20 µL PCR reaction mixture contained 4 µL of 5 × TransStart FastPfu Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase and 10 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycling conditions on an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 system were as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, annealing at 55 °C for 30 seconds and extension at 72 °C for 30 seconds; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Library preparation and sequencing

The resulting PCR products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel, and the target bands were excised and purified using a DNA Gel Recovery Kit (PCR Clean-Up Kit, Yuhua, China). The concentration of the purified amplicons was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equimolar amounts of the purified amplicons from each sample were pooled to create the final sequencing library. The library was then sequenced on an Illumina Noveseq platform to generate paired-end reads.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

This study employed a standardized pipeline for processing raw sequencing data. Quality control was performed using fastp [22], paired-end reads were merged using FLASH [23] and high-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated via denoising, dereplication and chimera removal with DADA2 [24] within the QIIME2 environment [25]. The ASV table was subsequently rarefied to 20,000 sequences per sample for normalization. Taxonomic annotation of ASVs was assigned using the QIIME2 feature-classifier against the SILVA database. The functional potential of the microbial communities was predicted using PICRUSt2 [26], with gene mappings referenced to the KEGG database.

Alpha diversity was assessed using metrics including Observed ASVs, Chao1, ACE, Shannon and Simpson indices. The Kruskal‒Wallis H test was used to compare inter-group differences in α-diversity indices among the three risk groups at each time point. The Friedman test was applied to evaluate longitudinal temporal changes in α-diversity indices within each risk group across the five follow-up time points. Beta diversity was evaluated by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on weighted UniFrac distance matrices, and the statistical significance of clustering was tested using Adonis. To account for the compositional nature of microbial relative abundance data and the non-independence of repeated measurements, analysis of composition of microbiomes (ANCOM) was further performed for robust validation of differentially abundant genera, with significant taxa defined as those with a W statistic ≥ 0.7 × k (k = 3). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was independently applied at each time point to identify microbial biomarkers, with a logarithmic LDA score threshold of 4.0 and a p-value < 0.05. For microbial co-occurrence network analysis, Spearman's rank correlations were calculated for all genus-level taxa after removing unclassified taxa, with p-values corrected using the Benjamini‒Hochberg (BH) method. Co-occurrence networks were constructed based on the screened correlation thresholds and subsequently visualized using Cytoscape software.

All high-throughput sequencing data analyzes were conducted on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Statistical analysis and visualization of microbial data were performed using R and QIIME2. Depending on data distribution, group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal‒Wallis H test, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Result

Sequence information

A total of 48 preschool children were enrolled in this study at the end of the follow-up period. The PSC-MCAT assessment revealed that the differences in mean dmft among children with different caries risk levels were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Furthermore, pairwise comparisons between the low-, moderate- and high-risk groups all showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.01) (Table 1). The mean age at baseline for preschool children was 36–47 months. After using the Illumina Noveseq 2000, 9,867,704 effective sequences were acquired from 240 saliva samples, with an average of 51,394 sequences per sample. The average length of the sequences was 423 bp. Ninety-seven percent of the qualified sequences were clustered, and 2,731 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were obtained. Good's coverage of the generated ASVs reached 99.9%. In the rarefaction curve, each curve first rose sharply and then flattened out with an increase in the number of sequences, indicating that the sequencing quantity was sufficient to cover all species in the samples (Figure 1A). There was a high evenness of saliva microbial composition in the samples, as seen in the rank abundance curve (Figure 1B).

Alpha diversity analysis based on 16S rRNA sequencing

In this study, α-diversity indices were calculated based on the ASVs of the samples. The results showed that the coverage indices of all sample groups were >0.998, indicating that the identified 16S rRNA sequences represent the vast majority of bacterial sequences present in the plaque samples. Kruskal‒Wallis tests (Table 2) showed that Ace, Chao1, Sobs and Shannon indices differed significantly among the three risk groups at baseline (p < 0.05). At the 12th follow-up, Ace and Sobs indices differed significantly among the three groups (p < 0.05). No significant differences in α-diversity indices were detected among the three groups at the 6th, 18th and 24th follow-up time points (p > 0.05). Friedman tests (Table 3) showed no significant temporal changes in any α-diversity index within each risk group across all five time points (all p > 0.05). The high-risk group displayed higher species richness than the low- and moderate-risk groups at most time points.

Beta diversity analysis based on 16S rRNA sequencing

PCoA analysis at the genus level was performed based on weighted UniFrac distances to compare samples across different risk levels at the same follow-up time and across different follow-up times within the same risk group, as shown in Figure 2. At baseline and the 12th follow-up, the low-, moderate- and high-risk groups showed partial separation, with statistically significant differences between the groups (p < 0.05), as illustrated in Figure 2A and C. In contrast, at the 6th, 18th and 24th follow-ups, the samples from the three risk groups largely overlapped, indicating similar microbial community structures without clear separation (p > 0.05), as shown in Figure 2B, D and E. When comparing samples within the low-, moderate- and high-risk groups across different follow-up times, the samples from the five time points exhibited partial separation, with statistically significant differences between the groups (p < 0.05), as presented in Figure 2F, G and H.

The distribution of microbial community structures among different risk groups

Based on taxonomic classification, a total of 16 phyla, 28 classes, 68 orders, 130 families, 287 genera and 645 species were annotated from the 240 plaque samples using the SILVA database.

At the phylum level, the microbiota in 99% of the samples was predominantly composed of seven relatively abundant phyla, including: Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Fusobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Patescibacteria and Campylobacterota, as shown in Figure 3. At the genus level, the highly abundant genera comprised Streptococcus, Leptotrichia, Neisseria, Actinomyces, Corynebacterium, Haemophilus and Fusobacterium, among others, as presented in Figure 4.

Comparison of microbial community structures among different risk group

Differential abundance analysis at the genus level, conducted at baseline, 6th, 12th, 18th and 24th follow-up time points, revealed that the abundance of multiple genera (ranging from 11 to 29) differed with statistical significance (p < 0.05) among the low-, moderate- and high-risk groups at each time point. Pairwise comparisons highlighted consistent patterns: genera such as Lautropia, Arachnia and Cardiobacterium were found to be enriched in the low-risk group at multiple time points (primarily when compared to the high-risk group). Conversely, Prevotella was significantly enriched in the high-risk group (compared with the low-risk) at all five time points. Furthermore, Leptotrichia, Veillonella, Selenomonas, Corynebacterium and Segatella were also confirmed to be enriched in the high-risk group at multiple time points. These differences were typically statistically significant between the high- and low-risk groups, or between the high-risk group and both the low- and moderate-risk groups, whereas differences between the low- and moderate-risk groups were often not significant, as shown in Table 4.

At baseline, ANCOM analysis identified seven significantly differential genera (W values ranging from 254 to 278, all with Reject null hypothesis = TRUE), including Segatella, Arachnia, Stomatobaculum, Megasphaera, Prevotella, Candidatus Saccharimonas and Lancefieldella. Among these, Segatella exhibited the highest W value (W = 278), indicating the most pronounced inter-group differences. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of long-term, regular professional fluoride varnish application on the oral microbial ecology of children with different caries risk levels. The findings revealed that long-term fluoride intervention can modulate the alpha and beta diversity of the microbial community, suppress the enrichment of caries-progression-related genera (e.g. Leptotrichia, Veillonella, Prevotella) in high-risk populations and significantly disrupt core cariogenic metabolic pathways (e.g. glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and starch and sucrose metabolism). Furthermore, the study demonstrated that while fluoride did not significantly alter the overall microbial composition, it could regulate microecological homeostasis by reshaping the interaction network of plaque microbiota—specifically by enhancing antagonistic relationships among microbes and promoting synergistic interactions among beneficial bacteria. These results indicate that the caries-preventive effect of fluoride relies not only on its classic physico-chemical remineralization mechanism but may also involve a time-dependent ecological modulation mechanism that promotes a shift in the oral microbial community toward a more balanced and healthier state.

The human oral cavity harbors a diverse microbial consortium, with the core microbiome in health characterized by phyla such as Firmicutes (e.g. Streptococcus, Veillonellaceae, Granulicatella), Proteobacteria (e.g. Neisseria, Haemophilus), Actinobacteria (e.g. Corynebacterium, Rothia, Actinomyces), Bacteroidetes (e.g. Prevotella, Capnocytophaga, Porphyromonas) and Fusobacteria (e.g. Fusobacterium) [27]. Previous research has described the community characteristics of microbes associated with dental caries. Comparative studies between caries-active and healthy children have consistently identified that specific taxa within plaque or saliva—including Veillonella, Actinomyces, Granulicatella, Leptotrichia, Thiomonas and Prevotella—are closely correlated with caries development [7,8,28–30]. Beyond taxonomy, metabolic dysregulation is central to cariogenesis. The intake and metabolism of dietary sugars play a pivotal role, as carbohydrates are widely acknowledged as the primary etiological agent in caries initiation and progression [31,32]. Notably, research has demonstrated that several carbohydrate-related metabolic pathways, including amino and nucleotide sugar metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and starch and sucrose metabolism, are significantly elevated in caries-active groups compared to healthy controls [33].

The composition of the supragingival microbiome is a key variable in caries formation, and understanding how to modulate the oral micro-ecosystem is critical for caries prevention [34]. Among the numerous preventive strategies available, fluoride varnish application remains a cornerstone preventive measure. An extensive body of research confirms that fluoride's primary preventive mechanisms are physical, acting by reducing the solubility of enamel and promoting its remineralization [35,36]. However, whether fluoride exerts direct and sustained antimicrobial or ecological-modulating properties in vivo remains inconclusive. Addressing this gap is a primary objective of the current study.

Previous research indicated that 3 days after fluoride treatment, the species richness (ACE and Chao1 indices) of the dental plaque microbiota increased slightly, subsequently decreased and essentially returned to baseline levels by the end of the second week. Species diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) showed little fluctuation at different time points post-fluoride treatment (baseline, 3 days and 14 days). This suggests that fluoride causes a mild disturbance to the plaque microbial community structure, and its impact on species richness and diversity is only transient [12]. In the present study, richness indices (ACE, Chao1, sobs) and the diversity index (Shannon) showed statistically significant differences among the low-, moderate- and high-risk groups at baseline, indicating that pre-existing differences in community composition already existed. At the 18th and 24th follow-ups, the differences in alpha diversity among the different risk groups were no longer significant. This result implies that long-term fluoride varnish application may gradually normalize the oral flora structure, leading to a gradual convergence in alpha-diversity differences among risk groups.

This study used the weighted_UniFrac distance to assess beta diversity differences between microbial communities. Weighted_UniFrac is a quantitative measure of community beta diversity that detects changes in the abundance of each lineage present, as well as changes in which taxa are present [37]. We combined this with Adonis analysis to evaluate inter-group differences, thereby obtaining more reliable results. At baseline and the 12th follow-up, there was observable separation among the three sample groups, with statistically significant inter-group differences. However, at the 6th, 18th and 24th follow-ups, the samples from the three groups largely overlapped, indicating similar and indistinct microbial community structures. This demonstrates that fluoride application may have a regulatory effect on the dynamic changes of the microbiota, reducing the compositional differences among the different risk groups. Research has shown that the beta-diversity similarity index of the oral microbial community in children decreased significantly within 3 days after fluoride application, indicating a significant change in microbial structure; however, this change gradually reverted to near-baseline levels after 14 days. This finding suggested that the effect of fluoride on the oral microbial community is highly transient [12]. In contrast, our study suggests that the regulatory effect of fluoride on the oral microbial community may be time-dependent. When the duration of application is sufficient, fluoride can significantly influence the microbial community structure, exerting a potential regulatory role. This highlights the potential significance of long-term fluoride application in promoting oral microbial balance.

In our analysis of inter-species differences, genera closely related to caries progression were enriched in the high-risk group, including Leptotrichia, Veillonella, Prevotella, Corynebacterium, Selenomonas and Segatella. In addition to the commonly recognized caries-associated genera (Leptotrichia, Veillonella and Prevotella), our study also noted a significant enrichment of Selenomonas in the high-risk group, a finding consistent with previous research [38]. The results of this study revealed that the abundance of microbial markers in the high-risk group showed a suppressed growth trend over time, while remaining at a consistently low level in the low- and moderate-risk groups. Based on this phenomenon, we speculate that long-term, regular fluoride application may alter the microbial community structure, thereby effectively inhibiting the growth of cariogenic bacteria and playing a positive role in caries prevention.

The occurrence of dental caries is intimately linked to excessive dietary carbohydrate intake and the host microenvironment. Frequent consumption of dietary carbohydrates promotes the synthesis of large quantities of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) and the accumulation of acidic metabolic products, leading to the formation of a cariogenic biofilm [39–41]. During frequent exposure to fermentable carbohydrates, microorganisms become embedded in an EPS-rich biofilm matrix while continuously producing acid. These acids, physically protected by the biofilm, are not easily buffered by saliva, allowing localized low-pH zones in the plaque to continuously select for aciduric microorganisms [42,43]. When excessive and frequent acid production surpasses the buffering capacity, the oral microbiota undergoes dysbiosis, thereby disrupting the homeostatic mineral balance and leading to enamel demineralization [6].

In vitro experiments have confirmed that the key metabolic effect of fluoride lies in its targeted inhibition of glycolysis and energy metabolism pathways. At the molecular level, fluoride can interfere with metabolic activity via two routes: (1) by directly binding to the active sites of key glycolytic enzymes, such as enolase or (2) by binding to heme-containing peroxidases, thereby indirectly inhibiting the glycolytic process [44]. Further in vitro studies on fluoride's enzyme-inhibitory effects show that fluoride can inhibit the activity of enzymes such as phosphatases and pyrophosphatases by competitively binding with metal cofactors (e.g. Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺). Its inhibition of enzymes related to glycolysis and the citric acid cycle (e.g. enolase, succinate dehydrogenase) is particularly significant [17,45–47]. In our study, the citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation and starch/sucrose metabolism pathways were significantly inhibited in the oral microbiota of the high-risk group. This suggests that fluoride may limit the acid-producing capacity of cariogenic bacteria by suppressing core metabolic pathways and diminishing their energy supply.

Research indicates that fluoride's metabolic regulation is pH-dependent [48,49]. Bradshaw [48]found that fluoride significantly reduces acid production in low-pH environments while simultaneously enhancing the survival rate of acid-sensitive bacteria. This suggests that fluoride can stabilize the composition of the microbial community in low-pH environments. Hamilton and Bowden [49] further confirmed that the antimicrobial and anti-metabolic effects of fluoride are significantly enhanced in low-pH environments. This phenomenon aligns with the observations in our study: in the moderate- and high-risk groups (i.e. populations more prone to acidic microenvironments), the inhibitory effects of fluoride on cariogenic bacterial growth and sugar metabolism were more pronounced.

Oral micro-ecological homeostasis manifests as a self-regulating system formed through a dynamic balance of synergism and antagonism among the host and its commensal microbes [50]. The ‘homeostasis’ of the oral ecosystem is not static; it undergoes changes throughout an individual's life, influenced by factors such as delivery mode, feeding methods, hormonal changes and aging [51]. Once this microecology becomes imbalanced, it can lead to various oral diseases, including dental caries and periodontal disease. Based on the ‘ecological plaque hypothesis,’ the essence of caries is an ecological collapse resulting from an imbalance in the microbial community structure, rather than an infectious disease dominated by a single pathogen. The bacterial composition of dental plaque in healthy individuals is relatively stable, a stability maintained by a dynamic equilibrium between synergistic and antagonistic microbial interactions [52]. Hall et al. also noted that an individual's supragingival microbiome possesses both stable and variable components [53]. However, the long-term effects of fluoride intervention on the re-establishment of microbial homeostasis remain unclear, as most observation periods in existing studies are less than 3 months. By conducting long-term observation with fluoride application every 6 months, our study demonstrates for the first time that even without significantly altering microbial composition, fluoride can still regulate microecological homeostasis by modulating the interaction patterns of the community network.

In this study, the dental plaque microbial networks of children with different caries risks exhibited significant heterogeneity. At baseline (pre-fluoride intervention), the high-risk group's microbial network showed lower complexity, characterized by sparse nodal connections and a low proportion of negative correlations (antagonism). This suggests a weak capacity for competition and metabolic inhibition among its microbiota. Research using metagenomics has found that the oral microbial structure in caries-active individuals is simpler than in healthy individuals, with fewer antagonistic relationships, indicating that the mutual opposition within the plaque community network is not active [33,34]. Following fluoride intervention, as time progressed, the negative correlations within the high-risk group's network significantly increased. Simultaneously, synergistic interactions among commensal bacteria enriched in the low-risk group (e.g. Capnocytophaga, Rothia) were strengthened. This indicates that fluoride may regulate homeostasis via a dual mechanism: (1) enhancing antagonism to disrupt the metabolic cooperation of cariogenic bacteria and (2) promoting synergy among beneficial bacteria to rebuild a defensive network structure.

Animal experiments provide a mechanistic explanation for these phenomena: a 12-week intervention with fluoridated water selectively inhibited the abundance of acid-producing bacteria (e.g. Parabacteroides, Bacteroides) in the murine oral cavity and downregulated genes related to central carbon metabolism [11]. This metabolic inhibition may alter microbial interaction patterns: the reduced glycolytic activity of cariogenic bacteria weakens their EPS synthesis ability, disrupting the physical biofilm barrier. This allows salivary buffering to penetrate, shifting the microbiome from an ‘acid-dominant’ dysbiotic state toward an ‘antagonistic-synergistic balanced’ homeostatic state. It has been noted that more attention should be paid to the behavior of microorganisms within these communities, not just their taxonomic presence [54]. From an ecological perspective, the key is not merely the bacterial species involved in caries formation, but the specific roles these bacteria play during the disease process and their complex interactions. A deeper investigation into these interactions will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of caries pathogenesis, thereby providing new strategies for prevention and treatment.

According to the ‘ecological plaque hypothesis,’ the goal of caries prevention should focus on re-establishing a healthy, stable oral microecology. A deeper understanding of microbial interactions can inform strategies that actively promote oral health; effective caries prevention requires correcting the factors that lead to this ecological dysbiosis [55]. The results of this study indicate that regular fluoride varnish application (every 6 months), while capable of modulating the microbial interaction network, has a limited caries-control effect on high-risk populations. A recent 24‑month randomised trial on 3‑year‑old children with active caries also supports this view [56]. This may be related to a ‘dose-effect threshold’ for re-establishing homeostasis, suggesting that a higher frequency of fluoride intervention (e.g. every 3 months) may be necessary to maintain dual metabolic and ecological regulation. Therefore, future efforts will involve adopting more proactive methods to promote oral microecological balance in high-risk populations. This includes exploring behavioral interventions for pediatric groups, as well as developing and applying pharmacological and biological methods capable of regulating the oral microecology, thereby improving the oral microenvironment from multiple dimensions to reduce the risk of caries and other oral diseases.

This study possesses notable strengths in its innovative ecological perspective and comprehensive analytical approach. Utilizing a longitudinal design with five sampling timepoints over 24 months, it extends beyond the conventional focus on fluoride's remineralization effect, offering robust evidence for its time-dependent influence on oral microbial ecology-particularly through reshaping microbial interaction networks to restore ecological homeostasis. Moreover, the research integrates multidimensional analyzes of community structure, predicted metabolic function and co-occurrence networks, effectively linking the suppression of caries-associated taxa and metabolic pathways with key ecological mechanisms such as enhanced microbial antagonism and synergy. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the inference of metabolic pathways relied on 16S rRNA-based bioinformatic prediction rather than metagenomic, metatranscriptomic or metabolomic data. Thus, while functional potential can be inferred, specific functional genes cannot be identified nor can actual metabolic activity be quantified. Second, the study evaluated only one intervention frequency (every six months) and lacked a comparison group with more frequent applications, thereby limiting direct assessment of dose–response relationships.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that biannual fluoride varnish application modulates the supragingival plaque microbial community structure in children, reducing microbial disparities between different caries risk groups. Prior to intervention, the high-risk group exhibited a distinct microbial imbalance characterized by enrichment of cariogenic genera. The regulatory effect of fluoride appears to be mediated through the suppression of key carbohydrate metabolism pathways, which inhibits the growth of caries-associated bacteria and reshapes microbial interaction networks toward a more stable state. Collectively, these findings underscore that while routine fluoride application promotes oral microbial homeostasis, children at elevated caries risk may require more frequent or tailored interventions to achieve and maintain long-term ecological balance.
