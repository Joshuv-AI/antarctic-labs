Prevalence and pattern of cancers among patients attending Al-Hayat Hospital in Boorama, Somaliland
Authors: Abdirashid S Yusuf, Abdihakim Mohamed H Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is a growing public health concern in developing nations, yet data from many regions, including Somaliland, remains scarce. The absence of population-based cancer registries means that hospital-based studies are crucial for initial insights into the local cancer burden.ObjectiveThis study aims to determine the prevalence and pattern of various cancers among patients diagnosed at Al-Hayat Hospital in Boorama, Somaliland, to provide foundational data for the region.MethodsA descriptive, retrospective study was conducted using patient record books at Al-Hayat Hospital from January 2018 to December 2020. Data on age, sex, year of diagnosis, sample type for diagnosis, and the confirmed type of cancer were extracted and analyzed using descriptive statistics.ResultsThis retrospective study analyzed 130 cases of suspected cancer at Al-Hayat Hospital between January 2018 and December 2020. The findings indicate a higher prevalence among males, who constituted 64.6% of the patients, compared to females at 35.4%. The majority of cases were observed in middle-aged and older adults, with 42.3% of patients aged 41–60 and 36.9% aged 61–80. A significant increase in diagnosed cases was noted over the study period, with the number of cases surging from 18 in 2018 to 82 in 2020. Prostate cancer was the most frequently diagnosed malignancy, accounting for 26.15% of all cases. Other common cancers included esophageal cancer (11.54%), breast cancer (8.46%), and skin cancer (7.69%).ConclusionThe study concludes that cancer represents a significant and growing public health concern for patients attending Al-Hayat Hospital in Boorama, Somaliland. The most prevalent malignancies identified were prostate, esophageal, and breast cancers. These hospital-based findings, the first of their kind for this region, offer crucial baseline data in an area lacking a comprehensive cancer registry. Introduction

Globally, cancer represents one of the most significant and escalating public health challenges of our time. In 2020 alone, there were an estimated 19.3 million new cases and 10 million deaths [6]. Projections indicate this burden will continue to grow, potentially reaching 28.4 million new cases annually by 2040. The most substantial increases are expected in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), which often possess limited resources for cancer care and are disproportionately affected by this rising epidemic [3, 8].

Sub-Saharan Africa, in particular, is witnessing a surge in cancer incidence and is now a leading cause of premature death in the region [1]. In 2020, the region recorded approximately 801,392 new cases and 520,158 deaths. This rising incidence is attributed to several factors, including population growth, an aging demographic, and the adoption of Westernized lifestyles associated with economic development, such as smoking, unhealthy diets, and physical inactivity [6]. Across the continent, breast, cervical, and prostate cancers have emerged as the most dominant malignancies [1].

Despite these alarming trends, data on cancer incidence and patterns in many parts of Africa remain extremely limited. It is estimated that literature from Africa constitutes only about 1% of global cancer registry publications [5]. A significant impediment to mounting an effective public health response is the absence of comprehensive, population-based cancer registries. Consequently, hospital-based studies serve as the primary and vital first step in quantifying the problem, identifying common cancer types, and informing priorities for health policy and planning [1].

In Somaliland, as in much of the Horn of Africa, the true burden of cancer is largely unknown, as healthcare systems have historically focused on communicable diseases. While data remains sparse, research from neighboring Somalia has begunto outline the landscape. Studies in Mogadishu have identified esophageal and hepatocellular carcinoma as leading malignancies, potentially linked to local environmental risk factors and dietary habits, such as the consumption of khatand hot beverages [4, 7]. Furthermore, a recent study from Hargeisa, Somaliland, found that breast, esophageal, and prostate cancers were the most common diagnoses, with a concerning majority of patients presenting at advanced stages [2].

While this emerging evidence points to cancer as a major health concern, there is a complete lack of published data from the Awdal region of Somaliland. There is no national cancer registry, and systematic data collection has not yet been established, leaving our understanding of the regional cancer landscape fragmented and reliant on small-scale reports. Al-Hayat Hospital is a key.

healthcare provider in Boorama, the capital city of the Awdal region, serving a diverse patient population. Documenting the cases seen at this facility can provide a crucial, though preliminary, snapshot of the local cancer burden. Therefore, this study aims to fill this critical knowledge gap by providing the first documented analysis of cancer prevalence and patterns from a key hospital in Boorama, offering essential baseline data and a comparison with other regional findings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a descriptive, retrospective, cross-sectional study conducted at Al-Hayat Hospital in Boorama, Somaliland. The hospital provides a range of medical services to the population of the Awdal region. Data was collected from the hospital’s patient registration and pathology record books.

Data collection

Records of all patients with a histopathologically-confirmed primary malignant cancer diagnosed between January 2018 and December 2020 were reviewed. A structured data collection form was used to extract the following information: patient demographics (age at diagnosis, sex at diagnosis), year of diagnosis, and cancer information (pattern of confirmed cancer type and type of biopsy).

Ethical considerations

Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the administrative authorities of Al-Hayat Hospital. To ensure patient confidentiality, all personal identifiers were removed from the data collection form, and the data was anonymized before analysis.

Results

A total of 130 cases of suspected cancer were recorded at Al-Hayat Hospital during the 3-year period from January 2018 to December 2020.

Year distribution

Table 1 reveals a significant and accelerating upward trend in the frequency of events from 2018 to 2020. The occurrences grew moderately from 18 in 2018 to 30 in 2019, but then surged dramatically to 82 in 2020. This indicates that the most recent year, 2020, was by far the most significant, accounting for a substantial majority of the activity with 63.08% of the total observations, a figure that is more than double the combined frequencies of the two previous years.

Pattern of cancers

Table 2 illustrates the distribution of 130 cancer and biopsy cases, highlighting the ten most frequent diagnoses. Prostate cancer is the most predominant, constituting 26.15% of all cases with 34 occurrences. Esophageal cancer follows as the second most common, accounting for 11.54% of the cases. Breast cancer and skin cancer are also significant, representing 8.46% and 7.69% of the diagnoses, respectively. Thyroid cancer makes up 6.15% of the cases, while head and neck squamous cell carcinomas account for 4.62%. Cancer of the uterine cervix and soft tissue masses each represent 3.85% of the total. Lymphoma and benign lipoma are the least frequent among the top ten, at 2.31% and 1.54%, respectively. A notable 23.85% of cases are categorized as “Others,” which encompasses a variety of less common cancer and biopsy types.

Discussion

This study provides the first documented overview of the prevalence and pattern of cancers among patients at Al-Hayat Hospital in Boorama, Somaliland. The findings reveal a significant cancer burden, with a notable predominance of cases among male patients (64.6%) compared to females (35.4%). This gender distribution differs from some global trends where combined cancer rates can be similar between sexes, but it aligns with the high prevalence of male-specific malignancies identified in this specific study population.The most striking finding in our study is the high prevalence of prostate cancer, which accounted for 26.15% of all diagnosed cases. This makes it the leading malignancy in our cohort. This finding is consistent with broader trends in sub-Saharan Africa, where prostate cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among men in at least 40 countries [1]. The rising incidence of prostate cancer in the region may be attributed to increased life expectancy, as advanced age is a primary risk factor, and potentially improved detection methods at the hospital level. Our data indicates that a significant portion of patients fell into the 61–80 age range, correlating with the typical onset age for this malignancy. Esophageal cancer was the second most common malignancy, representing 11.54% of cases. This finding is significant and aligns with regional data from the Horn of Africa. Previous studies in Mogadishu have also identified esophageal cancer as a leading malignancy [4]. The high prevalence of esophageal cancer in this region has often been hypothesized to be linked to local dietary habits, such as the consumption of hot beverages or the use of khat, although specific risk factors in the Boorama context require further investigation. Breast cancer was the third most common cancer overall and the leading cancer among women in this study, accounting for 8.46% of the total cases. While breast cancer is globally the most common cancer [6] and the leading cancer in women in sub-Saharan Africa, its lower percentage relative to prostate cancer in our study may reflect the demographic composition of the patients attending this specific hospital or potential barriers to women seeking care. Nonetheless, it remains a critical health priority. Skin cancer also presented a notable burden (7.69%), suggesting a need for public health education regarding skin protection. The study also noted a sharp increase in diagnosed cases from 2018 (18 cases) to 2020 (82 cases). This surge may reflect an actual increase in cancer incidence, but it is also likely indicative of improved record-keeping, increased diagnostic capacity at Al-Hayat Hospital, or growing community awareness leading to more patients seeking medical advice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective, single-center study, the findings cannot be generalized to the entire population of Somaliland. The results are influenced by hospital attendance patterns, diagnostic capabilities, and the quality of record-keeping. The male predominance in the study might reflect health-seeking behaviors specific to the region rather than true population incidence. Furthermore, it is highly probable that many cancer cases in the community go undiagnosed or are not recorded, meaning the true burden of cancer is likely much higher than what is reported here.

Conclusion

Cancer represents an emerging and significant public health problem among patients attending Al-Hayat Hospital in Boorama, Somaliland. Prostate, esophageal, and breast cancers are the most common malignancies observed in this facility. These hospital-based findings provide critical baseline information in a region devoid of comprehensive cancer data. The data underscores the urgent need for further research, the establishment of a population-based registry, and the development of targeted cancer control strategies, particularly focused on men’s health and early detection of prostate and esophageal malignancies.
