Catecholamines Activate a Tumour Progression Program in Differentiated Tumour Hepatocytes
Authors: Elise Lelou, Claudine Rauch, Hélène Dubois‐Pot‐Schneider, Marjorie Dufresne, Abolfazl Zekri Yazdi, Maelle Guillout, Luis Cano, Lise Desquilles, Nicolas Nesseler, Marc Bourgeois, Claude Bendavid, Julie Massart, Orlando Musso, Anne Corlu, Caroline Aninat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe sympathetic nervous system is involved in the progression and aggressiveness of several cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, the specific effects of sympathetic innervation and catecholamines on tumour hepatocyte behaviour remain poorly understood. In this study, we aimed to investigate the role of sympathetic fibres and catecholamines in the progression of HCC.MethodsThe distribution of sympathetic nerves was mapped in 10 human HCC samples using multiplex immunofluorescence labeling. Adrenergic receptors (ADR) and enzymes involved in catecholamine degradation were assessed in three HCC (HepaRG, BC2, Huh‐7) and one hepatoblastoma (HepG2) cell lines. The impact of catecholamines on gene and protein expression and metabolic pathways was assessed in differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes, and the results were verified in primary human hepatocyte (PHH) cultures. The clinical relevance of the findings was evaluated in two transcriptomics datasets.ResultsAbundant and tortuous sympathetic fibres were detected in non‐tumour liver tissues adjacent to the HCC invasion front. Differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes expressed both α1‐ and β2‐ADR and metabolized catecholamines to normetanephrine, metanephrine, and vanillylmandelic acid. In these cells, catecholamine exposure activated inflammation, cell proliferation, and extracellular matrix remodelling pathways associated with retro‐differentiation and primed epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition. The catecholamine‐induced program was detected in aggressive, ECM/STEM HCC subclasses with poor patient prognosis.ConclusionsCatecholamines induce an aggressive phenotype in differentiated tumour hepatocytes localized at the invasive front, suggesting their involvement in cancer progression. Understanding the interplay between the sympathetic nervous system and HCC may offer novel therapeutic opportunities.Keywords: α1A‐ and β2‐adrenoreceptors, differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes, epinephrine, hepatocellular carcinoma, norepinephrine, sympathetic innervation

Introduction

The liver is periodically exposed to catecholamines (norepinephrine and epinephrine) through their local production by sympathetic fibres or their presence in the circulation following their release from the adrenal medulla. The liver is also recognized as one of the major organs involved in catecholamine metabolism, due to the activity of specific enzymes such as monoamine oxidases A and B (MAOA and B) and catecholamine‐O‐methyltransferases (COMT) [1]. Furthermore, the presence of adrenergic receptors (ADRs) on liver cells indicates the involvement of these compounds in various physiological hepatic processes [1]. Consequently, catecholamines have been recognized for decades for their role in liver glucose metabolism, particularly in stimulating gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis in hepatocytes [2, 3]. They were also identified in the 1980s as auxiliary mitogenic factors that enhance hepatocyte proliferation during liver regeneration [4]. Recent investigations highlight their implication in the initiation and progression of various liver pathologies, including steatohepatitis [5], hepatic neuropathy during diabetes [6], as well as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [7, 8].

The nervous system's influence on cancer progression is an emerging field of study with promising therapeutic avenues [9, 10]. Recent studies have demonstrated that sympathetic innervation contributes to the aggressiveness of various cancers [11, 12, 13]. In the context of HCC, these effects are likely attributable to the modulation of the local microenvironment by neurotransmitters, which impact non‐parenchymal cells such as Kupffer cells [8], stellate cells [14], as well as hepatocyte‐derived tumour cells [7, 15]. Their actions ranged from fostering chronic inflammation in the tumour environment [8] to enhancing epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [7], inhibiting anoikis, promoting HCC cell invasion [7] or favouring cell growth [15]. However, human studies investigating the presence of sympathetic innervation in HCCs remain limited, and most in vitro experiments have been conducted on hepatic tumour cell lines that are not well characterized in terms of ADR expression. In this study, we applied multiplex immunofluorescence imaging to assess the sympathetic innervation in ten human HCC samples and observed that sympathetic fibres are predominantly present in non‐tumour tissues adjacent to the tumour invasion front. To understand the impact of catecholamines released on the tumoral hepatocytes, we conducted additional in vitro experiments. Three human HCC‐derived (Huh‐7, BC2, and HepaRG) and one hepatoblastoma‐derived (HepG2) cell lines were employed. Among them, two can differentiate into hepatocyte‐like cells (BC2) [16, 17] or both hepatocyte‐ and biliary‐like cells (HepaRG) [18]. In each cell line, we compared the expression of ADR, MAOA, and COMT, and our results indicate that only HepaRG‐hepatocytes expressed both α1‐ and β2‐ADR. The morphology, transcriptomic program, and metabolic features of differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes matched those of well‐differentiated HCCs [19]. This cell line is also characterized by a high cellular plasticity potential, constituting a good model for studying mechanisms of cancer progression [19, 20]. We thus observed that, in differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes, catecholamines induced pleiotropic effects associated with regeneration and retro‐differentiation, including inflammation, proliferation, primed EMT, and increased expression of stem cell markers. Additionally, tumour HepaRG‐hepatocytes treated with epinephrine display a transcriptomic program matching proliferative, ECM/STEM HCC subclasses, reinforcing the notion that sympathetic innervation impacts HCC aggressiveness, enhancing tumour progression.

Materials and Methods

Patient Specimens

HCC samples used in this study were obtained from the Biological Resources Center at Rennes University Hospital (BB‐0033‐00056). The clinical and demographical data from 10 HCC patients are shown in Table S1. Sample collection was reported to the Ministry of Education and Research (No. DC‐2008‐338). The study protocol complied with French laws and regulations and was approved by INSERM's Institutional Review Board (number 01‐036) in the context of the National Network of Liver Biological Resource Centers. Research involving human participants was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Cell Culture

HepaRG cells were cultured as previously described [21]. Briefly, bipotent HepaRG progenitors can proliferate and differentiate toward hepatocyte‐like and biliary‐like cells [18]. When differentiated, HepaRG hepatocyte‐like cells (HepaRG‐hepatocytes) were selectively detached with trypsin half‐diluted in PBS (0.025%) at room temperature and seeded in 2% DMSO medium at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/cm2. HepaRG‐hepatocytes were then maintained in 2% DMSO for 5 days prior to treatment. This protocol aimed to enrich the culture of differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes. The HepG2, Huh‐7 and HBG‐BC2 (BC2) cell lines were cultured as previously described [19]. Primary human hepatocytes (PHH) from histologically normal livers were obtained from non‐tumour liver tissues in patients undergoing partial hepatectomy for resection of liver metastases of extra‐hepatic cancers at Rennes University Hospital. PHH cell preparation was provided by Biopredic (Rennes, France). They were seeded at 2.6 × 105 cells/cm2 and cultured in HepaRG medium without DMSO for 1 day, followed by the addition of 2% DMSO for two additional days. PHH were treated on day three in a medium without DMSO or FBS.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 software (Version 7.0; GraphPad, San Diego, CA). Normality was evidenced by a Shapiro test. Comparisons between treatments were assessed using parametric (Student t‐test, one‐way ANOVA) or non‐parametric (Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis) tests depending on normality. Tukey's or Dunnett's multiple comparisons post hoc tests were performed after one‐way ANOVA and Dunn's multiple comparisons post hoc test after Kruskal–Wallis test.

See Supporting Information for the detailed description of multiplex immunofluorescence, cell treatments, RNA extraction, transcriptomics, RT‐qPCR, protein extraction and western blotting, catecholamines and their metabolites assays by LC–MS/MS, BrdU incorporation, Hoechst, Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA), gene expression in HCC cohorts from Roessler's (GSE14520) and Désert's (GSE237977) datasets.

Results

Sympathetic Fibres Are Mainly Observed at the Tumour Invasion Front in Human HCCs

Tumour innervation by sympathetic fibres was analysed in 10 human HCC samples by immunofluorescence using Protein Gene Product 9.5 (PGP9.5), as a specific nerve marker, and Tyrosine Hydroxylase (TH), as a specific marker of sympathetic terminations. Innervation within the tumour was limited, while numerous tortuous fibres were observed in non‐tumour tissues adjacent to the tumour invasion front (Figures 1, S1, and S2). Our staining was validated at the hepatic hilum, where large sympathetic fibres can be observed (Figure S1, positive control).

Higher density of sympathetic fibres is observed in the non‐tumour tissues adjacent to the invasion front of hepatocellular carcinoma compared to the intratumoral tissue. Ten human hepatocellular carcinoma sections were analysed by multiplex immunofluorescence using DAPI (blue), PGP9.5 (red) and TH (yellow) as markers of nucleus, nerves and sympathetic terminations, respectively. The merged images were captured with NDP.View2 software and analysed with QuPath. Patient 6 was only analysed for the tumour and was excluded from the analysis of the invasion front, as no sections were available. Results are expressed as mean ± SD from n = 10 for tumours and n = 9 for the invasion front (***p < 0.001).

High Expression of ADRA1A and ADRB2 Is Observed in Non‐Tumour Samples Matching HCC Specimens and in Differentiated HCCs

Given that catecholamines can be secreted by sympathetic nerve endings, we investigated the expression of ADRA1A and ADRB2, the predominant ADRs expressed in the liver tissue, using HCC datasets. ADRA1A and ADRB2 expression was higher in non‐tumour tissues than in tumour tissues, as evidenced in 247 HCCs from Roessler's dataset (Figure 2A). Additionally, in 1133 HCCs from Désert's dataset, higher expression levels of these receptors were detected in the differentiated non‐proliferative HCC subclasses (PV, perivenous and PP, periportal) compared to the proliferative, poorly‐differentiated subclasses ECM (extracellular matrix) and STEM (Figure 2B). Furthermore, in the TCGA‐LIHC dataset, both ADRA1A and ADRB2 were positively correlated with genes involved in Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes related to fatty acid and cholesterol metabolism, consistent with differentiated hepatocytes (Tables S2 and S3). In contrast, ADRA1A expression was negatively correlated with GO biological processes related to RNA and protein synthesis, consistent with the observation that low ADRA1A expression is associated with proliferative and stem‐like HCC subclasses (Table S2). Additionally, high ADRA1A expression was correlated with better overall survival (Figure S3). Collectively, these findings suggest that catecholamines may exert a stronger impact on the tumour invasion front and on differentiated rather than on dedifferentiated tumour hepatocytes.

Differentiated HepaRG‐Hepatocytes Mainly Express the α1A ‐ and β2‐Adrenoceptors and Are Metabolically Competent to Metabolize Catecholamines

To gain further insight into the role of catecholamines on differentiated tumour hepatocytes, we investigated three HCC and one hepatoblastoma cell lines (HepaRG, Huh‐7, BC2, and HepG2). All the tested cell lines expressed α1A‐ADR, whereas β2‐ADR was only expressed by HepaRG‐hepatocytes (Figure 3A). Higher expression of α1A‐ADR and β2‐ADR monomers was observed in HepaRG‐hepatocytes compared to PHHs (Figure 3A). However, as shown in Figure S4, western blot analysis indicated the presence of hetero‐ or oligomers as well as isoforms of α1A‐ADR in PHHs, which were not observed in the tested cell lines.

To assess the ability of these cell lines to metabolize catecholamines, the main enzymes implicated in their degradation were also analysed. All the cell lines expressed both the MAOA and the COMT (Figure 3B). Furthermore, the main metabolites of catecholamines (normetanephrine, metanephrine and vanillylmandelic acid) were detected in the supernatants of HepaRG‐hepatocytes exposed to norepinephrine or epinephrine for 24 h (Table S4).

Among the tested cell lines, HepaRG‐hepatocytes proved to be the closest to PHHs. As differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes expressed both α1A‐ADR and β2‐ADR and as they are considered a suitable model for mimicking cellular plasticity in HCC, we decided to further our investigation primarily using this model.

Epinephrine Induces Pleiotropic Effects Related to Tumour Progression in HepaRG‐Hepatocytes

To gain insight into catecholamine effects in HepaRG‐hepatocytes, we performed a microarray global gene expression analysis of HepaRG‐hepatocytes treated with 1 μM of epinephrine for 3 h. We identified 1565 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 1028 upregulated and 537 downregulated (Table S5). The main biological process enrichment analysis revealed that the top enriched gene ontology terms were associated with cancer progression. Positive regulation of angiogenesis, migration, inflammatory response, EMT, and ECM organization, as well as proliferation, were found among the top 20 biological processes (Figure 4A and Table S6). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed these results with enrichment of inflammatory processes (Hallmark_IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALLING and Hallmark_Inflammatory_response) as well as of EMT, hypoxia and angiogenesis signatures (Figure 4B and Table S7). Analysis of the top KEGG term enrichment among the upregulated DEGs revealed that they were involved in cytokine, PI3K‐Akt or MAPKs signalling pathways, complement and coagulation cascades and ECM interaction. Conversely, downregulated DEGs were mostly involved in xenobiotic metabolisms by cytochromes P450 (Table S8). We then validated the modulation of several genes involved in these processes by RT‐qPCR in HepaRG‐hepatocytes and PHHs (Figure 5). Epinephrine had similar effects in PHHs as in HepaRG‐hepatocytes on genes involved in EMT and angiogenesis (Figure 5A–D). However, epinephrine did not affect PHHs on ECM remodelling and inflammation gene expression (Figure 5E–H). Similar results were obtained for norepinephrine (Figure S5). Among the other cell lines, epinephrine induced a lower response than that observed in HepaRG‐hepatocytes in the BC2 cell line (Figure S6A–C) but was without effect on HepG2 and Huh‐7 cell lines (Figure S6D,E).

Induction of IL‐6 Is Mainly α1‐ADR and NF‐κB‐Dependent

Inflammation is a key trigger of hepatocyte retro‐differentiation, EMT and tumour progression. As catecholamines induce an inflammatory program in HepaRG‐hepatocytes (Table S5) we focused our study on interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and the STAT3 pathway, a well‐known pathway activated by this cytokine, which corresponds to one of the most significant signatures found with GSEA. Notably, IL6 was not induced in non‐tumour PHHs, nor in poorly differentiated liver cancer cells (HepG2, or Huh‐7) (Figures 5H and S6), suggesting that this phenomenon is linked to the transformed characteristics of differentiated HCC‐cells, such as HepaRG‐hepatocytes or BC2 cells. In HepaRG‐hepatocytes, IL6 mRNA transcription was induced rapidly, and IL‐6 protein production increased after 1 h following epinephrine exposure (Figure 6A). Both prazosin, a potent α1‐ADR antagonist, and ICI‐118551, a selective β2‐ADR antagonist, decreased the IL‐6 production (Figure 6B). However, the 8‐Br‐cAMP, a cAMP analog activating the main signalling pathway of β2‐ADR, did not affect IL‐6 production, suggesting that the non‐canonical signalling pathway is involved in this production after the β2‐ADR activation (Figure 6C). A 30‐min exposure to epinephrine induced p65 activation (Figure 6D) and SC‐514, an IKKβ inhibitor, prevented the IL‐6 production, indicating a NF‐κB‐dependent mechanism (Figure 6E). The production of IL‐6 was rapidly followed by the phosphorylation of STAT3 (Figure 6F,G) and this phosphorylation was mainly α1‐ADR‐dependent (Figure 6H). Thus, epinephrine, acting on α1A‐ADR, activated NF‐κB leading to IL‐6 production. As a consequence, IL‐6, acting in an autocrine manner, signals through its receptor, leading to STAT3 phosphorylation (Figure 6I).

Epinephrine Induces the Proliferation of HepaRG‐Hepatocytes

As IL‐6 is one of the major cytokines priming hepatocyte regeneration, preparing quiescent hepatocytes (G0 phase) to re‐enter the cell cycle (G1 phase), making them responsive to mitogens such as HGF to proliferate [22], we performed a BrdU incorporation assay after epinephrine treatment. Epinephrine induced DNA synthesis in HepaRG‐hepatocytes in an α1‐ADR‐dependent mechanism (Figure 7A,B). Accordingly, forskolin, an adenylate cyclase activator mimicking the β2‐ADR canonical pathway, could not induce cell proliferation. Interestingly, ERK1/2‐MAPK and c‐Jun, two markers of the early G1 phase, were rapidly activated by epinephrine (Figure 7C,D). Our transcriptomic data indicated that epinephrine induced several factors that favour hepatocyte proliferation, including HGF. This increase was also α1‐ADR‐, NF‐κB‐ and JAK‐dependent, since prazosin, SC‐514 and ruxolitinib, respectively, inhibited HGF expression (Figure 7E–G). In consistency with the above results, HGF induction after epinephrine treatment was not observed in PHH (Figure 7H). Lastly, CDK1, coding for a cyclin‐dependent kinase involved in S and G2/M phases of the hepatocyte cell cycle [23], was increased 24 h after epinephrine treatment (Figure 7I). Our results indicate that epinephrine promotes DNA synthesis after cell cycle entry and induces HepaRG‐hepatocytes proliferation via an α1‐ADR‐dependent mechanism. In addition, epinephrine treatment did not affect BC2, Huh‐7 and HepG2 cell line proliferation (Figure S7).

The Transcriptome of Epinephrine‐Treated HepaRG‐Hepatocytes Matches Aggressive and Poorly‐Differentiated HCCs With Bad Outcomes

IL‐6 has also been described as implicated in the retro‐differentiation of HepaRG cells [20]. As expected, a 24 h treatment with epinephrine led to a decrease in several differentiated hepatocyte markers such as ALB, ALDOB and CYP3A4 (Figure 8A). As already described for IL‐6 [20], this loss of hepatocyte‐specific markers is associated with increased EMT markers expression (SNAI1, TGFB3) and loss of the epithelial marker CDH1 expression (Figure 8B). Additionally, ECM collagens, glycoproteins, ECM receptors such as integrins and stemness markers (CD44 and SOX9) were enhanced after epinephrine treatment (Figure 8C). However, chronic treatment of HepaRG‐hepatocytes with epinephrine or norepinephrine did not result in the emergence of a mesenchymal phenotype (Figure S8).

To evaluate whether epinephrine‐treated HepaRG‐hepatocytes could be close to proliferative or stemness HCC subclasses, we compared their transcriptomic profile (upregulated DEGs (FC > 1.2)) with the Cairo liver development signature [24] and with signatures of HCC subclasses identified by Désert et al. (PV, perivenous; PP, periportal; ECM, extracellular matrix; STEM) [25] or Hoshida (S1, S2, S3) [26]. Supervised GSEA showed an enrichment in the up‐regulated genes in CAIRO_LIVER_DEVELOPMENT, as well as in DESERT_STEM_HEPATOPATOCELLULAR_CARCINOMA subclass and in S1 HOSHIDA_LIVER_CANCER_SUBCLASS signatures that confirmed the dedifferentiation of HepaRG‐hepatocytes in the presence of epinephrine (Figure 8D).

Discussion

Fine sympathetic fibres from the portal tracts penetrate the human liver lobule and reach the Disse space [5]. While recent studies have observed their degradation during the late state of non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease and attributed this phenomenon mainly to inflammation and severe steatosis [27], limited research has thoroughly characterized sympathetic innervation during HCC progression. Using a multiplex immunofluorescence labeling technique, we examined the distribution of sympathetic nerves in 10 human HCC samples. Our observations revealed sparse sympathetic fibres within the tumour, whereas a more noticeable presence was observed in non‐tumour tissues adjacent to the tumour invasion front. Chronic inflammation plays an important role in the development of HCC and could explain the low nerve fibre density within the tumour [28]. The difference in the ECM matrix composition between peritumoral and tumoral tissue could also be implicated, notably through modification in matrix stiffness that may facilitate or prevent axon guidance. However, the absence of nerves within the tumour does not necessarily indicate a lack of catecholamines in the tumour microenvironment. Epinephrine in the circulation can reach the tumour, and activated stellate cells have been reported to produce norepinephrine [29, 30].

Catecholamines interact with adrenergic receptors. Analysis of the expression of the main adrenergic receptors (ADRA1A and ADRB2) in hepatocytes reveals that they are expressed at a higher level in differentiated HCCs with a preserved mature hepatocyte phenotype. Altogether, these results suggest that catecholamines may mainly act on differentiated tumour hepatocytes. The HepaRG cell line shares characteristics with hepatic bipotent cells [31] and, once differentiated into hepatocyte‐like (HepaRG‐hepatocyte) or biliary‐like cells, can transdifferentiate [18]. Thanks to this plasticity, this cell line is recognized as a good model able to mimic either differentiated or stem‐like subclasses of HCC as well as the transition from one of these states to the other [19]. Additionally, after differentiation, specific trypsinization allows us to enrich the culture with HepaRG‐hepatocytes expressing both α1A‐ and β2‐ADR, like normal human hepatocytes, unlike HepG2, Huh‐7 and BC2 cell lines. The above evidence, combined with the fact that they can metabolize catecholamines to their physiological metabolites, as we showed by LC–MS/MS, led us to conduct experiments using HepaRG‐hepatocytes as a model of well‐differentiated HCC cells.

Catecholamines exert pleiotropic effects on these cells, including several that may be related to their previously described role as auxiliary mitogens during liver regeneration [32]. Abnormal liver regeneration is a process that occurs during hepatocarcinogenesis due to chronic inflammation and can lead to fibrosis. In humans, IL‐6 has been identified as a primary priming cytokine contributing to this phenomenon. Its production by Kupffer cells and hepatocytes in response to inflammatory stimuli prepares quiescent hepatocytes to exit the G0 phase of the cell cycle and to re‐enter the G1 phase, thanks to mitogens such as HGF and EGF [22]. In HepaRG‐hepatocytes, epinephrine and norepinephrine induce the production of IL‐6 and several mitogenic factors, such as HGF, HB‐EGF, and AREG. We showed that the production of IL‐6 by differentiated tumour hepatocytes appears to be primarily α1A‐ADR‐dependent and may be implicated in the proliferative effect of catecholamines on HepaRG‐hepatocytes. A low level of IL‐6 production was also produced by the differentiated BC2 cell line but was not observed in Huh‐7 or HepG2 cells despite the expression of this receptor in these cell lines. Moreover, epinephrine treatment did not induce proliferation of these cell lines. Notably, catecholamines have no effects on IL‐6 or HGF induction in PHH. This difference may result from the presence of various isoforms and/or oligomeric forms of α1A‐ADR in human hepatocytes that were not observed in HepaRG‐hepatocytes, potentially leading to distinct intracellular signalling pathways [33]. Additionally, the transformed state of differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes and/or their ability to transdifferentiate may contribute to this production.

Catecholamine‐induced inflammation could also be involved in a retro‐differentiation process. It is worth noting that IL‐6 has been shown to contribute to the retro‐differentiation of HepaRG‐hepatocytes into stem/progenitor cells [20]. This retro‐differentiation is characterized by an early activation of NF‐kB that induces IL‐6 secretion which in turn decreases the expression of differentiation markers (ADOLB and CYP3A4) and increases the expression of stem cell markers (SOX9, CD44) as observed following epinephrine treatment.

Our transcriptomic analysis also reveals that both angiogenesis (upregulation of VEGFA and ANGLPT4) and EMC remodelling (induction of MMP1, MMP3 and TIMP1) processes are induced by epinephrine in the HepaRG‐hepatocytes. Consequently, prolonged exposure to catecholamines could favour fibrosis, one of the hallmarks of HCC aggressiveness. This phenomenon may be exacerbated by the previously described action of norepinephrine on stellate cells [29]. Since the association of fibrosis and inflammation has been shown to contribute to aggressiveness and acquisition of stemness features in HCC, it may explain why epinephrine‐treated HepaRG‐hepatocytes align with the Hoshida_LIVER_CANCER_SUBLASS_S1 or the DESERT_STEM_CELL_HCC subclass, both of which are characterized by their aggressiveness [25]. Thus, the gene expression profile of differentiated HepaRG‐hepatocytes treated with epinephrine matched aggressive, proliferative/STEM HCC subclasses with poor prognosis.

Overall, this study sheds light on the role of catecholamines in the tumour progression by directly acting on differentiated tumour‐hepatocytes. As sympathetic nerves are more abundant in the tumour margin and ADRA1A and ADRB2 are more highly expressed in adjacent non‐tumour tissues, we hypothesize that catecholamines primarily act at the invasive front, where differentiated tumour cells are more exposed to sympathetic catecholamine release and where these signals may promote tumour progression and growth, or influence cancer cell dedifferentiation. Consistently, a lower risk of developing HCC on cirrhosis is observed in patients treated with the non‐selective β‐antagonist propranolol, and carvedilol, a β2 and α1‐ADR antagonist, has also been described to have beneficial effects in HCC [34, 35]. These results open the way for new adjuvant therapies in HCC.
