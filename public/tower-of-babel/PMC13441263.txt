Maternal Risk Factors for Omphalocele
Authors: Anna Fogelström, Taqiyah Chowdhury, Eva Hagel, Anna Löf Granström, Carmen Mesas Burgos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOmphalocele is a rare congenital abdominal wall defect characterized by the herniation of abdominal organs through the umbilical ring, covered by a protecting sac. The etiology is not fully understood, and little is known about the role of maternal factors. The aim of this study was to investigate the role of maternal risk factors in the development of omphalocele.MethodsA population-based case-control study was conducted using data from Swedish national registries between 1997 and 2016. The study included 207 mothers of children born with omphalocele (cases) and 2,070 controls, matched on the children’s sex and birth year. Conditional logistic regression analysis was used to estimate odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsMaternal obesity (adjusted OR: 1.82, 95% CI: 1.14–2.88) and IVF conception (adjusted OR: 4.49, 95% CI: 1.76–11.4) were associated with an increased risk of omphalocele. Multiparity was associated to decreased risk (adjusted OR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.25–0.77) as 58% of the omphalocele cases were firstborn.ConclusionsMaternal obesity and IVF conception are factors associated with an increased risk of omphalocele, whereas multiparity is linked to decreased risk.

Introduction

Although rare, with a reported birth prevalence of 1–3.8 per 10,000 pregnancies, omphalocele is still one of the most common congenital abdominal wall defects [1–6]. Omphalocele is characterized by herniation of the midgut and other abdominal organs through the umbilical ring into the cord, resulting from a defect in the closure of the ventral body wall during fetal development [7, 8]. The size of omphalocele varies, from small defects involving only intestinal loops to giant omphaloceles (GO), which may include herniation of additional abdominal organs such as the liver, stomach, spleen, bladder, and gonads [1, 9, 10]. The protruded organs are covered by a sac composed of peritoneum, Wharton’s jelly, and amnion that protects the organs from exposure to amniotic fluid and the external environments [2, 7].

Omphalocele occurs due to a failure in normal embryonic development of the abdominal wall and midgut return. Around the 6th week of gestation, the midgut undergoes rapid growth and temporarily physiologically herniates into the umbilical cord. Normally, the intestines return to the abdominal cavity by week 10–12 [1, 7, 8]. However, failure of the gut content to rotate and return to the abdominal cavity results in an omphalocele [1, 11] with malrotation and mispositioning of the intestine [11]. Although the precise etiology remains unclear, it is assumed to involve disruptions in embryonic development during this critical period of embryogenesis or potentially even earlier in development [7, 8].

Omphalocele is frequently associated with additional structural anomalies and genetic disorders, significantly increasing morbidity and mortality [9, 12]. The reported prevalence of associated malformations varies across studies, ranging from 23% to 78% [3, 5, 6]. Anomalies most commonly involve the cardiovascular system, genitourinary tract, central nervous system, and the gastrointestinal tract, but anomalies involving the musculoskeletal system and chromosomal anomalies are also frequent [3, 5, 9, 13]. Common genetic associations include trisomy 13, 18, and 21 and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome [2, 5, 6, 14].

It is well established that genetic variation and chromosomal anomalies can play a significant role in the development of omphalocele [7, 13]. In addition, several authors have explored the potential role of environmental factors such as maternal age, smoking, socioeconomic status, body mass index (BMI), etc., as possible contributors [12–14]. Maternal age, with both very young and advanced maternal age, as well as maternal obesity, has consistently been associated with an increased risk/prevalence of omphalocele [6, 13]. However, the precise impact of these factors remains insufficiently understood and not fully characterized. It is believed that omphalocele may arise from a complex interaction between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures, where the timing of these factors during early pregnancy, when the abdominal wall forms, may play a significant role [14, 15]. To our knowledge, no population-based study has explored the association between maternal factors and omphalocele within a Swedish context. The aim of this study was to investigate the maternal risk factors for omphalocele in a nationwide, population-based case-control study conducted in Sweden.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a population-based, register-based case-control study utilizing Swedish register data. The study population consisted of mothers of children born with omphalocele, and control participants were mothers of children without the condition. The outcome of omphalocele was identified using the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system, specifically the ICD-10 code Q79.2 (omphalocele/exomphalos), recorded in the National Patient Register (NPR) or the Medical Birth Register (MBR) between January 1, 1997, and December 31, 2016. Ten control children per case, matched on the child’s year of birth and sex, were randomly selected from the Population Register along with their mothers. These mothers constitute the control group. All identified cases of omphalocele were first ascertained [2], after which maternal exposure factors were analyzed and compared with those of corresponding controls to assess potential associations. To accurately link omphalocele outcomes with maternal exposures across the previously mentioned registers, the unique 10-digit personal identification number assigned to all Swedish residents was used to identify cases, control children and their mothers.

Data Source

Data were obtained from NPR, the MBR, the Register of Surveillance of Congenital Malformation and Chromosomal Abnormalities, the National Prescribed Drug Register and the Swedish Population Register, all with Swedish nationwide coverage. The NPR covers inpatient care since 1987 and specialist outpatient care since 2001 [16, 17]. The register demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy, with a median positive predictive value of 84% for diagnoses and 97% for surgical procedures. Missing data are generally low [17]. The MBR includes all pregnancies resulting in delivery since 1973 [18]. Missing records in the MBR account for only 0.5–3.9% of cases, and the risk of incomplete patient identification across linked registers is minimal, although prenatal testing data are not consistently available [19]. The Register of Surveillance of Congenital Malformations and Chromosomal Abnormalities provided data on reported malformations. The register covers both live birth and induced abortions due to fetal malformations, with abortion data available since 1999 [20]. The National Prescribed Drug Register provided information on maternal medication from 2005 onward [21]. The Population Register holds demographic data on everyone registered to live in Sweden [22].

Exposure and Covariates

Exposure data obtained from the MBR included maternal age, tobacco smoking, parity, BMI, in vitro fertilization (IVF), preeclampsia, urinary tract infection and pregestational diabetes. Data on preeclampsia were also obtained from the NPR using ICD codes O13, O14, 642D-642F (ICD-9) and O13, O14, O15 (ICD-10). Exposure to antidepressant and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) medication prior to delivery was defined as at least one dispensation of the prescribed medication within 2 years prior to pregnancy. The dispensation data were obtained from the National Prescribed Drug Register and identified through the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system codes (ACT codes) N06A and N06B.

To facilitate analysis, exposure data were further categorized. Maternal age was divided into five groups: <20, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34 and >35 years. Data on maternal tobacco smoking was categorized as yes or no, based on data collected at registration with maternal care and during the second trimester. Parity was segmented into three categories: 1, 2 and 3+ children. Data on maternal BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms (kg) divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2), was classified into four categories: under 18 (underweight), 18–24 (normal weight), 25–29 (overweight) and 30 or above (obese).

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges. The possible association between maternal exposure and the risk of fetal omphalocele was assessed using conditional logistic regression analysis. The results were presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Adjustments for potential confounders included maternal age (continuous), BMI (categorical), parity (categorical), and IVF (yes/no), which were selected based on their potential confounding effect and statistical significance in the unadjusted conditional logistic regression model. A p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Participants with missing data for a given covariate were excluded only from the corresponding statistical analysis but were presented in the descriptive tables as a separate category. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), and conditional logistic regression was performed using the survival R package (version 3.6–4) by a professional statistician.

Results

A total of 207 omphalocele cases and 2070 matched controls were identified from the Swedish national registries between 1997 and 2016. Parity appeared as a significant factor, with omphalocele occurring more frequently among firstborns (58%) and progressively lower risk in subsequent births (p ≤ 0.006). Discussion

Key Results

This population-based study examined maternal factors associated with omphalocele, using data from Swedish national registries. The study found that nulliparity, obesity (BMI >30), and conception through IVF were associated with an increased risk of neonates diagnosed with omphalocele, whereas multiparity was linked to a decreased risk.

Interpretation and Generalizability

Parity was significantly associated with the risk of omphalocele, with the highest rates observed among firstborns and a progressively lower risk in later births, even after adjusting for potential confounders (maternal age, BMI, and IVF). Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings: some identified increased risk among both nulliparous and multiparous women [23], whereas others found higher rates only among multiparous women [24]. These discrepancies may partly reflect methodological differences, including variation in how parity categories were defined and in the choice of reference group. One study did not have a subgroup of nulliparous women [24], and another study used primiparous women as the reference group in the analysis for OR [23], while the present study used nulliparous women. Moreover, factors such as previous fetal loss and fertility treatment, which influence not only parity but also the risk of birth defects, may also contribute to the observed pattern. Parity does not necessarily reflect a complete pregnancy history as previous miscarriages or terminations are not considered. This suggests that the association observed may not be attributable to parity itself but could instead reflect underlying fertility-related factors.

As expected and consistent with previous reports, maternal obesity was significantly associated with an increased risk of having a child with omphalocele [25, 26]. It can be hypothesized that women with high BMI experience fertility issues and, thus, more often undergo IVF treatment, potentially influencing the risk of omphalocele in infants. However, the association remained significant even after adjusting for potential confounders, including IVF. This finding supports earlier speculations and findings suggesting that metabolic or inflammatory pathways linked to obesity may adversely affect fetal development [27, 28].

The observed association between IVF conception and an increased risk of omphalocele aligns with previous findings from a Swedish population-based study, which examined the risk of various congenital malformations among infants conceived through IVF [29]. The authors discussed that the observed association may be influenced by characteristics commonly seen among women undergoing IVF, such as age, parity and prolonged involuntary childlessness, but also acknowledged the possibility of random variation [29]. In the current study, however, the observed association remained significant after adjustment for maternal age, BMI and parity. The tendency of higher age among mothers conceiving through IVF cannot account for the observed association as no significant statistical association was found between maternal age and omphalocele. This could suggest the adjusted variables reflect an underlying latent factor related to fertility challenges or difficulties conceiving, which cannot be directly measured or controlled for but still influence the risk of an infant being born with omphalocele. There is a possibility of other factors related to the IVF process such as epigenetic changes, embryo handling or underlying subfertility having an influence on this observed association.

To our knowledge, limited previous studies have explored the relationship between the development of preeclampsia and the presence of omphalocele. Bánhidy et al. [30] found no association between congenital abnormalities in offspring and maternal preeclampsia. Omphalocele was investigated but not as a separate entity. However, in the current study, a statistically significant association between the development of preeclampsia and pregnancies with omphalocele was observed. This may simply reflect random variation or could possibly be explained by the influence of other factors, e.g., parity and BMI, as the association was not adjusted for potential confounders. Interestingly, Romanelli et al. [31] proposed that fetal factors may contribute to the pathogenesis of preeclampsia, rather than maternal predisposition alone. Their hypothesis was based on the identification of a gene mutation and its potential role in the development of preeclampsia or HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet counts) among women who gave birth to infants with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, a condition often associated with omphalocele.

At the bottom of our table, we present data examining maternal use of antidepressants and ADHD medication as potential risk factors for omphalocele in the offspring. In our material, only a small number of expectant mothers had used these medications, which unfortunately limits the strength of the analysis, and no significant differences between cases and controls were found. However, the investigation remains relevant as both antidepressants and ADHD medications are widely used in society, and the use of ADHD medications is currently increasing rapidly among both children and young adults. Several studies have suggested that antidepressants, ADHD medications, and psychotropic medications may play a potential role in the development of congenital malformations, such as omphalocele [32–34]. Others found no evidence of an increased risk, although the authors highlight that more data are required to draw firm conclusions [35, 36]. Furthermore, many emphasize that the benefits of maintaining treatment during pregnancy may outweigh potential risks for both the mother and the child.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first to investigate maternal risk factors for omphalocele in a Swedish population. By using population-based, high-quality and retroprospectively collected data from Swedish national registers, we ensured complete coverage and minimized selection and ascertainment bias, while enabling the inclusion of a large cohort in the omphalocele context. These registers are well-validated and allow for accurate linkage through the unique personal identification number assigned to all Swedish residents, enabling accurate data collection across multiple registers.

The study has limitations related to missing data, most notably for BMI and tobacco exposure, with tobacco reporting particularly incomplete in the second trimester, with 49.8% among cases and 24.5% among controls. Although the national registers are considered valuable and generally reliable, with systematic loss being unlikely, the possibility of misclassification or missing information influencing the associations cannot be excluded. The extent of missing data in both groups may have reduced statistical power and limited the ability to detect associations. Another limitation concerns medication exposure data since the National Prescribed Drug Register includes data only from 2005 onward. Consequently, prior cases were excluded from medication-specific analyses, reducing the sample size. Exposure was based on dispensed prescription rather than confirmed intake, which could potentially introduce misclassification and lead to potential misinterpretation of associations.

GO is sometimes regarded as a distinct clinical entity facing other specific challenges, including pulmonary hypoplasia, pulmonary hypertension, and increased surgical complexity, then smaller omphaloceles [37]. However, none of the national registers used in this study contain information on the size or contents of the defect. Furthermore, as access to individual medical records was not available, it was not possible to identify the subgroup of GO within the cohort or to evaluate maternal risk factors specifically associated with this condition. A globally accepted definition of GO is still lacking, and consequently, study populations classified as GO vary across the literature, complicating comparisons between studies [38]. Establishing a clear and widely accepted definition would represent an important step forward. Such a consensus could also enable the introduction of a specific ICD subcode for GO in the future, thereby facilitating the identification of this subgroup in register-based studies.

A major limitation of the present study is the lack of data on pregnancies affected by omphalocele that did not result in a live birth, whether due to elective termination or spontaneous pregnancy loss. Since 1999, anonymized data on fetuses terminated due to fetal malformations or chromosomal abnormalities have been recorded in the Swedish National Register of Congenital Anomalies [20]. However, this register is known to have incomplete and partly unknown coverage regarding fetuses following induced terminations for fetal anomalies. Furthermore, because the register is anonymized and does not contain the parents’ identification number, it cannot be cross-linked with other national health registers. As a result, we lack access to the same level of detailed information for these pregnancies as for live-born children, both regarding maternal characteristics and fetal variables. Consequently, exposure variables could not be analyzed in a comparable manner for pregnancies that ended prior to live birth. Although it is possible that the coverage and completeness of the register have improved over time since its establishment in 1999, the data will remain anonymized, and analyses can only be performed at a group level. Addressing antenatal exposures and outcomes across all omphalocele pregnancies nonetheless represents an important, but methodologically challenging, area for future research.

Another less explored research area is that of environmental exposures. While the distribution of omphalocele across the four pediatric surgical centers in Sweden does not differ substantially, this fact does not provide information on the families’ place of residence, such as whether they live in rural or urban environments, with potential differences in air pollution exposure and lifestyle factors. Nor does it capture socioeconomic aspects, which have been of special interest and examined in previous studies. Colquitt and Burjonrappa [39] recently reported that mothers of children with omphalocele had a low median household income. Chircor et al. [40] found that 81% of the mothers had no personal income as a measure of socioeconomic status. Moreover, approximately 50% were single mothers, and parity emerged as an even more pronounced risk factor than in the present study, with 87.5% of mothers expecting a child with omphalocele being nulliparous [40]. Given that maternal age at first pregnancy is strongly influenced by socioeconomic conditions and that socioeconomic status in turn is affected by age at childbirth, these factors are closely interrelated and may act as confounders for one another. In the present study, maternal age was not significantly associated with the risk of having a child with omphalocele. This makes it particularly relevant to consider additional indicators of socioeconomic status. Incorporating data on maternal level of education and type of employment at the time of delivery could provide valuable complementary information. This and geographic differences could be feasible for future assessments and would allow for a more nuanced understanding of the potential role of socioeconomic and environmental factors in the etiology of omphalocele.

Conclusions

This population-based study indicates that maternal obesity and IVF conception are associated with an increased risk of having a child with omphalocele. Multiparity is associated with a decreased risk and mothers of children born with omphalocele are more likely to be primiparous.

Statement of Ethics

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This human study was approved by the Regional Ethical Committee in Stockholm, Swedish Ethical Review Authority – Approval No.: Dnr 2016/2265-31/1. Written informed consent from participants was not required in accordance with local or national guidelines (Health Data Register Act [1998:543] and associated regulation [2001:707]).
