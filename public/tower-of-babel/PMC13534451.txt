Study synergistic effect of Ti addition on microstructure and electrochemical performance of cost-effective Co-free AlCrFeNi high-entropy alloy with dual-phase architecture
Authors: Majid Naseri, Omid Imantalab, Amir Farrokhi, Ali Shanaghi, Tushar Sonar, Artem Okulov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herein, the effect of Ti addition on the microstructure and electrochemical behavior of cost-effective Co-free AlCrFe2Ni2 high-entropy alloy in a 0.5 M H2SO4 solution was investigated. Upon the introduction of Ti into the AlCrFe2Ni2 alloy, the crystal structure transitioned from face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC), and ordered body-centered cubic (B2) phases to a predominantly BCC/B2 structure in the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy. Furthermore, Ti addition increased the volume fraction of the interdendritic regions, and Ti-rich intermetallic regions were formed in the interdendritic regions, resulting in significant grain refinement. In the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy, this unique structure mitigates the negative influences of the dual-phase architecture and element segregation on corrosion performance, thereby promoting the formation of a more stable and thicker passive oxide film. These findings demonstrate that introducing Ti, as an excellent corrosion-resistant element, was an effective strategy for significantly improving the electrochemical behavior of cost-effective Co-free AlCrFeNi high-entropy alloys.

Introduction

Materials science is entering a new and exciting period, with challenges related not only to the never-ending demand for superior structural materials, but also for new and sophisticated materials for microelectronics, photonics, and high temperature applications1–5. High-entropy alloys (HEAs), first proposed by Yeh et al. in 20046, represent a novel category of multi-component alloys (MCAs) that deviate from conventional alloy design principles. Unlike traditional alloys based on one or two primary components, HEAs incorporate multiple principal elements in approximately equiatomic or near-equiatomic ratios7–10. This unique composition leads to a high mixing entropy, which for liquid and solid solutions is calculated as follows11:

Where R is the gas constant (8.314 J/(K.mol)) and xi corresponds to the molar fraction of the constituent element i. To promote the formation of single-phase solid solutions, the mixing entropy plays a crucial role in suppressing the formation of intermetallic compounds. Consequently, HEAs possess novel attributes for high-performance structural materials that often surpass those of conventional alloys12–14. According to the previous studies15–19, one well-known characteristic of single-phase face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs is their high plasticity but low tensile strength. Furthermore, single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) HEAs present exceptional hardness, strength, and resistance to softening at high temperatures. However, in addition to their superior mechanical properties, corrosion resistance is a crucial characteristic for their specific purposes or structural applications20–24.

The strategies for the design of HEAs typically produce advanced alloys with a high content of expensive metals, such as Co, Ta, Hf, V, Nb, W, and Mo. Meanwhile, Co is often embedded in the various HEAs systems, and it is evident that incorporating expensive elements increases the raw material costs25–29. Among the high-entropy alloy systems, AlCrFeNi HEAs show high potential for industrial applications due to their excellent mechanical properties, particularly wear resistance, and relatively low cost30–33. On the other hand, the diverse characteristics of HEAs are a result of the synergistic effects of their multi-principal elements, rendering them suitable for use in extreme environments34–38.

During the last decade, extensive research has been conducted worldwide on the corrosion resistance of HEAs in different environments. It is described that heat treatment and altering the elements or composition of HEAs can improve their corrosion resistance. For instance, Shi et al.39 studied the homogenization effect of 1250 °C heat treatment on the corrosion resistance of AlxCoCrFeNi HEAs in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. The research unveiled that the alloy’s multiphase structure experienced chemical segregation as the Al content was increased, which led to localized corrosion. Nevertheless, post-heat treatment increased corrosion resistance by reducing chemical segregation and homogenizing microstructures. Zhao et al.40 demonstrated that the effect of alloying elements on the corrosion of FeCoNiCr-based HEAs in supercritical water (550 °C, 25 MPa, 300 h) was element-specific: Mo improved resistance, while Ti and Mn reduced it. The corrosion behavior of nanocrystalline FeCrNiCo HEAs resulting from Al and Cu co-alloying was investigated by Song et al.41 with specific dedication to AlxCuy(FeCrNiCo)(100−x−y) (x = 6.44–50.51 at%, y = 8.49–40.31 at%) HEAs. These authors found that the passive film was primarily composed of aluminum oxide, while Cu decreased the content of unoxidized metals in the film, which in turn decreased the corrosion resistance of the alloy. Further research into the corrosion properties of non-equiatomic HEAs is essential to advance understanding, optimize performance, and establish a reliable foundation for future applications42–46.

The primary drivers for developing Co-free HEAs are cobalt’s criticality, high cost, and supply-chain volatility. Cobalt is a common alloying element in advanced materials, such as superalloys, soft magnetic composites, and HEAs, due to its ability to stabilize the ductile FCC phase and enhance corrosion resistance47–50. To overcome the cost and processing barriers imposed by Co, research should prioritize Fe-rich HEAs, which offer a more economical and manufacturable alternative51–56. Based on the similar physicochemical features of Fe, Ni, and Co, Dong et al.33 developed a Co-free AlCrFe2Ni2alloy. This new alloy demonstrated mechanical properties comparable to the Co-containing AlCoCrFeNi2.1 eutectic high-entropy alloys (EHEAs), highlighting its potential as a viable, lower-cost alternative.

While Ti addition is known to enhance mechanical properties via mechanisms such as lattice distortion and the precipitation of a hard Ti-rich C14 Laves phase57–61, its effect on electrochemical behavior in HEAs remains less clear and requires systematic investigation, particularly given titanium’s established corrosion-resistant properties. In many HEAs, corrosion resistance relies on a Cr-rich passive film (primarily Cr2O3), where Co is understood to play a key synergistic role by enhancing the kinetics of Cr diffusion to the surface and improving the passive film’s electronic structure for greater stability, a “Cr-led, Co-assisted” mechanism62–66. Consequently, the removal of Co from the system would fundamentally compromise this established protective mechanism. In Co-free systems, Ti therefore acts not merely as a substitute but as a primary passivation agent in its own right67. This study’s unique significance lies in elucidating the novel protective mechanism it enables: namely, the formation of a stable, composite Cr2O3-TiO2 passive film. While this composite film has been observed in Ti-modified FeCoNiCr systems, the role of Ti in initiating and stabilizing this film becomes dominant and essential in the absence of cobalt’s synergistic influence68–72.

A central, unresolved question is whether Ti simply compensates for the lost Co synergy or establishes a fundamentally superior passivation mechanism, a question of profound importance for designing next-generation corrosion-resistant alloys. Motivated by this fundamental inquiry, this study systematically investigates the effect of Ti addition on the microstructure and electrochemical performance of a cost-effective Co-free AlCrFeNi high-entropy alloy in a 0.5 M H2SO4 environment. Thermodynamic modeling was employed to predict the phase equilibria and quantitative phase composition of the designed alloys. Collectively, this work moves beyond simple elemental substitution to probe a potential paradigm shift in the alloy’s surface chemistry and corrosion protection mechanism. Overall, this work provides a rational guideline for the selection of cost-effective Co-free AlCrFeNiTi HEAs for demanding applications.

Experimental details

Preparation of alloys

Alloy ingots with nominal compositions of AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 were prepared from high-purity elements (> 99.9%) by vacuum arc melting between 1600 and 1650 °C. The as-cast ingots were then homogenized at 900 °C for 10 h and subsequently air-quenched. Thermodynamic simulations were performed using THERMOCALC software (version 2024b) with the TCHEA5 high-entropy alloy database, applying the Scheil-Gulliver (SHEIL) model, which assumes no diffusion in the solid phase. This model was selected over full equilibrium (Lever Rule) calculations due to its proven ability to accurately predict microsegregation and secondary phase formation in as-cast HEAs, where the sluggish diffusion effect kinetically inhibits homogenization during cooling73–75.

Microstructural characterization

The crystal structures of the alloys were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer with a Cu Kα source (λ = 1.54060 Å). Measurements were performed in a 2θ range from 15° to 95° with a step size of 0.02°, operating at 40 kV and 30 mA. Microstructural and compositional analyses were conducted using a JEOL JSM7001F field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) equipped with an Oxford INCA X-max 80 energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) system. The same FE-SEM was also used to examine the microstructure and elemental distribution of the specimens following electrochemical testing.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical characterization of the as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5HEAs was performed at ambient temperature (i.e., 20–25 °C) using a conventional three-electrode electrochemical cell connected to a P-45X potentiostat/galvanostat. The electrolyte was a 0.5 M H₂SO₄ solution. The cell configuration employed an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (RE), a platinum sheet counter electrode (CE), and the HEA specimens as the working electrodes (WEs). Prior to testing, each working electrode was mechanically polished using SiC sandpaper to a 3000-grit finish and subsequently immersed in the electrolyte for 3 h under open-circuit potential (OCP) conditions to attain a steady-state surface potential. Upon stabilization, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted across a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz, with a sinusoidal perturbation of 5 mV applied relative to the OCP. This was followed by potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) tests, which were initiated at −250 mV versus the RE and scanned anodically at a rate of 1 mV/s until passive film breakdown. Experimental data were acquired and processed using CorrWare Zplot software to model the electrochemical behavior with equivalent electrical circuits (EECs). To ensure statistical reliability, all electrochemical measurements were performed three times for each specimen.

Results and discussion

Advances in thermodynamic computation have significantly simplified the prediction of phase equilibria and precipitation. These simulations help the prediction of material behavior under specific conditions, facilitating an in-depth understanding of material properties. Figure 1 presents the phase composition as a function of temperature and the crystallization modeling of the AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5alloys during solidification. As shown in Fig. 1, the addition of Ti to the AlCrFe2Ni2alloy promotes the suppression of typically brittle dendritic FCC and BCC phases. Furthermore, the dominant phase in the designed system is a high-entropy metallic solid solution with a BCC/B2 structure. Meanwhile, the low-temperature phase equilibria, dominated by intermetallic compounds, are kinetically limited and therefore unlikely to be realized.

Figure 2 presents the XRD patterns of the as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 HEAs. The AlCrFe2Ni2 alloy consists predominantly of face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC), and ordered body-centered cubic (B2) phase structures, which is consistent with previous studies33,76. Notably, the BCC peak exhibits a significantly higher intensity than the FCC peak. The XRD pattern of the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy, in which most of the diffraction peaks originate from the BCC and B2 phases, differs from that of AlCrFe2Ni2. The addition of Ti enhances the alloy’s mixing entropy, triggering a high-entropy effect. This effect stabilizes the existing phases and promotes the formation of a BCC/B2 phase’s solid solution during solidification, resulting in significant structural changes77.

As observed in Fig. 2, the diffraction peaks corresponding to the BCC and ordered B2 phases overlap significantly. This overlap arises from their similar lattice parameters and the absence of distinct superlattice reflections within the detection limits of conventional laboratory XRD78–81. Consequently, definitive quantitative phase discrimination between the BCC and B2 phases via XRD alone is precluded, necessitating advanced techniques such as high-resolution synchrotron diffraction or neutron diffraction for precise quantification. Therefore, to qualitatively elucidate the phase constituents, the microstructure and elemental distribution were analyzed in detail using FE-SEM/EDS.

Back-scattered electron (BSE-SEM) micrographs and the corresponding EDS maps of the as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 HEAs are presented in Fig. 3. Additionally, Table 1 lists the chemical compositions of the constituent phases determined by SEM-EDS, which further corroborate the phases identified by XRD. The as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 alloy exhibits a dual-phase microstructure consisting of light gray and dark gray phases, i.e., [FeCr]-rich and [AlFeNi]-rich, respectively (Fig. 3(a)). Quantitative image analysis of the dark gray region, performed on a BSE-SEM micrograph using Image-Pro software, indicates that the [AlFeNi]-rich phase has a mole fraction of approximately 0.52. In contrast, the microstructure of AlCrFe₂Ni₂Ti₀.₅ consists of two distinct phases: (i) a continuous BCC/B2 matrix (interdendritic regions), which represents the dominant phase and contains moderate concentrations of Al, Cr, Fe, and Ni with relatively low Ti content, and (ii) Ti-rich dendrites (≈ 79 at% Ti by EDS), which are a separate intermetallic phase embedded within the BCC/B2 matrix (Fig. (3b)). Thus, the BCC/B2 structure refers exclusively to the matrix phase, while the Ti-rich dendrites are a distinct intermetallic phase dispersed within this matrix. However, direct transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmation of the B2 order and detailed phase morphology is not provided in this study and remains an important direction for future work. In Wei et al.’s work (Fig. 3 in their article77, a bright-field TEM micrograph of the as-cast AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy reveals the presence of small nanoparticles embedded within the B2 matrix phase. This observation is consistent with the XRD results. Furthermore, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern indicates that the dark laminated phase possesses a BCC structure, while the bright laminated phase corresponds to the B2 structure. These nanoparticles strengthen the alloy by acting as obstacles to dislocation motion, inducing localized dislocation pile-ups via the Orowan mechanism82–84. According to the results obtained by Wei et al.77, the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy exhibits a compressive yield strength of 1714 MPa, an ultimate strength of 3307 MPa, and an elongation of 43%, demonstrating an excellent combination of strength and ductility.

Potentiodynamic polarization tests were performed on the as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5HEAs in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution to evaluate their electrochemical performance. The resulting polarization curves, shown in Fig. 4(a), demonstrate their electrochemical response in this reducing acidic environment. Both alloys exhibit typical active–passive–transpassive behavior, as indicated in the anodic branch of the polarization curves. This behavior is characterized by three distinct regions: (I) active dissolution, (II) stable passivation, and (III) transpassive dissolution. Additionally, both HEAs exhibit a stable passive region, which lacks a distinct active-to-passive transition, suggesting the formation of a compact and adherent oxide film85–87. Within this passive region, slight current density fluctuations are attributed to the continuous nucleation, growth, and repassivation of localized pits88–90. The key electrochemical parameters, corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr), derived from the polarization curves are illustrated in Fig. 4(b). The corrosion potential and corrosion current density are key indicators of a material’s corrosion tendency. Higher Ecorr values and lower Icorr values are generally associated with improved corrosion resistance91–95.

As seen in Fig. 4(b), the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy exhibits a more passive corrosion potential and a lower corrosion current density than the AlCrFe2Ni2 alloy, indicating its lower corrosion rate and superior resistance to general corrosion in the studied acidic medium. Moreover, the passive breakdown potential and passive current density, key indicators of localized corrosion resistance, show that the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy outperforms AlCrFe2Ni2, exhibiting both a lower passive current and a more passive breakdown potential (Fig. 4(a)).With regard to their obtained results, it can be said that the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy forms a denser and more protective passive film, which provides enhanced resistance to localized corrosion in the 0.5 M H2SO4solution.

This study employed electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), i.e., a non-destructive, reliable, and rapid technique, to investigate the passivation characteristics of HEAs. The EIS spectra for as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5HEAs in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution at OCP condition are presented as Nyquist and Bode plots in Figs. 4(c) and (d), respectively. As seen in Fig. 4(c), the Nyquist spectra are imperfect semicircles, and the significantly larger semicircle diameter of AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 compared to AlCrFe2Ni2, directly reflects a much higher charge-transfer resistance and thus a far more protective passive film96–99. According to different impedance spectra indicated in Fig. 4, a one-time constant can be considered for the as-homogenized AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 HEAs EIS spectra. Hence, the electrical equivalent circuit (EEC) represented in Fig. 4(c) can be considered for simulating and modeling the experimental impedance spectra. It should be noted that the single time-constant model is the ideal case against which real systems are compared. It assumes (i) a perfectly smooth, homogeneous electrode surface, (ii) a single, uncomplicated charge-transfer reaction with no adsorbed intermediates, (iii) a double layer that behaves as a perfect, frequency-independent capacitor, and (iv) semi-infinite linear diffusion (which, importantly, is not in the Randles circuit itself; the classic Randles circuit uses a Warburg element in series with Rct to model diffusion, creating a two-time-constant circuit). Therefore, its use is justified when the system under investigation approximates these ideal conditions or as a first-order approximation to understand the primary bottleneck100,101. Moreover, the Bode plots in Fig. 4(d) show a primarily capacitive behavior at low-to-mid frequencies and a resistive response at high frequencies. Also, the presence of a single maximum phase lag in the middle frequency range indicates a single time constant. Additionally, the persistently high phase angles suggest the formation of a stable oxide passive film. Notably, the phase angle of the Ti-containing alloy remains above − 80° over nearly four decades of frequency, whereas the Ti-free alloy drops below − 70° at lower frequencies, revealing a more ideal capacitive response and superior film integrity in the presence of Ti52,98,102.

The credibility of impedance measurements requires the electrochemical system to satisfy the fundamental assumptions of linear system theory, i.e., causality, linearity, and stability. Thus, the consistency of the impedance spectra should be investigated using Kramers–Kronig Transformations (KKTs) analysis, which mathematically interconverts the real and imaginary impedance components103. The theoretical basis for KKTs relations is detailed in previous publications104–106. As shown in Fig. 5, the close agreement between the experimental EIS data and the K-K transformed curves for AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 HEAs in 0.5 M H₂SO₄ solution confirms the system’s adherence to the assumptions of linear system theory.

The resulting electrochemical parameters for the AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 HEAs immersed in 0.5 M H₂SO₄ solution are summarized in Table 2. With regard to their obtained results, the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy exhibited a significantly higher Rp value, demonstrating that Ti addition enhances the corrosion resistance of the passive film. This is consistent with the high n values (n > 0.85, Table 2), which indicate the formation of a compact and homogeneous oxide layer on the surface, and the notably higher n value of the Ti-containing alloy further reveals that Ti suppresses local defects and micro-pores in the passive film, leading to near-ideal dielectric behavior. The post-corrosion surface characteristics of the AlCrFe2Ni2 and AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 HEAs were examined using BSE-SEM after exposure to 0.5 M H₂SO₄ to clarify the microstructural origin of their distinct corrosion behaviors (Fig. 6; Table 3). The AlCrFe2Ni2alloy’s initial dual-phase architecture (FCC + BCC/B2) promotes pronounced microgalvanic interactions at the phase interfaces (Fig. 6(a)). Consequently, the corroded surface exhibits localized but intense dissolution, concentrated primarily at the dendrite/interdendritic boundaries. Although the overall pit density is low, preferential interfacial dissolution leads to a markedly heterogeneous morphology, with alternating regions of relative intactness and severe degradation of heterogeneous corroded patches. Meanwhile, Ti addition induces a microstructural shift toward a predominantly BCC/B2 solid solution, qualitatively reducing the FCC phase volume fraction (as evidenced by the decreased intensity of FCC peaks in the XRD pattern, Fig. 2), thereby diminishing the severity of microgalvanic coupling. As can be seen in Fig. 6(b), the corroded AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy possesses a substantially more uniform surface, exhibiting only sparse, shallow pits. Fine Ti-enriched dendritic structures, indicative of minor Ti segregation during solidification, are a noteworthy feature (see Fig. 3(b)). It is important to note that these dendrites promote a chemically stable and structurally compact passive film enriched in TiO2. It has been reported that this TiO2-enriched film exhibits superior thermodynamic stability in the studied solution compared to the oxides of Al, Fe, or Ni114–116. In fact, TiO2, synergistically integrated with Cr2O3 within the passive layer, enhances the film’s density, adhesion, and resistance to localized breakdown. Therefore, these microstructural and chemical advantages account for the improved electrochemical performance: reduced corrosion current density, a nobler corrosion potential, and an elevated pitting/film breakdown potential (see Fig. 4(a)).

The results of this study demonstrate the AlCrFe2Ni2 alloy suffers from interfacial instability and localized dissolution, resulting in a rough, heterogeneous corrosion morphology. In contrast, the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy benefits from a homogenized matrix and a highly protective, Ti-enriched passive layer that effectively suppresses pit nucleation and limits anodic reactivity. Therefore, the substantial enhancement in corrosion resistance observed for the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy is attributed to two combined effects: (i) reduced galvanic contrast from [FeCr]-rich phase suppression and (ii) the formation of Ti-segregated dendritic regions that enable a robust, TiO2-dominated passive film. However, as only a single Ti content (Ti₀.₅) was examined, the optimal Ti concentration remains undetermined, and no systematic relationship between Ti content, microstructure, and corrosion performance can yet be established. Therefore, further systematic studies with varying Ti concentrations are warranted to fully elucidate these interdependencies.

The corrosion performance of the present AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy can be meaningfully compared with previously reported AlCrFeNi-based HEAs investigated under similar electrochemical conditions. For instance, in Co-free AlCrFeNi-Tix alloys studied in both 3.5 wt% NaCl and 0.5 M H2SO4 solutions, Ti addition was reported to significantly influence passive film stability, where intermediate Ti contents led to improved corrosion resistance due to the formation of a more protective mixed oxide layer117. Similarly, Pratskova et al.118 reported that in AlxCoCrFeNiM (M = Ti, V, Si, Mn, Cu) HEAs, corrosion resistance strongly depends on elemental partitioning and phase distribution, with Ti-containing eutectic regions being particularly sensitive to corrosion attack, highlighting the critical role of microstructural heterogeneity in governing electrochemical behavior. In addition, studies on AlCoCrFeNi-based alloys in chloride-containing environments have shown that corrosion resistance is governed by the stability of Cr-rich passive films, while phase constitution and elemental segregation significantly affect pitting susceptibility119. In comparison, the present AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy exhibits competitive or improved corrosion resistance in acidic media, which can be attributed to the synergistic effect of Cr- and Ti-enriched passive film formation, thereby confirming the beneficial role of controlled Ti addition in enhancing passivation stability.

It is important to note that the present electrochemical evaluation was conducted in 0.5 M H2SO4, a highly aggressive acidic environment commonly used to assess general corrosion behavior and passive film stability under severe conditions. This medium provides a consistent basis for comparing the relative corrosion performance and passivation tendency of the investigated Co-free AlCrFeNiTi HEAs. However, corrosion behavior and passive film stability may vary depending on the chemical environment. In particular, alloying elements such as Ti may influence passive film characteristics differently in neutral chloride-containing solutions or in alkaline media. Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the corrosion performance and application potential of these alloys would benefit from further evaluation in different environments. Future work will focus on investigating pitting corrosion behavior in 3.5 wt% NaCl solution as well as passivation behavior in alkaline electrolytes to better define the applicable environmental range of the studied alloys.

Conclusions

This study investigates the effect of Ti addition on microstructural characterization and electrochemical behavior of cost-effective Co-free AlCrFe2Ni2 high-entropy alloy. The incorporation of Ti transformed the crystal structure from a mixture of face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC), and ordered body-centered cubic (B2) phases to a predominantly BCC/B2 structure in the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy. Furthermore, Ti addition promoted significant grain refinement and increased the volume fraction of the interdendritic regions, which served as preferential sites for the segregation of Ti-rich intermetallic phases. This unique microstructure in the AlCrFe2Ni2Ti0.5 alloy helped reduce the vulnerability to corrosion caused by initial dual-phase architecture and element segregation, facilitating the formation of a more stable and thicker passive oxide film. These findings demonstrate that Ti incorporation is an effective strategy for significantly enhancing the corrosion behavior of cost-effective, Co-free AlCrFeNi-based high-entropy alloys. Furthermore, further systematic studies with varying Ti concentrations are warranted to fully elucidate the relationship between Ti content, microstructure, and corrosion performance.
