Classification aggregation: a quantitative impossibility theorem
Yuval Filmus

Abstract
A group of individuals wishes to classify m m objects into n n categories in such a way that no class is left empty, a condition known as surjectivity . The opinions of the individuals are aggregated separately for each object using an aggregation function that can depend on the object. Maniquet and Mongin showed that if the aggregation functions are unanimous and the outcome must always be surjective, then the aggregation mechanism is dictatorial. Cailloux et al. showed that the same holds even if unanimity is relaxed to citizen sovereignty (each object can be classified into any category). We show that similar results hold even if we only require the outcome to be surjective with probability 1 − ϵ 1-\epsilon (with respect to an arbitrary symmetric i.i.d. distribution), provided that the aggregation functions are far from being constant. On the way, we characterize all aggregation mechanisms whose outcome is always surjective without any assumptions on the aggregation functions. Our approach uses a general result of Alekseev and Filmus which has wider applicability. We illustrate this by proving a similar impossibility result for aggregating equivalence relations.



1
Introduction

Arrow’s celebrated impossibility theorem
[
Arr50
,
Arr63
]
concerns elections among
n\geq 3
candidates in which each voter has to rank all candidates, and the desired output is a ranking of all candidates. The theorem states that the only voting mechanisms satisfying the two natural axioms IIA and Unanimity are dictatorships.

One might hope that the issue goes away if we allow the outcome, encoded as a list of pairwise rankings of the candidates, to be non-transitive (that is, not correspond to a ranking) with small probability (under the impartial culture distribution). Kalai
[
Kal02
]
ruled this out for neutral mechanisms, showing that if the probability of a non-transitive outcome is small, then the voting mechanism is close to a dictatorship. Mossel
[
Mos12
]
replaced the assumption of neutrality by the assumption that the mechanism is far from being constant. This assumption is necessary to rule out mechanisms which output a fixed ranking.

We prove a result in the style of Mossel in a different domain: classification aggregation. A group of individuals wishes to classify
m
objects into
n
categories in such a way that no category is left empty, a condition known as
surjectivity
. The opinions of the individuals are aggregated into a societal outcome on an object-by-object basis, a constraint which mirrors the IIA axiom. This problem can be traced back to Kasher and Rubinstein
[
KR97
]
, who considered the case
n=2
.

Maniquet and Mongin
[
MM14
,
MM16
]
showed that if
m\geq n\geq 3
and the mechanism satisfies Unanimity (if all individuals classify object
i
into category
j
then the outcome also classifies
i
into category
j
) and Surjectivity (the outcome is always surjective), then the mechanism is a dictatorship, up to a permutation of the categories. Cailloux et al.
[
CHOS24
]
proved a similar result under the weaker assumption of Citizen Sovereignty (for every object
i
and category
j
, there is an outcome which classifies
i
into category
j
), as well as for
m>n=2
. When
m=n=2
, other mechanisms exist, and Cailloux et al. described all of them.

Our main result shows that we cannot escape these impossibility theorems by relaxing Surjectivity.

Theorem 1.1

(Main)
.

Let
m\geq n\geq 2
such that
(m,n)\neq(2,2)
. Let
\mu
be a full support distribution on the set of surjective mappings
[m]\to[n]
which is invariant under applying permutations of
[n]
. We encode such mappings as vectors in
[n]^{m}
.

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
(depending on
m,n,\mu
) for which the following holds.

For every
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
there exists
\delta>0
such that the following holds for all
p
. Suppose that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
are
\delta
-surjective with respect to
\mu
:

\Pr_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\sim\mu}[(f_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},\dots,x^{(p)}_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(x^{(1)}_{m},\dots,x^{(p)}_{m}))\text{ is surjective}]\geq 1-\delta.

If
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are
\epsilon
-far from constant
(for every
i\in[m]
and
c\in[n]
,
\Pr[f_{i}=c]<1-\epsilon
, where the probability is with respect to the uniform distribution) then there exists
k\in[p]
and a permutation
\pi
on
[n]
such that for all
i\in[m]
,

\Pr_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i}(x)=\pi(x_{k})]\geq 1-\epsilon,

where the probability is with respect to the uniform distribution.

To explain the theorem, consider the following picture:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}x^{(1)}&x^{(1)}_{1}&\cdots&x^{(1)}_{m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x^{(p)}&x^{(p)}_{1}&\cdots&x^{(p)}_{m}\\
\hline\cr&f_{1}(x^{*}_{1})&\cdots&f_{m}(x^{*}_{m})\end{array}

Each row represents the classification of one of the individuals: for each of the
m
objects, it includes a category in
[n]
. Each row has to be surjective, meaning that all categories must be present. The individual classifications are aggregated using the functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
, which operate on each column separately.

The result of Maniquet and Mongin states that if (1) the outcome is always surjective and (2)
f_{i}(j,\dots,j)=j
for all
i,j
, then there exists
k\in[p]
and a permutation
\pi
on
[n]
such that
f_{1}(x)=\cdots=f_{m}(x)=\pi(x_{k})
. Cailloux et al. prove a similar impossibility result under (2’): for all
i,j
there is
x
such that
f_{i}(x)=j
.

Theorem

1.1
considers the experiment in which each individual samples a classification from a distribution
\mu
whose support is the set of all surjective classifications, and is invariant under permutations on
[n]
. It states that if (1’) the outcome is
almost
always surjective and (2”) the
f_{i}
are not approximately constant, then
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are close to a function of the form
\pi(x_{k})
. Condition (2”) is necessary to rule out functions such as
f_{i}(x)=\min(i,n)
.

A crucial feature of our theorem is that
\delta
depends on
\epsilon
and on
m,n,\mu
but not on
p
.

1.1
Outline of proof

The proof uses a general result of Alekseev and Filmus
[
AF26
]
on
approximate polymorphisms
. Polymorphism is a concept from universal algebra whose relevance to judgment aggregation first appears explicitly in unpublished work of Szegedy and Xu
[
SX15
]
. It already appears implicitly a few decades earlier in work of Rubinstein and Fishburn
[
RF86
]
.

Definition 1.2

(Polymorphism)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet. A
predicate
is a subset
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
for some
m
. A
(multi-sorted) polymorphism of
P
is a tuple of functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{p}\to\Sigma
(for some
p
) satisfying the following condition:

x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\in P\Longrightarrow(f_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},\dots,x^{(p)}_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(x^{(1)}_{m},\dots,x^{(p)}_{m}))\in P.

In classification aggregation the alphabet is
\Sigma=[n]
, and the predicate is
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
:

Definition 1.3

(Surjectivity)
.

Let
m\geq n
. The predicate
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}\subseteq[n]^{m}
consists of all vectors
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in[n]^{m}
such that
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}=[n]
.

Stated in this language, Maniquet and Mongin determined all polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
for
m\geq n\geq 3
which satisfy
f_{i}(j,\dots,j)=j
for all
i,j
.

Historically, polymorphisms were studied mostly in the uni-sorted setting, which corresponds to the condition
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{m}
, but the multi-sorted setting does appear in the literature
[
BJ03
]
.

The result of Alekseev and Filmus concerns
approximate polymorphisms
.

Definition 1.4

(Approximate polymorphism)
.

Let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
, and let
\mu
be a distribution on
P
. A tuple of functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{p}\to\Sigma
is a
\delta
-approximate polymorphism (with respect to
\mu
)
if

\Pr_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\sim\mu}[(f_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},\dots,x^{(p)}_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(x^{(1)}_{m},\dots,x^{(p)}_{m}))\in P]\geq 1-\delta.

The result of Alekseev and Filmus states that all approximate polymorphisms are close to exact polymorphisms. However, Alekseev and Filmus are only able to prove it for predicates satisfying an additional condition.

Definition 1.5

(Flexibility)
.

A predicate
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
is
flexible
if for all
i\in[m]
there is
x\in P
such that
y\in P
for all
y\in\Sigma^{m}
which differ from
x
only in the
i
’th coordinate.

Theorem 1.6

(Alekseev–Filmus)
.

Let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
be a flexible predicate, and let
\mu
be a distribution on
P
of full support. Denote by
\mu|_{1},\dots,\mu|_{m}
its marginals.

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
(depending on
P,\mu
) for which the following holds.

For every
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
there exists
\delta>0
such that the following holds. If
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{p}\to\Sigma
are a
\delta
-approximate polymorphism of
P
then there exists an exact polymorphism
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\colon\Sigma^{p}\to\Sigma
of
P
such that for all
i
,

\Pr_{x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\sim\mu|_{i}}[f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\neq g_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})]<\epsilon.

If
|\Sigma|=2
, then the same holds even without the assumption of flexibility.

It is easy to check that
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
is flexible for all
m>n
, but not when
m=n
. We conjecture that the theorem holds without the assumption of flexibility for any
\Sigma
.

In order to prove
Theorem

1.1
when
m>n
, we apply
Theorem

1.6
on
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
with the given distribution
\mu
. Since
\mu
is invariant under applying permutations of
[n]
, each marginal
\mu|_{i}
is the uniform distribution on
[n]
. When
m>n
, we can thus extend
Theorem

1.1
to arbitrary full support distributions
\mu
, with the caveat that the uniform distributions on
[n]^{p}
need to be replaced by
p
-th powers of
\mu|_{1},\dots,\mu|_{m}
.

The case
m=n
requires a completely different argument, which is spectral in nature and uses ideas from work of Alon et al.
[
ADFS04
]
on the traffic light problem.

In order to apply
Theorem

1.6
we need to know all polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
, not just ones satisfying Unanimity or Citizen Sovereignty. This is an inherent feature of this setting: the assumption that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are unanimous doesn’t guarantee that they are close to a unanimous polymorphism. Indeed, take

f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\begin{cases}j&\text{if }x_{1}=\cdots=x_{p}=j,\\
\min(i,n)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The probability that
x^{(i)}_{1}=\cdots=x^{(i)}_{p}
for some
i
is vanishingly small, and so
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are an
o(1)
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
, with respect to any
\mu
. However, they are not close to any dictatorship.

The same issue also arises in Mossel’s quantitative Arrow theorem
[
Mos12
]
, which is the special case of
Theorem

1.6
for the predicate
\mathsf{Lin}_{m}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\binom{m}{2}}
of all linear orders. Mossel
[
Mos09
]
resolves it by determining all polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Lin}_{m}
. Similarly, proving
Theorem

1.1
requires us to determine all polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
.

1.2
Impossibility domains without unanimity

Dokow and Holzman
[
DH09
,
DH10a
]
were interested in classifying predicates for which an impossibility theorem in the style of Arrow holds.

Definition 1.7

(Impossibility domain)
.

A predicate
P\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
is an
impossibility domain
if the only unanimous polymorphisms of
P
are dictators. That is, if
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\{0,1\}^{p}\to\{0,1\}
constitute a polymorphism of
P
satisfying
f_{i}(j,\dots,j)=j
for all
i,j
, then there exists
k\in[p]
such that
f_{1}(x)=\cdots=f_{m}(x)=x_{k}
.

Theorem 1.8

(Dokow–Holzman, as refined by Szegedy–Xu)
.

If a predicate
P\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
is not an impossibility domain then one of the following holds:

(i)

x_{1}\oplus x_{2}\oplus x_{3},\dots,x_{1}\oplus x_{2}\oplus x_{3}
is a (unanimous) polymorphism of
P
.

(ii)

\Maj(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),\dots,\Maj(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is a (unanimous) polymorphism of
P
.

(iii)

There is a unanimous polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
of
P
in which
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\{x_{1}\land x_{2},x_{1}\lor x_{2}\}
.

Dokow and Holzman
[
DH10c
]
considered the case of larger alphabet under the stronger assumption of supportiveness (
f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\}
), but were unable to provide a complete classification. Szegedy and Xu
[
SX15
]
used tools from universal algebra to complete the classification, and also to prove a similar classification under unanimity.

Theorem 1.9

(Szegedy–Xu)
.

If a predicate
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
is not an impossibility domain with respect to supportiveness, then this is witnessed by a polymorphism of arity
p=3
.

If a predicate
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
is not an impossibility domain with respect to unanimity, then this is witnessed by a polymorphism of arity
p=|\Sigma|
.

Without unanimity, a new type of polymorphism arises: a
certificate
. In the case of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
, a polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
is a certificate if for each
j\in[n]
, one of the
f_{i}
’s is the constant
j
function.

Definition 1.10

(Trivial domain)
.

A predicate
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
is
trivial
if for every polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{p}\to\Sigma
, one of the following cases holds:

(i)

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are dictatorial:
There exists an index
k\in[p]
and a permutation
\pi
on
\Sigma
such that

f_{1}(x)=\cdots=f_{m}(x)=\pi(x_{k}).

(ii)

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are a certificate:
There exists a partial mapping
\rho
from
[m]
to
\Sigma
such that

(a)

\rho
is a certificate: if
x_{i}=\rho(i)
for all
i\in\dom\rho
then
x\in P
.

(b)

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
conform to
\rho
:
f_{i}(x)=\rho(i)
for all
i\in\dom\rho
and all
x
.

We say that
P
is
p
-trivial
if the condition holds for a specific value of
p
.

Continuing our previous example, a certificate for
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
is a partial mapping
\rho
from
[m]
to
[n]
which is surjective. Functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
conform to
\rho
if
f_{i}
is the constant
\rho(i)
function for any
i\in\dom\rho
.

Filmus
[
Fil26
]
proves a result in the style of
Theorem

1.9
in this context. The result applies to predicates satisfying two mild technical conditions.

Definition 1.11

(Degeneracy)
.

A predicate
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
is
non-degenerate
if

(a)

P
has full projections: for every
i\in[m]
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
there exists
x\in P
such that
x_{i}=\sigma
.

(b)

P
depends on all coordinates: for every
i\in[m]
there are
x\in P
and
y\notin P
which differ only on the
i
’th coordinate.

Definition 1.12

(Vulnerability)
.

A predicate
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
is
vulnerable
if there exists
i\in[m]
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that all
x\in\Sigma^{m}
satisfying
x_{i}=\sigma
belong to
P
.

It is easy to check that
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
is non-degenerate and non-vulnerable.

In the setting of Filmus
[
Fil26
]
, which allows different alphabets for different coordinates, the assumption of non-degeneracy is without loss of generality: we can shrink the alphabet of each coordinate to its projection, and drop coordinates which
P
does not depend on. Vulnerability is needed to rule out non-trivial predicates such as OR (which has ORs as polymorphisms).

We can now state a consequence of the main result of Filmus
[
Fil26
]
.

Theorem 1.13

(Filmus)
.

Let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
be a non-vulnerable non-degenerate predicate.

(a)

If
|\Sigma|>2
and
P
is
1
-trivial then it is trivial.

(b)

If
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
and
P
is
1
-trivial then it is trivial unless there is a polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
such that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\{x_{1}\land x_{2},x_{1}\lor x_{2}\}
.

Using this result, we are able to determine all polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
, enabling us to deduce
Theorem

1.1
via
Theorem

1.6
.

Theorem 1.14

(Polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
)
.

Let
m\geq n\geq 2
, where
(m,n)\neq(2,2)
. If
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
constitute a polymorphism of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
then one of the following holds:

(i)

There exists
k\in[p]
and a permutation
\pi
of
[n]
such that

f_{1}(x)=\cdots=f_{m}(x)=\pi(x_{k}).

(ii)

For each
j\in[n]
there exists
i\in[m]
such that
f_{i}(x)=j
for all
x
.

The case
m=n
was already proved by Falik and Friedgut
[
FF14
]
. Using
Theorem

1.13
, we provide a quick proof of this case.

1.3
Equivalence relations

Fishburn and Rubinstein
[
FR86
]
considered a different kind of classification problem, which also appears later in Kasher and Rubinstein
[
KR97
]
. In this problem, a society of individuals is grouping a set of objects into equivalence classes. Each individual encodes their classification by stating, for any two objects, whether they are equivalent. The individual classifications are then aggregated by determining separately for any two objects whether they are equivalent.

In the language of polymorphisms, this corresponds to the following predicate.

Definition 1.15

(Equivalence predicate)
.

The predicate
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\binom{m}{2}}
consists of all vectors
x
satisfying the condition

x_{\{i,j\}}=x_{\{j,k\}}=1\Longrightarrow x_{\{i,k\}}=1

for all distinct
i,j,k
.

We think of elements of
x\in\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
as equivalence relations in which
i\sim j
if
x_{\{i,j\}}=1
.

Fishburn and Rubinstein, and later Kasher and Rubinstein, showed that all unanimous polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
are oligarchies, that is,
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{m}
, and the common value is a conjunction of inputs or a constant function.

We prove an analog of
Theorem

1.1
for this predicate, which we state using the language of polymorphisms.

Theorem 1.16

(Approximate polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
)
.

Let
m\geq 3
and let
\mu
be a full support distribution on
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
which is invariant under permutations of
[m]
.

Let
q
be the probability that
i\sim j
under
\mu
, and let
\nu
be the product distribution with marginals
q
.

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
(depending on
m
and
\mu
) for which the following holds.

For every
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
there exists
\delta>0
such that the following holds for all
p
. Suppose that
(f_{\{i,j\}})_{i<j}\colon\{0,1\}^{p}\to\{0,1\}
are a
\delta
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
with respect to
\mu
.

If
\Pr_{\nu}[f_{\{i,j\}}=0]<1-\epsilon
for all
i<j
then there exists a set
S\subseteq[p]
such that for all
i<j
,

\Pr_{\nu}\bigl[f_{\{i,j\}}(x)=\bigwedge_{s\in S}x_{s}\bigr]\geq 1-\epsilon.

This result follows from
Theorem

1.6
via the following characterization of all polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
.

Theorem 1.17

(Exact polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
)
.

Let
m\geq 3
. If
(f_{\{i,j\}})_{i<j}\colon\{0,1\}^{p}\to\{0,1\}
constitute a polymorphism of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
then there exists an equivalence relation
\sim
on
[m]
such that the following holds:

(a)

If
i\nsim j
then
f_{\{i,j\}}=0
.

(b)

For every equivalence class
A
of
\sim
of size at least
3
there exists a set
S\subseteq[p]
such that for every
i\neq j\in A
, we have

f_{\{i,j\}}(x)=\bigwedge_{s\in S}x_{s}.

(If
A=\{i,j\}
is an equivalence class of size
2
then
f_{\{i,j\}}
can be arbitrary.)

Moreover, for every equivalence relation
\sim
on
[m]
, all functions
f_{\{i,j\}}
satisfying the above constraints are indeed polymorphisms.

The interpretation is that there is an equivalence relation
\sim
such that the aggregated outcome is always a refinement of
\sim
. Moreover, if
A=\{i,j\}
is an equivalence class of
\sim
then
f_{\{i,j\}}
is arbitrary, and if
|A|\geq 3
then the restriction of
f
to
A
is an oligarchy which could depend on
A
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.17
reduces to the case
m=3
, which was proved by
[
Fil26
]
as part of the classification of polymorphisms of all symmetric predicates over
\{0,1\}
(when
m=3
, the predicate
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
consists of all vectors whose weight is not exactly
2
).

1.4
Related work

Theorem

1.1
falls under the umbrella of
approximate judgment aggregation
. Judgment aggregation
[
LP02
,
LP09
]
arose from results such as the doctrinal paradox
[
KS86
,
LP02
]
, which in our language states that all unanimous polymorphisms of the predicate

P_{\land}=\{(a,b,a\land b):a,b\in\{0,1\}\}

are oligarchies, that is, functions of the form
f(x)=\bigwedge_{i\in S}x_{i}
.
The predicate
P_{\land}
is
truth-functional
, meaning that some coordinates are unconstrained, and the remaining ones are a function of the others.

There are several ways to relax the condition of polymorphicity.
Theorem

1.1
relaxes
consistency
, which states that the result of applying the aggregation mechanism always satisfies the predicate.
There are several papers analyzing Arrow’s theorem from this perspective
[
Kal02
,
Kel10
,
Kel12
,
Mos12
]
.
Nehama
[
Neh13
]
proved an approximate version of the doctrinal paradox, but the bounds in his result depend on the number of voters. Filmus et al.
[
FLMM20
]
obtained an approximate doctrinal paradox in which the error parameters are independent of the number of voters. Chase et al.
[
CFM+22
]
proved a similar result for all truth-functional predicates over
\{0,1\}
, and Alekseev and Filmus
[
AF26
]
generalized this to arbitrary predicates over
\{0,1\}
and some predicates over larger alphabets.

Falik and Friedgut
[
FF14
]
considered a different relaxation, in which the aggregation mechanism is only approximately coordinate-wise. They proved an approximate version of Arrow’s theorem and of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem
[
Gib73
,
Sat75
]
in this setting.

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem assumes strategyproofness. Several papers prove approximate versions which relax this assumption
[
FKKN11
,
IKM12
,
MR15
]
.

2
Triviality

In this section we derive
Theorem

1.13
from the main results of
[
Fil26
]
. Our starting point is the following theorem, which results from a combination of
[
Fil26
, Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.4]
in the special case where all alphabets are the same.

Definition 2.1

(
\Phi
-triviality)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
be a predicate, and let
\Phi\subseteq\Sym(\Sigma)^{m}
, where
\Sym(\Sigma)
is the group of permutations of
\Sigma
.

The predicate
P
is
\Phi
-trivial
if for every polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{p}\to\Sigma
, one of the following cases holds:

(i)

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are dictatorial:
There exist
k\in[p]
and
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})\in\Phi
such that for all
i\in[m]
,

f_{i}(x)=\phi_{i}(x_{k}).

(ii)

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are a certificate
, as in
Definition

1.10
.

We say that
P
is
(p,\Phi)
-trivial
if this holds for a specific value of
p
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
be non-degenerate, and let
\Phi\subseteq\Sym(\Sigma)^{m}
.

If
P
is
(1,\Phi)
-trivial but not
\Phi
-trivial, then one of the following holds:

(i)

P
is
sticky
: there exist
i\in[m]
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that if
x\in P
then
x|_{i\to\sigma}\in P
, where
x|_{i\to\sigma}
is obtained from
x
by changing the
i
’th coordinate to
\sigma
.

(ii)

|\Sigma|=2
and there is a polymorphism
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{2}\to\Sigma
where all
f_{i}
are unanimous (
f_{i}(\sigma,\sigma)=\sigma
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
) and non-dictatorial (cannot be written as a function of a single coordinate).

(When
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
, in this case
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in\{x_{1}\land x_{2},x_{1}\lor x_{2}\}
.)

(iii)

P
has a polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{2}\to\Sigma
such that for all
x\in P
,

(f_{1}(x_{1},\cdot),\dots,f_{m}(x_{m},\cdot)),(f_{1}(\cdot,x_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(\cdot,x_{m}))\in\Phi,

where
f_{i}(a,\cdot)\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma
is obtained by fixing the first coordinate to
a
, and
f_{i}(\cdot,a)
is defined analogously.

Comparing
Theorem

2.2
and
Theorem

1.13
, we see that the stickiness obstruction is replaced by the weaker vulnerability obstruction, and that the last case of
Theorem

2.2
is missing from
Theorem

1.13
. The proof of
Theorem

1.13
amounts to showing that stickiness implies vulnerability, and that “Latin square polymorphisms” cannot exist when
\phi_{1}=\cdots=\phi_{m}
for all
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})\in\Phi
. We formalize this slightly more general result as a theorem, and then deduce
Theorem

1.13
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
be non-degenerate, and let
\Phi\subseteq\{(\phi,\dots,\phi):\phi\in\Sym(\Sigma)\}
.

If
P
is
(1,\Phi)
-trivial but not
\Phi
-trivial, then one of the following holds:

(i)

P
is vulnerable.

(ii)

|\Sigma|=2
and there is a polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{2}\to\Sigma
where all
f_{i}
are unanimous and non-dictatorial.

The proof uses a lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, let
P\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
be non-degenerate, and let
\Phi\subseteq\{(\phi,\dots,\phi):\phi\in\Sym(\Sigma)\}
.

If
P
is
(1,\Phi)
-trivial and
|P|=|\Sigma|
then
|\Sigma|=2
and there is a polymorphism
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{2}\to\Sigma
such that all
f_{i}
are unanimous and non-dictatorial.

Proof.

By non-degeneracy, there are permutations
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m}\in\Sym(\Sigma)
such that

P=\{(\pi_{1}(\sigma),\dots,\pi_{m}(\sigma)):\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

By non-degeneracy,
|\Sigma|\geq 2
. Suppose first that
|\Sigma|=2
, without loss of generality
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
. Then
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m}\in\{\id,\Neg\}
, where
\id(x)=x
and
\Neg(x)=\lnot x
. Define
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

f_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}x_{1}\land x_{2}&\text{if }\pi_{i}=\id,\\
x_{1}\lor x_{2}&\text{if }\pi_{i}=\Neg.\end{cases}

We claim that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
constitute a polymorphism of
P
. To check this, it suffices to consider
x^{(1)}=(\pi_{1}(0),\dots,\pi_{m}(0))
and
x^{(2)}=(\pi_{1}(1),\dots,\pi_{m}(1))
. Then

•

If
\pi_{i}=\id
then
f_{i}(x^{(1)}_{i},x^{(2)}_{i})=0\land 1=0=x^{(1)}_{i}
.

•

If
\pi_{i}=\Neg
then
f_{i}(x^{(1)}_{i},x^{(2)}_{i})=1\lor 0=1=x^{(1)}_{i}
.

Thus
(f_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},x^{(2)}_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(x^{(1)}_{m},x^{(2)}_{m}))=x^{(1)}
, showing that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are indeed a polymorphism.

Suppose next that
|\Sigma|\geq 3
. Let
\sigma,\tau\in\Sigma
be two different letters. Define
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{1}\to\Sigma
as follows:

f_{i}(a)=\begin{cases}\pi_{i}(\sigma)&\text{if }a=\pi_{i}(\sigma),\\
\pi_{i}(\tau)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For every
b\in\Sigma
,

(f_{1}(\pi_{1}(b)),\dots,f_{m}(\pi_{m}(b)))=\begin{cases}(\pi_{1}(\sigma),\dots,\pi_{m}(\sigma))&\text{if }b=\sigma,\\
(\pi_{1}(\tau),\dots,\pi_{m}(\tau))&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
constitute a polymorphism of
P
. Since
|\Sigma|\geq 3
, each
f_{i}
is neither constant nor a permutation, contradicting the
(1,\Phi)
-triviality of
P
.
∎

We can now complete the proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

In view of
Theorem

2.2
, it suffices to show that stickiness implies vulnerability, and to rule out
Item

iii
of that
theorem
.

Suppose that
P
is sticky: there exist
i\in[m]
and
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that
x\in P
implies
x|_{i\to\sigma}\in P
. Consider the functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{1}\to\Sigma
given by

f_{j}(\tau)=\begin{cases}\sigma&\text{if }j=i,\\
\tau&\text{if }j\neq i.\end{cases}

By assumption,
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
constitute a polymorphism of
P
. Since
P
is
(1,\Phi)
-trivial and
f_{i}
is constant,
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
must be a certificate. Since
f_{i}
is the only constant function among
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
, it follows that the certificate only involves the
i
’th coordinate. Therefore any
x\in\Sigma^{m}
satisfying
x_{i}=\sigma
belongs to
P
, and so
P
is vulnerable.

Next, we rule out
Item

iii
.
In view of
Lemma

2.4
, we can assume that
|P|>|\Sigma|
(otherwise
Item

ii
holds), and so we can find
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that there are at least two
x\in P
with
x_{1}=\sigma
. This implies that we can find
x,y\in P
and an index
i>1
such that
x_{1}=y_{1}
and
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
.

Suppose that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
constitute a polymorphism of
P
conforming to
Item

iii
. Since

(f_{1}(x_{1},\cdot),\dots,f_{m}(x_{m},\cdot)),(f_{1}(y_{1},\cdot),\dots,f_{m}(y_{m},\cdot))\in\Phi

we have, in particular,

f_{i}(x_{i},\cdot)=f_{1}(x_{1},\cdot)=f_{1}(y_{1},\cdot)=f_{i}(y_{i},\cdot).

On the other hand,

(f_{1}(\cdot,x_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(\cdot,x_{m}))\in\Phi,

which implies that
f_{i}(\cdot,x_{i})
is a permutation. This contradicts
f_{i}(x_{i},\cdot)=f_{i}(y_{i},\cdot)
, which implies that
f_{i}(x_{i},x_{i})=f_{i}(y_{i},x_{i})
.
∎

Theorem

1.13
follows immediately from
Theorem

2.3
by taking

\Phi=\{(\phi,\dots,\phi):\phi\in\Sym(\Sigma)\}.

Exact polymorphisms

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.14
. The proof rests on the following

:

Lemma 3.1
.

Theorem

1.14
holds when
p=1
and
n\geq 3
.

Theorem

1.14
follows almost directly.

Proof of
Theorem

1.14
.

The case
n=2
(and so
m>2
) follows directly from Filmus
[
Fil26
, Theorem 1.5]
.

The case
n\geq 3
follows from
Lemma

3.1
by
Theorem

1.13
, once we verify that
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
is non-degenerate and non-vulnerable:

•

\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
has full projections: this is easy to check.

•

\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
depends on all coordinates: it suffices to consider

in which
x_{i}
is the only coordinate equal to some

.

•

\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
is not vulnerable: this is because for any

, the constant

. ∎

In the rest of this section, we prove
Lemma

3.1
.

The case
m=n
was proved by Falik and Friedgut
[
FF14
]
. We provide a simple alternative proof.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
when
m=n
.

Consider the
n\times n
matrix
M
defined by
M(i,j)=f_{i}(j)
. In other words, the
i
’th row of
M
is the truth table of
f_{i}
.

For each permutation
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[n]^{n}
, we must have that
f_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,f_{n}(x_{n})
is also a permutation of
[n]
. This means that the “generalized diagonal”
M(1,x_{1}),\dots,M(n,x_{n})
is a permutation of
[n]
.
The permutations
i\mapsto i+t\pmod{n}
cover each entry of
M
exactly once, and so each of the numbers
1,\dots,n
appears exactly
n
times in
M
.

For any
i\in[n]
, any two entries of
M
labeled
i
must lie on the same row or on the same column, since otherwise we can construct a generalized diagonal passing through both of them.
Therefore either all
i
-entries are on the same row, or all
i
-entries are on the same column. Since there are exactly
n
entries labeled
i
, this means that there is either a row whose entries are all labeled
i
, or a column whose entries are all labeled
i
.

Since every row and column intersect, it follows that either all rows are constant (and so all columns are equal to some permutation of
[n]
), or all columns are constant (and so all rows are equal to some permutation of
[n]
).

If all rows are constant then each
f_{i}
is constant, and so
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
are of certificate type.

If all columns are constant then
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
are all equal to the same permutation, and so
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
are dictatorial.
∎

The case
m>n
is a lot more challenging, and will occupy the rest of this section.

We start with some definitions:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\{i\in[m]:f_{i}\text{ is constant}\},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\{j\in[n]:f_{i}\equiv j\text{ for some }i\in I\}.

Clearly,

|I|\geq|C|.

We denote
c=|C|
for short.

If
c=n
then
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are of certificate type, so from now on we assume

c\leq n-1.

The entire proof is driven by the following
lemma
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
r\notin C
there is a subset
X_{r}\subseteq[n]
of size

c+1\leq|X_{r}|\leq n-1

such that the set

I_{r}:=\{i\in[m]:f_{i}(s)=r\text{ for all }s\in X_{r}\}

satisfies
I_{r}\cap I=\emptyset
and

|I_{r}|\geq m-|X_{r}|+1.

Before we prove the
lemma
, let us explain what it means. It gives a witness showing that if
x\in\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
, then necessarily
f_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(x_{m})
contains
r
. Indeed, there are at least
|X_{r}|
coordinates of
x
whose value is in
X_{r}
. Since
|I_{r}|\geq m-|X_{r}|+1
, one of these coordinates belongs to
I_{r}
. If
x_{i}\in X_{r}
and
i\in I_{r}
then by definition,
f_{i}(x_{i})=r
.
We show that such a witness must always exist using Hall’s theorem.

Proof.

Consider the following bipartite graph:

•

The left side is
[n]
.

•

The right side is
[m]
.

•

We connect
s\in[n]
to
i\in[m]
if
f_{i}(s)\neq r
.

We claim that the graph does not have a matching saturating the left side. Indeed, suppose that
a\colon[n]\to[m]
were such a matching. We construct
x\in\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
as follows:

•

If
i\in\range a
, say
i=a(s)
, then
x_{i}=s
. (This guarantees that
x\in\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
.)

•

If
i\notin\range a
then we choose
x_{i}
so that
f_{i}(x_{i})\neq r
. This is possible since otherwise
r\in C
.

By construction,
f_{i}(x_{i})\neq r
for all
i\in[m]
, contradicting the assumption that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
constitute a polymorphism.

Since there is no matching saturating the left side, by Hall’s theorem there is a subset
X_{r}\subseteq[n]
such that
|N(X_{r})|\leq|X_{r}|-1
, where
N(X_{r})
is the neighborhood of
X_{r}
.

We claim that
I_{r}=[m]\setminus N(X_{r})
. Indeed,
i\in N(X_{r})
iff
f_{i}(s)\neq r
for some
s\in X_{r}
, and so
i\notin N(X_{r})
iff
f_{i}(s)=r
for all
s\in X_{r}
. It follows that

|I_{r}|=m-|N(X_{r})|\geq m-|X_{r}|+1.

Next, we show that
I_{r}\cap I=\emptyset
. Since
|N(X_{r})|<|X_{r}|
, the set
X_{r}
is non-empty, say
s\in X_{r}
. If
i\in I_{r}
then
f_{i}(s)=r
. Since
r\notin C
, this implies that
i\notin I
.

Thus
|I_{r}|\leq m-|I|\leq m-c
, which implies that

|X_{r}|\geq m-|I_{r}|+1\geq c+1.

Finally, we show that
|X_{r}|\leq n-1
. Indeed, otherwise we have
X_{r}=[n]
, and so
I_{r}
must be empty (since
i\in I_{r}
would imply
f_{i}\equiv r
). However,

|I_{r}|\geq m-|X_{r}|+1=m-n+1\geq 1.\qed

Observe that since
c\leq n-1
, applying the
lemma
to any
r\notin C
implies the stronger inequality

c\leq n-2.

The next two steps give upper and lower bounds on the quantity

\Delta:=\sum_{r\notin C}|I_{r}||X_{r}|.

We will obtain the lower bound via
Lemma

3.2
.
The upper bound will be a consequence of the elementary observation that for each
i\in[m]
, the sets
X_{r}
such that
i\in I_{r}
are disjoint.
We will show that the two bounds are consistent only if
c=0
, in which case they coincide. This implies that both of them are tight. The tightness of the lower bound turns out to imply that for each
r
, the set
X_{r}
is a singleton
\{x_{r}\}
and so
I_{r}=[m]
, implying that
f_{i}(x_{r})=r
for all
i\in[m]
. This will complete the proof.

We start with the upper bound.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have

\Delta\leq(m-c)n.

Proof.

We claim that for each
i\in[m]
, the sets
X_{r}
such that
i\in I_{r}
are disjoint. Indeed, suppose that
i\in I_{r}\cap I_{s}
and
t\in X_{r}\cap X_{s}
, where
r\neq s
. Then
f_{i}(t)=r
(since
i\in I_{r}
and
t\in X_{r}
) and
f_{i}(t)=s
(since
i\in I_{s}
and
t\in X_{s}
), and we reach a contradiction.

We deduce that

\Delta=\sum_{r\notin C}\sum_{i\in I_{r}}|X_{r}|=\sum_{i\notin I}\sum_{r\colon i\in I_{r}}|X_{r}|\leq|[m]\setminus I|\,n,

using
I_{r}\cap I=\emptyset
. The
lemma
now follows from
|I|\geq|C|=c
.
∎

The lower bound uses discrete concavity.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

\Delta\geq(n-c)\cdot\min\bigl((c+1)(m-c),(n-1)(m-n+2)\bigr)

and

\Delta\geq(n-c)m.

Furthermore, if the latter bound is tight then
c=0
and for all
r\in[n]
, we have
|X_{r}|=1
and
I_{r}=[m]
.

Proof.

Lemma

3.2
implies that for each
r\notin C
,

|I_{r}||X_{r}|\geq\theta(|X_{r}|),\text{ where }\theta(x)=x(m-x+1).

Moreover,
c+1\leq|X_{r}|\leq n-1
.

The function
\theta(x)
is strictly concave, and so
\theta(|X_{r}|)\geq\min(\theta(c+1),\theta(n-1))
. Together with
|[n]\setminus C|=n-c
, this gives the first inequality.
The second inequality follows similarly using
1\leq|X_{r}|\leq m
.

By strict concavity, if the second inequality is tight then
|X_{r}|\in\{1,m\}
for each
r\notin C
. Since
|X_{r}|\leq n-1<m
, we see that
|X_{r}|=1
for all
r\notin C
. Since
|X_{r}|\geq c+1
, this implies that
c=0
and so
C=\emptyset
. Finally,
Lemma

3.2
shows that
|I_{r}|\geq m
, and so
I_{r}=[m]
, for all
r\in[n]
.
∎

The next step is the most technical: we rule out
c\geq 1
by combining
Lemma

3.3
with the first lower bound in
Lemma

3.4
. This requires two technical inequalities. In both cases, we will use the inequality

2(n-1)>n,

which follows from
n>2
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
c\geq 1
then

(n-c)\cdot(c+1)(m-c)>(m-c)n.

Proof.

Since
m-c\geq m-n+2>0
, it suffices to show that
(c+1)(n-c)>n
.

The left-hand side is concave in
c
, and so
1\leq c\leq n-2
implies

(c+1)(n-c)\geq 2(n-1)>n.\qed

Lemma 3.6
.

If
c\geq 1
then

(n-c)\cdot(n-1)(m-n+2)>(m-c)n.

Proof.

If we subtract the right-hand side from the left-hand side then we obtain

x+c\bigl(n-(n-1)(m-n+2)\bigr),

where
x
doesn’t involve
c
. Since

(n-1)(m-n+2)\geq 2(n-1)>n,

we see that the validity of the
lemma
for all
c\leq n-2
would follow from its validity for
c=n-2
.

When
c=n-2
, the inequality in the statement of the
lemma
reads

2(n-1)(m-n+2)>(m-n+2)n,

which follows from
2(n-1)>n
since
m-n+2>0
.
∎

We can now show that
c=0
.

Lemma 3.7
.

We have
c=0
.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
c\geq 1
. Combining
Lemma

3.3
and the first inequality in
Lemma

3.4
gives

(m-c)n\geq\Delta\geq(n-c)\cdot\min\bigl((c+1)(m-c),(n-1)(m-n+2)\bigr).

This is ruled out by
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.6
.
∎

We can now complete the proof of
Lemma

3.1
.
Lemma

3.3
and the second inequality in
Lemma

3.4
give

mn\geq\Delta\geq nm,

and so
\Delta=nm
.
Lemma

3.4
thus implies that
|X_{r}|=1
and
I_{r}=[m]
for all
r\in[n]
, say
X_{r}=\{x_{r}\}
.

By definition of
I_{r}
, this shows that
f_{i}(x_{r})=r
for all
i\in[m]
. Clearly
x_{r}\neq x_{s}
for
r\neq s
, and so this completely defines
f_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
. We see that all
f_{i}
are equal, and the common value is the permutation which is the inverse of
r\mapsto x_{r}
. Thus
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are dictatorial.

3.2
Approximate polymorphisms (
m>n
)

In this section we deduce
Theorem

1.1
for
m>n
by combining
Theorem

1.14
with
Theorem

1.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
when
m>n
.

We claim that since
m>n
, the predicate
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
is flexible, and so we can apply
Theorem

1.6
. Indeed, for every
i\in[m]
we can find
x\in\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
such that
\{x_{j}:j\neq i\}=[n]
, so that
x\in\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
regardless of
x_{i}
.

Let
\epsilon_{0}>0
be the parameter given by
Theorem

1.6
for
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
and
\mu
.
Given
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, we let
\delta>0
be the parameter given by
Theorem

1.6
. Since
\mu
is invariant under applying permutations of
[n]
, the distributions
\mu|_{1},\dots,\mu|_{m}
are uniform.

Suppose that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
are
\delta
-surjective with respect to
\mu
, in other words are a
\delta
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
with respect to
\mu
, and moreover are
\epsilon
-far from constant.

Theorem

1.6
implies that there is a polymorphism
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
of
\mathsf{Surj}_{m,n}
such that for all
i
,

\Pr[g_{i}=f_{i}]\geq 1-\epsilon.

By
Theorem

1.14
,
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
are either dictatorial or a certificate. If they are dictatorial, we are done, so it remains to show that they cannot be a certificate.

Indeed, if
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
were a certificate then for some
i\in[m]
, the function
g_{i}
would be the constant
1
function. However, in that case
f_{i}
would be
\epsilon
-close to constant, contradicting the assumption that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are
\epsilon
-far from constant.
∎

3.3
Approximate polymorphisms (
m=n
)

In this section we complete the proof of
Theorem

1.1
by settling the case
m=n\geq 3
. In this case, we write
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
for
\mathsf{Surj}_{n,n}
; this is the set of all permutations of
[n]
, viewed as elements in
[n]^{n}
. Moreover,
\mu
is the uniform distribution over
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
, since this is the only distribution over
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
which is invariant under applying permutations of
[n]
.

The argument relies on the work of Alon et al.
[
ADFS04
]
, who use Hoffman’s bound to bound the size of independent sets in
K_{n}^{p}
, and to characterize independent sets of maximal and almost maximal size.

In order to make the argument more transparent, we start by sketching another proof of
Theorem

1.14
, which doesn’t rely on
Theorem

1.13
. We then use the same approach to prove
Theorem

1.1
.

3.3.1
Another proof of
Theorem

1.14

For
m\leq n
, let
\mathsf{Inj}_{m,n}\subseteq[n]^{m}
(the Injectivity predicate) consist of all vectors
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in[n]^{m}
where
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for
i\neq j
. We can view
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
as
\mathsf{Inj}_{n,n}
. Since injectivity is a pairwise condition, in some sense it suffices to understand the case
m=2
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
f\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
. Greenwell and Lovász
[
GL74
]
showed that if
f,f
constitute a polymorphism of
\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}
then
f
is a dictator. We describe one of their proofs.

For any
r\in[n]
, we can consider the function
f_{r}\colon[n]^{p}\to\{0,1\}
given by
f_{r}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{f(x)=r\}
. This function satisfies the following condition: if
x,y\in[n]^{p}
are such that
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}
for all
i\in[p]
, then
(f_{r}(x),f_{r}(y))\neq(1,1)
. Equivalently,
f_{r}
is the indicator function of an independent set in
K_{n}^{p}
. We say that
f_{r}
is
independent
.

Let
\mu(f_{r})
be the expectation of
f_{r}
with respect to the uniform distribution. Greenwell and Lovász show that if
f_{r}
is independent then
\mu(f_{r})\leq 1/n
, with equality iff
f_{r}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}=s\}
for some
k\in[p]
and
s\in[n]
. This easily implies that if
f,f
constitute a polymorphism of
\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}
then
f
is a dictator.

Alon et al.
[
ADFS04
]
gave a different proof of the inequality
\mu(f_{r})\leq 1/n
, using a technique known as Hoffman’s bound
[
Hof70
,
Fri08
,
Hae21
]
. Their proof shows moreover that if
\mu(f_{r})\geq 1/n-\epsilon
then
f_{r}
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to a function of the form
\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}=s\}
, that is,
\Pr[f_{r}(x)\neq\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}=s\}]=O(\epsilon)
. The latter bound is shown using an adaptation of the FKN theorem
[
FKN02
]
, a classical result in Boolean function analysis.

In contrast to the setting studied by Greenwell and Lovász and by Alon et al., in our case we have
n
different functions
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
(we use
g
rather than
f
to avoid a clash with the notation
f_{r}
). The argument of Alon et al. can be adapted to yield the following result.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
n\geq 3
.

Suppose that
g_{i,r},g_{j,r}\colon[n]^{p}\to\{0,1\}
are
cross-independent
: if
x,y\in[n]^{p}
are such that
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}
for all
i\in[p]
, then
(g_{i,r}(x),g_{j,r}(y))\neq(1,1)
. Then

(n-1)^{2}\mu(g_{i,r})\mu(g_{j,r})\leq(1-\mu(g_{i,r}))(1-\mu(g_{j,r})),

with equality iff
g_{i,r}(x)=g_{j,r}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}=s\}
for some
k\in[p]
and
s\in[n]
.

We prove a stronger result,
Lemma

3.13
, in
Section

3.3.2
.

In the rest of this subsection, we show how to derive
Theorem

1.14
for
m=n
using
Lemma

3.8
. The proof of
Theorem

1.1
for
m=n
follows the same approach.

Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
be a polymorphism of
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
for
n\geq 3
. For
i,r\in[n]
, we define

g_{i,r}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{g_{i}(x)=r\},\quad\mu_{i,r}=\mu(g_{i,r}).

If we put the numbers
\mu_{i,r}
in an
n\times n
matrix, then we obtain a bistochastic matrix
M
. Indeed, by definition
\mu_{i,r}\geq 0
; for all
i\in[n]
we have

\sum_{r\in[n]}\mu_{i,r}=1

by definition; and for all
r\in[n]
we have

\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i,r}=1,

(1)

since the left-hand side is the expected number of
r
’s in
g_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},\dots,x^{(p)}_{1}),\dots,g_{n}(x^{(1)}_{n},\dots,x^{(p)}_{n})
, where the vectors
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\in\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
are drawn uniformly at random.

Lemma

3.8
gives an additional constraint: if
i\neq j
then

(n-1)^{2}\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}\leq(1-\mu_{i,r})(1-\mu_{j,r}),

and so

n(n-2)\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}\leq 1-\mu_{i,r}-\mu_{j,r}.

(2)

Using these constraints, we are able to determine all possible values of the matrix
M
, and deduce the structure
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
.

Fix
i
and
r
. Summing
Equation

2
over all
j\neq i
and using
Equation

1
, we obtain

n(n-2)\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r})\leq(n-1)(1-\mu_{i,r})-(1-\mu_{i,r})=(n-2)(1-\mu_{i,r}).

Therefore

\mu_{i,r}=1\quad\text{or}\quad\mu_{i,r}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Now fix
r
and vary
i
. In view of
Equation

1
, we see that one of the following cases holds:

(i)

For some
i_{r}
we have
\mu_{i_{r},r}=1
and
\mu_{i,r}=0
for
i\neq i_{r}
.

In this case,

g_{i_{r}}=r.

(ii)

For all
i
we have
\mu_{i,r}=1/n
.

The equality clause of
Lemma

3.8
implies that in this case there are
k_{r}\in[p]
and
s_{r}\in[n]
such that for all
i
,

g_{i}(x)=r\text{ iff }x_{k_{r}}=s_{r}.

If the second case happens for any
r
then
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
are all non-constant. Considering all values of
r
, we deduce that one of the following cases holds:

(i)

For every
r
there exists
i_{r}
such that
g_{i_{r}}=r
. This is
Item

ii
of
Theorem

1.14
.

(ii)

For every
r
there exist
k_{r},s_{r}
such that for all
i
,

g_{i}(x)=r\text{ iff }x_{k_{r}}=s_{r}.

This results in a contradiction unless all
k_{r}
are equal and all
s_{r}
are different. This is
Item

i
of
Theorem

1.14
.

3.3.2
Proof of
Theorem

1.1
when
m=n

We actually prove a stronger result:
Theorem

1.6
holds for the predicate
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
when
n\geq 3
and
\mu
is the uniform distribution.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
n\geq 3
. There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
(depending on
n
) such that the following holds.

If
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
are an
\epsilon
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
(with respect to the uniform distribution), where
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, then one of the following cases holds:

(i)

There exists
k\in[p]
and a permutation
\pi
of
[n]
such that for all
i\in[n]
,

\Pr_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i}(x)\neq\pi(x_{k})]=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

(ii)

For each
j\in[n]
there exists
i\in[n]
such that

\Pr_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i}(x)\neq j]=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

In both cases, the probability is taken over the uniform distribution.

As in
Section

3.2
, this implies
Theorem

1.1
with
\delta=\Theta(\epsilon^{4})
.

In the remainder of this subsection, we prove
Theorem

3.9
.
Throughout the proof, we assume that
\epsilon
is “small enough” by setting
\epsilon_{0}
accordingly.

As in
Section

3.3.1
, define

f_{i,r}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{f_{i}(x)=r\},\quad\mu_{i,r}=\mu(f_{i,r}).

For
i\neq j
, the functions
f_{i,r},f_{j,r}
are
\epsilon
-cross-independent.

Definition 3.10

(Approximate cross-independence)
.

The functions
f_{i,r},f_{j,r}\colon[n]^{p}\to\{0,1\}
are
\epsilon
-cross-independent
if

\Pr_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\sim\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}}[f_{i,r}(x^{(1)}_{i},\dots,x^{(p)}_{i})=f_{j,r}(x^{(1)}_{j},\dots,x^{(p)}_{j})=1]\leq\epsilon,

where
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}
are drawn uniformly at random, and we index them using
i,j
.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\colon[n]^{p}\to[n]
are an
\epsilon
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
then for every
i\neq j
and
r
, the functions
f_{i,r},f_{j,r}
are
\epsilon
-cross-independent.

Proof.

Suppose that
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\sim\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
. If we remove all indices except for
i,j
, then we get samples from
\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}
instead.

If
f_{i,r}(x^{(1)}_{i},\dots,x^{(p)}_{i})=f_{j,r}(x^{(1)}_{j},\dots,x^{(p)}_{j})=1
then
f_{i}(x^{(1)}_{i},\dots,x^{(p)}_{i})=f_{j}(x^{(1)}_{j},\dots,x^{(p)}_{j})=r
, and so this happens with probability at most
\epsilon
.
∎

Similarly, we have the following analog of
Equation

1
.

Lemma 3.12
.

For every
r\in[n]
we have

\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i,r}=1\pm O(\epsilon).

Proof.

Define

N(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)})=\sum_{i\in[n]}f_{i,r}(x^{(1)}_{i},\dots,x^{(p)}_{i}),

which is the number of
r
’s in the “output”. Observe that

\Ex_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\sim\mathsf{Perm}_{n}}[N(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)})]=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i,r}.

By definition,
N
always lies in the range
\{0,\dots,n\}
. Since
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
are an
\epsilon
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Perm}_{n}
, we have
N=1
with probability at least
1-\epsilon
. The
lemma
immediately follows.
∎

The main technical step of the proof is a generalization of
Lemma

3.8
. The proof, which is essentially a version of Hoffman’s bound, uses Fourier analysis on the group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{r}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Suppose that
f_{i,r},f_{j,r}
are
\epsilon
-cross-independent. Then

(a)

We have

n(n-2)\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}\leq 1-\mu_{i,r}-\mu_{j,r}+O(\sqrt{\epsilon}).

(b)

If
\mu_{i,r},\mu_{j,r}\geq 1/n-\gamma
, where
0\leq\gamma\leq 1/n
, then there exist
k_{i,r}=k_{j,r}
and
s_{i,r}\neq s_{j,r}
such that

\Pr_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i,r}(x)\neq\mathbf{1}\{x_{k_{i,r}}=s_{i,r}\}]=O(\epsilon+\gamma),

and similarly for
j
.

Proof.

Setup.

Every function on
[n]^{p}
can be decomposed into its “degree
d
” parts:

f_{i,r}=\sum_{d=0}^{p}f_{i,r}^{=d},

and similarly for
f_{j,r}
; see
[
O’D14
, Chapter 8]
.

Each part
f_{i,r}^{=d}
belongs to the space
V^{=d}
of “pure degree
d
functions”. These spaces are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

\langle g,h\rangle=\Ex_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[g(x)h(x)],

where the expectation is with respect to the uniform distribution. This implies that

\langle f_{i,r},f_{j,r}\rangle=\sum_{d=0}^{p}\langle f_{i,r}^{=d},f_{j,r}^{=d}\rangle,

as well as

\|f_{i,r}\|^{2}=\sum_{d=0}^{p}\|f_{i,r}^{=d}\|^{2},

where
\|g\|=\sqrt{\langle g,g\rangle}
. Note that

\|f_{i,r}\|^{2}=\Ex_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i,r}(x)^{2}]=\mu_{i,r},

since
f_{i,r}
is Boolean (that is,
\{0,1\}
-valued).

The function
f_{i,r}^{=0}
is the constant function equal to
\mu(f_{i,r})
. Defining
f_{i,r}^{>d}=\sum_{e>d}f_{i,r}^{=e}
, this implies that

\|f_{i,r}^{>0}\|^{2}=\|f_{i,r}\|^{2}-\|f_{i,r}^{=0}\|^{2}=\mu_{i,r}-\mu_{i,r}^{2}=\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r}).

Formula from
[
ADFS04
]
.
Let
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\sim\mathsf{Inj}_{2,n}
. Alon et al.
[
ADFS04
, Claim 4.3]
proved the following formula:

\Ex[f_{i,r}(x^{(1)}_{i},\dots,x^{(p)}_{i})f_{j,r}(x^{(1)}_{j},\dots,x^{(p)}_{j})]=\sum_{d=0}^{p}\left(-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)^{d}\langle f_{i,r}^{=d},f_{j,r}^{=d}\rangle.

Since
f_{i,r}
and
f_{j,r}
are Boolean, the left-hand side is the probability that
f_{i,r}=f_{j,r}=1
. Therefore the assumption on
f_{i,r},f_{j,r}
implies

\epsilon\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}+\sum_{d=1}^{p}\left(-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)^{d}\langle f_{i,r}^{=d},f_{j,r}^{=d}\rangle.

(3)

Proof of
Item

a
.

If
\mu_{i,r}\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}
then the inequality trivially holds, since
1-\mu_{j,r}\geq 0
. Therefore we can assume that
\mu_{i,r},\mu_{j,r}\geq\sqrt{\epsilon}
.

Since
1/(n-1)\leq 1
,
Equation

3
implies that

\epsilon\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{d=1}^{p}\langle f_{i,r}^{=d},f_{j,r}^{=d}\rangle=\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\langle f_{i,r}^{>0},f_{j,r}^{>0}\rangle.

Cauchy–Schwarz implies that

\epsilon\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\|f_{i,r}^{>0}\|\|f_{j,r}^{>0}\|=\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sqrt{\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r})}\sqrt{\mu_{j,r}(1-\mu_{j,r})}.

Dividing by
\sqrt{\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}}\geq\sqrt{\epsilon}
, this gives

\sqrt{\epsilon}\geq\sqrt{\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sqrt{(1-\mu_{i,r})(1-\mu_{j,r})}.

Moving the second summand on the right to the left, multiplying by
n-1
, and squaring, this gives

\displaystyle(n-1)^{2}\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mu_{i,r})(1-\mu_{j,r})+(n-1)\sqrt{\epsilon}\bigl(2(1-\mu_{i,r})(1-\mu_{j,r})+(n-1)\sqrt{\epsilon}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu_{i,r})(1-\mu_{j,r})+O(\sqrt{\epsilon}).

Rearranging completes the proof.

Proof of
Item

b
.

To prove this
item
, we first show that
\|f_{i,r}^{>1}\|,\|f_{j,r}^{>1}\|
are small, and then appeal to
[
ADFS04
, Lemma 2.4]
.
Throughout the proof, we can assume that
\epsilon,\gamma
are small enough, since otherwise the
item
is trivial.

As in the proof of
Item

a
, we expand
Equation

3
, but this time we take out one more term:

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\langle f_{i,r}^{=1},f_{j,r}^{=1}\rangle-\frac{1}{(n-1)^{3}}\langle f_{i,r}^{>1},f_{j,r}^{>1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\|f_{i,r}^{=1}\|\|f_{j,r}^{=1}\|-\frac{1}{(n-1)^{3}}\|f_{i,r}^{>1}\|\|f_{j,r}^{>1}\|,

where the second inequality uses Cauchy–Schwarz (twice).

We would like to argue that
\|f_{i,r}^{=1}\|\approx\|f_{i,r}^{>0}\|
and similarly for
j
, and to this end we define
c_{i,r},c_{j,r}
by
\|f_{i,r}^{=1}\|=c_{i,r}\|f_{i,r}^{>0}\|
and similarly for
j
. Since
\|f_{i,r}^{>0}\|^{2}=\|f_{i,r}^{=1}\|^{2}+\|f_{i,r}^{>1}\|^{2}
, this implies that
\|f_{i,r}^{>1}\|=\sqrt{1-c_{i,r}^{2}}\|f_{i,r}^{>0}\|
. Altogether, we obtain

\epsilon\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sqrt{\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r})}\sqrt{\mu_{j,r}(1-\mu_{j,r})}\left(c_{i,r}c_{j,r}+\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\sqrt{1-c_{i,r}^{2}}\sqrt{1-c_{j,r}^{2}}\right).

In order to simplify this, we observe that
(1-c_{i,r}^{2})(1-c_{j,r}^{2})=(1-c_{i,r}c_{j,r})^{2}-(c_{i,r}-c_{j,r})^{2}
, implying that
(1-c_{i,r})^{2}(1-c_{j,r})^{2}\leq(1-c_{i,r}c_{j,r})^{2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sqrt{\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r})}\sqrt{\mu_{j,r}(1-\mu_{j,r})}\left(c_{i,r}c_{j,r}+\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}(1-c_{i,r}c_{j,r})\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i,r}\mu_{j,r}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sqrt{\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r})}\sqrt{\mu_{j,r}(1-\mu_{j,r})}\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\right)c_{i,r}c_{j,r}\right).

At this point we substitute the values of
\mu_{i,r},\mu_{j,r}
, obtaining

\epsilon+O(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\right)c_{i,r}c_{j,r}\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\right)(1-c_{i,r}c_{j,r}).

It follows that
c_{i,r}c_{j,r}\geq 1-O(\epsilon+\gamma)
, using
n\geq 3
. Since
c_{i,r},c_{j,r}\leq 1
, this implies that
c_{i,r}\geq 1-O(\epsilon+\gamma)
and so
\sqrt{1-c_{i,r}^{2}}=O(\sqrt{\epsilon+\gamma})
, and similarly for
j
. Therefore

\|f_{i,r}^{>1}\|^{2},\|f_{j,r}^{>1}\|^{2}=O(\epsilon+\gamma).

At this point we apply
[
ADFS04
, Lemma 2.4]
, which states that if
g\colon[n]^{p}\to\{0,1\}
satisfies
\Ex[g]=\mu
and
\|g^{>1}\|^{2}=\eta
then there exists a function
h\colon[n]^{p}\to\{0,1\}
, depending on a single coordinate, such that

\Pr[g\neq h]=O\left(\frac{\eta}{\mu-\mu^{2}-\eta}\right).

For small enough
\epsilon,\gamma
, this implies that there exist functions
h_{i,r},h_{j,r}
, each depending on a single coordinate, such that

\Pr[f_{i,r}\neq h_{i,r}]=O(\epsilon+\gamma),

and similarly for
j
.

Since
1/n-\gamma\leq\mu_{i,r}\leq 1/n+O(\sqrt{\epsilon})
, for small enough
\epsilon,\gamma
we see that
h_{i,r}
must be of the form

h_{i,r}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x_{k_{i,r}}=s_{i,r}\},

and similarly for
j
.

Observe that
h_{i,r},h_{j,r}
are
O(\epsilon+\gamma)
-cross-independent. For small enough
\epsilon,\gamma
, this implies that
k_{i,r}=k_{j,r}
and
s_{i,r}\neq s_{j,r}
.
∎

The next step is to determine the possible values of
\mu_{1,r},\dots,\mu_{n,r}
for each fixed
r
.

Lemma 3.14
.

For each
r\in[n]
, one of the following holds:

(i)

For some
i_{r}
we have
\mu_{i_{r},r}\geq 1-\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}
and
\mu_{i,r}=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon})
for all
i\neq i_{r}
.

(ii)

For all
i
we have

\mu_{i,r}=\frac{1}{n}\pm O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

Consequently, there exists an index
k_{r}\in[p]
and a permutation
\pi_{r}
on
[n]
such that for each
i\in[n]
,

\Pr_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i,r}(x)\neq\mathbf{1}\{x_{k_{r}}=\pi_{r}(i)\}]=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

Proof.

If
\mu_{i_{r},r}\geq 1-\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}
holds for some
i_{r}
then by
Lemma

3.12
we have
\mu_{i,r}=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon})
for all
i\neq i_{r}
, which puts us in
Item

i
.
In the rest of the proof, we assume that
\mu_{i,r}\leq 1-\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}
for all
i
, and show that
Item

ii
holds.

Fix
i
. Summing
Item

a
of
Lemma

3.13
for all
j\neq i
and using
Lemma

3.12
, we obtain

n(n-2)\mu_{i,r}(1-\mu_{i,r}\pm O(\epsilon))\leq(n-1)(1-\mu_{i,r})-(1-\mu_{i,r}\pm O(\epsilon))+O(\sqrt{\epsilon}),

which simplifies to

(1-\mu_{i,r})\cdot\mu_{i,r}\leq(1-\mu_{i,r})\cdot\frac{1}{n}+O(\sqrt{\epsilon}).

Since
1-\mu_{i,r}\geq\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}
, this implies that

\mu_{i,r}\leq\frac{1}{n}+O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

The same inequality holds for all
j\neq i
. Therefore
Lemma

3.12
implies that

1-O(\epsilon)\leq\mu_{i,r}+\sum_{j\neq i}\mu_{j,r}\leq\mu_{i,r}+\frac{n-1}{n}+O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}),

and so

\frac{1}{n}-O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon})\leq\mu_{j,r}.

The remaining claim follows directly from
Item

b
of
Lemma

3.13
.
∎

At this point we complete the proof much as in
Section

3.3.1
. If the second case happens for some
r
then each value of
f_{i}
is attained with probability at least
1/n-O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon})
. This conflicts with the first case, for small enough
\epsilon
. Therefore either the first case holds for all
r
, or the second case holds for all
r
.

If the first case holds for all
r
then
Item

ii
of the
theorem
holds. From now on, we assume that the second case holds for all
r
.

We start by showing that all
k_{r}
are equal. Indeed, if
k_{r}\neq k_{s}
then
x_{k_{r}}=\pi_{r}(1)
and
x_{k_{s}}=\pi_{s}(1)
with probability
1/n^{2}
, and so we obtain a contradiction for small enough
\epsilon
by considering
f_{1,r},f_{1,s}
. Thus all
k_{r}
are equal to some
k
. Similarly, considering
f_{i}
, we see that
\pi_{r}(i)\neq\pi_{s}(i)
for all
r\neq s
, and so there must exist a permutation
\rho_{i}
such that
\pi_{r}(i)=\rho_{i}(r)
for all
r
. Thus

\Pr_{x\sim[n]^{p}}[f_{i}(x)\neq\rho_{i}^{-1}(x_{k})]=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

It follows that

\Pr_{y\sim\mathsf{Perm}_{n}}[(\rho_{1}^{-1}(y_{1}),\dots,\rho_{n}^{-1}(y_{n}))\notin\mathsf{Perm}_{n}]=O(\sqrt[4]{\epsilon}).

If
\rho_{i}\neq\rho_{j}
, say
\rho_{i}(r)\neq\rho_{j}(r)
, then
(y_{i},y_{j})=(\rho_{i}(r),\rho_{j}(r))
occurs with probability
1/(n(n-1))
. However, in that case
\rho_{i}^{-1}(y_{i})=\rho_{j}^{-1}(y_{j})
, and we reach a contradiction for small enough
\epsilon
.
Thus all
\rho_{i}
are equal, and so
Item

i
holds.

4
Equivalence relations

4.1
Exact polymorphisms

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.17
. We rely on the following
lemma
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The forward direction of
Theorem

1.17
holds when
m=3
.

Proof.

The predicate
\mathsf{Equiv}_{3}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{3}
consists of all triples whose Hamming weight is not exactly
2
.

[
Fil26
, Theorem 1.6(5)]
, applied with
m=3
and
w=1
, states that the polymorphisms of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{3}
come in two types:

(i)

All
f_{\{i,j\}}
are equal, and the common value is
f(x)=\bigwedge_{i\in S}x_{i}
for some
S\subseteq[p]
.

This corresponds to the complete equivalence relation on
[3]
.

(ii)

At least two of the
f_{\{i,j\}}
are constant zero.

If
f_{\{I,J\}}
is the remaining function, then this corresponds to the equivalence relation in which the only relation is
I\sim J
. ∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.17
, forward direction.

Let
(f_{\{i,j\}})_{i<j}
be a polymorphism of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
.

Define a relation on
[m]
as follows:
i\sim i
for all
i
, and for
i<j
, let
i\sim j
if
f_{\{i,j\}}
is not constant zero.

We claim that this is an equivalence relation. Indeed, suppose that
f_{\{i,j\}}
and
f_{\{j,k\}}
are both not constant zero. Applying
Lemma

4.1
to
f_{\{i,j\}},f_{\{j,k\}},f_{\{i,k\}}
, we see that
f_{\{i,j\}}=f_{\{j,k\}}=f_{\{i,k\}}
, and so
f_{\{i,k\}}
is also not constant zero.

The first item in the
theorem
holds by construction. For the second item, let
A
be an equivalence class of
\sim
of size at least
3
. For any
i,j,k
, applying
Lemma

4.1
as before implies that for some set
S\subseteq[p]
,

f_{\{i,j\}}(x)=f_{\{j,k\}}(x)=f_{\{i,k\}}(x)=\bigwedge_{s\in S}x_{s}.

To complete the proof, we show that
f_{\{\ell,r\}}=f_{\{i,j\}}
for all
\ell,r\in A
. This is clear if
\{\ell,r\}=\{i,j\}
. If
|\{\ell,r\}\cap\{i,j\}|=1
, say
\ell=i
, then this follows by applying
Lemma

4.1
to
f_{\{i,j\}},f_{\{i,r\}},f_{\{j,r\}}
. Finally, if
\{i,j\}
and
\{\ell,r\}
are disjoint then this follows by applying
Lemma

4.1
to
f_{\{i,\ell\}},f_{\{i,r\}},f_{\{\ell,r\}}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.17
, backward direction.

Consider an arbitrary equivalence relation
\sim
on
[m]
, and functions
(f_{\{i,j\}})_{i<j}
satisfying the properties in the statement of the
theorem
.

Let
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(p)}\in\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
, and let
x_{\{i,j\}}=f_{\{i,j\}}(x^{(1)}_{\{i,j\}},\dots,x^{(p)}_{\{i,j\}})
. We need to show that
x\in\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
.

Suppose that
x_{\{i,j\}}=x_{\{j,k\}}=1
, where
i,j,k
are all different. We need to show that
x_{\{i,k\}}=1
.

The assumption implies that
i\sim j
and
j\sim k
, and so
i,j,k
belong to the same equivalence class of
\sim
. Consequently, there exists a set
S\subseteq[p]
such that

f_{\{i,j\}}(y)=f_{\{j,k\}}(y)=f_{\{i,k\}}(y)=\bigwedge_{s\in S}y_{s}.

Since
x_{\{i,j\}}=x_{\{j,k\}}=1
, we have
x^{(s)}_{\{i,j\}}=x^{(s)}_{\{j,k\}}=1
for all
s\in S
. Therefore
x^{(s)}_{\{i,k\}}=1
for all
s\in S
, and so
x_{\{i,k\}}=1
.
∎

4.2
Approximate polymorphisms

In this section we deduce
Theorem

1.16
by combining
Theorem

1.6
and
Theorem

1.17
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.16
.

Let
\epsilon_{0}
be the parameter given by
Theorem

1.6
for
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
and
\mu
.
Given
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, we let
\delta>0
be the parameter given by
Theorem

1.6
. Since
\mu
is invariant under permutations of
[m]
, we have
\mu|_{\{i,j\}}=\nu
for all
i<j
.

Suppose that
(f_{\{i,j\}})_{i<j}\colon\{0,1\}^{p}\to\{0,1\}
are a
\delta
-approximate polymorphism of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
with respect to
\mu
, and moreover
\Pr_{\nu}[f_{\{i,j\}}=0]<1-\epsilon
for all
i<j
.

Theorem

1.6
implies that there is a polymorphism
g_{\{i,j\}}\colon\{0,1\}^{p}\to\{0,1\}
of
\mathsf{Equiv}_{m}
such that
\Pr_{\nu}[f_{\{i,j\}}\neq g_{\{i,j\}}]\leq\epsilon
for all
i<j
.

Theorem

1.17
describes the possible forms of
g_{\{i,j\}}
, parametrized by an equivalence relation on
[m]
. We claim that this equivalence relation must be complete. Indeed, if
i\nsim j
then
g_{\{i,j\}}
is constant zero, and so
\Pr_{\nu}[f_{\{i,j\}}=0]\geq 1-\epsilon
, contradicting the assumption.

It follows that there exists
S\subseteq[p]
such that

g_{\{i,j\}}(x)=\bigwedge_{s\in S}x_{s}

for all
i<j
, from which the
theorem
immediately follows.
∎

5
Discussion

Some impossibility theorems in the literature concern a situation in which the individual votes satisfy one predicate, and the final outcome should satisfy a different predicate, potentially over a different alphabet. Examples include:

•

Gibbard
[
Gib69
]
considered the situation in which each individual specifies a weak order on
[m]
, and the desired outcome is a partial order on
[m]
. These correspond to the following predicates (in reverse order):

–

\mathsf{Part}_{m}\subseteq\{0,1,*\}^{\binom{m}{2}}
is the set of partial orders, encoded as specifying, for any
i<j
, whether
i\succ j
,
i\prec j
, or neither. We require transitivity:

i\succ j\text{ and }j\succ k\text{ implies }i\succ k.

–

\mathsf{Weak}_{m}\subseteq\{0,1,*\}^{\binom{m}{2}}
is the set of weak orders, which are partial orders specifying the additional axiom

i\succ j\text{ implies }i\succ k\text{ or }k\succ j\text{ for each }k\neq i,j.

Gibbard shows that the only unanimous polymorphisms in this setting are weak oligarchies: there exists a set
S\subseteq[p]
such that

(a)

If
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\succ}}b
for all
k\in S
then
a\succ b
.

(b)

If
a\succ b
then no
k\in S
satisfies
b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\succ}}a
.

Here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\succ}},\dots,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\succ}}
are the individual weak orders, and
\succ
is the aggregated partial order.

•

Dokow and Holzman
[
DH10b
]
considered the situation in which each individual specifies a linear order on
[m]
, and the desired outcome is a partial order on
[m]
. We encode linear orders as:

–

\mathsf{Lin}_{m}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\binom{m}{2}}
is the intersection
\mathsf{Part}_{m}\cap\{0,1\}^{\binom{m}{2}}
.

They showed that the only unanimous polymorphisms in this setting are oligarchies: the outcome is always a linear order, and there exists a set
S\subseteq[p]
such that
a\succ b
iff
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\succ}}b
for all
k\in S
.

This extension of the concept of polymorphism appears in the literature on promise CSPs
[
AGH17
,
BG21
,
BBKO21
]
. In this setting, we have two predicates
P,Q
, possibly on different alphabets, such that
P
is more restrictive than
Q
, in the sense that there is a coordinate-by-coordinate mapping from
P
to
Q
. A
(P,Q)
polymorphism is defined as in
Definition

1.2
, using
P
on the left and
Q
on the right.

Theorem

1.6
applies in this setting as well, where the flexibility condition is only needed for
P
(and is required even if
P
is a predicate on
\{0,1\}
). However, we don’t have an analog of
Theorem

1.13
. Indeed, given the examples above, it seems that we should aim for oligarchies rather than dictators.

A different shortcoming of our framework is the unspecified dependence between
\epsilon
and
\delta
in
Theorem

1.6
. In contrast to Mossel’s quantitative Arrow theorem
[
Mos12
]
, where the dependence is linear, the dependence in
Theorem

1.6
is of tower type. We conjecture that the dependence can be improved to polynomial or even linear.
