 ABSTRACT

Much of the radionuclide transport from a molten salt reactor (MSR) during operation may arise from
aerosols generated in the pump bowl or from condensation of vaporized species. The Visualizing
Insoluble Species and Transport of Aerosols (VISTA) apparatus was used to investigate such aerosol
generation, bubble transport, and insoluble species behavior in a molten LiCl-KCl eutectic salt system.
The VISTA apparatus provided an optically accessible quartz test cell coupled with simultaneous
shadowgraph imaging, particle tracking velocimetry (PTV)/particle image velocimetry (PIV), laser-
induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS), and high-resolution electrical low-pressure impactor (HR-
ELPI+) measurements. Experiments examined the effects of sparging orientation, sparge gas flow rate,
and cover gas sweep rate on bubble behavior and aerosol transport. Baseline tests were conducted with
LiCl-KCl, followed by tests in which 2.6 µm Ni powder was introduced as an insoluble species. In a first-
of-a-kind experiment, hybrid PIV-PTV measurements captured the motion of dispersed aerosols within
the test cell cover gas region. These measurements led to velocity estimations. The HR-ELPI+
measurements indicated that the cumulative aerosol response generally increased with both sparge and
sweep flow rate. Number-based particle size distributions were dominated by an ultrafine mode, with
geometric mean diameters (GMDs) of approximately 0.024 µm to 0.027 µm, whereas the estimated mass
distributions were dominated by larger particles with GMDs of approximately 4.7 µm to 4.8 µm. These
results demonstrate that a relatively small population of coarse particles indicate aerosol mass even when
ultrafine particles dominate the particle number distribution. LIBS detected Ni spectral features during
sparging, with signal intensity increasing as the Ni concentration increased from 0.01 mol % to
0.03 mol %. Cascade impactor analysis further indicated that Ni-containing salt enabled the transport of
greater mass than Ni-free salt. The increased aerosol mass collected was higher than what could be solely
attributed to the higher density of Ni. These results support the use of combined optical, spectroscopic,
and aerosol measurements for characterizing radionuclide transport and informing MSR off-gas and
source term models. Experience gained from VISTA experiments will be used to prepare for the more
challenging fluoride salt analyses, for which the use of quartz cells is not feasible. These experiments
have started to address the challenge of measuring the transport of particles in molten salt media—a
challenge relevant to the tracking of noble metal fission products in MSRs.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Molten salt reactors (MSRs) offer several potential advantages over conventional light-water reactors.
These advantages include high thermal efficiency, passive safety characteristics, and low-pressure
operation. However, these benefits are accompanied by challenges associated with circulating salt,
particularly in liquid-fueled systems where fission products may remain dissolved, deposit on structural
surfaces, evolve into the cover gas, or become associated with aerosols and particulates. Understanding
these transport pathways is essential for informing reactor safety, off-gas system design, and licensing [1,
2].

The off-gas system serves as part of the pressure boundary in an MSR and must retain radionuclides
released from the salt. Aerosols are important because they can transport salt-borne radionuclides,
corrosion products, and other nonvolatile species through the gas space and into downstream components
[3]. Their behavior depends strongly on their formation mechanisms and particle size distribution (PSD).
Fine aerosols formed by condensation or film-drop production are more readily entrained in the cover gas,
whereas large droplets generated by jet-bubble bursting or splashing tend to deposit near the source or
fragment into smaller particles. Characterizing aerosol generation and transport is therefore necessary for
predicting source terms, designing and evaluating off-gas treatment system performance, and supporting
safety analysis. Aerosol formation in molten salt systems is closely linked to two-phase flow phenomena
such as gas sparging, bubble rise, and bubble bursting at the free surface. These processes control the

 1

release of salt and other species into the gas phase. Experimental data on bubble-mediated aerosol
generation, PSDs, and insoluble species transport can support validation and refinement of the mechanical
aerosolization models used by industry source term analysis [4].

Within the US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) Advanced Reactor
Technologies (ART) MSR Program, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is focused on optical and
spectroscopic methods for tracking surrogate fission products and characterizing aerosol behavior and
transport. Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) has been shown to detect noble gases and
aerosolized salt species. The LIBS technique has also been demonstrated on large-scale molten salt loops
as a practical method of sampling bulk salt and gas composition in real time [5]. Shadowgraph imaging
has been used to measure bubble transport and droplet ejection with high spatial and temporal resolution
[6, 7], while particle image velocimetry (PIV) / particle tracking velocimetry (PTV) are nonintrusive
laser-based measurement techniques for acquiring fluid and particle flow velocities [8].

This report summarizes recent efforts made during FY 2026 in support of the DOE-NE work package,
26OR060201 Sensor Development Demonstration Related to MSR Monitoring Systems, to understand
aerosol generation and detection, as well as insoluble species behavior, in a molten salt system. The
results presented not only support our understanding of aerosols and insoluble species behavior but also
serve as surrogate data to improve understanding of radionuclide transport, development of robust
monitoring methods, and validation of models for use in MSR safety analyses.

2. VISUALIZING INSOLUBLE SPECIES AND TRANSPORT OF AEROSOLS EXPERIMENT

An apparatus for Visualizing Insoluble Species and Transport of Aerosols (VISTA) was employed
following upgrades to an existing system [6]. A eutectic salt composed of 60 mol % LiCl and 40 mol %
KCl was selected because of its relatively low melting temperature (approximately 350 °C) and
compatibility with quartz glass. This salt also exhibits a low Morton number, providing a useful lower-
bound case for molten salts of interest and a regime for which limited literature data are available.

2.1 SALT PREPARATION AND STORAGE TANK

The salt was prepared in an inert glovebox by loading a glassy carbon crucible with the salt constituents,
LiCl and KCl (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥ 99%), for a total mass of 1,865 g, corresponding to approximately
1,000 mL of eutectic salt. The powder mixture was stirred to promote homogeneity, after which the
crucible was placed in a sealed storage tank maintained under Airgas ultrahigh-purity (UHP) argon to
minimize oxygen and moisture exposure. The salt reservoir was positioned below the visualization
chamber. The lower storage tank was constructed from SS316 stainless steel with an inner diameter of
152 mm and a height of 333 mm. Four 400 W high-temperature heaters were mounted externally and
wired in parallel to two adjustable power supplies, allowing independent control of the upper and lower
tank regions. Three N-type thermocouples were attached to the outer wall to monitor temperature: one at
the bottom, and two along the side wall separated vertically by 150 mm.

The storage tank flange incorporated two feedthroughs serving as the vessel inlet and outlet. The primary
inlet had a diameter of 12.7 mm and contained a concentric 6.35 mm transfer tube. The annular gap
between the feedthrough and transfer line was used to introduce gas into the tank headspace. Both
feedthroughs were located approximately 140 mm above the liquid level. The transfer dip tube extended
to within 15 mm of the crucible bottom. A K-type SS316 sheathed thermocouple was immersed to the
same depth and positioned 50 mm from the transfer tube. Gas flow was directed through a manifold using

 3

Alicat Scientific, Inc. mass flow controllers. Tank pressure was monitored with an analog gauge and
protected by a 1,702 mbar relief valve, while a 1,033 mbar check valve on the outlet prevented air ingress.

2.2 VISUALIZATION CHAMBER

An upper viewing chamber was designed to provide optical access while maintaining elevated and
spatially uniform temperatures around the quartz test cell. The chamber was formed by an alloy SS316
six-way cross with borosilicate windows mounted on four side flanges for optical imaging. Slots
measuring 19.8 mm × 76.2 mm were machined into the top flange, and slots measuring
19.8 mm × 44.4 mm were machined into the bottom flange to accommodate quartz cell feedthroughs.
Heating was supplied by six 250 W high-temperature tape heaters (one for each of the flange tube
extensions) mounted on the chamber exterior. A separate 400 W heater was used for the transfer line. The
six heaters were grouped in parallel pairs on independent power supplies, with the transfer-line heater
being individually wired and controlled. A total of 11 K-type thermocouples monitored the upper
visualization chamber: 6 recorded the window temperatures as well as the top and bottom flanges, 1
recorded the exterior center wall, 2 recorded the transfer line, 1 recorded the furnace interior temperature,
and 1 monitored the interior quartz temperature/salt. The apparatus was insulated with 25.4 mm ceramic
fiber and enclosed in aluminum sheet metal to reduce heat loss and contain loose insulation fibers.

2.3 QUARTZ TEST CELL

A custom quartz test cell was fabricated in-house by scientific glass blowers to allow for direct
visualization of bubble transport, molten salt liquid–gas interface, and aerosols in the headspace. The cell
was formed from four flat quartz plates (3.18 mm thick) joined into a rectangular prism measuring
63.5 mm in width and 254 mm in length. Quartz tube extensions were welded to both the top and bottom
of the cell. The top included one centered 12.7 mm outer diameter tube and two off-center 9.5 mm outer
diameter tubes, which were used for internal temperature measurement, cover gas inlets, and cover gas
outlet. Two quartz tube (9.5 mm) extensions were attached to the bottom, one centered and one off-center.

The storage tank was connected to the quartz cell by a 6.35 mm SS316 transfer tube. This line was joined
to the glass feedthrough using a double-seal approach. The first seal was formed within the glass
extension, which was locally constricted to interface with a graphite cone. A welded ring on the SS316
tube compressed the graphite cone into the quartz constriction, thereby creating a seal and preventing
direct metal-to-glass contact. This design also eliminated the need for threaded engagement at the high-
temperature interface. The second seal differed slightly between the center gas bubbler port and the off-
center port. For the off-center transfer line, a conventional SS316 compression fitting with a graphite cone
was used, with the first seal minimizing molten salt exposure to the fitting. The centered extension was
not heated at the second lower seal and therefore employed a conventional Teflon ACE-glass threaded
connection.

2.4 BUBBLE INJECTION AND GAS HANDLING

A series of SS316 bubble injectors were fabricated to sparge the molten salt from the top and bottom as
deemed necessary. Each bubbler had an outer diameter of 3.18 mm and inner diameter of
1.4 mm ± 0.15 mm. The gas bubblers were inserted through the centered quartz feedthroughs using the
same double seal method described in Section 2.3. Gas delivery was controlled using a manifold supplied
with UHP argon. An adjustable pressure regulator and series of mass flow controllers mounted on a
manifold were used to control gas flow and to pressurize the lower tank for pneumatic transfer of molten
LiCl-KCl into the visualization chamber. The upper and lower chambers were mounted to an aluminum
frame, and Plexiglas panels were installed on all four sides to prevent external disturbances during
operation.

2.5.1 Shadowgraph

The optical shadowgraph technique was used to visualize and quantify gas bubbles rising and bursting
through the molten salt eutectic. In shadowgraph imaging, variations in the refractive index across the
bubble–salt interface refract transmitted light, producing a high-contrast image and enabling nonintrusive
measurements of bubble shape, position, and motion. Images of bubbles rising through the salt were
captured at 24 Hz and 2,000 Hz using a Phantom 440L camera. The camera field of view was centered on
the bubble injection location and focused on a vertical tube inserted through the top of the visualization
cell. The tube had a known outer diameter of 3.18 mm ± 0.025 mm and was used to establish the pixel-
to-millimeter conversion for image calibration. Image processing was performed using Python and
ImageJ image analysis platforms. Processing included determining the region of interest that would
exclude the liquid-level interface, background subtraction, image binarization, edge detection, perimeter
and equivalent diameter calculation, and centroid identification [7]. Figure 3 shows the application of
shadowgraph to capture the bubble behavior when the molten salt is sparged in downward and upward
directions.

2.5.2 Particle Tracking Velocimetry/Particle Image Velocimetry

Nonintrusive, laser-based particle tracking velocimetry (PTV) and particle image velocimetry (PIV) were
used to characterize molten salt motion and aerosol behavior within the quartz test cell. These techniques
are widely used [8, 9] in experimental fluid mechanics because they provide spatially resolved
measurements without the need to probe and disturb the flow intrusively. PIV provides a Eulerian
measurement of the fluid velocity field, whereas PTV provides a Lagrangian measurement of the motion
of individual particles. Particles in the molten salt and aerosols in the sweep cover gas were illuminated
within a planar measurement region using a laser sheet, and sequential images were recorded at a
prescribed frame rate.

 6

The PTV processing algorithms were based on the multiparametric particle tracking method implemented
in the PRANA codes [10, 11]. Several steps of image preprocessing (e.g., background image intensity
subtraction, top-hat filter, and Gaussian smoothing) were performed to reduce the effect of salt bubbles
and to improve the tracking. From the resulting images, particle images were identified using a dynamic
threshold pixel intensity of 10. The particle image sizes were then estimated using a Gaussian fit [12].
The postprocessed images, which have removed noise and reflections, are used for to determine the
velocities. A hybrid PIV-PTV approach was applied to improve the tracking efficiency of the PTV
algorithm. The multiparametric tracking algorithm utilized the particle size, intensity, previously
estimated position, and currently estimated position to identify the optimal particle match. Tracking of
particle images employed a 20-pixel search radius in subsequent images, along with pair matching
weights of 0.5 (range of 0–1) for particle-image size and maximum intensity. Flow field statistics (see
Figure 4) were calculated from datasets of PTV velocity vectors divided into constant spatial regions of
128 × 128 pixels (9.856 × 9.856 mm) with 50% overlap, yielding a final pass vector spacing equal to
32 pixels (2.46 mm) in the x– and y-directions. Figure 4 demonstrates the image processing workflow in
preparation for PIV-PTV.

In both the PIV and PTV configurations, a dual-head Nd:YLF 527 nm with a maximum power of 45 W
per head at 3,000 Hz was used to illuminate the test cell. A lens configuration with an optical arm and
collimator formed an approximately 1.5 mm thick vertical laser sheet in the region of interest near the
central plane of the quartz cell test section. The scattered light from the aerosols was recorded by the
same high-resolution camera used for the shadowgraphs. Image sequences were recorded and processed
using in-house algorithms for PTV and LaVision DAVIS software for PIV, including image
preprocessing, particle identification, background correction, displacement estimation, trajectory linking,
and removal of spurious vectors or tracks. The measurement configuration allowed molten salt, sweep
gas, and dispersed aerosol velocities to be acquired simultaneously within the VISTA apparatus.

2.5.3 Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy

The LIBS setup for this study used a mobile LIBS platform with a modular design, allowing various
sample stream configurations and spectrometers. The LIBS system used a 1,064 nm nanosecond Nd:YAG

 7

laser fired at 50–150 mJ per shot at 10 Hz (150 Nano-LG, Litron). The laser pulse was passed through a
beam expansion module before being focused through an angled window to the sample stream. The
angled window prevented damage to the optics from back reflections and sealed the optics from the
sample cell. The spectrometer used was a compact multichannel spectrometer with broad elemental
coverage (6-channel 4096CL, Avantes). The mobile LIBS platform was transported nominally 0.6 km
between buildings for testing on the VISTA apparatus (Figure 1) and did not require realignment after the
move. Spectra were measured on the spectrometer with delay and integration times of 15 µs and 1,000 µs,
respectively. The inlet and outlet of the LIBS cell were plumbed and operated under various conditions,
as described in section 2.5.4. The back of the LIBS cell was outfitted with a purge port to allow the cell to
be purged independently with UHP argon.

2.5.4 High-Resolution Electrical Low-Pressure Impactor

A Dekati Ltd. high-resolution electrical low-pressure impactor (HR-ELPI+) was used to collect and
measure airborne aerosol PSDs in real time with up to 500 size classes at 10 Hz resolution. The
instrument operates by first imparting a known positive electrical charge using a unipolar corona charger.
The charged aerosol then passes through a 14-stage cascade impactor, in which particles are separated
according to aerodynamic diameter. Each impactor stage is electrically isolated and connected to a
sensitive electrometer. As charged particles are collected on an individual stage, they generate an

 8

electrical current that is measured by the corresponding electrometer channel. The measured current is
proportional to the particle number concentration associated with that aerodynamic size fraction. The
HR-ELPI+ provides measurements spanning particle diameters from 6 nm to 10 µm. The impacted
aerosols are collected onto foils or filters for subsequent chemical analysis. This configuration enables
simultaneous real-time aerosol size distribution measurements and posttest characterization of particle
composition by aerodynamic size class. The sparge mechanism orientation (upward vs. downward),
sparge rate, and sweep rate effects on aerosols’ behavior and transport were investigated. Data on the
aerosol concentration were recorded; sparge and sweep rates ranged from 0 standard liters per minute
(SLPM) to 1 SLPM, respectively. Between each test condition, flow and aerosol generation were allowed
to stabilize for a minimum of 60 s before recording data. The sparge, sweep, and concentration contour
plots were generated from time-resolved PSD measurements collected using the HR-ELPI+. The 500-bin
resolved size distribution data were corrected and sorted by particle diameter and timestamp. HEPA-
filtered air and UHP argon were used as blanks. The number concentration for each measurement was
calculated by summing the concentrations across all retained particle size bins. The mass concentration
was then calculated by multiplying the particle number in each bin by the estimated mass of a spherical
particle of that diameter, assuming a LiCl-KCl particle density of 2,000 kg/m3. Because the particle mass
scales are proportional to the cube of particle diameter, mp ∝ Dp3, the mass metric is more sensitive than
the number concentration to the presence, entrainment, or transport of relatively large particles,
agglomerates, and droplets. The contour response for each test case was the sum of the time-resolved
concentrations. Thus, the plotted values represent cumulative sampled aerosol response over 90 s. The
data collected during the defined test condition values were interpolated to a regular grid using cubic
interpolation and are shown in Figure 6. The markers represent measured operating conditions, whereas
the colored surface between markers is an interpolated estimate.

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The total number concentration contour (Figure 6) shows an overall increase in aerosol response toward
both higher sparge and sweep flow rates. This result is most likely due to a coupled aerosol generation
and transport mechanism. Increasing the sparge rate increases gas injection, bubble formation, liquid
recirculation, turbulence, and gas–liquid interfacial disturbances. These effects can increase the
generation of droplets by bubble bursting [13], promote suspension of deposited or settled particles, and
increase particles or liquid droplet entrainment into the headspace. Greater sparge flow rates may
therefore increase the aerosol source strength. Increasing sweep flow rate is expected to increase
efficiency with which aerosols are removed from the headspace and transported through the sampling
system. At a low sweep flow rate, aerosols may experience greater residence times and therefore greater
losses from gravitational settling, surface deposition, agglomeration, or deposition within tubing and
fittings. An increase in the sweep rate can reduce these losses by decreasing residence time and increasing
advective transport in the headspace. In addition, cover gas sweep rates may increase shear near the liquid
surface and aid entrainment of droplets or particles that would otherwise remain within the vessel.

Following evaluation of the sparge and sweep gas on aerosol generation within an uncontaminated
system, Ni particles (Sigma-Aldrich, Ni ≥ 99.9% metals basis, average particle size 2.2–3.0 µm) were
injected directly into the molten salt visualization section via a 6.35 mm tube pressurized with argon. The
tube, which was connected to a series of ball valves, was preloaded in an inert glovebox before being
directly attached to the VISTA apparatus and submerged in the salt. The initial Ni injection was set to
0.01 mol %, and tests were carried out at a constant sparge flow rate of 1.0 SLPM while the sweep flow
rate was varied between 0.25 SLPM and 0.5 SLPM. The Ni concentration was increased to 0.03 mol %,
and a final test was conducted at a sparge flow rate of 1.0 SLPM and sweep flow rate of 0.5 SLPM. Table
1 gives the selected conditions in which the transport of Ni particles were evaluated, based on the
sparge/sweep rate sensitivity performed in section 2.5.4 using the HR-ELPI+.

3.1 AEROSOL TRACKING AND VELOCIMETRY

The PTV measurements focused on the transport of aerosols (>100 µm) generated from the sparging of
the molten salt and dispersed in the cover gas. Unlike PIV, which determines the average displacement of
a group of tracer particles within interrogation windows, PTV identifies and follows individual particles
over successive image frames. This Lagrangian approach provides the particle size, centroid location,
trajectory, and displacement of each tracked particle. Particle velocities were calculated from the change
in particle position between frames, and the resulting trajectories were used to understand transport and
flow behavior within the molten salt and in the head space. As illustrated in Figure 1 and Figure 4, the

 10

image plane is located near the center of the quartz test section and above the salt liquid level. Figure 7
shows the PSD and movement of particles in the headspace for a single case, with the sweep gas entering
from the top right and then recirculating above the salt liquid interface to exit through the top left of the
figure. The green and red vectors represent the instantaneous fluid and aerosol velocities, respectively.

For the upward sparge condition shown, with the salt liquid level located below the imaging plane and the
PIV-PTV field representing the cover gas space, the velocity vectors indicate how aerosolized salt is
entrained from the bursting of bubbles, carried upward, and ultimately directed toward the approximate
outlet. These large aerosols increase the risk of plugging in reactor systems because large aerosols are
more likely to deposit near flow restrictions. The measured average velocity identifies regions of
recirculation or low velocity. Comparing velocity fields with a fixed sparge rate while increasing the
sweep rate primarily results in enhanced gas removal and downstream transport to the LIBS and
HR-ELPI+ sensors. The entrance length of the cover gas increased with greater flow rates, allowing more
sweeping of aerosols near the liquid surface. The observed downward flow near the sparge tube reflects
oscillations due to bubble bursting events that entrain surrounding aerosols.

When correlated with the LIBS and HR-ELPI+ data, the PIVPTV results will help distinguish whether an
increase in aerosol signal arises from increased generation of aerosols, improved transport of aerosols
from the vessel, or a combination of both.

3.2 TRANSPORT PSD

The transported PSDs were multimodal and exhibited distinct number (Figure 8) and mass (Figure 9)
dominant size regimes. Across the tested conditions, the number-density PSDs were dominated by
ultrafine particles. Number geometric mean diameters (GMDs) ranged from 0.0239 µm to 0.0265 µm,
and number geometric standard deviations (GSDs) ranged from 1.44 to 1.67. These GMD and GSD
measurements indicate relatively narrow distributions centered near the dominant ultrafine mode. By
contrast, particle mass was skewed to the larger size regime, with mass GMDs of 4.68 µm to 4.82 µm and
mass GSDs of 1.76 to 1.79. The number-based D10 to D90 was approximately 0.019 µm to 0.029 µm,
whereas the mass-based D10 to D90 was approximately 2.22 µm to 7.52 µm. These modes are consistent
with aerosol generation by both bubble bursting films and vapor condensation mechanisms. Because the
small aerosols have low settling velocities, they are expected to remain airborne and be transported
efficiently by the sparge and sweep gas flows. By contrast, the mass PSDs are shifted toward larger
particle diameters, serving as evidence that relatively few particles of large diameter dominate the aerosol
mass. Larger particles are more susceptible to gravitational settling, inertial impaction, and wall

 12

deposition. Moreover, larger particles are expected to contribute strongly to local contamination and
downstream filter loading but may have shorter transport distances than fine number dominant aerosol. In
the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment, aerosol deposition was observed very close to the point of
generation, and larger droplets and particulates would be expected to deposit quickly in the off-gas system
[14]. However, these results show that the population of small particles would have a significantly longer
lifetime in the off-gas system. This longer lifetime would allow the particles to travel much further into
the downstream components of the system.

3.3 NICKEL LIBS SPECTRA

Time-resolved LIBS spectra from the upward sparging tests at 1 SLPM showed Ni intensity peaks in the
335–360 nm range. The spectra also contained strong features assigned to K, as expected from the salt
aerosols. The intensity for the spectra nearly doubled when the Ni concentration increased from
0.01 mol % to 0.03 mol %; the salt signals also increased. The results given in Figure 10 demonstrate that
LIBS can resolve salt-associated elemental signatures during gas sparging and can provide time-
dependent information relevant to aerosols, particles, and vapor transport from a molten salt system.

3.4 CASCADE IMPACTOR

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis on the impactor stage
substrates was used determine mass distributions [15]. The results showed that the Ni-containing salt
yielded more mass than the Ni-free salt by a factor of 38.5 (Figure 11). The largest single mass fraction
occurred in the 2.49 µm to 3.68 µm aerodynamic diameter cut-off size, which contained 25.1% of the
total mass. Approximately 87% of the mass was associated with particles larger than 0.95 µm. The
differential mass distribution peaks near 2.02 µm, supporting the conclusion that the Ni powder was
transported predominantly within the larger particle size aerosol population. The Ni-free salt contained
less total mass and exhibited a broader distribution across the measured size range. When normalized by
bin width, the Ni-free salt showed a distinct peak near 0.022 µm, indicating a relatively greater ultrafine
particle contribution despite its low overall mass contribution and in agreement with the number PSDs.

 4. SUMMARY

The VISTA apparatus was upgraded and operated to study the generation and transport of aerosols from a
sparged molten salt system. The apparatus employed a LiCl-KCl eutectic salt for compatibility with an
optically accessible quartz visualization cell. The integrated configuration enabled bubble imaging, flow
and particle velocimetry, elemental detection, and particle size characterization for a wide range (20 nm
to 1 mm) during the testing campaign. In a first-of-its-kind experiment, hybrid PIV-PTV measurements
demonstrated the ability to resolve the motion of aerosols in the cover gas as well as the motion of
bubbles and particles within the molten salt. These measurements can be used to evaluate aerosol
trajectories, residence times, transport directions, and the relationship between two-phase flow and
aerosol release.

The measured particle distributions were strongly dependent on whether particle number or particle mass
was considered. The number distributions were dominated by ultrafine particles ranging 0.024 µm to
0.027 µm, and the mass distributions were dominated by particles near 4.7 µm to 4.8 µm. This distinction
is important for reactor off-gas applications because ultrafine particles may remain airborne and be
transported over long distances. However, larger particles can contribute disproportionately to deposited
mass, local contamination, and filter loading but are expected to deposit early in the off-gas system.

The Ni powder was introduced into the salt as a surrogate insoluble species. LIBS detected Ni spectral
features in addition to K features associated with the LiCl-KCl salt. Increasing the Ni concentration from
0.01 mol % to 0.03 mol % produced a corresponding increase in the signal. The cascade impactor analysis
showed that the Ni-containing salt led to greater collected aerosol mass than the Ni-free salt. The results
of this analysis indicate that insoluble material can be transported within the larger-sized aerosol
population.

Overall, the FY 2026 work demonstrates the practicability of using the VISTA apparatus as a combined
platform for investigating bubble-mediated aerosol generation, insoluble particle transport, and real-time
elemental detection in molten salts. Figure 12 shows an image capture, recorded at a rate of 2,800 frames

 16

per second, of a single bubble bursting. The resulting image captures the visualization of film droplets
being generated. The results provide experimental data for improving mechanistic understanding of
molten salt aerosol behavior and for supporting the development and validation of source term and off-
gas transport modeling. Future work should focus on expanding the experimental database, improving
measurement validation, and establishing quantitative relationships between operating conditions, aerosol
generation, and insoluble species transport. The following are planned activities for future work:

• Expanding the operating condition matrix to include a wide bubble size distribution (µm to mm)
 relevant to MSRs.
• Improving PIV-PTV validation and uncertainty characterization to enable development of
 phenomenological models for aerosol transport.
• Developing quantitative LIBS calibration methods for transported metal particles to allow the
 determination of source terms and losses throughout the off-gas system.
• Characterizing aerosol composition by size class to provide validation data for source term codes
 such as MELCOR.
• Assessing sampling system and wall loss effects to reduce uncertainties in collected data that will be
 used for validation of aerosol behavior models.
• Evaluating additional insoluble species surrogates that are representative of noble metal particles and
 have solubility profiles that can be manipulated by changing the redox state of the salt.
• Preparing the system for representative molten salt (fluorides), which will require development of
 analytical methods that do not require an optically transparent containment vessel.
