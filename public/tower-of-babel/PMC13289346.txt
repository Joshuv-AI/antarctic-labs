Medication burden, adherence and health-related quality of life in COPD in China: a multilevel health system study
Authors: Xuedi Ma, Xinyi Li, Changcheng Shi, Yang Wang, Wei Li, Lihong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) represents a major public health challenge in China, with management delivered across all levels of the health system. Medication burden may undermine medication adherence and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), yet the pathways linking these factors remain unclear. This study evaluated the association between medication burden and HRQoL and examined whether medication adherence mediates this relationship from a multilevel health system perspective.MethodsA multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted across healthcare institutions participating in the Cough and Wheeze Pharmaceutical Care Clinic network between September 2023 and September 2024. Medication burden was assessed using the total prescribed drugs and the Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI). HRQoL was measured using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level Questionnaire, and adherence was evaluated with the Adherence to Refills and Medications Scale. Structural equation modelling assessed direct and indirect pathways, and mediation effects were estimated using percentile bootstrapping with 5000 resamples. Sensitivity analyses examined the robustness of the findings.ResultsA total of 2125 patients with COPD were included. Participants had a median of 1 (IQR 0–2) prescribed medications and a median MRCI of 8.0 (range 0.0–13.5). A higher number of medications was significantly associated with poorer HRQoL both directly (estimate = –0.025, 95% CI –0.038 to –0.012) and indirectly via lower adherence (estimate = –0.007, 95% CI –0.010 to –0.003). While MRCI demonstrated no significant direct association with HRQoL, an indirect effect through adherence was observed (estimate = –0.001, 95% CI –0.002 to –0.001). All findings remained consistent across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsAcross multiple levels of the healthcare system, medication adherence partially mediated the association between medication burden and HRQoL in patients with COPD. These findings highlight medication regimen optimisation and adherence support as important public health strategies for improving patient-centred COPD care.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has been a significant global health challenge all over the world. Studies indicate that there were 212.3 million prevalent cases of COPD worldwide, accounting for 3.3 million deaths and 74.4 million disability-adjusted life years.1 In China, the prevalence of COPD among people aged 40 and above is 13.7%, corresponding to an estimated 86 million cases.2 Given its significant impact, COPD has been incorporated into the national basic public health service programme in September 2024,3 underscoring the urgency of strengthening population-level prevention and long-term disease management. As COPD care is delivered across all tiers of China’s healthcare system while largely occurring in primary care,4 understanding modifiable determinants of patient outcomes is critical for improving the effectiveness and sustainability of chronic disease management at a health system level.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a key patient-centred outcome in COPD. It is frequently assessed using self-reported instruments, among which the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) is widely adopted.5 Importantly, HRQoL is not only an outcome measure but also a potential predictor of COPD exacerbations, hospitalisations, mortality and healthcare costs.6 In China, the convergence of a substantial disease burden, rapid population ageing, high levels of air pollution and inequities in healthcare access collectively exacerbates HRQoL impairment among patients with COPD, making its improvement a core priority and urgent need.710

Medication management is essential to controlling COPD symptoms and slowing disease progression, and ultimately improving HRQoL. However, regimen complexity and polypharmacy as two challenges compromise its effectiveness. Regimen complexity is particularly evident with inhaled therapies, the first-line treatment for COPD.11 The inherent complexity of inhaler devices contributes to high rates of usage errors, with a meta-analysis reporting error rates of 50–100% globally12, and a comparable rate of 61.3% in China.13 Such errors substantially undermine treatment effectiveness and health outcomes.12 Polypharmacy is also common, as disease progression often necessitates additional therapies such as prophylactic antibiotics.14 Both regimen complexity and polypharmacy have been associated with lower HRQoL.15 16

In China, health system factors may further amplify these challenges. Limited availability of inhaled therapies in primary care often necessitates multi-device regimens or oral alternatives, increasing regimen complexity.17 18 Meanwhile, the absence of a general practitioner-based system leads to fragmented care across multiple providers, elevating polypharmacy risk.19 Consequently, medication burden among patients with COPD in China warrants particular attention. However, the mechanisms through which medication burden influences HRQoL remain unclear.

Medication adherence, defined as the extent to which patients take medications as prescribed, has been shown to be significantly associated with quality of life among patients with COPD.20 Increased medication burden, reflected in polypharmacy and regimen complexity, is often associated with poorer medication adherence.21 These findings suggest that medication adherence may serve as a mediating factor linking medication burden to quality of life. Additionally, patient characteristics such as advanced age, smoking habits and comorbidity are associated with both medication burden and health outcomes, and may therefore act as confounders of these associations.2227 Meanwhile, hospital level is also associated with medication burden due to varying drug availability across healthcare tiers.17 18 28 Beyond their role as potential confounders, these factors may also modify the strength or direction of the associations, as the impact of medication burden on quality of life may differ across patient subgroups and healthcare settings.

In summary, this study aims to examine the direct association between medication burden and quality of life in patients with COPD, as well as the mediating role of medication adherence in this relationship. Furthermore, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore the potential moderating effects of patient characteristics. The findings are expected to generate evidence to inform targeted, health system-oriented interventions and public health strategies to improve HRQoL among people living with COPD.

Methods

Study design and population

This multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted at the Cough and Wheeze Pharmaceutical Care Clinic (CWPC) network, an initiative covering multiple provinces in China that integrates hospital and primary care pharmacy services to support patients with chronic airway diseases across care settings.29 Participating sites are certified healthcare institutions meeting standardised criteria, including dedicated consultation facilities and pharmacists with specialised respiratory care training and at least 3 years of relevant clinical experience.29 Pharmacists provide both in-clinic consultations and remote follow-up using digital tools to facilitate medication management and home-based self-care.

Data were collected between September 2023 and September 2024 through pharmacist-administered questionnaires, with responses entered directly into an electronic data capture system.

Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) patients who visited CWPC between September 2023 and September 2024; (2) physician-diagnosed COPD; (3) age ≥18 years and (4) completion of both the Adherence to Refills and Medications Scale (ARMS) and the EQ-5D-5L (online supplemental figure S1).

Quality of life

HRQoL was assessed using the EQ-5D-5L, which evaluates five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. Each domain is rated on a five-point scale, ranging from 1 (no problems) to 5 (extreme problems). The EQ-5D-5L utility index (UI) was calculated according to the value set developed by Nan Luo,30 where 1 represents full health and higher scores indicate better HRQoL.

Medication burden

Medication burden was assessed using two complementary measures: (1) the total number of prescribed drugs, encompassing both COPD and non-COPD medications, and (2) the complexity of COPD-specific regimens, quantified with the Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI). This dual approach allowed a comprehensive assessment of both the extent and intricacy of pharmacotherapy among patients with COPD.

To capture the overall polypharmacy burden, all prescribed medications including those for COPD and comorbid conditions were included, given the high prevalence of multimorbidity in this population.31 The total medication count was recorded for each participant as an indicator of polypharmacy. Specifically, the number of drugs referred to all physician-prescribed medications that the patient was using within the 2 weeks preceding the baseline visit.

To evaluate COPD regimen complexity, we applied the MRCI, which incorporates three components: dosage forms, dosing frequency and additional instructions.32 For each patient, each dosage form was counted once per regimen, while dosing frequency and additional instructions were assessed for each medication and then all scores were summed. Higher MRCI scores reflect greater regimen complexity.

Patients who were not prescribed any COPD-related medication received a complexity score of 0, reflecting the absence of COPD-related pharmacotherapy. Additional instruction scores were determined by trained pharmacists based on inherent drug properties and dosing frequency.32

Scoring was based on standardised MRCI weighting rules.32 If medication information was incomplete, we adopted a conservative imputation strategy: (1) for missing dosage form but known administration route (eg, ‘other oral drug’), we assigned the lowest corresponding form score (eg, 1 for a tablet or capsule) and (2) missing dosing frequencies were replaced with the standard recommended frequency. Overall, 651 participants (30.6%) required imputation for partially incomplete medication information.

All MRCI scores were initially calculated by a trained pharmacist and independently verified by one of the study authors. Discrepancies were resolved through review, and the finalised scores were used in the analysis.

Medication adherence

Medication adherence was assessed using the ARMS, a validated instrument developed for patients with chronic illnesses and previously applied in COPD populations.33 In this study, pharmacists administered the 12-item ARMS questionnaire verbally. Total scores range from 12 to 48, with higher scores indicating poorer adherence.34

Covariates and subgroup indicators

Covariates for the mediation analysis were selected a priori based on prior literature and clinical knowledge as potential confounders of the associations among medication burden, medication adherence and HRQoL. Specifically, age and sex are well-established demographic factors associated with both medication use patterns and health outcomes.22 25 CAT score reflects COPD symptom severity, as symptom burden may influence prescribing patterns and is independently associated with HRQoL.11 Smoking status and number of comorbidities are known to affect medication regimens and overall health status.25 27 Hospital tier was included as a proxy for disease severity and healthcare resource utilisation, given that drug availability and patient case-mix vary across healthcare tiers.17 18 28 A total of 23 comorbidities were identified, encompassing physical conditions (eg, hypertension and asthma) and mental health conditions (eg, anxiety and depression). A detailed list is provided in online supplemental table S1.

In the primary mediation analysis, these variables were included as covariates to adjust for potential confounding and satisfy the assumptions required for unbiased estimation of the direct and indirect effects. In addition, subgroup analyses stratified by these same variables were conducted to explore potential effect modification. In this context, the variables served as effect modifiers rather than confounders. Multigroup structural equation modelling (SEM) was used to formally test for interaction effects by comparing models with freely estimated path coefficients vs models with equality constraints across subgroups. A statistically significant likelihood ratio test (p<0.05) was interpreted as evidence of effect modification.

Sampling and sample size calculation

The required sample size was first estimated using the conventional events-per-variable rule for multiple regression. Our analytic model included nine variables (three primary variables and six covariates); applying the rule of 10–20 events per variable suggested a minimum of approximately 180 participants. Our dataset included 2125 participants, which far exceeded this minimum requirement.

To account for medication-related effects, we further applied prior methodological simulation studies,35 which indicated that a sample size of 2125 participants would provide >99% statistical power for this study. This number was deemed sufficient for the planned analyses.

Statistical analysis

For the descriptive statistics, the distribution of continuous variables was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test, the skewed data were summarised as median (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

For the mediation analysis, the hypothesised mediation model (figure 1A) was tested using SEM with the ‘lavaan’ package in R (V.4.5.1). The significance of the indirect effects was assessed using percentile bootstrapping with 5000 resamples, a method that accommodates sampling variability and provides robust estimates without relying on normality assumptions.36 Two indicators of medication burden (total number of drugs and MRCI for COPD regimens) were specified as predictors, medication adherence (ARMS score) as the mediator, and HRQoL (EQ-5D-5L UI) as the outcome. Three models were evaluated: (1) unadjusted; (2) adjusted for age and sex; and (3) fully adjusted for age, sex, CAT score, smoking status, number of comorbidities and treatment in a tertiary hospital (figure 1B). Direct and indirect effects were considered statistically significant at p<0.05.

All analyses were performed using R software (V.4.5.1).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 2125 participants were included in the study. The median age was 71.0 years (IQR 63.0–77.0), and 507 participants (23.9%) were female. The median total number of prescribed drugs was 1.0 (IQR 0.00–2.00). The median MRCI score was 8.0 (0.0–13.5), while the median ARMS score was 18.0 (IQR 15.0–23.0). The median EQ-5D-5L UI was 0.8 (IQR 0.6–0.9). Regarding respiratory outcomes, the median CAT score was 24.0 (IQR 18.0–27.0). Notably, CAT scores greater than 10 indicate a higher impact of COPD-related symptoms on patients’ daily life.37 Detailed descriptive statistics are presented in table 1.

Correlation of medication burden, medication adherence and HRQoL

The bivariate correlations among the study variables, presented in online supplemental table S3, demonstrated significant associations between the MRCI, the total number of prescribed drugs, medication adherence and HRQoL (all p<0.001). Notably, higher MRCI scores (higher complexity), a greater number of prescribed medications and lower adherence were all negatively correlated with HRQoL. These findings provided a strong rationale for conducting further analyses using a moderated mediation framework.

Mediating effects of medication adherence in the association between medication burden and HRQoL

In the unadjusted mediation model (table 2), a higher total number of prescribed drugs was significantly associated with poorer HRQoL (EQ-5D-5L UI) through both direct and indirect pathways. The direct effect of prescribed drug count on HRQoL was significant (estimate = –0.025, 95% CI –0.038 to –0.012, p<0.001), and the indirect pathway via medication adherence (ARMS score) was also significant (estimate = –0.007, 95% CI –0.010 to –0.003, p<0.001). The total effect was –0.031 (95% CI –0.045 to –0.018, p<0.001), indicating that a greater number of prescribed drugs was associated with poorer HRQoL both directly and indirectly through reduced adherence. Notably, approximately 23% of the total effect was mediated by adherence.

MRCI score showed no significant direct effect on HRQoL (estimate=0.000, 95% CI –0.002 to 0.002, p=0.882). However, the indirect effect through adherence was significant (estimate = –0.001, 95% CI –0.002 to –0.001, p<0.001). Despite this, the overall total effect was not statistically significant (estimate = –0.001, 95% CI –0.003 to 0.001, p=0.351), suggesting that MRCI influenced HRQoL predominantly through adherence but did not yield a significant overall association.

When adjusted for age and sex, the mediation results were essentially unchanged (table 2). After further adjustment for age, sex, CAT score, smoking status, number of comorbidities and treatment in a tertiary hospital (adjusted model 2), the findings remained broadly consistent with the unadjusted analysis (table 2). In this model, the direct effect of prescribed drug count on HRQoL was attenuated and no longer significant (estimate = –0.009, 95% CI –0.020 to 0.001, p=0.075), whereas the indirect effect via adherence remained significant (estimate = –0.003, 95% CI –0.005 to –0.001, p=0.005). The total effect of prescribed drug count was still significant but smaller in magnitude (estimate = –0.012, 95% CI –0.022 to –0.002, p=0.022). These findings indicate that, after adjusting for potential confounders, the adverse impact of medication burden on HRQoL was largely explained by reduced adherence.

Sensitivity mediation analyses were conducted under three scenarios: (1) excluding participants with imputed CAT scores, (2) excluding participants with imputed MRCI scores and (3) reclassifying smoking status as ever smoker versus never smoker. The results were consistent with the main analysis (online supplemental table S4).

Mediating effects of medication adherence in the association between medication burden and HRQoL by patient and hospital characteristics

In subgroup analyses, the overall mediation patterns were broadly consistent with the main analysis (figure 3). Stratification by disease severity, based on CAT score, revealed stronger associations among patients with higher symptom burden (CAT≥10). In this subgroup, both the direct effect (estimate = –0.023, 95% CI –0.038 to –0.009, p=0.002) and the indirect effect via adherence (estimate = –0.007, 95% CI –0.010 to –0.004, p<0.001) from the number of prescribed drugs to HRQoL were significant, resulting in a robust total effect (estimate = –0.030, 95% CI –0.045 to –0.016, p<0.001). Medication complexity was also associated with reduced HRQoL via decreased adherence (estimate = –0.001, 95% CI –0.001 to 0.000, p=0.001). In contrast, these associations were not significant in participants with lower CAT scores (< 10).

Hospital type also modified the observed associations. Among patients treated in tertiary hospitals, both the direct and indirect effects of the number of prescribed drugs on HRQoL were significant, with a total effect of –0.016 (95% CI –0.026 to –0.005, p=0.003). However, no significant effects were detected among those receiving care in non-tertiary hospitals.

Lastly, among patients diagnosed with COPD alone, without comorbidities, a significant indirect effect through adherence (estimate = –0.006, 95% CI –0.011 to –0.002, p=0.007) and a significant total effect (estimate = –0.020, 95% CI –0.035 to –0.004, p=0.010) were observed. In contrast, these pathways were not significant in individuals with comorbid conditions.

Discussion

This study investigated the associations between medication burden, medication adherence and HRQoL among a large sample of patients with COPD across China.38 Using a mediation framework, we demonstrated that a higher number of prescribed medications was associated with poorer HRQoL, and this relationship was partly mediated by reduced adherence to treatment. Although medication regimen complexity, measured by the MRCI, was indirectly associated with HRQoL via adherence, its total effect was not statistically significant. The mediation patterns remained consistent after adjustment for potential confounders, including demographic and clinical characteristics. Collectively, these findings highlight medication adherence as a key behavioural pathway linking medication burden to patient well-being.

Our findings align with a growing body of evidence emphasising the adverse impact of polypharmacy and complex medication regimens on patient-centred outcomes.39 40 Several studies have reported that a greater number of prescribed drugs and higher MRCI is associated with worse physical functioning, increased symptom burden and lower HRQoL in COPD and other chronic conditions.39 41 42 The current study extends this literature by quantifying the mediating effect of adherence, providing empirical support that excessive medication burden undermines adherence, which further compromises disease control and quality of life.

Notably, while some previous studies found both MRCI and medication count to be strong predictors of HRQoL,15 16 our results revealed a more nuanced pattern. The number of medications was directly and indirectly associated with HRQoL, whereas MRCI influenced HRQoL primarily through adherence, without a significant total effect. This distinction suggests that medication quantity may exert a more immediate psychological and behavioural burden on patients, whereas regimen complexity affects outcomes predominantly through its influence on adherence behaviours. The findings underscore the need to consider both dimensions of medication burden when evaluating treatment-related outcomes in COPD.

The significant mediating role of adherence indicates that the negative effect of medication burden on HRQoL operates largely through behavioural mechanisms. Patients facing multiple or complex regimens, especially the challenging technique required for COPD inhalation device, may experience treatment fatigue and reduced adherence motivation.43 Non-adherence can lead to inadequate symptom control and increased health-related distress, which result in poorer HRQoL.44 Conversely, maintaining high adherence despite polypharmacy could mitigate these adverse consequences.

The proportion of the total effect mediated by adherence (about 23%) highlights the substantial role of behavioural factors in shaping clinical and quality-of-life outcomes. The mediation effect remained significant after adjusting for disease severity (CAT score) and comorbidities, indicating that adherence acts independently of biomedical disease complexity.

Subgroup analyses further revealed heterogeneity. Among participants with higher symptom burden (CAT≥10), both direct and indirect effects of medication burden on HRQoL were evident, whereas these associations were not significant in those with milder symptoms. This suggests that medication burden has a stronger negative impact among patients already experiencing marked disease-related symptoms.45

Differences by hospital type further support this interpretation. The significant mediation observed among patients treated in tertiary hospitals may reflect more severe disease profiles or more intensive pharmacotherapy regimens in these settings.17 Conversely, in non-tertiary hospitals, simpler treatment patterns might reduce the likelihood of adherence-related mediation.17

Finally, the mediation effect was strongest among patients with COPD alone, without comorbidities. In individuals with multiple conditions, competing treatment priorities and polypharmacy may dilute the adherence and quality-of-life relationship.46 These subgroup patterns highlight the need for personalised interventions that consider patient age, disease severity and healthcare context when addressing medication-related challenges.

From a clinical perspective, these findings have important implications for COPD management. Prior research, including qualitative studies, has identified multiple socioeconomic and psychosocial barriers that may explain why higher medication burden undermines adherence. These include limited health literacy, inadequate understanding of medication regimens and their purpose, concerns about side effects, financial constraints and psychological factors such as depression and anxiety.4749 For patients with COPD, the complexity of inhaler devices and the need for correct technique pose additional challenges.50 Our findings, showing that medication burden affects HRQoL partly through reduced adherence, align with these qualitative insights and underscore the importance of addressing these underlying barriers in clinical practice.

Efforts to improve HRQoL should target not only pharmacologic optimisation but also behavioural strategies to sustain adherence. Regular assessment of medication burden, both in terms of number and complexity should become a standard component of COPD care. Simplifying treatment regimens, using fixed-dose combinations, and engaging pharmacists in medication review may reduce polypharmacy and improve adherence.51 52 Given the known barriers related to health literacy and regimen understanding, patient education should be tailored to individual needs, using teach-back methods and visual aids to ensure comprehension.53 As inhaler use is often central to COPD management, tailored education, digital adherence tools and motivational counselling are essential for effective self-management.54 At the system level, integrating adherence monitoring into chronic disease programmes may improve symptom control and patient-reported outcomes. Moreover, embedding pharmacist-led medication review and adherence support can strengthen continuity of care and empower patient self-management, ultimately improving quality of life.

This study offers several methodological strengths. The multicentre design and inclusion of participants from multiple provinces enhance the external validity within the CWPC network which includes over 1000 pharmaceutical clinics at all levels of the healthcare system. The mediation analysis framework allowed for an in-depth examination of the pathways linking medication burden, adherence and HRQoL, while adjustment for a broad set of covariates minimised confounding bias.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, exclusion of patients with incomplete questionnaires resulted in a reduced analytic sample, which may have introduced selection bias. This study was conducted in pharmaceutical care clinics where questionnaires were administered after clinical consultations on a voluntary basis, which limited the completeness of data collection. We compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants (online supplemental table S5), and although most characteristics were comparable, unmeasured factors related to both questionnaire non-completion and health outcomes may have biased our results. Our findings should therefore be considered exploratory, and future prospective studies with systematic data collection protocols are needed to validate these results. Second, medication count may underestimate pharmacotherapy burden,26 as it only reflected prescribed drugs used within 2 weeks before the baseline visit and excluded over-the-counter or intermittent medications. Inhaler combinations were counted as single prescriptions, and some patients may have discontinued treatment, reflecting suboptimal adherence.47 Third, incomplete prescription details required conservative imputation, which may underestimate medication complexity. Future studies with comprehensive prescription data are needed to validate these findings. Finally, the study sample was drawn from the CWPC network, which does not cover all provinces in China. As shown in figure 2, data from certain provinces were not available, limiting the geographic representativeness of the findings. To our knowledge, no publicly available nationally representative database in China currently captures medication burden (including regimen complexity), adherence behaviour and HRQoL simultaneously for patients with COPD. Future nationally representative studies incorporating these measures would help validate and extend our findings to a broader population.

Future interventional studies incorporating digital adherence metrics and pharmacist-led medication reviews to evaluate the impacts on HRQoL of such measures may provide more objective insights into adherence dynamics and the value of medication management for COPD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that medication adherence mediates the relationship between medication burden and HRQoL in patients with COPD. A greater number of prescribed medications was associated with poorer HRQoL, both directly and indirectly through reduced adherence. While regimen complexity appeared to influence HRQoL mainly via adherence, its total effect on HRQoL was not statistically significant. These findings highlight the central role of adherence-focused strategies in optimising COPD management. Simplifying treatment regimens and supporting sustained adherence behaviours may help improve patient well-being. Embedding such strategies into chronic disease management across all tiers of the healthcare system, including within basic public health service programmes, could provide a scalable and sustainable pathway to better COPD outcomes and more equitable, person-centred care.
