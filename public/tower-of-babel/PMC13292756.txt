Missing the real-world patient: clinical gaps in the biological age–DR association
Authors: Jiamin Wang, Jingyi Li
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Wang et al. recently published in The Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging (2026), which reported that biological age acceleration, as measured by three established algorithms, was significantly associated with incident diabetic retinopathy (DR) in the UK Biobank cohort [1]. The authors deserve recognition for their large-scale, prospective design and for integrating polygenic risk scores to explore gene–aging interactions. Their findings offer a promising direction for DR risk stratification. Nevertheless, we wish to raise several clinically relevant concerns that may limit the direct applicability of their results in everyday practice.

A major issue is that the study population does not reflect the diversity typically encountered in real-world diabetes care. The UK Biobank is predominantly of European ancestry, and the authors excluded participants with missing biomarker or genetic data. As a result, individuals from ethnic minority groups, those with lower health literacy, or those receiving fragmented healthcare – populations that often bear the highest DR burden – remain largely invisible in this analysis. In many clinical settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, such ‘real-world’ patients are the norm rather than the exception. Therefore, the claim of generalizability warrants caution.

Another clinical blind spot is the absence of longitudinal glycemic control as a time-varying covariate. Although the authors adjusted for baseline HbA1c, diabetes duration and subsequent glycemic trajectory are far stronger determinants of DR progression. In routine practice, a patient with poorly controlled diabetes for two decades faces a substantially different DR risk than one with well-controlled diabetes for five years, even if both show similar biological age acceleration. Without accounting for cumulative glycemic exposure or treatment changes such as insulin initiation, the observed effect sizes may be considerably confounded, which limits the utility of biological age acceleration as an independent clinical marker [2].

The outcome definition itself likely underestimates mild or treatable DR. The authors relied on hospital inpatient records (ICD-9/10 codes), which predominantly capture moderate-to-severe disease requiring specialist referral. In most healthcare systems, early-stage nonproliferative DR is detected through community-based retinal screening, not hospital admissions. Hence, the reported associations may not apply to the vast majority of early, asymptomatic DR cases – precisely the stage at which preventive intervention is most beneficial. This misclassification could lead to an overestimation of risk in advanced disease and an underestimation in milder forms [3].

Furthermore, the absence of detailed ophthalmic examination findings limits clinical actionability. The paper provides no information on macular edema, intraretinal hemorrhages, or microaneurysm counts. In practice, ophthalmologists do not treat a ‘risk score’ – they treat specific retinal lesions. Without linking biological age acceleration to validated retinal grading systems such as the ETDRS severity scale, it remains unclear whether an accelerated aging profile should prompt earlier referral, shorter screening intervals, or specific therapies. This disconnect between a systemic biomarker and retinal phenotypes hinders the translation of findings into actionable protocols [4].

Finally, the study does not address whether biological age acceleration is reversible or modifiable. From a clinical standpoint, the most pressing question is whether slowing biological aging – through lifestyle changes, metformin, GLP-1 agonists, or other interventions – could reduce DR risk. The current observational design cannot answer this. Without interventional or quasi-experimental evidence, clinicians may hesitate to incorporate biological age measures into routine DR management, as they offer no direct therapeutic guidance.

Future research should prioritize validation in diverse, real-world diabetic populations with complete treatment and glycemic histories, prospective integration with community-based retinal screening programs to assess performance in detecting early DR, and randomized trials or target-trial emulations testing whether interventions that reduce biological age acceleration also lower DR incidence. Only then will biological age measures transition from an elegant research tool to a clinically useful biomarker.

In summary, Wang and colleagues have opened an important door. However, closing the gap between biological age acceleration and clinical DR management will require addressing real-world complexities such as glycemic exposure, early disease detection, and the modifiability of aging itself. We look forward to seeing their future work in these directions.
