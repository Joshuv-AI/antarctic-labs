1 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 General Atomics Electromagnetic Systems (GA-EMS) together with The Nonwovens
Institute (NWI) and Harper International have executed on a development project to improve the
production efficiency and reduce the production cost of silicon carbide (SiC) fiber – a critical
material that advances US energy security and aerospace leadership.

 In the first budget period (BP1), the modified tow throughput (MTT) apparatus was
developed, enabling fiber spinning in the form of loose coils rather than tightly packed spools
(Figure 1) for batch processing of SiC fiber. This coil-based approach was proven to be capable
of uniform fiber crosslinking and ceramic conversion, as well as demonstrating key process
efficiencies while meeting the program fiber property targets.

Figure 1. Modified tow throughput (MTT) apparatus developed in BP1 which was used as
 the basis for the conceptual design of a continuous production process for SiC fiber in
 BP2.

 The second budget period (BP2) involved intensive research and development to bridge the
gap between batch and continuous production of SiC fiber, culminating in the conceptual design
of a continuous end-to-end process. The continuous design was shown to reduce fiber waste
during crosslinking from 250 g/kg (spool-based batch process) to 21 g/kg (coil-based continuous
process), lower carbon intensity from 83 kg CO2e / kgfiber to 7.7 kg CO2e / kgfiber and decrease
production cost by 60%. This reduction in energy and carbon intensity of the SiC fiber process is
directly aligned with DOE-AMMTO’s goals of reducing industrial energy intensity and GHG
emissions.

 The transition from batch to continuous processing represents a transformative milestone in
domestic SiC fiber production, demonstrating the feasibility of the continuous process as well as
significant advancements in process efficiency, waste reduction, cost reduction, and a reduction
in carbon intensity (Table 1). The insights gained from this initiative position the MTT-TCC
system as a scalable and competitive solution to meet the growing demand for SiC fiber, a
critical material for domestic energy and aerospace applications.

 Key recommendations following the success of this program are twofold. The first is to burn
down risk associated with transitioning SiC fiber production from the traditional batch-based

 5

production process to the fully continuous process by processing SiC fiber on representative
equipment. Fortunately, the Carbon Fiber Technology Facility (CFTF) at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) operates a carbon fiber processing line that shares design elements with the
MTT-TCC continuous thermal conversion system. Thus, implementation of the continuous MTT-
TCC approach at the CFTF or comparable facility would serve as a valuable real-world
validation of the concept.

 The second, is to take steps to secure domestic production for key supply chain elements.
Securing cost-effective supply chains, used in the production of SiC fiber, will be critical for long-
term production stability, particularly for the precursor polymer polydimethylsilane and sodium
metal.

 GA-EMS is developing three SiC fiber product lines. SiGA™- FLR fiber is an amorphous
fiber with tunable electrical properties, SiGA™ - FX is a crystalline near-stoichiometric fiber for
use in aerospace applications, and SiGA™ - FN is a crystalline near-stoichiometric fiber
specifically formulated for nuclear energy applications. Development of the end-to-end
production process is progressing and will be followed by scale up to pilot and full-scale
production.

2 INTRODUCTION
 Silicon carbide (SiC) fiber is a critical material for advanced engineering applications due to
its low density, thermal and chemical stability, high tensile strength, and low thermal expansion.
These properties make SiC fiber an essential component in SiC/SiC ceramic matrix composites
(CMC) for aerospace[1-4] and nuclear energy applications[5-13].

 6

 SiC fiber is produced using a multistep process[14] starting with the synthesis of
 polycarbosilane (a ceramic forming polymer), then spinning the polymer to produce a polymeric
 fiber, curing (crosslinking) the polymeric fiber to maintain its shape through thermal processing,
 and then a number of thermal processing steps to convert the polymeric fiber into high purity
 SiC fiber (Figure 2).

Figure 2. Processing steps for producing SiC fiber. The method used to cure (crosslink) the polymeric fiber
has a significant impact on fiber throughput, yield, and production cost.

 The method used to crosslink the polymeric fiber has a significant impact on the throughput,
 yield, and cost of the resultant SiC fiber. Crosslinking the fiber
 using a high energy electron beam is an established approach
 but is highly energy and capital intensive (infrastructure to
 support high energy electron sources, transformers, radiation
 shielding, etc.), slow (limiting overall output of the process),
 yield limited, and tied to a batch processing approach.
 Thermochemical crosslinking (TCC) is an oxidative process to
 crosslink the polymeric fiber. This is a low-temperature ambient
 atmosphere process which greatly reduces the complexity and
 capital intensity of the cure step and can be utilized in both
 batch and continuous processing.

 For both crosslinking methods, an amorphous SiC fiber is
 produced after the first thermal processing step where the
 polymeric fiber is pyrolyzed into a ceramic fiber. For the electron
 beam approach a carbon-rich amorphous fiber is produced and

 7

 Traditionally, SiC fiber has been produced using a spool-based batch process where
polymeric fiber is first spun onto a spool (Figure 2) for subsequent processing. This spool-based
approach imposes limitations on fiber throughput for both the electron beam and TCC
crosslinking approaches. In electron beam curing, the high energy beam crosslinks the polymer
fiber mass from the outside-in and due to the loss of electron energy as a function of fiber pile
depth, non-homogeneous crosslinking is observed. In TCC crosslinking, gas must diffuse into
the fiber mass and then into the individual filaments to crosslink the polymeric fiber. In a tightly
wound spool, gas diffusion into the inner fiber layers is limited and non-homogenous
crosslinking is observed. For both cases, the fiber production process must balance the fiber
throughput through the crosslinking step along with the yield loss due to inhomogeneous
crosslinking associated with increased fiber mass (Figure 3).

 To overcome limitations associated with the crosslinking process and
 reduce SiC fiber cost, GA-EMS developed the modified tow throughput
 (MTT) approach under this program. The MTT approach (Figure 4) spins
 fiber into loose coils with controlled diameter, pitch, and pile depth instead of
 tightly wound spools, improving the thermal and mass transfer in the
 crosslinking step for an overall increase in yield and throughput. The power
 of the MTT approach is that it can be used in both a batch process (with
 increased yield compared to the spool-based approach) as well as enabling
 the move to continuous processing to drastically reduce the production cost
 of SiC fiber.

3 BACKGROUND
 General Atomics Electromagnetic Systems (GA-EMS) specializes in developing advanced
technology, materials, and manufacturing methods. Building on a rich history with nuclear
energy technology, GA-EMS is a global leader in the development of SiC-SiC ceramic matrix
composite (CMC) based fuel cladding for nuclear energy generation - SiGA® cladding which
provides enhanced safety and operational benefits to the nuclear industry through high-
temperature stability and irradiation resilience. Commercially available SiC fiber, the enabling
material behind SiGA® cladding and all SiC-SiC CMCs, is prohibitively expensive and without a
domestic supply chain, impeding the commercialization of SiGA® cladding. The high cost of SiC
fiber is largely driven by the “state of the art” (SoA) production process that uses high-power
electron beam processing to crosslink the polymeric precursor fiber prior to ceramic conversion.

 8

The electron beam process is yield limited and significantly increases the complexity of the SiC
fiber production process resulting in a prohibitively expensive SiC fiber product.

 To overcome this barrier, GA-EMS developed the MTT-TCC process which enables the
continuous production of SiC fiber, resulting in decreased production cost, energy usage, and
carbon emissions.

 The project was organized into two main tasks according to budget period. The goal of Task
1.1 (Budget Period 1) was to design, build, and test the MTT apparatus for spinning fiber into
coils instead of spools. Task 1.1 was completed by integrating the MTT apparatus into the GA-
EMS fiber line to produce continuous fiber coils using the approach. The reduction in process
waste, carbon intensity, and production cost enabled by the batch MTT-TCC approach was
quantified as part of Task 1.1 The goal of Task 1.2 (Budget Period 2) was to expand the MTT-
TCC concept into a conceptual design of a continuous production process for SiC fiber and
quantify the reduction in process waste, carbon intensity, and production cost of the continuous
production process.

4.1.1 Budget Period 1 Fiber Properties
4.1.1.1 Composition of Fiber
 The crosslinking (cure) conditions of polycarbosilane polymer (PCS) fiber play a determining
role in the composition of the resultant amorphous SiC ceramic fiber and impact the
downstream process to achieve a high purity fiber. The PCS polymer reacts readily with the
atmosphere, incorporating oxygen into the polymer structure. This oxygen persists through
ceramic conversion and determines the oxygen content of the resultant fiber.

 10

 By controlling sources of contamination and optimizing the coil cure process, the oxygen
content of the resultant amorphous SiC fiber was 10.7 wt%.

4.1.1.2 Mechanical Performance
 The filament tensile strength, tensile modulus, and diameter
were all measured for SiC fiber produced using the single coil,
three coil, and control spool process. The control spool process
achieved the highest filament tensile strength (2.1 GPa) and
modulus (177 GPa). When processing fiber in a single coil or as
three coils, the filament tensile strength decreased to 1.9 and 1.5
GPa, while the modulus decreased to 170 and 156 GPa,
respectively (Figure 8).

4.1.1.3 Fiber Density
 The density of SiC fiber is dependent upon the temperature used during pyrolysis (ceramic
conversion), the oxygen content, carbon content, and its crystallinity. Oxygen-containing
amorphous fiber has a density in the 2.3-2.5 g/cc range. Amorphous fiber without oxygen but

 11

with excess carbon has a higher density of 2.6-2.7g/cc. Crystalline near-stoichiometric fiber has
the highest density of 2.9-3.0 g/cc. The density of the amorphous oxygen containing fiber
produced using the batch MTT coil process was 2.5 g/cc, meeting the program target.

4.1.1.4 Fiber Morphology
 SEM imaging of the SiC fiber obtained from the batch MTT coil process (Figure 9) shows
that the filaments have a smooth surface and a glassy cross section typical for amorphous SiC
fiber. The smooth filament surface also indicates that there is no fusing (melting) of the
filaments.

4.2 Budget Period 2
 With the successful demonstration of fiber coil formation using the batch MTT system
integrated into the GA-EMS SiC fiber line in Budget Period 1, a conceptual design for a
continuous production process was developed together with Harper International in Budget
Period 2.

 In general, polymer extrusion demands critical control during melt spinning to provide the
highest performing fiber product possible. Polycarbosilane (PCS) fiber is known for its fragility
and low strength, requiring unique designs and solutions. Specific design elements were used
to manage the unique features of the PCS polymer – working distance of the system, the draw
down and tensioning devices, length of the quench zone, spin finish chemistry, and the
minimization of contact points.

 The continuous thermal conversion system also incorporated specific design features that
would enable processing the fragile PCS fiber over a temperature range of nearly 2,000°C to
yield high quality SiC fiber. These features included the dimensions of the belt and the line
speed, materials of construction of the conveyor belts, independent furnace zones for ease of
maintenance, atmosphere control inside the furnaces, winding equipment for collecting coils,
and high temperature furnace design for processing fiber tows.

5 KEY ACHIEVEMENTS
 • Developed the MTT-TCC fiber spinning apparatus for forming fiber coils. Coils can be
 thermally batch processed resulting in increased yield and efficiency as well as enabling
 continuous thermal processing.

 • The MTT-TCC approach was shown to reduce waste, carbon emissions, production
 cost, while producing SiC fiber meeting property targets. This satisfied the Go/No-Go
 decision point to move to Budget Period 2.

 • MTT-TCC apparatus was integrated into the fiber spinning line to successfully produce
 continuous fiber coils.

 • Identified key processing parameters, equipment, and materials needed to collect and
 transition as-spun fiber directly from the spinning system onto a conveyor system for
 continuous thermal processing.

 • Designed a process capable of continuously processing SiC fiber from the fiber spinning
 stage through all thermal processing steps needed to produce a high purity, near
 stoichiometric SiC fiber.

 • Quantified the reduction in environmental emissions, energy usage, and production cost
 enabled by continuous production of SiC fiber using the MTT-TCC approach.

6 BENEFITS ASSESSMENT
 Implementation of MTT-TCC technology offers significant reduction in environmental
emissions as well as energy savings in the production of SiC fiber. The fully continuous MTT-
TCC production process reduces environmental emissions to just 7.7 kg CO2e/kgfiber from 83
CO2e/kgfiber and reduces the energy usage in fiber production to 0.38 MWh/kgfiber from 2.6
MWh/kgfiber.

 By reducing the cost of SiC fiber, the MTT-TCC technology broadens the applications space
for SiC ceramic matrix composites for energy and aerospace applications. SiC CMC fuel rods
can replace traditional metal fuel structures to improve fuel utilization and increase power output
of the existing LWR fleet as well as offering improved efficiencies, better fuel utilization, and less
waste generation in upcoming advanced reactor designs[6, 7, 9, 15-22]. SiC CMC materials
also improve the efficiency of aviation engines[3] where their use results in reduced fuel
consumption, lower emissions, and greater engine reliability.

 prevent breakage of the fragile fiber prior to ceramic conversion.

 • The cost of polycarbosilane and polydimethylsilane significantly impact the cost of SiC
 fiber. Securing supply chains and developing high yield production processes for these
 materials is needed to reduce production cost.

8 CONCLUSIONS
 The SiC fiber produced using the batch MTT-TCC approach during the first budget period
met the fiber property and process efficiency metrics. In budget period 2, the batch MTT-TCC
approach was extended by a conceptual design of a continuous production process capable of
producing 10 metric tons of SiC fiber per year.

 As shown in Table 1, the MTT-TCC approach developed under this program significantly
reduces the production cost of SiC fiber even when implemented as a batch process, reducing
production cost by 22%. The batch MTT-TCC approach is compatible with existing batch
processing methods established for SiC fiber production and provides near-term potential for
reducing the production cost of SiC fiber. Fully continuous processing, as envisioned by the
conceptual design performed under this program, shows tremendous benefit – reducing
production cost by 60%. The reduction in SiC fiber production cost enabled by the MTT-TCC
approach broadens the application space for SiC materials in advanced energy and aerospace
applications.

9 FUTURE WORK / RECOMMENDATIONS
 The development of the MTT-TCC process as part of this program has demonstrated
significant advancement in SiC fiber production. To fully realize the system’s potential and burn
down technical risk associated with the continuous process, several strategic next steps are
recommended. These actions focus on validating the system’s scalability and optimizing
material sourcing.

 Practical validation of the conceptual design produced under this program is a critical next
step in the continuous production of SiC fiber using the MTT-TCC approach. The cost of the SiC
fiber continuous thermal processing line represents a significant capital expenditure. Any
technical risk must therefore be minimized before such a capital investment. To do so,
processing SiC fiber on representative equipment is critically important. The Carbon Fiber
Technology Facility (CFTF) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) operates a carbon fiber
processing line that shares design elements to the continuous MTT-TCC and could serve as a
valuable validation of the concept at minimal cost.
