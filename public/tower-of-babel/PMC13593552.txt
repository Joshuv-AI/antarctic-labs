Introduction

Venous stenosis or occlusion is a clinically important issue in patients with long-term cardiovascular implantable electronic device (CIED) leads, particularly when lead revision, upgrade, or reimplantation is required.1,2 In patients with ipsilateral venous occlusion, potential strategies include contralateral lead implantation with tunneling, venoplasty, leadless pacemaker implantation in selected cases, or transvenous lead extraction (TLE) to regain venous access.1, 2, 3, 4 Maintaining venous access during extraction is particularly important when ipsilateral reimplantation is intended.

However, preserving venous access during TLE can be challenging, especially when residual fibrotic tissue surrounding the lead prevents the lead–tissue complex from being accommodated within the extraction sheath after lead tip detachment. Conventional strategies may require further advancement of the extraction sheath toward the distal lead, which can increase procedural complexity and vascular risk. We describe a novel technique, termed the Snare-Assisted Intravascular Lead-to-Wire Conversion (SAIL) technique, in which a detached lead is converted into a guidewire route using a femoral snare catheter as an intravascular conduit, allowing preservation of ipsilateral venous access for immediate reimplantation.

Case report

A 75-year-old man had been diagnosed with diabetes mellitus at 38 years of age and was started on insulin therapy at 51 years of age because of poor glycemic control, with a hemoglobin A1c level of 12%. At 62 years of age, he underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation at our institution for paroxysmal atrioventricular block. The implanted system consisted of an Evia DR-T generator (Biotronik, Berlin, Germany), an atrial lead 5554-53/tined (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), and a ventricular lead 5076-58/screw-in (Medtronic). He was subsequently followed in our pacemaker clinic. At approximately 68 years of age, he underwent living-donor kidney transplantation for end-stage renal disease.

At approximately 70 years of age, atrial lead noise due to atrial lead dysfunction was detected. He was initially managed with VVIR pacing at 50–120 beats per minute. At 75 years of age, although he had no apparent symptoms at a routine pacemaker clinic visit, his ventricular pacing burden had increased from 5% to 76%. He subsequently developed worsening exertional dyspnea and eventually experienced dyspnea even during activities of daily living at home. He was referred to our department for worsening heart failure, with elevated brain natriuretic peptide levels and pleural effusion. Echocardiography demonstrated rapid progression to severe tricuspid regurgitation, and he was admitted for further evaluation. On admission, the ventricular pacing burden had increased to 95%, and transthoracic echocardiography showed right ventricular dyssynchrony associated with atrioventricular dyssynchrony.

Atrial lead dysfunction required implantation of a new atrial lead; thus, preprocedural venography was performed and demonstrated occlusion of the left subclavian vein (Figure 1A). Therefore, TLE of the pre-existing pacemaker leads and ipsilateral reimplantation were planned.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Baseline fluoroscopy showed the pre-existing atrial and ventricular leads (Figure 1B). The helix of the ventricular lead was successfully retracted. Locking stylets (LLD EZ, Philips, Andover, MA) were inserted into both the atrial and ventricular leads. Initial adhesion dissection of the ventricular lead was performed using a 12-F laser sheath (GlideLight, Philips). After extraction of the atrial lead, further adhesion dissection of the ventricular lead was performed using a 9-F TightRail mechanical sheath (Philips) (Figure 1C). The laser sheath could be advanced to the level of the innominate vein; however, further advancement was limited by lead-to-lead adhesion. The atrial lead was similarly dissected to the same level using the 12-F laser sheath. Subsequently, a 9-F TightRail mechanical sheath was used to dissect calcified adhesions, allowing slight further advancement. During the dissection of the atrial lead using the 9-F TightRail sheath, the atrial lead tip was detached, and the atrial lead was successfully removed. However, an attempt to advance a guidewire through the retained TightRail sheath failed because the wire could not cross the occluded venous segment; therefore, venous access could not be secured.

Adhesion dissection of the ventricular lead was then continued using the 9-F TightRail sheath. During the procedure, the ventricular lead tip was detached from the myocardium (Figure 1D). However, residual fibrotic tissue surrounding the lead remained, and the lead–fibrotic tissue complex could not be fully accommodated within the TightRail sheath. Although the lead tip had already been detached and the entire lead system could be pulled toward the left prepectoral pocket, conventional complete extraction would have required further dissection toward the distal lead. Therefore, we performed the SAIL technique to preserve venous access instead of continuing additional dissection to the lead tip.

An Osypka Snare catheter (Osypka Medical GmbH, Berlin, Germany), consisting of a distal snare loop and a catheter shaft with an internal lumen, was introduced from the right femoral vein, and the detached ventricular lead was grasped (Figure 1E). The lead–snare system was pulled toward the left subclavian region (Figure 1F) and externalized through the left prepectoral pocket (Figure 2A and 2B). After externalization, the ventricular lead was removed while intentionally leaving the Osypka Snare catheter in place as an intravascular conduit (Figure 2C). The distal end of the Osypka Snare catheter was prepared for cutting at the pocket side (Figure 2D) and was then cut to open the catheter lumen (Figure 2E). A 0.035-inch guidewire was advanced through the lumen of the Osypka Snare catheter from the femoral side, with the back end of the guidewire inserted first to leave the soft angled tip on the intravascular side for subsequent implantation (Figure 2F). After confirming that the guidewire had been externalized from the pocket side through the snare catheter (Figure 2G), the Osypka Snare catheter was removed from the femoral side while maintaining the guidewire position (Figure 2H). This maneuver established a continuous guidewire route across the occluded venous segment from the left prepectoral pocket to the femoral vein.

Using this retained guidewire, a sheath was introduced, and ipsilateral pacemaker reimplantation was performed. A new Azure W2DR01 pacemaker generator (Medtronic) was implanted. A new atrial lead 5076-52 (Medtronic) was positioned in the right atrial appendage, and a new ventricular lead 3830-69 (Medtronic) was positioned in the right ventricular septum. The device was programmed to AAI⇔DDD mode, with a lower rate of 60 beats per minute and an upper tracking rate of 130 beats per minute. The procedure was completed without complications, and the postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 6.

Discussion

The key point of this case is that, in a patient requiring pacemaker lead revision with ipsilateral venous occlusion, the detached ventricular lead was not simply removed; instead, the pre-existing lead course was intentionally converted into a guidewire route, allowing preservation of ipsilateral venous access for immediate reimplantation. CIED lead-related venous stenosis or occlusion is a clinically relevant issue during lead revision or device upgrade, and contemporary consensus documents emphasize the importance of assessing venous patency before the procedure and planning an individualized strategy according to patient anatomy, procedural goals, and operator expertise.1 When ipsilateral venous access cannot be obtained, potential strategies include contralateral lead implantation with tunneling, venoplasty, leadless pacemaker implantation in selected patients, or TLE to regain access.1, 2, 3, 4 In particular, when reimplantation from the same operative site is intended, maintaining vascular access during extraction—either before extraction or through retained access via the extraction sheath—is an important procedural consideration.1 The SAIL technique was developed to address this specific problem by preserving the original lead route and converting it into a stable guidewire route before venous access is lost.

In conventional lead extraction procedures, even after the lead tip has been detached from the myocardium, residual fibrotic tissue surrounding the lead may prevent the lead–tissue complex from being fully accommodated within the extraction sheath. In such situations, further dissection toward the distal lead is often required to achieve complete extraction. The SAIL technique used in this case is designed to convert the pre-existing lead course into a guidewire route using the detached lead as a temporary rail and the Osypka Snare catheter introduced from the femoral vein as an intravascular conduit. This allowed preservation of ipsilateral venous access and provided a bridge to reimplantation while avoiding additional dissection toward the lead tip.

The femoral approach is an important adjunctive strategy in complex TLE procedures. A combined superior and femoral approach, also known as the tandem approach, combines extraction sheath manipulation from the superior approach with traction from the femoral side, thereby improving coaxial alignment between the lead and sheath and enabling more stable traction and countertraction.5 In addition, simultaneous traction from above and below has been reported to improve the relationship between the lead and the vessel wall and may reduce excessive tenting in the superior vena cava region.6 More recently, femoral snare-based techniques for leads without free ends, such as the Wire TRUST technique and WIPER TRUST technique, have been reported, demonstrating the usefulness of lead-grasping techniques using snares, guidewires, and pigtail catheters.7,8

However, the primary purpose of previously reported snare-related techniques has mainly been lead grasping, traction-direction optimization, or support of lead extraction. In contrast, the distinctive feature of the SAIL technique is not simply grasping and extracting the lead but intentionally replacing the venous access route that might otherwise be lost during extraction with a guidewire route. Thus, this technique should be regarded not only as an extraction technique but also as a bridging technique that connects extraction to reimplantation. Previous reports have described maintaining venous access by advancing a guidewire through an extraction sheath in patients with venous occlusion, followed by reimplantation using that access.3,4 However, such techniques generally require the extraction sheath to be sufficiently advanced to the occluded segment or near the distal portion of the lead. In the present case, although the ventricular lead tip had already been detached from the myocardium, residual fibrotic tissue surrounding the lead prevented the lead–tissue complex from being fully accommodated within the extraction sheath. Although the entire lead system could be pulled toward the pocket side, continuing conventional extraction would have required additional dissection toward the lead tip, potentially increasing procedural complexity and vascular risk. Therefore, the lead–snare system was externalized through the pocket side, and the retained snare catheter was used as an intravascular conduit to convert the pre-existing lead course into a guidewire route. This concept represents the core of the SAIL technique and highlights its unique role in venous access preservation. Several technical variations of this concept may be possible. For example, after the snare catheter has been externalized through the prepectoral pocket, one could attempt to advance a guidewire from the pocket side through the retained catheter. However, after cutting the pocket-side end of the Osypka Snare catheter, the cut end may become compressed and oval-shaped, making guidewire insertion from the pocket side difficult. Therefore, in the present case, the guidewire was advanced from the intact femoral side of the snare catheter. To maintain appropriate guidewire orientation for subsequent implantation, the 0.035-inch guidewire was inserted from its back end rather than from the angled tip, allowing the back end to be externalized from the pocket side while leaving the soft angled tip on the intravascular side.

The SAIL technique may offer several potential advantages. First, it may avoid the need for additional extraction sheath advancement toward the lead tip. In long-dwelling leads, dense fibrotic adhesion or calcification may be present in the subclavian vein, brachiocephalic vein, or superior vena cava region, and additional dissection may increase the risk of vascular injury. Second, because the technique uses the pre-existing lead course, it may provide a relatively reliable guidewire route across the occluded venous segment. Third, it may allow reimplantation through the same prepectoral pocket, potentially avoiding the additional invasiveness associated with alternative access routes and the future increase in lead burden associated with contralateral implantation.

Alternative pacing strategies should also be considered in such cases. In a patient with a history of end-stage renal disease, long-dwelling transvenous leads, venous occlusion, and severe tricuspid regurgitation, a dual-chamber leadless pacemaker may be an important option. If leadless pacing is selected either from the start with lead abandonment or after lead extraction, venous access preservation using the SAIL technique would not be necessary. Therefore, the SAIL technique should not be viewed as a replacement for leadless pacing, contralateral implantation, venoplasty, or other access strategies. Rather, it may be useful as a bridging technique in selected cases in which ipsilateral transvenous reimplantation is chosen.

Nevertheless, this technique is not applicable to all cases. The SAIL technique may be considered in noninfectious venous occlusion cases in which ipsilateral venous access preservation is required and the lead tip has already been detached from the myocardium. The technique should not be continued if strong resistance is encountered during externalization of the lead–snare system toward the left prepectoral pocket, vascular tenting is suspected, severe lead-to-vessel adhesion remains, or the course of the snare catheter cannot be safely confirmed under fluoroscopy. In particular, in patients with CIED infection, immediate reimplantation using the ipsilateral pocket or the pre-existing lead route should generally be avoided, and the indication for this technique must be considered with caution.

We have had initial experience with several cases using this technique at our institution, all of which were technically successful. However, the number of cases remains limited, and further accumulation of experience is necessary before the safety and reproducibility of the technique can be generalized. The feasibility of this technique may depend on several factors, including the inner diameter of the snare catheter, guidewire compatibility, lead diameter, adhesion site, and the anatomical characteristics of the occluded venous segment. Therefore, the SAIL technique should not be considered a standard procedure but rather a bailout or bridging technique for selected noninfectious venous occlusion cases. Further experience is needed to clarify the anatomical conditions, indications, and limitations under which this technique may be most useful.

Conclusion

In this case, the SAIL technique enabled conversion of a detached ventricular lead into a guidewire route, allowing preservation of ipsilateral venous access for immediate pacemaker reimplantation in a patient with venous occlusion. Using the retained snare catheter as an intravascular conduit, this technique may avoid additional dissection toward the lead tip in selected cases. The SAIL technique may serve as a useful bailout or bridging strategy in selected noninfectious venous occlusion cases requiring ipsilateral reimplantation.
