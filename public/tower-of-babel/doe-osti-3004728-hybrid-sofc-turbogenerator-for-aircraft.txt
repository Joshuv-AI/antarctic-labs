Public Executive Summary
1. Summary of activities

 The following paragraphs provide a high-level summary of the work performed during
 Phases I and II of this contract. Note that the order deviates from the WBS in places to
 better reﬂect the actual sequence of activities and the connections between them.
 Corresponding task numbers in the WBS are provided in parentheses after the task titles.
 There are a total of thirteen (13) tasks.

 1.1. Develop model of the Energy Storage and Generation (ESPG) system (2,11)

 Two system models were developed. The ﬁrst was developed using Numerical
 Propulsion System Simulation (NPSS). It offered the advantage of being able to
 incorporate high ﬁdelity representations of the various components but the
 disadvantage of being very complex and slow making it unsuitable for exploring the
 parameter space to identify optimum designs and operating conditions. So, a simpler
 model of the system was developed in MATLAB. This was used to assess the impacts of
 various operating parameters (altitude, Mach number, OPR, TIT, speciﬁc power of
 ATR/HX/SOFC, etc.) on the range/endurance of an LNG-fueled B737-class aircraft as well
 as the ARPA-E designated system performance targets of delivered overall system
 speciﬁc energy (kJ/kg), delivered overall system speciﬁc power (kW/kg), overall system
 fuel cost ($/kW-h), and system capital cost ($/kW-hr). The results of this model were
 used to identify optimum operating parameters for the ESPG system.

 1.2. Develop Pressurized SOFC test facility (3.3)

 A schematic illustration of the pressurized test system is presented in Figure 1. It
 consists of a pressure vessel enclosing a furnace, an anode ﬂow humidiﬁer, up to 7 mass
 ﬂow controllers that control cathode air, fuel ﬂow, and sweep gas, a micro pump for
 delivering water to the humidiﬁer, an electronically controlled backpressure regulator for
 maintaining the vessel pressure, a 3-zone temperature control system for maintaining
 vessel temperature, a water pump/chiller for maintaining the pressure vessel walls at a
 safe temperature, 6 pressure transducers for measuring pressures at various places in
 the system and 12 thermocouples for measuring temperatures at various places in the
 system. All of this equipment is controlled by a 1.5kW Scribner 890g test load. The load
 is programmed to manage the startup and shutdown of the cell as well as the acquisition
 of polarization curves.

1.3. High power cell development (3)

High power density Gadolinium-Doped Ceria (GDC) based cells were
developed/optimized and scaled in size from button cells to 25 cm2 cells and then
commercially relevant 100 cm2 cell size. Button cells were used for cell
architecture/composition optimization at UMD and to provide data for modelling in
Phase I. Subsequently the focus shifted to scale-up manufacturing and in Phase II
supplying 100 cm2 cells from both UMD and commercial partner Alchemity for pressure
testing and stack development.

   1.3.1. Modeling

 Down-the-channel models of the high-power-density GDC-based SOFCs in stand-
 alone operation were completed that captured the complex coupling of mixed-ionic
 electronic conductivity in the electrolyte phase with the complex electrochemical
 and heterogeneous CH4 reforming surface reactions. These models of the high-
 power densities showed the importance of maintaining mean GDC cell temperatures
 below 680 ◦C with adequate air cooling and endothermic CH4 reforming. The
 modeling results of 10 cm x 10 cm GDC cells show that power densities up to 1.3 W
 cm-2 can be achieved with high cathode excess air ratios operating on partially pre-
 reformed CH4 at 5.0 bar inlet pressures and 600 ◦C. These power densities can be
 matched by thin-ﬁlm YSZ-based cells (10 cm x 10 cm) at much higher inlet
 temperatures > 680 ◦C that require more costly materials for interconnects and seals.
 These stand-alone SOFC models, which have been presented in two archival papers,
 have been used to model the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC models.

 1.3.2. Manufacturing

 UMD fabricated button cells rapidly exceeded project milestones achieving 3.0
 W/cm2 at 0.5 V with hydrogen fuel and 1.9 W/cm2 at 0.5 V with reformate fuel, all at
 650°C. With this success we shifted to scaling cells to 25 cm2 which achieved 1.6
 W/cm2 at 0.5 V with hydrogen fuel and 1.43 W/cm2 at 0.5 V with reformate fuel at
 650°C. Stability testing of the 25 cm² cells at 0.75 V for 100 hours in hydrogen and 6
 hours in reformate revealed no observable degradation at 650°C, conﬁrming stable
 performance under operating conditions. Focus then shifted to scaling cells to 100
 cm2 and not only performance milestones with the larger cells but also
 manufacturing milestones of cell ﬂatness achieving ≤ 30 µm/cm over 30 cells
 fabricated. With this demonstrated focus transitioned to cell fabrication tech transfer

 7

to Alchemity. Alchemity then implemented quality control metrics on not only cell
ﬂatness but thickness and width dimensional tolerances required for stack assembly.

1.3.3. Testing

cells tested with H2 at 650◦C and 1 bar. Cells 1 and 2 meet the target current density
(open stars) and power density (solid stars) at 0.75V within the ±10% range and cell 3

1.4. ATR/HX/SOFC development (4, 5, 8, and 10)
    1.4.1. Modeling
 Comprehensive multiscale modeling frameworks for the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC
 have been developed to assess how the high power-density SOFCs operate with the
 integrated ATR and HX upstream of the SOFC anode and cathode respectively.
 Experimentally calibrated 2D channel-level models of ‘relevant’ commercial planar
 cells (ie. Elcogen ASC-400B that feature thin-ﬁlm YSZ and higher-conducting GDC
 electrolytes) show that power densities of 1.3 W cm-2 with partially pre-reformed CH4
 in the ATR/HX can be achieved under pressurized conditions (5 bar) with appropriate
 thermal management strategies. CFD modeling supported the design of manifolds
 and ﬂow channels, focusing mainly on achieving uniform ﬂow and minimizing
 pressure losses. The CFD model of the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC stack shows that
 partial fuel oxidation with air bleeds into the ATR up to 25% of air ﬂow for full CH4
 oxidation can preheat compressor outlet air from 400 °C to adequate SOFC inlet
 temperatures (> 500 °C for GDC) and H2 mole fractions (> 25%) to sustain high power
 densities above 1.1 W cm-2 for GDC cells at fuel utilizations above 70%.

 GDC electrolytes achieve superior performance at intermediate temperatures (below
 650 °C) due to higher ionic conductivity, but electronic leakage current above 700 °C
 reduces faradaic efficiency and necessitates higher cathode air ratios (12-15 for
 hydrogen, 6-9 for methane) to maintain temperature gradients below 15 °C cm-1.
 These models guided the selection of operating conditions to test 10 cm channel-
 length Elcogen YSZ and Alchemity GDC cells in the lab and validate the model’s

 9

predictions under relevant conditions. A transient ‘down-the-channel’ model
simulated ATR/HX startup behavior, assessed control strategies, and predicts that
multi-cell conﬁgurations can reach steady operation within minutes.

Some results from the ATR/HX/SOFC simulation are presented in Figure 8. Figure 8a
compares the power density of the high power GDC cells (black curves) to the COTS
YSZ-based cells (red curves). The maximum in the CDC power density is due to the
competition between ionic and electronic conductivity. While both increase with
temperature, electronic conductivity increases more dramatically above 650◦C
leading to increased electronic leakage and reduced net current output despite
improved ionic transport. This behavior is particularly pronounced in the large
platform cells studied here that operate under commercially relevant utilization
conditions where temperature gradients develop along the channel length due to fuel
consumption and heat generation. Unlike button cell tests where temperatures are
uniform, these temperature proﬁles suggest that downstream portions of the cell can
experience temperatures where electronic leakage becomes severe enough to cause
performance degradation, even when inlet temperatures are moderate.

Figure 8. a) Mean SOFC W cm-2 for 10 cm × 10 cm cells with thin-ﬁlm YSZ COTS electrolytes and with high-power
density GDC electrolytes operating at Vcell = 0.70 V on partially reformed CH4 for a range of SOFC inlet
temperatures for three different inlet pressure, and b) contour plots of mean W cm-2 vs. anode recycle fraction εrec
and ATR air stoichiometry εair for 10 cm × 10 cm cells with high-power density GDC electrolytes operating in
integrated ATR/HX/SOFC at Vcell = 0.75 V and excess cathode air ratio εair = 5 and HX air inlet at 400 °C.

Figure 8b shows how the cell’s performance is affected by the operating conditions
of the ATR/HX upstream. The coupling is more complex than in a cell with
recuperative preheating because one is actively destroying chemical exergy in the
ATR to avoid separate heat exchangers and the associated balance of plant penalties.
Increasing the cell inlet temperature requires one to burn more fuel in the ATR and
operate at higher excess air ratios to keep the cell from getting too hot. Since faradaic
efficiency decreases with higher inlet temperatures and higher ATR oxidation
fractions, recent work suggests that it may actually be best to operate GDC cells at

 10

inlet temperatures of 600◦C or below to keep faradaic efficiencies over 95%. The
details of this tradeoff are still being worked out and will be the subject of a future
paper.

1.4.2. Design
3-D ANSYS Fluent simulations that included the upstream and downstream anode
and cathode manifolds were used to assess the distribution of fuel ﬂows through the
ATR and SOFC anode and the distribution of air ﬂows through the HX and SOFC
cathode. The goal was to design manifolds that produce velocity distributions in the
porous ATR and anode channel, the ribbed channels of the HX, and the cathode ﬂow
channels that are well distributed across the 10 cm width of the cell. This was
achieved using a 1.2 mm thick interconnect, an ATR/anode ﬂow channel thickness of
0.3 mm, and a HX/cathode ﬂow channel of 0.5 mm. The interconnect on the
ATR/anode side is attached to a spacer plate that creates a frame to secure the SOFC
cell. A thermicullite seal is designed to separate adjacent interconnects and provide
a non-conductive compressive seal that avoids rigid connection. The design
modeling in ANSYS Fluent with the manifold showed that the design supported high
power densities above 1.0 W cm-2 with the well-distributed ﬂows over both the anode
and cathode ﬂow passages.

1.4.3. Construction
Figure 9c is an exploded view of the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC developed for lab-scale
testing. It is created by acquiring thin ferritic steel stock (1.2 mm thick Crofer 22-H)
and electrochemically etching the ATR/anode passages on one side and the
HX/cathode passages on the other side. The etched plates are coated with a thin
MnCo spinel coating to reduce oxidation build-up on the cathode side and to mitigate
Cr-poisoning from the oxidize steel surface. The etched interconnect ATR and anode
channels are covered by 0.2 or 0.4 mm thick, 441 stainless steel spacer plates
(depending on the SOFC cell thickness) that are laser-welded to form a 0.3 mm thick
passage that captures the ATR and forms a frame in which to place the SOFC
membrane electrode assembly. The ATR is an oxidized Fecralloy mesh coated with
Pt-loaded Al2O3 porous washcoat. A series of holes around the edges of the
interconnects provide clearance for bolts that clamp the layers between two 6.5 mm
thick end plates. Insulating washers isolate the clamping bolts from the end plates
ensuring that the stack is not shorted out. Thermicullite seals allow for the stack to
be disassembled and reassembled with only replacing the seals. Figure 9a and Figure
9b are cross-sectional views through the ATR and SOFC sections respectively

 11

showing seals, mesh ATR, and mesh current collectors. This conﬁguration was used
to test short stacks of the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC cells.

1.4.4. Testing
    1.4.4.1. Mines
 Single cell and 3-cell short stack versions of the ATR/HX/SOFC test article
 described above were tested at 1 bar in the facility illustrated in Figure 10a. The
 facility includes steam generation for the ATR, premixing of the steam with fuel
 (CH4 and/or H2) for the ATR inlet, and preheating of the air entering both sides of
 the ATR/HX element to 400◦C so as to simulate the outlet conditions of the gas
 turbine’s compressor. Figure 10b shows that these conditions were sufficient to
 achieve rapid light-off of the catalytic ATR followed by additional preheating that
 raised the SOFC inlet to desirable temperatures: above 550◦C for thin-ﬁlm YSZ-
 electrolyte cells and 500◦C for GDC-based electrolyte cells. A 3-cell
 ATR/HX/SOFC short stack of COTS cells (Elcogen with thin-ﬁlm YSZ-electrolyte
 MEAs) reached operating temperatures of 550◦C in < 30 min. Such start-up times
 are expected to be substantially reduced for larger ATR/HX/SOFC stacks where
 heat loss per stack volume will be much smaller. ATR/HX/SOFC tests with the
 COTS cells in single-cell and 3-cell short tasks have achieved MEA power
 densities of 0.67 W cm-2 at 0.7 V/cell operating with ATR/HX inlets slightly above
 400◦C and SOFC operating temperature around 700 ◦C. Simulated performance
 with the YSZ-based COTS cells shows pathway to higher power densities > 1.0 W
 cm-2 with higher temperature operation that can be achieved with higher
 operating. Tests of the thin-ﬁlm YSZ cells show that multiple shutdowns and start-
 up cycles can be achieved with repeatable power density results indicating the
 potential for the reliability of the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC stack design.

1.4.5. High power stack development (9)
UMD developed a stack test ﬁxture and implemented high current (400 A) testing
capability for its 100 cm2 high-power cells. Unfortunately issues with sealing and
contact resistance required additional work that limited its ability to demonstrate a
stack before the contract was cancelled.
Alchemity commissioned a facility capable of fabricating cells, assembling stacks,
and testing stacks up to 7 kWe. 100 cm2 cells were successfully produced in-house
meeting strict speciﬁcations for stack testing with detailed QA/QC processed
implementation and speciﬁcation sheets.
UMD also designed the 40-cell ‘through-ﬂow’ ATR/HX/SOFC stack illustrated in Figure
12. It features weight reduction and thermal control layers. The integrated unit is
projected to produce about 3.5 kW at a conservative cell power density of 1 Wcm-2
and deliver speciﬁc power of ~0.18 kW/kg. While this is substantially less than what
is required for ‘break even’ as described in an earlier section, it is more than enough
to exceed the overall REEACH system performance targets. A shorter version of this
integrated ATR/HS/SOFC stack was intended to be the test article for the experiments
at RTX.

 1.5. Commercialization (6, 13)
 Alchemity identiﬁed three short-term focus market segments during technology
 scaleup including: heavy-duty tracking powertrain market, power for data centers,
 and propulsion for aviation starting with UAVs/drones. As Alchemity is developing a
 10 kW building module (ﬁrst major market entry), the powertrain market can be
 addressed by integrating into 100 kW 2nd GEN module. With deployment of 100 kW
 systems, a 3rd GEN module (2-5 MW) will be developed for drones and APUs for
 smaller UAVs. This will open the market for datacenter skids.

2. How does the research add to the understanding of the area investigated?
 • Pressurization improves the performance (power density) of two different types of
 solid oxide fuel cells: COTS Ni-YSZ cells and advanced Ni-GDC cells. Increasing
 the pressure from 1 bar to 5 bar increases mean stack power density by
 approximately 33% for COTS Ni-YSZ cells and 50% for Ni-GDC cells.
 • The project developed methodologies, procedures, and test rigs for testing high
 temperature fuel cells at pressure.
 • The reformer/fuel cell component of an LNG-powered turbine-fuel cell hybrid for
 a B737 class aircraft needs to achieve at least 1.0 kWe kg-1 for ‘range neutrality’ -
 delivering the same range as the non-hybrid version of the aircraft. Improvements
 in speciﬁc power beyond 1.0 kWe kg-1 will result in increased range/endurance over
 the non-hybrid version.
 • Ni-GDC cells operating at relatively low temperatures < 680 °C can achieve power
 densities at 1 bar of up to 1.3 W cm-2 on simulated reformate.
 • Models of Ni-YSZ and Ni-GDC fuel cells predict mean power density
 improvements due to pressurization, optimal cathode excess air ﬂow, and cell
 operating voltage.
 • Models for the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC stack predicted system performance of
 over 1.1 W cm-2 for Ni-GDC cells at sustainable operating conditions with inlet air
 ﬂow temperatures of 400 °C.
 • The models for the integrated ATR/HX/SOFC stacks provided a basis to identify
 the excess cathode-air ﬂows that sustain high-power densities for Ni-GDC cells
 and COTS YSZ cells.
 • System-level models provided new insight as to how to reduce balance of plant
 weight by combining the reformer and fuel cell and thereby to integrate fuel cells
 into gas turbine ﬂow paths.

3. What are the technical effectiveness and economic feasibility of the methods and
 techniques investigated?
 • The models and lab-scale testing showed that the tightly integrated ATR/SOFC
 system envisioned for the project works and there are clear pathways to achieving
 over 1.3 W cm-2 for Ni-GDC cells in an integrated ATR/HX/SOFC at operating
 conditions with maximum temperatures low enough to use lower-temperature
 alloys.

4. How has the project been of beneﬁt to the public?
 4.1. Improved performance and lowered the cost of fuel cell systems
 4.2. Identiﬁed a technology that is capable of reducing fuel burn in 737-class aircraft
 by 25%. Since 80% of aviation fuel is consumed by 737-class aircraft, this
 amounts to a 20% reduction in the fuel consumption of the entire aviation sector.
 4.3. Trained students who continue to develop fuel saving technologies in the
 workforce.
 4.4. Launched a company (Alchemity Inc.) that will provide high paying jobs for
 workers in Maryland while delivering higher performance, lower cost fuel cells to
 the US and global markets.
 4.5. Shared the lessons learned from this project with the larger technical community
 by publishing a series of papers and giving talks at international conferences. This
 will enable the entire US population (and the world’s) to capitalize on our work.

Project Activities
The overall objective is to develop what amounts to a ﬂying powerplant for an all-electric
B737-class aircraft. The fuel is liquiﬁed natural gas (LNG) and the powerplant is a
turbogenerator (gas turbine driving a mechanical generator) equipped with scalable ~2kWe
reformer/solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) elements, located between the compressor and
combustor, that provide ~30% additional electric power at cruise. The result is a synergistic
system in which the fuel cell offers more efficient energy conversion while the engine
performs most of the ‘balance of plant’ functions that make stand-alone cells too heavy to
ﬂy. It also recovers waste energy in the fuel cell exhaust further increasing system efficiency
and enables the system to respond to rapid changes in load. Unique aspects of our
approach are the use of new Ni-GDC SOFC materials offering ~2x the power density of
traditional SOFCs, pressurization to further improve the SOFC’s performance, tightly
integrating the reformer and fuel cell to reduce weight, a ‘ﬂow through’ design that minimizes
pressure loss and is suitable for gas turbine integration. The work broke into ﬁve main areas:
1. System modeling to assess the technology’s impact on the overall performance of the
vehicle and to quantify the performance of the reformer/fuel cell system needed to meet the
program’s requirements, 2. Development of high-performance Ni-GDC SOFCs, 3.
Development of a scalable, ~2kWe integrated autothermal reformer / heat exchanger/ fuel
cell (ATR/HX/SOFC) module, 4. Development of a facility for testing cells and ATR/HX/SOFC
modules at pressure, 5. Demonstration of the ATR/HX/SOFC in a realistic gas turbine ﬂow
ﬁeld in order to achieve TRL6 by program end, and 6. Advancing commercialization efforts
by identifying initial markets, developing business plans, developing total cost of ownership
models, and advancing manufacturing capabilities.

There were several changes to the work plan along the way. The original intent was to
develop the SOFC technology on 25cm2 cells and then transition to 100cm2 cells. However,
the 10x10 work progressed much more rapidly than anticipated making some milestones
irrelevant (M3.1.2 and M3.3.3). Other tasks that had involved 25cm2 cells transitioned to
100cm2 cells (M3.2.2-M3.2.4). The ‘ﬂow bench’ that was to be developed to test the
integrated ATR/HX/SOFC at UMD was eliminated (M5.3.1-M5.3.5) in favor of tests to be
performed in Raytheon’s jet burner test facility in Phase II (M11 tasks). Difficulties
associated with cell cracking, sealing, the resistance of the cell-current collector interface,
and manufacturing cells in sufficient quantity/quality led to a mutual agreement between
UMD and the program manager to terminate the project early. Milestones retained for work
conducted between the decision to terminate (5/2/25) and the work stop date (7/31/25) were

 34

for continued cell development (M3.3.4, M3.4.2, and M3.4.3), stack development (M9.2.1
and M9.3.2), and ATR/HX/SOFC development (M10.2.1 and M10.3.1). The tests of the
integrated ATR/HX/SOFC at RTX were eliminated.

Some key outcomes of the work are:

 • Ni-GDC SOFC materials: 1W/cm2 at 0.75V and 1 bar at 650C on H2 vs 0.59W/cm2 for
 COTS Ni-YSZ cells.
 • Pressurizing cells to 5 bar increases power density by ~12% in COTS Ni-YSZ cells and
 ~30% in Ni-GDC cells. The effect is stronger in Ni-GDC cells because pressurization
 suppresses leakage due to the cells’ mixed ionic-electronic conductivity.
 • ATR/HX/SOFC must achieve 1 kW/kg to achieve ‘range neutrality’ ie. no loss in vehicle
 range/endurance when switching from turbogenerator to hybrid system.
 • The current version of the ATR/HX/SOFC can achieve ~650W/kg. Further
 improvements in the design should be able to achieve XXX W/kg.
