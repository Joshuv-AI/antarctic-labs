Localization from Pseudoranges: Quadrics and Duality
Mireille Boutin, Gregor Kemper

Abstract
. This paper gives a complete description of the solutions of the global positioning problem, emphasizing the under-determined case. We show that the solutions form a quadric, which may degenerate in various ways. Perhaps more surprisingly, the satellite positions also lie on a quadric, and these two quadrics exhibit a remarkable duality: They live on perpendicular affine spaces but share the same axis of symmetry. Moreover, the vertices of one quadric are the foci of the other and vice versa. The results of this paper are not only applicable to the global positioning problem, but to a wider class of problems known as pseudorange-multilateration. This includes a range of real-world localization problems where a signal is emitted at an unknown emission time, and received by sensors at known positions. In particular, the paper can be useful for solving an under-determined multilateration problem in the presence of additional constraints. We illustrate this with two examples: locating a cleaning robot on the ground and locating a raft on the ocean.



Introduction

Geolocation is the process of determining the physical position of a user or an event [
?
gentile2012geolocation]. This task arises in a wide range of applications. In logistics [
?
shamsuzzoha2011real,
?
shamsuzzoha2013performance,
?
dong2023design] and warehousing [
?
LeeWarehouse2019], for instance, tracking the location of moving objects is critical. In everyday life, most of us rely on our smartphones’ ability to pinpoint our location as we navigate and travel.

Geolocation can be performed using landmarks situated at fixed, precisely known locations. The distance to one of these landmarks is called the “range.” Geolocation from range values is obtained by finding the intersection of the circles or spheres defined by the ranges, a process called trilateration [
?
arlinghaus1994practical]. Alternatively, one can use the angles of the lines of sight to the landmarks and determine ones location by triangulation.

In 2D space, geolocation using range values requires at least three landmarks. This is because three circles generally intersect at a single point. In 3D space, that number increases to four because, in general, three spheres intersect at two points and so a fourth circle is required to disambiguate the position.
Thus, in outdoor areas with high cellular coverage, one can determine their location using four (generically positioned) cell towers. Similarly, in indoor environments, where cell phone reception is poorer [
?
pahlavan2000overview], one can instead geolocate in 3D space by using four (well-positioned) wifi signal emitters [
?
cheng2005accuracywifi,
?
retscher2007performance].
In some cases, the distances to the landmarks are only known up to an unknown (constant) shift. In that case, one talks about geolocation from “pseudoranges” and the underlying localization technique is called “multilateration.”

One example of this is the problem is gunshot localization, where a gun, fired at an unknown location and time,
is heard at known times at several positions nearby using an electro-acoustic system [
?
gunshothistory2013]. By dividing the reception times of the gun sound by the speed of sound, one obtains the
pseudoranges to the location from which the gun was fired. Multilateration can then be used to try to pinpoint that location. Other sound events (e.g., door clapping, car backfiring, fireworks) can also be located in a similar fashion.

Another well-known example is global positioning. In that case, a user receives the position and emission times of several precisely synchronized emitters. Outdoors, these could be satellites such as those from GPS or Galileo. Under water, these could be seafloor mounted emitters [
?
UAVacoustic1998] such as those used in long baselines (LBL) acoustic positioning systems [
?
BATISTAlongbaseline2015,
?
ZhangdesignLBL2019].
The clocks of the emitters are precisely synchronized with each other, but not with the user’s clock. Thus the difference between the emission times of the signals and the reception time on the user’s clock only reveals the distance to the emitters up to an unknown constant. As the constant is the same for all the emitters, this determines the pseudoranges to each of the emitters.

In general, geolocation requires the landmarks’ locations to be generic, that is to say, one needs to avoid certain “bad” configurations from which a unique location cannot be determined. Another situation to avoid is when the landmarks are close to being in a bad configuration, as this can create numerical issues that can prevent accurate localization when the data is perturbed by noise. For example, in global positioning using satellites, one should avoid satellite configurations that are visually close in the sky. This phenomenon is described by the so-called “geometric dilution of precision” [
?
langley1999dilution].
Other positions to avoid are the individual sheets of hyperboloids of revolution with one focus at the user position [
?
Boutin:Kemper:2024]. As shown in [
?
Boutin:Kemper:Notices], localization becomes numerically unstable as the emitters position gets closer to such a configuration.

Another issue that may arise is that fewer landmarks than needed are available, what we call “degenerated scenarios.”
For example,
in order to obtain a signal from a landmark using electromagnetic signals, it is necessary to have a clear line of sight to the landmark. This can be difficult in urban environments where high buildings and narrow streets can obstruct the signal [
?
chen2022phone] creating so-called
urban canyon
. Such signal blockage also occurs in natural settings, where the view can be obstructed by trees, hills, or large rocks. In some scenarios, an adversary may be purposefully jamming the satellite signals to prevent location.
Since a minimal number of landmark signal observations is necessary for unique location, there are many situations where geolocation is not possible.

One option to disambiguate the location when two few landmarks are in view is to use other signals available. For example,
workaround solutions have been proposed for global positioning in urban environment, such as using the phase of the signals [
?
mohamadi2025phase] or using other
telecommunications networks [
?
koelemeij2022hybrid].
Another option is to use known constraints on the user position.
For example,
assuming omnidirectional antennas, the minimum number of cell towers required to determine the 3D location of a user is four. But if the user is assumed to be on the earth surface, then this can be done with only three towers. In particular, if the user is assumed to be on the earth sphere, then the center of the earth can be used as a fourth landmark, and the earth sphere used as a fourth range. This is a reasonable assumption in many scenarios, e.g., when traveling at sea level (on a ship.) Assuming that the sphere centers do not lie on a straight line,
intersecting the four spheres then yields a single solution.
Similar, for global positioning, four is the minimum number of satellites required. With three satellites, the solution set is one-dimensional. But if the user is assumed to be on the earth sphere, three satellites yield a finite number of location possibility– at most four [
?
Boutin:Kemper:Eggermont:2025].

In the following, we focus on the problem of localization from pseudoranges
(e.g., global positioning, gunshot location, etc.) and analyze the degenerated
scenarios in detail: that is, the case where too few landmarks for precise
location are available. We show that the set of possible locations (i.e., the
solution set) determined by such problems form a quadric, which may degenerate
in various ways. We also show that the positions of satellites (in the global
positioning situation) or sensors (in the situation of gunshot location etc.)
corresponding to the solution set form a quadric as well, and these two
quadrics exhibit a remarkable duality: they live on perpendicular affine spaces but share the same axis of symmetry. Moreover, the vertices of one quadric are the foci of the other and vice versa. We invite readers to take a look at
Figure

1
already now, where the situation is illustrated.

This duality has practical consequences. For example, if there are three
sensors lying on a common plane, such as the ground plane, inside
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, our
results imply that the quadric of solutions is a conic section that passes
through this plane perpendicularly. This is important since if it is known that
the actual solution (e.g. the gunshot location) lies very close to that plane,
then it can be determined very accurately because of the perpendicularity.

Among other things, our results provide a clear description of the solution set of any degenerate localization from pseudorange problem, and hold for any space dimension. Thus, they can be used as a starting point to disambiguate the location, be it by using other signals (e.g., wifi or cellular towers) or by using a priori knowledge about the location such as geometric navigation constraints, as already mentioned.
We illustrate the latter using two scenarios. In the first scenario, presented in
Section

8.1
, an indoor robot vacuum uses emitters affixed to the ceiling to determine its location on the ground. In the second scenario, presented in
Section

8.2
, we discuss the problem of locating a raft on the ocean.

The well-known

\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert=t_{i}-b\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m),

1.2

1.1
in

5

1.3

The system

Let us recall a solution procedure for the sphere intersection

\langle\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\rangle_{Q}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{1}\rangle_{Q}-d_{1}\\

\langle\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{m},\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{m}\rangle_{Q}-d_{m}\end{pmatrix}

So if we write

\langle\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}=\{\mathbf{v}+b\cdot\mathbf{u}\mid b\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
, so
Theorem

2.1
implies that

g(b,0,\ldots,0)=0
for all
b
.

Conversely, assume that
g(b,0,\ldots,0)=0
for all
b
. Then by

Theorem

2.1
the solution set
\mathfrak{X}
is as asserted in the
proposition. The assumption also implies
\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert=1
. Let
(t,\mathbf{s})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\lVert^{2}-(t-b)^{2}=0
for all

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
. Then for all
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

0=\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}-b\mathbf{u}\lVert^{2}-(t-b)^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}-2\langle\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\rangle b-t^{2}+2tb,

so
t=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\rangle
and

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\lVert^{2}=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\rangle^{2}
. The second equation together with

\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert=1
implies

\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\rangle\mathbf{u}=t\mathbf{u}
,
so
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{v}+t\mathbf{u}
.
Conversely,
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{v}+t\mathbf{u}
implies

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\lVert^{2}-(t-b)^{2}=0
for all

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
. Since the condition

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\lVert^{2}-(t-b)^{2}=0
for all

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
applies in particular to

(t,\mathbf{s})=(t_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i})
, we obtain
\mathbf{s}_{i}=\mathbf{v}+t_{i}\mathbf{u}

for
i=1,\ldots,m
, so
m\leq 2
by the general linear position
hypothesis. Moreover,
\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\neq 0
implies
m>1
by

Example

2.2
, so
m=2
. By
Lemma

2.3
we get

\lVert\mathbf{s}_{1}-\mathbf{s}_{2}\rVert=|t_{1}-t_{2}|
.
∎

3.
The quadric of satellites

Having gotten a formula for the solution set
\mathfrak{X}

of
Equation

1.2
in
Theorem

2.1
, in this section we reverse our
point of view by considering the set

(3.1)

\mathcal{S}:=\Bigl\{(t,\mathbf{s})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \Bigl|\ \lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}=(t-b)^{2}\ \text{for all}\ (b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}\Bigr\}.

So clearly
(t_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i})\in\mathcal{S}
for all
i
. We continue to
use the definitions and notation from the previous section. The
following result determines
\mathcal{S}
under the additional
hypothesis that
Equation

1.2
has at least two solutions.

Theorem 3.1

(The locus of satellites)
.

Let
\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be in general linear position,

m\leq n+1
, and let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Form

\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
as defined before

Theorem

2.1
. Set

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:=\Bigl\{(t,\mathbf{s})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \Bigl|\ \langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle=\gamma_{j}\ (j=1,\ldots,k),\ t=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle-\alpha,\ \text{and}\ \ \widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0\Bigr\},

where

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s}):=\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle^{2}+2\langle\alpha\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}\rangle+\beta-\alpha^{2}.

Notice that the condition
“
\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle=\gamma_{j}
” in the definition of

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
means that

\mathbf{s}\in\langle\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}
.

(a)

For
i=1,\ldots,m
we have

(t_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

(b)

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.

(c)

If
|\mathfrak{X}|>1
, then

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Remark 3.2
.

The cases
|\mathfrak{X}|=1
and
|\mathfrak{X}|=0
are easily
dealt with: If
\mathfrak{X}=\{(b_{0},\mathbf{x}_{0})\}
, then

\mathcal{S}=\bigl\{(t,\mathbf{s})\mid\mathbf{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \text{and}\ \ t=b_{0}\pm\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\rVert\bigr\}.

If
\mathfrak{X}=\emptyset
then
\mathcal{S}={\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

\triangleleft

The following lemma is the heart of the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

In the situation of
Theorem

3.1
let
(t,\mathbf{s})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

and form the matrix
A^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(m+1)\times(n+2)}
by appending
the row
(-2t,2\mathbf{s}^{T},-1)
at the bottom of
A
as defined
by
Equation

2.4
. Let
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
be the set of solutions

(b,\mathbf{x})
of the system
Equation

1.2
, enlarged by adding
the pair
(t_{m+1},\mathbf{s}_{m+1}):=(t,\mathbf{s})
.

(a)

If
\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})=m
then

\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle=\gamma_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
and

t=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle-\alpha
.

(b)

If

\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle=\gamma_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
and

t=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle-\alpha
, then

\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathfrak{X}&\text{if}&\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0\\
\emptyset&\text{if}&\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})\neq 0\end{array}\right..

Proof.

(a)

Since
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})
, both matrices share
the same kernel. In particular, the vectors

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
\mathbf{w}_{j}\\
2\gamma_{j}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
(for
k=1,\ldots,k
) and

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{u}\\
2\alpha\end{smallmatrix}\right)
lie in the kernel of

A^{\prime}
. This implies
\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle-\gamma_{j}=0

and
-t+\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u}\rangle-\alpha=0
.

(b)

Let
b,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and set

\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}
. Then

(3.2)

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-(t-b)^{2}=\bigl\langle\textstyle\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}-\mathbf{s},\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}-\mathbf{s}\bigr\rangle\\
-\bigl(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle-\alpha-b\bigr)^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}+2\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle b+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}\gamma_{j}-2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}\rangle+\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^{2}b^{2}-2\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle b\\
+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}^{2}-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{k}\gamma_{j}+\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle^{2}+2\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle\alpha+2\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle b-\alpha^{2}-2\alpha b-b^{2}=\\
\bigl(\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^{2}-1\bigr)b^{2}+2\bigl(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle-\alpha\bigr)b+\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}-2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}^{2}+\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle^{2}+2\langle\alpha\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle-\alpha^{2}\\
=g(b,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})+\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s}).

Suppose
\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0
. If
(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
then
by
Theorem

2.1
,
\mathbf{x}
can be written as above and

g(b,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=0
. So
Equation

3.2
tells us that

\lVert\textstyle\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-(t-b)^{2}=0
, so

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
. Since

\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{X}
holds in any case, we have
equality. Now suppose
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, so there is

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
. Thus
(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
,
and by
Theorem

2.1
,

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}
with

g(b,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})=0
. We have

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}=(t-b)^{2}
, so
Equation

3.2
shows

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0
. Therefore
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}=\emptyset
if

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})\neq 0
. ∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

(a)

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Then

\langle\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle=\gamma_{j}
by the definition of
the
\gamma_{j}
, and
t_{i}=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}_{i}\rangle-\alpha

follows from
Equation

2.5
. From
Equation

2.2
we get

\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\rVert^{2}-t_{i}+\beta\bigr)
. Therefore

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s}_{i})=\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\rVert^{2}-(t_{i}+\alpha)^{2}+2\alpha(t_{i}+\alpha)-\bigl(\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\rVert^{2}-t_{i}^{2}+\beta\bigr)+\beta-\alpha^{2}=0.

So we have shown
(t_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

(b)

Let
(t,\mathbf{s})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Then

Lemma

3.3
(b)
tells us that

\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{X}
, so

\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}=(t-b)^{2}
for every

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
. Therefore

(t,\mathbf{s})\in\mathcal{S}
, and the inclusion

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is established.

(c)

With
g(b,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
as defined in

Theorem

2.1
, it is convenient to write

g_{0}(b):=g(b,0,\ldots,0)
, which is a polynomial of degree

\leq 2
. Let
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We see from
Theorem

2.1
that in the
case
k>0
, there exists an
\mathbf{x}
with

(b,\mathbf{x})\in\mathfrak{X}
if and only if
g_{0}(b)\leq 0
. In
the case
k=0
the condition is
g_{0}(b)=0
. In both cases,
if
g_{0}(b)=0
, then
\mathbf{x}
is unique. Thus in the case

k=0
the hypothesis
|\mathfrak{X}|>1
implies that there
are real numbers
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
such that
g_{0}(b_{i})=0
for
both
i
. In addition,
Theorem

2.1
shows that

\langle\mathfrak{X}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
,
the affine subspace generated by
\mathfrak{X}
, has
dimension
1
. On the other hand, in the case
k>0
, we see
that either
g_{0}(b)=0
for all
b
or there are real numbers

b_{1}\neq b_{2}
such that
g_{0}(b_{i})<0
for both
i
. In the
first subcase we can pick
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
with
g_{0}(b_{i})=0
, and
in the second we obtain

\dim\bigl(\langle\mathfrak{X}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}\bigr)=k+1
. What we need to remember from this discussion is the
following: First, there exist real numbers
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
and
points
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that

(b_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathfrak{X}
for both
i
. And second,

\dim\bigl(\langle\mathfrak{X}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}\bigr)=k+1

or else
g_{0}
is identically zero.

Now let
(t,\mathbf{s})\in\mathcal{S}
. Form matrices

A^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(m+1)\times(n+2)}
and

B^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(m+1)\times(n+1)}
by appending the rows

(-2t,2\mathbf{s}^{T},-1)
and
(2\mathbf{s}^{T},-1)
at the bottom of
A

and
B
, respectively.
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
clearly have rank
\geq m
. We claim that
\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}(B^{\prime})=m
. We first treat

B^{\prime}
, and first consider the case that
g_{0}(b)=0
for
all
b
. This case was treated in
2.4
, which tells
us that
m=2
and that
\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}
, and
\mathbf{s}
are
collinear. So we have
\operatorname{rank}(B^{\prime})=2=m
. In the case that

g_{0}
is not identically zero we have

\dim\bigl(\langle\mathfrak{X}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}\bigr)=k+1
. By way of contradiction, let us assume

\operatorname{rank}(B^{\prime})=m+1
. Then we can apply
Theorem

2.1
to the
enlarged system
Equation

1.2
with

(t_{m+1},\mathbf{s}_{m+1}):=(t,\mathbf{s})
, and this results in a set
of solutions
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
with

\dim\bigl(\langle\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}\bigr)<k+1
. But
\mathfrak{X}=\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
since

(\mathbf{s},t)\in\mathcal{S}
, so this is a contradiction. Thus
also in this case
\operatorname{rank}(B^{\prime})=m
.

To deal with the matrix
A^{\prime}
we use the

(b_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathfrak{X}
,
i=1,2
. Since

(\mathbf{s},t)\in\mathcal{S}
, the
(b_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
are also
solutions of the enlarged system. Therefore they also satisfy
the matrix version
Equation

1.7
, and we get

A^{\prime}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}-b_{2}\\
\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\\
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{1}\rVert^{2}-b_{1}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}_{2}\rVert^{2}+b_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{s}_{1}\rVert^{2}-t_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\lVert\mathbf{s}_{m}\rVert^{2}-t_{m}^{2}\\
\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{s}_{1}\rVert^{2}-t_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\lVert\mathbf{s}_{m}\rVert^{2}-t_{m}^{2}\\
\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

Since
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
, this shows that the first column of
A^{\prime}

lies in the span of the other columns. But
A^{\prime}
is obtained from

B^{\prime}
by adding the first column, so
\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}(B^{\prime})=m
.

Now
Lemma

3.3
tells us that

\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle=\gamma_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
and

t=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle-\alpha
, and, since

\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{X}=\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
, that

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0
. So
(t,\mathbf{s})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, which
completes the proof. ∎

Analogously to the quadric
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
of solutions we also consider the

quadric of satellites
, defined as

(3.3)

Q_{\mathrm{sat}}:=\bigl\{\mathbf{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid(t,\mathbf{s})\in\mathcal{S}\ \text{for some}\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}\bigr\},

with
\mathcal{S}
from
Equation

3.1
. It should be emphasized that even though the satellite positions
\mathbf{s}_{i}
lie on
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
, this does not mean that

is determined by the
\mathbf{s}_{i}
. Rather, it is determined by the
\mathbf{s}_{i}

together
with the
t_{i}
. For instance,
Example

4.2
shows that for the same
\mathbf{s}_{i}
, very different sorts of quadrics occur. If
|\mathfrak{X}|>1
, then by

Theorem

3.1
this consists of all points
\mathbf{s}
in the affine span of

\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}
that satisfy
\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0
. So
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}

is a quadric within

\langle\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}
, which may be
degenerate. For example, in the case of
2.4
,

and

are both equal to the line
{\mathbb{R}}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}
.

We give two examples.

Example 3.4
.

As a follow-up to
Example

2.2
we look at the case

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
. We have already seen that this is equivalent to
all
t_{i}
being equal, and it implies that the

around the

\mathbf{v}
. Applying

3.1
, we get

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}\rangle+\beta-\alpha^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}-r^{2},

so

is the sphere inside
\langle\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}
with radius

and center
\mathbf{v}
. Moreover,

\mathcal{S}=\bigl\{(-\alpha,\mathbf{s})\mid\mathbf{s}\in\langle\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\rangle_{\mathrm{aff}}\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{v}\rVert=r\bigl\}.

Recall that
-\alpha
is the common value of all
t_{i}
. See

Figure

3
for a picture.

\triangleleft

.

.

Take the points

,

We get

,
\mathbf{w}_{1}=(0,0,1)^{T}
,

, and, since

. Moreover,

4.
What do the quadrics look like?

In this section we take a closer look at the quadrics
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
and

Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
defined in
Equations

2.6
and
3.3
. A quick glance at

Figure

1
should give readers a good idea of the upshot of this
section. We keep the notation of the previous sections and make the
following three assumptions:

(1)

the points
\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are in
general linear position, with
m\leq n+1
,

(2)

the system
Equation

1.2
has at least two
solutions, and

(3)

the
t_{i}
are not all equal.

By
Theorem

2.1
,
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
consists of all

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}+y_{0}\mathbf{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j}
such that

. (It is convenient to write

here
instead of

Theorem

2.1
.) By

2.2
, our

(3)
implies
\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}
. We first assume

\|\mathbf{u}\rVert\neq 1
and treat the case

later. Set

(4.1)

e:=\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert,\quad\mu:=\frac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle-\alpha}{e^{2}-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho:=\frac{\bigl(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle-\alpha\bigr)^{2}}{e^{2}-1}-\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}+\beta.

Then a short calculation shows

(4.2)

g(y_{0},\ldots,y_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}^{2}+\operatorname{sgn}(e^{2}-1)\Bigl(\sqrt{|e^{2}-1|}\bigl(y_{0}+\mu\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}-\rho.

Since
|\mathfrak{X}|>1
this implies

if

\operatorname{sgn}(e^{2}-1)=1
.

Let us turn our attention to

, which is given by

3.1
. The theorem says that

,
which is equal to
\mathbf{v}+U
with

. We have
\mathbf{u}\in U
, so we can choose

\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m-2}^{\prime}
such that the
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\prime}
together with

form an orthonormal basis of

. Then every

\mathbf{s}\in Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
can be written as

(4.3)

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{v}+z_{0}\mathbf{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-2}z_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\prime}

with
z_{0},\ldots,z_{m-2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Theorem

3.1
also has the condition

\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{s})=0
. Substituting the above
\mathbf{s}

7.
More generality and duality

Until now we have assumed that the matrix

A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times(n+2)}
, as defined in

\mathbf{s}_{i}
and

real-world use of global positioning one often has more than five

approximative ones), in fact a without-loss assumption. We start by
giving a procedure that gives the solution set of the squared
system
Equation

1.2
without any rank hypothesis.

Procedure 7.1

(Compute the set of solutions of the squared global
positioning problem)
.

Input:

Points
\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and numbers
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Output:

The solution set
\mathfrak{X}
of
Equation

1.2
,
given as a quadric (which may be empty or otherwise degenerated).

(1)

Form the matrices
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times(n+2)}

and

given by
Equations

2.4
and
2.1
. Let

and reorder the
\mathbf{s}_{i}
and

(and with them
the rows of
A
and
B

m
rows of

are linearly independent. Then delete the last

rows
from
A
and
B

and

B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times(n+1)}
.

(2)

IF
\operatorname{rank}(B)=m
THEN compute the vectors and
scalars
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
as at the beginning of
Section

2
. Let

\mathfrak{X}
be defined as in
Theorem

2.1
, and let the function

\widetilde{h}
be given by

\widetilde{h}(t,\mathbf{s}):=\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}\rangle^{2}+2\langle\alpha\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}\rangle+\beta-\alpha^{2}\quad\text{for}\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \mathbf{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

(which in this case does not depend on

(3)

IF

\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

(4)

FOR

(5)

For a (possibly infinite) nonempty set

There are pairs

\mathbf{s}_{i}

Proposition 7.2
.

In the above situation, for

Remark 7.4
.

To push the idea of duality further, it is convenient to change the
notation by writing
M^{*}:=\mathfrak{X}(M)
for the set defined
by
Equation

7.1
, so
M^{*}
is the set of solutions
of
Equation

1.2
, but with potentially infinitely many
equations. Since there is complete symmetry between
(b,\mathbf{x})
and

(t,\mathbf{s})
in
Equation

7.1
, we have

M\subseteq M^{**}.

For a subset
N\subseteq M
we clearly have
M^{*}\subseteq N^{*}
. These two rules imply
M^{***}=M^{*}
. Passing from a set

M\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to
M^{**}
can be seen as a closure
operation, and we conclude that the closed sets are precisely those
of the form
M^{*}
. By
Remark

7.3
,
M
can be chosen finite of
size
\leq n+2
. To reconnect this notation with the one used
during the bulk of this paper, notice that in the special case

M=\bigl\{(t_{1},\mathbf{s}_{1}),\ldots,(t_{m},\mathbf{s}_{m})\bigr\}
, the sets

\mathfrak{X}
and
\mathcal{S}
defined in
Equations

1.4
and
3.1
are

\mathfrak{X}=M^{*}
and
\mathcal{S}=\mathfrak{X}^{*}=M^{**}
, so

\mathfrak{X}=\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

For “closed” subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
we thus have perfect
duality. If we project them to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we obtain the sets
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}

and
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
, which are geometrically more meaningful and which also
display some duality that is perhaps best illustrated by

Figures

1
and
3
. But this duality breaks down “on the
fringes” due to a loss of information from the projection. For
instance, if
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
is a line,
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
may be another line in the
situation of
2.4
, or a circle in the situation of

Figure

3
. The results of this paper can now be expressed by
saying that the projections to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of closed subsets
M
are
spheres, prolate spheroids, hyperboloids of revolution of two
sheets, pairs of points, affine subspaces, single points, or
empty. They also say that duality exchanges foci and vertices,
inverts the eccentricity, but preserves the axis of symmetry.

8.
Two application examples

8.1.
Indoor vacuum robot

Consider a robot vacuum rolling on the floor of an apartment.
The robot is using emitters positioned on the ceiling of the rooms of the apartment to determine its location.
The emitters are equipped with precisely synchronized clocks and their locations are known precisely.
They are continuously emitting their location and time of emission.

Assume that the robot is situated in a room with
m=3
non-collinear emitters. Assume also that the robot can receive the signals of all three emitters from any position on the ground of the room. Without loss of generality, we can assume that the ground floor is in the linear space spanned by the standard basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}
. Then
U=\langle\mathbf{s}_{3}-\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}-\mathbf{s}_{1}\rangle
is the linear space perpendicular to
\mathbf{e}_{3}
and so
U^{T}={\mathbb{R}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}
.

Since the three emitters are not collinear, there exists a unique circle on the ceiling plane that passes through the three emitters’ positions.
If the robot is positioned directly below the center of that circle, we are in the situation of
Example

2.2
. So the three pseudoranges received by the robots are equal,
\mathbf{v}
is the center of the circle and
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
. Then the set of possible solutions for the location of the robot is

Q_{\mathrm{sol}}=\{\mathbf{v}+y_{1}\mathbf{e}_{3}|y_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

In other words, it
consists of a line perpendicular to the ceiling and passing through both the true position of the robot and the center of the circle. From the pseudoranges alone, it would thus be impossible to determine the height of the robot in the room. But by intersecting the line
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
with the floor, a unique location can be determined.
Now the set
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
corresponds to the circle through the three emitters. Therefore, positioning an additional emitter on that circle would not provide any further information regarding the robot position. In particular, if the original three emitters were in the corners of a rectangular ceiling, an additional emitter positioned on the remaining corner would only provide redundant information. However, more information could be obtained by moving the additional emitter away from the circle. In particular,
if the robot’s position was not constrained to the floor plane,
then positioning an additional emitter on the ceiling while avoiding the circle through the original three emitters would
reduce the solution set to two mirror points above and below the ceiling, respectively.

Now, let us consider the situation where the robot on the ground is not situated directly below the center of the circle defined by the emitters. This is illustrated in
Figure

2
. Then we are in the situation discussed in
Section

4
. In that case, the solution space
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
for the robot position is contained in the 2D affine plane
\{v+y_{0}u+y_{1}e_{3}|y_{0},y_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
and is either an ellipse (2D spheroid), a hyperbola (2D hyperboloid), or a parabola (2D paraboloid). Using the fact that the robot is on the ground, one can intersect the corresponding quadric with the ground plane to better pin point the robot’s position. Intersecting the floor plane with a parabola (
e=1
) will yield a single solution; intersecting it with either an ellipse or a hyperbola (
e\neq 1
) will yield two possible solutions. One can use the condition
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\geq t
to try to rule out the extraneous solution. Recall that, when
Q_{\mathrm{sol}}
is an ellipse, then the set
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
is a hyperbola and vice versa. If
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
is an ellipse, then applying the condition
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\geq t
is guaranteed to yield a single possible solution for the robot position. However, when
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
is a hyperbola, then as we have proven (see
Figure

2
) that the emitters must be on one sheet of that hyperbola. This implies that there will be two solutions for the robot position.
Thus, the localization problem will have a unique solution on the ground in all cases except when
Q_{\mathrm{sat}}
is a hyperbola.

8.2.
Locating a raft on the ocean

Imagine a life raft drifting on the ocean. In order to be found, the people on the raft are emitting signals. These could be short bursts of sound, light flashes or electromagnetic signals from some device. If the signal is received by three non-collinear receivers, and if the clocks on the receivers are precisely synchronized, then the method discussed in this paper can be used to try to locate the raft.

Let us explore a first scenario in which there are three boats nearby and each of the boat receives the distress signal. Let us assume that the ocean surface is flat. In that case, the search space for the raft is a plane (the ocean) and the receivers can be assumed to be situated on the same plane.
While this examples bears a lot of resemblance to the previous example of the cleaning robot, the fact that the solution space and the receiver plane coincide makes a significant difference: now the quadric of solution passes through the ocean (receiver plane)
perpendicularly
.
The solution(s) being at the intersection point between the solution curve and the receiver plane, this should make the numerical solution robust to noise, as the intersection point should remain more or less the same when small errors are introduced in the time-of-arrival measurements and the boats move up and down with the waves.
