Vector peakon equations and isospectral flows in Clifford algebras Thanks: e-mail: szmigiel@math.usask.ca
A.N.W. Hone

Abstract
Starting from a spectral problem posed in a Clifford algebra with d d generators and Euclidean signature, we study an integrable, coupled system of PDEs that can be viewed as a vector perturbation of the Camassa–Holm equation with residual orthogonal symmetry. In the two-component case d = 2 d=2 , we show that the travelling wave solutions correspond to a Liouville integrable Hamiltonian system with two degrees of freedom, making use of a reciprocal transformation linking the coupled PDEs to a symmetry of the Hirota–Satsuma system. We also present a symmetry classification of all integrable two-component perturbations of Camassa–Holm, and find that besides the d = 2 d=2 system analyzed here, the coupled 2CH system studied by Olver and Rosenau (as well as by Chen, Liu and Zhang, and Falqui), and equations related to either of those systems by Miura transformations, we also obtain a new system that (to the best of our knowledge) has not been reported previously. For the case of an arbitrary number of components d d , we additionally investigate the short-pulse (high-frequency) regime, in which the limiting dynamics are governed by a vector-valued Hunter–Saxton-type system. Furthermore, we provide a detailed analysis of the corresponding measure-valued (weak) solutions associated with this system.

Introduction

In this paper we study the system of partial differential equations

\mathbf{m}_{t}=2u_{x}\,\mathbf{m}+u\,\mathbf{m}_{x}+\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{m},

(1.1)

where
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}(x,t)
is a
d

\mathbf{V}=(v_{\mu\nu})
is a sparse skew-symmetric
d\times d
matrix of the form

\mathbf{V}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mathbf{v}^{T}\\
-\mathbf{v}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right),

(1.2)

with
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1\nu})_{\nu=2,\ldots,d}
. The scalar field
u
and the components of
\mathbf{v}
are related to the components of
\mathbf{m}=(m_{\mu})_{\mu=1,\ldots,d}
through the constraints

u_{xx}-4{\alpha}u=2m_{1},\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad v_{1\mu,x}=-2m_{\mu}\quad\mathrm{for}\,\,\mu=2,\ldots,d,

(1.3)

where
{\alpha}
is an arbitrary constant. Writing (

\begin{array}[]{rcl}m_{1,t}&=&2u_{x}m_{1}+um_{1,x}+\sum_{\nu=2}^{n}v_{1\nu}m_{\nu},\\
m_{\mu,t}&=&2u_{x}m_{\mu}+um_{\mu,x}-v_{1\mu}m_{1},\qquad\qquad\mu=2,\ldots,d.\end{array}

(1.4)

The system (
1.1
) is a
d
-dimensional Euclidean analogue of the two-component system introduced in
[
4
]
, which arose from the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation and was subsequently extended to a Clifford algebra framework in
[
5

. A preliminary analysis of two-component Camassa–Holm dynamics with Minkowski signature can also be found in
[
5
]
. The present paper focuses on the corresponding setting with Euclidean signature. In addition, we place (
1.1
) within the broader framework of the symmetry approach to integrability: in particular, for
d=2
we obtain a complete classification of a well-defined class of integrable perturbations of the scalar Camassa–Holm equation within this vector/Clifford setting.

In the case when
\mathbf{v}=0

2\leq\mu\leq d

) reduces to the standard (scalar) Camassa–Holm equation, which was derived as an asymptotic model of shallow water waves in
[

As well as being completely integrable, the Camassa–Holm equation was found to combine many remarkable features, including smooth solitons, wave breaking, and an interpretation as an Euler-Poincaré flow on a diffeomorphism group, as well as a new class of weak soliton solutions known as peakons. The surprising combination of all these properties has led to many attempts to generalize from the scalar case to multi-component analogues of the Camassa–Holm equation.
A complex NLS-type analogue was presented by Fokas in
[
12
]
, based on the methodology of
bi-Hamiltonian structures and recursion operators developed previously with Fuchssteiner,
while a few different integrable two-component examples were
introduced via a tri-Hamiltonian approach by Olver and Rosenau
[
24
]
. One of the latter examples, commonly referred to as 2CH, was considered in more detail by Chen, Liu and Zhang
[
8
,
23
]
, and also Falqui
[
11
]
, who used a bi-Hamiltonian structure of hydrodynamic type as the basis for their analysis, generalizing the known structure of the original scalar CH equation. Another approach based on supersymmetry, due to Popowicz
[
27
]
, produced non-integrable equations with only one Hamiltonian structure.

Since that time, multi-component analogues of Camassa–Holm have been constructed from multiple different viewpoints, usually by starting from an appropriate family of Lax operators
[
17
,
22
,
30
,
31
]
, from associated geometric flows
[
28
]
, or as particular cases of a larger set of equations
[
6
]
. For a restricted class of two-component systems, there is also a classification of integrable coupled equations of
Camassa–Holm type
[
19
]
. However, the literature on such equations is now unfeasibly large, so the small selection of papers cited here is necessarily incomplete.

Given the plethora of different multi-component equations of Camassa–Holm type, it is worth pointing out some of the distinctive features of the system (
1.1
). This system
equips the scalar Camassa–Holm with internal degrees of freedom, represented by the skew-symmetric tensor
{\bf V}
, similar to an orbital momentum or spin.
The global symmetry of the system is
O(d-1)
, under which the tensor
{\bf V}
transforms into another tensor of the same type.
The Clifford algebra structure creates complex interactions between internal degrees of freedom. In the simplest case of peakon solutions, this Clifford structure influences the long-time behaviour, leading to coordinated energy exchange even when the peakons are spatially separated.

2
Lax pair and higher symmetries

We will now briefly review the setup of
[
5
]
.
The system (
1.1
) arises from a Lax pair defined in a Clifford algebra
\mathcal{C}\ell(W)
, where
W
is a
d
-dimensional vector space over a field
{\mathbb{F}}

with a symmetric bilinear form
(\,,\,)
.
We usually assume
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
, but sometimes
we work with
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
, and consider different real forms of
the underlying complex Clifford algebra. Upon choosing an orthonormal basis of generators
e_{\mu}
,
\mu=1,\ldots,d

such that

(e_{\mu},e_{\nu})=\varepsilon_{\mu}\delta_{\mu\nu}
,
for signs
\varepsilon_{\mu}=\pm 1
, the multiplication in

\mathcal{C}\ell(W)
is then given by

e_{\mu}e_{\nu}+e_{\nu}e_{\mu}=2\varepsilon_{\mu}\delta_{\mu\nu}\,1,

where
1
denotes the unit
1\in{\mathbb{F}}\subset\mathcal{C}\ell(W)
,
which will usually be omitted from formulae.
The signs
\varepsilon_{\mu}
determine the signature of the
bilinear form
(\,,\,)
: over
{\mathbb{C}}
one can always fix

\varepsilon_{\mu}=1
for all
\mu
, which is
our canonical choice for most of the paper, but on occasions when real solutions are being considered we
can allow
different signatures over
{\mathbb{R}}
.
The whole algebra is a direct sum
of graded components:

\mathcal{C}\ell(W)=\mathcal{C}\ell_{0}\oplus\mathcal{C}\ell_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{C}\ell_{n},

where
\mathcal{C}\ell_{0}=<1>\cong{\mathbb{F}}
,
\mathcal{C}\ell_{1}=<e_{\mu}>_{\mu=1,\ldots,n}\cong W
,

\mathcal{C}\ell_{2}=<e_{\mu}e_{\nu}>_{\mu<\nu}
, etc. We can also
equip
\mathcal{C}\ell(W)
with the trace
\mathrm{tr}
, a linear functional
on the Clifford algebra defined by
\mathrm{tr}(1)=1

and
\mathrm{tr}(c)=0
for all

c\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{j}
with
j\geq 1
, which then has the property
that

\mathrm{tr}(ab)=(a,b)\qquad\forall\,a,b\in W\cong\mathcal{C}\ell_{1}.

To construct a vector perturbation of the Camassa–Holm system,
we start from a linear
problem based on a string equation with a potential

M=\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}m_{\mu}\,e_{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{1},

that is

D_{x}^{2}\Phi=({\alpha}+{\lambda}M)\Phi,

(2.1)

where the wave function
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}\ell(V)
, with an arbitrary scalar constant
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}
and spectral parameter
{\lambda}
,
and couple this to a time evolution written as

D_{t}\Phi=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(a-b_{x})+bD_{x}\right)\Phi.

(2.2)

Following
[
4
,
5
]
, we refer to
the linear equation (
2.1
) as a (generalized) beam problem.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

The compatibility conditions for the Lax pair given by
(
2.1
) and (
2.2
) consist of the following two relations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}a_{x}+{\lambda}[b,M]&=&0,\\
{\lambda}M_{t}&=&2{\alpha}b_{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}b_{xxx}+\tfrac{{\lambda}}{2}\left([a,M]+b\,\overset{\leftarrow}{\cal L}_{M}+\overset{\rightarrow}{\cal L}_{M}\,b\right);\end{array}

(2.3)

in the above,
{\cal L}_{M}

is the operator
MD_{x}+D_{x}M
, and the arrow on top denotes whether it acts to the right or the left.

The equation (
1.1
) is an isospectral evolution,
corresponding to a particular solution of the compatibility
conditions (
2.3
), obtained by choosing

a=a_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{2},\qquad b=b_{0}+\frac{b_{-1}}{{\lambda}}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad b_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{0},\,\,b_{-1}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{1},

and this gives a consistent result provided that
b_{-1}
is constant. Without loss of generality, we can always gauge the linear system by multiplying
\Phi
in the linear system by a constant element (corresponding to a rotation in
SO(d)
), so that up to scale
we can fix
b_{-1}=e_{1}
. Then we find

b_{0}=u\in{\mathbb{F}},\qquad b_{-1}=e_{1}\in W,\qquad a_{0}=\sum_{\nu=2}^{d}v_{1\nu}\,e_{1}e_{\nu}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{2},

(2.4)

where
u
and the coefficients appearing in

a_{0}
are subject to the constraints (
1.3
).

We conclude this section with several remarks regarding the place of (
1.1
) in a larger context of integrable systems.
We note that we can rewrite (
1.1
) as an evolution equation in the Clifford
algebra, namely

M_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{M}D_{x}+D_{x}A_{M})\,u-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{M}D_{x}^{-1}C_{M}\,e_{1},

(2.5)

with
u=b_{0},e_{1}=b_{-1}
as in (
2.4
),
where
A_{M},C_{M}
respectively denote the anticommutator/commutator with respect to
M
.

A pair of elementary symmetries is produced by the choices

a=a_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{2},\qquad b=b_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{0},\qquad a_{0},b_{0}\quad\mathrm{const},

for where the constants
a_{0},b_{0}
are arbitrary elements
of degree 2 and 0, respectively, and the corresponding symmetries
can be written in combination as the
s
-flow

M_{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}[a_{0},M]+b_{0}M_{x},

while the first non-trivial symmetry is 3rd order, being given by the flow

M_{\tau}=\tfrac{1}{2}(4{\alpha}D_{x}-D_{x}^{3})\,\left(|\mathbf{m}|^{-\frac{3}{2}}\,M\right).

(2.6)

The latter is Hamiltonian, as it can be expressed as

M_{\tau}={\cal B}_{1}\frac{\delta H}{\delta M},

with the Hamiltonian operator

{\cal B}_{1}=(4{\alpha}D_{x}-D_{x}^{3})

(2.7)

and conserved functional

H=\int(\mathrm{tr}\,M^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.8)

The conserved density associated with
H
is

\rho=(\mathrm{tr}\,M^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}=\Big(\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}m_{\mu}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{4}}=|\mathbf{m}|^{\frac{1}{2}},

which for
d=1
recovers the well-known density
\sqrt{m}
for the Camassa–Holm equation.
The gradient of the Hamiltonian
H
is in the kernel of the
second Hamiltonian structure
{\cal B}_{2}
, which is compatible with

{\cal B}_{1}
, being given by the operator

{\cal B}_{2}=(A_{M}D_{x}+D_{x}A_{M})+C_{M}D_{x}^{-1}C_{M}

(2.9)

(cf. (
2.5
) above).
These operators were found in the work of Olver and Sokolov, who
considered integrable Hamiltonian systems defined in associative algebras

[
25
]
.

Two-component case: reciprocal transformation,

Next, we proceed to consider the most elementary solutions of (

Proof.

3.2
Travelling wave reduction

In this subsection, our main goal will be to describe the properties of the travelling wave solutions in the case
d=2
, namely for the two-component system (
3.1
). However, we begin by making some remarks about the case general of general
d
.
We choose the travelling wave
coordinate to be
z=x-ct
, let prime denote
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}

and assume zero background for the fields, which depend on
x
and
t
through the combination
z
only, so that

u,\,\mathbf{m}\to 0\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad|z|\to\infty.

Then from (
1.1
)
we obtain

-(c+u)\,\mathbf{m}^{\prime}=2u^{\prime}\,\mathbf{m}+V\mathbf{m}.

(3.17)

Taking the inner product with
\mathbf{m}
, and using the fact that the matrix
V
is skew-symmetric, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\log\big(|\mathbf{m}|^{2})\big)=-\frac{4u^{\prime}}{c+u},

(3.18)

and hence find the invariant

K=|\mathbf{m}|^{2}\,(c+u)^{4}=\mathrm{const}.

(3.19)

Zero background gives
K=0
, which by (
1.3
) forces
(u^{\prime\prime}-4\alpha u,\mathbf{v}^{\prime})\equiv(0,0)
for smooth profiles; therefore
nontrivial travelling waves with zero background cannot be smooth, so must be peaked/cusped, being realized when
c+u
vanishes at a crest. So we have

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
d
, the system (
1.1
) has no nontrivial smooth travelling waves with zero background.

In the light of the above result, the latter sections of the paper will focus on peaked soliton (peakon) solutions of (
1.1
), which are weak solutions. The rest of this subsection is
concerned with the smooth travelling waves for
d=2
.

In order to analyze the ODEs for travelling waves of the two-component system (
3.1
), we will make use of the reciprocal transformation (
3.6
), which can be reduced to the level of these ODEs. Our treatment relies on a general feature of similarity reductions of reciprocal transformations (see
[
2
]
, for instance), which is that under a change of independent variables, the roles of parameters and constants of motion can be switched. In particular, in the case of hodograph transformations (changing the time variable) in Hamiltonian systems, this phenomenon is referred to as “coupling constant metamorphosis”
[
14
,
26
]
. For the context at hand, we can state the following

Lemma 3.4
.

Under the reciprocal transformation given by

\mathrm{d}X=\rho\,\mathrm{d}x+{\cal F}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\mathrm{d}T=\mathrm{d}t,

(3.20)

the travelling wave solutions of the conservation law

\rho_{t}={\cal F}_{x},

moving with speed
c
,
admit the conserved quantity

k={\cal F}+c\rho=\mathrm{const}.

(3.21)

Moreover, for a fixed value of this constant, these solutions correspond to travelling waves of the reciprocally transformed PDE

(\rho^{-1})_{T}+({\cal F}\rho^{-1})_{X}=0

(3.22)

moving with speed
k
.

Proof.

Upon reducing to travelling waves with speed
c
,
the conservation law with density
\rho
and flux

\cal F
produces the ODE
-c\rho^{\prime}=\cal F^{\prime}
, where the prime denotes
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}
as before. This can be
rearranged as

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left({\cal F}+c\rho\right)=0,

which integrates to give the conserved quantity
k
as in
(
3.21
).
Now consider the reduction of the reciprocal transformation (
3.20
) to the travelling wave solutions: by a slight abuse of notation, we have the density
\rho=\rho(z)
, being a function of the similarity variable
z
, and

\rho\,\mathrm{d}z=\rho(\mathrm{d}x-c\,\mathrm{d}t)=\mathrm{d}X-{\cal F}\,\mathrm{d}t-c\rho\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}X-k\,\mathrm{d}T.

This can be rewritten as

\mathrm{d}Z=\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

(3.23)

where
Z=X-kT
is a travelling wave variable in the new
X,T
coordinates, with wave speed
k
. Then, with this value of the wave speed, we see from (
3.22
) that the travelling waves of the reciprocally transformed PDE satisfy

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}Z}\left(k\rho^{-1}-{\cal F}\rho^{-1}\right)=0,

which integrates and rearranges to yield (
3.21
), where now
c
is an integration constant. Thus we see that, subject to the relation (
3.23
) between the independent variables, there is a one-to-one correspondence between travelling wave solutions of the two reciprocally related PDEs, modulo the metamorphosis of coupling constants
c\leftrightarrow k
, exchanging the roles of these parameters.
∎

The above result is directly applicable to the case at hand, where we have the reciprocal transformation (
3.6
), with the flux of the conservation law being
{\cal F}=\rho u
. In the light of the preceding
lemma, to determine the travelling waves of the system
(
3.1
) it will be sufficient to consider travelling waves of (
3.10
) moving with speed
k\neq 0
, where the original wave speed
c
will appear as a parameter. Thus we take

\uppsi=\psi(Z),\qquad\vartheta=\theta(Z),\qquad Z=X-kT.

(3.24)

In the course of doing so, for the Hamiltonian theory it will be
convenient to apply imaginary rescalings to some of the variables/parameters, and consider solutions of
the form

\uppsi=\psi(Z),\qquad\vartheta=\mathrm{i}\varphi(Z),\qquad Z=X-\mathrm{i}CT,\qquad\mathrm{where}\quad k=\mathrm{i}C,

(3.25)

with the arbitrary constant
C\neq 0
(excluding the stationary case
C=0
).
In that case, the PDEs (
3.10
) reduce to a pair of ODEs for
\psi,\varphi
, given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}Z}\big(Ce^{\psi}\psi^{\prime\prime}+2e^{2\psi}\sinh\varphi+Ce^{-\psi}\big)&=&0,\\
C\varphi^{\prime\prime}+2e^{\psi}\cosh\varphi&=&0,\end{array}

(3.26)

where now the prime denotes
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}Z}
.
Since the first equation above is a total derivative, we can integrate it once, to obtain the constant of motion

K=-e^{\psi}\psi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{2}{C}e^{2\psi}\sinh\varphi-e^{-\psi},\qquad\mathrm{where}\quad K=\frac{\mathrm{i}c}{C}

(3.27)

by the result of Lemma

. Note that we will ultimately be interested in the case where

, the speed of the travelling waves of the two-component CH system, is real, and the parameter
C=-\mathrm{i}k
is pure imaginary, so the constant
K
will also be real.

After performing an integration as above, to obtain (

), we find that overall the system (

\begin{array}[]{rclrclrclrcl}\psi^{\prime}&=&\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{\psi}},&\varphi^{\prime}&=&\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{\varphi}},&p_{\psi}^{\prime}&=&-\frac{\partial h}{\partial\psi},&p_{\varphi}^{\prime}&=&-\frac{\partial h}{\partial\varphi},\end{array}

where

and

are canonically conjugate momenta, and
the Hamiltonian function is

(3.29)

In order to prove that the latter Hamiltonian system is completely integrable in the Liouville sense, we
need to find a second independent constant of motion that is in involution with

, which
is achieved by considering the reduction of the Lax pair to the travelling waves.

Theorem 3.5
.

Via the hodograph transformation (

(3.30)

where

\mathbf{L}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{1}{2}C(p_{\psi}+p_{\varphi})&0&Ce^{-\psi}&-\mathrm{i}\,{\lambda}^{-1}\\

(3.31)

Proof.

The travelling wave reduction of the Lax pair (

Remark 3.6

If we let

As mentioned above, the vector Camassa–Holm system

(We have restored the arbitrary parameter

The reciprocal transformation (
3.6

In order to do this, one should

is the new time variable corresponding to
the transformation of

Proposition 3.7

The extension of the reciprocal transformation

(3.35)

(3.36)

(3.38)

Proposition 5.11
.

The inner product of the first moment
\mathcal{M}_{1}
with
e_{1}
satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}(\mathcal{M}_{1},e_{1})=-\tr(\Phi_{x}^{(2)}).

(5.43)

Proof.

We want to compute
\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{j}x_{j}(M_{j},e_{1})
.
Since
\dot{x}_{j}=-u(x_{j})
and
\dot{M}_{j}=\bigl\langle u_{x}\bigr\rangle(x_{j})M_{j}+\frac{1}{2}[e_{1}\bigl\langle v\bigr\rangle(x_{j}),M_{j}]
we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{j}x_{j}(M_{j},e_{1})=\overbrace{\sum_{j}(-u(x_{j})+x_{j}\bigl\langle u_{x}\bigr\rangle(x_{j}))(M_{j},e_{1})}^{=I_{1}}+\overbrace{\sum_{j}x_{j}(\bigl\langle v\bigr\rangle(x_{j}),M_{j})(e_{1},e_{1})}^{=I_{2}}

(5.44)

We recall that
u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert(M(y),e_{1})\mathrm{d}y
which in the discrete case becomes

u(x_{j})=\sum_{k}\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert(M_{k},e_{1}).

Likewise,
\bigl\langle u_{x}\bigr\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sgn(x-y)(M(y),e_{1})\mathrm{d}y
. Hence,

\bigl\langle u_{x}\bigr\rangle(x_{j})=\sum_{k}\sgn(j-k)(M_{k},e_{1}).

After symmetrizing, this gives

\sum_{j}x_{j}\bigl\langle u_{x}\bigr\rangle(x_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert(M_{k},e_{1})(M_{j},e_{1}).

Thus we have

I_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert(M_{k},e_{1})(M_{j},e_{1}).

(5.45)

To compute
I_{2}
we need to evaluate
(\bigl\langle v\bigr\rangle(x_{j}),M_{j})
. Recall that

v(x)=-P_{V}\int_{R}\sgn(x-y)M(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Since
P_{V}M=M-P_{V^{\perp}}M=M-(M,e_{1})e_{1}
we get

\bigl\langle v\bigr\rangle(x_{j})=-\sum_{k}\sgn(j-k)M_{k}+\sum_{k}\sgn(j-k)(M,e_{1})e_{1},

and,

I_{2}=-\sum_{j,k}x_{j}\sgn(j-k)(M_{k},M_{j})(e_{1},e_{1})+\sum_{j,k}x_{j}\sgn(j-k)(M_{k},e_{1})(M_{j},e_{1})

After symmetrizing, we get

I_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert(M_{k},M_{j})(e_{1},e_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert(M_{k},e_{1})(M_{j},e_{1})

(5.46)

Combining (
5.45
) and (
5.46
)
we get

I_{1}+I_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert(M_{k},M_{j}).

(5.47)

For comparison, let us compute
\tr{\Phi_{x}^{(2)}}
in the right asymptotic region assuming the mass is discrete.
From (
5.30
) we get

\Phi_{x}^{(2)}=\sum_{j<k}(x_{k}-x_{j})M_{k}M_{j},

and

\displaystyle\tr{\Phi_{x}^{(2)}}=\sum_{j<k}(x_{k}-x_{j})\tr{(M_{k}M_{j})}=\sum_{j<k}(x_{k}-x_{j})(M_{k},M_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{j<k}(x_{k}-x_{j})(M_{k},M_{j})+\sum_{j>k}(x_{j}-x_{k})(M_{j},M_{k})).

Hence,

\tr{\Phi_{x}^{(2)}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\left\lvert x_{k}-x_{j}\right\rvert(M_{k},M_{j})

which implies the final claim.

6
Conclusions

We have studied a Clifford-algebraic generalization of the Camassa–Holm (CH) hierarchy in Euclidean
signature, in which the scalar CH variable is coupled to internal degrees of freedom taking values in a
Clifford algebra with
d
generators. This construction breaks the full
O(d)
symmetry to the residual

O(d\!-\!1)
invariance associated with rotations in the subspace orthogonal to the distinguished direction

e_{1}
, and can be interpreted as endowing the CH dynamics with “spin”-type internal modes. For
d=2
, we
used symmetry methods to classify the resulting perturbations of CH within this framework, obtaining the
system analyzed here as well as an additional integrable candidate.

A key point of the paper is that the integrable structure persists in several complementary guises. On the
one hand, the Hunter–Saxton (short-pulse) reduction admits a measure-valued formulation and a Neumann-type
spectral problem whose Weyl function evolves by an explicit isospectral flow. This leads to a concrete
forward/inverse scheme for discrete measures and provides a Stieltjes-type continued fraction expansion for
the Weyl function in the Clifford setting. On the other hand, via a reciprocal transformation, we relate
our model to the Hirota–Satsuma system, and we include a travelling-wave analysis that clarifies how the
internal variables modify the classical CH wave families.

To illustrate the dynamical impact of the internal degrees of freedom, we carried out
computations for
N
-peakon solutions in the Hunter–Saxton regime, with particular emphasis on the two-atom
case. In the example presented, the solution exhibits a striking asymptotic decomposition: as
t\to\infty

the field
u(x,t)
becomes effectively dominated, on any fixed observable spatial window, by a single
spatially localized oscillatory structure centered near
x_{1}(t)
, while the second component is transported
to
+\infty
and simultaneously flattens so that its contribution becomes negligible near
x_{1}(t)
. The
presence of persistent harmonic modes in the amplitudes is a genuinely new feature compared to the scalar
Hunter–Saxton dynamics and appears to be a robust signature of the Clifford coupling.

Several directions emerge naturally from this work:

1.

Physical interpretation.
Identify a compelling physical setting—for instance, in
fluid models with internal/charged degrees of freedom—in which the Clifford-valued generalization and
the residual
O(d\!-\!1)
symmetry arise from first principles.

2.

Signature dependence.
Determine how changing the signature of the underlying bilinear
form affects qualitative behaviour (e.g., boundedness versus growth, oscillatory versus hyperbolic modes),
and how this is reflected in the spectral data and time evolution.

3.

Higher-rank classification.
Extend the
d=2
symmetry classification to general
d
and
clarify which members of the resulting families are integrable, including the role of reciprocal
transformations and Hamiltonian structures.

4.

Peakon and spectral asymptotics.
Develop a systematic description of long-time behaviour
for multi-peakon solutions (scattering, clustering, and synchronization mechanisms), and relate these to
the invariants encoded by the Clifford Weyl function and its continued fraction data.

We expect that progress on these problems will sharpen the connection between the spectral theory of
generalized beam/string problems and the nonlinear dynamics of CH-type equations with internal
degrees of freedom. There is a wealth of other coupled CH-type models whose solutions exhibit interesting behaviours, such as “waltzing” peakons
[
9
,
32
]
, and we expect that pursuing an approach via spectral theory will shed further light on these as well.
