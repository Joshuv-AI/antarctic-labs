Clinical features and prognostic factors for spinal metastasis from sarcomatoid carcinoma: identifying candidates suitable for surgical intervention
Authors: Enyuan Fang, Ting Wang, Haiyi Gong, Jiawei Wu, Jian Sun, Na Cui, Jianru Xiao, Jian Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcomatoid carcinoma (SC) frequently metastasizes, with bone being the most common site, and the spine is often affected. However, dedicated studies on spinal metastasis from SC remain scarce, complicating the determination of appropriate surgical intervention strategies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 25 patients with spinal metastasis from SC. Clinical, radiological, pathological, treatment, and survival data were comprehensively evaluated. Prognostic factors were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox regression models, leading to the development of a simplified prognostic stratification system.ResultsAmong the 25 patients, 7 had lung-derived SC and 12 had SC of unknown primary origin. No significant prognostic differences were observed across different primary sites. Notably, 19 patients initially presented with symptoms attributable to spinal metastasis. SC spinal metastases lacked distinct characteristic imaging features. Among treatment modalities, only targeted therapy and immunotherapy demonstrated a trend toward improved survival outcomes. Multivariate analysis identified the number of metastatic lesions, D-dimer level, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS). A nomogram constructed from these three factors reliably predicted the 1-year survival rate. We further established a simplified prognostic stratification system that may assist in guiding surgical decision-making.ConclusionTargeted therapy and immunotherapy may confer survival benefits to patients with spinal metastasis from SC. Our prognostic stratification system, based on the number of metastatic lesions, D-dimer level, and NLR, enables estimation of OS and may facilitate risk-adapted surgical stratification, which may serve as a supplement to the present clinical frameworks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10030-9.Keywords: Sarcomatoid carcinoma, Spinal metastasis, Prognostic factors, Surgery, Prognostic evaluation

Background

Sarcomatoid carcinoma (SC) is a rare, highly malignant epithelial neoplasm defined by the coexistence of carcinomatous components and sarcomatoid mesenchymal differentiation [1]. Its unique histopathology confers aggressive biological features, including marked invasiveness and rapid progression, resulting in substantially poorer prognosis compared with conventional carcinomas [1, 2]. While the lung represents the predominant primary site, SC can arise in multiple organs, such as the genitourinary tract, gastrointestinal system, and head and neck region [1–5]. Notably, a considerable proportion of patients present with metastatic SC of unknown primary origin [6, 7], and clinical management strategies vary substantially according to the primary tumor site [4, 5, 8, 9].

Distant metastasis constitutes a hallmark of SC progression. Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data indicate that 54.8% of pulmonary SC cases present with metastatic disease at initial diagnosis, with bone being the most frequent site of dissemination [10]. Development of distant metastasis drastically reduces overall survival (OS), leading to an extremely poor clinical outcome [10]. Bone metastasis serves as an independent adverse prognostic factor in pulmonary SC [11], and the spine is among the most commonly involved skeletal sites. However, dedicated clinical investigations focusing specifically on spinal metastasis from SC remain scarce, representing an important unmet clinical and research gap.

Spinal metastasis is associated with severe and disabling complications. Compared with bone metastases at other sites, spinal lesions frequently cause spinal instability and neural compression, resulting in pain, numbness, motor weakness, paralysis, and urinary or bowel dysfunction, thereby substantially impairing quality of life (QoL) [12, 13]. Surgical intervention constitutes a core therapeutic strategy for symptomatic spinal metastasis, with decision-making primarily guided by neurological compromise, spinal mechanical stability, general patient status, and predicted survival duration [12, 14]. Nevertheless, due to the rarity and high malignancy of spinal metastasis from SC, survival estimates and validated prognostic factors for this subgroup remain poorly characterized, which hinders the determination of surgical indications and optimal operative strategies.

Accordingly, this retrospective study enrolled 25 surgically treated patients with pathologically confirmed spinal metastasis from SC. We aimed to summarize their clinical characteristics, delineate diagnostic and therapeutic patterns, identify independent prognostic factors, and establish an evidence-based framework to facilitate surgical decision-making, and individualized clinical management for this challenging disease entity.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study retrospectively enrolled patients diagnosed with SC who received surgical intervention at the Department of Orthopedic Oncology between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2024. All cases were pathologically confirmed as SC via postoperative histopathology. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients undergoing tumor resection for spinal SC metastasis in our center; (2) definitive postoperative pathological diagnosis of SC. Exclusion criteria included: (1) SC lesions localized to non-spinal sites (n = 7); (2) patients with SC confirmed by needle biopsy but without surgical resection (n = 3); (3) patients lost to follow-up (n = 4) (Fig. 1). Ultimately, a total of 25 eligible patients were included in the final analysis. This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital, Naval Medical University, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Shanghai, China). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives for data utilization and publication of relevant clinical and imaging materials.

Patient flow diagram

The surgical recommendations were mainly based on the Neurologic, Oncologic, Mechanical, and Systemic (NOMS) framework. Specifically, surgical advice was provided when patients were assessed to have obvious nerve compression and/or spinal instability (evaluated by the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score [SINS]), along with the corresponding symptoms (mainly including local pain and nerve compression symptoms), and the patients were considered tolerable for surgery based on the extent of systemic comorbidities and tumor burden. The final surgical decision was made jointly by the doctors, patients, and their families.

Patient characteristics

Comprehensive clinical data, including baseline demographics, therapeutic regimens, radiological manifestations, and pathological findings, were retrospectively extracted from institutional databases and inpatient medical records. Collected baseline characteristics encompassed age, gender, clinical symptoms, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), Frankel neurological stage, Epidural Spinal Cord Compression (ESCC) grade, and SINS. Tumor-related variables consisted of primary tumor origin, previous treatment of the primary lesion, Tomita score, and Tokuhashi score. Treatment-related factors involved surgical procedures, intraoperative blood loss, bone-protective agents (denosumab and bisphosphonates), postoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Pathological parameters, including the immunohistochemical (IHC) expression of Ki-67, pan-cytokeratin (pan-CK), and vimentin (VIM), were retrieved from postoperative pathological reports.

Peripheral laboratory indicators comprised hemoglobin (Hb), albumin (ALB), C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, alkaline phosphatase (AKP), and routine blood cell counts. Systemic inflammatory and immune indices, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), and CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR), were calculated accordingly. The preoperative laboratory tests were performed 1–3 days before surgery, and the postoperative laboratory tests were performed one day after surgery. Cutoff values for continuous variables were determined based on clinical reference ranges, optimal threshold calculations, and previously published evidence [13, 15].

Radiological data from computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) were collected from imaging archives. Experienced attending radiologists independently evaluated radiological features, including tumor size, margin characteristics, distant metastatic status, internal and peritumoral structures, density/signal intensity, contrast enhancement patterns, and the extent of bone destruction. Signal intensities on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) were referenced to skeletal muscle signals. The maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) was extracted from PET-CT reports.

Frankel grade and VAS score were adopted to evaluate the improvement of postoperative QoL. For patients with a survival time longer than 3 months, postoperative Frankel grade and VAS score were recorded at 3 months after surgery. For those who survived less than 3 months, the best postoperative Frankel grade and VAS score during the clinical course were documented instead.

For survival assessment, OS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to death or the last follow-up in February 2026 for surviving patients. All patients received standardized follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, followed by semiannual surveillance thereafter. Given the high incidence of synchronous multiple metastases in SC patients, progression-free survival (PFS) was not assessed in this study.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0.2, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software (Version 4.5.1, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed). Continuous variables were analyzed via analysis of variance (ANOVA). OS served as the primary endpoint and was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with group differences compared by the log-rank test. Variables yielding P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were incorporated into multivariate backward Cox regression models. A prognostic nomogram was constructed based on independent prognostic factors, and calibration curves were generated to validate the predictive accuracy of the nomogram. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 25 patients with spinal metastasis secondary to SC were enrolled in this study (Supplementary Table 1). The cohort consisted of 23 males and 2 females, with a mean age of 59.9 years (range, 29–76 years). Preoperatively, 23 patients presented with obvious neurological compression symptoms, predominantly numbness, pain, and motor weakness, among whom 2 developed paralyses. Primary origins included the lung (7 cases), kidney (3 cases), stomach (1 case), liver (1 case), and head and neck region (1 case); the remaining 12 cases had an unknown primary site. Six patients had received prior treatment for their primary malignancy, whereas 19 initially manifested symptomatic spinal metastasis as the first clinical presentation. Fourteen patients harbored a single metastatic lesion, and 11 had multiple (≥ 2) spinal metastases. All patients underwent spinal surgery, including gross total resection in 7 cases and subtotal separation decompression in 18 cases. Adjuvant therapies comprised bone-protective agents (16 patients), chemotherapy (5 patients), targeted therapy (10 patients), immunotherapy (7 patients), and radiotherapy (9 patients).

Radiological manifestations

Of the 25 enrolled patients, 22 underwent preoperative MRI evaluation. Among these, 20 (90.9%) exhibited hypointense signals on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), 17 (77.3%) showed hyperintense signals on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), 16 (72.7%) demonstrated ill-defined tumor margins, and 16 (72.7%) were accompanied by prominent paravertebral soft-tissue masses. All 22 lesions showed marked contrast enhancement. 19 patients received CT scanning, which uniformly revealed extensive osteolytic destruction and significant enhancement. In addition, 9 patients underwent PET-CT, with a mean SUVmax of 13.7 (range, 9.6–19.3). Pathological features

Histopathologically, SC was characterized by the coexistence of carcinomatous epithelial components and sarcomatoid spindle-cell stroma. Representative pathological images from two typical cases are illustrated in Fig. 3A. IHC staining was performed for all specimens. Tumors displayed high proliferative activity, with a mean Ki-67 index of 44.1%, and 80% of cases had a Ki-67 index ≥ 30%. The expression profiles of additional IHC markers, categorized as positive or negative, are summarized in a heatmap (Fig. 3B). The epithelial marker pan-cytokeratin (pan-CK) was positive in 16 of 18 specimens, and the mesenchymal marker vimentin (VIM) was positive in 18 of 19 specimens. No significant differences in IHC expression patterns were observed across SC metastases derived from different primary sites.

QoL assessment

QoL was evaluated by assessing preoperative and postoperative pain intensity and neurological function. In total, 14 (56%) patients achieved an improvement of at least one grade in postoperative Frankel grade (Fig. 4A). Notably, one patient experienced a deterioration in Frankel grade after surgery and survived less than one month postoperatively, suggesting rapid tumor progression. The mean VAS score decreased from 6.6 (range, 3–9) before surgery to 2.5 (range, 0–5) after surgery (Fig. 4B). 23 (92%) patients presented a certain degree of reduction in postoperative VAS scores. These findings indicated that surgery could help improve the QoL of patients with SC spinal metastasis, which also constitutes the primary therapeutic goal of surgical intervention for spinal metastasis [12].

Survival analysis

The median follow-up duration was 10 months, with a maximum follow-up of 54 months. At the final follow-up cutoff, only 3 patients remained alive. The 6-month, 1-year, and 2-year overall survival (OS) rates were 64.0%, 44.0%, and 19.2%, respectively. Univariate (Table 1) and multivariate analyses (Table 2) were performed to screen prognostic clinicopathological factors. Primary tumor origin exerted no significant influence on survival (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Neither surgical extent nor adjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or bone-targeted therapy significantly improved prognosis. In contrast, targeted therapy (P = 0.004) and immunotherapy (P = 0.094) were associated with favorable survival trends (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Additional prognostic variables identified in univariate analysis included KPS score (P = 0.022), Frankel stage (P < 0.001), number of metastatic lesions (P = 0.027), Tokuhashi score (P = 0.012), D-dimer level (P < 0.001), and NLR (P = 0.002).

Using these three independent predictors, we further established a simplified prognostic stratification system. One point was assigned for each high-risk feature: ≥ 2 metastatic lesions, D-dimer > 1 mg/L, and NLR > 4.5 (Fig. 7A). Patients with a score of 0 achieved a median survival of 37 months and a 1-year survival rate of 100%, supporting the application of gross total resection in this low-risk subgroup. Patients with a score of 1 had a median survival of 14 months and a 6-month survival rate of 90%, indicating that separation decompression surgery may be clinically beneficial. Patients with a score of 2 or 3 demonstrated an extremely poor prognosis, with a median survival of merely 4 months and a 6-month survival rate of 20%, suggesting limited survival benefit from surgical intervention in this high-risk population (Fig. 7B, C).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the present study represents the first investigation focusing on the surgical prognosis of patients with spinal metastasis originating from SC. A total of 25 patients were enrolled in this research, with a striking male predominance observed in the cohort; this demographic distribution is highly consistent with prior literature documenting a consistent male bias in SC patient populations [6, 7, 10, 11]. Patients diagnosed with SC inherently carry an extremely dismal long-term prognosis. For instance, Kaseda et al. [16] reported that pulmonary SC is associated with significantly inferior survival outcomes compared with other non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) subtypes, while Huey et al. [7] demonstrated worse survival in patients with unknown primary SC relative to those with metastatic carcinomas of unknown origin. In the present series, the overall prognosis of patients with spinal SC metastasis remained exceedingly poor, with a 6-month postoperative survival rate of merely 64.0%, which further highlights the critical necessity of rigorous patient selection for surgical intervention in this high-risk population.

Most patients presented initially with spinal metastasis symptoms, with definitive SC diagnosis only achieved postoperatively; only 6 patients had a preoperative SC diagnosis. Given the poor prognosis of spinal SC metastasis, radiological suspicion of SC is clinically critical and guides surgical choices. Literature reports imaging differences between SC and other carcinomas at the same site: hepatic SC shows more lymph node involvement, large irregular necrotic masses and delayed persistent CT enhancement [17, 18]; SC also has intense PET-CT FDG uptake (SUVmax > 10 in most cases) [19, 20]. Yet differentiation remains challenging, with only about 40% of pulmonary SC identified via clinical-imaging features [21]. In our series, spinal SC metastases typically showed T1WI hypointensity, T2WI hyperintensity, blurred margins, marked enhancement, paravertebral soft-tissue masses, osteolytic destruction and high SUVmax. Relative to other metastases, SC more often presents with paravertebral masses, osteolysis and elevated SUVmax, but no definitive radiologic criteria exist. AI-assisted radiomics may aid preoperative differentiation and deserves further study. Given the poorer prognosis of SC compared to other carcinomas, percutaneous biopsy may be recommended for patients with radiological suspicion of SC and mild-to-moderate neurological compression to guide subsequent treatment strategies.

Nearly half of the enrolled patients had spinal metastasis from unknown primary SC. The relatively small sample size may introduce inherent sampling bias, as patients with known primary SC and concurrent spinal metastasis might be less frequently referred for spinal surgical intervention. Despite this limitation, unknown primary SC is not an uncommon clinical entity, and affected patients often present with widespread metastatic disease at initial diagnosis [6, 7], posing formidable therapeutic challenges and necessitating heightened clinical attention. Furthermore, no significant prognostic differences were observed across patients with spinal SC metastasis from distinct primary sites (e.g., OS comparison between pulmonary SC and unknown primary SC, P = 0.857), indicating that prognostic stratification for spinal SC metastasis can be performed independently of the primary tumor origin. This finding is likely attributable to the uniformly high-grade malignant biological behavior inherent to SC, regardless of the primary anatomical site.

Although standard therapeutic regimens for SC vary according to the primary tumor site, no significant prognostic discrepancies were detected among patients with spinal SC metastasis from different origins in this study. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that most SC subtypes exhibit higher PD-L1 expression compared with corresponding conventional carcinomas, establishing immunotherapy as a core therapeutic cornerstone for SC across various primary sites, including the lung and liver [3, 9, 22, 23]. Our study enrolled patients over a 12-year period from 2012 to 2024, during which systemic treatment strategies, especially immunotherapy, have advanced rapidly and substantially. In our cohort, only a small proportion of patients (7/25) received immunotherapy, and the survival benefit was only marginal (P = 0.094). This phenomenon is largely attributed to historical treatment bias: earlier cases had limited access to modern immune agents, targeted therapy, and standardized systemic regimens, which may inevitably underestimate the real survival potential of contemporary SC patients with spinal metastasis. Integrating the present findings with existing published evidence, we recommend that immunotherapy be considered for all eligible patients with spinal SC metastasis who can tolerate systemic treatment. Well-designed prospective studies with standardized comprehensive therapy, including immunotherapy and targeted therapy, are warranted to further confirm the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy and other systemic regimens.

Chemotherapy and targeted therapy also constitute pivotal components of multimodal management for SC, with therapeutic regimens tailored to the primary tumor origin [24–26]. Given the high PD-L1 expression and favorable immunotherapeutic response observed across SC subtypes, combined chemo-immunotherapy or targeted-immunotherapy regimens are generally recommended as first-line treatment [8, 24, 27]. In the present cohort, only 5 patients received chemotherapy with no significant prognostic impact, whereas 10 patients underwent targeted therapy with a statistically significant improvement in survival (P = 0.004). These results should be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size and potential selection bias, and further validation in large-scale prospective cohorts is warranted.

While stereotactic body radiotherapy is a mainstay in current clinical guidelines for spinal metastases, radiotherapy showed no significant prognostic benefit in this study. Limited existing evidence suggests that SC is relatively radioresistant [28]. Given the prominent osteolytic nature of SC bone metastases, bone-protective therapy (particularly denosumab, given its favorable safety profile) is recommended for all affected patients, despite the absence of a significant prognostic association in our analysis. Notably, a previous case report demonstrated meaningful clinical benefit from bone-targeted radiotherapy combined with bisphosphonate therapy in patients with SC bone metastases [29], representing a promising therapeutic avenue for future investigation.

Metastatic tumor burden is a well-established prognostic factor in SC, with multiple metastatic lesions linked to drastically shortened survival compared with solitary metastases [7, 10]. Bone is among the most common metastatic sites, reported in 16.3% of pulmonary SC patients [10] and 48.1% of unknown primary SC patients [6] at initial diagnosis. The present study further validated the number of metastatic lesions as an independent prognostic factor for spinal SC metastasis. Prior research has identified site-specific prognostic factors for SC, including lymph node metastasis in esophageal SC [30], local invasion in hepatic SC [4], and vascular invasion, surgical resection, and chemotherapy in gallbladder SC [31]. Huey et al. [7] reported ECOG performance status and NLR as independent prognosticators for unknown primary SC, proposing that elevated systemic inflammation at diagnosis reflects more aggressive tumor biology and may predict treatment resistance. Similarly, NLR has been validated as a robust independent prognostic factor in oral SC and pulmonary SC [32, 33]. Accordingly, we incorporated readily accessible laboratory markers reflecting systemic inflammation, coagulation status, and nutritional status, and confirmed NLR as an independent prognostic factor for spinal SC metastasis. Notably, this study is the first to identify D-dimer as a novel independent prognostic biomarker in this patient population.

NLR mirrors the balance between systemic inflammation and host anti-tumor immunity. Mechanistically, tumor-associated neutrophils secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, along with matrix metalloproteinases, to drive extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and tumor proliferation and invasion [34, 35]. Neutrophils also generate neutrophil extracellular traps, which protect circulating tumor cells from immune surveillance, strengthen endothelial adhesion, and facilitate distant metastasis [34, 35]. Conversely, decreased lymphocytes including CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells weaken anti-tumor immune function, thereby promoting immune escape and tumor progression [36]. Overall, elevated NLR characterizes a pro-inflammatory, immune-suppressed, metastasis-prone microenvironment, accounting for its reliable prognostic significance in spinal SC metastasis.

Extensive metastatic disease drives a systemic hypercoagulable state, and D-dimer serves as a reliable surrogate marker of global tumor burden. Elevated D-dimer levels have been associated with poor prognosis in a multitude of malignancies, including lung cancer, gastric cancer, and osteosarcoma [37–39]. Mechanistically, elevated D-dimer indicates enhanced fibrin turnover and a hypercoagulable microenvironment conducive to tumor progression and distant metastasis [38]. Emerging evidence further demonstrates that high D-dimer levels are closely associated with resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors [40–42]. Increased D-dimer not only mirrors heavier tumor burden and heightened systemic inflammation, but also reflects the activation of procoagulant molecular programs, which actively mediate therapeutic resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [42, 43]. The prognostic significance of D-dimer in SC may therefore be partially mediated by attenuated efficacy of immunotherapy. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to report D-dimer as a prognostic biomarker for patients with SC.

Based on the three identified independent prognostic factors (number of metastatic lesions, D-dimer and NLR), we constructed a nomogram to predict 1-year postoperative survival in patients with spinal SC metastasis. Calibration curves verified its favorable predictive accuracy, yet the model was restricted to 1-year survival estimation owing to the small sample size and poor overall survival of the cohort. Given the comparable relative weights of these three factors, we developed a simplified scoring system assigning 1 point for each high-risk feature: ≥2 metastatic lesions, D-dimer > 1 mg/L and NLR > 4.5 (total score 0–3). Patients with a score of 0 had favorable outcomes (median survival 37 months, 1-year survival rate 100%) and were eligible for gross total resection. Those with a score of 1 had an intermediate prognosis (median survival 14 months, 6-month survival rate 90%), for whom separation and stabilization surgery was recommended in cases of neurological deficit or spinal instability. Patients with scores of 2–3 had extremely dismal outcomes (median survival 4 months, 6-month survival rate 20%), indicating limited survival benefit from aggressive surgery.

Notably, advances in minimally invasive surgical techniques, including pedicle screw fixation, minimally invasive decompression, endoscopic surgery, and robotic surgery, may provide new therapeutic opportunities for high-risk patients. Such procedures are associated with relatively less surgical trauma and faster postoperative recovery, and may improve the overall QoL in high-risk SC spinal metastasis patients with limited survival. Further investigations are required to validate these benefits. Meanwhile, we are currently conducting the clinical trial on percutaneous screw fixation combined with minimally invasive decompression and robotic surgery in patients with advanced spinal metastatic tumors.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgement. First, the relatively small sample size of only 25 patients may lead to insufficient statistical power and carry a substantial risk of model overfitting. Therefore, the current scoring system should only be regarded as an adjunct to established clinical frameworks such as the NOMS system, rather than a standalone definitive guideline for clinical decision-making. Second, although we constructed a prognostic nomogram and a simplified scoring system to guide surgical decision-making, external validation is lacking, and further multi-center studies are needed to verify its clinical utility. Third, the retrospective study design and prolonged enrollment period led to non-standardized multimodal adjuvant treatments, with a relatively low proportion of patients receiving immunotherapy; future prospective controlled studies are required to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study represents the first dedicated investigation of prognostic factors in patients with spinal metastasis from SC. No significant prognostic difference was observed across distinct primary tumor sites, while targeted therapy and immunotherapy may confer favorable survival benefits. Multivariate analysis identified the number of metastatic lesions, D-dimer level, and NLR as independent prognostic factors for OS. The established prognostic scoring system may enable survival estimation and facilitate risk-adapted surgical stratification for patients with SC spinal metastasis, which can serve as a supplement to conventional clinical frameworks including the NOMS system.
