Effects of ACSM protocol-compliant exercise on sarcopenia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chen Wang, Xiao Liu, Yu Liang, Qi Song, Hai Liu, Zhaochuan Xu, Xifang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia is a progressive, age-related skeletal muscle disorder characterized by a gradual loss of muscle mass, strength, and physical function, which is highly prevalent among older adults. This study aimed to investigate the association between compliance with American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) exercise guidelines and intervention outcomes in patients with sarcopenia.MethodsFour electronic databases were systematically searched from inception to May 2025. Exercise interventions were evaluated for compliance with ACSM recommendations and categorized into high or low ACSM protocol compliance groups. A random-effects model was used to calculate standardized mean differences (SMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsTwenty-nine randomized controlled trials, comprising 1,524 patients with sarcopenia, were included. Exercise with high ACSM protocol compliance significantly improved the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (SMD = 0.49; 95% CI 0.23, 0.76). Significant positive effects were observed for maximal voluntary contraction and the five-times sit-to-stand test performance; however, these findings remain inconclusive due to high subgroup heterogeneity and limited evidence. Grip strength demonstrated a larger effect size in the high ACSM protocol compliance group (SMD = 0.64; 95% CI 0.34, 0.94), but this was statistically significant only at the 70% threshold (p = 0.03), losing significance at the 75% (p = 0.19) and 80% (p = 0.24) thresholds, indicating substantial instability in the results. Subgroup differences for gait speed (p = 0.15) and the timed up-and-go test (p = 0.08) were not statistically significant. Exercise had no significant effect on body mass index or body fat percentage, and high ACSM protocol compliance was not associated with increased adverse events or dropout rates.ConclusionsExercise interventions with high ACSM protocol compliance significantly improved muscle mass in patients with sarcopenia. Although improvements were observed in grip strength, the five-times sit-to-stand test performance, and maximal voluntary contraction, inconsistent sensitivity analyses and high heterogeneity preclude definitive conclusions. For other outcomes, interventions with high ACSM compliance demonstrated no superior efficacy compared with other exercise protocols.Trial registrationThis systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251104971).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07872-2.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Exercise, Muscle mass, Physical function, Meta-analysis

Background

Sarcopenia is a progressive, age-related skeletal muscle disorder characterized by diminished muscle mass, reduced muscle strength, and/or impaired physical function [1], a concept first introduced by Rosenberg in 1989 [2]. It now represents a significant public health challenge among older adults, with its worldwide prevalence estimated at 10% to 27% in individuals aged 60 and above [3]. The clinical setting exerts a substantial influence on the prevalence rates, with estimates of 9%–11% for community-dwelling older adults, compared to 23%–24% for hospitalized patients and 31%–51% for nursing home residents, respectively [4]. Sarcopenia is associated with multiple adverse clinical events, including falls, fractures [5], impaired mobility, loss of independence [6], and a heightened risk of mortality [7, 8], thereby imposing a considerable medical and economic burden on both individuals and society. Currently, there is no approved pharmacotherapy specifically indicated for the management of sarcopenia [9], underscoring the growing importance of non-pharmacological interventions. Exercise training is considered the most impactful intervention, with well-documented benefits in slowing muscle atrophy, augmenting muscle strength, and improving physical performance [10, 11].

Extensive research consistently demonstrates that exercise training enhances muscle mass, bolsters muscular strength, and improves physical function in patients with sarcopenia [12]. However, findings regarding the most effective intervention protocol remain inconsistent [13, 14]. A consistent finding in recent meta-analyses is that the efficacy of exercise interventions for sarcopenia differs markedly depending on the specific modality employed [15, 16]. Resistance training is widely considered the primary method for augmenting muscle mass and strength. For sarcopenic individuals, high-intensity resistance exercise, often defined as 80% of a one-repetition maximum (1RM), is specifically recommended as the optimal strategy for maximizing muscular strength [17]. Aerobic training primarily improves cardiorespiratory function and endurance, thereby enhancing performance in daily activities, while balance training is crucial for reducing fall risk and improving gait stability. Both resistance training alone and multimodal approaches (combining resistance with aerobic or balance training) have demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing quality of life and functional capacity [18]. A consensus exists that multimodal therapeutic strategies, encompassing physical training and nutritional support, represent the most effective comprehensive strategy for sarcopenia management [19]. Whole-body vibration training is also considered an efficacious prophylactic and therapeutic modality [20]. Although exercise training as a non-pharmacological therapy offers substantial advantages for sarcopenic individuals, the mechanisms by which different training parameters (e.g., intensity, frequency) influence outcomes remain unclear, highlighting the need for robust clinical studies to identify optimal exercise prescriptions.

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), a leading global authority in exercise science, publishes evidence-based guidelines integrating physiological, clinical, and public health evidence, which have been adopted as international standards for exercise prescription. The ACSM has established broad physical activity recommendations for healthy adults, detailing specific parameters for cardiorespiratory, resistance, and flexibility training [21]. Sarcopenia involves a significant decline in muscle mass, strength, and physical function, often accompanied by multiple comorbidities and reduced physiological reserves. Therefore, directly applying exercise parameters designed for healthy individuals to this frail population theoretically poses a potential risk of excessive intensity or insufficient tolerance. However, in recent years, the applicability of this evidence-based framework to frail populations has been supported by empirical evidence. A meta-analysis by Pan et al. clearly demonstrated that exercise with high compliance with the ACSM guidelines was significantly superior to those with low compliance in improving frailty phenotypes and grip strength, confirming its efficacy in frail older adults [22]. Given the high overlap between frailty and sarcopenia in terms of pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical manifestations, this review adopts the ACSM framework as an evaluation benchmark. It aims to examine the effects of varying degrees of ACSM protocol compliance on body composition, muscle strength, and physical function in patients with sarcopenia, and to further assess the actual tolerability of the ACSM guidelines in this population.

Methods

Search strategy

To ensure comprehensive identification of eligible studies, four electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science—were systematically searched from inception through May 2025. The search strategy was developed based on the PICOS framework, using controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., “Exercise,” “Resistance Training,” “Plyometric Exercise,” “Traditional Chinese Exercise,” and “Sarcopenia”) combined with relevant free-text keywords. To maximize comprehensiveness, we additionally screened the reference lists of eligible studies and relevant systematic reviews. When required, the original study authors were contacted to obtain supplementary data. Supplementary Table S2 details the full search strategy.

Study selection criteria

Included studies met the following criteria: (a) published randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (b) individuals clinically diagnosed with sarcopenia; (c) interventions involving any exercise modality, such as resistance, aerobic, or flexibility training; and (d) control groups that received non-exercise comparators or alternative interventions, such as conventional physiotherapy or health education.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) non-original research, e.g., conference abstracts, reviews, animal studies, guidelines, or editorials; (b) control groups involving any form of exercise intervention; (c) concurrent use of pharmacological agents during exercise interventions; (d) studies with unavailable or non-extractable data; (e) absence of a control group or use of a non-randomized design; or (f) multiple articles stemming from a single trial.

Titles and abstracts underwent independent screening by two authors (CW and XL); full texts were retrieved if a study was deemed potentially eligible by either reviewer. Following the initial screening, the full texts underwent independent eligibility assessment by CW and XL. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (QS) until a consensus was reached. No restrictions were imposed on participants’ age, sex, publication year, or language.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data extraction was independently performed by two authors (CW and YL). A standardized Excel form was used for the systematic collection of various study parameters, including: bibliographic information (title, authors, year), methodological features (design, intervention protocols), subject demographics (age, sex distribution), training variables (frequency, intensity, duration, repetitions, sets), risk of bias assessment, and all relevant outcome data. Additionally, we systematically extracted and summarized adverse events and participant dropout rates reported across all included studies.

Following data extraction, we systematically appraised the exercise dosage and protocol compliance within the included studies. Each study’s exercise intervention was independently assessed by two authors (CW and HL) for compliance with the ACSM recommendations for aerobic capacity, muscle performance, and neuromotor function in healthy adults [21], which are considered applicable to patients with sarcopenia (Table 1). This evaluation focused on key elements of exercise dosage: frequency, intensity, duration, repetitions, and sets. Each parameter was rated on a scale of 0–2: 2 (full), 1 (partial/uncertain), and 0 (non-compliance). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer until agreement was achieved. Notably, this ACSM protocol compliance score reflects the extent to which the prescribed exercise complied with the guidelines in its design, and does not measure participant attendance or individual adherence to the sessions. Based on the total score, interventions were categorized as having high ACSM protocol compliance (≥ 70%) or low ACSM protocol compliance (< 70%).

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4 and STATA 17.0. All studies were stratified into two subgroups: “high ACSM protocol compliance” and “low ACSM protocol compliance”. Within-group heterogeneity was quantified using the Higgins I² statistic, classified as follows: low (I²≤ 25%), moderate (25% < I²≤ 50%), high (50% < I²≤ 75%), and very high (I²>75%). Given the expected heterogeneity in intervention frequency, dosage, duration, session length, and exercise type, a random-effects model was pre-specified to account for between-study variation. Effect sizes were reported as standardized mean differences (SMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Forest plots were generated to visualize pooled effect sizes and their distributions. Publication bias was assessed by visually evaluating the symmetry of the funnel plot, supplemented by quantitative validation using Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s linear regression test. A two-tailed p < 0.05 served as the predefined criterion for statistical significance. Furthermore, to assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses by setting the threshold for defining “high ACSM protocol compliance” at 75% and 80%, respectively.

Four authors (CW, XL, HL, and ZCX) independently evaluated the methodological quality of all included RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [23]. Based on the Cochrane Handbook [24], this tool encompasses seven elements: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. Each domain was assigned a rating of “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk”. The overall risk of bias was judged according to the following criteria: it was classified as “low” if all domains were rated “low risk”; as “some concerns” if any domain indicated “some concerns” without any “high risk” ratings; and as “high” if any domain was deemed “high risk”.

Results

Literature search

A total of 10,807 records were initially identified from four electronic databases (PubMed: n = 3,206; Embase: n = 1,803; Web of Science: n = 2,061; Cochrane Library: n = 3,737), with five further records obtained from other sources. After removing 4,176 duplicates, 6,631 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 194 articles underwent full-text eligibility assessment. Ultimately, 29 studies were included in the review [25–53]. The complete search and selection process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The search for this meta-analysis was updated through May 2025, identifying 29 eligible studies conducted between 2012 and 2024. No new qualifying studies were identified between January and May 2025. These studies involved a total of 1,524 participants (765 in intervention groups and 759 in control groups), with sample sizes ranging from 19 to 169 participants per trial and average ages ranging from 64 to 89 years (overall mean age: 74.2 years). After excluding the study that did not report sex distribution [29], sex was reported for 1,484 participants, of whom 1,023 (69.0%) were female and 461 (31.0%) were male. This sex distribution indicates that the study population was predominantly female, potentially reflecting the higher prevalence of sarcopenia in older women (Table 2).

Risk of bias

Across all studies, a low risk of bias was consistently found for random sequence generation and selective reporting. For allocation concealment, 11 studies demonstrated low risk due to adequate methods, in contrast to 18 studies classified as having unclear risk owing to incomplete reporting. Performance bias was predominantly high (28 studies at high risk, 1 at low risk), a direct consequence of the challenges inherent in blinding participants and personnel within exercise interventions. For blinding of outcome assessment, 20 studies were classified as low risk, whereas 9 were categorized as unclear risk due to insufficient reporting. Regarding incomplete outcome data, most studies (n = 23) were classified as low risk, whereas 3 studies with < 13 dropouts were judged as having unclear risk, and another 3 studies with ≥ 36 dropouts were rated as high risk. Finally, other bias sources included 17 low-risk, 7 unclear-risk, and 3 high-risk studies, attributable to small sample sizes and inadequate control of nutrition (Figs. 2 and 3).

Bias risk summary

Risk of bias graph

To determine whether high ACSM protocol compliance was associated with more rigorous study methodology, we analyzed the relationship between the risk of bias and ACSM protocol compliance scores. The risk of bias was quantified by summing the scores for each domain (0 = low, 1 = uncertain, 2 = high). A Pearson correlation analysis showed no significant correlation between the total risk of bias score and the compliance score (r = − 0.1137, p = 0.557). This indicates that the level of ACSM protocol compliance was independent of the methodological quality of the included studies.

ACSM protocol compliance

The compliance of the prescribed exercise protocols with ACSM recommendations was systematically evaluated across the 29 included studies. Fourteen studies demonstrated high ACSM protocol compliance (rates ≥ 70%), while fifteen studies demonstrated low ACSM protocol compliance (rates < 70%). The main reasons for insufficient compliance were deviations in dosage parameters (e.g., intensity, frequency, and duration) from ACSM recommendations and incomplete reporting of exercise prescription details in some studies, which limited accurate evaluation (Table 4).

Effects of ACSM protocol compliance on body composition

For appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), a meta-analysis of 14 studies (n = 689) revealed that exercise interventions significantly improved outcomes, with a pooled SMD of 0.27 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.45; p = 0.003) and low heterogeneity (I² = 24%). In subgroup analyses, the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (7 studies) showed a significant improvement (SMD = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.23, 0.76; p < 0.001), whereas the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (7 studies) yielded non-significant results (SMD = 0.10, 95% CI: − 0.10, 0.30; p = 0.32). Subgroup analyses (p = 0.02) further confirmed that exercise programs with high ACSM protocol compliance are essential for superior ASMI enhancement.

For body mass index (BMI), a meta-analysis of 10 studies (n = 385) demonstrated no significant effect of exercise interventions, with a pooled SMD of − 0.06 (95% CI: − 0.26, 0.15; p = 0.59) and no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). Subgroup analyses confirmed that neither the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (5 studies; SMD = 0.08, 95% CI: − 0.27, 0.43; p = 0.64) nor the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (5 studies; SMD = − 0.12, 95% CI: − 0.37, 0.12; p = 0.33) demonstrated significant effects. The difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.35).

For body fat percentage (BFP), a meta-analysis of 7 studies (n = 283) demonstrated no significant effect of exercise interventions, with a pooled SMD of − 0.18 (95% CI: − 0.54, 0.19; p = 0.34) and high heterogeneity (I² = 51%). Subgroup analyses confirmed similar findings, with neither the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (4 studies; SMD = − 0.15, 95% CI: − 0.64, 0.34; p = 0.54) nor the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (3 studies; SMD = − 0.23, 95% CI: − 0.91, 0.45; p = 0.51) demonstrating significant effects. The difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.86) (Fig. 4).

Effects of ACSM protocol compliance on muscle strength

For grip strength, a meta-analysis of 16 studies (n = 855) revealed that exercise interventions significantly improved grip strength, with a pooled SMD of 0.42 (95% CI: 0.21, 0.62; p < 0.001) and high heterogeneity (I² = 53%). In subgroup analyses, the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (8 studies) demonstrated significant improvements with an SMD of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.34, 0.94; p < 0.001), whereas the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (8 studies) demonstrated smaller but significant improvements (SMD = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.43; p = 0.03). Subgroup analyses (p = 0.03) further confirmed that exercise programs with high ACSM protocol compliance resulted in greater grip strength improvements.

For maximal voluntary contraction (MVC), a meta-analysis of 7 studies (n = 234) showed a significant improvement, with a pooled SMD of 1.19 (95% CI: 0.58, 1.81; p < 0.001) and very high heterogeneity (I² = 77%). Given the small number of included studies and high heterogeneity, these results should be interpreted with caution. In subgroup analyses, the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (3 studies) yielded an SMD of 2.24 (95% CI: 1.17, 3.30; p < 0.001), whereas the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (4 studies) yielded an SMD of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.28, 0.91; p < 0.001). Subgroup difference (p = 0.004) suggests that exercise programs with high compliance with the ACSM protocol may markedly improve MVC; however, because the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup included only 3 studies, the uncertainty surrounding this finding remains high (Fig. 5).

Effects of ACSM protocol compliance on physical function

For gait speed, a meta-analysis of 15 studies (n = 811) demonstrated that exercise interventions improved performance, with a pooled SMD of 0.55 (95% CI: 0.29, 0.82; p < 0.001) and high heterogeneity (I² = 70%). Subgroup analyses revealed a larger effect size for the high ACSM protocol compliance group (4 studies; SMD = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.24, 1.86, p = 0.01) compared to the low ACSM protocol compliance group (11 studies; SMD = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.69, p = 0.001). However, the difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.15), and since the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup included only 4 studies, the stability of the result is limited and should be interpreted with caution.

For the Timed Up and Go test (TUG), a meta-analysis of 10 studies (n = 536) demonstrated that exercise interventions improved performance, with a pooled SMD of − 0.67 (95% CI: − 0.92, − 0.42; p < 0.001) and moderate heterogeneity (I² = 48%). In subgroup analyses, the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (4 studies) demonstrated improvement (SMD = − 0.97, 95% CI: − 1.41, − 0.54; p < 0.001), whereas the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (6 studies) demonstrated a moderate effect (SMD = − 0.52, 95% CI: − 0.78, − 0.26; p < 0.001). Although the effect size was larger in the high ACSM protocol compliance group, the difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.08), and given the small number of studies in the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup, it is not yet possible to determine whether there is a clear dose-response relationship between the level of compliance with the ACSM protocol and the degree of improvement in the TUG.

For the five-times sit-to-stand test (FTSST), a meta-analysis of 7 studies (n = 328) showed that exercise interventions improved performance, with a pooled SMD of − 0.52 (95% CI: − 0.90, − 0.15; p = 0.006) and high heterogeneity (I² = 62%). Subgroup analyses revealed that the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (3 studies) exhibited an SMD of − 1.03 (95% CI: − 1.44, − 0.62; p < 0.001), whereas the low ACSM protocol compliance subgroup (4 studies) showed an SMD of − 0.21 (95% CI: − 0.52, 0.11; p = 0.21), which was not statistically significant. Subgroup analyses (p = 0.002) suggest that exercise with high ACSM protocol compliance may improve FTSST performance. However, given that the high ACSM protocol compliance subgroup included only 3 studies, the stability of this subgroup finding is limited (Fig. 6).

Adverse events and dropout rates

Regarding the safety and adherence of exercise interventions, the 29 included studies demonstrated overall good tolerability (Table 5). In terms of safety, the vast majority of studies (22/29) did not report any adverse events. Among the studies that explicitly reported adverse events, most (6 studies) reported none; only one study [47] reported a minor adverse event, specifically “muscle discomfort.” This indicates that exercise interventions designed with high ACSM protocol compliance did not increase the risk of injury.

With respect to participant adherence, dropout rates varied considerably across studies, ranging from 0% to 40%, but no trend was observed suggesting that higher ACSM protocol compliance led to higher dropout rates. The high ACSM protocol compliance group included the study with the highest dropout rates [41] but also three studies with 0% dropout [31, 34, 38], with most studies in this group maintaining dropout rates between 9% and 26%. Overall, exercise interventions with high ACSM protocol compliance did not result in higher rates of adverse events or dropouts.

Subgroup analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted a meta-regression analysis examining intervention duration, participant age, and sarcopenia diagnostic criteria. The results indicated that participant age was the primary source of grip strength heterogeneity, showing a significant negative correlation (coefficient = − 0.046, p = 0.009). This suggests that as participants age, the effectiveness of interventions in improving grip strength diminishes. Additionally, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria significantly influenced the outcomes for MVC (p = 0.020), walking speed (p = 0.045), and TUG (p = 0.029). Specifically, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria were significantly and negatively associated with the TUG test effect size (coefficient = − 0.119), suggesting systematic differences in effect sizes across studies using different diagnostic standards. However, none of the aforementioned covariates reached statistical significance (p > 0.05) for ASMI, BMI, BFP, or FTSST. This suggests that, beyond the analyzed variables, the high heterogeneity observed in BFP and FTSST may stem from other clinical or methodological differences not incorporated into the analysis. For instance, variations in specific exercise intensities, participants’ nutritional status, or intervention adherence across studies could all potentially contribute to heterogeneity, yet these factors are difficult to uniformly quantify based on the available data reports (Table S3).

3D Visualization

To further elucidate the interplay between exercise parameters and clinical outcomes, we constructed a three-dimensional scatter plot visualizing the distribution of SMDs across varying intervention frequencies and durations (Note: this visualization does not include exercise intensity, as intensity parameters were reported inconsistently in the original studies and are difficult to standardize and quantify). The X-axis represents the SMD, the Y-axis denotes exercise frequency (sessions per week), and the Z-axis indicates the intervention duration (weeks). Color-coded clusters in the figure represent multiple outcome measures (Figure S1).

The 3D visualization reveals a nonlinear relationship between exercise dosage parameters and the SMD. Analysis indicates that the vast majority of studies focused on a frequency range of 2–3 times per week and a duration of 8–12 weeks. This “moderate-dose” intervention model significantly improved both physiological indicators (grip strength, ASMI) and functional indicators (gait speed, TUG) of sarcopenia, with an SMD > 0.5, indicating moderate efficacy. However, it should be noted that this analysis did not include intensity data; “moderate dose” refers only to the combination of frequency and duration, not the intensity of the physiological load. Furthermore, while increasing frequency (> 3 times/week) or duration (> 24 weeks) maintained the effects, the SMD did not increase proportionally. This implies that exercise interventions with moderate frequency and duration, which often align with ACSM protocol recommendations, are sufficient to induce significant physiological adaptation and functional improvement in patients with sarcopenia. However, since intensity is a core component of the ACSM guidelines and is not reflected in this visualization, the above conclusions should be interpreted with caution in light of the intensity parameters reported in the specific studies.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias assessment

To validate the robustness of the meta-analysis results, sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the classification thresholds for ACSM protocol compliance. We raised the threshold for defining “high ACSM protocol compliance” from the original 70% to 75% and 80%, respectively, and reassessed the impact of different compliance levels on each outcome measure (Figure S2, Figure S3). The results indicated that among all measures, only grip strength exhibited sensitivity to threshold changes. The p-value for between-group differences shifted from 0.03 (70% threshold) to 0.19 (75% threshold) and 0.24 (80% threshold), transitioning from statistically significant to non-significant. For the remaining measures—including ASMI, BMI, BFP, MVC, gait speed, TUG, and FTSST—between-group comparisons showed no significant changes. In summary, except for grip strength, the findings for all outcome measures were consistent across different thresholds for ACSM protocol compliance, indicating robust stability and enhancing the reliability of the conclusions.

To assess potential publication bias, we evaluated outcomes reported in more than ten studies, namely ASMI (Fig. 7A), BMI (Fig. 7B), grip strength (Fig. 7C), gait speed (Fig. 7D), and TUG (Fig. 7E). The funnel plots for these results were broadly symmetrical on both sides. Begg’s test and Egger’s test yielded p-values greater than 0.05, indicating no significant publication bias for these outcomes (Table 6).

Discussion

Based on a comprehensive literature search and screening, this systematic review and meta-analysis included 29 RCTs involving 1,524 patients with sarcopenia. This study is the first to quantitatively evaluate the compliance of exercise program designs with the ACSM recommendations, stratifying interventions into high (14 studies) and low (15 studies) ACSM protocol compliance groups to systematically compare the effects of different compliance levels. Analysis revealed that exercise interventions with high ACSM protocol compliance demonstrated significant advantages in improving muscle mass (ASMI, SMD = 0.49 vs. 0.10). However, the findings exhibit considerable inconsistency regarding muscle strength and physical function. First, although grip strength exhibited subgroup differences in the initial analysis, sensitivity analyses indicated that this finding lacked robustness. When the compliance threshold was adjusted to 75% (p = 0.19) or 80% (p = 0.24), the differences between the high and low ACSM protocol compliance groups lost statistical significance. Second, for MVC and FTSST, although effect sizes appeared larger in the high ACSM protocol compliance group, high subgroup heterogeneity and the limited number of included studies precluded definitive conclusions. Additionally, no significant differences were observed between subgroups for gait speed (p = 0.15) and TUG (p = 0.08), indicating that the high ACSM protocol compliance did not demonstrate superiority in enhancing gait speed or dynamic balance capacity.

Research indicates that exercise significantly enhances muscle mass. Resistance training (RT) is considered the primary strategy for muscle hypertrophy [54, 55], exerting its effects through the activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway in muscle fibers (particularly type II fast-twitch fibers) via mechanical loading. This process promotes muscle protein synthesis, inhibits degradation, and ultimately induces muscle fiber hypertrophy [56]. Multimodal training likewise exerts beneficial effects on muscle mass [57]. However, when the exercise dose falls below the ACSM-recommended threshold, it may be insufficient to adequately activate the molecular pathways for muscle growth, thereby limiting protein synthesis and weakening the effect against muscle loss. It should be noted that while this study observed improvements in muscle mass, neither BMI nor BFP showed significant changes. This seemingly contradictory result reflects the complexity of body composition changes and the limitations of assessment metrics. First, BMI, as a ratio of weight to height, cannot distinguish between changes in muscle and fat. Resistance training often involves muscle gain and fat loss, leading to body composition remodeling while weight remains relatively stable, making BMI less sensitive to internal changes. Second, a significant reduction in BFP typically requires sustained energy deficit. The studies included in this meta-analysis primarily focused on muscle strength and physical function. Without strict dietary control, exercise intervention alone may be insufficient to induce a significant decrease in BFP within the intervention period.

Exercise interventions exert significant benefits on muscle strength in patients with sarcopenia, particularly in key indicators such as grip strength and MVC. Existing evidence consistently indicates that RT and multimodal training are the most effective approaches for improving grip strength [15, 18]. Furthermore, low-intensity blood flow restriction training exhibits muscle strengthening effects equivalent to those of traditional high-intensity RT [58]. Exercise with high ACSM protocol compliance may facilitate enhanced neural drive and motor unit synchronization, optimize fast-twitch fiber recruitment, induce neural adaptations, and provide the mechanical tension required for subsequent hypertrophy [59]. Conversely, exercise performed below the recommended dosage fails to provide sufficient overload stimulation, thereby limiting neural adaptations and preventing adequate activation and remodeling of fast-twitch fibers, which markedly attenuates improvements in muscle strength.

Exercise interventions significantly enhance physical function in patients with sarcopenia, as indicated by increased gait speed and reduced completion times in the TUG and FTSST. Existing evidence indicates that high-velocity resistance training excels in enhancing gait speed, TUG, and FTSST, whereas traditional resistance training yields distinct advantages in the 30-second chair stand test, though the overall effects of these two modalities did not differ statistically [60]. Proprioceptive training has also been shown to reduce TUG time and confer notable benefits for balance, gait stability, and fall risk reduction [61]. Consistent with these observations, our results indicate that high ACSM protocol compliance was correlated with superior enhancements in physical function.

Compared with existing meta-analyses, this review exhibits significant differences in the classification of intervention characteristics and outcome measures. Lu et al. [15] compared the effects of three exercise modalities on knee extensor strength and physical function in patients with sarcopenia, finding that resistance training and combined training improved knee extensor strength, gait speed, and TUG, while whole-body vibration training only improved TUG. Shen et al. [18] conducted a network meta-analysis comparing seven exercise types, concluding that combined resistance and balance training was most effective for improving gait speed and the TUG test, while combined nutritional supplementation enhanced grip strength. This review did not solely categorize interventions by exercise modality but instead classified them into high ACSM protocol compliance and low ACSM protocol compliance groups based on adherence to ACSM recommendations (frequency, intensity, and intervention duration), directly assessing the impact of protocol compliance on efficacy. Regarding outcome measures, Lu et al. primarily focused on knee extensor strength, gait speed, TUG, and FTSST test, while Shen et al. emphasized grip strength, gait speed, TUG, FTSST test, and quality of life. Our study not only covers these functional measures but also incorporates MVC, body composition indicators and safety outcomes (adverse events and dropout rates), providing more comprehensive evidence on efficacy and safety.

While this review provides valuable insights, certain limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, twenty-eight of the 29 included studies were rated as having a high risk of performance bias. This was primarily due to the inherent difficulty of blinding participants and personnel in exercise interventions, rather than deficiencies in other methodological aspects such as randomization or outcome measurement. Such bias may potentially overestimate the overall effectiveness of exercise interventions. Although the included studies primarily relied on objective measures, which tends to mitigate measurement bias, the widespread lack of blinding may still compromise the validity and reliability of effect estimates. Secondly, MVC, FTSST, gait speed, and TUG exhibit significant heterogeneity and are supported by limited evidence, resulting in inconclusive findings. Finally, exercise intensity was not analyzed as an independent variable. Although 3D visualization presented the distribution characteristics of exercise frequency and duration, the intensity dimension was not incorporated into a stratified analysis. This limitation prevented us from fully quantifying and comparing the independent contributions of different exercise intensities to sarcopenia outcomes. The current scoring system assigns equal weight to frequency, intensity, and duration, potentially leading to higher composite scores for high-frequency but low-intensity interventions. Future studies should specifically examine the dose-response relationship between exercise intensity and efficacy through stratified analyses, regression models, or weighted scoring methods to more precisely guide clinical exercise prescription development.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis indicates that exercise interventions with high ACSM protocol compliance significantly improve muscle mass in patients with sarcopenia. Although improvements were observed in grip strength, FTSST performance, and MVC, inconsistent sensitivity analyses and high heterogeneity preclude definitive conclusions regarding these findings. For other outcomes, interventions with high ACSM protocol compliance demonstrated no superior efficacy compared with other exercise protocols.
