Fringe subtrees of split trees and fractional split trees
Cecilia Holmgren, Jasper Ischebeck, Svante Janson

Abstract
We consider additive functionals X n ​ ( ϕ ) X_{n}(\phi) with small toll functions on split trees and a generalization of split trees, which we call fractional split trees, where the split vector does not need to sum up to 1. These additive functionals encompass e.g. the number of nodes, number of leaves and the number of fringe trees of a certain size. We show convergence of the first moment to a limit, which we can explicitly compute if s 0 = s 1 = 0 s_{0}=s_{1}=0 and for some models with Beta-distributed splitter. For s 0 + s 1 > 0 s_{0}+s_{1}>0 , the first moment is given in terms of negative moments of a perpetuity and can often be approximated to arbitrary precision with known bounds. In split trees and certain fractional split trees, the standard deviation is of smaller order than the first moment, where we show a weak law of large numbers. In other fractional split trees, the standard deviation is of the same order and we show a distribution limit using the contraction method.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we study fringe trees of
split trees
, a general class of random trees that encompasses many important data structures. The
split tree
is a random tree model introduced by
[
devroye:split-tree
]
that encompasses several other random tree models, like binary search trees, tries, digital search trees, quadtrees, simplex trees, median of
(2h-1)
trees and
m
-ary search trees. It consists of a
b
-ary tree (with
b≥2
), where each node can hold up to a fixed number
s≥1
of “balls”. The following introduction to split trees is from
[
broutin_total_2012
]
.

Consider an infinite rooted
b
-ary tree (every node has
b
children). The nodes are identified with the set of finite words on an alphabet with
b
letters,
\{1,\dots,b\}^{\ast}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\{1,\dots,b\}^{n}
. We abbreviate
[b]:=\{1,\dots,b\}
. The root is represented by the empty word
\varepsilon
. We write
u\preceq v
to denote that
u
is an ancestor of
v
(as words,
u
is a prefix of
v
). In particular, for the empty word
\varepsilon
, we have
\varepsilon\preceq v
for any
v\in[b]^{*}
. Concatenation of two strings
u,v
is denoted
uv
, so
P[b]
denotes all children of a set
P\subset[b]^{*}
.

A split tree
T_{n}
of cardinality
n
is constructed by distributing
n
balls (pieces of data) to the nodes
u\in[b]^{*}
. To describe the tree, it suffices to define the number of balls
n_{u}
in the subtree rooted at any node
u\in[b]^{\ast}
. The tree
T_{n}
is then defined as the smallest relevant tree, that is, the subset of nodes
u
such that
n_{u}>0
(which is indeed a tree).

In the model, internal nodes all contain
s_{0}\geq 0
balls, and external nodes can contain up to
s≥1
balls. The construction then resembles a divide-and-conquer procedure, where the partitioning pattern depends on a random vector of proportions. Let
\mathcal{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{b})
satisfy
V_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}V_{i}=1
; each node
u\in[b]^{*}
receives an independent copy
\mathcal{V}^{(u)}
of the random vector
\mathcal{V}
. Define
V_{(1)}≥V_{(2)}≥\dots≥V_{(b)}
as
\mathcal{V}
sorted in descending order. In the following, we always assume that
\mathbb{P}(V_{(1)}=1)<1
and
\mathbb{P}(V_{(b)}>0)>0
to avoid trivialities. The values
V_{1},\dots,V_{b}
are called
splitters
and
\mathcal{V}
is called
split vector
. Often
\mathcal{V}
is assumed to be exchangeable for simplicity, but we do not require this.
We can now describe
(n_{u},u\in[b]^{*})
. The tree contains
n
balls, and we naturally have
n_{\varepsilon}=n
. The split procedure is then carried on from parent to children as long as
n_{v}>s
.

Given the cardinality
n_{v}>s
and the split vector
\mathcal{V}^{(v)}=(V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{b})
of
v
, the cardinalities
(n_{v1},n_{v2},\dots,n_{vb})
of the
b
subtrees rooted at
v1,v2,\dots,vb
are multinomially distributed as

\operatorname{Mult}(n_{v}-s_{0}-bs_{1};V_{1},\dots,V_{b})+(s_{1},s_{1},\dots,s_{1}),

(1.1)

where
0\leq s_{0}
and
0\leq bs_{1}\leq s+1-s_{0}
. The parameters
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
can be explained as
s_{0}
balls being
retained
inside the node and
s_{1}
balls
redistributed
to each child.

In the proofs, it is often useful to compare (
1.1
) with a simpler, multinomially distributed variable,

(n^{\prime}_{v1},\dots,n_{vb}^{\prime}):=\operatorname{Mult}(n^{\prime}_{v};V_{1},\dots,V_{b}).

(1.2)

If
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
, then
n^{\prime}_{v}=n_{v}
in distribution as long as
n^{\prime}_{v}>s
.
We couple
n_{v}
and
n^{\prime}_{v}
in a way such that the difference
n^{\prime\prime}_{v}:=n_{v}-n^{\prime}_{v}
fulfills

\sum_{i=1}^{b}\lvert n^{\prime\prime}_{vi}\rvert≤\lvert n^{\prime\prime}_{v}\rvert+s_{0}+2bs_{1}≤\lvert n^{\prime\prime}_{v}\rvert+2s+2

(1.3)

for
v\in[b]^{*}
. This is e.g. possible by simulating both multinomial
distributions from the same sequence of i.i.d. variables with distribution

\mathcal{V}
.
We note also that this coupling, by induction on
\lvert v\rvert
, gives the upper
bound

n^{\prime\prime}_{v}\leq s_{1}.

(1.4)

For a node
v=(i_{1}\dots i_{m})\in[b]^{*}
, the term
n^{\prime}_{v}
is binomially distributed conditional on the split vectors
\mathcal{V}^{u}
of its parents
u\preceq v
. We call the conditional expectation of
n^{\prime}_{v}
the
mass
of
v
, given as

M_{v}^{n}:=n\prod_{j=1}^{m}V^{(i_{1}\dots i_{j-1})}_{(i_{j})}=nM_{v}^{1},

(1.5)

where for convenience
n
can be any positive real number.

We also consider a novel generalization of split trees, which we call
fractional split trees
, where the split vector is allowed to sum up to a number smaller than 1, i.e. we only have
\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}≤1
. Instead of (
1.1
),
(n_{v1},\dots,n_{vb})
is then distributed as the first
b
elements of

\operatorname{Mult}\bigg(n_{v}-s_{0}-bs_{1};V_{1},\dots,V_{b},(1-\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i})\bigg)+(s_{1},s_{1},\dots,s_{1},0).

(1.6)

The last element of (
1.6
) accounts for the possibility that a
ball disappears instead of going to one of the children. In order to make
sure the tree may grow to infinity with
n
, we
assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{b}\mathbb{P}(V_{i}>0)>1
.
(This makes the set of nodes with positive mass
M^{1}_{v}
a supercritical
branching process, and thus infinite with positive probability.
Otherwise, the set of nodes with positive mass would be finite a.s., and
then the size of tree would stay bounded as
n\to\infty
.)
Note that in a fractional split tree, leaves may have more than
s
balls.

This definition already hints at that fractional split trees appear as subtrees of split trees. E.g., a split tree where for every node the first child and its subtree is removed is a fractional split tree with splitter
(V_{2},\dots,V_{b})
.
If we, as another example, for each node in a split tree only consider the
k<b
children that have the highest
k
splitters
(V_{(1)},\dots,V_{(k)})
,
the resulting tree is a fractional split tree with split vector
(V_{(1)},\dots,V_{(k)})
, which does not sum up to 1 in general.

A
fringe subtree

T_{n}^{v}
of the tree
T_{n}
, where
v
is a node in
T_{n}
,
is defined as the subtree consisting of
v
and all its descendants in
T_{n}
.
The study of random fringe trees (i.e., the fringe tree rooted at a
uniformly random node
v
)
and asymptotic fringe tree distributions
was initiated by
[
Aldous1991AsymptoticFD
]
.

A general model to count different structures in trees are toll functions. A

toll function

\phi
is simply a function mapping trees with balls to

\mathbb{R}
. This toll function is then summed over all fringe subtrees of the
tree:
We define
X_{n}:=X_{n}(\phi)
as the sum

X_{n}(\phi):=\sum_{v\in T_{n}}\phi(T_{n}^{v}),

(1.7)

called
additive functional
of
\phi
.
As we only consider the tree
T_{n}
in this paper, we write
\phi(v):=\phi(T_{n}^{v})
as shorthand.
This definition allows for a uniform treatment of the number of nodes and of balls; to count nodes,
\phi\equiv 1
, and to count leaves use
\phi(T)=\mathbf{1}(T\text{ is a single leaf})
.

A very interesting case is the number of fringe subtrees with a certain number of balls. For some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the toll function

\delta_{k}(T_{n}^{v}):=\mathbf{1}(n_{v}=k)
, such that

X_{n}(\delta_{k})=\sum_{v\in T_{n}}\mathbf{1}(n_{v}=k).

(1.8)

Yet another important example is to count the number of fringe subtrees that are equal to a given tree
T\in[b]^{*}
, which can be counted as

X_{n}(\delta_{T})=\sum_{v\in T_{n}}\mathbf{1}(T_{n}^{v}=T)

(1.9)

with the toll function
\delta_{T}(T^{\prime}):=\mathbf{1}(T^{\prime}=T)
.
The distribution of a random fringe tree of
T_{n}

is then given by
(where
T
ranges over all trees)

\mathbb{P}(\text{a random fringe tree}=T)=\frac{X_{n}(\delta_{T})}{X_{n}(1)}.

(1.10)

The asymptotic fringe tree distribution as
n\to\infty
,
when it exists,
is thus given by the limits of these quotients.

To study this family of additive functionals is thus equivalent to studying
the distribution of a random fringe tree.
Similarly, one could count the number of trees that are isomorphic to
T
in some sense. For split trees, it is usually sufficient to consider
X_{n}(\delta_{k})
if
T
has
k
balls, because it is easy to see that

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}(\delta_{T})\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}=T\right)\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}(\delta_{k})\right].

(1.11)

The asymptotic behavior of
X_{n}(\delta_{k})
and
X_{n}(\delta_{T})
has been
studied for multiple random tree models, including e.g. conditioned Galton–Watson trees
[
Aldous1991AsymptoticFD
]
,
the recursive tree and binary search tree

[
Aldous1991AsymptoticFD
,
devroye_2003
,
wagner:additive
,
holmgren_limit_2015
]
,
the
m
-ary search tree
[
holmgren_cmj_2017
,
holmgren_multivariate_2017
]
,
preferential attachment trees

[
holmgren_cmj_2017
,
holmgren_multivariate_2017
,
holmgren_2023
]
,

[
holmgren_cmj_2017
,
holmgren_multivariate_2017
]
,
the trie
[
janson:trie
]
and the patricia trie
[
patricia-fringe
]
.
Asymptotic results for small toll functions in general have been found for
many random tree models, e.g. for recursive trees and binary search trees

[
holmgren_limit_2015
]
, for simply generated trees or conditioned
Galton–Watson trees
[
wagner:additive
,
janson:gw-fringe
]
and for trees
with given degree sequences
[
berzunza_ojeda_fringe_2025
]
. See

[
drmota:rt
, Section 3.1.2]
for an overview.

Because the subtree
T_{n}^{v}
of a node
v
is, conditional on the number
n_{v}
of balls, distributed as
T_{n_{v}}
, the mean of
X_{n}
only depends on
\phi_{n}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(T_{n})\right]
for
n≥1
and is

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\sum_{v\in T_{n}}\phi_{n_{v}}.

(1.12)

For nodes
\phi(T_{n})=\mathbf{1}(n>0)=1
; for balls
\phi(T_{n})=n
if
n≤s
and
\phi(T_{n})=s_{0}
if
n>s
. Note that in both cases
\phi_{n}=\phi(T_{n})
.
We will have more advanced examples in
Section

3
.

For fractional split trees there exists a unique
\beta\in(0,1]
such that

\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}^{\beta}=1,

(1.13)

which we call the
Malthusian parameter
of
\mathcal{V}
. Split trees have
\beta=1
. Observe that the condition
\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\mathbf{1}(V_{i}>0)>1
is what ensures
\beta>0
. Furthermore, we define
V
as a uniformly randomly chosen element of
\mathcal{V}
, i.e.
V:=V_{I}
, where
I
is uniformly distributed on
[b]
and independent of
\mathcal{V}
. We can alternatively characterize
\beta
as the value where
\mathbb{E}[V^{\beta}]=\frac{1}{b}
. Note that if
\mathcal{V}
is exchangeable, then
V_{1}
, …,
V_{b}
and
V
all have the same distribution.

We define a random variable
\widehat{V}
with the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{V}}
given as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{V}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{V}}(v)=bv^{\beta}.

(1.14)

Because
\mathbb{E}[bV^{\beta}]=1
, this is a probability measure. We regard
\widehat{V}
as
V

size-biased
with the factor
V^{\beta}
.
If
\beta=1
, this is just the regular size-biasing. We can alternatively
characterize
\widehat{V}
by the property that for every measurable function
f
,

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\widehat{V})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[bf(V)V^{\beta}\right]

(1.15)

if the expectation of
f(V)V^{\beta}
exists. We further define

\mu:=\mathbb{E}[-\ln\widehat{V}]=\mathbb{E}[-bV^{\beta}\ln V]=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\mathbb{E}[-V_{i}^{\beta}\ln V_{i}]\in(0,\infty).

(1.16)

We call it the
lattice case
that there exists a
d>0
such that
\ln V\in d\mathbb{Z}
a.s. and define

d:=\gcd(\ln V)
as the largest such
d
. If no such
d
exists, we formally set
d:=\gcd{\ln V}:=0
. We show in
Theorem

2.1
that the expected number of nodes has oscillations that are
d
-periodic in
\ln n
. For
x≥0
, we write
\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor_{d}:=d\left\lfloor\frac{x}{d}\right\rfloor
for the largest number in
d\mathbb{Z}
that is smaller or equal to
x
and
\left\{x\right\}_{d}:=x-\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor_{d}
. The lattice case is somewhat special, and most split tree models are nonlattice. Well-known exceptions are some tries and digital search trees, where the split vector is deterministic.

We denote by
x^{+}
the positive part of
x\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
x^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. For some statement
A
, we denote by
\mathbf{1}(A)
the indicator function that is 1 if
A
is true and 0 otherwise. For a random variable
X
, we write
X\sim\mu
if
X
has the probability distribution
\mu
and we write
\mathbb{P}_{X}
for the probability distribution of
X
, so that
X\sim\mu
is equivalent to
\mathbb{P}_{X}=\mu
. For two random variables
X
and
Y
, we denote by
X\overset{d}{=}Y
that
\mathbb{P}_{X}=\mathbb{P}_{Y}
.

For a function
G
on
\mathbb{R}
and a measure
U
on
\mathbb{R}
, we define the

convolution
of
G
and
U
as the function (when the integral exists)

(U*G)(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(x-y)\mathrm{d}U(y).

(1.17)

Note that if
\mathbb{P}_{X}
is the probability distribution of some random variable
X
, then
(\mathbb{P}_{X}*G)(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[G(x-X)\right]
.

2
Results

The purpose of this paper is the study of the asymptotic distribution and
properties of a random fringe tree in a random split tree
T_{n}
.
We state the results in a general form for additive functionals using rather
general toll functions.
Note, in particular, that choosing

\phi=1
gives the (random) number of nodes in the tree, and that

\phi=\delta_{T}
defined after (
1.9
)
gives the number of subtrees equal to
T
;
by combining them we can obtain the
distribution of a random fringe subtree, and the results below yield results
on the asymptotic fringe tree distribution, see (
1.10
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\phi
be a toll function such that
\phi_{n}=\mathrm{O}(n^{\beta-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
. This is e.g. true for counting leaves, for counting nodes and for counting fringe subtrees of a certain size.
Then, there exists a bounded, continuous,
d
-periodic function
\varpi:=\varpi[\phi]
, such that

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(\phi)]=\varpi(\ln n)n^{\beta}+o(n^{β}).

(2.1)

In the nonlattice case,
\varpi
is constant. If
\phi_{n}≥0
for all
n
and
\phi_{n}>0
for some
n≥s_{1}
, then
\varpi
is bounded away from
0
. Recall that for split trees
\beta=1
.

Unfortunately, the proof of
Theorem

2.1
does not give an explicit formula for
\varpi
, but we will show how to calculate it in special cases below in
Theorem

2.6
and
Corollary

2.13
.
With
Theorem

2.1
, we can use the contraction method to find the limit distribution of
n^{-β}(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}])
.
For two random variables
X
and
Y
on the same probability space, we
define the
L_{2}
distance between
X
and
Y
to be

\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert X-Y\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Convergence in
L_{2}
implies convergence in
probability and of first and second moments. For random variables
X
and
Y
that are not necessarily on the same probability space, we call a random variable
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
a coupling of
X
and
Y
if
X\overset{d}{=}X^{\prime}
and
Y\overset{d}{=}Y^{\prime}
. The Wasserstein
\ell_{2}
metric is then defined as the minimum of
\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime}\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}
over all couplings
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
of
X
and
Y
. Convergence in
\ell_{2}
is equivalent to convergence in distribution and convergence of first and second moments.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
\varpi
from
Theorem

2.1
is bounded away from zero. Let
\phi(v)
for
v\in[b]^{*}
be a function of
n_{v1},\dots,n_{vb}
that fulfills
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(T_{n})^{2}\right]=\mathrm{O}\left(n^{2(\beta-\delta)}\right)
for some
\delta>0
. This moment condition is in particular true for bounded toll functions, e.g. counting leaves, nodes or subtrees with a certain number of balls.
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}\right]}{n^{β}\varpi(\ln n)}\overset{\ell_{2}}{\longrightarrow}Y,

(2.2)

where
Y
has the unique centered square-integrable probability distribution satisfying

Y+1\overset{d}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}^{\beta}(Y^{(i)}+1),

(2.3)

where
Y^{(i)}
are independent copies of
Y
independent of
\mathcal{V}
. Therefore,

\Var\left(\frac{X_{n}}{\varpi(\ln n)n^{β}}\right)\longrightarrow\Var(Y)=\frac{\Var\left(\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}^{\beta}\right)}{1-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}^{2\beta}\right]}

(2.4)

and
Y=0
a.s. if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}^{\beta}=1
a.s. Note that this is always the case for split trees.

In particular, if
Y=0
a.s., we have a weak law of large numbers:

Corollary 2.3

(Weak law of large numbers)
.

Assume that
\phi
fulfills the conditions of
Theorem

2.2
and that
\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}^{\beta}=1
a.s. Then

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}\right]}{n^{β}\varpi(\ln n)}\overset{L_{2}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(2.5)

Remark 2.4
.

In the case of
Corollary

2.3
, we conjecture that
X_{n}
is typically asymptotically normal (possibly with a
d
-periodic variance), as this is true for many split tree models we know about.
For
m
-ary search trees with
m>26
it is known that
X_{n}(\phi)-\varpi n
is not asymptotically normal for general
\phi
, cf.
[
chern_phase_2001
]
. We plan to study this in a second paper.

Remark 2.5
.

The convergence (
2.2
) in
Theorem

2.2
also holds for multiple toll functions at once: If
\phi^{(1)},\dots,\phi^{(k)}
are toll functions fulfilling the conditions in
Theorem

2.2
, then
X_{n}(\phi^{(i)})
all converge to the same limit
Y
when scaled correctly, i.e.

\left(\frac{X_{n}(\phi^{(i)})-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}(\phi^{(i)})\right]}{n^{β}\varpi(\ln n)}\right)_{i=1}^{k}\overset{\ell_{2}}{\longrightarrow}(Y)_{i=1}^{k}.

(2.6)

The limit function
\varpi
is the easiest to calculate if
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
, because then
n^{\prime}_{v}
and
n_{v}
are the same as long as
n_{v}>s
. In this case, we have for the size of fringe trees:

Theorem 2.6
.

Assume that
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
and define for
k≥1
and
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-k\}
with
\Re z≥-β

M_{k}(z):=\frac{\Gamma(k+z)}{\mu k!}-\frac{b}{\mu}\sum_{j=0}^{s-k}\mathbb{E}\left[V^{k}(1-V)^{j}\right]\frac{\Gamma(k+j+z)}{k!j!}.

(2.7)

Note that the sum is empty if
k>s
.
If
k=β=1
, we define the value at
-1
to instead be

M_{1}(-1):=1-\frac{b}{\mu}\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[V(1-V)^{j}\right]}{j}.

(2.8)

In the nonlattice case, the constant
\varpi[\delta_{k}]
from
Theorem

2.1
for the toll function
\delta_{k}
counting fringe trees of size
k
(see (
1.8
)) is given by

\varpi[\delta_{k}]=M_{k}(-β)

(2.9)

and in the lattice case, the
d
-periodic function
\varpi[\delta_{k}]
has the Fourier series

\varpi[\delta_{k}](x)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{{2\pi imx}/d}M_{k}\left(-β-\frac{2\pi im}{d}\right).

(2.10)

The Fourier coefficients go to
0
exponentially fast, implying that
\varpi[\delta_{k}]
is smooth (infinitely differentiable).

Remark 2.7
.

An interesting fact is that (
2.7
) implies that the
asymptotic distribution of fringe tree sizes larger than
s
does not
depend on
V
other than through
\textmu
and
β
.

In the case of split trees (
β=1
),
(
2.7
) and (

) give for

the limit

\frac{1}{\textmu k(k-1)}
in the nonlattice case,
with oscillations given by (
2.10
) in the lattice case,
as was known before for tries

[
Janson_2012
,
janson:trie
]
.
The correction terms for
k≤s
can also be found in

[
Janson_2012
, Section 6]
and
[
janson:trie
, Subsection 4.7]
.
We note that in these results in
[
Janson_2012
,
janson:trie
]
,
the function there corresponding to
M_{k}(z)
in (
2.7
)
has an additional factor
b\,\mathbb{E}[V^{-z}]
in the first term;
this factor equals 1 for the arguments

-1-\frac{2\pi im}{d}
in (
2.9
) and
(
2.10
) and thus makes no difference for the result except
in the case
k=1
, where it combines with the pole of
\Gamma(k+z)
at
z=-1

which yields our special formula (
2.8
) for this case.
In our (more general) analysis below, (
2.8
) is instead
obtained by a special argument for this case; we thus obtain the same result
in different ways.

The following proposition shows that the limit function
\varpi
for a general toll function can be calculated from
\varpi[\delta_{k}]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

For a general toll function
\phi
with
\phi_{n}=\mathrm{O}\left(n^{β-δ}\right)
for some
δ>0
, the limit function
\varpi[\phi]
from
Theorem

2.1
is

\varpi[\phi]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varpi[\delta_{k}]\phi_{k}

(2.11)

In the lattice case, where
\varpi[\phi]
is a function, this series converges uniformly.

Even if
\varpi[\phi]
is given as a uniformly converging limit of smooth
functions, this does not mean that
\varpi[\phi]
itself is smooth. We can,
however, show that it is Hölder-continuous.

Proposition 2.9
.

Assume we are in the lattice case. If
\phi_{n}=\mathrm{O}(n^{β-δ})
for some
δ>0
, then

\varpi[\phi]
is Hölder-
\alpha
-continuous for every exponent
\alpha<2δ
with
\alpha≤1
;
furthermore, if
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
then
\varpi[\phi]
is
continuously differentiable.

In particular, for any
\delta>0
,
the Fourier series of
\varpi
converges uniformly.
Furthermore, if
δ>\frac{1}{4}
, the Fourier series of
\varpi
converges absolutely.

This bound is sharp if
δ≤β
because of the following:

Proposition 2.10
.

Assume we are in the lattice case.
If
\varpi
is a
d
-periodic, Hölder-
2\delta
-continuous function with exponent
2\delta≤1
such that
δ≤β
, then there exists a toll function
\phi
with
\phi_{n}=\mathrm{O}(n^{β-δ})
such that
\varpi[\phi]=\varpi
.
In particular, for every
δ≤\min(\frac{1}{2},β)
there exists a toll function
\phi
with
\phi_{n}=\mathrm{O}\left(n^{β-δ}\right)

such that
\varpi[\phi]
is not Hölder-
\alpha
-continuous
for any exponent
\alpha>2δ
.

For arbitrary
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
, we can link
\varpi[\delta_{k}]
with negative moments of a perpetuity in the nonlattice case as follows:

Theorem 2.11
.

Assume we are in the nonlattice case, and define for
k\in\mathbb{N}
the toll function
\delta_{≥k}:=\sum_{j≥k}\delta_{j}
counting fringe trees with at least
k≥1
balls. Assume that either
k>s
or
s≤s_{0}+bs_{1}
and
k>s_{1}
. For any
x>0
let
E_{x}
be Gamma-
\Gamma(x,1)
-distributed and independent of
\widehat{V}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}\left(E_{x}\in\mathrm{d}t\right)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(x)}t^{x-1}e^{-t}\mathrm{d}t
for
t≥0
. Further define
\Xi
as the unique integrable solution of

\Xi\overset{d}{=}(E_{s_{0}+(b-1)s_{1}}+\Xi)\widehat{V},

(2.12)

where
\Xi
is independent of
E_{s_{0}+(b-1)s_{1}}
and
\widehat{V}
. Then,

\varpi[\delta_{≥k}]=\frac{1}{βμ}{\mathbb{E}\left[(\Xi+E_{k-s_{1}})^{-β}\right]},

(2.13)

so

\varpi[\delta_{k}]=\frac{1}{βμ}{\mathbb{E}\left[(\Xi+E_{k-s_{1}})^{-β}-(\Xi+E_{k+1-s_{1}})^{-β}\right]}.

(2.14)

and for
k\to\infty
,

\varpi[\delta_{≥k}]\sim\frac{1}{βμk^{β}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varpi[\delta_{k}]\sim\frac{1}{μk^{1+β}}.

(2.15)

Remark 2.12
.

The condition that either
k>s
or
s≤s_{0}+bs_{1}
can be lifted by the methods detailed in
plus
Proposition

2.8
.

Since the negative moments of the perpetuity
\Xi
are hard to compute, we show in
how to approximate
\varpi
in the case of
s_{0}+s_{1}>0
with known error bounds.
There is, however, a special case in which the distribution of
\Xi
can be explicitly calculated.

Corollary 2.13
.

Assume that
V
is
B(a,s_{0}+(b-1)s_{1})
Beta-distributed for some
a>0
. Note that because
\mathbb{E}[V]≤\frac{1}{b}
, it must hold that
a≤\frac{s_{0}}{b-1}+s_{1}
with equality if and only if
β=1
.
Then,

\mu=\psi(a+β+s_{0}+(b-1)s_{1})-\psi(a+β),

(2.16)

where
\psi(t):=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(t)}{\Gamma(t)}
is the digamma function, and

\varpi[\delta_{k}]=\frac{\Gamma(a+k-s_{1})}{\mu\Gamma(a+k-s_{1}+β+1)}.

(2.17)

for
k>s
or
s≤s_{0}+bs_{1}
and
k≥s_{1}
.

For split trees with
\beta=1
, this
Corollary

2.13
applies more or less only to a generalization of

-ary search trees called generalized Quicksort by Hennequin
[

]
, which we explore in Examples

and

Toll functions

In

\frac{1}{d}\int_{0}^{d}\varpi(t)\mathrm{d}t&\text{ in the lattice case}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

3.1.1
Nodes, leaves and internal nodes

We start by one of the simplest cases: The number of nodes, leaves and internal nodes:

Example 3.1

(Expected number of nodes if
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
and

.

By (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) in

n
leaves and in expectation
\sim\frac{n}{k(k-1)\mu}
subtrees of size

, which sum up to
\sim n(1+\mu^{-1})
nodes
(using

).
In the lattice case, there are some oscillations around these values.

In the case of fractional fringe trees with

\delta_{1}

\varpi_{0}[\delta_{1}]=\frac{\Gamma(1-\beta)}{μ}-\frac{b\Gamma(1-β)}{μ}\mathbb{E}[V]=\frac{\Gamma(1-β)}{μ}\left(1-\mathbb{E}[bV]\right)

(3.2)

and
\varpi_{0}[\delta_{k}]=\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{\mu k!}

. The

2.8

[
GammaFractionSum
]
and is

\varpi_{0}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\delta_{k}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(2-β)}{βμ}=\frac{\Gamma(1-β)(1-β)}{β\mu}.

(3.3)

Therefore, the constant for the number of nodes is

\varpi_{0}[1]=\frac{\Gamma(1-β)}{μ}\left(\frac{1}{β}-\mathbb{E}[bV]\right).

(3.4)

We use the notation

since the toll function for the number of nodes is constant 1.

Let

be the toll function for the number of leaves.
Note that for

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{l}]=\frac{\Gamma(1-β)}{μ}\left(1-\mathbb{E}[bV]\right)+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{\mu k!}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i}\right)^{k}\right].

(3.5)

3.1.2
Balls remaining in the tree

In fractional split trees, balls can disappear instead of going to one of the children, so it is an interesting question to consider how many balls remain in the tree. Define the toll function

\phi_{b}(T_{n}):=s_{0}\mathbf{1}(n>s)+n\mathbf{1}(n≤s),

(3.6)

so that
X_{n}(\phi_{b})
counts the number of balls in the nodes of
T_{n}
. Since this toll function is bounded by
s+1
and only depends on
n
, we can apply Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.

For
Theorem

2.6
, we again assume
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
, which means that all balls are in leaves and
\phi_{b}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}k\delta_{k}
. If
β<1
, we have by (
2.11
)
and (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\varpi_{0}[\phi_{b}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\biggl(\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{\mu(k-1)!}-\frac{b}{\mu}\sum_{j=0}^{s-k}\mathbb{E}\left[V^{k}(1-V)^{j}\right]\frac{\Gamma(k+j-β)}{(k-1)!j!}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\biggl(\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{\mu(k-1)!}-\frac{b}{\mu}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell V^{\ell}(1-V)^{k-\ell}\right]\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{\ell!(k-\ell)!}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{\mu(k-1)!}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[bV\right]\right).

(3.7)

The first equality is by noting the double sum runs over all
k,j
with

k+j≤s
and changing indices, the second is by interpreting
the inner
sum as the expectation of a binomial
\operatorname{Bin}(k,V)
random variable. In the lattice case, this is the 0-th Fourier coefficient of
\varpi[\phi_{b}]
, implying
\varpi[\phi_{b}]
is oscillating around this value.

3.1.3
k
-protected nodes

A node is called
k
-protected (
k≥1
) if the shortest distance to a descendant that is not a leaf is at least
k

1
-protected nodes are internal nodes and
2
-protected nodes are internal nodes that have no children that are leaves.
The 2-protected nodes are the most studied case.

The number
X_{n}(\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}})
of
k
-protected nodes has the toll function

\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}(T):=\mathbf{1}\left(\forall v\in T,\lvert v\rvert<k:\deg(v)\neq 0\right),

(3.8)

where
\deg
is the outdegree, and
\lvert v\rvert
denotes the length of
v
as a string, which equals the distance of
v
to the root.
If
\beta=1
or
s_{1}>0
, a node
v
is a leaf if and only if
1≤n_{v}≤s
, so

\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}(T_{n})=\mathbf{1}\left(\forall v\in[b]^{\ast},\lvert v\rvert<k:n_{v}=0\text{ or }n_{v}>s\right),

(3.9)

Else, i.e. if
\beta<1
and
s_{1}=0
, a node could be a leaf just by all balls vanishing; this happens with probability
\mathbb{E}\big[(1-\sum_{i=1}^{b}V_{i})^{n-s_{0}}\big]
for
n>s
.

Since
\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}
is a bounded toll function,
Theorem

2.1
applies, and we have

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}(\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}})\right]=\varpi[\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}](\ln n)n^{β}+o(n^{β})

(3.10)

for a
d
-periodic function
\varpi
bounded away from zero.

Theorem

2.2
applies if
k=1,2
and
\beta=1
, because then
\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}
only depends on the numbers
n_{1},\dots,n_{b}
of balls that go to the children of the root, as can be seen from (
3.9
).

Assume
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
. As outlined in
,
\varpi[\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}]
can be calculated from the Mellin transform of

f^{k\mathrm{-prot}}(ł):=\mu^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\phi^{k\mathrm{-prot}}(\widetilde{T}_{ł})]
,
where
\widetilde{T}_{ł}
is
T_{n}
for

being Poisson
(ł)
distributed and independent. We have for
\beta=1

\displaystyle\mu f^{k\mathrm{-prot}}(ł)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall{v\in[b]^{k-1}}:n_{v}^{\prime}=0\text{ or }n_{v}^{\prime}>s\right)-\mathbb{P}(n_{\varepsilon}=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{v\in[b]^{k-1}}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{(M^{1}_{v}ł)^{j}}{j!}e^{-M^{1}_{v}ł}\right)\right]-e^{-ł}.

(3.11)

This product can be rewritten into a sum
of
1-e^{-\lambda}
(with Mellin transform
-\Gamma(z)
)
and a number of terms of the form
\mathbb{E}[ł^{a}Xe^{-Ył}]
for
some integers
a\geq 1
and random variables

and

(depending on the

\mathcal{V}^{(v)}
for

these terms give (using Fubini’s theorem) Mellin transforms of the form

\Gamma(z+a)\mathbb{E}[XY^{-z-a}]
, yielding a result similar to

[
janson:trie
, (4.66) and (4.68)]

Note that

is bounded, and also

since

\mathcal{M}[f^{k\mathrm{-prot}}](z)

(the apparent singularity at

Example 3.2
.

In the particular case

,

\mu f^{2\mathrm{-prot}}(ł)=1-e^{-ł}-2\mathbb{E}\left[Vłe^{-Vł}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}V_{2}ł^{2}\exp(-ł)\right],

which has Mellin transform

\mu\mathcal{M}[f^{2\mathrm{-prot}}](z)=-\Gamma(z)-2\mathbb{E}\left[V^{-z}\right]\Gamma(z+1)+\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}V_{2}\right]\Gamma(z+2).

\displaystyle\mu\mathcal{M}[f^{2\mathrm{-prot}}](-1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{z\to-1}-\frac{\Gamma(z+2)}{z}\cdot\frac{z\mathbb{E}\left[2V^{-z}\right]+1}{z+1}+\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}V_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{z}}z\mathbb{E}\left[2V^{-z}\right]\right|_{\mathrlap{z=-1}}+\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}V_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\Bigl(2\mathbb{E}\left[V^{-z}\right]+z\mathbb{E}\left[-2\ln(V)V^{-z}\right]\Bigr)\biggr|_{\mathrlap{z=-1}}+\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}V_{2}\right]

as found in a special case in

3.1.4
Outdegree

The number
X_{n}(\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k})
of nodes with a certain outdegree

has the bounded toll function

\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}(T_{n}):=\mathbf{1}(\deg(\varepsilon)=k)=\mathbf{1}\left(\left\lvert\{i:n_{i}>0\}\right\rvert=k\right)

(3.15)

only depending on
n_{1},\dots,n_{b}
, so Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
both apply. Therefore,

\frac{X_{n}(\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k})}{n^{β}\varpi[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]}-1\overset{\ell_{2}}{\to}Y,

(3.16)

with
\varpi[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]
from
Theorem

2.1
and
Y
from
Theorem

2.2
.

If
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
, we can use
Theorem

2.6
to calculate the limit
function
\varpi[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]
.
First, we note that (
3.15
) trivially yields
\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}_{n}=0
for
n<k
or
n≤s
.
By
Proposition

2.8
and
Theorem

2.6
, we have

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}_{n}\varpi_{0}[\delta_{n}]=\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}_{n}\varpi_{0}[\delta_{n}]=\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}_{n}\frac{\Gamma(n-β)}{μn!}

(3.17)

for any
j=0,\dots,s+1
with
\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}_{0}:=0
.

In order to keep the terms simple, we restrict ourselves to the case
k>s
.
It then follows from (
3.15
) and the inclusion-exclusion formula that, for every
n≥1
,

\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}_{n}=\sum_{l=b-k}^{b}(-1)^{l+k-b}\binom{l}{b-k}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[b]\\
\lvert I\rvert=l\end{subarray}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\sum_{i\in I}V_{i}\right)^{n}\right].

(3.18)

Write as a shorthand
V_{I}:=\sum_{i\in I}V_{i}
.
Then, if
\beta<1
,
the sum in (

) can be written as a linear combination of sums having the form

\frac{1}{μ}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n-\beta)}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-V_{I}\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{1}{μ}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Gamma(-\beta)\binom{\beta}{n}(-1)^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-V_{I}\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(-\beta)}{\mu}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{I}^{\beta}\right]

(3.19)

by Fubini’s theorem and summing the binomial series
(a Taylor series)
for
(1-t)^{\beta}
, evaluated at

.
If
\beta=1
, we take instead
j=2
in (
3.17
).
Since the resulting sum is a continuous function of
\beta>0
, we can
omit calculations and obtain the result directly by taking the limit as

\beta\to 1
of the resulting formula for
\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]
.
(The individual terms (
3.19
) diverge, but not their linear
combination.)
We thus arrive at the following result:

Example 3.3
.

Assume that
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
and
k>s
, and consider the toll function
\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}
counting

defined in (

). Then,

,

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]=\frac{\Gamma(-β)}{μ}\sum_{l=b-k}^{b}(-1)^{l+k-b}\binom{l}{b-k}\sum_{\mathclap{{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[b]\\
\lvert I\rvert=l\end{subarray}}}}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{I}^{β}\right].

(3.20)

If
β=1
, we instead have, by letting
\beta\to 1
in (
3.20
)
and using the Taylor expansion

\mathbb{E}[V_{I}^{β}]=\mathbb{E}[V_{I}]+(\beta-1)\mathbb{E}[V_{I}\ln(V_{I})]+\mathrm{O}\left((1-\beta)^{2}\right)
,

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\operatorname{deg-}k}]=\frac{1}{μ}\sum_{l=b-k}^{b}(-1)^{l+k-b}\binom{l}{b-k}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[b]\\
\lvert I\rvert=l\end{subarray}}}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{I}\ln(V_{I})\right].

(3.21)

Replacing
β
with

in (

) gives the

-th Fourier coefficient of

in the lattice case (also in

\beta=1

3.1.5
Shape functional

The shape functional is an example of a toll function that is unbounded. It is defined as the logarithm of the product of all subtree sizes,
thus by

X_{n}(\phi^{\mathrm{sh}})=\ln\prod_{v\in T_{n}}n_{v}=\sum_{v\in T_{n}}\ln(n_{v}).

(3.22)

The toll function is
\phi^{\mathrm{sh}}(T_{n})=\ln n
, and we can therefore apply Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. The shape functional is closely linked with binary search trees: For a binary split tree with
s=s_{0}=1
and
s_{1}=0
, the shape functional is given as
-\ln\mathbb{P}\left(T^{\mathrm{bin}}_{n}=T_{n}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}T_{n}\right)
, where
T^{\mathrm{bin}}_{n}
is a binary split tree independent of
T_{n}
. So if
T_{n}
is itself a binary split tree, the shape functional is its information content, and the expectation of the shape functional is its (Shannon) entropy.

Example 3.4
.

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
apply to
X_{n}(\phi^{\mathrm{sh}})
, and it follows that

X_{n}(\phi^{\mathrm{sh}})=\varpi(\ln n)n^{β}+o(n^{β}),

(3.23)

with a continuous,
d
-periodic function
\varpi
bounded away from zero. If
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
,

Theorem

2.6
and
Proposition

2.8
yield

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\mathrm{sh}}]=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{\Gamma(k-β)\ln k}{\mu k!}-\frac{b}{\mu}\sum_{j=0}^{s-k}\mathbb{E}\left[V^{k}(1-V)^{j}\right]\frac{\Gamma(k+j-β)\ln k}{k!j!}\biggr).

(3.24)

In the nonlattice case,
\varpi
is constant; in the lattice case, its
m
-th Fourier coefficient is given by replacing
β
in (
3.24
) with
β+2\pi im/d
. Note that the second sum in (
3.24
) vanishes if
s=1
. In the particular case of fringe trees with
s=1
and
\beta=1
, we have

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\mathrm{sh}}]=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\ln k}{k(k-1)}\approx 1.25775\mu^{-1}.

(3.25)

See similar results for tries in
[
janson:trie
]
, simply generated trees in
[
meir_log-product_1998
]
,
[
fill_limiting_2004
]
and various models in
[
wagner:additive
]
.

Note that when generalizing the shape functional to fractional split trees, we could have also used the number of nodes
X_{n}(1)
or the number of remaining balls
X_{n}(\phi_{b})
from (
3.6
) instead of
n
in the definition of
\phi^{\mathrm{sh}}
in (

). These also allow us to apply
Theorem

2.1
, but we can neither use
Theorem

2.2
nor can we calculate
\varpi
accurately using
Theorem

2.6
. However, an approximation based on
Proposition

2.8
is possible.

3.1.6
Number of subtrees

Let
\operatorname{subtrees}(T_{n})
be the number of subtrees of
T_{n}
and

\operatorname{subtrees}^{*}(T_{n})
the number of subtrees of
T_{n}
containing the

The latter are in bijection to antichains in

, as noted in

[

,
wagner:additive-aofa
]
where these are studied for

containing the root consists of the root and for every

, its

is either a subtree of

containing the root or

As noted by
[

,
wagner:additive-aofa
]

it follows from this fact that

3.1.7
Independence number, domination number and similar functionals

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, an
independent set
is a set
U\subseteq V
, so that no nodes in
U

are adjacent to each other.
The
independence number

\alpha(G)
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is then the maximal size of an independent set of
G
, i.e.

\alpha(G):=\max\{\lvert U\rvert\mid U\subseteq V,e\not\subset U\,\forall e\in E\}.

(3.29)

The computation of
\alpha(G)
for general graphs
G
is NP-hard
[
Karp_1972
,
Xiao_Nagamochi_2017
]
, but for a tree
T
there is a simple algorithm based on the inequality

\alpha(T)-1≤\max\{\lvert U\rvert\mid\varepsilon\notin U\subseteq T,e\not\subset U\,\forall e\in E\}≤\alpha(T).

(3.30)

Exactly one of the inequalities is sharp. If the left one is sharp, then the root
\varepsilon
is contained in all maximal independent sets of
T
and
T
is called
essential
. A node
v
is called essential if
T^{v}
is essential. With a little bit of work (cf.
[
Fuchs_Holmgren_Mitsche_Neininger_2021
, Lemma 2.1]
), one can show the following:

Lemma 3.6
.

A node is essential if and only if all of its children are not essential.
The essential nodes of
T
form an independent set
U
, with
\lvert U\rvert=\alpha(T)
. The toll function of
\alpha
is therefore
\phi^{\alpha}(T):=\mathbf{1}(\varepsilon\text{ is essential})
.

The independence number thus has a bounded toll function, and we can apply
Theorem

2.1
,
Theorem

2.6
and
Proposition

2.8
.

Example 3.7

(Independence number)
.

There exists a continuous,
d
-periodic function
\varpi[\phi^{\alpha}]
bounded away from zero, so that
as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(T_{n})\right]=\varpi[\phi^{\alpha}](\ln n)n^{β}+o(n^{β}).

(3.31)

If
s_{0}=s_{1}=0
and
s=1
, the mean
\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\alpha}]
of
\varpi[\phi^{\alpha}]
is given by

\varpi_{0}[\phi^{\alpha}]=\varpi_{0}[\delta_{1}]+\frac{1}{μ}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k-β)}{k!}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{\alpha}(T_{k})\right].

(3.32)

For split trees (

),

, and for

).

In
[
Fuchs_Holmgren_Mitsche_Neininger_2021
]
,

is calculated explicitly for binary search trees and random recursive trees,
and it is shown that

is asymptotically normal for these models.
Earlier,
[
janson:indnum
]
showed that for Crump–Mode–Jagers processes
\alpha(T_{n})\sim\varpi[\phi^{\alpha}]
a.s. Crump–Mode–Jagers processes encompass binary search trees,
m
-ary search trees, random recursive trees and preferential attachment trees.
There are a few more graph parameters which are for trees affine functions of the independence number, e.g. vertex cover number, matching number or clique cover number, see
[
Fuchs_Holmgren_Mitsche_Neininger_2021
, Remark 1.7]
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
and a subset
D\subseteq V
of nodes, we write

for the set of neighbors of
D
(including
D
).
The set
D
is called a

if

. The minimal size of a dominating set is called the

, which is also NP-hard on general graphs, see
[
kann
, Theorem A.1]
and
[
Karp_1972
]
.
The dominating number of trees can be computed similarly to the independence number by assigning every subtree a ‘type’, based on which of the inequalities in

\displaystyle D(T)

\displaystyle≤\min\left\{\lvert D\rvert\mid\varepsilon\in D\subseteq T,N(D)=T\right\}

\displaystyle≤\min\left\{\lvert D\rvert\mid D\subseteq T,N(D)\cup\{\varepsilon\}=T\right\}+1≤D(T)+1

(3.33)

is sharp.
If the first inequality is sharp (type F), a minimal dominating set cannot contain the root.
If the third inequality is sharp (type B), it is easier to dominate
T\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
than to dominate
T
.
(It is therefore better to dominate the root from its parent.)
If the second inequality is sharp (type R), neither is the case, and we can choose a dominating set containing the root without penalties, see
[
Fuchs_Holmgren_Mitsche_Neininger_2021
, Proof of Theorem 1.4]
.

Cockayne, Goodman and Hedetniemi
[
Cockayne_Goodman_Hedetniemi_1975
]
showed that the type of a node is determined by the types of its children: Parents of B have type R. If a node has at least one child of type R, but none of type B, it has type F. Else, all children are F and the node has type B.

Furthermore,
[
Cockayne_Goodman_Hedetniemi_1975
]
show that nodes of type R form a minimal set dominating every node except possibly the root.
So for the bounded toll function
\phi^{D}(T)=\mathbf{1}(T\text{ is of type R})
, we have
D(T_{n})-X_{n}(\phi^{D})\in\{0,1\}
and thus we can apply our results.

(Domination number)

For binary search trees and random recursive trees,

.

(

.

If in

3.3
Examples of fractional fringe trees

We close this example section with a few motivating examples for the study of fractional fringe trees.

3.3.1
Number of maxima

A point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d≥1
, is said to
dominate
another point
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
if
x_{i}≥y_{i}
for every
i=1,\dots,d
.
For a set of points
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a point is called a
(coordinatewise) maximum
if no other point in the set dominates it.
The study of these maxima has many applications, e.g. in graph drawing or heuristic search in artificial intelligence. See
[
chen_efficient_2003
]
for a comprehensive overview over applications and algorithms.

A special case is the number of coordinatewise maxima if
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are independently uniformly distributed on the triangle which is the convex hull of
(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
This can be described with a fractional fringe tree:
Consider the point
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})
where
m=x_{0}+y_{0}
is maximal. This point must be a coordinate maximum, and all other points will have
x+y<m
. Using
p_{0}
, we can divide the triangle into four smaller areas as in
Figure

1
:
A strip
x+y>m
, in which no points will be found, two triangles, one with
x>x_{0},x+y<m
and one with
y>y_{0},x+y<m
, in which further maxima will be, and a rectangle
x≤x_{0},y≤y_{0}
, in which the points are dominated by
p_{0}
.

p_{0}
2U(1-U)
U^{2}
(1-U)^{2}

Figure 1
:
The three areas in which the point with maximal sum splits up the triangle.

The important observation now is that the two triangles are scaled-down versions of the original triangle, and a point is a maximum if and only if it is either
p_{0}
or a maximum in either of the triangles. Given
m
, the value
U:=\frac{x_{0}}{m}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
because of symmetry and the other points are uniformly distributed on the convex hull of
(0,0),(0,m),(m,0)
. The sizes of the two smaller triangles are
U^{2}
and
(1-U)^{2}
of this triangle, so the maxima form a fractional split tree with

,
s_{1}=0
and splitter
(U^{2},(1-U)^{2})

(see (

by

.

This exponent of

is well known, in fact for all two-dimensional polygons, the order of the number of maxima can be either constant,

or

[

]
. The distribution is fully described in
[

]
, where the

term can be reduced to this problem on the triangle.

The fringe trees in the tree have an interesting interpretation:
Order all the maxima by their

. This is also the in-order traversal of the tree.
Parent nodes have higher sums than child nodes, so a maximum

is an ancestor of

(analogously for

is the unique longest subsequence in which

is the maximum. The size of the fringe tree can thus be considered the ‘influence’ of the maximum.

Here,

and (

3.3.2
Partial Search in Quadtrees

A (point) quadtree (short for quadrant tree) is a data structure to store a set of points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a way that allows for efficient searches in space.
The structure is build recursively as follows: The first element
x_{1}
is chosen as the
pivot element
and stored in the root of the tree. All other elements are compared coordinate-wise with this pivot and sorted into
2^{d}
sets, labelled by
\{0,1\}^{d}
. An element
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sorted into the set
with label
\big(\mathbf{1}(y_{1}>(x_{1})_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{1}(y_{d}>(x_{1})_{d})\big)
. The procedure is repeated on the
2^{d}
sets, which form the subtrees of the root, until no elements are left.

If we choose
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
i.i.d. uniformly on
[0,1]^{d}
, this forms a split tree with
b=2^{d}
,
s=s_{0}=1
,
s_{1}=0
and splitters

V_{I}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(U_{i}I_{i}+(1-U_{i})(1-I_{i})\right)
for
I\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, with
U_{1},\dots,U_{d}
i.i.d. distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
. If one now wants to find an element
y
, one can compare the pivot with the element and then continue to the set with label
\big(\mathbf{1}(y_{1}>(x_{1})_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{1}(y_{d}>(x_{1})_{d})\big)
until the element is found.

Something interesting happens if we only know a few of the coordinates of the element
y
; without loss of generality assume that we know the first
s<d
coordinates
y_{1},\dots,y_{s}
. This is called a partial search, and we have to continue to the
2^{d-s}
sets with labels
I
for which
I_{i}=\mathbf{1}(y_{i}>(x_{1})_{i})
for
i≤s
. Because the splitting depends on
y
, this is not a fractional split tree, and even if we choose
y
to be uniformly random, it still would not be one because of the branches then depend on each other. However, since the independence of the branches does not affect the mean, we still get the result that

Example 3.12
.

Let
T_{n}
be the nodes visited on a partial search on a
d
-dimensional quadtree, with
s<d
coordinates given and chosen uniformly at random. Let
\phi_{n}
be a toll function with
\phi_{n}=\mathrm{O}\left(n^{β-δ}\right)
for some
δ>0
,
where
0<\beta<1
is the solution to the equation

2^{d}=(β+1)^{d-s}(β+2)^{s}.

(3.41)

Then there exist a constant
\varpi_{d,s}[\phi]>0

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}(\phi)\right]=\varpi_{d,s}[\phi]n^{β}+o(n^{β}).

(3.42)

Proof.

This is a simple application of
Theorem

2.1
, the only thing left to explain is the formula (
3.41
). Because we chose the coordinate uniformly, the splitter of a branch is of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{s}\widehat{U}_{i}\prod_{i=s+1}^{d}U_{i}
, where
\widehat{U}_{i}
is the size-biased version of
U_{i}
. The reason for the size biasing is that the probability that a uniformly chosen coordinate falls into an interval scales linearly with the size of the interval. When the interval has size
U_{i}\sim B(1,1)
, that means that the interval a random coordinate is in has size
\widehat{U}_{i}\sim B(2,1)
. The equation for
\beta
in (

) then becomes

2^{s-d}=\mathbb{E}\left[V^{β}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U^{β}\right]^{d-s}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{U}^{β}\right]^{s}=\left(β+1\right)^{s-d}{\left(\frac{2}{β+2}\right)}^{s}.

(3.43)

Dividing both sides by
2^{s}
and inverting gives (
3.41
).
∎

In the case where
\phi_{n}=1
is the toll function counting nodes, this has
been shown by
[
Chern_Hwang_2003
]
, with the constant
\varpi_{d,s}[1]

explicitly given and the error term improved to
\mathrm{O}\left(1+n^{\Re β_{2}}\right)
, where

β_{2}
is the complex solution to (
3.41
) with the
second-largest real part (
β
itself having the largest real part).

For partial search,
\sum_{I\in\{0,1\}^{d-s}}V_{I}^{β}
is random, so we expect that
X_{n}(\phi)
has variance of order
n^{2β}
. However, we cannot apply
Theorem

2.2
because the branches are dependent if the coordinates
y_{1},\dots,y_{s}
are chosen at random.
An alternative approach to taking random coordinates
y_{1},\dots,y_{s}
is to consider instead the function mapping
(y_{1},\dots,y_{s})
to the number of nodes. This was done by Broutin, Neininger and Sulzbach
[
Broutin_Neininger_Sulzbach_2013
,
broutin_partial_2012
]
, who show that the variance is indeed of order

and this process converges when scaled down by
n^{β}
.

3.3.3
Random Disk Triangulation

[
Curien_LeGall_2011
]
studied a random recursive triangulation of the closed unit disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\lvert z\rvert≤1\}
as follows:

•

Start with a empty set
L_{0}:=\emptyset

•

In the
n
-th step, draw two random elements uniformly
x,y
from the unit circle
S^{1}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\lvert z\rvert=1\}
independently.

•

If the chord
\overline{xy}:=\{λx+(1-λ)y\mid λ\in[0,1]\}
does not intersect with any chord already in
L_{n-1}
, then
L_{n}:=L_{n-1}\cup\overline{xy}
, else
L_{n}:=L_{n-1}
.

If we continue this procedure infinitely to some closed set
L_{\infty}:=\overline{\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{n}}
, we obtain an infinite triangulation of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, which is where the name of this construction comes from.

The chords in
L_{n}
can also be described using a split tree. Let
\overline{x_{1}y_{1}},\dots,\overline{x_{n}y_{n}}
be the chords that are randomly drawn in each step.
When the first chord
\overline{x_{1}y_{1}}
is added, it splits
S^{1}
into two segments,
with relative length
U
and
1-U
, where
U\sim\unif[0,1]
, and every subsequent chord must have both end points in the same segment, or it would intersect with
\overline{x_{1}y_{1}}
. Since the points are drawn uniformly, each chord has a
U^{2}
chance to have both points in the first segment and a
(1-U)^{2}
chance to have both in the second segment. All other chords are discarded. The chords on the two segments now behave independently and in the same way as on
S^{1}
, so we have a fractional split tree with splitters
(U^{2},(1-U)^{2})
,
s_{0}=s=1
and
s_{1}=0
. This is the same fractional split tree as in the number of maxima from
Section

3.3.1
.

In this section, we prove

For

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m+n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m}\right]+\mathrm{O}\left(n\right).

(4.1)

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement for

Adding a ball to an internal node

or the new ball disappears. Since

is constant for

is bounded by a constant.

Let

Lemma 4.2

In the nonlattice case, as

.

.

To show (
