FAP-derived CCL2 is required for adequate monocyte/macrophage infiltration to support acute skeletal muscle injury repair
Authors: Xingyu Wang, Yinhang Wang, Ruoxi Yuan, Gregory R Smith, Frederique Ruf-Zamojski, Sachiko T Homma, Stuart C Sealfon, Lan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRepair of acutely injured skeletal muscle relies on an adequate inflammatory response predominated by monocyte/macrophage infiltration. The process requires injured muscles to produce C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2). The present study identified fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) as the primary source of CCL2 in acutely injured muscle, where the pro-inflammatory subcluster of FAPs expanded rapidly and expressed the highest level of CCL2. FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 largely abolished CCL2 production by acutely injured muscle, reducing monocyte/macrophage infiltration and impairing muscle regeneration. In vitro, the CCL2 expression by both mouse and human FAPs was induced by danger signal-containing muscle homogenates through Toll-like receptor signaling. The CCL2 expression by mouse FAPs was also induced by infiltrating neutrophils, partly through their secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Our findings suggest an important immune sentinel role for FAPs, as they sense muscle damage, produce CCL2 to recruit inflammatory monocytes, and promote injury repair.Keywords: CCL2, FAPs, monocytes and macrophages, acute muscle injury, muscle regeneration

Introduction

Skeletal muscle can repair acute injury with no or limited residual damage 2–3 weeks after injury, unless the injury is repetitive1 or the muscle volume loss is large.2,3 Muscle injury repair is a highly coordinated process, involving inflammation, muscle regeneration, revascularization, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling.4,5,6 Muscle regeneration relies on the activation and differentiation of muscle satellite cells (MuSCs).7,8,9 An adequate inflammatory response, triggered by injury and predominated by neutrophil and macrophage (MP) infiltration, is also essential to support injury repair.4,10,11,12

Infiltrating MPs in acutely injured skeletal muscle are differentiated from bone marrow-derived blood monocytes (MOs).13,14 Blood MOs consist of two different subsets in mice: Ly6ChiCCR2+ and Ly6CloCCR2− MOs.15 Injured skeletal muscle recruits Ly6Chi15 but not Ly6Clo16 MOs through CC chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) chemotaxis signaling.14,17,18,19 Once in injured muscle, the Ly6Chi MOs differentiate into Ly6Chi inflammatory MPs, which later switch into Ly6Clo MPs after phagocytosing necrotic muscle debris.14,20,21,22,23,24 During acute muscle injury repair, infiltrating MPs of different activation status actively regulate inflammation, muscle regeneration, and ECM remodeling in a spatiotemporal manner.12 We and others have shown that deficiency of CCR2 leads to diminished MO/MP infiltration and poor muscle regeneration after acute injury.17,18 We have also reported that C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) is the major ligand of CCR2 that mediates the recruitment of inflammatory MOs into acutely injured muscle, and that the CCL2 expression by injured muscle is required for this process.25 Expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines by fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) as early as 12 h after acute muscle injury has been reported.26 However, the primary cellular source at earlier time points before MO/MP infiltration, the mechanisms of CCL2 upregulation in FAPs, and the role of FAP-derived CCL2 in the initiation of MP infiltration have not been well studied.

By using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), the present study identified FAPs as the primary cellular source of CCL2 in skeletal muscle very early after acute injury, before the massive MO/MP infiltration. Sub-clustering of FAPs showed that these cells were heterogeneous, consisting of transcriptomically diverse subclusters in both un-injured and injured muscles. The pro-inflammatory subcluster of FAPs expanded rapidly after acute injury, and it was the major subcluster that expressed Ccl2. FAP-specific deletion of the Ccl2 gene, achieved in PdgfraCre-Ccl2flox/flox mice, significantly impaired MO/MP infiltration, leading to poor muscle regeneration and excessive fatty tissue deposition. In vitro, both mouse and human FAPs responded to damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from damaged skeletal muscle to produce CCL2 in a MyD88/TRIF-dependent manner, suggesting a sentinel role for FAPs. Infiltrating neutrophils isolated from muscles 9 h post-acute injury also stimulated the CCL2 expression by FAPs, with the response significantly reduced when the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) gene was deleted from FAPs. Neutrophils are the major cells that express IL-1β shortly after acute muscle injury. Our findings strongly suggest that FAPs are important immune sentinel cells, sensing muscle damage and producing CCL2 to recruit MOs, and that infiltrating neutrophils might also contribute to the stimulation of CCL2 expression by FAPs for the MO/MP infiltration in acutely injured skeletal muscle to support injury repair.

Results

FAP is the primary source of CCL2 early after BaCl2-induced acute skeletal muscle injury

To explore the cellular source of CCL2 in skeletal muscle early after acute injury, single-cell suspension prepared from quadriceps 2 h, 9 h, and 24 h after intramuscular injection of BaCl2 and from un-injured quadriceps were subjected to scRNA-seq analysis. Each sample was pooled from 5 mice to minimize individual variations. scRNA-seq was then performed with all samples simultaneously (see Table S1 for quality control). By filtering out the cells of low quality, we obtained 19,442 genes from 6,545 cells of un-injured quadriceps, 20,296 genes from 5,729 cells of quadriceps 2 h post-injury, 19,826 genes from 8,790 cells of quadriceps 9 h post-injury, and 19,308 genes from 7,363 cells of quadriceps 24 h post-injury. Functionally enriched cell clusters were generated by Uniform Mani-fold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimension reduction and were identified by the expression of cell type-specific marker genes (Figures 1A and S1A). A total of nine clusters were identified, including two MO/MP clusters (clusters 1 and 5), two neutrophil clusters (clusters 2 and 3), one FAP cluster (cluster 4), one MuSC cluster (cluster 9), one endothelial cell (EC) cluster (cluster 6), and one pericyte cluster (cluster 7). Cluster 8 was a mixture of T/B/NK cells. Majority of the cells were FAPs, neutrophils, and MOs/MPs, among which FAPs and neutrophils significantly expanded as early as 2 h post-injury, while MOs/MPs significantly expanded 9 h post-injury (Figures 1A and S1B). We next assessed the expression of Ccl2 gene by different cell types with feature plot (Figure 1B). The Ccl2 expression was barely detectable in un-injured quadriceps while was markedly increased in injured ones. At 2 h and 9 h post-injury, Ccl2 was expressed at the highest level by FAPs and at a much lower level by MOs/MPs and neutrophils. At 24 h post-injury, MOs/MPs became the predominant cells that expressed Ccl2 (Figure 1B). Likewise, flow cytometry analysis (FACS) of CCL2 protein production showed that only FAPs significantly expressed CCL2 in quadriceps at 9 h post-injury (Figures 1C and S2). These findings indicate that FAPs are the primary source of CCL2 at early time points (2 h and 9 h post-injury) before the massive MO/MP infiltration (Figure 1).

The pro-inflammatory subcluster of FAPs expand and express the highest level of Ccl2 early after BaCl2-induced acute skeletal muscle injury

FAPs are bi-potent muscle interstitial mesenchymal progenitors that can differentiate into fibroblasts and adipocytes.27,28,29 They play important roles in skeletal muscle injury and repair.29,30 Upon injury, they become activated, expand rapidly,27,31,32 and provide a favorable tissue environment to promote satellite cell-mediated muscle regeneration.27,33,34,35,36 We further studied FAP expansion at early time points after BaCl2-induced acute muscle injury by flow cytometry (FACS). Intramuscular FAPs were identified as CD31−CD45−Sca-1+PDGFRα+ cells27,28,29 (Figure S2). FACS analysis of single-cell suspension prepared from un-injured and injured quadriceps showed that the density of intramuscular FAPs increased significantly, approximately 10 times higher in injured quadriceps at both 12 h and 24 h post-injury than in un-injured quadriceps (Figure 2A). The finding was confirmed by PDGFRα immunostaining with quantification and comparison of PDGFRα+ cells in un-injured and injured quadriceps (Figures 2B and 2C). However, EdU-incorporation assay showed no active proliferation of intramuscular FAPs within 24 h post-injury (Figure 2D), suggesting an extramuscular source of FAP infiltration early after acute skeletal muscle injury. This is consistent with a recent study by Sastourné-Arrey et al. showing that no FAP proliferation was observed up to 1 day post-injury in glycerol- and cardio toxin-induced acute muscle injury models.37 The authors also showed evidence to suggest that the FAP-like cells, which were the adipose stromal cells residing in adjacent subcutaneous adipose tissue, might migrate to contribute to the FAP expansion in injured muscle at the early stage after injury.37

We and others have previously reported that intramuscular FAPs are highly heterogeneous, containing transcriptomically diverse subclusters at both steady state and disease state.29,38,39,40,41,42,43,44 The pro-inflammatory subcluster that features a differentially high expression of Ccl2 expanded in chronically injured dystrophic muscles.44 Here, we addressed whether this was also the case in acutely injured muscle. To this end, the transcriptome data of FAPs was selected for sub-cluster analysis. With data pooled from all four samples, five subclusters with distinct transcriptomes were identified: (1) Dpp4+ adventitial subcluster, featuring high expressions of Dpp4, Pi16, Sbsn, and Ly6c1; (2) parenchymal subcluster, featuring high expressions of Col15a1, Abca8a, Smoc2, and Col4a1; (3) pro-inflammatory subcluster, featuring high expressions of chemokine genes (Ccl2, Ccl7, Cxcl1, and Cxcl5) that regulate chemotaxis of neutrophils and MOs/MPs; (4) Stressed subcluster, featuring high expressions of Mt1, Serpina3n, Ldha, and Cxcl14; (5) Tenocytes-like subcluster, featuring high expressions of Tnmd, Fmod, Scx, and Clip2 (Figures 3A and S3; Table S2). Among these, the pro-inflammatory subcluster continuously increased its fraction and became the predominant FAP subset at both 9 h and 24 h post-injury (Figure 3B). Importantly, this subcluster upregulated Ccl2 expression in injured muscles compared to un-injured muscles, and it also expressed the highest level of Ccl2 compared to the other FAP subclusters in injured muscles at various early time points (Figure 3C). Similarly, the pro-inflammatory FAP subcluster also expressed the highest level of Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 (Figure S4), suggesting that this FAP subcluster might also play a role in recruiting neutrophils after acute muscle injury. Moreover, this subcluster expressed the highest level of Pdpn at 9 h and 24 h post-injury (Figure S4). Pdpn encodes podoplanin, which was reported to confer a pro-migratory phenotype on mesenchymal progenitor cells, facilitating their intravasation.45 The high Pdpn expression by the pro-inflammatory FAPs suggests that this subtype of FAPs might migrate from an extramuscular source upon injury, which was also suggested by Sastourné-Arrey et al.37 Future studies are needed to further address this possibility.

Senescent FAPs in aged skeletal muscle have been shown to markedly increase the expression of CCL2 compared with non-senescent FAPs,46,47 raising the question of whether the pro-inflammatory FAP subcluster, which was the predominant one that expresses Ccl2, is of senescent state. Cell senescence is a stable cell-cycle arrest that can be triggered in normal cells in response to various intrinsic and extrinsic stimuli, in which the activation of the p21WAF1/CIP1 and p16INK4A pathways plays a central role.48 Our scRNA-seq data showed that the pro-inflammatory FAP subcluster did not express Cdkn2a (encoding p16INK4A), and the expression level of Cdkn1a (encoding p21WAF1/CIP1) in this subcluster was comparable to that in the other FAP subclusters (Figure S4). Therefore, the CCL2 production by FAPs in non-aged, acutely injured muscle does not appear to be linked to cell senescence.

Corresponding to the expansion of pro-inflammatory FAPs and their high Ccl2 expression at 9 h and 24 h post-injury, intramuscular MOs/MPs changed their subcluster composition at these time points (Figure S5). With transcriptome data of MO/MP cell population pooled from all four samples, five MO/MP subclusters were identified (Figure S5A; Table S3), among which, the Lyve1+ and MHCIIhi subclusters resembled Timd4+ and/or Lyve1+ and/or Folr2+ (TLF+) and MHCIIhi resident MP clusters, respectively.49,50,51,52 We also identified one subcluster featuring genes of MOs (Plac8, Ly6c2, and Lyz2), one pro-inflammatory MP subcluster featuring a high-level expression of pro-inflammatory genes (Cxcl2, Il1b, and Ccl2), and one phagocytotic MP subcluster featuring a high-level expression of genes involved in phagocytosis/lysosome process (Lgmn, Ctsd, Ctsb, and Mrc1). In un-injured muscle, the Lyve1+ and MHCIIhi subclusters predominated, along with a minor fraction of MOs. After injury, the MO subcluster began to increase slightly at 2 h post-injury, when the two resident MP subclusters still predominated at this stage. The pro-inflammatory and phagocytotic subclusters began to emerge at 9 h post-injury. At 24 h post-injury, the MO, pro-inflammatory, and phagocytotic subclusters became the majority (Figure S5B). The early expansion and high production of CCL2 suggest that the pro-inflammatory FAPs may play an important role in initiating and promoting the recruitment of MOs/MPs.

FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 gene greatly reduces CCL2 production in acutely injured skeletal muscle

To further address the contribution of FAPs to CCL2 production, we generated PdgfraCre+-Ccl2flox/flox mice by crossing PdgfraCre+ transgenic mice with Ccl2flox/flox knock-in mice (Figure 4A). PdgfraCre−-Ccl2flox/flox littermates were used as controls. To assess the efficiency of Ccl2 gene deletion, we sorted FAPs and non-FAP intramuscular cells (nFAPs) from quadriceps of both PdgfraCre+-Ccl2flox/flox (Cre+) mice and PdgfraCre−-Ccl2flox/flox (Cre−) littermate controls at 9 h post-BaCl2-induced acute injury. The Ccl2 expression by FAPs and nFAPs was then determined by quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. As shown in Figure 4B, the Ccl2 mRNA level in Cre+ FAPs was reduced approximately 7-fold compared with that in Cre− FAPs, indicating high efficiency of Ccl2 deletion in Cre+ FAPs. Meanwhile, Ccl2 mRNA was barely detectable in either Cre+ or Cre− nFAPs (Figure 4B), indicating that FAPs were the major cell type that expressed Ccl2. FACS analysis of intramuscular FAPs at 9 h post-injury showed markedly reduced CCL2 protein expression in Cre+ FAPs compared with Cre− controls (Figure 4C), further confirming the high efficiency of Ccl2 deletion in Cre+ FAPs. At a whole-muscle level, both Ccl2 mRNA and CCL2 protein expressions were largely diminished in Cre+ muscles as assessed by qRT-PCR and ELISA, respectively, at both 9 h and 24 h post-injury (Figures 4D and 4E). Therefore, FAP is the major contributor of CCL2 in injured muscle shortly after acute injury.

FAP-specific deletion of the Ccl2 gene reduces MOs/MPs infiltration after acute skeletal muscle injury

We next addressed whether the FAP-derived CCL2 is critical for the recruitment of MOs/MPs by acutely injured muscle. To this end, we performed FACS analysis to quantify the intramuscular density of Ly6Chi, Ly6Clo, and total MOs/MPs in both Cre+ and Cre− quadriceps at days 0.6, 1, 3, 7, and 14 post-injury (Figures 5A and 5B). Compared with Cre− controls, the density of Ly6Chi MOs/MPs was significantly reduced in Cre+ quadriceps at days 0.6, 1, and 3 post-injury, while the density of Ly6Clo MPs was significantly reduced in Cre+ quadriceps at days 1 and 3 post-injury (Figure 5B). The largest reduction was observed at day 3 post-injury, when the peak of MP infiltration was reached in Cre− controls (Figure 5B). Despite the slightly higher percentage of Ly6Chi MPs observed in Cre+ than in Cre− quadriceps at day 3 post-injury, the intramuscular density of Ly6Chi MPs was significantly lower in Cre+ than in Cre− quadriceps, because the number of total MPs was greatly reduced in Cre+ muscles. Neither Ly6Chi nor Ly6Clo MOs/MPs showed difference in density between Cre+ and Cre− quadriceps at days 0, 7, or 14, the time points that represent before or after MO/MP infiltration. CD68 immunostaining at day 3 further confirmed the significant reduction of MOs/MPs in Cre+ quadriceps compared with Cre− controls (Figures 5C and 5D). As bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) also express PDGFRα53,54 and have been shown to promote myeloid cell differentiation via CCL2/CCR2 signaling,55 this may affect the fraction of MOs in bone marrow to account for the changes in MOs/MPs infiltration in injured muscle. To address this potential confounding factor, we compared the fraction of MOs in the bone marrow of Cre+ and Cre− mice by FACS, and no significant difference was detected (Figure S6A), indicating that the reduction in MO/MP muscle infiltration in Cre+ mice was unlikely to be caused by the difference in MO differentiation in bone marrow. Together, these findings indicate that the FAP-derived CCL2 is required for adequate MO/MP infiltration after acute skeletal muscle injury.

MOs/MPs express a broad panel of genes that regulate the inflammatory response during acute skeletal muscle injury repair.12,56 We previously reported that MOs/MPs expressed a higher level of Arg1, Tnf, Il1b, and Il6 at day 1 while a higher level of Il10, Igf1, and Tgfb1 at day 3 post-injury.56 We then addressed whether the whole-muscle expression of these genes was affected by the FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 with resultant reduced MO/MP infiltration. The expression of Arg1 at day 1 and the expression of Il10, Igf1, and Tgfb1 at day 3 post-injury were significantly reduced in Cre+ quadriceps compared with Cre− controls (Figure 5E), suggesting that MOs/MPs are the primary cells that express these genes. In contrast, the expression of Tnf, Il1b, and Il6 at day 1 post-injury was not different between Cre+ and Cre− quadriceps (Figure S6B), suggesting compensatory expressions of these genes by other cell types. Indeed, feature plots of scRNA-seq data showed a high-level expression of Tnf and Il1b by neutrophils and a high-level expression of Il6 by FAPs (Figure S6C). The accumulation of FAPs was not affected by the FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 (Figure S6D). The neutrophil accumulation, however, was increased at days 1 and 3 post-injury in Cre+ quadriceps (Figure S6D), which was likely caused by insufficient phagocytotic clearance of neutrophils due to reduced infiltrating phagocytotic MPs.57,58

FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 gene impairs acute skeletal muscle injury repair

Adequate MO/MP infiltration is required for muscle regeneration after acute injury.4,10,11,12,57 We next determined whether the reduced MO/MP infiltration caused by FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 would impair regeneration of injured muscle. For this purpose, we did H&E staining of quadriceps at day 21 post-injury. Myofibers in both Cre+ and Cre− quadriceps at this stage were central-nucleated and they were regenerated fibers (Figure 6A). However, the cross-sectional area (CSA) was significantly reduced and the fraction of small-sized myofibers was significantly increased in Cre+ quadriceps compared with Cre− controls (Figure 6B), indicating impaired myofiber regeneration caused by the FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2. Previous studies, including ours, have reported critical roles of MP-derived IGF-118,59,60 and GDF361,62 in myofiber regeneration. We found that the expression of both Igf1 and Gdf3 mRNA was significantly reduced in Cre+ quadriceps compared with Cre− controls at day 3 post-injury (Figure 6C), corresponding to the largest reduction of MO/MP density at this stage (Figure 5B). Thus, the lack of FAP-derived CCL2 does lead to decreased expression of Igf1 and Gdf3, which is most likely through reduced MO/MP infiltration, and this might contribute to the impaired muscle regeneration. In addition, the fatty tissue deposition was significantly increased in Cre+ quadriceps at day 21 post-injury as shown by H&E staining (Figure 6A), Sudan Black B staining (Figure 6D), and quantification of Sudan Black B staining signals (Figure 6E). But the fibrous tissue deposition, as assessed by collagen III immunostaining, was not different (Figure S7). Therefore, the lack of FAP-derived CCL2 leads to increased adipogenesis, but not fibrosis, in BaCl2-induced acute muscle injury.

As FAPs can undergo both adipogenic and fibrogenic differentiation,27,28,29 they might contribute to the increased fat deposition in the absence of FAP-derived CCL2. FAPs were identified as CD31−CD45−Sca-1+PDGFRα+ cells.27,28,29 Interestingly, the expression of PDGFRα by CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells in acutely injured quadriceps of wild-type (WT) mice decreased over time and was lost at day 7 post-injury (Figure S8), accompanying differentiation of FAPs at the late stage of acute muscle injury repair. The intramuscular density of CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells increased upon acute injury, peaked at day 7 post-injury, and receded afterward in both Cre+ and Cre− quadriceps (Figure 6F). Notably, a significant increase in the density of CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells was observed in Cre+ quadriceps compared with Cre− controls at day 7 post-injury (Figure 6F). A similar increase was also observed at day 14 post-injury but was not statistically significant (Figure 6F). These findings suggest a possible change in FAP activation and differentiation after acute muscle injury in the absence of FAP-derived CCL2. We then addressed whether the FAP-specific Ccl2 deletion could lead to adipogenic-biased differentiation of FAPs. To this end, the CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells and the other intramuscular cells were sorted from both Cre+ and Cre− quadriceps by FACS at days 7, 14, and 21 post-injury and then subjected to qRT-PCR analysis of expression of the genes reportedly involved in adipogenesis (Figure 6G). For convenience, CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells were labeled as FAPs and the other intramuscular cells were labeled as nFAPs in Figure 6G. Pro-adipogenic genes, Lep63 and Mmp14,64,65 were both highly expressed by CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells but barely by non-CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells. Their expression both peaked at day 14 post-injury and were both significantly higher in Cre+ than in Cre− cells (Figure 6G). GDF-10 has been shown to suppress adipogenesis,66 including in dystrophic muscle.43 Gdf10 was exclusively expressed by CD31−CD45−Sca-1+ cells with the level peaked at day 14 post-injury. However, the expression of this gene was significantly lower in Cre+ than in Cre− cells (Figure 6G). Taken together, these findings do seem to suggest an adipogenic-biased differentiation of FAPs in association with FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2, which might contribute to the increased fatty tissue deposition observed in injured muscle of PdgfraCre+-Ccl2flox/flox mice.

FAP production of CCL2 can be stimulated by danger signal released from injured muscle and by infiltrating neutrophils derived from acutely injured skeletal muscle

Tissue injury releases danger signals, including endogenous components that are released by necrotic cells and ECM breakdown, which are called DAMPs. The cells recognizing DAMPs through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) are considered sentinel cells. Binding of DAMPs to PRRs triggers activation of intracellular signaling pathways that upregulate pro-inflammatory chemokines and cytokines to initiate inflammation.67,68,69,70,71 To date, multiple types of DMAPs and PRRs that are involved in inflammation initiation have been identified,67,68,69,70 among which the Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are the best studied PRRs, and their signaling pathways appear the most critical to tissue inflammation.68,70 The early upregulation of Ccl2 expression by FAPs (within 2 h post-injury; Figure 1B) upon muscle injury suggests that FAPs may function as immune sentinel cells, sensing DAMPs released by injured muscle to express pro-inflammatory factors including CCL2. Indeed, FACS analysis showed that FAPs expressed multiple TLRs on the cell surface at both steady state (un-injured) and 9 h post-injury (Figure S9), which could enable FAPs to be activated by DAMPs/TLRs signaling. In addition, the expression of chemokines can also be stimulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines, in particular IL-1β,67,72,73,74,75,76 which was highly expressed by infiltrating neutrophils in injured skeletal muscle (Figure S6C). CellChat analysis of scRNA-seq data at 9 h post-injury showed that neutrophils indeed interacted with FAPs through Il1b-Il1r1 pair (Figure S10). Therefore, FAPs might also be stimulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines produced by infiltrated neutrophils to express CCL2 in injured muscle for subsequent recruitment of MOs/MPs.

To test these hypotheses, we isolated FAPs from steady-state limb muscles of WT mice and cultured them in vitro. FAPs were either left un-treated (UT) or stimulated with DAMP prepared from WT limb muscles, TLR4 ligand lipopolysaccharide (LPS), infiltrating neutrophils (at ratio 1:1) isolated from quadriceps 9 h post-injury, TNF-α, or IL-1β. Conditioned media were collected at 2 h, 6 h, and 18 h post-treatment and then subjected to CCL2 ELISA. As shown in Figures 7A and S11A, DAMP, LPS, neutrophils, TNF-α, and IL-1β all strongly stimulated FAP production of CCL2 at all time points. The findings suggest that FAPs can not only sentinel danger signals released by injured muscle but also interact with infiltrating neutrophils to produce CCL2 early after acute skeletal muscle injury. We next determined whether FAPs sensed DAMPs through TLR signaling. All TLRs signal through MyD88, except TLR3, which signals through TRIF, and TLR4 can signal through both MyD88 and TRIF.71,77,78,79 FAPs isolated from limb muscles of both WT and PdgfraCre+-Myd88flox/flox (Myd88−/−) mice were cultured in vitro and stimulated with either DAMPs or LPS in the absence or presence of TRIF inhibitor. Conditioned media were then collected at 6 h for CCL2 ELSIA. DAMPs or LPS greatly stimulated CCL2 production, which was suppressed partially by the deficiency of MyD88 or the inhibition of TRIF and almost completely by the combination (Figures 7B and S11B). In a similar setting, FAPs isolated from healthy human skeletal muscle also upregulated CCL2 when stimulated with either LPS or DAMP prepared from the same healthy human muscle (Figure S12), and the stimulation was completely abolished by combined inhibition of both MyD88 and TRIF (Figure S12). These findings indicate that both MyD88- and TRIF-dependent downstream signaling appear involved in DAMPs-stimulated CCL2 production by FAPs, suggesting a requirement of TLR signaling. We next addressed whether infiltrating neutrophils stimulated FAPs to produce CCL2 through IL-1 signaling. To this end, FAPs were isolated from limb muscles of both WT and PdgfraCre+-Il1r1flox/flox (Il1r1−/−) mice where the IL-1R gene (Il1r1) was deleted from FAPs. These cells were stimulated in vitro with either WT neutrophils or IL-1β for 6 h. Both IL-1β and neutrophils stimulated the CCL2 production by FAPs. The IL-1R deficiency in FAPs blocked the responses, especially the one that was stimulated by IL-1β (Figures 7C and S11C). The findings indicate that IL-1 might mediate, in part, the neutrophil-stimulated CCL2 production by FAPs. Together, our findings support an immune sentinel function for FAPs, sensing danger signals released from damaged muscle, interacting with infiltrating neutrophils, and producing CCL2 to initiate and promote MO/MP infiltration.

Discussion

Acute skeletal muscle injury induces a robust inflammatory response featured by early neutrophil infiltration and subsequent massive MO/MP infiltration to support muscle regeneration.10,11,12 CCR2-mediated MO/MP infiltration is critical for muscle injury repair, as MPs produce myotrophic cytokines, including IGF-1, to promote muscle regeneration.17,18,59,60 Production of CCL2, a major ligand of CCR2, by injured muscle is required for the adequate recruitment of MOs/MPs and proper repair of injury.25 However, the critical cellular source and the stimulating signals of CCL2 production in injured muscle were not well studied. By combining scRNA-seq analysis, FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2, and in vitro stimulation of FAPs, our present study has generated several findings that broaden and deepen the current knowledge of the inflammatory response induced by acute skeletal muscle injury and suggest an important immune sentinel role for FAPs in this response.

First, our study has identified FAPs as the primary cellular source of CCL2 early after acute injury. Previously published scRNA-seq data in acute muscle injury did show that FAPs expressed Ccl2 early after injury.26,38,39,80 However, the relative contribution of FAPs to total CCL2 produced by injured muscle and its role in recruiting MOs/MPs were not well addressed. By combining scRNA-seq, FACS, and FAP-specific Ccl2 deletion, we demonstrate that although both FAPs and MOs/MPs significantly express the Ccl2 gene, FAPs are the primary source of CCL2 early after acute injury, before the massive MO/MP infiltration. Moreover, the FAP-derived CCL2 is critical to the initiation and promotion of an adequate inflammatory response to support skeletal muscle injury repair, as deletion of Ccl2 from FAPs dampens MO/MP infiltration and impairs muscle regeneration.

FAPs are highly plastic, containing functionally diverse subclusters in both un-injured and injured skeletal muscles.26,38,39,44,80 Our previous study has shown that the fraction of the pro-inflammatory FAP subcluster, which expresses a higher level of Ccl2 than the other subclusters, increases dramatically in chronically injured dystrophic muscles.44 Our present study demonstrates that the pro-inflammatory subcluster of FAPs also expands greatly in acutely injured muscle, and it becomes the predominant FAPs 9 h post-injury, before massive MO/MP infiltration, with exceptionally high-level expression of Ccl2. Therefore, this subcluster of FAPs is the major cellular source of CCL2 early after acute injury. Interestingly, although the pro-inflammatory FAPs, as well as the total FAPs, expand significantly early after acute muscle injury, no proliferation is detected in these cells, suggesting an extramuscular source. In support of this notion, we detected high expression of Pdpn by the pro-inflammatory FAPs, and podoplanin encoded by Pdpn has been reported to confer a pro-migratory phenotype on mesenchymal progenitor cells.45 Our findings are consistent with a published study suggesting that the intramuscular accumulation of FAPs after acute injury might be achieved by migration of FAP-like adipose stromal cells residing in adjacent subcutaneous adipose tissue.37 The signaling mechanism underlying this FAP recruitment, however, remains elusive.

Second, our study suggests an important immune sentinel role for FAPs in acutely injured skeletal muscle. DAMPs released by injured tissue are sensed by PRRs, especially TLRs, that are expressed by sentinel cells, which in turn activate intracellular signaling pathways to upregulate pro-inflammatory chemokines and cytokines to initiate inflammation.67,68,69,70,71 Sentinel cells are critical regulators of inflammatory response and therefore can be potentially targeted for therapeutic manipulation of inflammation. Our study shows that FAPs in both un-injured and injured skeletal muscles express multiple TLRs. Importantly, DAMPs prepared from both mouse and human skeletal muscle can stimulate FAPs in vitro to produce CCL2, and the stimulation is TLR signaling-dependent, as targeting both MyD88 and TRIF signaling in FAPs blocks the stimulation. Our findings strongly suggest an immune sentinel role for FAPs. Future in vivo study targeting MyD88 and TRIF signaling specifically in FAPs is needed to further address the sentinel role of FAPs in acutely injured muscle.

Third, FAPs can not only sense DAMPs released by injured muscle but also interact with infiltrating neutrophils to produce CCL2 for MO/MP recruitment. Neutrophils are the earliest inflammatory cells that infiltrate injured muscle.10 Infiltrating neutrophils phagocytose damaged muscle debris and release reactive oxygen species (ROS), protease, and inflammatory cytokines to promote inflammation.10,11,81 Consistently, our scRNA-seq analysis shows that neutrophils not only expand before MOs/MPs accumulation but also are the major cell type that expresses Il1b and Tnf in acutely injured muscle. Both IL-1β and TNF-α have been reported as strong stimulants of CCL2 production.75,76,82 Our in vitro co-culture experiment also shows that the infiltrating neutrophils derived from acutely injured muscles can stimulate the CCL2 production by FAPs, and this stimulation depends partially on the IL-1R signaling, indicating an involvement of IL-1. It is thus likely that by secreting pro-inflammatory factors including IL-1β, earlier infiltrating neutrophils might stimulate the FAP production of CCL2 to promote subsequent MO/MP infiltration. However, whether infiltrating neutrophils are required or dispensable for sufficient CCL2 production by FAPs in acutely injured muscle needs to be further determined. FAPs, especially the pro-inflammatory subtype, might also play a role in neutrophil infiltration, as they are also the primary cellular source of several chemokines involved in neutrophil recruitment, including CXCL1 and CXCLl5. However, a recent study reported a critical role for platelets in neutrophil infiltration in acutely injured skeletal muscle.83 Whether the FAP-derived CXCL1 and CXCL5 contribute to adequate neutrophil infiltration after acute skeletal muscle injury needs to be further investigated.

Our findings are in line with the study by Yaghi et al., which described induction of an inflammatory subtype of intramuscular MSCs (encompassing FAPs) at 0.5 and 1 day after cardiotoxin-induced acute skeletal muscle injury.26 This subtype is highly similar to the pro-inflammatory FAP subcluster identified in our current study, expressing a differentially high-level of MP and neutrophil chemokines including Ccl2, Cxcl1, and Cxcl5.26 The authors have suggested that the inflammatory subtype is induced by IL-1β, TNFα and oncostatin M, which are highly expressed by myeloid cells at the time points analyzed.26 However, the authors have also shown that myeloid cells are dispensable for induction of the inflammatory subtype of MSCs, suggesting the existence of the other signaling mechanism driving the inflammatory subtype in injured muscle.26 Our data suggest that neutrophils might stimulate the pro-inflammatory activation of FAPs through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNFα and IL-1β. Our data further suggest that the immune sentinel role of FAPs might underlie the myeloid cell-independent induction of the inflammatory subtype of FAPs, particularly at very early time points, prior to MO/MP infiltration.

Fourth, our study suggests that the deletion of Ccl2 from FAPs might cause an adipogenic-biased differentiation of FAPs based on their upregulation of the Lep and Mmp14 genes, which promote adipogenic differentiation,63,64,65 and downregulation of the Gdf10 gene, which suppresses adipogenic differentiation.43,66 This might contribute to the observed increase in fatty tissue deposition in regenerated muscle. The mechanism underlying this is not entirely clear. As FAPs do not express the CCL2 receptor, CCR2, which is predominantly expressed by MOs and MPs, there is no direct CCL2/CCR2 signaling in FAPs. The adipogenic-biased differentiation of FAPs seems most likely driven by changes in MPs and impaired muscle regeneration that are caused by insufficient MO/MP infiltration. Poor muscle regeneration is often accompanied by fibrofatty tissue infiltration, and in our model, the main change is increased fatty tissue but not fibrous tissue infiltration.

Overall, our findings suggest an important immune sentinel role for FAPs in initiating the inflammatory response after acute skeletal muscle injury. They can sense danger signals released by damaged muscle tissue through TLRs-mediated signaling to produce chemokines to recruit MOs/MPs. The early infiltrating neutrophils produce a high level of pro-inflammatory cytokines including Il1b, which might enhance the FAP production of CCL2 to promote subsequent robust MO/MP infiltration. In addition to the well-reported role of FAPs as stromal cells providing a favorable tissue environment to promote satellite cell-mediated muscle regeneration,27,33,34,35,36 our present study has suggested a new role for FAPs in supporting muscle injury repair by initiating and promoting the inflammatory response predominated by MP infiltration. Mesenchymal cells as a source of CCL2 to recruit MOs/MPs and promote injury repair appear not limited to skeletal muscle, as Whelan et al. reported previously in a mouse skin wound model that the MSC-derived CCL2 is required for accelerating wound healing.84 In chronically injured dystrophic muscles of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) mouse models, mdx and mdx5cv mice, recruitment of MOs/MPs also depends on CCR2 signaling.85,86 Importantly, the pro-inflammatory FAPs also significantly expand and highly express Ccl2 in mdx5cv muscles.44 Therefore, FAPs might also play an important role in recruiting MOs/MPs in dystrophic muscles. Unlike in acutely injured muscle where MP-mediated inflammatory response is essential to proper injury repair, MPs in dystrophic muscles are generally pathogenic and contribute to dystrophy progression.12,85,86,87 Understanding the roles of FAPs in inflammation regulation in different muscle disease settings would be important for therapeutic manipulation of inflammation to promote injury repair.

Limitations of the study

scRNA-seq is unable to address whether the polynucleated myofibers might contribute to CCL2 production as myofibers are too large to be included in single-cell suspensions. Nevertheless, FAPs do appear to be the major cellular source of CCL2 in acutely injured muscle, as FAP-specific deletion of Ccl2 markedly reduced the whole-muscle production of CCL2. scRNA-seq was performed up to 24 h post-injury; thus, the potential role of FAPs in MP accumulation and function at later stage of acute muscle injury was not explored. The danger signals that potentially trigger the expression of CCL2 by FAPs in acutely injured muscle need to be characterized. Muscle homogenate may not accurately represent the danger signals released in vivo by injured muscle. Only male mice were used in this study, and the potential influence of genders was not assessed.

Method details

BaCl2-induction of acute skeletal muscle injury and muscle sample collection

Mice of 10–14 weeks of age were subjected to acute skeletal muscle injury. 100 μL of BaCl2 (1.2% (w/v)) was injected intramuscularly into the right quadriceps. The left quadriceps were left un-injected as contralateral control. Un-injured and injured quadriceps were then collected 2 h, 9 h, 14 h, 24 h, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, and 21 days post-injury. Tendons were removed from the tissue specimens. Collected muscle samples were thoroughly washed in 1 × PBS (pH 7.4) to remove blood contamination.

Histopathological analysis

Collected muscle samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen-chilled isopentane for 20 s. Cross sections were then obtained on a Leica cryostat at a thickness of 10 μm and were subjected to histopathological analysis as detailed below. For all staining below, sections were air-dried for 30 min at room temperature and then fixed in 4% (w/v) of paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H & E) staining

Fixed sections were washed in 1× PBS for 5 min. H & E staining was then performed as following: 1) stained in Mayer’s Hematoxylin solution (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA.) for 10 min; 2) rinsed in warm running tap water for 15 min; 3) washed in distilled water for 30 s; 4) placed in 95% (v/v) reagent alcohol for 30 s; 5) stained in Eosin Y solution (Alcoholic, Millipore Sigma) for 1 min; 6) dehydrated and cleared through 2 changes each of 95% reagent alcohol, absolute reagent alcohol, and xylene for 2 min each; 7) mounted with Poly Mount Xylene (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA). Stained sections were viewed and photographed under a bright-field microscope.

Sudan Black B staining

We followed a protocol from the neuromuscular lab in Washington University School of Medicine (https://neuromuscular.wustl.edu/pathol/histol/sudan.pdf) with modification. Briefly, fixed sections were washed with three exchanges of tap H2O. Sudan Black B staining was then performed as following: 1) dehydrated in 100% propylene glycol (Millipore Sigma) to for 5 min; 2) propylene glycol was decanted and section was stained in Sudan Black B (Millipore Sigma) solution (0.7% Sudan Black B in propylene glycol) for overnight; 3) washed with three exchanges of tap H2O; 4) differentiated in 85% propylene glycol for 3 min; 5) rinsed three times with deionized H2O and then gently mount the coverslips with VectaMount AQ Aqueous Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA.). Stained sections were viewed and photographed under a bright-field microscope.

Anti-PDGFRα immunostaining

Fixed sections were stained with Goat anti-mouse PDGFRα antibody (R & D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA.): 1) after being washed with PBS-T (PBS with 0.2% Triton) for 3 times at 5 min each, sections were incubated in 0.6% H2O2 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature; 2) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were blocked with 10% Normal Donkey Serum (Millipore Sigma) in PBS-T for 2 h at room temperature; 3) sections were incubated with Goat anti-mouse PDGFRα antibody (1:200 diluted in 10% Normal Donkey Serum with PBS-T) overnight at 4°C; 4) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated in Biotinylated Donkey anti-Goat IgG (R & D Systems, 1:250 diluted in 10% Normal Donkey Serum with PBS-T for 1 h at room temperature; 4) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated with Vectastain ABC reagent (Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at room temperature; 5)) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated with DAB substrate solution (Vector Laboratories) at room temperature until desired stain intensity developed; 7) mounted with Poly Mount Xylene (Polysciences). Stained sections were viewed and photographed under a bright-field microscope.

Anti-CD68 immunostaining

Fixed sections were stained with Rat anti-mouse CD68 antibody (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA.): 1) after three washes with PBS-T (PBS with 0.2% Triton), sections were incubated in 0.6% H2O2 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature; 2) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were blocked with 10% Normal Rabbit Serum (Millipore Sigma) in PBS-T for 2 h at room temperature; 3) sections were incubated with Rat anti-mouse CD68 antibody (1:400 diluted in 10% Normal Rabbit Serum with PBS-T) overnight at 4°C; 4) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated in Biotinylated Rabbit anti-Rat IgG (Vector Laboratories, 1:200 diluted in 10% Normal Rabbit Serum with PBS-T for 1 h at room temperature; 4) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated with Vectastain ABC reagent (Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at room temperature; 5)) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated with DAB substrate solution (Vector Laboratories) at room temperature until desired stain intensity developed; 7) mounted with Poly Mount Xylene (Polysciences). Stained sections were viewed and photographed under a bright-field microscope.

Anti-collagen III immunostaining

Fixed sections were stained with Goat anti-human collagen III antibody (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA.): 1) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were blocked with 10% Normal Donkey Serum (Millipore Sigma) in PBS-T for 2 h at room temperature; 2) sections were incubated with Goat anti-human collagen III antibody (1:10 diluted in 10% Normal Donkey Serum with PBS-T) overnight at 4°C; 3) after three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated with Alexa 488-conjunagted Donkey anti-Goat IgG (Invitrogen, 1:500 diluted in 10% Normal Donkey Serum in PBS-T; 4) after three washes with PBS-T, mounted with M.O.M mounting solution (Vector laboratories). Stained sections were viewed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Muscle single-cell suspension preparation

Muscle single-cell suspension was prepared by collagenase/dispase digestion. Briefly, each muscle was minced in 2.5 mL of digestion solution containing 1 U/ml of collagenase B and 1 U/ml of dispase II (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA) in PBS and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by addition of 10 mL of PBS with 10% of fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning, Glendale, AZ, USA). The mixture was then filtered through 70-μm cell strainer and subjected to centrifugation at 250 × g for 5 min. The pellet was collected, and the supernatant was centrifuged again at 250 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The pellets were combined, washed with PBS, and centrifuged at 670 × g for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 3 mL of PBS, filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer, layered on an equal volume of Lymphocyte-M solution (Cedarlane, Burlington, NC, USA), and centrifuged at 2100 × g for 45 min. Cells at the interface were collected in 10 mL PBS containing 10% FBS, centrifuged at 670 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and re-suspended in FACS buffer (1 × PBS, 0.5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA, Millipore Sigma), 0.1% (w/v) sodium azide).

Flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting

Single-cell pellets were washed once in ice-cold FACS buffer, centrifuged at 500 g for 3 min at 4°C, and re-suspended in ice-cold FACS staining buffer (1 × PBS, 2% (w/v) BSA, 2% (v/v) normal mouse serum (Millipore Sigma).

For FACS analysis of different cell types, cells were stained on ice for 20 min with fluorescence-labelled antibodies targeting cell-type specific markers. Monocytes/macrophages were identified as CD45+CD64+F4/80+ cells. Neutrophils were identified as CD45+Ly6G+ cells. Endothelial cells were identified as CD45−CD31+ cells. FAPs were identified as Sca-1+PDGFRα+CD45−CD31-α7-integrin- cells. Muscle satellite cells were identified as Sca-1−CD45−CD31-α7-integrin+ cells. Fluorescence-labelled corresponding normal IgG isotypes were included as negative controls for gating. All antibodies were from commercial providers (See Key Resources Table for details) and diluted for staining following manufacturer’s recommendations. After staining, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer before being analyzed. For intracellular staining of CCL2 following cell-surface staining, permeabilization and staining were performed with CytoFix/CytoPerm reagents from BD Bioscience (San Jose, CA, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after the final wash with FACS buffer, cells were 1) re-suspended in CytoFix/CtyoPerm buffer and incubated on ice for 20 min, 2) washed twice with Perm/Wash buffer, 3) stained with anti-mouse CCL2 (MCP-1) antibody or IgG isotype control for 20 min in Perm/Wash buffer, and 4) washed twice with Per/Wash buffer. Stained cells were suspended in FACS buffer and analyzed on an LSR II (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA) with BD FACS Diva software. Collected data were then analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR, USA).

To clean up single cell suspension for subsequent single-cell based analysis, cell sorting based on FSC/SSC was performed to exclude dead cells, cell duplexes, and tissue debris. Sorting FAPs and non-FAPs for qRT-PCR analysis were performed with antibodies listed above. Cell sorting was done by the Flow Cytometry Core of Boston University School of Medicine. Collected cells from 5 individual male mice were combined and pelleted by centrifuging at 300 × g for 10 min. For scRNAseq analysis, pellets were re-suspended in FBS supplemented with 10% of DMSO and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. For qRT-PCR analysis, pellets were dissolved in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) for RNA preparation.

In vivo cell proliferation assay

WT C57BL/6J mice of 10–14 weeks of age received intraperitoneal injection of EdU (100 mg/kg, Baseclick GmbH, Neuried, Germany) right before BaCl2 injection. Un-injured and injured quadriceps were then collected 12 h and 24 h post-injury. Single-cell suspension was prepared as described in the section of muscle single-cell suspension preparation. Cell proliferation was then analyzed by determining the incorporation of EdU using EdU Click 647 Kit (Baseclick GmbH) following manufacturer’s instruction, combined with FACS staining and analysis of FAPs and CD45+ cells as described in the section of flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting.

DAMPs preparation

Murine DAMPs were prepared from the limb muscles of un-injured WT C57BL/6J mice. Human DAMPs were prepared from the amputated leg muscles. In both cases, muscles were homogenized in 1 × PBS (100 mg muscle/1 mL PBS) using Bead Mill 24 homogenizer (Fisher Scientific). The homogenate was then frozen at −80 °C overnight. After being thawed at room temperature, the homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. Supernatant was then collected as DAMPs.

Neutrophil isolation

Injured quadriceps were collected 9 h after BaCl2 injection. Single-cell suspension was then prepared as described in the section of “Muscle single-cell suspension preparation”, except that the isolated cells were re-suspended in MACS buffer (1 × PBS, 2% (w/v) BSA, 2 mM EDTA) instead of FACS buffer. Neutrophils were then isolated using Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Negative selection, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) following manufacturer’s instruction. Isolated neutrophils were re-suspended in DMEM medium for the following assay.

FAP isolation and in vitro stimulation

Isolation and culture of skeletal muscle FAPs were modified from a published study.89 Murine FAPs were isolated from the hindlimb muscles of 14-week-old uninjured mice. Human FAPs were isolated from the amputated leg muscles. Non-muscle tissue was removed before muscles were digested with digestion buffer. All muscles from both hind limbs of 1 mouse, or 1 mg of human muscle, were minced in 5 mL of digestion solution containing 1 U/ml of collagenase B and 1 U/ml of dispase II (Roche Diagnostics) in PBS and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by addition of 10 mL of PBS with 10% (vol/vol) FBS. The mixture was then filtered through 70-μm cell strainer and subjected to centrifugation at 250 × g for 5 min. The pellet was collected, and the supernatant was centrifuged again at 250 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The pellets were combined, washed with DMEM containing 10% (vol/vol) FBS, and centrifuged at 670 × g for 10 min. Cells were resuspended in DMEM containing 20% (vol/vol) FBS and cultured in 10-cm tissue culture dish at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 90 min. Supernatant and floating cells were then discarded. The adherent cells were maintained in DMEM with 20% (vol/vol) FBS, and grown at 37 °C, 5% CO2 into confluency.

FAPs were then detached by trypsin-digestion, centrifuged at 300 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in DMEM with 20% FBS. Resuspended FAPs were plated in 6-well tissue culture dish at 5 × 104 cells/well, and cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2 overnight before being treated with stimuli. Stimuli for murine FAPs: 1) murine DAMP (1:10 dilution); 2) LPS (100 ng/mL, Millipore Sigma); 3) murine TNF-α (10 ng/mL, R & D Systems); 4) murine IL-1β (10 ng/mL, R & D Systems); and 5) isolated neutrophils (1:1 co-culture). Stimuli for human FAPs: 1) human DAMP (1:10 dilution); 2) LPS (100 ng/mL). To inhibit MyD88 or TRIF signaling with chemical inhibitors, MyD88-specific inhibitor T-J-M2010-590,91 (50 μM, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) or TRIF specific inhibitor resveratrol92,93 (100 μM, MedChemExpress) was added 1 h before the other stimuli. Conditioned supernatant and FAPs were collected for ELISA and qRT-PCR analysis, respectively, at 2-, 6-, and 18-h following treatment.

mRNA preparation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA samples were prepared using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Samples of macrophages and other cell types were prepared by cell sorting as stated above and dissolved in TRIzol reagent. In vitro stimulated FAPs were directly dissolved in TRIzol reagent. To prepare whole-muscle RNA, quadriceps, or diaphragm, collected from 14-week-old male mice, were homogenized in TRIzol reagent using Bead Mill 24 homogenizer (Fisher Scientific). Total RNA was then prepared following manufacturer’s instruction. TRIzol-prepared total RNA was further cleaned up using RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) following manufacturer’s instruction. Concentration of cleaned RNA was measured using NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). 1 μg of total RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase system (Invitrogen) following manufacturer’s instruction. Expression of genes at mRNA level was then analyzed by real-time PCR with PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) using StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Sequences and providers of the primers used are listed in Key Resources Table. The sequences and target genes of the primers were listed in Key Resources Table. Data was calculated by ΔΔCt method. For sorted cell analysis, data was presented as a bar graph with error bar showing standard deviation of triplicated PCR results. For the whole muscle analysis, data was presented as scatterplot showing results of each individual samples.

ELISA assay

Conditioned supernatant collected in “FAP isolation and in vitro stimulation” was assayed for CCL2 by ELISA. To prepare whole muscle extract for CCL2 ELISA, quadriceps 9 h and 24 h post-acute injury were collected, washed with PBS, and snap frozen. Following tissue homogenization in PBS with cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostic) in a Bead Mill 24 homogenizer (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA), samples underwent 3 freeze-thaw cycles and supernatant was collected after centrifugation (10,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C). The concentration of CCL2 protein was then assayed with mouse anti-CCL2 ELISA kit (R & D Systems) following manufacturer’s instruction.

Single-cell cDNA library preparation and sequencing

Mouse skeletal muscle cell suspensions with viabilities between 74.4 and 87.6% were run on 10X genomics chip at concentrations varying between 810,000 and 1,280,000 cells/mL for a targeted cell recovery of 10,000 cells per sample. This was done using the 10X genomics 3′v3.1 Dual index kit. Following the 10X Genomics Chromium controller run the Gel-beads in emulsion (GEMs) were transferred to strip tubes and subsequently put into a thermal cycler using the following parameters: 45 min at 53 °C, 5 min at 85 °C, hold at 4 °C. The samples were then stored in a −20°C freezer overnight. The next day samples were thawed at room temperature before adding the recovery agent to break the GEMs. Subsequently the cDNA was purified then amplified in preparation for gene expression library preparation. Next the gene expression libraries were generated by following 10X Genomics library preparation protocol and the size, concentration and molarity of these libraries were assessed using the Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Assay (Agilent Technologies, Lexington, MA, USA). The libraries were then pooled to 5 nM and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 instrument at an 850pM input and 2% PhiX spike in using a P3 100 cycle flow cell (Illumina, San Diago, CA, USA) resulting in over 25,000 reads per cell.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

The 10X CellRanger94 tool was used for demultiplexing, alignment, identification of cells, and counting of unique molecular indices (UMIs). Data were aligned to the GRCm38 mouse reference genome. Specifically, the CellRanger mkfastq command was used to demultiplex raw base call (BCL) files generated by Illumina sequencers into FASTQ files. The CellRanger count command was used to perform alignment and create UMI count matrices using parameters –expect-cells = 10,000. Droplets with at least 500 UMIs underwent further quality control with the SCTK-QC pipeline1. The median number of UMIs was 6,627, the median number of genes detected was 2,234, the median percentage of mitochondrial reads was 3.37%. Cells with less than 500 counts, less than 500 genes detected, or more than 10% mitochondrial counts were excluded.

The lowest 1% of genes were removed. Doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder.95 Normalized counts were calculated with scater (v1.30.1).96 Highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected using scran (v1.30.2)97 (FDR <0.05), followed by principal component analysis. Cell cycle phases were also estimated with scran. Sample- and cell cycle-driven batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v1.2.0).98 Shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graphs were constructed from Harmony-reduced dimensions, and Leiden clustering was applied using rank-based similarity across a range of nearest-neighbor settings. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to identify differentially expressed features between clusters and across samples. Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using the CellChat (v2.2.0)99 R package following default parameter settings. The default signaling database was replaced with the ConnectomeDB2025100 mouse resource to incorporate the most recent, literature-supported ligand-receptor interactions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The Mann-Whitney test was performed to compare between two groups. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical details of each experiment can be found in the legends of figures and supplementary figures.
