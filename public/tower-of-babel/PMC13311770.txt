Introduction

Biliary atresia (BA) is a rare but serious hepatobiliary disorder of infancy, characterised by progressive fibroinflammatory obliteration of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts. It affects approximately 1 in 3500 to 20 000 live births and remains the leading cause of neonatal cholestatic jaundice and a primary indication for paediatric liver transplantation.1,3 Although the pathogenesis remains unclear, proposed mechanisms include genetic and/or developmental susceptibility, environmental toxins or viral infections, and dysregulated immune-mediated inflammation.1 2 Without treatment, infants rapidly progress to cholestasis, fibrosis and end-stage liver disease, often resulting in death within the first 2 years of life.2 3 Early diagnosis and timely Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE), which restores bile flow by connecting the hepatic hilum to the intestine, is essential for improving outcomes.2 4

However, diagnosis of BA is often delayed due to limited clinical awareness, ultimately hindering surgical intervention at the most beneficial stage of disease.5 To address this issue, screening strategies have been developed to reduce the age at surgery. Among them, the two main approaches are stool colour card (SCC) screening and bilirubin testing. SCC has been shown to lower the age at surgery and increase the proportion of infants undergoing KPE before 60 days. But its sensitivity varies widely (68.6–100%), and some infants do not develop acholic stools until 2–5 months, limiting early detection.5,9 In addition, a two-stage bilirubin protocol in 124 385 newborns achieved 100% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity, reducing mean age at surgery by 19 days. However, concerns regarding cost and adherence may hinder broader implementation.10 In contrast, while existing screening strategies are initiated postnatally, antenatal identification of fetuses at risk of BA offers a more proactive approach that could fundamentally shift the diagnostic timeline. It may not only facilitate timely postnatal confirmation and earlier surgical intervention but also expand the therapeutic window for potential neoadjuvant treatments prior to KPE.

Growing evidence suggests that BA may originate antenatally. Ultrasound findings in the second and third trimesters have identified biliary anomalies, such as non-visualisation or abnormal gallbladder morphology and hepatic hilar cysts in fetuses later diagnosed with BA.11,14 Moreover, reduced levels of γ-glutamyl transpeptidase in amniotic fluid, an enzyme excreted into the fetal intestine via the biliary tract, provide further support for a prenatal onset.15,17 Additionally, elevated direct bilirubin within 24–72 hours after birth has been consistently observed in affected infants.10 18 19 Taken together, these biochemical and imaging markers support a shift in understanding BA as a congenital condition with postnatal progression.1 20 These insights underscore the potential feasibility and clinical rationale for exploring prenatal screening strategies for BA.

Whereas some antenatal ultrasound features of the fetal biliary system show promise for early identification of BA, robust evidence on their diagnostic accuracy remains limited.12,1421 22 To address this gap, we designed a multicentre, prospective cohort diagnostic accuracy study to assess the diagnostic performance of predefined prenatal imaging features for identifying BA. Postnatal follow-up will serve as the reference standard, with surgical exploration and intraoperative cholangiography when clinically indicated. By estimating sensitivity, specificity and predictive values, the study seeks to provide a robust foundation for potential prenatal screening protocols, facilitating earlier detection and intervention, and ultimately improving long-term outcomes for infants with BA.

Methods and analysis

Study status

Recruitment of study participants started on 5 August 2024. The expected end date of this study is 31 December 2027, when all included patients will have completed their follow-up visit. This project has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR).

Study design

This study is designed as a multicentre, prospective cohort diagnostic accuracy study to evaluate the diagnostic performance of prenatal biliary imaging abnormalities for the detection of BA. The primary outcome is a binary classification of postnatal diagnosis: BA present or absent, based on the reference standard described below.

Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Pregnant women who undergo prenatal imaging examinations at the six participating centres and provide written informed consent for participation in this study and for subsequent follow-up will be eligible for inclusion. Participants who experience miscarriage or undergo termination of pregnancy will be excluded.

Identification and enrolment

Participants will be identified prospectively through routine obstetric ultrasonography conducted as part of standard antenatal care. Screening will be carried out across six participating centres across China, namely, Shanghai Changning Maternity and Infant Health Hospital, Shenzhen Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital, Affiliated Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital of Nantong University, International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital, Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University and Shanghai First Maternity and Infant Hospital.

All participating centres will adopt uniform, predefined eligibility criteria and implement standardised recruitment procedures. All eligible women attending routine second-trimester or third-trimester scans will be consecutively identified and invited to participate. The coordinating centre, Children’s Hospital of Fudan University, will be responsible for overall study supervision, postnatal follow-up and data management.

Test methods

Index test

The index test refers to a prespecified antenatal biliary imaging assessment integrated into routine obstetric ultrasonography from 18 weeks of gestation until delivery. All enrolled pregnant women are scheduled to undergo ultrasound examinations at three standard gestational intervals (18–24, 28–34 and 36 weeks to term). All examinations will be conducted by certified sonographers in accordance with standardised scanning protocols across the six participating centres. Prior to study initiation, all sonographers will receive uniform training on the identification of predefined biliary imaging features. An overview of the main antenatal imaging features assessed is provided in table 1. Given the prospective design, they will all remain blinded to postnatal outcomes throughout the study period thereby minimising observer bias.

These categories are based on previously published studies. Cases with none of these features will be categorised as negative. Any findings not prespecified will be considered exploratory and will be analysed descriptively. In cases of suspected non-visualisation, a repeat scan will be performed within 1 week to confirm NVFGB. Cases with visualisation of the gallbladder on repeat examination will be recorded as transient non-visualisation and will not be classified as NVFGB.

All imaging findings will be recorded using a standardised data collection form. The assessability of individual imaging features and the frequency of non-assessable findings will also be recorded. To minimise bias, each ultrasound will be independently reviewed by two specialists with at least 10 years of experience in fetal imaging, both blinded to postnatal outcomes and clinical information.

Reference standard

The reference standard for confirming the diagnosis of BA will comprise postnatal clinical follow-up, supplemented by surgical exploration and cholangiography when deemed necessary, in accordance with clinical diagnostic guidelines. Based on the reference standard, each case will be categorised as either biliary atresia (BA) or non-biliary atresia (non-BA).

Infants with persistent cholestatic symptoms (eg, prolonged jaundice, pale stools and dark urine) will undergo further clinical, biochemical and imaging assessment. Where clinically indicated, surgical exploration and cholangiography will be undertaken. A diagnosis of BA will be confirmed if the gallbladder appears fibrotic or cord-like, or if white bile or no bile is aspirated on gallbladder puncture, with confirmation supported by histology. If the gallbladder is not completely atrophic, cholangiography via gallbladder cannulation will be performed. Failure to visualise the common bile duct, hepatic duct or intrahepatic ducts will confirm BA, whereas their visualisation will exclude the diagnosis.

Infants without persistent cholestatic symptoms during follow-up, including those whose symptoms resolve without surgical intervention, or in whom BA is excluded based on the above assessments, will be classified as non-BA. In addition, cases diagnosed with alternative conditions (eg, Alagille syndrome or cystic fibrosis) based on percutaneous transhepatic biopsy or genetic testing will also be categorised as non-BA.

Paediatric surgeons determining the final diagnosis will be blinded to the index test and all prenatal imaging findings. Postnatal clinical data, such as biochemical results and imaging, may be accessed where clinically necessary to support accurate reference standard diagnosis.

Data collection and diagnostic confirmation

Given the large overall sample size required for this prospective study (n=92 082; see the Sample size calculation section) and to reduce the burden of data collection, participants will be followed up through two complementary strategies: direct follow-up for a selected subset and indirect follow-up through data linkage for the remainder (figure 1). The direct follow-up subset will include all participants with positive index test results and a systematically sampled 1:1 matched group of participants with negative index test results (see the Direct follow-up subset sampling strategy section). Comprehensive demographic, clinical and imaging data, along with postnatal follow-up information, will be systematically collected for all participants in this subset. For participants not selected for direct follow-up, indirect follow-up will be conducted via health system linkage and outcome verification strategies to identify postnatal BA cases and determine the reference standard diagnosis (figure 1).

Direct follow-up subset sampling strategy

An overall study schematic is provided in figure 1. All participants will undergo the predefined index test as part of routine obstetric ultrasonography and will be classified as either index test-positive or test-negative. All positive cases will be included in the direct follow-up subset. For each test-positive case, the next consecutively screened fetus meeting the criteria for a negative result will also be systematically selected into the same subset to maintain a 1:1 ratio. Specially, if the subsequent case is also test-positive, additional cases will be sequentially reviewed until a test-negative fetus is identified, thereby preserving a 1:1 ratio of positive to negative cases within the direct follow-up subset. For instance, if two consecutive test-positive fetuses are identified, the following two test-negative cases will be selected accordingly to maintain the matched design.

Standardised case report forms will be used to collect maternal baseline characteristics, antenatal imaging findings and postnatal outcomes, ensuring consistent and comprehensive documentation of clinical data across the study period. Participants will be followed up for a minimum of 6 months after birth, until a definitive diagnosis of BA is confirmed or excluded. Other hepatobiliary disorders identified during direct follow-up will also be recorded as separate postnatal outcome categories.

Bias

Several strategies will be employed to minimise potential sources of bias. Verification bias may arise if not all participants receive the reference standard. To address this, participants not included in direct follow-up will undergo indirect diagnostic assessment through structured clinical follow-up and health system linkage, ensuring maximum ascertainment of final disease status. Observer bias will be reduced by ensuring that all prenatal imaging is interpreted by reviewers blinded to postnatal outcomes. Selection bias will be mitigated through the prospective, multicentre design and systematic sampling of index test-negative cases to ensure population representativeness. Potential bias from missing or indeterminate data will be addressed using predefined procedures, as outlined in the relevant section below.

Importantly, the risk of misclassification bias is expected to be minimal, owing to the progressive nature of BA, which is a non-self-limiting condition. Even in the absence of surgical confirmation, extended postnatal follow-up is likely to reveal hallmark clinical features, allowing accurate disease classification over time.

Statistics and analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using PASS software (V.21.0.3, NCSS, LLC, USA). Statistical analyses will be conducted using Stata V.18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Sample size calculation

This is a prospective diagnostic accuracy study. Based on previous literature and local clinical practice, the estimated sensitivity and specificity of the index test are assumed to be 0.93 and 0.89, respectively, with a permissible margin of error of 0.05. Given the low prevalence of BA, the sample size was primarily determined based on sensitivity. Assuming a power of 85% and a two-sided alpha of 0.05, a minimum of nine confirmed cases of BA are required. Based on an estimated birth prevalence of BA at 10.86 per 100 000 live births,7 a minimum of 82 873 pregnant women undergoing prenatal screening will be needed to ensure sufficient case accrual. Accounting for a potential 10% loss to follow-up, the total sample size is adjusted to 92 082 participants.

Recruitment will continue until this sample size is reached, with an additional 6-month period allocated for late enrolment and final diagnostic confirmation.

Baseline characteristics of participants

For descriptive statistics, continuous variables will be summarised as mean±SD if normally distributed, or as median and IQR otherwise. Categorical variables will be reported as frequencies and percentages.

For comparative analyses, normally distributed continuous variables will be compared using Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed variables will be analysed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables will be compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All tests will be two-sided, and a p value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Ultrasonographic features will be categorised as binary variables for analysis, including gallbladder visualisation, presence of a hepatic hilar cyst, the triangular cord sign and the subcapsular hepatic flow signal.

Diagnostic accuracy analysis

The index test results will be dichotomised as either positive or negative according to predefined imaging criteria.

Predefined abnormal prenatal biliary imaging findings will first be analysed collectively as a composite exposure. Additional analyses will further evaluate isolated and non-isolated abnormalities, as well as individual imaging features separately. The diagnostic performance will be assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, odds of being affected given a positive result.

Indeterminate or missing data

Indeterminate index test results, such as poor-quality ultrasound images or equivocal findings, will be addressed by repeat imaging (ideally within 1 week) and re-evaluation by an additional experienced reviewer. Where consensus cannot be reached, such cases will be excluded from the primary diagnostic accuracy analysis but retained for sensitivity analyses to assess potential impact on the results.

For missing reference standard data, such as participants without sufficient follow-up to confirm or exclude BA, diagnosis will be established using indirect methods, including structured health system linkage and outcome verification. If diagnostic classification remains unresolved despite these strategies, the participant will be excluded from the primary analysis. Given the prospective design, standardised protocols and close monitoring, the proportion of missing data is expected to be low.

Analysis of variability

No formal subgroup analyses of diagnostic accuracy are prespecified. However, exploratory analyses may be conducted to examine variability in diagnostic performance across clinically relevant strata, such as gestational age at scanning, participating centre or type of antenatal imaging abnormality. These analyses will be reported as exploratory and interpreted with caution.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (number 2024-30). All procedures will adhere to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national research ethics regulations.

Potential risks to participants are minimal, as the index test is incorporated into routine obstetric ultrasonography and no experimental intervention is introduced. Data confidentiality and participant privacy will be strictly maintained, with anonymised data stored in a secure, password-protected database accessible only to authorised personnel.

Study findings will be disseminated through regional, national and international academic conferences and peer-reviewed journals.
