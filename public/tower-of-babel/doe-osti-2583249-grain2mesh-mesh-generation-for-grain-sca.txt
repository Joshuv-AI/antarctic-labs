Grain2Mesh: Mesh Generation for Grain-Scale
 Nonlinear Elasticity Modeling
 Keegan Davis

 Abstract

 The nonlinear hysteretic behavior of rocks under cyclic load-

 ing is a crucial area of study in geomechanics. The macroscopic

 response of a variety of materials has been found to be contin-

 gent upon the behavior of the micro-scale structure. This project

 aims to develop a functional and maintainable software package

 for generating a multi-phase numerical mesh and accompanying

 simulation files for finite element modeling used in computational

 mechanics solvers. Meshes generated from images often lack key

 preprocessing that reduces noise and prevents mesh element dis-

 tortion that can increase computational cost. By incorporat-

 ing user feedback throughout, grain2mesh ensures a high-fidelity

 mesh that can be used to model grain-scale interactions such as

 shearing, crack propagation, and interfacial material contrast.

 Scientific applications of this software include material fractur-

 ing, stress-strain analysis for natural and engineered materials,

 and nonlinear meso-scale analysis.

I INTRODUCTION

 The physics describing the irregular behavior of granular materials is still
debated. To understand this complex phenomenon, computational modeling
and finite discrete element methods are used to simulate granular structures
under a variety of stress and strain conditions. Accurately modeling the
internal microscopic structure of these materials is essential for developing
realistic models as it has been found that the micro-scale composition and
structure of granular materials heavily influences the behavior of the bulk
material. To perform this modeling, a high-fidelity geometric representation,
called a mesh, is needed. Producing one of these meshes is no trivial task.
 Image-based mesh generation tools exist, but many are insufficient as the
lack key preprocessing steps that reduce noise that can increase computa-
tional cost. In the explicit solvers used for computational modeling, the time
step is dependent upon the smallest mesh element. Small features and jagged
edges in the original image can lead to inconsistent element sizes in the final
mesh which makes the simulation more expensive to compute. The software
package we developed, grain2mesh, incorporates image preprocessing, as well
as user input, to ensure the final mesh is suitable for finite discrete element
modeling. The design of the software and some of this potential applications
are discussed in the following sections.

II SOFTWARE ARCHITECTURE

 Grain2mesh follows a pipeline consisting of six primary steps: (i) filter-
ing and segmenting the raw input image, (ii) labeling individual grains and
identifying regions of pore space, (iii) creating a polygon mesh, (iv) construct-
ing the geometry in Cubit, (v) applying spline interpolation to smooth the
boundaries of grains, and (vi) generating the final triangular mesh. Through-
out the mesh generation process, user input is incorporated to validate the
output results and ensure a suitable final mesh is constructed.

 Figure 1: Input Images

 The grain2mesh pipeline begins with an unprocessed mesoscopic image.
Figure 1 shows examples of possible input images. Grain2mesh supports seg-
mentation for both binary and RGB images. A common method for gener-
ating images of materials is X-ray tomography. Specifically, micro-CT scans

 3

are often used to visualize the internal 3-dimensional structure of material
samples. Micro-CT scans produce binary images that distinguish material
and pore space. However, other methods such as petrography, which ex-
amines thin slices of rock under a microscope, and spectroscopy, which uses
various wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation to distinguish the compo-
sition of a sample, produce color images that identify different features and
materials within a sample. Each type of image is useful in different appli-
cations, but they require slightly different processing techniques. The grains
within a binary image are easily partitioned using watershed segmentation,
a common technique used for segmenting grains. However, to segment RGB
images, grain2mesh incorporates a self-training convolutional neural network
(CNN). The user can specify roughly how many different material classes they
want to segment and the CNN groups pixels accordingly. As the image is
passed through the CNN over consecutive training epochs, the segmentation
becomes more and more generalized. Eventually, the CNN outputs an image
segmented by material classes that can then be further processed to generate
a finite element mesh. Figure 2 shows an example of the CNN segmentation.

 The preprocessing phase of the grain2mesh pipeline first cleans the raw
input image. A Gaussian filter is used to remove small features from the
image and smooth edges. This step is important for finite element mesh
generation as the time step of the explicit solvers is restricted by the smallest
element size. Small features ultimately lead to small mesh elements which
can drastically increase the computational cost of simulation. Removing
these features is a unique capability of grain2mesh and one of the many
reasons it is a useful package for mesh generation.
 After filtering the input image, grains are segmented and individually la-
beled. As discussed previously, the segmentation process can vary depending
on the type of the input image, but the result is essentially the same. Grains
are identified and labeled so they can easily be classified and given the proper
material attributes for simulation. After segmenting the grains and identify-

 5

ing pore space, a polygon mesh is constructed pixel by pixel. The vertices,
facets, and phase of each pixel are used to create a geometric representation
of the sample material. This polygon mesh is the input to Cubit, a mesh
generation software developed by Sandia National Laboratories, that is used
by grain2mesh to construct the multi-phase numerical mesh. Figure 3 shows
an example of segmented grains colored by size and a sample polygon mesh.

 (a) Segmented Grains (b) Polygon Mesh

 Figure 3: Grain Segmentation and Polygon Mesh Construction

 The mesh generation phase of grain2mesh utilizes Cubit’s Python API
to reconstruct the polygon mesh in Cubit and take advantage of its meshing
optimization. The initial version of grain2mesh accomplished this recon-
struction one pixel at a time. The surfaces of each individual pixel had to
be periodically united to create a single Cubit entity representing the whole
geometry. This quickly became costly as even small images can have tens
or hundreds of thousands of pixels. Storing and tracking this many surfaces

 6

was cumbersome, expensive, and slowed down the program. To speed the
geometry reconstruction, grain2mesh now constructs Cubit surfaces by grain
as opposed to by pixel. Images have far less grains than pixels so there are
far fewer expensive unites needed throughout the process. The outer border
of each grain is traced along the pixel edges to produce a grain boundary
that can be filled in and made into a surface.
 The grain edges within the Cubit surface then need to be smoothed. This
is done by creating splines through the vertices of each border between grains.
The spline curves must meet a minimum length to ensure the mesh elements
are uniform in size, so small curves are merged into adjacent ones. The final
splined surface can then be meshed. The meshing process begins by removing
the pore space. Material regions are meshed and boundary conditions can
be added by the user. The final mesh is accompanied by a journal file that
logs the various mesh elements. The numerical mesh can then be used in
finite element models to simulate the material behavior. Below, Figures 4
and 5 show samples of the Cubit mesh at various stages within the meshing
process.

 Grain2mesh currently supports meshing for 2-dimensional surfaces. Fu-
ture versions of the software package will incorporate 3-dimensional mesh
support. Especially with micro-CT scans, it is easy to have 3-dimensional vol-
umetric data that could be meshed directly as opposed to in slices. For both
preprocessing and mesh generation, incorporating support for 3-dimensional

 8

mesh will involve redesigning the algorithms to construct features from vox-
els rather than pixels. Another challenge with this future feature will be
smoothing the boundary surfaces between volumes rather than individual
edges.

III APPLICATIONS

 The final mesh generated by grain2mesh is intended for use in finite el-
ement modeling software such as HOSS, a software suite developed by the
geomechanics team in EES-17 at Los Alamos National Laboratory. The finite
element method is useful for its discretization of a material into individual
elements and ability to model nonlinear behavior. Using numerical methods,
these software applications solve the partial differential equations that govern
the material domain across the multi-phase mesh. Doing so can help model
and explain the complex behavior of granular and heterogeneous materials.
 There are many applications of grain2mesh in geomechanics and material
science. A variety of materials respond to stress and strain in a non-classical
fashion. A few examples include polycrystalline solids, ceramic composites,
alloys, concrete, and other natural and engineered granular materials. Nu-
merical meshes can be used to model underground reservoirs that store fluid
and predict seismic activity. They can also be used in rupture simulations
and modeling for fault lines, deformation, and fracture propagation.
 Understanding complex natural phenomena relates to the Department of

Energy’s mission of ensuring national security, as well as energy, climate,
and environmental research. A better understanding of nonlinear elasticity
can help increase stability and safety in construction, as well as aid in risk
assessment and threat reduction.

IV CONCLUSION

 Scientific research is increasingly dependent on simulation. The ability to
produce accurate results relies on having precise models that correctly repre-
sent the subject being studied. In many fields, the simulated representation
of an object comes in the form of a numerical mesh. The ability to generate
accurate and representative meshes that are computationally efficient is a
real need for many scientists. Grain2mesh is a tool that provides scientists
with that ability and enables them to conduct high-quality research.
