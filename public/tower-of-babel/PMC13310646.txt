Quantifying the potential of alcohol policies for cancer prevention in the EU: a modelling study
Authors: Daniela Correia, Maria Neufeld, Carina Ferreira-Borges, Beatrice Lauby-Secretan, Harriet Rumgay, Isabelle Soerjomataram, Elisabete Weiderpass, Kevin Shield, Ahmed S. Hassan, Jürgen Rehm

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlcohol is a major preventable risk factor for cancer, causally linked to seven types of cancer. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted systematic searches in Ovid MEDLINE and Embase to identify reviews and primary studies assessing the impact of alcohol control policies on alcohol consumption and cancer incidence, from database inception to Nov 17, 2025, with no other language or date restrictions. Search terms included MeSH/Emtree and truncated terms related to each topical area combining terms specific alcohol policies (taxation, minimum pricing, other pricing policies, restriction on sales hours or days or density of outlets, prohibition, restriction or bans on marketing), with terms describing alcohol consumption or drinking or with cancer incidence. In addition, we conducted a second search based on the wider concept of availability restrictions as additional policy (for both searches see Supplementary Materials 1). No systematic reviews were found in either search. Five eligible primary studies were found for the European Union (EU) and neighbouring countries; all were modelling studies, and all but one focused exclusively on pricing mechanisms, primarily alcohol taxation. No studies were identified that quantified the impact of other major alcohol control policies, nor studies that examined integrated alcohol policy packages.

Added value of this study

This study extends the existing evidence base by quantifying the potential impact of three major WHO-recommended alcohol policies on cancer incidence in the EU: a taxation increase, restricting availability via a Sunday sales ban, and a national marketing ban. Using effect estimates derived from real-world policy evaluations and grounded in the causal evidence framework established in the IARC Handbooks of Cancer Prevention Volume 20A and 20B, we modelled expected reductions in cancer incidence over a 10-year horizon. In addition to single-policy scenarios, we evaluated an integrated policy scenario. While all policy scenarios reduced cancer incidence, the combined policy approaches resulted in larger population-level cancer prevention gains than individual interventions alone. Despite the less-than-additive effects of the individual policies, the combined scenario could potentially avert 9220 new cancer cases (95% UI: 8568–9833), or 6.5% (95% UI: 6.0%–6.9%) of all alcohol-attributable new cancer cases.

Implications of all the available evidence

The available evidence indicates that population-level alcohol control policies can meaningfully reduce cancer incidence in the EU. While alcohol taxation has been most frequently studied, other major alcohol control policies, such as availability restrictions and marketing bans, also have substantial cancer prevention potential. Integrating these policies into coordinated national strategies could enhance and sustain their impact. Strengthening EU-level guidance and support for the implementation of WHO-recommended alcohol policies, including raising minimum excise taxation levels, could contribute substantially to achieving cancer prevention objectives.

Introduction

Alcohol consumption is a major preventable risk factor for cancer,1 second only to tobacco smoking in Europe.2 Given this well-established causal association, identifying the most effective strategies to prevent alcohol-attributable cancers has become a public health priority.3 To address this knowledge gap, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) Handbooks of Cancer Prevention programme convened two expert working groups to systematically review and evaluate the evidence on a) the association between reduction or cessation of alcohol consumption and cancer risk (IARC Handbook Volume 20A),4 and b) the impact of population-based alcohol policies on alcohol consumption (Volume 20B).5

The IARC Handbook Volume 20A reviewed all possible mechanisms causally related to alcohol consumption and then evaluated the evidence of reversal upon cessation. It found strong evidence that cessation of alcohol consumption leads to reversal of carcinogenesis through a reduction in acetaldehyde metabolism, genotoxic effects (such as DNA damage), and immune and inflammatory pathways involving intestinal permeability and microbial translocation.4,6 Among the cancers causally associated with alcohol consumption1—oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, liver, colorectum, and female breast—the Working Group concluded that there is sufficient evidence that reduction or cessation of alcohol consumption reduces those of the oral cavity and oesophagus, but epidemiological evidence on the reversibility of risk remains limited for the other alcohol-related cancers.4,6 Complementing these findings, a large national cohort study in France showed that individuals with alcohol dependence who underwent rehabilitation or maintained abstinence experienced significantly lower cancer risks across all alcohol-related cancers.7

The IARC Handbook Volume 20B5 concluded that there is sufficient evidence that specific policy interventions reduce alcohol consumption. These include increasing alcohol taxes or minimum prices, restricting physical availability (through outlet density, hours or days of sales), reducing the minimum legal purchase or drinking age, or implementing strong marketing bans reduce alcohol consumption. There is also sufficient evidence for government monopolies and other coordinated, multiple policy interventions.5,8

The evaluated population-level alcohol control policies closely align with the World Health Organization (WHO)'s “best buy” strategies of increasing taxes or minimum prices, limiting availability, and regulating marketing, which are the most effective and cost-efficient population-level interventions.9 Moreover, most international recommendations emphasise that alcohol control policies should be implemented as part of an integrated policy framework—a consistent, structurally connected, and interdependent manner—to enhance population-level impact.10

This study aims to quantifythe potential impact of four population-level alcohol control policies— increased alcohol excise taxation, a Sunday alcohol sales ban, a national alcohol marketing ban, and an integrated alcohol policy—on alcohol-attributable cancer incidence in the European Union (EU) in 2022, based on effects observed in real-world studies. It provides the first estimates of the potential cancer-preventive potential of these policies for the EUregion (for all prior articles see Supplementary Materials 1).

Methods

Overall study design

This study is a multi-country modelling analysis estimating the potential impact on cancer incidence in 2022 if evidence-based alcohol control policies had been implemented across the 27 EU countries in 2012. Four policy scenarios were modelled: increased alcohol excise taxation, a Sunday alcohol sales ban, a national alcohol marketing ban, and an integrated policy approach combining all three interventions—each informed by a well-controlled and methodologically robust real-world study from a high-income country identified in IARC Handbook 20B.5 The impact on cancer was modelled through policy-related changes in recorded alcohol consumption, as the interventions evaluated affect recorded consumption only.

Data sources

Data were obtained from international monitoring systems. We obtained alcohol consumption, tax structures, and price data from the WHO; cancer incidence data from IARC; and population data from the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024. Full details are provided in Supplementary Materials 2.

Modelling of the impact of alcohol policy on alcohol consumption

Taxation

The taxation scenario was based on the 2009 increase in alcohol excise taxes implemented in the state of Illinois, United States (US), where excise taxes rose by 21% for beer and by 90% for wine and spirits.11 Existing national tax structures were retained. For countries with no excise tax on wine (n = 15), a volumetric excise duty was introduced so that excise tax accounted for 10% of the retail price; this was a conservative modelling assumption below the EU average excise tax share.

Beverage-specific retail prices were recalculated, and retail price percentage changes were converted into expected changes in recorded per capita alcohol consumption using beverage-specific price elasticities from meta-analyses (details provided in Supplementary Materials 3). For countries with missing information on tax rates and structures (France, Luxembourg, Malta), population-weighted regional average price changes by beverage type were used.

Sunday alcohol sales ban

The availability scenario modelled the effect of a Sunday alcohol sales ban in several US states.12 In that study, a ban on Sunday alcohol sales was associated with a reduction in relative recorded alcohol consumption of −0.04 (95% CI: −0.05 to −0.03) among men and −0.09 (95% CI: −0.10 to −0.08) among women. We applied these sex-specific relative reductions to the recorded component of alcohol per capita consumption of all EU countries, except Sweden, where the existing retail monopoly had already prohibited alcohol sales on Sundays in 2012. A sensitivity analysis for a more conservative scenario based on the partial relaxation of Saturday sales restrictions in Sweden is described in Supplementary Materials 6.

Strong marketing bans

The marketing scenario was based on a ban on the marketing of alcoholic beverages in all media, introduced in Norway in 1975.13 In that study, the ban led to a −0.074 (SE = 0.023, p = 0.002) reduction in log-recorded alcohol sales, corresponding to a relative reduction of −0.07 recorded alcohol per capita consumption.

Integrated alcohol policy

The integrated policy scenario14 reflects the implementation of the three WHO “best buys”—increased excise taxation, restricting physical availability, and a national marketing ban—as occurred in Lithuania during two major reform periods were (2008–2009, 2017–2018). Using the same methodological approach applied in the original evaluation, we independently estimated the effect of the integrated policies on recorded alcohol consumption using a time-series model (details provided in Supplementary Materials 3). The estimated relative reduction was −0.107 (95% CI: −0.160 to −0.053).

Modelling of the impact of alcohol consumption on cancer incidence

The modelling analysis included the seven cancer sites for which IARC found sufficient evidence of a causal relationship with alcohol consumption1—oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, liver, colorectum, and female breast—and assumed a 10-year latency between changes in alcohol exposure and observable effects on cancer incidence.15

Relative risk estimates were obtained from meta-analyses reviewed by the WHO Technical Advisory Group on Alcohol and Drug Epidemiology (2025).

The impact of changes in alcohol consumption on cancer incidence was estimated using a comparative risk-assessment framework adapted from Shield and colleagues.16 Alcohol population-attributable fractions (PAFs) were calculated by sex, age group, cancer site, and country, combining scenario-specific alcohol consumption distributions among past-year drinkers with fixed prevalence of former past-year drinkers and cancer-site–specific relative risks. PAFs were applied to cancer incidence data for 2022 to estimate alcohol-attributable cancer cases, and differences between baseline and counterfactual scenarios were used to quantify cases averted.

Full details on exposure modelling, relative-risk functions, and counterfactual scenario implementation and formulae are provided in Supplementary Materials 4.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the number of cancer cases averted in 2022 under each scenario and the corresponding fraction among all alcohol-attributable cancer cases.

Secondary outcomes included the total and alcohol-attributable age-standardised cancer incidence rates (ASIR) per 100,000 population aged 15 years and older, as well as their relative change under each policy scenario. Age-standardised rates were calculated using the EU population of 2022 as the reference.

Uncertainty analyses

Uncertainty in both the estimated change in alcohol consumption and cancer incidence was quantified using Monte Carlo-like simulation.17 Uncertainty in consumption change was based on the 95% CIs of price elasticities (taxation scenario) or the CIs of relative reduction estimates (availability, marketing, and integrated policy scenarios; for details see above). Uncertainty in the alcohol–cancer risk functions incorporated the CIs of cancer-site–specific RRs by sex and age group. Uncertainties were presented as 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) based on 1000 iterations for each scenario, sex, cancer site, and country. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3).

Ethics approval

The study does not include individual-level data and therefore did not require ethical approval.

Role of the funding sources

The funders of this study exerted no influence over the study's design, data acquisition, data analysis, data interpretation, content, or writing of this paper. The authors were not precluded from accessing data in the study, and give their approval for submitting this manuscript for publication.

Results

Alcohol exposure and alcohol-attributable cancer burden in the EU

In 2012, the mean adult (15+) alcohol per capita consumption (APC) in the EU was 11.2 L of pure alcohol per adult aged 15 years and older, ranging from 6.4 L in Cyprus to 17.1 L in Roumania (Table S4, Supplementary Materials 5). APC was higher among men, who consumed on average 3.6 times more alcohol than women. Recorded consumption accounted for approximately 90% of total alcohol consumed in the EU, but varied between countries, with the remaining share attributable to unrecorded and tourist consumption.

In 2022, considering the seven alcohol-related cancer sites, an estimated 907,233 cancer cases occurred in the EU (Table 1), of which 146,194 (16.1%) were attributable to alcohol consumption (4.9% of all 2,991,677 new cancer cases in the EU in 2022). Alcohol-attributable cancer burden differed markedly by sex and cancer site. Among women, breast cancer accounted for the largest share of alcohol-attributable cases (31,178 cases; 68.5%). At the EU level, and consistently across all scenarios, the largest absolute reductions in alcohol-attributable cancer incidence were estimated for female breast and oesophageal cancers. Under the integrated policy scenario, 3235 (95% UI: 2 9890–3527) and 2 0 80 (95% UI: 1891–2278) cases, respectively, would be averted (Fig. 2). Oesophageal cancer showed the largest relative reductions in alcohol-attributable cases across most scenarios, ranging from 4.4% (95% UI: 4.2–4.6) under the Sunday sales ban to 10.5% (95% UI: 9.5–11.4) under the integrated policy scenario (Table S7, Supplementary Materials 5), corresponding to approximately one in every 14 oesophageal cancer cases in the EU being averted. Alcohol-attributable female breast cancer was reduced by up to 10.4% (95% UI: 9.3–11.3), although this represented only around 0.9% of all female breast cancer cases. Intermediate relative reductions were estimated for cancers of the oral cavity (3.6–7.7%), pharynx (3.8–7.4%), and larynx (3.3–7.2%), while reductions for colorectal and liver cancers were smaller, generally remaining below 4% across scenarios.

Consistent with reductions in APC, relative reductions in alcohol-attributable cancer cases were broadly similar across countries under the integrated policy scenario, ranging from 3.2% in Roumania (95% UI: 2.5–3.9) to 9.6% in Estonia (95% UI: 7.7–12.0). The taxation scenario showed greater cross-country variation, with alcohol-attributable cancer cases averted reaching 14.5% in Finland (95% UI: 12.5–16.6) (Table 3).

Under the integrated policy scenario, alcohol-attributable ASIR declined to 34.5 per 100,000, an absolute reduction of 2.4 per 100,000 (−6.5%). Smaller but consistent reductions were observed under the taxation, Sunday sales ban, and marketing ban scenarios (Table S8, Supplementary Materials 5). Reductions varied across countries, reflecting baseline incidence and alcohol exposure; under the integrated policy scenario, absolute reductions ranged from 1.1 per 100,000 in the lowest-impact countries to 3.6 per 100,000 in the highest-impact countries. Similar geographic patterns were observed under the other scenarios, albeit at smaller magnitudes.

Discussion

Using the causal evidence framework established in the IARC Handbooks of Cancer Prevention Volumes 20A and 20B, this study modelled the potential reduction in cancer incidence under four alcohol policy scenarios based on real-world studies and applied to all EU countries. All three WHO “best buy” scenarios—increased alcohol excise taxation, restrictions on days of sale (Sunday ban), and a national alcohol marketing ban—as well as an integrated policy scenario would reduce cancer incidence. Under the integrated scenario per capita alcohol consumption in the EU would decrease by 9.1%, corresponding to more than 9200 averted cancer cases, or a 6.5% reduction in alcohol-attributable cancer cases.

Although the causal role of alcohol consumption in cancer is well established, alcohol control policies are rarely assessed through a cancer prevention lens. Our findings indicate that population-level alcohol policies markedly reduce cancer incidence across diverse policy domains and country contexts, underscoring their relevance, given that cancer is the leading cause of alcohol-attributable deaths in the EU. These public health gains need to be weighed against perceived downsides, such as restrictions on personal liberty (e.g., the freedom to purchase alcohol at any time, in the case of availability policies), or potential economic losses associated with reduced alcohol sales. However, the economic costs of alcohol use are substantial,18 far exceeding the revenue generated from excise taxation, and to date no alcohol policy intervention has been shown to produce net negative economic effects.19 Until such effects have been empirically demonstrated, the available evidence supports continued implementation of policy interventions to reduce not only the disease burden of alcohol use but also its attributable costs.

The policy scenarios examined were not intended to represent the most comprehensive or effective interventions within each policy. Rather, they illustrate the potential contribution of different alcohol policies to cancer prevention when implemented at scale. Thus, results should not be interpreted as comparative effectiveness among scenarios, but as quantitative illustrations of the cancer-preventive potential of alcohol policies.

The integrated policy scenario showed that simultaneous implementation of effective alcohol policies can produce reductions in consumption larger than do individual interventions alone, although smaller than the sum of their separate effects. This pattern is consistent with evaluations of integrated policy phases reported by some countries, where overlapping measures produced measurable but not fully additive impacts on population-level consumption.8,14

Policy effects varied across countries. This heterogeneity reflects differences in baseline alcohol consumption, population structure, taxation structures, beverage preferences, and the share of unrecorded consumption. For example, countries with higher shares of recorded consumption, such as Estonia, may experience larger effects, whereas countries with substantial unrecorded or cross-border consumption may require complementary measures. These findings underscore the importance of adapting alcohol policies to national contexts while maintaining alignment with WHO-recommended population-level strategies.20

A key strength of this analysis is the use of comparable data from the WHO and the IARC, combined with a modelling approach consistent with the WHO's alcohol monitoring framework.21,22 This ensures methodological consistency across all 27 countries and transparent estimation of alcohol-attributable cancer incidence under counterfactual policy scenarios. Another important strength is that all intervention scenarios were derived from real-world policy evaluations that had undergone rigorous causal assessment through the two-step review process used in the IARC Handbook Volume 20B.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the real-world studies and scenarios developed involve inherent constraints: the taxation scenario assumes constant own-price elasticities across countries, no cross-price elasticities between beverage types, full tax pass-through to retail prices, and representative prices based on a product of the most-sold brand per beverage category. In addition, taxation data from 2022 were used as a proxy for 2012 data due to limited historical availability. However, as modelled effects are based on relative rather than absolute tax changes, and alcohol taxation levels in EU countries have not changed often,23 these assumptions are unlikely to compromise the validity of our estimates.

Second, the marketing ban scenario is predicated on evidence from a pre-digital media environment. Contemporary alcohol marketing increasingly uses digital platforms and personalised advertising, which may amplify exposure, particularly among younger populations. Consequently, applying estimates from the 1975 Norwegian marketing ban to current contexts introduces uncertainty. Furthermore, the original study focused solely on recorded consumption trends in Norway, lacking an external control group. Additionally, our scenario does not account for potential substitution between alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages, including the growing market for no- and low-alcohol products, which may attenuate the estimated effect of a marketing ban in contemporary contexts.

Third, the Sunday sales ban scenario draws on studies examining the effects of lifting existing bans rather than the introduction of such bans. Nonetheless, restrictions on days and hours of sale have been implemented in several European countries, and reductions in sales hours are well established as an effective tool to reduce consumption and alcohol-attributable harm.24 A sensitivity analysis using a more conservative effect based on real-world restrictions in Sweden yielded significant results in the same direction (Supplementary Materials 6).

Fourth, the analysis does not explicitly account for compensatory behaviours such as shifts toward unrecorded alcohol or cross-border purchasing, which could attenuate policy effects in some settings. However, available evidence for tax increases suggests that such behaviours have not systematically undermined the impact of the policy.25 More broadly, our modelling approach seems to be conservative: time-series studies directly estimating the effects of alcohol policies on cancer mortality have reported larger effect sizes than those obtained through consumption-mediated pathways.26

Lastly, the analysis was limited to cancer sites where causality between alcohol consumption and cancer incidence has been established. The IARC Monographs evaluation analysis is now 14 years out of date, and thus an updated review of current evidence is needed to identify additional causally related cancer sites.

By translating reductions in alcohol consumption into absolute numbers of averted cancer cases, this study provides a tangible and policy-relevant metric that may be more easily understood than relative risks or attributable fractions alone. The findings reinforce the relevance of alcohol taxation, availability restrictions, and marketing bans—already endorsed as WHO “best buys”—as underused instruments for cancer prevention.

At the European level, opportunities exist to address this underuse through regulatory action. For example, EU minimum excise duty rates could be increased,27 particularly for wine, which remains untaxed in 15 countries of the EU, and stronger, harmonised standards for alcohol marketing could be considered for a substance that is toxic, intoxicating, and carcinogenic.

The current lack of stronger alcohol control policies for cancer prevention may be partly explained by limited awareness of the causal relationship between alcohol consumption and cancer, both within the EU28 and globally.29 Evidence suggests that public understanding of alcohol-related cancer risks remains low. As supported by public health authorities, including the former US Surgeon General30 and the WHO,3,10 policy approaches aimed at improving risk communication such as cancer-warning labels on alcohol containers may help address this gap.31 However, low public awareness is not simply a matter of insufficient information—it is also a product of deliberate efforts by the alcohol industry to obscure the evidence and resist regulatory action. Commercial determinants of health32 have been shown to be a major obstacle to implementing alcohol control policies in Europe, despite evidence of their effectiveness and cost-effectiveness.9 Framing alcohol control policies within a cancer prevention agenda may help build the political will needed to overcome these barriers.
