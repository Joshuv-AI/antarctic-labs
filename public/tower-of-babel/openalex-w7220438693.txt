Abstract
Objective: To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of muscle ultrasound parameters for sarcopenia and determine whether
this method can complement conventional assessment. Methods: A systematic review of diagnostic accuracy studies
was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-DTA. PubMed/MEDLINE was searched, with supplementary searches in
Europe PMC, Crossref, ClinicalTrials.gov, and study reference lists through September 17, 2026. Studies comparing
quantitative ultrasound parameters with explicit diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia were included. Risk of bias was
assessed using QUADAS-3. Results: Forty-five publications were included in the diagnostic accuracy synthesis. The
rectus femoris was the most frequently investigated muscle, mainly through muscle thickness and cross-sectional area.
Twelve studies provided verifiable 2 × 2 tables. In the three comparable studies of rectus femoris cross-sectional area,
sensitivity ranged from 62.5% to 100% and specificity from 53.8% to 92.0%. Clinical and methodological heterogeneity
precluded a valid meta-analysis. Of the 41 studies assessed with QUADAS-3, 38 had an overall high risk of bias.
Conclusion: Muscle ultrasound may support screening and complement sarcopenia assessment, particularly because it
is portable and suitable for bedside use. However, variation in protocols, muscles, diagnostic criteria, and cutoffs
currently prevents its use as a replacement for conventional methods.
Keywords: Sarcopenia; Ultrasonography; Sensitivity and Specificity; Diagnosis; Systematic Review.

1. Introduction
Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized muscle disease associated with aging and a range of clinical conditions. It
increases the risk of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality. The revised European consensus (EWGSOP2) considers low
muscle strength the primary indicator of the disease, confirms the diagnosis on the basis of low muscle quantity or quality, and
uses physical performance to determine severity. AWGS 2019 follows a similar framework, with cutoffs adapted to Asian
populations (Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020).
Conventional assessment combines tests of strength and physical performance with methods used to estimate muscle
mass. Handgrip dynamometry, the chair stand test, and gait speed are simple clinical measures, whereas dual-energy X-ray
absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) are used to estimate muscle mass. Computed tomography
and magnetic resonance imaging provide greater anatomical detail but are limited by cost, availability, the need to transport
patients, and, in the case of computed tomography, exposure to ionizing radiation (Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020).
Muscle ultrasound has emerged as a portable, nonionizing alternative suitable for bedside use. B-mode ultrasound can
provide measurements of muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, fascicle length, pennation angle, and echogenicity. These
parameters represent different aspects of muscle quantity, architecture, and quality and can be assessed even in frail individuals
or those with limited mobility (Nijholt et al., 2017; Perkisas et al., 2021).

The usefulness of these measurements, however, depends on examination standardization. Body position, anatomical
site, transducer orientation and pressure, equipment settings, and the analytical method all influence the results. Age, sex,
adiposity, edema, and hydration also affect the measurements, making it difficult to transfer cutoffs across populations and
clinical settings (Nijholt et al., 2017; Perkisas et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2023).
Previous reviews have reported promising diagnostic performance but combined studies that examined different
muscles, parameters, and definitions of sarcopenia. Fu et al. (2023) found variable accuracy and a high risk of bias, whereas
Yang et al. (2025) reported moderate-to-high performance for muscle-thickness measurements in older adults. Favorable findings
have also been reported in chronic kidney disease, although hydration status and protocol diversity limit comparisons among
studies (Yang et al., 2024).
Since those reviews were published, further studies have investigated elastography, radiomics, composite models, and
artificial intelligence tools. At the same time, it remains necessary to distinguish confirmed sarcopenia from isolated low muscle
mass and to determine whether ultrasound adds information to conventional methods rather than merely being associated with
them (Fu et al., 2023; Yik et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025).
This article aims to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of muscle ultrasound parameters for sarcopenia and determine
whether this method can complement conventional assessment.
Secondary objectives were to examine the potential role of ultrasound as a screening, replacement, or complementary
method and to assess the principal sources of heterogeneity and the methodological quality of the available evidence.

2. Methods
2.1 Study design and eligibility criteria
A descriptive investigation was conducted (Risemberg et al., 2026a) using a quantitative approach regarding the number
of articles selected to form the research corpus, and a qualitative approach regarding the discussions (Risemberg et al., 2026b;
Pereira et al., 2018).
A systematic review of diagnostic accuracy studies was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-DTA and, where
applicable, PRISMA 2020 (McInnes et al., 2018; Page et al., 2021). The review question was structured according to the
population, index test, target condition, reference standard, and accuracy outcomes.
Primary studies in adults were included if they assessed quantitative muscle ultrasound parameters and compared them,
in the same participants, with an explicit diagnostic criterion for sarcopenia. The primary population comprised people aged 60
years or older; studies of younger adults with conditions associated with sarcopenia were analyzed separately. Eligible index
tests included muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, echo intensity, measures of muscle architecture, elastography, and models
derived from these variables. Accepted reference standards were the EWGSOP, EWGSOP2, and AWGS consensus criteria or
equivalent definitions that included an assessment of muscle mass. Studies without diagnostic classification, purely correlational
investigations, case reports, reviews, protocols, and studies concerned exclusively with prognosis or treatment response were
excluded.
2.2 Information sources and search strategy
PubMed/MEDLINE was searched from database inception through September 17, 2026, without language restrictions.
Controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to sarcopenia, muscle loss, and ultrasonography were combined.

2.3 Study selection and data extraction
Records were compiled in a standardized matrix, and duplicates were removed using PMID, DOI, and title verification.
Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by full-text assessment according to the eligibility criteria. Publications
originating from the same cohort were linked to prevent double counting.
The extracted data included authorship, year, country, study design, population, sample size, diagnostic criterion,
method used to assess muscle mass, muscle examined, ultrasound protocol, parameter analyzed, cutoff, sensitivity, specificity,
area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and data from 2 × 2 tables. When a study reported several parameters from
the same cohort, the results were treated as correlated. True-positive, false-negative, false-positive, and true-negative counts
were reconstructed only when the published data yielded a unique integer solution.
2.4 Methodological quality and synthesis
Risk of bias and applicability concerns were assessed using QUADAS-3 across the participants, index test, targetcondition determination, and analysis domains (University of Bristol, 2024). Judgments were based on the information reported
in each study, and no overall numerical score was calculated. The assessment was performed by one reviewer and checked
against the recorded rationales and extracted data.
Studies were grouped according to the target condition, population, muscle examined, ultrasound parameter, and
reference standard. Quantitative synthesis was considered only for clinically comparable groups with verifiable 2 × 2 tables.
Under those conditions, sensitivity and specificity would be jointly estimated using a bivariate random-effects model or a
hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic model. When adequate comparability was not achieved, results were
presented individually and discussed in a narrative synthesis.
2.5 Ethical considerations
Because this review used only published data and information available in public registries, research ethics committee
approval and participant consent were not required.

3. Results
3.1 Study selection
The primary PubMed/MEDLINE search identified 1,401 records through September 8, 2026. After title and abstract
screening, 124 records proceeded to full-text assessment. Supplementary searches in Europe PMC and Crossref added nine
nonduplicate publications, and an updated search through September 17, 2026 identified seven additional publications, yielding
140 publications for assessment. Two study records without results were presented separately.

3.2 Characteristics of the diagnostic accuracy studies
The 45 publications included in the diagnostic accuracy synthesis were published between 2016 and 2026 and covered
community-dwelling, geriatric, hospital, and chronic-disease populations. Because the independence of cohorts described in
different publications could not always be confirmed, an aggregate participant total was not reported, thereby avoiding possible
double counting. The main study characteristics are summarized in Table 1.
Cross-sectional and prospective designs predominated. Populations were heterogeneous and included communitydwelling older adults, outpatients and inpatients, and groups with cancer, kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, Parkinson disease,
transplantation, liver disease, and other chronic conditions. Given this spectrum heterogeneity, studies in younger or highly
specific clinical populations were considered separately from the main geriatric population.
Reference standards were based mainly on AWGS 2019, EWGSOP2, or variations of these consensus criteria, with
muscle mass quantified using bioelectrical impedance analysis, DXA, or computed tomography. Operational definitions of the
target condition differed and included confirmed sarcopenia, isolated low muscle mass, probable sarcopenia, and modified
classifications. Consequently, findings carrying similar labels could not automatically be treated as equivalent.
The rectus femoris was the most frequently examined muscle, either alone or together with the vastus intermedius. Other
sites included the gastrocnemius, biceps brachii, brachioradialis, abdominal muscles, and multisite protocols. Index tests included
muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, echo intensity, muscle architecture, shear-wave elastography, normalized indices,
estimation equations, and artificial intelligence-assisted models. Cutoffs varied according to sex, muscle, position, normalization
method, and clinical condition.

3.3 Diagnostic performance and feasibility of quantitative synthesis
Twelve studies provided 2 × 2 tables that had been previously verified or could be reconstructed from exact
denominators and counts. Results based solely on rounded percentages were not converted into approximate counts. The
remaining diagnostic accuracy studies mainly reported AUC, sensitivity, or specificity without adequate denominators;
multivariable models; incomplete stratified results; or internally derived thresholds. When a study reported more than one model
or parameter, the findings were treated as correlated rather than as independent samples.
The updated search added two studies to the diagnostic accuracy synthesis and four to the secondary synthesis. One
record was not included because the target condition could not be confirmed from the available information.
No sufficiently homogeneous group was identified for meta-analysis. Three independent studies evaluated rectus
femoris cross-sectional area using complete consensus criteria; sensitivity ranged from 62.5% to 100.0%, and specificity ranged
from 53.8% to 92.0%. Differences in populations, diagnostic criteria, and cutoffs, together with the small number of cases in one
study, precluded a reliable pooled estimate. The findings were therefore presented individually in Figure 2.

3.4 Clinical role of ultrasound
Most studies evaluated ultrasound as a potential replacement or screening tool when DXA, computed tomography, or
other methods were unavailable. A smaller proportion incorporated ultrasound parameters into clinical, laboratory, or artificial
intelligence models. Most of these models, however, were developed and evaluated in the same sample.
Evidence of incremental value in the strict sense—a paired comparison of the conventional method alone with the same
method plus ultrasound—was insufficient. Improvements in AUC within composite models were therefore interpreted as
exploratory evidence of complementary potential rather than proof of additional clinical benefit.
3.5 Risk of bias and applicability
QUADAS-3 assessment was completed for 41 of the 45 publications in the diagnostic accuracy synthesis. Thirty-eight
studies had an overall high risk of bias and three had an overall unclear risk; none was classified as having a low overall risk. By
domain, risk was high in 12 studies for participant selection, 36 for the index test, 4 for target-condition determination, and 32
for analysis. The most frequent limitations were convenience or case-control sampling, incomplete descriptions of recruitment,
cutoffs derived and tested in the same sample, selection among multiple parameters, lack of external validation, poorly described
blinding, inconsistent denominators, and incomplete reporting of analyses.
Thirty-one of the 41 assessed studies raised high overall applicability concerns, whereas ten raised low concerns. The
main reasons were the inclusion of disease-specific populations, anatomical and technical variability in the index test, and the
use of target conditions other than confirmed sarcopenia. Four publications in the diagnostic accuracy synthesis were not assessed
with QUADAS-3. The domain-level judgments are summarized in Table 2.

4. Discussion
The findings of this review indicate that muscle ultrasound may assist in identifying sarcopenia, but it should not yet be
regarded as a stand-alone diagnostic test. Studies of community-dwelling older adults, hospitalized patients, and diverse clinical
populations have demonstrated discriminatory ability for muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, and mechanical properties. The
wide range of findings, however, shows that performance depends on the muscle examined, the protocol used, and the criterion
adopted to define sarcopenia (Barotsis et al., 2020; Sri-On et al., 2022; Zanotelli et al., 2024; Yoshida et al., 2024).
The rectus femoris was the most extensively investigated site. Its superficial position facilitates examination and favors
bedside use, which explains its inclusion in geriatric, cardiovascular, pulmonary, kidney, and oncology studies. Despite this
practical advantage, muscle thickness and cross-sectional area did not perform consistently. Rustani et al. (2019) found good
screening ability for muscle thickness, whereas Sri-On et al. (2022) and Yoshida et al. (2024) reported favorable findings in
older adults. More modest values were observed in hospitalized patients and some clinical populations (Zehnder et al., 2025;
Mondo et al., 2025).
Technical diversity also precludes treating “muscle ultrasound” as a single test. Thickness and area primarily reflect
muscle quantity, whereas echogenicity, elastography, and architectural measures are intended to represent composition and
mechanical properties. Studies of the biceps brachii, gastrocnemius, and quadriceps showed that several parameters may
discriminate sarcopenia, but their cutoffs were obtained in specific populations and were generally derived in the same sample
in which performance was evaluated (Li et al., 2020; Kuyumcu et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
2023; Shou et al., 2025).
The population examined was an important source of variation. In kidney disease, changes in fluid status may affect
both the bioelectrical impedance analysis used in the reference standard and muscle thickness or elasticity. Studies in
hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis populations reported promising findings but evaluated different muscles, criteria, and
models (Güner et al., 2024; De La Flor et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Chiu et al., 2025; de la Torre et al., 2026). A similar issue
arose in pulmonary disease, cancer, and Parkinson disease, where inflammation, treatment, activity level, and disease severity
may alter the relationship between ultrasound measurements and sarcopenia (Deng et al., 2021; Fernández-Jiménez et al., 2024;
Lim et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026).
Rectus femoris cross-sectional area was the only parameter supported by three independent studies with verifiable 2 ×
2 tables. Zanotelli et al. (2024), Lee et al. (2026), and de la Torre et al. (2026) reported sensitivities ranging from 62.5% to 100%
and specificities ranging from 53.8% to 92.0%. This spread cannot be attributed to chance alone: the studies involved hospitalized
older adults, community-dwelling women, and people receiving hemodialysis and used different diagnostic criteria and
thresholds. Under these circumstances, a pooled estimate would create an appearance of precision without representing a
clinically uniform protocol.
This interpretation is more conservative than those of earlier meta-analyses but does not contradict the potential they
identified. Fu et al. (2023) found low-to-moderate performance, with pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.72 for rectus femoris
thickness. Yang et al. (2025) estimated a sensitivity of 0.85 and specificity of 0.74 for muscle-thickness measurements in older
adults. Both reviews, however, acknowledged heterogeneity in muscles, cutoffs, and reference standards. By requiring
simultaneous comparability in population, test, target condition, and clinical role, the present review found an insufficient basis
for a single summary estimate.
In chronic kidney disease, the review by Yang et al. (2024) reported a sensitivity of 0.95 and specificity of 0.73 across
studies of cross-sectional area and elastography. More recent studies reinforce the feasibility of bedside assessment but also show
that morphological and mechanical parameters should not be combined as though they measured the same property. Moreover,

the influence of hydration requires protocols that specify the timing of examination relative to dialysis and the patient’s clinical

Composite models, radiomics, and artificial intelligence have expanded the range of analytical approaches. Favorable
findings have been reported for rectus femoris image-based models, combinations of clinical and ultrasound parameters, and
machine-learning systems (Yik et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; López-Gómez et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025). Most
of these models, however, were developed and tested in the same population. Without external validation, the apparent gain in
performance may reflect overfitting or the contribution of variables such as age, sex, and body mass index rather than the specific
value of ultrasound.
The QUADAS-3 assessment reinforces this concern. Thirty-eight of the 41 studies had a high overall risk of bias, mainly
because cutoffs were derived and evaluated in the same sample, multiple parameters were tested before the best-performing
result was selected, and external validation was uncommon. High areas under the curve should therefore be interpreted as initial
performance under study-specific conditions rather than confirmation that the same cutoff will perform similarly in another
population.
The studies by Rustani et al. (2019), Brockhattingen et al. (2025), Mondo et al. (2025), and Zehnder et al. (2025) help
clarify the most plausible clinical role of the method. Its speed, portability, and ability to assess patients with limited mobility
favor its use for screening or as an adjunct to conventional assessment. This role is particularly relevant where DXA or computed
tomography is not readily available. Even so, positive ultrasound findings should be interpreted alongside muscle strength,
physical performance, and clinical assessment, as recommended by sarcopenia consensus statements (Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019;
Chen et al., 2020).
Replacement of conventional methods would require between-operator reproducibility, prespecified cutoffs, and
consistent performance across centers. These conditions have not yet been demonstrated. Even studies reporting favorable results
used specific protocols for participant position, measurement site, and normalization, which limits the direct application of their
thresholds in other services (Barotsis et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2022; Sri-On et al., 2022; Yoshida et al., 2024).
Incremental value in the strict sense has likewise not been established. Some models achieved higher AUCs when
ultrasound parameters were combined with age, body mass index, albumin, or other variables, but few made a paired comparison
between a prespecified conventional model and the same model with ultrasound added. These findings therefore suggest
complementary potential but do not demonstrate improved clinical decision-making (Tang et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2025; Wang
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025).
The choice of cutoff should reflect the intended purpose of the examination. Greater sensitivity may be desirable in
screening to reduce false-negative results, whereas a replacement test would require simultaneously high sensitivity, specificity,
and reproducibility. Many studies used the Youden index in the development sample. Although this provides a statistical balance,
it does not account for prevalence, costs, or the clinical consequences of misclassification (Rustani et al., 2019; Sri-On et al.,
2022; Lim et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026).
Standardization remains essential. Participant position, anatomical landmark, transducer orientation and pressure,
contraction state, equipment settings, and image processing should be described in a reproducible manner. The SARCUS
recommendations provide an important foundation, but their application across studies remains inconsistent (Nijholt et al., 2017;
Perkisas et al., 2021). Simpler protocols may have greater clinical utility, provided that they retain adequate reliability and are
validated in different populations.
This review has limitations. The search focused on PubMed/MEDLINE and supplementary open sources, which may
have missed studies indexed exclusively in other databases. Study selection and methodological assessment were conducted

Future studies should recruit consecutive, representative samples, prespecify the clinical role of the test, and apply
consensus diagnostic criteria. Cutoffs should be defined before analysis or validated in independent cohorts. Complete 2 × 2
tables should be reported, interobserver agreement should be assessed, and the conventional approach should be directly
compared with the same approach supplemented by ultrasound.
Taken together, the evidence provides a favorable but cautious answer to the question addressed in this review. Muscle
ultrasound may complement sarcopenia assessment and offers clear practical advantages, particularly for screening and bedside
care. Its use alone as a replacement for DXA, BIA, computed tomography, or tests of strength and physical performance remains
insufficiently supported because no muscle, parameter, or cutoff has been consistently validated across populations.

5. Conclusion
Muscle ultrasound may complement conventional sarcopenia assessment, particularly as a portable screening and
bedside morphological assessment tool. The available evidence, however, does not yet support replacing established methods,
defining a universal parameter or muscle, adopting generalizable cutoffs, or claiming proven incremental clinical value.
Observed performance depends on the muscle, ultrasound parameter, technical protocol, population, and definition of
the target condition. Between-study heterogeneity, predominantly high risk of bias, and limited external validation precluded a
clinically defensible primary meta-analysis. The findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic potential
rather than definitive validation for stand-alone use.
Prospective multicenter studies using standardized protocols, prespecified thresholds, complete reporting of diagnostic
accuracy, and external validation are needed to determine the settings in which ultrasound offers additional benefit and how it
should be integrated with measures of strength, physical performance, and body composition.
