Multipliers of Beurling-Fourier algebras
Mahmood Alaghmandan, Olof Giselsson, Ebrahim Samei, Lyudmila Turowska

Abstract
For a locally compact group G G we introduce and study the reduced Beurling–Fourier–Stieltjes algebra, a weighted analogue of the reduced Fourier–Stieltjes algebra B r ​ ( G ) B_{r}(G) , together with the algebra of completely bounded multipliers of the associated weighted Fourier algebra. We show, in particular, that these two algebras coincide when G G is amenable. For a general locally compact group G G , we identify them as subspaces of B r ​ ( G ) B_{r}(G) and of the space of functions that locally belong to the Fourier algebra, respectively. Furthermore, we establish sufficient conditions on the group and the weight under which the algebra of completely bounded multipliers of the weighted Fourier algebra embeds into its unweighted counterpart.



1   Introduction

One of the standard function spaces used in the analysis of a locally compact group

G
is the Fourier algebra

A(G)
, introduced by P. Eymard
[
14
]
. It is defined as the unique predual of the group von Neumann algebra

VN(G)
, that is, as the associated noncommutative

L_{1}
-space. It is well known that

A(G)
can be realized as a Banach subalgebra of

C_{0}(G)
, and moreover, it determines the underlying group.

While
A(G)
has the drawback of a complicated norm structure, it has the significant advantage of being commutative. This allows one to study its Gelfand spectrum
\text{spec}A(G)
, which, as shown by Eymard, is canonically homeomorphic to

G
itself. This provides a nontrivial link between the structures of topological groups and Banach algebras.

On the other hand, the spaces of multipliers and completely bounded multipliers of

A(G)
have played a central role in the study of group properties such as amenability, weak amenability, the Haagerup property, and various approximation properties for the associated group

C^{*}
- and von Neumann algebras (see e.g.
[
6
,
12
,
7
]
). For example, a locally compact group

G
is amenable if and only if the multiplier algebra of

A(G)
coincides with the Fourier–Stieltjes algebra

B(G)
, the algebra generated by positive definite functions on

G
. The study of multiplier spaces has thus been a fundamental topic in abstract harmonic analysis
[
5
,
12
,
7
,
6
]
.

In recent years, several authors, including the second, third, and fourth authors, have investigated weighted versions of the Fourier algebra

A(G)
, known as Beurling–Fourier algebras

A(G,\omega)
, obtained by introducing weights that modify the norm structure of

A(G)

[
26
,
29
,
30
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
24
]
. When

G
is abelian, such algebras are isomorphic—via the Fourier transform—to classical Beurling algebras on the dual group
\hat{G}
, which explains the terminology.

In
[
29
,
18
,
20
,
24
]
, the Gelfand spectrum of Beurling–Fourier algebras was studied and realized as a subset of a suitable complexification of the underlying group, revealing interesting connections between harmonic analysis and geometry. Furthermore, in
[
30
]
, the existence of a bounded approximate identity for
A(G,\omega)

was characterized in terms of amenability of
G
, extending the classical Leptin–Herz theorem. More recently, weighted Fourier algebras have also been studied in the setting of compact quantum groups
[
16
,
25
]
.

In this paper, we initiate the study of a weighted analogue
B_{r}(G,\omega)
of the reduced Fourier-Stieltjes algebra
B_{r}(G)
defined using a weight inverse
\omega
on the dual of
G
.
We realize
B_{r}(G,\omega)

as a subspace of
B_{r}(G)
.
Since the dual of a Beurling–Fourier algebra
A(G,\omega)

is still
VN(G)
,
one can consider its completely bounded multipliers
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
.
Viewing these as functions on
G

we prove that

B_{r}(G,\omega)\subset M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)

and show that this inclusion is proper if and only if
G

is non-amenable, extending the corresponding result in the unweighted case.
Building on idea’s of Jolissaint in
[
21
]
when proving a characterisation of completely bounded multipliers of
A(G)
, as Herz-Schur multipliers, we show that
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
can be realized as a subspace of
A(G)^{\rm loc}
, the space of functions which locally belongs to
A(G)
. We further identify a broad class of groups for which
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is a subset of the completely bounded multipliers for the unweighted case. This is achieved by relating the problem to the structure of closed convex hulls of
G
-orbits of the multiplier algebra of the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{r}^{*}(G)
of
G
as well as the existence of traces on
C_{r}^{*}(G)
. Finally, we establish this inclusion for arbitrary groups under natural restrictions on the weight, such as central weights and weights induced from normal amenable subgroups, which constitute one of the main sources of examples of weights.

2   Preliminaries

Let
A
be a C
∗
-algebra, and let
(\pi,{\mathcal{H}})
be a
*
-representation of
A
. The closed linear span of
\{\pi(x)\xi:x\in A,\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}\}
is called the
essential subspace
of
(\pi,{\mathcal{H}})
. The
*
-representation
\pi
of
A
is said to be
nondegenerate
, if its essential subspace is
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Let
A^{**}
be the second conjugate of
A
and see it as the enveloping von Neumann algebra. We define the
multiplier algebra
of
A
as

M(A)=\{x\in A^{**}:xA\cup Ax\subseteq A\}.

It is easy to check that
M(A)
is a closed
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
A^{**}
and
M(A)=A
if and only if
A
is unital;
A
is moreover a closed ideal in
M(A)
.
If
(\pi,{\mathcal{H}})
is a faithful nondegenrate representation of
A
, then
M(A)
is
*
-isomorphic to

\{x\in B({\mathcal{H}}):x\pi(A)\cup\pi(A)x\subseteq\pi(A)\},

see
[
27
, Proposition 2.12.9]
.

Consider two
C^{*}
-algebras
A
and
B
and a
*
-homomorphism
\pi:A\rightarrow M(B)
. It is called
non-degenerate
if
\pi(A)B:=\{\pi(a)b:a\in A,b\in B\}
is dense in
B
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group, and let
\lambda^{G}
be the left regular representations of
G
. We will write
\lambda
instead of
\lambda^{G}
when there is no fear of ambiguity. We write
VN(G)
and
C_{r}^{*}(G)
for the von Neumann algebra and the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra of
G
, respectively, associated to
\lambda
. Let

\Gamma:VN(G)\rightarrow VN(G)\bar{\otimes}VN(G))

be the
co-multiplication
on
VN(G)
defined by
\Gamma(\lambda_{t})=\lambda_{t}\otimes\lambda_{t}
for every
t\in G
. Its restriction to
C^{*}_{r}(G)
, which we still denote by
\Gamma
, is a
*
-homomorphism

\Gamma:C^{*}_{r}(G)\rightarrow M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)).

Here and below we write
A\otimes B
for the minimal tensor product of
C^{*}
-algebras
A
and
B
.
Therefore,

\Gamma^{*}:M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G))^{*}\rightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G)^{*}=B_{r}(G),

where
B_{r}(G)
is the reduced Fourier-Stieltjes algebra of
G
.
As
\Gamma
is a non-degenerate
*
-homomorphism, it extends uniquely to a
*
-homomorphism

\Gamma:M(C^{*}_{r}(G))\rightarrow M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)).

We point out the following fact that we will use repeatedly throughout the article: The co-multiplication
\Gamma
has the property that either of the the sets

\{(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x):x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\}
and
\{(y\otimes 1)\Gamma(x):x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\}
spans a dense subset of
C^{*}_{r}(G\times G)\simeq C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
.

The topological dual
B_{r}(G)=C^{*}_{r}(G)^{*}
is a Banach algebra under the multiplication
B_{r}(G)\times B_{r}(G)\rightarrow B_{r}(G)
given by

(uv)(x)=\Gamma(x)(u\otimes v)

(2.1)

for all
u,v\in B_{r}(G)
and
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
. We note that, in (
2.1
), we consider
\Gamma(x)
as an element in
(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))^{**}\supset M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))
.

Finally,
B_{r}(G)
can be also identified with the weak* closure in the Fourier-Stieltjes algebra
B(G)\subset C_{b}(G)
of the matrix coefficients of the left regular representation with multiplication being pointwise multiplication. More precisely,
B_{r}(G)
is the algebra of matrix coefficients of representations
\pi
of
G
weakly associated to
\lambda
. For basic properties of
B_{r}(G)
and
B(G)
we refer the reader to
[
22
]
.

3   Weight inverses on the dual of locally compact groups

There are different ways to define weights on the dual of locally compact groups, see
[
18
,
26
,
29
]
. In this paper, we adopt the one proposed in
[
30
]
.

Definition
3.1

(
[
30
, Definition 2.1]
)
Let
G
be a locally compact group
G
. A
weight inverse
is an element
\omega
of
M(C^{*}_{r}(G))
with
\|\omega\|\leq 1
such that the following are satisfied:

(1)

The maps

C^{*}_{r}(G)\rightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G),x\mapsto x\omega

and

C^{*}_{r}(G)\rightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G),x\mapsto\omega x

have dense ranges;

(2)

there is
\Omega\in VN(G\times G)
with
\|\Omega\|\leq 1
such that

\omega\otimes\omega=\Gamma(\omega)\Omega.

Below, we highlight some properties of inverse weights that we will use further in the article.

Lemma
3.2

The element
\Omega\in VN(G\times G)
is a contractive right multiplier of
C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
, such that
\Gamma(x)\Omega\in M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))
for all
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G).
Moreover, for any
u,v\in B_{r}(G)
, the element
\Omega(u\otimes v)\in(C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))^{*}
,
determined by

\begin{array}[]{ccc}(\Omega(u\otimes v))(T):=(u\otimes v)(T\Omega),&\text{for $T\in C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)$,}\end{array}

(3.1)

extends to a bounded linear functional on
M(C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))
.

Proof.

For any
x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
, we have

(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x\omega)\Omega=(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)(\omega\otimes\omega)\in C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G).

Thus, by condition
(1)
on
\omega
(from Definition
3.1
) and the fact that the set
\{(1\otimes y)\Gamma(z):y,z\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\}
spans a dense subset of
C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
, we have

(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))\Omega\subseteq C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G).

Furthermore, this implies that
\Omega(u\otimes v)
as defined by (
3.1
) is a linear bounded functional on
C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
.
Thus it can be extended to a bounded linear functional on
M(C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))
as any bounded linear function on a C*-algebra extends (uniquely) to a bounded linear functional on its multiplier algebra.

\Box

Lemma
3.3

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then the kernels of
\omega
and
\omega^{*}
as operators in
{\cal B}(L^{2}(G))
, and kernels of
\Gamma(\omega)
and
\Gamma(\omega)^{*}
as operators in
{\cal B}(L^{2}(G\times G))
are trivial. Subsequently,
\operatorname{Ker}(\Omega)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Note that the set
\{x\xi:x\in C^{*}_{r}(G),\xi\in L^{2}(G)\}
is dense in
L^{2}(G)
. Also,
\omega C^{*}_{r}(G)
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)
by condition
(1)
of Definition
3.1
. Subsequently, we get
\{\omega x\xi:x\in C^{*}_{r}(G),\xi\in L^{2}(G)\}
is dense in
L^{2}(G)
. Therefore, the closure of
\operatorname{Ran}(\omega)
is equal to
L^{2}(G)
and hence
\operatorname{Ker}\omega^{*}
is trivial, as desired.

To prove the claim for
\Gamma(\omega)^{*}
, note that the linear span of
\{\Gamma(x)(1\otimes y):x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\}
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G\times G)
. Therefore, following the fact that
\omega C^{*}_{r}(G)
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)
, we have that the linear span of

\{\Gamma(\omega x)(1\otimes y)\eta:x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G),\eta\in L^{2}(G\times G)\}

is dense in
L^{2}(G\times G)
. Subsequently,
\Gamma(\omega)
has a dense range.

Similarly, as
\{(x\omega)^{*}:x\in C_{r}^{*}(G)\}
is dense in
C_{r}^{*}(G)
we get that the range of
\omega^{*}
is dense in
L^{2}(G)
and subsequently
\operatorname{Ker}\omega=\{0\}
. The arguments for
\operatorname{Ker}\Gamma(\omega)=\{0\}
are similar.
Now if
\Omega
has a non-zero kernel, then
\Gamma(\omega)\Omega=\omega\otimes\omega
cannot have a trivial kernel, a contradiction.

\Box

Remark
3.4

In
[
20
]
a weight inverse was defined equivalently as
\omega\in VN(G)
which satisfies condition (2) of Definition
3.1
and the condition
\operatorname{Ker}\omega=\operatorname{Ker}\omega^{*}=\{0\}
. Hence, by Lemma
3.3
, a weight inverse from Definition
3.1
is a weight inverse in the sense of
[
20
]
. This will be used when referring to
[
20
]
for properties of
\omega
and related algebras.

Proposition
3.5

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be locally compact groups and
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
be two weight inverses on
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively. Then
\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}
is a weight inverse on
G_{1}\times G_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma^{k}
be the co-multiplication of
VN(G_{k})
for
k=1,2
. Then for co-multiplication of
VN(G_{1}\times G_{2})
, denoted by
\Gamma^{12}
, we have

\Gamma^{12}(x\otimes y)=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id})(\Gamma^{1}(x)\otimes\Gamma^{2}(y))

where
x\in VN(G_{1})
and
y\in VN(G_{2})
and
\Sigma
is the flip map

\Sigma:VN(G_{2})\bar{\otimes}VN(G_{1})\rightarrow VN(G_{1})\bar{\otimes}VN(G_{2}).

Hence

\displaystyle(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})\otimes(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id}\left(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}\otimes\omega_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id}\left(\Gamma^{1}(\omega_{1})\otimes\Gamma^{2}(\omega_{2})\right)\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id}(\Omega_{1}\otimes\Omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma^{12}\left(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}\right)\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id}(\Omega_{1}\otimes\Omega_{2}).

Finally, we note that the maps
x\mapsto(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})x
and
x\mapsto x(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})
,
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G_{1}\times G_{2})
, have dense ranges as
C^{*}_{r}(G_{1})\odot C^{*}_{r}(G_{2})
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G_{1}\times G_{2})
. Hence, both conditions of Definition
3.1
satisfy for
\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}
so it is a weight inverse on
G_{1}\times G_{2}
.

\Box

4   Beurling-Fourier algebras

We can use weight inverse to construct the “non-commutative" analogue of Beurling algebras on locally compact groups, see for example
[
18
,
26
,
29
,
30
]
.

Recall first the Fourier algebra
A(G)
of
G
. It is the unique pre-dual of
VN(G)
and can be identified with the space of functions on
G
:

A(G)=\{g\ast\check{h}\;|\;g,h\in L^{2}(G)\}\subset C_{0}(G),

where
\check{h}(s)=h(s^{-1})
,
s\in G
, and

\xi\ast\check{\eta}(s)=\int\xi(t)\eta(s^{-1}t)dt=\langle\lambda_{s}\eta,\bar{\xi}\rangle;

A(G)
becomes a
commutative Banach algebra with respect to the pointwise multiplication and the norm given by

\|f\|_{A(G)}=\inf\|\xi\|_{2}\|\eta\|_{2},

where the infimum is taken over all possible decomposition
f=\xi\ast\check{\eta}
, see for example
[
22
]
. The duality between
VN(G)
and
A(G)
is given by

\langle u,T\rangle=(T\xi,\eta)

for
T\in VN(G)
and
u(s)=(\lambda_{s}\xi,\eta)=(\bar{\eta}\ast\check{\xi})(s)\in A(G);
here and through the rest of the paper we use
(\cdot,\cdot)
to denote the inner product on a Hilbert space and we keep notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for duality pairing between
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.

For
T\in VN(G)
and
f\in A(G),
we let
Tf\in A(G)
be given by

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\langle Tf,R\rangle:=\langle f,RT\rangle,&\text{for $R\in VN(G).$}\end{array}

The assignment
(T,f)\mapsto Tf
turns
A(G)
into a left
VN(G)
-module.

Definition
4.1

Let
G
be a locally compact group and
\omega
a weight inverse on it.
The corresponding
Beurling-Fourier algebra
is defined as

A(G,\omega):=\omega A(G)=\{\omega u:u\in A(G)\},

with

\|\omega u\|_{A(G,\omega)}:=\|u\|_{A(G)}\ \ \ (\forall u\in A(G)).

We have, for each pair
\omega u,\omega v\in A(G,\omega)
,

(\omega u)(\omega v)=\omega\Gamma_{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v))

and
\|\cdot\|_{A(G,\omega)}
is a norm making
A(G,\omega)
a commutative Banach algebra. Moreover, the dual of
A(G,\omega)
is
VN(G)
,
where the duality is given by

\langle\omega u,x\rangle_{\omega}:=\langle u,x\rangle,x\in VN(G),u\in A(G),

(see e.g.
[
20
, Proposition 2.3]
).
In particular, for each
u\in A(G)
and
s\in G
we get

(\omega u)(s)=\langle\omega u,\lambda_{s}\rangle=\langle\omega u,\lambda_{s}\omega\rangle_{\omega}.

(4.1)

It follows from
[
30
]
that
A(G,\omega)
is a completely contractive commutative Banach algebra (see also

[
18
,
20
,
26
,
29
]
). Furthermore, the spectrum of
A(G,\omega)
has been studied in
[
18
,
20
,
29
]
.
The following has been proved in
[
18
]
.

Theorem
4.2

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then there is a continuous injective embedding
\iota
from topological space
G
into the Gelfand spectrum of
A(G,\omega)
.

The following statement shows that the completely contractive Banach algebra structure on
A(G,\omega)
also behaves well with respect to the operator space projective tensor product.

Proposition
4.3

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be locally compact groups and
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
be two weight inverses on
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively. Then
A(G_{1}\times G_{2},\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})
is completely isometrically isomorphic to
A(G_{1},\omega_{1})\hat{\otimes}A(G_{2},\omega_{2})
as completely contractive Banach algebras.

Proof.

Note that both of the operator spaces
A(G_{1}\times G_{2},\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})
and
A(G_{1},\omega_{1})\hat{\otimes}A(G_{2},\omega_{2})
are preduals of
VN(G_{1})\bar{\otimes}VN(G_{2})
. Therefore, they are completely isometrically isomorphic through the mapping

\iota:(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})(u_{1}\otimes u_{2})\mapsto(\omega_{1}u_{1})\otimes(\omega_{2}u_{2})

where
u_{k}\in A(G_{k})
for
k=1,2
.
So we only need to prove that this mapping is an algebra homomorphism. Note that for each pair
u_{k},v_{k}\in A(G_{k})
,
k=1,2
,

\left((\omega_{1}u_{1})\otimes(\omega_{2}u_{2}),(\omega_{1}v_{1})\otimes(\omega_{2}v_{2})\right)\mapsto\left(\omega_{1}\Gamma^{1}_{*}(\Omega_{1}(u_{1}\otimes v_{1}))\right)\otimes\left(\omega_{2}\Gamma^{2}_{*}(\Omega_{2}(u_{2}\otimes v_{2}))\right)

is the multiplication in
A(G_{1},\omega_{1})\hat{\otimes}A(G_{2},\omega_{2})
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\iota((\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})\Gamma^{12}_{*}\left((\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id})(\Omega_{1}\otimes\Omega_{2})(u_{1}\otimes u_{2})\otimes(v_{1}\otimes v_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\iota((\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id})(\Gamma^{1}_{*}\otimes\Gamma^{2}_{*})\left((\operatorname{id}\otimes\Sigma\otimes\operatorname{id})(\Omega_{1}\otimes\Omega_{2})(u_{1}\otimes u_{2})\otimes(v_{1}\otimes v_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\iota((\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})(\Gamma^{1}_{*}\otimes\Gamma^{2}_{*})\left((\Omega_{1}\otimes\Omega_{2})(u_{1}\otimes v_{1})\otimes(u_{2}\otimes v_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\Gamma^{1}_{*}(\Omega_{1}(u_{1}\otimes v_{1})))\otimes\omega_{2}(\Gamma^{2}_{*}\left(\Omega_{2}(u_{2}\otimes v_{2})\right).

\Box

5   Reduced Beurling-Fourier-Stieltjes spaces

In this section we define a weighted analog of the reduced Fourier-Stieltjes algebra.

For
u\in B_{r}(G)
and
x\in M(C_{r}^{*}(G))
, we let
xu
and
ux
in
B_{r}(G)
be given by

\langle xu,y\rangle:=\langle u,yx\rangle\ \ \text{and}\ \ \langle ux,y\rangle:=\langle u,xy\rangle\ \ \ (y\in C_{r}^{*}(G)).

Definition
5.1

The reduced Beurling-Fourier-Stieltjes space of
G
is defined to be the Banach space

B_{r}(G,\omega):=\{\omega u:u\in B_{r}(G)\}

with
\|\omega u\|_{B_{r}(G,\omega)}:=\|u\|_{B_{r}(G)}
for each
u\in B_{r}(G)
.

If
u\in B_{r}(G)
, then
u(t)=(\pi(t)\xi,\eta)
,
t\in G
, for a non-degenerate representation
\pi
of
G
weakly associated to
\lambda
, and then
(\omega u)(t)=(\pi(t)\pi(\omega)\xi,\eta)
,
t\in G
.
We note also that the norm is well-defined follows from the density of the map
C_{r}^{*}(G)\to C_{r}^{*}(G)
,
x\mapsto x\omega
. Also,
we have

A(G,\omega)
is a subset of
A(G)
and
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is a subset of
B_{r}(G)
. The following shows that equality for the corresponding algebras occurs only in the trivial cases.

Corollary
5.2

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

The inclusion
A(G,\omega)
into
A(G)
is surjective.

(ii)

The inclusion
B_{r}(G,\omega)
into
B(G)
is surjective.

(iii)

\omega
is invertible in
M(C^{*}_{r}(G))
.

(iv)

\omega
is invertible in
VN(G))
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv) follows from
[
20
, Proposition 2.6]
. (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is trivial.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Suppose that the map
{\bf m}_{\omega}:B_{r}(G)\rightarrow B_{r}(G)
,
u\mapsto\omega u
, is a bijection, that is
{\bf m}_{\omega}
is invertible. Then so is

{\bf m}_{\omega}^{*}:C^{*}_{r}(G)^{**}\rightarrow C^{*}_{r}(G)^{**}

which is indeed given by
{\bf m}_{\omega}^{*}(x)=x\omega
(
\forall x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)^{**}
).
But since
C^{*}_{r}(G)^{**}
has an identity, there is some
\omega^{-1}\in C^{*}_{r}(G)^{**}
so that
{\bf m}_{\omega}^{*}(\omega^{-1})=1
i.e.
\omega^{-1}\omega=1
. This implies that
\omega\omega^{-1}(\omega x)=\omega x
for any
x\in C_{r}^{*}(G)
. However,
\omega(C_{r}^{*}(G))
is dense in
C_{r}^{*}(G)
and
C_{r}^{*}(G)
is weak*-dense in
C_{r}^{*}(G)^{**}
, showing that
\omega\omega^{-1}=1
. As
M(C^{*}_{r}(G))
is a C*-subalgebra of
C^{*}_{r}(G)^{**}
and C
∗
-algebras are inverse closed,
\omega^{-1}\in M(C_{r}^{*}(G))
.

\Box

We call a Banach algebra
{\cal A}

faithful
if it satisfies the following property: for
b\in{\cal A}
, if
aba^{\prime}=0
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in{\cal A}
, then
b=0
.

Theorem
5.3

Let
G
be a locally compact group and
\omega
be a weight inverse on it. Then
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is a completely contractive faithful Banach algebra with pointwise multiplication. In addition,
A(G,\omega)
is an ideal in
B_{r}(G,\omega)
.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in B_{r}(G)
. By Lemma
3.2
, we have
\Omega(u\otimes v)\in M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G))^{*}
. Therefore,
\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v))\in C^{*}_{r}(G)^{*}=B_{r}(G)
. Consequently,
\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v))\in B_{r}(G,\omega)\subset B_{r}(G)
.
Then, for each
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),x\omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(u\otimes v),\Gamma(x\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(u\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\Gamma(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma(\omega)\Omega(u\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega\otimes\omega)(u\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega u)(\omega v),x\rangle,

where the first two pairings and the last one is the
(B_{r}(G),C_{r}^{*}(G))
-pairing and the rest is the one between
M(C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))^{*}
and
M(C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G))
.

Hence
(\omega u)(\omega v)=\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v))
and therefore it belongs to
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. Furthermore, since the mappings
\Gamma^{*}
,

v\mapsto\omega v\ \ (v\in B_{r}(G)),\ \ \text{and}\ \ w\mapsto\Omega w\ \ (w\in B_{r}(G\times G))

are completely contractive, we have that the given product,

(\omega u,\omega v)\mapsto\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v))

(5.1)

is completely contractive.

Now let
v\in A(G)
and
u\in P_{r}(G)
, the space of positive definite functions weakly associated to
\lambda
. Then
v(s)=(\lambda_{s}\xi,\eta)
for some
\xi,\eta\in L^{2}(G)
.
Also, since
u
is positive definite, there is a GNS construction
(\pi,\mathcal{K},\zeta)
so that
\langle u,x\rangle=(\pi(x)\zeta,\zeta)
(
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
). Let
y\in C_{r}^{*}(G)
be arbitrary. Write also
\pi
for the corresponding group representation of
G
on
\mathcal{K}
. One has, in particular, that
\pi
is weakly associated to
\lambda
(see
[
22
]
).
Using
that
(y\otimes 1)\Gamma(x)\in C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
and
\pi
is nondegenerate,
for each
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle(\omega(u\cdot y))(\omega v),x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega\otimes\omega)(u\cdot y\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma(\omega)\Omega(u\cdot y\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(u\cdot y\otimes v),\Gamma(x\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(u\otimes v),(y\otimes 1)\Gamma(x\omega)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((\pi\otimes\text{id})((y\otimes 1)\Gamma(x\omega)\Omega)(\zeta\otimes\xi),\zeta\otimes\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((\pi\otimes\text{id})(\Gamma(x\omega))(\pi\otimes\text{id})(\Omega)(\zeta\otimes\xi),(\pi\otimes\text{id})(y\otimes 1)(\zeta\otimes\eta))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((\pi\otimes\text{id})\circ\Gamma)(x\omega)\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}),

where
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\mathcal{K}\otimes L^{2}(G)
. By Fell’s absorption principle, we have that there is a sum
\bigoplus_{i\in I}\lambda
which is unitarily equivalent to the group representation
\pi\otimes\lambda
. Having this equivalence, one can find vectors
\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}
so that,

((\pi\otimes\text{id})\Gamma(x\omega))\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=\big(\big(\bigoplus_{i\in I}x\omega\big)\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}).

But the map

z\mapsto\big(\big(\bigoplus_{i\in I}z)\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt(z\in VN(G))

is a normal functional in
VN(G)_{*}=A(G)
. Equivalently, there is some
\sigma\in A(G)
so that

\langle(\omega(u\cdot y))(\omega v),x\rangle=\langle\sigma,x\omega\rangle

for every
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Hence,
(\omega(u\cdot y))(\omega v)=\omega\sigma
which is an element in
A(G,\omega)
. Since every element of
B_{r}(G)
can be written as a sum of four elements in
P_{r}(G)
, and by Cohen’s factorization theorem
B_{r}(G)=B_{r}(G)\cdot C_{r}^{*}(G)
, we have that
A(G,\omega)
is an ideal in
B_{r}(G,\omega)
.

To prove that
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is faithful, suppose that
v\in B_{r}(G)
is fixed so that for every
u\in A(G)
,
(\omega v)(\omega u)=0
.
For an arbitrary pair
u^{\prime}\in B_{r}(G)
and
y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
, let
u
be
u^{\prime}\cdot y
as an element in
B_{r}(G)
.
Therefore, for each
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
we have

\displaystyle 0=\langle(\omega v)(\omega u),x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(v\otimes u)),x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle v\otimes u,\Gamma(x\omega)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle v\otimes u,\Gamma(x)\Gamma(\omega)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle v\otimes u,\Gamma(x)(\omega\otimes\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(v\otimes u^{\prime})\cdot(1\otimes y),\Gamma(x)(\omega\otimes\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle v\otimes u^{\prime},(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)(\omega\otimes\omega)\rangle.

But we know that the space spanned by
\{(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x):x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\}
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Meanwhile,
\omega\otimes\omega
, as an element in
M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G))
, has a dense range. Therefore, the space spanned by
\{(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)(\omega\otimes\omega):x,y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\}
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)
. But if
v\neq 0
, then there is some
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
so that
\langle v,x\rangle\neq 0
; consequently,
\langle v\otimes v,x\otimes x\rangle\neq 0
which is a contradiction.

\Box

We note that similar to the duality
A(G,\omega)^{*}=VN(G)
, one can readily show that

is the dual space of
C^{*}_{r}(G)
where for each

and

\langle\omega u,x\rangle_{\omega}:=\langle u,x\rangle

given that the second product

is from the duality
(B_{r}(G),C^{*}_{r}(G))
. Here we use
\cdot_{\omega}
to denote the action of elements of

on an element

i.e.

\langle\omega v,\omega u\cdot_{\omega}x\rangle_{\omega}:=\langle(\omega u)(\omega v),x\rangle_{\omega}

for every

.

Corollary
5.4

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then the linear span of

is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)
.

Proof.

To show this, note that if
\omega v\in B_{r}(G,\omega)

is such that
\langle\omega v,\omega u\cdot_{\omega}x\rangle_{\omega}=0
for all
u\in A(G)
and
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
, then
(\omega u)(\omega v)=0

for all
u\in A(G)
.
By the last part of the proof of Theorem
5.3
,
\omega v=0
.

\Box

We recall that a Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a
dual Banach algebra
if
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{*})^{*}
for a closed
\mathcal{A}
-submodule
\mathcal{A}_{*}
of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
. It is known that
B_{r}(G)
for locally compact groups
G
are forming dual Banach algebras. In the following we show that this is also the case for
B_{r}(G,\omega)
.

Lemma
5.5

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is a dual Banach algebra.

Proof.

To show that
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is a dual Banach algebra, it suffices to show that the dual action
\omega v\cdot_{\omega}x
(
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
and
v\in B_{r}(G)
) yields an element in
C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Recall that
C^{*}_{r}(G)^{*}=B_{r}(G)
and therefore,
C^{*}_{r}(G)
acts on
B_{r}(G)
continuously. Based on this action and by Cohen’s factorization theorem we have
v=v^{\prime}\cdot y
for some
y^{\prime}\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
and
v^{\prime}\in B_{r}(G)
. Moreover, as
(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)\in C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
, by Lemma
3.2
,
(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)\Omega
belongs to
C_{r}^{*}(G)\otimes C_{r}^{*}(G)
, and the slicing
\operatorname{id}\otimes v
on
C^{*}_{r}(G\times G)=C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)
maps the latter into
C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Using this, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,\omega v\cdot_{\omega}x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega u)(\omega v),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(u\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v,\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes(v^{\prime}\cdot y),\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(u\otimes v^{\prime})\cdot(1\otimes y),\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(u\otimes v^{\prime}),(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,\operatorname{id}\otimes v^{\prime}\left((1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)\Omega\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,\operatorname{id}\otimes v^{\prime}\left((1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)\Omega\right)\rangle_{\omega}.

Since
u\in B_{r}(G)
was arbitrary, it gives

\displaystyle\omega v\cdot_{\omega}x=\operatorname{id}\otimes v^{\prime}\left((1\otimes y)\Gamma(x)\Omega\right)\in C_{r}^{*}(G).

\Box

6   Weighted generalization of the Herz restriction theorem

To lift weights from subgroups we apply the theory of duality for subgroups developed in
[
36
]
. Here we mainly use the modern treatment of the topic in the language of locally compact quantum groups in
[
10
]
. Lifting of weights from subgroups to the full group has been considered and studied in the literature, see
[
18
,
26
,
29
]
. However, in this section, we develop a comprehensive approach based on inverse weights.

Let
H
be a closed subgroup of
G
. Then by
[
10
, Theorem 3.3]
and its proof (see also
[
22
, Proposition 2.6.6]
), there exists a normal injective
*
-homomorphism
\gamma:VN(H)\rightarrow VN(G)
such that

\gamma(\lambda^{H}_{s})=\lambda_{s}^{G}

(6.1)

where
\lambda^{G}
and
\lambda^{H}
denote the left regular representations of
G
and
H
, respectively.
Furthermore, the restriction of
\gamma
to
C^{*}_{r}(H)
, still denoted by
\gamma
, is a non-degenerate
*
-homomorphism
\gamma:C^{*}_{r}(H)\rightarrow M(C^{*}_{r}(G))
. In fact, using Cohen’s factorization theorem,
\gamma(C^{*}_{r}(H))C^{*}_{r}(G)
and
C^{*}_{r}(G)\gamma(C^{*}_{r}(H))
are equal to
C^{*}_{r}(G)
.
Let us again use
\gamma
to show the unique extension
\gamma
as a
*
-homomorphism from
M(C^{*}_{r}(H))
into
M(C^{*}_{r}(G))
.

Note that since
H
is a closed subgroup of
G
, the same holds for
H\times H
with respect to
G\times G
. Then using (
6.1
), we have that

\gamma\otimes\gamma:VN(H)\bar{\otimes}VN(H)\rightarrow VN(G)\bar{\otimes}VN(G)

(6.2)

and by
[
10

(\gamma\otimes\gamma)\circ\Gamma_{H}=\Gamma_{G}\circ\gamma

(6.3)

where
\Gamma_{G}
and
\Gamma_{H}
are the co-multiplication of
VN(G)
and
VN(H)
, respectively.

Proposition
6.1

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a closed subgroup
H
. If
\omega
is a weight inverse for
H
, then
\gamma(\omega)
is a weight inverse for
G
.

Proof.

As we saw before,
\gamma(\omega)
is an element in
M(C^{*}_{r}(G))
with
\|\gamma(\omega)\|\leq\|\omega\|\leq 1
. Let
\epsilon>0
and
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
be given. Since
C^{*}_{r}(G)=\gamma(C^{*}_{r}(H))C^{*}_{r}(G)
, there is a decomposition
x=\gamma(y)x^{\prime}
for
y\in C^{*}_{r}(H)
and
x^{\prime}\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Since
\omega C^{*}_{r}(H)
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(H)
, there is some
y^{\prime}\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
so that
\|\omega y^{\prime}-y\|<\epsilon/\|x^{\prime}\|
. Therefore,

\|x-\gamma(\omega)\left(\gamma(y^{\prime})x^{\prime}\right)\|\leq\|y-\omega y^{\prime}\|\|x^{\prime}\|<\epsilon.

Therefore,
\gamma(\omega)C^{*}_{r}(G)
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)
. A similar argument implies that
C^{*}_{r}(G)\gamma(\omega)
is dense in
C^{*}_{r}(G)
.

Note that since
H\times H
is a subgroup of
G\times G
, we have the following restriction from (
6.2
)

\gamma\otimes\gamma:M(C^{*}_{r}(H)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(H))\rightarrow M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)).

Subsequently, using (
6.3
) and condition
(2)
on
\omega
from Definition
3.1
, we get that

\displaystyle\gamma(\omega)\otimes\gamma(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\gamma\otimes\gamma)(\omega\otimes\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\gamma\otimes\gamma)(\Gamma_{H}(\omega)\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G}(\gamma(\omega))(\gamma\otimes\gamma)(\Omega).

Hence,
\gamma(\omega)
is a weight inverse for
G
.

\Box

Since
\gamma
is a normal injective
*
-homomorphism, its adjoint restricted to
A(G)
forms a mapping
\gamma_{*}:A(G)\rightarrow A(H)
.
As
\gamma
is injective and hence an isometry,
\gamma_{*}
is a surjection, (see
[
10
, Theorem 3.7]
). In fact, in the light of (

), it is easy to show that
\gamma_{*}(u)
is nothing but the well-known Herz restriction of elements of
A(G)
to
A(H)
. Here we give a generalization of this result to weighted Fourier algebras.

Proposition
6.2

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a closed subgroup
H
. For each weight inverse
\omega
on

,
the restriction map
u\mapsto u|_{H}
maps

onto
A(H,\omega)
.

Proof.

Let
u\in A(G)
. Therefore,
\gamma(\omega)u\in A(G,\gamma(\omega))
. Note that for each

and

, by (

), we have

\displaystyle\gamma(\omega)u(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\gamma(\omega)u,\lambda^{G}_{s},\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,\gamma(\lambda_{s}^{H})\gamma(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{*}(u),\lambda_{s}^{H}\omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\gamma_{*}(u),\lambda_{s}^{H}\rangle.

Therefore,
\gamma(\omega)u|_{H}=\omega\gamma_{*}(u)

. Since

\Box

7   Completely bounded multipliers of
A(G,\omega)

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Classically, it is known that every element of
MA(G,\omega)
can be considered as a bounded continuous function on the maximal ideal space of
A(G,\omega)
. But by Theorem
4.2
, we can restrict every element of
MA(G,\omega)
to a bounded continuous function on
G
. As
A(G,\omega)
is a dense subspace of
A(G)
, the restriction of
MA(G)
to
G
is injective, i.e. if there are two distinct element in
MA(G,\omega)
, their restrictions to
G
are also distinct.
In the light of this observation, from now on we identify every element of
MA(G,\omega)
, and in particular elements of
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
, the space of completely bounded multipliers on
A(G,\omega)
, with their (bounded continuous) restriction to
G
.
Recall that
A(G,\omega)^{*}=VN(G)
and hence has an operator structure inherited from
VN(G)
. In particular,
{\bf m}_{\phi}\in M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
if
{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}:VN(G)\to VN(G)
is completely bounded.

In this section and the next one, we investigate
M_{\rm cb}(A(G,\omega))
and some of its natural properties. We start with the following which is a generalization of a characterization of
M_{\rm cb}(A(G))
to the weighted case from
[
12
, Theorem 1.6]
. The proof is a modification of the original one and is presented here for the sake of completion.

Proposition
7.1

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
and let
\phi
be a function of

Proof.

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle CB(A(G,\omega),\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{C}))

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}CB(A(G,\omega),\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{C}))

Theorem

|\omega^{*}|
is also a weight inverse that admits the same Beurling-Fourier algebra

(i)
By the preceding proposition, for every
\zeta
and

\displaystyle\psi:(t,s)\mapsto\phi(ts^{-1})(\lambda_{s}^{*}\omega\lambda_{s}\zeta,\theta)\ ,\ G\times G\to\mathbb{C},

,

, be a representation of

\xi

Remark

(i) In Section

Theorem

G

Suppose otherwise, i.e. there is

C_{c}(G)
, the space of continuous functions on

and for every

,

\|\lambda_{s}g\lambda_{s}^{*}-\lambda_{s}h\lambda_{s}^{*}\|_{C^{*}_{r}(G)}=\|\lambda_{s}(g-h)\lambda_{s}^{*}\|_{C^{*}_{r}(G)}=\|g-h\|_{C^{*}_{r}(G)},

we may assume that

,

. Now, by our assumption, there are
\epsilon>0
and
\phi\in B_{r}(G)=C^{*}_{r}(G)^{*}
such that for every

\left|\int_{G}\phi(t)f(s^{-1}ts)dt\right|\geq\epsilon.

On the other hand, by
[
8
, Theorem, p.211]
,

has Kunze-Stien property so that there is

such that
\phi\in L^{p}(G)
(in fact, this holds for every

). Let
\psi\in C_{c}(G)
with

\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{p}(G)}<\frac{\epsilon}{2\|f\|_{L^{q}(G)}},

where

is the conjugate of

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
.
Then, by combining the above relations, letting

(\gamma_{G}(s)g)(t)=g(s^{-1}ts)\ \ \ (s,t\in G,g\in L^{p}(G)),

and noting that

is unimodular and hence

is an isometry, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{G}\phi(t)f(s^{-1}ts)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{G}(\phi(t)-\psi(t))f(s^{-1}ts)dt\right|+\left|\int_{G}\psi(t)f(s^{-1}ts)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{p}(G)}\|\gamma_{G}(s)f\|_{L^{q}(G)}+\left|\int_{G}\psi(t)f(s^{-1}ts)dt\right|

\displaystyle=\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{p}(G)}\|f\|_{L^{q}(G)}+\left|\int_{G}\psi(t)f(s^{-1}ts)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+|(\gamma_{G}(s)f,\bar{\psi})|.

Hence we have

|(\gamma_{G}(s)f,\bar{\psi})|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\ \ \ (s\in G).

Since

, we must then have that

is not weakly mixing

. Hence it must have a finite-dimensional subrepresentation
[
2
, Theorem 1.9]
.
In particular, it is an amenable representation in the sense of Bekka,
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
. But that is not possible because, by
[
1
, Theorem 2.4]
, the conjugate representation is amenable if and only if
G

is an inner-amenable group. However,
G
is connected so that it is inner-amenable if and only if it is amenable (see
[

, Theorem 1]
), and hence compact by
[

, Theorem 8.7.6]
, which is not the case here.

M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is a subspace of
M_{\rm cb}A(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
. Since
H
is normal,
G
acts on
H
by conjugation:

\displaystyle s\cdot h:=shs^{-1}\ \ \ (s\in G,h\in H).

This induces an action on
VN(H)
having both
C^{*}_{r}(H)
and
M(C^{*}_{r}(H))
as
G
-invariant C
∗
-subalgebras. In particular,

\lambda_{s}^{*}\gamma(C_{r}^{*}(H))\lambda_{s}\subset\gamma(C_{r}^{*}(H)).

Let
x\in C_{r}^{*}(H)
and write
x_{s}
for the preimage
\gamma^{-1}(\lambda_{s}\gamma(x))\lambda_{s}^{*})\in C_{r}^{*}(H)
.
As
H
is amenable, the functional
1\in L^{\infty}(H)
,
1:\lambda(g)\mapsto\int_{H}g(h)dh
,
g\in L^{1}(H)
, extends uniquely to a multiplicative linear functional on
M(C_{r}^{*}(H))
(see e.g. the proof of
[
30
, Lemma 4.2]
for details). Let
\chi:\gamma(C_{r}^{*}(H))\to\mathbb{C}
be given by

\chi(\gamma(a))=\langle a,1\rangle,a\in C_{r}^{*}(H).

Observe that for every
g\in L^{1}(H)
,

\gamma(\lambda^{H}(g)_{s})=\lambda_{s}^{*}\gamma(\lambda^{H}(g))\lambda_{s}=\int_{H}g(t)\lambda_{s^{-1}\cdot t}^{G}dt.

Therefore

\chi(\gamma(\lambda^{H}(g)_{s}))=\int_{H}g(t)\langle\lambda_{s^{-1}\cdot t}^{G},1\rangle dt=\int_{H}g(t)dt=\langle\lambda^{H}(g),1\rangle

and hence

\chi(\gamma(a_{s}))=\langle a,1\rangle,\ \ a\in C_{r}^{*}(H).

Let
\phi\in M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
. By extending
\chi
to a positive linear functional on
B(L^{2}(G))
,
it follows from the proof of Theorem
7.4
that
(t,s)\mapsto\phi(ts^{-1})\chi(\gamma((\omega_{H}f)_{s}))
is a Schur multiplier.
As
\chi(\gamma((\omega_{H}f)_{s}))=\langle\omega_{H}f,1\rangle\neq 0
for some
f\in C_{r}^{*}(H)
by the density condition on
\omega_{H}
, we obtain that
the mapping
(s,t)\mapsto\phi(ts^{-1})
is a Schur multiplier. This completes the proof.

\Box

It is known, for a locally compact group
G
,
M_{\rm cb}(A(G))
is a dual Banach algebra with the predual
Q(G)
obtained by completion of
L^{1}(G)
in the norm

\|f\|_{Q(G)}=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{G}f(s)u(s)ds\right|:u\in M_{\rm cb}A(G),\|u\|_{\rm cb}\leq 1\right\},

see
[
12
, Proposition 1.10]
.
In the following, we show that this is also the case for
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
.
To prove this, we need the following lemma.

Lemma
7.11

Let
G
be a locally compact group and
\omega
be a weight inverse on it. Then the linear space spanned by
\{(\omega u)(\omega v):u,v\in A(G)\}
is dense in
A(G,\omega)
.

Proof.

Let
{\cal A}
be the closure of the linear subspace constructed by
\{(\omega u)(\omega v):u,v\in A(G)\}
in
A(G,\omega)
.
If the inclusion
{\cal A}\subseteq A(G,\omega)
is proper, there is non-zero
x\in VN(G)
such that the restriction of
x
to
{\cal A}
is
0
. Therefore, as shown in the proof of Theorem
5.3
,

\displaystyle 0=\langle(\omega u)(\omega v),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma_{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(u\otimes v),\Gamma(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v,\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle.

Since
A(G)\odot A(G)
is dense in
A(G\times G)
, we get that
A(G\times G)
is a subset of the kernel of
\Gamma(x)\Omega
.
Let
\xi,\eta\in L^{2}(G\times G)
be chosen arbitrarily. Therefore, for
u\in A(G\times G)
given by

u(t,s)=((\lambda_{t}\otimes\lambda_{s})\xi,\eta)\ \ \ \ (t,s\in G),

we get

0=\langle u,\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle=(\Gamma(x)\Omega\xi,\eta).

But since by Lemma
3.3
,
\operatorname{Ker}(\Omega)=\{0\}
,
\Omega
has a dense range. Therefore, the last equality implies that
\Gamma(x)=0
which is a contradiction.
\Box

Let
A
be a completely contractive Banach algebra,
X
is right
A
-module and
Y
is a left
A
-module. The
A
-module tensor product of
X
and
Y
is the quotient space
X\hat{\otimes}_{A}Y:=X\hat{\otimes}Y/N
, where

N=\langle x\cdot a\otimes y-x\otimes a\cdot y\;|\;x\in X,y\in Y,a\in A\rangle,

and
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the closed linear span. It follows that

CB_{A}(X,Y^{*})\cong N^{\perp}\cong(X\hat{\otimes}_{A}Y)^{*},

where
CB_{A}(X,Y^{*})
denotes the space of completely bounded right
A
-module maps
\Phi:X\rightarrow Y^{*}
(see
[
3
, Corollary 3.5.10]
).

In
[
9
, Remark 5.9]
, J. Crann showed that, for a locally compact group
G
,
M_{\rm cb}A(G)
is completely isometric to the dual space of
A(G)\hat{\otimes}_{A(G)}C^{*}_{r}(G)
.
In the theorem below, we extend this result to Beurling-Fourier algebras and show that
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is a dual operator space.
It is known that
M_{\rm cb}A(G)
is a dual Banach algebra. We believe that this should hold for
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
as well.
By Theorem
8.3
and Lemma
5.5
, this is true for amenable groups.

Theorem
7.12

Let
G
be a locally compact with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is completely isometric to the dual space of
A(G,\omega)\hat{\otimes}_{A(G,\omega)}C^{*}_{r}(G)
.

Proof.

Consider
A(G,\omega)\hat{\otimes}C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Then it is know that
(A(G,\omega)\hat{\otimes}C^{*}_{r}(G))^{*}
is isometrically isomorphic to
CB(A(G,\omega),B_{r}(G,\omega))
by the equation
[
13
, 7.1.15]
. Now let
X
be the closed linear subspace of
A(G,\omega)\hat{\otimes}C^{*}_{r}(G)
given by

X:=\left\langle(\omega u)(\omega v)\otimes x)-(\omega u\otimes(\omega v)\cdot_{\omega}x):u,v\in A(G,\omega),x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\right\rangle.

Then
X^{\perp}\subseteq CB(A(G,\omega),B_{r}(G,\omega))
is a weak
∗
-closed subspace. Also, for each
T
in
CB(A(G,\omega),B_{r}(G,\omega))
we have that
T\in X^{\perp}
if and only if
T((\omega u)(\omega v))=T(\omega u)(\omega v)
. But, by Lemma
7.11
, we know that products are dense in
A(G,\omega)
and, by Theorem
5.3
,
A(G,\omega)
is an ideal in
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. Therefore,
T(A(G,\omega))\subseteq A(G,\omega)
. Consequently, there is some
\phi\in M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
so that
{\bf m}_{\phi}=T
. We have thus shown that
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is completely isometric to
X^{\perp}
, and hence
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is a dual Banach space where the weak
∗
topology on
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
is derived through embedding
M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)\rightarrow(A(G,\omega)\hat{\otimes}C^{*}_{r}(G))^{*}
given by

\langle{\bf m}_{\phi},(\omega u)\otimes x)\rangle=\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega u),x\rangle_{\omega}.

\Box

8   Completely bounded multipliers of
A(G,\omega)
for amenable groups

In the final section, we consider Beurling-Fourier algebras on locally compact amenable groups. We start with the following, which characterize the amenability of the group in terms of (bounded approximate) identities.

Proposition
8.1

Let
G
be a locally compact group with a weight inverse
\omega
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

G
is amenable.

(ii)

A(G,\omega)
has a bounded approximate identity.

(iii)

B_{r}(G,\omega)
includes the constant function
1
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) is the main result of
[
30
]
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Let
(v_{\alpha})
be in
A(G)
so that
(\omega v_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is a bounded approximate identity of
A(G,\omega)
.
Since the unit ball of
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is weakly
∗
compact (as a dual Banach space) and contains the net
(\omega v_{\alpha})
, there is a weak
∗
cluster point
\omega v_{0}
for the net in
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. We claim that
\omega v_{0}
is the constant function
1
. To do so, first note that for each
u\in A(G)
and
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle(\omega u)(\omega v_{0}),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega v_{0}),x\cdot_{\omega}(\omega u)\rangle_{\omega}

(8.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha}\langle(\omega v_{\alpha}),x\cdot_{\omega}(\omega u)\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha}\langle(\omega u)(\omega v_{\alpha}),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,x\rangle_{\omega}

where (
8.1
) is justified due to the fact that
B_{r}(G,\omega)
is a dual Banach algebra, by Lemma
5.5
, and
(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
may have been replaced by one of its subnets.
Hence
(\omega u)(\omega v_{0})=\omega u
for every
\omega u\in A(G,\omega)
. Therefore, we have that
\omega v_{0}\in B_{r}(G,\omega)
is the constant function
1

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is immediate if we recall that
B_{r}(G,\omega)\subseteq B_{r}(G)
and
1\in B_{r}(G)
if and only if
G
is amenable.

\Box

Recall that for each
u\in A(G)
and
x\in VN(G)
we have

\displaystyle\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega u),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,{\bf m}^{*}_{\phi}(x)\rangle_{\omega}

(8.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,{\bf m}^{*}_{\phi}(x)\rangle.

Now let us define
{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u)
given by

\langle{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u),x\rangle:=\langle u,{\bf m}^{*}_{\phi}(x)\rangle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt(x\in VN(G)).

Note that based on (
8.2
),
{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u)
is a normal map on
VN(G)
. Equivalently,
{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u)\in A(G)
for every
u\in A(G)
. One can readily show that for each
u\in A(G)
,

{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega u)=\omega{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u).

(8.3)

Proposition
8.2

Let
G
be an amenable locally compact group, and let
\omega
be a weight inverse on it. Then for each
\phi\in MA(G,\omega)
,
{\bf m}^{*}_{\phi}
maps
C^{*}_{r}(G)
into
C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Furthermore,
\phi
is also a multiplier of
B_{r}(G,\omega)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Since
G
is amenable, by Proposition
8.1
,
A(G,\omega)
has a bounded approximate identity. Also
C^{*}_{r}(G)
is an
A(G,\omega)
-bimodule, by Lemma
5.5
. Therefore, by Cohen’s factorization theorem, there is a
v\in A(G)
and
x^{\prime}\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
so that
x=\omega v\cdot_{\omega}x^{\prime}
. Now for each
u\in A(G)
we have

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega)u,x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega u),\omega v\cdot_{\omega}x^{\prime}\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega u)\omega v,x^{\prime}\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega u{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega v),x^{\prime}\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega u)(\omega{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(v)),x^{\prime}\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma_{*}(\Omega(u\otimes{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(v)),x^{\prime}\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{*}(\Omega(u\otimes{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(v)),x^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(v),\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\rangle.

Now note that
{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(v)\in A(G)
. Since
A(G)
is an
C^{*}_{r}(G)
-bimodule and
C^{*}_{r}(G)
has a bounded approximate identity, we have
v^{\prime}\in A(G)
and
y\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
so that
{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(v)=v^{\prime}\cdot y
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes(v^{\prime}\cdot y),\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(u\otimes v^{\prime})\cdot(1\otimes y),\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v^{\prime},(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,\operatorname{id}\otimes v^{\prime}\left((1\otimes y)\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega u,\operatorname{id}\otimes v^{\prime}\left((1\otimes y)\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\right)\rangle_{\omega}

But similar to the former proofs,
(1\otimes y)\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\in C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G)
, by Lemma
3.2
. So by slicing by
v^{\prime}\in A(G)\subseteq B(G)=C^{*}_{r}(G)^{*}
, we have that
\operatorname{id}\otimes v^{\prime}\left((1\otimes y)\Gamma(x^{\prime})\Omega\right)
belongs to
C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Since
u
was chosen arbitrarily, this proves that
{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
.

Next, we prove that
\left({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}|_{C^{*}_{r}(G)}\right)^{*}
is a multiplier of
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. To do so, first note that for each
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
and
u,v\in A(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v,\Gamma({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x))\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(u\otimes v),\Gamma({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x))\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma_{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}((\omega u)(\omega v)),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf m}_{\phi}(\omega u)\;\omega v,x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma_{*}(\Omega({\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u)\otimes v),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{*}(\Omega({\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u)\otimes v),x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}(u)\otimes v,\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v,{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id}(\Gamma(x)\Omega)\rangle.

Since the algebraic tensor product
A(G)\odot A(G)
is dense in
A(G\times G)
, we have

\Gamma({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x))\Omega={\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id}(\Gamma(x)\Omega).

Finally, consider
{\bf n}_{\phi}:=\left({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}|_{C^{*}_{r}(G)}\right)^{*}
. Similarly to the definition of
{\tilde{\bf m}}_{\phi}
, one can prove that there is a bounded mapping
{\tilde{\bf n}}_{\phi}:B_{r}(G)\rightarrow B_{r}(G)
so that

{\bf n}_{\phi}(\omega u)=\omega{\tilde{\bf n}}_{\phi}(u)

for every
u\in B_{r}(G)
. Now let
u,v\in B_{r}(G)
and
x\in C^{*}_{r}(G)
. Then

\displaystyle\langle{\bf n}_{\phi}((\omega u)(\omega v)),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega u)(\omega v)),{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma^{*}(\Omega(u\otimes v)),{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v,\Gamma({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}(x))\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\otimes v,{\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id}(\Gamma(x)\Omega)\rangle.

Here note that
\Gamma(x)\Omega
is an element in
M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G))
. So using universality of the multiplier algebra of
C^{*}
-algebras, one may consider
M(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G))
as a subspace of
(C^{*}_{r}(G)\otimes C^{*}_{r}(G))^{**}
and therefore,
({\bf m}_{\phi}^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id})^{*}|_{B_{r}(G\times G)}
would be nothing but
\tilde{\bf n}_{\phi}\otimes\operatorname{id}
. So we have

\displaystyle\langle{\bf n}_{\phi}((\omega u)(\omega v)),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\tilde{\bf n}}_{\phi}(u)\otimes v,\Gamma(x)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma^{*}(\Omega({\tilde{\bf n}}_{\phi}(u)\otimes v)),x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega\Gamma^{*}(\Omega({\tilde{\bf n}}_{\phi}(u)\otimes v)),x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\omega{\tilde{\bf n}}_{\phi}(u))\omega v,x\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf n}_{\phi}(\omega u)\omega v,x\rangle_{\omega}.

This proves the desired result.

\Box

Theorem
8.3

Let
G
be a locally compact group and
\omega
be a weight inverse on it. Then
MA(G,\omega)=M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)=B_{r}(G,\omega)
if and only if
G
is amenable.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is amenable then, by Proposition
8.1
,
A(G,\omega)
is a completely contractive Banach algebra which has a bounded approximate identity.
But
[
11
, Theorem 6.2]
implies that for such a Banach algebra, every multiplier is completely bounded, i.e.
MA(G,\omega)=M_{\rm cb}A(G,\omega)
.

Also, since
A(G,\omega)
is an ideal in
B_{r}(G,\omega)
,
B_{r}(G,\omega)
can be considered as a subset of
MA(G,\omega)
. On the other hand, by Proposition
8.2
, every element in
MA(G,\omega)
can be considered as a multiplier of
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. But since
G
is amenable, by Proposition
8.1
,
1\in B_{r}(G,\omega)
. This implies that
MA(G,\omega)
is a subset of
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. Ergo,
MA(G,\omega)=B_{r}(G,\omega)
.

Conversely if
MA(G,\omega)=B_{r}(G,\omega)
, then the constant function
1
belongs to
B_{r}(G,\omega)
. Then by Proposition
8.1
,
G
has to be amenable.

\Box
