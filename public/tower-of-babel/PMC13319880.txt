Network Analysis of Sleep, Mental Health, and Stress in Korean Dentists
Authors: Chisung Yuh, In-Seok Song, Heon-Jeong Lee, Young-Mee Lee, Chul-Hyun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsDentists are vulnerable to occupational stress, poor sleep, and related mental health problems. However, prior studies have treated sleep quality as a single aggregate score, obscuring which specific sleep dimensions are most closely connected with mental health and occupational stress. This study applied psychological network analysis to examine the interrelationships among individual sleep quality components, mental health indicators, and occupational stress in Korean dentists.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey of 229 Korean dentists was conducted in 2016. Six Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) components, the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Brief Encounter Psychosocial Instrument-Korean (BEPSI-K), Physician Job Stress (PJS), and Dentist Job Stress (DJS) scales were analysed. Centrality indices, bridge centrality, and bootstrap stability were computed.ResultsThe network revealed 31 non-zero edges among 11 nodes. The strongest edges were depression–anxiety (partial r = 0.60), physician–dentist job stress (0.42), and subjective sleep quality–daytime dysfunction (0.35). Depression showed the highest strength centrality (z = 1.65). Bridge centrality analysis identified daytime dysfunction (bridge strength = 0.385) and sleep latency (0.224) as the primary sleep-domain nodes connecting to mental health and stress domains. The network demonstrated excellent stability (CS-coefficient = 0.70).ConclusionDaytime dysfunction and sleep latency emerged as prominent bridge nodes linking sleep quality with mental health and occupational stress in dentists. These findings highlight specific sleep-related domains that may warrant attention in future screening and intervention studies.Clinical relevanceRather than treating poor sleep as a single construct, dental workforce well-being programmes could target daytime dysfunction and sleep latency as specific domains for screening and support. Early identification of depressive symptoms, which occupied a central position in the network, may help prevent broader deterioration of occupational functioning.Key words: Network analysis, Sleep quality, Depression, Occupational stress, Dentists

Introduction

The oral healthcare workforce faces well-documented occupational challenges. Dental practice requires prolonged static postures, sustained fine motor coordination, confined working environments, and high-stakes clinical decision-making under time pressure.1, 2, 3 These demands expose practitioners to significant psychosocial strain, and accumulating evidence indicates that dentists experience elevated levels of depression, anxiety, and occupational stress compared with other healthcare professionals.3,4 Impaired mental health in dental practitioners has implications not only for individual well-being but also for workforce sustainability, clinical performance, and the quality of oral healthcare delivery.

Sleep quality is an important determinant of professional functioning among healthcare workers. Poor sleep is associated with impaired cognitive function, diminished clinical decision-making, and increased risk of occupational errors.5,6 For dentists, sleep disturbances carry particular occupational significance, given that dental procedures demand sustained attention and precise motor control.7

Previous studies using this dataset have examined various aspects of mental health in Korean dentists. Song and Kim developed and validated the Dentist Job Stress (DJS) scale, demonstrating its association with depression and sleep quality.1 Song et al. investigated occupational stress as a predictor of depression, anxiety, and sleep quality using multiple regression.2 Yuh et al. applied latent profile analysis to identify 3 distinct mental health profiles. While these studies provided valuable insights, they share common methodological features: regression-based methods examine predictors of a single outcome, and latent profile analysis classifies individuals without revealing specific inter-variable pathways.7 Importantly, all 3 approaches treated sleep quality as a unidimensional construct (global PSQI score), leaving unresolved which specific sleep dimensions are most closely connected with mental health and occupational stress.8,9

Psychological network analysis offers a complementary framework for examining these relationships.10 In this approach, psychological variables are modelled as mutually related nodes within a network of conditional associations.11 The network structure reveals how variables cluster, which are most central, and which serve as bridges between clinical domains.12 This methodology has been increasingly applied to the interplay between sleep and mental health in various populations,13,14 but no study has examined the network structure of sleep quality components, mental health, and occupational stress specifically in dental professionals.

The present study addresses this gap by applying psychological network analysis to delineate the conditional associations among individual PSQI components, depressive symptoms, trait anxiety, psychosocial stress, and both general and dentist-specific occupational stress. Specifically, we aimed to1: estimate the network structure using a Gaussian Graphical Model2; identify the most central nodes3; determine which sleep components serve as bridge nodes connecting the sleep domain to mental health and stress; and4 evaluate the robustness of the network through bootstrap analyses.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study utilised data from an online survey of Korean dentists conducted between September and November 2016. Telephone numbers of licensed dentists actively treating patients were obtained through local branches of the Korean Dental Association and related academic societies. Survey links (SurveyMonkey) were sent via text message to approximately 1520 dentists. After excluding incomplete responses (n = 52) and those without informed consent (n = 1237), the final sample comprised 229 dentists. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dankook University (IRB No. 2016-07-024-002) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measures

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

Sleep quality was assessed using the Korean version of the PSQI,15,16 a 19-item instrument generating 7 component scores (each 0-3): subjective sleep quality (C1; overall self-rating of sleep), sleep latency (C2; time taken to fall asleep and frequency of difficulty initiating sleep), sleep duration (C3; total hours slept per night), habitual sleep efficiency (C4; ratio of time asleep to time in bed), sleep disturbances (C5; frequency of nocturnal awakenings due to specific causes), use of sleeping medication (C6), and daytime dysfunction (C7; extent to which sleep quality impairs daytime functioning). A global score >5 indicates poor sleep quality. Six components were included as network nodes; C6 was excluded because 92.6% of participants scored zero, yielding a zero-inflated distribution (skewness = 4.32) incompatible with the Gaussian Graphical Model.

Mental health measures

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Korean version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D),17,18 a 20-item scale (total range 0-60; ≥16 indicating risk for clinical depression). Trait anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory Form Y-2 (STAI),19 a 20-item scale (range 20-80). Psychosocial stress was measured using the Brief Encounter Psychosocial Instrument-Korean version (BEPSI-K),20 a 5-item questionnaire (mean score; ≥2.4 indicating the stressed group).

Occupational stress measures

The Physician Job Stress scale (PJS) is a 9-item abbreviated version of the Doctor Job Stress scale,21, 22, 23 scored on a 5-point Likert scale (sum range 9-45). The Dentist Job Stress scale (DJS) is a 6-item instrument specifically developed and validated for measuring dental practice-related stress,1 also scored on a 5-point scale (sum range 6-30).1

Statistical analysis

The psychological network was estimated using a Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM), which represents pairwise conditional independence relationships as partial correlations, thereby controlling for the influence of all other variables included in the network, though not for external covariates.11 The nonparanormal transformation was applied to all variables prior to estimation, relaxing multivariate normality assumptions and accommodating the ordinal nature of PSQI components.24 The network was regularised using the graphical LASSO with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC; gamma = 0.5),25,26 producing a sparse network retaining only robust edges.

Node strength and expected influence were computed as primary centrality indices, reported as standardised z-scores.11,27 Betweenness and closeness centrality were calculated for descriptive completeness but should be interpreted with caution given known instability concerns in psychological networks.28 Bridge centrality was calculated to identify nodes connecting predefined communities12: (1) Sleep Quality (C1-C5, C7), (2) Mental Health (CES-D, STAI), and (3) Job Stress (BEPSI-K, PJS, DJS). Network accuracy was evaluated through nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 iterations) for edge weight confidence intervals and case-dropping bootstrap (500 iterations) for centrality stability, with the correlation stability (CS) coefficient reported.

All analyses were performed using Python 3.11 with NumPy,29 SciPy,30 scikit-learn,31 NetworkX,32 and Matplotlib.33

To examine whether demographic factors influence the network structure, we additionally conducted a Network Comparison Test (NCT).34 The NCT is a permutation-based procedure that tests the equivalence of network structures between two independent groups. We used the R package Network Comparison Test with 1000 permutations and tested 2 aspects: (1) network structure (M-statistic; the largest absolute edge difference) and (2) global strength (S-statistic; the sum of absolute edge weights). We selected sex as the primary comparison variable for 2 reasons. First, sex is the most consistently reported moderator of sleep, mental health, and occupational stress associations in prior literature,35, 36, 37 and demonstrating network invariance across the most theoretically suspected moderator therefore constitutes a strong test of robustness. Second, sex provided a well-powered binary comparison in our sample, with adequate sample sizes in both subgroups (male n = 155; female n = 74).

Results

A total of 229 dentists completed all survey instruments. The demographic and occupational characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. The majority were male (67.7%), married (75.4%), and worked in private practice (66.4%). The mean age was 41.6 ± 9.7 years (range: 25-61). Over half of the participants reported working 8 to 10 hours per day (56.1%), and 54.6% rated their job satisfaction as average (Table 1).

The three strongest edges were: depression–anxiety (partial r = 0.60; 95% CI 0.51-0.65), physician–dentist job stress (0.42; 95% CI 0.31-0.50), and subjective sleep quality–daytime dysfunction (0.35; 95% CI 0.23-0.44). Edge weight difference tests (Supplementary Figure 3) confirmed that the depression–anxiety edge was significantly stronger than all others. Notable cross-domain edges included depression–psychosocial stress (0.18), anxiety–psychosocial stress (0.18), sleep latency–depression (0.17), daytime dysfunction–depression (0.13), and daytime dysfunction–psychosocial stress (0.13). Within the sleep domain, subjective sleep quality (C1) was connected to daytime dysfunction (C7; 0.35), sleep latency (C2; 0.18), sleep disturbances (C5; 0.16), and sleep duration (C3; 0.16). The bivariate correlation matrix is presented in Supplementary Figure 2 for comparison with the partial correlation network.

Centrality indices

Figure 2 presents standardised centrality indices. Depression had the highest strength centrality (z = 1.65), followed by subjective sleep quality (C1; z = 1.06) and anxiety (z = 1.05). The same ordering held for expected influence. Sleep efficiency (C4; z = −1.63) and sleep duration (C3; z = −1.52) were the least central nodes, suggesting these sleep dimensions are relatively peripheral in the broader symptom network.

Bridge centrality

Bridge centrality analysis (Figure 3) revealed that daytime dysfunction (C7) had the highest bridge strength (0.385) among sleep-domain nodes, followed by sleep latency (C2; 0.224) and subjective sleep quality (C1; 0.142). These three components appear to function as the primary pathways linking sleep to mental health and occupational stress. From the mental health domain, depression (bridge strength = 0.628) and anxiety (0.449) showed high bridge centrality. In the stress domain, psychosocial stress (BEPSI-K; 0.569) exhibited the highest bridge strength, connecting to both sleep and mental health variables.

Network stability and accuracy

The case-dropping bootstrap analysis (Figure 4) demonstrated excellent stability. The CS-coefficient was 0.70 for both strength and expected influence, well above the recommended threshold of 0.50.11 Edge weight bootstrap confidence intervals (Supplementary Figure 1) confirmed that 15 of 55 edges were statistically significant, with the narrowest intervals for the strongest edges.

Sensitivity analysis: network comparison by sex

To examine the potential influence of sex on the network structure, we conducted an NCT comparing male (n = 155) and female (n = 74) dentists. The analysis revealed no significant differences in either network structure (M = 0.199, P = .107) or global strength (S = 0.876, P = .397), supporting the appropriateness of analysing the sample as a whole. Detailed statistics are presented in Supplementary Table 1, and a visual comparison of the male and female networks is shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply psychological network analysis to examine the conditional associations among individual sleep quality components, mental health indicators, and occupational stress in dental professionals. By examining individual PSQI components rather than the global PSQI score, this study identifies specific sleep domains that appear more closely connected with mental health and occupational stress than would be apparent in variable-aggregated analyses.1,2,7

Bridge nodes: daytime dysfunction and sleep latency

The most notable finding is the identification of daytime dysfunction (C7) and sleep latency (C2) as the primary bridge nodes connecting the sleep domain to mental health and stress. Daytime dysfunction (C7), which reflects the extent to which sleep quality impairs an individual's ability to function during waking hours, exhibited the highest bridge strength (0.385) among sleep components and was directly connected to depression and psychosocial stress. Sleep latency (C2), which measures the time taken to fall asleep and the frequency of difficulty initiating sleep, was the second most prominent bridge node (bridge strength = 0.224) and was directly connected to depression (partial r = 0.17). This is consistent with recent network studies identifying daytime dysfunction as a pivotal node linking sleep problems to broader psychopathology.13,14

The bridge role of daytime dysfunction may be relevant to occupational performance in dentistry, where sustained concentration and fine motor control are required.6,38 However, the present cross-sectional data do not allow conclusions about temporal ordering or causality. Whether daytime dysfunction is a consequence of poor nocturnal sleep, a manifestation of underlying depression, or both remains to be determined in longitudinal studies.

Sleep latency, the second most prominent bridge node (bridge strength = 0.224), was directly connected to depression (partial r = 0.17). Cognitive hyperarousal – characterised by rumination and worry at bedtime – has been proposed as a shared correlate of prolonged sleep latency and depression,39,40 which may be consistent with this bridge connection. As partial correlations are undirected, the bridge role of these nodes should be interpreted as strong cross-domain conditional associations rather than as causal mediators.41

Central role of depression

Depression emerged as the most central node (strength z = 1.65), directly linked to anxiety (0.60), psychosocial stress (0.18), sleep latency (0.17), daytime dysfunction (0.13), and dentist-specific job stress (0.11). The depression–anxiety edge was the strongest connection and significantly stronger than all other edges, consistent with the well-documented comorbidity between these conditions.42 The high centrality of depression, together with the finding that 43.7% of participants exceeded the CES-D cutoff, supports the importance of considering routine mental health screening and support pathways within the dental workforce.

Dual stress connectivity patterns

An informative structural feature is the distinction between general and dentist-specific stress pathways. Psychosocial stress (BEPSI-K) showed the highest bridge strength (0.569) among stress-domain nodes and was directly connected to both depression and anxiety, as well as daytime dysfunction. In contrast, dentist-specific job stress (DJS) was connected primarily to anxiety and depression, with a strong within-domain link to physician job stress (0.42). These findings suggest that general psychosocial stress has broader cross-domain connectivity, while dentist-specific stress shows its strongest cross-domain connections via anxiety and general stress.

Implications for dental workforce well-being

The network perspective suggests several concrete applications for the dental workforce. For screening, brief assessment of daytime dysfunction and sleep onset difficulties could be incorporated into routine occupational health evaluations for dentists, as these bridge nodes may signal broader sleep–mental health difficulties. For intervention targets, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) – which targets sleep latency through stimulus control and sleep restriction – could be adapted for dental professionals.43 Addressing daytime dysfunction through workplace scheduling modifications, such as optimising break patterns during clinical sessions, could also be explored. Chronotherapeutic approaches targeting circadian regulation of sleep onset and daytime alertness may also warrant investigation in this population.44 For workplace programmes, dental associations or hospital dental departments could develop targeted well-being interventions focusing on sleep hygiene education with particular attention to the identified bridge nodes, rather than treating sleep quality as a unitary construct. More broadly, while the present study focused on subjective sleep quality, parallel evidence linking psychological distress to other stress-related dental health concerns – such as bruxism in stress-exposed populations – supports the wider relevance of integrating mental health considerations into dental occupational health frameworks.45

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the partial correlations represent conditional associations, not causal effects. Future studies employing longitudinal or time-series network approaches (eg, temporal and contemporaneous network models) are needed to clarify the directional and dynamic relationships among sleep, mental health, and occupational stress in dentists.41 Second, the sample size (n = 229), while adequate for the 11-node network,11 limits the detection of weaker edges. Third, the response rate (15.2%) introduces potential selection bias. Fourth, all measures were self-reported, which may introduce common method variance and inflate the observed associations. The partial correlation structure of the GGM partially mitigates this concern by removing variance shared with all other variables, thereby reducing – though not eliminating – the influence of a common method factor. Future studies could incorporate objective measures (eg, actigraphy for sleep, physiological stress markers) to complement self-report data. Fifth, the C6 (sleep medication) component of the PSQI was excluded from the network because of its strongly zero-inflated distribution in the present sample, which provided minimal statistical variance for stable estimation in the regularized GGM. Although this exclusion may omit some clinically meaningful information, sleep medication use was rare in this working-age dentist sample. Future studies in clinical populations or older cohorts with higher prevalence of sleep medication use are needed to evaluate the role of C6 within the network structure. Seventh, the data were collected in 2016, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, and occupational stressors and sleep patterns in dental practice may have shifted substantially in subsequent years. Although the present findings provide a useful pre-pandemic baseline of the structural interrelations among sleep, mental health, and stress in Korean dentists, replication studies with post-pandemic data are needed to verify the generalizability of these network associations to current occupational contexts. Eighth, PSQI component scores are ordinal (0-3); although the nonparanormal transformation accommodates non-normality, future studies might additionally examine polychoric-based networks as a sensitivity analysis. Finally, the variables included were determined by the instruments administered in the original survey and do not encompass all potentially relevant constructs (eg, burnout, personality traits, physical health conditions, or lifestyle factors). Additionally, the a priori community definitions, while theoretically grounded, may not fully reflect the underlying structure, and data-driven community detection might yield different groupings that could affect bridge centrality estimates. Future work could incorporate broader variable coverage and data-driven community detection.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the utility of psychological network analysis in clarifying which sleep-related domains are most closely linked with mental health and occupational stress in Korean dentists. Daytime dysfunction and sleep latency emerged as candidate bridge targets, while depression occupied a central position in the overall network. These findings identify specific sleep and mental health domains that may inform future longitudinal and intervention studies aimed at supporting the well-being and occupational functioning of dental practitioners.
