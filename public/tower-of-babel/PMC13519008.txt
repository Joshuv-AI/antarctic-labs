Retrospective GIS-based spatial analysis of dengue case distribution across housing typologies in Malaysia
Authors: Muhammad Syamsul Hazry Yahya, Siti Aekbal Salleh, Nabilah Naharudin, Eran Sadek Said Md Sadek, Rahmat Dapari, Ahmad Mohiddin Mohd Ngesom, Zul Aizat Mohamad Fisal, Nazri Che Dom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue transmission remains a major public health challenge in rapidly urbanising regions, yet spatial risk patterns may vary across residential environments. This study investigated how housing typology, surrounding land use, and built–vegetation interfaces may represent environmental zones associated with elevated dengue occurrence in Kuala Selangor, Malaysia. Using validated georeferenced dengue surveillance records collected between 2020 and 2024, we applied GIS-based spatial analyses across three dominant residential housing typologies: terrace/landed housing, high-rise housing, and traditional/rural housing. Terrace housing demonstrated relatively stronger and more spatially continuous clustering patterns compared with other housing environments. Dengue occurrence in these areas was more closely associated with dense built-up surroundings and nearby vegetation. High-rise housing showed more localized clustering within highly built environments, whereas traditional and rural housing exhibited broader but weaker clustering patterns across more environmentally mixed landscapes. Overall, the findings suggest that dengue spatial distribution patterns differ according to housing structure and surrounding environmental context. The built–vegetation interface may represent an important environmental zone associated with elevated residential dengue occurrence, particularly in dense terrace housing settings. These findings highlight the importance of adapting vector control strategies to local residential environments, including targeted management of peri-domestic vegetation in terrace housing, infrastructure-focused interventions in high-rise settings, and broader environmental management approaches in rural areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57679-9.

Introduction

Dengue fever remains one of the most significant mosquito-borne diseases globally, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where environmental conditions favour the proliferation of Aedes mosquitoes1,2. Rapid urbanisation, population growth, and environmental transformation have intensified dengue transmission, especially in Southeast Asia, including Malaysia3. The disease is primarily transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus, which are highly adapted to human environments and exploit both artificial and natural water-holding containers as breeding habitats4. Despite ongoing vector control strategies, dengue incidence continues to rise, suggesting that current approaches may not fully capture the complex spatial and environmental determinants of transmission.

Existing studies have demonstrated that dengue risk is closely associated with land use characteristics, urban density, and environmental features such as vegetation and water bodies5,6. Conventional control strategies, including source reduction, fogging, and larviciding, are often applied uniformly across regions without considering spatial heterogeneity7. Recent advances in spatial epidemiology and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have enabled more refined analyses of dengue distribution, including hotspot mapping and environmental risk assessment8,9. However, many studies treat urban environments as homogeneous, overlooking the role of housing typology and its interaction with surrounding land use in shaping transmission dynamics10–12. At the local level, Malaysia continues to experience recurrent dengue outbreaks, with districts such as Kuala Selangor exhibiting a mix of urban, peri-urban, and rural environments13,14. This heterogeneity presents a critical challenge for vector control, as transmission patterns may vary substantially across different housing structures and environmental contexts. Current surveillance systems provide detailed case data, yet there remains a gap in translating these data into multi-scale, typology-specific risk insights that can inform targeted interventions. In particular, the role of the built–vegetation interface as a potential microenvironment for vector proliferation has not been systematically quantified across different housing types15. Although climatic conditions and vector abundance are recognized as important determinants of dengue transmission, the present study primarily focused on residential spatial structure, land-use composition, and built–vegetation interface associations within a retrospective GIS-based analytical framework.

Previous studies have shown that dengue transmission is frequently associated with peri-domestic environmental conditions where human settlements intersect with vegetation, water-associated environments, and fragmented urban ecological landscapes16. These transitional built–vegetation interfaces may create environmentally favorable microhabitats for Aedes mosquitoes by providing shade, resting sites, humidity retention, and abundant artificial breeding containers within close proximity to human populations17. Several spatial and entomological studies have reported increased mosquito abundance and dengue occurrence near vegetated residential edges, particularly in densely built peri-urban environments where ecological and residential features coexist at fine spatial scales18. Such edge-associated environmental heterogeneity may contribute to localized clustering of dengue transmission, although the magnitude and spatial organization of these relationships may differ across residential housing typologies19.

Previous studies have demonstrated associations between dengue occurrence, urban land use, and environmental characteristics20–22. However, many spatial epidemiological studies continue to treat urban environments as relatively homogeneous despite substantial variation in housing structure, settlement density, and surrounding ecological conditions. Comparative spatial analyses across residential housing typologies remain limited, particularly in rapidly urbanising Malaysian settings where built and vegetated environments frequently coexist at fine spatial scales. To address this gap, the present study investigated how dengue spatial distribution patterns vary across three dominant residential housing typologies in Malaysia: terrace/landed housing, high-rise housing, and traditional/rural housing. Using GIS-based spatial analyses, the study examined differences in clustering behaviour, surrounding land-use composition, and proximity to built–vegetation interfaces to better understand how environmental context may influence residential dengue risk.

Although the spatial analytical techniques employed in this study, including Kernel Density Estimation (KDE), Ripley’s K-function, buffer analysis, and distance–decay assessment, are well-established epidemiological approaches, the novelty of the present study lies in the integration of these complementary spatial methods within a unified housing typology-specific analytical framework. Rather than applying isolated hotspot mapping alone, the study comparatively examined spatial clustering behaviour, surrounding land-use composition, and built–vegetation interface associations across terrace/landed, high-rise, and traditional/rural housing environments using individual-level georeferenced dengue surveillance records. This integrated multi-scale spatial epidemiological framework provides a more environmentally contextualized assessment of residential dengue risk and highlights how heterogeneous urban and peri-urban residential structures may influence observed spatial transmission patterns. The study therefore contributes to applied spatial epidemiology by demonstrating how housing-specific environmental characterization may support more targeted environmental surveillance and context-sensitive vector control planning in heterogeneous residential landscapes.

Material and method

Study site and population

This study was conducted in Kuala Selangor District, Selangor, Malaysia, a coastal region characterised by a mix of urban, peri-urban, and rural environments (Fig. 1). The district comprises several mukim, including Kuala Selangor, Ijok, Jeram, Api-Api, Pasangan, Bestari Jaya, Ulu Tinggi, Tanjong Karang, and Ujong Permatang, representing diverse land use types such as built-up areas, agricultural land, vegetation, and water-associated environments23. This heterogeneity provides a suitable setting for examining spatial variation in dengue transmission. Residential areas were categorised into three dominant residential housing typologies: (i) terrace/landed housing, typically located in urban and peri-urban zones with high population density and close housing proximity; (ii) high-rise housing (apartments and condominiums), representing vertically structured and densely populated environments; and (iii) traditional/rural housing, characterised by low-density settlements embedded within mixed ecological landscapes such as crops, vegetation, and water bodies. These typologies differ in structural configuration, population density, and environmental context, enabling comparative analysis of transmission dynamics. These three housing typologies represented the dominant residential categories associated with dengue case occurrence within the study area and therefore formed the primary analytical groups in this study. Additional surveillance categories labelled as ‘Others’ and ‘Special Categories’ were excluded from comparative spatial analyses due to their heterogeneous characteristics, limited representation, and inconsistent residential classification. The study population comprised all reported dengue cases between 2020 and 2024 within the study area, obtained from the national surveillance system (e.g., e-Dengue, Ministry of Health Malaysia). Cases represent clinically and laboratory-confirmed infections and include georeferenced residential locations. Only cases with complete spatial information were included in the analysis. All data were anonymised prior to processing in accordance with ethical and data protection requirements.

Study design

A retrospective spatial epidemiological study design was employed using georeferenced dengue surveillance records collected between 2020 and 2024. The analytical framework focused primarily on georeferenced dengue surveillance records and spatial environmental characteristics. Climatic variables and entomological surveillance indicators were not incorporated due to differences in spatial and temporal resolution, limited availability of standardized fine-scale datasets across the full study period, and the exploratory spatial focus of the present study. Spatial analyses were subsequently conducted across residential housing typologies and surrounding environmental contexts.

The overall analytical workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2, which outlines the sequential integration of data collection, housing typology stratification, multi-scale spatial analysis, and outcome interpretation. The study began with the collection of georeferenced dengue case data and high-resolution land use datasets, which were pre-processed and integrated within a Geographic Information System (GIS) environment. Only complete and validated dengue surveillance records between January 2020 and December 2024 were included in the final analyses. Preliminary database exports containing incomplete 2025 records were excluded prior to spatial processing and were not used in any statistical analysis, figure generation, or interpretation presented in this study. Subsequently, dengue cases were stratified into three dominant residential housing typologies; terrace/landed housing, high-rise housing, and traditional/rural housing based on their spatial location and surrounding built environment characteristics. This classification enabled the separation of transmission dynamics according to structural and environmental contexts. Following stratification, a series of multi-scale spatial analyses were conducted. These included kernel density estimation (hotspot mapping) to identify areas of high case intensity, spatial clustering analysis to quantify aggregation patterns across distances, land use overlay analysis to examine environmental associations. Buffer analysis was performed to characterize the surrounding land-use composition near dengue case locations at progressively increasing spatial scales. Then, distance–decay analysis was used to evaluate how dengue occurrence varied relative to proximity from the built–vegetation interface. The outputs from these analytical steps were then synthesised to generate comparative risk gradients and housing-specific transmission profiles. This workflow highlights how dengue transmission is shaped by the interaction between housing typology and surrounding land use, providing a structured and reproducible framework for multi-scale spatial epidemiological analysis.

Data collection and management

Dengue case data were obtained from the national surveillance system (e.g., e-Dengue, Ministry of Health Malaysia) for the period 2020–2024. The dataset consisted of georeferenced confirmed dengue cases, with each record including latitude, longitude, date of notification, and residential location. All records were anonymised prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality and compliance with ethical standards. Only cases with complete and valid spatial information were retained, while duplicate entries and records with missing coordinates were excluded during the data cleaning process. During data verification, preliminary surveillance exports containing incomplete records outside the validated analytical period were identified and removed. The final analytical database used for all spatial analyses, figures, and interpretations consisted exclusively of confirmed dengue cases recorded between 2020 and 2024.

Land use data were derived from high-resolution spatial datasets and classified into seven categories: built-up areas, vegetation (trees), crops, flooded vegetation, water bodies, bare ground, and rangeland. All spatial layers were standardised to a common coordinate system (WGS 1984) and processed within a Geographic Information System using ArcGIS Pro (version 3.1). The classification was further verified through visual interpretation of high-resolution imagery to ensure consistency and accuracy. Housing typologies were assigned through spatial overlay analysis, whereby dengue case locations were intersected with land use and built environment layers. Residential locations were classified into terrace/landed, high-rise, and traditional/rural housing categories based on spatial and environmental characteristics. Housing typologies were classified using objective spatial criteria derived from building morphology, settlement density, and surrounding land-use composition. Terrace/landed housing was defined as horizontally connected residential units within densely built urban or peri-urban environments. High-rise housing included vertically structured residential buildings such as apartments and condominiums characterized by high building density and multi-storey configurations. Traditional/rural housing referred to low-density detached settlements embedded within heterogeneous ecological landscapes dominated by vegetation, crops, or water-associated land cover. Mixed-use or transitional residential environments were evaluated based on the dominant surrounding spatial characteristics within the immediate residential context. Classification priority was determined using the predominant residential structure, settlement density, and adjacent land-use composition identified through GIS overlay and high-resolution satellite imagery interpretation. This classification was validated using satellite imagery to minimise misclassification. Ambiguous residential locations that could not be confidently assigned to a single housing typology during initial classification were manually reviewed using high-resolution satellite imagery and local spatial context interpretation. Where uncertainty persisted, cases were excluded from typology-specific analyses to minimise misclassification bias. Classification consistency was independently reviewed by the research team prior to final spatial analysis. The classification framework was intended to support reproducible spatial epidemiological stratification of residential environments rather than formal administrative housing categorization.

Only the three dominant residential housing typologies (terrace/landed, high-rise, and traditional/rural housing) were retained for typology-specific spatial analyses. Records categorized as ‘Others’ or ‘Special Categories’ within the preliminary surveillance database were excluded due to heterogeneous land-use characteristics and insufficient sample size for meaningful comparative spatial interpretation. Classification was validated using high-resolution satellite imagery and local spatial context interpretation. All datasets were subsequently integrated into a unified GIS database for analysis. During data verification, surveillance records were screened for duplicate entries, incomplete spatial coordinates, inconsistent residential classifications, and temporal inconsistencies. Only complete and validated dengue case records collected between January 2020 and December 2024 were retained for final spatial analysis. Derived variables such as land use proportions and normalised risk indices were computed to support comparative assessments across housing typologies.

All spatial analyses were conducted using individual georeferenced dengue case points rather than aggregated areal units to preserve fine-scale spatial variability and minimize ecological aggregation bias. The selected buffer distances (50 m, 100 m, 250 m, and 500 m) were intended to represent progressive residential environmental scales surrounding dengue case locations, ranging from immediate peri-domestic surroundings to broader neighbourhood-level landscape contexts. Smaller buffers (50–100 m) were used to characterize localized environmental conditions and potential near-residential exposure zones, whereas larger buffers (250–500 m) were intended to capture broader land-use heterogeneity and surrounding ecological context. Preliminary exploratory assessment of spatial scale consistency was performed to minimize excessive overlap between adjacent distance bands while preserving interpretable environmental gradients across housing typologies. Although the selected spatial scales were considered appropriate for exploratory environmental characterization, more extensive scale-sensitivity analyses and continuous spatial modelling approaches should be explored in future studies to further evaluate robustness across alternative spatial resolutions. Data analysis

All spatial analyses were conducted using individual-level georeferenced dengue case data within a point-pattern analytical framework. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE), Ripley’s K-function, buffer analyses, and built–vegetation interface assessments were performed using pooled individual dengue case locations aggregated across the full study period (2020–2024). Temporal clustering, seasonal modelling, and dynamic spatiotemporal analyses were beyond the scope of the present study.

Spatial hotspot analysis

Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) was used to visualize areas of relatively concentrated dengue occurrence and identify broad hotspot patterns across housing typologies3. KDE estimates the density of point events over space by smoothing observed case locations within a defined bandwidth. The KDE function is expressed as:

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f\left( {x,y} \right)$$\end{document} is the estimated density at location (x, y), n is the total number of cases, h is the bandwidth, di is the distance between the estimation point and case i, and K is the kernel function. KDE surfaces were generated separately for each housing typology to visualise hotspot intensity and spatial distribution patterns. KDE was performed using an adaptive bandwidth of 250 m, selected based on preliminary sensitivity testing and the spatial distribution characteristics of urban and peri-urban dengue clusters. The selected bandwidth provided an optimal balance between oversmoothing and excessive local fragmentation in hotspot visualization.

Spatial clustering was further quantified using Ripley’s K-function, which evaluates the spatial dependence of events across multiple distances20. The K-function is defined as:

where A is the study area, nnn is the number of observed cases, dij is the distance between cases i and j, and I is an indicator function equal to 1 when dij ≤ di, and 0 otherwise. To stabilise variance and facilitate interpretation, the transformed L-function was applied:

Observed values were compared with Complete Spatial Randomness (CSR) envelopes. Isotropic edge correction was applied during Ripley’s K-function analysis to minimise boundary effects associated with irregular study area geometry. CSR envelopes were generated using Monte Carlo simulations with 999 permutations to evaluate statistically significant departures from spatial randomness at multiple spatial scales. Values above CSR indicate clustering, while values approaching CSR suggest a random spatial pattern. This analysis was conducted across multiple spatial scales to capture scale-dependent aggregation patterns.

Spatial overlay analysis was performed to examine the relationship between dengue case locations, hotspot surfaces, and land use categories. Dengue cases and KDE outputs were intersected with land use layers to identify co-location patterns. The association between case distribution and environmental features was interpreted based on the spatial correspondence between high-density areas and specific land use types.

The interface was operationalized by extracting shared polygon boundaries between the built-up land-use class and vegetated classes (trees, crops, and flooded vegetation) within the classified land-use map using GIS overlay and boundary intersection procedures in ArcGIS Pro. Statistical and spatial tools

All spatial analyses were conducted using ArcGIS Pro (version 3.1) for geoprocessing, mapping, and spatial modelling. Statistical analysis and data visualisation were performed using R software (version 4.3.2), with relevant packages for spatial statistics and graphical representation. The present study primarily employed exploratory spatial analytical approaches to characterize spatial heterogeneity, clustering behaviour, and environmental associations of dengue transmission across housing typologies. The analyses were intended to identify spatial patterns and relative gradients rather than establish causal or predictive relationships. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) was performed to visualize areas of relatively concentrated dengue occurrence and identify broad hotspot patterns across housing typologies.

Assumptions and limitations

This study assumes that georeferenced dengue case locations accurately represent exposure environments and that land use classifications reliably reflect on-ground conditions.However, residential case locations recorded within surveillance systems may not necessarily represent the true sites of dengue exposure or mosquito–human contact. Individuals may acquire infections in non-residential environments such as workplaces, schools, markets, recreational spaces, or transit settings. Consequently, the spatial patterns identified in this study should be interpreted as residential spatial associations and potential environmental exposure contexts rather than definitive transmission locations. It also assumes that proximity to environmental features, particularly the built–vegetation interface, is a valid proxy for vector habitat suitability. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relies on secondary surveillance data and does not include direct entomological measurements, which may limit causal inference. Second, land use data are treated as static and may not capture temporal environmental changes. Third, behavioural and socio-economic factors influencing dengue transmission are not explicitly incorporated.

Although the surveillance database contained temporal notification information, the present study did not perform temporally stratified or dynamic spatiotemporal analyses. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as pooled retrospective spatial associations across the 2020–2024 analytical period. The present study primarily employed exploratory spatial analytical approaches intended to characterize spatial patterns rather than establish causal relationships. Although permutation-based spatial clustering analyses were performed, inferential regression-based modelling incorporating demographic, socioeconomic, entomological, climatic, and behavioural covariates was beyond the scope of the present exploratory spatial epidemiological framework. Several potentially important confounding factors were not incorporated into the present analytical framework. Population density, human mobility patterns, socioeconomic status, healthcare accessibility, vector control activities, and household behavioural practices may substantially influence dengue transmission independently of housing typology and surrounding land-use composition. Consequently, the spatial patterns identified in this study likely reflect the combined effects of environmental, demographic, behavioural, and public health-related processes rather than isolated housing-associated mechanisms alone. Although preliminary surveillance exports contained incomplete records beyond 2024, only validated datasets within the defined analytical period (2020–2024) were included in the final analyses to ensure temporal consistency and data reliability. Despite these limitations, the study provides a robust spatial framework for understanding dengue dynamics and offers a foundation for future research integrating ecological, behavioural, and climatic variables.

Results

Spatiotemporal clustering of dengue across housing typologies

Distinct spatial heterogeneity in dengue transmission was observed across housing typologies, highlighting the influence of built environment structure on disease dynamics (Fig. 3). Terrace and landed housing areas exhibited extensive, continuous, and spatially interconnected hotspot clusters, particularly concentrated along urban–peri-urban transition zones. These clusters formed elongated and contiguous patterns, suggesting sustained transmission driven by high population density, close housing proximity, and the presence of peri-domestic breeding habitats. The persistence and spatial continuity of these hotspots indicate that transmission in terrace housing is not only intense but also spatially reinforced through neighbourhood-level connectivity. In contrast, high-rise and institutional housing displayed markedly different spatial characteristics. Hotspots in these settings were smaller, discrete, and spatially fragmented, often confined within specific buildings or clusters of buildings. This pattern suggests that dengue transmission in high-rise environments is more localized and potentially driven by vertical stratification, limited dispersal of vectors between floors, and shared but contained breeding sites such as water storage systems or poorly maintained infrastructure. The spatial discontinuity of hotspots implies reduced horizontal spread compared to landed housing, reflecting structural constraints imposed by vertical living environments. Traditional and rural housing typologies demonstrated broader yet less intense hotspot distributions. Unlike the compact clustering observed in urban settings, hotspots in these areas appeared more diffuse and spatially scattered across larger geographic extents. This pattern is indicative of lower population density and more dispersed housing arrangements, where transmission is influenced by wider environmental factors such as vegetation, natural water bodies, and agricultural land use. Rural housing showed broader but less concentrated spatial clustering patterns, driven by ecological suitability rather than dense human aggregation.

These observed spatial patterns were further substantiated by multi-scale spatial clustering analysis (Fig. 4), which quantified the degree of case aggregation across increasing spatial distances. Terrace housing consistently exhibited clustering values above the complete spatial randomness (CSR) threshold across multiple spatial scales, suggesting relatively stronger spatial aggregation patterns compared with other housing typologies. Dengue cases remain highly clustered regardless of distance, reinforcing the interpretation of sustained and spatially cohesive transmission within densely built environments. In comparison, high-rise housing showed significant clustering at shorter distances, reflecting localized transmission within confined spatial units. However, as the spatial scale increased, clustering values gradually approached CSR, indicating a transition from clustered to near-random distribution. This scale-dependent pattern suggests that while transmission is concentrated within individual buildings or immediate surroundings, it does not extend broadly across larger spatial extents, supporting the notion of compartmentalized transmission dynamics in vertical housing systems. Conversely, traditional and rural housing exhibited relatively weak clustering intensity across all spatial scales, with most values approaching or closely aligning with CSR. This indicates a near-random spatial distribution of cases at broader scales, consistent with dispersed settlement patterns and heterogeneous environmental conditions. The absence of strong clustering suggests that transmission in these settings is less driven by spatial proximity between households and more influenced by localized ecological niches that are not spatially contiguous.

Influence of land use on dengue distribution

The spatial relationship between dengue cases, surrounding land use, and housing typologies revealed a pronounced environmental structuring of transmission dynamics (Fig. 5). The integration of land use classification with kernel density surfaces demonstrated that dengue occurrence is not randomly distributed across the landscape but is strongly conditioned by the interaction between built environments and ecological features.

In terrace and landed housing areas, dengue hotspots were predominantly embedded within highly built-up zones interspersed with fragmented vegetation patches. These hotspots were frequently located at the interface between residential structures and small pockets of greenery, such as roadside vegetation, unmanaged gardens, and shaded communal areas. This spatial configuration suggests a synergistic interaction between human habitation and peri-domestic ecological conditions that favour Aedes breeding. The juxtaposition of impervious surfaces (e.g., roofs, drains, containers) with shaded, humid microenvironments likely enhances water retention and reduces evaporation, creating optimal microhabitats for larval development. Consequently, transmission in terrace housing appears to be strongly driven by fine-scale environmental heterogeneity, where even small vegetated patches within dense urban settings can significantly amplify risk. In contrast, high-rise and institutional housing displayed a markedly different land use association. Dengue cases in these areas were largely concentrated within homogenous, densely built urban landscapes with minimal surrounding vegetation. The clustering of cases within such environments suggests that transmission is primarily driven by localized breeding habitats within the built infrastructure itself, rather than external ecological inputs. Potential breeding sites in these settings may include rooftop water tanks, drainage systems, elevator shafts, and poorly maintained communal facilities. The limited presence of vegetation implies that vector proliferation is less dependent on natural ecological conditions and more influenced by anthropogenic factors, including building maintenance, water management practices, and human behaviour. This reinforces the notion that high-rise environments support compartmentalized transmission dynamics, where risk is concentrated within structurally defined spaces. In traditional and rural housing typologies, dengue cases were distributed across a more heterogeneous land use matrix, including vegetation, agricultural crops, flooded areas, and natural water bodies. Unlike the concentrated hotspots observed in urban settings, cases in these areas were more spatially dispersed, reflecting the influence of broader ecological conditions. The presence of vegetation and water-associated land use provides abundant natural and semi-natural breeding habitats, while lower housing density reduces the intensity of human–vector contact within a confined space. However, the co-occurrence of agricultural activities, standing water, and shaded environments likely sustains vector populations over larger spatial extents. This indicates that dengue transmission in rural settings is shaped by landscape-level ecological processes rather than localized built environment interactions.

Importantly, Fig. 5 highlights that the relationship between land use and dengue distribution is highly dependent on housing typology, with distinct transmission pathways emerging across environments. Terrace housing demonstrates a hybrid system where built and vegetated elements interact to create high-risk microhabitats. High-rise housing reflects a predominantly built-environment-driven transmission system with minimal ecological modulation. In contrast, traditional and rural housing represents an ecologically driven system where diverse land use components collectively influence vector ecology and disease spread.

Multi-scale buffer analysis of surrounding land use composition

Buffer-based analysis revealed clear scale-dependent variation in land use composition surrounding dengue cases across housing typologies (Fig. 6). In terrace housing, built-up areas dominated at proximal distances (50–100 m), accounting for approximately 70–80% of the surrounding land cover, while vegetation constituted around 15–25%. At larger buffer distances (250–500 m), the proportion of vegetation increased to approximately 30–45%, accompanied by a reduction in built-up areas to 50–60%, indicating a pronounced urban–vegetation transition zone. This gradient suggests that dengue cases are frequently situated near interface environments where built structures and vegetated patches co-exist, enhancing microhabitat suitability for Aedes breeding. High-rise housing maintained a consistently high proportion of built-up land across all buffer distances, typically exceeding 80–90%, with vegetation and water-related land use contributing less than 10–15% collectively. This homogeneity reinforces the role of dense urban infrastructure in shaping transmission, where environmental variability is minimal and breeding is likely confined to localized anthropogenic habitats within buildings. In contrast, traditional and rural housing exhibited a more balanced land use composition across all buffer scales. Vegetation accounted for approximately 40–55%, built-up areas ranged between 20 and 35%, and water-related land use (including flooded vegetation and surface water) contributed around 10–20%. These proportions remained relatively stable across increasing buffer distances, reflecting a heterogeneous environmental matrix where multiple land use components simultaneously influence vector ecology. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the environmental context of dengue cases differs substantially by housing typology: terrace housing is characterized by sharp urban–vegetation gradients, high-rise housing by homogenous built dominance (> 80%), and rural housing by mixed ecological landscapes with relatively even land use distribution.

Distance–decay relationship of dengue risk

The relationship between dengue case density and distance from the built–vegetation interface demonstrated a consistent distance–decay pattern across all housing typologies (Fig. 7). Peak normalized case density was concentrated within the immediate interface zone (0–50 m), where values reached approximately 0.80–1.00, indicating the highest transmission intensity. Beyond this zone, case density declined progressively with increasing distance, confirming the interface as a critical microenvironment for dengue risk. This decay pattern was most pronounced in terrace housing, where case density dropped sharply from approximately ~ 1.00 at 0–50 m to ~ 0.40–0.50 at 100–250 m, and further declined to < 0.20 beyond 500 m. This steeper gradient suggests a closer spatial association between dengue occurrence and proximity to the built–vegetation interface in terrace housing environments, suggesting that transmission is highly localized and driven by micro-scale environmental conditions within peri-domestic spaces.

In high-rise housing, the decay pattern was more moderate. Case density decreased from approximately ~ 0.80–0.90 near the interface to ~ 0.50–0.60 at intermediate distances (100–250 m), and gradually approached ~ 0.30–0.40 beyond 500 m. This gentler slope indicates reduced sensitivity to surrounding vegetation and suggests that transmission is less dependent on external ecological interfaces, likely due to the dominance of internal building-related breeding sites.Traditional and rural housing exhibited the most gradual decline in case density across distance. Values decreased from approximately ~ 0.70–0.80 at 0–50 m to ~ 0.50–0.60 at 250 m, and remained relatively elevated (~ 0.30–0.40) even at larger distances (> 500 m). This extended decay pattern reflects broader ecological influences, where vegetation, water bodies, and agricultural landscapes sustain vector populations across wider spatial extents, resulting in more diffuse transmission. Overall, the distance–decay analysis demonstrates that dengue risk is strongly mediated by proximity to the built–vegetation interface, but the strength and scale of this relationship vary by housing typology. Terrace housing exhibits steep, localized risk gradients, high-rise housing shows moderate and spatially constrained decay, while traditional and rural housing reflects a more gradual and ecologically distributed transmission pattern.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that dengue spatial distribution patterns differed consistently across dominant residential housing typologies, with terrace housing exhibiting relatively stronger clustering and closer association with built–vegetation interfaces compared with high-rise and traditional/rural housing environments. This study demonstrates that dengue spatial distribution patterns vary according to housing typology and surrounding land-use composition. Terrace/landed housing exhibited relatively stronger and more spatially continuous clustering, whereas high-rise housing showed localized aggregation and traditional/rural housing displayed broader but weaker clustering patterns.

These findings suggest that housing structure and environmental context may influence how dengue risk is spatially organized across residential settings. Previous Malaysian studies similarly identified human settlements as major land-use predictors of dengue occurrence, particularly in densely populated urban environments13. Broader urban dengue literature has also consistently highlighted the interaction between settlement characteristics and ecological suitability, particularly vegetation and water-associated environments, in shaping dengue risk24–28. The present findings extend this understanding by suggesting that these environmental relationships may differ according to housing form and surrounding built structure. It is important to note that the spatial analyses in this study were based on residential georeferenced dengue case locations obtained from surveillance records. As human mobility patterns were unavailable, the identified spatial associations should not be interpreted as definitive transmission or exposure sites. Instead, the observed clustering patterns likely reflect broader residential environmental contexts potentially associated with dengue risk.

It is important to note that the spatial analyses in this study were based on residential georeferenced dengue case locations obtained from retrospective surveillance records and static land-use datasets representing the broader environmental context during the analytical period. As human mobility patterns, workplace exposure, school-related transmission, and temporally dynamic environmental changes were not available within the present dataset, the identified spatial associations should not be interpreted as definitive exposure or transmission locations. Instead, the observed clustering patterns likely reflect broader residential environmental contexts potentially associated with dengue occurrence. In addition, static land-use representation may not fully capture seasonal environmental variability, urban development changes, or short-term ecological fluctuations occurring throughout the 2020–2024 study period. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory spatial epidemiological observations intended to characterize broad residential environmental associations rather than temporally explicit transmission pathways or causal exposure mechanisms.

Terrace/landed housing showed extensive hotspot continuity, high built-up dominance near residential areas, and increasing vegetation influence across wider buffer distances. Dengue occurrence also declined more rapidly with increasing distance from the built–vegetation interface. Together, these findings suggest that terrace housing environments may support closer spatial interactions between dense residential connectivity and peri-domestic ecological conditions. This interpretation aligns with previous entomological studies showing that shaded peri-domestic environments frequently support Aedes persistence29. Rajarethinam et al. reported higher Aedes-positive breeding site proportions in peri-domestic areas compared with strictly domestic environments30. Environmental characteristics such as shade, detritus accumulation, and water-holding containers were also associated with immature mosquito presence30–32. These observations support the possibility that fragmented vegetation adjacent to dense residential blocks may create environmentally favorable microhabitats for vector persistence. The present findings also provide additional context to previous Malaysian land-cover studies. Ng et al. observed that dengue seroprevalence generally increased with urban development and reduced vegetation cover32. In contrast, our results suggest that at finer neighbourhood scales within already urbanized residential areas, small vegetated patches located near dense housing may still be associated with localized dengue occurrence. This difference likely reflects scale-dependent environmental effects rather than contradictory ecological mechanisms.

High-rise housing demonstrated clustering primarily at shorter spatial distances, with weaker clustering observed at broader scales. Built-up land cover consistently dominated surrounding buffer zones, while vegetation-related influences appeared comparatively limited. These findings suggest that dengue occurrence in high-rise settings may be more strongly associated with localized structural and infrastructural conditions than with broader surrounding landscape composition. Previous Malaysian studies support this interpretation. Ab Hamid et al. demonstrated that both Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus could persist within multi-storey residential buildings, although abundance varied by floor level33. Few reseachers similarly reported that vertical residential environments may facilitate localized dengue transmission requiring housing-specific control strategies34–36. Together, these findings suggest that high-rise dengue risk should not be viewed simply as a generalized urban phenomenon, but rather as a structurally compartmentalized transmission environment.

From a practical perspective, these findings indicate that vector control in high-rise settings may require greater emphasis on internal building environments, including rooftop water storage, drainage systems, service ducts, shared corridors, and container habitats within common residential spaces. Traditional/rural housing exhibited broader but weaker clustering patterns, accompanied by relatively stable mixed land-use composition across buffer distances and slower distance–decay gradients. These observations suggest a more spatially diffuse environmental context in which dengue occurrence may be associated with broader ecological suitability rather than intense localized clustering.

Previous rural–urban gradient studies similarly demonstrated that Aedes ecology varies substantially across settlement contexts37. Rural environments may continue to support mosquito persistence through natural and artificial breeding habitats distributed across larger ecological mosaics38. Consequently, weaker clustering in rural settings should not be interpreted as reduced epidemiological importance, but rather as a different spatial organization of transmission risk characterized by lower density and wider environmental permissiveness.

One important finding of this study was the recurring association between dengue occurrence and proximity to the built–vegetation interface across all housing typologies, although the magnitude of this association differed substantially. Terrace housing showed the steepest distance–decay relationship, whereas high-rise and rural housing demonstrated weaker but still detectable interface associations. Previous reviews have consistently identified vegetation and water-associated environments as important but context-dependent dengue correlates39,40. Our findings help explain some of these inconsistencies by suggesting that vegetation effects are unlikely to operate uniformly across housing environments. Instead, the epidemiological relevance of vegetation may depend on its spatial relationship with residential density, built structure, and local environmental configuration. Similar observations have been reported in studies demonstrating that vegetation-related dengue associations vary substantially after adjustment for urban and socioeconomic factors41. The findings of this study should be interpreted as exploratory spatial epidemiological observations intended to characterize broad housing-associated spatial patterns rather than establish definitive mechanistic transmission pathways. The observed spatial clustering patterns should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as they may also reflect broader urban demographic structure, mobility-related exposure, differential reporting behaviour, and variability in local vector control implementation. The present findings are intended to characterize broad residential environmental associations rather than establish definitive causal pathways of dengue transmission.

The present findings may also support more operationally targeted dengue control strategies tailored to specific residential environments. In terrace and landed housing areas, where dengue occurrence was more closely associated with built–vegetation interfaces, vector control efforts may benefit from intensified management of peri-domestic vegetation, shaded outdoor resting sites, drainage systems, and artificial water-holding containers located near residential boundaries. In high-rise environments, interventions may require greater emphasis on internal building infrastructure, including rooftop water storage systems, elevator drainage areas, service corridors, refuse accumulation sites, and shared residential facilities that may facilitate localized mosquito persistence. In traditional and rural housing settings, broader environmental management approaches focusing on dispersed breeding habitats, water-associated environments, and community-based source reduction activities may be more appropriate. Spatially targeted surveillance and intervention strategies based on housing typology and surrounding environmental context may improve operational efficiency, prioritization of high-risk areas, and allocation of vector control resources within public health programs. These findings further support the need for adaptive dengue control policies that account for environmental and residential heterogeneity rather than applying uniform intervention strategies across all urban settings.

The present analytical framework was designed primarily as an exploratory spatial epidemiological assessment intended to characterize spatial heterogeneity, clustering behaviour, and environmental associations across residential housing typologies rather than develop validated predictive transmission models. Consequently, the observed spatial patterns should not be interpreted as definitive predictive risk estimates or causal transmission forecasts. Although the identified housing-associated environmental gradients and clustering patterns may support exploratory environmental surveillance and spatial prioritization within dengue control programmes, formal predictive modelling would require integration of additional climatic, entomological, behavioural, demographic, and mobility-related datasets. Future studies incorporating inferential spatial regression, machine learning approaches, Bayesian hierarchical modelling, and spatiotemporal forecasting frameworks may provide more robust predictive assessment of dengue transmission risk across heterogeneous residential environments.

Beyond environmental transmission dynamics, dengue infection is increasingly recognized as a complex systemic disease involving immune dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, neuroinflammatory responses, and potential neurological complications. Although the present study focused on spatial epidemiological patterns rather than clinical outcomes, identifying environmental contexts associated with elevated dengue occurrence may contribute indirectly to understanding broader population-level disease burden. Emerging evidence additionally suggests that natural bioactive metabolites and plant-derived compounds may modulate inflammatory pathways, oxidative stress, and viral replication mechanisms associated with dengue pathophysiology42. These perspectives highlight the importance of integrating spatial epidemiology with host-response biology and translational therapeutic research in future dengue investigations.

Implications for targeted vector control

The comparison with prior studies underscores that housing-specific intervention design is essential. For terrace housing, the results support intensified control within short distances of residential–vegetation edges, especially in shaded peri-domestic spaces where containers, drains, and small unmanaged green patches coincide. For high-rise settings, the literature and our findings both indicate the need for building-centred surveillance, including floor-sensitive inspection, communal infrastructure audits, and routine monitoring of service areas. For traditional and rural housing, a wider ecological lens is needed, combining household source reduction with management of water-associated and vegetated surroundings. These implications also align with integrated vector management principles, which emphasize tailoring interventions to local ecological and social conditions rather than applying uniform control packages across all settings. The present study contributes to that agenda by showing that “where” dengue occurs is tightly linked to “what kind of housing and land-use configuration” surrounds the case.

Open research questions and future directions

Several questions emerge from this comparison with previous work. First, the built–vegetation interface should be tested using direct entomological measurements, because the current study infers ecological suitability from case distribution and land-use configuration rather than from larval or adult vector abundance. Second, high-rise transmission warrants finer vertical analysis, including floor-specific case mapping and building-service audits, given prior evidence that Aedes can persist across many storeys. Third, future studies should integrate microclimate variables such as humidity, shading, and surface temperature, as these may explain why certain vegetated edges amplify risk while others do not. Fourth, predictive models should explicitly include interaction terms between housing typology and land-use composition, rather than treating environmental predictors as spatially uniform.

Future studies should incorporate inferential statistical modelling approaches, including spatial regression, generalized additive models, and Bayesian hierarchical frameworks, to quantitatively evaluate the independent and interacting effects of housing typology, land-use composition, and environmental variables on dengue transmission intensity. Integration of weekly or seasonal dengue surveillance data may further support explicit spatiotemporal modelling of temporal transmission trajectories, seasonal clustering behaviour, and dynamic environmental interactions. In addition, incorporating population density metrics, mobility datasets, socioeconomic indicators, entomological surveillance, and vector control intervention data could improve understanding of the interacting environmental and social determinants shaping dengue transmission across residential environments. Longitudinal studies examining land-use change, urban expansion, and dengue incidence would also help clarify whether interface-associated transmission patterns intensify as peri-urban districts continue to develop.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dengue transmission is highly structured by the interaction between housing typology and surrounding land use. Terrace housing promotes concentrated, interface-driven transmission, high-rise environments exhibit localized and infrastructure-dependent patterns, while traditional and rural settings show more diffuse, ecologically mediated dynamics. The built–vegetation interface emerges as a critical microenvironment, with peak risk occurring within close proximity and declining with distance. By integrating multi-scale spatial analyses, this study provides a robust framework for understanding dengue risk and highlights the need for targeted, typology-specific vector control strategies aligned with local environmental conditions.
