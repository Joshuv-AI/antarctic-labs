Minimum Sum Set Cover: Structures and Algorithm
Zhongyi Zhang, Yixin Cao

Abstract
A set cover of a hypergraph H H is a set of vertices intersecting every hyperedge. In the minimum sum set cover problem, vertices are selected one by one; each edge pays the position of the first vertex that hits it, and the objective is to minimize the total cost. When H H is a graph, this is the minimum sum vertex cover problem. A solution is specified by a set cover S S together with an ordering of its vertices. While the classical set cover problem seeks to minimize | S | |S| , the minimum sum variant favors covering many edges early and may prefer larger covers. This motivates a natural question: how large can the gap between τ → \overrightarrow{\tau} and τ \tau be? We prove an upper bound τ → ≤ τ ​ log 2 ​ | E ⁡ ( H ) | \overrightarrow{\tau}\leq\tau\log_{2}\lvert E(H)\rvert , and show that for any positive n n , there exists a hypergraph H H on n + 3 n+3 vertices with τ = 3 \tau=3 and τ → = n \overrightarrow{\tau}=n . For graphs, we obtain stronger bounds: we prove τ → ≤ 2 ​ τ ​ log 2 ⁡ τ \overrightarrow{\tau}\leq 2\tau\log_{2}\tau , improving the bound of Liu et al. [Theor. Comput. Sci., 2025], and we construct graphs with τ → = Ω ⁡ ( τ ​ log ⁡ τ log ⁡ log ⁡ τ ) \overrightarrow{\tau}=\Omega\left(\frac{\tau\log\tau}{\log\log\tau}\right) , nearly matching this upper bound. On the algorithmic side, we show that minimum sum set cover is fixed-parameter tractable on bounded-rank hypergraphs, parameterized by τ → \overrightarrow{\tau} , extending the algorithm of Liu et al. for graphs (i.e., rank-two hypergraphs).

Introduction

A
hypergraph

H
consists of a vertex set
V(H)
and hyperedge set
E(H)
, where each hyperedge is a nonempty subset of
V(H)
.
The
rank

r(H)
of a hypergraph
H
is the maximum cardinality of any of the edges in
H
.
A
set cover
of a hypergraph is a set of vertices that intersects every hyperedge.

In the literature, a set of vertices that covers all hyperedges is typically referred to as a
hitting set
, whereas a set cover is a collection of hyperedges whose union encompasses the entire vertex set. These concepts are equivalent because a set cover of a hypergraph corresponds to a hitting set of its dual hypergraph, where the roles of vertices and edges are interchanged. We follow this established convention, as introduced by Feige et al.
[
3
]
.

There are many variations of this fundamental concept.
A solution consists of an ordered set cover. When the
i
th chosen vertex is processed, every previously uncovered hyperedge containing it becomes covered and contributes cost
i
.
A
minimum sum set cover
of a hypergraph is a set cover associated with a permutation of its vertices that minimizes the total cost of covering all hyperedges.
When the hypergraph is a graph (rank-two hypergraph), we have
vertex covers
and
minimum sum vertex covers
.

The classical set cover problem asks for a set cover of minimum cardinality.
In contrast, the minimum-sum variant rewards covering many edges early.
Consequently, a minimum sum set cover does not need to be minimum, or even minimal, as a set cover.
For example, in the hypergraph on vertex set
\{1,\ldots,7\}
and the following eleven hyperedges (blue hyperedges contain vertex
2

\{1,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}3}\},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{1,2,4\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{1,2,5\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{1,2,6\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{1,2,7\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\{1,3,4\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\{1,3,5\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\{1,3,6\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\{1,3,7\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{2,4,5\}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\{3,6,7\}},

The first vertex

Let
\tau(H)
denote the size of a minimum (classical) set cover of
H
.
Since a hypergraph may admit multiple optimal minimum sum set covers of varying sizes, we define
\overrightarrow{\tau}(H)
to be the maximum cardinality of a set cover appearing in an optimal solution.
The example above shows that
\overrightarrow{\tau}(H)
can be strictly larger than
\tau(H)
.
This raises a basic structural question:

What is the possible gap between
\overrightarrow{\tau}(H)
and
\tau(H)
?

This question is nontrivial even for graphs, where the ordering objective can force optimal solutions to use substantially more vertices than a minimum vertex cover.
Liu et al.
[
6
]
showed that for all graphs
G
,
\overrightarrow{\tau}(G)=O(\tau(G)^{1.5}).

We significantly improve this upper bound. For hypergraphs, the upper bound has to rely on
|E(H)|
as well.

Theorem 1.1
.

•

For any graph
G
with
\tau(G)\geq 2
, it holds
\overrightarrow{\tau}(G)\leq 2\tau(G)\log_{2}\tau(G)
.

•

For any hypergraph
H
with
\tau(H)\geq 2
, it holds
\overrightarrow{\tau}(H)<\tau(H)\log_{2}|E(H)|\leq r(H)\tau(H)\log_{2}|V(H)|
.

The main observation behind the proof is that at any step, the remaining sub-hypergraph admits a set cover of size at most
\tau(H)
. Consequently, the vertex maximizing immediate coverage must remove at least a
1/\tau(H)
fraction of the remaining edges. This implies that the number of uncovered edges decays exponentially, by a factor of
(1-1/\tau(H))
at each step.
For graphs
G
, this implies that
|E(G)|\leq\tau(G)^{2}
after removing the vertices contained in every minimum vertex cover and every minimum sum vertex cover.

We show by construction that the
|E(H)|
term for hypergraphs is inevitable.
Our construction is based on encoding an
n
-vertex graph
G
into a hypergraph
H_{G}
with three “special” vertices that form the only minimum set cover, while
V(G)
forms the only other minimal set cover.
The minimum sum objective forces almost all vertices in
V(G)
to be used before those special vertices.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any integer
n\geq 3
, there exists a hypergraph
H
such that
\tau(H)=3
and
\overrightarrow{\tau}(H)=n
.

For graphs, we are only able to furnish an almost tight construction matching the upper bound in Theorem

, leaving a gap of
\log\log\tau(G)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There are bipartite graphs
G
such that
\overrightarrow{\tau}(G)=\Omega\left(\frac{\tau(G)\log\tau(G)}{\log\log\tau(G)}\right)
.

For the lower bound, we use a connected bipartite graph
B_{q}
inspired by the seminal construction of Johnson
[
5
]
for the degree-greedy algorithm for the vertex cover problem.
We start with a matching of
2n^{q}
edges on
L
and
R_{0}
.
The set
L
is the only minimum vertex cover of the graph.
The other side

has
q

R_{1},\ldots,R_{q}
, each of
n^{q}
vertices. They are connected to
L
in a way that with the removal of the last group, the graph is decomposed into
n
components, each isomorphic to
B_{q-1}
.

We show that any minimum sum vertex cover proceeds through the graph in a highly constrained way.
In particular, it starts with all the vertices in
R_{q}
, whose removal leaves
n

B_{q-1}
.
For each of them, we either take the vertices in the
L

).
The same pattern must repeat in the copies

, leading to a lower bound
\overrightarrow{\tau}(B_{q})=\Omega(n^{q+1})
.
The claimed bound follows from these by setting

.

The approximation and complexity of the minimum sum set cover problem were initiated by Feige et al.
[
3
]
, who studied approximation algorithms and hardness for the general problem.
Their reduction implies that it is NP-hard to compute
\overrightarrow{\tau}
.
Recently, Liu et al.
[
6
]
complemented this by showing that the problem remains NP-hard even when the vertex set of a minimum sum set cover is provided.

We study the parameterized complexity of the minimum sum set cover problem.
Formally, we consider the following decision problem. Given a hypergraph
H
and integers
k
and
w
, does there exist a set cover
S
of
H
and a permutation of
S
such that
|S|\leq k
and the cost is at most
w
.
Parameterizing by
w
alone is not meaningful, since every yes-instance satisfies
w\geq|E(H)|
.
The more natural parameterization is to use
\overrightarrow{\tau}
.
Liu et al.
[
6
]
showed that the minimum sum vertex cover problem, minimum sum set cover on rank-two hypergraphs, is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) with respect to
\overrightarrow{\tau}
.

The algorithm of Liu et al.
[
6
]
finds the solution set
S
by extending a minimal vertex cover
S^{\prime}
, which can be found in FPT time.
The most crucial observation behind their low complexity is that for each of the remaining positions of
S\setminus S^{\prime}
, one only needs to consider a small number of candidates: since
S^{\prime}
is a vertex cover, the number of edges effectively covered by any vertex in
S\setminus S^{\prime}
depends solely on its position relative to the sequence of
S^{\prime}
.
Heavily relying on the fact that each edge involves precisely two vertices, the algorithm cannot be easily adapted to hypergraphs.

We present a simple branching algorithm for the minimum sum set cover problem on bounded-rank hypergraphs, first using sunflowers to identify a bounded set of candidate vertices, and then exhaustively searching over subsets and permutations of these candidates.
The algorithm iteratively applies the Sunflower Lemma
[
2
]
to the hypergraph induced by the uncovered edges (i.e.,
H-S
): as long as this hypergraph is large, we find a sunflower with more than
k-|S|
petals and branch on the (at most
r(H)
) vertices in its core, since any set cover of size at most
k
must contain at least one of them. Once no such sunflower exists, the lemma implies that the remaining instance has size bounded as a function of the parameter, and we can exhaustively enumerate all ways to complete the solution set to size at most
k
and all permutations of this set, selecting one of minimum sum cost.

Theorem 1.4
.

The minimum sum set cover problem can be solved in time

O\left(r(H)^{r(H)+k}k^{r(H)}|E(H)|+(r(H)k)^{r(H)k}\right).

It remains open whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable when the rank of the input hypergraph is unbounded. See more discussion in Section
6
.

Related work.

Feige et al.
[
3
]
showed that a greedy algorithm approximates minimum sum set cover within a ratio of 4, and it becomes NP-hard to achieve a constant-approximation better than 4.
On graphs, the approximation ratio can be improved to 16/9
[
1
]
, while the lower bound, assuming the Unique Games Conjecture and P
\neq
NP, is 1.014
[
7
]
.
It remains to see whether these ratios can be improved if FPT time is allowed.

Upper bounds: Theorem
1.1

We let
H-S
denote the subhypergraph of
H
induced by
V(H)\setminus S
, i.e., the hypergraph with vertex set
V(H)\setminus S
and hyperedge set

\{e\setminus S\mid e\in E(H),e\cap S=\emptyset\},

and it is further shortened to
H-v
when
S
consists of a single vertex
v
.

The minimum sum set cover problem asks for a set cover
S
and a permutation of its vertices. It is more convenient to extend this permutation into a bijection
\sigma:V(H)\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,|V(H)|\}
by arbitrarily assigning vertices in
V(H)\setminus S
into
\{|S|+1,\ldots,|V(H)|\}
.
Note that any ordering of
V(H)
induces a feasible extension of an ordering of a set cover.
For an ordering
\sigma:V(H)\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,|V(H)|\}
, define the
effective coverage
of the
i
th vertex as

r_{\sigma}(i)=\left|\{e\in E(H)\mid\min_{v\in e}\sigma(v)=i\}\right|.

Abusing notation, we also write
r_{\sigma}(\sigma(v))
as
r_{\sigma}(v)
.
The cost of minimum sum set covers of
H
, denoted as
\phi(H)
, is

\phi(H)=\min_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{|V(H)|}i\cdot r_{\sigma}(i)=\min_{\sigma}\sum_{e\in E(H)}\min_{v\in e}\sigma(v).

Throughout the paper, we will consider solutions
(S,\sigma)
with
r_{\sigma}(i)>0
for all
i=1,\dots,|S|
.
Indeed, if
r_{\sigma}(|S|)=0
, then the last vertex of
S
is redundant; if
r_{\sigma}(i)=0
for some
i<|S|
, then the vertex at position
i
does not cover any edge at that position; removing it and shifting all later vertices one step earlier yields another solution with strictly smaller cost.
Under this assumption, the set
S
is implicit from the ordering
\sigma
: its vertices are exactly those that appear before any edge is first covered. Equivalently,

|S|=\max_{e\in E(H)}\min_{v\in e}\sigma(v).

Henceforth, we will simply use
\sigma
to denote a solution, and
S_{\sigma}
the implied vertex set.

Observation 2.1
.

Let
H
be a hypergraph on
n
vertices, and
\langle v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\rangle
a minimum sum set cover of
H
. For all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, the ordering
\langle v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}\rangle
is a minimum sum set cover of
H-\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}
.

Proof.

If
H-\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}
admits a better solution
\sigma
, then concatenating
\langle v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i-1}\rangle
and
\sigma
yields a solution of strictly smaller total cost.
∎

In an optimal solution, the numbers
r_{\sigma}(\cdot)
are non-increasing.

Lemma 2.2
.

In any optimal solution
\sigma
of
H
, it holds

r_{\sigma}(1)\geq r_{\sigma}(2)\geq\cdots\geq r_{\sigma}(|V(H)|).

Proof.

By Observation
2.1
, it suffices to show that
r_{\sigma}(1)\geq r_{\sigma}(2)
.
Let
u
and
v
be the vertices with
\sigma(u)=1
and
\sigma(v)=2
.
Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be obtained from
\sigma
by switching the order of
u
and
v
.
For each hyperedge containing
u
but not
v
, the cost is
1
in
\sigma
and
2
in
\sigma^{\prime}
.
For each hyperedge containing
v
but not

, the cost is
2
in
\sigma
and
1

\sigma^{\prime}
.
All the other hyperedges have the same cost in
\sigma

\sigma^{\prime}

By the optimality of

.
∎

In any minimum sum set cover

The proof of Lemma

is simple because switching two juxtaposed vertices has no impact on the effective coverages of other vertices. In the later proofs, we need to switch vertices that are not juxtaposed, for which we need a simple arithmetic inequality for comparing two solutions of the same hypergraph.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\rangle
and
\langle s^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,s^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
be two non-increasing sequences of nonnegative integers such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\prime}_{i}
. If there exists an integer
t\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that

\displaystyle s_{i}

\displaystyle\leq s^{\prime}_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,t,

(2)

\displaystyle s_{i}

\displaystyle\geq s^{\prime}_{i},\quad i=t+1,\ldots,n.

(3)

then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\cdot s_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\cdot s^{\prime}_{i},

with equality if and only if all inequalities in (
2
) and (
3
) are equalities.

Proof.

For
i=1,\dots,n
, define
d_{i}=s_{i}-s^{\prime}_{i}
, and
D_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
.
Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\prime}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\;=\;0.

From (
2
) and (
3
) we have
d_{i}\leq 0
for
i\leq t
, and
d_{i}\geq 0
for
i>t
.
Then
D_{n}=0
, and
D_{i}\leq 0
for all
i=1,\dots,n
: when
i\leq t
,
D_{i}
is the sum of non-positive terms, and for
i>t
, it is the negation of the sum of nonnegative terms.

Thus,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\,(s_{i}-s^{\prime}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\,d_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{i}d_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=k}^{n}d_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(D_{n}-D_{k-1})=-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}D_{k}\geq 0,

where we use the convention
D_{0}=0
.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
D_{k}=0
for all
k=1,\dots,n-1
, which in turn is equivalent to
d_{i}=0
for all
i
, i.e.
s_{i}=s^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i
.
∎

.

Proposition 3.2

The cost of any solution that orders

.

Proof.

, and hence a solution of
G
.

. Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+3}i\cdot r_{\pi}(i)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\cdot r_{\pi}(i)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}3i\left(2^{n-i}-\delta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\left(3\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\cdot 2^{n-i}\right)-\left(3\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\delta_{i}\right).

5
A parameterized algorithm

We present a simple fixed-parameter branching algorithm for the minimum sum set cover problem on bounded-rank hypergraphs.
Recall that the rank
r(H)
of a hypergraph
H
is the maximum cardinality of any of the hyperedges in
H
, and we consider the decision version: given a hypergraph
H
and integers
k
and
w
, does there exist a set cover
S
of
H
and a permutation of
S
such that
|S|\leq k
and the cost is at most
w
.

We say that a solution
\sigma
of an instance
(H,w,k)
is
feasible
if
|S_{\sigma}|\leq k
and its total cost is at most
w
.
For the minimum sum vertex cover problem, the set of any feasible solution must contain all vertices with degree greater than
k
. This is no longer true in our setting. Instead, we require the Sunflower Lemma
[
2
]
.

A
sunflower
in a hypergraph
H
consists of a (possibly empty) subset
K\subseteq V(H)
, called the
core
, and a subset
P\subseteq E(H)
, called the
petals
, such that the intersection of every pair of distinct hyperedges in
P
is exactly
K
. The size of the sunflower is
|P|
.

Lemma 5.1

(Sunflower Lemma
[
2
]
)
.

If a hypergraph
H
with no repeated edges has more than
r(H)\cdot r(H)!(k-1)^{r(H)}
hyperedges, then
H
contains a sunflower with
k
petals, and such a sunflower can be computed in time polynomial in
|E(H)|
,
|V(H)|
, and
k
.

If there is a sunflower with
k+1
petals, then any set cover of
H
with at most
k
vertices must contain at least one vertex from its core
K
.
Since
K
cannot be larger than the size of any petal, we have
|K|\leq r(H)
. Thus, we can branch on choosing a vertex from
K
: this is why our algorithm only works on bounded-rank hypergraphs.
We repeat this until there is no sunflower with
k+1
petals, or
k
vertices have already been selected.
By the Sunflower Lemma, the number of edges, and hence the number of vertices, in the remaining instance is bounded. We can then find the remaining vertices of the solution set by branching, and finally enumerate all possible orderings of this set.

We now summarize the algorithm in Figure
3
and use it to prove Theorem
1.4
.

1.

S\leftarrow\emptyset
;

2.

while

|E(H-S)|>r\cdot r!(k-|S|)^{r}
and
|S|\leq k

do

2.1.

if

|S|=k

then return
“no”;

2.2.

find a sunflower of
H-S
with core
K
;

2.3.

if

K=\emptyset

then return
“no”;

2.4.

guess a vertex
v\in K
and add it to
S
;

3.

remove all isolated vertices in
H-S
;

4.

guess a subset
T\subseteq V(H-S)
with
|T|\leq k-|S|
and
S\leftarrow S\cup T
;

5.

guess an bijective mapping
\sigma\colon S\rightarrow\{1,2,\dots,|S|\}
;

6.

if

S
is a set cover of
H
and the cost of
\sigma
is
\leq w

then return
“yes”;

else return
“no.”

Figure 3
:
The parameterized algorithm for minimum sum set cover.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We use the algorithm described in Figure
3
.
Since the algorithm returns “yes” only when a solution is explicitly constructed, it suffices to show that a solution must be returned for every yes-instance.
Let
S
denote the set of elements contained in an optimal solution, and let
r=r(H)
.

The algorithm has two phases: first, finding the vertex set
S
(steps 1–4), and then computing an ordering of
S
(step 5).
We find the solution set
S
by branching.
During this process, we work with the hypergraph
H-S
: all hyperedges intersecting
S
have already been covered.
If
H-S
still has more than
r\cdot r!(k-|S|)^{r}
hyperedges, then by the Sunflower Lemma we can find a sunflower with
k-|S|+1
petals
[
2
]
.
Any solution must contain a vertex from the core of this sunflower; otherwise, it would need to use a distinct vertex to cover each petal, which would require more than
k
vertices and thus be infeasible.
Thus, there cannot be a feasible solution when the core is empty.
Therefore, either step 2.3 correctly returns “no,” or at least one branch in step 2.4 adds a vertex from the optimal solution.

Once the algorithm exits the loop in step 2, we are allowed to add at most
k-|S|
additional vertices, and these can be found by enumeration: we branch over all subsets
T\subseteq V(H-S)
with
|T|\leq k-|S|
.

We now analyze the running time.
The search tree in step 2 has depth at most
k
, and the branching factor is at most the core size, which is bounded by
r
. Thus, this phase takes time
O(r^{|S|+1}\cdot r!(k-1)^{r}m)
, where
m=|E(H)|
.
After step 2, we have
|E(H-S)|\leq r\cdot r!(k-|S|)^{r}
by the Sunflower Lemma, and hence
|V(H-S)|\leq r|E(H-S)|\leq r^{2}\cdot r!(k-|S|)^{r}
because there are no isolated vertices. Thus, there are
O\bigl((r^{2}\cdot r!(k-1)^{r})^{k-|S|}\bigr)

subsets to be enumerated in step 4.
Step 5 takes
O(k!)
time.
Overall, the total running time is
O\left(r^{r+k}k^{r}m+(rk)^{rk}\right)
.
∎

Let us remark that we have not made an attempt to optimize the running time. For example, Step 5 can be improved using the Held–Karp dynamic program
[
4
]
.

6
Concluding remarks

The most compelling open question is to close the
\log\log\tau(G)
gap between the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
for graphs and the lower bound given by the construction in Theorem
1.3
.

We also leave open the fixed-parameter tractability of minimum sum set cover on general hypergraphs when parameterized solely by
\overrightarrow{\tau}
.
Since
\overrightarrow{\tau}
and
\tau
are polynomially related (Theorem
1.1
), minimum sum vertex cover is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to
\tau

[
6
]
. For hypergraphs, this is very unlikely to remain true.
In fact, we conjecture that minimum sum set cover is NP-hard even on hypergraphs with bounded minimum set cover number.
One possible approach toward proving this would be to adapt our reduction from Theorem
1.2
, for which

\phi(H_{G})=3\phi(G)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}3\left(2^{n-i}-(n-i)\right).

However, this reduction produces instances whose size grows exponentially.
