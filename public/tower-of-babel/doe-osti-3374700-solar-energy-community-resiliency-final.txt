 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Solar Energy CommUnity Resiliency (SECURE) project aimed to support
development of resilient community microgrids to improve grid reliability and maintain
power during times of crisis. The SECURE project approached this objective by
addressing key technical and business challenges impeding implementation of resilient
community microgrids.

To achieve the goals of this project, we pursued several specific objectives through
comprehensive design and requirements development. This included the creation of the
solution architecture, detailed requirements, analytical approaches, and component
designs for the four core elements of our approach:
 1. Development of decentralized autonomous community control (DAC) technology
 to support flexible microgrid boundaries.
 2. Creation of islandable communication systems
 3. Implementation of active power reserve capabilities for grid-forming photovoltaic
 (PV) inverters to address stability and regulation challenges.
 4. Establishment of viable economic frameworks for community microgrids.

The project team evaluated the performance of decentralized autonomous community
(DAC) control strategies and islandable communication techniques within a controlled
laboratory environment. These tests assessed the connectivity and interface capabilities
of the communication systems, particularly their ability to integrate with third-party field
devices such as distributed energy resources (DERs), switches, capacitors, and voltage
regulators. Additionally, a grid-forming photovoltaic (GFM PV) inverter equipped with an
active power reserves algorithm was successfully integrated into the test bed and tested
for its ability to enhance microgrid stability and regulation.

The laboratory demonstrations confirmed the technical effectiveness of the proposed
solutions, showcasing advancements in autonomous control, resilient communications,
and inverter functionality. Publicly available documentation on economic frameworks,
resilience valuation methodologies, and microgrid analysis approaches further supports
the feasibility of these solutions for broader industry adoption. These contributions are
instrumental in lowering implementation costs, reducing operational risks, and improving
technologies that enhance microgrid performance—ultimately strengthening the
reliability and resilience of public power supply during wide-area outages.

Although the project did not advance to its proposed field demonstration phase, the
completed laboratory testing and the development of industry-accepted analysis and
interconnection methodologies represent a significant achievement. These outcomes
directly address key barriers to community microgrid implementation and provide a
foundation for future deployment, offering tangible benefits to utilities, DER owners, and
the public at large.

 I. Background:
What is a Community Microgrid?
A microgrid is a group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources (DER)
with a defined electrical boundary that can connect and disconnect from the wider
electric power system and act as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid. For
decades, single-customer back-up systems have been the predominant microgrid type;
recently, microgrid implementations have expanded to serve a wide range of customer
and utility needs. Microgrid development has become an important component of the
modern grid; see, for example, the US Department of Energy’s Microgrid Initiative [1].
This paper focuses on community microgrids, which encompass multiple interconnected
customers and DER.
Community microgrids may have different ownership structures. A utility may own and
operate both DER and controller, or a third party might own and operate the controller
and DER. Regardless of ownership, the underlying technical characteristics are similar:
the microgrid boundary includes multiple customers and all or part of a distribution
feeder. The ownership framework may have more notable impacts on the microgrid’s
design and economics-driven operational strategy [2] [3]. This whitepaper addresses
both utility- and third party-owned microgrids as community microgrids, to discuss
collectively their technical merits and challenges.

For broader background and context, a detailed overview of other microgrid
implementation types and drivers is provided in Microgrids: Expanding Applications,
Implementations, and Business Structures [4] and Program on Technology Innovation:
Microgrid Implementations: Literature Review [5].
Motivation and Benefit
Resilience and reliability are key drivers for any microgrid implementation. Traditional
single-customer microgrids are effective, but not designed to help communities or
minimize the broader outage impacts. Further, to the extent that single-customer

                                             Page 8 of 172

microgrids prevent coordination between resources, they may conflict with broader
community needs. Increasingly, affected communities have access to solar, storage and
other re-energizing resources, but coordinated control systems capable of acting on the
community’s behalf are still under development.
Expanded community value
Community microgrids are designed to provide reliability to critical services and
infrastructure, and quantifying that value is relatively well-understood. Researchers in
academic, public commission, and regulatory arenas are working on the complex
challenge of quantifying the value of community microgrid resilience [7].
The development of community microgrids aligns with U.N. Sustainable Development
goals to “ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all”
and “build resilient infrastructure, promote inclusive and sustainable industrialization and
foster innovation”[8]. These microgrids can offer a more equitable pathway to
community resilience and reliability, by serving all customers in the electrical boundary,
including those who do not own DER.
For example, while a small subset of customers can afford to pay for an isolatable,
single-site backup power system (that is, single-customer, behind-the-meter
microgrids), most customers with solar lose all power during distribution grid outages,
because the DER must cease to energize during the outage. Community microgrids,
provide the means to harness that underutilized, renewable capacity resource. Given
the increasing percentage of behind-the-meter (BTM) solar installed on distribution
grids, feeder-level microgrids present an opportunity to leverage existing DER as
additional local generation capacity during an outage.
With appropriate rate mechanisms in place, utilities can leverage the ability of
community microgrids to provide grid-connected services during normal economic-
dispatch operations, thereby making the system more valuable to all customers.
For further insights on the research into the value of community microgrids, interested
readers can access the Applying EPRI’s Microgrid Cost-Benefit Framework [3],
Resiliency and Microgrids: Events and Materials [9], and Grid Modernization: Metrics
Analysis [10].

 II. Project Objectives
Summary of the Statement of Project Objectives:
This project developed and demonstrated key technologies needed to achieve the DOE
SETO goals. The overall goal was to increase the resiliency of the grid, and to do this
through community microgrids that can operate at the feeder level during times of crisis
while also optimizing reliability and value during normal conditions.

The SECURE project achieved the objectives of the project through the following
efforts:
 1. Identify and develop viable economic frameworks that can justify benefits to all
 stakeholders addressing the current regulatory and economic barriers to
 community microgrids
 2. Develop and demonstrate the capability of De-Centralized Autonomous
 Community Control (DAC) for communities to independently isolate from the
 surrounding grid, to start and to operate reliably during times of crisis and

                                       Page 9 of 172

 operate more economically and reliably during normal operation. Design
 comprehensive interconnection requirements for community microgrids
 3. Develop and demonstrate adaptive, islandable communication systems that
 enable community microgrids to function when normal communications (e.g.,
 cellular or Internet) would be unavailable.
 4. Design, develop and evaluate an active power reserve control algorithm for grid
 forming PV. This approach can address the challenge of cost and limited lifetime
 of storage as well as the complexity of shedding loads in community microgrids
 with high solar penetration.

Effort 1: Viable Economic Frameworks for Community Microgrids
To address the lack of clear business cases for the individual parties involved in a
community microgrid, the project developed viable economic frameworks that can justify
benefits to DER owners, consumers, and utilities. Outcomes include: 1) Broad industry
engagement and awareness of the community microgrid vision and research, 2)
Multiple documented frameworks with diverse stakeholder consensus, and 3) Improved
regulatory understanding of community microgrids.

Effort 2: De-Centralized Autonomous Community Control (DAC)
A community controller was developed and demonstrated to address the gap that
presently exists between central control (e.g. DMS at the distribution utility) and individual
control (e.g. a single site microgrid control). In BP1 and BP2, the project team focused
on the identifying and designing the DAC’s key functionalities, develop requirements of
the key elements, identify sensor and data requirements for situational awareness,
implement and lab validate the functional requirements.

Effort 3: Islandable Communication Systems
This effor focused on developing and demonstrating adaptive communication systems
that enable community microgrids to function when normal comms (e.g., cellular or
Internet) would be unavailable. Cyber and functional aspects related to pre-configuring
communication system devices with knowledge of the electrical system topology were
also investigated.

Effort 4: Active Power Reserves Capability for Grid Forming PV Inverters
The objective of this effort was to develop control strategies that will allow PV GFM to
operate with active power reserve and thereby enhance the ability of solar DER to provide
power under dynamic community load conditions.

 III. Project Results and Discussion:

Effort 1: Viable Economic Frameworks for Community Microgrids
Cost-Effectiveness Metrics for Community Microgrids
Introduction
Metrics that estimate the cost-effectiveness of community microgrids should be clearly
defined, calculable with readily available data, and should give a reliable indication of
whether the community microgrid could be considered financially justified. What it
means for a community microgrid to be cost-effective or financially justified can vary
depending on whose perspective is being considered, how resilience is valued, and how
equitably costs and benefits are distributed. This report lays out several metrics that are
useful in different circumstances, though metrics alone can only ever provide directional
guidance for decision making.
The metrics defined below include a ratepayer impact measure, which compares the
present value of costs and benefits to a utility of building, owning, and operating a
community microgrid, a participant cost test, that compares the present value of costs
and benefits for each category of community-driven microgrid party (utility, customer,
aggregator), and a cost of resilience metric that levelizes the total costs of a microgrid
over kWh of energy it supplies during grid outages. The key inputs for these metrics
include:
 • The value of resilience (only for the participant cost test), as estimated by a
 number of methods described below
 • The schedule of costs imposed by the microgrid to each party
 • The schedule of financial benefits generated by microgrid DER for each party if
 the DER are not included in the “business as usual” case
Additional detail is provided in the Appendix.

                                          Page 15 of 172

Metric Inputs
Parties and Participants
What is considered “cost-effective” depends on the question, “cost-effective for whom?”.
Community microgrids naturally pull together several parties into one financial
arrangement and each of these parties might not agree that a microgrid is or is not cost-
effective. To be cost-effective overall, all involved parties should consider the microgrid
cost-effective individually.
 • Customers in microgrid boundary
 Community microgrids necessarily include multiple customers, each of whom
 should consider the microgrid cost-effective for themselves if they bear any of the
 costs.
 • Utility
 The local electric utility will be a part of every community microgrid because the
 distribution equipment they own and operate is a required component of the
 microgrid.
 • 3rd party microgrid aggregator
 Some community microgrids will employ a 3rd party organization to manage cash
 flows between the microgrid customers, the local electric utility, and any other
 parties involved, to coordinate communication, and potentially to control parts of
 the microgrid.
 • Ratepayers
 If the ratepayers bear any of the costs of a community microgrid, they should
 consider the investment to be cost-effective either because they recognize the
 microgrid as the least-cost best-fit solution to a problem that needs to be solved
 or because the benefits of the microgrid extend sufficiently to all ratepayers.
 • Society
 The broader society can bear the burdens of emissions and inequity. Community
 microgrids should present a positive prospect for society in these respects.
Financial Benefit Categories
Not every community microgrid need include every possible value stream. A microgrid
entirely built around existing DER may generate little value outside the value of
resilience.
 • Energy
 o Energy generation and shifting
 DER in community microgrids can generate electricity, as in the case of
 solar or other generation, and can shift energy in time to better align
 electricity demands with generation, as in the case of energy storage. The
 value of electricity generated or shifted by DER in the microgrid can be
 included. If the DER is customer-owned, customer-sited, and reduces the
 customer’s electricity bills, it may contribute to that customer’s cost-
 effectiveness assessment. Similarly, DER owned and operated by a 3rd
 party organization may benefit that organization by generating or shifting
 energy for wholesale energy markets or similar. The quantity and timing of

                                      Page 16 of 172

 electricity use and generation in any community microgrid will impact the
 utility’s cost of service, which can influence the cost-effectiveness of the
 microgrid to the utility. Note that microgrids built using existing DER will
 not necessarily include all these elements in the incremental benefits of
 the microgrid.
 o Energy use reduction
 Community microgrids may include energy efficiency investments in the
 overall project. These can reduce the amount of energy going to end uses
 within the microgrid footprint, which can benefit customers through energy
 bill reductions and the utility through cost of service reductions.
 • Ancillary services
 Some DER can be accredited to provide ancillary services and the financial value
 of these ancillary services can be used to offset some of the costs of the
 microgrid. The financial value may be realized by the DER owner or operator and
 passed through to the appropriate party through a 3rd party aggregator or similar.
 • Resource adequacy / capacity
 By reducing the coincident peak demand from the microgrid , overall resource
 adequacy requirements could be lower relative to the demand profile before the
 microgrid, resulting in a lower cost of service for the utility. This value may be
 passed to customers or 3rd parties through a demand response program or
 similar. A variable factor here is the extent to which the benefit of lowered peak
 demand accrues to customers within the microgrid boundary, as opposed to the
 utility’s ratepayers in general; this will depend on the ownership and
 administrative structure of the microgrid components.
 • Transmission or distribution upgrade deferral
 Generation, energy shifting, and load management in the microgrid may,
 explicitly or implicitly, result in less extreme loading on distribution or
 transmission equipment, which can reduce the cost of service for the utility.
 • DERMS Value
 • Grid-forming PV inverter incremental value
Non-Financial Benefit Categories
  • Resilience: While some benefits of resilience are arguably quantifiable in
 economic terms, there are other benefits of resilience that are more challenging
 to quantify. These include, but are not necessarily limited to, the following:
 o Increased trust in the electric grid (leading to a greater willingness to
 electrify and, in turn, contribute to decarbonization efforts). Households
 will, for example, be more likely to switch to an induction stovetop and
 purchase an EV if they are confident they will be able to cook and
 transport themselves without concern for grid outages.
 o Social stability. If an interruption in one community results in unrest, then
 society as a whole will incur costs that are likely to be borne, in part, by
 those not directly affected by interrupted service. (A well-known example

                                       Page 17 of 172

 of social unrest in the wake of an interruption is the New York City
 blackout of 1977.) These costs can, for instance, include insurance claims
 that cause everyone’s premiums to increase, as well as the perception
 that public safety is at greater risk.
 o The psychological impacts of isolation, lack of
 communications/stimulation. Isolation can exact a toll in the absence of
 resilience, but the cost of these psychological impacts is challenging to
 quantify. This can manifest as implications for education and lifetime
 income potential. For example, COVID studies note how education
 suffered from students being at home; power and internet outages and
 associated lack of instruction and resources could have similar effects.
 o Additional costs individuals might bear (beyond those associated with
 social stability, access to goods and services, etc.) because others lack
 power. This requires knowing how far the impacts (and therefore the
 benefits of resilience) extend beyond an interruption’s geographic scope
 and duration. For example, a volunteer community group operating a soup
 kitchen might curtail its activities due to a lack of power, which could affect
 individuals not directly affected by an interruption.
While some benefits are challenging to quantify, they are benefits nonetheless. If they
can be identified, they merit consideration in a cost-benefit analysis, even if only on a
qualitative basis. In some cases, the qualitative merits of resilience value may alone be
sufficient to justify resilience investments—for example, in situations where a resilience
investment is likely to preclude civil unrest and thereby enhance social stability. (In this
situation, a resilience investment might be deemed worthwhile even if quantitative
measures show costs to be significantly higher than quantifiable benefits.) In other
circumstances, a hard-to-quantify benefit may be the deciding factor in a cost-benefit
analysis that would otherwise show an ambiguous or borderline result; the qualitative
benefit might be used as a deciding factor in an investment decision.

Utility-Driven Community Microgrid Cost-Effectiveness Metrics
Utility-driven community microgrids are generally implemented to solve a particular
problem that results in sub-standard quality for service that will be corrected by the
microgrid. In these cases, the community microgrid represents the least-cost best-fit

                                       Page 18 of 172

solution, coming in at a lower overall cost to ratepayers than alternatives like new
transmission, grid hardening, or other potential interventions. Economic metrics can
address the “least-cost” component of this decision but cannot identify the “best-fit”
solution. Details are provided in the Appendix regarding the metric addressing the cost
part of the decision.

Community-Driven Community Microgrid Cost-Effectiveness Metrics
Community-driven community microgrids may involve many individuals, customer
accounts, 3rd party organizations, and the electric utility all working together. At
minimum, for the microgrid to be considered cost-effective, each party in this complex
arrangement needs to recover their own costs and ideally all costs and benefits will be
distributed equitably. There are many ways that a distribution of costs and benefits
could be evaluated for equity, but these come down to subjective evaluations of the
value of resilience for each customer, the scale and scope of customers’ electric
demands during grid outages, and accounting for socialized costs through the electric
utility, so this report focuses on meeting the cost-effective criteria rather than evaluating
equity.
Utility Cost-Effectiveness
If a community-driven community microgrid is installed somewhere that relies on
distribution equipment from the local electric utility and the ability to coordinate control of
this equipment with the rest of the microgrid, then the utility is implicated for microgrid
development and operation costs. These costs support a quality of service above the
minimum standards for the area, so should not be borne by the utility’s ratepayers at
large and should be collected from the microgrid participants that directly benefit from
the resilience it provides. If these costs are fully recovered from microgrid participants,
the utility may consider the microgrid to be cost-effective.
Customer Cost-Effectiveness
In community-driven community microgrids, participating customers may be charged for
their participation. Participation payments might go to a 3rd party microgrid aggregator,
for example, who will use that money to fund microgrid costs. For customers like this,
the value of the resilience provided by the microgrid should be greater than the costs
they pay.
Some customers may bring their own DER to the microgrid. Presumably, these
customers will receive the full financial benefits from their DER, so the costs and
benefits of the microgrid are incremental to the DER from their perspective, meaning
that their decision to participate in the microgrid can consider the DER costs as sunk
costs and leave them out of the cost-effectiveness calculation. DER that are built for the
microgrid or upgraded for the microgrid will count toward the total microgrid costs to be
socialized among participants. Some community microgrids may compensate DER
owners for supporting the microgrid using their own DER during grid outages as well.
3rd Party Cost-Effectiveness
Some community microgrids might employ a 3rd party microgrid aggregator. This
organization will find the microgrid to be cost effective if cash flows are balanced,
including any profit taking. Cash flows for the third party may include distribution service
payments to the utility, DER costs, DER value in wholesale markets, participant

                                        Page 19 of 172

payments, microgrid integration costs, communications costs, controls costs, and debt
service costs.
Participant Cost Test
For each of the parties involved, the cost-effectiveness of a community-driven
community microgrid can come down to a present value benefit to cost ratio participant
cost test that compares the cost of participation to the benefits received, including the
value of resilience. Individual customers in a microgrid may value resilience differently,
but some method for calculating the value of resilience is required to compute this cost
effectiveness metric. This metric calculates the present value of all costs incurred by
each microgrid participant and compares this to the present value of all benefits
received by each microgrid participant individually. Customer decision making can be
much more complex than is reflected in this metric, so this should not be used to assess
the likelihood of participation or willingness to pay, but rather should be used as a
financial indicator of the cost-effectiveness of the microgrid.
The other parties involved, particularly the utility and a 3rd party aggregator, must also
consider the microgrid to be cost-effective, so this metric takes three parts – one part for
the collection customers in the microgrid, one part for the utility, and one part for a 3rd
party aggregator. All three cost-effectiveness tests must pass for the microgrid to be
considered cost-effective.

Cost of Resilience
If calculating the value of resilience proves difficult or impossible, cost-effectiveness can
be assessed by understanding the unit cost of resilience in $/kWh of energy served
during grid outages. This metric can be compared to a value of lost load estimate
(VoLL) or to other benchmarks for reasonable amounts of money to spend to cover
loads during grid outages. To calculate this metric, the present value of non-resilience
financial benefits is subtracted from the present value of all costs associated with the
microgrid and levelized over each kWh of energy delivered by the microgrid during grid
outages.
This metric does not separate individual perspectives and relies on the assumption that
costs and benefits are appropriately distributed among the utility, the customers, and
any 3rd parties. Under this assumption, this metric can help inform whether the microgrid
as a whole has the potential to be cost-effective.

Effort 3: Islandable Communication Systems
Resilient Community Microgrids are intended to enable operation of a localized power
system during emergency events resulting in grid outages. This can be accomplished
using distributed energy resources (DER) to provide energy and enabling autonomous
power system control at the feeder level to provide the ability to operate without local
human operators. The decentralized control architecture is expected to maximize
community benefit during normal conditions by interfacing upstream with central
systems and isolating and coordinating with peer systems to provide power to local
communities during emergency events. These are sometimes referred to as “black sky”
events. They have occurred in the US after major hurricanes. Significant effort has gone
into securing the grid to prevent “black sky” events from cyber-attacks. Widespread grid
outage will likely result in telecommunications outages for fixed and wireless
commercial services when the backup power for those commercial services is
exhausted. Backup power for commercial telecom providers typically ranges from a few
hours to a day. To allow the Resilient Community Microgrid to remain in operation when
commercial communication networks are unavailable, an islandable communication
system paired with the islandable power system of the microgrid is required. Like a
microgrid, the islandable communications network will transition to its autonomous
mode of operation when it finds the normal infrastructure connections are unavailable. It
must also be able to “black start” into an islanded mode for the use case where the
microgrid startup is delayed, or if the microgrid itself has a temporary outage during the
emergency event. The focus of this research is on wireless technologies, but it is
acknowledged that wireline communication technologies (fiber, copper, and Power Line
Carrier) may also be employed as a component of a resilient microgrid network based
on availability and cost factors.

Test Environment
Cellular Networks Utilized
For this test, two cellular networks were utilized. One was a commercial cellular
network, and one was a private cellular network. The SIM cards for both networks were
configured in a manner to ensure isolation from public traffic.
Commercial Network
For the commercial network, that required the utilization of a private APN. Although this
did not utilize completely private infrastructure, it did virtually isolate the traffic and
provided sufficient traffic engineering to enable a semi-private network experience by
creating a private virtual space to operate within. The utilization of a private APN is
common practice within utilities that cannot put forth the capital required for private
infrastructure. By using a private APN, a commercial cellular provider can virtually

                                         Page 26 of 172

manage the network assets to give the appearance and performance characteristics
required for a utility to have an experience like that of a private network consisting of
infrastructure that they own. However, the private APN does operate on the cellular
network provider’s public infrastructure. Thus, it is possible that an abundance of
consumer traffic may impact the performance of a network using a private APN, unless
performance guarantees are provided within the contract.
Private Network
The private network was implemented using LTE in the Citizens Broadband Radio
Service (CBRS) spectrum. CBRS is a unique shared spectrum band authorized by the
FCC in the USA. It is available nationwide, on a shared basis with federal incumbents.
To prevent interference to incumbents, operation requires permission from a Spectrum
Access System (SAS). The connection to the SAS takes place over the Internet, and a
successful grant must be obtained every 24 hours (with regulatory changes in effect as
of June 2024). This is acknowledged as contradictory to the project goals of having the
private network operate independently of the Internet and commercial services in case
of significant outage events. The choice of CBRS was based on the expectation that the
project would conduct a field demonstration, at an initially unknown utility location. It
was also necessary to pre-stage and integrate the system at other locations. Acquiring
temporary permissions to access licensed LTE spectrum in multiple locations would be
challenging. For an actual build of such a system, the utility building the microgrid could
work out an agreement with a commercial cellular carrier to allow operation in a portion
of their spectrum – if and only if their commercial network was not operating. Other
approaches such as LTE in Unlicensed Spectrum 1 for the private network could be
 0F

viable, depending on the location of the microgrid and the local use of unlicensed
spectrum.

The Core
The core of the network consisted of a general-purpose computer. Within this machine
were several software components. Specifically, there was a hypervisor, which is virtual
machine management system, that ran several virtual machine instances on the
computer. These instances managed different elements of the network. Moreover,
these instances instantiated several network interfaces that partitioned the sections of
the network management into discrete structures. This was important for two reasons:
 1. The utilization of a CBRS network required that the core send heartbeat signals
 to a Spectrum Access System. This service was accessible through a public
 internet connection. To facilitate this, EPRI utilized a cellular modem with a
 public APN. By doing this, EPRI was able to improve the mobility or the core.
 2. With access to the public internet needed by the CBRS network management
 system, a method was required to isolate the private network from public traffic
 and intrusion. As a result, the utilization of virtual machines enabled more control
 over interface access and improved security between software instances running
 on the host machine.
The core network had two main methods for configuration and access: a web server
and through SSH. Throughout this project, the web server was typically used. It is

                                                         Page 28 of 172

within this environment that routes were established to enable UE-to-UE connectivity
among the CBRS SIM cards.
The Network Management System and eNodeB
The Network Management System and eNodeB worked together to enable appropriate
configurations and system requirements for the network. This included the
configuration of the eNodeB RF parameters as well as the security requirements for the
Spectrum Access System.
The Network Management System was contained within one of the virtual machine
environments on the general-purpose computer that hosted the core. In addition, much
of its configuration was managed through its web server; however, there were some
system-level configurations that were required to be made in the virtual machine
environment. Within in this environment, care had to be taken to ensure that traffic
destined for the Spectrum Access System would be transmitted successfully.
Because the Network Management System utilized hostname addresses rather than IP
addresses to contact the Spectrum Access System, it was important to verify the correct
DNS settings. Moreover, these DNS setting had to be configured to ensure that the
hostname of the virtual machine did not deviate from what was expected by the Network
Management System. For example, if the Network Management System was expecting
a host, named “localhost,” to submit changes, then any transient hostnames assigned
via DNS must match the configurations set within the Network Management System.
EPRI had encountered errors where the eNodeB would shut down and all services were
offline due to this error. This error resulted from the utilization of DNS Servers from the
commercial carrier assigned to the SIM of the public-facing modem. Once changed
within the Network Management System’s Virtual Machine, the transient hostname was
automatically modified to what was expected into the system. It is important to note that
this was only a temporary fix. A more robust solution would require the Network
Management System to use the transient hostname assigned via DNS within the Virtual
Machine of the Network Management System.
The User Equipment (UE)
The UEs for the private network and the commercial network were the same devices.
By utilizing dual SIMs, automatic failover could be configured. To accomplish this,
several parameters had to be configured. First, a GRE tunnel was established to link
the modems to each other over the commercial network. Even though the private
network’s core was configured for UE-to-UE communication, the utilization of the GRE
tunnel provided redundancy. Moreover, due to routing changes within the two
networks, Open Shortest Path First (OSPF) routing was utilized. This enabled the
modems to select the most accommodating routing depending on the network to which
they were attached. This eliminated the need to utilize several different routing entries
and any additional configuration required at the time of failover. This resulted in
automatic route establishment that facilitated the automatic failover goal. Two different
modem vendors were used, and both were unable to failover and back between two
networks without manual intervention of some type. The issues were identified as bugs

                                      Page 29 of 172

or implementation issues in the modems. The vendors are aware, and the capability
should be addressed in future firmware updates.

Conclusion

The Islandable Communication task in the SECURE project is to develop and evaluate
an Islandable communications network that can maintain connectivity and support the
microgrid operation, even if the communications networks outside the microgrid are
unavailable.
The final phase of the Islandable Communications task of the SECURE project
consisted of integrating the islandable communications network into a microgrid
simulation at NREL. Initially integration was planned for a microgrid implemented as a
mix of simulation and physical hardware. As the project evolved, the microgrid became
fully simulated. The simulation environment was structured to break out the
communication paths that would pass over a communication network in a field
                                       Page 33 of 172

demonstration. The Islandable Communication network was integrated into the
simulation environment, allowing both a commercial cellular carrier and a Private LTE
network to be evaluated. The microgrid simulation showed differences in performance
and behavior when operating over the wireless networks (compared to operating over
wired Ethernet in the lab). These differences point to further research opportunities to
better understand how microgrid communication carried over real-world or impaired
networks affects the microgrid system behavior.

The support of multi-SIM operation with autonomous failover between cellular networks
was found to be an immature capability for cellular modems. Modem vendors exhibited
challenges performing fail-over and fail-back without manual intervention.

Changes in performance of the microgrid simulations were observed when running over
the wireless network with limited bandwidth and higher latency, compared to the wired
Ethernet normally used in the lab environment. Further research could explore how
microgrid control algorithms could be modified to be more tolerant of communication
limitations.

Resilient Microgrids are a foremost example of where a resilient islandable
communication network is an important design consideration. Many utilities are building
Private LTE, which ensures the level of resilience required for microgrid operation in
extended outage scenarios without requiring a separate network. For the many utilities
that have not built Private LTE, the concept of an Islandable network that normally
operates on commercial cellular but can fail over to a localized network can serve in
resilient microgrids and may provide benefits for other related use cases.

Effort 4: Active Power Reserve from Grid-forming (GFM) PV Plants
Active power reserve concept overview
In order for GFM DERs to follow the load and to have proper active power sharing in
microgrid operation, active power-frequency (P-f) droop as illustrated in Figure III-5 can
be enabled as the primary control function at each GFM DER. By activating P-f droop
control, a GFM DER has the capability to provide down-reserve (i.e., following a load
reduction event, as system frequency increases, the down-reserve function enabled in
the DER reduces its active power output).
However, P-f droop control does not guarantee up-reserve from a GFM PV-type DER.
When it operates at the maximum power point (𝑃𝑃𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚 in Figure 6) and load increases, the
GFM PV-type DER cannot further increase its active power output, and its frequency
drops as indicated by the vertical power limit line at 𝑃𝑃𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚 . The frequency drop will cause
other DERs with remaining reserves to increase the power output and balance the load.

Requirements and Specifications for GFM PV plants APR in a Fully inverter-based
microgrid
This section describes the requirements and specifications of GFM PV plant APR in a
fully inverter-based microgrid (i.e., without any slower-response resources like
synchronous generators). The requirements will cover both steady state and dynamic
performance of the GFM plant. The goal of the requirements is to firstly ensure proper
steady state frequency inside the microgrid while balancing the load. Moreover, the
frequency dynamics are also constrained by the requirements to prevent unnecessary
load shedding and/or tripping of behind-the-meter DERs.

Operation Modes of PV and BESS
In a fully inverter-based microgrid with high PV penetration, a BESS (or storage-based
resource) is still required to supply load during nighttime and during hours when the
solar irradiance is low and the loading levels are high. The power and energy rating of
the BESS should be designed considering the amount of (critical) load inside the
microgrid and the expected operational duration of the microgrid.
In an islanded microgrid, depending on the maximum available PV generation and the
loading level, there can be different operation scenarios, which are illustrated in Figure
III-6. In scenarios (a) and (b), the load is greater than the maximum available PV
generation (i.e., excess load). Therefore, the BESS should discharge to balance the
load. In scenario (a) where the excess load is greater than the BESS’ maximum
discharge rate, load curtailment is required to maintain power balance. On the other
hand, in scenarios (c) and (d), the available PV generation is higher than load (i.e.,
excess PV), and it can be used to charge the battery. However, when the excess net
power is greater than the BESS’ maximum charge rate, curtailment of PV generation is
necessary.

Figure III-6. Operation Scenarios in a fully GFM inverter-based microgrid with PV and
BESS with PV net load along the horizontal axis. Scenario: a) load must be curtailed, b)
BESS must discharge, c) BESS can charge, and d) PV must be curtailed.
The operation modes of PV, BESS and load corresponding to each scenario are
designed as shown in Table III-2. Note that when there is excess load, the PV plant is
designed to work at MPPT to reduce the BESS discharging and thereby increase the
duration of microgrid operation. When there is excess PV generation, the PV continues
to work at MPPT to maximize BESS charging and is only curtailed when the excess PV
generation surpasses the BESS’ maximum charge rate. With this design approach, PV

                                       Page 36 of 172

is working at MPPT in most of the scenarios and provides only down reserve. It
provides up reserve only after it is curtailed due to excess PV generation.

Requirements and Specifications for GFM PV Plant APR
The primary P-f droop control of PV and BESS plants together with the dispatch from
DAC should be properly designed to realize the operation modes. An important factor to
consider here is that the maximum charge and discharge rates of BESS are not fixed
but vary with the state of charge (SoC) of the battery. If the SoC is low, a BESS can
charge at a higher charge rate and make better use of the excess PV generation. When
the SoC is close to fully charged, the BESS may limit or reduce the charge rate and
when the SoC is full, the BESS cannot charge any more.
As a result, fixed droop settings of the PV and BESS plants cannot achieve the
operation modes shown in Table III-2. Instead, DAC should closely monitor the SoC of
the BESS and the resulting maximum charge and discharge rates. This requires the
BESS to be able to send information regarding its SoC to the DAC. Based on this
information and the load and PV forecast, DAC can then dispatch the P-f droop settings
of GFM PV and BESS plants through tertiary control. Since the droop settings will be
updated periodically, both the GFM PV and BESS should have the capability to modify
their droop settings (both reference values and the droop slope) through commands
issued by the DAC.

Requirements and Specifications for GFM PV plants APR in a microgrid with slower-
response resources
This section presents the requirements and specifications associated with APR of GFM
PV plants in a mixed-source microgrid (i.e., with slower-response resources such as
synchronous generators). In this scenario, the APR can be provided by the synchronous
generators or the GFM PV plants, or both. However, the slower governor response can
cause issues if the APR is solely provided by synchronous generator (SG), which
necessitates up-reserves from GFM PV plants.
Steady State Requirements
Compared with fast-responding inverters, the governor response of a SG is slow. As a
result, it picks up the load after a load increase in a much slower rate. In a microgrid
with high PV penetration, the inertia level of the system is low. This coupled with the
slower governor response of SG can result in large frequency drop following a load
increase, if the power reserve is provided only by the SG. To avoid this, PV plants
should provide up-reserves and help pick up a portion of the load following a load
increase.
A method to set the droop curves of the PV and the SG P-f functions to achieve this is
shown in Figure III-11. This setting approach guarantees that there is sufficient
headroom from the PV plant even when the available power is at the minimum
estimated value. Notice that this method again assumes that PV and load forecast is
available to the DAC and the DAC can dispatch the PV and SG plant droop curve
periodically. To ensure that the dynamic frequency following a worst-case load
disturbance is bounded within an acceptable frequency range, the slope of the SG and
PV droop slopes should be properly designed. Two ways to design the droops slopes
will be discussed in the following sections.

                                     Page 40 of 172

Figure III-11. The proposed P-f droop setting approach for PV and SG plants.
Dynamic/Transient Requirements
In designing the steady state requirements with PV plants providing up-reserves to help
microgrid frequency, it is again assumed that PV plants act much faster than SGs. As
discussed in chapter 3, even though inverter-based plants are able to respond fast,
without explicit requirements, the dynamic response of the plants may not be sufficient to
maintain stability and acceptable frequency dynamics following a load disturbance.
Therefore, dynamic/transient requirements for GFM PV plants are required to ensure
stable and acceptable frequency dynamics in the microgrid. A microgrid with multiple
inverter-based GFM PV plants and a SG is considered and the requirements for each
individual plant as well as a setting guideline considering the interaction between multiple
GFM plants and the SG are developed.
Requirements on a Single GFM Plant
For a single GFM PV plant, the same requirement presented in chapter 3 can be applied
here. The requirements ensures that each individual GFM PV plant is capable to limit the
frequency deviation following a load disturbance and provide sufficient damping. These
individual plant requirements help the entire microgrid frequency in a mixed-source
microgrid, even though they cannot guarantee the performance of the microgrid
frequency, which calls for a system level setting guideline to be described next.
Setting Guideline Considering the Interaction between Multiple GFM Plants and SG
As mentioned, the individual plant requirements can improve the performance of the
overall system, but they are no longer sufficient to guarantee acceptable frequency
dynamics and stability considering the interaction between multiple plants. A setting
guideline to set the droop slopes of the various GFM plants and the SG to ensure
satisfactory frequency dynamics in the microgrid is necessary. Details on how to
develop this are provided in Appendix E.

Case Study A: Simulation based Method to Determine the Droop Slopes
Since the previous analytical method to determine the droop slopes involves assumptions
and approximations which may not hold true for all the scenarios, in this section, a
simulation-based method is proposed as an alternative approach. Since the droop slopes
are obtained based on simulation studies, it does not include approximations. However,
it requires accurate dynamic simulation models that faithfully represent the behavior of
both the SG and the PV GFM plants. Given that such models are available, simulation
studies with the worst-case planned load disturbance can be conducted with different
slope settings to identify the range of settings that results in satisfactory frequency
performance.

                                     Page 42 of 172

Assume that the worst-case load increase that the microgrid is planned for is 10% of
peak load, which is 400kW. A series of simulation studies can be done starting from PV
plants not providing up reserve (i.e., working at MPP). The results for this scenario are
shown in Figure III-13 as Case 1. As can be seen, following the load increase at 7.5s,
the PV plant only increases its output momentarily then settles to the MPP at 1500kW.
The SG picks up the entire 400kW load. As a result, the frequency dips to around 58Hz.
The droop slope of the PV is then gradually increased. As another scenario, the PV
droop slope is set such that the PV plant will pick up 200kW following the load step
increase, as shown in Figure 4-5 (Case 2). Because of the faster active power response
from PV, the frequency dip in this scenario is around 58.75 Hz. Suppose the acceptable
frequency nadir is above 58.5 Hz, the PV droop slope in this scenario can be used as a
lower limit. In other words, when setting the droop slope for PV according to Figure 4-1,
it should not be below the value identified in Case 2. Using a greater PV droop slope
can further improve the frequency response, however, it requires more up-reserve from
the PV plant. Increasing the up-reserve impacts economics as the PV needs to curtail
further and the SG needs to burn more fuel. Therefore, the PV droop slope should be
set close to the limit identified whenever possible.
Moreover, both the SG and the PV plant droop slope affect the frequency nadir while
the PV droop has more impact in general. Therefore, instead of searching for both SG
and PV droop slopes, the SG droop slope can be fixed at the highest value allowed by
the SG (e.g., 2%) while searching for the lowest PV droop slope without violating the
nadir requirement.

 IV. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
This project investigated and furthered research on the practicality of activating and
using solar and storage resources located within resilient community microgrids to
energize critical loads during times of crisis. The measurable aspects of these goals
include: 1) Successful operation of a feeder-level microgrid via community controller
validated through controller-hardware-in-the-loop lab demonstration, 2) Implemented
and demonstrated isolatable communication systems through lab testing, 3)
Development of reduced dependency on inertia from rotating machinery via active
power reserve capability implemented in a commercial GFM solar PV inverter, 4)
comprehensive assessment and consensus regarding community microgrid viable
economic frameworks, 5) Development of industry-accepted microgrid interconnection
and assessment practices which reduce risk and costs associated with the
implementation of community microgrids.

The laboratory demonstrated technical advancements and public documentation of
economic frameworks, resilience valuation methodology, and microgrid analysis
methodology are key contributions to the wider industry adoption and advancement of
community microgrids. Lowering the cost of implementation, reducing risk associated
with microgrid installation and operation, and improving the technologies that can
enhance the operation of microgrids are essential to improving the reliability and
resilience of public power supply when wide area systems are unavailable.

Although this project was unable to move forward to its proposed field demonstration
phase, the completed laboratory testing and industry-accepted analysis and
interconnection methodologies developed under this project still represent a significant
accomplishment toward the overall project objectives.

 V. Path Forward:
This area of research and development will continue as more community microgrids are
implemented. The project team intends to continue working with community microgrid
implementers and interconnecting utilities to assist in the effective implementation of
these systems. In particular, the microgrid interconnection analysis and assessment
methodology is being utilized in several ongoing microgrid development projects. It will
continue to be refined and integrated into industry standards development, including the
revision of IEEE 1547.4 – Guide to DER Intentional Island Systems. The cost
effectiveness metrics for evaluating resiliency methodologies will also continue to be
investigated and refined as part of EPRI’s public Value of Resiliency Working Group.
The technical advancements implemented in the Siemens microgrid controller, Solectria
Grid-forming PV inverter, and islandable communication network are ready for field
demonstration. The primary barrier to field testing these technologies is the need for
utility-grade test evaluation before such products can be demonstrated on systems that

 Appendix B – Community Microgrid Design and Implementation Details, Options
 and Requirements
 Designing, implementing, and operating community microgrids comes with a unique set
 of technical requirements that prompt considerations beyond the traditional BTM
 microgrid. This appendix details the variety of technical requirements associated with
 community microgrids.
 Outage Recovery
 A resilient microgrid will withstand and rapidly recover from system disruptions and
 extreme events and maintain critical functionality within the microgrid area. Customers
 with critical loads benefit from the rapid restoration of local power during significant
 distribution grid outages. Coordination of community microgrids can improve restoration
 time for wider areas and support distribution grid recovery. Locally-deployed community
 microgrids can black start to rapidly energize critical feeder portions as islands, while
 long-duration outages are addressed by the utility. This outage recovery capability
 provides two key customer values:
 • Reduced outage duration
 • Wider coverage during island operations
 Figure 2 illustrates how community microgrid controllers can provide wider load
 coverage following an outage. At left, a feeder has numerous isolated microgrid backup
 systems that operate independently during a utility outage. At right, feeder-level
 microgrids service a broader set of customers by power-sharing DER in the islanded
 areas.

                                                   Page 57 of 172

Figure 16
Limited outage coverage with isolated backup microgrids (left) versus wider island coverage with community
microgrids (right)

Increased Reliability
A reliable microgrid will consistently and dependably deliver high-quality power to
customers during grid disturbances and utility outages, and its reliability can be
quantified by the frequency and duration of outages for priority loads within the
microgrid. Community microgrids can increase grid reliability as well as customer
reliability in island operations.
Island Operation Reliability
Customers with sensitive loads or those located at the ends of long distribution lines can
benefit from the microgrid reliability providing continuous, local power during distribution
grid outages. Community microgrids add broader reliability by expanding load coverage
during an outage and increasing the number of grid-forming DER that support the island
voltage and frequency stability. Further, certain community microgrid implementations
provide distributed controller redundancy, enabling portions of the wider microgrid to
remain online in the event of a controller failure.
Providing reliability services during island operations offers:
 • Increased critical load coverage during an outage through DER power sharing
 • Reduced transient effects of intermittent generation during islanding. Controller
 coordination across the wider-area microgrid smooths the impact of intermittent
 or unreliable generation sources during island operations.
 • Greater control reliability through the distributed control architecture. Local
 control autonomy allows sections of the community microgrid to continue
 operating when one controller fails. This modularity improves reliability compared
 to a single, centralized controller.
Grid-Connected Reliability Services
Microgrids can also be used to improve distribution grid operations while grid-
connected. The microgrid controller can group the DER it manages to provide services
such as capacity and voltage support. These services can be coordinated across the
community microgrid to augment the benefits to the grid. DER in community microgrids

can also provide transmission system services while grid-connected such as energy,

Microgrids with Flexible Boundaries
Every microgrid is characterized by a clearly-defined electrical boundary. This is critical
to ensure clear, controllable points of isolation from the wider distribution grid and to
safely energize the microgrid area during island operation. A community microgrid
should be designed to meet this requirement; however, certain community microgrids
may permit multiple configurations, and have flexible boundaries. (See Figure 5.) These
microgrids use smart switches to serve critical loads during outages and to expand in
order to serve interruptible loads based on DER availability. This concept has been
demonstrated in a project on the Chattanooga Electric Power Board (EPB) power grid
[11].

With flexible boundaries, the challenge is to appropriately coordinate protection
equipment and design studies for the various island configurations. Each switching and

effective grounding, and other island-specific conditions to ensure safe operations.
In certain cases, microgrids with flexible boundaries are managed by distributed,
networked microgrid controllers. Networked microgrids are created by enabling
neighboring microgrid controllers to facilitate the synchronization of two or more areas,
to form a wider microgrid boundary area. This technology and related control
requirements are explored in Test Plan for a Networked Microgrid Controller [12].
Communications Infrastructure
The communications infrastructure for a community microgrid requires thoughtful
design. Community microgrids may include multiple DERs and customer microgrids with
multiple points of interconnection (POI) to the external area’s electric power system
(EPS). Managing a community microgrid may require intelligent control schemes for
operational coordination between multiple customer microgrids and DER along a feeder
or multiple feeders as illustrated in Figure 6. Communication pathways for a community
microgrid include DER, microgrid controller(s), relays, voltage regulators, the distribution
SCADA system, utility-owned IT equipment including routers, remote terminals for serial

                                                  Page 61 of 172

communications, and firewalls. Control systems managing single-customer microgrids
lack situational awareness of the grid to enable flexible microgrid boundaries through
synchronized circuit sectionalizing schemes. Central controllers may have full visibility
when communication systems are healthy but may fail to operate when needed most.

Today’s communication architectures do not support “islanding” of a subset of
communicating microgrids, solar resources, or other devices in a community microgrid,
to form autonomous local telecommunication networks during a crisis involving loss of
communication with the utility control system. In radio-based systems, necessary
infrastructure base stations may be far from the solar resources, leaving microgrids
unable to power critical telecommunications loads. In the traditional ‘mesh’ architecture,
the network management and authentication servers are remote and require backhaul
connection—in some cases, internet access—to initiate communication between
devices. The communication technologies and pathways for peer-to-peer controller
interactions for a community microgrid should be considered for their availability during
all operational scenarios, particularly during wide-area outages.

Cybersecurity
Cybersecurity is an important consideration when planning a community microgrid. An
advanced microgrid control system, typical for a community microgrid, presents multiple
communication layers. This structure poses a risk to distribution system stability and
critical customer loads, so these concerns should be attended to early in the design of a
community microgrid. Major threats to consider: loss of the microgrid controller, loss of
control of the isolation device at the microgrid boundary, the potential for cloud-based
services and remote access to expose controllers to external attacks (for example.,
cloud-based optimization services), secure provision of remote vendor access,
interoperability gateways between utility-owned equipment and third-party equipment,
and device-to-device communication failures.

                                               Page 62 of 172

To address these issues, key recommendations include:
 • provide identity and access management (IAM) tools
 • establish configuration change management protocols
 • ensure communication integrity, data encryption, and authentication
 • design for network boundary protection and segmentation.
Detailed guidance on microgrid cybersecurity is provided in Cybersecurity Architecture
for Microgrid Integration [13]and Cybersecurity Considerations for Distributed Energy
Storage [14].
Island Operations
All microgrids must meet utility standards when interconnected with the utility grid and
when reconnecting with the grid after an outage. For a single-customer BTM microgrid,
these standards do not apply when isolated from the grid. However, community
microgrids are also required to follow utility standards when operating in standalone
mode. The IEEE 1547-2018 standard requires DER in a community microgrid to follow
grid-connected requirements during island operation because in an island state the
DER are still connected to the Area EPS (see Figure 7). Regardless of ownership and
ruling guidelines, the technical objective is the same: safe, controllable electricity
distribution within the microgrid, supplying load at suitable voltage and frequency. The
inherent ability of microgrids to intentionally energize portions of the grid during outages
brings safety challenges to the design, including backfeed during unintentional
islanding, sensitivity to natural disasters such as fires and flooding, and effective
grounding.

Figure 21 Relationship between Local and Area EPS within a Community Microgrid; note that the isolated island
includes the Area EPS

Community microgrid island operation design should address four key categories of
concern:
Power Quality and Control/Support of Voltage and Frequency – The microgrid
controller should maintain microgrid power quality, voltage, and frequency within

                                                Page 63 of 172

standard (ANSI, IEEE, NEC) limits under all operating states. This includes
contributions of actively and passively controlled DER and their responses to rapid
changes in load and generation balance during island operations.
Protection Equipment and Coordination – Protection equipment within the microgrid
must be able to respond effectively to fault conditions on the microgrid, isolate, and
protect equipment and people during a fault event. This includes differentiating between
intentional and unintentional island events. Effective grounding while islanded, as well
as detecting fault conditions during island operation and black start is critical to the
system protection scheme.
Dispatch – External microgrid control functionality, such as interactions between
microgrid controller and utility control infrastructure, and allowable DER and non-DER
dispatch strategy – particularly when entering and exiting island operations – should be
clearly defined.
Communication – As needed, a communication interface between microgrid
controller(s) and the utility control infrastructure (such as DERMS or SCADA) should be
clearly defined and agreed upon. Considerations for such an interface are detailed in
Microgrid Interconnection Requirements: First Edition [15].
Voltage, Frequency, and Power Quality Management
DER interconnected to community microgrids may be subjected to a wider range of
voltage and frequency during island operations due to the significantly lower inertia of
islanded microgrids. Typical DER interconnection studies focus on ensuring that the
DER prevent unacceptable disturbances to power quality, voltage, or frequency (when
grid-connected). However, the DER in a community microgrid must also tolerate the full
range of acceptable voltage and frequency anticipated during island operations to
prevent nuisance tripping and loss of load-generation balance.
The impact of community DER tripping behavior while the microgrid is islanded is
illustrated below (See Figure 8). In the diagram, Community Microgrid A is designed to
maintain frequency within IEEE 1547-2018 default ride-through operational limits during
a large load step; this allows the uncontrolled DER (such as BTM solar inverters) to
continue supporting the microgrid throughout the disturbance because they are required
to ride-through this abnormal condition. Some DER in Microgrid B start to trip when
frequency drops into the “Shall Trip” zone; this causes a cascading outage in the
microgrid, leading to a blackout.

DER Control and Power Sharing
While it is common for single-customer microgrids to rely on no more than two DER
types, community microgrids will almost certainly rely on at least two, if not three or
more DER types. Further, community microgrids often include both front-of-meter (FTM)
and behind-the-meter (BTM) DER. Due to differing device responses and controllability
of resources, the community microgrid control strategy may be complex. For example,
the BTM solar PV in a community microgrid may only have passive control through
static volt-var curves, while the FTM energy storage can be directly managed to follow a
variety of mode changes and setpoints.
To operate as an island, the microgrid must have at least one grid-forming (GFM)
resource that can provide stable voltage and frequency to the islanded system. (The
EPRI tutorial Grid-Forming Inverters [16], provides more detail.) The challenge in a
community microgrid is to ensure the DER have compatible power sharing controls and
are managed accordingly during island operations to maintain system stability.
The following discussion is drawn from Implementing Microgrid Control Strategies: First
Edition [17]; refer to the full report for additional background on microgrid control
strategies.
Microgrid Controller
To coordinate the complex power sharing and sequencing of a community microgrid,
the microgrid controller and control layers play an essential role. Microgrid control
systems can be categorized hierarchically, based on the type of control and the degree
of DER control needed. (See Figure 9.)
• Primary controls are commonly enabled locally at the DER and require fast control
 actions to regulate DER output.
• Secondary controls reside external to the DER, either co-located with the local
 DER controller or in a separate microgrid controller. In grid-connected mode, these
 enable grid supportive services from the microgrid as a single controllable entity. In

                                                  Page 65 of 172

 islanded mode, secondary controls provide frequency and voltage compensation
 due to local droop controls and output impedances.
• Tertiary controls regulate power exchange between the microgrid and the main
 grid by economically optimizing the microgrid, considering grid needs and local
 constraints using the microgrid controller.

Figure 23 Hierarchical microgrid control layers and relative control speed

Secondary and tertiary controls can co-exist in a single microgrid controller. These two
layers differ in control tightness. Secondary controls update DER setpoints much more
frequently, at the seconds scale, while tertiary control actions occur mostly at the
minutes scale. For details on microgrid control layers and related functions, see
Implementing Microgrid Control Strategies [17].
Power Sharing in Islanded Mode
The operation of a community microgrid must account for the combination of grid-
following and grid-forming DER that serve the microgrid load during island operations.
The droop curves of voltage- and frequency-droop GFM resources will also impact the
stability of the microgrid during island operations.
In an islanded community microgrid with multiple GFM DER, the system frequency and
voltage are determined by the programmed droop-slopes and the real and reactive
power setpoints of individual droop-enabled resources. With only primary controls
enabled, maintaining system voltage and frequency within the nominal operating range
is challenging during power system events.
Implementing secondary controls in microgrid controllers mitigates these issues, to
restore system voltage and frequency deviations produced when the primary droop
controls respond to an imbalance between load and generation. Secondary controls
respond slower than primary controls to changes in microgrid load or generation.
Accordingly, they are implemented externally, in a separate controller (for example, the
community microgrid controller) that communicates less frequently to the DER’s primary
controller, to compensate for the frequency and voltage deviations.
Figure 10 illustrates the sequence of controls for frequency and voltage compensation.
During a sudden load or generation change in an islanded microgrid, the grid-forming

                                                   Page 66 of 172

DER automatically increases or decreases output power to meet the new system need.
This change in system conditions can move the GFM DER to a new voltage and
frequency. The ability of the microgrid to remain within acceptable operational limits of
voltage and frequency depends on the combined GFM DER droop slopes and current
operating state. A load step in excess of the microgrid’s design tolerance can cause
system frequency and voltage to exceed nominal operating limits. In that case,
secondary control functions must be implemented in the community microgrid
controllers to apply new operational setpoints and restore frequency and voltage to
within nominal ranges.

Figure 24 Illustration of microgrid frequency restoration using secondary controls. Source: EPRI [17]

It is critical to consider these controller responses with respect to equipment protection
and disturbance ride-through settings. The secondary controller must restore the
microgrid frequency and voltage fast enough to prevent other DER from tripping; it
should be designed such that the largest anticipated load keeps the primary DER
response within Mandatory Operation limits defined by IEEE 1547-2018 (see Appendix
A) or equivalent.
Microgrid Protection
The design and appropriate coordination of protection equipment is essential to the
successful deployment of a community microgrid.
The following resources offer detailed guidance and additional considerations for
microgrid protection design: Microgrid Protection [18], “Microgrid Protection Standards”
[19], Applications of Adaptive Protection on the Current and Future System [20],
Microgrid Protection Systems [21], and Effective Grounding for Inverter-Connected DER
[22].
System Faults
When a microgrid operates in an isolated island mode configuration, protection-related
system characteristics—such as short-circuit current, neutral grounding, and

                                                  Page 67 of 172

bidirectional power flows—can change, impacting the ability of the protection equipment
to isolate faults.
When designing and reviewing a community microgrid’s island protection schemes, the
following should be taken into consideration:
 • Sensitivity of overcurrent relays can be a major challenge, due to limited fault
 current contributions from DER.
 • Adjusting overcurrent protection for anticipated directional sensitivity may
 improve selectivity during island operations, due to bidirectional power flows.
 • Voltage-restrained overcurrent protection can improve sensitivity to low-current
        faults [21].
 • Voltage protection may be used to respond to events; however, this can cause
 unreliable protection coordination for community microgrids with large amounts of
 dispersed DER, due to a lack of selectivity [18].
 • Active management of smart switches and breakers with multiple relay settings
 groups may be needed to adequately protect the islanded microgrid from faults
 while maintaining coordination upstream of the microgrid when it reconnects to
 the grid.
Effective Grounding
Effective grounding of DER is required in a microgrid, particularly during island
operations. The grounding source to a distribution feeder is typically located at the
substation—outside of the microgrid boundary. When a community microgrid is
islanded, it must have a grounding source within its boundary to limit ground fault
overvoltage (GFO), stabilize the line to neutral voltage, and serve unbalanced loads [6].
A low-impedance zero-sequence path can be switched-in during island operations or
permanently connected. A permanently-connected grounding transformer within the
microgrid boundary must be carefully designed to prevent desensitization of protection
upstream of the microgrid. To avoid conflicting with grid-connected ground pathways,
the microgrid grounding source may be designed to exclusively switch-in during island
operations. Supplemental grounding solely designed to limit GFO for grid-connected
inverter-based DER may not provide the neutral ground needed to serve unbalanced
loads during island operations. The Authority Having Jurisdiction (e.g., distribution
utility) is responsible for setting the grounding requirements for the microgrid. For
detailed background and guidance on effective DER grounding practices, refer to Power
System Grounding with Microgrids [23], and Effective Grounding for Inverter-Connected
DER [22].
Managing unintentional islanding settings
Islanding occurs when DER continue to energize a microgrid’s load after disconnection
from the utility source. Intentional islanding is planned in advance and carefully
engineered for reliability and power-quality purposes. Unintentional islanding occurs by
accident. Unscheduled islanding occurs when an external fault or abnormal grid
condition triggers the microgrid to form an intentional island. (See Figure 11 and
Appendix A for additional information and definitions.)
With traditional DER, unintentional islanding with the utility system is something to be
avoided. In a microgrid, the DER can transition to an established island state through

                                      Page 68 of 172

controlled, intentional islanding. For community microgrids, DER sensitivity to island
conditions can complicate the microgrid’s reliability. The DER are intended to supply
power to the isolated community microgrid during an external grid loss. It is possible
that the anticipated voltage, frequency, and power quality conditions of the intentional
island may trigger individual inverter onboard islanding detection. In that event, the
community microgrid may be unable to leverage uncontrolled BTM DER and may
collapse. However, the DER islanding detection should remain active and cease to
energize in the event of a fault within the microgrid. (For more on detection methods,
see Taxonomy for Inverter-Island Detection Methods [24].)

Figure 25 Illustration of intentional and unintentional islanding

Dispatch of Distribution Automation Equipment
When a community microgrid is operating as an island, it may not be able to rely on a
stable communication link with the DSO. The autonomy of a community microgrid
controller should be clearly defined during its design stage to capture necessary
communication pathways and interfaces to non-DER. To enable feeder-level autonomy,
the locally deployed microgrid controller must have visibility to the status of distribution
control equipment like voltage regulators, capacitors, and sensors.
Transition Methodology
Although the time spent transitioning to and from island operations only accounts for a
small portion of a microgrid’s lifetime operation, the design and testing of this transition
can comprise a large majority of the microgrid implementation and interconnection study
process. This holds even more weight for the island transition design of community
microgrids.
As with all microgrids, the community microgrid designer must determine whether the
microgrid will use closed (make-before-break) or open (break-before-make) transitions
between island and grid-connected operations. (See Appendix A for definitions of these
terms.) It is possible (and common) for the transition to island to be different from the
transition to grid-connected state. For the microgrid owner, there will be a design trade-
off between cost and load sensitivity when selecting the transition type. For the
interconnected utility, the transition method may impact power quality or protection
equipment coordination in addition to customer reliability.
Entering Island
In order for a community microgrid to enter island without temporarily disrupting local
loads, a closed transition design is required.
Closed Transition to Island: In a closed transition to islanded operation, the microgrid
loads receive continuous supply of power, and local DER remain online supplying the
local load while the interconnection isolation equipment opens. This is often referred to
                                                     Page 69 of 172

as a “make-before-break” or “seamless” transition type. Such transfers require special
control and equipment to enable a successful uninterrupted or very fast transfer. Two
methods are used: contact overlap and electronic (static) transfer between the two
sources.
An example closed transition to island process is illustrated in Figure 12.
 1. In step 1, the microgrid remains connected to the main grid, likely with power
 exchange.
 2. The microgrid controller responds to either a command or the specified abnormal
 grid conditions, per IEEE standards [25] and adjusts local DER output to match
 island demand as close to zero import/export as possible.
 3. In step 3, the microgrid disconnects the POI from the main grid to form the island.

Figure 26 Closed transition to island

 However, the community microgrid controller, DER and protection relays within the
microgrid boundary will all impact the feasibility of such closed transitions. This is
because the microgrid design must consider both Scheduled and Unscheduled island
transitions. (See Appendix A for definitions of these terms.)
To execute a Scheduled Island transition, the microgrid controller dispatches DER
within the microgrid boundary to match local demand and bring import and export near
zero, then open the POI. In a Scheduled Island scenario, the controller has ample time
to coordinate a local load-generation balance and modify DER operational mode
settings before opening the POI. For Scheduled Island transitions, the designer should
verify that the control deadband managing the “zero” import/export is smaller than the
designed reserve margin of anticipated load step size during island. (See Figure 13. ) A
larger step change may cause tripping of protection equipment and DER during the
transition due to voltage or frequency swings. A mid-feeder microgrid executing a
closed transition must isolate itself from the downstream loads before adjusting
microgrid DER output to zero import/export.

during an island transition

For unscheduled islanding, closed island transitions become much more complex to
implement on a community microgrid scale. Unscheduled island transitions occur in
response to abnormal grid conditions; however, the community microgrid closed
transition operates in a tight window between ride-through and unintentional islanding
prevention requirements. (See Figure 14.)

Black Start
While some microgrids may be designed to be capable of transitioning to island
operations without loss of local power, all microgrids must be capable of black starting
an island in the event of an unexpected grid outage. The ability to sequence and
manage a black start island transition remains equally important, although more

                                                  Page 71 of 172

complex, for community microgrids. If the microgrid cannot black start, it will not be able
to provide critical services after a fault or failure to execute a closed transition during a
wider-utility outage. The DER must have sufficient current rated capacity to provide
inrush current to energize transformers, line loading, induction motor starting currents,
residential HVAC-type loads, and other system dynamics without tripping relay
equipment [18] [26].
Open Transition to Island: In an open transition to islanded operation, the microgrid
isolation equipment must be opened before the local DER are connected and
configured to serve load in island operation. This is often referred to as a “break-before-
make” transition type. An example open transition to island process is illustrated in
Figure 15.
 1. In normal operation, the microgrid is connected to the main grid, exporting or
 importing power
 2. After loss of the main grid, the POI breaker trips and deenergizes the microgrid.
 3. The microgrid black-starts and energizes the islanded, local grid.
Normally in this process, the loads experience a momentary outage before operation of
the microgrid is restored in its islanded configuration.

Reconnecting to the Grid
As for procedures transitioning a community microgrid into island operations, the
reconnection process presents design trade-offs and potential interconnection
concerns.
Closed Transition to Grid-connect: In a closed transition to grid-connected operation,
the islanded microgrid remains energized while the microgrid circuit is synchronized
with the grid-side circuit. The isolation device at the POI closes when voltage,
frequency, and phase angle differences are within acceptable tolerances. As Figure 16
illustrates, a closed transition to grid-connected operation proceeds as follows:
 1. The microgrid is operating as an island.
 2. When the main grid supply returns, the microgrid controller initiates an ‘enter
 service delay’ procedure. A ‘synchro-check’ is performed to ensure the
 differences in voltage, frequency, and phase angle between the grid and the
 microgrid are within acceptable limits

                                        Page 72 of 172

 3. The POI breaker closes to re-connect the microgrid with the main grid.
This type of uninterrupted power delivery maintains critical, sensitive loads, but can
cause backfeed to the distribution system if improperly designed or executed.

Figure 30 Closed transition to grid-connected operation

Two methods are applied for synchronizing the microgrid to the grid during reconnection
[27] [28]:
Active synchronization: If the microgrid voltage and frequency can be controlled
sufficiently, then the microgrid controller aligns the voltage and frequency to the utility
power system and then recloses.
Passive synchronization—Reconnection can be blocked by a sync-check relay. The
microgrid controller initiates reclose, and the system reconnects only when the two
systems are within synchronization tolerances. If the systems are badly out of sync,
reconnection may not be possible.

Open Transition to grid-connected operations: An open transition to grid-connected
operations requires de-energizing the microgrid before the isolation breaker is closed, to
avoid disturbances to the utility grid. Once reconnected, the DER can be restarted if
desired. Figure 17 illustrates an open-transition to grid connected operation.
 1. The microgrid is operating as an island.
 2. When the main grid supply returns, the microgrid goes offline.
 3. The POI breaker closes to energize the loads in the microgrid. After the ‘enter
 service delay’ the DER begin to come online.

                                                  Page 73 of 172

Figure 31 Open transition to grid-connected operation

An open transition is the easiest and least expensive option for grid reconnection, but
the impacts to critical loads within the microgrid should be considered.
Because community microgrids include portions of the utility distribution feeder, the
location of the microgrid with respect to the external distribution network becomes
particularly important. (See Figures 3 and 4 for illustrations of end-of-line and mid-
feeder microgrids.) Mid-feeder locations may require special treatment
For example, the distribution utility may require mid-feeder microgrids to follow specific
sequencing when closing isolation breakers during reconnection. The recommended
practice is to reconnect a mid-feeder microgrid by closing the upstream interconnection
breaker before closing the downstream interconnection breaker [29]. This sequence
prevents the community microgrid from inadvertently energizing downstream portions of
the feeder, loads that the microgrid is not designed to supply when islanded.
For open (break-before-make) reconnections, the transfer sequence is relatively fast (on
the order of seconds); for closed (“seamless”) reconnections, the transfer sequence
may be slower depending on the synchronizing controller and GFM DER. Although the
closed reconnection design prevents the community microgrid participant from
experiencing a power disruption, the customers downstream of a mid-feeder community
microgrid may experience unnecessary reconnection delays and extended outage
duration while the community microgrid synchronizes to the upstream distribution grid.
PG&E has developed a best-practices guide for community microgrids, recognizing
issues raised by particular configurations and infrastructure constraints [1] It
recommends real-time controller-hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) testing of any closed
transition design to ensure devices are correctly coordinated in the process [1]. CHIL
testing can also be used to improve the microgrid controller sequencing algorithms by
analyzing the transition process and timing.
For more on transition stages between grid-connected and islanded operations, see
Microgrid Interconnection Requirements: First Edition [15].

Interconnection Considerations
As a group of DER that connect to the grid, a microgrid is beholden to the same
interconnection requirements as individual DER. However, the variability of site
configurations and combination of different DER types make microgrid interconnections
challenging.
Interconnection requirements and related analysis normally covers several topics,
including impacts on the following:
 • Utility voltage and voltage regulating equipment
 • Overcurrent protection
 • Effective grounding
 • Islanding prevention
 • Harmonics
 • Voltage flicker
 • Load rejection overvoltage
 • Installation above flood levels or use of submersible designs
 • Telemetry and metering equipment
Many of these integration issues will also apply when the microgrid is operating in
standalone (islanded) mode, but the circuitry, control modes, and power flows may be
quite different than when grid connected. When a microgrid is in standalone mode,
several additional issues arise that must be studied:
 • Voltage control
 • Frequency control
 • Matching generation with load
 • Synchronization
 • Black start capability
 • Protection settings
 • Unbalance
Beyond the straightforward compliance of individual DER, however, the interconnection
analysis of a community microgrid must consider the coordinated behavior of the DER,
microgrid controller(s), and protective relays in the microgrid area. The behavior and
interactions between these devices must be considered during island operations and
the transitions to and from grid-connected state.

Business and Economic Frameworks
An economic framework and business model is necessary to analyze the costs or
operational constraints of a community microgrid. The economic framework establishes
a perspective for the analysis and establishes which items will be included (or
excluded). Such items may be specific impacts or physical changes caused, directly or
indirectly, by the project. Impact may include easily-identified macro effects known to
regulators or policymakers, but engineers and technical analysts may need to translate
these into specific physical changes or economic results. Further, a framework may
outline a sequence of steps or modules intended to answer certain intermediate
questions.

The Economic Question and the Problem of Perspective to Develop Community
Microgrid Solution Sets
The economic structure of a community microgrid is driven by a variety of factors. Key
questions establish core characteristics:
 • Who owns and controls the microgrid and its component resources?
 • Does it interface with a wholesale market or is it tied to a vertically integrated
 utility?
 • Is it connected to a distribution-only utility?
 • Is the entity a full- or partial-requirements customer of another utility?
Utility rate issues rest at the forefront of microgrid business models. For microgrids that
serve multiple customers, cost allocation becomes murky, especially for community
resiliency applications designed for societal benefit beyond the encompassed
customers. Important considerations include [4] [9]:
 • How should these costs be allocated to a utility’s rate base?
 • Should customers outside this installation pay to reinforce hardware that does
 not ultimately connect to their meters?
 • What if no widespread outage occurs and the microgrid never islands?

                                       Page 80 of 172

 • Do customers have the option to opt out of a microgrid project that may increase
 their utility bills?
 • Is there any protection for “customers” of microgrids to ensure they are not
 paying excessive costs for the microgrid services?
 • Who is liable for any damage caused by the electric distribution system when a
 third party is operating the microgrid?
Microgrid Business Models
Three microgrid business models are commonly supported today; Figure 19 describes
their = main features and relative degree of utility control [4]. The Third-Party Model
places control of the microgrid in the hands of end users and/or their solution providers.
In certain cases, the third-party model is offered in the form of “microgrid-as-a-service”
products. The Integrated Utility Model places ownership and control of all of the
microgrid infrastructure as well as generation and storage assets with the utility. The
Unbundled Model encompasses the most wide-ranging set of possible ownership and
operating arrangements for a community microgrid; one entity—the utility, end user, or
third party—owns or finances the microgrid, while another entity operates the microgrid.

SECURE Communities
EPRI aims to address several of the challenges associated with community microgrids
through the recently launched project Solar Energy CommUnity REsiliency (SECURE),
funded by the U.S. Department of Energy Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO)
[33]. SECURE aims to address the challenges of community microgrids by advancing
key control technology and bringing industry stakeholders together to form
requirements.
The project takes a bottom-up approach to energizing the grid during wide-area power
outages. Communities in feeder sections of the broader grid will be able to energize
autonomously and connect with peer communities to maximize community benefit
during normal and emergency conditions. The project will develop a novel
communication architecture that re-establishes connectivity between local controllers,
DER, and microgrids when nearby cellular communication towers are unavailable. In
addition, the project will facilitate a community microgrid stakeholder working group to
address gaps in present business and regulatory frameworks that lack basic
mechanisms to support the community microgrid vision.
Conclusions and Key Take-Aways
Utility- and community-owned microgrids provide opportunities to enhance distribution
grid resilience and reliability. The inherent design characteristics of these microgrid
types—leveraging front-of-meter infrastructure and existing DER—lend themselves to
an efficient and flexible design that serves an essential community need. Community
microgrids, which include multiple interconnected customers and FTM distribution
feeder equipment, may be owned and operated by the utility, a third-party, or a hybrid of
the two.
This paper is intended to provide a technical primer for understanding community
microgrids, expanding upon Grid Considerations for Microgrids [6] by delineating island-
related microgrid considerations. To provide adequate safety and equipment protection
in the community microgrid, it is critical to consider island system characteristics and
design or upgrade the microgrid protection scheme accordingly.
The safe and reliable design and operation of a community microgrid is inherently more
complex than that of a single-customer microgrid. This arises from the combination of
directly and indirectly controlled DER, protection requirements, and power quality
constraints during islanding. Further complications emerge from island and
reconnection transition management, as well as the need for secure communication
pathways between utility-owned and third-party owned equipment. However, managing
these issues yields broad customer reliability benefits through leveraging existing BTM
DER to support the microgrid.
This discussion has focused on requirements for the operation of a single community
microgrid. Developing requirements for distributed peer-to-peer community microgrid
controller interactions will require further research. Additional technology gaps and
challenges are also identified here, some of which will be addressed in the upcoming
SECURE project. Understanding these challenges can help developers and those
interested in the progress of community microgrids prepare for the practicalities of their
implementation.

Appendix C – Cost-Effectiveness Metrics for Community Microgrids
Value of Resilience
The primary driver for microgrids is to improve local resilience and reliability issues.
Thus, financial justification for a community microgrid typically relies on the value of the
resilience and reliability the microgrid provides (i.e., the value of supplying power to end
uses when grid electricity is not available). So, metrics that measure the cost-
effectiveness of the microgrid should include this value in addition to the financial value
the microgrid can generate and the costs it incurs. In some cases, the value of
resilience may be included implicitly - for example, when a utility-driven community
microgrid is the lowest-cost solution to a reliability problem that needs to be solved. Or
the value of resilience can be calculated explicitly and included in the benefits
considered by a cost-effectiveness metric.
One approach to the valuation of resilience is to assume that the value of resilience is
equal to the negative consequences which are avoided by the provision of resilience. In
areas where shorter-term interruptions (less than 24 hours) are the principal concern, it
is feasible to use the Interruption Cost Estimate (ICE) Calculator ( ) to determine the
costs incurred due to an interruption. However, the ICE calculator is better suited to
situations where reliability, rather than resilience, is the goal, as generally resilience
events are considered to be those lasting more than 24 hours. The method for
calculating the value of resilience explicitly may vary depending on customer segments
represented in the microgrid, data availability, or critical services backed up by the
microgrid, etc. For some critical services, such as hospitals, FEMA has developed cost-
effectiveness thresholds, based on dollars per square foot of hospital space, to value
on-site generator projects which can be used as a proxy for the value of resilience
associated with hospitals (Pre-Calculated Benefits for Certain Hospital Generators to
Demonstrate Cost-Effectiveness (fema.gov) ).
It may be appropriate to use Value of Lost Load (VoLL) figures to determine the value of
reliability or resilience for some service interruptions. VoLL assigns a cost to a service
interruption (differentiated by customer category such as residential, commercial, etc.)
based on the number of MWh not delivered as a result of the interruption. However, just
as the ICE calculator is only intended for shorter-duration interruptions, VoLL is an
imperfect method for determining the value of resilience because it measures each lost
MWh equally and the impacts of an interruption tend to increase geometrically rather
than linearly as the duration of that interruption increases. For the use of either the ICE
calculator or an approach based on VoLL, it will be necessary to estimate of the
average aggregate annual avoided interruption time so that a reasonable figure for the
value of resilience (or reliability) can be calculated and incorporated into cost-benefit
analysis of a prospective microgrid. To determine the complete value of resilience (or
reliability) associated with a microgrid, it is necessary to consider the present value of
lost load over the expected lifetime of the microgrid components.
Macroeconomic approaches to calculating the value of resilience may be more
appropriate to longer-duration interruptions as they can, to some extent, account for the
compounding impacts of prolonged interruptions, particularly if they account for supply
chain impacts. (Note, however, that such approaches may be of limited use for a
microgrid encompassing an area that is primarily residential and contains little or no

                                       Page 83 of 172

economic activity.) One possible macroeconomic approach would be to use the electric
energy intensity of the economy – that is, the input of kWh per dollar of economic output
– and assume that for each lost kWh, a certain percentage (less than 100%) of the
associated output will be foregone. To an extent, such an approach does capture the
cascading effect on supply chains of prolonged interruptions. The challenge is in
identifying the appropriate percentage (we can call it an “adaptation factor”) by which to
reduce economic output for each lost kWh. In addition, this approach only provides a
baseline value of resilience, since it does not account for losses beyond foregone output
(e.g., food spoilage, compromised emergency services).
For areas with limited economic activity, or of smaller scope, it may not be appropriate
to use macroeconomic approaches to value resilience – in such situations, a more
appropriate approach might be to evaluate the willingness of residents to pay (“WTP”) to
avoid an interruption. For example, surveys on what they would consider paying for
household battery backup can be conducted. One advantage of such an approach is
that it does not require an estimate of the amount of time service is interrupted over the
life of the microgrid components; however, acquiring statistically sound data about WTP
can be costly and challenging.
The value of resilience calculation can also take different forms, depending on which
dimensions of resilience value are being considered. For example, the value of
resilience can extend to those not in the microgrid boundary if the microgrid enables
critical services for microgrid non-participants during grid outages. These benefits would
not always be included when evaluating the cost-effectiveness of the community
microgrid to microgrid participants. When assessing the value of resilience provided by
a microgrid, however, it should be recognized that a microgrid and its various
components can provide benefits (e.g., peak shaving) that are unrelated to resilience
and therefore less than 100% of a microgrid’s costs may be attributable to resilience –
this should be considered in any cost-benefit analysis that encompasses the value of
resilience.
The value of resilience can also be derived from the cost of preventive behavior – i.e.,
the cost that customers would incur if they were to take individual action to prevent or
mitigate the impact of a service interruption. EPRI has begun to develop an extensible
approach to estimating the value of resilience at the community scale (which is also
often the same scale as a microgrid) based on the cost of preventive behavior. This
approach, known as the “Modular Template Framework” (
https://www.epri.com/research/products/000000003002027408 ) presents a “benefit”
value based on the cost of hypothetical preventive behavior (i.e., customer willingness-
to-pay) that can be plugged into cost-benefit analyses of resilience investments in DER
and/or microgrids. It is designed to develop benefit estimates for a specific type, or
category, of built environment that can be applied – with appropriate normalization – to
similar built environments located elsewhere. EPRI is continuing to refine this
approach; in particular, since preventive measures such as microgrids often provide
sources of value in addition to resilience (e.g., load management), EPRI plans to
develop an approach to isolate the value of a preventive measure that is directly
attributable to resilience from the other sources of value provided by a microgrid or
DER.

                                      Page 84 of 172

DER Value
All microgrids include distributed energy resources (DER) which can have financial
value themselves. The microgrid’s DER can be monetized through energy sales (or
offset energy purchases), ancillary services provisioning, demand response or other
program participation. The value of these services can help offset some of the costs of
the microgrid as a whole and may be considered in cost-effectiveness metrics. In some
cases, the DER will already exist, at which point only the costs and benefits incremental
to the microgrid development should be included.
Incrementality
In all cases, the metrics used to estimate the cost-effectiveness of community
microgrids should only consider the incremental benefits brought by the microgrid. For
example, benefits beyond the “business as usual” scenario if the microgrid was not built.
In some cases, a new microgrid may be able to leverage DER that already exist in the
microgrid boundary. In this case, neither the costs nor the baseline benefits from this
DER should be included in the cost-effectiveness calculation, though integrating this
DER into the microgrid may incur costs and enable benefits that should be counted.
When calculating these benefits care should be taken to avoid “double counting” value
streams provided by the microgrid. For example, a storage system participating in an
aggregation program that provisions resource adequacy capacity should not also be
allocated a separate benefit for reducing the peak net load in the region based on the
value of reducing resource adequacy requirements.
Analysis Horizon
Community microgrids are complex constructions with equipment types that span a
large range of potential lifetimes. Similarly, the lifetime of microgrid equipment might be
very different from the lifetime of an alternative. In cases where a cost-effectiveness
metric supports decision making between options that differ greatly in their lifetime
characteristics, there may be a need to annualize the metric or extend the analysis
horizon to cover the full lifetime of the longest-lived equipment, making assumptions
about replacement costs for shorter-lived equipment when needed.
Utility- vs Community-Driven Community Microgrids
This report distinguishes between community microgrids that are community- versus
utility-driven. The key difference is which party is responsible for covering and
potentially redistributing the costs of the community microgrid. All community microgrids
are justified in part by the resilience benefit of the microgrid. But whether that resilience
is needed to provide an equitable level of utility service to the customers in the microgrid
or represents a customer need above and beyond the standard quality of service
determines in part whether the microgrid should be driven by the utility or by the
community. In the case of a utility-driven community microgrid, the utility is responsible
for all aspects of implementation, operation, and maintenance of utility-owned
equipment associated with that community microgrid. The utility opts to implement the
microgrid as the least-cost option to meet quality of service requirements in their
territory, and typically recovers the costs of the microgrid through rate recovery.
Community-driven community microgrids recover their costs primarily from the
customers in the microgrid whose needs exceed typical utility quality of service.

                                       Page 85 of 172

Stakeholders
Definitionally, community microgrids bring together several parties into one microgrid
arrangement. Some metrics are sufficient to evaluate cost-effectiveness for one
stakeholder but may neglect decision making criteria relevant to another stakeholder.
For utility-driven community microgrids, this may be less relevant because stakeholders
other than the utility and its ratepayers should not be financially impacted. But
community-driven community microgrids face a challenge to equitably distribute costs
and benefits to all parties, including the utility and its ratepayers, each individual
customer in the microgrid, and potentially a 3rd party microgrid aggregator. Applying a
metric individually to each stakeholder may be required to evaluate overall cost-
effectiveness.

Ratepayer Impact Measure (RIM)
A net present value ratepayer impact measure, based on the California Public Utilities
Commission’s Standard Practice Manual 3 is proposed to evaluate the cost-
 2F

effectiveness of utility-driven community microgrids. This metric considers the present
value of costs associated with developing the microgrid and compares them to the
present value of avoided costs, which are calculated as the costs of the alternative
solution. If the local quality of service issue could be remedied by new transmission, for
example, then the present value of upfront and ongoing costs over the analysis horizon
associated with developing the new transmission will count as benefits for the microgrid
in this RIM metric.
In this metric, there is no need to estimate a value of resilience because any resilience
benefits above minimum quality of service requirements may not be considered
explicitly in the decision despite their value. Resilience benefits could, however, still be
considered from a qualitative perspective.
This metric relies on a well-chosen analysis horizon that can appropriately capture
ongoing and replacement costs for both long- and short-lived equipment and assumes
that no equipment has residual value at the end of the analysis horizon.
When calculating this metric, it is assumed that the value of energy sales are not
impacted by the presence of the microgrid.

The tested microgrid includes five GFL inverters modeled as controllable current
sources. These inverters measure the local voltage (𝑉𝑉𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚 ) and calculate the frequency

angle and d-axis voltage (𝑣𝑣𝑑𝑑 ). The microgrid DMS dispatches active (𝑃𝑃∗ ) and reactive

Modeling of Gas Generator and Loads
The gas generator is modeled using a governor model and a standard IEEE AC1A type
excitation system model. The microgrid DMS sends active and reactive power
commands to the generator model, and discrepancies between the commanded and
actual power are multiplied by the droop gain to adjust the generator’s frequency and
voltage. The generator’s frequency and voltage set points are maintained at 60 Hz and
1 p.u., respectively. The governor model adjusts the mechanical power input based on
the speed error to maintain the correct frequency, while the excitation system model
controls the field voltage to maintain the terminal voltage.
The microgrid load module’s profile is measured at the feeder’s head without individual
load measurements. The planning value for each load is used, and its ratio to the total
load at the feeder head estimates the load value for each module. The power factor is
constant at 0.9, and both phase-to-ground and per-unit phase-to-ground voltages
characterize the system’s voltage levels. Different load types are modeled using
parameter 𝑁𝑁, with 𝑁𝑁 = 0 for constant power, 𝑁𝑁 = 1 for constant current, and 𝑁𝑁 = 2
for constant impedance, ensuring accurate load value calculations based on overall
load measurements while considering specific load characteristics.

Summary
In evaluating the black start procedure for both summer and winter scenarios, several
key performance metrics have been established to assess system functionality. The first

                                                                                  Page 103 of 172

metric, "Permissions to Island," requires that the MGMS should not initiate a black start
without obtaining the necessary permission for islanding. MGMS ensures that all
essential devices are available and properly connected before proceeding, and it will not
attempt a black start if any critical checks fail. For instance, in Test Case #1, MGMS
verifies the communication status of all DERs before starting the black start process.
The second evaluation metric, "Island Settings Application," involves MGMS updating
the settings of all DER units to comply with the island configuration, according to the
system design. During the black start process, MGMS monitors the grid status (such as
the occurrence of islanding) and communicates this information to all GFM DER units.
Once the first GFM unit, such as BESS3, is activated, MGMS updates the grid status to
"main grid available" so that other GFM units can synchronize their frequency with
BESS3.
The third metric, "Isolation Check," requires MGMS to confirm the status of all isolation
switches and breakers, ensuring they are opened before the black start procedure
begins. In Test Case #1, this includes verifying communication links across all
equipment, particularly ensuring the status of breakers at the feeder head.
The fourth metric, "DER Mode Control," mirrors the second in that MGMS must properly
dispatch the operational modes and settings for DERs during both the black start and
islanding processes. MGMS communicates grid status to GFM units and adjusts their
operating modes accordingly.
The final metric, "Cold Load Pickup Order," ensures that MGMS adheres to the
designated cold load pickup sequence and observes the configured timing intervals
between load stages. In Test Case #1, MGMS follows the proper sequence for closing
circuit breakers and energizing loads systematically.
In this test case, GFM units are managed by the MGMS controller. These units can be
activated and brought online via control commands issued by the MGMS. Once online,
they generate active power to meet load demands and provide reactive power to
support voltage regulation. In this test case, the MGMS is configured to issue only
START commands to the GFM units. Although the GFM PV unit and the GFM BESS
units share identical droop control settings, the generation capacity of the GFM PV unit
is constrained by the available solar irradiance in the scenario. This limitation reflects
the PV unit’s dependence on the variable nature of solar resources, in contrast to the
BESS units, which are not subject to such constraints.

In this case, MGMS controller provides coordination of power generation and
consumption across the two microgrid systems during island operation (starts from the
black start scenario) and dispatch GFM and GFL units. The system configuration is
shown in Figure D-32. Different from the Baseline Case, the diesel generator is
disconnected in Test Case #1 and the system only has BESS and PV units. The control
target is to maximize the supply time of critical loads.
Summer Case
Figure D-36 presents the irradiance profile and load profiles utilized in this test case,
providing essential context for the simulation. Table IX-18 summarizes the initial

                                       Page 104 of 172

condition the summer case. Figure D-37 and Figure D-38showcase the overall
simulation results, offering a comprehensive view of the system's performance under
varying conditions.
In Figure D-37, it is observed that PV units PV 1 and PV 2 are intentionally shut down
by MGMS controller between 11 AM and 2 PM. During this period, the SOC of BESS1
and BESS3 reaches the upper threshold of 95%. Consequently, both BESS units enter
standby mode, receiving zero power dispatch commands. BESS3 also receives a
frequency command of 60Hz for standby mode. When the SOC of all BESS units fall
below 95%, all GFL PV units resume operation.
Figure D-38 provides a zoomed-in plot of the final two hours of the simulation. The
system operates normally until the SOC of BESS1 reaches 20% at approximately 17:42
(marking the timestamp of T1, as shown in bottom plot of Figure D-38). At this point,
BESS1 transitions to standby mode with zero power setpoints. Shortly after, at around
17:48, the SOC of BESS2 also falls below 20% (marking the timestamp of T2),
prompting it to enter standby mode with a frequency command of 60Hz. Despite its
standby mode, BESS2 continues to supply power due to its GFM characteristics until its
SOC drops below 19% at around 17:54 (marking the timestamp of T3). At this point, the
MGMS disconnects BESS2 from the system. Following the disconnection of BESS2, the

Test Case #3 – Island Secondary Frequency and Voltage Control:

to maintain island operation. It ensures the regulation of system frequency close to its
nominal value and maintains POI voltage within normal ranges by dispatching GFM
inverters. The specific system configuration is depicted in Figure D-32.
To establish the islanded test system, the feeder head breakers T1 and T2 are kept
open, while the tie-breaker T3 is closed. This configuration isolates the test system from
the main grid, forming an islanded environment. In this setup, only GFM and GFL units
are taken into consideration.

conditions, are shown in Figure D-49-Figure D-55. These results depict the system's

Test Case #4– Island Constraint Management:
In this scenario, an overvoltage condition is simulated, which arises due to a
combination of high PV generation and low load demand. During island operations,
MGMS will coordinate DERs and capacitor banks to mitigate the voltage constraints on
the feeder. Specifically, the MGMS will adjust the output of PV units, manage the
charging cycles of battery energy storage systems, and control the switching of
capacitor banks to regulate the voltage levels. This coordinated approach ensures that
the microgrid remains stable and operates efficiently despite the imbalanced generation
and load conditions. The test is initiated with MGMS disabled, causing the unacceptable
frequency and voltage conditions at the feeder head. MGMS is then activated 7.26
minutes after the island simulation begins.
Figure D-56 to Figure D-62 present the results of these operations. These figures
illustrate the MGMS's ability to successfully manage system voltage and frequency
within desirable limits. The data depicted in these figures highlight how the MGMS
dynamically adjusts setpoints of voltage and frequency in real-time to counteract the
overvoltage, ensuring that the microgrid maintains optimal performance and reliability
during islanded operations.
The data highlights the MGMS's ability to enhance the microgrid's resilience and
flexibility, allowing it to handle variations in generation and load without compromising
stability. Despite the effectiveness of the MGMS, the figures also indicate that
fluctuations in generation and load were minimal, resulting in relatively stable control
commands for voltage and frequency after the initial adjustments. This demonstrates
MGMS’s proficiency in quickly stabilizing the system and maintaining consistent
operational conditions.

criteria are specific to the MGMS's functional requirements; a broader set of
performance metrics should be applied to the overall microgrid reconnection process.
One key evaluation metric is the “Permission to Reconnect.” The MGMS should not
initiate the reconnection procedure unless the "Permission to Reconnect" signal is
enabled. For both the summer and winter cases, once the MGMS detects that the grid-

capable of updating the DER settings to accommodate the grid-connected
configuration. In Test Case #6, the MGMS monitors grid conditions and communicates
this information to the DER units. The DERs then use this data to adjust their operating
modes accordingly for grid-connected operation.
The third metric is "DER Mode Control," requiring the MGMS to accurately dispatch the
DER operating modes and settings needed for synchronization and reconnection to
grid-connected operation. As illustrated in Figure D-80-Figure D-82 and Figure D-86-
Figure D-88, unlike standard secondary controls, the MGMS can issue more precise
frequency and voltage set points for synchronization.
The final evaluation metric is "Synchronization Check." The MGMS ensures that a close
signal is not sent to the grid isolation breaker until the microgrid's frequency and voltage
deviations from the grid-side measurements fall within allowable tolerances. In Test
Case #5, the thresholds for successful reconnection are set at a voltage magnitude
difference of 30V, a frequency deviation of 0.05Hz, and a phase angle difference of 0.05
radians.

Appendix E – Grid Forming PV with Active Power Reserves
Active Power Reserves Design
Steady State Requirements
To maintain appropriate steady state frequency in the microgrid, the P-f droop settings
of the GFM PV plant and the BESS needs to be adjustable by the DAC. Based on the
current conditions of the DER (i.e., BESS SoC and PV irradiance), a few methods to
determine the droop curves of PV and BESS are proposed and described next. Note
that for all the scenarios, it is assumed that the forecasts on PV available power and
load are available to the DAC when selecting and applying the settings during island
operations. Moreover, the settings are general in the sense that it applies to all the
scenarios shown in Figure III-6, regardless of whether the PV available power is higher
or lower than the load.
Scenario 1: SoC of the BESS is low, minimize battery discharging and maximize battery
charging

Figure IX-96. P-f droop settings for PV and BESS plants when SoC of the BESS is low.

When the SoC of the BESS is low, the P-f droop curve settings in Figure IX-90 are
enabled in the PV and BESS plants to maintain steady state frequency within an
acceptable range whenever possible. In this scenario, the DAC will operate to minimize
battery discharging and maximize charging by enabling such settings in the GFM PV
and BESS. Therefore, if the available PV (Ppv_mpp) is less than the load, PV operates
at MPPT reducing the discharge rate of BESS. If the excess load is higher than the
BESS’ maximum discharge rate, it indicates that the total available generation in the
microgrid is less than the load. This will drive the BESS frequency to drop along the
vertical line section (Pb_maxd) and eventually trigger the underfrequency loading
shedding. Note that the BESS discharge rate is restricted in this scenario because the
SoC is low.
When the available PV is greater than the load, PV continues to operate at MPPT.
Suppose if the excess PV power (PV output minus load) exceeds the BESS’ maximum
charge rate (Pb_maxc), the battery no longer follows the slope section of the droop
curve in Figure IX-90. It starts tracking the vertical line section (-Pb_max) in the BESS
droop curve, that increases the frequency output. This rise in output frequency further
reduces/curtails the PV output power along its droop curve.

                                     Page 145 of 172

Scenario 2: SoC of the BESS is high, minimize battery discharging and limit battery
charging

Figure IX-97. P-f droop settings for PV and BESS plants when SoC of the BESS is high.

When the SoC of the battery is high, the DAC chooses the droop settings in Figure
IX-91 and updates the PV and BESS. As a result, the battery limits its charge rate to be
significantly less than the maximum charge rate. These settings minimize battery
discharging when there is excess load and charges the battery (at a limited rate) when
there is excess PV generation. With the settings in Figure IX-91, if the available PV is
less than the load, PV works at MPPT and therefore the BESS discharge rate is
minimized. When the available PV is greater than the load, PV keeps working at MPPT
and the excess PV power is used to charge the battery. If the excess PV power
exceeds the BESS’ charge rate limit as indicated by the vertical line section in BESS’
droop curve (Pref_BESS), the battery no longer follows the slope section of the droop
curve in Figure IX-91. It starts tracking the vertical line section (Pref_BESS) in the droop
curve that increases the frequency, and the PV output power gets curtailed. Note this
charge rate limit (Pref_BESS) is significantly less than the maximum charge rate (-
Pb_max) to protect the battery since the SoC is high.

                                      Page 146 of 172

Scenario 3: SoC of the BESS is 100%, minimize battery discharging and no battery
charging

Figure IX-98. P-f droop settings for PV and BESS plants when SoC of the BESS is
100%.
When the SoC of the battery is 100%, the DAC chooses the droop settings in Figure
IX-92 and updates the PV and BESS such that the battery cannot charge any more.
With these settings, if the available PV is less than the load, PV works at MPPT and
therefore the discharge rate of BESS is reduced. When the available PV is greater than
the load, system frequency increases and the PV output power gets curtailed similar to
the previous scenarios.
In summary, for all the three scenarios, the droop curve for PV can be set as a straight
line between the points (Load_min-Pref_BESS, Freq_high) and (Ppv_max, Freq_norm).
Note that the points are illustrated as active power-frequency pairs, and Freq_high and
Freq_nom denote the upper bound of the acceptable frequency range and the nominal
frequency, respectively. For BESS, the droop curve can be set as a straight line
between the points (Load_max-Ppv_min, Freq_low) and (Pref_BESS, Freq_norm),
where Freq_low denotes the lower bound of the acceptable frequency range.

Dynamic/Transient Requirements
In all the scenarios discussed above, whenever the PV available power is less than the
load, the PV works at MPPT such that the discharge rate of BESS is reduced, which
increases the duration of the islanded microgrid, enhancing resiliency. Moreover, with
this design, the up-reserve in the island is primarily provided by BESS while the PV
primarily provides down-reserve, thereby, minimizing the discharge rate of BESS and
maximizing its charging whenever possible. This can be done because both BESS and
PV plants are inverter-based and they both have the capability to respond fast to adjust
the active power output following a change in load.
However, even though inverter-based plants are able to respond fast, without explicit
requirements, the dynamic response of the plants may not be sufficient to maintain
stability and acceptable frequency dynamics following a load disturbance. Therefore, in
this section, dynamic/transient requirements for the GFM plant are developed to ensure
stable and acceptable frequency dynamics in the microgrid. A microgrid with multiple
inverter-based GFM plants is considered and the requirements for each individual plant

                                     Page 147 of 172

are presented first. Subsequently, a setting guideline is presented to limit the overall
frequency deviation and maintain microgrid stability considering the interaction between
multiple GFM plants.
Requirements on a Single GFM Plant
The dynamic frequency requirements developed in this subsection are to be applied
under system “normal operating conditions”, where the active and reactive power as
well as current output from the GFM plant are expected to be within its continuous or
short-term rating following the disturbance. Note that in standards addressing grid-
connected DERs such as IEEE Std 1547™-2018, normal operating conditions are
defined by grid voltage and frequency within a certain region. However, in microgrid
operations, since the voltage and frequency of the microgrid is largely governed by the
GFM plant, it is proposed here to define the “normal operating condition” based on
system events. For example, if the microgrid is designed and the GFM plant sized to
handle load step change up to 20% of the microgrid peak load without causing damage
to sensitive load or tripping of the grid following DERs, then load step changes under
20% are included in normal operating conditions.
In contrast, more severe disturbances such as a low impedance fault in the microgrid
can lead to abnormal operating conditions under which the GFM plant is likely to reach
its physical limits (e.g., current limit) and a different set of requirements are applicable.
The proposed requirements are constructed as follows:
• Considering a scenario where a composite load is connected at the reference point
 of applicability (RPA) of the GFM plant, and the kVA rating of the load is 𝑥𝑥1 % of the
 GFM plant rating (𝑥𝑥1 should be specified between 0 and 100). 𝑥𝑥2 % of the load is
 three-phase induction motor load while the rest is constant impedance load with a
 power factor of 𝑥𝑥3
• Following specified load change events (e.g., motor start up, load rejection), the
 GFM plant should maintain its RPA frequency and voltage to be within specified
 regions as illustrated in Figure 3-5 and the maximum rate of change of frequency
 (ROCOF) should be less than 𝑥𝑥4 Hz/s
• If voltage or frequency oscillations occurs following the event, it should have a
 damping ratio greater than 𝑥𝑥5 %

Figure IX-99. Illustration of the allowed frequency region following the specified
disturbances.

                                       Page 148 of 172

As the voltage and frequency dynamics inside the microgrid are not only determined by
the GFM plant but also affected by dynamic loads and equipment in the system and the
interaction between them, ideally, the requirements can be established considering a
detailed microgrid model including the dynamic loads and equipment. However, such a
detailed model may not be practical. Therefore, the requirements are developed based
on a generic composite load model.
With this model, the worst-case load disturbance under normal operating conditions
inside the microgrid is then constructed by specifying the rating of the load, the
percentage of the motor load and a set of specified load change events. With the load
disturbance, the GFM plant is then required to regulate the frequency and the ROCOF
at the plant RPA to be within a specified region. Since the frequency dynamics should
not cause momentary cessation or tripping of the grid following DERs to avoid
cascading effects, the frequency region and the ROCOF should be specified inside the
ride through region of the grid following DERs (e.g., inside the ride through region
defined in IEEE Std 1547™-2018). Moreover, if oscillation occurs, sufficient damping
ratio is required to make sure the system is stable, and the oscillation is damped down
fast enough.
Setting Guideline Considering the Interaction between Multiple GFM Plants
The requirements for individual plant are sufficient when there is only one GFM plant in
the microgrid. When there are multiple GFM plants, the individual plant requirements
should still apply to improve the performance of the overall system. However, they are
no longer sufficient to guarantee acceptable frequency dynamics and stability
considering the interaction between multiple GFM plants. To improve upon the
individual plant requirements, this subsection presents a setting guideline to set the
droop slopes of the various GFM plants.

Setting Guideline Considering the Interaction between Multiple GFM Plants and SG
As mentioned, the individual plant requirements can improve the performance of the
overall system, but they are no longer sufficient to guarantee acceptable frequency
dynamics and stability considering the interaction between multiple plants. A setting
guideline to set the droop slopes of the various GFM plants and the SG to ensure
satisfactory frequency dynamics in the microgrid is necessary.

Setting guidelines to limit the overall frequency deviation
Given a maximum load deviation, Figure 4-2 and Figure 4-3 can be directly created for
the given diesel generator parameters to select droop gains that limit the frequency
deviation at the diesel generator and average inverter frequency deviation, which serves
as the first method to set the droop slopes in Figure 4-1.
Note that this method assumes that the GFM PV inverter is based on Droop Control and
the diesel generator can be effectively approximated by a second-order reference model
that mimics a SG with speed droop governor and first-order turbine dynamics. As in the
case of the average frequency dynamics of a microgrid with grid-forming PV and a battery,
the approximations used to derive the bounds are typically justified for systems with
strong coupling. However, in a weakly coupled microgrid, (reference) governor gains and
inverter droop gains can be selected based on the guidelines above. Then, the margin
needs to be increased to account for the deviations from the average reduced-order
dynamics.

Main GFM Functionality:
For the main GFM system, there was a need to verify that the new software could
tolerate large load changes in islanded mode, and that the switching frequency used
for black-start operation could also be used in grid-tied operation. The solutions were
found through recreating the conditions in a Simulink simulation of the GFM system
and solving them where possible, and examining data collected during fault conditions
where it was not possible to recreate the issue in simulation.

The problems that were facing the system were found and tackled in the following
order, and were as follows:
 1. In islanded mode, the GFM was unable to handle loads larger than 65 kW, at
 which point the GFM would continue to increase the output current but reduce
 the AC voltage setpoint, thus maintaining the 65 kW active power output, even
 while the AC current was increasing.
 2. In islanded mode, if the GFM needed to have a load that was larger than 50 kW
 removed, it would be unable to remove it in one go without causing the AC
 voltage to spike and tripping the Unit.
 3. In grid-tied mode, the active power output would go negative upon closing the
 final contactor, and cause the DC voltage to spike, causing the inverter to trip.
 The inverter would occasionally manage to not trip, but it would not be able to
 follow the active power setpoint and only maintain an output close to 0% Pref.

For each of these aforementioned problems the solutions that would eventually be
implemented were found as follows:

 1. During testing, it was found that when the 65 kW active power limit was
 reached, Vd ref in the reactive power controller (Vref in Figure 96) was reaching
 its maximum value (Figure 97). And since the inverter still needed to increase

increase the AC current (Figure 98). The solution that was found was to
increase the range of the Vd ref setpoint by increasing the output limits of the
integrator in the reactive power controller (highlighted in Figure 96).

For the APR testing phase, there was a need to ensure that the GFM could operate
connected to a Photovoltaic (PV) emulator and follow the PV curve, and to validate
the voltage regulation capabilities of the APR Subsystem developed as part of the
SECURE project. During testing of the original GFM, it was found that the DC
voltage greatly impacted the stability of the inverter hence why it was necessary to
ensure that the GFM can operate with a PV emulator. To demonstrate the impact of
the DC supplies further, Figure 120 and Figure 121 show how the stability of the DC
power supply directly impacts the stability of the AC waveform produced during
black-start/islanded operation. During testing, in order to be able to have the inverter
produce 150+ kW of power, two other Solectria inverters were used as power
supplies, however this caused the DC voltage to be more unstable since these
inverters are not optimized for this kind of operation, and thus the AC waveforms
produced were also more unstable. This can be seen in Figure 120 where the AC
waveform produced by the GFM when the power supply is another Solectria inverter
has some irregular spikes, as compared to Figure 121 where the power supply is just
a magna in DC power supply mode and the AC waveform produced is much more
stable and consistent. From this, it was clear that PV emulator testing was essential.

 Test Conclusion/Observations
The general GFM operation tests were successful in verifying inverter operation and
ability to handle the aforementioned longstanding problems with standard grid-
forming operation. In testing, it was reaffirmed that the most important aspect of the
controller to tune for general operation is the virtual admittance controller, since it
seems to be the component that most impacts stable operation during black-start
operation and due to the importance in regulating the reference current to ensure
stable transitions between states during grid-tied operation. It should also be noted
that the parameter changes to the virtual admittance controller needed to allow for
stable startup operation of the GFM would also reduce stead-state stability slightly
compared to what the initial values for the virtual admittance controller seemed to
suggest from the simulation. However, baseline operation in all contexts were
confirmed, and the inverter is now more robust that it has been. The next steps with
respect to general operation are to undergo THD analysis as well as implement
improvements in the state-machine that controls how the inverter changes between
operating modes (islanding and grid-tied).

For the APR subsystem, all the tests were also successful, however there is still
work to be done to improve stability, similar to general operation. There were initially
issues with getting the inverter to produce power when connected to the PV
emulator, and this was found to be connected to a slight offset in the AC voltage
sensors, which were recalibrated to be slightly more accurate, then the GFM was
able to operate connected to the PV emulator without instability in the AC waveform.

                                             Page 163 of 172

However, the GFM did struggle with higher active power outputs, specifically it
struggled with some overshoot issues when the PV-curve was flatter. The active
power controller would cause some oscillations about Vmpp, which made it hard to
test. Thus it was required to ensure that the power level was low enough so that the
inverter did not reach the part of the PV-curve with smaller gradient. When operating
in the steeper part of the PV-curve the GFM was able to follow the curve without
much oscillation, and regulate the DC voltage exactly as desired. Thus there needs
to be more analysis of how the active power controller regulates the DC voltage. This
seems to be the main issue in improving operation of the GFM, both in standard
operation and with APR active.
