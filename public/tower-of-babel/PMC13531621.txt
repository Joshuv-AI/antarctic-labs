Association Between Cardiovascular Risk Factors and Pan-Coronary Plaque Burden, Phenotype, and Vulnerability
Authors: Jakub Chmiel, Stefano Andreaggi, Sekeun Kim, Iris McNulty, Hang Lee, Adrian P. Banning, Krzysztof L. Bryniarski, Piotr Musialek, Ik-Kyung Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular risk factors (RFs) are commonly used in clinical practice to predict future adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including acute coronary syndromes (ACS). A recent study reported an association between RFs and plaque vulnerability in patients with ACS.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the association between RFs (modifiable and non-modifiable) and pan-coronary plaque burden, plaque phenotype, and features of vulnerability.MethodsIn patients undergoing 3-vessel optical coherence tomography imaging, modifiable (dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, smoking) and non-modifiable (age, sex, family history) RFs were recorded. Plaque number, plaque phenotype, and vulnerable features were analyzed.ResultsA total of 534 plaques from 131 patients (36.6% ACS) were analyzed. As the number of RFs increased, the number of plaques (P trend = 0.001) as well as plaques with a vulnerable phenotype (thin-cap fibroatheromas [TCFAs], P trend = 0.001) increased. An increasing number of RFs was also associated with a higher number of vulnerable features (P trend < 0.001), including more thin fibrous caps (P trend = 0.001), lipid-rich plaques (LRPs), macrophages, microvessels, and cholesterol crystals (all P trend ≤ 0.001). In multivariable analyses, only modifiable RF burden was associated with increased pan-coronary vulnerability (incidence rate ratio [IRR]: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.18-1.57; P < 0.001), including a higher prevalence of TCFAs (IRR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.20-1.83; P < 0.001), LRPs (IRR: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.15-1.59; P < 0.001), and cholesterol crystals (IRR: 1.50; 95% CI: 1.21-1.87; P < 0.001).ConclusionsAs the number of cardiovascular RFs increased, the number of plaques, plaques with a high-risk phenotype, and vulnerable features also increased. Only increasing modifiable RF burden was associated with greater pan-coronary vulnerability, including more TCFAs and LRPs. Clinical Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01110538

Cardiovascular risk factors (RFs) are well-established predictors of adverse clinical outcomes, including acute coronary syndrome (ACS).1 Current guidelines emphasize modifiable RFs—including elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, high blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, obesity, and cigarette smoking—as primary targets for cardiovascular disease prevention, although nonmodifiable RFs, such as age, sex, and family history of coronary artery disease (CAD), also contribute substantially to cardiovascular risk.1,2 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) enables visualization of plaque structure at near-microscopic resolution, including features of plaque vulnerability. These vulnerable features have also been linked to adverse coronary events.3, 4, 5, 6 The increased vulnerability in patients with multiple RFs may explain poor outcomes; however, the association between cardiovascular RFs and coronary vulnerability has not been fully elucidated. Previous OCT studies have primarily examined individual RFs or modifiable and non-modifiable RFs in isolation or have focused on specific clinical cohorts, such as patients with ACS.7,8 Moreover, they predominantly analyzed individual lesions or vessels, presenting a partial and incomplete picture of pan-coronary vulnerability.9, 10, 11, 12

In the present study, we investigated the associations between cardiovascular RF burden (modifiable and non-modifiable) and pan-coronary plaque number (plaque burden), plaque phenotype, and vulnerability in patients undergoing 3-vessel OCT imaging.

Methods

Study population

From the Massachusetts General Hospital OCT registry (NCT01110538), we selected patients who underwent OCT imaging of all 3 major epicardial coronary arteries and did not have prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Demographic, medical history, and laboratory data were recorded from the medical records at each participating site. Cardiovascular RFs were divided into modifiable (dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, smoking) and non-modifiable (age ≥62 years, male sex, and family history of CAD). Age ≥62 years (the cohort median) was used as the cutoff for patient age stratification. Definitions for other RFs are provided in the Supplemental Material.

The Massachusetts General Hospital OCT registry was approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) and by the IRB at each participating site. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before enrollment, and the registry study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The current study was deemed “exempt” by the Mass General Brigham IRB.

OCT analysis

OCT imaging was performed using a frequency-domain (C7/C8; OCT Intravascular Imaging System, St. Jude Medical) or time-domain (M2/M3; Cardiology Imaging Systems, LightLab Imaging) OCT system. All deidentified OCT images were analyzed at the core laboratory of Massachusetts General Hospital by 2 independent investigators blinded to patient data, using an offline review workstation (St. Jude Medical), and in accordance with previously established criteria and definitions (Supplemental Appendix). Any discordance was resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. OCT-defined features of vulnerability—thin fibrous cap, lipid-rich plaque (LRP), macrophages, microvessels, and cholesterol crystals—were identified according to the literature; definitions are detailed in the Supplemental Material.13

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical data as absolute frequencies and percentages. The overall number of RFs was the sum of modifiable and non-modifiable RFs. The modifiable and non-modifiable RF burdens were assessed in each patient as the number of modifiable and non-modifiable RFs present, respectively. The cohort was divided into groups according to the number of all, modifiable, or non-modifiable RFs, or into 3 groups with 0 to 2, 3 to 4, or ≥5 RFs. In each plaque, plaque phenotype and the presence of each vulnerable feature were assessed. For patient-level analyses, these plaque-level variables were aggregated across all plaques within each patient. Trends were assessed using the one-way Jonckheere-Terpstra test. P value for the trend test was estimated using a Monte Carlo permutation procedure (100,000 permutations). A trend line based on the least-squares method was fitted to the weighted mean outcome across ordered RF categories. In multigroup analyses, differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test; when significant, pairwise post hoc comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. Regression analyses were performed using generalized linear models, with Poisson regression as the default and negative binomial regression used when overdispersion was detected (phi >1.3). Results are reported as incidence rate ratio (IRR) with 95% CIs, and statistical significance was assessed using Wald z-tests. For plaque-level analysis, the generalized estimating equations approach, with log-link Poisson and logit-link binomial model assuming independent correlation structure, was used to account for the clustering of multiple lesions in a single patient. Sensitivity analyses were performed by additionally adjusting the primary models for lipid-lowering therapy and ACS presentation. In exploratory analyses, modifiable RF burden was replaced by individual modifiable RFs, with adjustment for non-modifiable RF burden. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

A total of 534 plaques were identified in 131 patients, including 144 (27.0%) fibrous plaques, 215 (40.2%) thick-cap fibroatheromas (ThCFAs), 104 (19.5%) thin-cap fibroatheromas (TCFAs), and 71 (13.3%) fibrocalcific plaques. Across all plaques, 1,012 vulnerable features were identified, including 104 (10.3%) thin fibrous caps, 319 (31.5%) LRPs, 254 (25.1%) macrophages, 262 (25.9%) microvessels, and 73 (7.2%) cholesterol crystals. TCFAs showed the highest mean number of vulnerable features (3.7 ± 1.0), followed by ThCFAs (2.3 ± 0.9), while fibrocalcific and fibrous plaques on average exhibited fewer than one vulnerable feature (0.7 ± 0.8 and 0.7 ± 0.7, respectively; P < 0.001).

The average patient had 3.4 (±1.3) RFs, including 2.0 (±1.0) modifiable and 1.4 (±0.7) non-modifiable RFs (Supplemental Figure 1). Patients with a higher number of RFs had higher serum creatinine (P < 0.001) and were more likely on aspirin (P = 0.007), statins (P < 0.001), and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (P < 0.001; Table 1). With an increasing number of modifiable RFs, white blood cell count (P trend = 0.032), glucose (P trend <0.001), hemoglobin A1c (P trend <0.001), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (P trend = 0.001), and creatinine (P trend = 0.003) also increased. With an increasing number of non-modifiable RFs, serum creatinine level increased (P trend <0.001).

Vulnerable features according to the number of modifiable and non-modifiable RFs

Differential associations of modifiable and non-modifiable RFs with vulnerable features

Figure 3 shows the multivariable associations of modifiable and non-modifiable RF burdens with total and individual vulnerable feature counts at the patient level. Only an increasing number of modifiable RFs was independently associated with a higher total number of vulnerable features (IRR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.18-1.57; P < 0.001), as well as with higher numbers of each individual vulnerable feature, including thin fibrous caps (IRR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.20-1.83; P < 0.001), LRP (IRR: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.15-1.59; P < 0.001), macrophages (IRR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.08-1.50; P = 0.004), microvessels (IRR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.08-1.45; P = 0.003), and cholesterol crystals (IRR: 1.50; 95% CI: 1.21-1.87; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table 4). By contrast, the number of non-modifiable RFs was independently associated with only 2 of the 5 vulnerable features: macrophages (IRR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.01-1.57; P = 0.042) and microvessels (IRR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.10-1.63; P = 0.004). These associations remained significant after adjustments for lipid-lowering therapy and ACS presentation (Supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

The main findings of our study are: 1) as the number of RFs increased, plaque burden and pan-coronary vulnerability increased; 2) modifiable RF burden was strongly associated with pan-coronary vulnerability and with an unfavorable shift in pan-coronary atherosclerosis morphology—fewer stable fibrous plaques and more high-risk TCFAs and LRPs; 3) non-modifiable RF burden was not associated with pan-coronary vulnerability but was associated with a greater plaque burden, driven by a higher number of fibrocalcific plaques.

To date, epidemiological studies linking cardiovascular RFs to clinical events and intravascular imaging studies of coronary atherosclerosis biology have largely progressed in parallel. Our study bridges these 2 lines of evidence by providing a comprehensive assessment of the association between increasing cardiovascular RF burden and unfavorable changes in pan-coronary atherosclerosis morphology on OCT.

Vulnerability: plaque, pan-coronary, patient

The prognostic relevance of plaque morphology is supported by prospective outcome data. First, intravascular ultrasound-based studies identified LRPs, particularly those with thinner fibrous caps, as predictors of adverse cardiovascular events.14, 15, 16 Later, in CLIMA, OCT-identified vulnerable features were linked with future coronary events at 1- and 5-year follow-up, particularly when multiple high-risk features coexisted.3,4 OCT-identified TCFAs, even when physiologically nonobstructive, were associated with an increased risk of subsequent coronary events.5,6

The concept of the “vulnerable plaque”—a rupture-prone atherosclerotic lesion—was initially promoted as a promising predictor of myocardial infarction and cardiovascular mortality. These predictions proved largely correct, but the clinical implications of these findings have been shown to be limited. While plaque vulnerability does predict a high likelihood of rupture, only a small fraction of ruptures translates into clinically meaningful events, and the question of which vulnerable plaque will become symptomatic remains unanswered.15,17 Those observations once again underscore that the pathogenesis of myocardial infarction is more complex than presumed; the interplay between plaque morphology, plaque burden, and thrombogenic milieu was suggested.17 Accordingly, the “vulnerable plaque” should be considered within broader risk profiling and interpreted as an imaging-derived coronary risk marker rather than a plaque-specific predictor of myocardial infarction. This concept is supported by evidence that nonculprit lesions in patients with ACS have more vulnerable features than those without ACS and that multiple TCFAs are associated with higher rates of major adverse coronary events than a single TCFA.18,19 Thus, each individual “vulnerable plaque” represents one component of a patient’s pan-coronary vulnerability; following this logic—the more “vulnerable plaques” present, the greater the chance that one of them will trigger a clinical event. Rather than arising solely from plaque disruption, an acute coronary event may also require specific thrombosis-promoting conditions. However, a greater number of “vulnerable plaques” increases the frequency of plaque disruptions, thereby increasing the likelihood that a disruption coincides with a thrombosis-prone milieu and results in a clinical event.19,20 A single-vessel OCT assessment to evaluate pan-coronary vulnerability proved insufficient; it misclassifies about 25% of patients as TCFA-free and misses more than 50% of TCFAs.21 Considerable expectations for pan-coronary vulnerability assessment rest on noninvasive imaging modalities.22

In line with this evidence, we aimed to address patients’ pan-coronary vulnerability and did so by assessing all plaques across the 3 major coronary vessels. Beyond addressing each vulnerable feature individually, we also assessed them collectively at the pan-coronary level. Although this approach was previously used only at the plaque level, it more accurately reflects broad pan-coronary vulnerability than assessment of an individual plaque or vessel.19,20 Our results show increasing pan-coronary vulnerability, including higher numbers of TCFAs and LRPs, across an increasing number of cardiovascular RFs; the average patient with a high number of RFs (at least 5) had over twice as many TCFAs and LRPs compared to the average patient with a low number of RFs (2 or fewer) (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Our results implicate a gradual increase in the plaque-driven risk of acute coronary events with the number of modifiable cardiovascular RFs.

Cardiovascular risk factors and OCT-findings

OCT-defined features of vulnerability in relation to cardiovascular RFs have been partially explored previously. Previous studies showed that current smoking, dyslipidemia, and elevated hemoglobin A1c levels were associated with a higher prevalence of TCFA.9,10 Suzuki et al11 showed a higher prevalence of LRPs in diabetic patients. Seegers et al12 showed increasing prevalence of TCFAs and LRPs in culprit lesions with age, but only in women. Vergallo et al23 investigated the relationship between nonculprit lesions in the 3 major coronary arteries and Framingham Risk Score categories, finding more plaques, macrophages, cholesterol crystals, and microvessels, but no LRPs or TCFAs, with increasing risk category.

Recent studies from our laboratory addressed the relationship between cardiovascular RFs and culprit-vessel vulnerability in ACS patients. Covani et al7 showed that a higher number of modifiable RFs was associated with greater plaque vulnerability, including a higher prevalence of TCFAs, LRPs, macrophages, microvessels, and cholesterol crystals, particularly in culprit lesions. In contrast, Scalamera et al8 found no association between the number of non-modifiable RFs and the prevalence of vulnerable features.

The present study substantially expands on these prior observations. First, we assessed plaques across the 3 major coronary arteries rather than restricting the analysis to the culprit vessel. Second, rather than focusing solely on vulnerable ACS patients, we included a broader cohort; however, generalizability is limited to patients without prior PCI and eligible for 3-vessel OCT. Third, we evaluated not only vulnerable plaque features but also overall plaque number and phenotype. Fourth, we analyzed both modifiable and non-modifiable RFs, allowing comparisons of their independent contributions. Fifth, we also included obesity as a major modifiable RF. Our study provides a broader and more comprehensive assessment of the relationship between RF burden and OCT-defined coronary plaque characteristics.

Our findings support and extend the observations in ACS presented by Covani et al7 by showing that the association between modifiable RF burden and vulnerability is evident and independent at the pan-coronary level in all-comer patients. This association remained consistent after adjustment for lipid-lowering therapy and ACS presentation (Supplemental Table 5). Moreover, we demonstrated parallel unfavorable changes in plaque phenotypes associated with modifiable RFs—fewer stable fibrous plaques and more high-risk TCFAs (Figure 3).

Non-modifiable RFs were associated with more plaques, particularly fibrocalcific plaques. In contrast to Scalamera et al8, we observed a positive trend between the number of cardiovascular RFs and vulnerable features (Figure 2). This difference likely reflects both the study population and the imaging strategy discussed above. Notably, in multivariable analysis, non-modifiable RFs were not associated with the total number of vulnerable features but remained associated with a higher prevalence of macrophages and microvessels. This implies that a positive trend between non-modifiable RFs and overall number of vulnerable features (Figure 2) was driven by the modifiable RFs. In the plaque-level analysis (Supplemental Table 6), an increasing number of non-modifiable RFs was associated only with a higher prevalence of microvessels. This suggests that the association between non-modifiable RFs and macrophages observed at the pan-coronary level (Figure 3) was driven solely by the number of plaques—rather than by a change in plaque morphology.

Overall, we observed a clear dissociation between modifiable and non-modifiable RFs—modifiable RFs were not associated with an increased number of plaques but with a shift toward more vulnerable morphology of coronary atherosclerosis, while nonmodifiable RFs were associated with an increased number of plaques, particularly fibrocalcific plaques, and were not associated with vulnerability.

Cardiovascular risk factors and clinical outcome

The Framingham Heart Study provided foundational evidence linking cardiovascular RFs to hard cardiovascular endpoints, identifying the major “traditional” modifiable cardiovascular RFs, including elevated blood pressure, cholesterol levels, diabetes, and smoking.1 Contemporary guidelines also recognize obesity as clinically important for cardiovascular prevention.2 Although some studies attributed much of the risk associated with obesity to its clustering with other modifiable RFs, substantial evidence supports an independent association between obesity and cardiovascular risk.1,24,25 Recently, an analysis of over 1.5 million participants showed that 5 modifiable RFs—dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and current smoking—accounted for over 50% of incident cardiovascular disease and about 20% of all-cause mortality.25 Considering its rising prevalence and clinical relevance, we therefore decided to include obesity among the modifiable cardiovascular RFs.2,24,25

The average patient in our study had 2.0 modifiable RFs, and the most prevalent were dyslipidemia and hypertension, affecting 6 out of 10 patients, followed by current smoking and diabetes present in 3 out of 10 patients, and obesity, which affected only 6% of the study cohort. The Framingham Heart Study also identified older age and male sex as strong predictors of hard cardiovascular outcomes.1 Evidence for family history of CAD is less robust; however, it is considered a risk-modifier in current guidelines.2 The average patient in our study had 1.4 nonmodifiable RFs, 7 out of 10 were male, half were classified as older according to the study design, and 1 in 6 had a family history of CAD. Consistent with prior studies, we evaluated cardiovascular RFs jointly as a composite measure.7,8

Modifiable risk factors, non-modifiable risk factors, and vulnerability

CAD is an inflammatory disease, and modifiable RFs have been shown to promote inflammation at the systemic and vascular levels.26, 27, 28 Inflammation promotes plaque progression and is indirectly associated with increased plaque vulnerability.29, 30, 31 In our cohort, as the burden of modifiable RFs increased, white blood cell count also increased. Cardiovascular RFs likely contribute to patient vulnerability not only by shaping plaque biology but also by promoting a prothrombotic milieu.32 Current guidelines emphasize modifiable RFs as primary targets for cardiovascular disease prevention.2 By addressing modifiable cardiovascular RFs, primary and secondary prevention of myocardial infarction have been shown to significantly reduce its first occurrence and recurrence.2 In our study, modifiable RFs were associated with increased vulnerability—in patients with at least 4 modifiable RFs, 1 in 2 plaques was a TCFA and 4 in 5 were LRPs (Supplemental Figure 5), averaging 2 TCFAs and 2 other LRPs per patient (Figures 1 and 2). Notably, TCFAs and LRPs themselves were shown to be modifiable—lipid-lowering therapy thickens the fibrous cap and reduces lipid burden.33,34 Therefore, in line with our findings, TCFAs and LRPs may represent the modifiable imaging-derived biological manifestation through which, beyond the prothrombotic state, modifiable RFs are associated with a higher incidence of myocardial infarction. These findings further strengthen the rationale for early, intensive, and sustained control of modifiable RFs to mitigate future coronary events.

Non-modifiable RFs, particularly older age, are more commonly associated with chronic low-grade inflammatory pathways and vascular remodeling.35 We did not observe an increase in pan-coronary vulnerability with an increasing non-modifiable RF burden. However, non-modifiable RFs were associated with more plaques, particularly fibrocalcific plaques, in line with the findings of Scalamera et al.8 This may be explained by a chronic lower-grade inflammatory milieu that contributes to slower plaque growth and, over time, to calcific remodeling.36,37

Study Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, although the data were prospectively collected, the analysis was performed retrospectively. In addition, 3-vessel OCT was performed at the discretion of the operator in patients with stable hemodynamics and appropriate anatomy for OCT imaging. Furthermore, patients with prior PCI were excluded by design. These limitations reduce the generalizability of our findings to an all-comer cath-lab population. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted primarily in PCI-naive patients without chronic total occlusion in whom 3-vessel OCT is feasible. Second, although OCT imaging was performed in all 3 major coronary arteries, the far distal segment with a lumen diameter smaller than the size of the OCT catheter (0.9 mm) was not assessed. Third, because of the shallow penetration of OCT, plaque volume and vascular remodeling were not assessed. Fourth, light attenuation from LRP might have hampered the accurate identification of some vulnerable features.

Conclusions

An increasing number of cardiovascular RFs was associated with greater coronary plaque burden and vulnerability. Modifiable RF burden was associated with greater coronary vulnerability, whereas nonmodifiable RF burden showed no association. These findings emphasize the importance of focusing on modifiable RFs for primary and secondary prevention.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with CAD, pan-coronary vulnerability increases in proportion to the number of RFs especially modifiable cardiovascular RFs.
