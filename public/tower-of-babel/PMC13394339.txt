An Exploration of Australian Radiographers' Perceptions of and Experiences With Imaging Autistic People
Authors: Yasmina Lin, Laura Di Michele, Frances Gray, Franziska Jerjen, Yobelli Alexandra Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAutistic individuals referred for medical imaging examinations may face barriers without appropriate adjustments. Strengthening the relationship between patients and the healthcare system is crucial, as positive patient experiences can improve adherence and future engagement with healthcare services. This study explored Australian radiographers' experiences and perceptions of imaging autistic patients. Terminology preferences among autistic individuals vary across countries, communities and individuals. This paper adopts identity‐first language, ‘autistic person’ or ‘autistic patient’, aligned with recommendations from Australian organisations and medical imaging researchers.MethodsSemi‐structured online interviews were conducted via Zoom with 10 diagnostic radiographers. Anonymised transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis to identify patterns of meaning across participants' experiences.ResultsAll radiographers (n = 10) reported experience performing medical imaging procedures for autistic patients. Four themes were identified: (1) Educational resources and recommendations, (2) Organisational factors affecting radiographic practice, (3) Patient and carer factors affecting radiographic practice, and (4) Key strategies that facilitate positive patient care.ConclusionAlthough radiographers lacked formal training specific to caring for autistic individuals, they attributed positive patient experiences to their patient‐centred approach, existing knowledge, communication skills, teamwork, and personal attitudes. Radiographers expressed interest in targeted training and remain committed to high‐quality patient care. Improving the accessibility of radiology departments through collaboration with autism organisations and the broader community is essential to support better imaging experiences for all patients. Future research should prioritise the perspectives of autistic individuals and their families to comprehensively understand their medical imaging experiences and identify the range of meaningful, patient‐defined indicators of quality care.Keywords: medical imaging, patient‐centred care, person‐centred care, radiographers

Introduction

Access to high‐quality healthcare services is a social determinant of health and it is imperative to address inequities in health outcomes among diverse populations [1, 2]. Enhancing the relationship between patients and the healthcare system is crucial, as patients' positive experiences increase patient adherence to interventions and support future engagement with healthcare services [3, 4]. Motivation to evaluate inclusivity of care in medical imaging is reflected in the Australian Government's drive to improve the physical and mental health outcomes of autistic people [5], given reports that almost three‐quarters of autistic individuals have complex healthcare needs [6]. In the medical model, autism is traditionally conceptualised as a disorder; however, autistic academics and self‐advocates support the neurodiversity paradigm, which reframes autism as a natural variation in human neurobiology and experience [7, 8]. Autistic individuals commonly describe diverse strengths and challenges, including uneven skill profiles, sensory differences, and the impacts of repeated social and environmental barriers [9, 10]. In Australia, autism affects approximately 1.1% of the population and autistic individuals are reported to require varying levels of assistance with cognitive or emotional tasks, mobility, and communication [6]. These factors contribute to increased healthcare complexity and are relevant to healthcare contexts, where sensory demands and communication requirements are heightened.

Internationally, there is also a growing need for quality person‐centred care in the practice of medical imaging, and investigations relating to autistic patients have recently been conducted in Switzerland [11], the United Kingdom [12] and the United Arab Emirates [13]. Combined, these studies revealed systemic lapses or inadequacies in the communication between services, radiographers and autistic service users, and highlighted a lack of guidelines or formal training protocols to equip radiographers with up‐to‐date knowledge and relevant imaging practices in their local context. This lack of education also extends to radiography students and suggests that junior radiographers are less prepared and confident in imaging autistic patients [8]. Previous studies [11, 12] have also revealed that insubstantial provisions for autism care were possibly linked to implications on patient management and workflow, as well as an over‐reliance on carers or guardians.

Part of the challenges in the medical imaging environments for autistic individuals may stem from environmental factors, such as lights, loud noises, low temperatures, and crowded spaces, inherent to healthcare settings and medical imaging equipment [14, 15]. The modality for which an autistic patient presents will also influence the techniques employed to assist in ensuring the procedure is completed successfully. In Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT), after assessing the patient's emotional state and a risk assessment, the solution is often sedation [11, 12, 16, 17, 18]. Despite the risk of rare side effects, sedation is crucial for calming patients, thus reducing motion artefacts to complete scans. However, sedation is not typically used in general radiography; rather, it is the radiographic technique, reliant on radiographers' competency and experience, which is employed to ensure diagnostic images. This is highly pertinent in a radiological context, where a patient's experience during medical imaging procedures can impact future compliance and perceptions regarding medical imaging [19].

The research team aims to improve education of patient‐centred care in radiography students, by identifying education and practice gaps within the radiography workforce relating to autistic individuals. Hence, the aims of this study were to explore the perceptions and experiences of Australian radiographers towards managing radiographic examinations for autistic patients and to explore recommendations to optimise radiographic techniques used by radiographers to meet the needs of autistic patients who require radiographic services. We acknowledge that the terminology used for and preferred by autistic individuals varies within and between countries, populations, organisations and individuals. In this paper, the authors adopted identity‐first language, ‘autistic person’, ‘autistic patient’, based on recommendations from Australian organisations [20, 21] and medical imaging researchers [22].

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Issues

This research employed a social constructivist framework, which aimed to understand individuals' lived experience by acknowledging how meaning and knowledge are actively created through language and social interaction among individuals [23]. As such, a key concept is that an individual's actions are not rooted in the world's actual reality but instead founded upon the meaning they have for the individual [23, 24, 25]. As a way of reconciling and appreciating the differences in perspective, social constructivism has been employed in numerous autism and mental health research papers to frame studies [26]. This approach to the data was vital for uncovering perceptions and the ambiguities in conversations [27, 28]. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ) [29] was used to report findings for this qualitative study. Ethical approval was granted from the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (Project no. 2023/HE000893).

Participants and Recruitment

Participants were invited to participate in the study via an online expression of interest (EOI), electronically distributed through the Australian Society for Medical Imaging and Radiation Therapy (ASMIRT). The EOI landing page included a Participant Information Statement (PIS) with study details. Inclusion criteria were practising radiographers working in Australian radiology departments who had clinical experience imaging autistic patients. Interviews were conducted between May and June 2024. All participants provided written consent to participate in the study. An initial target sample of approximately 8–12 participants was considered for this study, informed by the study design and data collection methods aimed at a relatively homogenous participant group.

Data Collection

The interview guide was developed by the researchers based on consultation with international medical imaging academics, previous radiography literature [8, 11, 12, 13, 14], as well as general studies relating to autism in healthcare [5, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 26, 30]. A pilot testing of the interview questions were undertaken with academic colleagues, there were no changes made. Direct consumer input from autistic individuals was not obtained during the development of the interview guide. Data was collected using semi‐structured interviews to allow for detailed accounts of personal experiences. The interview questions related to participants' ability to recognise autistic patients who present for imaging procedures and the availability of relevant clinical information (e.g., referral notes), the presence of departmental guidelines and prior education or training relating to autism. Participants were also asked to describe strategies used before, during and after imaging procedures, including their effectiveness, potential improvements, and associated challenges. Communication barriers and facilitators in interactions with autistic patients were also explored. Follow up prompts were used as required, (e.g., ‘Can you tell me more?’, ‘How did that happen’) to elicit deeper insights. Interviews were undertaken by three researchers (A3, A4, A5) via Zoom at a time convenient to each study participant. All interviews were conducted in English and designed to be completed in approximately 30 min. Participants were reminded of the study goals and justification for conducting the research at the commencement of the interview. Data collection and preliminary analysis occurred concurrently, and recruitment was ended once conceptual saturation was reached, with no new themes emerging in the later interviews.

Data Analysis

Interview audio data was transcribed using Zoom's automatic transcription tool. The transcripts were checked against the digital recordings by the research team to ensure accuracy, and these recordings were subsequently deleted. Each participant was randomly assigned a unique code (e.g., R1—R10) and any names or identifying information provided by participants were anonymised (e.g., Hospital 1, Patient A). The qualitative data from the interview transcripts were then analysed using a reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) framework modelled after the Braun & Clarke paper (2019) and organised using the NVivo14 software [31]. The six phases for RTA were followed by the research team: (1) Data familiarisation; (2) Coding; (3) Generating initial themes; (4) Develop and revise themes; (5) Refine, define and name themes; (6) Manuscript writing [31].

Preparation for data analysis commenced with all researchers actively engaging in data familiarisation by reading the interview transcripts individually and making reflexive notes and memos for each one. Then, the initial coding phase took place in two teams of two researchers. Transcripts were analysed line‐by‐line and coded [31]. Only data that was deemed relevant to the experiences and perceptions of the radiographers in imaging autistic individuals were coded. This process ensured that each notion remained grounded in the data instead of being pre‐determined. Each code created was stored on NVivo14, which allowed our research team to make continual comparisons between existing codes in the codebook and new data from each transcript [31, 32], each new concept was given a unique code, while similar concepts were collated under the same codes. After the coding process, two codebooks were formed, and each pair generated their initial themes from their respective codebook. This initial theme generation occurred wherein codes were grouped under broader terms, assisting with the identification of any emergent patterns or trends [31, 32]. Conceptual saturation was achieved as no new codes or concepts were emerging from the eighth interview transcript, nevertheless, the rest of the transcripts were analysed to confirm saturation and the formation of final themes via consensus [31].

Prior to and throughout the analysis process, reflexive journaling and peer debrief were adopted to identify individual assumptions and reduce bias. Reflexivity was adopted throughout the research process, from decisions made about study design to the data analysis and discussion. The research team was made up of four medical radiation science academics, two with existing clinical experience, and one radiography student. As such, all researchers had a connection to the medical imaging discipline, which allowed for a comprehensive understanding of the unique setting of this study: patient care within radiology departments. All researchers identified as female. Consideration of individual researchers' experience and perceptions related to the topic were continuously discussed among the research team.

Results

Ten radiographers consented to participate in the study, and a total of 10 interviews were conducted. Participating radiographers were working in metropolitan public health settings in New South Wales, South Australia, and Queensland. The participants had experience in general radiography, CT, and/or MRI. During thematic analysis, four themes were defined: (1) Educational resources and recommendations, (2) Organisational factors affecting radiographic practice, (3) Patient and carer factors influencing radiographic practice, and (4) Key strategies that facilitate positive patient care.

Educational Resources and Recommendations

This theme combined concepts under three sub‐themes: ‘Formal training’, ‘Informal and general training’, ‘Educational needs and recommendations for future training’. A summary of quotes representing this theme is presented in Table 1. All participants shared information about the available educational resources in their departments, categorised into formal and informal methods. Several participants mentioned that participation in general training courses taught useful skills for caring for autistic patients. Other participants had brought their own knowledge and experiences to the workplace, and so their training on autism was informal, personal and self‐driven. All participants shared their recommendations for future training programs to improve knowledge and strategies, as they noted specific gaps in radiographer education that should be addressed.

Organisational Factors Affecting Radiographic Practice

This theme explored organisational factors, and how these structures affected the way radiographers could modify their practice. Organisational factors are defined as the internal factors within an organisation or department, namely it's structure, environment, resources, team dynamics and employee competencies [33]. For this study, the above factors fell under the following three sub‐themes; ‘Systems and processes’, ‘Specific adaptations to the environment’ and ‘Sufficient dedicated time’. A summary of quotes representing this theme are presented in Table 2. Some participants described a lack of organisational guidelines in place to manage and streamline procedures for autistic patients, whilst other participants mentioned their department had an organisational system in place to identify autistic individuals, or those requiring additional support. One participant had developed systems and processes after personal education into the topic. Regarding specific adaptations to the environment, participants noted there are inherent challenges in radiology departments, which can be barriers for autistic patients. Participants also described situations and scenarios where they were able to adapt the clinical environment to cater to a patient's needs and requests, with extended time being a key aspect.

Patient and Carer Factors Influencing Radiographic practice

This theme was divided into four sub‐themes; ‘Influence of carer in imaging procedure’, ‘Communication’, ‘Patient‐centred Care’, and ‘Patient presentation’. A summary of quotes for this theme are presented in Table 3. Participants noted the unique role that guardians and carers play in the dynamics between radiographers and autistic patients. Radiographers mentioned how carers can act as both a barrier or enabler for the imaging process and that it is important to ensure that the carer's needs were considered as part of the examination. It acknowledged the diversity among autistic patients means that individually tailored approaches to care are most effective.

Discussion

This study explored radiographers' perceptions of practice when imaging autistic individuals, focusing on radiographers' knowledge and training, current practice surrounding procedures for autistic individuals, and the availability of resources to facilitate modified care. Participants in our study acknowledged that when navigating medical imaging departments, autistic individuals may face challenges and barriers that can be exacerbated by inadequacies in provider‐patient communication, environmental and systemic adaptations, and examination adjustments, similar to previous studies [14, 15]. Radiographers suggested that not all departments had environmental modifications and adjustments in place for autistic patients, such as a quiet room; however, some clinical sites had organisational systems to identify patients in need of extra support. Despite a lack of resources to provide tailored care for autistic patients, all participants in our study reported confidence in adjusting their technique and practicing a patient‐centred approach to ensure each patient's unique needs were considered. The literature shows that radiographers recognise the need to adapt medical imaging procedures for autistic individuals, with more than 98% of participants in the study by Abdelrahman et al. [13] and Stogiannos et al. [12] reporting modification to their practice. It will also be important to understand how adaptations to practice in medical imaging are experienced by autistic individuals themselves. Hence, incorporating lived experience perspectives from autistic patients is suggested for future research.

Previous studies reveal an absence of existing guidelines to standardise and streamline the preparation and performance of radiographic procedures for autistic adults and children [12, 13, 15, 30, 34]. Findings from our study aligned with the literature and showed that not all study participants were aware of policies or guidelines in Australian medical imaging departments regarding the care of autistic patients during medical imaging procedures. Despite a lack of autism‐specific guidelines, this was not considered to affect delivery of care. Specifically, radiographers reported that high‐quality radiographic care was attributed to services delivered with effective communication, teamwork, positive attitudes, and most importantly, a person and family‐centred approach, where time was taken to properly connect and interact with patients and carers. Although specific adjustments were not evaluated against best practice recommendations in our study, in previous studies [11, 12, 13] researchers reported that radiographers' adjustments adhered to recommended practices or aligned consistently with interventions provided in the broader literature.

While results from our study suggest that radiographers are confident in patient care adaptations, limited knowledge and autism‐specific resources across radiology departments is a common insufficiency reported by the literature [35]. The reliance on individual radiographers' experience and informal knowledge sharing, while reflective of professional commitment, may also result in variability in care that is not consistently aligned with individuals' needs or preferences. Without direct input from autistic patients and their caregivers, it remains unclear whether current adaptations address the aspects of the imaging experience that matter most to those accessing care. Ultimately, lack of specific knowledge is considered to lead to suboptimal patient experiences in medical imaging, and inequities in health outcomes [19]. The Australian Government has developed the ‘National Roadmap to Improve the Health and Mental Health of Autistic People’, [5] which signals a need for change to improve autistic people's physical and mental health outcomes. Part of the plan is to improve support services for autistic individuals and their families, improve the quality and safety of autism‐affirming healthcare, and improve autism education and training for health professionals. None of the participants in our study had received formal training relating to autism and related patient care. Instead, learning opportunities and training were gained through informal routes that study participants had voluntarily attended. Of these informal routes, those directly related to autism were in‐services provided by colleagues or presentations at professional conferences. This may suggest a lack of knowledge base on best practice specific to autistic patients, but also a lack of access to educational resources, which is similar to previous findings [19, 35]. Abdelrahman et al. [13], reported that more than 90% of radiographers in their study had not had any formal training regarding autism. In the study by Abdelrahman et al. [13], 80% of participants indicated desire for further training to improve their radiographic practice with autistic patients. This is similar to the current study, where the majority of participants expressed interest for tailored education or further informal avenues of training.

Tailored education for radiographers would support improved patient care delivered to autistic patients, although many study participants relied on their dedication to patient‐centred care to deliver high quality care for all patients presenting for imaging in the radiology department. This is similar to previous studies [11, 12, 13], where researchers noted that radiographers had devised their own ways of caring for autistic patients with techniques passed along through colleague experience; however, improvements in training and knowledge were considered necessary to provide more consistent and standardised care.

Future Research

Future research should prioritise the perspectives of autistic individuals and their families to comprehensively understand the medical imaging experience and identify the range of meaningful, patient‐defined indicators of quality care. Embedding lived experience perspectives within medical imaging research and practice may contribute to improved patient experiences, reduced healthcare inequities, and better long‐term outcomes.

Limitations

This study has a small sample size. However, sampling was performed throughout Australian medical imaging departments in different Australian states [36]. Despite this, the data reached saturation, as no new concepts were emerging after the eighth interview. There may also be bias in the participant sample, as participants who opted into the study may have a greater interest and positive attitudes to high‐quality patient care. Another potential limitation is social desirability bias; however, it was made clear to participants that their answers would remain anonymous and confidential.

Although this study explored radiographers' experiences and perceptions regarding imaging autistic patients, it did not include direct participation from autistic individuals. This reflects the study's focus on identifying education and practice gaps within the radiography workforce rather than examining patient perspectives. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the absence of autistic voices is a limitation, as the lived experience is essential to fully understand barriers to care. To mitigate this limitation, we consulted existing literature [7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 22, 26, 34] and medical imaging colleagues with experience in this study topic. Future research on educational approaches will prioritise participatory approaches that directly involve autistic people to ensure recommendations authentically reflect needs and preferences for those with lived experience.

Conclusion

Experiences of Australian radiographers towards managing radiographic examinations for autistic patients are similar to international studies. Radiographers are comfortable in their ability to deliver high‐quality care to all patients requiring imaging, and they employ patient‐centred care to achieve their goals. Colleagues also play an important role in supporting knowledge and skill development. Despite this, there was a positive attitude towards further education and guidance aimed to increase radiographers' knowledge, communication, and accommodation of modifications for diverse patient presentations. In the future, it will be important to explore recommendations to optimise radiographic techniques used by radiographers to meet the needs of autistic patients who require radiographic services. Creating more accessible imaging departments is a process that will involve collaboration with autism organisations and patients, developing robust educational plans for autism awareness and knowledge in graduating and established radiographers, and championing individualised care.
