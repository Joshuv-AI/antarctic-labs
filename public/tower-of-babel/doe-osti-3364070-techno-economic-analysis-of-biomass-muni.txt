EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents an assessment of the cost and performance of three gasification systems
fed with alternative solid feedstocks. Three gasification plant configurations with carbon capture
and storage (CCS) fed with biomass, municipal solid waste (MSW), and mixed plastic waste
(MPW) were analyzed and compared using standard technoeconomic analysis techniques. The
plant site was selected to be a generic location in the United States (U.S.) eastern Texas region
with access to geologic carbon dioxide (CO2) storage in the East Texas Basin. The MSW assumes
an example composition based on sorted MSW heading to a landfill from within the Texas
region and the MPW assumes bales of plastics rejected to a landfill from a material recovery
facility (MRF). Study cases are not reflective of specific, current commercial experience since
there is limited biomass gasification commercial operating experience and no MSW or MPW
gasification commercial experience for hydrogen production. However, thought was put into the
design basis assumptions to present examples of commercial, integrated systems. A summary of
the plant configurations is shown in Exhibit ES-1.

The National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) has standardized assumptions for process
modeling and cost analysis in its Quality Guidelines for Energy System Studies (QGESS)
documents. QGESS guidance was followed where applicable, with changes or clarifications
noted. This ensures sound, consistent, and transparent technology assessments and

Aspen Plus® (Aspen) models were developed for the three gasification configurations. The
models were used to generate energy and mass balances, which were used as the basis for
generating capital and operating cost estimates. Performance data were based upon published
reports, information obtained from vendors and users of the technology, performance data
from design/build projects, and engineering judgment.
The capital and operation and maintenance (O&M) costs for each of the cases were estimated
using a scaling approach using reference costs from prior NETL studies according to the 2024

report is the levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH), which is the revenue that must be received by
the producer per kilogram of hydrogen (H2) produced to meet all capital and operating costs of

The primary observations that can be made are as follows:
 • The largest contributor to the BEC for biomass gasification is the gasifier and accessories
 section ($277 MM, 52 percent). The largest contributor to the BEC for the MSW and

 H2, which has the smallest scale of all cases and suffers from lack of economies of scale.
 • For all cases, the largest portion of the LCOH is attributed to the capital costs, ranging

 MSW+MPW gasification cases, as CO2 T&S costs are about 3.5 and 5 times larger than
 the biomass case for the MSW and MSW+MPW gasification cases, respectively. This
 shows the important of CO2 pipeline networks to avoid the high costs of dedicated
 pipelines for small flow rates.
This study focuses on hydrogen production from gasification of alternative feedstocks. Due to
the adaptability of gasification technology, this work may be expanded to explore the
production of other valuable products. These include syngas, electricity, combined heat and
power, synthetic fuels, chemicals, or polygeneration concepts to produce a combination of
products. These alternative products could offer different economic advantages and market
opportunities compared to a hydrogen product.
Cost reduction strategies are posed based on the study findings. Among these is removing the
carbon capture system, which would decrease capital expenditures, variable O&M costs, utility
demands, and the costs associated with CO2 T&S. This modification is expected to provide a
moderate cost reduction with a tradeoff of higher stack CO2 emissions.

1 INTRODUCTION
The objective of this study is the completion of reference cost and performance models for
gasification systems fed with biomass, municipal solid waste (MSW), and mixed plastic waste
(MPW). This report presents the results of a techno-economic analysis (TEA) of three study
cases for hydrogen production via gasification with carbon capture and storage (CCS). TEAs are a
means of evaluating the cost and performance of a technology and are developed for a variety
of purposes, including establish a commercial benchmark; estimate the commercial potential of
a technology; inform research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) priorities; inform
business or policy decisions; and assess economic uncertainty and risk. The goal is to present
results in a consistent and transparent manner, following National Energy Technology
Laboratory (NETL) Quality Guidelines for Energy System Studies (QGESS) documents, wherever
applicable.
The first of the three study cases is torrefied, woody biomass gasification in an entrained flow
gasifier with CCS (Case 1). The second study case includes MSW gasification in a fluidized bed
gasifier with CCS (Case 2). The third study case is similar to Case 2, but with the addition of a
portion of MPW to the MSW feedstock, in a fluidized bed gasifier with CCS (Case 3). Study cases
are not reflective of current commercial experience since there is limited biomass gasification
commercial operating experience and no MSW or MPW gasification commercial experience for
hydrogen production. However, thought was put into the design basis to select study
assumptions to present an example of a commercial integrated system.
The balance of the report is organized as follows:
 • Section 2: Literature Review – a review of literature to inform the study case selection
 and definition process
 • Section 3: Design Basis – details assumptions for performance and cost modeling for the
 study cases
 • Section 4: Study Cases – provides information on the process description, performance
 modeling, and cost estimating for the study cases
 • Section 5: Results & Discussion – compares performance and cost results of the study
 cases and explores how various parameters affect the cost results in sensitivity analyses
 • Section 6: Conclusion – summarizes key study results and takeaways and includes a
 discussion of follow-on analyses that could provide additional insights

2 LITERATURE REVIEW
An extensive literature review was conducted to inform the study case selection and definition
process. The objectives of the literature review were to provide a discussion of supply chain of
various alternative feedstocks, feedstock selection and composition, feedstock cost
development, gasification processes, plant examples and key players.

2.1 WOODY BIOMASS AS A GASIFICATION FEEDSTOCK
2.1.1 Biomass Feed
Biomass is a term used to describe a wide range of alternative, non-fossil-based feedstocks that
can be used for energy or chemicals productions via gasification, combustion, incineration,
pyrolysis, and more. Biomass used as dedicated energy crops is typically divided into two
categories: herbaceous and woody biomass. In this study, the focus was on dedicated energy
crop biomass within the woody biomass category.
Dedicated energy crop biomasses have similar but distinct characteristics compared to coal.
Overall, biomass typically has a higher moisture content, lower bulk density, more fibrous
composition, higher organic volatile matter, lower ash fusion temperature, and lower energy
density (Kelsall & Baruya, 2022). Compositionally, biomass has less fixed carbon, lower nitrogen,
lower sulfur, higher phosphorus, higher alkali and alkaline earth elements, higher oxygen, and
higher hydrogen (H2) compared to coal (Kelsall & Baruya, 2022). Higher H2 content typically
results from greater biomass inclusion in the feedstock, particularly with woody biomass (NETL,
undated).
Woody biomass is defined as “the trees and woody plants, including tops, needles, leaves, and
other woody parts, grown in a forest, woodland, or rangeland environment, which are by-
products of forest management,” by the United States (U.S.) Forest Service (2011). Examples of
woody biomass that are most used for energy include short-rotation woody crops like hybrid
poplar, pine, and willow (White, 2010).
Herbaceous biomasses are typically perennial grasses that are harvested annually and include
switchgrass, miscanthus, bamboo, sweet sorghum, tall fescue, kochia, and wheatgrass
(Bioenergy Technologies Office, undated). This type of biomass has been shown to produce high
quantities of ash and corrosive components when gasified.
Other biomass feedstocks include agricultural crop residues, forestry residues, algae, wood
processing residues, sorted municipal waste, and wet waste and have not been considered in
this report (Bioenergy Technologies Office, undated).
For gasification, a woody biomass dedicated energy crop was of interest, since it is a robust fuel
that has been used as an energy source for centuries, it has more beneficial characteristics
compared to herbaceous biomass, and it has been demonstrated as an appropriate fuel source
for gasification.

2.1.2 Biomass Handling & Preparation
There are different pre-treatment options for biomass that improve its performance in a gasifier.
Although biomass handling and preparation may not be technology-specific, this discussion
focuses on biomass handling and preparation that is most beneficial for biomass performance in
an entrained flow gasifier. The higher moisture content and lower bulk density create
constraints for biomass handling, transport, and storage. The fibrous composition impedes
grindability in existing coal pulverizers, resulting in larger, irregularly shaped particles (Kelsall &

 7

Baruya, 2022). Pre-treatment methods for biomass can improve transport, handling, and
gasification characteristics and include the following:
 • Beneficiation (foreign material removal)
 • Comminution (size reduction)
 • Torrefaction (thermal treatment)
 • Densification (pelletization)
Beneficiation, or foreign material removal, is the treatment and removal of raw materials to
improve physical or chemical properties. This includes foreign material removal, ash removal,
wet and dry screening, impacting and abrasion, and flotation with liquids and air (Forest
Concepts, LLC, 2019).
Comminution, or size reduction, is the action of reducing materials to small particles or
fragments in order to optimize handling storage, transportation, and conversion. Energy
requirements for comminution of biomass is dependent on the desired particle physical
properties (Miao, Grift, Hansen, & Ting, 2010). Uneven size distribution may cause bridging and
clogging of hoppers and augers, inhibition of particles spreading in moving and fluidized beds,
an increase in dust and particulate formation, and reduction of gasifier efficiency (Wang &
Stiegel, 2017).
Torrefaction, or thermal treatment, allows for a reduction in the mass of the feedstock while
maintaining most of the chemical energy. Specifically, 90 percent of the initial energy content is
retained but only 70 percent of the mass is retained in the final torrefied product (Bergman,
2005). Torrefied biomass has superior grindability compared to untreated or fresh biomass,
since after torrefaction biomass loses part of its fibrous structure. While torrefied biomass is
more brittle, it has the drawback of decreased mechanical strength and increased dust
formation. Implementing pelletization with torrefaction can help to mitigate the drawbacks of
torrefied biomass (Bergman, 2005). With entrained flow gasifiers especially, torrefaction can
increase syngas yields (Wang & Stiegel, 2017).
Densification, or pelletization, improves the storability of torrefied biomass by reducing water
uptake. Water uptake decreases as biomass is processed through chipping, torrefaction, and
pelletization, as demonstrated in water immersion and high humidity tests: steam-exploded
pellets performed the best (Eddings, 2021). Pelletization is also useful to standardize the fuel
and increase its bulk density (Wang & Stiegel, 2017).
Overall, for the biomass gasification case study, comminution and torrefaction were considered
in the study to improve handling, transportation, energy density, and performance of biomass in
the gasifier. Beneficiation was excluded from consideration due to the low level of foreign
materials and contaminants in the selected biomass. Pelletization of the biomass was deemed
unnecessary for this study.

2.1.4.1 Entrained Flow Gasifiers
The Air Products General Electric (GE) gasifier is an entrained-flow, slurry-fed, downward-fed,
refractory-lined gasifier. The additional water in the slurry feed creates a syngas product with
higher H2 to carbon monoxide (CO) ratios but lowers gasifier thermal efficiency. The gasifier has
been demonstrated at the Polk power station in which eucalyptus grove biomass was co-fired
with coal to make up to 1.2 percent of the feed. The test had one minor incident where
oversized biomass plugged the suction of one of the pumps (NETL, 2022). Overall, it was found
there was no technical impediment to incorporating a small percentage of biomass into the
feed. The gasifier was also demonstrated at the North Fork power station. For this project,
Western Energy Systems and San Francisco-based Phoenix Energy signed an agreement with GE
to provide equipment for an integrated biomass gasification solution. The project had a 1 MW
engine and used excess forest residue as the biomass of choice (GE, 2015).
The Air Products Shell gasifier is an entrained-flow, dry-fed, pressurized, slagging, up-flow
gasifier. This gasifier has proven biomass experience via the Nuon Power project at Buggenum,
Netherlands. The 253 MWe project tests co-gasification of coal and biomass with up to 30 wt%
biomass in the feed. By 2011, the project increased biomass to 50 wt%. In 2013, the installation
closed due to low energy prices and high cost of operation (NETL, undated).
Other less-common entrained-flow gasifiers included the bioliq® BtL process that was
developed as a pilot plant at Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. The gasifier was tested at
pressures of 40 bar and 80 bar for roughly 1,200 hours of operation, feeding 920 tons of slurry

 14

and using wood- and straw-type biomass (Eberhard, et al., 2020). The Nexterra gasifier was
developed at the University of British Columbia, had a size of 8.5 MW, and operated at a
temperature ranging 875–1,075 °C. The biomass used was scraps from construction and
demolition and municipal tree trimmings that had MC ranging 20–35 percent, and the syngas
produced had a H2/CO ratio of 0.26 to 0.54 (Oveisi, et al., 2018).

2.2 MUNICIPAL SOLID WASTE AS A GASIFICATION FEEDSTOCK
2.2.1 Historical Timeline
In the United States in the 1700s, no refuse was collected. In the 1800s, there were local dumps
and municipal waste dumps. In the early 1900s, backyard incinerators were typically used, but
these were banned in 1957. By the 1950s, there was a change from open dumps to landfills. In
1961, sanitary landfill standards were developed and in 1965, solid waste management
practices were put in place. In 1970, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) was formed
and in 1976, the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) was passed, which promoted
a shift to energy recovery via waste-to-energy (WTE) and recycling. WTE plants were fed as-
received MSW and there are many different combustion concepts; however, some resistance
included environmental issues (e.g., dioxins) and alterative interest in materials recovery (EPA,
2025a). In the early 1980s, the first material recovery facility (MRF) was put into operation in
Connecticut. In 1984, there were hazardous and solid waste management amendments added
to RCRA. In the 1990s, single stream recycling to MRFs was implemented and large-scale
composting became more common. In the 2000s, over the last 20 years, there has not been
significant change in how MSW is processed, except for the implementation of other food
management techniques. Exhibit 2-6 shows how the United States has managed MSW over
time, from 1960 to 2018, per data from EPA (2025a).

2.2.7 Challenges & Recommendations
Challenges for gasification are considered in selecting the gasification and syngas cleaning
process. Tar formation is due to the complex nature of the MSW feed. Two tar characteristics
are found with MSW gasification: (1) light hydrocarbons like toluene, styrene, naphthalene, and
(2) heaving components like phenol, biphenyl, and acenaphthylene. Operating temperature is
important to control tar formation. Feed preparation will also need to be addressed by
considered prior gasifier testing experience, MRF residue, and screening technology. Catalytic
reforming step is also an option to recover heating value from tar. Ash processing is necessary to
maintain non-hazardous ash, with potential secondary treatment. Finally, use of off-the-shelf
equipment will require innovation to minimize maintenance and achieve operability.
Location is important and a location near an MSW MRF has benefits. One option is to consider a
region without recycling (i.e., rural areas or small cities) and locate the gasification plant at the
landfill. Separation processes will be necessary to produce a gasifier feed. There is a trade-off
with resource recovery and greenhouse gas emission reduction. Another option could be to
consider a city that contracts with an MSW sorting provider. Assume metals and glass are sold
and assume food organics are sold as biofuel; these components are easy to separate out. It is
also recommended to send yard trimmings, textiles, and rubber to landfills. As a result, the
paper, wood, and plastics are assumed to be used for gasification. For plant capacity, the MSW
feed availability and the gasifier technology are important in selecting the plant capacity.

2.3.3 Plastic Supply Chain
From a MPW perspective, the current linear materials flow involves extraction, virgin plastic
manufacture, use, and discarding, resulting in low recycling rates.
MRFs sort, separate, and bale household or commercially generated recyclables. There are an
estimated 300 MRFs in the United States and they mostly receive material products with
minimal food or trimmings. MRF capacities range from 50 to over 700 tpd. They handle various
applications, including single-stream, dual-stream, mixed waste, and pre-sorted materials.
While polyethylene terephthalate (PET) (#1) and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) (#2) have
established markets, the mechanical recycling of polypropylene (PP) (#5), the most abundant
plastic, is less established but increasing over time. However, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) (#3), low-
density polyethylene (LDPE) (#4), PP (#5), polystyrene (PS) (#6), and others (#7) are typically
landfilled or incinerated for WTE. The residual MPW rate and composition vary significantly
between MRFs and locations due to differences in sorting technology, operations, process
controls, infrastructure, feed variability, contaminants (e.g., cadmium, chromium, mercury (Hg),
lead, aluminum, antimony, iron, nickel metals), and available markets. A significant portion,
around 30 percent, of MRF-fed plastic often ends up in landfills due to it not being cost-effective
to process or having low market value.
Messages from MRF representatives based on interviews state that incoming stream challenges
for MRFs are contamination and worker safety (Rep, 2020). Reducing the levels of
contamination can be aided by improving education and implementing new collection methods.
MRF processing capabilities are limited by current equipment. Substantial amounts of material
cannot be reliably processed or marketed. This includes polystyrene, shredded paper, aseptic
containers, and plastic film. Furthermore, it is important to maintain an acceptance list for items

 28

 able to be recycled. Contaminants present processing challenges for incoming materials; for
 example, food waste leads to substantial contamination, moisture is a common contaminant,
 and biological waste, such as human or animal waste, can pose safety hazards. A major
 processing challenge is facility and equipment downtime caused by contaminants.

2.3.6 Challenges & Recommendations
Several challenges impact the MPW supply chain and gasification process. These include the
diverse, limited, and evolving nature of the MPW supply chain, the varied and complex
properties of plastics (e.g., mixed polymers, additives, and contaminants), and the inherent
difficulties in gasification (e.g., high volatile release, complex feeding, and tar formation).
Limited data for gasification process design and scale-up, along with integrated system
challenges such as feed availability and consistency, process reliability, product off-take
commitments, financial viability, and competition with local funding for recycling services, also
pose significant hurdles. Drivers affecting available MPW include policy changes (e.g., banning

 30

single-use plastics), future MRF and advanced recycling scenarios, advances in sorting
technology, gasification system technology, competing processing options, and co-feeding
opportunities. These factors inform research and development priorities related to capacity,
feed, and integrated system technology.

subsystem, was determined and internal recycled water recovered from various sources, such as
water knockout from syngas or the zero liquid discharge (ZLD) system, was applied to offset the
water demand.
The difference between demand and recycling is raw water withdrawal. Raw water withdrawal
is defined as the water removed from the ground or diverted from a publicly owned treatment
works (POTW) for use in the plant for all purposes, such as the ammonia (NH3) scrubber and

 37

cooling tower makeup. In this study, raw water was assumed to be provided 50 percent by a
POTW and 50 percent from groundwater.
The difference between water withdrawal and process water discharge is defined as raw water
consumption, which is also accounted for as the portion of the raw water withdrawn that is
evaporated, transpired, incorporated into products, or otherwise not returned to the water
source from which it was withdrawn. Consumption represents the net impact of the process on
the water source.
The water balances presented in subsequent sections include the water demand of the major
water consumers within the process, the amount provided by internal recycle, the amount of
raw water withdrawal by difference, the amount of process water returned to the source, and
the raw water consumption, again by difference.

3.6.3 CO2 Capture & Storage

3.9 COST ESTIMATING METHODOLOGY
The cost estimating methodology was adapted from the methods presented in the 2024
revision of the “Cost Estimation Methodology for NETL Techno-economic Assessments” QGESS

 42

document (NETL, 2024c). All costs are reported in January-2023 dollars. Select portions are
repeated in this report.
Capital Costs:
The capital cost estimates documented in this report reflect uncertainty ranges that fall within
AACE International (AACE) Class 5 estimates, which range from -25 to +50 percent. This report
intends to represent the next commercial offering and relies on scaled vendor cost estimates for
component technologies. It also applies process contingencies at the appropriate subsystem
levels to account for expected but undefined costs, which can be a challenge for emerging
technologies.
Costs of Mature vs. Emerging Technologies:
The cost estimates for plant designs that only contain fully mature technologies and that have
been widely deployed at commercial scale reflect Nth-of-a-kind on the technology
commercialization maturity spectrum. The costs of such plants have dropped over time due to
“learning by doing” and risk reduction benefits that result from serial deployments as well as
from continuing RD&D.
However, the cost estimates for plant designs that include technologies that are not yet fully
mature (e.g., any plant with CCS) use the same cost estimating methodology as for mature plant
designs, which does not fully account for the unique cost premiums associated with the initial,
complex integrations of emerging technologies in a commercial application. Thus, it is
anticipated that early deployments of these plants may incur costs higher than those reflected
within this report.
Other Factors:
Actual reported project costs for this plant type are also expected to deviate from the cost
estimates in this report due to project- and site-specific considerations (e.g., contracting
strategy, local labor costs, seismic conditions, water quality, financing parameters, local
environmental concerns, weather delays) that may make construction more costly. Such
variations are not captured by the reported cost uncertainty.
Future Cost Trends:
Continuing RD&D is expected to result in designs that are more advanced than those assessed
by this report, leading to costs that are lower than those estimated here.

3.9.1 Capital Costs
As illustrated in Exhibit 3-13, there are five levels of capital costs to consider in constructing a
plant.
Bare Erected Cost (BEC) comprises the cost of process equipment, on-site facilities, and
infrastructure that support the plant (e.g., shops, offices, labs, road), and the direct and indirect
labor required for its construction or installation.
Engineering, Procurement, and Construction Cost (EPCC) comprises the BEC plus the cost of
services provided by engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) contractor. The EPC

 43

services include detailed design, contractor permitting (i.e., those permits that individual
contractors must obtain to perform their scopes of work, as opposed to project permitting,
which is not included here), and project/construction management costs.
Total Plant Cost (TPC) comprises the EPCC cost plus project and process contingencies.
Total Overnight Cost (TOC) comprises the TPC plus all other “overnight” costs, including owner’s
costs. TOC is an overnight cost, expressed in base-year dollars and as such does not include
escalation during construction or construction financing costs.
Total As-Spent Cost (TASC) comprises the sum of all capital expenditures as they are incurred
during the capital expenditure period for construction including their escalation. TASC also
includes interest during construction, comprised of interest on debt and a return on equity.
BEC, EPCC, TPC, and TOC are overnight costs and are expressed in base-year dollars. The base
year is the year in which all technology costs are expressed for the comparison of technologies
from a standard starting point. TASC is expressed in mixed, current-year dollars over the entire
capital expenditure period, which is assumed in this study to last five years for gasification-
based plants.

The three study cases analyzed included biomass gasification (Case 1), MSW gasification (Case

4.1 CASE 1 – BIOMASS GASIFICATION
The plant configuration for Case 1 is represented by the block flow diagram provided in Exhibit
4-1 with the associated stream table provided in Exhibit 4-2.
The as-received torrefied biomass (Stream 1) is mixed with slurry water recovered from the ZLD
system (Stream 2) plus makeup water (Stream 3) and fed to the gasifier as a slurry. An air stream
(Stream 4) enters the elevated-pressure air separation unit (ASU) to produce a high-purity
oxygen stream for the gasifier (Stream 5), with the remaining components of air vented to the
stack (Stream 6). Slag (Stream 7) is formed in the entrained flow gasifier system. The hot, raw
syngas (Stream 8) then enters a section with quench cooling, cyclones, and filters. The quench
water comprises water recovered from the ZLD system (Stream 9) plus makeup water (Stream
10). The cooled syngas (Stream 11) then goes through water gas shift. The shifted syngas
(Stream 12) goes through the ammonia scrubber and additional low-temperature heat recovery
(LTHR) stages. Process water (Stream 13) is added for ammonia scrubbing, and the effluent
water (Stream 14) is recycled to the ZLD system while sour water stripper off gas (Stream 15) is
sent to the gas-fired boiler. The ammonia-free syngas (Stream 16) goes through mercury
removal then the stream (Stream 17) is sent through a ZnO bed sulfur removal step, which
removes a large portion of the sulfur in the syngas. The resulting syngas (Stream 18) then goes
through the ADIP-Ultra pre-combustion CO2 capture system, which removes 99 percent of CO2
in the syngas and consumes makeup water (Stream 19). The ADIP-Ultra system produces low-
pressure CO2 (Stream 20) and high-pressure CO2 (Stream 21) which are compressed in multiple
stages. A CO2 dryer utilizing triethylene glycol removes moisture from the partially compressed
CO2 stream (Stream 22), and the water is vented (Stream 23) to the stack. The dried CO2 stream
(Stream 24) is further compressed to liquid CO2 pipeline conditions, resulting in a pipeline-ready
CO2 product (Stream 25). The clean syngas (Stream 26) is then sent to the pressure swing
adsorption (PSA) unit for further purification. The PSA recovers 80 percent of hydrogen in the

 51

stream (Stream 27) and then compresses the product to pipeline-ready conditions (Stream 28).
The off-gas stream (Stream 29) is mixed with the sour gas from the ammonia scrubber system
and pre-heated (Stream 30). Air is added (Stream 31) and the mixture is fired in the gas-fired
boiler. Heat is recovered from the hot gas stream to satisfy internal heat needs of the plant as
well as to raise steam (Stream 32) which is used internally, for example, in the ADIP-Ultra CO2
capture system and to produce power via steam turbine. The resulting flue gas is mixed with
other vent streams and sent to the stack (Stream 33).

4.2 CASE 2 – MSW GASIFICATION
The plant configuration for Case 2 is represented by the block flow diagram provided in Exhibit
4-14 with the associated stream table provided in Exhibit 4-15.
The as-received, sorted, and pelletized MSW (Stream 1) is mixed with steam (Stream 2) and fed
to the fluidized bed gasifier. An air stream (Stream 3) enters the elevated-pressure ASU to
produce a high-purity oxygen stream for the gasifier (Stream 4), with the remaining components
of air vented to the stack (Stream 5). Ash (Stream 6) is formed in the fluidized bed gasifier
system. The hot, raw syngas (Stream 7) then enters a section with quench cooling, cyclones, and
filters. The quench water comprises water recovered from the ZLD system (Stream 8). The
cooled syngas (Stream 9) then goes through the HCl scrubber, which uses water from the ZLD
system (Stream 10) mixed with a solution of 50 wt% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (Stream 11) to
neutralize HCl in the syngas. The resulting water effluent stream from the scrubber (Stream 12)
is recycled to the ZLD system and crystallizer. The scrubbed syngas (Stream 13) then goes
through water gas shift. The shifted syngas (Stream 14) goes through the ammonia scrubber
and additional LTHR stages. Process water (Stream 15) is added for ammonia scrubbing, and the
effluent water (Stream 16) is recycled to the ZLD system while sour water stripper off-gas
(Stream 17) is sent to the gas-fired boiler. The ammonia-free syngas (Stream 18) goes through
mercury removal then the stream (Stream 19) is sent through a ZnO bed sulfur removal step,
which removes a large portion of the sulfur in the syngas. The resulting syngas (Stream 20) then
goes through the ADIP-Ultra pre-combustion CO2 capture system, which removes 99 percent of
CO2 in the syngas and consumes makeup water (Stream 21). The ADIP-Ultra system produces
low-pressure CO2 (Stream 22) and high-pressure CO2 (Stream 23), which are compressed in
multiple stages. A CO2 dryer utilizing triethylene glycol removes moisture from the partially
compressed CO2 stream (Stream 24), and the water is vented (Stream 25) to the stack. The dried
CO2 stream (Stream 26) is further compressed to liquid CO2 pipeline conditions, resulting in a
pipeline-ready CO2 product (Stream 27). The clean syngas (Stream 28) is then sent to the PSA
unit for further purification. The PSA recovers 80 percent of hydrogen in the stream (Stream 29)
and then compresses the product to pipeline-ready conditions (Stream 30). The off-gas stream
(Stream 31) is mixed with the sour gas from the ammonia scrubber system and pre-heated
(Stream 32). Air is added (Stream 33) and the mixture is fired in the gas-fired boiler. Heat is

 69

recovered from the hot gas stream to satisfy internal heat needs of the plant as well as to raise
steam (Stream 34) which is used internally, for example, in the ADIP-Ultra CO2 capture system
and to produce power via steam turbine. The resulting flue gas is mixed with other vent streams
and sent to the stack (Stream 35). The ZLD system and crystallizer utilizes sulfuric acid (H2SO4)
acid at 99 wt% (Stream 36) to neutralize any excess NaOH and water is removed to form a salt
waste stream.

4.2.2 Cost Estimating
The cost estimating methodology was described in Section 3.9. Exhibit 4-23 shows a detailed
breakdown of the capital costs for Case 2. Note that process descriptions for items 4.9 and 4.10
were taken from the NREL report serving as the basis for the cost estimate. The report states
that the circulating fluid bed gasifier model includes a gasifier reactor, a char combustion
reactor, and a syngas reformer reactor (Worley & Yale, 2012). The purpose of the char
combustion reactor (Item 4.9) is to burn solid, carbon-rich residue left after initial fuel
conversion and to provide high heat needed for the endothermic gasification reactions. The
syngas reformer reactor (Item 4.10) is designed to convert hydrocarbon molecules in syngas to

4.3 CASE 3 – MSW+MPW GASIFICATION
The plant configuration for Case 3 is represented by the block flow diagram provided in Exhibit
4-27 with the associated stream table provided in Exhibit 4-28.
The as-received, sorted, and pelletized MPW and MSW (Stream 1) is mixed with steam (Stream
2) and fed to the fluidized bed gasifier. An air stream (Stream 3) enters the elevated-pressure
ASU to produce a high-purity oxygen stream for the gasifier (Stream 4), with the remaining
components of air vented to the stack (Stream 5). Ash (Stream 6) is formed in the fluidized bed
gasifier system. The hot, raw syngas (Stream 7) then enters a section with quench cooling,
cyclones, and filters. The quench water comprises water recovered from the ZLD system (Stream
8). The cooled syngas (Stream 9) then goes through the HCl scrubber, which uses water from the
ZLD system (Stream 10) mixed with a solution of 50 wt% NaOH (Stream 11) to neutralize HCl in
the syngas. The resulting water effluent stream from the scrubber (Stream 12) is recycled to the
ZLD system and crystallizer. The scrubbed syngas (Stream 13) then goes through water gas shift.
The shifted syngas (Stream 14) goes through the ammonia scrubber and additional LTHR stages.
Process water (Stream 15) is added for ammonia scrubbing, and the effluent water (Stream 16)
is recycled to the ZLD system while sour water stripper off-gas (Stream 17) is sent to the gas-
fired boiler. The ammonia-free syngas (Stream 18) goes through mercury removal then the
stream (Stream 19) is sent through a ZnO bed sulfur removal step, which removes a large
portion of the sulfur in the syngas. The resulting syngas (Stream 20) then goes through the
ADIP-Ultra pre-combustion CO2 capture system, which removes 99 percent of CO2 in the syngas
and consumes makeup water (Stream 21). The ADIP-Ultra system produces low-pressure CO2
(Stream 22) and high-pressure CO2 (Stream 23), which are compressed in multiple stages. A CO2
dryer utilizing triethylene glycol removes moisture from the partially compressed CO2 stream
(Stream 24), and the water is vented (Stream 25) to the stack. The dried CO2 stream (Stream 26)
is further compressed to liquid CO2 pipeline conditions, resulting in a pipeline-ready CO2
product (Stream 27). The clean syngas (Stream 28) is then sent to the PSA unit for further
purification. The PSA recovers 80 percent of hydrogen in the stream (Stream 29) and then
compresses the product to pipeline-ready conditions (Stream 30). The off-gas stream (Stream
31) is mixed with the sour gas from the ammonia scrubber system and pre-heated (Stream 32).
Air is added (Stream 33) and the mixture is fired in the gas-fired boiler. Heat is recovered from

 86

the hot gas stream to satisfy internal heat needs of the plant as well as to raise steam (Stream
34), which is used internally, for example, in the ADIP-Ultra CO2 capture system and to produce
power via steam turbine. The resulting flue gas is mixed with other vent streams and sent to the
stack (Stream 35). The ZLD system and crystallizer utilizes H2SO4 acid at 99 wt% (Stream 36) to
neutralize any excess NaOH and water is removed to form a salt waste stream.

4.3.2 Cost Estimating
The cost estimating methodology was described in Section 3.9. Exhibit 4-36 shows a detailed
breakdown of the capital costs for Case 3. Note that process descriptions for items 4.9 and 4.10
were taken from the NREL report serving as the basis for the cost estimate. The report states
that the circulating fluid bed gasifier model includes a gasifier reactor, a char combustion
reactor, and a syngas reformer reactor (Worley & Yale, 2012). The purpose of the char
combustion reactor (Item 4.9) is to burn solid, carbon-rich residue left after initial fuel
conversion and to provide high heat needed for the endothermic gasification reactions. The
syngas reformer reactor (Item 4.10) is designed to convert hydrocarbon molecules in syngas to

5.1 PERFORMANCE RESULTS
Exhibit 5-1 shows the auxiliary load breakdown by equipment for the three study cases. Overall,
the cases have a similar auxiliary load, when normalized to hydrogen production. For all cases,
the largest auxiliary load is associated with the ASU, ranging 3.3 to 3.7 kWh/kg H2. For MSW and
MSW+MPW gasification, the normalized feedstock pre-processing auxiliary load (0.6 and 0.7
kWh/kg H2) is significantly larger than the biomass gasification feedstock pre-processing
auxiliary load (0.1 kWh/kg H2).

 Exhibit 5-1. Auxiliary load by equipment

 104

Exhibit 5-2 shows a graph of the CGE and ETE for the three study cases. MSW+MPW gasification
has the highest CGE (61.3 percent) due to having a larger hydrogen content in the feedstock to
begin with. Biomass gasification has the lowest CGE (58.8 percent) due to having a lower
hydrogen content in the feedstock as well as having a higher relative feedstock heating value,
which increases the energy input of the system and relatively reduces the efficiency. The highest
effective efficiency is biomass gasification (56.3 percent) and the lowest ETE is MSW gasification
(54.4 percent), likely due to the relative amount of auxiliary power needed for these cases.

 Exhibit 5-2. Cold gas efficiency and effective thermal efficiency

The water use for the three study cases is shown in Exhibit 5-3, normalized to H2 production.
Most of the water consumption is attributed to the cooling tower. The case with the smallest
raw water consumption is MSW gasification with a value of 30.3 L/kg H2 and the case with the
largest raw water consumption is biomass gasification with a value of 38.4 L/kg H2. This is likely
due to the biomass gasification case utilizing a slurry-fed gasifier system, which increases water
consumption.

 Exhibit 5-3. Water use

Exhibit 5-4 shows a summary of pollutant emissions for the three study cases. NOx emissions
are limited by the use of low-NOx burners in the design. Sources of NOx emissions include the
combustion of fuel gas components with air. Because NOx emissions are determined by
assuming a target NOx emissions rate of 2.5 ppmv at 15 mol% O2; therefore, NOx emissions are
proportional to the flue gas flow rate. The NOx emissions are the lowest in the biomass
gasification at 0.67 kg/1,000 kg H2. The production of PM is a result of the presence of ash in
the feedstocks and a function of particulate capture systems. The PM emissions are similar
among the cases at around 0.69–0.72 kg/1,000 kg H2.
SO2 emissions are varied across the three cases, due to the variation of the amount of sulfur in
the feedstock with a constant removal rate by the ZnO sulfur guard bed. Biomass gasification
shows the lowest SO2 emissions at 0.03 kg/1,000 kg H2 due to biomass having the lowest sulfur
content of all feedstocks. MSW gasification shows the highest SO2 emissions at 0.93 kg/1,000 kg
H2 due to the MSW feedstock having the most sulfur and producing the least hydrogen.

5.2 COST RESULTS
Exhibit 5-5 shows the breakdown of the BEC by process area for a) biomass entrained flow
gasification and b) MSW fluidized bed gasification. The largest contributor to the BEC for
biomass gasification is the gasifier and accessories section ($277 MM, 52 percent). The largest
contributor to the BEC for the MSW and MSW+MPW gasification is the other category ($52–56
MM, 31–33 percent), which includes buildings and structures, instrumentation and control, and
accessory electric plant. The explanation for this may be due to the large difference between
the reference cost scale and the study case scale as well as the low exponents suggested by the
cost scaling QGESS for these cost accounts.

Exhibit 5-6 shows the TOC breakdown and TASC, normalized by hydrogen production rate. The
TOC is made up of the BEC (56 percent), home office expenses (8–9 percent), project
contingency (12–14 percent), process contingency (3–5 percent), and owner’s costs (18–19
percent). The lowest TOC and TASC is seen in biomass gasification, at $10,942/(kg H2/day) and
$13,996/(kg H2/day), respectively, due to economies of scale benefits in this case. The highest
TOC and TASC is seen in MSW gasification, at $23,129/(kg H2/day) and $29,584/(kg H2/day),

Exhibit 5-7 shows the breakdown of the LCOH for the three study cases. The error bars depicted
represent the uncertainty range for AACE Class 5 estimates, which are -25 to +50 percent of
capital costs. As expected, the lowest LCOH is seen in biomass gasification case at $9.72/kg H2,
which has the largest scale of all cases and benefits from economies of scale, despite including a
cost for the feedstock. The highest LCOH is seen in the MSW gasification case at $19.23/kg H2,
which has the smallest scale of all cases and suffers from lack of economies of scale. For all
cases, the largest portion of the LCOH is attributed to capital costs, ranging 45–49 percent of the
total. The second largest portion of the LCOH is fuel costs for biomass, at 21 percent, and fixed
O&M costs for MSW and MSW+MPW gasification at 21–22 percent. The CO2 T&S cost is also an
important cost contributor for especially the MSW and MSW+MPW gasification cases, as CO2
T&S costs are about 3.5 and 5 times larger than the biomass case for the MSW and MSW+MPW
gasification cases, respectively. This shows the important of CO2 pipeline networks to avoid the
high costs of dedicated pipelines for small flow rates. For comparison with the three cases
explored in this study, cost results for two coal gasification cases, without and with CCS, are
provided from a previous NETL hydrogen study (Lewis, et al., 2022). The LCOH for coal
gasification without CCS, in 2023$, is estimated to be $4.23/kg H2 and with CCS is estimated to
be $5.35/kg H2.

5.3 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
Sensitivity analyses were conducted to understand how changes in key parameters impact the
LCOH for the three study cases. Sensitivity analyses allow the understanding of key cost drivers
and suggest focus areas for RD&D to lower costs. In addition to the three cases explored in this
study, two coal gasification cases, without and with CCS, taken from a previous NETL hydrogen
study are included on the sensitivity plots to provide comparison and context (Lewis, et al.,
2022). Although the original NETL hydrogen study is reported in 2018$, the cost data has been
updated to the 2023$ basis of this report.
Exhibit 5-8 shows the sensitivity of the LCOH to changes in the CF. Note that changes in CF are
assumed to occur with no impact on the design or cost of the gasification plant. The base case
CF for all three cases is 80 percent. Increases in CF have little impact on the LCOH. However,
decreases in CF cause significant increases in the LCOH in a non-linear manner, with the MSW
gasification case surpassing $25/kg H2 at 50 percent CF.

 Exhibit 5-8. Sensitivity of LCOH to capacity factor

 111

Exhibit 5-9 shows the sensitivity of the LCOH to changes in the biomass feedstock price. The
base case biomass feedstock price is $181/ton. The LCOH and biomass price have a linear

6 CONCLUSION
The TEA conducted on gasification-based processes determines key performance and cost
metrics including ETE (%) and LCOH ($/kg H2).
Performance design basis assumptions were defined for use in model development. The site
location was selected to be a generic U.S. southwestern location in East Texas, H2 product purity
defined at 99.9 vol%, and a plant capacity of 1,000 tpd, 200 tpd, and 240 tpd chosen for

 116

biomass, MSW, and MSW+MPW cases, respectively. The capital costs were determined to fall
within AACE Class 5 estimates, which carry an error of -25 to +50 percent. Feedstock and
product prices were assumed to be $181/ton for biomass feedstock, $0/ton for MSW or
MSW+MPW feedstocks, $53.2/MWh for grid electricity, and $23.3–126.5/tonne for CO2 T&S.
The results of the TEA showed that the ETE ranged 54.4–56.3 percent. The plants imported 6.0
MW, 1.9 MW, and 2.6 MW of electricity from the grid, respectively for biomass, MSW, and
MSW+MPW gasification cases. The LCOH results were $9.72/kg H2 for biomass, $19.23/kg H2 for
MSW, and $15.32/kg H2 for MSW+MPW gasification cases. The capital cost represents 47–49
percent of the total cost.

6.1 FUTURE WORK
The findings of this study can provide a benchmark for future RD&D efforts aimed at improving
performance, lowering costs, and reducing emissions. Future work should focus on reducing the
high capital and operating costs identified in these case studies, as they currently pose a
significant barrier to commercial adoption.
A key finding is that economies of scale have a substantial impact on final hydrogen costs.
Specifically, the limited feedstock availability in rural areas restrict plant capacity for MSW and
MPW gasification, increasing per-unit costs. Relocating plants to regions with higher feedstock
availability, such as the East Coast, or co-feeding with coal could increase plant scale and reduce
capital expenditures. The co-feeding of coal can also offer operational benefits, such as reduced
tar formation.
Another cost-reduction strategy involves removing the carbon capture system, which would
decrease capital expenditures, variable O&M costs, utility demands like steam and electricity,
and the costs associated with CO2 T&S. While this modification alone may not render the
processes fully cost-competitive, it is expected to provide a moderate cost reduction with a
tradeoff of higher stack CO2 emissions.
Additionally, this study’s focus on hydrogen production can be expanded to explore the
production of other valuable products from gasification. This includes syngas, electricity,
combined heat and power, synthetic fuels, or chemicals. These alternative products could offer
different economic advantages and market opportunities compared to a hydrogen product.
