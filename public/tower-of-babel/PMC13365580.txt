Experimental and numerical investigation on basaltic soil under RC square footings
Authors: Zaid S Alajlan, Sabry Fayed, EL-Said A Bayoumi, Saad A Yehia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe accurate ability to predict the distribution of contact stress under reinforced concrete (RC) footings is important for the safety and serviceability of shallow foundations. Conventional analytical models idealizing the footing as rigid and soil as homogenous fail to capture the stress concentration and redistribution effects, especially under non-uniform loading. Earlier studies are mostly concentrated on sand; however, basalt soil has different mechanical characteristics as it possesses high stiffness, angularity, and interlocking effects. It also ignores stiffness loss due to concrete cracking. This study aims to fill these gaps through an experimental and numerical investigation of RC square footings resting on basaltic soil and the influence of the reinforcement ratio, yield strength of steel and strength of concrete. Within the laboratory conditions, four footings having different reinforcement ratios of 0.19%, 0.36%, 0.54% and 3.43% were tested under monotonic loading. Central and edge displacements were measured. Using a validated finite element model, a parametric study expanded the investigation to include reinforcement ratios of 0.54% to 4.80%, steel yield stresses of 240 MPa to 450 MPa and concrete compressive strengths of 20 MPa to 60 MPa, allowing systematic consideration of these parameters on central contact stress, ultimate load, deformation and energy absorption. The findings revealed that enhancing the reinforcement ratio from 0.54% to 4.80% resulted in an increase of 73.4% in central contact stress, 34.1% in ultimate load, and 55% in energy absorption, respectively. Increase in steel yield stress from 240 MPa to 450 MPa caused a 25.2% increase in central contact stress, 13% in ultimate load and 3.74% in energy absorption in laminated composite panel. The increase of concrete compressive strength from 20 MPa to 60 MPa increased central contact stress by 117.4% ultimate load by 70.4% and energy absorption by 270% showing this factor as dominant. These results show that the performance of footing on stiff basaltic soil mainly depends on the concrete strength and amount of reinforcement whereas careful use of steel yield stress. The insights provided by the study are critical for practical design. Furthermore, non-uniform contact stresses, stiffness degradation, and soil–structure interaction need to be accounted for optimizing strength and ductility.Keywords: RC footings, Contact stress distribution, Basaltic soil, Reinforcement ratio, Soil–structure interaction, Finite element analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

One of the most popular foundation mechanisms in typical civil engineering work is isolated foundations, where the intricate relationship between the supporting soil medium and the reinforced concrete footing controls structural safety and functionality. The main method for moving stresses from the superstructure to the earth is through shallow foundations, which also regulate bearing ability and settlement tendency1. As a result, a key issue in geotechnical and foundation engineering has long been the precise description of contacting distribution of stress and deformation under isolated concrete footings.

Boussinesq created the first analytical approaches for footing performance, which Borowicka later improved. Borowicka idealized the footing-soil relationship as a stiff foundation supported by an elastic, homogenous, semi-infinite medium. Although these classical formulations offered important theoretical insights, they were eventually demonstrated to be at odds with practical findings because they projected unreasonably large stress concentrations close to footing edges under middle loads2,3. These differences prompted in-depth laboratory studies to measure contact stresses directly under stiff footings on coarse soils.

The distribution of contact stress under footings on sand is parabolic or bell-shaped, with greatest stress occurring close to the footing center and decreasing toward the margins, according to a large number of experimental investigations4. Subsequent experimental research, however, questioned this presumption. Murzenko4 illustrated the significant impact of soil nonlinearity and footing stiffness on stress redistribution processes by showing that contact stress distribution may instead display a saddle-shaped profile, marked by decreased or almost zero stresses at the footing margins. Despite these developments, the majority of earlier studies gave little thought to the behavior of reinforced concrete (RC) and instead concentrated on stiff or idealized footings. In real-world design, RC footings gradually fracture under increasing loads, causing stiffness deterioration that has a direct impact on soil-structure interaction. However, existing design methods typically ignore the change of footing stiffness during cracking and assume homogeneous or simplified contact stress distributions. Furthermore, despite the gradual mobilization of shear strength with increasing load, soil reaction is frequently seen as constant.

Significant differences between observed contact stress distributions and conventional elastic assumptions were found in the latest experimental work by Fouda et al.5 on full-scale square footings lying on sand. The soil–footing interaction was simulated using finite element modeling, however the impact of stiffness degradation on stress redistribution and failure development was not thoroughly examined. The widely accepted assumption of uniform contact stress is more representative of flexible foundation behavior, as Yamin’s complementary numerical studies6 added that rigid circular foundation on sandy foundations display non-uniform, saddle-like contact stresses at final ability. More recently, Fouda et al.7 showed that cracking-induced stiffness degradation and soil characteristics both influence contact stress below reinforced concrete foundations. Their nonlinear finite element simulations demonstrated that while higher reinforcement ratios improve ultimate load capacity and dramatically change stress distribution patterns, decreased footing stiffness results in lower edge-to-center stress ratios. These results demonstrate the need for more experimental confirmation and blatantly defy accepted theoretical presumptions.

These findings also demonstrate the wider importance of soil reinforcing methods in enhancing soil–structure interaction. Investigations of footing behavior under complex loading are complemented by experiments on soybean urease-induced carbonate precipitation (SICP)-stabilized anchors, which provide light on how soil alteration might improve load transmission, interface strength, and stiffness. For example, soybean urease-induced carbonate precipitation (SICP)-stabilized anchors were studied by Zhang et al.8, who demonstrated that SICP improves soil-anchor bonding. The best cementation (~ 1.75 mol/L) improves cohesion, shear strength, stiffness, and forms a homogenous CaCO3 interfacial layer. Direct shear and interface tests show strain-hardening in soil and strain-softening at the interface. In a different research, Chen and Zhu9 created a member-size pullout test to mimic anchor construction and ground pressure in order to explore the behavior of the soil–anchor interface. Interface shear strength is mostly independent of ground pressure, according to experiments and a response-surface-derived soil density simulation. Boundary impacts are reduced if specimen-to-anchor diameter proportions are greater than 5–6.7.

Yet, a significant drawback of current research is that it mostly focuses on sand as the underlying soil. Because of their strong particle angularity, high stiffness, and improved interlocking actions, basaltic soils have different mechanical properties from regular sands. Shear strength, stiffness, and dilatancy response are all known to be governed by particle shape. For instance, Cho et al.10 showed that enhanced particle angularity considerably enhances inter-particle interlocking and shear strength. Santamarina and Cho11 highlighted the crucial importance of particle shape in regulating the stiffness and compressibility of granular soils.

Particle form significantly influences small-strain rigidity and damage, with irregular particles promoting force chain generation and postponing stiffness breakdown during loading, according to more recent research12–14. Furthermore, there is still a dearth of experimental information on reinforced concrete footings on basalt soil, especially with regard to failure processes and the development of contact stresses beneath the footing. In order to close this gap, the failure load and contact stress distribution beneath reinforced concrete square footings on basalt soil are investigated experimentally and numerically in this work. It offers proof of how basalt soil affects load-carrying capacity, failure modes, and stress redistribution, providing insights not seen in sand-based research or traditional theoretical frameworks.

Practical program

Materials

For the experimental study, the footing models were cast from normal-weight concrete with a design compressive of 25 MPa. The cement used was 42 MPa of strength, river sand, crushed basalt aggregate of 12 mm maximum size (all in accordance with the specification), and mixing water (free from chloride) of final setting time. For every cubic meter of batching plant concrete, 300 kg of cement, 150 kg of water, 600 kg of sand, and 1300 kg of basalt was used. The mechanical tests for standard cubes were carried out at 28 days, which resulted in 26 MPa and 2.3 MPa respectively compressive and tensile strengths. As part of the experimental program, three footing samples are reinforced with 4 mm diameter steel bars. The mechanical behavior was evaluated using tensile tests displaying a tensile strength of 360 MPa; elastic modulus of 200GPa; and elongation of 18%. In comparison, one footing sample of footings contained steel bars of 16 mm-diameter. The tensile strength, elastic modulus and elongation of these bars are 550 MPa, 200GPa and 11% respectively. The base soil employed in this research is a well-graded basaltic soil possessing particle sizes ranging from 0.4 to 12 mm, an internal friction angle (ϕ) of 40°, and a dilatancy angle (ψ) of 20°15.

Four reinforced concrete (RC) footings were fabricated in the experimental work with the same designed mix in order to achieve similar consistency in the material properties of each specimen. The dimensions of each footing were 400 mm in width and length, whereas, the height was 100 mm providing a compact geometry for the bending tests (Table 1). To analyze flexural behavior, the application of reinforcement was done exclusively on tension face of footings. As illustrated in Fig. 1. The steel for footing F36 was designed using ten 4 mm bars per direction (10D4mm), giving a ratio of 0.36% with a total steel weight of 360 g. Test setup

The loading test was executed with the use of a hydraulic jack that was oriented vertically and had a capacity of 300 tons (Fig. 3). A load cell is placed on hydraulic jack which means having a rigid frame reaction steel jack which means stable loading and calibrated. Soil foundation interaction was simulated in heavy steel tank of plan dimensions 80 × 80 cm and height 70 cm. The tank walls were designed thick enough to avoid lateral deformation and preserve boundary integrity when loading. The well-graded basalt soil used in the tank had particle size ranging from 0.4 to 12 mm. To ensure that the soil is in a loose condition by which uniformity of initial conditions is maintained in all tests, the soil was removed completely after the test. The specimen of the footing was placed in the tank’s geometric centre just below the piston for the symmetric transfer of loads. To symbolize the column footprint and ensure an even application of load, a rigid steel plate measuring 10 × 10 cm was placed at the centre of the footing area.

Test set-up: (a) Experimental test set-up, (b) Schematic details of test set-up.

Before the major loading phase, a small seating load was applied incrementally so that the loading plate, footing and soil were in full contact with each other. In other words, the seating load was applied so that the soil was allowed to loosen up a bit. The settlement in vertical direction was measured during the test using the use of the displacement gauges at the centre and edge of the footing. This load was applied in monotonic manner at a constant loading rate of 1 kN/s until the desired loading condition was reached after instrumentation.

Numerical modeling strategy

The finite element analyses were performed using ABAQUS v.2021 software to simulate the behavior of the concrete footing in contact with the underlying sand soil. The ABAQUS/Explicit solver was employed to represent the nonlinear tensile response of concrete, particularly during crack initiation and propagation. Validation of the numerical model was achieved by comparing the simulated results with experimental measurements, which revealed a strong correlation in terms of ultimate load capacity and average settlement behavior. The robustness of the numerical results is influenced by several modeling parameters, including mesh discretization, selection of material constitutive laws, definition of the concrete–soil interaction, and the imposed boundary conditions.

Geometry description and contact interaction modeling

The FE model was developed incorporating the complete experimental setup that is soil, concrete footing, columns, and reinforcing steel, with proper assignment of material. Any comprehensive modeling details, such as geometric configuration, element formulations, interaction properties and boundary conditions were defined. Concrete footing was constructed from the same dimensions obtained from experimental testing. Soil and concrete footing were modeled by eight-node linear brick elements with reduced integration having (C3D8R) and four-node surface element having (R3D4) was used to simulate the columns as rigid bodies. The reinforcement bars were modelled as two-node truss elements (T3D2), which have three translational degrees of freedom at each node. (Fig. 4)16–19.

Element types of the tested specimens.

The footing was assigned with a refined mesh (20 mm), while the surrounding soil mesh was of a coarser type (40 mm) as shown in Fig. 5, to sort out accuracy and speed of computation. The contact from one surface to the other one is surface to surface and hard contact defined in the normal direction while friction is penalty based in the tangential direction. The normal behaviour obeyed the default pressure–overclosure formulation which prevents tensile stress transfer thus excessive penetration. Frictional resistance due to tangential interaction was considered on the assumption that a coefficient of friction of 0.40 was applicable to the concrete–soil contact. Using the embedded element technique, the concrete was modeled as the host region, while the embedded elements were the reinforcement bars, to capture concrete-steel interaction. This mechanism ensures complete kinematic compatibility of the materials and allows for efficient transfer of forces without any extra contact.

Additionally, loading was introduced through a displacement-controlled scheme applied at a reference point coupled to the top steel plate, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The soil base was fully restrained, and lateral displacements along the soil boundaries were prevented. The analysis was performed using a dynamic explicit solver, which is well suited for nonlinear problems involving concrete cracking and the resulting stiffness reduction20.

In compression, the concrete behaves elastically, then strain hardens and at the end, softens beyond ultimate strength. Steel reinforcement

For the steel reinforcement, a bilinear stress-strain relationship, as recommended by Kachlakev40, was employed, capturing the initial linear elastic behavior up to the yield stress, followed by a plastic region with reduced stiffness extending to the ultimate stress, thereby representing both the elastic and post-yield response of the steel bars.

In ABAQUS, soil was modeled using Drucker-Prager constitutive model. Failure modes

The test specimens and numerical models showed a flexural failure mode due to yielding of the main bottom longitudinal reinforcement in the tension zone (Fig. 7). In the tests, the first flexural cracks occurred on the sides of all the footings. After the test was conducted, the soffits of footings were inspected and recorded. All specimens exhibited a similar failure mechanism associated with flexure. Throughout the test, the initial vertical flexural cracking took place around 70% and 80% of the ultimate load. This crack initiated from the bottom surface only and then propagated up waning the loading at four sides of the footing. In some instances, crack visibility was observed on the concrete surface, as noted before. To improve clarity in the figures presented (e.g., Fig. 7(d)), after testing, a marker was used to trace selected cracks. This technique was purely for presentation; it did not change the latent crack patterns or their interpretation. The numerical model was validated in detail through an extensive comparison of crack patterns the experimenters observed with the damage contours predicted in the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) model in ABAQUS. The results of the numerical investigations were found to be in good agreement with experiments with respect to the coordinates of initiation and the path followed by cracks. In both methods, flexural cracking developed at the bottom surface close to the edges of the footing, which then propagates upward towards the center of loading. The tensile damage contours (DAMAGET) calculated from the FE model were comparable to the experimentally observed cracks in terms of distribution. This satisfactory match confirmed that the FE model was able to capture the stiffness reduction induced by cracking.

Effect of reinforcement ratio (Group I)

The Group GI experiment was created to study the influence of bottom reinforcement ratio. The effect is on the contact stress distribution, ultimate load carrying capacity, deformation behavior, and energy absorption of reinforced concrete square footings resting on basaltic soil. Five footings (F54, F120, F214, F335 and F480) were studied which have same plan dimensions (400 × 400 mm), thickness (100 mm), compressive strength of concrete (20 MPa), and yield stress of steel (240 MPa). Group GI comprised of specimens having reinforcement ratios of 0.54% (baseline, F54) to 4.80% (F480). To represent lightly reinforced footings, a lower-bound value of 0.54% has been taken as a reference. This allowed for an easy understanding of the effect of reinforcement on contact pressure, ultimate load, and energy absorption. The value is somewhat lower than typical practical reinforcement ratios, the parametric study, however, extends to higher ratios up to 4.80%. This covers the range often used in foundation design. This way, the findings will be relevant in practice and the influence of reinforcement on stiffness and cracking behavior will be established for a wide range of levels.

Central contact stress at failure

Experimental results showed a significant rise in central contact stress at the failure load with an increase in reinforcement ratio (Fig. 8). In this study, the current contact stress is defined as the average normal contact stress over a finite region around the geometric center of the footing-soil interface, which corresponds approximately to the area of a single finite element at the contact surface. Stress values at ultimate load step from all integration points in this region are averaged out to yield a good representative measure, which is also mesh independent. The reference footing F54 had a central contact stress of 276.4 kPa and footings F120, F214, F335, and F480 increased by 33%, 48.5%, 60.3%, and 73.4%, respectively (Table 4). This phenomenon can be ascribed to an increase in flexural stiffness caused by the greater reinforcement ratio. As the reinforcement content is increased, the onset and development of flexural cracking is delayed, which reduces the stiffness degradation and retains a relatively stiff response at higher loads. The footings subjected to particularly high loads tend to drop a greater portion of the load directly on soil at the footing center. Thus, it leads to a great deal of stress in that area. Further insight into this behavior can be gleaned from the two-dimensional contact stress contours (Fig. 9), that give the distribution of these stresses over the entire footing base. When the loads are lower, the stress is evenly distributed because the footing was inherently stiff and uncracked in the beginning. Ultimate load capacity

The ultimate load capacity increased progressively with the increasing of reinforcement ratio (Fig. 10). The ultimate load increased from 143.1kN for F54 to 169.6kN (+ 18.5%) for F120 and 187.95kN (+ 31.3%) for F214. Beyond a reinforcement ratio of approximately 2.14%, the rate of increase in ultimate load diminished, with footings F335 and F480 showing marginal gains of 33.7% and 34.1%, respectively (Table 4). The failure mechanism that governs is transforming in this manner. At small ratios of reinforcement, failure is governed by yielding of steel and rapid degradation of stiffness and, thus, reinforcement plays the dominating role in increasing load carrying capacity. Because of the increases in reinforcement ratios, the stiffness of the footing is sufficiently increased so that the failure mechanism becomes more structural capacity dominated rather than that of the soil bearing resistance Consequently, continued increments of reinforcement lead to diminishing returns with respect to ultimate load.

P-δ curves of the Group GI footings.

Deformation response

The ultimate deflection exhibited a moderate increase with reinforcement ratio, rising from 4.5 mm for F54 and F120 to 5.0 mm for F214 and F335, and reaching 5.5 mm for F480 (Table 4). This increase reflects the combined effect of higher load levels and delayed cracking, which allows the footing to sustain larger deformations before failure. Unlike lightly reinforced footings, highly reinforced specimens maintain their integrity over a broader load range, enabling greater displacement capacity without sudden stiffness loss.

Energy absorption capacity

Table 4 presents the calculated absorbed energy (E) values for the tested specimens, providing insights into their toughness and energy-dissipation capacity under loading. The absorbed energy represents the total work performed on a footing up to failure and was determined by integrating the area under the load–deflection curve. Numerical integration was carried out using the trapezoidal rule, where E corresponds to the cumulative sum of incremental load–deflection products up to the ultimate load17,41,42(Fig. 11), as described in Eq. 13, with the central settlement adopted as the representative deflection. This parameter serves as a key indicator of a footing’s ability to resist cracking, sustain deformation, and maintain structural integrity under progressive loading43,44.

Absorbed energy assessment38.

The energy absorption capacity showed a substantial enhancement with increasing reinforcement ratio. Compared to F54 (430kN·mm), the absorbed energy increased by 7.8%, 31.23%, 32.6%, and 55% for F120, F214, F335, and F480, respectively. This significant improvement is attributed to the combined effect of increased strength and ductility. Higher reinforcement ratios delay reinforcement yielding and suppress excessive crack widening, allowing the footing to sustain higher loads over larger deformation ranges. The results demonstrate that reinforcement ratio plays a critical role not only in enhancing load capacity but also in improving the ductile performance and energy dissipation potential of RC footings resting on stiff, interlocking basaltic soils.

The findings of Group GI clearly demonstrate that the reinforcement ratio strongly influences both contact stress distribution and global structural performance. While increasing reinforcement enhances central contact stress and ultimate load, the diminishing gains beyond a certain reinforcement level indicate that soil-controlled failure becomes dominant. This highlights the importance of considering footing stiffness degradation and soil–structure interaction in design, particularly for footings resting on basaltic soils, where high soil stiffness can amplify stress concentrations beneath relatively rigid footings.

Effect of steel yield stress (Group GII)

Group GII was formulated to isolate the effect of reinforcement yield stress on the structural performance and contact stress distribution of reinforced concrete footings resting on basaltic soil (Table 3). Five footings (F54, F54-S280, F54-S360, F54-S400, and F54-S450) were analyzed. All specimens shared identical geometry (400 × 400 × 100 mm), reinforcement layout (15D4 mm, µ = 0.54%), and concrete compressive strength (20 MPa). The only parameter varied was the steel yield stress, which ranged from 240 MPa in the reference footing (F54) to 450 MPa in the most highly reinforced specimen (F54-S450), covering the typical range of conventional and high-strength reinforcing steels.

Central contact stress at failure

More steel yield stress is providing increase in the failure load which provides increase in central contact stress (Fig. 12). Compared to the reference footing F54 (276.4 kPa), the central contact stress increased marginally by 1.66% for F54-S280 and 5.5% for F54-S360, followed by a more pronounced increase of 19.4% and 25.2% for F54-S400 and F54-S450, respectively (Table 4). This behavior can be attributed to the fact that, prior to yielding, reinforcement primarily contributes to stiffness through its elastic modulus, which remains essentially unchanged across different steel grades. As a result, the initiation and progression of cracking—and hence stiffness degradation—are not significantly affected by the variation in yield strength alone. The two-dimensional contact stress contours (Fig. 13) across all samples, the overall nature of the stress distribution remains similar, with only slight increases in the concentration at the center as the yield strength rises. Unlike the reinforcement ratio, an increase in yield strength does not change the redistribution pattern of stresses much, as the stiffness of the footing and crack pattern remain unchanged. Based on the findings, it appears that steel yield strength has very little effect on stress redistribution and stiffness degradation within the examined range, thereby contributing less to the development of contact stress distribution.

Ultimate load capacity

The ultimate load capacity exhibited a steady but limited increase with steel yield stress (Fig. 14). The ultimate load increased from 143.1kN for F54 to 150.5kN (+ 5.17%), 156.9kN (+ 9.64%), 160.1kN (+ 11.88%), and 161.7 kN (+ 13.0%) for yield stresses of 280, 360, 400, and 450 MPa, respectively (Table 4). The diminishing rate of improvement at higher yield stresses indicates that the failure mechanism gradually transitions from steel-controlled yielding toward soil-controlled bearing failure. Once the reinforcement yield stress exceeds a certain threshold, further increases do not significantly enhance load capacity because the footing–soil system becomes governed by the bearing resistance and stiffness of the basaltic soil rather than the strength of the reinforcement.

Deformation behavior

The ultimate deflection remained constant at 4.5 mm for all specimens in Group GII, despite the increase in ultimate load (Table 4). This observation suggests that increasing steel yield stress primarily enhances strength rather than ductility for footings with low reinforcement ratios. The deformation capacity is controlled by the onset of concrete cracking and soil deformation, both of which are largely unaffected by changes in steel yield stress when reinforcement quantity remains unchanged.

Energy absorption capacity

A gradual increase in energy absorption was observed with increasing steel yield stress (Table 4). Compared to the reference footing F54 (430kN.mm), the absorbed energy increased by 1.93%, 3.16%, 3.60%, and 3.74% for F54-S280, F54-S360, F54-S400, and F54-S450, respectively. The relatively modest improvement in energy absorption reflects the limited contribution of higher yield stress steel to post-cracking ductility when the reinforcement ratio is low. While higher yield stress delays yielding and slightly increases the load-carrying capacity, it does not significantly alter the deformation profile or crack pattern, resulting in only minor gains in overall energy dissipation.

As the results of Group GII, using a higher yield stress of reinforcement improves the ultimate load as well as the central contact stress of RC footings on basaltic soil and benefits are progressive rather than one which is more substantial for constant reinforcement ratio. The results show that for shallow foundations, working towards improving footings on stiff granular soil, increasing reinforcement quantity is more effective than only using higher grade steel. It has direct relevance to economical and rational design in the zone, where high-strength reinforcement is easily accessible and the soil bearing capacity controls failure.

Effect of concrete compressive strength (Group GIII)

Group GIII was designed to investigate the influence of concrete compressive strength on the contact stress distribution, load-carrying capacity, deformation characteristics, and energy absorption of reinforced concrete footings resting on basaltic soil. Five footings (F54, F54-C25, F54-C35, F54-C45, and F54-C60) were analyzed. All specimens had identical geometry (400 × 400 × 100 mm), reinforcement layout (15D4 mm, µ = 0.54%), and steel yield stress (240 MPa). The only variable was the concrete compressive strength, which increased from 20 MPa in the reference footing to 60 MPa, representing the range of normal- to high-strength concretes commonly used in shallow foundations.

Central contact stress at failure

Substantial increase in central contact stress at the failure load was evident with an increase in concrete strength (Fig. 15 Compared to the reference footing F54 (276.4 kPa), the central contact stress increased by 50.5% for F54-C25 and continued to increase to 69.5%,102%, and 117.4% for concrete strengths of 35, 45, and 60 MPa respectively (Table 4). This behavior is principally connected with an increase in stiffness and hindrance in the cracking of higher-strength concrete. The footing experiences less tensile cracking, and its stiffness is less degraded by load, with increasing compressive strength, which means that it responds more stiffly with increasing load. As a result, a larger part of the applied load is transferred to the central portion of the soil below. The contours of the contact stress (Fig. 16) clearly show the evolution of the stress with strength. Stress discontinuities are observed at lower strengths, where the stress distribution is diffuse in nature, indicating a flexible loading of the cementitious material. On the other hand, higher strength footings show greater stress concentration in the center. This is due to less redistribution from cracking and more stiffness. This shows the role of concrete strength in controlling stiffness degradation and stress redistribution with it confirming to be a most influential parameter on the three-dimensional contact stress field in soil - footing system.

Ultimate load capacity

The ultimate load capacity increased markedly with concrete compressive strength, rising from 143.1kN for F54 to 181.7kN (+ 27.0%) for F54-C25 and reaching 243.8kN (+ 70.4%) for F54-C60 (Table 4). Unlike Groups GI and GII, no clear plateau was observed within the investigated range, indicating that concrete strength remained a dominant parameter governing failure (Fig. 17). This response reflects the dual role of higher-strength concrete in improving both flexural capacity and compressive stress resistance in the compression zone of the footing. Stronger concrete enhances the footing’s ability to sustain higher bending moments while simultaneously increasing its resistance to localized compressive stresses induced by soil reaction, thereby postponing both structural and soil-controlled failure mechanisms.

Deformation behavior

The ultimate deflection increased progressively with concrete strength, from 4.5 mm for F54 to 9.5 mm for F54-C60 (Table 4). This trend indicates that higher-strength concrete allows the footing to undergo larger deformations at higher load levels before failure. Although high-strength concrete is often associated with reduced material ductility, the observed increase in deflection is governed by the global footing–soil interaction, where delayed cracking and increased load capacity enable greater overall displacement prior to collapse.

Energy absorption capacity

Energy absorption capacity showed the most considerable improvement among the various parametric groups. Grounded Energy absorption increased by 107% for F54-C25 and reached by F54-C60 270% in comparison with Reference footing (Table 4). The significant improvement is ascribed to the synergistic effects of a substantially higher load capacity, in conjunction with increased deformation at failure, resulting in a much larger area under the load–deflection curve. Findings indicate that an increased compressive strength of concrete not just improves its strength but significantly improves the toughness of footing–soil system. Foundations that are loaded in a extreme manner depend on energy dissipation capacity performing well under realistic conditions.

The group GIII finding shows compressive strength of concrete is most dominating parameter affecting contact stress distribution, failure load and energy absorption of RC footings resting on the basaltic soil. Growing the yield stress of steel or the reinforcement ratio does not help as much structural capacity and development of soil stress as improving concrete strength does. These results highlight that concrete stiffness degradation and strength evolution should be explicitly considered in foundation design, particularly for footings on stiff granular soils where the classical assumption of uniform stress is not valid.

Role of basaltic soil characteristics in stress redistribution

While footing stiffness degradation governs contact stress evolution, the intrinsic properties of basaltic soil critically influence both the magnitude and pattern of stress redistribution. Basaltic soil differs markedly from conventional sands4,5,7, and these differences underpin the observed footing behavior. Its high internal friction angle (φ = 40°) mobilizes greater shear resistance at the footing edges, mitigating the stress reductions predicted by classical elastic models and producing a less pronounced saddle-shaped distribution than in lower-friction granular media. The positive dilatancy angle (ψ = 20°) induces volumetric expansion during shearing, generating additional confinement beneath the footing center and amplifying central stress concentrations (Figs. 9 and 13, and 16). Moreover, the angular morphology of crushed basaltic aggregates enhances particle interlocking, which (i) slows soil stiffness degradation under progressive loading and (ii) promotes an “arching” mechanism that redirects load from deforming edges to the stiffer core. Collectively, these basaltic soil characteristics result in elevated center-to-edge stress ratios, highlighting the distinct mechanical response relative to conventional sand-supported footings.

Conclusion

In this study, reinforced concrete (RC) square footings resting on basaltic soil having failure mechanisms, contact stress redistribution and stiffness degradation due cracking were investigated. The investigation was done using experimental and numerical studies. Given the experimental observations, validated finite element simulations and extensive parametric analyses, the following conclusions are drawn:

The contact stresses resulting from RC footings placed on basaltic soil show high concentration at the center and low concentration of stress at edge upon failure. The shape of contact stresses was quite non-uniform at failure. The resulting stress distribution is markedly non-uniform and differs significantly from classical elastic solutions and design codes that assume a uniform stress distribution at the footing base.

This incorporation of cracking made the footings more flexible and thus led to reduced central contact stresses. This generation of cracking thus intensified the central stress concentrations in the footings.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

This study provides novel experimental and numerical insights into RC footings on basaltic soil, yet several limitations should be noted. The experimental program included only four footings, with a single specimen per reinforcement ratio (0.19–3.43%), limiting statistical reliability and repeatability. Contact stress distributions were not directly measured; validation relied on global responses (load–deflection curves, failure modes, crack patterns), which, while supportive of the numerical model, do not fully capture local stress variations. Moreover, the exclusive focus on basaltic soil precludes direct comparison with conventional sands, limiting definitive attribution of observed behaviors to soil-specific characteristics. Future research should address these gaps through expanded experimental campaigns with replicates (n ≥ 3), direct measurement of contact stresses using thin-film sensors or DIC, and comparative studies with conventional sands under identical loading and footing configurations. Additional parametric investigations—covering footing thickness, aspect ratio, eccentric or combined loading, cyclic effects, and varying soil densities—would further enhance applicability. Finally, the validated numerical framework can support development of simplified, design-oriented models incorporating cracking-induced stiffness degradation and non-uniform contact stress, bridging advanced simulation insights with practical foundation design on stiff granular soils.
