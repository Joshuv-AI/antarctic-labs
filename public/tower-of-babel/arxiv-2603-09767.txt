Optimal Control in Age-Structured Populations: A Comparison of Rate-Control and Effort-Control
Jiguang Yu, Louis Shuo Wang, Ye Liang

Abstract
This paper investigates the dynamics and optimal harvesting of age-structured populations governed by McKendrick–von Foerster equations, contrasting two distinct harvesting mechanisms: rate-control and effort-control. For the rate-control formulation, where harvesting acts as a direct additive removal term, we derive first-order necessary optimality conditions of Pontryagin type for the associated infinite-horizon optimal control problem, explicitly characterizing the adjoint system, transversality conditions, and control switching laws. In contrast, the effort-control formulation introduces harvesting as a multiplicative mortality intensity dependent on aggregate population size. We demonstrate that this aggregate dependence structurally alters the optimality system, formally generating a nonlocal coupling term in the adjoint equation that links all ages through the total stock. By combining rigorous variational control derivations, and explicit stationary profiles, this work clarifies the profound mathematical and bioeconomic distinctions between additive and multiplicative harvesting strategies.

In this paper we focus on two harvesting mechanisms in age-structured models.
The first is a rate-control formulation, in which harvesting enters the
state equation as a direct removal term. The second is an effort-control
formulation, in which harvesting acts as an additional mortality intensity and
the instantaneous yield is proportional to both effort and stock abundance.
First, for the infinite-horizon harvesting problem, we derive necessary optimality
conditions of Pontryagin type, including the adjoint system, transversality
condition, switching relations for the distributed harvesting control and the
boundary inflow control, and complementary slackness for the state constraint.
We then compare rate-control and effort-control formulations to
highlight the structural differences between them. In particular, we exhibit the
nonlocal coupling term in the effort-control adjoint equation induced by
aggregate population dependence and thereby make explicit how the choice of
harvesting mechanism changes the analytical structure of the optimality system. The contributions of the paper can be summarized as follows:

(i)

We derive necessary optimality conditions for an infinite-horizon
age-structured harvesting problem. They include the adjoint equation, boundary
and transversality conditions, switching conditions for the controls, and
complementary slackness relations associated with the nonnegativity constraint
on the state.

(ii)

We clarify the mathematical and economic distinction between
rate-control and effort-control mechanisms by comparing the way they enter
both the state dynamics and the profit functional, and by exhibiting the
nonlocal adjoint term induced by aggregate population dependence in the
effort-control setting.

3
Model and assumptions

We consider age-structured population models on the age interval
[0,A]
, where

A\in(0,\infty)
denotes the maximal age and
t\geq 0
is time. The state variable

x(t,a)\geq 0
represents the population density of individuals of age
a
at time
t
.
Throughout the paper,
a
denotes age and
t
denotes time. The boundary input

p(t)\geq 0
describes the inflow of newborn or newly introduced individuals at age

a=0
.

Our aim is to compare two harvesting mechanisms within a common age-structured
framework. The first one is a rate-control model, in which harvesting acts as
a direct removal term. The second one is an effort-control model, in which
harvesting enters as an additional mortality intensity and may depend on the aggregate
population size. The rate-control model will serve as the main setting for the infinite-horizon maximum principle, while the effort-control model will be used to identify the structural differences induced by multiplicative control
and nonlocal population dependence.

3.1
The two model classes

Model R (rate-control).

The age-structured population dynamics with direct harvesting rate
u
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}x(t,a)+\partial_{a}x(t,a)

\displaystyle=-\mu(t,a)\,x(t,a)-u(t,a),

\displaystyle t>0,\;a\in(0,A),

(1)

\displaystyle x(t,0)

\displaystyle=p(t),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,

(2)

\displaystyle x(0,a)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(a),

\displaystyle a\in[0,A].

(3)

Here
\mu(t,a)\geq 0
is the natural mortality rate,

is the harvesting
rate applied directly to individuals of age
a
, and

is the boundary recruitment
or stocking control.

Model E (effort-control).

The age-structured population dynamics with multiplicative harvesting effort

is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}x(t,a)+\partial_{a}x(t,a)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\mu(E(t),a)+w(t,a)\bigr)x(t,a),

\displaystyle t>0,\;a\in(0,A),

(4)

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{A}x(t,a)\,da,

\displaystyle t\geq 0,

(5)

\displaystyle x(t,0)

\displaystyle=p(t),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,

(6)

\displaystyle x(0,a)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(a),

\displaystyle a\in[0,A].

(7)

In this formulation,

(A1)

The maximal age

is finite, with
A\in(0,\infty)
.

(A2)

The initial datum satisfies

x_{0}\in L^{1}(0,A),\qquad x_{0}(a)\geq 0\ \text{for a.e. }a\in(0,A).

(A3)

In Model R, the natural mortality coefficient

\mu:[0,\infty)\times[0,A]\to[0,\infty)

(A4)

In Model E, the density-dependent mortality coefficient

\mu:[0,\infty)\times[0,A]\to[0,\infty),\qquad(E,a)\mapsto\mu(E,a),

is bounded on bounded subsets of

is continuously differentiable in

a\in[0,A]
.

(A5)

The controls belong to the admissible classes
\mathcal{U}_{R}
or

, respectively, and the boundary inflow

is nonnegative.

(A6)

All state trajectories considered in the paper satisfy the
nonnegativity constraint (

).

Under these assumptions, each model is understood in the usual mild sense along
characteristics. In particular, for every admissible control pair, the corresponding
state equation is a first-order transport equation with inflow boundary condition at

a=0
. For the rate-control model this leads to a linear transport problem, while the
effort-control model is nonlinear because of the aggregate term
E(t)
and the
multiplicative control structure. These two features are precisely what distinguish
the mathematical structure of the two harvesting mechanisms.

The two model classes play different roles in the sequel.
For Model R, we study both steady states and an infinite-horizon discounted harvesting problem. In particular,
the rate-control framework is used to derive necessary optimality conditions of Pontryagin type.
For Model E, our purpose is more limited. We use the effort-control formulation to
exhibit how multiplicative harvesting and aggregate dependence modify the structure
of the state equation, the objective functional, and the corresponding adjoint system.
This will make clear why the effort-control case naturally generates a nonlocal term
in the formal dual equation.

To avoid any ambiguity in notation, we summarize the symbols used throughout the
paper:

t\;\text{: time},\qquad a\;\text{: age},\qquad x(t,a)\;\text{: age density},\qquad p(t)\;\text{: boundary inflow},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &u(t,a)\;\text{: harvesting rate in Model R},\qquad w(t,a)\;\text{: harvesting effort in Model E},\\[4.0pt]
&E(t)=\int_{0}^{A}x(t,a)\,da\;\text{: aggregate population size}.\end{aligned}

This set of unified notation will be used consistently in all subsequent sections.

4
Infinite-horizon optimal control for the rate-control model

In this section we consider the infinite-horizon harvesting problem associated
with the rate-control model. Our goal is to derive a set of first-order necessary
conditions in the spirit of Pontryagin’s maximum principle. Since the present paper
focuses on the structural form of the optimality system, we formulate the result
under the standing assumptions of Section
3
and, in addition, under a regularity
framework ensuring that the variational calculations below are justified.

Now we have the optimal control problem. We consider the following infinite-horizon discounted optimization problem.

Problem 4.1
.

Maximize

J(u,p,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}c(t,a)\,u(t,a)\,da-k(t)\,p(t)\right)\,dt

(13)

over admissible controls
(u,p)\in\mathcal{U}_{R}
and corresponding states
x
,
subject to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}x(t,a)+\partial_{a}x(t,a)

\displaystyle=-\mu(t,a)x(t,a)-u(t,a),

\displaystyle t>0,\ a\in(0,A),

(14)

\displaystyle x(t,0)

\displaystyle=p(t),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,

(15)

\displaystyle x(0,a)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(a),

\displaystyle a\in[0,A],

(16)

\displaystyle x(t,a)

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{for a.e. }(t,a)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,A].

(17)

Here
r>0
is the discount rate,
c(t,a)
is the unit harvesting value, and

k(t)
is the boundary inflow cost.

In order to interpret the necessary conditions below as a justified first-order
optimality system rather than as a purely heuristic calculation, we impose the
following assumptions.

(R1)

Data and admissible controls.

The coefficients satisfy

c,\mu\in L^{\infty}\bigl((0,\infty)\times(0,A)\bigr),\qquad k\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty),\qquad r>0,

and the initial datum satisfies

x_{0}\in L^{1}(0,A),\qquad x_{0}(a)\geq 0\ \text{ a.e. }a\in(0,A).

The admissible controls satisfy

u\in L^{\infty}\bigl((0,\infty)\times(0,A)\bigr),\qquad p\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty),

together with the box constraints

0\leq u(t,a)\leq u_{\max},\qquad 0\leq p(t)\leq p_{\max}

for a.e.
(t,a)
and a.e.
t
, respectively.

(R2)

Well-posedness of the state equation.

For every admissible pair
(u,p)
, the state equation
(
14
)–(
16
) admits a unique mild solution

x=S(u,p)\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl([0,\infty);L^{1}(0,A)\bigr),

and this solution satisfies
x(t,a)\geq 0
for a.e.
(t,a)
.

(R3)

Differentiability of the control-to-state map.

The control-to-state map

S:\mathcal{U}_{R}\to L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl([0,\infty);L^{1}(0,A)\bigr),\qquad(u,p)\mapsto x,

is Gâteaux differentiable along admissible directions, and for every admissible
variation
(\delta u,\delta p)
the derivative

\delta x=DS(u,p)[\delta u,\delta p]

is the unique mild solution of the linearized system associated with
(
14
)–(
16
).

(R4)

Integrability of the objective functional.

For every admissible pair
(u,p)
, the discounted objective is well defined:

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}|c(t,a)u(t,a)|\,da+|k(t)p(t)|\right)\,dt<\infty.

(R5)

Regularity of adjoint and multiplier.

The adjoint variable
\lambda
and the state-constraint multiplier
\eta

belong to classes for which all terms in the Lagrangian and in the integration-by-parts
formulas are meaningful, and such that

e^{-rt}\lambda\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl((0,\infty)\times(0,A)\bigr),\qquad\eta\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl((0,\infty)\times(0,A)\bigr),\qquad\eta\geq 0.

(R6)

Justification of integration by parts and transversality.

The products

e^{-rt}\lambda\,\delta x,\qquad e^{-rt}\lambda\,\partial_{t}\delta x,\qquad e^{-rt}\lambda\,\partial_{a}\delta x

are integrable on finite cylinders, and the integration-by-parts identities used are valid. Moreover, for every admissible first variation
\delta x
, the
boundary pairing at infinity vanishes:

\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rT}\lambda(T,a)\delta x(T,a)\,da=0.

(R7)

Existence of a KKT multiplier for the state constraint.

The nonnegativity constraint on the state is qualified in a form that guarantees
the existence of a multiplier
\eta\geq 0
satisfying the complementary slackness
relation

\eta(t,a)x^{*}(t,a)=0\qquad\text{for a.e. }(t,a)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,A].

Under assumptions (R1)–(R7), the variational calculation leading
to the necessary conditions below is understood as a justified first-order derivation
of the optimality system for Problem
4.1
.

To derive the optimality system, we introduce a current-value adjoint variable

\lambda(t,a)
for the state equation and a current-value multiplier
\eta(t,a)\geq 0

for the state constraint
x(t,a)\geq 0
. Formally, consider the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,p,x;\lambda,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}c(t,a)\,u(t,a)\,da-k(t)\,p(t)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\Bigl(\partial_{t}x(t,a)+\partial_{a}x(t,a)+\mu(t,a)x(t,a)+u(t,a)\Bigr)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\eta(t,a)\,x(t,a)\,da\,dt.

(18)

Let
(u^{*},p^{*},x^{*})
be an optimal triple. For admissible perturbations

(\delta u,\delta p)
, define

u^{\varepsilon}:=u^{*}+\varepsilon\,\delta u,\qquad p^{\varepsilon}:=p^{*}+\varepsilon\,\delta p,

for
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small, chosen so that feasibility is preserved. Let
x^{\varepsilon}
denote the corresponding perturbed state, and let

\delta x:=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}

be the induced first variation of the state. By differentiating the state equation
with respect to
\varepsilon
, one obtains the linearized system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta x(t,a)+\partial_{a}\delta x(t,a)

\displaystyle=-\mu(t,a)\delta x(t,a)-\delta u(t,a),

(19)

\displaystyle\delta x(t,0)

\displaystyle=\delta p(t),\qquad\delta x(0,a)=0.

(20)

We now evaluate the first variation of the current-value Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
in (
18
).
Differentiation at
\varepsilon=0
yields

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}c(t,a)\,\delta u(t,a)\,da-k(t)\,\delta p(t)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\Bigl(\partial_{t}\delta x(t,a)+\partial_{a}\delta x(t,a)+\mu(t,a)\delta x(t,a)+\delta u(t,a)\Bigr)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\eta(t,a)\,\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt.

(21)

After collecting the
\delta u
-terms, this becomes

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\bigl(c(t,a)-\lambda(t,a)\bigr)\delta u(t,a)\,da\,dt-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}k(t)\,\delta p(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\bigl(\partial_{t}\delta x(t,a)+\partial_{a}\delta x(t,a)\bigr)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\bigl(-\mu(t,a)\lambda(t,a)+\eta(t,a)\bigr)\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt.

(22)

We next transfer derivatives from
\delta x
to the adjoint variable by integration
by parts. For the time derivative term, one has

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\,\partial_{t}\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rT}\lambda(T,a)\delta x(T,a)\,da

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{A}\lambda(0,a)\delta x(0,a)\,da

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}\partial_{t}\bigl(e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\bigr)\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt.

(23)

Since
\delta x(0,a)=0
, the term at
t=0
vanishes. Moreover,

\partial_{t}\bigl(e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\bigr)=e^{-rt}\bigl(\partial_{t}\lambda(t,a)-r\lambda(t,a)\bigr).

Similarly, for the age derivative term,

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\,\partial_{a}\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,A)\delta x(t,A)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,0)\delta x(t,0)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\partial_{a}\lambda(t,a)\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt.

(24)

Using
\delta x(t,0)=\delta p(t)
, substitution of (
23
) and
(
24
) into (
22
) yields

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\bigl(c(t,a)-\lambda(t,a)\bigr)\delta u(t,a)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\bigl(\lambda(t,0)-k(t)\bigr)\delta p(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\Bigl(\partial_{t}\lambda(t,a)+\partial_{a}\lambda(t,a)-\bigl(r+\mu(t,a)\bigr)\lambda(t,a)+\eta(t,a)\Bigr)\delta x(t,a)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,A)\delta x(t,A)\,dt-\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rT}\lambda(T,a)\delta x(T,a)\,da.

(25)

The coefficient of the interior state variation vanishes provided the adjoint satisfies

Remark 4.3

(Scope of the theorem)
.

Theorem

is stated as a necessary optimality system for the

justify the variational calculation. On an unbounded time horizon, the role of the
adjoint variable and of the transversality condition at infinity requires particular

purpose of this section is to identify the structural form of the adjoint system,
the switching laws, and the complementary slackness relation that will later be
compared with the effort-control formulation.

5
Effort-control model and the nonlocal adjoint term

We now turn to the effort-control formulation introduced in Section
3
. In contrast
to the rate-control model, the state equation is nonlinear because the mortality
coefficient depends on the aggregate population size

E(t)=\int_{0}^{A}x(t,a)\,da,

and the control enters multiplicatively through the term
w(t,a)x(t,a)
. For this
reason, and in order not to overstate the level of rigor at this stage, we restrict
ourselves here to a formal derivation of the associated adjoint system under
sufficient smoothness assumptions.

For the effort-control problem, consider the infinite-horizon discounted functional

J_{E}(w,p,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}c(t,a)\,w(t,a)x(t,a)\,da-k(t)\,p(t)\right)\,dt,

(39)

subject to the nonlinear age-structured dynamics

\displaystyle\partial_{t}x(t,a)+\partial_{a}x(t,a)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\mu(E(t),a)+w(t,a)\bigr)x(t,a),

\displaystyle t>0,\ a\in(0,A),

(40)

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{A}x(t,a)\,da,

\displaystyle t\geq 0,

(41)

\displaystyle x(t,0)

\displaystyle=p(t),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,

(42)

\displaystyle x(0,a)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(a),

\displaystyle a\in[0,A].

(43)

The purpose of this section is not to establish a complete Pontryagin principle for
(
39
)–(
43
), but rather to identify the form of the nonlocal
term that appears in the dual equation when the mortality rate depends on the
aggregate state variable
E(t)
.

Assume for the moment that all data are sufficiently smooth and that the state,
control, and adjoint variables are regular enough for differentiation under the
integral sign and integration by parts to be justified. Introduce a current-value
adjoint variable
\lambda(t,a)
for the state equation and write the current-value
Lagrangian in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{E}(w,p,x;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}c(t,a)\,w(t,a)x(t,a)\,da-k(t)\,p(t)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\Bigl(\partial_{t}x(t,a)+\partial_{a}x(t,a)+\bigl(\mu(E(t),a)+w(t,a)\bigr)x(t,a)\Bigr)\,da\,dt.

(44)

Let
(w^{*},p^{*},x^{*})
be an admissible triple and let
\delta x
be the first variation
of the state induced by admissible perturbations. The terms involving
\delta x
arise
from three sources:

(i)

the payoff density
c(t,a)w(t,a)x(t,a)
;

(ii)

the local term
\bigl(\mu(E(t),a)+w(t,a)\bigr)x(t,a)
in the state equation;

(iii)

the dependence of
\mu(E(t),a)
on the aggregate state

E(t)=\int_{0}^{A}x(t,s)\,ds.

The first two sources give the expected local contributions. The third one generates
an additional nonlocal term. Indeed, differentiating
\mu(E(t),a)x(t,a)
with respect
to the state yields

\delta\!\bigl(\mu(E(t),a)x(t,a)\bigr)=\mu(E(t),a)\,\delta x(t,a)+\mu_{E}(E(t),a)\,x(t,a)\,\delta E(t),

where

\delta E(t)=\int_{0}^{A}\delta x(t,s)\,ds.

Substituting this into the first variation of (
44
), the nonlocal contribution
becomes

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\mu_{E}(E(t),a)x(t,a)\,\delta E(t)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\lambda(t,a)\mu_{E}(E(t),a)x(t,a)\left(\int_{0}^{A}\delta x(t,s)\,ds\right)\,da\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rt}\left(\int_{0}^{A}\mu_{E}(E(t),\sigma)x(t,\sigma)\lambda(t,\sigma)\,d\sigma\right)\delta x(t,s)\,ds\,dt.

(45)

Therefore, this contributes the coefficient

-\int_{0}^{A}\mu_{E}(E(t),s)x(t,s)\lambda(t,s)\,ds

to the interior
\delta x
-variation.

The local terms are handled exactly as in the rate-control case: integrating by parts
in
t
and
a
transfers the derivatives from
\delta x
onto the adjoint, while the
discount factor produces the term
r\lambda
. The contribution of the running payoff

c(t,a)w(t,a)x(t,a)
yields the local source term
-c(t,a)w(t,a)
in the adjoint
equation, and the multiplicative mortality term
w(t,a)x(t,a)
contributes

+w(t,a)\lambda(t,a)
.

These formal calculations lead to the following statement.

Proposition 5.1

(Formal adjoint system for the effort-control problem)
.

Assume that the coefficients, controls, and state are sufficiently smooth, that

\mu(E,a)
is continuously differentiable with respect to
E
, and that all
integration-by-parts steps are justified. Then the current-value adjoint variable
associated with (
39
)–(
43
) formally satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\lambda(t,a)+\partial_{a}\lambda(t,a)

\displaystyle=\bigl(r+\mu(E(t),a)+w(t,a)\bigr)\lambda(t,a)-c(t,a)w(t,a)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{A}\mu_{E}(E(t),s)x(t,s)\lambda(t,s)\,ds,\qquad t>0,\ a\in(0,A).

(46)

The corresponding terminal-age and transversality conditions are formally given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\qquad\qquad\lambda(t,A)=0\qquad\text{for }t\geq 0,\\[4.0pt]
&\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{A}e^{-rT}\lambda(T,a)\delta x(T,a)\,da=0\quad\text{for every admissible first variation }\delta x.\end{aligned}

(47)

Remark 5.2
.

The structural contrast with the rate-control model is now transparent. In the
rate-control case, the adjoint equation is local in the age variable and involves
only the state-constraint multiplier as a source term. In the effort-control case,
the dependence of
\mu
on the aggregate population size produces the additional
nonlocal term

\int_{0}^{A}\mu_{E}(E(t),s)x(t,s)\lambda(t,s)\,ds,

which couples all ages through the total population level
E(t)
. This is the
main analytical feature distinguishing the effort-control formulation from the
rate-control formulation.

5Throughout this section we assume that the coefficients are time-independent.

\mu(t,a)=\mu(a),\qquad c(t,a)=c(a),\qquad k(t)=k,

Proposition 6.5

(Adjoint ODE and explicit representation)

7
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we have systematically compared two fundamental harvesting mechanisms within the framework of continuous-time, age-structured population dynamics. By juxtaposing a rate-control formulation with an effort-control formulation, we have highlighted the profound ways in which the choice of management variable dictates the analytical and structural properties of the resulting optimal control problem.

Mathematical and Structural Implications

From a mathematical perspective, the distinction between additive and multiplicative controls is stark. In the rate-control setting, the control variable
u(t,a)
enters the state equation as a direct, additive sink. This preserves an affine transport structure, allowing for the rigorous derivation of an infinite-horizon maximum principle. The necessary optimality conditions, derived in Section
4
, reveal an adjoint equation that is strictly local in the age variable, driven only by the discount rate, natural mortality, and the state-constraint multiplier.

Conversely, the effort-control formulation introduces a bilinear coupling through the multiplicative term
w(t,a)x(t,a)
. More importantly, incorporating a density-dependent mortality coefficient
\mu(E(t),a)
transforms the system into a nonlinear transport equation. As demonstrated in Section
5
, this aggregate population dependence fundamentally alters the dual system. The variational derivation exposes a nonlocal integral term in the adjoint equation,

\int_{0}^{A}\mu_{E}(E(t),s)x(t,s)\lambda(t,s)\,ds,

(58)

which formally couples the shadow prices across all ages through the total stock. This nonlocal feedback presents significant analytical challenges but offers a mathematically richer representation of density-dependent ecological constraints.

Bioeconomic and Ecological Insights

The analytical differences between the two models are closely mirrored in their bioeconomic behavior, as evidenced by our stationary reductions and numerical simulations. The rate-control mechanism is mathematically aggressive; the explicit affine Volterra representation shows that direct removal can rapidly drive specific age classes to zero. This requires strict nonnegativity constraints and heavily relies on bang-bang control strategies to prevent localized population collapse. The corresponding yield curve grows steeply but abruptly levels off as state constraints become active.

By contrast, the effort-control mechanism is inherently self-regulating. The explicit multiplicative exponential profile ensures that the extraction scales proportionally with the available stock. Consequently, the yield curve is strictly concave, and population depletion is less severe at equivalent nominal harvesting intensities. This mathematical behavior aligns with classical bioeconomic intuition: effort-based management naturally cushions against overexploitation because diminishing stock abundance simultaneously reduces the instantaneous catch.

Limitations and Future Directions

While our results clarify the structural dichotomy between rate and effort controls, several avenues for future research remain. First, the derivation of the adjoint system for Model E presented here is primarily formal. Establishing a rigorous, functional-analytic proof of the infinite-horizon maximum principle for this nonlinear, nonlocally coupled system—particularly concerning the precise formulation of the transversality conditions at infinity—remains a formidable mathematical challenge.

Second, our framework assumes purely deterministic population dynamics. Integrating stochastic demographic fluctuations or environmental noise into these structured models is a critical next step. Recent advances in the well-posedness of stochastic ecological systems suggest a viable pathway for generalizing the present optimal harvesting results to noisy, uncertain environments.

Finally, extending the current single-species framework to multi-species models with age-structured predator-prey dynamics or spatial heterogeneity would allow for the investigation of optimal harvesting policies under complex ecological interactions. The nonlocal adjoint structures identified in this paper will likely play a central role in resolving the optimality systems of such high-dimensional bioeconomic models.

A.4
Rate-control versus effort-control: yield and depletion

Finally, Figure
7
compares the two harvesting mechanisms by
sweeping a common harvesting intensity
h
over the interval
[0,0.5]
.

Figure 7:
Comparison of rate-control and effort-control harvesting.
(a) Stationary yield as a function of harvesting intensity: the rate-control yield
grows rapidly and then levels off under the numerical truncation induced by the
state constraint, whereas the effort-control yield is concave throughout.
(b) Aggregate population depletion: rate-control depletes the stock more
aggressively at equal intensity.

For the rate-control model, we set

u(a)=h\,\mathbf{1}_{[2,8]}(a),

and define the stationary yield by

Y_{R}(h)=\int_{0}^{A}u_{\mathrm{actual}}(a)\,da,

where
u_{\mathrm{actual}}
denotes the numerically truncated extraction rate used
to enforce the nonnegativity constraint
x(a)\geq 0
.

For the effort-control model, we set

w(a)=h\,\mathbf{1}_{[2,8]}(a),

and define

Y_{E}(h)=\int_{0}^{A}w(a)x(a)\,da,

where
x(a)
depends on
h
through the steady-state equation with density-dependent
mortality

\mu(E,a)=\mu(a)+\alpha E,\qquad\alpha=0.002.

Panel (a) shows that the rate-control yield initially increases steeply and then
levels off once the nonnegativity constraint becomes active through the numerical
truncation of the admissible extraction. By contrast, the effort-control yield is
concave from the outset, since higher effort simultaneously depletes the stock on
which the yield depends. Panel (b) shows the corresponding aggregate population

E(h)=\int_{0}^{A}x(a)\,da

under each mechanism. At a given intensity, the rate-control model depletes the
stock more aggressively than the effort-control model, which is partially
self-regulated by the multiplicative structure
w(a)x(a)
.
