Abstract




Equilibrium in the Canonical Stackelberg Triopoly via Response Functions and Fixed Point Theory

abstract:
We analyze a canonical extension of the Stackelberg duopoly to a sequential framework, where each firm strategically anticipates the reactions of all subsequent players. In a triopoly (three-firm) settings, we obtain existence and uniqueness of market equilibrium via a reformulation of the equilibrium conditions that draws on coupled fixed-point theory. Even with linear demand, convergence of myopic best-response dynamics is not guaranteed. A recursive equilibrium formulation enables the analysis of the limiting case as the number of participants grow.

keywords:
Stackelberg triopoly, market equilibrium, response functions, fixed point theory.

1
Introduction

Oligopoly markets are defined by strategic interdependence: each firm’s decision depends on expectations about competitors’ behaviors. This interdependence is explicit when firms act sequentially rather than simultaneously, allowing earlier decisions to shape the strategic environment faced by later movers. Such sequential interactions arise in industries with capacity commitments, supply-chain ordering, infrastructure investment, and platform ecosystems, where firms make decisions in stages and later participants respond to earlier commitments.

While the classical Stackelberg model focuses on a leader–follower duopoly, many markets feature more than two dominant firms and exhibit sequential play. In the multi-firm markets, the equilibrium analysis is more intricate than in the duopoly case because the equilibrium properties depend on nested best-response structures, and convergence of myopic best-response dynamics is not guaranteed even under simple demand specifications. This paper develops an analytical frameworks capable of capturing the interdependence of sequential decisions and the stability of equilibrium outcomes.

The main technical ingredient of the proposed framework is a characterization of the first order equilibrium conditions as a tuple-fixed point problem of reaction functions.

This characterization is one-to-one (Theorem

Moreover, we propose contractive-type sufficient conditions for the existence of tuple-fixed point which imply that the second order conditions are satisfied, that is these contractive-type conditions are sufficient for the existence of equilibrium.

There are at least four advantages of the proposed approach. First, searching for a fixed point of response type of functions is very intuitive, and lands well to computing, estimating, and simulating of long-run equilibria. Relatedly, analysis of the convergence inform directly the empirical tractability of the model.

The challenges of computing and simulating equilibria are not unique to these settings. In general non-linearities and/or complementarities in payoffs contribute to these types of challenges. See the analysis and discussion in

Second, the technical tools we introduce can handle more non linear demand and more complex cost functions. Specifically, even a slight relaxation of the simplest demand and cost function cases results on situations with no explicit solutions. Third, and related, this approach, dispenses the “extreme” rationality assumptions presumed in the non-cooperative equilibrium concept. Firms may, respond in a noisy way (a form of bounded rationality), subject to errors, as long as they respond continuously and converge to the market equilibrium. Finally, as mentioned above, we obtain sufficient conditions not only for the existence of solution of the first order conditions but also for the second order conditions, and thus equilibrium.

In the first part of the paper, section 2, we introduce our framework for a tripodal market. We consider both Cournot and Stackeberg equilibria via an illustrative example. In section 3, we discuss the drawbacks and limitations of the traditional approach based on optimization and, as an alternative approach, introduce the coupled-fixed point technology together with our main technical tools that will later deliver results on existence and uniqueness of market equilibria (Theorem
1
). We also obtain conditions for convergence of the Picard iteration and bounds on the rate of convergence. In section 4, we circle back with a characterization of equilibrium via tuple-fixed point (Theorem
2
). In the context of our tripodal application, we demonstrate that contractive conditions guarantee that both the first order and second order conditions are met by a single equilibrium. Finally in section 5, we analyze the large market dynamic and compare Cournot and Stackelberg equilibria for linear and quadratic costs, generalizing the results in
[
3
]
. To summarize, linear costs amplify first-mover advantage and strategic differentiation in Stackelberg markets, creating more pronounced output and profit hierarchies. Non-linear (quadratic) costs dampen these effects by penalizing high output, leading to more balanced outcomes. In very large markets, both cost structures yield the same competitive benchmark, and the differences vanish. Importantly, for finite and intermediate sized markets, linear costs open the Stackelberg-Cournot gap in outputs while quadratic costs lead to more balanced Stackelberg outputs across firms and the Cournot–Stackelberg gap in output and price is smaller.

Broadly speaking, this paper develops an approach to canonical Stackelberg oligopoly models grounded in two fundamental elements: response functions and mixed tripled fixed points. By reformulating equilibrium conditions as fixed-point problems, we derive sufficient conditions for the existence, uniqueness, and convergence of market equilibrium under transparent and economically interpretable assumptions. In doing so, the paper bridges classical oligopoly theory with modern fixed-point methods, offering a flexible and mathematically rigorous framework for analyzing equilibrium and stability in Stackelberg-type markets.

We believe that our technical analysis and the tools we introduce can be re-interpreted in a wider set of strategic models and information settings. For example, consider settings when firms’ make decisions simultaneously but in the presence of asymmetric information. In contrast to the Cournot model, where each firm maximizes its profit over the entire set of feasible quantities, in the Stackelberg framework the first firm maximizes over all possible quantities, the second firm maximizes over quantities conditional on the expected reaction of the first firm, and the third firm maximizes over an even smaller set determined by the observed or anticipated actions of the first two firms.

Preliminaries

Consider three companies competing for the same pool of consumers, each aiming to meet market demand through a total output level
Z=x+y+z
. The market price is given by the inverse demand function

P(Z)=P\!\left(x+y+z\right),

which is assumed to be continuous and, when necessary, differentiable. Each firm faces production costs characterized by a cost function
c_{i}(t)
,
i=1,2,3
.

Firms profit functions are:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Pi_{1}(x,y,z)=xP\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{1}(x),\\[2.84526pt]
\Pi_{2}(x,y,z)=yP\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{2}(y),\\[2.84526pt]
\Pi_{3}(x,y,z)=zP\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{3}(z).\end{array}\right.

2.1
The Cournot Triopoly

In the classical Cournot model, firms act simultaneously and non-cooperatively, i.e. each firm takes the output levels of its competitors as given when determining its own production (
[
17
,
20
,
44
,
46
]
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\max\{\Pi_{1}(x,y,z):x\geq 0\},\\
\max\{\Pi_{2}(x,y,z):y\geq 0\},\\
\max\{\Pi_{3}(x,y,z):z\geq 0\}.\end{array}\right.

Specifically, firms assume that rivals do not adjust their output in response to its decision. Under these assumptions, the first-order necessary conditions for optimality yield the system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi_{1}}{\partial x}(x,y,z)=P\!\left(x+y+z\right)+xP^{\prime}\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{1}^{\prime}(x)=0,\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi_{2}}{\partial y}(x,y,z)=P\!\left(x+y+z\right)+yP^{\prime}\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{2}^{\prime}(y)=0,\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}(x,y,z)=P\!\left(x+y+z\right)+zP^{\prime}\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{3}^{\prime}(z)=0.\end{array}\right.

(1)

A Cournot-Nash equilibrium is a vector of outputs that maximizes each firm’s payoff given the output levels of its competitors. Any such equilibrium must satisfy system (
1
). However, the converse does not necessarily hold: solutions to the first-order system may fail to correspond to profit-maximizing strategies. Therefore, additional conditions are required to ensure that a solution of (
1
) represents an equilibrium.

In particular, when payoff functions are defined on bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is important to determine whether the maximum is attained in the interior or on the boundary of the feasible set
[
20
]
. Moreover, system (
1
) may admit multiple solutions. To ensure that a solution
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})
corresponds to a maximum, sufficient conditions are typically imposed. These often require concavity of the payoff functions
\Pi_{i}
, or alternatively, that the second-order conditions hold:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Pi_{1}}{\partial x^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})<0,\\[2.84526pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Pi_{2}}{\partial y^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})<0,\\[2.84526pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Pi_{3}}{\partial z^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})<0.\end{array}\right.

(2)

Remark 1
.

For simplicity, whenever we assert the existence of an implicitly defined function, we assume that appropriate regularity conditions are satisfied, without stating them explicitly. Furthermore, whenever sufficient conditions hold, we assume that derivatives of such functions can be obtained via implicit differentiation.

Remark 2
.

In a few special cases of inverse demand and cost functions, system (
1
) permits explicit derivation of response functions of the form

x=b_{1}(y,z),\quad y=b_{2}(x,z),\quad z=b_{3}(x,y).

These response functions are grounded players’ profit maximization choice, mapping the output choices of a players competitors to its own output choice. Of course, a fixed point of these functions
(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
is a solution to the first order conditions (
1
). The converse is also true.

In the general case, e.g. with non-linear demand, system (
1
) may not permit explicit derivation of these response mappings. Then, asserting equilibrium existence and uniqueness and/or empirical microfounded empirical analysis are not feasible. An alternative approach capable of handling general functional forms is developed in
[
19
,
6
]
, in which the structure of system (
1
) is used to motivate ad hoc response functions of the form:

x=b_{1}(x,y,z),\quad y=b_{2}(x,y,z),\quad z=b_{3}(x,y,z).

As before, there is one-to-one correspondence between fixed points of these response functions and solutions to the first order conditions, making the approach suitable to analyze existence and uniqueness. Further, these ad hoc response functions inherit the broad interpretation of the response functions grounded on the profit maximization in the most simple functional forms. Specifically, these response function define a myopic adaptive dynamic and, ideally, this dynamic converges to the (unique) equilibrium. In this myopic adaptive dynamic, every period firms respond to the output quantities of their competitors and their own quantities. In this sense this dynamic converges to the long-run equilibrium.

2.2
The Canonical Stackelberg Triopoly

Within the Stackelberg framework
[
48
]
, firms interact in a sequential rather than simultaneous manner. In contrast to the Cournot setting, where each firm treats competitors’ output levels as fixed, the Stackelberg model assumes that one firm (the leader) commits to its production level first, while the other firms (followers) choose their outputs after observing this decision.

As a result, the leader takes into account the reaction functions of the followers when making its decision, whereas the followers optimize given the leader’s announced output. This introduces an asymmetry between players, not necessarily in terms of information, but in terms of timing and strategic advantage.

For the rest of the article, let
\Pi_{i}(x,y,z)
be the payoff function of player
i=1,2,3
. We use the convention that the index indicates the order of the play. Thus, player
3
is the follower with first-order condition

\frac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}(x,y,z)=P\!\left(x+y+z\right)+zP^{\prime}\!\left(x+y+z\right)-c_{3}^{\prime}(z)=0

which yields the her response function
z=b_{3}(x,y)
, either explicitly or implicitly.

Next, player
2
(the intermediate player) knows the response function
b_{3}
of the follower, and maximizes the payoff
\Pi_{2}(x,y,b_{3}(x,y))
, taking
x
as given. Note that, in contrast to the Cournot model, the use of partial derivatives here requires care, since
z=b_{3}(x,y)
depends on
y
. To simplify notation, define

\Phi_{2}(x,y)=\Pi_{2}(x,y,b_{3}(x,y)).

Differentiating with respect to
y
, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{2}(x,y)}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=P\!\left(x+y+b_{3}(x,y)\right)+yP^{\prime}\!\left(x+y+b_{3}(x,y)\right)\left(1+\frac{\partial b_{3}(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)-c_{2}^{\prime}(y)=0,

which is the first-order condition for player
2
. This equation defines the response function
y=b_{2}(x)
, either explicitly or implicitly.

At the final stage, player
1
(the leader) anticipates the response functions of both followers and maximizes

\Pi_{1}\bigl(x,\,b_{2}(x),\,b_{3}(x,b_{2}(x))\bigr)=xP\!\left(x+b_{2}(x)+b_{3}(x,b_{2}(x))\right)-c_{1}(x).

The corresponding first-order condition is

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\Pi_{1}\bigl(x,b_{2}(x),b_{3}(x,b_{2}(x))\bigr)

(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\left[xP\!\left(x+b_{2}(x)+b_{3}(x,b_{2}(x))\right)-c_{1}(x)\right]=0.

Thus, the Stackelberg equilibrium is characterized by the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}(x,y,z)&=&0,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{2}(x,y)}{\partial y}&=&0,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Pi_{1}\bigl(x,b_{2}(x),b_{3}(x,b_{2}(x))\bigr)&=&0.\end{array}\right.

(4)

If
(\xi,\eta,\theta)
is a Stackelberg equilibrium, then it satisfies (
4
). In particular,
\xi
solves (
3
), while
\eta=b_{2}(\xi)
,
\theta=b_{3}(\xi,\eta)=b_{3}\bigl(\xi,b_{2}(\xi)\bigr)
.

To ensure that the solution corresponds to a maximum, the second-order conditions must hold:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Pi_{3}}{\partial z^{2}}(\xi,\eta,\theta)&<&0,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{2}}{\partial y^{2}}(\xi,\eta)&<&0,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\Pi_{1}\bigl(x,b_{2}(x),b_{3}(x,b_{2}(x))\bigr)\Big|_{x=\xi}&<&0.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Main Result

It may be difficult or even impossible to solve the first-order conditions explicitly, both in the Cournot model and in the Stackelberg framework. For instance, if the inverse demand function is given by

P=10-\arctan(x+y+z)
and the cost functions take the form

C(t)=te^{t}
, then explicit closed-form expressions for the response functions generally cannot be obtained.

For these cases we can adopt the static approach to Cournot in
[
6
,
19
]
, in which the structure of the first order conditions are used to re-cast the optimality conditions into a fixed point problem.

3.1
Mixed Tripled Fixed Points

Definition 1
.

Let
(X_{i},d_{i})
,
i=1,2,3
, be metric spaces and set
X:=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}
.
Let also
F_{i}:X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}\to X_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
be mappings.
Define the hierarchical admissible map
S:X\to X
by

S(x,y,z)=\Bigl(\underbrace{F_{1}\bigl(x,\,F_{2}(x,y,F_{3}(x,y,z)),\,F_{3}(x,y,z)\bigr)}_{S^{1}(x,y,z)},\;\underbrace{F_{2}(x,y,F_{3}(x,y,z))}_{S^{2}(x,y,z)},\;\underbrace{F_{3}(x,y,z)}_{S^{3}(x,y,z)}\Bigr).

Definition 2
.

Let
S:X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}\to X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}
be the map from Definition
1
, with

S(x,y,z)=\big(S^{1}(x,y,z),\,S^{2}(x,y,z),\,S^{3}(x,y,z)\big)
.
For
(x,y,z),(u,v,w)\in X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}
, define the summing metric
M_{1}
by

M_{1}\big((x,y,z),(u,v,w)\big):=d_{1}(x,u)+d_{2}(y,v)+d_{3}(z,w).

The architecture-dependent short-hands are

\displaystyle M_{2}^{S}\big((x,y,z),(u,v,w)\big)

4
Market Equilibrium in the Stackelberg Model

4.1
Set up and a Characterization

Our approach is to re-cast the first order optimality conditions (
4
) as a tripled fixed point problem. Specifically, for Firm 3 (the follower) the condition
\dfrac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}(x,y,z)=0
is equivalent to

\dfrac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}(x,y,z)+z=z
. Define the response map

F_{3}(x,y,z)=\frac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}(x,y,z)+z
, so that the follower equilibrium condition is

F_{3}(x,y,z)=z
. Suppose that there exists an implicit function
z=z(x,y)
making this equilibrium condition an identity, i.e.

F_{3}\bigl(x,y,z(x,y)\bigr)=z(x,y)\quad\mbox{if and only if}\quad\frac{\partial\Pi_{3}}{\partial z}\bigl(x,y,z(x,y)\bigr)=0.

For Firm 2 (the middle player), the first-order condition

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dy}\,\Pi_{2}\bigl(x,y,F_{3}(x,y,z)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi_{2}}{\partial y}\bigl(x,y,F_{3}(x,y,z)\bigr)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi_{2}}{\partial w}\bigl(x,y,F_{3}(x,y,z)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial y}(x,y,z)=0.

is equivalent to
F_{2}(x,y,z)=y
, where as before
F_{2}(x,y,z)=\frac{d}{dy}\,\Pi_{2}\bigl(x,y,F_{3}(x,y,z)\bigr)+y
.

Finally, for firm 1 (the leader), the conditions
F_{1}(x,y,z)=x
are equivalent to the first order condition

\dfrac{d}{dx}\Pi_{1}(x,y,z)=0
, where

F_{1}(x,y,z):=\frac{d}{dx}\,\Pi_{1}\bigl(x,\,F_{2}(x,y,z),\,F_{3}(x,y,z)\bigr)+x.

.

Thus we arrived at an equivalence between the first order (maximization) conditions for equilbrium in the canonical Stackelberg model and the problem of mixed tripled fixed points. Define
\mathcal{G}_{1}:X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}\to X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}
by

\mathcal{G}_{1}(\overline{x})=\Big(F_{1}\big(x,F_{2}(x,y,F_{3}(\overline{x})),F_{3}(\overline{x})\big),\;F_{2}(x,y,F_{3}(\overline{x})),\;F_{3}(\overline{x})\Big),

(8)

where we use the notation
\overline{x}=(x,y,z)
.

In a Stackelberg tripodal market, an ordered triple
(x,y,z)

4

Abstracting for a moment from the second order sufficient conditions for the solutions of (

The first order equation for the follower is obtained from the equation

5.2
Total Surplus

Under the inverse demand
P(Q)=A-BQ
, total surplus is

TS(Q)=\int_{0}^{Q}P(t)\,dt-QP(Q)=AQ-\frac{B}{2}Q^{2}-AQ+BQ^{2}=\frac{B}{2}Q^{2}.

Hence

TS(Q_{n}^{C,Q})=\frac{B}{2}(Q_{n}^{C,Q})^{2},\qquad TS(Q_{n}^{S,Q})=\frac{B}{2}(Q_{n}^{S,Q})^{2}.

By (
15
) it follows
TS(Q_{n}^{C,Q})<TS(Q_{n}^{S,Q})
and that the benefits for the consumers are more in the Stackelberg’s settings than in the Cournot model.
Since

\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{C,Q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S,Q}=M=\frac{A}{B}

it follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}TS(Q_{n}^{C,Q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}TS(Q_{n}^{S,Q})=\int_{0}^{M}(A-Bt)\,dt-MP(M)=\frac{A^{2}}{2B}.

For the numerical values used in this paper,

\frac{A^{2}}{2B}=9607520160.

5.3
Quantities

For every
n\geq 2
, the hierarchical Stackelberg model produces strictly more total output
than the Cournot model:

Q_{n}^{S,Q}>Q_{n}^{C,Q}.

Consequently, the Stackelberg price is lower and total surplus is higher for every finite

n\geq 2

\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S,Q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{C,Q}=M=\frac{A}{B}=619920,

so the large-market limit is the same competitive benchmark. However, the hierarchical
Stackelberg sequence converges faster than the Cournot sequence.

5.4.5
Summary

For a fixed cost structure, Stackelberg yields weakly higher total output and weakly lower
price than Cournot, with strict inequalities for
n\geq 2
.

For linear costs, there holds
Q_{n}^{S,L}>Q_{n}^{C,L}
and
P_{n}^{S,L}<P_{n}^{C,L}
.

For quadratic costs, we have
Q_{n}^{S,Q}>Q_{n}^{C,Q}
and
P_{n}^{S,Q}<P_{n}^{C,Q}
.

Thus, in both cost specifications, Stackelberg competition generates more total output and a lower price than Cournot competition.

For a fixed market structure, linear costs and quadratic costs can have different finite-
n
orderings, while their limiting quantities are
\frac{A-c}{B}=M-\frac{1}{2}
under linear costs and

\frac{A}{B}=M
under quadratic costs.

The fastest convergence result is the linear-cost Stackelberg formula
Q_{n}^{S,L}=\widetilde{M}(1-2^{-n})
,
which implies geometric convergence, whereas the remaining three cases converge at rate

O(n^{-1})
.

More succinctly:

•

Effect of the cost structure:

Quadratic costs penalize large outputs more strongly, leading to lower equilibrium quantities and higher prices for finite
n
.

•

Effect of market structure:

Stackelberg competition yields higher output and lower prices than Cournot competition.

•

Large-market limit:

(Q,P)\to\left(\frac{A-c}{B},\,c\right)
for linear cost,
(Q,P)\to\left(\frac{A}{B},\,0\right)
for quadratic costs.

Appendix A
Background on Tupled Fixed Points

A.1
Fixed Points for Ordered Triples of Maps

The Banach fixed point concept
[
7
]
, despite being over 100 years old, has numerous extensions and implementations. The generalizations can be classified in different ways. We are only interested in the recommended generalization
[
22
]
, which alters the concept of fixed points. Coupled fixed points, introduced in
[
22
]
, are significant in this context.

By considering a map of two variables
F:A\times A\to A
(
[
10
,
22
]
) the notion of a coupled fixed point
(x,y)\in A\times A
of
F
in
A
is defined as
x=F(x,y)
and
y=F(y,x)
.

The concept of exploring fixed points for their existence and uniqueness in cyclic maps, i.e.,
T:A\to B
and
T:B\to A
, verses self-maps was initially presented in
[
28
]
.

By combining the ideas of cyclic maps and coupled fixed points the meaning of fixed points was broadened to coupled fixed points for cyclic maps of two variables in
[
43
]
by investigating of maps
F:A\times A\to B
,
F:B\times B\to A
, and the search of sufficient conditions that will ensure the existence of an ordered pair
(x,y)
so that
x=F(x,y)
and
y=F(y,x)
.

However, the equations
x=F(x,y)
and
y=F(y,x)
that define the linked fixed points
[
10
,
22
]
frequently result in an ordered pair
(x,y)
such that
x=y
. This limitation was solved in
[
51
]
, wherein a modified coupled fixed point for an ordered pair of maps
(F,G)
was developed. It is suggested in
[
51
]
to combine two maps
F:A\times B\to A
,
G:A\times B\to B
and defining an ordered pair
(x,y)
as a coupled fixed point for
(F,G)
if
x=F(x,y)
and
y=G(x,y)
. The definition from
[
10
,
22
]
is obtained when
G(x,y)=F(y,x)
, and
A=B
.

According to
[
22
]
, it has been suggested to seek for an ordered triple
(x,y,z)
fulfilling
x=F(x,y,z)
,
y=F(y,x,y)
, and
z=F(z,y,x)
,
[
9
]
. In
[
24
]
, generalizations based on the aforementioned have been presented, i.e.
x=F(x,y,z)
,
y=G(x,y,z)
, and
z=H(x,y,z)
for
F,G,H:A^{3}\to A
. The concept of a tripled fixed point extends to tripled points for ordered triples of maps
(F,G,H)
that meet the contractive type condition. We will use a simplified version of the main results from
[
24
]
.

A.2
Hardy-Rogers Fixed Point Theorem

Definition 5
.

(
[
23
,
38
]
)
Let
(X,\rho)
be a metric space. A map
T:X\to X
is called a Hardy-Rogers map if there exist non-negative constants
a_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4,5
, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{5}a_{i}<1
and for all
x,y\in X
the following inequality holds:

\rho(Tx,Ty)\leq a_{1}\rho(x,y)+a_{2}\rho(x,Tx)+a_{3}\rho(y,Ty)+a_{4}\rho(x,Ty)+a_{5}\rho(y,Tx).

(16)

As pointed out in
[
23
]
, by the symmetry of the metric
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
, inequality(
16
) implies:

\rho(Tx,Ty)\leq a_{1}\rho(x,y)+\frac{a_{2}+a_{3}}{2}\left(\rho(x,Tx)+\rho(y,Ty)\right)+\frac{a_{4}+a_{5}}{2}\left(\rho(x,Ty)+\rho(y,Tx)\right).

Therefore, without loss of generality, we may consider Hardy-Rogers maps to satisfy the simplified inequality:

\rho(Tx,Ty)\leq k_{1}\rho(x,y)+k_{2}\left(\rho(x,Tx)+\rho(y,Ty)\right)+k_{3}\left(\rho(x,Ty)+\rho(y,Tx)\right),

(17)

with
k_{1}+2k_{2}+2k_{3}<1
.

Special cases include:

•

k_{2}=k_{3}=0
: Banach contraction
[
7
]
,

•

k_{1}=k_{3}=0
: Kannan contraction
[
27
]
,

•

k_{1}=k_{2}=0
: Chatterjea contraction
[
16
]
.

In the rest of this work, we will assume Hardy-Rogers maps satisfy inequality (
17
).

Theorem 5
.

(
[
23
]
)
Let
(X,\rho)
be a complete metric space and
T:X\rightarrow X
be a Hardy-Rogers map, then:

1.

there is a unique fixed point
\xi\in X
of
T
and, moreover, for any initial guess
x_{0}\in X
, the iterated sequence
x_{n}=Tx_{n-1}
for
n=1,2,\ldots
converges to the fixed point
\xi
;

2.

there holds a priori error estimate:

\rho(\xi,x_{n})\leq\frac{k^{n}}{1-k}\rho(x_{0},x_{1})
;

3.

there holds a posteriori error estimate:
\rho(\xi,x_{n})\leq\frac{k}{1-k}\rho(x_{n-1},x_{n})
;

4.

the rate of convergence is:
\rho(\xi,x_{n})\leq k\rho(\xi,x_{n-1})
,

where
k=\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{1-k_{2}-k_{3}}
and
k_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
are the constants from (
17
).

Proposition 6
.

(Response functions in the hierarchical Stackelberg model)
Let

\Pi_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=x_{k}\Bigl(A-B\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\Bigr)-cx_{k}^{2}

with
A=30996
,
B=\frac{1}{20}
, and
c=\frac{1}{40}
.

The output of follower number
k
, i.e., player
n-k
is equal to

x_{k}=\mu_{\,n-k+1}\Bigl(M-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}x_{i}\Bigr),\qquad k=1,\dots,n,

where
M=20A=619920
,
\mu_{m}=\frac{1-\lambda_{m-1}}{3-2\lambda_{m-1}}
,
\lambda_{0}=0
, and

\lambda_{m}=\frac{1+\lambda_{m-1}-\lambda_{m-1}^{2}}{3-2\lambda_{m-1}}
for
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

Step 1: the last player:

Fix
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
and denote
S_{n-1}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-1}
. Then player
n
solves

\max_{x_{n}}\{\Pi_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\}=\max_{x_{n}}\left\{x_{n}\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}(S_{n-1}+x_{n})\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x_{n}^{2}\right\}.

After expansion, we obtain
\Pi_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=Ax_{n}-\frac{1}{20}S_{n-1}x_{n}-\frac{3}{40}x_{n}^{2}
.

Hence
\frac{\partial\Pi_{n}}{\partial x_{n}}=A-\frac{1}{20}S_{n-1}-\frac{3}{20}x_{n}
.
The first-order condition
\frac{\partial\Pi_{n}}{\partial x_{n}}=0
yields

A-\frac{1}{20}S_{n-1}-\frac{3}{20}x_{n}=0,

or equivalently,
20A-S_{n-1}-3x_{n}=0
.

Therefore,
x_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})=\frac{M-S_{n-1}}{3}=\frac{619920-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}}{3}
. Since

\frac{\partial^{2}\Pi_{n}}{\partial x_{n}^{2}}=-\frac{3}{20}<0
, this critical point is the unique maximizer.

Step 2: the player
n-1
:

Now fix
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-2}
and denote
S_{n-2}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-2}
. Substituting the best response of player
n
, we obtain
x_{n}=\frac{M-(S_{n-2}+x_{n-1})}{3}
. Player
n-1
maximizes the reduced payoff

\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})=x_{n-1}\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}\bigl(S_{n-2}+x_{n-1}+x_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})\bigr)\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x_{n-1}^{2}.

Let
x=x_{n-1}
,
S=S_{n-2}
. Then
x_{n}(x)=\frac{M-S-x}{3}
,
x_{n}^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{3}
. Thus

\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-2},x)=x\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}(S+x+x_{n}(x))\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x^{2},

and differentiation gives
\frac{d\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-1}}{dx}=A-\frac{1}{20}(S+x+x_{n})-\frac{x}{20}(1+x_{n}^{\prime})-\frac{x}{20}
. Since
x_{n}^{\prime}=-1/3
, this becomes
\frac{d\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-1}}{dx}=A-\frac{1}{20}(S+x+x_{n})-\frac{x}{12}
. Substituting

x_{n}=\frac{M-S-x}{3}
, we have

S+x+x_{n}=\frac{2S+2x+M}{3}.

Hence the first-order condition is
A-\frac{1}{20}\cdot\frac{2S+2x+M}{3}-\frac{x}{12}=0
.
Since
20A=M
, it follows that

2M-2S-7x=0
.

Therefore,

x_{n-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-2})=\frac{2}{7}(M-S_{n-2})=\frac{2}{7}\Bigl(619920-\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}x_{i}\Bigr).

After that,

x_{n}=\frac{M-S_{n-2}-x_{n-1}}{3}=\frac{M-S_{n-2}-\frac{2}{7}(M-S_{n-2})}{3}=\frac{5}{21}(M-S_{n-2}),

and thus the aggregate output of the last two players is
x_{n-1}+x_{n}=\frac{11}{21}(M-S_{n-2})
.

Step 3: the player
n-2
:

Proceeding one step further, let
S_{n-3}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-3}
. From the previous step, once
x_{n-2}
is fixed, the last two players jointly produce
\frac{11}{21}(M-S_{n-3}-x_{n-2})
.

Let us put
x=x_{n-2}
. Hence player
n-2
payoff will be

\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3},x)=x\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}\Bigl(S_{n-3}+x+\frac{11}{21}(M-S_{n-3}-x)\Bigr)\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x^{2}=0.

Setting
S=S_{n-3}
, we obtain
S+x+\frac{11}{21}(M-S-x)=\frac{10}{21}(S+x)+\frac{11}{21}M
.
Therefore,

\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-2}(x)=x\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}\Bigl(\frac{10}{21}(S+x)+\frac{11}{21}M\Bigr)\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x^{2}.

Differentiation yields to

\frac{d\widetilde{\Pi}_{n-2}}{dx}=A-\frac{1}{42}S-\frac{11}{420}M-\frac{41}{420}x.

Using
A=M/20
, and simplifying the last equality we get
10M-10S-41x=0
.

Consequently,
x_{n-2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})=\frac{10}{41}(M-S_{n-3})=\frac{10}{41}\Bigl(619920-\sum_{i=1}^{n-3}x_{i}\Bigr)
.

Backward recursive structure:

The previous computations reveal the general pattern. Let
F_{m}(S)
denote the total output produced by the last
m
players in the hierarchy when the previously chosen aggregate quantity is
S
. We claim that
F_{m}(S)=\lambda_{m}(M-S)
, for a recursively defined sequence
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
.

For
m=1
, the last player solves
\max_{x}\left\{x\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}(S+x)\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x^{2}\right\}
. The first-order condition gives
x=\frac{M-S}{3}
.
Therefore,
\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{3}
and
F_{1}(S)=\frac{M-S}{3}
.

Assume now that
F_{m-1}(T)=\lambda_{m-1}(M-T)
for every feasible
T
. Consider a subgame with
m
players and suppose that the current leader chooses
x
after the previous aggregate quantity
S
has already been produced. Then the remaining
m-1
followers jointly produce

F_{m-1}(S+x)=\lambda_{m-1}(M-S-x).

Hence the leader’s reduced payoff is

\widetilde{\Pi}_{m}(x;S)=x\Bigl(A-\frac{1}{20}\bigl(S+x+\lambda_{m-1}(M-S-x)\bigr)\Bigr)-\frac{1}{40}x^{2}.

Since
S+x+\lambda_{m-1}(M-S-x)=\lambda_{m-1}M+(1-\lambda_{m-1})S+(1-\lambda_{m-1})x
,
we obtain

\widetilde{\Pi}_{m}(x;S)=Ax-\frac{\lambda_{m-1}M}{20}x-\frac{1-\lambda_{m-1}}{20}Sx-\frac{1-\lambda_{m-1}}{20}x^{2}-\frac{1}{40}x^{2}.

Differentiating with respect to

, we get

\widetilde{\Pi}_{m}^{\prime}(x;S)=A-\frac{\lambda_{m-1}M}{20}-\frac{1-\lambda_{m-1}}{20}S-\frac{3-2\lambda_{m-1}}{20}x.

Using

, the first-order condition
\widetilde{\Pi}_{m}^{\prime}(x;S)=0
becomes

(1-\lambda_{m-1})(M-S)=(3-2\lambda_{m-1})x
, and therefore

x=\mu_{m}(M-S),\qquad\mu_{m}=\frac{1-\lambda_{m-1}}{3-2\lambda_{m-1}}.

(18)

Consequently, the total output of these

and hence

\lambda_{m}(M-S)=\mu_{m}(M-S)+\lambda_{m-1}\bigl((M-S)-\mu_{m}(M-S)\bigr).

Dividing by

Substituting

(19)

∎

is defined recursively by

Q_{0}^{S,Q}=0,\qquad Q_{n}^{S,Q}=\frac{M^{2}+MQ_{n-1}^{S,Q}-(Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})^{2}}{3M-2Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}},\qquad n\geq 1.

(20)

Proof.

Let
Q_{n}^{S,Q}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}

Since the initial previously chosen quantity is

Proposition 8
.

(Monotonicity, boundedness, and convergence of the aggregate Stackelberg output)
The sequence
\left\{Q_{n}^{S,Q}\right\}_{n\geq 0}
defined by (
20
) is strictly increasing, bounded above by
M
, and convergent with
\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S}=M
.

Proof.

Define the function
f:[0,M)\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(x)=\frac{M^{2}+Mx-x^{2}}{3M-2x}
. Then the recurrence relation (
20
) can be written as
Q_{n}^{S,Q}=f(Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})
for
n\geq 1
.

We first prove by induction that
Q_{n}^{S,Q}\in[0,M)
for
n\geq 0
.

For
n=0
, this is obvious since
Q_{0}^{S,Q}=0
.

Assume that the inequality holds true for
n-1
, i.e.,
0\leq Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}<M
. By the inequality

3M-2Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}>3M-2M=M>0,

it follows that
Q_{n}^{S,Q}
is well defined, i.e. the denominator is strictly positive.

Next, we show that
Q_{n}^{S,Q}<M
. Since the denominator is strictly positive,
the inequality
Q_{n}^{S,Q}<M
holds if and only if the inequality

\frac{M^{2}+MQ_{n-1}^{S,Q}-(Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})^{2}}{3M-2Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}}<M

holds.
The last inequality is equivalent to
0<2M^{2}-3MQ_{n-1}^{S,Q}+(Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})^{2}
.

After factoring, we obtain
(M-Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})(2M-Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})>0
,
which holds ture, because
Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}<M
. Therefore
Q_{n}^{S,Q}<M
.

Also, the numerator
M^{2}+MQ_{n-1}^{S,Q}-(Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})^{2}=M^{2}+Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}(M-Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})

is positive, and the denominator is positive as well, so
Q_{n}^{S,Q}\geq 0
.

Thus,
Q_{n}^{S,Q}\in[0,M)
holds for all
n\geq 0
.

In particular,
\left\{Q_{n}^{S,Q}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is bounded above by
M
.

Now we show that
\left\{Q_{n}^{S,Q}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is strictly increasing. For
x\in[0,M)
,

f(x)-x=\frac{M^{2}+Mx-x^{2}}{3M-2x}-x=\frac{(M-x)^{2}}{3M-2x}.

Since
x<M
, the denominator is positive, and thus

f(x)-x>0
for all
x\in[0,M)
. Because
Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}\in[0,M)
, we have
Q_{n}^{S}=f(Q_{n-1}^{S,Q})>Q_{n-1}^{S,Q}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Hence the sequence is strictly increasing.

Since
\left\{Q_{n}^{S,Q}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is increasing and bounded above, it is convergent. Let

\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S,Q}=L.

By continuity of
f
on
[0,M)
and the fact that
L\leq M
, passing to the limit in the recurrence relation yields
L=\frac{M^{2}+ML-L^{2}}{3M-2L}
. Therefore,
L=M
, i.e.,
\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S,Q}=M
.
∎

Remark 3
.

By proposition we can say that the aggregate output in the hierarchical Stackelberg model converges to the limit
M
faster than the aggregate output in the Cournot model.

Proof.

Define
g(x)=\frac{x(1+x)}{1+2x}
for
x>0
. Then
\rho_{n}=g(\rho_{n-1})
.
We get
g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1+2x+2x^{2}}{(1+2x)^{2}}>0
. Hence
g
is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
.

We prove by induction that
\rho_{n}\leq\frac{2}{n+2}
for all
n\geq 1
.

For
n=1
there holds
\rho_{1}=\frac{\rho_{0}(1+\rho_{0})}{1+2\rho_{0}}=\frac{2}{3}=\frac{2}{1+2}
.

Assume that the inequality holds for
n-1
, i.e.,
\rho_{n-1}\leq\frac{2}{n+1}
.

Since
g
is increasing,
\rho_{n}=g(\rho_{n-1})\leq g\!\left(\frac{2}{n+1}\right)
.
After some computations we get that

g\!\left(\frac{2}{n+1}\right)=\frac{2(n+3)}{(n+1)(n+5)}\leq\frac{2}{n+2}.

holds for all
n\geq 1
. Thus
\rho_{n}\leq\frac{2}{n+2}
.

Consequently
1-\frac{Q_{n}^{S,Q}}{M}=\rho_{n}\leq\frac{2}{n+2}

\frac{Q_{n}^{S,Q}}{M}\geq 1-\frac{2}{n+2}=\frac{n}{n+2}
, which implies
Q_{n}^{S,Q}\geq\frac{Mn}{n+2}=Q_{n}^{C,Q}
.

Finally, since
g
is strictly increasing and the induction starts with equality only at
n=1
, the inequality becomes strict for every
n\geq 2
. Therefore

Q_{n}^{S,Q}>Q_{n}^{C,Q}
for all
n\geq 2
.

By Proposition
10
we have the equality

\rho_{n}=\frac{\rho_{n-1}(1+\rho_{n-1})}{1+2\rho_{n-1}}
and can rewrite it as

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{n}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1+2\rho_{n-1}}{\rho_{n-1}(1+\rho_{n-1})}=\displaystyle\frac{1+\rho_{n-1}}{\rho_{n-1}(1+\rho_{n-1})}+\frac{\rho_{n-1}}{\rho_{n-1}(1+\rho_{n-1})}\\

Hence
\frac{1}{\rho_{n}}-\frac{1}{\rho_{n-1}}=\frac{1}{1+\rho_{n-1}}
.
Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}=0

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{n}}-\frac{1}{\rho_{n-1}}\right)=1
.

Now the sequence

, so by Stolz–Cesàro theorem,

.

Thus
\frac{1}{\rho_{n}}\sim n
, hence
\rho_{n}\sim\frac{1}{n}
. Multiplying by

.

On the other hand,

Therefore, the Stackelberg residual is asymptotically one-half of the Cournot residual, which shows that the hierarchical Stackelberg aggregate output converges to the limit

Cournot and Stackelberg Models for a Linear Inverse Demand and linear cost functions

Let consider a market model with a linear price function and a linear cost function for all players, i.e.,

\Pi_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=x_{k}\Bigl(A-B\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\Bigr)-cx_{k}
with
A=30996
,
B=\frac{1}{20}
, and
c=\frac{1}{40}
.

This model is widely used for illustrations in text books
[
20
,
44
]
. Let us use the notation

M=\frac{A}{B}=619920
.

It is easy to get the total Cournot output for linear cost function
Q_{n}^{C,L}=\frac{n(A-c)}{B(n+1)}
. The corresponding price is
P_{n}^{C,L}=\frac{A+nc}{n+1}
. Each firm’s output is
x_{n}^{C,L}=\frac{A-c}{B(n+1)}
and
each firm’s profit is
\Pi_{n}^{C,L}=\frac{(A-c)^{2}}{B(n+1)^{2}}
, and total industry profit is

\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},n}^{C,L}=\frac{n(A-c)^{2}}{B(n+1)^{2}}
.

C.2
Comparison of the two Cournot cost structures

In the present numerical setting
2c=B
, we have

Q_{n}^{C,L}=\frac{n(A-c)}{B(n+1)}=\frac{\left(M-\frac{1}{2}\right)n}{n+1},\qquad Q_{n}^{C,Q}=\frac{Mn}{n+2}.

Thus
Q_{n}^{C,L}-Q_{n}^{C,Q}=\frac{n(2M-n-2)}{2(n+1)(n+2)}

For finite
n
, the sign of
Q_{n}^{C,L}-Q_{n}^{C,Q}
is determined by the sign of

2M-n-2
. In our illustrative example,
Q_{n}^{C,L}>Q_{n}^{C,Q}
and
P_{n}^{C,L}<P_{n}^{C,Q}
for
n<1239838
, which is actually closer to a competitive free market, rather than an oligopoly one. Then, for an oligopoly market,
Q_{n}^{C,L}>Q_{n}^{C,Q}
, i.e., a linear cost function leads to greater outputs at the equilibrium levels.

If
M=\frac{A}{B}
is small, then for small number of players in the market will
happen
Q_{n}^{C,L}<Q_{n}^{C,Q}
. Thus the equlibrium level of production depend both on the price functions and the cost function.

Thus, in the present linear-demand framework, quadratic costs lead to
lower output and therefore to a higher market price.

C.3
Asymptotic Cournot comparison as
n\to\infty

Linear costs:

Q_{n}^{C,L}=\frac{n(A-c)}{B(n+1)}
,
Q_{\infty}^{C,L}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{C,L}=M-\frac{1}{2}
, and
P_{\infty}^{C,L}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}^{C,L}=c
.

Quadratic costs:

Q_{\infty}^{C,Q}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{C,Q}=M
, and
P_{\infty}^{C,Q}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}^{C,Q}=0
.

Profits:

\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},\infty}^{C,L}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},n}^{C,L}=0
and

\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},\infty}^{C,Q}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},n}^{C,Q}=0
.

C.4
Stackelberg equilibrium with linear costs

By similar, just a little bit easier calculations, to those from Section
B
we can obtain for linear costs
C(x)=cx
we get
x_{k}^{S,L}(n)=\frac{A-c}{B}2^{-k}
, for
k=1,\dots,n
. This model have been commented in
[
3
]
, that each firm output is half of the output of th immediate predecessor.

After a summation on
k
we get
Q_{n}^{S,L}=\frac{A-c}{B}\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr)
. Hence
P_{n}^{S,L}=c+(A-c)2^{-n}
.

Profits:

\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},n}^{S,L}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{A-c}{B2^{k}}\left(A-B\frac{A-c}{B}(1-2^{-n})\right)-c\frac{A-c}{B2^{k}}\right)=\frac{(A-c)^{2}}{B}\cdot\frac{2^{n}-1}{4^{n}}.

C.5
Comparison of the two Stakelberg cost structures

In the present numerical setting
2c=B
and
A=MB
, we have

Q_{n}^{S,L}=\frac{A-c}{B}\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr)=\frac{2M-1}{2}\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr)=\left(M-\frac{1}{2}\right)(1-2^{-n})

and an asymptotic behavior
Q_{n}^{S,Q}\sim\frac{M(n-1)}{n}
for large
n
.

Consequently, for large
n
there holds
Q_{n}^{S,L}>Q_{n}^{S,Q}
and
P_{n}^{S,L}<P_{n}^{S,Q}
.

Thus, in the present linear-demand framework, quadratic costs lead to
lower output and therefore to a higher market price.

C.6
Asymptotic Stakelberg comparison as
n\to\infty

Linear costs:

Q_{\infty}^{S,L}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S,L}=\frac{A-c}{B}
and

P_{\infty}^{S,L}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}^{S,L}=c
.

Quadratic costs:

Q_{\infty}^{S,Q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}^{S,Q}=M
and
P_{\infty}^{S,Q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}^{S,Q}=0
.

Profits:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},n}^{S,L}\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},\infty}^{S,L}=0
and

\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},\infty}^{S,Q}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi_{\mathrm{tot},\infty}^{S,Q}=0
.

C.7
Comparison between Cournot and Stackelberg

For linear costs, using the representations for equilibrium quantities it is ready to observe
that
(1-2^{-n})>\frac{n}{n+1}
for all
n\geq 2
it follows that
Q_{n}^{S,L}>Q_{n}^{C,L}
and hence

P_{n}^{S,L}<P_{n}^{C,L}
.

For quadratic costs, by Proposition
11
, there holds
Q_{n}^{S,Q}>Q_{n}^{C,Q}
and hence

P_{n}^{S,Q}<P_{n}^{C,Q}
.

Thus, in both cost specifications, Stackelberg competition generates more total output and a lower price than Cournot competition.
