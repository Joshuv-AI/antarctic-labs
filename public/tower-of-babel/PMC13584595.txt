A school-year mini basketball curricular program promotes fundamental movement skills of urban Chinese preschoolers with typical development
Authors: Shouwen Zhang, Juntao Yan, Yan Shao, Junqian Yan, Colleen s O’Connell, Menghan Wu, Meiqi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to investigate the effects of a school-year skill-based mini basketball program on the fundamental movement skills (FMS) of urban Chinese preschoolers. A cluster randomized trial was conducted, in which 18 intact classes were randomly assigned to either the mini basketball (MB; 9 classes) or conventional physical education (CP; 9 classes) group. Children in the MB group received the MB curricular intervention, while the CP group received regular physical education curriculum representing standard practice in urban Chinese preschools. Motor development was evaluated by the Test of Gross Motor Development 2nd Edition. The results of the 2 × 2 (time × group) multivariate analysis of variance with repeated measures showed that the MB program was associated with greater improvements in FMS compared to the CP program, suggesting an added benefit beyond typical developmental progression. Our findings also revealed that the effects of MB on object control were influenced by age, with four- and five-year-old children demonstrating greater improvements than three-year-olds. These findings suggest that MB appears to be a feasible and potentially effective intervention to promote FMS development in preschool settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07207-5.Keywords: Preschool, Structured mini basketball program, Physical education curricular program, Motor development

Fundamental movement skills (FMS)—comprising locomotor (e.g., running, jumping), object control (e.g., throwing, catching), and stability (e.g., balancing) skills—are foundational for children’s physical, cognitive, and psychosocial development [1, 2]. Proficiency in FMS during early childhood is strongly associated with improved physical health outcomes, including healthier body mass index [3], enhanced cardiorespiratory fitness [4], and reduced risk of metabolic disorders [5]. Beyond physical benefits, FMS competence correlates with advanced executive functioning [6], academic readiness [7], and emotional resilience [8]. Specifically, preschoolers with well-developed FMS exhibit greater participation in physical activities (PA), reducing sedentary behavior and fostering long-term PA adherence [9]. Conversely, motor skill deficits in preschoolers are linked to heightened risks of anxiety, social withdrawal, and depressive symptoms [10–12], highlighting the urgent need for evidence-based strategies to promote FMS during this critical developmental period.

Despite their importance, global studies report declining FMS proficiency among children, with many failing to meet developmental milestones by school entry [13, 14]. Early childhood (ages 3–6 years) represents a sensitive period for motor skill acquisition, where neural plasticity and environmental exposure synergistically shape lifelong movement patterns [15]. While structured physical activity programs are recommended to optimize this window [16], conventional preschool physical education (PE) curricula often prioritize unstructured play over systematic FMS instruction [17].

Mini-basketball (MB)—a developmentally adapted version of basketball designed for young children—may represent a promising intervention to address this gap. Unlike traditional basketball, MB simplifies rules, uses smaller equipment (e.g., lower hoops, lighter balls), and emphasizes fun, skill-building activities tailored to preschoolers’ motor capabilities [18]. MB integrates dynamic, task-oriented activities that target agility, coordination, and power, aligning with FMS developmental milestones [19, 20]. Compared to other sports (e.g., handball, soccer), MB uniquely combines locomotor (e.g., running, changing direction), object control (e.g., dribbling, passing), and stability skills (e.g., landing, pivoting) in a single play-based format, which may be particularly beneficial for holistic motor development [9].

Although studies have reported benefits of MB in improving locomotor skills in elementary school-aged children (6–12 years; [21, 22], its applicability to preschoolers—particularly during the imitative stage (ages 3–5 years), when motor learning occurs through observation and repetition—remains understudied. This oversight is significant, as preschoolers require pedagogical approaches that balance skill progression with play-based engagement [13]. Recent research suggests that MB, when modified with age-appropriate challenges (e.g., simplified drills, storytelling integration), can enhance motor competence while maintaining high engagement levels in preschoolers [23].

To address this gap, we conducted a cluster-randomized study to evaluate the potential feasibility and efficacy of a year-long curricular MB program in promoting FMS in Chinese preschoolers. This study builds upon previous research demonstrating the benefits of structured motor skill interventions [5] while addressing the critical need for age-appropriate, sport-based programs in early childhood settings [16].

Methods

Participants

We recruited 356 Chinese preschoolers (48% female; Mean Age = 4.7 years, SD = 0.8) from 18 intact classes across three urban preschools in Beijing. Using a cluster-randomized design, we assigned classes to either the mini-basketball (MB; n = 9 classes) or conventional physical education (CP; n = 9 classes) condition. Participants came predominantly from middle-class families. Exclusion criteria included: (a) lack of parental consent, (b) child unwillingness to participate, or (c) documented health conditions restricting physical activity participation (as reported by parents or teachers).

Instruments

We assessed fundamental movement skills (FMS) using the Test of Gross Motor Development-Second Edition (TGMD-2; Ulrich, 2000). This standardized assessment comprises two subscales: locomotor skills (Running, galloping, hopping, leaping, horizontal jumping, and sliding) and object control skills (Striking, dribbling, catching, kicking, overhand throwing, and underhand rolling). Each skill was evaluated using 3–5 performance criteria scored dichotomously (1 = present, 0 = absent). Following standard administration protocols [24], trained assessors: (1) Provided verbal instructions and physical demonstrations; (2) Allowed one practice trial; (3) Recorded two scored trials per skill; (4) Offered additional clarification when needed. Raw scores were converted to age- and gender-normed standard scores using TGMD-2 norms.

Procedures

We conducted the study during the 2020–2021 academic year (September-July), with assessments occurring two weeks before and after the intervention period. Both groups received equal intervention dosage (Frequency: 3 sessions/week; Session duration: 45 min).

Mini-Basketball (MB) program: The intervention followed the Mini-basketball Coaching Guideline for 3-6-Year-Olds (Chinese Basketball Association & China School Sports Federation, 2020). To maintain engagement across the school year, the MB program incorporated game-based learning elements, including storytelling themes, varied game formats, and progressive challenges. These features were designed to balance skill progression with play-based engagement. A detailed overview of the Mini-Basketball program structure, including session duration, frequency, and specific skill progressions, is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

CP program: The CP curriculum represents the standard physical education practice in urban Chinese preschools, which typically emphasizes creative movement and free play without structured object-control instruction. This comparison was intentionally chosen to evaluate whether a structured, skill-based program yields additional benefits over prevailing practice, rather than to compare basketball to another specific sport. Both groups received equal physical activity dosage (three 45-minute sessions weekly), ensuring that observed differences can be attributed to the structure and content of instruction rather than total activity time. No basketball-specific activities were included in the CP condition.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic data was compared between groups using chi-square tests. A 2 × 2 (time × group) multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) with repeated measures was used to compare changes in TGMD-2 scores between groups. Percentage stacked bar charts were used to clearly compare changes in overall motor development. Kruskal-Wallis H tests were conducted to examine age-specific training effects. Post-hoc Mann-Whitney U tests with Bonferroni adjustments were used for pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Preliminary data screening confirmed that assumptions for MANOVA (i.e., normality, homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices) were met. One outlier was identified, and mean imputation was applied. Data was analyzed using Stata 17 [25] and visualized using the “ggplot2” package [26] in R (Version of 4.1.0; R Core Team, 2016). The alpha level for all statistical procedures was set at 0.05.

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Capital Normal University. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participating children. All the procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments.

To evaluate overall changes in motor development by the school-year intervention, a 2 × 2 MANOVA with repeated measures was performed using two subtests (i.e., locomotor and object control) as dependent variables. The factors were group (MB and CP) and time occasion (baseline and posttest). Age-specific impact

Kruskal-Wallis H tests were conducted to investigate the age-specific changes in motor development after the school-year curricular program. Because the impact of conventional PE was not of interest in the present study and no obvious trend was observed in the data (Fig. 4), it is not discussed further. No significant age effect was found in the locomotor scale [3yrs old: Median (Mdn) = 8, range = 15; 4yrs old: Mdn = 8, range = 20; 5yrs old: Mdn = 8, range = 19], H(2) = 3.900, p = 0.142 (Fig. 4). However, age significantly affected the performance of object control, H(2) = 7.983, p = 0.018. Children aged 4yrs old (Mdn = 11, range = 18) and 5yrs old (Mdn = 10, range = 17) had greater improvement in object control performance than children aged 3yrs old (Mdn = 8, range = 15; Fig. 4). Post-hoc Mann-Whitney U tests using Bonferroni adjustments confirmed that the 3yrs old group significantly differed from both 4yrs old and 5yrs old groups, U(n3yrs old = 52; n4yrs old = 67) = 19.380, z = 2.557, p = 0.011, and U(n3yrs old = 52; n5yrs old = 23) = 22.144, z = 2.156, p = 0.031; whereas there was no significant difference between 4yrs old and 5yrs old groups, U(n4yrs old = 67; n5yrs old = 23) = 2.764, z = 0.279, p = 0.780. Task-specific baseline and posttest motor performance are presented in Table S2, Figs. 5, and Fig. 6.

Discussion

Although extensive research has examined curricular-based physical activity interventions in school-aged populations [9, 27], few studies have targeted preschoolers [28]. In this school-year cluster randomized trial, the results suggest that a curricular MB program, compared to a conventional PE program, may be both feasible and potentially beneficial for enhancing FMS (locomotor and object control) in preschool-aged children. Although both groups were expected to show some improvement due to normal developmental progression over the academic year, the MB group demonstrated significantly greater gains, offering preliminary evidence of the added benefit of a structured program beyond typical maturation.

Age-specific effects were also observed. The four- and five-year-olds demonstrated significantly greater improvements in object control skills than the three-year-olds, consistent with developmental expectations, as older preschoolers typically possess greater cognitive and motor readiness for complex, ball-based tasks [29]. No age-related differences were observed in locomotor performance, possibly because these skills emerge earlier in development [29]. These findings raise the possibility that the MB program effectively accelerated skill acquisition in the older subgroup, whereas three-year-olds may require more foundational activities.

Marked increases were observed in several locomotor patterns. Improvements in galloping, sliding, and horizontal jumping may reflect the possibility that MB training enhances dynamic balance, leg strength, and coordination [19]. In contrast, the MB training had less pronounced effects on leaping and hopping. One interpretation is that these movements require higher coordination and may therefore take longer to develop [30, 31].

Children engaged in the MB program also developed improved object control ability, making greater progress in dribbling, striking, and catching than in rolling, throwing, and kicking. Clear age-related changes were observed, with four- and five-year-old children benefiting more than three-year-old children, consistent with developmental expectations, as many object control skills are still emerging at age three [32]. Considering ball size [30], the age effect was more apparent in small-ball tasks (striking, rolling, throwing) than in large-ball tasks (dribbling, catching, kicking). Because the MB protocol emphasized regular practice of large-ball skills, these features may have contributed to consistent improvement across all age groups in large-ball control. Successful small-ball tasks, in comparison, may require extra visual-motor control that develops later in childhood [10, 30, 33–37].

Despite the improvements described above, only a small proportion of MB children achieved average or above-average TGMD-2 classifications at posttest. This may reflect the stringent percentile thresholds of the TGMD-2 norms [24], which require crossing absolute boundaries that children with delayed baselines may not reach within one school year. Statistical significance and normative classification address different constructs: the former detects group mean differences, whereas the latter indicates individual attainment of developmental benchmarks [30]. Thus, while the MB program accelerated skill acquisition, longer or more intensive interventions may be needed to shift normative status for children with initial delays.

The study featured a few strengths. The study used a previously-established MB training guideline that was released by the Chinese Basketball Association, enhancing generalizability. The use of multi-center research design and cluster randomization further enhanced the generalizability of the findings [38, 39]. Because the present study analyzed the age-specific effects of the intervention, the findings offer insights into the feasibility of MB in early childhood education. Additionally, anecdotal feedback from teachers indicated high levels of enjoyment and sustained engagement throughout the year, suggesting that the MB program’s use of game-based learning, storytelling, and progressive skill challenges effectively maintained children’s interest.

However, the study has some limitations. These include the lack of demographic data, physical health measures, parental involvement, and socioeconomic status. Additionally, there was an uneven sample size among the three age groups, with fewer five-year-olds than three and four-year-olds, which may have affected the statistical power. Due to loss of access to participants after they left the preschools (e.g., graduation, transfer to other schools), a follow-up test to observe longitudinal intervention effects was not included. Future research should formally assess engagement and enjoyment as potential mediating factors, address these limitations, replicate the study in different populations, and consider classifying children into developmental stages (e.g., initial, elementary, mature) for each FMS component to provide more granular insight into intervention effects than standard scores alone [40, 41].

Conclusion

The study provides insights into the impact of a curricular MB program on FMS in preschoolers compared to the conventional PE curriculum. The results after a one-year academic intervention suggest that MB training may be a feasible and potentially effective strategy for promoting FMS in this population, offering added value beyond typical developmental maturation. Additionally, this study highlights age-specific effects: The MB program had a consistent effect on locomotor performance across children aged three to five years, whereas object control skills were more influenced by age, with four- and five-year-old children showing greater improvements than three-year-olds. This pattern was also apparent in coordination-based tasks. Regarding practical implications, these findings support the consideration of MB for preschoolers. Further research is needed to understand the long-term effects of preschoolers’ MB engagement.
