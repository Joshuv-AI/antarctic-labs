Implementing seasonal malaria chemoprevention in conflict-affected settings: experiences from Northern Bahr el Ghazal, South Sudan
Authors: Abubaker Rom Deng Ayuiel, Francis Okot, Jamshed Khan, Denis Mubiru, Atemthi D Dau, Amanya Jacob, Ezbon W Apary, Simon Peter Katongole, Aweno Norman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSouth Sudan, a country with a high malaria burden, faces considerable health system challenges due to decades of civil war, which disrupts access to essential services. For three years, the Malaria Consortium has implemented Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention (SMC) in Aweil South and Aweil West Counties; however, coverage remains below optimal at 79%, falling short of established targets. This study examines the barriers and enablers to accessing and distributing SMC during the 2024 round.MethodsA qualitative study design was used, involving 60 stakeholders involved in implementing SMC. Data were collected through 42 key informant interviews and 18 in-depth interviews with caregivers of eligible children who received all SMC doses or none, as well as those of ineligible children who received SMC. The data were analyzed using deductive thematic analysis.ResultsFactors that enabled SMC distribution and access included government support, community trust, efficient logistics, integrated activities, access to vital information, involvement of Boma health workers, supervisor transportation, and organized malaria management. The main challenges included shortages of healthcare workers, drug shortages, geographic and transportation difficulties, caregiver relocation, delayed referrals, and poor communication.ConclusionThese findings underscore the necessity for targeted interventions to address resource constraints, enhance workforce capacity, communication, and logistics infrastructure, thereby ensuring sustainable SMC delivery in conflict-affected areas. Strengthening these areas will be crucial for maximizing the impact of SMC as a malaria prevention strategy in such environments.Keywords: Seasonal malaria chemoprevention, Conflict-affected settings, South Sudan, Enablers, Barriers

Introduction

Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) involves the monthly administration of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine plus amodiaquine (SP + AQ) during the high transmission season [1]. It is effective in preventing new malaria infections by maintaining therapeutic drug levels in the blood [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended SMC since 2012 in areas with highly seasonal malaria transmission, such as sub-Saharan Africa, particularly to reduce malaria episodes in children under five [3]. Past programs in countries such as Nigeria, Burkina Faso, and Chad have demonstrated that when SMC coverage exceeds 90%, there are significant reductions in malaria morbidity and mortality. SMC has been implemented in South Sudan since 2022, where malaria transmission peaks during a specific seasonal period [4]. As part of this intervention, children are treated monthly with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine plus amodiaquine (SPAQ) to sustain therapeutic antimalarial levels during peak transmission seasons [5]. This approach has been shown to reduce clinical malaria and related mortality significantly [6].

Aweil South and Aweil West are two counties in Northern Bahr el Ghazal, South Sudan, that depend heavily on farming and cattle raising [7]. The people in these areas have limited access to essential health services because most health facilities are either non-functional or damaged due to prolonged civil conflict, displacement caused by flooding, poor road conditions, the difficulty of accessing remote areas, or a lack of funding for these facilities [8]. They face major challenges, including inadequate infrastructure, frequent flooding, displacement, food insecurity, and limited access to essential services [9]. As a result, their populations are highly reliant on humanitarian aid and require targeted efforts to build resilience. Malaria is a huge concern in these counties, with seasonal epidemics exacerbated by flooding and limited access to healthcare. In 2024, malaria was responsible for 30% of all reported deaths and 52% of medical visits. The prevalence rate among children under five was 32.5%, i.e., about 33 in every 100 children that were tested for malaria during the assessment tested positive (the numerator was children under five who tested positive, and the denominator was the total number of children under five surveyed) [10].

From June to October 2024, Malaria Consortium distributed SMC to children aged 3–59 months in two counties to prevent malaria. Despite its proven effectiveness, the 2024 End of Round Survey reported a substandard SMC coverage of 79%, which falls short of the standard target for successful implementation. This shortfall highlighted the need to identify and eliminate barriers to achieving optimal coverage in these conflict-affected areas. To explore the challenges and opportunities in delivering SMC, a qualitative study was conducted involving stakeholders in Aweil South and Aweil West Counties.

Methodology

Study location

The study was conducted in November 2024 in the Aweil South and Aweil West counties of Northern Bahr el Ghazal State, South Sudan. These counties experience heavy seasonal rainfall, leading to flooding, and have seasonal malaria transmission patterns that peak between June and October each year [8]. Conflicts in Aweil South are mostly community-based in Aweil East and Gogrial West Payams, stemming from competition over land and water and from ongoing cycles of revenge among livestock owners and farmers. This includes cattle raids, abductions, and property damage, which increase tensions. The return of people from Sudan is adding to the social and economic stress. In Aweil West, disputes over land and clashes with Sudanese cattle keepers over grazing land and water, particularly in payams like Ayat West and Gomjuer West, often lead to conflict and crop damage. Refugees and those returning from Sudan face violence and harassment, further straining local communities. In addition, the ongoing tribal tensions in Sudan, which on some occasions have broken down into insurgencies, have also affected the organization of health services in the study area [11].

Study design

This qualitative study employed key informant interviews to explore the challenges and opportunities associated with implementing SMC in conflict-affected settings.

Study population

Eligible participants included those directly or indirectly involved in implementing SMC in 2024. The Key informant interviews consisted of caregivers of children who received SMC that year, local authority officials, State Ministry of Health officials, drug distributors (Boma health workers), County Health Department staff, healthcare facility staff, cultural and religious leaders, community elders, and Malaria Consortium personnel.

Sample size and sampling

A total of 60 key informant interviews were conducted, providing a detailed exploration of caregivers' and stakeholders' experiences, perceptions, and views regarding the 2024 SMC round. The interviews included a variety of respondents: one State Surveillance Officer, four PHCC In-charges (Clinical Officers), two County Health Department Officers, one State Public Health Officer, one State Pharmacist, one State M and E Director, two Malaria Consortium staff members, 15 Payam Leaders/Chiefs, three Religious Leaders, 10 Boma Healthcare Workers (acting as Drug Distributors or Supervisors), one town crier (Social Mobilizer), and one Refugee Coordinator. Additionally, 18 in-depth interviews were held with caregivers of eligible children. Six of these caregivers had children who received all SPAQ doses across five cycles. Six caregivers’ children received no doses, and six caregivers' children were ineligible but still received SPAQ. The interviews were conducted until saturation was reached, at which point no further views emerged [12]. This focused approach yielded a thorough understanding of how the program was implemented. Data collection

Key informant interview guides were used to collect relevant data on stakeholders' perceptions of the enablers and challenges affecting SMC drug distribution during the 2024 round. Each county had one research assistant who conducted in-depth interviews with respondents selected by community-level enumerators, as well as key informant interviews. Interviews were conducted in English with key informants who spoke English fluently. In-depth interviews with caregivers of children and key informant interviews with local leaders who were not fluent in English were conducted in Dinka, the local language, and then translated back into English by trained, experienced research assistants. This approach ensured the collection of high-quality data throughout the interviews, thereby preventing language barriers from interfering with data collection. Each interview took between 35 and 60 min.

In-depth interviews were conducted with caregivers of eligible children who completed all SMC doses and cycles, as well as with caregivers of children who did not complete all SMC doses or cycles or participated in the SMC campaign at all. These interviews were conducted in private settings to ensure participants felt comfortable and could share their thoughts openly. The interviews with caregivers provided a deeper understanding of their thoughts, experiences, and perspectives on SMC. Structured IDI guides specific to each group were used to collect the data. Before the interviews, participants provided verbal consent, and those who could read and write received a consent sheet to review before signing to confirm their consent. The researchers reached data saturation after 28 interviews, but conducted three additional interviews to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using a deductive thematic analysis approach. Three authors analyzed the data. Predetermined themes were used to guide the analysis, including: (a) factors enabling access to SMC services, and (b) challenges to accessing SMC. These themes were based on prior knowledge of the program and evaluation experience. Two authors first familiarized themselves with the transcripts, independently analyzing one key informant and one in-depth interview using Microsoft Excel. They coded the data by attaching labels to text segments and linking them to relevant quotes, capturing both similar and differing perspectives. The codes were grouped under themes (e.g., “enablers to SMC” as “ESMC”). The data analysts first met to agree on a unified coding strategy and apply it consistently across all transcripts. They then compared notes on the codes every three days to ensure clarity, coherence, and an accurate representation of participant views. The emerging patterns, relationships, and inconsistencies were identified. The codes were then clustered into themes and subthemes to group related patterns. The third author then refined the codes and harmonized the themes by excluding or merging irrelevant or weak codes into broader categories. The analyzed data and theme names were finalized. The entire process took two weeks.

Summary of the findings

Discussion

The rollout of Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention (SMC) in Aweil South and Aweil West counties in 2024 highlights both the potential and the challenges of delivering malaria prevention in vulnerable areas. Success was mainly due to strong government backing, integration into national malaria control strategies, and the key role of Boma Health Workers (BHWs).

The study noted community trust as a factor in improving SMC uptake. This study's findings align with earlier research on the feasibility of SMC in Aweil South, South Sudan, which investigated the implementation of SMC in areas affected by conflict and humanitarian crises [8]. Trust is essential for making the program sustainable, and it can be strengthened through ongoing training and support. Investing in local health workers and integrating the program with other services is crucial for ensuring everyone has equal access to SMC in fragile contexts.

The role of community health workers in community drug distribution programs. Their presence in communities, understanding of local culture, and door-to-door delivery of drugs made access possible in conflict-affected and hard-to-reach places. The community health workers, like BHWs, not only distribute drugs but also engage communities, dispel myths, and connect SMC with broader health systems [13]. Their flexibility ensures continuity even in unstable situations, making them essential in fragile areas [8]. Strengthening their skills, providing incentives, and incorporating them more fully into the health system will improve coverage and support equitable malaria prevention.

Integrating SMC into national malaria strategies was another key factor in enabling SMC success. Combining efforts with other malaria interventions, such as surveillance, case management, and mosquito net distribution, strengthened the program's continuity and credibility. Government support improved legitimacy, pooled resources, and enabled training and partnerships. Experiences from Nigeria, Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Chad confirm that such integration improves sustainability and community trust [14–16]. Ongoing integration of SMC into national strategies for South Sudan is crucial for long-term success and coherence with broader malaria control initiatives.

However, the study also reveals several problems that hinder implementation, with broader implications for the implementation of SMC in vulnerable settings. Severe flooding disrupted logistics, blocked transport routes, displaced families, and extended mosquito breeding seasons, increasing the risk of malaria. While efforts like pre-positioning medicines and using boats were made, weak infrastructure and staff shortages limited their effectiveness. Experiences in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Mozambique show the need for delivery methods that can withstand climate challenges and adapt to flood-prone areas [11, 17–20]. Climate impacts, displacement, and weak infrastructure complicate delivery, necessitating flexible and context-specific strategies. Lessons from other countries indicate that resilience can be built through adaptable distribution models, investments in transportation infrastructure, and links to other health services [21, 22]. South Sudan’s experiences highlight the importance of tailored approaches that address both structural limitations and community needs.

Staffing shortages and inadequate training for BHWs also limited coverage. Heavy workloads, poor bicycle upkeep, and little supervision reduced efficiency in drug distribution and community mobilization. Evidence from other sub-Saharan countries suggests that better planning strategies that optimize routes and minimize walking distances can enhance efficiency [21]. Targeted investments in transportation, training, and supervision are essential to improve SMC delivery in South Sudan.

Drug stock shortages caused by diversion occurred where medicines meant for children in the SMC program were redirected to treat non-SMC patients at health facilities, damaging the program's integrity. Misuse of drugs of this nature has previously been reported in South Sudan due to weaker monitoring systems [18–20]. Better accountability, stronger supply chain management, and clear guidelines are needed to protect SMC operations to tackle this.

Population data proved crucial but problematic in the delivery of SMC. Influxes of refugees from Sudan sharply increased demand, leading to under-allocation in some regions and waste in others. Poor estimates disrupted supply chains and reduced effectiveness, similar to issues seen in other African regions where displacement complicates planning [11, 23]. Improving data systems, using real-time monitoring, and adapting supply chains to changing population movements are necessary for fair distribution.

Communication gaps also weakened the implementation efforts. Limited mobile network coverage in remote areas disrupted coordination, supervision, and reporting. Delays in sharing stock levels or adverse events reduced responsiveness and accountability. Similar challenges have been reported in Nigeria, The Gambia, and Mali, where offline-capable applications, community radio, and infrastructure improvements were suggested [14, 15, 19]. For South Sudan, enhancing connectivity and using reliable communication tools will be vital to ensuring coverage in high-risk areas.

Strengths and limitations

This study has two major strengths: its emphasis on real-world implementation in a high-malaria-burden, conflict-affected area, and its use of qualitative data to gather in-depth insights from stakeholders. This approach increases the reliability of the findings for policy purposes. However, the study has some limitations that warrant caution in interpreting and using its findings. The results are based on data from only two counties, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other areas of South Sudan or similar contexts. The sample size and selection process could also introduce biases, such as overrepresenting communities that are easily accessible. Additionally, the study's cross-sectional design doesn't allow for conclusions about cause and effect. Another limitation is that the study relies on self-reported data, which may be subject to memory bias. Despite this, efforts were made to cross-check sources to minimize the potential issues.

Conclusion

This study investigated the factors that enabled and hindered the distribution of, and access to, seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) in the conflict-affected Aweil South and Aweil West counties of South Sudan during the 2024 round. The study found that delivering SMC in rural, conflict-affected areas of South Sudan mainly relied on community trust, government support, efficient logistics, and the involvement of local health workers. However, shortages of drug distributors, disruptions to the drug supply, poor transportation, displacement due to flooding, and inadequate communication all limited coverage. To improve access in future SMC distribution and related areas, it is crucial to strengthen supply chains, increase healthcare workforce capacity, address challenges such as limited resources, flooding, and mobility constraints, and implement innovative approaches to reach remote areas, including mobile clinics.
