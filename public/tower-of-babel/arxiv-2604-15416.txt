StoSignSGD: Unbiased Structural Stochasticity Fixes SignSGD for Training Large Language Models
Dingzhi Yu, Rui Pan, Yuxing LiuTong ZhangUniversity of Illinois Urbana-Champaign dingzhiyu01@gmail.com, {yuxing6, ruip4, tozhang}@illinois.edu

Abstract
Sign-based optimization algorithms, such as SignSGD, have garnered significant attention for their remarkable performance in distributed learning and training large foundation models. Despite their empirical superiority, SignSGD is known to diverge on non-smooth objectives, which are ubiquitous in modern machine learning due to ReLUs, max-pools, and mixture-of-experts. To overcome this fundamental limitation, we propose StoSignSGD , an algorithm that injects structural stochasticity into the sign operator while maintaining an unbiased update step. In the regime of (online) convex optimization, our theoretical analysis shows that StoSignSGD rigorously resolves the non-convergence issues of SignSGD, achieving a sharp convergence rate matching the lower bound. For the more challenging non-convex non-smooth optimization, we introduce generalized stationary measures that encompass prior definitions, proving that StoSignSGD improves upon the best-known complexity bounds by dimensional factors. Empirically, StoSignSGD exhibits robust stability and superior efficiency across diverse large language model (LLM) training regimes. Notably, in low-precision FP8 pretraining—a setting where AdamW fails catastrophically—StoSignSGD remains highly stable and yields a remarkable 1.44 × \times to 2.14 × \times speedup relative to established baselines. Furthermore, when fine-tuning 7B LLMs on mathematical reasoning tasks, StoSignSGD delivers substantial performance gains over both AdamW and SignSGD. Finally, to dissect the mechanisms driving its success, we develop a sign conversion framework capable of transforming any general optimizer into its unbiased, sign-based counterpart. Utilizing this framework, we deconstruct the core components of StoSignSGD and present a comprehensive ablation study to empirically validate our algorithmic design choices.

2.2
Unbiased Stochastic Sign Operator

We now formally introduce the coordinate-wise stochastic sign operator.

Definition 1

(Stochastic Sign Operator)
.

For any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{G}=(G_{1},\cdots,\allowbreak G_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
satisfying
\left|x_{i}\right|\leq G_{i}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x})=\sgn\left(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{G}\odot\mathbf{n}\right),\textnormal{ where }\mathbf{n}\sim\unif\left([-1,1]^{d}\right).

We inject coordinate-wise uniformly distributed noise into the sign operator. This deliberate corruption is key to preventing the divergence observed in vanilla SignSGD.

Proposition 1
.

For any
\mathbf{G}\succeq\mathbf{x}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x})\right]=\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{G}=(x_{1}/G_{1},\cdots,x_{d}/G_{d})
.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}_{G_{i}}(x_{i})=\pm 1\right)=\frac{G_{i}\pm x_{i}}{2G_{i}},\forall i\in[d]
. Taking expectations yields the result.
∎

1
reveals that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{G}}(\cdot)
secretly retains anisotropic gradient magnitudes even though only sign information is transmitted. Replacing
\sgn(\cdot)
with
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{G}}(\cdot)
makes the expected update coincide with preconditioned SGD. Because the operator is
unbiased only after coordinate-wise rescaling
, the choice of
\mathbf{G}
, which sets the per-coordinate normalization level, is decisive. As elaborated in later sections, our
\mathbf{G}
adapts coordinate-wise along the optimization trajectory, yielding theoretical guarantees that contain no explicit dimensional factors.

Convex Theory

In this section, we delve into online convex optimization (OCO), which is the canonical framework for non-smooth convex optimization. This setting covers stochastic convex optimization in

Here, we briefly introduce the online learning setup, leaving a comprehensive overview to
(

;

;

. In each round
t=1,\dots,T
, the learner plays
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, after which an adversary reveals a convex loss
f_{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\mathbf{g}_{t}\in\partial f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\textnormal{Regret}_{T}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right).

Our goal is to design a sign-based no-regret learning algorithm that achieves sublinear regret for non-smooth losses
\left\{f_{t}\right\}_{t\in[T]}

We formally introduce the online StoSignSGD in
Algorithm

1

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{t}=\max\left\{\mathbf{G}_{t-1},\left|\mathbf{g}_{t}\right|\right\},\qquad\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathbf{G}_{t}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta_{t}\cdot\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{G}_{t}}(\mathbf{g}_{t})\right],

Further Theoretical and Empirical Justifications

We provide further theoretical and empirical reasoning to justify the design of StoSignSGD and explain why alternative sign conversions, such as SignAdamW and SignAdaMax, fall short. From the perspective of non-smooth optimization theory, our design of the preconditioner
\mathbf{G}_{t}
, i.e., Trick 3 plus Trick, already gives the best possible convergence for sign-based optimizers (see
Remark

2
), whose tightness is also justified by
2
.
When it comes to smooth optimization, managing the variance of the stochastic updates is critical for convergence
(
19
)
. To understand this, we analyze the variance of the three sign-converted methods. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\sigma}_{t}}(\mathbf{m}_{t}):=\sgn\left(\mathbf{m}_{t}+\bm{\sigma}_{t}\odot\mathbf{n}_{t}\right)
and define the signal-to-noise ratio
\mathbf{r}_{t}:=\mathbf{m}_{t}/\bm{\sigma}_{t}
. The (conditional) variance for coordinate
i
is exactly
\mathrm{Var}([\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\sigma}_{t}}(\mathbf{m}_{t})]_{i})=1-\mathbf{r}_{t,i}^{2}
. Consequently, the total variance across all coordinates is
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\sigma}_{t}}(\mathbf{m}_{t}))=d(1-\|\mathbf{r}_{t}\|^{2}_{\text{RMS}})
. This equation illustrates a direct mathematical relationship: a smaller relative magnitude
\mathbf{r}_{t}
strictly inflates the per-step variance. When we evaluate the RMS-norms of
\mathbf{r}_{t}
for StoSignSGD, SignAdamW, and SignAdaMax along their optimization trajectories (see
Figure

4
and the last column of
Table

4
), a clear pattern emerges: StoSignSGD sustains a significantly larger
\mathbf{r}_{t}
compared to the alternatives. By dynamically coupling
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
and
\mathbf{m}_{t}
(Trick 2), StoSignSGD ensures that the structural noise does not overwhelm the gradient signal. This allows the algorithm to smartly leverage the sign-plus-noise mechanism without incurring excessive stochasticity. Rather than suffering from unchecked variance, StoSignSGD achieves an optimal balance: it injects just enough structural stochasticity to benefit from its exploratory properties, while preserving a sufficiently large
\mathbf{r}_{t}
to keep the update variance strictly under control for stable convergence.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose StoSignSGD, a novel variant of SignSGD that introduces unbiased structural stochasticity into the vanilla sign operator. Our theoretical analysis demonstrates that StoSignSGD fixes the non-convergence issue of standard SignSGD and improves upon prior best-known complexity bounds by dimensional factors. Empirically, extensive LLM pretraining and fine-tuning experiments reveal the substantial practical efficiency gains achieved by our method. To further elucidate these empirical advantages, we develop an unbiased sign conversion framework for general optimizers. This framework demystifies the role of structural stochasticity in StoSignSGD, providing robust theoretical justification for our algorithmic design.

Appendix A
Related Work

Sign-based optimization

SignSGD (and its momentum variant Signum) was first introduced by
17
, who established an
O(1/T+1/B)
convergence rate for smooth non-convex objectives, where
T
denotes the number of iterations and
B
the batch size. This result suggests that the convergence of SignSGD relies heavily on large-batch training regimes. In non-smooth settings,
66
,
139
develop convex and deterministic counterexamples where SignSGD diverges. Owing to its communication efficiency, SignSGD subsequently attracted extensive attention in distributed learning settings
[
18
,
62
,
117
]
. The general idea of injecting noise into the sign operator appears at
[
62
,
117
,
125
,
27
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
. However, most of them neither consider the coordinate-wise noise nor do they allow the noise level to adapt to the optimization trajectory. These drawbacks lead to dimension-dependent convergence rates as well as stronger assumptions, such as bounded gradients.

Several works have further extended or reinterpreted sign-based methods. For example,
29
proposed a generalized version of SignSGD that is closely related to Adam, complementing another line of work that seeks to explain Adam’s effectiveness from the perspective of sign descent
[
16
,
73
]
. On the theoretical side,
125
derived a tighter
O(1/\sqrt{BT})
convergence for Signum, albeit with less favorable dimension dependence. This analysis was later improved by
60
, who obtained better dimension factors under weaker assumptions. Beyond vanilla sign descent,
27
proposed SignSVRG, which combines sign-based updates with variance reduction techniques
[
63
,
147
]
. This direction was further advanced by
59
, who incorporated the STORM estimator
[
31
]
and extended the method to distributed settings.

Empirically, the origins of sign-based optimization can be traced back to RProp
[
116
]
. A notable recent breakthrough in this domain is the Lion optimizer
[
26
]
, discovered through symbolic search. Built upon Signum, Lion maintains two momentum buffers with decoupled roles, assigning greater importance to the current gradient. Through a range of large-scale training tasks,
26
showed that Lion can converge substantially faster than AdamW while achieving comparable or better generalization. On the theoretical side,
36
provided the first general convergence analysis of Lion, which was later sharpened by
61
under weaker assumptions and further extended to distributed optimization. More recently,
144
is the first to theoretically justify the potential benefits of Signum and Lion over AdamW. Their analysis establishes convergence under generalized heavy-tailed noise and removes the dimensional dependence present in prior works
[
36
,
61
]
.

Non-smooth optimization

In convex settings, non-smooth optimization arises naturally in online convex optimization
[
154
]
and has been studied extensively over the years
[
48
,
102
]
. In the offline setting, there is likewise a rich literature on non-smooth convex optimization
[
22
,
49
]
. Transitioning to non-convex settings, the seminal work of
146
provided the first finite-time analysis of stochastic gradient methods based on their newly proposed
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary points. Such measures are essential, since
70
established hardness results for finding even near approximately-stationary points. Moreover,
64
showed that no deterministic algorithm can identify
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein points in dimension-free time. Beyond first-order methods, gradient-free approaches have also been proposed and analyzed for this challenging setting
[
80
,
25
,
86
,
87
]
.

A recent major breakthrough is the online-to-non-convex conversion (O2NC) framework proposed by
30
. The key idea is to use an online learning algorithm to predict and determine the next update step within the conversion procedure. Remarkably,
30
showed that an optimal regret bound for online learning immediately yields an optimal complexity bound for non-smooth non-convex optimization. Building on this framework,
150
introduced exponential random scaling into O2NC and showed that SGD with momentum can efficiently find
(\delta,\epsilon)
-stationary points.
84
further incorporated gradient clipping into this framework and established high-probability guarantees under heavy-tailed gradients. These results were subsequently refined by
88
, who developed an elegant analysis of OGD itself under heavy-tailed noise. More broadly, the O2NC framework has also been utilized to justify the theoretical benefits of Adam
[
7
,
5
]
and schedule-free SGD
[
6
]
, and has recently enabled a deeper understanding of quasi-Newton and optimistic methods
[
58
,
108
]
.

Low-precision training

Low-precision training has progressed from mixed-precision recipes that keep selected tensors in higher precision to increasingly aggressive native low-bit regimes. Early work showed that stable training can be achieved by combining low-precision arithmetic with loss scaling and higher-precision master parameters
[
93
]
, while later studies developed hardware-friendly low-bit formats, including hybrid FP8 designs and more systematic 8-bit floating-point specifications
[
126
,
100
,
94
]
. A recent survey provides a comprehensive overview of methods, challenges, and opportunities in low-precision LLM training
[
46
]
.

Building on these foundations, recent works have demonstrated the practical viability of low-precision training for LLMs.
110
studied dynamic scaling strategies for FP8 training and inference of GPT- and Llama-style models, while
109
proposed an FP8 automatic mixed-precision framework in which gradients and optimizer states can also be quantized effectively.
39
further scaled FP8 training to trillion-token LLMs and revealed long-horizon instabilities that do not appear in shorter runs. Meanwhile, optimizer-state compression has become an important complementary direction:
34
developed 8-bit optimizers via block-wise quantization, and
137
further compressed optimizer states and activations to improve the memory efficiency of FP8 training. Beyond FP8, low-precision training has also expanded to more aggressive formats:
138
developed hardware-compatible INT4 transformer training,
23
,
1
showed that end-to-end FP4/NVFP4 training can be competitive for billion-scale LLM pretraining,
95
presented practical recipes for pre-training LLMs with MXFP8, and
53
pushed toward fully FP8 GEMM LLM training at scale. Industrial technical reports such as DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3 further indicate that low-precision training is becoming an important systems direction for modern LLM development
[
81
,
141
]
.

At the same time, another line of work studies why low-precision training fails and how to stabilize it.
136
identified loss spikes associated with under-estimated AdamW second moments in large-scale low-precision training.
75
systematically quantified the degradation in LLM training stability caused by aggressive precision reduction, while
15
formalized scale propagation as an end-to-end recipe for low-precision LLM training. Most recently,
111
traced an important failure mode of low-precision Transformer training to FlashAttention
[
32
,
33
,
121
]
. In contrast to these works, our goal is not to design yet another stabilization recipe for AdamW, but to show that replacing AdamW with a sign-based optimizer can itself provide a simple and robust alternative in highly constrained low-precision regimes.

B.1.3
A Closer Look into Momentum

To back up our findings in
Section

B.1.2
and provide a mechanistic explanation for why StoSignSGD reduces the sample complexity required to learn fundamental concepts, we perform a simple empirical study on momentum and conclude that it is the
amplified momentum tails that reflect the acquisition of ubiquitous foundational skills
.

In the context of deep learning, fundamental knowledge and core concepts correspond to structural patterns that appear with high frequency and ubiquity across the training data distribution
[
13
,
120
]
. Because these highly recurrent patterns are present in nearly every mini-batch, their corresponding stochastic gradients consistently point in the same optimization direction. The momentum term, acting as an exponential moving average, constructively accumulates these consistent gradient signals while destructively canceling out the fluctuating, zero-mean noise of batch-specific or rare features
[
127
]
. Therefore, the successful acquisition of highly recurrent, foundational "skills" will naturally manifest as a significant amplification of momentum magnitudes along specific coordinate axes.

Figure 3:
Momentum (
\left|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right|
) distribution at the end of training.

To verify this dynamic, we follow the setup detailed in
Section

B.3
and analyze the momentum distributions during the GPT-2 training trajectory on the Alpaca dataset. We isolate 16 timestamps near the conclusion of training (totaling 1,224 steps) and compute the coordinate-wise absolute values of the momentum vector,
|\mathbf{m}_{t}|
. Treating each dimension as an individual data point, we construct a value-quantile plot. Comparing StoSignSGD against the standard SignSGD baseline, the results in
Figure

3
consistently demonstrate that StoSignSGD yields a momentum distribution with significantly larger magnitudes, particularly in the highest echelon (
>0.9
quantile). This pronounced surge in high-quantile momentum directly indicates that StoSignSGD more effectively locks onto and amplifies the consistent gradient directions associated with ubiquitous data patterns. This geometric behavior perfectly corroborates our empirical findings: by confidently amplifying the most reliable gradient signals, StoSignSGD naturally excels at isolating and reinforcing the foundational rules required for core mathematical reasoning. Ultimately, this momentum-driven amplification serves as a tangible metric for the model’s structural learning. Because StoSignSGD successfully shields these consistent, high-frequency signals from being disrupted by batch-specific stochasticity, it locks onto fundamental features much faster than standard methods. This geometric stability provides a direct explanation for our empirical observations: by efficiently isolating and reinforcing these core rules, StoSignSGD achieves superior sample efficiency on tasks that strictly require robust foundational knowledge, such as the mathematical reasoning benchmarks evaluated in GSM8k and MATH.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{\left\langle\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t}\right\rangle}_{:=M_{t}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\right\rangle\right].

Let’s be precise about the information available at each step. The filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
represents the history of all random events up to the beginning of round
t
. This includes the initial points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}
and the sequences of gradients, iterates, and any other random variables from rounds
1,\cdots,t-1
. Thus, we know that
\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable. So we deduce that
\mathbb{E}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t}\right\rangle\right]=0
, i.e.,
\left\{M_{t}\right\}_{t\in[T]}
is a martingale difference sequence. This immediately implies that
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}M_{t}]=0
. Hence, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}M_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\right\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\right\rangle\right]\leq 2D_{\infty}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}\sqrt{T}.

Finally, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\right\rangle\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}\right]+\frac{D_{\infty}^{2}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}}{2\eta_{T}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\eta_{t}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2D_{\infty}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}\sqrt{T}+\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}\left(\frac{D_{\infty}^{2}}{2\eta_{T}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\eta_{t}}{2}\right).

Plugging in
\eta_{t}=D_{\infty}/\sqrt{2t}
and
\eta_{t}\equiv D_{\infty}/\sqrt{T}
respectively completes the proof.
∎

D.1
Proof of Theorem
8

The analysis for
Algorithm

12
follows from the online-to-nonconvex conversion framework introduced by
30
. First, we construct a telescoping sum of function values analogously to
30
. Under
4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+s(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t-1})),\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}{s}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+s\bm{\Delta}_{t})\mathrm{d}{s},\bm{\Delta}_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}\right\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+s\bm{\Delta}_{t})\mathrm{d}{s}-\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}\right\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{s_{t},\mathcal{O}_{t}}[\mathbf{g}_{t}]-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+s\bm{\Delta}_{t})\mathrm{d}{s},-\bm{\Delta}_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}-\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{s_{t}}[\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+s_{t}\bm{\Delta}_{t})]-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+s\bm{\Delta}_{t})\mathrm{d}{s},-\bm{\Delta}_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}-\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right],

where the last step necessitates the randomized scaling trick
[
146
]
, i.e., evaluating stochastic gradients at a random point on the line segment between adjacent iterates. Similar to (
6
), we have

\displaystyle-\Delta_{f}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{T})-f(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}-\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right].

Different from (
7
), we define

\displaystyle\forall k\in[K],\forall t:(k-1)N<t\leq kN,\quad\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+n})

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{k}:=\mathbf{u}_{(k-1)N+1}=\cdots=\mathbf{u}_{kN}=-D_{\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k}/\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k}\right|.

(14)

Then we perform the nearly identical procedure as in (
8
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{(k-1)N+n},\mathbf{u}^{k}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+n}),\mathbf{u}^{k}\right\rangle\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\langle\underbrace{\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+n})-\mathbf{g}_{(k-1)N+n}}_{:=\bm{\epsilon}_{n}^{k}},\mathbf{u}^{k}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k},\frac{-D_{\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k}}{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k}\right|}\right\rangle\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\epsilon}_{n}^{k}\right\|_{1}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{u}^{k}\right\|_{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{k}\right\|_{1}\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{\infty}\sqrt{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+n})\right\|_{1}\right]+KD_{\infty}\sqrt{N}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1},

The regret bound in (
9
) remains almost intact:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t},\bm{\Delta}_{t}-\mathbf{u}_{t}\right\rangle\right]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}3\cdot(2D_{\infty})\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}\sqrt{N}=6K\sqrt{N}D_{\infty}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}.

The only difference lies in the constant
3
, which improves upon the
2+\sqrt{2}
used in
Algorithm

12
. That’s simply because we leverages a constant learning rate of
\eta_{t}=2D_{\infty}/\sqrt{N}
.
Analogously to (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+n})\right\|_{1}\right]\leq\frac{\Delta_{f}}{D_{\infty}KN}+\frac{7\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(15)

Similar to
Section

C.5
, the remaining procedure is to find a realization of the stationary measure defined in
3
. For any
k\in[K]
, we upper bound the quantity
\left\|\nabla f(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k})\right\|_{1,\infty}^{[\delta]_{\textrm{Gs}}}
by realizing the uniform distribution supported on the set
S_{k}=\left\{\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+1},\cdots,\mathbf{w}_{kN}\right\}
, i.e.,
\inf_{S\subseteq B_{\infty}(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k},\delta),\frac{1}{\left|S\right|}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in S}\mathbf{y}=\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k}}
is relaxed by the realization
S=S_{k}
. Thus, it’s necessary to show that
S_{k}\subseteq B_{\infty}(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k},\delta)
. For any
\mathbf{w}_{n_{1}},\mathbf{w}_{n_{2}}\in S_{k},(k-1)N+1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}\leq kN
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{w}_{n_{1}}-\mathbf{w}_{n_{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{w}_{n_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{n_{1}}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{n_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{n_{2}-1}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{n_{2}}-\mathbf{w}_{n_{2}-1}\right\|_{\infty}

(16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(s_{n_{1}}-1)\bm{\Delta}_{n_{1}}\right\|_{\infty}+\sum_{n=n_{1}+1}^{n_{2}-1}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|-s_{n_{2}}\bm{\Delta}_{n_{2}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\Delta}_{n_{1}}\right\|_{\infty}+\sum_{n=n_{1}+1}^{n_{2}-1}\left\|\bm{\Delta}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\bm{\Delta}_{n_{2}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle D_{\infty}+(n_{2}-1-(n_{1}+1)+1)D_{\infty}+D_{\infty}\leq D_{\infty}N,

where the third inequality is due to
s_{n}\sim\unif([0,1])
and the last step follows from
\left\|\bm{\Delta}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\leq D_{\infty}
. Then, in the same view of (
12
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{w}_{n}\in S_{k},\left\|\mathbf{w}_{n}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{w}_{n^{\prime}}\in S_{k}}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}_{n^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\overset{\eqref{eq:Sk-diam-as}}{\leq}ND_{\infty}\Longrightarrow S_{k}\subseteq B_{\infty}(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k},ND_{\infty}).

By choosing
D_{\infty}=\delta/N
, we derive that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k})\right\|_{1,\infty}^{[\delta]_{\textrm{Gs}}}=\inf_{S\subseteq B_{\infty}(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k},\delta),\frac{1}{\left|S\right|}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in S}\mathbf{y}=\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k}}\left\|\frac{1}{\left|S\right|}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in S}\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{(k-1)N+n})\right\|_{1}.

Since
\overline{\mathbf{w}}\sim\unif\left(\left\{\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{1},\cdots,\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k}\right\}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\overline{\mathbf{w}})\right\|_{1,\infty}^{[\delta]_{\textrm{Gs}}}\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{k})\right\|_{1,\infty}^{[\delta]_{\textrm{Gs}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{(k-1)N+n})\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:pre-bound-as}}{\leq}

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{f}}{\delta K}+\frac{7\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\epsilon+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon=\epsilon,

where the last step follows from

\displaystyle N=\left\lceil\frac{441}{4}\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\|_{1}^{2}\epsilon^{-2}\right\rceil,\ K=\left\lceil\frac{3\Delta_{f}}{\epsilon\delta}\right\rceil.

D.2
Conversion Between Stationary Points

To better clarify the relationship between different stationary measures, we introduce the following slightly stronger version of
3
.

Definition 4

(
(\delta,\epsilon)
-
\ell_{p,q}
-Goldstein stationary point (Version 2))
.

Given
\epsilon,\delta>0
,
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
, and an almost-everywhere differentiable function
f
,
\mathbf{x}
is an
(\delta,\epsilon)
-
\ell_{p,q}
-Goldstein stationary point (Version 2) of
f
if

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{p,q}^{[\delta]_{\textnormal{Gs-v2}}}:=\inf_{S\subseteq B_{q}(\mathbf{x},\delta),\,P\in\mathcal{P}(S),\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim P}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]=\mathbf{x}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim P}\left[\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right]\right\|_{p}\leq\epsilon.

Compared with
3
,
4
relaxes the uniformly weighted finite set in
3
to an arbitrary distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}(S)
. Therefore,
4
is a stronger notion in the sense that its defining infimum is taken over a larger class of feasible objects, and hence can only be smaller.

The reason we use
3
, rather than
4
, in
Algorithms

12
and
8
is that the analysis in
Section

D.1
only constructs uniformly distributed random variables, namely distributions that are finite and uniformly weighted on their supports. Thus, the proof of
8
already suffices to establish the weaker stationarity notion in
3
.

The purpose of introducing
4
is to bridge
2
and
3
. The next lemma shows that, under an additional Lipschitz assumption,
2
reduces to a nearby instance of
4
.

Lemma D.1

(Reduction of
2
to
4
, adapted from Lemma 2.4 in
151
)
.

Fix
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
, and let
r_{*}
be the dual norm of
r
. Assume that
f
is differentiable and
G
-Lipschitz with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{r}
, i.e.,
\left|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})\right|\leq G\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{r}
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{p,q}^{[\lambda]}\leq\epsilon
, then for every
\rho>0
, there exists a point
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}\leq\rho\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})\right\|_{p,q}^{[2\rho]_{\textnormal{Gs-v2}}}\leq\epsilon\left(1+\frac{2C_{p,r_{*}}G}{\lambda\rho^{2}}\right),

where
C_{p,r_{*}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0}}\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{p}/\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{r_{*}}
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By
\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{p,q}^{[\lambda]}\leq\epsilon
, there exists
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim P}[\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{x}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right]\right\|_{p}+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}^{2}\right]\leq\epsilon+\varepsilon.

(17)

Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}:=\mathbf{x}+\min\left\{1,\frac{\rho}{\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}}\right\}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}),\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}:=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}],

and let
\widetilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the distribution of
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
. Since
\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}\leq\rho
almost surely, Jensen’s inequality gives
\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}\leq\rho
. Also,

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{q}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}+\left\|\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{q}\leq 2\rho,

hence
\supp(\widetilde{P})\subseteq B_{q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}},2\rho)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\widetilde{P}}[\mathbf{y}]=\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
.

Now let
A:=\left\{\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}>\rho\right\}
. On
A^{c}
we have
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{y}
, so

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})\right]\right\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right]\right\|_{p}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right\|_{p}\mathbb{I}[A]\right].

Since
f
is
G
-Lipschitz with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{r}
, we have
\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{z})\right\|_{r_{*}}\leq G
almost everywhere, and therefore

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right\|_{p}\leq\left\|\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})\right\|_{p}+\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\right\|_{p}\leq 2C_{p,r_{*}}G

almost everywhere. Combining this with (
17
) and Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})\right]\right\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\varepsilon+2C_{p,r_{*}}G\mathbb{P}(A)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\varepsilon+2C_{p,r_{*}}G\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}^{2}\right]}{\rho^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon+\varepsilon)\left(1+\frac{2C_{p,r_{*}}G}{\lambda\rho^{2}}\right).

Since
\widetilde{P}
is feasible in
4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})\right\|_{p,q}^{[2\rho]_{\textnormal{Gs-v2}}}\leq(\epsilon+\varepsilon)\left(1+\frac{2C_{p,r_{*}}G}{\lambda\rho^{2}}\right).

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
completes the proof.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we obtain the following specialization in the geometry used throughout this paper, namely the
\ell_{1,\infty}
setting. By choosing
\rho=\delta/2
in
D.1
, the support radius
2\rho
becomes exactly
\delta
, which matches the radius parameter in
4
.

Corollary 9
.

Assume that
f
is differentiable and
G
-Lipschitz with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
. If
\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{1,\infty}^{[\lambda]}\leq\epsilon
, then for every
\delta>0
, there exists a point
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta/2\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})\right\|_{1,\infty}^{[\delta]_{\textnormal{Gs-v2}}}\leq\epsilon\left(1+\frac{8G}{\lambda\delta^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Apply
D.1
with
p=1
,
q=\infty
, and
r=\infty
. Then
r_{*}=1
and
C_{1,1}=1
. Setting
\rho=\delta/2
completes the proof.
∎
