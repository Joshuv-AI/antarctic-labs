Decoding the functional landscape and resistome profile of the gut microbiome in the Pangwala tribal community of India
Authors: Khem Raj, Pallak Sharma, Mohammad Riyaz, Yogesh S Shouche, Kanika Multani, Milani Sharma, Manpreet Dhaliwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe human gut microbiome consists of a complex and diverse community of commensal microorganisms and has been under extensive research consideration in the past few decades. Although several recent studies have targeted the determination of bacterial composition of the ecosystem, the knowledge about the mycobiome, virome, and functional attributes of the same remains scarce. The aim of the present study was to investigate the functional and resistome profile of the gut microbiome in the Pangwala tribal community of India using a combined Whole Metagenome Shotgun (WMS) sequencing and bioinformatics approach.ResultsThe findings revealed a remarkable diversity of microorganisms inhabiting the gut of both groups, with similar level of diversity among the dominant genera like Prevotella, Bifidobacterium and Succinivibrionaceae. The mycobiome was dominated by the subkingdom Dikarya (74%), while Fungi incertae sedis accounted for 23% of the total fungal species in both groups. The virome analysis showed the dominance of the Caudoviricetes class, with bacteriophages being the most dominant. Moreover, functional analysis identified the prominent metabolic pathways and the key gene families involved in the pathways, highlighting Prevotella copri as the major contributor. Additionally, the study identified the resistome and showed that there were more than 100 potential antibiotic-resistant genes (ARGs) and high levels of resistance to vancomycin in both groups.ConclusionThis study presents a comprehensive overview of the gut microbiome in the Pangi population, detailing both in its taxonomic structure and functional traits. The results show that, despite the high degree of diversity in the gut microbiome, there seems to be evident functional redundancy, which underlines a core stable microbiome. The resistome profile offers complete exploratory picture of the resistome and establishes a valuable baseline for future studies. Furthermore, we anticipate that these findings will add valuable insights to understand the Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) stewardship in the light of one health aspect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05248-5.Keywords: Whole-Metagenome Shotgun (WMS) sequencing, Metagenomics, Bioinformatics, Tribal gut microbiome, Taxonomic classification, Functional analysis, Resistome profiling

Background

The gut microbiome is a fascinating world that is full of complex and varied variety of microbes just waiting to be discovered [1, 2]. It comes as no surprise that the gut microbiome has a major role in the promotion of human health because of its proximity to the intestinal mucosa and its complex interaction with the immune system [3]. Recently, the gut microbiome has attracted a lot of attention, and with the development of metagenomic research, our knowledge of the resident species has significantly increased [4]. More importantly, the recent application of modern sequencing technologies has enabled the identification of uncultivated microorganisms in the gut microbiome, thus broadening our understanding of the composition and potential roles of gut microbial species, which has enabled the unlocking of the secrets of this critical ecosystem [5, 6]. Earlier 16s amplicon-based sequencing technique was one of the most prominent techniques utilized in the characterization of bacterial communities [7]. Nevertheless, it is important to note that this technique also has its limitations. Specifically, it may not be capable of detecting the presence of fungi and viruses in a given sample, nor the functional role of microbial genes, using solely the sequence of a single gene [7]. As a result, in light of these limitations, the Whole-Metagenome Shotgun (WMS) sequencing technique has recently emerged as the gold standard in the analysis and identification of the functional aspects of the human microbiome [8–10]. This approach can process and interpret massive amounts of metagenomic data, enabling the detection of microorganisms from all domains of life [11]. As a result, taxonomic and functional insights could be effectively extracted from the data produced by WMS sequencing. This can also significantly advance our knowledge of not only the metabolic capabilities, but also the prevalence and variety of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes (resistome) within the gut microbiome from the standpoint of One Health (Human–Animal–Environment) concept [12].

India, with a population that embodies a delightful blend of various cultures is well known for its extraordinary cultural diversity [13]. However, there hasn’t been much population-level research done on the gut microbiota in Indian tribes using representative sampling throughout the region [14]. Furthermore, the majority of research is based on data produced by 16 S amplicon sequencing rather than WMS sequencing, providing an indirect measure of both microbial diversity and function [15–17]. Thus, a pilot study has been performed to explore the fascinating diversity of an Indian tribe, which is vividly represented in the village of Pangi, situated in the Chamba district of Himachal Pradesh. The Pangi village is home to a thriving population which has been successfully maintaining their own unique cultural heritage. This study aims to interpret the comprehensive composition of the whole gut microbiome (bacteriome, virome, and mycobiome) in the tribal community using the WMS sequencing approach. It aims to identify and analyse any potential variations that may exist in the gut microbiome of individuals who are native to Pangi or have spent their entire lives in this region, compared to those who have migrated to urban cities in search of better livelihood opportunities. Taking into account the One Health initiative launched by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), the aim of this research is to highlight the unexplored microbial landscape and its functional aspects that may develop significant implications in human health and disease.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and metadata collection

The study was conducted in accordance with the approval given by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Panjab University, under identification number (PUIEC/2018/140/R/29/10). The recruitment of healthy volunteers was primarily based on a thorough inclusion-exclusion criteria, which included a comprehensive health status questionnaire and complete dietary details (Additional file 1). The primary inclusion/exclusion criteria included a number of inquiries, including age (criteria: between 18 and 55 years), whether they self-identified as healthy, and if they had moved to an urban area or were native residents. Queries were also made regarding any diseases or injuries, as well as any stomach-related issues that may have occurred in the past three months. Pregnant and breastfeeding women were not selected to participate in the study. Moreover, three months before the collection of the samples, the participants of the study should not have used any antibiotics. The Indian Council of Medical Research’s (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017) were strictly taken in to account while collection of samples. A total of 100 healthy volunteers were selected for the study, and they were divided into two distinct groups. The first group comprised of 50 permanent residents of the Pangi valley, who mainly depend on agriculture for their livelihood. The second group comprised of 50 migrants who had shifted to urban areas 15–20 years ago for better employment opportunities and greater access to higher education. The final analysis was done using 28 healthy volunteers, who were excluded based on interviews and quality control analysis. Moreover, an equal number of volunteers were selected for each group to counter any disparities in the results.

Collection of fecal samples, metagenomic DNA extraction and WMS sequencing

Faecal samples were collected in dupliacte from selected individuals from both native and migrant populations. A negative control was also included by leaving an open RNA later tube in the environment where the sample collection was taking place overnight to capture the background microbial contaminants. This was done to minimize contamination risks. All samples were stored within 10 min of collection at -20 °C, and were transported in 6mL RNA later solution. This was done to eliminate the possibility of any environmental contamination in the samples. The QIAamp® PowerFecal® Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Germany), was used to extract DNA from fecal samples, and the DNA fragments were visualized using a 1% agarose gel. The sequencing library was prepared using 2 × 150 bp paired-end chemistry, and the Illumina NovaSeq platform was employed to perform the WMS sequencing.

Bioinformatic analysis

The raw reads generated by next generation sequencing (NGS) from all samples were processed using the bioBakery pipeline. Quality control tool, KneadData (vo.12.0) was used to filter out low quality sequences as well as host DNA contamination, using GRCh37 as human genome reference. The read quality metrics produced by KneadData have been included in Additional file 2 for further reference. The raw reads exhibited a mean sequencing depth of 28.4 ± 7.3 million reads. The mean GC content was 46.29%, and the average Phred score was 35.83. KneadData identified and removed adapter sequences using Trimmomatic, and eliminated any potential host contamination by aligning data with bowtie2 alignment software (version 2.5.1). The taxonomic characterization was conducted by employing MetaPhlan, a tool in the bioBakery suite (version 4.0.6), which employed it’s ChocoPhlAnSGB database to provide species-specific characterization [18]. The summary of the reads analyzed by MetaPhlan analysis, including the number of reads mapped and the number of reads not in the reference or unmapped reads are represented in Additional file 2. For the assessment of the viruses and fungi in the samples, Kaiju (version 1.10.1) was used, which had fungal and viral specific databases [19]. Functional annotation was carried out using HMP Unified Metabolic Analysis Network (HUMAnN) (v3.8), for the identification and quantification of microbial pathways and gene families found in WMS sequencing data [20]. Finally, Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) (v6.0.3) was utilized to predict the presence of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) [21]. This tool compared the genetic sequences of our data with a comprehensive database of known antibiotic resistance genes, and identified any matching sequences based on homology.

Data analysis and visualization

Data analysis and visualization was carried out using R [22, 23]. The results of MetaPhlan were analysed in R studio using packages like readr (version 2.1.5), tidyverse (version 2.0.0), dplyr (version 1.1.4), ggplot2 (version3.5.0) and vegan (2.6.4). The output files from MetaPhlan were properly filtered and log transformed for effective visualization. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon Index, while beta diversity was determined by Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. Additionally, a correlation matrix was also constructed in order to comprehend the relationship between the most prevalent microbial taxa and the different dietary variables most frequently consumed by the healthy volunteers of both groups. A slightly different approach was taken to analyze the Kaiju output files, which were directly imported into the Galaxy web browser. The Krona plots were generated and downloaded as HTML files [24, 25]. Furthermore, the results of HUMAnN produced three output files: gene families, path abundance, and path coverage. These files were analyzed in R using similar packages. The main focus on the results obtained from the RGI tool was to identify effective antibiotics and the key resistance mechanisms found within the human gut, which was done using R packages like tidyverse, ggplot, dplyr, stringr (version 1.5.1), reshape2 (version 1.4.4), and plotly (version 4.10.4).

Results

Diversity of microbial species in gut microbiome

The results provided important insights not only into the microbial diversity, but also into the overall functionality and resistome of the gut microbiome in this tribal population. The comprehensive taxonomic profile of the total microbiota present within the gut microbiome of both groups using MetaPhlAn revealed a total of 16,526 taxonomic level entries for the native group and 15,764 for the migrated group. Nearly all taxonomic entries were categorized up to the genus level; however, a few entries only included partial data, which signified either a novel species or a lack of information for those particular species in the tool’s database. After complete pre-processing; the top taxonomic entries for the native group in decreasing order of their abundance included Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, Megamonas, Clostridium, Treponema, Butyrivibrio, Faecalibacterium, Fusicatenibacter, Lachnospiraceae and Mitsuokella, whereas for the migrated group, the top abundant species were Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, Lachnospiraceae, Megasphaera, Ligilactobacillus, Mitsuokella, Coprococcus, Faecalibacterium, Megamonas and Treponema, with difference in their relative abundances; represented using a comparative bar plot (Fig. 1a). The results of alpha diversity calculated using the Shannon Index yielded a value of 7.24238 for the native group and 7.30088 for the migrated group. These results indicate high species richness and evenness reflecting a rich ecosystem that supports diverse microbial life. Furthermore, the results of Beta diversity calculated using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity indicated higher within group dissimilarity compared to the native dataset. These results were visualized using Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) plot and a box plot which have been illustrated in Fig. 1(b) and 1(c) respectively.

While this study examines the diversity of the gut microbiome in individuals residing in Pangi compared to those who have migrated to urban cities, dietary patterns significantly influence the stability, functionality, and diversity of the microbial community within the human gut [26]. The habitual diet of these healthy volunteers was recorded in an effort to interpret microbiome variation (Additional file 1). Therefore, in order to determine the frequency of consumption of different food groups, such as fermented foods, packaged foods, fruits, protein, grains, and vegetables, dietary intake data was gathered. Initially, MaAsLin2 was used for multivariable association studies involving volunteer sex, age, and diet. Due to the small sample size, none of the diet-associated microbial trends—especially those within the Prevotella clade—remained significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. As a result, the MaAsLin2 results are only offered as supplemental (Additional file 2). Thus, Spearman Rank correlation was used which was the more appropriate for exploratory investigation of this nature. The monotonic relationships between microbial species and dietary variables were described using Spearman rank correlation. Numerous moderate correlations (ρ 0.4–0.6) were found in the analysis. Interestingly, different Prevotella clades showed differing degrees of association with particular dietary factors. Furthermore, the genus Bifidobacterium demonstrated positive associations with protein sources, grains, and vegetables, and negative associations with fermented foods. The complete results, including other prevalent species, are presented in Fig. 2.

The output files from Kaiju contained taxonomic information on the mycobiome of both groups, organized in a hierarchical order. The results indicated a nearly identical mycobiome, with Dikarya as the dominant subkingdom in both groups (74%), while Fungi incertae sedis accounted for 23% of the total mycobiome. The total reads assigned as fungi by Kaiju were 7,300,054 for the native group and 9,696,227 for the migrated group. Dikarya further consisted of Ascomycota (69% in native and 68% in migrated group) and Basidiomycota (29% in both groups), and Ascomycota included two taxonomic clades; Saccharomyceta and Taphrinomycotina. Saccharomyceta further comprised of Pezizomycotina, Saccharomycetales, Sordariomyceta, Eurotiomycetes, Dothideomycetes, Pezizales, and 1% Orbilia oligospora, whereas Taphrinomycotina included Schizosaccharomyces, Protomyces, Pneumocystis, and Saitoella complicate. Furthermore, Basidiomycota incorporated Agaricomycotina, Ustilaginomycotina, Pucciniomycotina, and Wallemia. Lastly, Fungi incertae sedis included Mucormycotina, Chytridiomycetes, Microsporidia, and Kickxellaceae. Figure 3 illustrates the diversity of gut mycobiome in both groups.

Glimpses into the gut virome of both groups, revealed a multitude of viruses that reside in the gut as commensals (Fig. 4). Total viral reads assigned by Kaiju for the native group were 3,848,833 and 4,416,857 for the migrated group. A vast number of viruses belonged to Heunggongvirae—83% in native and 81% in migrated, majority of them being bacteriophages. In the native group, 14% of viruses were Varidnavaria, 2% Riboviria, and 1% remained unidentified, whereas among the migrated population, 16% were Varidnavaria, 1% Riboviria, while 2% still remains unclassified. Subsequent investigation revealed that the entire realm of Heunggongvirae comprised of Caudoviricetes. Notably, even though the results showed comparable diversity in both groups, a major difference could be observed in the percentage of Ceduovirus in the gut, which was considerably high in the native group (16%) compared to the migrated group (0.04%). With this, the profiling of the complete diversity within the gut microbiome for both native and migrated groups was successfully accomplished.

Functional analysis of the gut microbiome

HMP Unified Metabolic Analysis Network (HUMAnN) analysis provided comprehensive functional profiles of the microorganisms in the gut microbiome of the tribal people under study. Initially, the native group comprised of 9,626,110 gene families, while the migrated group consisted of 10,349,412 gene families. After removing rows with unclassified species and incomplete UniRef IDs, a total of 2,803,867 entries remained for the native group and 3,193,112 entries for the migrated group. Notably, the proportion of unclassified species was approximately 30.94% for the native group while in the migrated group it accounted to about 29.59%, indicating a lack of information on these species within the tool’s database. After completely processing the files to retain only unique gene level entries, the final count for the gene families was determined to be 254 for the native group and 260 for the migrated group. The relative abundance values for these unique gene families were then log transformed, and the data was effectively represented in a more concise manner using a bar plot as shown in Fig. 5. Apart from this, a heat map was also generated to compare the relative abundance of different gene families within the community (Fig. 6).

The last of the HUMAnN analysis provided path coverage files that contained pathway coverage scores conveying the degree of certainty about the activeness of a pathway. A score of 1 indicated a high level of confidence in the pathway’s activity, while a score closer to 0 suggested that the pathway is present but not actively involved in the biological process. Among the 484 native pathways, only 115 demonstrated sample coverage of 1. Conversely, out of the 492 pathways found in the migrated group, 128 exhibited sample coverage of 1, surpassing the native pathways. Resistome characterization

The analysis conducted by Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) offered valuable insights into the resistome of the tribal population. The results gave information about the wide range of Antibiotic Resistance Genes (ARGs) and their corresponding antibiotics to which the gut bacteria had developed resistance against. Consequently, our study identified that the native group demonstrated resistance to 118 distinct antibiotics, whereas the migrated group exhibited resistance to 114 antibiotics, with almost comparable resistance composition for both native and migrated microbiomes (Fig. 11). The highest level of antibiotic resistance was seen against vancomycin alone, although vancomycin in combination with teicoplanin also showed significant resistance. Tetracycline, cholorotetracycline, doxycycline, oxytetracycline, minocycline, and florfenicol were among the other leading antibiotics against which resistance could be observed in all the healthy volunteers. Moreover, resistance was noted across various antibiotic classes such as betalactams, polymyxins, streptogramins, macrolides, sulfonamides, and fluoroquinolones (Fig. 11).

Percentages indicate the relative frequency of antibiotics for which resistance was detected within each group

The findings of RGI gave full details on the resistance mechanisms against these antibiotics, antimicrobial resistance gene families, and predicted DNA and protein sequences. Furthermore, prevalence was also calculated from RGI output using strict parameters, the results of which have been provided in Additional file 5. Getting into further details, we could observe that the most prevalent mechanisms were antibiotic target alteration, protection of antibiotic targets, antibiotic efflux, inactivation of antibiotics, and replacement of antibiotic targets (Fig. 12). Hence, the data provided valuable insights into the gut resistome, shedding light on its intricate linkage with human gut functionality and the immune system within both native and migrated microbiomes.

Discussion

This research work brings out in-depth information about the bacteriome, mycobiome, and virome, along with the functional ability of the gut microbial populations in the native and migrated communities. Prevotella copri was found to be the most dominant microbial species found in both native and migrated communities, and it was found to be relatively more dominant in the native community compared to the migrated one. This species has been predominantly linked to nutritious vegetable and high fiber-rich diets in many previous studies [27]. This species has garnered significant interest in recent years due to its diverse and sometimes conflicting associations. Prevotella, an anaerobic, Gram-negative, non-spore forming bacterium, is known to be highly prevalent in the human body, particularly in the oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract [28, 29]. There is evidence indicating that the rise in Prevotella copri abundance is associated with a significant decrease in Bacteroides species [27]. Several research indicates that Prevotella flourishes when consuming a plant-based or fiber-rich diet, and a decrease in its prevalence has been linked to high-fat and high-sugar diets, most commonly seen in westernized diets [28, 30]. This could also be noted in our findings, where there was a relatively higher abundance of Prevotella species in the native group than in the migrated group. In both groups, Bifidobacterium was found to be the second most abundant genus, with a relatively higher abundance in the migrated group than in the native group. Bifidobacteria are microorganisms that have a high G + C DNA content and belong to the phylum Actinobacteria [31]. The majority of these bacteria have demonstrated their potential to function as probiotic and postbiotic strains, as they may be capable of producing and distributing essential vitamins including riboflavin, thiamine, vitamin B6, and vitamin K, as well as related bioactive molecules such as folic acid, niacin, and pyridoxine [32]. Surprisingly Succinivibrionaceae was identified as one of the top abundant taxa in both groups, exhibiting significantly higher abundance in the native group compared to the migrated group. The family Succinivibrionaceae is made up of gammaproteobacteria that are gram-negative, strictly anaerobic, non-spore-forming species [33]. This finding suggested a prevalence of a non-vegetarian diet, as these microorganisms are commonly found in the rumen of cattle and sheep [34]. The potential influence of proximity to livestock could also be another contributing factor. A significant number of individuals within the native community under study inhabit the ground floor of their residences, which often coexist with cow or goat sheds, separated solely by wooden partitions. It has been reported that the Succinivibrionaceae family uses hydrogen to create succinate, which serves as a precursor to propionate production [35]. Propionate, a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), serves as a vital regulator of energy metabolism and metabolic function in the gut [36]. Furthermore, it plays a crucial role in maintaining the balance of the gut environment, thereby contributing to improved overall intestinal gut health [36]. In order to evaluate similarities in species composition, we also compared species found in our study with those from other well-established Indian cohorts. The results showed a prevalence of similar species across these studies, with organisms such as Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia appearing as the most abundant as well [37–39] .

In recent years, various dietary elements have been recognized as potential modulators of the gut microbiome’s composition and metabolic functions, which may have positive or negative impact on human health [40]. Therefore one of our goals was to investigate the extent to which these dietary factors influence the modulation of microbial abundance, either promoting an increase or a decrease. Our study indicated a positive correlation between Bifidobacteriaceae, Lactobacillaceae and protein intake. This finding aligns with earlier studies that have demonstrated how the consumption of plant-based proteins, such as pea protein or whey protein (a major part of the diet of volunteers), can increase the levels of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus [41]. Furthermore, the overall abundance of Eubacteriales indicated a positive correlation to fruits, grains, vegetables, and packaged foods. In contrast to this, the family Clostridiaceae exhibited negative correlations to the same dietary variables, suggesting how diet can influence the relative and absolute abundance of gut microbiome [42–45].

The present work also attempted to unchart the composition of gut mycobiome, profiling the uncharacterized fungi that have been relatively underexplored until now. Our findings highlighted the presence of many beneficial fungal species in both groups. Species such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Debaryomyces hansenii, which are known for their positive effects on human health as probiotics, showed a higher relative abundance in the native group compared to the migrated group [46, 47]. In addition, our results showed the presence of different species of Malassezia, which are traditionally known to be associated with the skin microbiota but are also found in the gut and other habitats [48]. The role of Malassezia in human health is multifaceted, demonstrating a mutually beneficial activity in atopic dermatitis and and also playing a protective role against other skin infections [49]. The protective function is due to its ability to compete with skin pathogens such as Candida auris [49]. In our study, we also found Malassezia pachydermatis, which has been known to be a potential cause of fungal infections in infants in intensive care units [50]. Notably, the relative prevalence of Malasezzia was equal in both groups. A low prevalence of plant pathogens such as Puccinia graminis, Fusarium oxysporum, Colletotrichum spp., Fusarium graminearum, and Ustilago maydis were also found. The presence of these plant pathogens in both groups may be attributed to several reasons. In case of native group, it may be attributed to their practice of storing water in special wooden barrels and their close affinity to nature through agriculture, which is also their primary source of livelihood. It can also be linked to the consumption of local herbs, wild fruits, and vegetables. Regarding the migrated group, their diet of raw vegetables and salads may be the reason for their presence. The CUG Ser1 clade, which was also identified in this study, is an important phylum of microbes that includes opportunistic pathogens. This clade was identified to be equally abundant in both groups, and it is named based on the translation of its CTG codon to serine instead of leucine [51]. It includes species such as Candida albicans, Candida parapsilosis, and Candida tropicalis [51].

In addition to the mycobiome, an effort was made to explore the complex web of viruses that exist in the gut microbiome. A substantial number of phages were identified in the findings, some of which included Lactococcus phage, Escherichia coli phage D6, Clostridium phage phiCD211, uncultured phage cr44_1, Mycobacterium phage Kumao, Bacillus phage G and Escherichia phage 500465-1. These phages have been widely acknowledged for their important role in maintaining the balance and health of the host [52]. They are an integral part of the gut microbiome and play a significant role in regulating the microorganisms and contributing to the overall gut health and function [52]. Furthermore, the significantly higher percentage of Ceduovirus in the gut microbiome of individuals in the native compared to the migrated group was quite interesting. Although, earlier studies have recognized the presence of the Ceduovirus phages in the dairy environment across the globe, there have been very few studies on the functional role of this virus [53–55]. Overall, analysis through a taxonomic lens suggests that the gut microbiome of individuals in the native group is comparatively healthier than the migrated group. However, the findings from the functional analysis suggest a slightly different scenario.

The results offer a thorough understanding of the functional abilities of the gut microbial community profiles, establishing the significance of functional analysis over taxonomic profiling. More specifically, it enabled us to explore both the active and inactive functional pathways, allowing us to identify the active gene families associated with these pathways. Previous studies have identified various metabolic pathways in the gut and the crucial role of microorganisms in these pathways in the maintenance of optimal human health as well as the progression of diseases [56, 57]. However, our study not only identified the major metabolic pathways in the gut microbiome but also identified the major gene families associated with these pathways. It is noteworthy that the “sucrose biosynthesis II” pathway was found to be the most abundant and active pathway in the native group, while in the migrated group, the “dTDP-β-L-rhamnose biosynthesis” pathway took the lead in terms of abundance and activeness. The major contributor for sucrose biosynthesis II pathway turned out to be Faecalibacterium prausinitizii, whereas for the dTDP-β-L-rhamnose biosynthesis pathway, Prevotella copri was the major contributing microorganism. The top pathways in the native group, “sucrose biosynthesis II” may indicate the body’s compulsion to store energy and use it during time of need [58]. Notably, this pathway is also the fourth most abundant in the migrated group. However, the most abundant pathway in the migrated group, “dTDP-β-L-rhamnose biosynthesis”, is the second most abundant in the native group. This particular pathway has been linked to enhanced gut health because of its major role in cell wall growth, bacterial phagocytosis, and the inhibition of sepsis-induced organ damage by rhamnose [59, 60]. Moreover, the rank of the “guanosine ribonucleotides de novo biosynthesis” pathway is ranked second among the migrated pathways and fifth among the native pathways. The function of this pathway has been associated with cell wall maintenance, as well as the processes of heredity, development, and growth [61]. Dysregulation in this pathway have been demonstrated to be linked with the onset of different human diseases [62]. Therefore, the increased abundance of the “guanosine ribonucleotides de novo biosynthesis” pathway in the migrated group is noteworthy.

While exploring the functional aspect in more detail, one of the most significant observations made in the course of this study is the existence of functional redundancy. This explains why different microorganisms belonging to different taxonomic groups have been able to make similar contributions to an ecosystem, which is achieved by the possession of redundant functional traits [63–65]. Despite the considerable microbial diversity evident in our results for alpha diversity, the same set of microorganisms was found to be the major contributors in almost all metabolic pathways, with Prevotella copri being identified as the major contributing microorganism in both groups. This observation provides a very valid hypothesis that could help explain the ability of the gut microbiome to stabilize the gut environment, even during stress conditions or dysbiosis, ultimately playing a role in maintaining human health. However, this equilibrium can also be disrupted by the regular consumption of antibiotics. The use of medication has now become a significant concern, as it is often used inappropriately and has various unintended effects on human hosts, posing specific, yet not fully characterized, risks, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [66, 67]. Recent studies have revealed that antibiotics can have a significant effect on the development of the intestinal microbiota in both short-term and long-term [66, 68, 69]. The intestinal microbiota plays a crucial role in the production of specific enzymes that possess the ability to modulate the efficacy of both systemic and oral medications [70]. The interaction between the intestinal microbiota and medications not only provides an easy way to inhibit pathogenic microorganisms but also possesses the ability to influence the development of intestinal microbial populations [71, 72]. The human gut microbiota possesses a reservoir of genes that provide resistance to antibiotics, which has a potential role in the development of multidrug-resistant pathogens due to horizontal gene transfer [73].

Antibiotic resistance (AMR) is a major challenge to the global health of mankind due to its raising rate of mortality linked to bacterial infections [74]. The human gut microbiome, also known as the gut resistome, is one such reservoir of all circulating antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and their combined abundances [75]. However, the evolutionary dynamics of human gut resistome and their transmission patterns still remain unclear [66, 76]. Even though prior research has shown that different mobile genetic elements (MGEs) may be responsible for ARG transmission and increased abundance. These MGEs may use horizontal gene transfer (HGT) to spread into closely related species within the gut microbiome. While direct antibiotic usage data of study participants were not available, our findings from the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) offered valuable insights into the reservoir of antibiotic resistance genes (resistome) within the gut of individuals under our study. The highest level of resistance observed against vancomycin alone and vancomycin in combination with teicoplanin in both groups is a cause for concern, as this antibiotic is used as a last resort for addressing serious infections resulting from Gram-positive bacteria, including Enterococcus species, Staphylococcus aureus, and Clostridium difficile [77]. Studies have reported that VanA-type VRE is characterized by high-level resistance to vancomycin and teicoplanin, while VanB-type VRE only shows resistance to vancomycin [78]. The rise of VRE (vancomycin-resistant enterococci) has emerged as a significant public health concern across many Asian countries [74]. The particular strain in question is a significant contributor to both hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) and bloodstream infections [74]. Its resistance mechanism involves antibiotic target alteration, which was also the most prevalent resistance mechanism identified in our findings (close to 76% in both groups), thus confirming our results. The second most common mechanism was antibiotic target protection, which is most commonly seen in the case of tetracyclines and related antibiotics. These antibiotics were also found to have the second highest level of resistance, following vancomycin and teicoplanin. Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria have developed significant resistance to tetracyclines as it is widely used for animal prophylaxis and treatment, as well as for managing certain human infections worldwide [79–81]. More importantly, the tetQ gene and the macrolide resistance gene ermF are located on a 65-kbp conjugative transposon called CTnDOT, which is present in 80% of Bacteroides spp. isolates [82]. Exposure to low levels of tetracycline triggers CTnDOT excision and conjugative transfer, leading to its rapid spread among Bacteroides strains, which were also observed as one of the most abundant in our results [82]. Our findings revealed a higher abundance of Bacteroides species compared to Enterococcus species, which coincided with our results of increased tetracycline prevalence. While vancomycin resistance genes exhibited relatively higher abundance or prominence in our samples, tetracycline resistance genes were more pervasive across individuals, leading to higher prevalence (Additional file 5). Moreover, an alarming rise of resistance against more than 100 antibiotics is a significant cause for concern, as commensals living in gut could potentially lead to the development of resistant clones of opportunistic pathogens [82]. The present study indicates that, besides the inequitable distribution of healthcare facilities, the role of environmental matrices like suboptimal water quality, inappropriate sanitation practices, and sediments of natural aquatic environments plays an important role in determining the resistome [83, 84]. These factors underline the significance of the One Health concept. Generally, data about antibiotic resistance in terms of the gut resistome is scarce globally, especially in the context of tribal regions [85]. Thus, the present study offers valuable information regarding the resistome diversity in the gut of the tribal population of Pangi, which may serve as a basis for future antimicrobial resistance studies in India.

Going by the previous reports of studies, we expected large differences between the native and migrated gut microbiota, with migration potentially having a profound effect on the microbiota. Contrary to our expectations, the current study presents a completely different picture. Interaction with the volunteers from the native as well as the migrated group helped in realizing that the lifestyle patterns were vastly different. The volunteers in the native group were in close contact with domesticated animals, consumed natural spring water as their drinking water, and used cow dung as their main fuel for cooking. On the other hand, the migrated group, residing in urban areas, had completely different patterns of lifestyle. The effect of Western lifestyle appeared to have contributed significantly to the younger generation of the tribal community as well. A major portion of their diet now comprised of imported foods, which are rich in preservatives and additives and are readily available through civil means at a relatively lower cost. Moreover, the findings also reveal that the individuals in both groups share a common, fundamental microbiome that remains unchanged over time. This might be one of the reasons for the diversity levels being similar in both groups. Through the integration of Whole-metagenome shotgun (WMS) sequencing and comprehensive computational analysis of the gut metagenome, we not only succeeded in identifying the diverse microorganisms (bacteriome, mycobiome, and virome) present in the gut, but also in understanding their potential functional abilities through a new perspective. However, the main limitation of the current study is the relatively small cohort size (n = 28) and a lack of a validation cohort. To make inferences more reliable, further studies in a larger and more representative cohort is required.

Conclusion

The fact that both cohorts had a highly diverse gut microbiome with relatively similar microbiomes and functional redundancy suggests that these microbes play a crucial role in sustaining a stable core microbiome and supporting a variety of physiological functions. The widespread resistance to over 100 antibiotics, including significant resistance to tetracyclines and vancomycin, is also noteworthy. However, the limitations of this study must be acknowledged, particularly the small sample size of healthy volunteers. The next crucial step is to validate the results of this study in a larger independent cohort. This will provide opportunities for further research in the areas of tribal studies and AMR surveillance on gut microbiome. In conclusion, we aspire that the findings and deductions made during this study will contribute valuable insights to the current body of literature on taxonomic, functional, and resistome analysis concerning tribal populations around the globe.
