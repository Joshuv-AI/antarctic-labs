The Linear Centroids Hypothesis: How Deep Network Features Represent Data
Thomas Walker, Ahmed Imtiaz Humayun, Randall Balestriero, Richard Baraniuk

Abstract
Identifying and understanding the features that a deep network (DN) extracts from its inputs to produce its outputs is a focal point of interpretability research. The Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) identifies features in terms of the linear directions formed by the inputs in a DN’s latent space. However, the LRH is limited as it abstracts away from individual components (e.g., neurons and layers), is susceptible to identifying spurious features, and cannot be applied across sub-components (e.g., multiple layers). In this paper, we introduce the Linear Centroids Hypothesis (LCH) as a new framework for identifying the features of a DN. The LCH posits that features correspond to linear directions of centroids, which are vector summarizations of the functional behavior of a DN in a local region of its input space. Interpretability studies under the LCH can leverage existing LRH tools, such as sparse autoencoders, by applying them to the DN’s centroids rather than to its latent activations. We demonstrate that doing so yields sparser feature dictionaries for DINO vision transformers, which also perform better on downstream tasks. The LCH also inspires novel approaches to interpretability; for example, LCH can readily identify circuits in GPT2-Large. Code to study the LCH can be found here.

Introduction

Understanding and identifying which characteristics of the input a deep network (DN) extracts and utilizes to form its output is important to facilitate their reliable deployment.
The crux of interpretability research is to identify these
features of the DN
and their influence on the computational graph, often referred to as the
circuits of the DN

(
2
;
46
)
.

The current state-of-the-art for identifying the features of a DN is the
Linear Representation Hypothesis
(LRH), which posits that features are represented as linear directions in a DN’s latent activation space
(
12
;
41
)
.
The LRH has enabled the development of powerful interpretability tools; however, it suffers from three critical limitations.
First, it abstracts away from individual sub-components of the DN (e.g., neurons, layers or attention mechanisms), which makes it difficult to link features to the computational graph
(
46
)
.
Second, it is unclear whether all linear directions of latent activations correspond to features, making it susceptible to identifying spurious features
(
50
)
.
Third, its disjoint application to individual latent spaces has encumbered the field with the task of contextualizing features across sub-components
(
3
)
.

In this paper, we introduce a new framework for identifying the features of DNs.
The
Linear Centroids Hypothesis (LCH)
identifies the features of a DN based on how it organizes the geometry of its input space into local “experts.”

This is motivated by the fact that continuous piecewise affine (CPA) DNs (e.g., ReLU networks) exactly and other smooth networks (e.g., transformers) approximately partition their input space into a Voronoi-like tiling of polytopal regions, on which distinct functional mappings (i.e., experts) operate
(
7
)
.
The LCH posits that
features correspond to the linear directions of the
centroids
of these regions.

Crucially, a DN’s centroids can be efficiently calculated for any differentiable DN sub-component, since they are equal to the row-sum of its input-output Jacobian.
This transitions feature identification from understanding where in the latent space an input lies to understanding the action of the local expert operating on the input, which provides a grounded and mechanistic perspective to interpretability.

Our contributions are the following.

(C1)
A framework for interpretability that is not susceptible to identifying features irrelevant to the computations in the DN, unlike the LRH (see
Figure

3
).

(C2)
A demonstration that simply exchanging LRH’s latent activations for LCH’s centroids improves current interpretability techniques and eliminates the necessity of intricate procedures to extract hierarchical DN features (see
Figure

4
).

(C3)
The introduction of novel interpretability techniques to facilitate the efficient identification of the circuits of a DN (see
Figure

6
).

(C4)
A new method for constructing gradient-based saliency maps–the local centroid–that is faithful to the behaviors of the DN (see
Figures

1
and
8
).

In short, the LCH turns feature discovery into the study of the alignment of the local experts of a network, so that interpretability becomes mechanistic by construction rather than post hoc. Our new centroid-based lens helps unify feature dictionaries, probing, circuit discovery, and saliency maps under a single geometric object: the network’s induced input-space partition.

Deep Networks as Utilizing Local Experts

In this section, we demonstrate how continuous piecewise affine (CPA) DNs can be intuitively thought of as employing local experts to form their outputs.
In particular, we demonstrate that centroids, which are computable for arbitrary DNs, describe the arrangement and behavior of these experts.

Deep Networks and their Approximations.

A DN
f:\mathbb{R}^{d^{(0)}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{(L)}}
, with the convention that
d^{(0)}=d
, is a composition of
L
functions
f^{(\ell)}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell-1)}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell)}}
.
These functions, referred to as layers, typically consist of an affine transformation followed by a nonlinearity.
We will denote the mapping across multiple layers as
f^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{2})}}
for
1\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq L
.

Using spline approximation theory
(
32
;
45
)
, DNs can be approximated to arbitrary precision or even characterized exactly using CPA splines
(
5
)
.
More specifically, one can write
f({\bm{x}})\approx{\bm{A}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}}
, where
{\bm{A}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(L)}\times d}
and
{\bm{b}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(L)}}
are the affine parameters specific to the
linear region

\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
encompassing
{\bm{x}}
.
Here
\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})
identifies the equivalence class of
{\bm{x}}
under the collection of all equivalence classes
\mathcal{V}
constructed by
\sim
where
{\bm{x}}_{1}\sim{\bm{x}}_{2}
if and only if
{\bm{x}}_{1}
and
{\bm{x}}_{2}
are in the same linear region.

Similar approximations can be constructed for each layer
f^{(\ell)}
to obtain

f({\bm{x}})\approx{\bm{A}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}^{(L)}}^{(L)}\left(\dots\left({\bm{A}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}^{(1)}}^{(1)}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}_{\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}^{(1)}}^{(1)}\right)\dots\right)+{\bm{b}}_{\omega^{(L)}_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}}^{(L)},

where
\omega_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}^{(\ell)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell-1)}}
denotes the linear region for the mapping
f^{(\ell)}
encompassing
f^{(1\leftarrow\ell-1)}({\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell-1)}}
.
For CPA approximations of sub-components of the DN, say
f^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{2})}}
for
1\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq L
, we adopt a similar notation.
When the DN employs CPA nonlinearities (e.g., ReLU), these approximations are exact
(
5
)
.

This piecewise discretisation of the output of a DN sub-component, as the application of distinct affine transformations to local regions of the input space, enables us to interpret a DN sub-component as forming its output by employing local experts.

The Geometry of a Deep Network.

The arrangement of the regions in which the experts of a CPA DN operate
\left\{\omega^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}_{\bm{\nu}}\right\}_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{V}}
, is what we refer to as the
geometry of the DN sub-component
.

On the one hand, the regions forming this geometry can be thought of as being constructed by the intersection of a collection of hyperplanes
(
36
;
6
;
16
)
.
Indeed, each layer of the sub-component has a set of nonlinearities (neurons), each of which determines an activation level set within its input space (i.e., the hyperplane between being active or inactive in the case of the ReLU nonlinearity).
In a hierarchical fashion, starting from the first layer, these hyperplanes intersect in the input space of each layer to construct regions in the input space of the sub-component

(
7
;
23
)
.

On the other hand, the geometry of a DN sub-component can be parametrized as a
power-diagram subdivision

(
7
)
, which is a hierarchical combination of
power diagrams

(
25
)
.
Power diagrams are generalizations of the canonical Voronoi tiling.
A Voronoi tiling is the parametrization of the input space partition induced by the
K
-means clustering algorithm; it is constructed by identifying a finite set of
centroid
vectors and then assigning each point in the input space to the closest centroid with respect to the Euclidean distance.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a collection of
Q
centroid-radius pairs

\left\{\left({\bm{\mu}}_{q},\tau_{q}\right)\right\}_{q=1}^{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
,
a power diagram tessellates
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
Q
disjoint tiles
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{Q}\}
such that
\cup_{q=1}^{Q}\omega_{q}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
with each tile given by

\small\omega_{q}=\left\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:q=\argmin_{q^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,Q\}}\left(\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{q^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\tau_{q^{\prime}}\right)\right\}.

(1)

The distance minimized in (
1
) is called the Laguerre distance
(
25
)
, and differs from the Euclidean distance only in the addition of the weighting provided by the radius parameter.
In the sequel, we will focus on centroids, though the radii are also of considerable interest; indeed, it is the presence of the radius that means that a centroid of a region in a power diagram need not be contained within the region itself.

The power diagram subdivision induced by the DN sub-component is then constructed recursively through layer-wise power diagrams.
The first layer of the DN sub-component partitions the input space
\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}
as
\Omega^{(\ell_{1})}
through a power diagram.
The second layer of the DN sub-component then partitions the projections of each tile
\omega^{(\ell_{1})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1})}}
induced by
f^{(\ell_{1})}
as a power diagram.
These are then pulled back to
\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}
to yield a finer partition of the input space
\Omega^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{1}+1)}
.
This partition is an example of a power diagram subdivision.
Continuing sequentially for each layer of the DN sub-component constructs the complete power diagram subdivision
\Omega^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}
of the DN sub-component
(
7
)
.

Each region
\omega^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}_{\bm{\nu}}
in a power diagram subdivision has a
centroid

{\bm{\mu}}^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}_{\bm{\nu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}
and
radius

\tau^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}_{\bm{\nu}}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Despite each tile
\omega^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}_{\bm{\nu}}
being defined implicitly through a combinatorial intersection of half-spaces, its centroid is defined explicitly and computable independently of other regions.

Proposition 2.2

(
7
)
.

Let
\mathbf{J}_{{\bm{x}}}\left(f^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell_{2})}\times d^{(\ell_{1}-1)}}
denote the input-output Jacobian of
f^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}
at
f^{(1\leftarrow\ell_{1}-1)}({\bm{x}})
. Then,
{\bm{\mu}}^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}_{\bm{\nu}({\bm{x}})}=\left(\mathbf{J}_{{\bm{x}}}\left(f^{(\ell_{1}\leftarrow\ell_{2})}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}
.

Through the power-diagram subdivision parametrization, centroids describe the arrangement of a DN’s experts.
With Proposition
2.2
, centroids additionally describe the action of the expert as they integrate the sensitivity of the output with respect to the input across each output dimension.

Importantly, Proposition
2.2
efficiently extends this geometrical perspective beyond CPA DNs, as Jacobian vector products are readily computable for any differentiable mapping.

The Linear Centroids Hypothesis

In
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
, we make explicit what we mean by the
features of a DN
, connect them to the geometry of DNs, and derive the Linear Centroids Hypothesis (LCH).
We provide evidence for the LCH in
Section

3.3
, and then demonstrate how it is not susceptible to identifying spurious features in
Section

3.4
.

3.1
Defining Features and Circuits

Input samples have characteristics (e.g., the characteristic of having whiskers may be present for input images of cats).
Regions of the input space of a DN consist of a combination of these characteristics, which can be extracted by the DN to help generate its output (e.g., extracting the characteristic of having whiskers to help classify the input as a cat).

Definition 3.1
.

The characteristics of input samples that a DN sub-component
extracts
and
utilizes
to form its output are known as the
features
of the DN sub-component,
with the precise influence of a feature on the computational graph of the DN sub-component being referred to as a circuit.

We will refer to features that have been extracted but not utilized as
spurious
features.
The task of interpretability is to identify the features and circuits of a DN sub-component that are not spurious
(
46
)
.
We relate Definition
3.1
to other known formalizations of the features of a DN sub-component in
Appendix

A
.

By studying the activation-level sets of nonlinearities, we can already distinguish between features and spurious features as follows.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
be two sets of inputs with distinct semantic features.
For a CPA network to have consistently different activation patterns for
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, it is necessary that the latent activations of these sets of inputs at an intermediate layer of the network are separable by a hyperplane.

Proof.
Let
f
be a CPA (ReLU) network with each layer of the network also being CPA.
To have consistently different activation patterns for the inputs of
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, it means that at some layer a neuron activates exclusively for the latent activations of only
\mathcal{X}_{1}
or
\mathcal{X}_{2}
.
Therefore, the activation level-set of this neuron must separate the latent activations of these sets of inputs.
Because the activation level-set of a ReLU neuron is a hyperplane, the latent activations of the inputs
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
must lie in distinct half-spaces defined by this hyperplane.
∎

Lemma
3.2
is only a necessary condition for the representation of a feature.
It represents the
extraction
component of feature formation.

Utilization
comes only when nonlinearities migrate their activation level-sets to separate the latent activations, as these dictate when the behavior of the DN sub-component changes.

This explains the known limitations
(
51
;
38
;
46
;
50
)
of current interpretability techniques, which identify features by clustering the latent activations of inputs
(
26
;
52
;
21
)
.
These techniques assume that latent activations conform to the
Linear Representation Hypothesis
(LRH).
The LRH posits that the features of a DN can be identified with directions formed by the latent activations of inputs
(
12
;
41
)
.
Thus, the LRH only guarantees the identification of
extracted
features.
This results in an ambiguity surrounding its interpretation
(
51
)
, its brittleness when applied to downstream tasks
(
38
;
50
)
, and its insufficiency for explaining the functional behavior of DN sub-components
(
46
)
.

3.2
Connecting Features to Deep Network Geometry

In light of Lemma
3.2
, its proof and recalling how the nonlinearities construct the geometry of a DN sub-component, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3.3
.

The features of a DN sub-component are represented by collections of linear regions whose boundaries are parallel. (Formalized in
Appendix

B
.)

We can support Hypothesis
3.3
with prior work that uses the geometry of DN sub-components to characterize their properties.

24
demonstrates that the generalization and robustness properties of DNs – including transformers, residual networks, and convolutional neural networks – emerge as the geometry of the DN simplifies.
Namely, linear regions migrate from the training samples toward the decision boundaries.
Similarly, the relationship between latent activations and the distance to the boundary of the linear region in which they reside was linked to the toxicity property of large language models
(
8
)
.
Likewise, the density of linear regions in the geometry of DN sub-components has been connected to the reasoning capabilities of large language models
(
11
)
.

To identify when nonlinearities form linear regions with parallel boundaries, and thus identify a feature under Hypothesis
3.3
, we can utilize the power-diagram subdivision perspective of a sub-component’s geometry.

Theorem 3.4
.

The features of a DN sub-component for a collection of inputs
\mathcal{X}
are equivalent to linear directions of the corresponding centroids. (Formalized and proved in
Appendix

B
.)

In light of the interpretation of centroids as describing the arrangement and behavior of the local experts of a DN,
Theorem

3.4
is perhaps not surprising.
Intuitively, it says that features correspond to inputs whose local experts align, as measured by cosine similarity.
Using
Theorem

3.4
, we equivalently state our hypothesis (i.e., Hypothesis
3.3
) as
the features of a DN sub-component being represented by collections of centroids forming linear directions
, and term it the
Linear Centroids Hypothesis
(LCH).

3.4
The Linear Centroids Hypothesis Mitigates Spurious Feature Identification

In the application of the LRH for interpretability, there is an ambiguity as to whether all latent activations that form linear directions correspond to features
(
51
)
.
In contrast, with
Theorem

3.4
, we showed that linear centroids are equivalent to features under our hypothesis, Hypothesis
3.3
.

To compare the susceptibility of LCH and LRH to spurious feature identification, we train a convolutional neural network to classify the FashionMNIST dataset
(
56
)
.
However, at initialization, we color the images by sampling from a discrete set of 10 colors and varying the degree of correlation between the coloring and the dataset labels.
When the coloring is fully correlated, the color feature is not spurious; when it is random, it is spurious.
We evaluate the extent to which the color feature is linearly represented by training a linear probe to predict color from the DN’s latent activations or centroids and measuring its accuracy.

In the left panel of
Figure

3
, it is clear that the centroids of the color feature form linear directions to the extent that the feature is not spurious, in contrast to latent activations, which are linear even when the feature is spurious.

In the right panel of
Figure

3
, we consider training sparse autoencoders on the latent activations and the centroids of the last layer multi-layer perceptron of DINOv2
(
40
)
and DINOv3
(
47
)
vision transformers.
For a given activation type (i.e., latent activations or centroids computed at the last multi-layer perceptron block of the models), we compare the obtained sparse autoencoder dictionaries by computing the maximum cosine similarity of each DINOv2 feature with the full DINOv3 dictionary.
Since DINOv3 refines the features learned by DINOv2, the dictionary should exhibit high cosine similarities with those features.
Indeed, this is what we observe for the dictionaries obtained using centroids.
In contrast, for the dictionary obtained using latent activations, a large proportion of the features have cosine similarities of around

This bimodal distribution suggests that the identified features do not correlate across the models and are instead spurious artifacts.
In particular, it is contrary to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis

In

5
Discussion

The Linear Centroids Hypothesis (LCH) reframes interpretability around a geometric object that is intrinsic to how a network computes:
the centroids of its induced partition
, which summarize the action of a network’s local experts and how their arrangement.
This shift makes interpretability more
mechanistic by construction
, and helps address a key limitation of LRH-style analyses: linear directions in activation space that can be predictive yet only weakly tied to the computation that produces a behavior.

Crucially, the LCH is
operational
: centroids are efficiently accessible for differentiable components via Jacobian vector products, enabling a drop-in replacement for activations in standard interpretability pipelines.
In our experiments, this simple swap yields cleaner feature dictionaries, more robust probing, and more targeted circuit localization, suggesting that centroids provide a better substrate for feature discovery.

Limitations and Future Work.

The power diagram subdivision of a DN is parameterized by both centroids and radii.
This study focuses entirely on the centroids, leaving the radii—which determine whether a centroid is contained within its own region—unexplored.
Future research should investigate the radius parameters of the power diagrams.
Integrating radii analysis could provide a more comprehensive geometric interpretation of the network’s local experts.

This work introduced the
local centroid
as a faithful, gradient-based saliency map to highlight relevant features for single inputs.
Future efforts should extend this application, testing local centroids across a wider variety of DN architectures and broader input distributions to further validate their explanatory power.

Impact Statement.

This work contributes to the foundations of interpretability by proposing the
Linear Centroids Hypothesis
(LCH), a geometry-grounded view of features that is more directly tied to computation than post hoc linear directions in activation space.
By treating features as directions spanned by centroids of a model’s induced partition, the LCH supports more faithful feature discovery and component-level analysis, which can improve reliability when interpreting or auditing model behavior.

The LCH framework is broadly applicable – centroids are accessible for any differentiable components – and so it can be integrated into existing interpretability pipelines (e.g., sparse dictionaries, probing, circuit localization).
At the same time, stronger interpretability can enable both beneficial uses (debugging, safety, transparency) and misuse (more targeted manipulation of model behavior); we therefore encourage careful evaluation, transparency about limitations, and responsible release of any tooling built on these methods.

Appendix A
Comparison to Prior Work

Features.

Definition
3.1
is analogous to the notion of a DN concept used in
41
, which provides a rigorous theoretical characterization of the LRH.
In particular, in
41
, a DN feature is a variable that leads to a particular output when caused by a context.
In Definition
3.1
, the context would correspond to the input samples that possess a given characteristic.
The variable notion of
41
would then be equivalent to the
extraction
requirement of Definition
3.1
, since the assumed distribution on the input space would define a distribution over these regions.
Similarly, the referenced causation on the output would be equivalent to the
utilization
requirement of Definition
3.1
.

Circuits.

Circuits were initially introduced in
39
to deal with the apparent poly-semantic nature of neurons.
That is, specific neurons were observed to trigger on seemingly semantically disjoint inputs, whereas ensembles of neurons demonstrated more reliable activation patterns.
Instead, our notion of a circuit arises naturally as the responses of the components of a DN to a feature.
In particular, this response is likely to involve multiple neurons or components of a DN, given the DN’s compositional construction.

Appendix B
Formal Theory

Hypothesis B.1
.

A feature of the
\ell^{\text{th}}
layer of a DN is a collection of regions constructed by hyperplanes whose normals have a pair-wise cosine similarity bounded below by
1-\epsilon
.

Proposition B.2
.

The features in the input space of the
\ell^{\text{th}}
layer of a DN sub-component are represented by centroids that form an affine subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{(\ell-1)}}
with deviations proportional to approximately
\sqrt{2\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Consider the linear regions of the features (as per Hypothesis
B.1
) as a sequence of adjacent linear regions
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}
separated by hyperplanes
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{k-1}
.
Let their corresponding centroids be
{\bm{\mu}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{k}
.
The shared boundary
\Pi_{i}
between region
i
and
i+1
has a normal vector
n_{i}={\bm{\mu}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}_{i+1}
.
By Hypothesis B.1, the boundaries are approximately parallel, meaning for, any
i,j
, we have
\cos(\theta_{i,j})\geq 1-\epsilon
.
Let
d=\frac{{\bm{n}}_{1}}{\|{\bm{n}}_{1}\|_{2}}
be the unit direction vector of the first normal.
An arbitrary centroid
{\bm{\mu}}_{m}
(
1\leq m\leq k
) can be reached from the first centroid via the telescoping sum

{\bm{\mu}}_{m}={\bm{\mu}}_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}{\bm{n}}_{i}.

We can decompose each normal vector
{\bm{n}}_{i}
into a component parallel to
{\bm{d}}
and a perpendicular component
{\bm{u}}_{i}
(where
{\bm{u}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{d}}=0
)

{\bm{n}}_{i}=\|{\bm{n}}_{i}\|_{2}\cos(\theta_{1,i}){\bm{d}}+\|{\bm{n}}_{i}\|_{2}\sin(\theta_{1,i}){\bm{u}}_{i}.

Substituting this decomposition into our sum yields

{\bm{\mu}}_{m}={\bm{\mu}}_{1}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\|{\bm{n}}_{i}\|_{2}\cos(\theta_{1,i})\right){\bm{d}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\|{\bm{n}}_{i}\|_{2}\sin(\theta_{1,i}){\bm{u}}_{i}.

The first two terms,
{\bm{\mu}}_{1}-c\cdot{\bm{d}}
, define a 1-dimensional affine subspace.
The third term represents the orthogonal deviation from this subspace.
Since
\cos(\theta_{1,i})\geq 1-\epsilon
, using
\sin^{2}(\theta)=1-\cos^{2}(\theta)
, we find that
\sin(\theta_{1,i})\leq\sqrt{2\epsilon}
.
Thus, we see explicitly that every centroid
{\bm{\mu}}_{m}
lies along the same 1-dimensional affine subspace, with orthogonal deviations bounded by a factor proportional to
\sqrt{\epsilon}
.
∎

Theorem B.3
.

The features of a DN sub-component are represented by centroids that form approximate affine subspaces.

Proof.

For the forward implication, we use the same proof as Proposition
B.2
to show that any feature within the input space of the DN sub-component (as formalized by Hypothesis
B.1
) yields centroids that form an approximate affine subspace.
It remains to show that any feature of the DN sub-component that exists within the input space of an intermediate layer
l
yields centroids within the input space of the DN sub-component which form approximate affine subspaces.

Proposition
B.2
establishes that the centroids at the input of layer
l
form an approximate affine subspace.
The inputs corresponding to these centroids lie in one of finitely many linear regions, as the map to layer
l
is continuous piecewise-affine (CPA).
Let
{\bm{A}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{A}}_{m}
be the linear maps operating on these linear regions
\omega^{(l)}_{1},\dots,\omega^{(l)}_{m}
.

By Proposition
2.2
, the centroids in the input of layer
l
are mapped back to the input space of the DN sub-component via the input-output Jacobian. Therefore, for a centroid
{\bm{\mu}}^{(l)}_{h}
at
{\bm{h}}\in\omega_{i}^{(l)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d^{(l-1)}}
where
{\bm{h}}=f^{(1\leftarrow l-1)}({\bm{x}})
, we have

{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{(1\leftarrow l)}=({\bm{J}}_{{\bm{x}}}(f^{(1\leftarrow l)}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}

Using the chain rule, this expands to

{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{(1\leftarrow l)}={\bm{J}}_{{\bm{x}}}(f^{(1\leftarrow l-1)})^{\top}{\bm{J}}_{{\bm{h}}}(f^{(l)})^{\top}\mathbf{1},

which simplifies to

{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{(1\leftarrow l)}={\bm{A}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\mu}}^{(l)}_{{\bm{h}}}

Therefore, because the centroids
{\bm{\mu}}^{(l)}_{h}
form an approximate affine subspace, and the mapping
{\bm{A}}_{i}^{\top}
is linear for each region
i
, the resulting centroids
{\bm{\mu}}^{(1\leftarrow l)}_{x}
form
m
approximate affine subspaces.
∎
