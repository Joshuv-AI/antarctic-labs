Plant-based diet quality and gut microbiota in relation to cardiometabolic risk in Korean adults
Authors: Ji-Hee Shin, Eun-Ji Song, Mi Young Lim, Hye-Jo Choi, Jihye Kim, Young-Do Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study investigated the association between plant-based diet quality and gut microbiota and explored whether integrating microbiota profiles with dietary indices improved strength of association with selected cardiometabolic outcomes.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we analyzed cohort data collected from 2,388 Korean adults between 2017 and 2019. Dietary quality indices, including the overall plant-based diet index (PDI), healthful PDI (hPDI), and unhealthful PDI (uPDI), were derived from a food frequency questionnaire. Gut microbiota were profiled using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Cardiometabolic risk factors included obesity, abdominal obesity, elevated fasting glucose, elevated triglycerides, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and elevated blood pressure.ResultsA higher hPDI score was associated with reduced obesity risk (OR = 0.72; 95% CI, 0.57–0.91) and improved gut microbiota α-diversity. A higher uPDI score was associated with increased risk of elevated fasting glucose (OR = 1.23; 95% CI, 1.00–1.52) and low HDL-C levels (OR = 1.35; 95% CI, 1.07–1.70), and lower α-diversity. hPDI and uPDI scores were also associated with differences in gut microbial community structure. For obesity and elevated fasting glucose, models integrating plant-based diet indices with gut microbiome features showed higher area under the curve than dietary-only models. Genus-level analyses identified key bacterial genera associated with dietary scores and metabolic traits. The predictive performance for low HDL-C remained modest across models.ConclusionsPlant-based diet quality was associated with gut microbiota composition and cardiometabolic health. Integrating gut microbiome features with dietary assessments provided greater explanatory value for obesity and elevated fasting glucose, although longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and causal pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00394-026-03991-z.Keywords: Plant-based diet index, Gut microbiota, Cardiometabolic risks, Obesity, Hyperglycemia, Dyslipidemia

Introduction

Plant-based diets have health benefits and are more environmentally sustainable than diets with a higher proportion of animal products [1, 2]. However, not all plant foods are equally beneficial to health [3]. Considering the quantity of animal food intake, three indices have been developed to assess the healthiness of plant-based diets: the overall plant-based diet index (PDI), healthful plant-based diet index (hPDI), and unhealthful plant-based diet index (uPDI). The PDI reflects overall adherence to plant-based foods regardless of their healthfulness. The hPDI measures adherence to diets higher in healthy plant foods such as whole grains, fruits, and vegetables and lower in less healthy plant foods (refined grains and foods high in sugars) and animal foods. In contrast, the uPDI, a diet inverse to the hPDI, emphasizes higher intake of plant foods high in refined grains and sugars in the context of an overall plant-based diet. Thus, while the PDI captures the overall degree of plant-based eating, the hPDI and uPDI further distinguish the quality of plant-based dietary patterns. Higher hPDI scores have been associated with a reduced incidence of coronary heart disease [3], type 2 diabetes [4], and intermediate conditions (hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia) [5–7], whereas higher uPDI scores have been associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases in Western and Asian populations who habitually consume a plant-based diet [8, 9].

A substantial body of research demonstrates the influence of the gut microbiota on host health and disease pathophysiology [10]. Diet, along with geographical location, lifestyle, age, and medication, strongly influences on the composition of the gut microbiota [11, 12]. For example, plant-derived foods promote the production of specific metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids) by the gut microbiota, which directly influence the host’s immune system, metabolism, and other physiological functions [13]. Several studies have established a link between the quality of plant-based diets and gut microbiota composition, including potential effects on cardiometabolic health [14–16]. Research specifically investigating the association between plant-based diet quality and the gut microbiome remains limited, particularly in Asian populations.

Despite some westernization, Asian diets, characterized by high carbohydrate and plant-based food intake, are associated with gut microbiomes that differ from those of Western populations, potentially affecting metabolic function [17]. However, studies investigating the potential interplay between gut microbiome composition, plant-based diet scores, and cardiometabolic risk factors such as obesity and metabolic syndrome in Asian populations remain scarce. In a cross-sectional general population setting, intermediate cardiometabolic phenotypes may provide practical and informative indicators of cardiometabolic risk when clinical endpoints are unavailable. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between plant-based diet quality indices and gut microbiota composition and to assess whether incorporating gut microbiota profiles provides additional explanatory value for selected cardiometabolic risk factors.

Methods

Study population

Participants were recruited from Chung-Ang University Hospital and Chungbuk National University Hospital in Korea between 2017 and 2019. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: antibiotic use within the past three months, history of major gastrointestinal surgery, active or uncontrolled gastrointestinal disorders, cancer, or significant chronic diseases that could affect cardiovascular risk factors. Pregnant or lactating women were also excluded [18]. These exclusion criteria were applied during recruitment at the participating hospitals according to the cohort protocol. Because the present study used a preassembled dataset, the number of individuals excluded within each specific comorbidity category was not separately available.

Initially, 2398 participants were assessed for eligibility. A total of 2,388 participants were included in the dietary and clinical analyses. For microbiome-based analyses, two additional samples were excluded during quality-control review because they were identified as microbial community outliers, resulting in 2,386 participants. For microbiome association analyses, one further participant with missing Bristol Stool Scale data was excluded, leaving 2385 participants (Supplementary Fig. 1). Because the present study was a secondary analysis of an existing cohort with previously collected dietary, clinical, and gut microbiome data, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Chung-Ang University (IRB number 1750-002-281) and Chungbuk National University Hospital (IRB number 2019-04-014).

Dietary assessment and calculation of plant-based diet index score

A validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) with 106 items covering the previous year was used to assess usual diet intake [19]. Participants reported both the frequency of consumption and the usual portion size for each of the106 food items. The FFQ included nine response options for consumption frequency, ranging from “almost never” to “three times per day,” and three options for portion size (0.5 serving, 1 serving, and 2 servings) [20].

Using the FFQ, three plant-based dietary indices (hPDI, uPDI, and PDI) were created based on a previous study [21]. In brief, all food items were grouped into 17 predefined food categories based on nutrient composition and culinary similarity [22], and further classified into healthy plant foods (whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, coffee, and tea), less healthy plant foods (refined grains, potatoes, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets and desserts, and salty foods), and animal foods (animal fat, dairy, eggs, fish and seafood, meat, and miscellaneous). Consistent with the existing literature, plant foods were classified into healthy and less healthy plant food groups according to previously documented associations between specific food items and major health outcomes. Salty foods, such as kimchi and vegetables pickled with salt or soy sauce, were categorized as less healthy plant foods due to their high sodium content [23]. Vegetable oils and fruit juices were not treated as independent food groups because the FFQ did not separately assess oil intake, and fruits and fruit juices were queried together. Alcoholic beverages were also excluded from the indices, given the inconsistent evidence their associations with health outcomes. Mixed dishes (e.g., pizza, hamburgers, sandwiches), which were listed individually in the FFQ, were classified as miscellaneous animal foods.

After total energy adjustment, participants were assigned quintile-based scores. For the PDI, all plant food groups received positive scores (e.g., Q1 = 1, Q2 = 2, Q3 = 3, Q4 = 4, Q5 = 5), whereas for the hPDI, only healthy plant foods were positively scored, and for the uPDI, only less healthy plant foods were positively scored. In all indices, animal food groups were inversely scored (e.g., Q1 = 5, Q2 = 4, Q3 = 3, Q4 = 2, Q5 = 1). The summed scores across food groups were further categorized into quintiles to ensure comparability with previous studies [3, 24]. The theoretical score range for all three indices was 17–85. For descriptive group comparisons, we defined the low-score group as participants in the first and second quintiles and the high-score group as those in the fourth and fifth quintiles to contrast lower and higher adherence to each dietary pattern.

Assessment of cardiometabolic risk factors

The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Blood samples were collected after a 12-hour fast at the study site. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting glucose, and triglyceride (TG) levels were measured using an ADVIA 1650 Chemistry Analyzer (Siemens, Tarrytown, NY, USA) and Cobas c 702 modules (Roche Diagnostics System, Basel, Switzerland). Blood pressure was assessed using a TM-2657P automated monitor (A&D Company, Saitama, Japan). Cardiometabolic risk factors were defined as follows: obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 80 cm (for women) or ≥ 90 cm (for men)), and elevated fasting glucose (glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL), TG (plasma TG ≥ 150 mg/dL), HDL-C (plasma HDL-C < 50 mg/dL (for women) or < 40 mg/dL (for men)), and blood pressure (systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg, and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg) [25, 26].

Microbiome sample collection and profiling

Details of sample collection and gut microbiome profiling have been described previously [18]. Briefly, fresh stool samples were collected using a commercial fecal sampling kit (OMNIgene GUT; DNA Genotek, Ontario, Canada), stored at − 80 °C until shipment, and transported by same-day courier under temperature-controlled conditions to preserve sample integrity. Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and the V3/V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Because of the large number of samples, 16S rRNA sequencing was conducted across multiple runs rather than in a single batch. Although standard control samples for inter-batch calibration were not included, all samples were processed using the same DNA extraction protocol, amplification target region, sequencing platform, and bioinformatic pipeline.

16S rRNA gene sequencing reads were processed using the QIIME2 pipeline [27]. The DADA2 plugin [28] was applied for demultiplexing and quality filtering, followed by additional filtration steps, retaining only bacterial-domain features with sequence lengths between 380 and 450 bp. The “vsearch uchime-denovo” program [29] was used for de novo chimeric filtration. The filtered data were assigned to amplicon sequence variants using the DADA2 plugin. Taxonomic assignment was conducted using a pre-trained Naïve Bayes classifier against the Silva-138 reference sequences. Species assignment was performed using “vsearch usearch_global” based on 99% sequence identity. To assess within- and between-sample gut microbial diversity, alpha-diversity indices (Shannon diversity, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity [PD], observed features, and Pielou’s evenness) and beta-diversity metrics (unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances) were calculated using the “core-metrics-phylogenetic” module of the QIIME2 platform.

Machine Learning (ML) analysis

The Random Forest (RF) ML model was used to predict the risk of cardiometabolic factors using the R packages “randomForest” and “ROCR.” The dataset was randomly divided into a training dataset (70%, n = 1670) and a test dataset (30%, n = 716). The model was trained on the training dataset, and the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) was used to assess the classifier’s predictive ability in the test dataset, reflecting both sensitivity and specificity. For genus-level ML analyses, bacterial genera detected in fewer than 10% of participants were excluded before model training to reduce sparsity and improve model stability. Model performance was evaluated in the test set using AUC, and additional performance metrics are provided in the Supplementary Table 2. To improve interpretability, RF variable importance was calculated using the mean decrease in accuracy metric, and the top 10 ranked genera were selected for downstream analyses. These top-ranked genera were subsequently examined using multivariable-adjusted association analyses with the corresponding dietary scores and clinical traits.

Covariates

Years of education were categorized into three groups: ≤6 years (no education or completion of elementary school), 7–12 years (completion of middle or high school), and > 12 years (completion of university or higher education). The average monthly household income was classified into three categories: low (< 1,500 US dollars [USD]), middle (1,500–3,100 USD), and high (≥ 3,100 USD). Physical activity status was determined by combining walking, moderate activity, and vigorous activity throughout the week and dividing the total into tertiles to classify individuals as having light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity levels. Alcohol consumption status and smoking status were each categorized into three groups: never, former, and current. Stool consistency was assessed using the Bristol Stool Scale and included as a covariate in the microbiome association analyses. Participants were not excluded solely on the basis of stool consistency; however, one participant with missing Bristol Stool Scale data was excluded from the corresponding analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD or percentages. To assess the associations among PDI scores, cardiometabolic risk factors, and gut microbial species, multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models were used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The model was adjusted for age, sex, sampling location, education, income, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and total energy intake. The reference values were consistently set as the lowest PDI value. To evaluate the dissimilarity in gut microbial composition between the PDI groups, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used with 999 permutations, and p-values were corrected for the false discovery rates. The “Multivariate analysis by linear models 2 (MaAsLin2)” package in R Studio with default settings was used for the multivariate association analyses. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided p-value threshold of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio version 2022.12.0, build 576 (R Studio, Boston, MA, USA) [30], or GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0. (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com).

Association between PDI scores and gut microbial community structure

We assessed whether the PDI scores were associated with the α- or β-diversity of the gut microbiota. Individuals with high hPDI and overall PDI scores had greater α-diversity in all indices than those with low hPDI and overall PDI scores (all p-values < 0.05; Fig. 1A). Conversely, individuals with high uPDI scores showed lower α-diversity for all indices, except for Pielou’s evenness, compared to those with low uPDI scores (all p-values < 0.05; Fig. 1A) For β-diversity, significant discrepancies in the intestinal microbial communities were observed between the high and low hPDI score groups. This observation was consistent for both uPDI and overall PDI (all p-values < 0.05; Fig. 1B).

Model performance according to the combination of PDI scores and gut microbiome features

The predictive performance for cardiometabolic risk using combined PDI scores and gut microbiome features is shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2. These analyses were restricted to diet–outcome pairs that showed statistically significant associations in the multivariable logistic regression analyses presented in Table 2, namely hPDI–obesity, uPDI–hyperglycemia, and uPDI/PDI–low HDL-C. For both obesity and elevated fasting glucose, the clinical-only models showed the highest overall performance, with AUCs of 0.659 and 0.632, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, the dietary index-only models showed limited predictive performance for obesity (hPDI-only, AUC = 0.501) and elevated fasting glucose (uPDI-only, AUC = 0.492) (Fig. 2A and E). The addition of genus-level microbiome features increased the AUC to 0.595 for obesity in the hPDI + genus model and to 0.565 for elevated fasting glucose in the uPDI + genus model, indicating improved predictive performance compared with the dietary index-only models, although the combined models remained below the clinical-only models (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 2A and E). For obesity, the combined model performed similarly to the genus-only model (AUC = 0.600), whereas for elevated fasting glucose, the combined model showed slightly better performance than the genus-only model (AUC = 0.553). For low HDL-C, overall performance remained modest. In the PDI-based model, the AUC increased from 0.488 for the PDI-only model to 0.548 after the addition of genus-level microbiome features, whereas in the uPDI-based model, the AUC changed only marginally from 0.530 for the uPDI-only model to 0.540 for the uPDI + genus model (Supplementary Fig. 2A and E).

To further interpret the microbial features contributing to the main models, we ranked bacterial genera according to Random Forest variable importance based on the mean decrease in accuracy (Fig. 2B and F). In the hPDI–obesity model, Caproiciproducens, Prevotella, and Bacteroides were among the top-ranked genera, whereas Escherichia/Shigella, Parabacteroides, and Bacteroides were the leading contributors in the uPDI–elevated fasting glucose model. Multivariable-adjusted analyses of the top-ranked genera showed heterogeneous associations with the corresponding dietary scores and metabolic phenotypes (Fig. 2C, D, G, H). In the hPDI–obesity model, higher hPDI was associated with a lower relative abundance of Caproiciproducens and a higher relative abundance of Roseburia, whereas obesity was associated with a lower relative abundance of Caproiciproducens and Blautia and a higher relative abundance of Prevotella and Roseburia (Fig. 2C, D). In the uPDI–elevated fasting glucose model, the directions of association across the top-ranked genera were more modest and heterogeneous, with no clear dominant pattern across genera in relation to uPDI or elevated fasting glucose (Fig. 2G, H). Among the top-ranked genera in the uPDI–low HDL-C model, Veillonella was significantly more abundant in the high-uPDI group than in the low-uPDI group (Supplementary Fig. 2G).

Follow-up analyses of top-ranked genera identified by the random forest models

To further characterize the biological relevance of the top-ranked genera identified by the Random Forest models, we examined their multivariable-adjusted associations with the corresponding dietary scores and metabolic traits (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In the hPDI–BMI model (Fig. 3A), Roseburia showed a significant positive association with hPDI, whereas Caproiciproducens showed a significant inverse association with BMI, and Prevotella showed a significant positive association with BMI (Fig. 3A). In the uPDI–fasting glucose model, Escherichia/Shigella showed significant positive associations with both uPDI and fasting glucose, while the remaining genera showed comparatively modest and heterogeneous patterns (Fig. 3B). For the HDL-related models, Supplementary Fig. 3 summarizes the adjusted associations of the top-ranked genera with uPDI or PDI and HDL-C. In the uPDI–HDL-C model, Veillonella showed a significant positive association with uPDI, whereas Prevotella showed a significant inverse association with HDL-C (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In the PDI–HDL-C model, Prevotella also showed a significant inverse association with HDL-C, while Blautia showed a significant positive association with HDL-C (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Additional heatmaps showed that several of these top-ranked genera were associated with specific dietary components (Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, Roseburia was positively associated with fruit intake and inversely associated with dairy food and egg intake in both the hPDI–obesity and uPDI–fasting glucose models. Caproiciproducens showed a positive association with dairy intake in the hPDI–obesity model, and Blautia was also positively associated with dairy intake across the obesity- and HDL-related models. In contrast, Prevotella did not show consistent significant associations with individual food groups in these heatmaps, although it was inversely associated with HDL-C in both HDL-related models. Likewise, Escherichia/Shigella and Veillonella were more clearly associated with the corresponding dietary scores, showing positive associations with uPDI, than with specific food groups. Overall, these follow-up analyses suggest that the top-ranked genera identified by the Random Forest models reflected not only overall dietary patterns and metabolic traits but also specific food group–level associations.

Discussion

In this large-scale cross-sectional study of Korean adults, the healthiness of a plant-based diet was associated with gut microbiome diversity and composition and linked to cardiometabolic health. A higher hPDI score, which was linked to improved α-diversity, was associated with a reduced risk of obesity, whereas a higher uPDI score, which was linked to reduced α-diversity, was associated with an increased risk of elevated fasting glucose and low HDL-C. In this study, models integrating gut microbiome features with plant-based diet quality indices provided additional explanatory value for obesity and elevated fasting glucose than dietary index-only models.

Compared with self-reported dietary questionnaires, the gut microbiome may serve as a more integrative biomarker of diet-related physiology. Questionnaire-based dietary assessment, particularly FFQ-based assessment of habitual intake, is vulnerable to recall bias, misreporting, and systematic measurement error, which can attenuate diet–disease associations [31, 32]. By contrast, the gut microbiome is an objective host-associated profile that may reflect cumulative dietary exposure together with host biological context. Long-term dietary patterns have been linked to broad microbial configurations [33], and deeply phenotyped cohort studies have shown that gut microbiome features track habitual diet together with fasting and postprandial cardiometabolic markers [15]. In addition, host genetic variation and diet jointly shape specific gut taxa [34], and longitudinal metagenomic studies suggest that individual-specific microbial signatures can remain relatively stable over time while retaining associations with host phenotypes and circulating metabolites [35, 36]. Accordingly, the gut microbiome may serve as a more integrative and physiologically relevant biomarker than dietary questionnaires alone; however, given the nature of the cross-sectional study, it should be interpreted as a complementary measure rather than a substitute for conventional dietary assessment.

Consistent with our findings, adherence to hPDI was inversely associated with obesity in metabolically unhealthy adolescents and adult women [37, 38]. Conversely, adherence to uPDI has been associated with an 8% higher risk of type 2 diabetes in a meta-analysis [39]. Mechanistically, a healthy plant-based diet can reduce obesity risk by lowering energy density and providing antioxidants and phytochemicals that reduce inflammation and improve metabolic health [40–42]. In contrast, an unhealthy plant-based diet, characterized by high intake of refined carbohydrates and added sugars, but low in essential nutrients, might lead to elevated fasting glucose levels due to high glycemic index foods that impair insulin sensitivity and disrupt normal glucose metabolism [4]. For example, bioactive food components such as dietary fiber and polyphenols from healthy plant foods are associated with increased gut microbiome diversity [43, 44]. Dietary fiber alleviates intestinal inflammation by altering gut microbiome composition and activity, enhancing the production of short-chain fatty acids by gut bacteria, and decreasing the formation of potentially harmful compounds like trimethylamine N-oxide and secondary bile acids [13]. Our findings support the hypothesis that the association between plant-based diet healthiness and cardiometabolic disorders (obesity and elevated fasting glucose) may be linked to gut microbiome composition. These results confirm that the healthiness of plant-based diets was associated with microbial diversity and gut microbiome composition. Similar findings have been observed in different ethnic populations. A greater hPDI score was associated with increased microbial richness and specific microbiota species associated with favorable health outcomes in young Arab women [45]. The hPDI was positively associated and the uPDI negatively associated with microbiome α-diversity, driven by microbial evenness in older US adults [46]. The hPDI score was associated with greater microbial diversity and distinct gut microbiome profiles, which were linked to improved cardiometabolic health in a Chinese population [14].

To support the interpretation of biologically plausible and testable mechanistic hypotheses beyond broad association patterns, we focused our analysis on specific genera identified by the Random Forest models. Among the identified taxa, Roseburia is a well-recognized butyrate-producing genus, and butyrate has been linked to maintenance of epithelial barrier function, modulation of intestinal inflammation, and improved metabolic homeostasis [47, 48]. The positive association of Roseburia with hPDI and fruit intake in our data is therefore consistent with the possibility that healthier plant-based dietary patterns promote a fiber-associated microbial configuration that may support short-chain fatty acid production and metabolic health.

In contrast, Escherichia/Shigella showed positive associations with both uPDI and fasting glucose. This genus is commonly regarded as a marker of dysbiosis and inflammatory gut environments, and enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae has been linked to endotoxin-related host responses that may contribute to insulin resistance and glycemic dysregulation [49]. The role of Prevotella appears to vary depending on the context. Although it is commonly associated with carbohydrate- or fiber-rich diets, certain lineages may contribute to branched-chain amino-acid biosynthesis and potentially unfavorable metabolic outcomes under specific host conditions [50].

The biological interpretation of Veillonella and Caproiciproducens is more tentative. Veillonella is a lactate-utilizing genus [51]. In this study, its positive association with uPDI suggests that its increased abundance may reflect a diet-related shift in microbial fermentation rather than a direct beneficial effect. Diets high in refined carbohydrates or processed plant foods may alter fermentation patterns and potentially increase the availability of lactate as an intermediate metabolite, thereby favoring the growth of lactate-utilizing bacteria such as Veillonella. Given that Veillonella was associated with uPDI rather than HDL-C itself in our analysis, this genus may be better interpreted as a marker of an unhealthy plant-based dietary pattern than as a direct mediator of low HDL-C. Members related to Caproiciproducens have been implicated in chain-elongation metabolism and the production of short- and medium-chain fatty acids, although evidence linking this genus to human cardiometabolic outcomes remains limited [52]. Finally, Blautia has been associated in several studies with more favorable metabolic phenotypes and anti-inflammatory effects, and its positive association with HDL-C in our data may be consistent with this interpretation [53]. Taken together, these genus-level patterns suggest several possible mechanisms linking plant-based diet quality, gut microbial metabolism, and cardiometabolic risk, including short-chain fatty-acid–related effects, inflammation linked to dysbiosis, and context-dependent fermentation.

When we compared models using clinical variables with dietary, microbiome, and combined models, the clinical model showed the best overall performance (Supplementary Table 2). This was expected, as clinical variables directly reflect current metabolic status. However, microbiome-based models may still provide additional insight by linking dietary exposure with host physiology, rather than replacing traditional risk assessment. Therefore, our findings do not suggest that the gut microbiome outperforms clinical predictors, but rather that it can serve as a complementary biomarker to better understand diet-related cardiometabolic risk.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not explore the causal relationship between PDI scores, gut microbiota, and cardiometabolic risk because of our cross-sectional study design. In addition, although we adjusted for key confounders, residual confounding may persist due to incomplete information on pre-existing conditions and medication use that could influence gut microbiota composition. Moreover, because 16 S rRNA sequencing was conducted across multiple runs without dedicated standard control samples for batch harmonization, technical variation related to batch processing cannot be completely excluded. Second, 16 S rRNA sequencing provides limited taxonomic resolution and functional insight, and lower-level taxonomic interpretations should therefore be made cautiously. Future studies incorporating shotgun metagenomic sequencing and metabolomic profiling are needed to validate and extend these findings. Finally, the ML models were not validated in an independent cohort. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to explore the associations between plant-based diet quality, specific gut microbiota, and cardiometabolic risk, suggesting that gut microbiota profiles may capture additional diet-related biological variation relevant to cardiometabolic risk in the Korean population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, plant-based diet quality was associated with gut microbial composition, and integrating gut microbiome features with diet quality indices showed stronger associations with obesity and elevated fasting glucose in this study. However, because the clinical model showed the best overall performance, our findings do not suggest that the gut microbiome outperforms conventional clinical predictors. Rather, they support the view that the gut microbiome may serve as a complementary biomarker that links dietary exposure with host physiology and thereby provides additional insight into diet-related cardiometabolic risk. Future studies using longitudinal designs with metagenomic and metabolomic profiling, together with external validation, are needed to clarify temporal relationships, identify underlying biological mechanisms, and evaluate the reproducibility of these findings.
