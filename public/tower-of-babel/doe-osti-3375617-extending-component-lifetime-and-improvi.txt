 Executive Summary
Inverter reliability remains one of the most persistent challenges limiting the performance,
availability, and economic viability of utility‑scale photovoltaic (PV) plants. Industry data
consistently show that inverters account for the highest share of corrective maintenance events
and unplanned outages across PV fleets. These failures result in energy losses, increased O&M
costs, and reduced confidence in long‑term solar asset performance. Motivated by these
challenges, this project—Extending Component Lifetime and Improving Inverter Reliability
(ECLAIIR)—was undertaken to systematically investigate inverter degradation and failure
mechanisms, develop predictive maintenance capabilities, and establish data‑driven pathways to
improve service life and reduce the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) for large‑scale PV
systems.

The primary goal of the project was to identify pre‑failure signatures in string inverters using
both lab‑based accelerated lifetime testing and field‑based data and to develop predictive
maintenance algorithms that can anticipate inverter faults before they occur. Through
collaboration with inverter testing laboratory, solar PV plant owner, and failure‑analysis experts,
the project advanced the technical understanding of inverter reliability. By instrumenting
inverters with thermistors, humidity sensors, power‑quality meters, and acoustic sensors, the
research established how multiple sensing modalities can reliably detect deviations from normal
behavior hours to days before failure. These findings substantially enhance scientific
understanding of inverter failure kinetics and provide the PV industry with the most
comprehensive cross‑OEM characterization of early‑stage failure indicators reported to date.

Technically, the project demonstrated the effectiveness of predictive maintenance by developing
and validating the PreDICT (Predictive Diagnostics of PV Inverters Using Condition
Monitoring and Trend Analysis) framework—a multi‑layer diagnostic architecture combining
peer‑to‑peer analytics, historical trend modeling, and advanced machine‑learning techniques
such as the Sequential Conditional Variational Autoencoder (SCVAE). This predictive model
achieved more than 90% accuracy in detecting pre‑failure conditions and provided up to four
days of lead time before inverter failure in field scenarios. Economically, the project’s LCOE
analysis showed that predictive maintenance can reduce lifetime energy losses and minimize
corrective maintenance interventions. Modeling indicated that, depending on inverter failure
rates and replacement timelines, predictive maintenance can significantly reduce LCOE impacts
associated with inverter downtime: from as high as 19.4% under conventional maintenance
strategies to 0.1%–10.17% when predictive analytics are adopted. These results confirm that
predictive maintenance is both technically feasible and economically advantageous for utilities
and plant operators.

The project’s findings also have broad public benefit. By improving inverter reliability and
reducing downtime, predictive maintenance directly increases electricity generation from
existing PV assets. Enhanced reliability lowers operational costs for utilities, which can translate
over time into lower energy costs for consumers. Furthermore, the project’s technical
publications, conference presentations, and industry workshops ensure that knowledge gained is
shared broadly across the solar industry, supporting workforce development and enabling
utilities of all sizes to adopt modern asset‑health monitoring practices. The retrofitting case study

and service‑life prediction framework further support informed decision‑making for aging PV
fleets, helping operators extend system life and reduce electronic waste.

In summary, the ECLAIIR project significantly advanced the state of knowledge on inverter
degradation, demonstrated the technical and economic value of predictive maintenance, and
delivered actionable tools and insights that support more reliable, cost‑effective, and sustainable
PV plant operation. The outcomes of this project will continue to inform utility practices, guide
inverter design improvements, and strengthen the long‑term performance of solar assets
nationwide.

 Background
Solar photovoltaic (PV) systems have become a cornerstone of the evolving global electricity
generation portfolio. Among the critical components of PV systems, inverters play a pivotal role
by converting direct current (DC) generated by solar modules into alternating current (AC)
suitable for grid integration and end-use applications. In addition to power conversion, modern
inverters provide grid support functions, safety features such as anti-islanding, and monitoring
capabilities that enhance system performance and reliability[1]. Despite these advancements,
inverters remain the most failure-prone component in utility-scale PV plants, accounting for the
majority of corrective maintenance events and downtime [2]. Improving inverter reliability is
therefore essential for reducing operational costs, minimizing energy losses, and achieving lower
Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) [3].

Historical data and industry surveys consistently identify inverters as the leading cause of PV
plant outages. Studies report that approximately 70% of all operations and maintenance (O&M)
events in large-scale PV plants are related to inverter failures [1]. Common failure modes include
degradation of electronic components such as cards and boards, contactors, fans or cooling
systems, insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs), and capacitors [2][4]. These failures are
often driven by thermal stress, environmental exposure (humidity, dust), electrical surges, and
manufacturing defects. The reliability curve for inverters typically follows the “bathtub” model,
with infant mortality failures early in life, a steady-state failure rate during useful life, and wear-
out failures toward the end of service life [5].
Inverter failures have a direct and significant impact on PV plant economics. Downtime reduces
energy production, leading to revenue loss and increased LCOE. A recent study quantified that
inverter failure rates ranging from 2.5% to 15% annually can increase LCOE by up to 19.4%,
depending on replacement lead times [3]. Longer downtimes exacerbate these losses, particularly
when spare parts or technical support are delayed due to OEM exits or supply chain constraints.
Consequently, improving inverter reliability and reducing downtime through advanced
maintenance strategies is critical for sustaining PV plant profitability [3][5].
Traditional maintenance approaches—reactive and preventive—are insufficient to address the
complexity of inverter failures. Reactive maintenance responds only after a failure occurs,
resulting in prolonged downtime and high costs. Preventive maintenance, typically time-based,
may reduce some risks but cannot predict component-level failures accurately [2]. Predictive
maintenance, leveraging real-time monitoring and data analytics, offers a transformative
alternative. By identifying pre-failure signatures and enabling proactive interventions, predictive
maintenance minimizes downtime, extends component life, and reduces O&M costs [4][6].
Recent research and field demonstrations highlight the potential of predictive maintenance
through sensor-based retrofitting. Advanced sensors—such as thermistors for temperature,
humidity sensors, acoustic sensors for detecting switching anomalies, and electrical sensors for
monitoring harmonics—are integrated into inverters to capture early indicators of failure [4][6].
Laboratory accelerated lifetime testing (ALT) and field deployments have shown that anomalies
in temperature profiles, humidity levels, and harmonic distortion can serve as reliable pre-failure
signatures. For example, a rise in IGBT temperature or capacitor heating beyond nominal ranges
was observed hours before inverter failure during ALT tests [4]. Similarly, field data revealed

that failed inverters operated significantly hotter than baseline units under similar conditions,
validating the predictive model [3][6].
Repowering—replacing obsolete inverters with newer models—and retrofitting—adding sensors
to existing inverters—are emerging strategies to enhance reliability. Drivers for repowering
include OEM exits, lack of spare parts, and technological advancements such as improved grid
support features [5]. However, repowering introduces challenges related to mechanical
compatibility, electrical design, communication protocols, and permitting. Lessons learned from
case studies emphasize the importance of selecting inverters with similar capacity, adequate
MPPT channels, and compliance with site-specific design constraints to minimize rework and
downtime [5]. Retrofitting older inverters with sensors is often necessary to implement
predictive maintenance effectively, especially when intrinsic monitoring capabilities are limited
[4][6].
Despite promising results, predictive maintenance adoption in the PV industry remains limited.
Key challenges include standardizing retrofitting practices, refining failure prediction models,
and addressing data quality issues. Further research is needed to evaluate sensor cost-
effectiveness, optimize alarm systems to reduce false positives, and develop scalable analytics
frameworks for real-time diagnostics [2][4]. Integration of machine learning and physics-based
models holds potential for improving prediction accuracy and enabling widespread
implementation of predictive maintenance strategies [6].

 Project Objectives

Project Objectives
Inverters are a critical component for generating PV electricity. They have been identified as the
largest source of corrective maintenance and downtime in large-scale PV plants. Seminal
research on documenting inverter failures indicates that electrical components are, cumulatively,
the biggest culprit. Extending component lifetime towards improving inverter reliability is
important for numerous reasons, including continuing to reduce to the levelized cost of energy
(LCOE) from PV plants and increasing reliance on inverter-based resources to provide grid
support as penetration levels increase. Improving inverter reliability is also important towards
achieving the 2¢/kWh levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) targeted by SETO, especially for the
vision of 50-year service lives for PV plants. This project uses a mix of field-based and lab-based
testing to better understand inverter failure modes, identify opportunities for improving inverter
reliability, develop service lifetime estimates and predictive maintenance for inverters, and
establish best practices for inverter-focused retrofitting at large-scale PV plants.
Impact on National Goals
 • Energy System Performance: Enhanced inverter reliability reduces operational
 disruptions, enabling higher PV penetration and grid support from inverter-based
 resources. This strengthens integration of inverter-based generation and supports power
 system reliability objectives.
 • Economic Benefit: Lower inverter failure rates reduce maintenance costs and downtime,
 improving plant economics and investor confidence. Predictive maintenance strategies
 and retrofit best practices will minimize lifecycle costs and optimize asset performance,
 contributing to U.S. energy affordability and competitiveness.
Expected Outcomes
 • Validated predictive maintenance approach with >90% accuracy.
 • Service life prediction methodology for inverter components across climates.
 • Quantified impact of inverter reliability improvements on LCOE.
 • Publicly available retrofit case study and best-practice guidelines.
Significance, Innovation, and Advances
This project introduces instrumented inverters for real-time monitoring, integrates accelerated
lifetime testing with field data, and applies data-driven predictive analytics. These innovations
will:
 • Advance inverter reliability modeling and predictive maintenance.
 • Provide actionable insights for OEMs and plant operators.
 • Enable long-term PV plant operation aligned with DOE’s cost and reliability targets.

Subtasks 1.3: Collect and aggregate information about inverter predictive maintenance

The team began by conducting a comprehensive literature review on inverter reliability, focusing
on component failure frequency, failure mechanisms, failure signatures, and methodologies for
predicting failures. Key findings from this review included:
 • Inverter reliability issues have persisted in commercially operating solar PV plants for
 approximately four decades. Despite improvements, inverters remain the largest source of
 corrective maintenance and downtime.
 • Software and component failures are the primary causes of inverter downtime.
 Components most likely to fail include cards/boards, contactors, fans/cooling systems,
 insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs), and capacitors.

                                          Page 12 of 79

 EPRI

 • Failure mitigation measures may include high-quality upfront design with additional
 margin on power ratings. However, these improvements often conflict with industry cost-
 reduction trends, underscoring the need for robust quality assurance programs.
 • Enhancements such as improved inverter sealing, reduced operating temperatures, and
 preventative maintenance can help mitigate failures.
 • Some researchers have explored inverter lifetime prediction using physical models,
 which can inform component selection during design.
 • There is limited data on practical methods for predicting component failures. Many lab-
 scale studies are overly complex for field applications, and promising techniques require
 further validation.
The findings were disseminated during an EPRI webinar in March, accompanied by a Pareto
chart illustrating component failure distribution.

Subtask 3.1: Finalize accelerated lifetime test sequence.

During PQ5, EPRI and RETC collaborated to design the most effective accelerated lifetime
testing plan for the project. Initial discussions focused on the capabilities of the environmental
chamber, including control of ambient temperature, humidity, power, and input voltage/current.
The team also evaluated potential inverter failure modes such as thermal stress, voltage faults,
and creep. Based on these considerations, multiple test stages were planned to capture sensor
behavior under different stress conditions, while ensuring that the tests were neither excessively
harsh—leading to artificial failure modes—nor too mild, which could delay failures and impact
the project timeline. Field scenarios such as seasonal thermal cycling, daily power cycling, and
voltage fluctuations were incorporated into the planning. A flowchart of the proposed test
sequences was developed (Figure Subtask 3.1).

By PQ7, the accelerated lifetime test sequence was finalized based on these discussions. The
team adopted an iterative approach, revising the sequence after each batch of tests to reduce the
risk of missing predictive signals or inducing failures prematurely. Initial batches revealed that
even with increased stress, inverters did not fail as expected, preventing the capture of predictive
signals. However, all sensor data collected during normal operation proved valuable for
establishing baseline performance for predictive maintenance.

To address these challenges, the team explored several strategies:
 1. Revising test sequences to introduce harsher conditions that would stress components
 without causing immediate failure under unrealistic scenarios.
 2. Modifying inverter protection thresholds to prevent derating at elevated temperatures,
 enabling operation at full power under stress conditions. This required discussions with
 OEMs and internal experimentation using failed Inverter A.
 3. Considering induced failures as a last resort, given budget and time constraints limiting
 chamber exposure to approximately six weeks per inverter.

During PQ10 and PQ11, the root cause analysis of the failed Inverter A revealed that the failure
of the DC bus capacitors and IGBT Z3 in the interleaved three-level boost converter was due to
an insufficient gate-drive design. Disassembly showed that the lower half of the DC bus
capacitors were vented, and one high-side IGBT on the front-end boost converter was shorted
across collector, emitter, and gate terminals (Figure Subtask 3.3f).

Figure subtask 3.3f
Following identification of these failures, the boost section and its drivers were reverse
engineered to determine the underlying cause. The circuit schematic (Figure Subtask 3.3g)
confirmed that the inverter employed an interleaved three-level boost topology. Analysis
indicated that IGBT Z3 failed due to a short circuit across its terminals, likely triggered by high
dV/dt signals coupled through parasitic Miller capacitance during turn-off. This insufficient gate-
drive design resulted in spurious turn-ons, forcing the IGBT to operate in the linear region,
which caused elevated junction temperatures and eventual failure. The malfunction cascaded,
disrupting charging cycles and inducing overvoltage conditions that ultimately led to the failure
of output capacitors. Design improvements—such as enhanced gate-drive circuitry and better
management of parasitic capacitances—were identified as critical measures to prevent similar
failures in future inverter designs.

Subtask 7.2. Develop recommendations for implementing predictive maintenance

This task was successfully completed. The objective was to analyze data from lab-based inverter
testing to identify sensor value changes that occur prior to failure and to develop actionable
recommendations for signals, thresholds, and analytical techniques suitable for deployment in
field-based instrumented inverters.
The analysis began with collecting and processing sensor datasets from accelerated lifetime tests,
including measurements such as DC link voltage, IGBT temperature, heat sink temperature, and
AC/DC currents. After cleaning and synchronizing the data, failure events were identified, and
the periods leading up to these events were examined in detail. This approach enabled the
establishment of baseline operational ranges and the detection of deviations that serve as early
indicators of failure.
Results revealed clear precursor patterns for several critical signals. For example, IGBT and
capacitor temperatures were observed to rise by 15–20 °C above baseline within 24–48 hours
prior to failure. Similarly, significant fluctuations in current harmonics were identified as
additional indicators of impending failures. These findings formed the basis for defining
preliminary failure signatures that can inform predictive maintenance strategies.
Based on these observations, the team developed recommendations for monitoring key signals,
including IGBT temperature, heat sink temperature, current harmonics, and inverter cabinet
humidity. Proposed thresholds included temperature increases exceeding defined limits from
baseline within a specified time window and current harmonics deviating by more than ±5%
from nominal values.
For data analysis, moving average techniques combined with anomaly detection were suggested
for temperature signals, while variance-based detection methods were recommended for
monitoring current harmonics. Additionally, advanced machine learning approaches such as
Random Forest and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models were identified as promising
tools for predictive diagnostics.
The completion of this task provides a strong foundation for implementing predictive
maintenance capabilities in field-deployed instrumented inverters. These recommendations will
guide future monitoring frameworks aimed at improving inverter reliability and reducing
unexpected downtime in large-scale PV plants.

String or central inverters are typically subject to thermal and electrical stress. Over-temperature
conditions e.g. due to fan degradation, airflow obstruction, fluctuations on electrical loads, or
interface semiconductors (used in switching of inverters) aging are strong precursors to de-rating
and forced outages. Traditionally, in condition-based monitoring threshold alarms (fixed °C
limits) fail to account for seasonal and load dependent variability and cannot detect relative
deterioration. Proposed framework PreDICT addresses this by combining cross-sectional (across
inverters) and longitudinal (within inverter) evidence with a probabilistic model of normal
behavior.

Framework for PreDICT
PreDICT is a data-driven framework for early detection of photovoltaic (PV) inverter’s thermal
anomalies and emerging failures. The framework combines: (i) peer-to-peer inverters
comparison across the plant, (ii) self-historical baselines, (iii) a seasonally aware estimation of
the ideal temperature at the current operating point, and (iv) statistical trend evaluation using
probabilistic scores. Together these pillars create alerts that have shown lead time in days to
field-observed issues while maintaining relatively low false positives. A data-driven digital twin
of PV inverter is developed using a Sequential Conditional Variational Autoencoder (SCVAE)
which is conditioned only on exogenous signals (weather, DC power). The model learns
normative temporal behavior and provides probabilistic anomaly indicators via reconstruction
error and negative log-likelihood. The predictive maintenance framework, PreDICT (Predictive
Diagnostics for Inverters using Condition Monitoring and Trend Analysis), integrates multiple
analytical layers:

                                           Page 58 of 79

 EPRI

 1. Peer-to-peer inverter comparison detects units operating at higher temperatures
 than peer baseline at comparable loading.
 2. Self-historical trends track the drift relative to the device’s own historical norm.
 3. Seasonal ideal temperature estimation computes the expected temperature at
 given operating conditions. This is made possible with the help of data driven
 digital twin model using a Sequential Conditional Variational Autoencoder
 (SCVAE) which is tuned using exogenous signals (weather and DC power).
 4. Statistical trend evaluation monitors the probability of observations under learned
 normal model (SCVAE) via negative log-likelihood (NLL) and its changes over
 time.
These diagnostics are fused and translated into alerts with actionable context for maintenance.

Sequential Conditional Variational Auto-Encoder? (SCVAE): Estimating the ideal temperature
SCVAE algorithm is used to estimate the inverter’s “ideal” internal temperature at each
timestamp as the mean of a conditional predictive distribution learned by a sequential model.
The inputs are the current operating conditions 𝑋𝑡 (e.g., power, ambient, irradiance) and a short
history 𝐻𝑡 of recent temperatures and drivers. A compact temporal encoder produces a latent
state, and a decoder outputs (𝜇̂ 𝑡 , 𝜎̂𝑡 ), yielding 𝑇𝑡 ∣ (𝐻𝑡 , 𝑋𝑡 ) ∼ 𝒩(𝜇̂ 𝑡 , 𝜎̂𝑡2 ). We take 𝜇̂ 𝑡 as the ideal
temperature now, and compute residuals 𝑒𝑡 = 𝑇𝑡 − 𝜇̂ 𝑡 , and standardized errors 𝑧𝑡err = 𝑒𝑡 /𝜎̂𝑡 as
anomaly scores. Alerts trigger when point or persistent deviations exceed tuned thresholds. So in
summary, the SCVAE learns and provides the “ideal” temperature (theoretically any failure
signals humidity, power flickers, THDs, and many more…) and helps to compare with the
current signals to compute the relative errors. These errors helps to determine if the current
timestamp is a anomaly behavior or normal operation. As a part of training the model, a
modification is included to learn the seasonality effect. A day of the year (DoY) parameter is
passed as input to remove the seasonal variations.

PreDICT combines four complementary approaches to decide whether an inverter’s temperature
behavior is abnormal and whether the deviation is persistent and actionable. Peer-to-peer
benchmarking compares a unit with its fleet at the same moments to cancel shared weather and
load effects. Self-historical comparison checks the unit against its own past by load bins and by
weekly thresholds to reveal gradual drift or step changes. A sequential model estimates what the
ideal temperature should be right now from the current load, ambient conditions, irradiance, and
recent history, and departures from that estimate indicate abnormal operation. Statistical trend
evaluation converts those departures into simple time-series scores with persistence rules,
producing graded and explainable alerts while suppressing noise. In practice, any one of these
approaches, and their fusion, can provide pre-failure signals with around 5 days of lead time, and

Discussion: The project team successfully developed and validated a predictive maintenance
approach capable of identifying inverter failures ahead of time by correlating deviations in
sensor behavior with impending failure events. Using data from accelerated lifetime testing, the
team analyzed time‑synchronized measurements—including DC link voltage, IGBT and
heat‑sink temperatures, AC/DC currents, cabinet humidity, and harmonic distortion—to establish
baseline operational profiles and detect early deviation patterns. Clear pre‑failure signatures were
consistently observed: IGBT and capacitor temperatures rose by 15–20 °C above baseline 24–48
hours before failure, THDi exhibited sharp spikes minutes to hours before shutdown, and
humidity showed sudden excursions seconds to minutes before capacitor‑related failures. These
signatures served as reliable early‑warning indicators and enabled the development of
recommended thresholds for key signals such as temperature increases beyond defined limits and
current harmonic deviations exceeding ±5%.
Building on these findings, the team implemented the PreDICT framework, which integrates
peer‑to‑peer comparison, historical trend analysis, ideal‑behavior estimation, and statistical trend
evaluation to detect anomalies before failure occurs. A Sequential Conditional Variational
Autoencoder (SCVAE) model was developed to reconstruct expected sensor outputs using
contextual inputs such as weather and DC power. Deviations between predicted and measured
values were used to assign anomaly scores. Field demonstrations showed that this model
consistently detected anomalies with daily resolution, providing up to four days of advance
warning before failure. Performance evaluation metrics—including Negative Log‑Likelihood
(NLL), ELBO, and F1‑score—confirmed that the failure predictions achieved an accuracy
exceeding the milestone requirement of > 90% true positive rate, with Type I and Type II error
rates quantified as part of validation. This predictive maintenance approach enables proactive
interventions, reduces unplanned downtime, and provides a robust foundation for deployment
across utility‑scale PV plants.

 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Significant Accomplishments
1. Discovery and Validation of Pre-Failure Signatures Across Three OEMs
A major accomplishment of this project was the successful identification and validation of
pre-failure signatures across inverters from all three participating OEMs. Through accelerated
lifetime testing inside a controlled thermal chamber, the team captured consistent precursor
behaviors using four distinct sensor types—thermistors, humidity sensors, electrical sensors, and
acoustic sensors. Distinct temperature rises, humidity excursions, elevated THDi levels, and
characteristic high-frequency acoustic patterns were repeatedly observed prior to component
failure. These findings are highly significant because they represent one of the first multi-OEM
demonstrations of reliable, cross-platform predictive indicators in string inverters. The ability to
detect failures hours to days in advance lays the foundation for practical, field-deployable
predictive maintenance, reducing downtime and enabling data-driven O&M strategies across the
PV industry.

2. Development of a Robust Predictive Maintenance Framework (PreDICT)
The project achieved a major breakthrough with the development and validation of PreDICT, a
predictive maintenance framework integrating sensor-based condition monitoring,
machine-learning-based anomaly detection, and statistical trend analysis. By combining
peer-to-peer inverter comparison, historical trend evaluation, and ideal-behavior modeling with a
Sequential Conditional Variational Autoencoder (SCVAE), PreDICT demonstrated up to four
days of lead time before failure in field conditions. This accomplishment is highly impactful for
the solar industry, as it represents a transition from reactive or scheduled maintenance to fully
condition-based maintenance. Such predictive capabilities can meaningfully reduce
inverter-related downtime, decrease O&M costs, and improve energy yield—key factors for
meeting long-term LCOE reduction targets in utility-scale PV plants.

3. Comprehensive Root-Cause Analysis and Design Recommendations
Another major achievement of the project was the extensive root-cause analysis conducted on
both field-failed and chamber-failed inverters. Through detailed physical inspection, electrical
testing, and component-level forensics performed by TEAMSESCO, the project identified key
failure mechanisms—including water ingress due to inadequate sealing, foreign material
contamination, improper terminal connections, cooling system degradation, and premature
electrolytic capacitor failures. By pairing these findings with literature review and component
stress-kinetics analysis, the team developed targeted design improvement recommendations such
as transitioning from electrolytic to thin-film capacitors, upgrading AC and DC terminal
interfaces, and improving enclosure sealing practices. These insights directly support inverter
manufacturers and plant owners in reducing early-life failures and extending equipment service
life.

4. Economic Validation of Predictive Maintenance Through LCOE Analysis
The project delivered an industry-valuable LCOE analysis quantifying the economic benefit of
predictive maintenance compared to corrective and preventive strategies. Using the EPRI LCOE
Calculator integrated with SAM and site-specific reliability data, the analysis demonstrated that
traditional maintenance approaches can increase LCOE by 0.5% to 19.4% depending on failure

                                           Page 72 of 79

 EPRI

rate and replacement time. In contrast, assuming a 50% reduction in inverter failures enabled by
predictive maintenance, LCOE impacts were significantly reduced to 0.1% to 10.17%. This
accomplishment provides a strong business case for utility-scale adoption of predictive
maintenance, showing that reliability gains translate directly into measurable long-term
economic benefits.

Challenges Encountered and Lessons Learned
Throughout the project, several challenges emerged that led to critical lessons learned. First,
inducing controlled inverter failures in environmental chambers proved more difficult than
expected, requiring multiple adjustments to test sequences and protective settings. This
highlighted the inherent robustness of modern inverter designs and reinforced the need for
realistic, field-aligned stress protocols. Second, procurement delays and OEM-specific
constraints caused schedule impacts early in the project, emphasizing the need for flexible test
plans and diversified supplier engagement. Third, field experimentation revealed the sensitivity
of inverters to installation practices—particularly sealing integrity, terminal torque, and
environmental exposure—underscoring that reliability issues often arise from balance-of-system
factors as much as from internal electronics. These challenges ultimately strengthened the
outcomes by promoting more rigorous testing, deeper forensics, and more relevant
recommendations.

Conclusions
The project delivered a comprehensive, multi-faceted set of accomplishments that advance the
state of inverter reliability and predictive maintenance in the PV industry. It successfully
demonstrated that inverter failures can be detected early through multi-sensor monitoring,
validated a predictive maintenance framework capable of actionable lead times, and provided
new technical insights into failure mechanisms and component-level stressors. The economic
analysis confirmed that predictive maintenance offers meaningful LCOE advantages,
establishing strong justification for industry adoption. Despite challenges in accelerated testing
and field conditions, the project’s adaptive methodology, expert elicitation, and extensive
dissemination ensured that the results are robust, validated, and broadly applicable. Collectively,
these outcomes contribute significantly to improving inverter reliability, reducing downtime, and
enabling long-term performance and cost reductions for utility-scale PV plants.

 Path Forward

Building on the technical accomplishments and insights generated throughout the project, the
next phase focuses on transitioning the research outcomes into practical, scalable solutions for
the broader PV industry. The project has demonstrated the value of predictive maintenance
analytics, advanced reliability modeling, and data‑driven asset health assessment, particularly
through the development of the PreDICT framework and inverter service‑life estimation
methods. With the completion of system demonstrations, failure analyses, and techno‑economic
evaluations, the foundation is now in place to move from research validation toward industry
deployment and cross‑asset expansion.
Several strategic opportunities emerged during the project:
 • Utility partners expressed strong interest in adopting the predictive maintenance tools
 after seeing improvements in fault detection and early identification of pre‑failure
 conditions.
 • Industry feedback highlighted the need to expand economic models to reflect the
 reliability characteristics of central inverters, which dominate U.S. utility‑scale PV
 deployments.
 • The methodologies developed for inverters—such as SCADA‑based anomaly detection,
 degradation tracking, and data‑driven failure mode modeling—were found to be
 applicable to a broad range of PV plant assets.

These outcomes collectively position the team to advance the tools, datasets, and analytical
frameworks into wider industry application, ensuring long‑term impact and supporting continued
evolution toward predictive, data‑driven O&M practices. The following subsections summarize
the key pathways for continued development and implementation.

1. Deployment of the Predictive Maintenance Model Through EPRI’s Inverter Health
Monitoring Supplemental Project
The predictive maintenance model developed under the ECLAIIR project will be transitioned
into broader industry application through EPRI’s PV-Plant Health Monitoring supplemental
project. Multiple utility members have already expressed strong interest in adopting the model,
particularly due to the demonstrated success in identifying pre-failure signatures and improving
inverter reliability. The path forward will involve integrating the PreDICT framework and its
machine-learning-based analytics into EPRI’s existing monitoring platform, enabling real-time
inverter health scoring and early-warning diagnostics across diverse PV fleets. As additional
utility participants join the supplemental project, their operational data will further refine model
accuracy, enhance threshold calibration, and expand the library of failure modes. This will help
scale the predictive maintenance approach from a single demonstration site to a standardized,
widely deployable solution supporting utilities nationwide.

2. Expansion of LCOE Modeling to Central Inverters Using the SUPER Dataset
While the current LCOE model was developed and validated for string inverter systems, industry
feedback and market trends indicate that the majority of U.S. utility-scale PV plants—over

                                            Page 75 of 79

 EPRI

90%—rely on central inverter architectures. As a result, the project team is actively extending
the LCOE model to accommodate central inverter reliability characteristics using the SUPER
(Solar Unscheduled Production Events and Reliability) dataset. This work involves mapping
observed central inverter failure rates, replacement times, and operational profiles into the
cost-benefit framework originally built for string inverters. Developing a central-inverter-specific
LCOE model will significantly increase the relevance and applicability of the project’s economic
findings, providing utilities, developers, and financiers with more accurate lifetime cost
projections. Once completed, the expanded model will help quantify how predictive maintenance
strategies could reduce costs and improve energy availability across the dominant inverter
technology in today’s PV fleet.

3. Extending the Predictive Maintenance Framework to Other PV Plant Assets
Finally, the methodologies, analytical tools, and lessons learned from the inverter-focused
predictive maintenance study will be extended to other critical PV plant assets, including
transformers, trackers, and BOS components. Many of the failure mechanisms observed—such
as thermal stress, environmental exposure, and load-driven degradation—are equally relevant to
these assets, making them strong candidates for similar predictive monitoring approaches. By
applying the SCADA-based anomaly detection techniques, thresholding logic, and machine
learning architectures developed in this project, the team will build asset-specific health models
capable of identifying degradation trends before they result in system failures. This cross-asset
expansion will support a more holistic plant-level health monitoring framework, enabling
operators to shift from reactive maintenance toward a fully predictive, data-driven maintenance
strategy across the entire PV ecosystem.
