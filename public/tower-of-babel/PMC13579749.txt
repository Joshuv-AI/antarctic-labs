The effect of enhanced external counterpulsation on sleep quality and quality of life in patients with chronic insomnia: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiuli Xu, Yinfen Wang, Shaohui Ye, Cuicui Meng, Qi Zhang, Lin Deng, Guifu Wu, Tong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsomnia is highly prevalent in the general population and increases the risk of somatic and mental disorder. It leads to considerable strain on healthcare systems and contributes to societal burdens. Current recommended treatments for chronic insomnia are limited, and a proportion of patients do not respond adequately. Enhanced external counterpulsation (EECP) is an effective and noninvasive therapy for ischemic vascular disease. Findings from our previous pilot trial demonstrated its potential therapeutic effect on chronic insomnia. This current large-scale randomized controlled study aims to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of EECP in chronic insomnia, as well as its underlying mechanisms.MethodsThis single-center, two-armed, randomized, single-blinded, parallel sham-controlled trial will enroll 98 participants with chronic insomnia aged 18–75 years. Based on a 1:2 ratio, participants will be randomly assigned to the sham EECP group (control) or the EECP group (experimental) to receive 45-minute sessions. Five sessions will be held per week for a total of 35 sessions. This trial will include six assessments (i.e., pre-intervention, mid-intervention [20th session], post-intervention [35th session], and 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month follow-ups) over 14 months. The primary outcome is the change in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score post-intervention. Secondary outcomes include PSQI score changes at mid-intervention and at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-ups; sleep parameters (total sleep time, efficiency, latency, and awakenings); anxiety, depression and quality of life scores at mid-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-ups; along with peak oxygen uptake, heart rate at 1-minute recovery, vascular endothelial function, and blood biomarkers post-intervention.DiscussionThis study will report on the short-, medium-, and long-term efficacy of EECP in chronic insomnia, as well as its underlying mechanisms. The findings may offer a new treatment option for refractory patients and help improve productivity, reduce comorbidities, and lower healthcare costs.Trial registrationChiCTR, ChiCTR2500101872 (URL: https//www.chictr.org.cn/). Introduction

Insomnia is highly prevalent and affects 12–20% of the general population [1, 2]. Chronic cases further exacerbate healthcare resource utilization and socioeconomic costs. Long-term data show that 86% of patients report persistent symptoms at ong-year follow-up, and 59% continue to experience insomnia after five years, indicating its chronic trajectory [3]. Chronic insomnia is defined as difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening accompanied by daytime symptoms such as daytime fatigue, depressed mood, and poor concentration, occurring at least three times per week for more than three months [2]. It can reduce productivity, and increase absenteeism from work, use of health care services, and risk of accidents in traffic sites and workplaces [4–6]. It is also associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, mental disorders, and reduced quality of life (QoL) and life expectancy [7–11].

Currently, recommended treatments for chronic insomnia are limited to cognitive behavioral therapy and approved hypnotics; however, not all patients respond to them [2]. Cognitive behavioral therapy is considered the most effective treatment for insomnia, but given the limited number of trained psychologists, high cost and low patient willingness, it is rarely used among Chinese patients [12]. Although several hypnotics are available, including benzodiazepines, sedative antidepressants, and melatonin agonists, their use is generally restricted to no more than four weeks, to prevent habituation or dependence [2]. Furthermore, hypnotics may lead to adverse reactions such as cognitive decline, memory impairment, rebound insomnia, falls, daytime sleepiness, and traffic accidents) [2, 13]. Adjunctive treatments such as exercise, light therapy, music therapy, and noninvasive brain stimulation therapy have also shown potential benefits for insomnia, but because of insufficient evidence, current guideline does not recommend them as standalone interventions [2]. As such, exploring complementary or alternative therapies for insomnia continue to receive much attention.

In our clinical practice, we have observed that some patients with cardiovascular disease and comorbid insomnia report varying degrees of improvement in sleep quality following enhanced external counterpulsation (EECP) treatment. EECP is a non-invasive, safe, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved intervention for ischemic heart disease. Evidence shows that EECP also has beneficial effects on other ischemic vascular diseases, including ischemic cerebrovascular disease, ocular ischemic disease and sudden deafness [14]. Several clinical studies in China have reported that the EECP therapy can effectively alleviate insomnia in older patients, individuals with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and pilots [15–17]. However, the poor methodological quality and varied treatment strategies in these studies have resulted in a low level of evidence. Previously, our research team has conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial and found that 35 sessions of EECP treatment (45 min per session) significantly improved the sleep quality and QoL in patients with chronic insomnia, with therapeutic effects maintained for three months [18]. However, the evidence was insufficient to support EECP as an adjunctive treatment for chronic insomnia, given the relatively small sample size (n = 23 per group) of the pilot study and unclear underlying mechanism.

EECP device features three pairs of pneumatic cuffs wrapped around the patient’ s calves, thighs, and buttocks. It operates in synchronization with the cardiac rhythm, triggered by the electrocardiographic R-wave. During early diastole, the cuffs inflate in a distal-to-proximal sequence, compressing the lower-extremity vascular bed and driving blood retrograde into the aorta, thereby increasing aortic diastolic pressure and enhancing perfusion to vital organs (e.g., the brain and heart) [19, 20]. In late diastole, the cuffs then deflate simultaneously, thus reducing cardiac afterload. Evidence demonstrate that EECP can result in immediate and sustained benefits by improving hemodynamics (i.e., increased perfusion of vital organs) and enhancing flow-induced shear stress [19, 21]. EECP-mediated increases in blood flow shear stress yield extensive vascular biological effects: (1) Upregulation of angiogenic and vasodilatory factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), and nitric oxide, thereby promoting angiogenesis and vasculogenesis, enhancing collateral circulation, and ultimately improving endothelial function and increasing blood perfusion [21–23]; (2) Downregulation of proinflammatory factors, such as nuclear factor kappa B [NF-κB], tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNFα]), thus attenuating inflammation [24, 25]; etc. EECP has also been demonstrated to improve aerobic capacity (measured by peak oxygen uptake [peak VO2], autonomic dysfunction, anxiety and depression, and quality of life [18, 26–29].

However, there remains a paucity of robust research evidence to conclusively verify and elucidate the efficacy and underlying mechanisms of EECP in the treatment of insomnia. Domestic scholars have posited several potential mechanisms, including [14, 17, 30]: (1) EECP may enhance cerebral perfusion, thereby improving the blood supply and nutritional status of brain cells, which is conducive to the recovery and maintenance of neuronal function; (2) It may elevate VEGF levels, promoting angiogenesis and subsequently improving tissue blood supply; (3) It may modulate the levels of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and norepinephrine (NE), thereby regulating neurotransmitter balance. The therapeutic effects of EECP extend beyond hemodynamic optimization to include suppression of inflammatory responses and the modulation of autonomic nervous system, and improvement of aerobic capacity. These mechanisms may underlie the beneficial effects of EECP in patients with insomnia, a condition frequently associated with autonomic imbalance, chronic inflammation and impaired peak VO2 [31–33]. This is also the potential mechanism that this study aims to further clarify.

Current evidence on the effectiveness of EECP therapy for chronic insomnia should be interpreted cautiously because of variability in study populations, small sample sizes, and methodological differences across studies. Significant gaps also persist regarding the overall impact of EECP therapy on sleep quality in patients with chronic insomnia. To address these gaps, we will conduct a large-scale randomized sham-controlled trial to systematically evaluate the efficacy and underlying mechanisms of EECP for chronic insomnia across short-, medium-, and long-term follow-ups. The findings may help establish EECP as an adjunctive or alternative treatment for patients who are unresponsive to current recommended therapies. Clinically, we hypothesize that EECP will improve sleep quality, fatigue, mental status, and QoL and these effects will be sustained for 3–12 months. Mechanistically, we propose that EECP will positively affect physiological markers, including whole-body metabolic rate (as reflected by peak VO2)and parasympathetic nerve function (assessed by heart rate recovery at 1-minute [HRR1]), neuroendocrine factors (melatonin, cortisol,5-HT and NE), angiogenic factors (VEGFA and VEGFR) and clock genes (cryptochrome 1 and 2, [Cry1, Cry2]) [23, 34–39]. Moreover, we hypothesize that EECP will improve inflammatory biomarkers (NF-κB and TNFα), and endothelial function [32, 40].

Method

Ethical approval

This study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (protocol version 2.0; approval code: 2025-021-02; approval date: 23 April 2025) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (NO. ChiCTR2500101872). This trial will adhere to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study design

This exploratory study is a prospective, single-center, single-blind (participants and outcome assessors), two-arm, randomized, parallel sham-controlled trial that will be conduct at the Cardiovascular Rehabilitation Center of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Shenzhen, China). The trial protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines for interventional trial design, and full compliance is documented in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 1).

Given our preliminary evidence that EECP exerts a positive effect on improving sleep quality in patients with chronic insomnia, this study adopts a 1:2 (control: experimental) random allocation ratio. This design is based on ethical considerations to allow more participants access to potentially effective treatment, and it also enhances statistical power by enabling a larger total sample size than a 1:1 ratio would require.

Ninety-eight eligible participants with chronic insomnia will be randomly assigned in a 1:2 ratio to either the sham EECP group (control group: 33 participants; 5 × 45-min sessions per week; 35 sessions in total) or the EECP group (experimental group: 65 participants: 5 × 45-min session per week; 35 sessions in total). Evaluations will be conducted at pre-intervention (baseline), mid-intervention (20th session), and post-intervention (35th session), as well as at 3-, 6-, and 12-months follow-ups. EECP and sham EECP interventions

Participants in the EECP group (experimental) will receive therapy at a cuff pressure of 0.025 MPa for 45-minute sessions, five sessions per week, totaling 35 sessions. The duration, frequency, and total number of sessions will be identical for sham EECP group (control; 45 min/session, five sessions/week, 35 sessions total) but at a lower cuff inflation pressure of 0.009 MPa. It was previously determined that for sham EECP, a cuff inflation pressure of 70 mmHg (0.009 MPa) is adequate to maintain the appearance and sensation of EECP intervention, yet insufficient to induce blood pressure alteration [18, 26]. An experienced nurse will be responsible for the EECP operation (P-ECP/TM instrument, Chongqing, China) in the EECP treatment room.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome is the change in PSQI score following 35 interventions. The PSQI is a 19-item self-report instrument that evaluates general sleep quality over the preceding month across seven domains: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleep medication use, and daytime dysfunction [41]. PSQI scores > 5 indicate clinically significant sleep impairment, and higher scores suggest poorer sleep quality [42]. The Chinese PSQI version has demonstrated good reliability and validity [43].

Secondary outcome measures

Peak oxygen uptake and heart rate recovery at 1-minute

Peak VO₂ will be measured by maximal cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET; COSMED, Germany) at pre-intervention and post-intervention using individualized ramp protocols (10–20 W/min) on a cycle ergometer. Protocols will be tailored to participants’ anthropometrics and exercise profiles to achieve a test duration of 8–12 min. After a 3-minute rest and a 3-minute warm-up (0 W, 55–65 RPM), the workload will increase progressively until volitional exhaustion. Continuous monitoring includes (1) a 12-lead electrocardiogram and oxygen saturation; (2) manual blood pressure measurement every 5 min and at peak exercise; and (3) breath-by-breath gas analysis (10-second intervals) for VO2, carbon dioxide production and ventilation. Peak VO₂ will be calculated as the mean of final 20-second measurements. As an indicator of parasympathetic nerve function, HRR1 represents the heart rate decline at the first minute of recovery, calculated by subtracting the 1-minute post-exercise heart rate from the peak exercise heart rate. Identical protocols will be maintained pre- and post-intervention.

Vascular endothelial function

Vascular endothelial function will be assessed using endothelium-dependent flow-mediated dilation (FMD) of the right brachial artery before and after treatment. Measurements will be obtained with a high-resolution ultrasound system (UNEXEF 38G, Japan) following guideline-specified protocols [44]. Results will be calculated as the percentage change in peak diameter relative to baseline [44].

Sleep diary

Sleep diaries are widely used for assessing sleep–wake patterns in individuals with insomnia [45]. In this study, participants will be instructed to record key sleep metrics daily upon waking. These include bedtime, wake-up time, sleep onset latency, nighttime awakenings, total sleep time, sleep efficiency (calculated as [total sleep time / time in bed] × 100%), and daytime naps.

Fatigue

Fatigue Scale 14 (FS14) and Fatigue Severity Scale 9 (FSS9) will be used to assess fatigue. The FS-14 consists of 14 items and evaluates fatigue using no/yes responses (0–1 points per item) [46]. Items are categorized into two subscales: Physical Fatigue (items 1–8) and Mental Fatigue (items 9–14). The total score (sum of Physical Fatigue and Mental Fatigue items) ranges from 0 to 14, where higher scores reflect higher levels of fatigue. The FSS9 is the most widely used self-report measure for fatigue assessment [47]. It is a brief and robust tool for reliably assessing and quantifying fatigue in clinical and research settings [48]. It includes 9 items rated on a 1–7 Likert scale, with higher average scores reflecting greater fatigue severity.

Anxiety and depression

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) will be used to measure anxiety and depression symptoms over the past month [49]. It includes 14 items: seven about anxiety (HADS-A) and seven assessing depression (HADS-D). Each item is rated on a 4-point scale (0–3), with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. The HADS has demonstrates reliability and validity across populations [49].

Health-related quality of life

QoL will be assessed using the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36). This tool includes two main sections: (1) Physical Component Summary (PCS), comprising physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, and general health; (2) Mental Component Summary (MCS) comprising vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. All scores are standardized to a 0–100 scale, where higher values reflect better QoL. A 5-point increase in PCS or MCS scores is deemed clinically meaningful [50]. The Chinese version of the SF-36 has been validated as a reliable and valid tool for assessing health-related QoL in general populations [51].

Participants will self-administer the online questionnaires and sleep diary via WJX app (Changsha Ranxing IT Ltd., China) at pre-intervention, mid-intervention, post-intervention, and at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-ups.

Blood collection and biochemical assays

Pre- and post-intervention venous blood samples will be obtained from participants after an 8-hour fast via antecubital venipuncture using EDTA-coated collection tubes (08:00–08:30 a.m.). Plasma separation will be performed through low-temperature centrifugation (4 °C, 1600 × g, 10 min) followed by storage at − 80 °C for biomarker quantification, including (1) neuroendocrine markers: melatonin, cortisol, NE, and 5-HT; and (2) inflammatory/angiogenic mediators: TNF-α, VEGFA, and VEGFR. Concurrently, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) will be isolated using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Blood samples will be sent to the laboratory for centrifugation, plasma/PBMC extraction, and frozen storage within 120 min of isolation. Plasma biomarkers will be quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. PBMCs gene expression (NF-κB, Cry1 and Cry2) will be analyzed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction. All assays will be performed in a single batch post-intervention to minimize inter-assay variability.

Assessment of adverse events

Adverse events such as skin injury or lower limb numbness or pain, occurring either during or after each intervention will be recorded. The severity of adverse events will be graded using a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from mild to lethal). These will be later independently verified by two physicians with comprehensive patient data access.

Monitoring

Monitoring will be conducted by research staff adhering to the standard operating procedures, with results reported to the principal investigator within 2 weeks. Any adverse events or unintended effects will be promptly addressed by the principal investigator, reported to the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University’s Eighth Affiliated Hospital, and communicated to the research team. Given EECP’s established safety profile as a routine and non-invasive intervention, a data monitoring committee is not required.

Data collection, management, and confidentiality

Demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, gender, medical history) will be systematically recorded using standardized case report forms. Follow-up assessments will be performed according to the study protocol during scheduled visits, and efficacy endpoints (e.g., mean change in PSQI scores from pre-intervention) and adverse events will be documented in real time. All data collected using case report forms will undergo source verification and will be audited for consistency with medical records.

Participants’ names will be entered into a confidential registry, and all patient identifiers will be replaced with participant codes rather than their actual names. Only essential information directly relevant to the research (e.g., demographic characteristics and biomarker data) will be collected. Collected blood samples will be used exclusively by researchers and securely disposed of after study completion.

Data will be stored in paper and electronic formats in accordance with Good Clinical Practice requirements. Privacy will be protected through anonymization, access control, and encryption technologies. Paper documents will be stored in fireproof cabinets, and electronic data will be encrypted with access logs recorded.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated based on post-intervention PSQI scores from a prior study [18] and was calculated for an independent two-sample t-test comparing post-intervention PSQI scores between groups. The experimental group showed a post-intervention mean PSQI score of 6.96 (standard deviation [SD] = 3.08), compared to 9.48 (SD = 3.13) in the control group [18]. Using PASS 15 software, we calculated the sample size with a two-sided significance level of 5% and a statistical power of 90%. With a 1:2 allocation ratio (control: experimental), the required total sample size is 78 participants. After accounting for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the final target sample size has been set to 98 participants (33 controls and 65 experimental).

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

A simple randomization procedure was employed. Using SPSS 26.0, the investigator XX generated random numbers and assigned participants to control or experimental groups according to the randomization sequence. Eligible participants with chronic insomnia will be randomly assigned in a 1:2 ratio to either the sham EECP group or the EECP group.

The envelope method was used for allocation concealment. Cards indicating each participant’s group assignment (Group A: experimental; Group B: control) were placed into opaque envelopes in advance. The number were written on the outside of each envelope (numbered in random sequence order), and the enrollment order of participants were consistent with the envelope numbers. An independent assistant will assign eligible participants their corresponding numbered envelopes and open the envelopes in the presence of both the participants and the EECP operator.

Before group allocation, participants will be informed by the investigator XX about the two study groups (experimental and control) differing in counterpulsation pressure but being identical in terms of the number, duration, and frequency of intervention sessions. Participants will, however, remain blinded to the specific pressure values assigned to each group. The principal investigator and the EECP operator will be aware of the specific intervention details for each group, while outcome assessors and participants will be blinded to these details throughout the trial. Per protocol, planned unblinding will occur after completion of data collection and analysis, although emergency unblinding may be performed in the event of severe adverse events to ensure participant safety.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 will be used for statistical analyses. The intention-to-treat principal will be applied (including all randomized participants in the statistical analysis), and multiple imputation will be performed for missing values. The normality of all variables will be evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For continuous variables, normally distributed data will be presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normal data will be presented as median (interquartile range). For binary variables, data will be expressed as the number of participants (n) and percentage.

Comparisons of quantitative data, such as demographic characteristics and preoperative laboratory examination indicators, will be performed using non-paired t-test. Comparisons of qualitative data, such as gender and adverse events, will be conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test, as appropriate.

For peak VO₂, HRR1, and blood biomarkers, paired t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (for non-normally distributed data) will be used for intra-group comparisons, whereas unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests will be used for inter-group comparisons. For the primary outcome (PSQI score) and other secondary outcomes with normal distribution, two-factor-repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) will be used to analyze intra- and inter-group differences. When significant differences are detected, Bonferroni post-hoc tests will be performed. For non-normally distributed data, intra- and inter-group differences will be analyzed using non-parametric tests. Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses will be performed on PSQI scores and other secondary outcome indicators. In this study, a P value less than 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Intergroup differences in PSQI scores will be further verified through (a) per-protocol analysis (including only participants who complete all 35 sessions and post-intervention measures) and (b) mean imputation approach for missing data.

Publication and dissemination policy

We plan to actively engage with the professional community by presenting our research at relevant conferences and publishing both the study protocol and trial outcomes in specialized medical journals, particularly those focusing on neurology, psychiatry, rehabilitation, and public health. After data analysis, we will promptly share the findings with all trail participants and healthcare practitioners. If promising, we will disseminate our findings to public health policymakers and social decision-makers to advocate for the implementation of an evidence-based EECP intervention aimed at enhancing sleep quality and well-being among individuals with chronic insomnia.

Discussion

Beyond nocturnal sleep disturbances, insomnia significantly impairs daytime functioning, elevates the risk of somatic and psychological disorders, reduces QoL, and increases socioeconomic burdens. This warrants a comprehensive evaluation of EECP for chronic insomnia. To that effect, we will conduct a large-scale, randomized, sham-controlled trial to systematically assess EECP’s efficacy in chronic insomnia. Extending our prior efficacy-focused research [18], in this RCT, we will further investigate EECP’s underlying mechanisms, thereby bridging the gap between laboratory evidence and practical clinical application.

Key methodological strengths of this tiral include (1) a longitudinal design with 12-month follow-up to assess sustained therapeutic outcomes and (2) multidimensional biomarker profiling encompassing (a) neuro-immune-endocrine axis (melatonin, cortisol, 5-HT, Cry1, Cry2, NFκB, TNFα, VEGFA/VEGFR), (b) autonomic function (NE, HRR1), (c) whole body metabolic rate (peak VO₂). However, a major methodological limitation is the absence of objective sleep monitoring (e.g., polysomnography or actigraphy) due to their unavailability in our hospital, which may introduce bias in outcome interpretation.

Insomnia is considered to be a disorder of hyperarousal experienced throughout the entire day [52, 53]. Leading theories highlight the critical role of cognitive, emotional, and physiological hyperarousal in both its onset and persistence [54]. The mechanistic exploration of the present trial will mainly focus on the physiological arousal model, which will be evaluated by measuring the whole-body metabolic rate (peak VO2), autonomic nerve function, and neuroendocrine measures [35, 55]. Since the 1990s, research has consistently linked insomnia to elevated whole-body metabolic rates [56, 57]. Conversely, current evidence suggests that poor sleep quality or chronic insomnia may be associated with reduced peak VO₂ [33, 58], highlighting the need for further investigation to clarify this paradoxical relationship.

The autonomic nervous system is a critical modulator of physiologic processes during sleep. Patients with untreated sleep disorders often present with symptoms of autonomic dysfunction. This is generally characterized by excessive sympathetic activity relative to parasympathetic tone, which impairs both sleep initiation and maintenance [31]. Under normal conditions, parasympathetic activity increases during the transition from wakefulness to sleep. HRR1 assessed through maximal CPET has proved to be a reliable indicator of cardiac autonomic function, with decreased HRR1 values reflecting reduced vagal tone [59, 60]. This autonomic dysfunction may trigger a pathological sequence that contributes to the development of insomnia and precedes endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffening, potentially linking insomnia to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [53, 61].

Insomnia also exerts pro-inflammatory effects comparable to, or potentially exceeding, those of key demographic factors (e.g., age) and biobehavioral variables (e.g., physical activity) [32, 62]. Evidence shows that insomnia triggers elevated secretion of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α [62]. Notably, NF-κB activation in PBMCs precedes systemic inflammatory marker increases in insomnia patients [63, 64]. Molecular studies further demonstrate that circadian clock-gene Cry deletion exacerbates pro-inflammatory cytokine production via NF-κB pathways, whereas Cry1 overexpression mitigates vascular inflammation induced by sleep deprivation [37, 38]. As a core circadian regulator, the Cry gene appears to exert rhythmic anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing NF-κB and related pro-inflammatory cascades. Chronic inflammation, a known contributor to cardiovascular disease and depression [65], may thus act as a key mechanistic link between insomnia and these conditions [66].

Emerging evidence also shows that insomnia is associated with endothelial dysfunction [40, 67], suggesting endothelial dysfunction as a plausible mediator between insomnia and cardiovascular complications. Given that vascular endothelial dysfunction acts as both an early atherosclerotic marker and an independent cardiovascular risk factor [40], development of interventions that simultaneously improve sleep and endothelial function is an important research direction. As described above, EECP exhibits positive effects such as anti-inflammatory effects and improvements in vascular endothelial function and enhancement of peak VO2. These may collectively contribute to the intervention’s therapeutic potential in insomnia by improving sleep quality and clinical outcomes in chronic insomnia.

In conclusion, this study aims to delineate the short-, medium-, and long-term therapeutic efficacy of EECP for chronic insomnia, along with its mechanistic pathways, thereby positioning it as a promising new treatment option Successful completion of this trial and its promising outcomes will not only offer a therapeutic option for chronic insomnia but may also yield socioeconomic benefits, such as improved QoL and labor productivity, as well as reduced insomnia-related complications (e.g., cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, mental disorders), and healthcare burdens.
