A Case of Breast Metastasis from Primary Rhabdomyosarcoma of the Lower Leg
Authors: Mutsuki Furukawa, Koshi Matsui, Emi Kanaya, Shiho Nagasawa, Kota Nobukiyo, Misato Araki, Kenta Sukegawa, Shinichi Sekine, Kenichi Hirabayashi, Tsutomu Fujii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTINTRODUCTIONRhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common malignant soft tissue tumor in children, with the alveolar subtype associated with an aggressive clinical course and poor prognosis. Metastasis to the breast from RMS is extremely rare, particularly in preadolescent patients, and optimal local treatment strategies have not been established.CASE PRESENTATIONWe report the case of a 9-year-old girl with alveolar RMS originating in the left lower extremity. The patient underwent wide resection of the primary tumor and dissection of the regional lymph node, followed by multimodal therapy, including chemotherapy and proton beam radiotherapy, and achieved complete remission. Three months after the completion of treatment, she developed a palpable mass in the right breast. Imaging studies revealed multiple breast lesions without evidence of local recurrence at the primary site. Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of metastatic RMS. Preoperative chemotherapy resulted in a limited tumor response, and local control was pursued. An endoscopic-assisted nipple–areola-sparing mastectomy with partial resection of the pectoralis major muscle was performed, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Immunohistochemical findings supported the diagnosis of metastatic RMS. Comprehensive genomic profiling of the metastatic lesion identified a TP53 pathogenic variant. However, germline testing was not performed, and the clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain. Despite aggressive multidisciplinary treatment, the patient developed widespread metastatic disease and died approximately 4 years after the initial diagnosis.CONCLUSIONSBreast metastasis from alveolar RMS is rare but should be considered in pediatric patients who present with breast masses after treatment. Surgical resection may contribute to local control and QOL in selected cases. Additional cases are needed to clarify the role of surgery and to establish optimal treatment strategies, particularly in patients with suspected hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes.

INTRODUCTION

RMS is a malignant soft tissue tumor arising from mesenchymal tissue. It accounts for approximately 8% of all pediatric malignancies and is the most common malignant soft tissue tumor in children.1) The International Classification of Rhabdomyosarcoma categorizes RMS into 3 major prognostic groups: a favorable prognosis group (botryoid and spindle cell types), an intermediate prognosis group (embryonal type), and a poor prognosis group (alveolar type and undifferentiated sarcoma).2) The alveolar subtype, as observed in the present case, is associated with a particularly poor prognosis, as it predominantly affects young patients, frequently arises in the extremities, and has a high likelihood for distant metastasis.3,4)

Here, we report a rare case of recurrent alveolar RMS with breast metastasis, along with a review of the relevant literature.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 9-year-old girl initially presented with pain in her left lower leg, which was managed conservatively by a local physician. Subsequently, progressive swelling developed in the same region, prompting referral to the orthopedic department of a general hospital. Imaging revealed a 7-cm mass within the left gastrocnemius muscle. A biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of alveolar RMS of the left lower leg. The patient had no prior history of malignancy.

FDG-PET demonstrated metastasis to the left popliteal lymph nodes. According to the Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group staging system, the disease was classified as Stage 3 or Group IIc. Treatment was initiated based on the Japan Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group intermediate-risk classification. Following wide resection of the primary tumor and dissection of the left popliteal lymph node, the patient was enrolled in a phase II clinical trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of VAC2.2/VI therapy for intermediate-risk RMS. She received VAC chemotherapy followed by VI chemotherapy. Concurrently, proton beam radiotherapy (41.4 Gy in 23 fractions) was administered. All treatments were completed approximately 10 months after the initial surgery, and posttreatment imaging confirmed a complete remission.

Three months after the completion of therapy, the patient noticed a palpable mass in her right breast while bathing. A Mammotome biopsy was performed, revealing diffuse proliferation of atypical round cells. Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the tumor cells were positive for desmin and myoglobin and negative for cytokeratin AE1/AE3, which is consistent with metastatic recurrence of RMS. Histopathological findings consistent with metastatic RMS were obtained. Detailed histopathological findings are presented in Fig. 1. She was referred to our institution for further evaluation and treatment. Physical examination revealed a palpable 3-cm mass in the right B quadrant of the breast. Breast ultrasonography revealed a well-circumscribed, lobulated hypoechoic mass measuring 29 mm with heterogeneous internal architecture and cystic components, as well as an additional 10-mm lesion in the right C quadrant (Fig. 2A and 2B). Breast MRI revealed a 31-mm ring-enhancing mass in the right B quadrant and a similar lesion in the right C quadrant (Fig. 3A and 3B). PET-CT revealed increased uptake corresponding to these lesions, with no evidence of recurrence at the primary site (Fig. 4).

After multidisciplinary discussion with the pediatric oncology team, preoperative VAC chemotherapy was administered to assess chemosensitivity. After approximately 5 months, ultrasonography revealed no significant change in overall tumor size; however, the solid component decreased while the cystic component increased (Fig. 5). These findings suggested treatment-related tumor degeneration with a reduction in viable tumor burden despite the absence of a decrease in overall lesion size. Considering the limited response observed, although the tumor may have partially regressed, further chemotherapy was unlikely to result in complete disappearance. Therefore, nipple–areola-sparing mastectomy was performed. Considering the patient’s age and cosmetic outcomes, an endoscopic-assisted right nipple–areola-sparing mastectomy was performed. Owing to the large cystic component, the cyst fluid was aspirated intraoperatively to facilitate safe resection and prevent tumor spillage. Partial resection of the pectoralis major muscle was required because of suspected invasion. The total operative time was 145 min, and blood loss was 65 mL. Gross examination of the resected specimen revealed a 35-mm cystic lesion containing hemorrhagic fluid. Histologically, diffuse proliferation of atypical pleomorphic tumor cells was observed, resembling that of the primary tumor (Fig. 6A and 6B). Although overt rhabdomyoblastic differentiation was not evident morphologically, immunohistochemical staining was positive for desmin, myogenin, and MyoD1, confirming skeletal muscle differentiation, supporting the diagnosis of metastatic RMS (Fig. 6C–6E).

Postoperatively, IE chemotherapy was initiated, along with adjuvant radiotherapy to the right breast (41.4 Gy in 23 fractions). However, ifosfamide-induced encephalopathy developed, necessitating discontinuation of IE therapy. The patient subsequently completed VAC and VI chemotherapy, followed by maintenance vincristine therapy. Treatment for recurrent disease was completed slightly more than 1 year after surgery.

Comprehensive genomic profiling (FoundationOne CDx) performed on the preoperative biopsy specimen revealed no actionable mutations but identified a TP53 variant, c.637C>T (p.Arg213*), with a VAF of 79.48% as a secondary finding. Estimated tumor content was approximately 70%. Because of the early age at onset and a family history of childhood cancer in a paternal uncle, a hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome was considered. However, germline testing was not performed.

Six months after treatment for breast metastasis was completed, pelvic MRI revealed a new tumorous lesion with suspected lymph node metastasis in the left abdomen, which was consistent with a second recurrence. Further evaluation demonstrated widespread metastases involving the bone marrow, lymph nodes, pleura, bilateral breasts, chest wall, liver, spleen, peritoneum, and uterus. The patient was transitioned to palliative care and died approximately 4 years after the initial diagnosis and just under 2 years after mastectomy.

DISCUSSION

RMS is a malignant soft tissue tumor arising from mesodermal or mesenchymal tissue. In Japan, the incidence is estimated to be approximately 4.6 cases per million children, corresponding to 60–80 new cases annually.5) RMS can arise in nearly any anatomical location, with the most common primary sites being the genitourinary tract, head and neck region, and extremities. Distant metastases most frequently involve the lungs (about 40%–50%), bone marrow (about 20%–30%), and bones (about 10%–20%), whereas breast metastasis is rare.6) Hays et al. reviewed approximately 3500 cases of RMS and reported breast metastasis in 19 cases (0.5%), of which 18 were histologically classified as the alveolar subtype. The primary tumor sites included the extremities (8 patients), nasopharynx/paranasal sinuses (7 patients), and trunk (7 patients). In these 19 patients, breast metastases developed after a disease-free interval of 43–155 weeks (median, 79 weeks) following initial treatment, and patients ranged in age from 13 to 21 years.7) Kebudi et al. reported 70 cases of breast metastases from RMS in patients younger than 19 years, based on institutional experience and a literature review from 1980 to 2014. The median age was 14 years, and approximately 90% of the patients were histologically classified as having alveolar RMS. Breast metastases accounted for 1.5% of all RMS cases and 7.5% of all metastatic RMS cases.8) These epidemiological features are consistent with those observed in the present case.

Metastasis to the breast from extramammary malignancies is extremely rare, accounting for only 0.5%–2.0% of all breast malignancies.9,10) The most common primary tumors metastasizing to the breast include malignant melanoma, lymphoma, ovarian cancer, and lung cancer.11) One proposed explanation for the rarity of breast metastases is that in age groups most commonly affected by malignancy, breast tissue is largely replaced by adipose and fibrous tissue, resulting in reduced blood flow. In contrast, breast tissue in younger patients is more highly vascularized, which may facilitate hematogenous metastasis.12)

Breast metastases from RMS are reported predominantly in adolescent girls. Several studies have suggested that increased vascularization of the mammary gland during puberty may contribute to this phenomenon. Another proposed mechanism involves IGF signaling, as mammary epithelial and stromal cells secrete growth factors such as IGF-I and IGF-II, and increased expression of IGF receptors on RMS cells may promote metastatic colonization of breast tissue.13) In the present case, menarche occurred at 10 years of age, the patient’s breast development was within the normal range, and there was no evidence of precocious puberty.

In Japan, RMS is typically treated at institutions that participate in the Japan Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group. Patients are stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, and multimodal therapy combining risk-adapted chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery is the standard approach.14) In accordance with the Japanese Society for Pediatric Hematology/Oncology guidelines, all patients with alveolar RMS without distant metastasis are classified as intermediate risk. The current standard chemotherapy regimen for intermediate-risk RMS consists of 42 weeks of VAC2.2 therapy, including vincristine, actinomycin D, and high-dose cyclophosphamide.6) The patient in the present case received this regimen as part of a phase II clinical trial.

However, a standard local treatment strategy for breast metastases from RMS has not been established. Previous reports describe heterogeneous approaches, including surgical resection and radiotherapy. Hayes-Jordan et al. reported prolonged survival in a patient who underwent surgical resection of a recurrent lesion other than bone metastasis.15) Persic and Roberts reported a patient who achieved 10-year survival following radiotherapy alone for breast metastases.16) Nevertheless, clear guidelines for the management of breast metastases from RMS are lacking.

The present case involved primary alveolar RMS of the left lower extremity with subsequent metastasis to the breast. For the metastatic lesion, local control was pursued in addition to systemic therapy, and a nipple–areola-sparing mastectomy was performed. Given that suspected invasion of the pectoralis major muscle raised concerns about inadequate local control and potential deterioration of QOL, adjuvant radiotherapy was administered despite the patient’s young age. Although surgery and radiotherapy achieved local control while preserving cosmetic outcomes and QOL, long-term survival could not be achieved. Given the rarity of breast metastases from RMS, additional cases are required to clarify the clinical significance and indications of surgical intervention as a local treatment modality.

Pathogenic variants in the tumor suppressor gene TP53 have been reported in RMS with distinct frequencies depending on somatic or germline origin. Somatic TP53 pathogenic variants are observed in approximately 13% of fusion-negative cases and 4% of fusion-positive cases.17) In contrast, germline TP53 pathogenic variants are identified in approximately 3% of all RMS cases, with a higher frequency of up to 11% reported in anaplastic RMS.18) Germline TP53 variants are also known to be associated with LFS. Comprehensive genomic profiling (FoundationOne CDx) identified a pathogenic TP53 variant, c.637C>T (p.Arg213*), with a VAF of 79.48%. Because the assay was performed on tumor tissue and germline testing was not conducted, the variant could not be definitively classified as somatic or germline in origin. Although the relatively high VAF may raise the possibility of a germline alteration, the origin of the variant cannot be inferred from VAF alone. Because somatic TP53 mutations are more frequently reported in RMS, a somatic origin cannot be excluded.

Recent studies have shown that a proportion of pathogenic variants identified through tumor-only comprehensive genomic profiling are subsequently confirmed as germline alterations. However, the conversion rate varies substantially according to the gene involved, clinical context, and tumor type. Therefore, confirmation by dedicated germline testing remains essential before diagnosing a hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome.19)

Given the patient’s early onset of RMS and family history of childhood cancer in a paternal uncle, an underlying hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome was considered. However, germline testing was not performed, and a diagnosis of LFS could not be established.

Although a family history of childhood cancer in a paternal uncle was noted, detailed information regarding the specific cancer type was not available despite careful inquiry, and therefore a comprehensive pedigree could not be constructed.

LFS is a hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline TP53 mutations and is characterized by an increased risk of various malignancies, including childhood sarcomas, early-onset breast cancer, brain tumors, and adrenocortical carcinoma.20) However, because LFS could not be confirmed in the present case, the implications for treatment selection remain speculative. This case highlights the importance of considering genetic counseling and confirmatory germline testing when hereditary cancer predisposition is suspected.

CONCLUSIONS

We report a rare case of breast metastasis from alveolar RMS in a 9-year-old girl. Our findings highlight the clinical and pathological characteristics of this unusual metastatic presentation. Despite multidisciplinary treatment, including chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy, long-term survival was not achieved.
