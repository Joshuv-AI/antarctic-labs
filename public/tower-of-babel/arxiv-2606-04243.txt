Structural properties of the implicit function defined by an integral self-consistency equation
Ivan Viakhirev

Abstract
We study the integral equation ∫ 0 m η ​ ρ ​ ( η ) C − η ​ 𝑑 η = 1 , C > m , \int_{0}^{m}\frac{\eta\,\rho(\eta)}{C-\eta}\,d\eta=1,\qquad C>m, where ρ \rho is a C 1 C^{1} probability density on [ 0 , M ] [0,M] vanishing polynomially at η = M \eta=M . Setting ℐ + ​ ( m ) := lim C ↓ m ∫ 0 m η ​ ρ ​ ( η ) / ( C − η ) ​ 𝑑 η \mathcal{I}^{+}(m):=\lim_{C\downarrow m}\int_{0}^{m}\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)\,d\eta and Ω := { m ∈ ( 0 , M ) : ℐ + ​ ( m ) > 1 } \Omega:=\{m\in(0,M):\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)>1\} , the displayed equation determines C C implicitly as a function of m m on Ω \Omega , and our object of study is the dimensionless ratio β ⁡ ( m ) := C ⁡ ( m ) / m \beta(m):=C(m)/m . Write h ⁡ ( η ) := η ​ ρ ​ ( η ) h(\eta):=\eta\rho(\eta) . Our main result (Theorem 7 ) establishes openness of Ω \Omega , C 1 C^{1} -smoothness of β \beta , a sign formula identifying β ′ ​ ( m ) \beta^{\prime}(m) with a positively-weighted integral of d ​ h / d ​ ln ⁡ η dh/d\ln\eta , transfer of monotonicity from h h to β \beta , and existence of an interior critical point of β \beta when h h is unimodal and two technical hypotheses hold. Numerically, β \beta has a single critical point in seven log-concave test densities (mostly Beta-type), in support of a separate uniqueness conjecture. A bimodal density that violates both unimodality and log-concavity exhibits three critical points; this shows that dropping the two hypotheses jointly admits multiple critical points, but does not separate their roles. Applications to preferential-attachment networks are mentioned only as motivation.



1
Introduction

1.1
The equation and the object of study

Let
M\in(0,\infty)
and let
\rho
be a probability density on
[0,M]
. For

m\in(0,M]
and
C>m
, define

F(C,m)\;:=\;\int_{0}^{m}\frac{\eta\,\rho(\eta)}{C-\eta}\,d\eta-1.

(1)

We study the equation
F(C,m)=0
.

When this equation has a unique solution
C(m)>m
, the ratio

\beta(m)\;:=\;\frac{C(m)}{m}

(2)

is the central object of this paper. The set of
m
for which a solution exists
is characterized by the limit

\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)\;:=\;\lim_{C\downarrow m}\,(F(C,m)+1)\;=\;\lim_{C\downarrow m}\int_{0}^{m}\frac{\eta\rho(\eta)}{C-\eta}d\eta\;\in\;(0,\infty],

(3)

which exists by monotone convergence (the integrand increases as
C\downarrow m
).
When
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)<\infty
, the limit equals the improper integral

\int_{0}^{m}\eta\rho(\eta)/(m-\eta)d\eta
.

1.2
Origin and motivation

Equations of the form (
1
) arise in the mean-field
analysis of preferential-attachment dynamics with heterogeneous
fitness; see Section
6
for references and for the
limits of the analogy. In these applications it is the ratio

\beta=C/m
, rather than
C(m)
, that carries an intrinsic meaning:
it is dimensionless, it is bounded below by
1
throughout the
domain, and it controls the relevant degree-distribution exponent.
Analytically, the substitution
\eta=mt
in (
1
) turns
the equation into one for
\beta
alone on the fixed interval

[0,1]
(Lemma
4
), which is what makes the
derivative formula (Theorem
7
(c)) clean.

1.3
Main results

Write
h(\eta):=\eta\rho(\eta)
. Under
hypotheses (D0)–(D3) on
\rho
(Section
2.1
),
Theorem
7
establishes the following five
properties of
\beta
.

(a)

Domain.

\beta(m)
is defined exactly on the open
set
\Omega:=\{m\in(0,M):\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)>1\}\subset(0,M)
.

(b)

Smoothness.

\beta\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
\beta>1
on
\Omega
.

(c)

Sign formula.
For
m\in\Omega
,

\mathrm{sign}\bigl(\beta^{\prime}(m)\bigr)\;=\;\mathrm{sign}\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\beta(m)-t}\cdot\frac{dh}{d\ln\eta}\Big|_{\eta=mt}\,dt\right).

(4)

(d)

Monotonicity transfer from
h
to
\beta
.
On any
sub-interval of
\Omega
where
h
is monotone and non-constant,

\beta
is strictly monotone in the same direction.

(e)

Critical point under unimodality.
Suppose
h
is
unimodal with mode
\eta^{*}_{h}\in(0,M)
, and let
\mathcal{J}(m)

denote the integral on the right-hand side
of (
4
). If there is
m^{\sharp}\in(\eta^{*}_{h},M)
with

[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]\subset\Omega
and
\mathcal{J}(m^{\sharp})<0
,
then
\beta
has a critical point
m^{\star}\in(\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp})
.

The content of (
4
) is that
\beta^{\prime}(m)
has the
sign of a positive-weight average of the logarithmic derivative

dh/d\ln\eta
over
(0,m]
:
\beta
inherits monotonicity from
the elasticity of
h
, and a critical point of
\beta
is the
location at which positive and negative contributions of

dh/d\ln\eta
, weighted by the implicit-function kernel

1/(\beta(m)-t)
, balance. Part (d) is the case where the
elasticity has a definite sign throughout; part (e) is the simplest
case where it changes sign once. Part (e) requires two technical
hypotheses, conventionally labelled

(H1)

\mathcal{J}(m^{\sharp})<0
for some
m^{\sharp}\in(\eta^{*}_{h},M)
,

(H2)

[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]\subset\Omega
,

which we will refer to by these names. (H1) is the substantive
sign-change condition; (H2) is automatic whenever
\rho>0
on

(0,M)
(in particular for log-concave
h
). We verify (H1)
numerically for all seven log-concave test densities of
Section
5
.

A uniqueness conjecture.

If, in addition to the hypotheses of (e),
h
is log-concave on

(0,M)
, we conjecture that
m^{\star}
is the unique critical point
of
\beta
on
\Omega
(Conjecture
10
). A
bimodal example showing what happens when both hypotheses fail is
discussed in Section
5
.

2
Setup and preliminaries

2.1
Hypotheses on
\rho

We use the following hypotheses throughout. Let
M\in(0,\infty)
and let
\rho

be a function on
[0,M]
.

(D0)

\rho\geq 0
and
\int_{0}^{M}\rho(\eta)\,d\eta=1
.

(D1)

\rho\in C^{1}([0,M])
, with one-sided derivatives at the
endpoints.

(D2)

There exist
\alpha>1
,
K<\infty
and
\eta_{0}\in(0,M)

such that
\rho(\eta)\leq K(M-\eta)^{\alpha-1}
for all
\eta\in[\eta_{0},M]
.
That is,
\rho
vanishes polynomially at
\eta=M
at order at least

\alpha-1
; in particular
\rho(M)=0
. This holds for Beta-type
densities
\rho(\eta)\propto\eta^{a}(M-\eta)^{b}
with
b\geq 1
and fails
for any density with
\rho(M)>0
(log-normal, exponential, Gamma);
the question of extending the analysis past (D2) is taken up in
Section
6
.

(D3)

\rho
is not identically zero on any subinterval of
(0,M)
.
Equivalently,
\rho>0
on a positive-measure subset of every
subinterval of
(0,M)
.

Define
h(\eta):=\eta\rho(\eta)
. By (D1),
h\in C^{1}([0,M])
, with
derivative
h^{\prime}(\eta)=\rho(\eta)+\eta\rho^{\prime}(\eta)
; by (D2),
h(M)=0
.

2.2
Preliminary lemmas

Theorem
7
relies on five lemmas: a finiteness
criterion for the limit
\mathcal{I}^{+}
(Lemma
1
),

C^{1}
-regularity and strict
C
-monotonicity of
F

(Lemma
2
), boundary values of
F
in
C

(Lemma
3
), the reparametrisation
\eta=mt
that
makes
\beta=C/m
the only unknown (Lemma
4
), and
openness of
\Omega
(Lemma
5
). Of these, only
Lemma
1
uses the polynomial-decay hypothesis (D2).

Lemma 1

(Finiteness criterion for
\mathcal{I}^{+}
)
.

Under
(D0), (D1), (D2)
with exponent
\alpha>1
:

(i)

At the endpoint
m=M
:
\mathcal{I}^{+}(M)<\infty
,
driven by the polynomial vanishing of
\rho
supplied by (D2).

(ii)

At interior
m\in(0,M)
:
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)<\infty

iff
\rho(m)=0
, and
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)=+\infty
otherwise. The
integrability when
\rho(m)=0
comes from the local Lipschitz bound
on
\rho
implied by (D1).

Proof.

(i):
By monotone convergence (the integrand

\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)
increases pointwise as
C\downarrow M
to

\eta\rho(\eta)/(M-\eta)
),

\mathcal{I}^{+}(M)\;=\;\int_{0}^{M}\frac{\eta\rho(\eta)}{M-\eta}\,d\eta,

(5)

which is finite by the following splitting. On
[0,\eta_{0}]
the
integrand
\eta\rho(\eta)/(M-\eta)
is continuous on a compact set
with denominator
\geq M-\eta_{0}>0
, hence bounded. On
[\eta_{0},M]
,
hypothesis (D2) gives

\frac{\eta\rho(\eta)}{M-\eta}\;\leq\;\frac{MK(M-\eta)^{\alpha-1}}{M-\eta}\;=\;MK(M-\eta)^{\alpha-2},

(6)

and
\int_{\eta_{0}}^{M}(M-\eta)^{\alpha-2}\,d\eta<\infty
since
\alpha>1
.
Hence
\mathcal{I}^{+}(M)<\infty
.

(ii),
\Leftarrow
:
Assume
\rho(m)=0
with
m\in(0,M)
. By
(D1),
\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, and the mean value theorem at

\rho(m)=0
gives
\rho(\eta)\leq\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{\infty}(m-\eta)
on
[m/2,m]
.
For
C>m
,
(m-\eta)/(C-\eta)\leq 1
, so on
[m/2,m]
the integrand

\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)
is dominated by
m\,\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
;
on
[0,m/2]
it is dominated by
\|\rho\|_{\infty}
(because

C-\eta\geq m/2
). The resulting constant dominator is
L^{1}
, so
dominated convergence as
C\downarrow m
gives

\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)\;=\;\int_{0}^{m}\frac{\eta\rho(\eta)}{m-\eta}\,d\eta\;<\;\infty.

(7)

(ii),
\Rightarrow
:
Assume
\rho(m)>0
. By continuity,

\rho(\eta)\geq\rho(m)/2
on
[m-\delta,m]
for some
\delta>0
. For
C

slightly greater than
m
,

\int_{m-\delta}^{m}\frac{\eta\rho(\eta)}{C-\eta}\,d\eta\;\geq\;\frac{(m-\delta)\rho(m)}{2}\,\ln\frac{C-(m-\delta)}{C-m}\;\longrightarrow\;+\infty\quad\text{as }C\downarrow m,

(8)

so
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)=+\infty
.
∎

Remark 1

(Implication for
\Omega
)
.

Lemma
1
(ii) gives
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)=+\infty>1
at
every
m
with
\rho(m)>0
, so

\{m\in(0,M):\rho(m)>0\}\;\subset\;\Omega\;\subset\;\{m\in(0,M):\rho(m)>0\}\cup\{m:\rho(m)=0\}.

(9)

By (D3), the lower set is dense in
(0,M)
. Whether
M
is a limit
point of
\Omega
from below depends on whether
\mathcal{I}^{+}(M)>1
,
a nontrivial extra condition.

Lemma 2

(Smoothness and strict monotonicity in
C
)
.

Under
(D0), (D1)
:

(i)

F\in C^{1}\bigl(\{(C,m):0<m<M,C>m\}\bigr)
;

(ii)

\partial_{C}F(C,m)=-\int_{0}^{m}\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)^{2}\,d\eta\leq 0
;

(iii)

\partial_{m}F(C,m)=m\rho(m)/(C-m)
.

Under additionally
(D3)
:
\partial_{C}F(C,m)<0
strictly for all
(C,m)
in the domain.

Proof.

Part (i)–(iii) under
(D0), (D1)
only.

We prove
C^{1}
on each compact set

K_{m_{0},m_{1},\varepsilon}:=\{(C,m):m_{0}\leq m\leq m_{1},\ m+\varepsilon\leq C\leq 1/\varepsilon\}

for fixed
0<m_{0}<m_{1}<M
,
\varepsilon\in(0,m_{0}/4)
; the open domain is exhausted
by such sets. Since
C^{1}
-regularity is a local property, this suffices to
conclude
F\in C^{1}
on the full open domain.

Continuity of
F
.
The integrand
\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)
is continuous
in
(C,m,\eta)
on the relevant set, and bounded by

M\|\rho\|_{\infty}/\varepsilon
uniformly. Dominated convergence gives continuity.

\partial_{C}F
.
The pointwise derivative

-\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)^{2}
is bounded by

M\|\rho\|_{\infty}/\varepsilon^{2}
, hence integrable, so
differentiation under the integral sign
(
Folland 1999
, Thm. 2.27)
applies and
yields the formula for
\partial_{C}F
. The integrand

\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)^{2}
is non-negative by (D0), so

\partial_{C}F\leq 0
.

\partial_{m}F
.
The integrand of
F
does not depend on
m

except through the upper limit; the Leibniz rule yields

\partial_{m}F=m\rho(m)/(C-m)
, continuous since
\rho\in C^{1}
and

C-m\geq\varepsilon
.

Both partial derivatives are continuous on the compact set

K_{m_{0},m_{1},\varepsilon}
by dominated convergence, and a standard
result on functions of two variables (e.g.
Folland 1999
, §2.5
)
gives
F\in C^{1}
there. This establishes (i)–(iii) under
(D0), (D1).

Strict negativity under
(D3)
.
Under (D3),
\rho>0

on an open subinterval of
(0,m)
, on which the non-negative
integrand
\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)^{2}
is strictly positive. The
integral is therefore strictly positive, giving
\partial_{C}F<0

strictly throughout the domain.
∎

Lemma 3

(Boundary values)
.

Under
(D0), (D1)
, for fixed
m\in(0,M]
:

(i)

\lim_{C\downarrow m}F(C,m)=\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)-1
(with
+\infty
allowed);

(ii)

\lim_{C\to\infty}F(C,m)=-1
.

Proof.

(i):
By definition of
\mathcal{I}^{+}
.

(ii):
For
C\geq 2m
,
\eta\in[0,m]
:
C-\eta\geq m
, so

\eta\rho(\eta)/(C-\eta)\leq\rho(\eta)
, integrable. Pointwise the integrand

\to 0
. Dominated convergence gives
F\to-1
.
∎

Lemma 4

(Reparametrization)
.

For
m>0
and
C>m
, the substitution
\eta=mt
gives
F(C,m)+1=m\Phi(C/m,m)
, where

\Phi(\beta,m)\;:=\;\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t\rho(mt)}{\beta-t}\,dt,\qquad\beta>1.

(10)

Equivalently,
F(C,m)=0
iff
m\Phi(\beta,m)=1
where
\beta=C/m
.

Proof.

The substitution
\eta=mt
,
d\eta=m\,dt
sends
\eta\in[0,m]
to
t\in[0,1]
, and

\int_{0}^{m}\frac{\eta\rho(\eta)}{C-\eta}\,d\eta\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(mt)\rho(mt)\cdot m}{C-mt}\,dt\;=\;m\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t\rho(mt)}{(C/m)-t}\,dt\;=\;m\,\Phi(\beta,m).

(11)

∎

Lemma 5

(Openness of
\Omega
)
.

Define
\Omega:=\{m\in(0,M):\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)>1\}
. Under
(D0), (D1), (D2), (D3)
,

\Omega
is open in
(0,M)
.

Proof.

Fix
m_{0}\in\Omega
.

Case
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m_{0})<\infty
:
By Lemma
3
(i),

\lim_{C\downarrow m_{0}}F(C,m_{0})=\mathcal{I}^{+}(m_{0})-1>0
, so by strict
inequality and continuity in
C
there exists
C_{0}>m_{0}
with
F(C_{0},m_{0})>0
.

Case
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m_{0})=+\infty
:
By
Lemma
3
(i) (with
+\infty
allowed), there exists

\delta_{0}>0
with
F(m_{0}+\delta_{0},m_{0})>0
. Set
C_{0}:=m_{0}+\delta_{0}
;
by Lemma
2
,
F
is continuous in a neighbourhood of

(C_{0},m_{0})
, so there exists
\varepsilon>0
with
F>0
on the open
ball
B((C_{0},m_{0}),\varepsilon)
. Pick

\varepsilon^{\prime}<\min(\varepsilon,\delta_{0}/2)
(the bound
\delta_{0}/2

keeps the projection
U:=\{m:|m-m_{0}|<\varepsilon^{\prime}\}
strictly below

C_{0}
, so
C_{0}>m
on
U
and the pair
(C_{0},m)
stays in the domain
of
F
). Then
F(C_{0},m)>0
for all
m\in U
.

In both cases we obtain an open neighborhood
U\ni m_{0}
in
(0,M)
with

\sup U<C_{0}
and
F(C_{0},m)>0
for all
m\in U
.

For
m\in U
:
F(\cdot,m)
is strictly decreasing in
C

(Lemma
2
(ii) under (D3)) with
F(C_{0},m)>0
and
F\to-1
as
C\to\infty
.
Hence
\sup_{C>m}F(C,m)\geq F(C_{0},m)>0
, which by Lemma
3
(i)
equals
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)-1
. Therefore
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)>1
and
m\in\Omega
.
∎

3
The structural theorem

Assumption 6

(Unimodality)
.

h(\eta)=\eta\rho(\eta)
has a mode
\eta^{*}_{h}\in(0,M)
, in the sense that
h
is
strictly increasing on
(0,\eta^{*}_{h})
and strictly decreasing on
(\eta^{*}_{h},M)
.

Define

\mathcal{J}(m)\;:=\;\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\beta(m)-t}\cdot\frac{dh}{d\ln\eta}\Big|_{\eta=mt}\,dt\;=\;m\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t\,h^{\prime}(mt)}{\beta(m)-t}\,dt,

(12)

the second equality by
dh/d\ln\eta=\eta h^{\prime}(\eta)
at
\eta=mt
, which gives

(dh/d\ln\eta)|_{\eta=mt}=mt\,h^{\prime}(mt)
and pulls the factor
m
outside the
integral. The integrand of the second form is continuous on
[0,1]
and
bounded by
\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}/(\beta(m)-1)
, so
\mathcal{J}(m)
is well-defined
wherever
\beta(m)
is.

Theorem 7

(Properties of the implicit ratio
\beta
)
.

Assume
(D0), (D1), (D2), (D3)
with exponent
\alpha>1
, and set

\Omega\;:=\;\{m\in(0,M):\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)>1\}.

(13)

Then:

(a)

(Domain.)

\Omega
is open in
(0,M)
. For each

m\in\Omega
, equation
F(C,m)=0
has a unique solution
C(m)\in(m,\infty)
. For

m\in(0,M)\setminus\Omega
, no solution exists.

(b)

(Smoothness.)

\beta(m):=C(m)/m\in C^{1}(\Omega)
, with
\beta(m)>1
on
\Omega
.

(c)

(Sign formula.)
On
\Omega
,

\beta^{\prime}(m)\;=\;\frac{\mathcal{J}(m)}{-m^{2}\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi(\beta(m),m)},\qquad\mathrm{sign}\bigl(\beta^{\prime}(m)\bigr)\;=\;\mathrm{sign}\bigl(\mathcal{J}(m)\bigr),

(14)

with
\partial_{\beta}\Phi(\beta(m),m)<0
so the denominator
of (
14
) is strictly positive.

(d)

(Monotonicity transfer.)
If
h
is non-decreasing and not
constant on
(0,m)
, then
\beta^{\prime}(m)>0
. If
h
is non-increasing and not
constant on
(0,m)
, then
\beta^{\prime}(m)<0
.

(e)

(Critical point under unimodality.)
Suppose
Assumption
6
holds and:

(H1)

there exists
m^{\sharp}\in(\eta^{*}_{h},M)
with
\mathcal{J}(m^{\sharp})<0
;

(H2)

[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]\subset\Omega
.

Then there exists
m^{\star}\in(\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp})
with
\mathcal{J}(m^{\star})=0
,
equivalently
\beta^{\prime}(m^{\star})=0
.

Beyond Theorem
7
we conjecture a uniqueness statement
under log-concavity of
h
; see Conjecture
10
and
Section
5
.

Remark 2

(Reading the sign formula)
.

The integrand of
\mathcal{J}(m)
in either form of (
12
)
is continuous on
[0,1]
: the denominator
\beta(m)-t\geq\beta(m)-1>0

is bounded away from zero on
\Omega
and
h^{\prime}
is bounded by (D1).
The prefactor
1/|\partial_{\beta}\Phi(\beta(m),m)|

in (
14
) becomes large as
m\to\partial\Omega
(where

\beta(m)\downarrow 1
), so the implicit-function setup degenerates at
the boundary of the domain.

The per-point monotonicity in part (d) lifts to a sub-interval statement:

Corollary 8

(Sub-interval monotonicity)
.

If
h
is non-decreasing and not constant on
(0,m)
for every
m
in a
sub-interval
J\subset\Omega
(in particular, if
h
is non-decreasing and
not constant on
(0,\sup J)
), then
\beta
is strictly increasing on
J
.
Symmetrically, if
h
is non-increasing and not constant on
(0,m)
for
every
m\in J
, then
\beta
is strictly decreasing on
J
.

Proposition 9

(Behavior of
\beta
at
\partial\Omega
)
.

If
m_{n}\in\Omega
with
m_{n}\to m_{\infty}\in\partial\Omega\cap[0,M]
and

\mathcal{I}^{+}(m_{\infty})=1
, then
\beta(m_{n})\to 1
.

Proof.

Passing to a subsequence, either
\beta(m_{n})\to\beta_{\infty}\in[1,\infty)

or
\beta(m_{n})\to\infty
.

Case
\beta(m_{n})\to\infty
.
Equivalently
C_{n}=\beta(m_{n})m_{n}\to\infty
.
Fix
C^{*}\geq 2M
large enough that
F(C^{*},m_{\infty})<0
(possible by
Lemma
3
(ii)). By continuity of
F
in
m
,

F(C^{*},m_{n})\to F(C^{*},m_{\infty})<0
. Since
C_{n}\to\infty
, eventually

C_{n}>C^{*}
, and then
F(C_{n},m_{n})\leq F(C^{*},m_{n})<0
by monotonicity of

F
in
C
(Lemma

(ii)), contradicting
F(C_{n},m_{n})=0
.

Case
\beta_{\infty}>1
.
Then

F(\beta_{\infty}m_{\infty},m_{\infty})<\sup_{C>m_{\infty}}F(C,m_{\infty})=\mathcal{I}^{+}(m_{\infty})-1=0
,
again contradicting
F(C(m_{n}),m_{n})=0\to F(\beta_{\infty}m_{\infty},m_{\infty})
.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
7

The proof draws on Lemmas
1
–
5
.
Part (a) is the implicit existence statement: for each
m\in\Omega
,

F(\cdot,m)
is strictly decreasing on
(m,\infty)

(Lemma
2
) and crosses zero between its

+\infty
-side limit
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)-1>0

(Lemma
3
(i)) and its limit
-1
at infinity
(Lemma
3
(ii)), so
C(m)
exists and is unique.
Smoothness in (b) is then the implicit-function theorem. The sign
formula (c) comes from differentiating

G(\beta(m),m)\equiv 0
in the reparametrisation (
10
)
of Lemma
4
. Parts (d) and (e) follow from (c).

4.1
Proof of (a): domain

Openness of
\Omega
is Lemma
5
.

For
m\in\Omega
: Lemma
2
(ii) gives
F(\cdot,m)
strictly
decreasing on
(m,\infty)
. Lemma
3
(i) gives

\lim_{C\downarrow m}F=\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)-1>0
. Lemma
3
(ii)
gives
F\to-1<0
. By continuity and strict monotonicity, a unique

satisfies
F(C(m),m)=0
.

For
m\in(0,M)\setminus\Omega
:
\sup_{C>m}F(C,m)=\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)-1\leq 0
.
Since

is strictly decreasing in

(Lemma

(ii)),
the supremum equals the limit as

F(C,m)<\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)-1\leq 0
for all

, and the equation

has no solution.

4.2
Proof of (b): smoothness and bound

Fix
m_{0}\in\Omega
. Since
C(m_{0})>m_{0}
by part (a), the point
(C(m_{0}),m_{0})

lies in the open domain
\{(C,m):C>m,\,0<m<M\}
where
F\in C^{1}

(Lemma
2
). Since
\Omega
is open (part (a)), there is an open
neighborhood
V\subset\Omega
of
m_{0}
. By Lemma
2
,

F\in C^{1}
near
(C(m_{0}),m_{0})
, and
\partial_{C}F<0
there. The implicit
function theorem
(
Krantz and Parks 2002
, see e.g.)
produces a
C^{1}

function
\widetilde{C}
on some open

V_{0}\subset V
containing

, with
F(\widetilde{C}(m),m)=0
and

\widetilde{C}(m_{0})=C(m_{0})
. Uniqueness of the solution at each

(part (a)) forces

on

. Since
m_{0}
is arbitrary,

, hence
\beta=C/m\in C^{1}(\Omega)
.

Bound
\beta(m)>1
:

by part (a).

4.3
Proof of (c): sign formula

We work with the reparametrized form (Lemma
4
): on
\Omega
,

G(\beta(m),m)=0
where
G(\beta,m):=m\Phi(\beta,m)-1
.

Step 1: Setup.
The proof of (c) uses the
C^{1}
regularity of
\beta
established in (b); the chain rule below applies. Fix
m_{0}\in\Omega
. By openness (
\Omega
open) and part
(b) (
\beta
continuous), there is a compact neighborhood
K\subset\Omega
of

m_{0}
on which
\beta_{K}:=\min_{m\in K}\beta(m)>1
is attained. Pick

\beta_{-}\in(1,\beta_{K})
and
\beta_{+}>\max_{m\in K}\beta(m)
.

Step 2: Partial derivatives of
G
.

On the rectangle
[\beta_{-},\beta_{+}]\times K
the denominator

\beta-t
is bounded below by
\beta_{-}-1>0
, and
\rho
and
h^{\prime}
are
bounded by (D1). The pointwise
\beta
- and
m
-derivatives of the
integrand of
G+1
are therefore dominated by constants
(
\|\rho\|_{\infty}/(\beta_{-}-1)^{2}
and
\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}/(\beta_{-}-1)

respectively), so differentiation under the integral sign applies
and gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\Phi(\beta,m)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t\,\rho(mt)}{(\beta-t)^{2}}\,dt\;<\;0,

(15)

\displaystyle\partial_{m}G(\beta,m)

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t\,h^{\prime}(mt)}{\beta-t}\,dt,

(16)

both continuous in
(\beta,m)
by dominated convergence. The strict

\partial_{\beta}\Phi<0
uses (D3). Hence

on

[\beta_{-},\beta_{+}]\times K

\partial_{\beta}G=m\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi<0
.

Step 3: Identify
\partial_{m}G
with
\mathcal{J}(m)/m
.

The second form of (

) gives directly

\partial_{m}G(\beta(m),m)\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t\,h^{\prime}(mt)}{\beta(m)-t}\,dt\;=\;\frac{\mathcal{J}(m)}{m}.

(17)

Step 4: Chain rule.
Differentiating
G(\beta(m),m)\equiv 0

at
(\beta(m),m)
gives

0=\partial_{\beta}G\cdot\beta^{\prime}(m)+\partial_{m}G
, so

\beta^{\prime}(m)\;=\;-\frac{\mathcal{J}(m)/m}{m\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi(\beta(m),m)}\;=\;\frac{\mathcal{J}(m)}{-m^{2}\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi(\beta(m),m)}.

(18)

The prefactor is strictly positive on
\Omega
since
\partial_{\beta}\Phi<0
,
so
\mathrm{sign}(\beta^{\prime}(m))=\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{J}(m))
.

4.4
Proof of (d): monotone case

Suppose
h
is non-decreasing and not constant on
(0,m)
. By (D1),
h\in C^{1}
,
so
h^{\prime}\geq 0
everywhere on
(0,m)
(a
C^{1}
non-decreasing function has
non-negative derivative pointwise).

If
h^{\prime}\equiv 0
on
(0,m)
, then
h
is constant, contradicting the
non-constancy assumption. Since
h
is non-constant on
(0,m)
, there exist

a<b
in
(0,m)
with
h(a)<h(b)
; by the mean value theorem, there is

\eta_{1}\in(a,b)
with
h^{\prime}(\eta_{1})=(h(b)-h(a))/(b-a)>0
. By continuity of

h^{\prime}
(which is in
C^{0}
since
h\in C^{1}
), there is an open interval

I\subset(0,m)
containing
\eta_{1}
on which
h^{\prime}\geq\delta>0
for some

\delta
. The set
T:=\{t\in(0,1):mt\in I\}
is an open
subinterval of
(0,1)
of positive Lebesgue measure.

In
\mathcal{J}(m)=m\int_{0}^{1}t\,h^{\prime}(mt)/(\beta(m)-t)dt
(second form of (
12
)):

•

the prefactor
m>0
;

•

the weight
t/(\beta(m)-t)>0
for
t\in(0,1]
(since
\beta(m)>1\geq t
);

•

h^{\prime}(mt)\geq 0
everywhere;

•

h^{\prime}(mt)\geq\delta
on
T
.

Hence the integrand is
\geq 0
a.e. and strictly positive on the
positive-measure set
T
, giving
\mathcal{J}(m)>0
. By part (c),

\beta^{\prime}(m)>0
. The non-increasing case is symmetric.

4.5
Proof of (e): conditional critical point

Step 1:
\mathcal{J}(\eta^{*}_{h})>0
.
By (H2), the closed interval

[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]\subset\Omega
; in particular
\eta^{*}_{h}\in\Omega
, so
by part (b)
\beta(\eta^{*}_{h})>1
is well-defined and
\mathcal{J}(\eta^{*}_{h})

is well-defined. By Assumption
6
,
h
is strictly increasing on

(0,\eta^{*}_{h})
, hence non-decreasing and not constant on
(0,\eta^{*}_{h})
.
The hypothesis of part (d) (with
m=\eta^{*}_{h}
) is thus satisfied, and
applying (d) at
m=\eta^{*}_{h}
yields
\beta^{\prime}(\eta^{*}_{h})>0
, equivalently (by
(c))
\mathcal{J}(\eta^{*}_{h})>0
.

Step 2: Sign change.
(H1) gives
\mathcal{J}(m^{\sharp})<0
.

Step 3: Continuity of
\mathcal{J}
on
[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]
.

By (H2) the compact interval
[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]
lies in
\Omega
,
and by (b)
\beta
is continuous there, so

\beta_{-}:=\min_{[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]}\beta(m)>1
. On this interval
the integrand of
\mathcal{J}(m)=m\int_{0}^{1}t\,h^{\prime}(mt)/(\beta(m)-t)\,dt

is uniformly bounded by
m^{\sharp}\,\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}/(\beta_{-}{-}1)
.
Dominated convergence and continuity of the prefactor
m
then give

\mathcal{J}\in C^{0}([\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}])
.

Step 4: Intermediate value theorem.
Since
\mathcal{J}
is
continuous on
[\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp}]
with
\mathcal{J}(\eta^{*}_{h})>0

and
\mathcal{J}(m^{\sharp})<0
, there exists

m^{\star}\in(\eta^{*}_{h},m^{\sharp})
with
\mathcal{J}(m^{\star})=0
, hence

\beta^{\prime}(m^{\star})=0
by (c).

4.6
A uniqueness conjecture and a bimodal counterexample

Theorem
7
gives no uniqueness statement for the
critical point produced in part (e). We conjecture uniqueness under
strict log-concavity of
h
, and we examine numerically a bimodal
density showing what can happen when this hypothesis fails.

Conjecture 10

(Uniqueness under log-concavity)
.

Assume
(D0)–(D3)
, hypotheses
(H1), (H2)
of
Theorem
7
(e), and that
h(\eta):=\eta\rho(\eta)
is
positive on
(0,M)
and strictly log-concave:

\log h\bigl(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\bigr)\;>\;\lambda\log h(x)+(1-\lambda)\log h(y)\qquad(x\neq y\text{ in }(0,M),\ \lambda\in(0,1)).

(19)

Then the critical point
m^{\star}
produced by
Theorem
7
(e) is the unique critical point of

\beta
on
\Omega
.

Remark 4

(On the hypotheses of Conjecture
10
)
.

The positivity
h>0
on
(0,M)
is stated explicitly because, without
it,
h(\eta)=0
would make
\log h=-\infty
and (
19
)
trivially true. Strict log-concavity together with
h(0)=h(M)=0

already implies unimodality, which is therefore not listed
separately. Hypothesis (H1) is not implied by log-concavity; we
verify it numerically in all seven log-concave test densities of
Table
1
. Finally,
h>0
on
(0,M)
implies
\rho>0

there, so Lemma
1
(ii) gives
\Omega=(0,M)
and the
conjecture is a statement on the full interval.

The seven log-concave rows of Table
1
support the
conjecture: in each case
\beta^{\prime}
has a single sign change on the
tested portion of
\Omega
. The examples span a range of vanishing
orders (
\alpha\in\{3,4,5\}
), polynomial and non-polynomial
densities, and modes throughout
(0,M)
. The list is biased toward
Beta-type densities; a systematic test of non-Beta-type log-concave
densities (for instance, polynomial-times-trigonometric or
polynomial-times-logarithmic) would strengthen the numerical case.

To probe what fails when log-concavity is dropped, we examine the
following bimodal density.

Example 11

(Bimodal
\rho
violating both unimodality and log-concavity)
.

For
\sigma\in(0,0.1]
(we take
\sigma=0.05
) let

\rho(\eta)\;\propto\;\eta\,(1-\eta)^{2}\,\bigl[e^{-(\eta-0.3)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}+e^{-(\eta-0.7)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\bigr],\qquad\eta\in[0,1],

(20)

normalised on
[0,1]
. Each factor in (

) is
smooth, so

and (D1) holds. The factor

gives
\rho(0)=0
; the factor
(1-\eta)^{2}
gives

; (D0) and (D3) are immediate. Setting

; differentiating this symmetry at

We claim

is bimodal at

is then a

is the local minimum
between them. A direct search at the same precision finds exactly

on
(0,1)
,

\eta\;\in\;\{0.30895,\;0.5,\;0.69105\}.

(22)

The same picture holds at
\sigma=0.06
(three sign changes of

\mathcal{J}
at

), while at

the middle root of
\mathcal{J}
falls below our
numerical tolerance and we report three sign changes only for

.

Since

on

(ii) gives

Numerically (row 8 of Table

6
Discussion

6.1
Bianconi–Barabási networks and condensation

In the Bianconi–Barabási model
(
Bianconi and Barabási 2001
)
,

\rho
is the fitness density of nodes,
m
is the maximum fitness,
and
C(m)
is the normalisation in the rate equation governing the
growth of node degrees; the ratio
\beta=C/m
controls the resulting
degree distribution. The threshold
\mathcal{I}^{+}(m)=1
is
conjecturally related to the Bose–Einstein condensation transition
in the deterministic mean-field reduction. The relationship to the
stochastic model is delicate: there,
m
is a random order
statistic, and most fitness densities of empirical interest
(log-normal, exponential) fail (D2). For rigorous probabilistic
treatments of preferential-attachment models with fitness, see

Borgs et al. 2007
and

Dereich and Mörters 2009
. Theorem
7
(a)
characterises when the deterministic mean-field equation admits a
solution; the sign formula (c) describes how the structural ratio
depends qualitatively on the upper fitness limit. A rigorous
reduction of the stochastic model to (
1
) is beyond the
scope of this paper.

6.2
Reinforcement-driven selection

Selection rules of the form
P(i)\propto x_{i}^{\beta}
, with
x_{i}
an
accumulated score, arise in several settings, including the
nonlinear preferential-attachment networks of

Krapivsky and Redner 2001
. When a self-consistency equation of
the form (
1
) emerges as the mean-field reduction in
such a setting, the qualitative behaviour of
\beta
is described by
Theorem
7
.

6.3
The (D2) restriction

The principal limitation of the present analysis is hypothesis (D2),
which excludes the densities most relevant in network applications
(log-normal, exponential, Gamma). A natural approach is to use the
boundary regularisations

\rho^{(R,\alpha)}(\eta)=\rho(\eta)(R-\eta)^{\alpha-1}/Z_{R,\alpha}
,
which satisfy (D2) for finite
R
, and to study the limit

R\to\infty
. Whether the limit

m^{\star}_{\alpha}:=\lim_{R\to\infty}m^{\star}_{R,\alpha}
exists for
log-normal
\rho
is open.
