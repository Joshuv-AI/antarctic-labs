Standardized low-flow ventilation with PEEP maintenance during bronchoscopy in mechanically ventilated ICU patients: a pilot trial

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFiberoptic bronchoscopy in mechanically ventilated ICU patients can markedly increase airway resistance and peak inspiratory pressure (PIP), limit effective tidal volume delivery, and provoke transient hypoxemia, hypercapnia, dynamic hyperinflation, and hemodynamic instability. However, per-procedural ventilator management remains heterogeneous.MethodsThis single-center trial with before-and-after design assessed the feasibility and safety of implementing a standardized low-flow ventilation protocol, and characterized its physiological effects (particularly peak inspiratory pressure), during bronchoscopy in adults intubated and ventilated in volume-assist-control mode.ResultsDuring the observational phase (n = 36), ventilator settings reflected usual practice (increased pressure alarm and reduced PEEP; no routine flow reduction). Following a 1-month training period, the intervention phase (n = 35) implemented an inspiratory flow rate of 20 L/min, unchanged PEEP, and a reduced respiratory rate to achieve an I:E ratio of 1:2. Physiologic and ventilator data were automatically recorded at 1-min intervals. The primary endpoint was peak inspiratory pressure (PIP), a physiological endpoint reflecting the mechanical effect of the intervention. Secondary endpoints included minute ventilation (MV), end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2), oxygenation metrics, ventilator alarms, and hypotension, feasibility and procedural safety. PIP decreased substantially (45 [35, 65] cmH2O vs. 82 [59, 93] cmH2O; p < 0.001), resulting in a reduction in the time spent with pressure alarms (3.4% vs. 24%; p < 0.0001). However, MV (5.2 [4.6, 5.9] L/min vs. 6.4 [4.1, 7.3] L/min; p = 0.2), and EtCO2 (36 [30, 40] mmHg vs. 31 [27, 39] mmHg; p = 0.4) did not differ significantly. PEEP was maintained in the intervention group (8 [6, 10] cmH2O vs. 2 [0, 5] cmH2O; p = 0.001). The protocol was demonstrated short-term procedural safety signals, as average SpO2 (98.9 [93.7, 100.0]% vs. 99.9 [96.8, 100.0], p = 0.082), and hypotension incidence (8.6% vs. 14%; p = 0.7) did not differ significantly.ConclusionsThis protocol implementation study demonstrates that standardized low-flow ventilation with PEEP maintenance is feasible and safe during bronchoscopy in mechanically ventilated patients, with a significant reduction in peak inspiratory pressure as a robust mechanical signal. Alveolar ventilation and hemodynamic tolerance were preserved. Background

Fiberoptic bronchoscopy is an essential diagnostic and therapeutic tool in the intensive care unit (ICU). In intubated patients, it is commonly used to aspirate secretions in cases of atelectasis and for diagnostic sampling, including aspiration and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) [1, 2]. However, introducing a bronchoscope into the ventilator circuit increases inspiratory resistance, as predicted by Poiseuille’s law, and can lead to a reduction in effective tidal volume and transient alterations in gas exchange [3]. Specifically, bronchoscopy elevates peak inspiratory pressures (PIP) in volume-controlled modes, frequently resulting in tidal volume limitation despite increasing the pressure alarm to its maximum setting (typically 100 cmH₂O). Conversely, expiratory flow limitation can induce intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and dynamic hyperinflation, particularly in patients with pre-existing intrinsic PEEP [4, 5]. Furthermore, elevated PIP is associated with adverse outcomes. In a cohort of ICU patients ventilated for acute respiratory failure, early measurements of PIP independently predicted mortality, even after adjusting for disease severity and comorbidities [6–8]. Although transient, high airway pressures induced by bronchoscopy may promote barotrauma and hemodynamic instability associated with dynamic hyperinflation [9, 10]. Several studies have reported significant complications related to bronchoscopy in mechanically ventilated patients in the ICU, including transient hypoxemia, hypercapnia, increased plateau pressure, bronchospasm, bleeding, and hemodynamic instability [11, 12].

To mitigate these harmful effects, various ventilatory adjustments have been proposed, such as transiently increasing FiO2, maintaining or adapting PEEP, and modifying the ventilator mode; however, no clear consensus has emerged on the optimal parameters to use during the procedure [13, 14]. A recent worldwide survey revealed significant heterogeneity in the management of ventilator settings, with volume control being equally used as pressure control, and PEEP was reduced by half the respondents [15]. This heterogeneity underscores the need for comparative studies to define an optimal strategy that strikes a balance between maintaining alveolar ventilation, limiting dynamic hyperinflation, and improving gas exchange during the procedure. To date, only a few trials have been conducted. One study compared volume control vs. pressure control modes [5]. This study reported few per-procedure outcomes and found no significant changes in oxygenation and respiratory mechanics 1 h after bronchoscopy. Another trial in patients with planned bronchoscopy under general anesthesia compared a strategy with zero PEEP and a strategy with 8–10 cmH2O PEEP combined with recruitment maneuvers, which found a significant reduction in post-procedural atelectasis [16]. The VentSetFib trial was not published when this study was completed; however, its protocol also proposed a reduction in inspiratory flow [17].

We hypothesized that reducing inspiratory flow rates while maintaining PEEP would primarily reduce peak inspiratory pressure while preserving alveolar ventilation during bronchoscopy. We designed a prospective before-and-after protocol implementation study to assess the feasibility and safety of this standardized approach, and to characterize its mechanical effects during bronchoscopy in mechanically ventilated ICU patients.

Methods

Design and patients

This was a single-center, prospective, before-and-after study conducted in a mixed-bed intensive care unit at the Lyon Sud University Hospital in Lyon, France, between October 2023 and April 2025. Eligible participants were adults who were intubated, mechanically ventilated in volume control mode, and required bronchoscopy for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. Exclusion criteria were plateau pressures > 30 cm H2O prior to the procedure, untreated pneumothorax, and age < 18 years.

Bronchoscopy procedure

For each bronchoscopy meeting the established criteria, a worksheet (available on the bronchoscopy cart) was completed to record relevant data and document patient enrollment. Most data were recorded via the electronic health record software. All patients were ventilated using volume-assist-control (VAC) mode. Preoxygenation prior to bronchoscope insertion and per-procedure fractional inspired oxygen (FiO2) were determined at the physician’s discretion in both phases. Sedation was optimized before the procedure to ensure patient tolerance and minimize inspiratory effort. Depending on the clinical indication and patient status, a neuromuscular blocking agent bolus could be administered to facilitate patient–ventilator synchrony and optimize thoracopulmonary compliance [15]. Throughout the study period, a single model of single-use fiberoptic bronchoscope (Broncoflex Vortex®, The Surgical Company, France), with an outer diameter of 5.6 mm, was utilized. The bronchoscope was introduced via a sealed connector (with a specific valve) to minimize volume loss and maintain positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) during the procedure. Two ventilator models were employed during the study: Hamilton-C6 (Hamilton, Switzerland) and Maquet Servo-I (Maquet, Germany).

Tracheobronchial suction during bronchoscopy was standardized using a wall suction source set at − 200 mmHg (− 27 kPa).

Observational and intervention phase

During the initial “control” phase, investigators adapted ventilator settings according to standard practice. A preliminary survey indicated that none of the physicians utilized flow reduction. Overall, physicians adjusted the pressure-alarm setting and decreased the PEEP setting, reflecting established global practices. No specific instructions were provided during the first observational phase. Following the conclusion of this period, the new proposed strategy was presented, discussed and adopted by the team. A 1-month training period followed without data collection.

Rationale for these settings: inspiratory flow reduction was implemented to lower peak inspiratory pressure, allowing complete delivery of the set tidal volume despite the elevated airway resistance. The respiratory rate was reduced to keep the I:E ratio at or below 1:2 and thereby limit the risk of expiratory flow limitation and dynamic hyperinflation. PEEP was deliberately maintained rather than reduced, because PEEP reduction—although a common maneuver during bronchoscopy as confirmed in our observational phase—may be futile when other ventilator adjustments effectively limit hyperinflation, and may even be deleterious by promoting derecruitment in hypoxemic patients.

Data collection

A bedside worksheet was used to record the precise time of bronchoscope insertion, patient identity, and check of correct settings. Most of the physiologic data were collected using the EHR (Intellispace Critical Care and Anesthesia®, Philips healthcare, USA) at the rate of one point per minute. Thus, physiologic data were automatically recorded during the procedure: blood pressure, SpO2, heart rate, ETCO2. Simultaneously, ventilator settings and measurements—including tidal volume, respiratory rate, minute ventilation, PEEP, FiO2, peak, and plateau pressures—were exported to the EHRs. Consequently, physiologic and demographic data were collected directly from the EHR and recorded in the case report form.

Baseline values are the values recorded in the EHR during the minute immediately preceding bronchoscope insertion. Unless otherwise specified, the per-procedure values reported in the analyses are the arithmetic mean of the per-minute values recorded between bronchoscope introduction and removal. Minute ventilation was retrieved from the ventilator and corresponds to the expired minute volume.

To report the time spent with ongoing pressure alarm, we used the measured peak pressure and the alarm setting. The investigator recorded an estimation of the alarm time (as a percentage) on the worksheet, along with the procedure indication, BAL volume, and sedation used.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) during bronchoscopy, as a physiological endpoint directly reflecting the mechanical effect of the intervention.

The secondary endpoints included minute ventilation (MV) and end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2), gas exchange assessed by continuous SpO2 recording, hemodynamic tolerance (continuous blood pressure recording, hypotension episodes requiring therapeutic intervention), and pressure-alarm burden. Protocol feasibility and procedural safety were assessed as pre-specified study objectives.

Statistical analyses

Quantitative data are expressed as medians [interquartile range]. Patient data were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for comparisons of medians and the chi-squared test for proportions. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2) and GraphPad Prism® (USA).

As a pilot/feasibility study with the primary aim of demonstrating implementation of the protocol, characterizing a physiological signal on PIP, and informing a subsequent randomized controlled trial, no formal sample-size calculation was performed. A pre-specified target of at least 30 patients per phase was set, in line with published recommendations for pilot trials aiming to estimate variability of the primary outcome and inform a subsequent powered RCT.

Results

Seventy-one patients were enrolled in the study, comprising 36 patients in the observational phase (October 2023–June 2024) and 35 patients in the interventional phase (July 2024–April 2025).

Bronchoscopy procedure

The premedication strategy was similar between the control and intervention groups, with comparable use of sedation boluses (47.2% vs. 37.1%, p = 0.5357) or neuromuscular blocking agents (55.6% vs. 48.6%, p = 0.7). The average procedure duration was similar (11.5 [7.0, 14.0] min for the control group and 10.0 [6.0, 15.0] min for the intervention group, p = 0.5).

The detailed respiratory variables at baseline and during bronchoscopy are summarized in Table 2. As expected, we observed a significant reduction in PIP (82 [59, 93] cmH2O vs. 45 [35, 65] cmH2O in the control and intervention groups, respectively; p < 0.001). Baseline SpO2 was significantly lower in the intervention group, although the SpO2/FiO2 ratio was comparable (Table 2). The average SpO2 during bronchoscopy was not significantly lower during the intervention phase (98.9 [93.7, 100]% vs. 99.9 [96.8, 100.0]%, p = 0.082).

Minute ventilation was numerically lower in the intervention phase compared to the control phase, although the difference in the mean per-procedure minute ventilation was not significant (5.20 [4.60, 5.90] vs. 6.35 [4.10, 7.30] L/min, respectively, p = 0.2). Difference in end-tidal carbon dioxide was not significant either (36 [30, 40] vs. 31 [27, 39] mmHg, p = 0.2).

Alarms and hemodynamic tolerance

Pressure-alarm time was estimated by calculating the minutes with PIP ≥ 100 cmH ₂ O over the total procedure minutes. Pressure-alarm time was significantly lower in the intervention group (3.4%) compared to the control group (24%) (p < 0.0001). However, the percentage of time spent with any ventilator alarm activated (as reported by the operator) did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.4), likely due to minute ventilation alarms, which were not modified in the protocol.

Reported episodes of hypotension occurred in 14% of patients in the control group compared to 8.6% in the intervention group (p = 0.7; Table 3). The hypotension index, calculated as (baseline MAP) minus (minimal MAP), was similar between the two groups (4 [0, 17] mmHg vs. 2 [-1, 13] mmHg with the intervention, p = 0.6). Multivariable analysis

In a multivariable linear regression analysis adjusting the per-procedure PIP for baseline PIP, BMI and COPD status, the between-phase reduction in PIP remained large and statistically significant (adjusted β = − 27.5 cmH₂O, 95% CI [− 38.4 to − 16.6], p < 0.001), with a magnitude similar to the unadjusted estimate (β = − 24.8 cmH₂O). The corresponding multivariable analysis on per-procedure minute ventilation confirmed the absence of a statistically significant between-phase difference after adjustment (adjusted β = − 0.42 L/min, 95% CI [− 1.23 to + 0.39], p = 0.31). Discussion

In this study, we implemented a standardized ventilatory protocol during bronchoscopy in critically ill patients, utilizing a fixed inspiratory flow of 20 L/min and unchanged PEEP. As a pilot study, our primary aim was to establish feasibility, confirm procedural safety, and detect a mechanical signal, rather than to demonstrate superiority on alveolar ventilation endpoints. The protocol proved feasible, demonstrated short-term procedural safety signals, and produced a large and significant reduction in peak inspiratory pressure (PIP).

PIP was selected as the primary endpoint because of its direct procedural relevance: high PIP during bronchoscopy triggers ventilator alarms and limits the delivered tidal volume in volume-controlled ventilation. However, PIP primarily reflects airway resistance under flow conditions, and it does not directly reflect alveolar pressure or lung stress, particularly in volume-controlled ventilation with ongoing inspiratory flow. Thus, the clinical implications of PIP reduction remain uncertain. Plateau pressure, which would more closely reflect alveolar pressure, was not feasible to measure repeatedly during the procedure in this pragmatic design; however, plateau pressures recorded before-and-after bronchoscopy were unchanged in both groups (Table 2), arguing against substantial dynamic hyperinflation under either strategy.

The decrease in peak airway pressure led to a substantial reduction in the duration of pressure alarms; however, this did not translate into a significant reduction in alarms perceived by operators and reported subsequently, although this result is based partly on investigator estimation and may be subject to imprecision. This likely reflects the continued activation of the minute ventilation alarm, as the protocol did not alter its threshold and was probably triggered during procedures in the intervention phase.

As expected, the protocolized reduction in respiratory rate resulted in decreased minute ventilation. Our initial hypothesis was that reducing minute ventilation (to avoid dynamic hyperinflation) would still permit improved alveolar ventilation compared to unchanged flow, which can result in incomplete volume delivery due to pressure alarms. This study demonstrated that both methods resulted in decreased minute ventilation; however, there was no significant difference between the groups during the procedure. Interestingly, EtCO2 was not significantly increased throughout the procedure. This may be attributable to frequent suctioning during bronchoscopy, which could have produced artifactual drops in EtCO2. However, PaCO₂ was not measured during bronchoscopy and EtCO₂ may be unreliable in this setting because of suction-related artifacts. Therefore, conclusions on alveolar ventilation are, therefore, necessarily limited and true CO₂ retention cannot be excluded in the absence of PaCO₂ measurements. Nevertheless, there was no signal of clinically relevant hypercapnia, gas exchange and hemodynamic tolerance were preserved, suggesting an absence of harm during the procedure.

A relatively high PEEP level was maintained during the intervention phase (8 [6, 10] cmH₂O) compared to that during the control phase (2 [0, 5] cmH₂O; p < 0.001), which did not prevent the reduction in peak pressure. It is possible that this elevated PEEP aided in preventing derecruitment, a phenomenon frequently associated with bronchoscopy [10].

This effect may improve gas exchange during bronchoscopy. However, oxygenation did not appear to improve during the intervention phase. The average SpO2 during the procedure was even lower during this phase. Nevertheless, this finding should be tempered by the fact that baseline SpO2 was already lower during this period. To account for this confounding factor, we examined the average SpO2/FiO2 ratio, which did not significantly change between phases. We also utilized a desaturation index, which was similarly not significantly different between the two procedures. Furthermore, the difference in oxygenation may be obscured by another confounding factor: the intervention group had a significantly higher BMI. Considering that BMI is associated with reduced functional residual capacity and an increased risk of derecruitment during apneic or reduced ventilation [18], we believe that a positive effect of this higher PEEP on oxygenation cannot be ruled out.

Despite increased PEEP, the intervention group demonstrated good hemodynamic tolerance. Hypotension was rarely reported, with similar incidence rates observed in both groups. Furthermore, the hypotension index showed no significant difference between the groups.

The risk of increased dynamic hyperinflation was assessed by measuring plateau pressure at the end of the procedure, which was low in both groups and remained unchanged when comparing baseline and post-procedure measurements (Table 2).

The results of the VentSetFib randomized trial were published after the end of the present study in early 2026 [19]. In this randomized trial in 46 patients, the intervention was also a reduction in flow (≤ 25 L/min), but PEEP was set to 5 cmH2O, RR was set at a fixed value of 16 breaths/min, and tidal volume was reduced at 5 mL/kg PBW. The authors used a composite ventilatory endpoint with either a reduction of MV < 50% of baseline or failure to deliver tidal volume because of the pressure-alarm limit, which was reached in 96% of the control group vs. 4% of the intervention group. Similar to this study, they demonstrated the expected benefit of reducing flow in reducing PIP. However, we may have contrasting results regarding alveolar ventilation. The reduction in inspiratory flow and RR, and even tidal volume in the VentSetFib trial, leads to decreased MV. Nevertheless, the authors of the VentSetFib trial reported improved MV in the intervention group, although it did not translate into a significant difference in PaCO2 during bronchoscopy. In the present study, we failed to measure a significant difference in MV during bronchoscopy; the MV improvement due to flow reduction was likely canceled out by the “baseline” MV reduction due to RR reduction. Our protocol for RR reduction was more stringent, with a 20% decrease (23 to 18 bpm) vs. 11% for the VentSetFib trial (18 to 16 bpm), resulting in a greater decrease in set MV. This suggests that RR can be relatively preserved without resulting in an increased I:E ratio [19].

Like the VentSetFib trial, this study features a bundled intervention combining several concurrent ventilator modifications, and the observed effects can be attributed to different components: inspiratory flow reduction is mechanistically the principal driver of the observed PIP reduction; reduction of the respiratory rate mainly mitigates the risk of dynamic hyperinflation but mechanically lowers the set minute ventilation; maintenance of PEEP plausibly contributes to limiting alveolar derecruitment during the procedure without measurable hemodynamic cost in our cohort.

This study had several limitations. First, the absence of randomization resulted in an imbalance at baseline, including a higher body mass index, increased rates of diabetes and hypertension in the intervention group, and a greater prevalence of pneumonia; conversely, COPD exacerbations were more frequent in the control group. This discrepancy in admission diagnoses may be attributable to seasonal effects and may have biased the observed mechanical and ventilatory effects. The adjusted differences in PIP and MV were unchanged, but given the limited sample size, this multivariable model was exploratory, and our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a causal effect of the intervention. The difference in ICU mortality is also more likely to be a reflection of the baseline severity imbalance rather than of a deleterious effect of the intervention.

Generalizability is limited by the single-center design, the use of a single bronchoscope diameter (which may influence airway resistance and reproducibility), and exclusive use of volume-controlled ventilation. Furthermore, the protocol is not directly transposable to pressure-controlled modes, although the principle of PEEP maintenance during bronchoscopy is likely transposable. The applicability of the protocol across the full range of ARDS severity grades also remains to be confirmed.

Finally, as a single-center study, these results require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Conclusions

This pilot study demonstrates that a standardized low-flow ventilation strategy with PEEP maintenance during bronchoscopy in mechanically ventilated ICU patients is feasible and safe, with a robust mechanical signal of significantly reduced peak inspiratory pressure. No increase in risk of hypercapnia, hypoxia, or hemodynamic instability was observed. These results provide a sound rationale for a powered randomized controlled trial to evaluate the clinical impact of this strategy on patient-centered outcomes.
