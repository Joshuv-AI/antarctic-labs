Cost-Matching Model Predictive Control for Efficient Reinforcement Learning in Humanoid Locomotion
Wenqi Cai, Kyriakos G. Vamvoudakis, Sébastien Gros, Anthony Tzes

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a cost-matching approach for optimal humanoid locomotion within a Model Predictive Control (MPC)-based Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework. A parameterized MPC formulation with centroidal dynamics is trained to approximate the action-value function obtained from high-fidelity closed-loop data. Specifically, the MPC cost-to-go is evaluated along recorded state–action trajectories, and the parameters are updated to minimize the discrepancy between MPC-predicted values and measured returns. This formulation enables efficient gradient-based learning while avoiding the computational burden of repeatedly solving the MPC problem during training. The proposed method is validated in simulation using a commercial humanoid platform. Results demonstrate improved locomotion performance and robustness to model mismatch and external disturbances compared with manually tuned baselines.



I
Introduction

Humanoid robotics has advanced rapidly in recent years. Humanoid locomotion poses particular challenges due to intermittent contacts, high degrees of freedom, and strict safety constraints, while also requiring robustness to disturbances and modeling errors
[
15
]
.

Model Predictive Control (MPC) has emerged as a dominant paradigm for humanoid locomotion because it provides a systematic mechanism to encode task objectives and enforce constraints through online optimization. To satisfy real-time requirements, many MPC pipelines rely on simplified or reduced-order models that enable lightweight computation and high update rates
[
7
]
. However, such simplified models may neglect important effects present during high-fidelity execution (e.g., contact realization, inertial coupling, and actuator or tracking-stack abstractions). Consequently, robust performance often relies heavily on expert tuning of cost weights and terminal penalties
[
14
]
. Alternatively, richer formulations that tighten kinematic consistency or incorporate more detailed dynamics
[
9
]
can improve fidelity but typically increase computational complexity and complicate real-time deployment
[
12
]
. In practice, this trade-off is often addressed using predictive–reactive hierarchies, where an MPC planner generates reference trajectories that are executed by a higher-rate tracking module
[
24
]
. While effective for real-time operation, this architectural separation can amplify systematic planning–execution mismatches and make performance sensitive to cost design and tuning, particularly when operating with short horizons
[
5
]
.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated strong capability in learning robust locomotion behaviors directly from data with efficient runtime execution
[
19
]
. However, humanoid locomotion represents a challenging learning regime. Training from scratch is often sample-inefficient and sensitive to reward design and curriculum choices, and successful deployments typically rely on large-scale simulation pipelines with additional mechanisms to mitigate sim-to-real discrepancies (e.g., domain randomization
[
13
]
and system identification
[
16
]
). Furthermore, purely learned policies do not naturally enforce hard safety and contact constraints
[
23
]
. These limitations have motivated control–learning formulations that connect RL with adaptive optimal control concepts and provide explicit convergence or stability analyses under disturbances
[
6
,
10
]
. In this direction, MPC–learning hybrids aim to retain constrained optimization for deployment while leveraging learning to improve performance under model mismatch and uncertainty
[
25
,
21
]
.

In this context, this work builds upon our prior MPC-based Reinforcement Learning (MPC-RL) framework—initiated in
[
11
]
and further developed in subsequent works (e.g.,
[
22
,
2
,
1
]
)—which treats a parameterized MPC as a structured value/action-value function approximator or policy approximator. Within this framework, the predictive model, cost function, and constraints of the MPC are parameterized, and closed-loop behavior is improved by adjusting these parameters using RL signals. This approach allows learning to operate within the MPC structure, thereby preserving constraint handling and interpretability
[
3
]
. However, a key limitation arises in complex, time-critical humanoid locomotion stacks: standard gradient-based MPC-RL methods typically require repeatedly solving the MPC optimization within the learning loop, making training prohibitively expensive when the MPC itself is already operating near real-time computational limits.

Motivated by this challenge, we propose Cost-Matching MPC (CM-MPC), an efficient learning procedure that preserves the MPC-RL perspective while avoiding solve-in-the-loop training. The central idea is to learn the MPC parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
by minimizing the discrepancy between an MPC surrogate cost-to-go
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
and a measured long-horizon return
Q^{\mathrm{meas}}
computed from closed-loop trajectories. Rather than differentiating through repeated MPC solves,
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is evaluated by rolling out the parameterized predictive model along recorded action segments and accumulating parameterized stage costs, together with differentiable penalties for state-constraint violations. This formulation enables efficient gradient-based learning while maintaining deployment as a standard constrained receding-horizon MPC controller.

Contributions.
The contributions of this work are twofold. First, we introduce a cost-matching learning framework that extends our prior MPC-RL formulation while substantially reducing the computational burden during training by eliminating the need for repeated MPC solves in the learning loop. The proposed framework is generic and can be applied to systems formulated as Optimal Control Problems (OCPs). Second, we demonstrate the approach on humanoid locomotion by developing a constrained OCP formulation based on centroidal dynamics and validating the learning procedure in high-fidelity simulation. An open-source implementation of the framework is also provided at
https://github.com/RISC-NYUAD/humanoid_mpc_cost_matching
.

II-A

Centroidal Dynamics

A complete
centroidal dynamics
model is adopted in this work, building on the OCS2 centroidal formulation
[
8
,
20
]
. Let
\mathcal{I}
denote the inertial (world) frame and
\mathcal{B}
the base frame attached to the robot trunk. The generalized coordinates of the robot are defined as
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}=[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{b}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{b}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}_{j}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{6+n_{j}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the base position in
\mathcal{I}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents the base orientation (ZYX Euler angles), and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
denotes the joint angles.

The state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{12+n_{j}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{ang}}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
concatenates the centroidal momentum
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}
and the configuration
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{lin}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{ang}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the linear and angular momenta expressed in
\mathcal{I}
. The control input
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{12+n_{j}}
consists of the contact wrenches for the left (
L
) and right (
R
) feet together with the joint velocities

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{c,L}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{c,R}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{c,i}=[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{c,i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{c,i}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
represents the contact force and moment exerted at the
i
-th foot (
i\in\{L,R\}
), and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
denotes the commanded joint velocity.

The system dynamics
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})
is

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{L,R\}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{c,i}+M\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}},

(1a)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}}_{\mathrm{ang}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{L,R\}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{c,i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\times\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{c,i}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{c,i},

(1b)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{b}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}_{b}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(1c)

where
M
denotes the total mass and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}
is the gravitational acceleration. The vector
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{c,i}
denotes the moment arm from the Center of Mass (CoM) to the contact point of foot
i
, expressed in the inertial frame
\mathcal{I}
. The matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})
maps generalized velocities to the configuration derivative
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}}
. These transformation-related quantities can be efficiently computed using rigid-body algorithms provided by the Pinocchio library
[
4
]
. The base velocity variables
(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{b},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}_{b})
are determined by the centroidal momentum
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}
and the joint velocities
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}
through

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{b}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}_{b}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{b}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}_{b}\end{bmatrix}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})
is the Centroidal Momentum Matrix (CMM)
[
18
]
, which captures the inertial coupling between base and limb motions. The matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{b}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{j}
denote the corresponding block partitions of the CMM. The base velocity can therefore be obtained as

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{b}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\omega}}_{b}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{j}\right)
, ensuring that the kinematic evolution remains consistent with the centroidal momentum dynamics.

II-B

Constraints

The feasible state–input set is defined through equality and inequality constraints. To distinguish phase-dependent constraints, let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{st}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sw}}
denote the sets of stance and swing phases, respectively.

II-B
1
Inequality Constraints

•

Joint Limits. The joint configurations are restricted by mechanical limits:
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}_{j}^{\min}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}_{j}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}_{j}^{\max}.

•

Foot Collision. To prevent self-collision, the minimum distance between the left and right feet, computed using Signed Distance Fields (SDF)
\Phi_{\text{sdf}}
, must maintain a safety margin
d_{\mathrm{safe}}
:
\Phi_{\text{sdf}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\geq d_{\mathrm{safe}}.

•

Friction Cone. To avoid foot slippage, the contact force for any active foot must lie within the friction cone:
\sqrt{\left(f_{c,i}^{x}\right)^{2}+\left(f_{c,i}^{y}\right)^{2}}\leq\mu f_{c,i}^{z},\,\forall i\in\mathcal{P}_{st}.

•

Center of Pressure (CoP). To maintain contact stability with foot dimensions
(d_{x},d_{y})
, the CoP must remain within the support region:
|m_{c,i}^{x}|\leq d_{y}f_{c,i}^{z},\,|m_{c,i}^{y}|\leq d_{x}f_{c,i}^{z},\,\forall i\in\mathcal{P}_{st}.

II-B
2
Equality Constraints

•

Zero Velocity. During stance, the foot maintains rigid ground contact and the linear velocity of the foot contact frame is constrained to zero:
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{c,i}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{c,i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},\,\forall i\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{st}},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{c,i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})
denotes the contact Jacobian.

•

Zero Wrench. Feet in the swing phase are contact-free, and their contact wrench must vanish:
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{c,i}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},\,\forall i\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sw}}.

•

Normal Velocity Tracking. The normal (
z
-axis) velocity of the swing foot is constrained to follow a reference profile
v_{\mathrm{ref}}^{z}
to ensure accurate landing:
v_{c,i}^{z}=v_{\mathrm{ref}}^{z}(t),\,\forall i\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sw}}.

II-C

Optimal Control Objective

Humanoid locomotion is formulated as a constrained OCP over a finite prediction horizon
N
. Given the centroidal dynamics and feasibility constraints, the planner seeks an optimal control sequence that minimizes

J_{\mathrm{MPC}}:=T(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}),

(2)

where
L(\cdot)
and
T(\cdot)
denote the stage and terminal costs, respectively. The stage cost is composed of

L=L_{\mathrm{trac}}+L_{\mathrm{base}}+L_{\mathrm{com}}+L_{\mathrm{swin}}+L_{\mathrm{torq}},

(3)

capturing reference tracking, dynamic stability, and physical plausibility. Specifically:

•

We enforce tracking of the reference state trajectory
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}
while regularizing the control effort:

L_{\mathrm{trac}}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Q}}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{R}}}^{2}.

•

A task-space kinematic penalty is imposed on a base-attached frame to regularize upper-body motion:
L_{\mathrm{base}}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{base}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mathrm{base}}}^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{base}}\in\mathbb{R}^{18}
collects the task-space tracking errors. This term mitigates upper-body oscillations and promotes an upright posture during locomotion.

•

To improve balance robustness, the horizontal CoM position is regularized toward the midpoint of the active foot contacts:
L_{\mathrm{com}}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{com}}^{xy}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{mid}}^{xy}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mathrm{com}}}^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{com}}^{xy}
denotes the CoM position projected onto the horizontal plane, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{mid}}^{xy}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{c,L}^{xy}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}_{c,R}^{xy}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})\big)
denotes the midpoint between the left and right foot contacts.

•

To regulate foot clearance and landing orientation, deviations of the swing-foot pose and twist from their references are penalized:
L_{\mathrm{swin}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sw}}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{sw},i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mathrm{sw}}}^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{sw},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{18}
denotes the task-space tracking error of the swing-foot frame relative to its reference trajectory.

•

To discourage contact force distributions that generate large joint torques, the contact wrench is regularized through the induced generalized torques:
L_{\mathrm{torq}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{st}}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{c,i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{c,i}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mathrm{torq}}}^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{J}}_{c,i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}})
denotes the contact Jacobian.

The terminal cost is defined as a quadratic penalty on the terminal state,

T(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})=\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}^{\mathrm{ref}}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Q}}_{f}}^{2}.

(4)

The above OCP provides a structured mechanism for generating feasible locomotion behaviors. Nevertheless, model mismatch and unmodeled effects can lead to suboptimal MPC policies and degraded closed-loop performance. To address this issue, the next section introduces a data-driven adaptation of the MPC parameterization using rollout data collected during execution.

III
Cost-Matching MPC-Based

Reinforcement Learning

We present the cost-matching method using the general Markov decision process (MDP) notation in order to emphasize that the proposed approach applies to any problem that can be formulated as a constrained OCP and for which real-world data is available.

III-A

Parameterized MPC as a Q Function

Consider a discounted infinite-horizon MDP with state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{S}
, action
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\in\mathcal{A}
, stage cost
L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})
, and discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1]
. The objective is to minimize the expected discounted cumulative cost. To obtain a structured and differentiable approximation of the optimal policy and its associated action-value function, we employ a finite-horizon parameterized model predictive control (MPC) scheme as a function approximator. The parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
jointly parameterizes the predictive model and cost functions of the MPC, enabling data-driven adaptation in the presence of model mismatch and uncertainties.

Parameterized MPC scheme

Given a measured state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}
, the horizon-
N
parameterized MPC problem is

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}\;

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}L_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}),

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\quad\forall\,\,i=0,\ldots,N-1

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}),\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},

(5b)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},

(5c)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}^{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}^{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},

(5d)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}.

(5e)

The predictive model
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
, stage cost
L_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
, and terminal cost
T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
are parameterized and adapted through learning.

MPC-induced action-value function

To enable learning without repeatedly solving the optimization problem, we define the MPC-induced action-value along given actions recorded from the real system. For a length-
N
action segment
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0:N-1}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{N-1}\}
, the predicted rollout is

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},~~\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{i}),\quad i=0,\dots,N-1.

(6)

Note that even when the recorded actions are feasible on the real system, the predicted trajectory generated by (
6
) may violate state-related constraints due to model mismatch. Consequently, constraint-violation penalties are incorporated directly into the rollout-based value evaluation. Define the per-stage and terminal violation residuals

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}\big[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{i})\big]_{+}\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{i})\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{N}:=\begin{bmatrix}\big[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})\big]_{+}\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})\end{bmatrix},

(7)

where
[\cdot]_{+}
denotes the element-wise operator
[y]_{+}=\max(y,0)
. Let the weights
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{f}\succ\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}
, and define

\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{i}):=\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}}^{2},\quad\phi_{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{N}):=\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{N}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{f}}^{2}.

(8)

The MPC-induced action-value function is then defined as

\displaystyle Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{\mathrm{MPC}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0:N-1}):=

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})+\phi_{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{N})+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Big(L_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{i})+\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{i})\Big),

(9)

where
\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}_{i}\}
are generated by (
6
) and (
7
). Importantly, (
9
) can be evaluated through forward rollout and cost accumulation, and is differentiable with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
, without requiring the solution of (
5
) during training.

III-B

Action-Value Evaluation via Data Rollouts

Consider a dataset of trajectories collected from the real environment
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k},\ell_{k})\}_{k=0}^{M-1}
, where
(M\!\gg\!N)
denotes the trajectory length and
\ell_{k}=L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})
is the observed stage cost. The trajectories need not be optimal. For each time index
k
, we define the measured discounted return-to-go along the recorded trajectory as

Q^{\mathrm{meas}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}):=\sum_{i=0}^{M-k-1}\gamma^{i}\,L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k+i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k+i}).

(10)

This quantity represents the long-term cost accumulated by the system under the executed actions. In the proposed framework,
Q^{\mathrm{meas}}
serves as the learning target, while
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{\mathrm{MPC}}
provides a finite-horizon, model-based approximation evaluated along the same recorded action segment. The discrepancy between these quantities primarily reflects a systematic mismatch between the real system and the parameterized predictive model.

Given an index
k
, we compute
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
by initializing (
6
) at
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
and rolling out the parameterized model over the recorded action segment
(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k+N-1})
. This estimate is compared with
Q^{\mathrm{meas}}
, which accumulates the discounted costs along the measured trajectory from
k
to
M\!-\!1
.

From an RL perspective,
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
and
Q^{\mathrm{meas}}
represent two evaluations of the action-value at the same anchor state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
for the executed action segment: a finite-horizon model-based surrogate and the measured rollout return. While typically
M\!\gg\!N
, the discount factor
\gamma
in
Q^{\mathrm{meas}}
and the terminal shaping
T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})
in
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}
compensate for the horizon mismatch. Discounting attenuates far-future costs, while the terminal term summarizes the residual cost-to-go beyond the prediction horizon. This observation highlights the importance of terminal cost design in short-horizon MPC and motivates learning its relative weight to align the surrogate with long-term performance.

III-C

Cost-Matching Objective and Gradient

The key idea behind the learning objective is to adjust
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
such that the MPC-induced value
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
evaluated along recorded action segments matches the long-term return
Q^{\mathrm{meas}}
observed on the real system.

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
and the measured returns (
10
), the cost-matching objective is

\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\;\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\big(Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}-Q^{\mathrm{meas}}\big)^{2}\right].

(11)

The expectation in (
11
) is approximated using empirical averages over mini-batches. For a fixed recorded action segment,
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is obtained via the forward rollout (
6
) and accumulation of the cost and penalty terms in (
9
). Therefore,
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
can be computed by differentiating through the rollout recursion and the corresponding cost and penalty evaluations. The loss gradient is

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[2\big(Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}-Q^{\mathrm{meas}}\big)\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\right].

(12)

Since training does not require solving (
5
), gradient evaluation reduces to differentiating a length-
N
rollout and scales linearly with the horizon length
N
. The parameters are updated using a first-order optimization method,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}-\alpha\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}),

(13)

with step size
\alpha>0
. After convergence, the learned parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}^{\star}
define a structured MPC controller deployed online by solving (
5
) in a receding-horizon fashion.

III-D

Convergence of Cost Matching

The cost-matching update (
13
) is a stochastic optimization of (
11
). The following theorem establishes convergence to a first-order stationary point under standard smoothness and bounded-variance assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

For each sample
\xi=(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0:N-1},Q^{\mathrm{meas}})
, define
q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}):=Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}^{\mathrm{MPC}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{0:N-1})
. Assume that
q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})
is continuously differentiable in
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}
, and that there exist constants
B_{Q},G_{Q},L_{Q}>0
such that, for all samples
\xi
and all
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}^{\prime}
,

|q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})-Q^{\mathrm{meas}}|\leq B_{Q},\qquad\|\nabla q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})\|\leq G_{Q},

\|\nabla q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})-\nabla q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}^{\prime})\|\leq L_{Q}\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}^{\prime}\|.

Moreover, the mini-batch gradient estimator
\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})
used in (
13
) is unbiased and has bounded variance, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}]=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}),\,\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})\right\|^{2}\middle|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}.

\square

Theorem 1

(Convergence of cost matching)
.

Suppose that Assumption 1 holds and the loss
\mathcal{L}
in (
11
) has Lipschitz-continuous gradient with constant
L_{\mathcal{L}}=2(G_{Q}^{2}+B_{Q}L_{Q})
. Let the parameter sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j}\}
be generated by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j}-\alpha\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})
,
with a constant step size
0<\alpha\leq 1/L_{\mathcal{L}}
. Then, for any
K\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{\inf})}{\alpha K}+\alpha L_{\mathcal{L}}\sigma^{2},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\inf}:=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})
. In the full-batch case (
\sigma=0
),
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})
is monotonically nonincreasing and
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})\to\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}
as
j\to\infty
.

Proof.

For a single sample
\xi
, the gradient of the squared matching error is

\nabla\big(q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})-Q^{\mathrm{meas}}\big)^{2}=2\big(q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}})-Q^{\mathrm{meas}}\big)\nabla q_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}).

By Assumption 1, this gradient is Lipschitz with constant
2(G_{Q}^{2}+B_{Q}L_{Q})
, hence
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is also Lipschitz with the same constant. Using the standard descent lemma
[
17
]
for smooth objectives, we have

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j+1})\leq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})-\alpha\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})^{\top}\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})+\frac{L_{\mathcal{L}}\alpha^{2}}{2}\|\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})\|^{2}.

Taking the conditional expectation and using the unbiased and bounded-variance assumptions yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j+1})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})]-\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}_{j})\|^{2}]+\frac{L_{\mathcal{L}}\alpha^{2}}{2}\sigma^{2},

where the factor
1/2
follows from
\alpha\leq 1/L_{\mathcal{L}}
. Summing this inequality from
j=0
to
K-1
gives the stated bound. The full-batch case follows by setting
\sigma=0
.
∎

shows that the proposed cost-matching update converges to a first-order stationary set of (
11
) under Assumption
1
, which is compatible with our setting since training is performed over a bounded rollout buffer.

III-E

Constraints and Feasibility

The treatment of constraints differs between the learning and deployment phases. During training, the MPC-induced action-value function (
9
) incorporates state-related constraints through differentiable penalty terms based on the violation measures defined in (
8
). This soft-penalty formulation allows constraint violations to be evaluated along data-driven rollouts without requiring the predicted trajectory to remain feasible, which is essential when the parameterized model does not perfectly capture the true system dynamics.

After training, the learned parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}^{\star}
define a structured MPC controller that is deployed by solving the constrained optimization problem (
5
) in a receding-horizon manner. In this phase, input and state constraints are enforced as hard constraints, thereby ensuring feasibility and safety in closed-loop operation. Because the learning process shapes the MPC model and cost to match the long-term performance of the real system, the resulting controller benefits from both data-driven adaptation and the constraint-handling guarantees inherent to MPC. This separation—soft constraint handling during learning and hard constraint enforcement during online MPC execution—enables efficient gradient-based training while preserving the safety and feasibility guarantees of MPC at runtime.

IV-C

Implications for Locomotion Control

For bipedal locomotion, this instantiation yields several practical benefits.

(i) Mitigating short-horizon bias.

By matching
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}
to long-horizon returns, the learned stage and terminal shaping terms effectively act as a tail-cost approximation, mitigating the short-horizon bias inherent in real-time humanoid MPC.

(ii) Compensating model mismatch.

The learned parameters adapt both the prediction model and the objective to systematic discrepancies between the planning model and high-fidelity execution, including modeling inaccuracies and abstraction effects introduced by the tracking controller.

(iii) Robustness to disturbances.

When trajectories are collected under external disturbances, the measured returns reflect the recovery behaviors of the system. Consequently, the learned MPC objective is implicitly tuned toward policies that improve disturbance rejection under the same constrained MPC structure.

(iv) Policy improvement.

Although the update minimizes a value discrepancy rather than directly maximizing return, improving the fidelity of the MPC internal cost-to-go makes the receding-horizon minimizer of the surrogate objective more consistent with the true long-term performance, thereby yielding improved closed-loop policies.

(v) Training efficiency.

Each update differentiates through a length-
N
rollout without repeatedly solving the MPC optimization problem within the learning loop, resulting in a computational cost that scales linearly with
N
.

VI
Conclusion

This paper presents a cost-matching framework for learning the parameters of a constrained centroidal MPC controller for humanoid locomotion. By matching the MPC surrogate cost-to-go to measured long-horizon returns from closed-loop rollouts, the method avoids repeated MPC solves in the learning loop. Simulations show improved value consistency and enhanced robustness to model mismatch and external disturbances, suggesting that cost matching is a practical and computationally efficient way to shape long-horizon locomotion behavior while retaining standard constrained MPC deployment.
