Adipose tissue plays a crucial role in obesity-induced postoperative neurocognitive disorders
Authors: Clémence Tropis, François Labaste, Marie Sallese, Alizée Dortignac, Ophélie Pereira, Sophie Le Gonidec, Fabrice Ferré, Vincent Pey, Raphaëlle Billerach, Guillaume Bouisset, Lionel Dahan, Philippe Valet, Vincent Minville, Cédric Dray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative neurocognitive disorder (PND) is characterized by cognitive deficits that emerge after surgery. Although often reversible within weeks or months, PND can persist and lead to significant central dysfunction and an increased risk of dementia. Given that obesity is a chronic low-grade inflammatory condition previously associated with structural and functional brain alterations, we hypothesized and demonstrated that it could be a significant risk factor for PND.MethodsWe assessed the incidence of PND and cognitive performance in obese individuals undergoing cardiac or orthopedic surgeries. In parallel, we developed a preclinical model combining diet-induced obesity in mice with surgically induced tibial fracture. Pharmacological (ketoprofen), surgical (adipose tissue ablation), and behavioral (calorie restriction) interventions were used to target obesity-associated inflammation.ResultsCompared with nonobese individuals, obese patients presented a greater incidence of early postoperative cognitive changes and lower cognitive performance (p < 0.009). In mice, the combination of obesity and surgery results in exacerbated signs of PND. Importantly, obesity-related PND in mice was prevented by interventions that reduced central and systemic inflammation.ConclusionsThese findings identify obesity-driven fat mass and inflammation as strong risk factors for PND for the first time. Targeting obesity-associated inflammatory mechanisms may represent an effective strategy to reduce the incidence of this debilitating postoperative condition.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08629-6.

Introduction

Obesity has heavily impacted society worldwide, as it has progressively become a major public health concern. This burden is not only due to an association with metabolic disorders [1, 2] but also because of various comorbidities and complications, such as cardiovascular diseases [3], cancer [4] and brain alterations [5]. In addition to metabolic consequences, obesity has been recognized as a driver of cognitive impairments in both rodents [6] and humans [7, 8] and a reduction in brain volume. Notably, a particularly deleterious impact of obesity has been reported regarding the hippocampus, a key brain structure that supports cognition and memory [9, 10]. Among the underlying mechanisms for these central defects are changes in cellular and endocrine functions observed with excessive adipose tissue development that lead to systemic low-grade inflammation [11, 12], alterations in the blood‒brain barrier (BBB) [13, 14], microglial activation [15] and neuroinflammation [16], which are associated with alterations in cognitive pathways [17, 18].

Surgery is associated with cognitive changes, named perioperative neurocognitive disorders [19]. Whereas surgery-induced postoperative delirium (POD) is a short-term alteration of consciousness that can be rapidly reversed (hours to a few days), more prolonged and persistent postoperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) marked by alterations in learning, memory, attention and executive functions, similar to those observed in dementia, can occur [20, 21]. Prolonged PNDs are poorly characterized and are diagnosed only by comparisons of pre- and postsurgical cognitive performance. However, PND has been closely linked with advanced age [22, 23] and low preoperative cognitive reserve [24–26] and is associated with an unbalanced inflammatory response after surgery [27–29], leading to alterations in the BBB and microglial overactivation [30, 31] and consequently neuronal alterations [32, 33].

Other risk factors, such as diabetes, have been linked to PND development in both humans [34, 35] and rodents [36], indicating the potential role of dysregulated metabolism at PND onset. However, in the context of metabolic impairment, little is known about the synergistic effect of well-described obesity-related inflammation on the development of PND. Taken together, these observations suggest that obesity-related low-grade inflammation could represent a major risk factor for PND, which is potentially mediated by alterations in adipose tissue. Thus, gaining deeper insight into and specifically targeting the role of adipose tissue in obesity-associated PND may represent an innovative and novel preventive and/or therapeutic strategy.

Here, we demonstrated that obesity per se is directly associated with the development of early postoperative cognitive changes in humans and mice, with a potential link to the inflammatory status of adipose tissue. By combining obesity and invasive surgery in mice, we demonstrated that obesity-associated PND is mediated by both an increase in BBB permeability and an imbalance of glial cells, which specifically leads to functional declines associated with neuron-altered activation and transmission of information. Notably, subcutaneous adipose tissue near the site of surgery was crucial for the onset of PND in obese mice. In this context, reversing obesity features via a nutritional approach considerably reduces the incidence of PND in mice, paving the way for a preventive strategy potentially translatable to humans.

Results

Obesity exacerbates surgery-induced cognitive alterations in humans

Previous metadata analysis of the relationship between obesity and PND did not reveal a clear association [37], possibly due to heterogeneity among the inclusion criteria and/or overall study design. In this study, we compared lean and obese individuals with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 who had undergone cardiac (n = 69) or orthopedic surgery (n = 37), excluding all patients with preoperative cognitive alterations (MMSE < 20) (Supplemental Figure S1). Cognitive functions were assessed pre- and postoperatively via cognitive tests and biological sampling (see section “Materials & methods”). While patients with obesity had a greater BMI (33.2 kg/m2 vs. 23.3 kg/m2, p < 0.0001) and presented with obesity-associated metabolic alterations, such as hypertension (p < 0.0001) and diabetes (p < 0.004), age, sex and surgery type were evenly distributed across the groups (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). The main criterion for study was the occurrence of postoperative cognitive changes, such as post-operative delirium (POD) and/or PND. Compared with lean patients, obese patients presented lower cognitive performance (p < 0.009) not attributable to delirium (p < 0.49) but rather only to the percentage of decline in PND (p = 0.0001) (Table 1). Moreover, regarding the occurrence of neurocognitive disorders, obesity but also type 2 diabetes and anti-diabetic drugs, pre-operative anti-depressant, ketamine, intra-operative parameters and delirium appeared to be significantly increased in the patients who developed neurocognitive disorder after surgery (Table 2). Thus, we tested all those parameters in a multivariate model to identify their risk associated with PND. Among all those parameters, only obesity appeared to be a strong risk factor for PND, as determined by multivariate analysis (OR 7.26, 95% CI (2.08–25.33), p = 0.002), together with POD and ketamine, as previously described [38, 39] (Table 3). Obesity promotes surgery-mediated cognitive dysfunction in mice

We next wished to model PND in mice. To do so, we first induced obesity in C57BL6/j male mice by feeding them a high-fat diet (HFD) for 12 weeks, which resulted in all the hallmarks of obesity in terms of excess body weight and adipose tissue mass [40] (Supplemental Figure S2A-H), systemic and adipose tissue inflammation [41] (Supplemental Figure S2I-K), and impaired glucose homeostasis and metabolism (Supplemental Figure S2L-R). We then surgically induced a tibial fracture as described by Minville et al [42]. The day after surgery, mice were subjected to a battery of hippocampus-dependent memory tests, including contextual fear conditioning (CFC), object location (OL), and social memory test (SMT), to evaluate their cognitive performance (Fig. 1A). In the CFC test, mice learn to associate a specific context with an aversive stimulus; memory is assessed by measuring freezing behavior upon re-exposure to the same context. In the OL test, which relies on spatial memory, mice are exposed to two identical objects and, after a delay, one object is displaced, intact memory is reflected by a preference for exploring the relocated object. In the SMT, mice are exposed to a conspecific and memory is evaluated based on their ability to discriminate between familiar and novel individuals, reflecting social recognition memory. We found that, compared with ND mice, surgery impaired performance in these hippocampus-dependent tasks, with more pronounced deficits in obese mice. Specifically, HFD mice exhibited reduced freezing in the CFC test, indicating impaired contextual memory (Fig. 1B), failed to preferentially explore the displaced object in the OL test (Fig. 1C), and showed reduced social discrimination, indicative of impaired learning and recall in the SMT (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, hippocampus-independent memory, assessed using the cued fear conditioning test, did not show any differences between WT and HFD mice after surgery (Supplemental Figure S3A). This highlights the specificity of the surgical impact on hippocampus-dependent memory alterations observed in PND. Moreover, other parameters that could explain the increased incidence of PND in HFD mice after surgery remained unchanged when comparing obese and lean mice. Indeed, pain, measured by withdrawal responses in the von Frey test, increased similarly in both groups 1 hour after surgery and returned to baseline within 1 day (Supplemental Figure S3B). Similarly, locomotor activity, assessed by both total distance traveled and velocity, was affected by the HFD diet but not by surgery (Supplemental Figure S3C–D). In addition, detailed movement analysis using the KWB did not reveal any differences between lean and obese mice after surgery (Supplemental Figure S3E). Obesity promotes surgery-induced cognitive dysfunction associated with alterations in hippocampal plasticity

Direct analysis of the hippocampus revealed that only obese mice presented significant decreases in the neuronal markers NeuN [43] and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (Fig. 2A–C). While the decrease in BDNF expression persisted over time, the change in NeuN expression was transient, with a return to the basal level by 24 hours as shown by the NeuN quantification by histology and flow cytometry (Fig. 2D–F and Supplemental Figure S4A-I). This transient decrease points to impaired neuronal activation and not to neuronal cell death, as already mentioned [44]. Moreover, HFD-fed mice specifically presented lower expression of postsynaptic genes responsible for memory storage and recall [45], including Creb, Arc, Bdnf and Fos, after fracture (Fig. 2G). Finally, tibial fracture also induced a significant decrease in the number of PSA-Ncam-positive cells within the hippocampus, a learning and synapse remodeling marker [46], and associated gene dysfunction, particularly in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 2H–K). Notably, without fracture, HFD-fed mice did not display alterations in any of these cognition markers (Supplemental Figure S4J-M). Obesity is associated with dysregulated inflammatory reaction after surgery

Tibial fracture-induced PND is driven by an unbalanced acute inflammatory process that begins at the injury site and then spreads systemically to the central nervous system [28, 29]. We confirmed the critical role of inflammation in obesity-associated PND development by treating mice perioperatively with the anti-inflammatory agent ketoprofen, similar to the effects observed postoperatively in treated aged rats [47]. Ketoprofen treatment restored cognitive function in obese mice after surgery in the OL test (Supplemental Figure S5A) by improving systemic inflammation (Supplemental Figure S5B), cognitive markers (Supplemental Figure S5G-K) and local inflammation at the site of surgery (Supplemental Figure S5L).

The role of activated glial cells, including microglia and astrocytes, in obesity and PND has been previously reported [15, 30]. Indeed, microglia play a key role in neuroinflammation as the brain’s first line of defense [48], whereas astrocyte activation, known as astrogliosis, is a secondary pathway involved in the well-documented unbalance of central inflammation [49, 50]. In the basal state, obesity promotes microglial overactivation, as evaluated by IBA-1 (ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1) hippocampal protein expression and an associated decrease in GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein), an astrocyte activation marker (Supplemental Figure S6A). Interestingly, 72 h after fracture, this regulation was completely reversed since obese mice presented a significant decrease in IBA-1 and an associated increase in hippocampal GFAP (Fig. 3A–C). The long-term (24 h) surgery-induced increase in GFAP expression, together with the blunted expression of IBA-1, may suggest that astrogliosis is specifically involved in obese mice due to failure of the central immune response. No changes in the number of Iba1-positive or CD45low/CD11b-positive microglia and in the number of GFAP- or ACSA2-positive astrocytes were observed under any condition (Supplemental Figure S6B-H). Thus, we hypothesized that even if obese mice display basal microglial overactivation, after surgery, these cells may not be further activated or may present phenotypic and metabolic changes that impair their response, leading to astrogliosis. The local response at the subcutaneous adipose tissue is pivotal for obesity-associated PND in mice

To validate the causative role of obesity in PND development, we placed obese mice on a calorie restriction (CR) regimen. CR is known to have beneficial effects on weight, adiposity, metabolic parameters and cognition [53, 54]. After 8 weeks of HFD feeding, obese mice were fed a normal diet for 4 weeks. As expected, this induced a significant reduction in whole-body and adipose tissue weights and gene expression (Supplemental Figure S7A-D). Strikingly, calorie restriction restored cognition after surgery, as measured by both the OL and SMT tests (Fig. 4A–C), and maintained NeuN and BDNF expression (Fig. 4D–F and Supplemental Figure S7E-F), together with a lower degree of inflammation at the site of surgery, as evidenced by greater expression of IL-10 (Supplemental Figure S7G). These results highlight the potential translation of our findings to the clinic, as they suggest that modifications of the diet before invasive surgery may be a preventive strategy for obesity-induced PND. However, obesity is associated with multiple changes that could promote the onset of PND. First, we found that obesity-associated metabolic homeostatic defects do not appear to be involved in the observed induction of PND shortly after surgery since no major change in metabolism was observed after 4 weeks of calorie restriction (Supplemental Figure S8A-E). Thus, we hypothesized that obesity might play a role by itself through a change in white adipose tissue, as obesity-induced modifications in adipose tissue are widely known [11]. Interestingly, surgery did not impact whole-body weight or the weights of the visceral or subcutaneous ipsilateral or contralateral adipose tissue depots (Supplemental Figure S8F-I). Discussion

This study provides the first direct evidence across clinical and preclinical models that obesity is an independent risk factor for early postoperative cognitive changes development, with a direct link to the excess of adipose tissue and its localization and inflammation. Our human cohort analysis indicated that a loss of cognitive capacity after surgery occurs primarily in obese patients who have undergone either cardiovascular or orthopedic surgeries, which are two surgeries with a known high risk for PND [55]. This alteration does not seem to be the consequence of postoperative delirium. These results confirm the findings of a recent clinical study that characterized obesity as a risk factor for PND, among other metabolic impairments, such as metabolic syndrome or elevated HDL-C plasma levels [56]. Even if further studies with larger cohort sizes, later cognitive assessments to identify prolonged PND and more specific tests, such as verbal learning tests, color-word tests, letter digit span tests and the MoCA, are needed to confirm this association. The presented results highlight patient BMI as a crucial preoperative consideration regarding the risk of invasive surgery on cognitive capacity in the postoperative period. Moreover, while we identified differences in the adipose tissue inflammatory profile between obese patients and lean patients, no associations with PND were detected (data not shown). This can be explained by the low number of patients and deeper analysis of the adipose tissue sampled and the blood with markers of inflammation and neurodegeneration, such as NfL, which could strengthen our findings. Consequently, a better characterization of the link between obesity and its association with the onset of PND is needed. To address this limitation, we used a diet-induced obesity mouse model together with tibial fracture to demonstrate that obesity represents an important contribution to alterations in hippocampal-associated memory, as evaluated by various cognitive tests. This effect does not appear to be due to the well-described obesity-related hyperalgesia, anxiety [57, 58] and/or fracture-induced locomotor defects. Our results are in accordance with those of a previous study that showed that both aging and obesity are associated with a greater incidence of PND in mice [59]. In addition, we found that PND was characterized by defects in neuronal activity and synaptic plasticity based on decreased expression of NeuN, BDNF and PSA-Ncam in the hippocampus of obese mice with tibial fractures. As lower expression of PSA-Ncam could also be associated with immature neurons, further experiments investigating neurogenesis should be performed via doublecortin (DCX) staining of dividing neurons in the dentate gyrus.

Another aspect of PND that we found to be strongly associated with this condition is inflammation. Among the potential targets involved in an exacerbated inflammatory response in obese mice, we first focused our attention on the brain. Despite basal microglia-primed activation, obese mice do not display stronger microglial reactivity after fracture. Even if we cannot rule out a priming effect of obesity on central structures, this unexpected result may suggest that, as already shown by the Wyss-Coray team [60], cells in a highly inflammatory environment are prone to phenotypic changes that act to maintain the unbalanced inflammation. Further experiments are needed to definitively validate this hypothesis in the context of obesity-associated surgery. On the other hand, the observed increase in the astrocyte response to surgery strongly suggests that unbalanced inflammation occurs due to a lack of, or a maladapted, response by microglia [50]. This hypothesis is reinforced by the increased permeability of the BBB, which has been previously tightly associated with instances of neuroinflammation [61]. As previously reported in other contexts [62], we hypothesized that such central responses may arise due to inflammation in the periphery, as only obese-fractured mice presented an increase in systemic inflammatory cytokines that was prevented by ketoprofen treatment. Additionally, hematomas collected from the site of surgery in obese mice presented a stronger inflammatory profile that may contribute to increased systemic inflammation. Taken together, these data strongly suggest that obesity may trigger an unbalanced systemic and local inflammatory response that results in cognitive dysfunction. Unfortunately, targeting inflammatory processes via global pharmacological strategies such as ketoprofen should not be proposed for all patients because of important side effects (e.g., nephrotoxicity), and those treatments should be monitored closely in terms of dosage and duration to adapt to each patient [63].

The mechanism by which obesity contributes to such inflammatory responses is not clear. First, obesity is currently associated with a defect in metabolic homeostasis, and several studies have demonstrated the deleterious effects of diabetes and metabolic syndrome in human [34, 35] and rodent [36] cohorts subjected to different types of surgery. Even if no modification of the metabolic phenotype has been observed after tibial fracture, we cannot rule out a potential role of obesity-associated chronic metabolic disturbance in the onset of obesity-related PND. However, a preliminary study promoting the onset of nonobese diabetes in mice via a high-fat/high-sugar diet demonstrated that metabolic alterations alone are not sufficient to promote tibial fracture-induced local hyperinflammation (data not shown). Consequently, we focused our attention on a better understanding of the role of white adipose tissue during obesity in PND development, hypothesizing that white adipose tissue could act through cellular transfer, secretion or innervation. Interestingly, we observed that subcutaneous adipose tissue in obese mice, which is specifically localized close to the site of surgery, is involved in an increase in inflammation. Indeed, even though surgery did not impact adipose tissue weight or adipocyte size distribution, immune cells and adipogenic markers were altered in the subcutaneous adipose tissue localized close to the fractured site in both ND- and HFD-fed mice without a change in the other white adipose tissue depots (i.e., the visceral and contralateral subcutaneous adipose tissues). This result, together with the observed hyperinflammation and the large numbers of proinflammatory immune cells in the white adipose tissue of obese mice in the basal state, reinforces the concept that locally altered white adipose tissue could mediate surgery-induced cognitive disorders. We definitively validated this notion by ablating the whole local subcutaneous adipose tissue in obese mice and showing protection from PND. This result paves the way for a new understanding of PND, as it highlights the inflammatory state of the local adipose tissue rather than the systemic profile that contributes to the condition. However, to reinforce the translational potential of our results, our findings here must be generalized to other models of invasive surgery. Indeed, we assume that sternotomy-associated surgery, which is often practiced in patients with obesity for cardiac issues, preferentially impacts visceral adipose tissue. Consequently, evaluating the extent to which the well-known proinflammatory profile of obese visceral adipose tissue may locally contribute to major cognitive decline and/or longer PND is highly important. In line with these findings, in a preliminary study, we observed that laparotomy induced a decrease in BDNF and tight junction protein (Claudin5/Occludin) expression in the brains of HFD-fed mice compared with those of ND-fed controls (data not shown). Further experiments are needed to better understand the role of the differently localized fat pads in terms of the type of surgery. Another limitation of our study resides in the generalizability of the obtained results to both sexes. Although clinical results do not differ between males and females, it will be of great interest to study the consequences of both obesity and surgery in female mouse models to better assess the potential protective effects of estrogens. For example, estradiol supplementation protected old female mice from laparotomy-induced PND [64], whereas in humans, no association was found between estradiol supplementation and improved cognition after cardiac surgery [65]. Another study demonstrated that estrogen supplementation prevents PND in laparotomy-induced cognitive decline in aged male mice [66]. Moreover, while LPS injection induced cognitive decline and microglia activation in aged male rats, this was not observed in aged females or in aged ovariectomized females, demonstrating the sexual dimorphism in the response may exist independently of ovarian hormones [67]. Thus, no clear conclusions can yet be drawn regarding the mechanisms underlying potential sexual dimorphism in PND, and future studies will need to address this limitation. Finally, to envision new preventive strategies for patients with obesity who will undergo invasive surgery, we showed that obese mice under calorie restriction do not present surgery-associated cognitive dysfunction. This result is reinforced by basal cognitive improvement in humans who exhibit a loss of adiposity two years after bariatric surgery [68]. Taken together, these data suggest that achieving significant weight loss prior to surgery may be an effective strategy to prevent obesity-associated PND. However, several important considerations must be taken into account when translating these findings to the clinic. Indeed, precautions are needed regarding the risk of malnutrition in patients with obesity [69]. Patients with obesity who undergo calorie restriction may suffer from malnutrition due to deficiencies in protein micronutrients, which can have a negative impact on their morbidity/mortality. Moreover, drastic weight loss aggravates the impact of malnutrition on patient health, cognitive status, psychology and physical status, increasing the risk of sarcopenia [70]. Thus, the feasibility of preoperative weight loss in obese surgical patients depends on the timing of surgery and the patient’s ability to adhere to caloric restriction. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of preoperative weight loss interventions in gastrointestinal cancer surgery found that intentional preoperative weight loss was feasible, with completion rates ≥ 96.9%, while preserving skeletal muscle mass and nutritional parameters [71]. Longer preoperative waiting periods, combined with structured dietary programs and close monitoring, may further improve the feasibility of weight loss interventions. Moreover, for emergency surgeries, anti-inflammatory strategies, such as preoperative dietary interventions rich in omega-3 fatty acids or targeted anti-inflammatory agents, represent complementary approaches that may also reduce PND risk, although their efficacy and safety in this population remain to be established. Clinical trials investigating the modulation of inflammatory response in bariatric surgery are currently ongoing to evaluate how different strategies affect inflammation after surgery (NCT06915558). Finally, future clinical trials should test whether preoperative weight loss and anti-inflammatory strategies are effective and safe in obese surgical patients. These trials should compare preoperative calorie restriction with or without micronutrient and anti-inflammatory nutritional support, versus standard care. Primary outcomes should include cognitive function at several time points, morbidity and mortality, sarcopenia, and nutritional status. Adding inflammatory biomarkers and brain imaging could help clarify the mechanisms and identify which patients are most likely to benefit.

Materials & methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our human study examines males and females, and similar findings are reported for both sexes, as expected from the literature. Our study examined male mice because male animals exhibit less variability in phenotype, and the available literature demonstrates an increase in PND occurrence specifically in males compared with females.

Human cohort

The Impact of Obesity on Postoperative Cognitive Dysfunction: The Role of Adipose Tissue Study (ODCOTA) was conducted as an observational, prospective, monocentric, longitudinal study in the cardiovascular and orthopedic surgery departments of the Toulouse University Hospital. This work was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and approved by the local research ethics committee (Comité d’éthique pour la recherche du CHU de Toulouse no.NTC04907565) in 2021. An a priori sample size calculation was performed on the basis of previously published data [72, 73], in which the incidence of postoperative delirium in the lean group was 0.25 and 0.50 in the obese group. Using these estimates, a power of 80%, a two-sided alpha risk of 0.05, and an assumed incidence of 0.25 in the lean group and 0.50 in the obese group, we obtained a required sample size of n = 57 per group. To account for potential missing data or incomplete variables in the final analysis, we planned to include additional patients in the study. Patients were enrolled in the study between October 2021 and February 2023 and were followed at 6 months after surgery. Patients with a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 25 kg/m2 were included in the lean group, and patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were assigned to the obese group. All patients were 60 years or older and were undergoing planned surgery. The exclusion criteria included urgent surgeries, patients with a Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 20 or preoperative cognitive disorders.

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of postoperative cognitive changes, which were defined as delirium and/or PND. The presence of delirium was verified daily. Trained research personnel, including nurses, assessed the presence of PND. The occurrence of delirium was evaluated via the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) adapted for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) test, which is used when a patient is admitted to our intensive care unit. The main criteria were as follows: sudden onset, inattention, disorganized thoughts, alterations in the level of consciousness, and associated criteria (disorientation, memory loss, abnormal perception, psychomotor disorders, and sleep–wake disorders). If any of these indicators were present, the patient was scored as delirious. Patients were assessed during the first seven days after surgery. The duration of delirium was calculated as the number of days between the initial positive delirium assessment and the final positive delirium assessment.

The presence of PND was evaluated by assessing changes in cognitive function one day before surgery (baseline) and five days after surgery by medically trained personnel. We choose the MMSE for patients who underwent cardiac surgery and the TICS (Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status) for those who underwent orthopedic surgery as measures of cognitive function because they are commonly used in geriatric patients and have been used to diagnose PND. After surgery, MMSE and TICS evaluations were performed on day 5. Finally, we attempted to identify the risk factors for PND in the population via biological sample analysis. To do so, blood was collected on the day of surgery and the day after surgery. Subcutaneous adipose tissue was also collected at the site of surgery.

Animals

All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal studies and were approved by an ethics committee (US006/CREFRE Oncopole 23-3U1301-CD-05). C57BL6/J WT male mice at 8 weeks of age were obtained from Janvier Laboratory (St-Berthevin, France). The mice were randomly assigned to groups. The mice were housed conventionally at a constant temperature (20–22 °C) and humidity (50–60%) in the animal room, with a 12–12 h light–dark cycle and free access to food and water. The animals were fed standard rodent chow (normal diet from SAFE A04) or a HFD with 60% calories from fat (Research Diet D12492) for 12 weeks. The number of animals used for the behavior test was calculated via G*Power analysis.

Tibial surgery

Unilateral closed tibial fracture was performed under 2% sevoflurane (Abbott, Suresnes France) anesthesia and buprenorphine (Buprecare code 2145464, 100 μg/kg) as an analgesic as previously described. All the mice (lean and obese) were exposed to the same level of drugs on the basis of their weight. Briefly, a closed fracture of the right hind paw was produced via a specially designed fracture apparatus (blunt guillotine). Before fracture, intramedullary pinning was performed under sterile conditions; a hole was made percutaneously above the tibial tuberosity via a 27-gauge needle (BD® Microlance − 27 G 3⁄4 L 19 mm). The needle was then directed into the medullary canal. By rotating the needle, the canal was reamed 5 mm up to the ankle joint. The end of the needle was cut as short as possible so that the skin could roll over and cover it. No sutures were used. The mouse was then placed with the leg on the anvil so that the blunt guillotine lined up with the proximal third of the tibia. The weight of 300 g was dropped from a height of 10 cm to fracture the tibia shaft. Closed fracture was confirmed postmortem by examining whether the nail was intramedullary or not and whether the bone fractured.

Ketoprofen treatment

ND- and HFD-fed mice were injected intraperitoneally with NaCl or 50 mg/kg ketoprofen 1 h prior to tibial fracture and 24 h after fracture.

Adipose tissue ablation

The subcutaneous adipose tissue of obese mice was surgically removed to determine the role of specific depots of adipose tissue in PND development. In one group of mice, the right subcutaneous adipose tissue (abli), which was ipsilateral to the fracture, was ablated, whereas in the other group, the left adipose tissue (ablc), which was contralateral to the fracture, was removed. Briefly, the animals were anesthetized with 2% sevoflurane (Abbott, Suresnes, France). The paw was then shaved to ensure a clean surgical zone. The adipose tissue was ablated, and buprenorphine (100 μg/kg) was used as an analgesic. The mice were subsequently weighed every day for two weeks.

Calorie restriction

C57BL6/J WT mice at 8 weeks of age were obtained from Janvier Laboratory (St-Berthevin, France) and were fed a HFD with 60% calories from fat (Research Diet) for 8 weeks. The mice were then switched to standard ad libitum rodent chow (a normal diet) for an additional 4 weeks to induce calorie restriction.

Oral glucose tolerance test

The mice were fasted overnight before the test. Briefly, glycemia was measured with a glucometer (Glucocheck, Roche, France) before and after oral administration of a 60% glucose solution (3 g of glucose per kg of weight). The results are expressed as areas under the curve.

Plasma metabolic parameters

Blood was collected in heparin-coated tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C, after which the plasma phase was collected and stored at −80 °C until use. Metabolic parameters were measured with an ELISA kit for insulin (Alpco-Eurobio Scientifics 80-INSMU-E10) and colorimetric kits for free glycerol and free fatty acids (Sigma F6428).

Behavioral studies

Behavioral experiments were performed during the first half of the light phase, and the data were scored by two independent scorers.

Fear conditioning test

Fear conditioning allows one to measure hippocampus-dependent memory of the association of a shock to a context (contextual fear conditioning (CFC)) and the memory of the association of the shock to an auditory cue, which does not involve the hippocampus. On the first day, the mice were placed in the conditioned context (Noldus EthoVision) for 150 seconds. Then, a sound cue (75–80 Db, 7 kHz, 30 s) was emitted, ending with an electric shock (0.75 mA, 2 last seconds of the sound cue) delivered by a grid floor. The tone and foot‒shock pairings were repeated with an intertrial interval of 60 s, followed by 30 s of exploration before the mouse was taken back to its home cage. The next day, fear is evaluated by measuring freezing behavior, which is defined as the complete absence of every movement except respiration. It was first measured while the mice were placed in the conditioned context for 4 minutes to assess contextual memory. One hour and a half later, the mice were placed in an alternative context for 2 min, followed by two more minutes with the sound cue to assess tone fear memory. All apparatuses and testing chambers were cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal. The results are expressed as the percentage of freezing in the recall phase. The experimental settings, adapted from Sayegh et al [74] were chosen to allow us to obtain similar and high freezing rates for both the context and the sound cue.

Social memory test

The 7-trial social memory test, adapted from Rey et al. [75], is based on the fact that social interaction with unfamiliar mice decreases when the mouse becomes familiar. The habituation of the exploration of a conspecific can thus be considered a measure of social memory. Each mouse was placed in a large chamber (25 × 35 cm) for a 10-min habituation period, with an empty cylindrical small cage placed in the center (8 cm diameter). A stimulus unfamiliar mouse, also C57BL6/J from the same sex but never encountered before, was subsequently introduced into the small cage for six successive trials of 5 min (interval of 10 min without mice between every trial). In the seventh trial, a novel stimulus unfamiliar with a mouse was introduced to measure social learning. The next day, the mice were placed in the chamber again, with the first stimulus mouse used to recall social memory. The direct interactions between the tested mouse and the stimulus mouse were scored manually by the experimenter. The results are expressed as a percentage of the investigation time compared with the first trial in each group. All apparatuses and testing chambers were cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal.

Object location test

An object location test measures spatial memory. It evaluates the ability of a mouse to discriminate between a novel and a familiar spatial location measure. Each mouse was first placed in an open field with a visual cue (striped pattern) for 10 minutes of habituation without objects. After 1 hour, during acquisition, two identical objects were placed in the middle of the open field. The mice were allowed to explore for 10 min, during which the time spent exploring the two objects was recorded. The next day, the test phase was performed by placing the mouse back in the same open field where one of the two objects had been moved to a novel location. The position (left or right) of the displaced object was chosen according to the preference of the mouse during acquisition pseudo randomly to reduce bias toward a particular position. The mice were allowed to explore the objects for 10 min. The direct interactions were scored manually by the experimenter. The percentage of interaction was calculated as follows: time exploring the displaced object/total time exploring * 100. A preference index above 50% indicates a preference for the displaced object, and 50% indicates no preference (random choice). All apparatuses and testing chambers were cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal.

Automatic analysis of locomotion in the center and periphery of the open field (Noldus EthoVision) during the habituation phase also allowed measurements of locomotion and anxiety.

Von Frey experiments

Mechanical allodynia was assessed on the right hind paws of the mice. Each mouse was placed in the von Frey chamber for 30 minutes of habituation. Three filaments were used: 0.16, 0.6 and 1.2 g. The filament was applied on the plantar zone with enough force to cause the filament to bend and remain in contact for a total of 3 seconds. Each filament was tested 5 times with at least 5 minutes between each stimulation. If no withdrawal response was observed, the next highest filament was tested. The withdrawal response is characterized by triphasic paw retraction or paw licking. Animals were tested before surgery and at 1 h, 6 h, 24 h, 3 days and 7 days post-surgery, with each animal serving as its own control. All apparatuses and testing chambers were cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal.

Kinetic weight bearing

The kinetic weight bearing (KWB) system is based on footprint tracking in spontaneously moving animals. Parameters such as the maximum force (mN), mass velocity (N/s), speed, cadence, and step patterns are processed while the mouse walks in a corridor with sensors. Each mouse was placed in the KWB apparatus 5 successive times before surgery and at 3 days post-surgery, with each mouse acting as its own control. All apparatuses and testing chambers were cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal.

Tissue collection

The animals were euthanized via intracardiac PBS perfusion under general anesthesia with 2% sevoflurane (Abbott, Suresnes, France) and exsanguinated. Biological samples of blood, brain or hippocampus; surgical hematoma; and white adipose tissue (visceral and subcutaneous) were collected for analysis.

Blood‒brain barrier permeability analysis

BBB disruption was investigated via Evans blue dye (EB; Sigma‒Aldrich, E2129) extravasation methods. EB (2%) was prepared in PBS and filtered to remove particles. The animals were anesthetized during all the procedures. Briefly, 4 mL/kg diluted EB was retro-orbitally injected and allowed to circulate for 1 h. Mice were perfused with 15 mL of PBS, and the brains were isolated immediately. For quantitative measurement, the brains were homogenized in 500 µL of formamide (Sigma‒Aldrich). The samples were incubated at 55 °C for 24 h to extract the EB from the brain tissue. The samples were subsequently centrifuged, the supernatants containing formamide were collected, and the absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 610 nm via Varioskan. The results are expressed as the amount of EB (ng) per tissue amount (mg).

Plasma cytokine evaluation

The plasma cytokine concentrations were determined via a Simple PlexTM Assay (ProteinSimple, CA, USA) on the EllaTM instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A panel of four cytokines, namely, CXCL-10, IL-1β, IL-6 and TNFα, was used on a preskit cartridge. Ella™ was calibrated via the in-cartridge factory standard curve. All samples were measured after a 1:2 dilution. The cartridge was inserted into the EllaTM device for 70 minutes. The concentrations are expressed in pg/mL. All the samples were assayed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SEMs. For the human cohort, the data were analyzed via descriptive statistics for the lean vs. obese groups and the absence of PND vs. PND via the Mann‒Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Variables with p < 0.1 in the univariate model without PND vs. PND were retained as candidates for inclusion in the multivariate model and were entered, simultaneously adjusting for all of them. The associations between different covariates and dependent variables were calculated via multivariate analysis, with an initial threshold set at a p value of <0.05. After the exclusion of collinear covariates, stepwise regression (backward elimination) was applied, starting with all the variables initially chosen and then progressively removing non-significant ones. We only retained parameters that remained statistically significant in the multivariate model (e.g., p < 0.05) after mutual adjustment. Model performance was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test for calibration, the AUC for discrimination, and the proportion of correctly classified patients for overall classification accuracy. For comparisons of the two independent variables (diet (ND/HFD) and surgery (no fracture/ fracture)), we performed two-way ANOVA followed by four pairwise Student’s t-tests with a Tukey post-hoc correction. For social interaction trials, each trial was normalized to the 1st trial for each group and each trial was compared to the baseline using one-sample T-test. For object location groups the groups were compared with a one-sample T-test compared to 50%. For comparison of two groups a Student’s 2-sided t-test was used. p values less than 0.05 (* or #), 0.01 (** or ##) or 0.001 (*** or ###) or (**** or ####) were considered statistically significant. # is used for comparison to control diet. Analysis was performed on GraphPad software.
