Determining parameter impact in systems biology models via sensitivity analysis: a comparative approach
Authors: Kelsey I. Gasior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mathematical modeling is a powerful tool to understand biological phenomena, predict behavior, and guide experiments. Model parameters represent experimentally derived rates, and it can be challenging to understand how parameters affect the system’s output(s). Global sensitivity analysis (GSA) can help determine how uncertainty in model outcomes can be attributed to parameters. Methods like Sobol’ indices provide detailed analysis but at a high computational cost. Thus, it may be necessary to precede it by another method to reduce the parameter space. Morris Method Screening (MMS) is a qualitative analysis that can provide information about sensitivity and parameter interactions, making it a good partner for Sobol’. Partial Rank Correlation Coefficient (PRCC) may also be a viable option as it has a similar cost to MMS. This work tests whether a ranking (PRCC) or screening (MMS) method is better when used in combination with Sobol’ analysis. Using a model of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition as a test case, this work shows that PRCC-Sobol’ and MMS-Sobol’ can produce diverging results, making it difficult to analyze how biological events are informed by specific rates and quantities. However, the differences between these methods mean that these techniques may be most effective when all three are used together.

Introduction

Mathematical modeling is a powerful tool to understand biological phenomena, predict systems’ behavior, and guide future experiments. The parameters in these models represent rates and quantities present in biological systems1. Determining how changes to these parameters affect the system’s output(s) can be complicated and requires thorough investigation. Global sensitivity analysis (GSA) is a powerful method to help determine how uncertainty in specific model outcomes, or quantities of interest (QoI) can be attributed to parameters2–4. Within the varied parameter set, all parameters are varied at once, and GSA provides information about parameters that may have epistemic or aleatory uncertainty3,5,6. This analysis can guide future exploration but requires a delicate balance: large parameter sets and wide parameter ranges can provide a better understanding of their model mechanisms, but analysis could be computationally expensive3,5,7. Considering that many mathematical models describe real-world systems and results can have significant consequences shaping experimental directions, choices must be made carefully and methodically during analysis.

There are many different GSA techniques available, and the benefits of several of these are outlined in Fig. 1. One method is Partial Rank Correlation Coefficient (PRCC)7. PRCC is a ranking method, meaning that it puts parameters in order based on their effect on model output variability7. This method is usually combined with Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), a type of Monte Carlo sampling8. LHS divides the K parameter spaces into N subsections and randomly selects a value from each subunit (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N > \frac{4}{3}K$$\end{document}N>43K) before combining them into parameter sets 8,9. PRCC ranks the individual parameter values and outputs and quantifies the monotonic relationship between them through the correlation coefficient (ρ)10. It has moderate computational requirements and provides correlation directionality (ρ ∈ [−1, 1])7. Within this range, ∣ρ∣ ≈ 1 indicates that the QOI is sensitive to the input parameter, while ρ ≈ 0 indicates non-sensitivity 11. A threshold (∣ρ∣ > 0.5) is often used 10,12–16. However, PRCC requires the outcome change monotonically when parameters are varied individually. PRCC is a ranking method with a moderate computational cost and the resulting correlation coefficient (ρ) that can determine whether a parameter positively or negatively impacts the output. However, PRCC requires that outcomes change monotonically when each parameter is varied individually7–10. Therefore, the PRCC results were used in Step 2 to reduce the parameter set for Sobol' analysis. Conversely, MMS is a screening method that can also be used to reduce the parameter set. Like PRCC, MMS has a moderate computational cost that does not require monotonicity, but it can only provide semi-quantitative information about nonlinearity and parameter interactions3,7,19. In Step 3, MMS was performed on the EMT model to reduce the parameter set before Sobol' was performed. Sobol' can both rank and screen parameters to provide detailed information about parameter impacts and interactions but, unlike MMS and PRCC, it has a high computational cost5,7,20.

LHS-PRCC is not the only method for assessing parameter impact. Morris Method Screening (MMS), which uses one-at-a-time (OAT) sampling and determines the variation in the derivative index over the parameter domain, may also be appropriate7,17,18. Unlike PRCC, MMS is a screening method, which means that it helps determine which parameters are unidentifiable and thus have little effect on variability in the output7. In this methodology, a finite difference scheme approximates the derivative and the average of many samples produces semi-informative statistics about the QoI, such as the mean and standard deviation (μ*, σ2). MMS normalizes the parameter range from [0, 1], divides it into l levels, and uses a step-size of Δ to select the parameter value3,5,7. While MMS does not require monotonicity, it also cannot determine the exact impact of a parameter. MMS only indicates the relative importance and interaction associated with a parameter3,7,19. Thus, it may be best to use it as a screening method to reduce the space before using more robust analysis, such as Sobol’5.

Sobol’ is a variance-based method that uses ANOVA-like decomposition to determine the impact of the parameters on outcome variations5,20,21. Sobol’ can provide detailed information about the parameter-outcome relationship, such as the total parameter impact (ST), primary impact (S1), interaction effects (ST − S1), and one-one parameter interactions (S2) 2,5,7. While Sobol’ does not have a strict sensitivity cut-off, nor can its statistics provide directionality, it can both screen and rank parameters7,22–24. Unless ST = 0, the total impact is used to determine the relative importance of a parameter while the primary impact (S1) can be used for ranking5,7.

Unfortunately, when modeling real-world scenarios, performing Sobol’ alone is not always feasible. This issue could be due to a number of reasons, including the high computational cost5,7,12. Thus, there is a need to find methods for making Sobol’ more tractable when analyzing these complex systems. One possibility would be to use a screening technique, such as MMS, to reduce the parameter space to a small subset that would allow for Sobol’ to be performed5. Another option would be to forgo Sobol’ entirely and choose a different method, such as LHS-PRCC. However, PRCC would require monotonicity and would lack information about complex interactions governing the system9. A third option would be to use PRCC prior to Sobol’, reducing the parameter space before obtaining information about important parameter impacts and interactions in the system9,12.

This work seeks to understand whether a screening method, such as MMS, or a ranking method, such as PRCC, is better for parameter set reduction prior to Sobol’ when dealing with complex biological problems. To examine how MMS- and PRCC-derived subsets compare under Sobol’, a theoretical two-equation model of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is used as a test case15. EMT is the process by which epithelial cells lose their adhesive nature and adopt mesenchymal-associated invasiveness, allowing carcinoma cells to migrate away from a primary tumor and form metastases25–27. This model presents a unique opportunity to gain both mathematical and biological insights. The model is small in size with only two equations, and it has been shown that PRCC can be performed16,28. However, the model does also presents some complications. It has been hypothesized that there is a bistable switch underlying EMT: transitioned cells can maintain the mesenchymal phenotype in the absence of extracellular factors14. Multiple model input variables in this model seek to capture multiple bistable switches in response to multiple external factors, meaning that the screen-rank comparison must be carried out under several different treatment groups. Further, with 11 parameters to raise the computational cost and preliminary results highlighting some numerical instabilities, performing Sobol’ alone will not adequately capture the complicated behavior. Thus, for this model, Sobol’ will be preceded by a reduction methodology.

To perform a comprehensive inquiry into using combined sensitivity analyses in complex, multi-scale systems, as well as the biases introduced by researchers, a methodical approach was used. The model was exposed to eight different combinations of the input variables (TGF-β, cellular contact) and the parameters were varied over two different ranges (±10%, ±25% of the original model value in Table 1) to capture the heterogeneity inherent in the tumor, possible bistable behavior, and variability due to lack of certainty about a parameter value. MMS-Sobol’ and PRCC-Sobol’ were then performed on the long-term values of the of the two model variables associated with mesenchymal (Slug) and epithelial (E-cadherin) phenotypes. Ultimately, comparing a screening method to a ranking method prior to Sobol’ highlights potential issues that may arise during analysis. While Slug results show conclusive agreement on which parameters control this factor, E-cadherin results differ based on parameter range, treatment group, and methodology. Not only do MMS and PRCC select different subsets, but important parameters from PRCC directly conflict with parameters highlighted by Sobol’. These differences suggest that different sampling methods may skew the results. But this work also shows that, given the pros and cons of each method, all three are needed to derive a better picture of the dynamics underlying the system. Together, they suggest new avenues for computational and experimental exploration, as well as warnings when applying and using these analytical techniques.

Discussion

This work highlights the issues that can emerge when using common combined GSA methods over ill-defined parameter ranges in biomathematical modeling. Creating a biologically relevant mathematical model relies on well-known and accurately measured parameter ranges. A wider parameter range chosen for GSA alludes to the certainty of the parameter measurement. Here, two ranges were used to test how different levels of certainty can affect sensitivity results. Additionally, due to the size of the parameter range and numerical instabilities present for certain parameter combinations, this model cannot be subjected to Sobol’ as a whole. Instead, it is necessary to perform combined methodologies to reduce the parameter space to the most impactful parameters and then apply Sobol’ to glean important details about their impact on desired outcomes and the effects of their interactions. This work presents two possible combinations for analysis: MMS-Sobol’ and PRCC-Sobol’. MMS and PRCC have different benefits and drawbacks as a first step, but both need to be combined with another analysis, such as Sobol’. PRCC is a ranking method and uses a hard cut-off to determine sensitivity, but it can only indicate the correlation, not the magnitude of change, between an input and an output9. Further, the ∣ρ∣ ≥ 0.5 meant that for some cases, only one parameter was initially selected by PRCC for further analysis. MMS is a screening method, and the semi-quantitative information given about parameter-outcome relationships means a second analysis is also necessary. While different cut-offs can be used and screening can be subjective, a cut-off of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{2}{\mu }_{\max }^{* }$$\end{document}12μmax* for each treatment group was used for MMS sensitivity here. The limits in information available from PRCC and MMS make them both candidates for parameter space reduction prior to Sobol’ use. However, the results from this study comparing the use of PRCC and MMS to reduce the parameter space give mixed results. While the differences in sampling and analysis did not appear to extensively influence Slug across both the ±10% and ±25% parameter ranges, choosing MMS or PRCC for parameter space reduction did produce varying results for E-cadherin.

Figure 6 summarizes Figs. 2–5, highlighting the most important parameter for each range, treatment group, and methodology, as well as the overlap in parameters chosen by PRCC and MMS for subsequent Sobol’ analysis. Slug results showed consistency across parameter ranges, methodologies, and levels of exogenous TGF-β. The Sobol’ subsets chosen by PRCC and MMS were the same for 10 of the 16 treatment groups. Further, for both the ±10% and ±25% ranges of Slug, as cellular contact for the cell increased, the importance of the production rate of Slug (α2) increased. Additional inspection of Figs. 2–5 shows an increase in importance of the suppression of Slug by membrane-bound E-cadherin (k1). The agreement between MMS-Sobol’ and PRCC-Sobol’ results suggests an analytical recipe to apply moving forward: (1) reduce the parameter set via MMS to the most important parameters; (2) apply Sobol’ analysis to determine the total impact and interaction effects of each parameter; (3) use PRCC to determine whether changes in these parameters are having positive or negative impacts on Slug. With the variability present in the E-cadherin results, one question that arises is whether this is due to the ∣ρ∣≥0.5 cut-off used in selecting the PRCC-subset. While this threshold has been used in previous work, it is an arbitrary choice made further undefined by the inclusion of the top three parameters if two did not pass this threshold. To determine the effects of this selection methodology, additional analysis was run using ∣ρ∣ ≥ 0.25, as shown in Supplementary Figure S23. Lowering the threshold for sensitivity altered 16 of the 32 original PRCC-subsets by either including an extra parameter for analysis or losing a parameter that fell below ∣ρ∣ ≥ 0.25. Of these 16 sets, nine agreed with the MMS-subsets, leaving seven for further analysis: six E-cadherin and one Slug. Lowering of the ρ threshold changed some of the PRCC-subsets, but similar patterns emerged. More treatment groups in E-cadherin than Slug disagreed between MMS and PRCC. However, lowering the ρ threshold did not change the subsequent Sobol’ analysis. The parameters identified as important by Sobol’ in these subsets remained consistent. Thus, it is necessary to look to other areas to understand the variability present in the E-cadherin results.

The variability present in the E-cadherin results could be from several sources in the model and analysis. One such source could be the structure of the model or, specifically, the E-cadherin equation. This model is a small two-equation system with two bifurcations: a reversible bistable switch with respect to cellular contact (C) and an irreversible bistable switch with respect to TGF-β (T). The bistability present in C = 1 and C = 2 when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T=0\frac{{\rm{ng}}}{{\rm{mL}}}$$\end{document}T=0ngmL is apparent when the E-cadherin outputs are examined. Supplementary Figures S1–S16 show the distribution of E-cadherin and Slug values at t = 10,000 min for Sobol’ analysis in Figs. 2–5. These plots show how the distribution and bistable behavior changes depending on which parameters are varied within the analysis. E-cadherin distributions are different depending on whether it was an MMS- or PRCC-subset, as well as the size of the parameter range. These differences could indicate that the bifurcations present in the model are creating complexity for further analysis. Additionally, this model was created with multiple simplifications used to describe complex intracellular processes. The shifting of the parameter ranges for α2 or k1 for analysis could point to a need for further expansion of these terms in future iterations of the model to fully understand and capture these mechanisms. Thus, E-cadherin variability could be structural.

Another possible source of variability in the E-cadherin results could stem from the analysis performed. The ±10% and ±25% ranges chosen for the parameters in this model were chosen due to a lack of knowledge about the values. Further experiments could and should be performed to help determine the best range for these parameters before analysis is applied. It may also be prudent to conduct approximate Bayesian computation based on sequential Monte Carlo (ABC-SMC) to determine the posterior parameter distributions. ABC methods can be used to find the posterior distributions where likelihood functions are computationally expensive to estimate by comparing observed data to simulated outputs. In ABC-SMC, parameter values are sampled from the prior distribution and are transmitted through intermediate distributions until it depicts the target distribution. ABC-SMC could give better parameter estimates and intervals and provide information about parameter inferability for low computational cost29. Performing ABC-SMC prior to combined sensitivity techniques, such as MMS and Sobol’ has been used before to analyze mathematical models30 and may help provide better parameter ranges for this multi-bifurcation model before performing sensitivity analysis.

A final reason for the differing results in E-cadherin here could be due to the original question posed: ranking (PRCC) vs. screening (MMS). First, PRCC and MMS have different sampling methods over the parameter space and provide different information about the impact of the parameters. Second, due to the different constraints present in each method, use of either PRCC or MMS prior to Sobol’ may inadvertently cause the user to inflict their own bias. One benefit of PRCC is that ρ ∈ [−1,1], meaning that a set threshold could be chosen and applied to all analysis. Here, while the choice of ρ is arbitrary, if it applied consistently, the bias in it may be missed. In contrast, there is no set cap on μ* in MMS, making it difficult to potentially determine what may be an appropriate cut-off for sensitivity. Lack of objectivity here can have drastic consequences in systems where parameters have similar μ* values or large values of σ2. Thus, the objectivity involved applying the same threshold value for ρ makes PRCC “user-friendly,” but could also mean information is missed that could have been provided by MMS.

While this work uses an objective methodology for both PRCC and MMS, it highlights where that objectivity fails and how insights from both methods can help with analysis. Similar to a set value of ρ in PRCC, a cut-off of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5{\mu }_{\max }^{* }$$\end{document}0.5μmax* was used for MMS to create a standardized system for analysis in a methodology that does not have the sensitivity parameters μ* and σ2. Conversely, the ∣ρ∣ ≥ 0.5 initially produced some PRCC subsets containing only 1 parameter, which is insufficient for Sobol’ analysis. Extending the parameter subsets to include the top 3 parameters implements flexibility where needed, as would occur in MMS. This flexibility is most likely why lowering the threshold to ∣ρ∣ ≥ 0.25 in Supplementary Figure S23 did not change the results significantly. Thus, understanding both methods and how they function can help produce a better subset for Sobol’ analysis. Further, given the variability in the results, as well as the differences in the sampling methods, rather than determining whether a screening method or a ranking method should precede Sobol’, this work shows that all three are necessary for adequate analysis. Semi-quantitative analysis via MMS can suggest parameters that PRCC may have excluded; Sobol’ can provide in-depth information about parameter impact and interaction; PRCC can determine whether these parameters are positively or negatively correlated with the QoI.

Whether the methodologies agree or diverge, what becomes clear is that sensitivity analysis must be performed following model development. Agreement between analyses can suggest specific areas as experimental targets and modeling expansion, while discrepancies can show areas that need improvement and retooling. But what is paramount to remember is that, although the model examined here is theoretical, the underlying phenomenon is not. Preventing cells within a primary tumor from transitioning is imperative in preventing metastasis. The GSA performed, in addition to other works with this model, highlights how the levels of E-cadherin and Slug present in different treatment groups is analogous to shifts in these intracellular components depending on the position in the tumor and exposure to exogenous factors. Variability in the tumor microenvironment demands methodical analysis to help propose thoughtful, directed methods of therapeutic treatment. The combined efforts of MMS, PRCC, and Sobol’ here highlight the difficulty in building accurate models and determining the appropriate analysis when the dynamics underlying real-world process are complex and multi-scale. But using these GSA techniques together will ultimately provide the most insight for continued interrogation of the models capturing biological processes. Ultimately, the results of these combined analyses play a key role in the computational-experimental feedback loop underlying biomathematical research.

Mathematical model of EMT

Gasior et al. developed a mathematical model to examine the changes in membrane-bound E-cadherin (E) and the transcription factor, Slug (S) and capture the bistable behavior observed in MCF7 breast carcinoma cells15,27. The model is detailed in Equations 1–2. A schematic of this model is found in Fig. 7A, while parameter values and definitions are in Table 115. In addition to production (α1, α2) and degradation (β1, β2), this model incorporates the influence of both pro-epithelial and pro-mesenchymal factors on the bistable switch underlying EMT. In the absence of cell-cell contact, E-cadherin can be endocytosed but, in the presence of neighboring cells, it translocates to the membrane to interact with E-cadherin on neighboring cells31. Additionally, E-cadherin is anchored at the membrane on the intracellular side by members of the catenin family, such as β-catenin32. By interacting with E-cadherin at the membrane, as well as the full functioning of the GSK-3β degradation complex32,33, free β-catenin is kept in a low quantity and is unable to translocate to the nucleus to activate production of the transcription factor Slug. In15,16,28, this system assumes that cell-cell contact (C) is continuous. In a system without exogenous TGF-β, cells that have 6 neighbors (C = 6) begin as epithelial cells. If the cell were to lose enough cell-cell contact, it would transition to the mesenchymal phenotype. A cell without any contact (C = 0 cells) exists in the mesenchymal state. However, if that cell were to gain enough cellular contact back, it would transition back to the epithelial phenotype. Cells with 2 neighbors (C = 2) can also begin as epithelial cells but are capable of attaining the mesenchymal phenotype with the addition of exogenous TGF-β. Cells with 3+ neighbors begin as epithelial cells and are unable to go a bistable switch with respect to TGF-β, no matter how much exogenous TGF-β is added15. Latin hypercube sampling and partial rank correlation coefficient

Gasior16,28 used Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and Partial Rank Correlation Coefficient (PRCC) to understand the sensitivity of E-cadherin (E) and Slug (S) values at t = 10,000 min to changes in the 11 parameters outlined in Equations 1–2 and Table 115,16,28. The model was subjected to two different levels of exogenous TGF-β (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T=0\frac{{\rm{ng}}}{{\rm{mL}}}$$\end{document}T=0ngmL and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T=3.64\times 1{0}^{-6}\frac{{\rm{ng}}}{{\rm{mL}}}$$\end{document}T=3.64×10-6ngmL) and four different levels of cell-cell contact (C = 0,1,2,6 cells), creating 8 treatment groups over which sensitivity was examined. Parameters were varied over ranges that included ± 10% and ±25% of the original value given in Table 116,28. These ranges are listed in Supplementary Table S1. PRCC requires that the E-cadherin and Slug values at t = 10,000 min are monotonic when each parameter is varied individually7–9. Additionally, while the original model does not produce negative values for E-cadherin and Slug, some values for α2 and k1 produced negative values when explored over the ±10% and ±25% ranges. Thus, the parameter ranges for α2 and k1 were shifted to accommodate monotonic behavior and prevent negative values at t = 10,000 min when parameters were varied individually.

Latin Hypercube Sampling requires that the K parameter spaces are sampled N times, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N > \frac{4}{3}K$$\end{document}N>43K8. In this sampling methodology, the parameter space is divided into N equiprobable intervals and then each interval is sampled8. With 11 parameters, N > 15, but to adequately sample the space,16,28 chose N = 10,000 samples from uniform parameter distributions. Initial conditions were established for each level of cell-cell contact, and time-course simulations were run in MATLAB for t = 10,000 min. The values of E and S were then recorded at this time point and rounded to the nearest 10−4 to preserve monotonicity.

The partial correlation identifies the linear relationship between each ranked input parameter and the ranked values of E and S after controlling for the effect of the other parameters. In these equations, Y is the model output, Xi is the parameter of interest, and X~i are all other parameters. V is the variance, and E is the expected value. Thus, S1 is the ratio of the variance of the expected value given knowledge of the parameter to the total variance, while S2 is the ratio of the variance of the expected value given knowledge of the chosen parameter (Xi) and the other parameter (Xj) in all combinations to the total variance2,5,40.
