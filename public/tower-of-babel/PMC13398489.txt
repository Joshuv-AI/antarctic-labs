Peer review of the pesticide risk assessment of the active substance Trichoderma atroviride 77B
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Fernando Álvarez, Maria Arena, Domenica Auteri, Sofia Batista Leite, Marco Binaglia, Anna Federica Castoldi, Arianna Chiusolo, Angelo Colagiorgi, Mathilde Colas, Federica Crivellente, Chloe De Lentdecker, Isabella De Magistris, Teresa Capilla Chavez, Gabriella Fait, Franco Ferilli, German Giner Santonja, Varvara Gouliarmou, Alessio Ippolito, Frederique Istace, Dimitra Kardassi, Aude Kienzler, Anna Lanzoni, Roberto Lava, Renata Leuschner, Alberto Linguadoca, Mariano Lopez Romano, Jochem Louisse, Christopher Lythgo, Oriol Magrans, Iris Mangas, Silvia Mazzega, Andrea Mioč, Ileana Miron, Tunde Molnar, Laura Padovani, Vincenzo Padricello, Martina Panzarea, Juan Manuel Parra Morte, Simone Rizzuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conclusions of the EFSA following the peer review of the initial risk assessments carried out by the competent authority of the rapporteur Member State, the Netherlands, for the pesticide active substance Trichoderma atroviride 77B and the considerations as regards the inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 are reported. The context of the peer review was that required by Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council. The conclusions were reached on the basis of the evaluation of the representative uses of Trichoderma atroviride 77B as a fungicide on solanaceae (such as tomato and pepper), strawberry and grapevine. The reliable endpoints, appropriate for use in regulatory risk assessment, are presented. Missing information identified as being required by the regulatory framework is listed. Data gaps were identified.Keywords: fungicide, peer review, pesticide, risk assessment, Trichoderma atroviride 77B

SUMMARY

Trichoderma atroviride 77B is a new active substance for which, in accordance with Article 7 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council, the rapporteur Member State (RMS), the Netherlands, received an application from Andermatt Group AG on 19 September 2019 for approval. In addition, the applicant submitted an application for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. Complying with Article 9 of the Regulation, the completeness of the dossier was checked by the RMS and the date of admissibility of the application was recognised as being 20 April 2020.

An initial evaluation of the dossier on Trichoderma atroviride 77B was provided by the RMS in the draft assessment report (DAR), and subsequently, a peer review of the pesticide risk assessment on the RMS evaluation was conducted by EFSA in accordance with Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. The following conclusions are derived.

The proposed uses of Trichoderma atroviride 77B as a fungicide applied by foliar spraying on solanaceae (such as tomato and pepper) and strawberry in field, permanent greenhouse and non‐permanent protected structures and on grapevine (field application), result in a sufficient fungicidal efficacy against the target pests.

The assessment of the data package revealed no issues that could not be finalised or that need to be included as critical areas of concern with respect to identity, biological properties of the microbial active substance and physical and technical properties of the representative formulation and analytical methods.

In the area of mammalian toxicology, the available evidence was not considered sufficient to exclude the presence of metabolites of concern (data gap).

In the area of residues, for non‐viable and viable residues, data gaps were identified for qualitative and quantitative information on potential occurrence of toxicologically relevant metabolites of potential concern (emodin) and of their potential in situ production following treatments under conditions of the representative uses (tomatoes, peppers, strawberries and grapevine). In addition, a data gap for a comprehensive literature search was identified. The consumer risk assessment cannot be finalised.

An MRL application for inclusion of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B into Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 has also been submitted. The criteria of the respective guidance were considered and none of those applicable to active substances which are microorganisms can be concluded as met.

With respect to fate and behaviour into the environment, it has been demonstrated that Trichoderma atroviride 77B may persist and multiply in soil in concentrations considerably higher than the natural background levels, considering repeated applications over the years for the representative field uses. Satisfactory information was not available to support the viability/population dynamics of Trichoderma atroviride 77B in natural water and sediment systems considering all the life cycle phases. Environmental exposure to metabolites of potential concern (present in the MCPC or formed in situ) is not addressed pending further data to complete its identification and hazard characterisation.

In the area of ecotoxicology, it is not possible to conclude on the infectivity and pathogenicity for fish and aquatic plants. In addition, the risk assessment to non‐target organisms exposed to secondary metabolites of potential concern potentially produced could not be finalised.

BACKGROUND

Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council 1 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Regulation’) lays down, inter alia, the detailed rules as regards the procedure and conditions for approval of active substances. This regulates for the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) the procedure for organising the consultation of Member States and the applicant(s) for comments on the initial evaluation in the draft assessment report (DAR), provided by the rapporteur Member State (RMS), and the organisation of an expert consultation, where appropriate.

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA is required to adopt a conclusion on whether an active substance can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided in Article 4 of the Regulation (also taking into consideration recital (10) of the Regulation) within 120 days from the end of the period provided for the submission of written comments, subject to an extension of 30 days where an expert consultation is necessary, and a further extension of up to 150 days where additional information is required to be submitted by the applicant(s) in accordance with Article 12(3).

Trichoderma atroviride 77B is a new active substance for which, in accordance with Article 7 of the Regulation, the RMS, the Netherlands (hereinafter referred to as the ‘RMS’), received an application from Andermatt Group AG on 19 September 2019 for approval of the active substance Trichoderma atroviride 77B. In addition, the applicant submitted an application for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. Complying with Article 9 of the Regulation, the completeness of the dossier was checked by the RMS and the date of admissibility of the application was recognised as being 20 April 2020.

The RMS provided its initial evaluation of the dossier on Trichoderma atroviride 77B in the DAR, which was received by EFSA on 22 September 2023 and then updated in February 2024 (The Netherlands, 2024). The peer review was initiated on 21 March 2024 by dispatching the DAR to the Member States and the applicant, Andermatt Group AG, for consultation and comments. EFSA also provided comments. In addition, EFSA conducted a public consultation on the DAR. The comments received were collated by EFSA and forwarded to the RMS for compilation and evaluation in the format of a reporting table. The applicant was invited to respond to the comments in column 3 of the reporting table. The comments and the applicant's response were evaluated by the RMS in column 3.

The need for expert consultation and the necessity for additional information to be submitted by the applicant in accordance with Article 12(3) of the Regulation were considered in a teleconference between EFSA and the RMS on 20 August 2024. On the basis of the comments received, the applicant's response to the comments and the RMS's evaluation thereof, it was concluded that additional information should be requested from the applicant and that EFSA should conduct an expert consultation in the areas of potential effects on human/mammalian health, residues and potential effects on non‐target organisms.

The outcome of the teleconference, together with EFSA's further consideration of the comments, is reflected in the conclusions set out in column 4 of the reporting table. All points that were identified as unresolved at the end of the comment evaluation phase and which required further consideration, including those issues to be considered in an expert consultation, were compiled by EFSA in the format of an evaluation table.

The conclusions arising from the consideration by EFSA, and as appropriate by the RMS, of the points identified in the evaluation table, together with the outcome of the expert consultation and the written consultation on the assessment of additional information, where these took place, were reported in the final column of the evaluation table.

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA should adopt a conclusion on whether Trichoderma atroviride 77B can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided in Article 4 of the Regulation, taking into consideration recital (10) of the Regulation.

A final consultation on the conclusions arising from the peer review of the risk assessment took place with Member States via a written procedure in March–April 2026.

This conclusion report summarises the outcome of the peer review of the risk assessment on the active substance and the formulation for representative uses evaluated on the basis of the representative uses of Trichoderma atroviride 77B as a fungicide on solanaceae (such as tomato and pepper), strawberry and grapevine as proposed by the applicant. In accordance with Article 12(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, risk mitigation options identified in the DAR and considered during the peer review, if any, are presented in the conclusion.

The microbial active substance and the formulation for representative uses

Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B is a wild‐type, indigenous fungus deposited at the Leibniz Institute DSMZ‐German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, under the accession number DSM 32801 and at the NCF (South African National Collection of Fungi) under the accession number PPRI 9088.

The formulated product for the representative uses for the evaluation was ‘T‐77’, a wettable powder (WP) containing 1.0 × 109–1.0 × 1010 CFU/g (nominal 2.0 × 109 CFU/g) of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B.

The information on the active substance and the formulation for representative uses, including the co‐formulants in this formulation, was considered in the overall assessment during the peer review. None of the co‐formulants is an unacceptable co‐formulant listed in Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 2 ; however, two co‐formulants are currently approved active substances under Regulation (EC) 1107/2009 3 , 4 one at doses well below the approved EU uses and also listed under other EU Regulations and one is a basic substance. Information on the content of some components of a co‐formulant is missing (data gap, see Section 10)4.

Details on the composition of the formulations cannot be reported in conclusions because of the provisions in Article 63(2)(d) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009; however, this information was fully available and evaluated during the peer review. A proposal for classification of the formulation(s) according to Regulation (EC) 1272/2008 was provided by the applicant and assessed by the RMS (please see Volume 3 MP of the DAR).

The representative EU uses evaluated were professional foliar spray application for the control of Botrytis cinerea on solanaceae (such as tomato and pepper) and strawberry in field, greenhouse (permanent) and non‐permanent protected structures and on grapevine (field application). Full details of the GAP can be found in the list of end points in Appendix A.

Data were submitted to conclude that the use of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B according to the representative uses proposed at EU level results in a sufficient fungicidal efficacy against targeted pests, following the guidance document SANCO/10054/2013‐rev. 3 (European Commission, 2013).

A data gap has been identified for a search of the scientific peer‐reviewed open literature on the microbial active substance and its metabolites of (potential) concern, dealing with side effects on health, residues, the environment and non‐target species and published within the 10 years before the date of submission of the dossier, to be conducted and reported in accordance with EFSA guidance on the submission of scientific peer‐reviewed open literature for the approval of pesticide active substances under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (EFSA, 2011) (see Section 10).

CONCLUSIONS OF THE EVALUATION

General aspects

With regard to the co‐formulants contained in ‘BT‐77’, sufficient toxicological data was available for all components. For one co‐formulant 5 (present above 50% in the final formulation), information on the content of a component is missing (data gap, see Section 10) (Peer Review Experts TC 177). 6 Except for this missing data, EFSA considered that the available toxicological information sufficiently addressed the genotoxicity and repeated dose toxicity potential of ‘BT‐77’ over the short‐ and long‐term, and alerts were identified for one co‐formulant added in this formulation (present in significant amounts in the final formulation). 7 The alerts relate to the harmonised classification proposed in the RAC Opinion as Carc. 1B and STOT RE 1 (lungs, inhalation).

The availability of ecotoxicity data with the formulation for representative uses ‘77B’ was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts TC 178 8 (refer to Section 6). It was noted that formulation data were only available for bees (see Section 6). A data retrieval search for available ecotoxicity data for the individual components was not available, and therefore, it was not possible to reach a conclusion on the safety of the formulation for representative uses (data gap, see Section 10). Pending on the outcome on identified missing data for mammalian toxicology for one of the components in the formulation for representative uses, further consideration of non‐target organisms may be necessary.

SPECIFICATION OF THE MICROORGANISM, PHYSICAL AND TECHNICAL PROPERTIES AND METHODS OF ANALYSIS

The following guidance documents were followed in the production of this conclusion: European Commission (2012) and EFSA FEEDAP Panel (2012).

The microbial pest control agent (MPCA) is not isolated and purified but directly formulated to the microbial pest control product (MPCP); therefore, the specification was set on the MPCP based on batch data from industrial scale plant production. The minimum and maximum content of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B in the MPCP was 1.0 × 109–1.0 × 1010 CFU/g. It is noted that the potential of the strain to produce metabolites of potential concern is open (see Sections 2 and 3.2); therefore, their analysis with validated analytical methods in the MPCP might be required. The content of microbial contaminants in the MPCP was below the limits defined in the Working Document on Microbial Contaminant Limits (OECD, 2012).

Phylogenetic analysis based on sequence of rpb2 and tef1 genes was used to identify the microorganism as belonging to Trichoderma atroviride species. Based on SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms) in the partial rpb2 and tef1 sequences, Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B can be distinguished from other closely related T. atroviride strains; however, this was not confirmed by any data (data gap, see Section 10).

It was demonstrated that the formulation for representative uses T‐77 is stable when it is stored for 24 months at a temperature < 4°C in its commercial packaging.

Acceptable methods were available for the determination of the microorganism in the MPCP and for determination of the content of contaminating microorganisms in the MPCP. Residue definitions were not set for Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B; therefore, monitoring methods are not required (see Sections 4 and 6).

BIOLOGICAL PROPERTIES OF THE MICROORGANISM

Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B is a naturally occurring, non‐modified filamentous fungus originally isolated from the senescing grape foliage in South Africa.

Historical background: Trichoderma species are well‐studied microorganisms with a long history of use as biological control agents in agriculture, but also, among others, for enzyme and feed production. T. atroviride is a soil‐borne fungus naturally occurring also in Europe.

The target organism for the representative uses is Botrytis cinerea.

The entire host range for Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B is not known nor it is possible to determine. However, given Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B was isolated from senescing grape foliage, and senescing plant material is a commonly cited habitat for species of T. atroviride, it is reasonable to assume that this is its main habitat.

Mode of action: Trichoderma spp. antagonise phytopathogens by mycoparasitism, competing for nutrients and space, by producing bioactive secondary metabolites as well as by inducing systemic resistance of plants.

Infectiveness, dispersal and colonisation ability: The ability of the strain to grow at 37°C has not been resolved; however, the strain is concluded as having no potential infectivity/pathogenicity to mammals. The RMS indicates that a study on growth temperature (carried out in compliance with GLP and performed in a GLP facility – Lab GLP compliance statement submitted – although not signed by the submitter) was made available to the RMS after the end of the clock stop and thus is not taken into account in this conclusion.

Trichoderma afroharzianum has recently been reported as a plant pathogenic fungus on maize (Zea mays) in several regions of Europe, where it has been associated with ear rot disease. The first cases were described in 2018, and the species is currently included in the EPPO Alert List as a causal agent of ear rot on maize (EPPO, RS 2022/087; 2023/194; 2024/140). In addition to the references cited in the EPPO reports, recent publications in peer‐reviewed journals and on preprint servers have provided further evidence on the pathogenicity of Trichoderma strains on maize (Harish et al., 2025; Pfordt et al., 2024; Pfordt, Douanla‐Meli, Schäfer, et al., 2025; Pfordt, Douanla‐Meli, Voll, et al., 2025; Pfordt & von Tiedemann, 2025). Some of these recent studies have shown that several isolates of T. atroviride may also cause ear rot disease to maize when the plant is previously wounded or damaged (e.g. by insects). The extent to which Trichoderma atroviride 77B can also be pathogenic to maize or other non‐target plants is currently unknown.

Secondary metabolites of (potential) concern: Based on the available literature search, Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B can produce volatile organic compounds (e.g. 6‐pentyl‐2H‐pyran‐2‐one or 6‐pentyl‐alpha‐pyrone, 6‐PP) as metabolites of potential concern. According to the literature, it is noted that some Trichoderma species have the genetic capacity of producing other secondary metabolites known for the genus, such as anthraquinones (e.g. genotoxic emodin), peptaibols, polyketides, cytochalasans and camptothecin; however, this was not fully investigated in the literature for the strain 77B and cannot be excluded (data gap, see above). No whole genome sequencing and chemical analyses were provided. Further discussion on the relevance for mammalian health is provided in Section 3.

Antifungal susceptibility: Testing of Trichoderma atroviride 77B with several antimycotic agents following EUCAST reference method E. DEF.9.3 9 allowed to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for amphotericin B, anidulafungin, caspofungin, micafungin, isavuconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole and voriconazole. These antimycotic agents correspond to three different modes of action. Based on these data, it was suggested that, in case of need, polyenes and azoles are probable antimycotic agents for treatment (with the lowest MIC for amphotericin B). It is noted that EUCAST breakpoints for Trichoderma species, together with the tested antifungal agents, were not available to allow the strain to be clearly categorised as resistant or susceptible to these substances.

POTENTIAL EFFECTS ON HUMAN/MAMMALIAN HEALTH

The toxicological profile of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B and its metabolites was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review TC 177 in June 2025.

Mammalian infectivity and pathogenicity of the micro‐organism and sensitisation effects

As regards medical information, there is no evidence that Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B species causes infection in immuno‐competent healthy humans. However, Trichoderma species can act as opportunistic human pathogens. No adverse health effects have been reported in workers as a result of exposure to Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B during production and handling.

The species Trichoderma atroviride is not included in the EFSA qualified presumption of safety (QPS) list, as filamentous fungi are currently excluded from QPS evaluation due to safety and complexity considerations (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2025).

Current methods for testing dermal sensitisation are not suitable for microorganisms, and no validated test methods exist for assessing sensitisation via inhalation. Based on their characteristics, microorganisms such as Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B shall be regarded as potential sensitisers. Therefore, the non‐specific labelling with the warning phrase ‘Contains Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B. Microorganisms may have the potential to provoke sensitising reactions’ is recommended.

Due to their pathogenic effects on other fungi, several Trichoderma species are used in biocontrol. There is no known specific human or animal (pets or livestock) pathogen within the genus Trichoderma, while some opportunistic infection has been reported (see medical information reported in this section).

No signs of toxicity or pathogenicity have been detected (technical grade material) after acute administration via oral and intratracheal route in rats of the active Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B. Microbial analysis of fluids and tissues/organs was only investigated after oral and intratracheal exposure and demonstrated full clearance (no infectivity) of the active organism.

Skin and eye irritation has been investigated in rabbits after exposure to the formulation for representative uses; slight and reversible effects were observed. The use of personal protective equipment (PPE) can be considered as non‐specific risk mitigation measure since microorganisms are also potential sensitisers.

It is concluded that Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B has no potential for infectivity/pathogenicity in humans based on the available data.

Mammalian toxicity of the metabolites of (potential) concern

Among the metabolites of potential concern, Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B can produce the volatile compound 6‐PP (6‐pentyl‐2H‐pyran‐2‐one or 6‐pentyl‐alpha‐pyrone) and other metabolites known to be produced by the genus such as anthraquinones (e.g. genotoxic emodin), peptaibols, polyketides, cytochalasans and camptothecin.

Toxicological information from the literature was provided on 6‐PP and emodin only. No information has been submitted on the other metabolites that might be produced by Trichoderma atroviride 77B (see Section 2), since they are not considered of concern based on the lack of evidence indicating their toxicological relevance (e.g. camptothecins, cytochalasins, peptaibols and polyketides).

It is noted that 6‐PP (6‐pentyl‐2H‐pyran‐2‐one) is a volatile organic compound authorised as a food flavouring substance (FL no. 10.031) by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 872/2012. 10 No analytical identification of 6‐PP demonstrating its production by Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B is available (see Section 2). Based on the assessment for the flavouring, a NOAEL of 12.1 mg/kg bw/day was established in a 90‐day study in rats with 6‐PP. The substance was concluded not to pose safety concerns at estimated levels of intake as flavouring substance (EFSA CEF Panel, 2011) (see Section 4), and information from available literature on other Trichoderma strains indicated that it would not be of toxicological concern.

Adverse effects in the respiratory tract of 6‐PP (irritation and histamine release) are not expected to be of concern for non‐dietary exposure, considering that personal protective equipment (PPE) is required for microbial PPPs. For bystanders and residents, the non‐dietary exposure resulting from the supported uses is not expected to trigger a concern, considering the available weight of evidence from the food flavouring assessment of 6‐PP and the lowest threshold for classification as sensitiser.

Based on an EFSA Scientific Opinion, 11 aloe‐emodin (belonging to anthraquinones' family and structurally related to emodin) is a potential genotoxic compound. The lack of WGS analysis and analytical determination does not allow to exclude potential concerns based on exposure considerations (data gap, see Sections 2 and 9.1).

The RMS and Member States (MSs) supported the view that no metabolites of potential concern for human health associated with Trichoderma atroviride 77B are identified. Regarding 6‐PP, it is approved as food flavouring, while the production of emodin is expected to be negligible in the technical material and expected to be at low levels after application.

EFSA is of the view that the identification and the exposure to metabolites of potential concern for dietary and non‐dietary (e.g. emodin) risk assessment cannot be finalised based on the available data (e.g. available literature data as well as lack of analytical determination) (see data gap, see Sections 2 and 9.1).

All the experts agreed that the WGS analysis would be useful to exclude the presence of the gene cluster for emodin and other metabolites.

In situ production of secondary metabolites of potential toxicological concern (such as emodin) cannot be excluded based on the available data, and if demonstrated, further toxicological investigation might be needed (see Section 4).

The available information is not sufficient to conclude on the production and identification of other metabolites (including individual metabolites belonging to various families/groups) by Trichoderma atroviride 77B (see data gaps in Sections 2 and 9.1). Pending their identification, further investigations on their toxicological properties might be needed to conclude on the safety of non‐dietary exposure to the product (data gap, see Section 9.1).

RESIDUES

For what concerns the inclusion into Annex IV of Regulation (EC) N° 396/2005 the assessment in the residue section is considering the Commission guidance SANCO/11188/2013 Rev. 2 (European Commission, 2015). Trichoderma atroviride 77B is not included in approved as a basic substance under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (Criterion I). It is also not listed in Annex I of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 (Criterion II).

Trichoderma atroviridae 77B was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review TC 177 in June 2025 12 (joint toxicology and residue discussion). The secondary metabolite emodin may have adverse effects on human health (see Section 3.2). No residue data for the representative uses on secondary metabolites are provided.

Based on the available data, hazardous properties of secondary metabolites cannot be excluded and criterion III of the guidance is not considered to be met.

The representative uses foresee foliar spray treatments until harvest (BBCH 89) up to 10 foliar spray applications on tomato and peppers (outdoor and indoor); up to seven on strawberries (outdoor and indoor); and up to four on grapes (outdoor). A growth stage of BBCH 89 represents edible commodities at the harvest stage and consumer exposure cannot be excluded (criterion V of the guidance is not considered to be met). Furthermore, potential in situ formed metabolites (e.g. emodin) cannot be excluded and were not investigated.

Therefore, a data gap has been set for qualitative and quantitative information of residues of metabolites of potential concern (e.g. emodin) (see Sections 2 and 3.2) on edible commodities under conditions of the representative uses for the indoor uses of tomato, peppers and strawberries and for the outdoor uses including the representative use on grapes (for data gap, see Section 9.1).

A data gap for adequate experimental evidence on the absence/presence, persistence and multiplication of viable cells of Trichoderma atroviride 77B on edible commodities (tomatoes, peppers, strawberries and grapes) after treatment according to the representative uses is set. EFSA concluded in the residue expert meeting 13 that no experimental data are available to substantiate the levels of emodin and 6‐PP that are occurring on the treated crops or endophytically under representative conditions of use (data gap, see Section 9.1). EFSA considers that the potential formation of 6‐PP does not raise a concern for consumers. This conclusion is supported by a previous assessment of 6‐PP as a food flavouring (see Section 3.2), in which no safety concerns were identified at an estimated intake of 84 µg/person/day. Should Trichoderma atroviride 77B produce 6‐PP, the consideration of the final application rate before harvest and average EU crop yields (FAO, 2026) for the representative uses, the results of secondary metabolite production experiments with Trichoderma strains in the literature, and the high volatility of metabolite 6‐PP indicate that 6‐PP residues on crops are likely to be very low, if present at all. Consequently, dietary exposure is expected to be well below the intake level established as safe by the EFSA CEF Panel in its assessment of 6‐PP as a flavouring. The RMS and MS experts expressed the view that there are no metabolites of concern identified based on expected low levels after application (see Section 3.2).

Consequently, there is insufficient information to conclude whether exposure to viable and/or non‐viable residues (referring to emodin, a metabolite of potential concern) linked to the representative uses of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B on edible commodities is lower, equal to or higher than concentrations naturally occurring (see Sections 2 and 3.2) (criterion IV of the guidance is not fulfilled).

The available data and information seem insufficient to finalise the consumer risk assessment because the extent of the consumer exposure to metabolites of potential concern (e.g. emodin) cannot be assessed (see Sections 2, 3.2 and 9.1).

In conclusion, none of the criteria of the above‐mentioned guidance can be concluded as met.

ENVIRONMENTAL FATE AND BEHAVIOUR

Satisfactory information has been provided in relation to potential interference of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B with the analytical systems for the control of the quality of drinking water provided for in Directive 98/83/EC (see specific Annex VI decision‐making criteria in Part II of Commission Regulation (EU) No 546/2011). As these methods require pathogenic bacteria to be identified and confirmed as absent, it was considered unlikely that filamentous fungi or their conidia would interfere with methodologies used for such determinations.

During the peer review, a data requirement was identified to update the scientific literature search to address the limited information available in the dossier concerning environmental exposure. This updated literature review should have considered all potentially relevant taxonomic levels for the environmental risk assessment (i.e. the Trichoderma genus, Trichoderma atroviride species and other phylogenetically related Trichoderma species). The purpose of this review was to enable a more comprehensive characterisation of the potential environmental risks associated with the proposed uses of Trichoderma atroviride 77B as a pesticide, including those related to secondary metabolites of potential concern. However, the applicant did not provide an updated literature review, and this remains identified as a data gap (see Section 10).

Fate and behaviour in the environment of the microorganism and any other forms

A study measuring the persistence and multiplication in soil of Trichoderma atroviride 77B has not been provided. The need for data to demonstrate the decline of Trichoderma atroviride 77B in soil was identified during the peer review. Since no further data specific to that strain has been provided, the conclusion on persistence and multiplication in soil is based on data available on other strains of this species. A study monitoring selected strains of T. atroviride and T. harzianum under field conditions after their application to the soil has been provided. The introduced fungi were monitored during the period of lettuce cultivation in three different fields and in two subsequent vegetative seasons after lettuce harvest. Microbiological (agar plates) and molecular methods (PCR) were used for identification and quantification. The persistence of Trichoderma strains in the soil was maintained at elevated levels of 105–106 CFU/g soil for at least 10 weeks after fungal application in all three experiments. The number of Trichoderma spp. that naturally inhabit the soil of the experimental field reached up to 103–104 CFU/g soil. After 2 years, T. atroviride and T. harzianum were detected in soil at concentrations of 8× 104 and 9 × 104 CFU/g soil. These values were significantly higher than for control soil (0.48 × 104 CFU/g soil). This study was not performed according to GLP and it is not confirmed if it was performed by an officially recognised testing facility. Additional information sourced from the open literature shows that Trichoderma species can persist in soil for more than 2 years above the natural background level following open field application. It should be also noted that the growth of Trichoderma species can fluctuate seasonally, influenced by factors such as nutrient availability, environmental conditions and competition in soil with other microorganisms. Therefore, information on infectivity and pathogenicity to non‐target organisms is necessary (see Section 6).

The worst‐case scenario PED soil is calculated to be 6.67 × 104 CFU/g dry soil that would result following the application of the MPCP according to the proposed uses (see Appendix A). Whereas this value represents a worst case when a single application is considered, this value does not necessarily account for potential accumulation when the microorganism is applied over several seasons on the same field. Applicant should demonstrate that available PEDs in soil for field uses do address plateau levels after application in repeated seasons (data gap, see Section 10).

Studies retrieved from the literature show that strains of Trichoderma species (including Trichoderma atroviride) can survive and proliferate in aquatic environments (including maritime waters and drinking water sources). Several of these studies were considered of limited reliability by the RMS. The applicant provided a study performed under GLP investigating the survival of Trichoderma atroviride 77B after application of the MPCP in sterilised water at 12°C under irradiated and dark conditions with and without supplementary glucose. Trichoderma atroviride 77B population numbers remain stable over the investigated period of 10 days in sterile synthetic surface water under both light and dark conditions and with and without amendment with glucose. Slight proliferation is observed only in the experiment under dark conditions without glucose (up to 114% after 10 days). The results cannot be extrapolated to other conditions including higher temperatures and natural waters. During the peer review, a data gap was identified for the applicant to substantiate the fate and behaviour of Trichoderma atroviride 77B in surface water and sediment compartments considering all the life cycle phases, including spores (data gap, see Section 10).

Although inadequate information was provided on the survival, competitiveness and population dynamics of Trichoderma atroviride 77B in air, survival and competitiveness are expected to be limited as the strain under evaluation is a soil organism. However, conidia and spores of Trichoderma atroviride 77B may be dispersed by air to nearby areas surrounding the application site.

Although inadequate information was provided on the mobility of Trichoderma atroviride 77B, horizontal and vertical mobility of Trichoderma atroviride 77B in soil is limited although dispersal to adjacent soil habitats or in groundwater cannot be excluded for the representative uses. As it has been concluded that Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B has no potential for infectivity/pathogenicity in humans (see Section 3), its eventual presence in groundwater or drinking water is considered of no concern for human or animal health.

Fate and behaviour in the environment of secondary metabolite(s) of (potential) concern produced by the microorganism under relevant conditions

Trichoderma species in general and Trichoderma atroviride have been reported to produce many toxins/secondary metabolites (see e.g. Li et al., 2019; Missbach et al., 2023). 14 The production of secondary metabolites is highly strain specific and can vary depending on environmental conditions and the presence of other organisms.

The presence of genes encoding secondary metabolites in the genome of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B has not been investigated. There is insufficient evidence to unequivocally demonstrate that Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B does not produce secondary metabolites of potential concern (see Section 2). A data gap was identified during the peer review to address the production by Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B of metabolites of potential concern for human and animal health and for non‐target species (see Sections 3 and 6). Concentration of those metabolites potentially present in the environmental compartments (soil, surface water, groundwater or air) after application of the product according to the representative use will need to be determined. POTENTIAL EFFECTS ON NON‐TARGET ORGANISMS

Birds and mammals

Only one study with the active substance was available for birds. No adverse effects were reported, and no indication of infectivity or pathogenicity was reported. The available information was considered sufficient to finalise the assessment of infectivity and pathogenicity of T. atroviride strain 77B to birds, and it can be concluded that the active substance is neither infective nor pathogenic to birds.

Studies are available for effects on the rat. Based on the data provided, there is no indication that the T. atroviride strain 77B is infective or pathogenic to wild mammals (see also Section 3).

The available literature studies revealed the potential for T. atroviride strain 77B to produce various secondary metabolites (see Sections 2 and 5.2). As no information is available on the possible adverse effects for birds and wild mammals, it is not possible to exclude potential concerns. Hence, the identification, hazard characterisation and exposure assessment of secondary metabolites produced by T. atroviride strain 77B could not be finalised with the limited data set and a low risk cannot be concluded for the intended uses in field and non‐permanent structures (data gap leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). Based on the representative use in permanent structures of T. atroviride strain 77B and in line with EFSA (2015), minimal exposure to secondary metabolites is expected; hence, low risk can be concluded for birds and mammals.

Aquatic organisms

Studies were not available with the active substance or the formulation for representative uses for any aquatic organism. Published peer‐reviewed open literature studies were available for T. atroviride. The available information and the risk assessment were discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 178 (June 2025). 15 The experts at the meeting considered the information from the advice of the EFSA Working Group on microbial pesticides and noted that several lines of evidence could be used to address the risk for aquatic organisms. While the RMS indicated that the generation of additional data would not be necessary, the experts noted that aquatic studies with the formulation are ongoing and expected to be submitted for the product authorisation. Considering the information currently available, the experts at the meeting agreed it is not sufficient to finalise the aquatic risk assessment. Data were available for aquatic invertebrates and algae from the EFSA Conclusion on T. atroviride strain AGR2 (EFSA, 2022), resulting in a low risk for these organism groups for the representative use. Thus, based on the read‐across, Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B is not expected to have adverse effects from infectivity and pathogenicity on aquatic invertebrates and algae. 16 However, a data gap leading to an assessment not finalised is identified for the potential infectivity and pathogenicity to fish and potential adverse effects to aquatic plants other than algae from T. atroviride strain 77B for the representative use (see Section 9.1).

No indication is available with regard to the production of secondary metabolites in the aquatic environments. Toxicity data were not available for secondary metabolites to perform a hazard characterisation (data gap leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1), except for several peptaibols for which literature data indicated potential toxicity to aquatic invertebrates and aquatic larval stages of terrestrial invertebrates. The risk assessment for secondary metabolites could not be finalised for aquatic organisms (fish, freshwater invertebrates, algae and aquatic plants other than algae) for the intended uses (see also Section 5.2).

Bees

The available information is sufficient to finalise the assessment of infectivity and pathogenicity. No clear and consistent indication of infectivity or pathogenicity can be derived. The information available from the peer‐reviewed literature indicates that Trichoderma‐based products do not affect the bumble bees' adults and larvae, as Trichoderma spp. were not able to survive and proliferate on the bumble bees' bodies. This extrapolation was accepted during the peer review of the active substance. Based on the available laboratory studies, a sufficiently large margin of safety (MoS) was derived for oral and contact exposure. It is considered that, in combination with the peer‐reviewed literature, sufficient information is available to conclude that the active substance is neither infective nor pathogenic to honeybees.

No information was available demonstrating that the potentially produced metabolites of concern were present in the studies. Toxicity data were not available for secondary metabolites to perform a hazard characterisation (data gap leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). The risk assessment for secondary metabolites could not be finalised for honey bees for the representative uses in field and non‐permanent structures (see also Section 5.2). The RMS disagreed and considered the risk assessment for secondary metabolites could be considered as finalised. Based on the representative use in permanent structures of T. atroviride strain 77B and in line with EFSA (2015), minimal exposure to secondary metabolites is expected; hence, low risk can be concluded for bees. A low risk can be concluded for bees for the intended uses in permanent structures, as exposure to secondary metabolites can be considered minimal.

Non‐target arthropods other than bees

No studies were provided with T. atroviride strain 77B. Only one peer‐reviewed open literature study was provided on the effects of other Trichoderma spp. on termites (e.g. Coptotermes formosanus and Odontotermes formosanus). This study was accepted during the peer review to be used in the assessment of T. atroviride strain 77B. While some behavioural changes were reported, no adverse effect was highlighted. Based on the available data, a margin of safety (MoS) for non‐target arthropods of > 0.001 is derived following the proposed applications of T. atroviride strain 77B. Nevertheless, considering the information available in the DAR regarding (i) the lack of reported infectivity or pathogenicity of Trichoderma atroviride strain 77B, (ii) the available peer‐reviewed literature in the DAR, (iii) the lack of reported effects, the available information is considered sufficient to finalise the assessment of infectivity and pathogenicity for T. atroviride strain 77B. It can be concluded that the active substance is neither infective nor pathogenic to non‐target arthropods.

Sufficient data were not available on the effects of secondary metabolites produced by T. atroviride strain 77B on non‐target arthropods other than bees (data gap, leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). A low risk cannot be concluded for the intended uses in field and non‐permanent structures. The RMS disagreed and considered the risk assessment for secondary metabolites could be considered as finalised. Based on the representative use in permanent structures of T. atroviride strain 77B and in line with EFSA (2015), minimal exposure to secondary metabolites is expected; hence, low risk can be concluded for non‐target arthropods other than bees.

Soil organisms

No study is available for soil macro‐organisms other than earthworms. No study is available with the active substance or the representative formulation for earthworms. Limited peer‐reviewed open literature studies are available with other Trichoderma spp. Adverse effects were not observed in relevant studies. The studies found no evidence of infectivity or pathogenicity.

The available data were considered sufficient to finalise the infectivity and pathogenicity assessment of T. atroviride strain 77B to earthworms.

Toxicity data were not available for secondary metabolites to perform a hazard characterisation (data gap leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). The risk assessment for secondary metabolites could not be finalised for earthworms for the representative uses in field and non‐permanent structures (see also Section 5.2). The RMS disagreed and considered the risk assessment for secondary metabolites could be considered as finalised. Based on the representative use in permanent structures of T. atroviride strain 77B and in line with EFSA (2015), minimal exposure to secondary metabolites is expected; hence, low risk can be concluded for earthworms.

No study is available with the active substance or the representative formulation for soil microorganisms. Limited peer‐reviewed open literature studies are available with other Trichoderma spp. No study indicating adverse effects for non‐target soil microorganisms from T. atroviride was found in the review by Mudgal et al. (2013). The available information was considered sufficient to finalise the risk assessment. A low risk can be concluded for soil microorganisms for all intended uses.

Additional information

During the peer review, EFSA became aware of literature studies carried out with T. harzianum (now T. afroharzianum) strains T39 and T12 reporting pathogenicity to maize. These studies are not available in the DAR. It should be noted that plant pathogens should be generally considered host and strain specific. Hence, extrapolation from maize to other non‐target terrestrial plants and from T. atroviride strain 77B to other strains or Trichoderma spp. (and vice versa) is uncertain. Therefore, with the available data, it cannot be concluded whether T. atroviride strain 77B has the potential to exert a pathogenic effect on terrestrial plants.
