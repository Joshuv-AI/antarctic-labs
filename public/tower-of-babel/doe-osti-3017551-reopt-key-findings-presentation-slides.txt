 Modeling Approach
The below REopt analysis was performed based on direction and interest from the
community of Shelter Island:
 • Eight sites (commercial and residential) in Shelter Island were considered for
 distributed (behind-the-meter) solar and battery storage installations.
 • The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) software REopt was used to
 optimally size distributed solar and battery storage equipment by minimizing life-
 cycle costs.
 • A preliminary analysis was conducted for all eight sites, providing solar and
 storage sizes that minimize cost or satisfy other objectives including emissions
 reductions.
 • Three sites were subsequently chosen for a “deep dive” analysis: residential
 home, school, and recycling center.

 Key Takeaways: Preliminary Analysis
• Solar is cost-effective for all analyzed buildings: payback periods between 7 and 10 years regardless of incentives

 • Residential buildings (90% of island load): good case for solar deployment with a short payback period—approximately 7 years

• Battery storage (combined with solar) is also cost-effective, with some considerations:

 ◦ Demand charges ($/kW) from commercial rates make batteries more cost-effective

 ◦ Not recommended for residential buildings under classic rate 180 (no time-of-day component), but cost-effective under time-of-day (TOD) rates

• Time-of-day rates must be analyzed case-by-case:

 ◦ Residential super off-peak rates are beneficial (+20% value) when using battery storage, whereas off-peak rates are not

 ◦ In general, TOD rates are not cost-effective (solar or battery) for commercial buildings, except for the Police Station

• School building is a good option for providing resiliency as an emergency shelter:

 ◦ Large electric load and space (compared to other analyzed buildings) yield a large solar and battery storage system

 ◦ System is also economically favorable for the resiliency scenario (up to 14 days)

 ◦ Good location for deploying large backup batteries, operating them for cost reduction during most of the year, and charging them before severe weather and/or
 expected outage conditions

 ◦ Possible disadvantage: critical load may be large during long outages (assuming school would be operating).

 Key Takeaways: Deep Dive Analysis
• New TOD utility rates provide marginal gains over classic rates
  for solar only.
• However, TOD utility rates present higher cost savings, when
  coupled with battery storage, for residential buildings (with
  additional resiliency benefits).
• Per community request to examine emissions impacts:
  • If the main goal is to reduce emissions, switching from a furnace (oil or propane)
 to an air-source heat pump should be the focus. The reduction in direct emissions
 is significantly higher than the reduction in indirect (grid) emissions from solar and
 batteries.

 Solar Net Metering Summary
•  Residential:
 o PV rated capacity ≤25 kW (according to Long Island Power Authority [LIPA]) and 110% of site load (annual kWh).
 o Excess generation paid monthly as a credit in the next bill (same rate as customers without generation).
• Commercial:
 o PV rated capacity ≤750 kW (according to LIPA) and 110% of site load (annual kWh).
 o Excess generation paid monthly as a credit in the next bill (same rate as customers without generation).
 o Larger systems (>750 kW or 110% annual kWh) are allowed as “Large On-site/Off-site” customers but paid differently (value of distributed
 energy resources [VDER] rate).
• Remote net metering (RNM):
 o Allows generation from a “host” PV array (location/meter) to offset other “satellite” meters.
 o Same customer/corporation must own/lease the property designated to the host and satellite accounts.
 o RNM eliminates demand ratchet (75% of the highest demand over previous year) for host and satellite meters—very beneficial.
• Community solar:
 o Customer signs up for the community solar project with a certain subscription percentage and pays a fee to a generator facility.
 o Community solar project feeds electricity directly to the grid.
 o Production credits are distributed to the customers based on their subscriber percentage.
 o The allocated kWh is included on the customer’s bill as a credits.
Note: According to LIPA tariff, a customer can participate in only one of the above. Details on net metering are provided in the appendix.

 Preliminary Analysis Key Findings
• Solar only:
 o Solar is cost-effective for all sites (payback periods from 7 to 10 years).
 o Solar is sized to maximize either net metering limit (110% annual kWh) or rooftop space.
 o Solar/load fraction between ~60% and 100% (electricity share offset by solar).
• Solar + battery: Batteries are also cost-effective for all sites, except for residential buildings under classic utility rate.
• Net-zero energy: Offsetting energy consumption is not always possible given net metering and rooftop space constraints.
• Net-zero emissions (indirect grid emissions): Completely offsetting indirect emissions is possible but expensive (requires
  very large battery).
• Utility rates: TOD rates enable batteries and greater savings for residential buildings but are not cost-effective for most
  commercial buildings (compared to classic rates).
• Energy efficiency: Reduces site load and solar sizes proportionally resulting in additional cost savings (reduces net metering
  limit).
• Resiliency (assumed providing 50% load need for three outage scenarios):
 o Cost-effective for all sites except Sylvester Manor (Farmstand meter).
 o Similar results for 3–14 days of resiliency (self-sufficient system; solar recharges battery).
• Incentives:
 o Reduces payback periods proportionally to 4–7 years.
 o Residential buildings have higher cost savings because of the New York state solar incentive.

 Net Metering Policy From LIPA Tariff
 • Residential net metering:
 o PV rated capacity ≤25 kW (according to LIPA) and 110% of site load (annual kWh).
 o Excess generation is paid monthly as a credit in the next bill (same rate as customers without generation).
 • Commercial net metering
 o PV rated capacity ≤750 kW (according to LIPA) and 110% of site load (annual kWh).
 o Excess generation is paid monthly as a credit in the next bill (same rate as customers without generation).
 o Larger systems (>750 kW or 110% annual kWh) are allowed as “Large On-site/Off-site” customers but paid differently (as VDER).
 • Remote net metering (RNM):
 o Allows generation from a “host” PV array (location/meter) to offset other “satellite” meters.
 o Same customer/corporation must own/lease the property designated to the host and satellite accounts.
 o Customers are not required to own the generating equipment at the host site.
 o RNM eliminates demand ratchet (75% of the highest demand over previous year) for host and satellite meters: very beneficial.
 o Monthly rollover: “If the credit designated for an individual Satellite Account exceeds the charges on that bill, the remaining balance of the credit allocated to that individual Satellite
 Account will be returned to the Host Account for distribution in the following month.”
 o Annual excess purchase: “Once every 12 months, on the customer’s anniversary date, any credits still remaining with the Host Account will be converted back from monetary credits to
 kWh credits, and purchased by us at the same rate for annual reconciliation specified for stand-alone net metering customers.”
 o RNM participants (both host and satellites) are considered “Large Off-site” customers according to LIPA, which are rewarded via VDER.
 • Other considerations:
 o PSEGLI net metering policy lacks transparency (especially for commercial customers). Information was mainly derived from LIPA’s tariff sheet.
 o There is a national trend of reducing benefits of solar net metering programs (e.g., VDER tariff in New York or Net Energy Metering 2.0/3.0 in California). PSEGLI has not yet fully
 incorporated the new VDER tariff (separate governing authority), so it might be beneficial to accelerate deployment to take advantage of original net metering program.
 o Interconnection costs are paid by the customer.

 Community Solar
• Referred to as “Community Distributed Generation” (CDG) on LIPA’s tariff.
• CDG host: nonresidential customer.
• Satellite customers may own or contract for a portion of the excess generation accumulated at the meter of the CDG host.
• The CDG host will be assigned to an applicable nonresidential service classification.
• Customers (satellites) can subscribe only to one community solar host.
• CDG customers cannot participate in net metering or remote net metering programs.
• Percentage of a satellite customer must be greater than 1,000 kWh annually (~2.7 kWh/day or 83 kWh/month).
• No more than 40% of the excess generation of the CDG host may serve CDG satellites with a maximum annual demand of 25 kW or greater
 (multi-unit buildings can be divided into individual unit meters).
• What happens if the host cannot find enough satellite customers to distribute the entire load?
 o “Any Excess Generation remaining after the allocation will remain with the CDG Host account as an energy credit to be allocated to the
 Satellite accounts in future billing periods.”
• Excess generation allocated to the satellite account will be applied to the CDG satellite account as if the customer were individually net
 metered.
• Decision making:
 o Price: Contractors will have an overhead fee but enjoy economies of scale. Should compare cost savings and investments over the
 lifetime of the project (e.g., 20 years).
 o Other considerations: Amount that can be purchased (community solar may have limited availability whereas your rooftop can
 accommodate more and vice versa); community solar cannot be used for resiliency (charge batteries).

 period.

• Net present value (NPV) of the DER system: The life-cycle cost savings (difference in LCC) between the
 business-as-usual (BAU) case and the investment case (OPT).

 NPV LCCBAU LCCOPT REopt identifies the life-cycle cost-optimal
